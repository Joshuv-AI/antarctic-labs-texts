Histone acetyltransferase KAT6A contributes to colon cancer malignant progression by inhibiting ferroptosis
Authors: Junyu Huo, Hongyuan Chen, Xiaoqiao Zhang, Dong Sun, Hongguang Sheng, Yugang Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColon cancer (CC) is a malignant cancer with high incidence and poor prognosis.Ferroptosis could induce iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxidation and oxidative death of cancer cells. This work aimed to elucidate the role of KAT6A, a lysine acetyltransferase, in CC development.MethodsWe collected tumor and paired non-tumor samples from patients with CC and analyzed the RNA and protein level of KAT6A using quantitative real-time PCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. We conducted KAT6A knockdown in CC cells and determined cell proliferation and ferroptosis. Cell proliferation was measured by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) and colony formation assay. Ferroptosis was identified by measuring the levels of lipid ROS, intracellular iron and Fe2+, malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH). The in vivo effects of KAT6A were assessed by xenograft mouse model. Protein levels of KAT6A and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) were checked by western blotting assay. The enticement of acetylation on lysine 9 of histone3 (H3K9ac) and RNA polymerase II on GPX4 gene was analyzed by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay.ResultsThe RNA and protein level of KAT6A is notably higher in tumor tissues compared with the non-tumor sections. Depletion of KAT6A suppressed in vitro and in vivo CC cell growth. Overexpression of KAT6A reversed the erastin-induced CC cell death. knockdown of KAT6A significantly elevated the intracellular level of MDA, induced accumulation of total iron, Fe2+ and lipid ROS, and suppressed the level of GSH. The knockdown of KAT6A caused a decrease in the expression of GPX4 and enrichment of H3K9ac on GPX4 gene.ConclusionKAT6A promotes the proliferation of CC cells and suppresses ferroptosis via epigenetic regulation of GPX4. Our work presented KAT6A as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target for treatment CC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15368-2.

Introduction

Colon cancer (CC) ranks the third in cancer incidence and the second in mortality rate according to the latest epidemiology, which causes a huge burden on health and the economy [1]. Great advancement has been made in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of CC such as immunotherapy and targeted treatment [2, 3]. However, the current clinical management is still far from achieving satisfactory results. The prognosis of CC patients varies in individuals. Although prognostic factors, such as staging and cancer embryo antigen (CEA), have been established, the accuracy for predicting the prognosis of patients remains unsatisfactory [4, 5]. It is still imperative to discover more accurate prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets to guide clinical management and achieve better prognosis.

Ferroptosis is a newly recognized non-apoptosis-regulated cell death manner that characterized by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxidation and subsequent oxidative death [6]. Several intrinsic signaling regulators and small molecular inhibitors and inducers have been identified to modulate the process of ferroptosis [7]. System xc-(xCT) that mainly composed of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) transports intracellular glutamate to the extracellular space and extracellular cystine into the cell, which is then transformed into cysteine for glutathione (GSH) synthesis [8, 9]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) reduces the endogenous neutralization of PUFAs-OOH to PUFAs-OH, ultimately reducing ROS accumulation [10, 11]. The SLC7A11 and GPX4 hence are regarded as indicators of ferroptosis and ideal therapeutic targets for cancer treatment [12]. Previous studies have emphasized the important role of ferroptosis in several cancers including hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, and other digestive system tumors such as CC [12].

Lysine acetyltransferase 6 A (KAT6A, also known as MOZ and MYST3) is a histone acetyltransferase belonging to the MYST family that catalyze the histone acetylation and gene expression [13]. KAT6A consists of a nuclear positioning domain, a double C2H2 zinc finger domain bound to the tail of acetylated histone, a HAT domain, an area rich in glutamate/aspartic acid, and a sub-activated domain rich in serine and methionine [14]. KAT6A is reported to play a key role in hematopoietic stem cell maintenance, cell cycle regulation and cell aging [15]. In addition, KAT6A is considered to be an oncogene in human cancers, including breast cancer, glioma and leukemia [15–17]. It is reported that KAT6A can not only catalyze histone acetylation, but also acetylation of non-histones [14]. KAT6A inhibitors have been developed and KAT6A inhibition presents great potential in the prospect of cancer treatment [18]. Noteworthy, the role of KAT6A in CC has not been reported yet.

In this work, we aimed to clarify the important role of KAT6A as epigenetic regulator in malignant progression, especially in ferroptosis, of CC.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimen

Patients with CC (n = 50) that hospitalized in Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University from December 2020 to August 2022 were recruited in this study. The tumor tissues and paired non-tumor sections were collected during surgery. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) or fast-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experiments. All patients have signed the informed consents before the study.

Cell lines and treatment

The CC cell lines CACO2, HT29, LOVO, SW620, SW480, and normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460 were bought from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The NCM460 was cultured in RPMI 1640, HT29 in McCoy’5 A, and LOVO, SW620, SW480 in F12K medium that contains 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, USA). To induce ferroptosis, cells were treated with erastin (5 µM) for 24 h.

Cell transfection

The siKAT6A, KAT6A overexpression vectors, GPX4 overexpression vectors, and negative control vectors were synthesized by GenePharma (China). Cell transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to manufacturer’s instruction.

Cell viability and proliferation

Cell viability was checked by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China) and colony formation assay. For CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, respectively. Then CCK-8 reagent was added into each well and incubate for 2 h. The absorbance values at 450 nm were measured and calculated for cell viability.

To conduct colony formation experiment, 1000 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated for 10 days. The visible colonies were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min. The number of colonies was counted and calculated.

Quantitative real-time PCR

The total RNA was extracted from CC tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent and was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using SuperScript III reagent (Thermo, USA). The gene expression was quantified by using SYBR (Thermo, USA) and normalized to GAPDH expression. The gene expression was calculated following the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Western blotting

Total protein extracts from tissues or cells were obtained using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). After quantification using BCA assay kit(Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), 30 µg protein were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes(Millipore, USA). The blots were blocked in 5% skim milk and probed with primary antibodies against KAT6A(1:2000, HY-P86929, MCE, Shanghai, China), GPX4(1:1000, HY-P80450, MCE, Shanghai, China), SLC7A11(1:1000,A13685,ABclonal, Wuhan, China), and β-actin (1:1000,4967,Cell Signaling Technology, USA) at 4 °C overnight. Next day, the bolts were probed with secondary antibody IgG and ECL reagent for visualization.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The enrichment of H3K9ac and RNA polymerase II on the GPX4 gene promoter was analyzed using a ChIP assay kit (EZ-CHIP, Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into DNA fragments with an average size of 500 bp. The sheared chromatin was then immunoprecipitated by incubation with anti-H3K9ac antibody (1:25, 9649,Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control) at 4 °C overnight with rotation. After a series of washes, the precipitated protein-DNA complexes were eluted and reverse cross-linked. The DNA was purified by treatment with RNase A and proteinase K, followed by phenol-chloroform extraction. The enrichment of specific GPX4 promoter regions was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the following primers:

Forward:5’-AGGGATTAGAGTCCAGGCGA-3’,reverse:5’-CCCAACAGTTCCTCCTGCAA-3’.

In vivo model

All animal experiments were conducted following the approval by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. BALB/c SCID mice that aged 5-wek-old were bought from Vital River Laboratory (Beijing, China). The mice were inoculated with 5 × 106 cells/50 µl PBS per site in the right fat pad of mice. After culture for 4 weeks, the mice were euthanized using cervical dislocation without the use of anesthetic drugs to avoid causing unnecessary pain to the animals, and the tumors were removed and photographed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm thick slices. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-KAT6A (1:2000), anti-GPX4 (1:500), and anti-SLC7A11 (1:200). After washing, the sections were incubated with a HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate kit, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were imaged under a light microscope.

IHC scoring was performed using the H‑score method, which was calculated as follows: H‑score = ∑(Pi × i), where Pi represents the percentage of stained tumor cells (0–100%) at each intensity score i (0: no staining, 1: weak, 2: moderate, 3: strong). The final H‑score ranges from 0 to 300.

Detection of ferroptosis

The ferroptosis was identified by measurement of lipid ROS, intracellular iron and Fe2+, malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) levels using C11-BODIPY (Thermo, USA), Iron Assay Kit (Abcam, USA), MDA assay kit, and GSH assay kit, respectively.

Statistics

Data in this research are shown as mean ± SD of at least three biological repeat and analyzed using SPSS software (Version 20.0). The comparison between two or multiple groups was conducted using student’s t test and ANOVA analysis with Tukey’s post hoc test. Values with p less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

KAT6A is highly expressed in CC clinical samples

To determine the role of KAT6A in CC, we first detected the expression of KAT6A in clinical CC samples. In this study, 50 patients were included, and their clinical information is shown in Table S1. There were no significant differences in the expression of KAT6A in cancer tissues among patients with different ages, genders, M stages, or differentiation degrees. However, T stage and N stage were significantly correlated with the expression level of KAT6A. As shown in Fig. 1A and D, the RNA and protein level of KAT6A were notably higher in tumor tissues compared with the non-tumor sections. On the other hand, the ferroptosis suppressors GPX4 and SLC7A11 also exhibited notably enhanced expression in CC tumor tissues compared with the non-tumor parts (Fig. 1B-D). Correlation analysis of the expression levels of KAT6A, GPX4, and SLC7A11 showed that KAT6A was significantly positively correlated with GPX4 expression, while it was positively correlated with SLC7A11 expression, but not significantly(Fig. 1F-G). These data indicated that KAT6A may act as a promoter in CC development and functions through regulating ferroptosis.

KAT6A regulates ferroptosis of CC cells

Subsequently, we explored whether KAT6A regulated CC cell ferroptosis in vitro. We firstly measured the expression of KAT6A in several CC cell lines and selected appropriate cell lines for following experiments. We observed that the expression of KAT6A was significantly elevated in CC cells, including the CACO2, HT29, LOVO, SW620 and SW480, compared with normal colon mucosal epithelial cell line NCM460 (Fig. 1E). We selected the SW620 and SW480 for subsequent knockdown experiments due to their high expression of KAT6A.

We treated CC cells with ferroptosis inducer erastin and performed KAT6A overexpression to check the alteration of cell death and ferroptosis. The KAT6A overexpression vectors and siKAT6A significantly upregulated and downregulated the KAT6A level in CC cells, respectively (Fig. 2A). Results from CCK-8 and colony formation indicated that erastin significantly suppressed CC cells growth and overexpression of KAT6A reversed this effect (Fig. 2B-D). Moreover, knockdown of KAT6A significantly elevated the intracellular level of MDA (Fig. 2E), induced accumulation of total iron, Fe2+ and lipid ROS (Fig. 2F-H), and suppressed the level of GSH (Fig. 2I), suggesting the induction of ferroptosis. We also observed remarkably decreased expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 in SW620 and SW480 cells under depletion of KAT6A (Fig. 2J).

KAT6A epigenetically regulates GPX4 in CC cells

We wondered if KAT6A regulated the GPX4 expression in CC cells. Based on the function of KAT6A as acetyltransferase, we examined the acetylation of GPX4 gene under depletion of KAT6A. The knockdown of KAT6A led to decreased level of acetylation on lysine 9 of histone3 (H3K9ac) in both SW620 and SW480 cells (Fig. 3A). Further detection by ChIP assay demonstrated the decreased enrichment of H3K9ac and RNA polymerase II on GPX4 gene under depletion of KAT6A (Fig. 3B), suggesting the suppressed transcription of GPX4 gene. The mRNA level of GPX4 in CC cells was notably suppressed by KAT6A knockdown (Fig. 3C). These data indicated that KAT6A epigenetically regulated GPX4 in CC cells.

KAT6A modulates CC cell ferroptosis via regulating GPX4

Next, we explored whether KAT6A regulated CC cell death via targeting GPX4. We observed that knockdown of KAT6A suppressed the proliferation of CC cells along with suppressed GPX4 expression, whereas overexpression of GPX4 could suppressed this effect (Fig. 4A and B). Moreover, GPX4 could remarkably suppress the siKAT6A-induced ferroptosis in CC cells, manifested by decreased the intracellular level of MDA, suppressed accumulation of total iron, Fe2+ and lipid ROS, as well as elevated GSH level (Fig. 4C). These data demonstrated that KAT6A modulated CC ferroptosis via regulating GPX4.

KAT6A/GPX4 regulatory axis promotes in vivo CC tumor growth

Subsequently, we examined the effects of the KAT6A/GPX4 regulatory axis using an in vivo CC xenograft model. As shown in Fig. 5A-B, knockdown of KAT6A decreased the tumor growth and overexpression of GPX4 recovered tumor size. The suppressed GPX4 expression by siKAT6A was also observed in the xenograft model (Fig. 5C). Besides, KAT6A depletion notably elevated the level of MDA, stimulated accumulation of total iron, Fe2+ and lipid ROS, as well as decreased the GSH level in mouse tumors, whereas ectopic expression of GPX4 suppressed these phenomena (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Colon cancer remains a malignancy with poor prognosis that severely threatens patients’ lives. Despite therapeutic advancements over the past decades, clinical outcomes for CC patients remain unsatisfactory, underscoring the imperative to explore novel and efficient therapeutic targets. In this study, we investigated the role of KAT6A as a potential regulator of CC progression. KAT6A has been increasingly recognized for its correlation with cancer prognosis and participation in various oncogenic processes, including cell proliferation, stemness, and drug resistance [13, 17, 19]. Notably, our findings in CC align with reports from other malignancies; for instance, KAT6A amplification correlates with poor progression-free survival in endometrial serous carcinoma [16] and is considered a potential oncogene in breast cancer [20]. The significant upregulation of KAT6A we observed in CC tissues suggests it may similarly serve as an important prognostic marker in this malignancy, though this requires further clinical validation. Multiple signaling pathways have been identified to be regulated by KAT6A through its acetyltransferase function [21, 22], consistent with our observation that KAT6A depletion significantly suppressed both in vitro and in vivo growth of CC cells.

Previous studies have established KAT6A’s involvement in cancer progression through various mechanisms. In hepatocellular carcinoma, KAT6A expression correlates with sorafenib resistance and promotes progression through YAP signaling [23]. In glioblastoma, KAT6A acetylates histone H3 at lysine 23 (H3K23) to activate PIK3CA transcription via TRIM24 recruitment, consequently promoting PI3K/AKT signaling and facilitating proliferation and migration [22].Our study extends these findings to colorectal cancer by demonstrating that KAT6A epigenetically regulates GPX4 expression through H3K9 acetylation.

While we also observed downregulation of SLC7A11 following KAT6A depletion, our ChIP data specifically link KAT6A to the transcriptional activation of GPX4 rather than SLC7A11. This observation raises the intriguing possibility that KAT6A coordinates a broader ferroptosis-regulatory network. The downregulation of SLC7A11 may represent an indirect adaptation secondary to GPX4 inhibition and the ensuing lipid peroxidation stress, or it could be mediated through other KAT6A-dependent transcription factors. Future studies employing techniques such as ChIP-seq for KAT6A and H3K9ac will be essential to comprehensively map its genomic targets and definitively determine whether SLC7A11 is a direct target or a secondary consequence within this network.

Furthermore, it is important to distinguish the level of acetylome regulation addressed in this study. Our data unequivocally demonstrate that KAT6A promotes GPX4 transcription through histone acetylation at its gene locus. However, as a acetyltransferase, KAT6A’s potential to catalyze acetylation extends beyond histones to non-histone proteins. Therefore, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that KAT6A might also regulate GPX4 protein stability, localization, or activity through direct acetylation. This represents a distinct layer of post-translational regulation that remains to be explored. Investigating the GPX4 protein for potential KAT6A-dependent acetylation, and its functional consequences, constitutes an exciting and separate avenue for future research that would further elucidate the multifaceted regulation of ferroptosis.

The therapeutic potential of ferroptosis induction in cancer treatment has gained considerable attention [12]. Ferroptosis participates in multiple aspects of tumor biology, including growth, immune regulation, and metabolism [7]. GPX4 serves as a pivotal negative regulator of ferroptosis by reducing ROS accumulation and oxidative stress [24, 25]. making its inhibition a promising therapeutic strategy [26–28]. Small molecules like RSL3, which directly suppress GPX4 expression and activity, significantly induce ferroptosis in cancer cells and inhibit tumor growth [29]. In our study, KAT6A depletion led to characteristic ferroptotic features, including GPX4 downregulation, lipid ROS and Fe2+ accumulation, elevated MDA production, and decreased GSH levels. While these biomarkers are consistent with ferroptosis induction, we acknowledge that they may also be influenced by other cell death pathways. Future studies employing ferroptosis-specific inhibitors (e.g., ferrostatin-1) in KAT6A-silenced models would help confirm the exclusive dependence on ferroptotic pathways and strengthen this conclusion.

Furthermore, an important implication of our study lies in the potential impact of KAT6A-regulated ferroptosis on the metastatic cascade. Although the current work primarily focuses on cell proliferation and tumor growth, the interplay between ferroptosis and cellular plasticity could critically influence migration and invasion. Emerging evidence suggests that cells undergoing EMT, a key driver of metastasis, develop a heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis [30]. Therefore, we postulate a model wherein KAT6A, by sustaining GPX4 expression and suppressing ferroptosis, provides a crucial protective shield for colon cancer cells. This shield may enable them to withstand the substantial oxidative stress inherent to the EMT process and subsequent migration/invasion steps. Conversely, depletion of KAT6A would remove this protection, leading to ferroptotic death preferentially eliminating those cells with high metastatic potential. This intriguing hypothesis, which positions the KAT6A-GPX4 axis as a gatekeeper of ferroptosis-sensitive, pro-metastatic cell states, constitutes a major focus of our ongoing investigations.

From a translational medicine perspective, our findings provide a strong rationale for the therapeutic application of KAT6A inhibitors in colorectal cancer. Emerging studies have reported that specific KAT6A inhibitors exhibit anti-tumor activity in models of breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and glioma. Based on our mechanistic insights, repurposing these inhibitors for colorectal cancer treatment—particularly in combination with standard therapies or ferroptosis inducers—may yield synergistic anti-tumor effects. It is noteworthy that molecular subtypes of colorectal cancer, such as microsatellite instability (MSI) and microsatellite stability (MSS), may influence KAT6A expression and function, thus providing a direction for future development of precision medicine strategies.

Collectively, our findings establish KAT6A as a key epigenetic regulator of ferroptosis in colorectal cancer, primarily through direct transcriptional control of GPX4. The potential involvement of SLC7A11, though mechanistically unresolved in this study, suggests KAT6A may coordinate a broader regulatory network governing ferroptosis sensitivity. These insights position KAT6A as a promising therapeutic target whose inhibition could leverage ferroptosis induction for CC treatment.

Conclusion

To summarize, our work identified that KAT6A promotes the proliferation of CC cells via regulating ferroptosis. Molecular mechanism study further revealed the epigenetic regulation of GPX4 by KAT6A in CC cells. Our work presented KAT6A as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target for treatment CC.
