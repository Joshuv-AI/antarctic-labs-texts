Seafood consumption and incident diabetes mellitus in middle-aged and elderly Chinese: results from the 4 C study
Authors: Xiang Hu, Liang Wang, Zhi-yun Zhao, Li-juan Yang, Hui-hui Deng, Wei Pan, Meng-ying Chen, Jie-li Lu, Mian Li, Yu Xu, Min Xu, Tian-ge Wang, Gang Chen, Qin Wan, Gui-jun Qin, Li Yan, Gui-xia Wang, Ying-fen Qin, Zuo-jie Luo, Xu-lei Tang, Ya-nan Huo, Ru-ying Hu, Zhen Ye, Li-xin Shi, Zheng-nan Gao, Qing Su, Yi-ming Mu, Jia-jun Zhao, Lu-lu Chen, Tian-shu Zeng, Xue-feng Yu, Qiang Li, Li Chen, Yin-fei Zhang, You-min Wang, Hua-cong Deng, Chao Liu, Sheng-li Wu, Tao Yang, Yu-fang Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is an urgent need to implement population-based actions to prevent diabetes mellitus (DM) in China. However, the current knowledge is limited on a prospective association of seafood intake with DM risk in Chinese adults. We aimed to determine the association between seafood consumption and the incident DM in a nationwide cohort of Chinese populations.MethodsA prospective cohort study of 104,816 participants, free of DM, aged ≥ 40 years across various geographical regions in China was conducted at baseline (China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer study). Habitual consumptions of seafood were assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire, and DM was diagnosed according to the WHO 1999 criteria. Primary outcomes were the incident DM, presented as hazard rations (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that were examined with Cox proportional model with adjustments for relevant covariates across quartiles of seafood consumptions, with the lowest quartile as the reference. Dose-response analyses were evaluated using the restricted cubic spline regression with tests for nonlinearity.ResultsDuring the mean follow-up period of 3.60 years, 7,105 incident DM cases were present among the 104,816 participants. Participants at the highest quartile of seafood-consumption amount had a lower risk of DM than those at the lowest (HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.80–0.94). There is a steep decrease in the risk of DM with the increasing consumption of seafood within the cumulative amount of 200.00 g/week, which further strengthened gradually thereafter (P for nonlinearity < 0.001).ConclusionsIn the middle-aged, elderly Chinese population, higher seafood consumption was nonlinearly associated with a lower risk of incident DM, and the cutoff amount of 200.00 g/week was appropriate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01338-2.

Association of seafood consumption with diabetes risk may be differed by the geographical populations.

So far, there was no conclusive evidence on the association between seafood consumption and the incident diabetes among a nationwide population of Chinese.

What is the association of seafood consumption with incident diabetes in Chinese population?

What is new?

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic non-communicable disease with a global prevalence of 9.3%. China accounted for a quarter of the world’s adult population, increasing prevalence from 9.7% in 2010 to 11.2% in 2015–2017 [1]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to implement population-based policies and interventions to prevent or delay the development of DM and DM-related complications in China [2].

Many prospective cohort studies suggested the cardiometabolic benefits of seafood [3, 4], because of a high content of n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), mainly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). Laboratory findings at cellular levels and in animals suggest that EPA and DHA may improve the insulin sensitivity and secretion via the anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombotic actions [5, 6].

Meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies found that nearly no change or slight increase in DM risk in Western populations with higher seafood consumption, in contrast, a significant decrease in DM risk was observed in Asian populations [7, 8]. This geographical difference might be partially attributed to varied dietary habits [7] and genetic susceptibility [9, 10]. Although the previous cohorts in Japan and China indicated that seafood was associated with a reduced risk of DM [11, 12], this association among Chinese might be potentially influenced by the significant geographical and temporal differences in food patterns. Chinese individuals consume less seafood than Japanese individuals and experienced the rapid transition in food pattern and disease profile over the past decades [13]. Moreover, the beneficial association was only observed in a prior cohort of Shanghai adults but not among the nationwide Chinese population, which may have caused selection bias [12]. So far, no data has presented the association of seafood consumption with DM risk in a nationwide cohort of the Chinese population.

The China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4 C) Study is a multicentered, nationwide, prospective cohort study that enrolled middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals (≥ 40 years) to explore the relationship between metabolic factors and specific clinical outcomes [14, 15]. Recent evidence based on the 4 C study has shown that health-diet scores, determined by four components including the consumption of seafood, were associated with a reduced risk of DM [14]. Another evidence from the 4 C study, however, observed no significant association between total circulating PUFA and incident diabetes [16]. While a number of studies indicated that other nutrients and biochemical factors, such as isodine [17], vitamins [18], gut microbiota, and so on [19], might mediate the associations between seafood consumption and DM. Because it is unclear which components derived from seafood play the dominant role in the onset and development of DM, the conclusion on the association of seafood consumption with DM risk in the nationwide cohort of the Chinese population has not reached consistent. We thus speculated that a higher seafood intake would be inversely associated with the incident DM in the Chinese population. The present prospective cohort study based on the 4 C study aims to examine whether higher intakes of seafood were associated with a lower risk of DM among the middle-aged and elderly Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study design of the 4 C study has been described in detail previously [14, 15]. In brief, a total of 193,846 participants were enrolled from local resident registration systems of 20 communities from various geographical regions in China to represent the general population at baseline between 2011 and 2012. Every participant was followed up and invited to complete an in-person re-visit during 2014–2016. The exclusion criteria include (a) subjects who missed one or more measures of OGTT and HbA1c for baseline (5,955), (b) subjects who had DM at baseline (40,499), or (c) subjects with missing seafood taking data at baseline (8,443). The participant size decreased to 138,949, of which 17,728 subjects did not participate in the follow-up examination. A total of 16,405 participants were further excluded because of the absence of glucose measurement at follow-up. The final sample size was 104,816 (Fig S1). The Medical Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Shanghai, China) approved this study, and all study participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Clinical and lifestyle data collection

At baseline and follow-up visits, the clinical and laboratory data was collected from the local community clinics. After an overnight fast of more than 10 h, all participants provided their blood samples. Anthropometric measurements, OGTT, and laboratory examinations were carried out following standardized protocols [14, 15]. BMI was calculated as weight [kg]/height2 [m2].

The definition of each component of metabolic status was described previously [14, 15], based on the well-acknowledged criteria. In brief, prediabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) between 100 mg/dL (5.6mmol/L) and < 126 mg/dL (7.0mmol/L), or 2-hour plasma glucose (2hPG) between 140 mg/dL (7.8mmol/L) and < 200 mg/dL (11.1mmol/L) [20]; hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 140mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥ 90mmHg, or receiving an antihypertensive treatment [21]; dyslipidemia was defined as serum total cholesterol (TC) level of ≥ 6.22mmol/L; serum triglyceride (TG) level of ≥ 2.26mmol/L; serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) level of ≥ 4.14mmol/L; serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) level of < 1.04mmol/L; or taking lipid-lowering medications [22], overall obesity/overweight was defined as a BMI of ≥ 25.0 kg/m2 [23], and central obesity was defined as waist circumference (WC) ≥90 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women [24].

Trained and experienced technicians administered a comprehensive set of standardized questionnaires to collect socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle and habits, family history, and medical history (diseases and medicine). Regional Socio-demographic Index (SDI) category was low SDI region (< 50th percentile) and high SDI region (≥ 50th percentile) [25]. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to assess physical activity [26]. The metabolic equivalent (MET), calculated to estimate average weekly energy expenditure active (600 MET-min per week), acted as the cutoff value for the sufficiency of physical activity [27]. Sleep duration was categorized as short and long relative to the media value in this study population (8 h). Current smokers were the participants who smoked ≥seven cigarettes/week for at least six months [14], and current alcohol drinkers were the participants who drank alcohol ≥once a week in the past six months [15].

Dietary data assessments

A food frequency questionnaire allowed the collection of habitual dietary intake that includes the consumption frequency and portion size of typical food items during the previous 12 months [14]. Seafood intake was evaluated as a single food category, encompassing all types of fish, shrimp, crab, shellfish, and other marine products. Seafood intake was calculated by multiplying the reported consumption frequency by the corresponding consumption amount. The calculated value was then converted into grams per week for analysis. The quartiles of the cumulative amount of seafood intake per week were Q1 < 23.33 g/week, 23.33 ≤ Q2<50.00 g/week, 50.00 ≤ Q3<200.00 g/week, and Q4 ≥ 200.00 g/week. We derived an estimated total energy intake using the following approach: For each food item in the FFQ, we assigned an energy density value (kcal/100 g) based on the China Food Composition Table [28]. The estimated total energy intake (kcal/day) was then calculated as the sum of energy contributions from all food items. Dietary intakes of n-3 PUFA (EPA and DHA), mainly based on seafood consumption, were estimated according to the latest published data on the content of n-3 PUFAs in Chinese common food [29].

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was the incident DM diagnosed according to the 1999 WHO diagnostic criteria based on OGTT [20]. DM was defined as FPG of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2hPG of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or a self-reported previous diagnosis of DM by healthcare professionals, or current use of antidiabetic medications or insulin [20]. The secondary outcome was the changes in HOMA-IR and HOMA-β during the follow-up, and the HOMA-IR and HOMA-β were calculated with equation: fasting insulin (µIU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/405, and HOMA-β was calculated with equation: (360×fasting insulin [µIU/mL])/(fasting glucose [mg/dL] – 63), as previously described [15].

Statistical analyses

The Shapiro-Wilk test examined the normality of the distribution of continuous variables. The categorical data was shown as numbers and percentages. The continuous data was shown either by mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normal-distribution data or by a median with an interquartile range 25%–75% for skewed data, respectively. For continuous variables, including seafood intake, extreme outliers were handled using winsorization to reduce their potential influence on the analysis. Specifically, values below the 1st percentile were replaced with the value at the 1st percentile, and values above the 99th percentile were replaced with the value at the 99th percentile. The study population was ranked according to the quartiles of cumulative amount of seafood intake per week. The trends of change in variables across quartiles of seafood intake was calculated by a general linear model for continuous variables, and by chi-square test for categorical variables.

The Cox proportional models were used to estimate the crude or multivariate-adjusted HRs with 95% CIs for incident DM (per 10,000 person-years) across the quartiles of seafood intake with or without the additional adjustments for baseline socio-demographic parameters, lifestyle factors, prevalent diseases, with the lowest quartile as a reference category. The follow-up time was defined as the duration from baseline to the new-onset DM for the individuals developing DM during follow-up and that from baseline to follow-up visits for the individuals without incidental DM, respectively. Interaction tests were conducted to test whether multivariable-adjusted HRs statistically differed between the strata analyzed by including each strata factor simultaneously at baseline, seafood intake, and the respective interaction terms (the strata factor multiplied by seafood intake) in the Cox regression model. The linear regression analysis with multivariable adjustments determined the associations between seafood intake and changes of log-transformed HOMA-IR (Δ log HOMA-IR) and log-transformed HOMA-β (Δ log HOMA-β) given the biological effects on insulin sensitivity. Additionally, non-linear dose-response associations between seafood intake and the incidence of DM were assessed using restricted cubic splines with four knots (5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) for the cumulative amount of seafood intake. Tests for nonlinearity, which compared a model containing only the linear term with a model containing the linear and restricted cubic spline terms, were conducted using likelihood ratio tests. Finally, sensitivity analyses in which the incident DM cases during the first half years of follow-up or self-report cases were excluded, the participants with missing glucose measures during the follow-up were additionally enrolled, or DM was diagnosed according to the ADA criteria [30] were respectively conducted to test the statistical robustness of the final resuts. SAS 9.2 software was used for the statistical analyses. All reported P-values were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 104,816 individuals without DM were eligible for the study (age median: 55.50 years). During the mean follow-up period of 3.60 years ranging from 1.60 years to 6.90 years, 7,105 (6.78%) incident DM events occurred among the 104,816 participants. For study participants with lowest to highest quartiles of the cumulative amount of seafood intake per week, the incidences of DM were 7.34% (95% CI: 7.00%–7.68%), 6.80% (95% CI: 6.53%–7.07%), 6.53% (95% CI: 6.26%–6.81%), and 6.50% (95% CI: 6.15%–6.84%), respectively, showing a significantly decreasing trend (P for trend < 0.001).

Estimated dose-response associations between seafood intake and incident DM

Figure 1 shows the associations of seafood intake across quartiles of the cumulative amount per week with the risk of incident DM. Participants at the highest quartile of seafood-intake amount had a significantly lower risk of incident DM than those at the lowest quartile (HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.80–0.94, P < 0.001). Figure 2 presented a dose-response relationship of seafood intake with incident DM. The dose-response association was statistically nonlinear (P for nonlinearity < 0·001), with a sharp decrease in incident DM up to an intake of 200·00 g/week of cumulative amount of seafood intake, which became flatter thereafter. The secondary analyses showed a significant association between the highest quartile of seafood intake and an increase in Δ log HOMA-β compared with the lowest quartile (β = 2.04 × 10− 2, 95% CI: 0.82 × 10− 2–3.27 × 10− 2, P = 0.001), whereas there was no significant decrease in Δ log HOMA-IR from Q1 to Q4 (Table S1).

Subgroup analyses on the association between seafood intake and incident DM

When stratified by socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and prevalent metabolic disorders (Table 2), participants at the highest quartile of seafood-intake amount remained at a lower risk of incident DM than those at the bottom quartile intake in most of the strata analyzed. There were significant interactions of the seafood intake with residence (P < 0.001), living regions (P = 0.007), physical activity (P = 0.002), sleep duration (P < 0.001), prediabetes (P = 0.009), hypertension (P = 0.007), and central obesity (P < 0.001) on the risk of incident DM, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Once the incident DM during the first half years of follow-up or self-report cases were excluded (Table S2), the multivariate-adjusted HRs for the top quartiles of seafood intake compared with the bottom quartile were 0.87 (95% CI: 0.80–0.95) and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.81–0.97), respectively. In sensitivity analyses additionally enrolling the participants using the diagnosed criteria according to the ADA criteria and with missing glucose measures during the follow-up and, the directions of the inverse association were not significantly changed, and HRs were 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.86) and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.97), respectively.

Discussion

This study was the first nationwide cohort supporting a non-linear association between seafood intake and incident DM, to the best of our knowledge. This study, to some extent, explained the underlying mechanism of insulin secretion in middle-aged and elderly Chinese. Our major findings indicated a steep decline in the DM risk with the increasing consumption of seafood within the cumulative amount of 200.00 g/week, which further strengthened gradually thereafter. In the secondary analysis, participants at the highest quartile of seafood intake had a significant increase in changes of HOMA-β than those at the lowest quartile, suggesting that the improvement of insulin secretion might be involved in the mechanism behind this observed benefit. Such evidence helps fill the gap that lacks detailed associations of seafood consumption with the incident DM in nationwide Chinese adults. The evidence lent support to the seafood’s benefit in the primary prevention of DM [7, 8] and generalized it to the typical Chinese population with different socio-demographic characteristics and geographical regions.

The epidemiological findings concentrated on the impacts of seafood on the incident DM varied by the geographical regions with different eating habits. Meta-analyses of independent prospective cohorts showed that, in contrast to the association of more fish intake with a higher incidence of DM in Western countries, fish intake was inversely associated with incident DM in Asian countries [7, 8]. Consistent with the previous findings [7, 8], the present cohort study in Chinese, a typical Asian population, indicated that a higher level of seafood intake was related to a lower risk of incident DM. The reason for this geographical difference remains unclear. It is supposed that seafood intake may be a marker of healthy dietary patterns, reflecting the difference in dietary structure, food preference, and preparation methods related to the risk of DM between Eastern and Western countries. In addition, the findings from the genetic studies might also explain the geographical differences to some extent. In Chinese Hans, it was found that n-3 PUFAs modulated the associations of genome-wide association study-identified genetic variants with DM risk [9], and correspondingly, high genetic risk abolished the benefits of dietary intake n-3 PUFA on DM [10]. These findings suggested the existing interaction between dietary n-3 PUFAs and genetic factors on DM.

Nevertheless, even among the Asian population, the conclusion differed by men and women. Previously, the results varied due to the gender difference in Chinese and Japanese adults [11, 12]. By contrast, the present study showed that sufficient seafood intake was consistently associated with a lower risk of incident DM in both men and women. A possible explanation was the change and diversity in dietary patterns and disease profiles in China [13] and the increased knowledge of DM. The accumulating science popularization materials on the internet in recent years potentially reduced the inequality of the knowledge on prevention of DM via healthy diets between genders.

It was supposed that the potential benefit of seafood intake on DM development would accumulate at a higher level. The previous meta-analysis supported this assumption on the Asian population, which reported that the incidence of DM was decreased with the accumulation of fish or seafood intake [31]. In line with the previous study, the present study also uncovered that the increasing amount of seafood intake was associated with decreasing incidence of DM. However, this association was non-linear, showing a steep decrease in the incidence of DM at lower levels of seafood intake and became modest after the seafood intake exceeded the amount of 200.00 g/week, which American Heart Association recommends as one of healthy dietary behaviors [32]. This study suggested that the 200.00 g/week amount also might be an appropriate cutoff value of seafood recommended for the Chinese middle-aged and elderly population with a high dose-effectiveness ratio. Additionally, our main findings also indicated that seafood intakes were more effective in primary DM prevention in rural residence and non-coastal individuals. This result might be partially attributed to the amount of seafood intake likely to be relatively lower in individuals living in rural and non-coastal regions due to the small amount of readily available seafood. This difference perhaps increased the opportunity to detect incremental benefits in the participants with the highest seafood intake than those with the bottom.

There have been rare data reporting the interactions between the prevalent metabolic disorders and seafood intake on the incident DM. The present study showed that a higher level of seafood intake was influential on the primary prevention of DM for individuals, regardless of metabolic disorders. Our interaction analyses showed that this beneficial effect of seafood intake was more pronounced in individuals without prevalent prediabetes, hypertension, and central obesity. These risk factors, considered as the components of metabolic syndrome, were associated with an increased level of inflammation [33]. The higher grade of inflammation in the metabolic comorbidities might have attenuated in populations with habitual seafood intake, but might not have eliminated the beneficial effect of seafood on DM. Presumably, due to the prominent anti-hyperlipidemic effect of seafood intake rich in n-3 PUFAs [34], the presence of dyslipidemia could hardly interfere with the effects of seafood intake on incident DM.

We observed significant interactions between seafood intake and lifestyle factors, including physical activity and sleep duration. Specifically, the inverse association between seafood consumption and diabetes risk was attenuated among participants who reported sufficient physical activity or adequate sleep. This finding is consistent with the ACTID trial, which reported that adding exercise to an intensive dietary intervention provided only modest additional glycemic benefit over diet alone [35], suggesting that protective effects may be attenuated depending on the baseline level of other factors that operate through overlapping biological pathways. A plausible explanation is that these lifestyle factors share a common anti-inflammatory pathway contributing to reduced diabetes risk [36–39]. When one protective factor (e.g., sufficient physical activity or adequate sleep) is already present, baseline inflammation may be relatively low, thereby limiting the additional anti-inflammatory benefit conferred by another protective factor, such as seafood intake. Therefore, our findings indicate that the protective effect of seafood consumption on diabetes risk is attenuated when such protection has already been established through other lifestyle factors.

The biological mechanism behind the cardiometabolic benefits of seafood consumption remains unclear. First of all, omega-3 (n-3) PUFAs might be one of the components responsible for seafood’s biological function, which are proven by epidemiological studies [40]. In support of this comment, the present study estimated that in the highest quartile of seafood intake, both of intake of DHA and EPA were higher than the amount in other quartiles. The dietary intake will also influence the circulating level of long-chain free fatty acids. Recent published study from the same center (4 C study) showed that seafood (fish) consumption was directly linked to circulating levels of DHA and EPA [16].The benefit of seafood on DM is theoretically established by studies in vitro and in vivo based on the improvement of insulin resistance and anti-inflammatory effects of marine n-3 PUFAs [5, 41], as well as beneficial impacts on prevention of insulin secretion defect [6, 42]. However, recent data from 4 C study did not find the association between circulating levels of DHA and EPA with incident DM [16]. The explanation, to some extent, might be circulating long-chain FFAs indicate both dietary intake and endogenous metabolism, the latter of which is affected by genetic and environmental factors [16]. Additionally, the present study revealed seafood consumption was also related to other DM-related lifestyle factors (food preference, exercise habit, sleep quality), which exerted interactive effects on this association. Moreover, seafood also rich in high-quality protein and other essential nutrients, such as vitamin D, vitamin B12, the B vitamins niacin and pantothenic acid, as well as the trace elements such as iodine and Se, which further contributes to a better nutritional status [18]. For instance, proteins from seafood attenuates insulin resistance and accompanying inflammation in adipose tissue in rats, which might be relevant to the alteration in microbiota tyrosine and phenylalanine metabolism [18]. The secondary analyses of the present study also explored the underlying mechanism based on the biochemical data, suggesting that higher consumption of seafood contributed to the improvement of insulin secretion, which was consistent with the previous laboratory evidence [6, 42]. On another aspect, dietary seafood protected against the adverse effects of the exposure to hazardous substance on glycemic disorders. Recent study from Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention revealed that seafood consumption significantly mitigated the associations between per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances burdens and prediabetes/diabetes [43].

One of the strengths of the present study was the use of a large-population cohort with more than 100,000 participants to ensure the temporal sequence between the exposure (seafood intake) and the outcome (incident DM). In addition, because the present study was conducted in different regions of China, the study represented nationwide data of individuals with different socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and metabolic health status, thus providing comprehensive and representative results. Finally, the sensitivity analyses strengthened the present findings and made them more convinced.

There are also several limitations of this study. The major limitation is that the frequency and amount of seafood intake were entirely dependent on the self-reported data collected from food frequency questionnaires, contributing to recall bias and misestimation. Future studies with more detailed dietary questionnaires are needed to further clarify the specific seafood components driving the observed association. Furthermore, given the different educational levels of the participants, the questionnaires were designed to be simple. Therefore, seafood included aquatic products, regardless of their sources and kinds. This definition perhaps led to the overestimation of seafood from marine source. Moreover, specific components, such as n-3 PUFAs, could not be calculated and determined acutely. Additionally, detailed information on circulating n-3 PUFAs (e.g., EPA and DHA) was not available in the present study, which limited our ability to further evaluate the specific associations between marine n-3 fatty acids and the risk of diabetes. Future studies incorporating more detailed assessments or biomarker measurements are needed to further clarify these associations. Third, the present studies distinguished neither cooking methods nor cooking utensils. Consumption of fried and deep-fried fish, as well as the use of batter and type of frying-media may potentially bias the association direction. Another limitation of the study is the relatively short period for follow-up. Therefore, it remained unclear whether the association observed in the present study would attenuate or disappear in a longer follow-up period. Fourth, we did not apply the matching approach at baseline to minimize the possibility of indication bias that might cause confounding and more robust estimation. Finally, because of the rapid change in the dietary pattern and habits in recent decades among the Chinese population, the association should be interpreted with caution and further verified in the future based on different large-scale cohorts. Lastly, DM was diagnosed without distinguishing the types of pathogenesis in this study. It was hard to differentiate type 1 DM and type 2 DM with little clinical evidence such as autoantibodies, clinical progress, and the response to insulin treatment. In consideration of the high proportion of type 2 DM in patients with DM in China (> 90%) and the age range of the present study (≥ 40 years), almost all of the DM could be regarded as type 2 DM in the present study.

Conclusions

For the Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults, increased seafood intake contributed to the decreased risk of incident DM, and the amount of 200.00 g/week was appropriate. Our major evidence supported the current recommendation of seafood intake in the primary prevention of DM among Chinese populations.
