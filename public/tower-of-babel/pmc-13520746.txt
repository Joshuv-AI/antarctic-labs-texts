Insights into Active Sites of Dehydrogenation–Condensation–Dehydration Steps in Ethanol-to-1,3-Butadiene Conversion over Tailored MgO–SiO2 Catalysts
Authors: Zhefei Zhang, Qiangqiang Zhu, Bin Wang, Honglin Ren, Chunliang Shang, Xinyi Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The conversion of ethanol to 1,3-butadiene over MgO–SiO2-based catalysts has attracted significant interest. Nevertheless, a precise understanding of the multistep reaction network and the nature of the active sites remains elusive. We herein report a strategically designed series of model catalysts with systematically varied Mg/Si ratiosSiO2, 0.5MgO–SiO2, 4MgO–SiO2, and MgOenabling the unambiguous identification of active site structures. Through comprehensive characterization and kinetic evaluation using distinct feedstocks (ethanol, acetaldehyde, their mixtures, and crotyl alcohol), we decouple the complex reaction pathway and establish quantitative structure–activity relationships for each key step: dehydrogenation, aldol condensation, and dehydration. The 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst, comprising amorphous Mg2SiO4, possesses the highest density of basic sites and delivers the superior activity for ethanol dehydrogenation to acetaldehyde (0.087 μmol·g–1·s–1). In marked contrast, the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst, characterized by unique Si­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si­(OMg)­(OSi)3 structures, exhibits the highest concentration of acid sites. This specific acidic architecture drives exceptional performance in both acetaldehyde condensation to crotonaldehyde (0.865 μmol·g–1·s–1) and crotyl alcohol dehydration to 1,3-butadiene (7.355 μmol·g–1·s–1). Interestingly, even pure SiO2, with its distinct Si–OH acid sites, shows notable dehydration activity (5.344 μmol·g–1·s–1), highlighting that both the acid site identity and density collectively govern dehydration activity. This work provides a novel mechanistic blueprint, demonstrating that ethanol-to-1,3-butadiene conversion follows a precise site-demand sequence: dehydrogenation is favored on strong bases, while condensation and dehydration are accelerated on tailored acid sites. These insights offer a clear strategy for the design of highly selective multifunctional catalysts for complex cascade reactions.

Introduction

1,3-Butadiene, the most important conjugated diene, is used as a monomer of synthetic rubbers, elastomers, and polymer resins. At present, more than 90% of 1,3-butadiene is obtained as a byproduct of ethylene through extractive distillation of the naphtha steam-cracking C4 fraction. The transition to lighter feedstocks may impact 1,3-butadiene production, potentially raising prices due to the lower cost and greater availability of ethane from shale gas. Consequently, ethanol-derived 1,3-butadiene has gained renewed interest as ethanol is a readily available biomass-derived feedstock with significant potential in the chemical industry.

1,3-Butadiene production from ethanol was achieved during World War II in the USSR and USA, usually referred to as the Lebedev and Ostromislensky processes, respectively. The Lebedev process directly catalyzed the conversion of ethanol to 1,3-butadiene in a single step using MgO/SiO2-based catalysts. In contrast, the Ostromislensky process involved a two-step procedure, where the first stage entailed the dehydrogenation of ethanol to acetaldehyde over a Cu/SiO2 catalyst, followed by the conversion of the acetaldehyde and ethanol mixture to 1,3-butadiene on a Ta/SiO2 catalyst. Recently, varieties of catalysts, including MgO–SiO2-, − ZrO2–SiO2-, − HfO2–SiO2-, − Y2O3–SiO2-, − and Ta2O5–SiO2-based − catalysts, have been reported. The MgO–SiO2-based catalysts have attained special attention since they typically perform well in 1,3-butadiene yield.

The reaction route and active sites of the ethanol conversion to 1,3-butadiene are sophisticated and have not been perfectly clarified. Despite all this, there is a consensus that 1,3-butadiene can be produced from ethanol via the aldol condensation mechanism, − as presented in Scheme . The reaction mechanism generally follows a well-defined sequence of four key steps: (1) the initial dehydrogenation of ethanol to acetaldehyde; (2) the aldol condensation of acetaldehyde, involving both the coupling of acetaldehyde molecules and subsequent dehydration; (3) the Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reduction of the resulting crotonaldehyde to crotyl alcohol, with ethanol serving as the hydrogen donor; and (4) the final dehydration of crotyl alcohol to produce 1,3-butadiene. This sequential mechanism underscores the complex cascade of dehydrogenation, condensation, reduction, and dehydration reactions essential for the catalytic conversion process.

Aldol Condensation Pathways of Ethanol Conversion to 1,3-Butadiene −

Relationship between the catalyst structure, catalyst acid–base property, and catalytic activity has been characterized over the MgO–SiO2 catalysts. Initially, Kvisle et al. compared the catalytic performance of individual MgO or SiO2, the MgO–SiO2 material synthesized by the wet kneading method, and the MgO–SiO2 material synthesized by the physical mixing method. It was concluded that there were unclear Mg–O–Si interactions between MgO and SiO2 since the MgO–SiO2 catalyst synthesized by wet kneading presents a higher activity than that of other catalysts. In addition, they compared the UV–VIS spectra of MgO and MgO–SiO2, concluding that the addition of SiO2 in MgO results in structural modifications on the MgO such as an enhancement in the flat, stepped, and kinked defects. Early work by Cavani et al. demonstrated that an Mg/Si atomic ratio between 9 and 15 provides an optimal balance of strong basic sites for the ethanol activation and medium-strength Lewis acid sites (proposed as Mg2+ adjacent to Si4+) for the dehydration of the formed alkenols. Kyriienko et al. identified that the MgO–SiO2 contact zone generates Lewis acid sites critical for butadiene synthesis, with an equal mass ratio of MgO:SiO2 (1:1) proving optimal when promoted by ZnO. Recently, the formation of magnesium silicates and their important role in catalytic performance have been demonstrated by Chung et al. and in our previous study in one-step and two-step processes, respectively. The fundamental role of Mg–O–Si interfacial structures was further confirmed by mechanochemical synthesis, which enhances activity by creating active sites for aldol condensation on these structural fragments. Men et al. established that the Mg–O–Si chemical bond, formed via the strong MgO–SiO2 interaction during wet kneading, is essential for creating balanced acid–base sites, achieving record productivity (0.025 mol gcat –1 h–1) over hierarchical flower-like MgO nanostructures on SiO2 spheres. The identification of magnesium silicates greatly relies on the 29Si MAS NMR technique instead of XRD analysis, which could not detect the amorphous phase of magnesium silicates. The high catalytic performance was attributed to the formation of special magnesium silicates. Szabó et al. prepared the mesoporous MgO–SiO2 and found that the Mg–O–Si bonds were formed and contributed to the increased acidity/basicity of the catalysts.

Subsequent work provided deeper mechanistic and kinetic insights. A microkinetic analysis by Da Ros et al. revealed a temperature-dependent shift in the rate-limiting step: acetaldehyde condensation limits the reaction at 300–400 °C, while ethanol dehydrogenation becomes rate-determining at 450 °C. More recently, the impact of water from aqueous ethanol feeds was studied, showing that hydrated sites at the magnesia–silica interface remain active for C–C coupling, enabling >60% butadiene selectivity even with 80 vol % aqueous ethanol over the catalysts with MgO:SiO2 ratios of 1:1 and 3:1. Computational investigations by Zhang et al. tried to reveal the whole reaction mechanism. Meanwhile, Taifan et al. utilized periodic density functional theory calculations to examine the reactive pathways for the 1,3-butadiene formation from ethanol on an MgO surface. Zhang et al. utilized density functional theory to study the Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reaction on the surfaces of MgO(100) and MgO(110).

Despite these significant advances, several critical gaps remain. First, while the importance of Mg–O–Si interfacial sites is widely accepted, the distinct catalytic roles of specific magnesium silicate phases (e.g., Si­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2, Si­(OMg)­(OSi)3, and amorphous Mg2SiO4) in each elementary step (dehydrogenation, condensation, and dehydration) have not been systematically decoupled. Second, most previous studies rely on bulk catalysts with mixed phases, making it difficult to unambiguously attribute activity to a single structure. Third, a quantitative, step-specific activity comparison across the entire reaction sequence on structurally defined catalysts is lacking, although the overall rate-limiting step has been investigated.

To address these gaps, we strategically designed four model catalysts with controlled Mg/Si ratios (pure SiO2, pure MgO, 0.5MgO–SiO2, and 4MgO–SiO2), featuring different types of amorphous magnesium silicates. These catalysts were characterized by N2 adsorption–desorption, XRD, SEM-EDX, 29Si MAS NMR, NH3-TPD, CO2-TPD, and pyridine-IR. Their catalytic performance was systematically evaluated in each reaction step using ethanol, ethanol/acetaldehyde mixtures, acetaldehyde, and crotyl alcohol as feedstocks. By establishing, for the first time, a quantitative structure–activity relationship for each of the three key steps, we provide a deeper understanding of how catalyst design influences the multistep reaction mechanism. These insights will inform the rational design of more efficient MgO–SiO2-based catalysts for sustainable butadiene production.

Experimental Section

Materials and Chemicals

Silica gel (SiO2, 20–60 μm), magnesium oxide (MgO, 50 nm, 99.9% metals basis), and Mg­(NO3)2·6H2O were purchased from Aladdin. Ethanol, acetaldehyde (99.5%), and crotyl alcohol (96%) were provided by Adamas Reagent Co. Deionized water was employed in all reactions.

Catalyst Preparation

The MgO–SiO2 samples were synthesized via the impregnation method using an excess of water as the solvent. The synthesis of the MgO–SiO2 catalyst with an Mg/Si ratio of 0.5 is provided as an example. 12.8 g of Mg­(NO3)2·6H2O and 6.0 g of SiO2 were dissolved in 100 mL of water under stirring at room temperature. The resulting mixture was heated to 70 °C and continuously stirred until the water evaporated, forming a gel. This gel was then dried at 120 °C for 18 h and subsequently calcined at 500 °C in air for 4 h. The 4MgO–SiO2 sample with an Mg/Si ratio of 4 was prepared following the same procedure.

The SiO2 and MgO obtained from Aladdin were calcined at 500 °C in air for 3 h with a heating ramp of 10 °C min–1 before activity evaluation and characterization.

Catalyst Characterization

A Micromeritics ASAP 2020 HD 88 surface area and porosity analyzer was employed to perform the nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis. Before the measurement, the samples were activated at 200 °C in a vacuum of 1.33 × 10–3 Pa for 12 h. Afterward, the samples were moved to the analysis station to implement adsorption–desorption analysis.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was performed via a Bruker diffractometer with Cu radiation (40 kV, 120 mA). The data were collected in a 2θ range of 5–90° with a 0.2° angular step and 8 s counting time per step.

29Si magic-angle spinning (MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker AV300 spectrometer operating at a magnetic field strength of 7.05 T. The measurements were performed at a 29Si resonance frequency of 59.6 MHz with a rotor spinning frequency of 5 kHz. The experimental parameters included a recycle delay of 30 s and a pulse length of 5.0 μs. Samples were packed into 4 mm zirconia rotors for analysis, and tetramethyl silane (TMS) was served as the chemical shift reference.

The morphology of the samples was characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (HITACHI S-4800, HITACHI Ltd., Japan) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) for elemental analysis.

Fourier transform infrared resonance (FT-IR) spectroscopy of adsorbed pyridine (Pyridine-IR) was carried out using a Thermo Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer equipped with a high-temperature environmental cell. Prior to the measurement, 0.015 g of the sample was pressed into a tablet and packed into the cell. The sample was kept for 1 h at 350 °C in a vacuum, followed by cooling to 200 °C to collect a reference spectrum. After further cooling to 50 °C, pyridine was led into the cell. The catalyst was heated then to 200 °C and held for 10 min before the IR spectra were recorded.

The acidic and basic properties of the MgO–SiO2-based materials were performed using temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) of NH3 and CO2 (NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD), respectively. The TPD patterns were normalized by the material weight. Typically, 0.2 g of the sample was pretreated in a glass U-tube at 400 °C for 30 min in He flows (50 mL min–1), followed by cooling to 50 °C. NH3 or CO2 gas was introduced into the tube at a flow rate of 50 mL min–1 for 15 min to remove physiosorbed ammonia. Afterward, the material was treated at 80 °C in He flows (50 mL min–1) for 30 min. The TPD signal was collected between 80 and 500 °C.

Catalyst Evaluation

Catalytic activity was performed in a fixed-bed quartz reactor tube with an 8 mm internal diameter, operating at atmospheric pressure. Nitrogen was selected as a carrier gas with a flow rate of 5 mL min–1. Prior to feedstock, the selected catalyst (0.6 g) was packed into the quartz reactor, heated at 5 °C min–1 to 350 °C, and kept for 1 h. The reactants were subsequently introduced to the reactor. The activity evaluations were performed at 350 °C for 1 h time on stream. Among the reactants, ethanol, the mixture of ethanol and acetaldehyde (ethanol/acetaldehyde), and crotyl alcohol were fed by a Razel syringe pump, whereas acetaldehyde was fed using a saturator, which was kept in an ice bath at 0 °C.

After the reaction, the products were heated to 180 °C via a stainless-steel pipe to ensure uniform mixing and then directed to an online Shimadzu gas chromatography (GC) equipped with an HP Plot Q column and an FID detector. It can detect ethylene, diethyl ether, propylene, 1,3-butadiene, and other C4 compounds. Methane was selected as an unreactive internal standard to ensure reliable mass balances. Heavier hydrocarbons, including C6 and C8 components, were collected by a cold trap at 4 °C and analyzed by an offline GC equipped with an HP-FFAP column. Quantification of the primary products was based on calibration curves received by injecting known amounts of the different components. Unknown products were identified using an Agilent GC-MS equipped with a VF-5 ms column.

We anticipated that MgO–SiO2-based catalysts with varying structures and acid–base properties could be obtained by controlling the Mg/Si ratio. To explore their potential as active sites in different reaction steps of ethanol conversion to 1,3-butadiene, we prepared individual SiO2 and MgO, along with 0.5MgO–SiO2 (Mg/Si ratio of 0.5) and 4MgO–SiO2 (Mg/Si ratio of 4).

XRD and 29Si MAS NMR

Figure a presents the XRD patterns of the MgO–SiO2 materials. The SiO2 and 0.5MgO–SiO2 samples exhibit broad and low-intensity bands (2θ = 20–30°), characteristic of amorphous silica. No MgO-related peaks were detected in the 0.5MgO–SiO2 sample, indicating minimal crystalline MgO content. In contrast, the 4MgO–SiO2 sample displays strong diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline MgO, confirming its formation.

(a) XRD and (b) 29Si MAS NMR patterns of SiO2, 0.5MgO–SiO2, 4MgO–SiO2, and MgO materials.

The 29Si MAS NMR spectra of the MgO–SiO2 materials are depicted in Figure b, allowing us to detect the amorphous structure of the catalysts. The signals detected by 29Si MAS NMR spectra are classified into Qn species, where n represents the number of siloxane units bonded to silicon. , The SiO2 presents two signals at −110 ppm for siloxane groups (Q4, Si*­(OSi)4) and −100 ppm for simple silanol (Q3, Si*­(OH)­(OSi)3), which is typical for amorphous silica materials. , Compared with SiO2, noticeable changes were observed in the patterns of the MgO–SiO2 catalysts. The signals at −110 and −100 ppm weaken significantly, while new signals are observed at −62, −84, and −92 ppm, revealing the transformation of SiO2 into other structures. The signals at −84 and −92 ppm are attributed to Q2 (Si*­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2, Mg/Si = 0.33) and Q3 (Si*­(OMg)­(OSi)3, Mg/Si = 0.25) units, corresponding to amorphous hydrous magnesium silicate structures. The signal at −62 ppm could be ascribed to forsterite Mg2SiO4. Normally, the forsterite Mg2SiO4 signal at −62 ppm is crystalline and could be detected by the XRD technique. The presence of forsterite Mg2SiO4 is not detected via XRD in this study, indicating that the crystalline structure of the forsterite Mg2SiO4 might be converted to an amorphous structure due to the distortion of Mg sites. No signals of SiO2 could be observed in the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst, indicating that the SiO2 has been almost converted to amorphous magnesium silicates. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 sample is primarily composed of amorphous magnesium silicates containing Q2 and Q3 structures with Mg/Si ratios of less than 0.5, while the 4MgO–SiO2 material is dominated by amorphous Mg2SiO4.

Py-FTIR and 29Si MAS NMR

Py-FTIR analyses were performed to identify Lewis (L) and Brönsted (B) acid sites on the MgO–SiO2 materials. Based on the previous interpretations of Py-FTIR, peaks at 1610, 1575, 1490, and 1445 cm–1 correspond to pyridine adsorbed on L, L, L+B, and L acid sites, respectively, whereas that at 1540 cm–1 is linked to protonated pyridine species on B acid sites. As depicted in Figure a, the absence of the peak at 1540 cm–1 and the presence of peaks at 1447 and 1607 cm–1 reveal that the four materials are dominated by L acid sites. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 sample exhibits the highest density of L acid sites, and a gradual increase of MgO content results in a decrease of acid sites. For SiO2, no pyridine-derived bands were observed in the Py-FTIR spectrum at 200 °C (Figure a). However, 29Si MAS NMR analysis (Figure b) revealed the presence of Si–OH groups, with Si­(OH)­(OSi)3 and Si­(OSi)4 accounting for 28 and 72% of the Si environments, respectively. These Si–OH species are known to possess weak Brønsted acidity. Their absence in the Py-FTIR spectrum is therefore attributed to their insufficient acid strength to retain pyridine at the desorption temperature of 200 °C, rather than to a complete lack of Brønsted acid sites.

NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD Analyses

NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD analyses were conducted to detect the strengths and densities of acid and base sites, respectively. The results are shown in Table and Figure .

(a) NH3-TPD and (b) CO2-TPD profiles of the SiO2, 0.5MgO–SiO2, 4MgO–SiO2, and MgO catalysts.

The weak peak presented by SiO2 shows that it has a low density of acid sites. An asymmetrical and broadband in the whole temperature range was observed for the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst. The NH3-TPD band of the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst was divided into three peaks centered at 170, 245, and 310 °C. With MgO content increasing, a lower strength of the peaks was observed for the MgO–SiO2 catalysts. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 and 4MgO–SiO2 catalysts contain both weak (170 °C) and strong (310 °C) acid sites, whereas the SiO2 and MgO catalysts have weak acid sites at 170 °C. The maximum density of weak and strong L acidic sites obtained by the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst exhibits that the Mg2+ from the magnesium silicates provides more and stronger L acidic sites than the Mg2+ from the MgO catalyst.

CO2-TPD analysis was performed to detect the basicity of O2– anions on the surface of these catalysts (Figure b). No base sites were observed for the individual SiO2. For the SiO2–MgO catalysts, three peaks centered at 170, 245, and 310 °C could be observed, while the maximum density of base sites is obtained by the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst. However, the MgO catalyst has no strong basic acid at 310 °C. The basic site density of the 0.5MgO–SiO2 and 4MgO–SiO2 catalysts is more than that of the individual MgO. This means that the strong and high-density base sites primarily originate from the Mg–O–H bond rather than the Mg–O bond of individual MgO. The types of basic sites at different temperatures were identified by Janssens et al. through a combination of CO2-TPD and CO2–IR techniques over the SiO2–MgO-based catalysts. Three bands in the CO2-TPD patterns at different temperatures were classified into types of basic sites: hydroxyl groups with weak basicity, moderately basic Mg–O pairs, and highly basic O2– anions. As presented by 29Si MAS NMR, increased MgO content results in increased magnesium silicates with a high Mg/Si ratio, which might be associated with the increased density of highly basic O2– anions.

The comprehensive characterization results have successfully elucidated the structural configurations of these MgO–SiO2 catalysts. 29Si MAS NMR analysis identified the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst as primarily composed of distinct silicate structures, specifically Si*­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si*­(OMg)­(OSi)3 units. In contrast, the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst was characterized by a heterogeneous composition, with combined 29Si MAS NMR and XRD analyses confirming the coexistence of amorphous Mg2SiO4 and crystalline MgO phases. Silica, as a neutral support material, typically does not possess a significant density of acidic or basic sites. While surface hydroxyl groups are present on silica, they are relatively weak and do not exhibit strong acidic or basic properties.

Surface property measurements revealed a pronounced dichotomy in active site distribution: the 0.5MgO–SiO2 composition exhibited maximum acid site density, while the 4MgO–SiO2 sample demonstrated superior basic site concentration. These enhanced surface characteristics were fundamentally correlated with the formation of specific magnesium silicate species, whose structural evolution directly influenced the catalytic properties. Particularly noteworthy is the observed enhancement in both density and strength of basic sites in amorphous Mg2SiO4, a phenomenon that can be attributed to the simultaneous generation of strongly basic O2– anions within the catalyst matrix.

Design and Investigation of All Reaction Routes

As concluded above, Toussaint’s aldol condensation mechanism is generally accepted in the conversion of ethanol to 1,3-butadiene. To further confirm the mechanism, a systematic investigation was performed by a measurement of the initial reaction rates for each of the key steps using the four materials. The conversions of reactants were kept below 20% by tuning the WHSV (g g–1 h–1). In addition, the side reactions, including ethanol dehydration pathways leading to ethylene and diethyl ether formation and its conversion to butanol, are considered as well.

In general, butanol production from ethanol has a mechanism similar to that of 1,3-butadiene production over basic oxide catalysts. The difference in ethanol conversion to 1,3-butadiene and butanol exists in the last step: dehydration of crotyl alcohol to form 1,3-butadiene and its hydrogenation to form butanol.

Dehydration and Dehydrogenation–Condensation of Ethanol

The one-step process for the conversion of ethanol to 1,3-butadiene is evaluated first. The ethanol conversion to acetaldehyde is the first step in the ethanol upgrade to 1,3-butadiene and butanol. The ability of these catalysts to convert ethanol to acetaldehyde has a significant effect on 1,3-butadiene formation. The measurement of initial reaction rates was carried out using ethanol as the feed to evaluate their oxidation–condensation and dehydration abilities.

The results are exhibited in Figure . Figure a presents the ethanol conversion and product selectivity. The product contains dehydrogenation–condensation products including 1,3-butadiene and butanol and dehydration products including ethylene and ethyl ether (Scheme ). The absence of acetaldehyde was observed in the product for the SiO2, 0.5MgO–SiO2, and 4MgO–SiO2 catalysts, which indicates that the ethanol conversion to acetaldehyde is the rate-determining step in the 1,3-butadiene formation from ethanol. It means that the formation ability of 1,3-butadiene/butanol depends on the dehydrogenation ability of ethanol to acetaldehyde. The order of ethanol conversion is 0.5MgO–SiO2 > 4MgO–SiO2 > SiO2 > MgO. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst demonstrates optimal ethanol conversion efficiency, whereas the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst exhibited superior selectivity toward 1,3-butadiene production. The highest selectivity of dehydration products including ethylene and ethyl ether was obtained by the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst. The absence of 1,3-butadiene was observed in the product for the SiO2.

Figure b shows the initial formation rate of products over the four catalysts. The highest formation rate of 1,3-butadiene was gained by the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst, whereas the highest formation rates of ethylene and ethyl ether were given by the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst. These results indicate that the 1,3-butadiene could be formed by the Mg-containing catalysts, among which the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst has the highest formation ability of 1,3-butadiene. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 material tends to catalyze the ethanol dehydration to form ethylene and ethyl ether. The butanol acting as a main product was obtained for the MgO catalyst.

Figure c,d presents the correlation between the acid density and ethanol dehydration rate and between base density and ethanol dehydrogenation rate, respectively. It was observed that the density of acid sites presents a positive correlation with the formation rate of dehydration products including ethylene and ethyl ether, while the density of base sites has a positive correlation with the formation rate of the dehydrogenation–condensation product 1,3-butadiene. More specifically, the density of base sites is positively associated with the formation rate of acetaldehyde from ethanol because of the rate-determining step of ethanol dehydrogenation to acetaldehyde in the formation of 1,3-butadiene.

Condensation of Acetaldehyde to Crotonaldehyde

The acetaldehyde condensation was generally regarded as the rate-determining step in a two-step process. The MgO–SiO2 materials were evaluated using acetaldehyde as a feedstock here. As shown in Figure a,b and Scheme , the product is mainly crotonaldehyde. The formation of 4-hydroxyl-4-methyl-2-pentanone (HMP) was observed over the 4MgO–SiO2 and MgO catalysts as well. This means that no 1,3-butadiene could be formed when there is no ethanol in feedstock. At the same time, the SiO2 and 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalysts give a high crotonaldehyde selectivity of 99%. The order of crotonaldehyde formation rate is 0.5MgO–SiO2 > 4MgO–SiO2 > SiO2 > MgO. This result indicates that all of the catalysts have acetaldehyde condensation ability, in which the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst shows the highest ability of condensation. Notably, the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst demonstrated superior catalytic activity with an initial reaction rate of 0.865 μmol g–1 s–1, significantly exceeding the performance of other catalyst formulations. This enhanced activity can be attributed to the generation of strong Lewis acid sites originating from the Mg–O–Si structural units, which are essential for facilitating the acetaldehyde condensation process. Interestingly, the SiO2 catalyst exhibited a notable initial reaction rate of 0.191 μmol g–1 s–1 for acetaldehyde condensation, a phenomenon that has received limited attention in the previous literature.

Condensation of Acetaldehyde to Crotonaldehyde and HMP

The result from Figure c presents that the initial reaction rate of acetaldehyde condensation is related to the density of acid sites. It could be noted that the MgO has a comparable density of acid sites with SiO2, whereas it gives a lower initial rate of acetaldehyde condensation. This might be attributed to the difference between the Si–OH sites of SiO2 and L acid sites of MgO. It is noteworthy that 4-hydroxy-4-methyl-2-pentanone (HMP) was observed over the 4MgO–SiO2 and MgO catalysts. HMP is not a direct product of consecutive acetaldehyde condensation but is likely formed via an ‘acetaldehyde → acetone → HMP’ pathway. First, acetaldehyde is oxidized to acetate species on the catalyst surface, followed by the coupling of two acetate intermediates to generate acetone and CO2. Subsequently, acetone undergoes self-aldol condensation catalyzed by basic sites to form HMP. The result from Figure d indicates that the formation of HMP is associated with both the density of acid and base sites. A higher ratio of acid/base sites contributes to forming the HMP. The appearance of HMP represents an undesired competing pathway that diverts the key acetaldehyde intermediate, and its formation reflects the tendency of the catalyst (particularly high ratio of acid/base sites) to promote side reactions.

Effect of the Catalyst Structure and Acid–Base Properties on the Dehydrogenation–Condensation–Dehydration Steps of Ethanol Conversion to 1,3-Butadiene

Structural characterization reveals distinct acid–base properties among the MgO–SiO2 materials, with their site distribution and strength being strongly dependent on composition. The binary 0.5MgO–SiO2 and 4MgO–SiO2 systems demonstrate significantly enhanced acidity and basicity compared to their individual SiO2 and MgO counterparts. Specifically, SiO2 exhibits weak Brønsted acidity primarily through its Si–OH groups. The 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst shows superior Lewis acidity, featuring the highest density of acid sites derived from Mg2+ cations in magnesium silicate structures characterized by Si*­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si*­(OMg)­(OSi)3 configurations. In contrast, the 4MgO–SiO2 system possesses the highest concentration of basic sites, originating from Mg2+ in amorphous Mg2SiO4 phases. The pure MgO catalyst displays relatively weak basicity, with its active sites stemming from O2– anions in Mg–O bonds.

Consistent with previous reports, the formation of Mg–O–Si interfacial structures and the resulting acid–base balance are critical for catalytic performance. Early studies have shown that an optimal Mg/Si ratio (e.g., 9–15) or an equal mass ratio of MgO:SiO2 provides balanced strong basic sites and medium-strength Lewis acid sites (Mg2+ adjacent to Si4+) essential for ethanol activation and butadiene synthesis. , The Mg–O–Si chemical bond, generated via a strong MgO–SiO2 interaction during wet kneading or mechanochemical synthesis, has been identified as the key to creating active sites for aldol condensation. , In line with these findings, our results confirm that the magnesium silicate interfaces (e.g., Si­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si­(OMg)­(OSi)3 configurations) in the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst are responsible for its superior Lewis acidity, while the 4MgO–SiO2 system’s high basicity originates from O2– in amorphous Mg2SiO4 phases. It is worth noting that the measurable dehydration activity observed for SiO2 can be attributed to its Si–OH groups, which, despite being too weak to retain pyridine at 200 °C, still exhibit catalytic competence under reaction conditions. This observation underscores the important distinction between acid site strength as probed by pyridine desorption and actual catalytic performance.

Product analysis revealed the complete consumption of acetaldehyde during ethanol conversion over SiO2, 0.5MgO–SiO2, and 4MgO–SiO2 catalysts, suggesting that ethanol dehydrogenation to acetaldehyde represents the rate-limiting step in the reaction pathway. This observation aligns with microkinetic analyses in the literature, which reported a temperature-dependent rate-determining step: acetaldehyde condensation limits the reaction at 300–400 °C, whereas ethanol dehydrogenation becomes rate-limiting at 450 °C. The structure–activity relationship analysis demonstrates a positive correlation between the 1,3-butadiene formation rate and catalyst basicity. Among the MgO–SiO2 series, the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst, possessing the highest density of basic sites, exhibits superior performance in facilitating the ethanol-to-acetaldehyde conversion, thereby achieving enhanced 1,3-butadiene production rates. These findings collectively indicate that while strong acid sites play a crucial role in ethanol dehydrogenation to acetaldehyde, the overall 1,3-butadiene formation efficiency is predominantly governed by the catalyst’s basic properties. The aldol condensation of acetaldehyde exhibits a strong dependence on catalyst acidity, with crotonaldehyde formation kinetics being directly correlated to acid site density. While the SiO2 material, featuring Si–OH Brønsted acid sites, demonstrates a moderate reaction rate of 0.191 μmol g–1 s–1 in acetaldehyde condensation, the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst surpasses this performance significantly. With its optimized acidic properties derived from the Si*­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si*­(OMg)­(OSi)3 configurations, the 0.5MgO–SiO2 system achieves a remarkable crotonaldehyde formation rate of 0.865 μmol g–1 s–1. These findings highlight the crucial role of magnesium silicate interfaces in enhancing catalytic performance for acetaldehyde aldol condensation. The reaction pathway can be effectively facilitated by both Lewis acid sites and Si–OH Brønsted acid sites, with the former demonstrating superior activity in the optimized 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst system.

In the synthesis of 1,3-butadiene from ethanol and acetaldehyde mixtures, the formation kinetics demonstrate a clear correlation with catalyst acidity. The introduction of ethanol significantly alters the reaction pathway by serving as a hydrogen donor, facilitating the reduction of crotonaldehyde to crotyl alcohol. This mechanistic shift is evidenced by the complete absence of crotonaldehyde in the product distribution, confirming that the aldol condensation of acetaldehyde represents the rate-limiting step in the overall conversion process from ethanol/acetaldehyde to 1,3-butadiene. The rate-determining step shifts from dehydrogenation to condensation depending on catalyst composition. This compositional dependence is supported by the observation that when ethanol alone is used as the feedstock (Figure ), the 4MgO–SiO2 catalyst, with its higher basicity, exhibits superior butadiene formation, indicating that dehydrogenation limits the overall rate. In contrast, when the EtOH/AA mixture is used (Figure ), the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst, with its higher acidity, becomes the most active, demonstrating that condensation becomes the rate-limiting step once acetaldehyde is supplied.

The catalytic conversion of crotyl alcohol to 1,3-butadiene or butanol represents the final step in the reported production pathways of these compounds from ethanol. Among the tested catalysts, 0.5MgO–SiO2 demonstrated superior performance, achieving a remarkable 1,3-butadiene formation rate of 7.355 μmol g–1 s–1, which correlates with its maximum density of acidic sites. Notably, a pure SiO2 material, despite possessing the lowest density of acidic sites, also exhibited significant catalytic activity with a 1,3-butadiene formation rate of 5.344 μmol g–1 s–1, only slightly lower than that of the 0.5MgO–SiO2 catalyst. These findings demonstrate that both Lewis acid sites and Si–OH groups are effective active sites for catalyzing the dehydration of crotyl alcohol.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically investigated four well-defined MgO–SiO2-based catalysts to elucidate the links among composition, acid–base properties, and catalytic performance in ethanol-to-1,3-butadiene conversion. We demonstrate that basic sites (from amorphous Mg2SiO4 in 4MgO–SiO2) drive ethanol dehydrogenation, while acid sites (from Si­(OMg)­(OH)­(OSi)2 and Si­(OMg)­(OSi)3 in 0.5MgO–SiO2) promote aldol condensation and dehydration. Notably, even pure SiO2 exhibits significant dehydration activity via Si–OH groups despite low acid site density, revealing that the acid site identity plays a significant role alongside site density. By decoupling the cascade reaction, we further show that the rate-determining step shifts from dehydrogenation to condensation depending on catalyst composition. These findings provide three original contributions: (1) unambiguous assignment of specific silicate structures to individual reaction steps; (2) quantitative evidence that the acid site identity is as important as density; and (3) a mechanistic blueprint showing a composition-dependent shift in the rate-determining step. Collectively, this work offers a strategic framework for designing selective multifunctional catalysts for complex cascade reactions.
