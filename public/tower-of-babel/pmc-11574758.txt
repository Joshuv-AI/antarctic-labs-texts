Pharmacologic Induction of ERα SUMOylation Disrupts Its Chromatin Binding
Authors: Lizhen Wang, Ting Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Estrogen receptor α (ERα)-positive breast cancer patients are typically treated with ERα inhibitors, including selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) and selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs). However, the distinct pharmacological properties of various ERα inhibitors remain incompletely understood. In this study, we employed formaldehyde cross-linking followed by ERα immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry to reveal that fulvestrant, the first FDA-approved SERD, induces the interaction between ERα and SUMO E3 ligases PIAS1 and PIAS2. Biochemical and genomic assays confirmed that fulvestrant induces SUMOylation of ERα, which inhibits ERα′s binding to chromatin DNA. In addition, raloxifene (a SERM) and elacestrant (the first FDA-approved oral SERD) were identified as compounds that similarly induce ERα SUMOylation and inhibit its chromatin interaction. Our findings reveal a mechanism by which select ERα inhibitors disrupt ERα function through SUMOylation, offering insights for the development of next-generation ERα-targeted therapies.

Introduction

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins are critical regulatory mechanisms that govern protein function, localization, stability, and interaction networks.1 These modifications, occurring after the translation process, include phosphorylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, acetylation, methylation, and glycosylation, among others.2 Through the dynamic and reversible nature of PTMs, cells can rapidly respond to internal and external stimuli, fine-tuning protein activity, and ensuring proper protein turnover. Dysregulation of PTMs can lead to aberrant protein function and is implicated in a variety of diseases.3

Drug-induced PTMs provide a strategy to modulate protein function for therapeutic purposes. For example, arsenic trioxide (As2O3) is a therapeutic agent that induces the SUMOylation and subsequent degradation of the promyelocytic leukemia-retinoic acid α (PML-RARα) fusion protein, resulting in remarkable efficacy in treating acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL).4,5 Similarly, immunomodulatory imide drugs (IMiDs), such as lenalidomide and pomalidomide, promote the degradation of several neosubstrates by binding to the CRL4-CRBN E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, which leads to targeted protein ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.6 Another innovative approach involves proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), which recruit target proteins to E3 ubiquitin ligases, facilitating their ubiquitination and subsequent degradation.7 In contrast, deubiquitinase-targeting chimeras (DUBTACs) stabilize specific proteins by recruiting deubiquitinases to remove ubiquitin from the targets, preventing their degradation.8

SUMOylation is a post-translational modification involving the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to lysine residues on target proteins. This process is mediated by a cascade of enzymatic activities involving E1 activating enzymes, E2 conjugating enzymes, and E3 ligases, which together ensure the precise and reversible nature of SUMOylation.9 Unlike ubiquitination, which often targets proteins for degradation, SUMOylation typically regulates protein stability, localization, and interaction networks. It plays a crucial role in various cellular processes, including DNA repair, transcriptional regulation, and cell cycle progression.10 Consequently, understanding the intricacies of SUMOylation and its regulatory mechanisms provides valuable insights into cellular homeostasis and offers potential therapeutic targets for disease intervention.

Breast cancer stands as one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women worldwide, with estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) playing a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. ERα, a nuclear hormone receptor, orchestrates a myriad of biological processes by modulating gene expression in response to estrogen signaling. Targeting ERα has been a cornerstone in the treatment of ERα-positive breast cancer.11 These therapies encompass selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), such as tamoxifen, which competitively inhibit estrogen binding to ERα,12 and selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), such as fulvestrant, which promote ERα degradation and reduce receptor levels in cancer cells.13 However, the unique pharmacological properties of different ERα inhibitors are not yet fully understood.

In this study, we utilized a combination of biochemical, genetic, and pharmacological approaches to discover that fulvestrant enhances the interaction between ERα and the SUMO E3 ligases PIAS1, PIAS2, and TOPORS, thereby inducing ERα SUMOylation. Through ChIP-seq analysis, we found that fulvestrant-induced SUMOylation of ERα inhibits its binding to chromatin DNA. Additionally, we identified two other ERα inhibitors, elacestrant and raloxifene, as compounds capable of inducing ERα SUMOylation to inhibit its chromatin interaction. These findings highlight the role of SUMOylation in regulating ERα proteostasis under the influence of select ERα inhibitors.

Results and Discussion

Fulvestrant Induces Ubiquitin-Independent Modification of ERα

Fulvestrant is known to induce ERα (encoded by ESR1) degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system.13 We first reconstituted ERα expression by transiently expressing it in ERα-negative 293T cells and observed that fulvestrant treatment led to the reduction in ERα protein levels (Figure 1A). In contrast, treatment with the ERα agonist estradiol did not affect ERα protein levels. To ensure the observed effect is through direct ERα binding, we expressed the L539D mutant of ERα, which is resistant to fulvestrant binding14 and found that fulvestrant treatment no longer led to the reduction in ERα levels. Next, we pretreated cells with the ubiquitin activating enzyme (E1) inhibitor TAK-24315 and observed fulvestrant-induced ERα degradation was inhibited. In addition, bands of higher molecular weights were observed by ERα Western blotting when cells were treated with both TAK-243 and fulvestrant. TAK-243 alone did not result in the formation of these higher molecular weight species (Figure 1A). These observations indicate that fulvestrant induces a form of ubiquitin-independent PTM of ERα.

Fulvestrant Induces Binding of SUMO E3 Ligases to ERα

To understand the biochemical basis of fulvestrant-induced ERα modification, we initially employed conventional immunoprecipitation under nondenaturing conditions to capture ERα tagged with a 3xFLAG tag (Figure S1A). However, most ERα proteins were resistant to elution from the antibody-conjugated beads (Figure S1B). To overcome this challenge, we used formaldehyde to stabilize protein–protein interactions before performing immunoprecipitation under denaturing conditions. Western blotting analysis revealed that treatment with as little as 0.05% formaldehyde resulted in the appearance of high molecular weight ERα species, which were more prominent in fulvestrant-treated cells compared to untreated cells (Figure 1B). We then optimized a workflow to perform formaldehyde cross-linking followed by immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry (XL-IP-MS) to identify proteins that display increased association with ERα upon fulvestrant treatment (Figure 1C).

We compared the mass spectrometry results across the three treatment groups (vehicle, estradiol, and fulvestrant), with all samples pretreated with TAK-243. We then focused on the proteins with the highest number of peptides in the fulvestrant-treated group (Table S1). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of resulting proteins revealed an enrichment of proteins with “ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding” function in the fulvestrant-treated group (Figure 1D). Further examination of the gene list revealed the enrichment of SUMO proteins (SUMO1/2/3), SUMO E3 ligases (PIAS1/2/3), and a SUMO-dependent E3 ligase (RNF111 and TOPORS) in fulvestrant-treated group (Figure 1E).

We further validated the mass spectrometry results using formaldehyde cross-linking followed by immunoprecipitation and Western blotting. These experiments confirmed the specific association of PIAS1, PIAS2, TOPORS, and RNF111 with ERα in the fulvestrant-treated group in comparison to vehicle- or estradiol-treated group (Figure 2A). However, PIAS3 validation was unsuccessful due to significant background binding. Importantly, the L539D mutation blocked the interaction of PIAS1, PIAS2, TOPORS, and RNF111 with ERα following fulvestrant treatment (Figure 2A). These findings indicate that fulvestrant induces SUMOylation of ERα.

Fulvestrant Induces ERα SUMOylation

To investigate whether fulvestrant induces ERα SUMOylation, we cotransfected 293T cells with plasmids expressing 8xHis-tagged SUMO1/2/3 and 3xFLAG-tagged ERα. Following purification of lysates by nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin, we performed Western blotting using an anti-FLAG antibody and observed poly-SUMOylation of ERα in a fulvestrant concentration-dependent manner (Figure 2B). To further validate these findings, we employed RNA interference to knock down UBC9, the sole SUMO E2 enzyme (Figure 2C), or treated cells with the SUMO E1 inhibitor TAK-981.16 Both UBC9 knockdown and TAK-981 treatment effectively blocked fulvestrant-induced SUMOylation of ERα (Figure 2D).

To investigate whether endogenous ERα in ERα-positive cells undergoes fulvestrant-induced SUMOylation, we employed CRISPR-Cas9 technology to introduce a 3xFLAG tag at the N-terminus of ERα in ERα-positive breast cancer cells MCF7 (Figure 3A). Following the generation of knockin cells, we stably expressed 8xHis-SUMO1, 8xHis-SUMO2, and 8xHis-SUMO3 in these cells. Subsequent pulldown by Ni-NTA resin revealed fulvestrant-induced polySUMOylation of ERα as observed in 293T cells (Figure 3B). Treatment of cells with the SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 effectively blocked fulvestrant-induced ERα SUMOylation (Figure 3B). These findings conclusively demonstrate that fulvestrant induces SUMOylation of endogenous ERα in MCF7 cells.

Raloxifene and Elacestrant Induces ERα SUMOylation

To investigate whether other ERα inhibitors can induce ERα SUMOylation, we examined 4-OH tamoxifen, raloxifene, and elacestrant (Figure 3C). While both 4-OH tamoxifen and raloxifene are classified as SERMs, raloxifene induced weak ERα SUMOylation, whereas 4-OH tamoxifen did not induce ERα SUMOylation (Figure 3D). Elacestrant, the first oral SERD recently approved,17 also induced ERα SUMOylation (Figure 3D,E). However, fulvestrant is the strongest SUMOylation inducer among all tested ERα inhibitors. To further validate these results, we cotransfected 293T cells with 3xFLAG-ERα and 8xHis-SUMO1/2/3. Following treatment with different ERα modulators, we performed FLAG IP to enrich 3xFLAG-ERα and then detected the presence of SUMOylation by anti-His Western blot. We observed the induction of ERα SUMOylation by fulvestrant, raloxifene, and elacestrant, which could be fully blocked by TAK-981 (Figure 3F).

SUMOylation Inhibits the Binding of ERα to Estrogen Response Elements

ERα is a transcription factor that specifically binds to DNA sequences known as the estrogen response elements (EREs), which are located in the regulatory regions of estrogen-responsive genes. We next conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by next-generation sequencing (ChIP-seq) to evaluate ERα binding to EREs in MCF7 cells. By aligning ERα ChIP-seq peaks centered around EREs, we observed reduced ERα binding to EREs across the fulvestrant, elacestrant, raloxifene, and 4-OH tamoxifen treatment groups (Figure 4A,B).

To further investigate the biological implications of ERα SUMOylation, MCF7 cells were pretreated with the SUMO E1 inhibitor TAK-981 before exposure to fulvestrant, elacestrant, raloxifene, or 4-OH tamoxifen, followed by ERα ChIP-seq analysis. We observed that TAK-981 prevented the displacement of ERα from EREs induced by fulvestrant, elacestrant, and raloxifene, but not by 4-OH tamoxifen. Interestingly, when SUMOylation is inhibited, fulvestrant, elacestrant, and raloxifene even led to increased ERα occupancy at EREs compared to the vehicle-treated group (Figure 4C,D). These metagene findings could be confirmed by track examples of multiple known ERα target genes (Figures 4E,F and S2A–F). We further validated ChIP-seq results by ChIP-qPCR of four ERα-occupied genomic loci (Figure 4G). These findings underscore the role of SUMOylation in mediating the functional impact of select ERα inhibitors, including fulvestrant, raloxifene, and elacestrant, on modulating ERα chromatin binding.

Conclusion

In this study, we employed formaldehyde cross-linking immunoprecipitation to unbiasedly identify proteins interacting with ERα under fulvestrant treatment. Mass spectrometry analysis revealed a significant increase in peptides corresponding to SUMO1, SUMO2, and SUMO3, along with enrichment of SUMO E3 ligases PIAS1, PIAS2, and PIAS3 in the fulvestrant-treated group. We further demonstrate that fulvestrant induces SUMOylation not only in exogenously expressed ERα in 293T cells but also in endogenous ERα in MCF7 cells. ChIP-seq analysis in MCF7 cells revealed that fulvestrant diminishes ERα binding to its target genes, an effect reversed by blocking ERα SUMOylation. Similar findings have been observed previously.18 However, in contrast to the previous study, our study provided direct biochemical evidence for the pharmacologically induced interaction of E3 ligases with ERα. In addition, we used a selective SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 instead of overexpressing a deSUMOylase to examine the impact of SUMOylation on ERα binding to chromatin.

Our study also identified the ubiquitin E3 ligase RNF111 and TORPOS with increased association with ERα in the presence of fulvestrant. RNF111 and TORPOS are SUMO-targeted E3 ubiquitin ligases that ubiquitinate SUMO-modified proteins.19 Whether RNF111 and TORPOS are responsible for SERD-induced degradation of ERα necessitate future investigation.

In addition to fulvestrant, two other ERα inhibitors, elacestrant and raloxifene, were found to induce ERα SUMOylation, inhibiting its binding to EREs. Taken together, these findings reveal SUMOylation as a common effect triggered by a subset of ERα inhibitors, suggesting that enhanced SUMOylation could be a feature for more efficacious ERα-targeted therapies.

Methods

Cell Culture

MCF7 and HEK-293 cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere using Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, C11965500BT). The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, F8318), 2 mM l-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, G7513), and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122).

Transient Expression of 3xFLAG-ERα in 293T Cells

CAG promoter was chosen to drive ERα expression, full-length ERα cDNA was cloned into the lentiviral vector lenti-CAG-hPGK-Blast. For transient expression, half a million 293T cells were transfected with 20 ng of the Lenti-CAG-3xFLAG- ERα plasmid, along with 480 ng of pcDNA3.1, using 1.5 μL of 1 mg mL–1 polyethylenimine. After 6–8 h of transfection, the culture media were replaced. Twenty-four h post-transfection, cells were treated with compound according as described in the fire legend.

Lentiviral Transduction

Lentivirus was produced in 293T cells by cotransfection of lentiviral vectors with psPAX2 (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA, 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene, 12259) at a ratio of 5:3:2. Three days post-transfection, the cell culture supernatant containing lentivirus was harvested and filtered using a 0.45 μm filter membrane. For viral transduction, target cells were incubated with the lentivirus in the presence of 8 μg/mL Polybrene (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Twenty-four h post-transduction, the medium containing the lentivirus was replaced with fresh medium. Two days post-transduction, cells were selected based on the antibiotic resistance encoded by the lentiviral vectors.

Western Blotting

Standard SDS-PAGE and Western blotting protocols were employed with the following modifications. For preparation of total lysates, cells were rinsed with Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) to remove residual medium and then lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1% SDS, freshly supplemented with 1 unit/mL of benzonase and 1× cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein concentrations of the lysates were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay method. For total protein blotting, 10–30 μg of protein was resolved on SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes with a pore size of 0.5 μm. The membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk PBST (0.1% v/v Tween-20) for 30 min before incubation with primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used, diluted in 5% nonfat milk PBST: anti-ERα (Abcam, ab16660, 1:2500), anti-FLAG-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592, 1:10 000), anti-HA (Cell Signaling Technology, 3724S, 1:2500), anti-UBC9 (Proteintech, 10070–1-AP, 1:5000), anti-His (Origene, TA150088, 1:4000), anti-β-ACTIN-HRP (Huaxingbio, HX18271, 1:10 000), and anti-GAPDH-HRP (Abclonal, AC035, 1:8000). The following HRP-linked secondary antibodies were used, diluted in PBST: antirabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074S, 1:10 000) and antimouse IgG (Zsbio, Beijing, China, ZB-2305, 1:10 000). The M5 HiPer ECL Western HRP Substrate (Mei5bio, Beijing, China, MF074–01) was employed for the detection of HRP enzymatic activity. Western blot images were captured using a VILBER FUSION FX7 imager.

Formaldehyde Cross-Linking Immunoprecipitation-Mass Spectrometry

Anti-FLAG M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA F3165) was coupled to magnetic epoxy beads (Beijing Yunci Technology Co., Beijing, China) at the ratio of 10 mg of anti-FLAG antibody/mg of beads in the presence of 1 M ammonium acetate and 0.1 M sodium phosphate, pH 7.4 at 37 °C overnight. For the preparation of cell pellets for immunoprecipitation, cells grown on 15 cm tissue culture dishes were treated with indicated compounds, rinsed with cold DPBS to remove residual medium, and then cross-linked with 0.1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT. Formaldehyde was subsequently removed by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in 1.25 mM glycine and incubated at RT for 2 min to quench cross-linking. After incubation, cells were washed with cold DPBS and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. To ensure complete cell lysis, cells were lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1% SDS, freshly supplemented with 1 unit/mL benzonase and 1× cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysates were then diluted by 20-fold with 0.1% NP-40 IP buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40). A portion of the lysate was set aside as input, while the remaining lysate was incubated with anti-FLAG-conjugated magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Following incubation, a portion of the supernatant was collected as the unbound fraction, and the magnetic beads were washed twice with high NP-40 wash buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) and twice with high salt wash buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40). For Western blotting, captured proteins were eluted by heating in 1× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C, 700 rpm for 5 min. For immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, beads were washed once with 500 μL of TBS buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl). Proteins were then eluted with 25 μL of Elution Buffer I [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 2 M urea, 5 ng/μL sequencing grade modified trypsin, freshly supplemented with 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] at 30 °C with shaking at 400 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a new centrifuge tube using a magnetic stand. Next, 50 μL of Elution Buffer II (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 2 M urea, 5 mM iodoacetamide) was added to the tube containing the magnetic beads, after incubation on the magnetic stand, the supernatant was combined with the supernatant from Elution Buffer I (this step was repeated once). The combined eluates were then digested overnight at 32 °C with shaking at 400 rpm in the dark. The following day, 1 μL of trifluoroacetic acid was added to the digested samples, which were then dried using a vacuum concentrator. The dried samples were subjected to mass spectrometry analysis performed by DeepKinase.

Formaldehyde Cross-Linking Immunoprecipitation for Western Blotting

We cotransfected half a million 293T cells with 500 ng plasmids expressing 3xHA-tagged PIAS1/2/3 (or TOPORS, RNF111) along with 500 ng of plasmids expressing 3xFLAG-tagged ERα following the protocol outlined in the “Transient Expression of 3xFLAG-ERα in 293T Cells” method above. Cells grown on 6-well tissue culture dishes were treated with the indicated compounds, then rinsed with cold DPBS to remove residual medium. The cells were subsequently cross-linked with 0.1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT. The formaldehyde was carefully aspirated immediately after cross-linking. Cells were then rinsed with 1.25 mM glycine and incubated at RT for 2 min to quench the cross-linking reaction. After incubation, the cells were washed with cold DPBS and subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen. The subsequent steps followed the “Formaldehyde cross-linking immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry” method above.

N-terminal 3xFLAG-tagging of Endogenous ERα

A single guide RNA (sgRNA) (5′-AGGGTCATGGTCATGGTCCG-3′) targeting the genomic region immediately downstream of the ESR1 start codon was inserted into the PX459 vector (Addgene 62988). The knock-in repair template for 3xFLAG integration was constructed in a pTOPO-TA vector (Mei5bio, Beijing, China), harboring a BSD-P2A-3xFLAG sequence flanked by two 1000 bp homology arms corresponding to the upstream and downstream sequences of the ESR1 genomic locus. To generate 3xFLAG-ERα knockin cells, 3 million MCF7 cells were transfected with 2 μg of PX459-sgESR1, 2 μg of the repair template, and 12 μL TransIT-X2 (Mirus Bio, MIR 6000). Selection was performed with 2 μg/mL puromycin until puromycin-sensitive MCF7 cells were eliminated. Subsequently, selection was carried out with 10 μg/mL blasticidin for 7 days, followed by removal of blasticidin. Multiple clones were isolated, and successful integration of the N-terminal 3xFLAG tag was confirmed by Western blotting using an anti-FLAG-HRP antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592, 1:10 000).

Ni-NTA Pulldown Assay

Wild-type 3xFLAG-ERα was cloned into the lenti-CAG-MCS-hPGK-Blast vector, while 8xHis-SUMO1, 8xHis-SUMO2, and 8xHis-SUMO3 were cloned into the lenti-CAG-MCS-hPGK-Hygro vector. For the Ni-NTA pulldown assay, 0.6 million 293T cells were seeded per well in six-well tissue culture plates. After overnight attachment, the cells were cotransfected with 500 ng of lenti-8xHis-SUMO1/2/3 and 500 ng of lenti-3xFLAG-ERα. The following day, cells were pretreated with 10 μM MG-132 for 3 h, followed by treatment with either DMSO or indicated doses of fulvestrant for an additional 3 h. Subsequently, cells from each well were rinsed once with DPBS and lysed in 500 μL of Buffer 1 (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 8 M urea, 10 mM imidazole, 0.1% NP-40) freshly supplemented with 1 unit/mL benzonase and 1× cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 15 000 RCF at 4 °C for 10 min. Each clarified lysate sample was then mixed with 10 μL of prewashed Ni-NTA agarose beads (SMART Life Sciences, Changzhou, China, SM00801). Tubes containing the bead-lysate mixtures were rotated for 4 h at RT, followed by two washes with Buffer 1, two washes with a 1:3 mixture of Buffer 1 and Buffer 2 (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 20 mM imidazole, 0.1% NP-40), and one wash with Buffer 2. His-SUMO-conjugated proteins were eluted from the beads by boiling in 1× SDS sample buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole.

Co-immunoprecipitation of SUMO with 3xFLAG-ERα

For MCF7 cells with 3xFLAG-ERα knock-in on 15 cm dishes, the cells were treated with the indicated compounds, washed with cold DPBS, and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. For 293T cells, we cotransfected 500 000 cells with 500 ng of plasmid expressing 8xHis-tagged SUMO1 (or SUMO2/3), along with 500 ng of plasmid expressing 3xFLAG-tagged ERα, following the protocol described in the “Transient Expression of 3xFLAG-ERa in 293T Cells” section above. Cells grown on 6-well tissue culture dishes were treated with the indicated compounds, then washed with cold DPBS and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. To ensure complete cell lysis, cells were lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1% SDS, freshly supplemented with 1 unit/mL benzonase and 1× cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysates were then diluted by 20-fold with 0.1% NP-40 IP buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40). A portion of the lysate was set aside as input, while the remaining lysate was incubated with anti-FLAG-conjugated magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Following incubation, the magnetic beads were washed twice with high NP-40 wash buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) and twice with high salt wash buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40). For Western blotting, captured proteins were eluted by heating in 1× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C, 700 rpm for 5 min.

ChIP Assay

For chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), MCF7 3xFLAG-ERα knockin cells were seeded at a density of nine million cells per 15 cm dish in DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS. The following day, cells were pretreated with 10 μM MG-132 and 3 μM TAK-981 for 3.5 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with 100 nM of fulvestrant, elacestrant, 4-OH tamoxifen, or raloxifene for 2.5 h before harvesting for ChIP analysis. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT and the reaction was quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. After two washes with ice-cold DPBS, cells were harvested and resuspended in NP-40 lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% NP-40, supplemented with 2× cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail), followed by incubation on ice for 5 min. The cell lysate was then carefully layered onto a 24% sucrose cushion of NP-40 lysis buffer and centrifuged at 12 000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. Pellets were washed with 1 mM EDTA (in DPBS), resuspended in Glycerol buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 75 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 50% glycerol, supplemented with 0.85 mM DTT). Next, cell pellets were resuspended in nuclei lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 7.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 M urea, 1% NP-40, supplemented with 1 mM DTT), followed by centrifugation. The pellets were washed twice with 1 mM EDTA (in DPBS), then resuspended in sonication buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, supplemented with 2.5 μL 1 M CaCl2 and 1 μL Micrococcal Nuclease (NEB)], and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Sonication was performed using a Sonicator (Q-Sonica, Q800R3) at 30% power for 10 rounds of 30 s pulses with 30 s intervals. Following centrifugation at 12 000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min, supernatant containing fragmented chromatin (30 μL) was saved as input for ChIP, while the remaining chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 2 μg of FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804). The immunoprecipitation of complexes was carried out for 2 h at 4 °C using 30 μL of Dynabeads protein G (Invitrogen, 10003D) that had been prewashed with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (in DPBS). Subsequently, the beads were washed twice with High-Salt wash buffer (500 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0) for 2 min with gentle rotation at 4 °C, followed by one wash with LiCl wash buffer (0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP-40, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA), and finally, two washes with TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). Cross-linking was reversed by heating the beads in 150 μL elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) at 65 °C for 15 min, followed by a repeat elution step. RNA was digested with 0.1 mg mL–1 RNase A for 1 h at 37 °C, and proteins were digested with 4 μL of 20 mg mL–1 proteinase K (supplemented with 12 μL of 5 M NaCl) for 1 h at 55 °C, then further incubated at 65 °C for 6–10 h. Samples were transferred to phase lock gel and mixed with 300 μL of phenol–chloroform (25:24:1) for one round of DNA extraction. Centrifugation was performed at 15 000 rpm, 4 °C for 15–20 min, followed by transferring the aqueous phase to a fresh 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. To the aqueous phase, 0.3 μL of 20 mg mL–1 glycogen, 30 μL of 3 M sodium acetate, and 750 μL of cold ethanol were added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at −20 °C to precipitate DNA. The following day, samples were centrifuged at 15 000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min, and the DNA pellet was washed twice with 70% cold ethanol. After the final centrifugation, the DNA pellet was dissolved in 31 μL of 1× low TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA). Libraries were prepared with the KAPA DNA HyperPrep Library Kit (Roche, KK8502), and samples were sequenced with the Novaseq PE150 platform. Paired-end sequencing was employed with a read length of 150 base pairs. The primers used for ChIP-qPCR were as follows: human IGFBP4, forward 5′- TAAGCAGGGTTGGGCAAGGAAAAGTTAC-3′, reverse 5′-GACACACATTCATCCACATGTGCCTT-3′; human GREB1, forward 5′-TGGTGACACTTGGCGTCCTG-3′, reverse 5′-GTCTGCAGCTGACAGAGGAGAC-3′; human PREX1, forward 5′-TCTCCCTGCCCAGTGACTCT-3′, reverse 5′-CCTGTGTGCAATCAGATCACCTT-3′; human TEX14, forward 5′-TGGGTCTCAGTCACCCTGTTCC-3′, reverse 5′-CCTTTGACTCTGCTAGAATCAGGCT-3′; human β-ACTIN, forward 5′-AGGATTCCTATGTGGGCGACG-3′, reverse 5′-GAGCCACACGCAGCTCATTGTA-3′. All quantitative RT–PCR programs were 95 °C for 3 min, 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, followed by a melting curve. Relative ChIP enrichment was evaluated after normalization for β-ACTIN expression using the standard ΔΔCT method. Two independent biological replicates were performed.

Analysis of ChIP-seq Data

Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked and adaptor sequences were trimmed using fastp (v0.23.2).20 Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome hg38 using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1).21 The resulting SAM files were sorted and indexed using Samtools (v1.10.2)22 sort and index functions to generate BAM files. Bigwig files were then generated by the function bamCoverage of deepTools (v3.4.3)23 from BAM files. Read coverage of bigwig files was normalized as Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM). Peak detection was done using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1).24 ERE Motif enrichment analysis was performed by HOMER (v4.11)25 with the function findMotifsGenome.pl. ERE sequences were identified using HOMER’s scanMotifGenomeWide.pl function. An overlap.bed file was created through the following steps: initially, top peaks (fold enrichment >20) were selected from the DMSO-treated group, followed by overlapping these peaks with the ERE motif. Read coverage profiles and heatmaps were generated using deeptools from Bigwig files, utilizing the overlap.bed file as a reference. Examples of genome tracks were visualized in the bigwig format using IGV (v2.17.3).26
