Mental disorders in childhood and adolescence: a systematic review and meta-analysis of epidemiological data
Authors: Paraskevi Tatsiopoulou, Aspasia Serdari, Eleni Bonti, Nikos Zilikis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental disorders in children and adolescents are widespread and can have lasting effects on their emotional, social, and academic growth. The present meta-analysis examines the global prevalence of mental disorders, revealing significant regional variability.MethodsThe present systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature used the PRISMA-2020 statement and the protocol was registered and is publicly available in PROSPERO. Data were retrieved from different databases, specifically PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and PsycINFO. The search for relevant studies, and the quality assessment of the included studies was carried out independently by two researchers, while discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the third researcher, who verified the assessments and contributed to the consensus.ResultsThe study included 37 original research articles with a total number of 1,599,337 participants who were diagnosed with mental disorders by DSM or ICD. The results showed extreme heterogeneity. In Asia, the prevalence was 14.53% (I2 = 99.76%), while in Africa, it was reported to be 9.86% (I2 = 78.83%). America showed the highest prevalence, 30.64% (I2 = 99.95%), and Europe also demonstrated similar results, 30.44% (I2 = 99.99%). These findings are consistent with prior meta-analyses, which report varying global prevalence rates. The high heterogeneity of the findings makes future research necessary to explore the sources of variability.ConclusionsMental disorders impact a significant segment of global youth population, with notable differences among regions and types of disorders. Our findings emphasize the critical requirement for unified public health initiatives, early identification methods, and culturally relevant interventions focused on youth mental health.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07532-6.

Introduction

A mental or psychological disorder involves significant impairments in cognitive functions, emotional regulation, or behavior, often causing distress or dysfunction in key areas of life [1]. The term “mental health conditions” broadly includes mental disorders (MD), psychosocial issues, and other conditions linked to distress, impaired functioning, or self-harm risk [1, 2].

MDs have a significant economic impact, affecting individuals, families, and societies. By increasing productivity and lowering healthcare and social expenses, wise investments in mental health services and prevention techniques can have a significant positive economic impact [3]. Preventing mental disease and associated burden entails determining and changing a person’s traits, exposures, and qualities that endanger their mental health. The WHO [1, 2] identified three key domains of risk factors that influence mental health, emphasizing the complex interplay of personal, social, and environmental elements: individual attributes and behaviors, social and economic circumstances, and environmental factors [2]. In addition, the age of onset for MDs is crucial for preventing adverse childhood and adolescent experiences that impact adult mental health. Mental health problems with an early onset in childhood and adolescence can significantly impact development and extend into adulthood, with over half of adult MDs originating in early life [4]. Besides risk factors like age, grade levels, and education levels, the financial strain of a family drives the early onset of mental health problems in children [5].

Lifetime prevalence varies by gender and socioeconomic factors, affecting up to 50% of individuals aged 20–30. Notably, the beginning of around half of MDs is in adolescence, with three-quarters emerging by age 25 [6]. Cross-national and global studies show that MDs often begin early in life and pose a substantial public health burden. The World Mental Health surveys (2001–2022) reported a lifetime prevalence of around 29% and a morbid risk above 50% by age 75, with median onset in late adolescence [7]. In 2019, the global mean prevalence of MDs among individuals aged 5 to 24 years was 11.63%, affecting 293 million out of 2.516 billion children and youths. Over 31 million (1.22%) had a substance use disorder (SUD), anxiety disorder (AD) was the most common (3.35%), while schizophrenia was the least prevalent (0.08%). Prevalence varied by age, rising sharply during adolescence [8]. The global burden of MD in children and adolescents (less than 20 years old) was studied between 1990 and 2019 using the Global Burden of Disease 2019 database. MD caused 21.5 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in this age group in 2019, with a notable increase in AD, major depressive disorder (MDD), conduct disorder (CD), and eating disorders (ED) [9]. In high-income nations, prevalence among children under 18 was 12.7%, yet only 44.2% received treatment [10]. However, a scoping review identified a gap in systematic reviews addressing the prevalence of MDs among children and adolescents in Europe, specifically from community studies conducted between 2015 and 2020 [11].

A number of additional variables linked to the mental health of children and adolescents, such as age, gender, education, household socioeconomic status, sedentary lifestyle, social media use, comorbidities, family relationships, parents’ psychosocial conditions, and experiences related to COVID-19. Reviews found that anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, suicidal behavior, stress-related disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and other mental health problems were highly prevalent during the COVID-19 pandemic [12]. Higher prevalence rates for sleep disturbances (42%) and both anxiety and depression symptoms (31%) were linked to variables like age, gender, education level, grade level, geographic location, and use of technology [13].

In Greece, mental health issues are worsened by socioeconomic challenges and a strained healthcare system. Approximately 1.4 million people, or 12% of the population, are affected by mental health conditions. In children and adolescents aged 5 to 14, mental health disorders account for 24.6% of DALYs, making them a major cause of disability. Up to 11% of teenagers exhibit mental health symptoms that call for a professional assessment; these problems are probably made worse by the COVID-19 epidemic [14].

Recent research emphasizes the influence of socio-economic, demographic, and psychological factors on mental health outcomes [15], highlighting an escalating mental health crisis among youths, underscoring the necessity for evidence that can guide preventive strategies and reforms in services [16]. These insights reinforce the need for a revised systematic review and meta-analysis of MDs in children and adolescents on a global scale.

Objective

The main objective of the present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to give a thorough understanding of the prevalence, risk factors, and consequences of mental diseases in the global population by synthesizing the body of available research. Given the importance of infancy and adolescence for development, the burden of mental illnesses can be reduced with early detection and treatment. Furthermore, this systematic review identifies methodological discrepancies, points out gaps in the literature, and provides guidance for future research. Additionally, it helps educators, clinicians, and legislators create evidence-based preventative and intervention plans that are suited to the mental health requirements of kids. Overall, by comparing results across various populations and circumstances, it can provide information about differences in the effectiveness and accessibility of mental health care, assisting initiatives to enhance health outcomes.

Methodology

The present study is a systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature that used the PRISMA-2020 statement.

Search strategy

The systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was registered and is publicly available in PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews, under the registration code CRD420250630757 and is available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420250630757. The search for relevant studies was carried out independently by two researchers (PT and AS). Data were retrieved from different databases, specifically PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and PsycINFO. The search was conducted between September and December 2024 using the appropriate keywords. The strings were as follows: “prevalence”[Title/Abstract] OR “epidemiology”[Title/Abstract] OR “prevalence”[MeSH Terms] AND (mental disorders[Title/Abstract] OR psychiatric disorders[Title/Abstract] OR mental illness[Title/Abstract] OR mental diseases[Title/Abstract] OR mental disorders[MeSH Terms]) AND (children[MeSH Terms] OR adolescents[MeSH Terms]).

The search strategy adopted a PICO model. The PICO model served as the foundation for the search strategy and study selection. The PICO format offers a clear and succinct framework for developing a research question; by dissecting the question into its constituent parts (Participants, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes), researchers can make sure that their study is well-defined and focused, which enhances the transparency, reproducibility, and clarity of research. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

– Studies involving children and adolescents (typically aged 1–18 years) diagnosed with psychiatric disorders.

–Primary research studies and more specifically cross-sectional, cohort, case-control studies, epidemiological studies reporting on the prevalence, incidence, or distribution of psychiatric disorders, as these studies provide primary data that have not been collected or analysed in previous research.

– Studies that report key epidemiological outcomes such as prevalence rates, incidence rates, or risk factors associated with psychiatric disorders.

– Studies published the last 15 years in English language. However, if a limited number of relevant studies are identified, we may consider including studies published in other languages with the help of translation services.

– Studies conducted in diverse geographical regions, allowing for a global or regional analysis.

– Studies not focusing on psychiatric or MDs

– Editorials, commentaries, reviews, or other non-primary research articles

– Studies with insufficient sample size because they must have sufficient sample size for the results to be statistically reliable.

– Studies that lack clear diagnostic criteria (e.g. DSM-V, ICD-10) for mental health disorders to ensure the validity of diagnoses.

– Studies that lack clear and valid outcome measures related to mental health disorders.

Selection process and data collection

After retrieving records from databases, duplicates were removed. The remaining records were screened for relevance based on titles and abstracts to determine whether they met the inclusion criteria. A uniform data extraction form was developed, which included sections for participant information, research design, comparisons, authors, and study features, as well as areas for treatments, comparisons, and outcomes of interest. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies that passed the initial screening, and they were carefully examined to make sure they met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction of the included studies was also conducted independently by the two researchers (PT and AS). In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (AV) was involved to verify and resolve the discrepancies through consensus.

Data extraction

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome of interest was to determine the prevalence of MDs in children and adolescents aged 0 to 18 years. All available prevalence data were gathered from the included studies. MDs were classified according to DSM-V [17] and ICD-10 [18], while, in addition, some studies used tools as the Children and Adolescent Psychiatric Assessment (CAPA) [19, 20], the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children (MINI-KID) [21] and the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) [22]. Prevalence estimates were extracted for diagnostic categories including depressive, disorders, AD, ADHD, and others. In cases where multiple prevalence estimates were reported for the same disorder, the most recent and detailed data was selected.

Other variables collected

In addition to prevalence outcomes, each study‘s characteristics (e.g., author, year of publication, country, study design), sample characteristics (e.g., total sample size, age range, sex distribution), diagnostic criteria, and tools used (e.g., DSM-V, ICD-10, CAPA, CDI) were extracted.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Studies were considered eligible for synthesis based on the set inclusion criteria. First, a tabulated comparison of study characteristics was used to assess consistency. In cases where relevant data were missing or unclear, they were noted as “Not Reported” in the data extraction form, and no assumptions were made unless explicitly justified by the authors of the original study. Statistical analyses were conducted using the R software environment (version 4.5.0 [23]). Effect sizes (e.g., odds ratios, proportions, or mean differences) were calculated for each study selected for meta-analysis, ensuring they are standardized for comparison. Data were gathered into structured tables and visually presented using forest plots to present individual study estimates and overall pooled prevalence. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator across studies (ranging from three to 10–14) examining the prevalence of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents across different populations and continents, including Europe, Asia, America, Australia, and Africa. A fixed-effects model assumed a common true effect across studies, while a random-effects model assumed variability in true effects, often used when there is high heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was assessed using statistics like the I2, Q-test, and tau-squared, which help determine how much variation across studies is due to real differences rather than random error. Sensitivity analyses were also performed by omitting lower-quality studies. Additionally, publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression and Kendall’s rank correlation tests.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the included studies was conducted independently by the same two researchers (PT and AS) via the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools [24] and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tools [25]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the third researcher (AV), who verified the assessments and contributed to the consensus.

Study selection

Several full-text articles were excluded for well-justified reasons to ensure methodological rigor and alignment with the review’s objectives. Specifically, out of the 57 articles assessed for eligibility, 9 articles were excluded for failure to meet the predefined inclusion criteria regarding the target population, the type of intervention, or the outcomes assessed (Reason 1) and 11 for insufficient or incomplete data reporting, which prevented reliable data extraction and synthesis (Reason 2).

Study characteristics

Among the population studies, 37 research articles were finally included, totaling 1,599,322 children and adolescents, diagnosed with MDs by DSM or ICD, from all continents Asia, America, Australia, Europe, and Africa. 10 studies were conducted in Asia and included 66,033 participants. Among the included countries in Asia, 6 studies were conducted in China, 2 in Nepal, 1 in Malaysia, 1 in Indonesia, and 1 in Ardabil. 14 studies were retrieved concerning primary research conducted in America and Australia, and a total of 101,038 participants were included. In Europe a total of 10 studies were included with 1,419,121 participants. The studies concerning Africa included an overall number of 3,145 participants. Quality assessment of the included studies using the JBI and GRADE tools

Assessing the methodological quality of the included studies using JBI revealed that 36 out of 37 studies included were rated as having low to moderate risk of bias, while only one had high risk of bias. Moreover, the GRADE approach used to evaluate the certainty of the evidence for the main outcomes demonstrated moderate to high certainty, indicating a reasonable level of confidence in the synthesised results.

Sensitivity analysis was carried out by removing lower-quality studies that were identified as high risk of bias according to the JBI tool, in Asia, America, Australia and Europe; however, this was not feasible in the African region due to the limited number of included studies from that continent, which did not allow for meaningful re-analysis. The pooled prevalence estimates remained stable after exclusion, further supporting the reliability and robustness of the main findings.

Asia

A sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding studies with high or moderate risk of bias according to the JBI tool. After removing Jha et al. [33], Dhimal et al. [28], and Molavi et al. [34], the pooled prevalence estimate for MDs in children and adolescents across Asia was 13.96% (SE = 0.0180, 95% CI [10.43%, 17.49%]), indicating slightly lower and more precise estimates. The heterogeneity decreased (I2 = 99.34%, τ2 = 0.0022), although it remained substantial, suggesting persistent true variability across studies. Importantly, publication bias was not detected (Egger’s test: p = 0.9440; Kendall’s τ = −0.048, p = 1.000), further supporting the robustness of the findings.

America & Australia

To assess the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the study by Johnson et al. [42], which was rated as moderate risk of bias with a moderate GRADE level. The resulting pooled prevalence remained stable at 30.14% (SE = 0.0727, 95% CI [15.89%, 44.39%]), consistent with the original estimate of 30.64%. Although heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 99.96%, τ2 = 0.0686), the consistency of the pooled estimate supports the reliability of the overall findings. Publication bias was not indicated by Egger’s test (p = 0.193) or Kendall’s tau (p = 0.164), reinforcing confidence in the validity of the results.

Europe

To test the robustness of the European studies, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding three studies [52, 58]; and [55]) that were rated as moderate risk of bias. The revised pooled prevalence estimate was 38.15% (SE = 0.0918, 95% CI [20.15%, 56.16%]), slightly higher than the original 30.44%, but within a statistically consistent range. Heterogeneity remained extremely high (I2 = 99.98%, τ2 = 0.0588), indicating that variability in prevalence persists across studies. No evidence of publication bias was observed (Egger’s test: p = 0.130; Kendall’s tau: p = 1.000). These findings suggest that the overall result is robust, and no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate.

Subgroup and metaregression analysis

To seek answers on the high heterogeneity, we fitted random-effects meta-analyses on the logit of proportions (PLO) with REML estimation. All prevalence estimates and CIs are reported after inverse-logit back-transformation to the proportion scale (0–1), presented as percentages for readability. Heterogeneity statistics (τ2, τ, I2, H2, Q) were computed on the transformed scale.

Asia

A random-effects meta-analysis of six Asian studies (k = 6 [26–29, 32 and 33]) estimated the pooled prevalence of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents at 13.0% (95% CI [9.1%, 18.3%]), corresponding to approximately one in eight youths. Heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99.6%, Q(5) = 1783.05, p < 0.001), consistent with substantial variation across studies. A subgroup analysis by methodological quality (JBI rating) showed no significant differences in prevalence between low-risk studies (13.8%) and moderate/high-risk studies (12.0%) (QM(1) = 0.51, p = 0.474). This indicates that study quality, at least as rated by the JBI tool, does not account for observed variability. A subgroup analysis indicated that among the different diagnostic tools, only the K-SADS showed a statistically significant positive effect (estimate = 16.46, SE = 7.02, Z = 2.5, p = 0.019, 95%CI: 2.71–0.21). A meta-regression using midpoint age suggested a non-significant downward trend in prevalence with increasing age (b = −0.0271, p = 0.085). Estimated prevalence decreased from 15.8% in younger cohorts (≈10 years) to 10.5% in older cohorts (≈20 years). While not statistically conclusive, this pattern suggests that younger samples may show slightly higher prevalence, possibly reflecting developmental vulnerabilities or methodological factors (e.g., parental vs. self-report). Age explained about 29% of heterogeneity (R2 = 28.6%), suggesting some explanatory power but leaving most heterogeneity unaccounted for. In summary, psychiatric disorder prevalence among Asian youth is estimated at around 13%, with extremely high heterogeneity that cannot be explained by study quality or age alone. This indicates the presence of other sources of variability (e.g., diagnostic tools, cultural differences in symptom reporting, or study contexts) that future research should investigate.

America and Australia

A random-effects synthesis of 10 studies [38, 40–42–48, 62] from America and Australia estimated a pooled prevalence of 5.1% (95% CI [4.1%, 6.1%]) for psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate to high (I2 = 84.3%), and the Q-test indicated significant dispersion around the mean (Q(9) = 69.97, p < 0.001). Study quality (JBI low vs. moderate/high) did not moderate prevalence (QM(1) = 0.13, p = 0.716). Back-transformed subgroup estimates were similar, at 5.5% in low-risk studies and at 5.0% in moderate/high-risk studies, indicating that design quality, as rated by JBI, does not explain the between-study variance. A subgroup analysis indicated that the most diagnostic tools detected significantly fewer cases compared to the intercept. Specifically, DSM-IV (estimate = −1.25, SE = 0.36, Z = −3.51, p = 0.0004, 95% CI: −1.94 to −0.55), DISC-IV (estimate = −1.66, SE = 0.42, Z = −3.93, p < 0.001, 95% CI: −2.49 to −0.83), ICD-9 (estimate = −0.02, SE = 0.01, Z = −2.69, p = 0.007, 95%CI: −0.04 to −0.01), and K-SADS-PL (estimate = −1.89, SE = 0.07, Z = −26.14, p < 0.001, 95% CI: −2.03 to 1.75) all showed statistically significant negative effects, while DSM-IV-TR and DSM-V did not yield significant results. A meta-regression on mean age found a null, near-zero slope (b = 0.0007, p = 0.813) and no practical change in predicted prevalence across typical adolescent ages after back-transformation, suggesting that age does not account for the residual heterogeneity in these samples. The moderator R2 was 0%.

Europe

A random-effects meta-analysis of six European studies (k = 6 [50, 53, 55–58]) estimated the pooled prevalence of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents at 29.6% (95% CI [18.0%, 46.5%]), indicating that nearly one in three European youths meet diagnostic criteria. Heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99.9%, Q(5) = 1168.61, p < 0.001), highlighting substantial differences in prevalence estimates across studies. A subgroup analysis by methodological quality (JBI rating) revealed no significant differences between low-risk (26.7%) and moderate/high-risk (35.1%) studies (QM(1) = 0.25, p = 0.617), suggesting that study quality did not explain heterogeneity. However, subgroup analysis revealed that ICD-9 (estimate = −2.93, SE = 0.02, Z = −168.85, p < 0.001, 95%CI: −2.97 to −2.90) and ICD-10 (estimate = −1.7338, SE = 0.002, Z = −706.07, p < 0.001, 95%CI: −1.74 to −1.73) were the only diagnostic tools showing statistically significant effects. In Contrast, SDQ, DAWBA, DSM-IV, and DSM-V did not demonstrate significant associations. Μeta-regression showed that mean participant age was a significant moderator (b = 0.0634, SE = 0.0145, z = 4.36, p < 0.001), with prevalence increasing sharply across adolescence. Prevalence was estimated at 17% in pre-adolescents (~10 years), 31% in mid-adolescents (~15 years), and nearly 48% in young adults (~20 years). Age explained almost 79% of between-study variance (R2 = 78.7%), making it the primary driver of heterogeneity in European studies.

Discussion

The findings of this meta-analysis provide vital insights into the pooled prevalence of the illness across different locations, demonstrating significant diversity in prevalence rates. The total pooled prevalence estimate under the random-effects model demonstrates considerable heterogeneity across the included studies. The reported prevalence rates may be impacted by variations in study populations, methodology, and regional factors, as seen by this high degree of heterogeneity. The findings were supported by a moderate to high certainty of evidence, as assessed by the GRADE approach, which took into account the overall risk of bias, consistency, and precision of the studies included. A sensitivity analysis that excluded the studies with high and moderate risk of bias revealed no meaningful change in the pooled prevalence estimates, further confirming the results’ robustness and reliability. Collectively, both the certainty assessment and the stability of the findings indicate that the drawn conclusions are strongly supported and unlikely to be significantly affected by methodological variations.

The findings show clear differences in occurrence between various geographic areas. With a pooled prevalence estimate of 14.53% (95% CI: 9.81%, 19.24%), and extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.76%) in Asia, almost all of the observed variation was attributable to study variations rather than chance. Similarly, the estimated pooled prevalence in Africa was lower than in Asia, at 9.86% (95% CI: 7.44%, 12.27%). An I2 value of 78.8% from the heterogeneity analysis indicated moderate to significant between-study variability. Even though this degree of variation is less than that seen in Asian research, it nevertheless implies that contextual elements including socioeconomic status, medical facilities, and environmental factors have an impact on the stated prevalence rates. In America, the overall pooled prevalence estimate was 30.64% (95% CI: 17.41%, 43.87%), similar to the estimate for Asia. However, extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 99.95%) indicates that nearly all the observed variation is due to between-study differences. The subgroup analysis depicted that the high heterogeneity can be attributed to the diagnostic tool. In contrast, in Europe, the meta-analysis results showed a substantially higher pooled prevalence of 30.44% (95% CI: 15.68%, 45.21%). This estimate suggests that, on average, the prevalence in Europe is much higher than in Asia, Africa, and America.

The present results are in accordance with those obtained from other meta-analysis. A meta-analysis of community studies from 27 countries across all world regions estimated that 13.4% (95% CI: 11.3–15.9%) of children and adolescents are affected by MDs. Significant heterogeneity was observed, which was attributed to factors such as sample representativeness, sample frame, and diagnostic interview methods [63]. A meta-analysis of ten epidemiological studies involving 18,282 children (aged 12–83 months) from eight countries found a pooled prevalence of MDs at 20.1%. The 95% confidence interval for this prevalence was between 15.7% and 25.4% [64]. Also, higher percentage was observed in a meta-analysis of 157 articles that identified a percentage of overall prevalence of 17.6% (95% CI: 16.3–18.9%) [65].

However, the extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 99.99%) and a high Q-statistic indicate that the studies included in the meta-analysis exhibit significant variability. The differences in prevalence and associated factors reported across countries may partly reflect variations in methodological approaches, sample sizes, sample characteristics, the type of stigma assessed, prevailing attitudes toward mental illness, and the specific study regions or settings. In addition, research from African nations has highlighted that stigma and barriers to mental health care are strongly shaped by broader socioeconomic challenges, including poverty, limited resources, and high rates of unemployment [66], 67].

Across regions, our analyses revealed both consistencies and differences. In Asia, the pooled prevalence of psychiatric disorders was approximately 14–15% (95% CI 9–18%), but with extreme heterogeneity (I2 ≈ 99.6%). Neither study quality (JBI) nor age significantly explained this variance, though age showed a borderline trend toward higher prevalence in younger samples. In subgroup analysis, the K_SADS was the only tool that demonstrated a significant positive effect (p = 0.019), while other diagnostic approaches did not yield significant results. In Europe, the pooled prevalence was higher, around 20–30%, again with substantial heterogeneity (I2 ≈ 99.9%). Unlike Asia, here age emerged as a strong moderator: older adolescent samples showed significantly greater prevalence (meta-regression slope b ≈ 0.06, p < 0.001), explaining nearly 79% of between-study variance. However, even after accounting for age, heterogeneity remained high, suggesting that additional unmeasured factors (diagnostic thresholds, sociocultural context, service access) contribute. In subgroup analysis, ICD-9 and ICD-10 emerged as the only diagnostic tools yielding significant effects, while other tools such as SDQ, DAWBA, DSM-IV, and DSM-V did not show significant results. In America and Australia, pooled prevalence was lower, at ~5% (95% CI 4–6%), and heterogeneity was moderate (I2 ≈ 84%), markedly less than in Asia and Europe. Neither JBI study quality nor sample age predicted prevalence, indicating relative stability across methodological strata. This suggests a more consistent epidemiological picture in these regions, though cultural and methodological factors may still underlie the residual heterogeneity. In subgroup analysis, several diagnostic tools -including DSM-IV, DISC-IV, ICD-9, and K-SADS-PL- were associated with significantly lower prevalence estimates, whereas DSM-IV-TR and DSM-V showed no significant effects.

Taken together, these findings confirm that psychiatric disorder prevalence among children and adolescents varies meaningfully by region, with Asia and Europe showing higher and more heterogeneous estimates, while America and Australia show lower, more consistent estimates. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression clarify that study quality does not drive differences, while age is a key factor only in Europe.

However, the study has some limitations, including the very high residual heterogeneity, which indicates that additional unmeasured moderators (e.g., impairment thresholds, socioeconomic or cultural context) remain unexplained. Furthermore, our analysis relied on aggregate (study-level) data, limiting causal inference. First, the extreme heterogeneity across studies limits the interpretability of pooled estimates, suggesting that regional and methodological differences significantly impact prevalence rates. Second, differences in diagnostic criteria and assessment tools across studies may contribute to variability, making direct comparisons challenging. Third, publication bias could influence the findings, as studies with significant results are more likely to be published than those with null findings. Nonetheless, the consistent detection of substantial heterogeneity underscores the need for harmonized epidemiological methods in youth psychiatry research. Future research should address these limitations by standardizing diagnostic criteria, conducting subgroup analyses, and using meta-regression techniques to explore sources of heterogeneity.

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the significant burden on the mental health of children and adolescents worldwide, confirming the need for prevention, early identification, and supportive intervention. Enriching the clinical practices with the applied findings can improve early diagnosis by using valid and standardized diagnostic tools. The data highlights that at a policy healthcare level, mental health services for children, adolescents, and their families need to be strengthened, particularly in low income countries. Turning to future research, a focus on underrepresented areas and the use of common diagnostic criteria would be beneficial in enhancing the comparability and generalizability of findings.
