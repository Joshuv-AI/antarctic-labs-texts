Abstract
. We obtain Liouville type theorems for degenerate elliptic equation with a drift term and a potential. The diffusion is driven by Hörmander operators. We show that the conditions imposed on the coefficients of the operator are optimal. Indeed, when they fail we prove that infinitely many bounded solutions exist.

1.
Introduction

The study of degenerate-elliptic Schrödinger-type equations has gained considerable attention in recent years due to their intricate structural properties and their connections to various fields of analysis and geometry. In this paper, we focus on the equation

(
E
)

\mathcal{L}u=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}u+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(x)X_{i}u-Q(x)u=0\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

where
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
is a family of smooth vector fields satisfying the Hörmander rank condition,

which are not necessarily
left - invariant with respect to any Lie - group structure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The coefficients
b_{i}(x)
and the potential function
Q(x)
are assumed to satisfy suitable regularity and positivity conditions.

Our main objective is to establish optimal conditions on the vector fields and the potential
Q
that ensure the validity of the Liouville property for solutions of (

E

). More precisely, we aim to determine when every bounded, smooth solution of (

E

) must necessarily be trivial, i.e.,

(
LP
)

if
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a bounded solution of (

E

), then
u\equiv 0
.

As it frequently happens when
global properties
are involved,
the question of the Liouville property for degenerate operators has been mainly studied
in the context of Lie groups, under the assumption
Q\equiv 0
, see, e.g.,

[
5
,
12
,
14
,
16
]
. The case of (not necessarily invariant) Hörmander operators, like
the ones considered here, is not as well studied; in this direction,
we mention e.g., the papers
[
13
,
15
]
, where the Authors consider again the case
Q\equiv 0
.
We stress that, in all the aforementioned papers,
the Authors employ techniques different from those considered here.

For nonzero potential functions, a Liouville theorem for the equation on a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold was obtained in
[
9
]
. Inspired by this approach, the work in
[
3
]
considered the equation

\Delta u+\langle b(x),\nabla u\rangle-Q(x)u=0

in a bounded, connected open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This result was further generalized in
[
4
]
to the setting of divergence-form operators:

\mathrm{div}\big\{A(x)\nabla u\big\}-Q(x)u=0\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

where
A(x)
is a symmetric, uniformly elliptic matrix with
C^{2}
entries, and
Q(x)
is a non-negative, locally Hölder-continuous function.

In this paper, we extend the results of
[
9
,
3
,
4
]
to the degenerate-elliptic setting of (

E

), where the operators involved exhibit a highly non-Euclidean structure. This generalization presents new challenges, particularly due to the presence of the vector fields
X_{i}
satisfying the Hörmander condition, which induce a sub-Riemannian geometry. We develop novel techniques tailored to this setting, addressing the fundamental difficulties arising from the degeneracy of the operator and the interplay between the drift terms and the potential function.

1.1.
Statement of the main result

We now turn to state and describe our main result.
To this end, we
fix here the main assumptions we require on the vector fields

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, and we refer to Section
2
for some related comments.

Throughout what follows, we set

\mathcal{L}_{0}u=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{j}^{2}u+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(x)X_{i}u,

and we refer to the vector field
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(x)X_{i}
as the
drift of
\mathcal{L}
.

Notation.
In what follows, we will exploit the
notation listed below.

i)

\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the
Lie algebra of the smooth
vector fields
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

ii)

If
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\mathrm{Lie}(A)
denotes the

smallest Lie sub-algebra of
\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;

iii)

If
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is fixed, we set

X_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};

iv)

Given any
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we denote by
X^{*}

the formal adjoint of
X
(with respect
to the usual scalar product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
), that is,

(1.1)

u\mapsto X^{*}u=-Xu-\mathrm{div}(X_{x})u.

By definition,
X^{*}
is the unique first-order operator such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u\,Xv\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\,X^{*}u\,dx,

for every
u,v\in\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, provided that at least one of the two functions has
compact support.
Note that
X^{*}
is not necessarily a vector field, unless

\mathrm{div}(X_{x})\equiv 0.

v)

If
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(with
m\geq 2
), we set

(1.2)

\begin{split}\ast)\,\,&\nabla_{X}f=(X_{1}f,\ldots,X_{m}f)\quad\forall\,\,f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\\
\ast)\,\,&\mathrm{div}_{X}(F)=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}F_{i}\quad\forall\,\,F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{m})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m}).\end{split}

We explicitly point out that, as in the classical case when
X_{i}=\partial_{x_{i}}
,
the above differential operators are related by the following formulas

(1.3)

\begin{split}\mathrm{i)}\,\,&\textstyle\mathrm{div}_{X}(\nabla_{X}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}u,\\
\mathrm{ii)}\,\,&\mathrm{div}_{X}(uF)=\langle\nabla_{X}u,F\rangle+u\,\mathrm{div}_{X}(F),\end{split}

holding true for every
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and every
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{m})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Furthermore, owing to (
1.2
) we can re-write
\mathcal{L}_{0}
as

\mathcal{L}_{0}u=\mathrm{div}_{X}(\nabla_{X}u)+\langle\mathbf{b}(x),\nabla_{X}u\rangle,

where
\mathbf{b}
is the vector-valued function
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
.

Assumptions
.

We assume that
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
(with
m\geq 2
)
is a family of
smooth vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(that is,

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
) such that

(H1)

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are homogeneous of degree
1

with respect to a family of
non-isotropic dilations

\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the following form

\delta_{\lambda}(x):=(\lambda^{\sigma_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}x_{n}),

where
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and

1=\sigma_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\sigma_{n}
.
This means, precisely, that for every
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,\lambda>0
one has

X_{i}(f\circ\delta_{\lambda})=\lambda(X_{i}f)\circ\delta_{\lambda}\quad(i=1,\ldots,m).

(H2)

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy the
Hörmander rank condition at
x=0
, that is,

\mathrm{dim}\big\{Z_{0}:\,Z\in\mathrm{Lie}(X)\big\}=n,

Let
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
be an exhaustion function with compact sublevels, which is
C^{1}
outside some compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Given any
r>0
large enough, we set

(1.4)

\mathcal{S}(r)=\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Note that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)=S(x)^{T}\nabla\mathcal{N}(x)

at every point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus K
, for a suitable
n\times m
matrix
S(x)
, see (
2.2
). Therefore we have

0\leq\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}(x)|}\leq|S(x)|^{2}|\nabla\mathcal{N}(x)|

and thus with an abuse of notation we agree to set

\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}|}=0

at all points where
|\nabla\mathcal{N}|=0
. We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfy assumptions
(H1)
-
(H2)
.
Moreover, let
\mathbf{b}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
Q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
be an exhaustion function with compact sublevels which is
C^{1}
outside some compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We suppose that

(S)

Q\geq 0
and
Q\not\equiv 0
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(G)

there exist constants

\kappa,\,\rho_{0}>0
and a function
\hat{q}:(\rho_{0},+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
,

continuous and non identically
0
on
\mathcal{I}_{0}=(\rho_{0},+\infty)
, such that

i)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathcal{N}(x)>\rho_{0}
, we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle Q(x)\geq|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x));

(1.6)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{b}(x)|^{2}+(\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b}))_{-}\leq\kappa\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathcal{N}(x)}\!\!\!\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\Big)^{2}\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x));

ii)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathcal{N}(x)\leq\rho_{0}
, we have

(1.7)

|\mathbf{b}(x)|^{2}+(\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b}))_{-}\leq\kappa Q(x).

Here,
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
and

(\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b}))_{-}
is the negative part of
\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})

(
as defined in the above notation (
1.2
)
)
, that is,

\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})_{-}=\max\{-\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b}),0\}.

Then, if there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
such that

(1.8)

\int_{\rho_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}(r)}\exp\Big\{\Lambda\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{r}\sqrt{\hat{q}(s)}\,ds\Big)^{2}\Big\}\,dr=+\infty,

the Liouville property (

⁢
L
P

) holds for equation (

E

).

Remark 1.2
.

Note that if in Theorem
1.1
we let
Q\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then by condition
(G)
we immediately derive that it must also be
\mathbf{b}\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence equation (

E

) reduces to

\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}u=0,

for which the Liouville property is known to hold, see e.g.
[
13
]
.

, is shown in Section

in

-
(H2)

2)

\mathcal{L}
is as in (

), with

Moreover, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrary open set and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

C^{k}_{+}(\mathcal{O})=\{f\in C^{k}(\mathcal{O}):\,\text{$f\geq 0$ pointwise in $\mathcal{O}$}\}.

Some relevant properties of
\mathcal{L}

As anticipated, we begin this section
by listing some distinguished properties of
\mathcal{L}

which follow from assumptions
(H1)
-
(H2)
; we will repeatedly exploit these properties
in the sequel.

To begin with we observe that, by combining

(H1)
-
(H2)
, it is not difficult to recognize
that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy Hörmander’s Rank Condition
not only at the origin, but
at every point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
, Remark 6]
), that is,

(2.1)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\big\{Z_{x}:\,Z\in\mathrm{Lie}(X)\big\}\big)=n,\qquad\text{for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$};

hence,
\mathcal{L}
falls in the class of the so-called
Hörmander operators
.
As a consequence of this fact, we infer the following
key properties.

1)

By the celebrated Hörmander’s Hypoellipticity
Theorem
[
11
]
, the operator

\mathcal{L}
is
C^{\infty}
-hypoelliptic
in every open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; this means, precisely, that
for every open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
we have

\text{$\mathcal{L}u=f$ in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

In particular, if
u
is any distributional solution of (

E

), then

u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2)

The operator
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
the following Strong Maximum Principle
(SMP):

assume that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a
connected open set, and let

u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be such that

\mathcal{L}u\geq 0
on
\Omega
; if
u
attains a non-negative maximum in
\Omega
, then
u
is
constant in
\Omega
(see, e.g.,

[
7
, Corollary 3.1]
for a proof).

3)

The operator
\mathcal{L}
also satisfies the
following Weak Maximum Principle (WMP):

let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a bounded open set, and let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
; then,

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u\geq 0&\text{in $\Omega$}\\
u\leq 0&\text{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\text{$u\leq 0$
throughout $\Omega$}

(see, e.g.,
[
2
, Proposition 10.16]
for a proof).

4)

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is
non-totally degenerate
(NTD), that is,

A(x)\neq 0\quad\text{for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

where
A(x)
is the
principal matrix of
\mathcal{L}
.
In fact, by direct computation we see that
the matrix
A(x)
takes the following explicit expression

(2.2)

\begin{gathered}A(x)=S(x)\cdot S(x)^{T},\\
\text{where
$S(x)=\big((X_{1})_{x}\cdots(X_{m})_{x}\big)\in M_{n,m}(\mathbb{R})$}\end{gathered}

(since we can write
\mathcal{L}u=\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u)+Wu-Q(x)u
for some
W\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
);
on the other hand, since (
2.1
) holds,
for every fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists

1\leq k_{x}\leq m
such that

(X_{k_{x}})_{x}\neq 0,

and thus
A(x)=S(x)\cdot S(x)^{T}\neq 0
, as claimed.

We also point out that,
since the vector fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy condition
(H1)
, for every
i=1,\ldots,m
we have

\operatorname{div}((X_{i})_{x})\equiv 0
(here,
\operatorname{div}

denotes the usual divergence operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
),
see, e.g.,
[
6
, Section 1.5]
; hence,
by (
1.1
) we have

X_{i}^{*}=-X_{i},

and the following integration by parts formula holds

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}vX_{i}u\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}uX_{i}v\,dx

for every
u,v\in\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, if at least one of the two functions has compact support.

Remark 2.1
.

We explicitly remark that the above properties hold for any Hörmander operator of the form

\mathcal{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}+Z-c

with
Z\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
c\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
c\geq 0
.

Auxiliary results

Now that we have recalled the most relevant properties
of
\mathcal{L}
,

which will be used (as key ingredients)
in the proof of Theorems

The first result we aim to prove is the following

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
(non-trivial) bounded solution of (

E

).
Then, there exists a solution
\hat{u}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (

E

),
further satisfying

\text{$0<\hat{u}\leq 1$ pointwise in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

To prove Proposition
2.2
, we first establish the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists a bounded, open and convex neighborhood
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the origin
with the following property:
for every
Z\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, every
c\in C_{+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
every
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
and every
\varphi\in C(\partial\Omega_{1})
,
there exists a
unique

u\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
s.t.

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}u=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}u+Zu-c(x)u=f&\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1})$}\\
u=\varphi&\text{pointwise on $\partial\Omega_{1}$}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.4
.

As it will be clear for the proof, in Lemma
2.3

it is enough to assume that the vector field
Z
and the functions
c

are defined (and smooth) on an open neighborhood of the origin; we also point out that
the neighborhood
\Omega_{1}
constructed in Lemma
2.3
depends only on the vector fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
.

Proof.

We split the proof in two steps.

Step 1.
We start with the case when
c>0
. Since
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy the Hörmander condition, the matrix of the principal part of the operator
\mathcal{P}
, given by (
2.2
), is non-totally degenerate. Hence there exist
\nu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\nu_{0}|=1
,
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of the origin such that

(2.5)

\langle A(x)\nu,\nu\rangle\geq\delta>0

for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
and all
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\nu|=1
and
|\nu-\nu_{0}|<\varepsilon
. Then following
[
7
, Corollary 5.2]
we can define

\Omega_{1}=B_{M+\eta}(M\nu_{0})\cap B_{M+\eta}(-M\nu_{0})

for a suitably small
\eta>0
and a suitably large
M>0
, so that
\Omega_{1}\subset\mathcal{V}
and it satisfies the
non-characteristic exterior ball condition
at each point of its boundary. In other terms, for every
x\in\partial\Omega_{1}
there exists a ball
B=B_{r}(\xi)
such that

\overline{B}\cap\overline{\Omega}_{1}=\{x\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\langle A(x)(\xi-x),(\xi-x)\rangle>0.

Therefore by
[
7
, Theoerem 5.2]
we can conclude that
for every
Z\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, every
c\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
c>0
,
every
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
and every
\varphi\in C(\partial\Omega_{1})
, there exists a unique solution

u\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
of (
2.4
).

Step 2.
Now we consider the general case when
c\geq 0
. Let
\Omega_{1}
be the neighborhood of the origin constructed in Step 1. Let
Z
,
c
,
f
and
\varphi
be as in the statement of the Lemma; we set

\psi(x)=K-e^{-\alpha\langle x,\nu_{0}\rangle}

for suitable
K,\alpha>0
to be chosen later such that
\psi>0
on
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, and we define

\mathcal{Q}u=\frac{1}{\psi}\mathcal{P}(\psi u).

Now it is easy to see that

\mathcal{Q}u=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}u+\widetilde{Z}u-\widetilde{c}(x)u

with
\widetilde{Z}\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\widetilde{c}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

\widetilde{Z}u=Zu+\frac{2}{\psi}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\psi X_{i}u,\qquad\widetilde{c}=c-\frac{1}{\psi}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X^{2}_{i}\psi+Z\psi\right).

Moreover a simple computation using (
2.5
) shows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\psi(x)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X^{2}_{i}\psi(x)+Z\psi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\alpha\langle x,\nu_{0}\rangle}}{K-e^{-\alpha\langle x,\nu_{0}\rangle}}\left(\alpha^{2}\langle A(x)\nu_{0},\nu_{0}\rangle-\alpha\langle Z_{x},\nu_{0}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-\alpha\langle x,\nu_{0}\rangle}}{K-e^{-\alpha\langle x,\nu_{0}\rangle}}\left(\delta\alpha^{2}-\alpha\|Z_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})}\right)>0,

provided that
\alpha,K>0
are chosen sufficiently large.

By Step 1 there exists a unique solution
v\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
of

\begin{cases}\mathcal{Q}v=\frac{f}{\psi}&\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1})$}\\
v=\frac{\varphi}{\psi}&\text{pointwise on $\partial\Omega_{1}$}.\end{cases}

Then
u=\psi v\in C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
is clearly a solution of problem (
2.4
), and it is unique.

2)

given any

c\in C^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

Proof.

For every

.

Let

be a bounded nontrivial solution of (

be as in Lemma

and, accordingly, let

be the unique distributional solution to problem

Note that, since

is

Note also that by the Weak Maximum Principle

v_{k}\geq 0\quad\text{ in }\,\,\mathcal{V}_{k}\,.

Furthermore, since

From (

) we deduce that

v_{k}\geq u_{+}\quad\text{ in }\,\,\mathcal{V}_{k}\,.

Moreover, for any

\zeta_{R}
), we get

\displaystyle Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{Q(x)}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}+\frac{f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}}{4(2\tau-t)^{2}}\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq-\frac{3}{4}|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}}{(2\tau-t)^{2}};

moreover, from (
1.6
) (and since
R>2\rho_{0}
), we also have

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}|\mathbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})\leq C_{1}\big(|\mathbf{b}|^{2}+\mathrm{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})_{-}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C_{1}\kappa\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\bigg(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathcal{N}(x)}\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\bigg)^{2}\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))

\displaystyle\qquad=16C_{1}\kappa(2\tau-t)^{2}\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}}{(2\tau-t)^{2}},

for some
C_{1}>0
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\mathbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\Big(-\frac{3}{4}+16C_{1}\kappa(2\tau-t)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{recalling that $0\leq t\leq\tau$})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\Big(-\frac{3}{4}+64C_{1}\kappa\tau^{2}\Big).

As a consequence, if we choose
\tau_{0}>0
so small that

64C_{1}\kappa\tau_{0}^{2}\leq 3/4

(notice that the smallness of
\tau_{0}
only depends on
C_{1}
and on
\kappa
),
we conclude that

Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\mathbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})\leq 0,

for every
x\in\Omega
with
\mathcal{N}(x)>R
and
0\leq t\leq\tau
,
provided that
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.

-  Case II:

\mathcal{N}(x)<R
.
In this case, by definition of
\zeta_{R}
we infer that

(3.6)

Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}=Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}=-\frac{Q(x)}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\gamma_{R}^{2}.

To estimate the term
\vartheta_{1}|\mathbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})
, instead, we
consider two different sub-cases, according
to the growth condition in assumption
(G)
.

\mathrm{\emph{-\,\,CaseII)}}_{a}
:
\mathcal{N}(x)>\rho_{0}
.
In this case,
by exploiting once again (
1.6
) (and by taking into account the explicit
definition of
\gamma_{R}
), we get

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\mathbf{b})\leq C_{1}\kappa\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\mathcal{N}(x)}\!\!\!\sqrt{\hat{q}}\,ds\Big)^{2}\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))\\[1.42271pt]
&\qquad(\text{since we are assuming $\mathcal{N}(x)<R$})\\[1.42271pt]
&\qquad\leq 16C_{1}\kappa\,\gamma_{R}^{2}\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\,\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))\\
&\qquad=16C_{1}\kappa(2\tau-t)^{2}\,\gamma_{R}^{2}\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}.\end{split}

Gathering (
3.6
)-(
3.7
),
and using (
1.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\textbf{b})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\gamma_{R}^{2}\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\big(16C_{1}\kappa(2\tau-t)^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{recalling that $0\leq t\leq\tau$})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\gamma_{R}^{2}\,|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\frac{\hat{q}(\mathcal{N}(x))}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\big(64C_{1}\kappa\tau^{2}-1\big).

As a consequence, if we choose
\tau_{0}>0
so small that
64C_{1}\kappa\tau_{0}^{2}\leq 1

(notice that, once again, the smallness of
\tau_{0}
only
depends on
C_{1}
and
\kappa
), we conclude that

Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\textbf{b})\leq 0,

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\rho_{0}<\mathcal{N}(x)<R
and
0\leq t\leq\tau
,
provided that
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.

\mathrm{\emph{-\,\,CaseII)}}_{b}
:
\mathcal{N}(x)\leq\rho_{0}
.
In this case, instead, by exploiting
(
1.7
)
(and by recalling that
R>2\rho_{0}
), we have the following easier estimate

(3.8)

\begin{split}\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\textbf{b})&\leq C_{1}\kappa Q(x)\\
&=C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{2\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\bigg)^{2}Q(x)\\
&\leq 16C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\gamma_{R}^{2}Q(x)\\
&=16C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}(2\tau-t)^{2}\gamma_{R}^{2}\frac{Q(x)}{(2\tau-t)^{2}},\end{split}

where the constant
\kappa^{\prime}>0
is given by

\kappa^{\prime}:=\kappa\,\bigg(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{2\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\bigg)^{-2}.

We explicitly stress that
\kappa^{\prime}
only depends
on
\kappa,\,\rho_{0}
and on the function
q
,
which are fixed
from the very beginning
in assumption
(G)
.

Then, gathering (
3.6
) and
(
3.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\textbf{b})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\gamma_{R}^{2}\frac{Q(x)}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\big(16C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}(2\tau-t)^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{recalling that $0\leq t\leq\tau$})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\gamma_{R}^{2}\frac{Q(x)}{(2\tau-t)^{2}}\big(64C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\tau^{2}-1\big).

As a consequence, if we choose
\tau_{0}>0
so small that

64C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\tau_{0}^{2}\leq 1

(note that the smallness of
\tau_{0}
only
depends on
C_{1}
and on the structural constants
appearing in assumption
(G)
, since the same is true of
\kappa^{\prime}
), we conclude that

Q(x)(\zeta_{R})_{t}+|\nabla_{X}\zeta_{R}|^{2}+\vartheta_{1}|\textbf{b}|^{2}-2\operatorname{div}_{X}(\textbf{b})\leq 0,

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathcal{N}(x)\neq R
and
0\leq t\leq\tau
,
provided that
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.

Summing up, if we choose
\tau_{0}>0
so small that

(3.9)

64C_{1}\kappa\tau_{0}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{4}<1\quad\text{and}\quad 64C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\tau_{0}^{2}\leq 1,

we finally conclude that (
3.5
) is satisfied, provided
that
0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.
With (
3.5
) at hand
we continue with the proof of (
3.2
), assuming from now on that

\text{$0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{0}$, where $\tau_{0}$ is as in
\eqref{eq:choicetauzero}}.

In particular, by combining (
3.5
) with
(
3.4
), we obtain

(3.10)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q(x)\varphi_{R,R_{1}}^{2}v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)e^{\zeta_{R}(x,\tau)}dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q(x)\varphi_{R,R_{1}}^{2}v_{+}^{2}(x,0)e^{\zeta_{R}(x,0)}dx\\
&\qquad\qquad\leq c\iint_{S_{\tau}}|\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}v^{2}_{+}e^{\zeta_{R}}dxdt.\end{split}

We then turn to estimate the two sides of (
3.10
).

As for the left-hand side we observe that,
owing to the properties of
\phi
in (
3.3
)
(and taking into account the
definition of
\varphi_{R,R_{1}}
), we immediately get

\text{$\varphi_{R,R_{1}}\equiv 1$ on $\{x:\,\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R\}$};

from this, since by (
3.1
) we have

v_{+}(\cdot,0)=(u_{0}-1)_{+}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we obtain

(3.11)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q(x)\varphi_{R,R_{1}}^{2}v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)e^{\zeta_{R}(x,\tau)}dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q(x)\varphi_{R,R_{1}}^{2}v_{+}^{2}(x,0)e^{\zeta_{R}(x,0)}dx\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q(x)\varphi_{R,R_{1}}^{2}v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)e^{\zeta_{R}(x,\tau)}dx\\
&\qquad(\text{since $\zeta_{R}(\cdot,\tau)=-\gamma_{R}^{2}/\tau$ on $\{\mathcal{N}\leq R\}$})\\
&\qquad\geq e^{-\gamma_{R}^{2}/\tau}\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R\}}Q(x)\,v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)\,dx.\end{split}

As for the right-hand side, instead, we first observe that,
again by using the properties of
\phi
in (
3.3
), we immediately get

\text{$\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}\neq 0$ only on $\{x:\,R<\mathcal{N}(x)<R_{1}\}$};

from this, since by (
3.1
) we have
-1\leq v(x,t)\leq e^{t}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{S_{\tau}}|\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}v^{2}_{+}e^{\zeta_{R}}dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\tau}v_{+}^{2}(x,t)\,e^{-f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}/(2\tau-t)}\,dt\Big)dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\tau e^{2\tau}\int_{\{R<\mathcal{N}(x)<R_{1}\}}e^{-f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}/(2\tau)}|\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{recalling that $\varphi_{R,R_{1}}=\phi\circ\mathcal{N}$})

\displaystyle\qquad=\tau e^{2\tau}\int_{\{R<\mathcal{N}(x)<R_{1}\}}e^{-f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2}/(2\tau)}|\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{N}(x))|^{2}|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}(x)|^{2}\,dx=:(\bigstar).

In view of the ‘
\mathcal{N}
- radial’ expression of the last integral
in the above estimate, we then use Federer’s Coarea formula, obtaining

\displaystyle(\bigstar)

\displaystyle=\tau e^{2\tau}\int_{R}^{R_{1}}e^{-f_{R}(r)^{2}/(2\tau)}(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}\Big(\int_{\{x:\mathcal{N}(x)=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\Big)\,dr

\displaystyle(\text{recalling the definition of $\mathcal{S}(r)$ in \eqref{eq:defFactorS}})

\displaystyle=\tau e^{2\tau}\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\mathcal{S}(r)\,e^{-f_{R}(r)^{2}/(2\tau)}(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}\,dr=:(2\bigstar).

Summing up, we have proved the estimate

(3.12)

\iint_{S_{\tau}}|\nabla_{X}\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}v^{2}_{+}e^{\zeta_{R}}dxdt\leq\tau e^{2\tau}\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\mathcal{S}(r)\,e^{-f_{R}(r)^{2}/(2\tau)}(\phi^{\prime}(r))^{2}\,dr,

holding true
for every
\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}([0,+\infty)
satisfying the properties
in (
3.3
).
To proceed further, we now consider the following specific choice of
\phi

\phi(r):=-\bigg(\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\frac{e^{f_{R}(z)^{2}/(2\tau)}}{\mathcal{S}(z)}\,dz\bigg)^{-1}\,\int_{R_{1}}^{r}\frac{e^{f_{R}(z)^{2}/(2\tau)}}{\mathcal{S}(z)}\,dz

for every
r\in[R,R_{1}]
,
\phi\equiv 0
in
[R_{1},+\infty)
and
\phi\equiv 1
in
[0,R]
.

Clearly,
\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}([0,+\infty))
and
\phi
obviously satisfies
the properties in (
3.3
); hence, we are entitled to exploit estimate (
3.12
)
with
this choice of
\phi
, getting

(3.13)

\begin{split}&\iint_{S_{\tau}}|\nabla\varphi_{R,R_{1}}|^{2}v^{2}_{+}e^{\zeta_{R}}dxdt\leq\tau e^{2\tau}\bigg(\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\frac{e^{f_{R}(r)^{2}/(2\tau)}}{\mathcal{S}(r)}\,dr\bigg)^{-1}.\end{split}

All in all, by combining (
3.11
) - (
3.13
) with
(
3.10
) (and by taking into account the

explicit expression
of
f_{R}(r)
when
r>R
), we
obtain

(3.14)

\begin{split}&\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R\}}Q(x)\,v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)\,dx\\
&\qquad=c(\tau,R)\bigg(\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}(r)}\exp\bigg\{\frac{1}{32\tau}\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{r}\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\Big)^{2}\bigg\}\,dr\bigg)^{-1},\end{split}

where
c(\tau,R)>0
is
explicitly given by

c(\tau)=c\tau e^{2\tau+\gamma_{R}^{2}/\tau}.

With (
3.14
) at hand, we finally
come to the end of the proof. First of all we observe that,
if
\Lambda>0
is as in (
1.8
),
by possibly shrinking
\tau_{0}>0
we may assume that

\frac{1}{32\tau}\geq\frac{1}{32\tau_{0}}\geq\Lambda

(note that this further shrinking again depends only on
structural constants
);
as a consequence, from estimate (
3.14
) we derive that

(3.15)

\begin{split}&\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R\}}Q(x)\,v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)\,dx\\
&\qquad\leq c(\tau,R)\bigg(\int_{R}^{R_{1}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}(r)}\exp\bigg\{\Lambda\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{r}\sqrt{\hat{q}(t)}\,dt\Big)^{2}\bigg\}\,dr\bigg)^{-1}.\end{split}

Now, recalling that
R_{1}>R
was arbitrarily fixed,
by taking the limit as
R_{1}\to+\infty
in the above
(
3.15
) and by using
assumption (
1.8
), we get

\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R\}}Q(x)\,v_{+}^{2}(x,\tau)\,dx\leq 0,

from which we readily derive that

v(x,\tau)\leq 0\quad\text{for every $x\in\mathrm{supp}(Q)$ with $\mathcal{N}(x)\leq R,\,0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{0}$}.

Then, recalling that also
R>2\rho_{0}
was arbitrarily fixed, we
obtain

v(x,\tau)\leq 0\quad\forall\,\,x\in\mathrm{supp}(Q),\,0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{0}.

Now, by repeating the
very same argument
exploited so far,
but integrating on the strip
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[\tau_{0},\tau_{0}+\tau]

and considering the function

\zeta_{R}(x,t):=-\frac{1}{2\tau-t+\tau_{0}}f_{R}(\mathcal{N}(x))^{2},

we can plainly recognize that
v\leq 0
on
\mathrm{supp}(Q)\times[\tau_{0},2\tau_{0}]
,
and hence

v(x,\tau)\leq 0\quad\forall\,\,x\in\mathrm{supp}(Q),\,0\leq\tau\leq 2\tau_{0}.

By iterating this argument, and by exploiting in a crucial way
the fact that
\tau_{0}>0

is a
universal number remaining unchanged at any iteration
, we conclude that

v(x,\tau)\leq 0\quad\forall\,\,x\in\mathrm{supp}(Q),\,\tau\geq 0.

This is precisely the desired (
3.2
), and the proof is finally complete.
∎

4.
The case of Carnot groups

As anticipated in the Introduction, one of the main examples of smooth vector
fields satisfying our assumptions
(H1)
-
(H2)
is given by
the
generators of the horizontal layer
of a Carnot group
(see Appendix
A
for the relevant definitions and few basic facts).
In this section we rephrase our main Theorem
1.1
in this context,
where it takes a simpler form (due to the possibility
of explicitly computing
\mathcal{S}(r)
).

Let then
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
be a Carnot group on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with

1\leq m\leq n
generators; moreover, let
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})

be the horizontal layer of
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
, and let

X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}

be a (linear) basis of
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
. Since we have recognized in the Appendix
that the set
X
satisfies
both
the assumptions
(H1)
and
(H2)
stated
in the Introduction, we are entitled
to apply Theorem
1.1
to the operator

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(x)X_{i}-Q(x),

provided that
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
Q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

satisfy assumptions
(S)
-
(G)
in the statement
of this theorem, with respect to a
suitable
exhaustion function

\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,+\infty)
; thus, if we assume that

\int_{\rho_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}(t)}\exp\Big\{\Lambda\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{r}\sqrt{\hat{q}(s)}\,ds\Big)^{2}\Big\}\,dt=+\infty

(here,
\Lambda,\rho_{0}>0
and
\hat{q}
are as in assumptions
(S)
-
(G)
),
we can conclude that
u\equiv 0
is the unique bounded solution of the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
.

We now aim at showing that,
in the present context
, it is possible to choose
the exhaustion function
\mathcal{N}
in such a way that the ‘geometric’ factor

\mathcal{S}(r)=\int_{\{\mathcal{N}(x)=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

can be
explicitly computed
.
Indeed, if we consider the operator

\textstyle\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}

(which is usually referred to as a
sub-Laplacian on
\mathbb{G}
), we know
from a deep
result by Folland
[
8
]
that
there exists a function

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

which satisfies the following properties:

i)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
;

ii)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}
is a
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous norm on
\mathbb{G}
, that is,

(a)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(x)=0\Leftrightarrow x=0
;

(b)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(\delta_{\lambda}(x))=\lambda\,\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(x)
;

iii)

denoting by
D\geq n
the
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{G}
, the map

\Gamma(x)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}^{2-D}(x)\qquad(\text{with $x\neq 0$})

is the
unique, global fundamental solution
of
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
, that is,

(a)

\Gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
;

(b)

\Gamma\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Gamma(x)\to 0
as
x\to+\infty
;

(c)

for every
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Gamma\,\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}\varphi\,dx=-\varphi(0).

Clearly, the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}
is an exhaustion function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
moreover, owing to property ii), it is possible to prove the following

surface mean value formula
for
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
(see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 5.5.4]
):

if
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is such that
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
then for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
r>0
we have

(4.1)

u(x)=\frac{\beta_{D}}{r^{D-1}}\int_{\{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(y)=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}|^{2}(x^{-1}*y)}{|\nabla_{y}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(x^{-1}*y)|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y),

where
\beta_{D}>0
is a constant only depending on
D
. Choosing, in particular,

u\equiv 1
and
x=0

(which is the neutral element of
\mathbb{G}
), from
(
4.1
) we then get

\frac{\beta_{D}}{r^{D-1}}\int_{\{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(y)=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}|^{2}(y)}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}(y)|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=1.

As a consequence, we obtain

(4.2)

\mathcal{S}(r)=\int_{\{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}=r\}}\frac{|\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}|^{2}}{|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\beta_{D}r^{D-1}\quad\text{for every $r>0$}.

In view of (
4.2
), if we apply
Theorem
1.1
with the choice
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}
,
we then plainly derive
the following result in the context of
Carnot groups
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
be a Carnot group on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
with homogeneous dimension
D\geq n
and
1\leq m\leq n
generators. Moreover, let
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})

be the first layer of
\mathbb{G}
, and let

X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}

be a linear basis of
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
. Setting
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}
,
we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}
the unique homogeneous norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\Gamma(x)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}^{2-D}(x)\qquad(x\neq 0)

is the global fundamental solution of
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
, see Folland
[
8
]
. Finally, let

\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m}),\qquad Q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

be smooth functions satisfying Assumptions
(S)
and
(G)
in the statement
of Theorem
1.1

(
for some
\kappa,\rho_{0}>0
and
\hat{q}:(\rho_{0},+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
,
with respect to
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{G}}
)
.

Then, if there exists
\Lambda>0
such that

(4.3)

\int_{\rho_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{r^{D-1}}\exp\Big\{\Lambda\Big(\int_{\rho_{0}}^{r}\sqrt{\hat{q}(s)}\,ds\Big)^{2}\Big\}\,dr=+\infty,

the Liouville property (

⁢
L
P

) holds for the equation

\textstyle\mathcal{L}u=\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u+\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(x)X_{i}u-Q(x)u=0.

5.
Examples

In this section we provide some examples
of application of our main result, that is, Theorem
1.1
; in order to be
as concrete as possible, most of these examples will be given in the context
of the
Carnot groups
, where Theorem
1.1
takes the more
explicit form given in Theorem
4.1
.

Example 5.1
.

For a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
let

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle(x,y,t)*(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\Big(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle-\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(x,y,t)=(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda^{2}t)\quad(\text{hence, $\sigma_{1}=\cdots=\sigma_{2m}=1,\,\sigma_{2m+1}=2$}).

On the other hand, when

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
m\geq 2
, and let
Q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m}),\,\text{$Q\geq 0$ on $\mathbb{H}^{m}$}

be such that

(6.2)

out of some compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{m}
, and for some constant

Then, if
\alpha>2
there exist infinitely many
distinct and bounded solutions
of equation

(6.3)

\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}u-Qu=0\quad\text{on $\mathbb{H}^{m}$}.

More precisely, given any
\gamma>0
there exists a bounded solution
u_{\gamma}

of the above equation such that, for every fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

(6.4)

u_{\gamma}(x,y,t)\to\gamma\quad\text{as ${|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}\to+\infty$}.

The proof of Theorem
6.1
relies on the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{m}
be a bounded open set satisfying the
following
(intrinsic) exterior ball condition
: for every
\zeta\in\partial\Omega
there exist

z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{m},\,r>0
such that

{\{z:\,\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}(z_{0}^{-1}*z)\leq r\}}\cap\overline{\Omega}=\{\zeta\}

(
here,
*
is the group law
\mathbb{H}^{m}
and
z_{0}^{-1}
is the inverse of
z_{0}

with respect to
*
)
. Moreover, let

be
a non-negative

Then, given any

, the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}u-c(z)u=0&\text{in $\Omega$}\\
u=\varphi&\text{in $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}

has a unique solution

.

Proof.

This result follows from Abstract Potential Theory, see, e.g.,
[

.
In particular, the validity of Axioms 1 - to - 3 in
[

, Sec. 4]

is proved in
[

, while the validity of

as a potential on

the results in the cited

This ends the proof.

Lemma 6.3

Let

Thanks to Lemmas
6.2
-
6.3
, we can now prove Theorem
6.1
.

Proof (of Theorem
6.1
).

We split the proof
into two steps.

Step I).
In this first step we construct, for every fixed

\gamma>0
, a
bounded function

u_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})

which solves (
6.3
).
To this end we consider, for every fixed
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
the following Dirichlet problem for the operator
L=\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}-Q(x)

(6.6)

\begin{cases}Lu=0&\text{in}\,\,\Omega_{j}=B_{j}\times(-j,j)\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{m}\\
u=\gamma&\text{in}\,\,\partial\Omega_{j}\\
\end{cases}

(here,
B_{j}
denotes the usual Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
with centre
0

and radius
j
).

Owing to the assumptions on
Q(z)
, and since the cylinder
\Omega_{j}

satisfies the
intrinsic exterior ball condition

in Lemma
6.2
(as it is convex), we know from the cited
Lemma
6.2
that problem (
6.6
) has a unique solution
u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
.

We then claim that the following facts hold.

(1)

For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(6.7)

0\leq u_{j}(z)\leq\gamma\quad\text{for any}\,\,\,z\in\overline{\Omega}_{j}.

(2)

The sequence
\{u_{j}\}_{j}
is
decreasing
.

-
Proof of Claim
(1). On the one hand,
since
u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
satisfies
(
6.6
) and since
\gamma>0
,
from the Weak Maximum Principle for
L
(see Remark
2.1
)
we immediately derive that

u_{j}(z)\geq 0
for every
z\in\overline{\Omega}_{j}
.

On the other hand, setting
w_{j}=u_{j}-\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
, again by
exploiting the fact that

u_{j}
solves (
6.6
) (and since
Q\geq 0
) we derive that

•

Lw_{j}=Q\gamma\geq 0
on
\Omega_{j}
;

•

w_{j}(\zeta)=u_{j}(\zeta)-\gamma=0
for every
\zeta\in\partial\Omega_{j}
.

Gathering these facts, we can exploit once again
the
Weak Maximum Principle for
L
, obtaining
w_{j}=u_{j}-\gamma\leq 0
on
\overline{\Omega}_{j}
.
Hence, Claim (1) is proved.

-
Proof of Claim
(2). We apply once again the Weak Maximum Principle
for
L
. First of all, since

u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
solves problem (
6.6
), setting

w_{j}=u_{j+1}-u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})

we have (notice that, by definition,
\Omega_{j}\subseteq\overline{\Omega}_{j}\subseteq\Omega_{j+1}
)

Lw_{j}(z)=0\quad\text{for every $z\in\Omega_{j}$}.

Moreover, on account of (
6.7
) we also get

\text{$w_{j}(\zeta)=u_{j+1}(\zeta)-u_{j}(\zeta)=u_{j+1}(\zeta)-\gamma\leq 0$
for all $\zeta\in\partial\Omega_{j}\subseteq\Omega_{j+1}$}.

Therefore, by the Weak Maximum Principle we conclude that
w_{j}\leq 0
in
\overline{\Omega}_{j}

and, in particular, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we conclude that

u_{j+1}\leq u_{j}\quad\text{in}\,\,\,\overline{\Omega}_{j}.

This completes the proof of Claim (2).

Now, by combining Claim (1) and Claim (2) we deduce that the sequence
\{u_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is
decreasing and bounded
on
\overline{\Omega}_{j}
; this, together with the fact that

\textstyle\bigcup_{j\geq 1}\Omega_{j}=\mathbb{H}^{m},

ensures that there exists

u_{\gamma}:\mathbb{H}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(6.8)

\begin{split}\mathrm{i)}\,\,&\text{$u_{\gamma}(z)=\lim_{j\to+\infty}u_{j}(z)$ for every $z\in\mathbb{H}^{m}$}\\[2.84544pt]
\mathrm{ii)}\,\,&\text{$0\leq u_{\gamma}(z)\leq\gamma$ for every $z\in\mathbb{H}^{m}$}.\end{split}

We then turn to show that
u_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})
and that
u_{\gamma}
solves
equation (
6.3
).

To this end we first observe that, by construction,
u_{\gamma}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})

is a
distributional solution
of (
6.3
):
indeed, given any test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})
, if
j
is sufficiently large
(so that
\Omega_{j}\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)
) we have

0=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}(Lu_{j})\varphi\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}u_{j}(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}\varphi-Q(z)\varphi)\,dz,

since
u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
is a
(classical) solution
of (
6.6
); from this, by a standard dominated - convergence argument
based on property ii) we get

0=\lim_{j\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}u_{j}(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}\varphi-Q(z)\varphi)\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}u_{\gamma}(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}\varphi-Q(z)\varphi)\,dz,

and this proves that
Lu_{\gamma}=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{H}^{m})
(by the arbitrariness of
\varphi
).

On account of this fact,
and since
L
is
C^{\infty}
- hypoelliptic (by Hörmander’s theorem,
see Remark
2.1
), we
thus conclude that
u_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})
(up to modifying such a
function on a set with zero Lebesgue measure), and therefore

Lu_{\gamma}=0\quad\text{pointwise on $\mathbb{H}^{m}$}.

Step II).
In this second step we prove that
the function
u_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{m})
, which we
know to be a solution of equation (
6.3
), satisfies also
(
6.4
).

To this end we define, for every
j\in\mathbb{N},\,j>R_{0}
,

w_{j}(z)=u_{j}(z)-\gamma+\delta V(z)\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{R_{0}})

(where
u_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{j})\cap C(\overline{\Omega}_{j})
is the
unique solution of the Dirichlet problem (
6.6
),
while
V
and
R_{0}
are as in Lemma
6.3
); then, we claim that

(6.9)

\text{$w_{j}\geq 0$ pointwise on $\Omega_{j}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{R_{0}}$},

provided that the constant
\delta>0
is properly chosen.

To prove this claim, it suffices to apply the Weak Maximum Principle
for
L
to the function
w_{j}

on
\Omega_{j}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{R_{0}}
. Indeed, owing to (
6.5
)
(and recalling that
u_{j}
solves
problem (
6.6
)), we have the following computations:

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle Lw_{j}(z)=Q(z)\gamma+\delta LV(z)\leq Q(z)(\gamma-\delta)\quad\text{for all $z\in\Omega_{j}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{R_{0}};$}

\displaystyle\mathrm{ii)}\,\,

\displaystyle w_{j}=\delta V\geq 0\quad\text{on $\zeta\in\big(\partial B_{j}\times[-j,j]\big)\cup\big((\overline{B}_{j}\setminus B_{R_{0}})\times\{\pm j\}\big)\subseteq\partial\Omega_{j}$};

\displaystyle\mathrm{iii)}\,\,

\displaystyle w_{j}(\zeta)\geq-\gamma+\delta V(R_{0})\quad\text{for all $\zeta\in\partial B_{R_{0}}\times[-j,j]$}.

We explicitly notice that, in point iii), we have also used (
6.7
).

In view of these facts, if we choose
\delta>0
in such a way that

1)\,\,\delta\geq\gamma,\qquad 2)\,\,\delta\geq\gamma/V(R_{0}),

we can apply the Weak Maximum Principle
for
L
, thus obtaining (
6.9
).

Now we have established (
6.9
), we can easily conclude the proof
of (
6.4
). Indeed, owing to the cited (
6.9
), and letting

j\to+\infty
, we derive that

u_{\gamma}(z)=\lim_{j\to+\infty}u_{j}(z)\geq\gamma-\delta V(z)\quad\text{for all $z\in\mathbb{H}^{m}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{R_{0}}$}.

From this, since we have already recognized that
u_{\gamma}\leq\gamma
on
\mathbb{H}^{m}

(see (
6.8
) - ii)), by letting
{|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}\to+\infty
with the help
of (
6.5
) we conclude that

\text{$u_{\gamma}(x,y,t_{0})\to\gamma$ as ${|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}\to+\infty$}.

This ends the proof.
∎

Appendix A
A brief review on Carnot groups

In this appendix we review
the basic definitions and facts on homogeneous
Carnot groups which have been used in the paper.
We follow the exposition in
[
6
]
, to which
we refer for all the omitted proofs and the details.

To begin with, we recall that
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)

is a
Lie group
(on
\mathbb{R}^{n})
if

(1)

(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)
is an algebraic group, with neutral element
e
;

(2)

the map
*
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For every fixed
x\in\mathbb{G}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote, respectively,
by
\tau_{\alpha}
and
\rho_{\alpha}
the left-translation
and the right-translation by
x
, on
\mathbb{G}
, that is,

(a)

\tau_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\tau_{x}(y):=x*y
;

(b)

\rho_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\rho_{x}(y):=y*x
.

Given a Lie group
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)
and
a smooth vector field
Z

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we say that
Z
is

left-invariant on
\mathbb{G}
if we have

(A.1)

Z\big(u\circ\tau_{\alpha})=(Zu)\circ\tau_{\alpha},

for every
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and every fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote by
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
the set of the left-invariant vector fields
on
\mathbb{G}
and we call it the
Lie algebra of
\mathbb{G}
.

Owing to identity (
A.1
), it is quite easy
to prove the following result.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},*)
be a Lie group with neutral
element
e
,
and let
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
be the Lie algebra of
\mathbb{G}
.
Then the following facts hold.

(1)

\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
is a Lie algebra and
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G}))=n
;

(2)

for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

there exists precisely
one
J_{i}\in\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
such that

J_{i}u(e)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}(e)\qquad\big(\text{for every
$u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$}\big);

more precisely, we have

J_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}(x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}},

where
\mathcal{J}_{\tau_{x}}
denotes the Jacobian
matrix of
\tau_{x}
at
y=e
, and

(a_{i1}(x),\ldots,a_{iN}(x))^{T}
is the
i
-th column
of the matrix

\mathcal{J}_{\tau_{x}}(e)
.

(3)

If
J_{1},\ldots,J_{N}
are as above, then
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{N}\}
is a
(linear) basis of

\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
, which is usually called the

Jacobian basis of
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
.

Let now
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)
be a Lie group on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
let us assume that there exist

natural numbers
1=\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\sigma_{n}
such that

(A.2)

\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\delta_{\lambda}(x):=(\lambda^{\sigma_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}x_{n}),

is an automorphism of
\mathbb{G}
for every
fixed
\lambda>0
, that is,

\delta_{\lambda}(x*y)=\delta_{\lambda}(x)*\delta_{\lambda}(y),\qquad\text{for every
$x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

Then, the triplet

\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
is called a
homogeneous
(Lie) group
(on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), and

\{\delta_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
is usually
referred to as the
family of dilations

of
\mathbb{G}
. The number

\textstyle D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\geq n

is called the
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous dimension
(of
\mathbb{G}
).

Let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
be a homogeneous group
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(with
\delta_{\lambda}
as in (
A.2
))
and let
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{N}\}

be the Jacobian basis of
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
. It is very
easy to recognize that
J_{i}

is
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree
\sigma_{i}

(for every fixed
i=1,\ldots,n
), that is,

J_{i}(u\circ\delta_{\lambda})=\lambda^{\sigma_{i}}\,(J_{i}u)\circ\delta_{\lambda},

for every
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and every

\lambda>0
; we then define the space

V_{1}(\mathbb{G}):=\mathrm{span}\big\{J_{i}:\,\sigma_{i}=1\big\}\subseteq\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G}),

and we call such a space the
horizontal layer

of
\mathbb{G}
.

Remark A.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
be a homogeneous group and let

V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
be the horizontal layer of
\mathbb{G}
. Since

V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
is spanned by vector fields which are

\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree
1

it is straightforward to recognize that

X\in V_{1}(\mathbb{G})\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\text{$X$ is $\delta_{\lambda}$-homogeneous
of degree $1$}.

If
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},*,\delta_{\lambda})
is a homogeneous
Lie group on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and if

(A.3)

\mathrm{Lie}\big(V_{1}(\mathbb{G})\big)=\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G}),

we say that
\mathbb{G}
is a (homogeneous)
Carnot group

(here, by
\mathrm{Lie}(V_{1}(\mathbb{G}))
we mean the smallest Lie sub-algebra
of
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G})
containing
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
).
The number

m=m(\mathbb{G}):=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big(V_{1}(\mathbb{G})\big)=\mathrm{card}\big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:\,\sigma_{i}=1\big\},

is usually referred to as the
number of generators

of
\mathbb{G}
.

Let now
X=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
be
any fixed
(linear) basis of
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
.
It is not difficult to recognize that the Lie-generating condition
(
A.3
), jointly with the fact that
\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{G}))=n
, ensures that

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy Hörmander’s condition at every point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
thus, since the
X_{i}
’s are also
\delta_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree
1

(as they belong to
V_{1}(\mathbb{G})
, see Remark
A.2
), we conclude that

\text{\emph{$X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}$ satisfy Assumptions $(H1)$ and $(H2)$ in the Introduction}}.

The second-order
differential operator

\textstyle\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{2},

is called a
sub-Laplacian
on
\mathbb{G}
.
