Stochastic Backscatter Model for Unstructured Solvers
Angelo Passariello

Abstract




1
Problem statement

The Stochastic BackScatter (SBS) model involves the generation of a set of random variables featured by specific correlations in space and time
(
Kok 2017a
)
. To obtain these variables a three-step procedure is employed:

1.

A three-dimensional field (say,
dV_{i}
) of completely uncorrelated normally-distributed stochastic quantities is generated first.

2.

An implicit smoothing of
dV_{i}
is performed to add an exponentially-decaying spatial correlation. The spatially-correlated field will be denoted as
dW_{i}
. The scale for decay is proportional to the LES filter width.

3.

Three Langevin equations are solved to add an exponentially-decaying temporal correlation to the smoothed field
dW_{i}
. The latter appears as source term in the Langevin equations.

The implicit smoothing (point 2 of the above procedure) is carried out by solving three nested elliptic problems in the computational space (
x_{\scalebox{0.6}{I}}x_{\scalebox{0.6}{J}}x_{\scalebox{0.6}{K}}
), as shown below:

d\bm{x}\left(1-b^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\scalebox{0.6}{K}}^{2}}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\scalebox{0.6}{J}}^{2}}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\scalebox{0.6}{I}}^{2}}\right)dW_{i}=8b^{3/2}dV_{i}

(1)

In the previous equation,
b
represents a scalar (which is proportional to the LES filter width) and
d\bm{x}
is an elemental volume. It is clear that this approach is functional for structured grids only. To extend the SBS model to unstructured CFD solvers, a Laplacian smoothing could be employed instead:

d\bm{x}\left(1-b^{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\,dW_{i}=8b^{3/2}dV_{i}

(2)

In fact, Eq. (
1
) is an approximation of Eq. (
2
) with an error of order
\mathcal{O}(b^{4})
.
However, the spatial correlation resulting from Eq. (
2
) must be derived mathematically. This is the objective of the present dissertation.

2
Green’s function for the Laplacian smoothing

Let
G(\bm{x})
be the three-dimensional Green’s function satisfying the following equation:

G(\bm{x})-b^{2}\,\nabla^{2}G(\bm{x})=\delta(\bm{x})

(3)

where
\delta(\bm{x})
denotes the three-dimensional Dirac delta function and
b
is a scalar factor.
Let
\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})
indicate the Fourier transform of
G
, defined as:

\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-i\,\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{x}}\,G(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}

(4)

The following convention is employed for the inverse Fourier transform:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i\,\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{x}}\,\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})\,d\bm{\kappa}

(5)

Here,
d\bm{x}
and
d\bm{\kappa}
represent two infinitesimal “volumes” in the spatial and frequency domain respectively.
Fourier-transforming both sides of Eq. (
3
), the following expression is obtained:

(1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2})\,\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})=1

(6)

from which
\hat{G}
can easily be derived:

\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})=\frac{1}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}

(7)

Transforming
\hat{G}
back to the spatial domain, one gets:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{x}}}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}\,d\bm{\kappa}

(8)

The computation of this integral is not immediate. First of all, let us rotate the wavenumber vector (i.e.,
\bm{\kappa}
) using a generic matrix
\mathbf{R}
to obtain a new wavenumber vector,
\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}
:

\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}\bm{\kappa}

(9)

Using a well-known property of rotation matrices (i.e.,
\mathbf{R}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}^{T}
),
\bm{\kappa}
can be expressed as a function of
\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}
:

\bm{\kappa}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}

(10)

The modulus of
\bm{\kappa}
is obviously preserved by the rotation. This can be shown as follows:

\bm{\kappa}^{T}\bm{\kappa}=\bm{\kappa}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}

(11)

\bm{\kappa}^{T}\bm{\kappa}=(\mathbf{R}\bm{\kappa})^{T}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}

(12)

\bm{\kappa}^{T}\bm{\kappa}=(\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})^{T}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}

(13)

|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}=|\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|^{2}

(14)

In a similar way, it can be proved that:
d\bm{k}=d\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}
.
The exponential in Eq. (
8
) deserves more attention.
By substituting Eq. (
10
) in Eq. (
8
), the aforementioned term becomes:

e^{i\,(\mathbf{R}^{T}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{x}}=e^{i\,\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}

(15)

In conclusion:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,d\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}

(16)

We have proved that
G(\bm{x})
is invariant to rotations.
Consequently, the matrix
\mathbf{R}
can be constructed in such a way to simplify the computation of the integral in Eq. (
16
).
Let us choose
\mathbf{R}
such that the vector
\bm{x}
becomes aligned with the
z
-axis of the physical space:

\mathbf{R}\bm{x}=|\bm{x}|\,\hat{\bm{z}}

(17)

Then, if a spherical coordinate system is employed, the dot product in Eq. (
15
) can be expressed as:

e^{i\,\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}\cdot(|\bm{x}|\,\hat{\bm{z}})}=e^{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|\,\cos\theta}

(18)

where
\theta
denotes the polar angle (i.e.,
\theta=\cos^{-1}(\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\hat{z}}/|\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|)
).
The volume element
d\bm{\kappa}
in spherical coordinates is written as:

d\bm{k}=|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\,d|\bm{\kappa}|

(19)

where
\phi
represents the azimuthal angle.
By substituting Eqs. (
18
)-(
19
) in Eq. (
16
), one finally obtains:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{e^{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|\,\cos\theta}}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d|\bm{\kappa}|

(20)

Let us solve the angular part first:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi=2\pi\;

(21)

\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|\,\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta\xrightarrow{u=\cos\theta}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|\,u}\,du=\left[\frac{e^{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|\,u}}{i\,|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|}\right]_{-1}^{1}=\frac{2\,\sin(|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|)}{|\bm{\kappa}|\,|\bm{x}|}

(22)

By substituting Eqs. (
21
)-(
22
) in Eq. (
20
):

\begin{split}G(\bm{x})&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\cdot(2\pi)\cdot\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}\,\frac{2\sin(|\bm{\kappa}||\bm{x}|)}{|\bm{\kappa}||\bm{x}|}\,d|\bm{\kappa}|\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}|\bm{x}|}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{|\bm{\kappa}|\,\sin(|\bm{\kappa}||\bm{x}|)}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}\,d|\bm{\kappa}|\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}b^{2}|\bm{x}|}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{(b|\bm{\kappa}|)\,\sin\left(b|\bm{\kappa}|\frac{|\bm{x}|}{b}\right)}{1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2}}\,d(b|\bm{\kappa}|)\end{split}

(23)

Using the residue theorem (see the Appendix for details), it can be proved that:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{u\,\sin(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{2}\,e^{-\alpha}

(24)

Here, in particular:
u=b|\bm{\kappa}|
,
\alpha=|\bm{x}|/b
.
The expression of the Green’s function can finally be computed:

G(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}b^{2}|\bm{x}|}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\,e^{-|\bm{x}|/b}=\frac{e^{-|\bm{x}|/b}}{4\pi b^{2}|\bm{x}|}

(25)

3
Spatial correlation of the stochastic differentials

Known
G(\bm{x})
, the general solution for the implicit Laplacian smoothing in Eq. (
2
) can be written as:

dW_{i}(\bm{x},t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\,8b^{3/2}dV_{i}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t)

(26)

If
dV_{i}
is completely uncorrelated both in space and in time, then it follows that:

\begin{split}\langle dW_{i}(\bm{x},t)\,dW_{j}(\bm{y},s)\rangle&=64\,b^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})G(\bm{y}-\bm{y}^{\prime})\langle dV_{i}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\,dV_{j}(\bm{y}^{\prime},s)\rangle\\
&=64\,b^{3}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})G(\bm{y}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\,d\bm{x}^{\prime}\\
\end{split}

(27)

where the angular brackets denote ensemble averaging.
It is trivial to show that the integral in Eq. (
27
) is invariant to translations of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
. Indeed, letting
\bm{a}
be a generic vector, one can write:

\begin{split}\langle dW_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{a},t)\,dW_{j}(\bm{y}+\bm{a},s)\rangle&\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})G(\bm{y}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\,d\bm{x}^{\prime}\\
&\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}+\bm{a}-\bm{x}^{\prime})G(\bm{y}+\bm{a}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\,d\bm{x}^{\prime}\\
&\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{a}))G(\bm{y}-(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{a}))\,d(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{a})\end{split}

(28)

Without loss of generality, one can thus translate the coordinate system of
-\bm{y}
. The following integral must thus be solved:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{x}-\bm{y}-\bm{x}^{\prime})G(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,d\bm{x}^{\prime}

(29)

By definition, the above equation represents the convolution of the Green function
G(\bm{x}-\bm{y})
with itself. For notational simplicity, the following substitutions are made:
\bm{x}-\bm{y}\leftrightarrow\bm{r}
,
\bm{x}^{\prime}\leftrightarrow\bm{r}^{\prime}
.
It is known that the Fourier transform of the convolution product of two functions is equal to the product of their respective Fourier transforms. Namely:

\mathcal{F}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})G(\bm{r}^{\prime})\,d\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\}=[\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})]^{2}

(30)

Therefore, we can compute the convolution integral by anti-transforming
[\hat{G}(\bm{\kappa})]^{2}
(whose expression is known from the previous Section). To be precise, we have to calculate:

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{r}}}{(1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2})^{2}}\,d\bm{\kappa}

(31)

By following the same approach used to compute the Green’s function, it can be shown that:

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{r}}}{(1+b^{2}|\bm{\kappa}|^{2})^{2}}\,d\bm{\kappa}=\frac{e^{-|\bm{r}|/b}}{8\pi b^{3}}

(32)

To conclude, the following spatial correlation of the stochastic differentials is obtained:

\langle dW_{i}(\bm{x},t)\,dW_{j}(\bm{y},s)\rangle=64\,b^{3}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s)\cdot\frac{e^{-|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|/b}}{8\pi b^{3}}=\frac{8}{\pi}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s)e^{-|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|/b}

(33)

For the sake of completeness, the spatial correlation obtained from Eq. (
1
) is also shown
(
Kok 2017a
)
:

\langle dW_{i}(\bm{x},t)\,dW_{j}(\bm{y},s)\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s)\left(e^{-|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}/(2b^{2})}+\mathcal{O}(|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{3})\right)

(34)

It must be emphasised that the exponential decay in space that is achieved with the implicit Laplacian smoothing is comparatively weak.

Appendix: application of the residue theorem

It must be proved that:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{u\,\sin(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{2}\,e^{-\alpha}

(35)

To this aim, the following remarkable results from Complex Analysis will be used:

Residue theorem.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be a simply connected open subset of the complex plane containing a finite list of points
a_{1},...,a_{n}
and
f
be a function holomorphic on
\mathcal{U}_{0}=\mathcal{U}\backslash\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
.
Letting
\gamma
be a closed rectifiable curve in
\mathcal{U}_{0}
, and denoting the residue of
f
at each point
a_{k}
by
\mathrm{Res}(f,a_{k})
, and the winding number of

\gamma
around
a_{k}
by
I(\gamma,a_{k})
, the line integral of
f
around
\gamma
is equal to
2\pi i
times the sum of residues, each counted as many times as
\gamma
winds around the respective point:

\oint_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^{n}I(\gamma,a_{k})\,\mathrm{Res}(f,a_{k})

(36)

If
\gamma
is a positively oriented simple closed curve,
I(\gamma,a_{k})
is
1 if
a_{k}
is in the interior of
\gamma
and 0 if not, therefore:

\oint_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum\mathrm{Res}(f,a_{k})

(37)

with the sum over those
a_{k}
inside
\gamma
.

Jordan’s lemma.

Consider a complex-valued, continuous function
f
, defined on a semicircular contour

C_{R}=\{R\,e^{i\theta}\mid\theta\in[0,\pi]\}
of positive radius
R
lying in the upper half-plane, centered at the origin. If the function
f
is of the form
f(z)=e^{i\alpha z}g(z),\quad z\in C
with a positive parameter
\alpha
, then Jordan’s Lemma states the following upper bound for the contour integral:

\left|\int_{C_{R}}f(z)\,dz\right|\leq{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}M_{R}\quad{\text{where}}\quad M_{R}:=\max_{\theta\in[0,\pi]}\left|g\left(R\,e^{i\theta}\right)\right|.

(38)

with equality when
g
vanishes everywhere, in which case both sides are identically zero. An analogous statement for a semicircular contour in the lower half-plane holds when
\alpha<0
.

To prove the identity in Eq. (
35
), let us first consider the following integral:

C(\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{i\alpha u}}{1+u^{2}}\,du

(39)

with
\alpha
positive and
u\in\mathbb{R}
. We can evaluate
C(\alpha)
through the residue theorem. We have to define a convenient curve
\gamma
in the complex plane. The latter can be constructed as the union of the semi-circumference of radius
R
centred in the origin of the complex plane and of the segment
[-R,R]
lying on the real axis.
Referring to the statement of Jordan’s lemma and looking at the integrand in Eq. (
39
), we notice that:

f(z)=e^{i\alpha z}\,g(z)=\frac{e^{i\alpha z}}{1+z^{2}}\longrightarrow g(z)=\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}

(40)

Computing the maximum of the modulus of
g
is trivial:

M_{R}=\frac{1}{1+R^{2}}

(41)

According to Jordan’s lemma:

0\leq\left|\int_{C_{R}}\frac{e^{i\alpha z}}{1+z^{2}}\,dz\right|\leq\frac{1}{1+R^{2}}

(42)

As a consequence:

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\left|\int_{C_{R}}\frac{e^{i\alpha z}}{1+z^{2}}\,dz\right|=0

(43)

This means that the application of the residue theorem on
\gamma
(when the radius
R
tends to infinity) will directly yield
C(\alpha)
. The only pole of
f
included in
\gamma
is
z=i
. We can easily evaluate the residue in that point according to the definition:

\mathrm{Res}(f,i)=\lim_{z\to i}\,(z-i)f(z)=\lim_{z\to i}\frac{e^{i\alpha z}}{z+i}=\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{2i}

(44)

Recalling Eq. (
37
), one can write:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{i\alpha u}}{1+u^{2}}\,du=2\pi i\,\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{2i}=\pi e^{-\alpha}

(45)

Since the integral is real, only the real part of the integrand function contributes non-zero values, namely:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{i\alpha u}}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\cos(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\pi e^{-\alpha}

(46)

Furthermore, the real part of the integrand function is even. As a consequence:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\cos(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\cos(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{2}e^{-\alpha}

(47)

By deriving both sides of the previous equation with respect to
\alpha
, one obtains:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{u\sin(\alpha u)}{1+u^{2}}\,du=\frac{\pi}{2}e^{-\alpha}

(48)

which is exactly the identity we wanted to prove.
