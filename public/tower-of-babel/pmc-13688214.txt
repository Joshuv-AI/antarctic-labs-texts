Incidence Trends of Colorectal Cancer by Social and Behavioral Factors—A Pooled Cohort Study
Authors: Emilia Muhonen, Karri Seppä, Heidi Ryynänen, Johan G Eriksson, Tommi Härkänen, Pekka Jousilahti, Paul Knekt, Seppo Koskinen, Satu Männistö, Ossi Rahkonen, Maarit A Laaksonen, Janne Pitkäniemi, Sanna Lappi‐Heikkinen, METCA Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTColorectal cancer (CRC) represents more than 10% of the global cancer burden. Unlike in some other cancers, no single risk factor accounts for most CRC cases. Despite the well‐studied overall incidence trend of CRC, little information is available on risk factor‐specific incidence trends of CRC. This study aims to evaluate the rarely studied incidence trends of CRC in males and females with variable levels of exposure to specific risk factors, including education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and body mass index (BMI). We used Finnish pooled cohort data from seven cohorts linked to the population‐based Finnish Cancer Registry. The pooled data included 224,048 subjects, of whom 2006 were diagnosed with CRC. CRC incidence adjusted for known risk factors increased annually by 1.7% in males and 1.0% in females. In risk factor‐stratified analysis, the average annual percentage increase in CRC incidence was especially high in current male smokers. This study further strengthens the evidence that living with overweight or obesity, having a tall body height, or living with diabetes mellitus (DM) associates with an increased risk of CRC. Cigarette smoking was associated with an increased CRC risk in males, while heavy alcohol consumption was associated with an increased risk in females. In this study, education level or physical inactivity were not statistically significant risk factors for CRC. Our findings highlight the need for effective public health strategies, with special emphasis on preventing overweight, obesity, and smoking along with other modifiable lifestyle factors known to affect CRC risk.Keywords: cohort study, colorectal cancer, incidence trend, risk factors

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, representing around 10% of the global cancer burden in 2022 [1]. Globally, the age‐standardized incidence rate of CRC increased from 22.2 (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 21.3–23.0) per 100,000 persons to 26.7 (95% UI: 24.6–28.9) per 100,000 persons between 1990 and 2019 [2]. More than 1.9 million new cases of CRC, including anal cancer, emerged in 2022, and the incidence was higher in males than in females. The incidence rates of CRC are three to four times higher in economically transitioned countries compared with transitioning countries [1]. Notably, stabilizing or decreasing trends of CRC are seen in highly developed countries, while an increasing cancer burden is observed in countries in transition [3]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted in 2024 found that CRC incidence was 11% higher and CRC‐caused mortality was 21% higher in subjects who had lower neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES) compared with those who had a higher neighborhood SES [4]. In Finland, the incidence rate of CRC was higher in individuals with higher education levels and higher SES compared with those with lower education levels and lower SES [5].

Unlike in some cancers, like lung cancer and skin melanoma, no single dominant risk factor accounts for most CRC cases [6]. Risk factors of CRC include male sex [6, 7], inflammatory bowel disease [7], diabetes mellitus (DM) [8, 9], and family history of CRC [7, 10]. Genetic, environmental, hormonal, and nutritional factors that lead to greater adult height are also considered to increase the CRC risk [6, 11, 12]. Lifestyle factors that increase the CRC risk include a high body mass index (BMI) [7, 10, 13, 14, 15] and a large waist circumference [15]. Sedentary behaviors associate with an increased risk of CRC [7], while physical activity may decrease the risk [10, 11, 16]. Other lifestyle factors that increase the CRC risk include tobacco smoking [7, 10, 13, 17, 18], alcohol consumption [7, 10, 11, 13, 17, 19], particularly alcohol intake above 30 g per day [11], and the consumption of processed and red meat [7, 11, 13, 19, 20, 21]. Lifestyle factors associated with a decreased risk include consumption of dietary fibers, especially from wholegrains [11, 19, 22], dietary and supplementary calcium [11, 22, 23], and dairy products [11, 19, 22].

Healthy food habits and diets following nutritional recommendations have become more common since 1979 in all regions of Finland. Concurrently, obesity has become more frequent in the population [24]. Meanwhile, the consumption of fruits and vegetables has increased in both males and females, while the consumption of red and processed meat has decreased in males [25]. Still, according to a 2018 report, only 14% of Finnish males and 22% of Finnish females met the recommendations for fruit and vegetable intake, whereas 21% of Finnish males and 74% of Finnish females met those for red and processed meat intake [26]. In 2017, 67% of Finnish males and 76% of Finnish females consumed less dietary fiber than recommended [26]. The underlying causes of obesity have changed due to increased consumption of ultra‐processed foods [27, 28] and increased sedentary behavior [29]. Further, patterns of smoking and alcohol consumption with respect to cigarette contents [30] and types of alcoholic drinks [31] have changed.

Despite the well‐studied overall incidence trend of CRC, little information is available on risk factor‐specific incidence trends of CRC. Our study aims to evaluate the rarely studied incidence trends of CRC in males and females with variable exposure levels to specific risk factors, including education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and BMI.

Materials and Methods

We used the METCA (Prospective METa Cohort Study of Cancer Burden in Finland) project, which pools data from seven health studies in Finland between 1972 and 2015 [32]. It included one cohort from the University of Helsinki: Helsinki Health Study (HHS, 2000–2002, end of follow‐up in 2015) [33], and six cohorts from the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL): The Finnish Mobile Clinic Health Examination Follow‐up Study (FMCF, 1973–1976, end of follow‐up in 2013) [34], National FINRISK Study (comprising nine studies conducted every 5 years from 1972 to 2012, end of follow‐up in 2013) [35], Mini‐Finland Health Survey (MFH, 1978–1980, end of follow‐up in 2013) [34], Health 2000 Study (H2000, 2000–2001, end of follow‐up in 2015) [36], Helsinki Birth Cohort Study (HBCS, 2000, end of follow‐up in 2013) [37], and Regional Health and Well‐being Study (ATH) [38]. The ATH study consisted of two datasets: ATH1 and ATH2. ATH1 was collected in 2010–2011 (end of follow‐up in 2015) and ATH2 in 2012–2015 (end of follow‐up in 2015) [32, 38]. Data requirements for the health studies included in the METCA project were: having information on the known or suspected cancer risk factors and having a national personal identity code available to link both cancer and vital status information individually [32]. Study subject inclusion was based on some variant of random selection from the Finnish population in all cohorts except for HBCS, which is a birth cohort [32]. After the exclusion of subjects diagnosed with CRC before the beginning of the follow‐up, the pooled data included 224,048 subjects, of whom 2006 were diagnosed with CRC. About 45% (N = 100,522) of the subjects were males and 55% (N = 123,526) were females. Most of the studies had been validated earlier, with validation results presented in the cohort profiles of each study [34, 35, 36]. Heterogeneity in cancer associations between the studies has been assessed by Pitkäniemi et al. for colorectal and lung cancer [32].

We extracted and harmonized information on education levels, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, height, and DM. Information on education levels, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and DM had been collected using self‐reported questionnaires in all cohorts from THL. Body weight and height had been measured clinically in all other cohorts from THL (FMCF, FINRISK, MFH, H2000, and HBCS) except for ATH, which collected information on these exposures using questionnaires alone. Information on sex, birth year, and age at baseline was registry‐based information in all cohorts from THL [32]. In HHS, information on exposures had been collected using self‐reported questionnaires. Additionally, in HHS, information on exposures had been collected during health examinations for those subjects who agreed to participate in these examinations [33]. Sufficiency and consistency over various cohorts were requirements for cohort inclusion, and as close a match as possible was sought in terms of the survey questions for each considered variable in each cohort [32]. We handled missing covariates as a separate category in all analyses.

We classified education levels as low (reference group), middle, or high. Classification was made according to tertiles of the number of years of schooling by sex and birth cohort (eight cohorts by the year of birth: < 1920, 10‐year periods from 1920 to 1979, and 1980 or later) to overcome changing secular trends [32]. Subjects who had never smoked cigarettes (HBCS) or never smoked cigarettes regularly (FMCF, FINRISK, MFH, H2000, ATH) were classified as “never smokers,” subjects who reported having smoked cigarettes regularly but not at the time of the health survey were classified as “former smokers,” and subjects who reported smoking cigarettes regularly were classified as “smokers” [32, 39]. Subjects who reported using 0 g of alcohol per week (MFH) or per month (FMCF), never using alcohol (FINRISK, H2000, HBCS), or not using alcohol within the past year (ATH) were classified as “non‐users” (reference group) [32]. Alcohol use of less than 7 (females) or 14 (males) units per week was classified as “moderate use,” and alcohol use of 7 (females) or 14 (males) units or more per week as “heavy use.” Subjects who reported exercises maintaining physical fitness (e.g., jogging, swimming, running, ball games, heavy gardening, competitive sports) were classified as “active” (reference group), subjects who reported light physical activities (e.g., walking, cycling, light housework) during leisure time were classified as “moderately active,” and subjects who reported 0 times of moderate physical activity per week (HBCS) or not engaging in physically burdening activities or hobbies during leisure time (FMCF, FINRISK, MFH, H2000, ATH) were classified as “inactive” [32, 40].

BMI was calculated with the formula weight (kilograms)/height (meters) squared (kg/m2) from clinically measured or self‐reported weight and height [32]. Subjects with a BMI of less than 25 kg/m2 were considered the reference group. Subjects with a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or over but less than 30 kg/m2 were considered as living with overweight, and subjects with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or over were considered as living with obesity [41]. Body height (measured or self‐reported) was classified in tertiles by sex and birth cohort (eight cohorts by the year of birth: before 1920, periods of 10 years from 1920 to 1979, and 1980 or later), with increasing cut‐off points for tertiles toward the later birth cohorts [32]. The exact categorization is given in Table S1. For males born before 1920, those 168 cm or shorter were classified into the shortest tertile (reference group) and those taller than 173 cm into the tallest tertile. For males born in or after 1980, the respective cut‐off points were 176 cm (reference group) and 182 cm. For females born before 1920, those 154 cm or shorter were classified into the shortest tertile (reference group) and those taller than 159 cm into the tallest tertile. For females born in or after 1980, the respective cut‐off points were 163 cm (reference group) and 168 cm.

We used the personal identity code when linking the survey data to the population‐based Finnish Cancer Registry at the individual level. Linking to the registry data was performed by owners of the study survey data, and the analyses of the pooled data were conducted using pseudonymized data. The Finnish Cancer Registry holds national cancer data of high coverage and accuracy since 1953 [42]. We retrieved incident CRCs for the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD‐10) codes C18.0–C20.99 from the cancer registry. The follow‐up started at the time of the individual health survey and ended at the date of CRC diagnosis, death, or the end of the follow‐up (in 2013–2015, depending on the study).

Statistics

Age‐standardized incidence rates of CRC were calculated by risk factors over the entire period 1972–2015 and separately for five calendar periods (decades from 1972–1981 to 2002–2011 and a 4‐year period of 2012–2015). We used the World Health Organization's (WHO) World Standard Population for 2000–2025 in 14 age groups (18–24 years, 5‐year groups from 25–29 to 80–84 and ≥ 85 years) as weights in the standardization. We modeled both the effects of risk factors and the effects of calendar periods on cancer incidence using Poisson regression with log person‐years as the offset, where the cause‐specific hazard of CRC is the product of the baseline hazard and the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs). The HRs were based on Model 1, which included the effects of age and calendar period (the same categorization as above) and their interactions, and the main effect of time since survey (< 10 and ≥ 10 years). In addition, we estimated the average annual percent change (AAPC) based on Model 2, which included the main effects of age, calendar year, and time since survey. The effects of risk factors and the AAPC were estimated both by adjusting and without adjusting for the effects of other risk factors (education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, height, and DM). We used the likelihood ratio test to assess the statistical significance of AAPC and heterogeneity in the AAPCs between risk factor groups. The heterogeneity tests were based on models that also included the interaction between age and risk factor groups. Information on alcohol consumption was not available from the FINRISK cohorts in 1972, 1977, and 1982, or from the HBCS cohort, and physical activity or height measurements were not available from the FMCF and MFH cohorts (“missing by protocol”). The corresponding covariates from these cohorts were treated as a separate missing category in the risk factor analysis. To assess the effect of potential exposure misclassification in longer follow‐ups, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricting the follow‐up to the first 10 and 15 years after the survey. Heterogeneity of the studies was partially accounted for in the analyses by allowing the baseline hazard of cancer to vary by calendar period and follow‐up time.

Results

CRC occurred in 1020 (1.0%) males and in 986 (0.8%) females (Table 1). Around one in three (32.3%) males reported current smoking, while 18.3% of females reported current smoking. Fewer males (12.5%) reported alcohol abstinence compared with females (23.4%). It should, however, be noted that information on alcohol use was missing by protocol for 16.5% of males and 14.1% of females. A larger proportion of males (18.0%) reported themselves as physically active compared with females (14.0%), while more females (47.4%) reported being moderately active compared with males (44.0%). Information on physical activity was missing by protocol for 13.1% of males and 10.9% of females. Nearly half (43.7%) of the males and one in three (31.9%) females were living with overweight, while the proportions of males and females living with obesity were 15.8% and 18.3%, respectively.

Relative Risk of CRC in Males and in Females

Former or current smoking was associated with an increased risk of CRC in males (Table 2). The age‐standardized incidence rates of CRC in males were 29.1/105 in never smokers (reference group), 34.8/105 in former smokers (multivariate: HR 1.27, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08–1.49), and 39.7/105 in current smokers (multivariate: HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.18–1.66). Males living with overweight or obesity had an increased CRC risk compared with males with normal body weight. The age‐standardized incidence rates of CRC were 29.9/105 in males with normal body weight (reference group), 36.5/105 in males living with overweight (multivariate: HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.45), and 43.0/105 in males living with obesity (multivariate: HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.17–1.72). In males, the CRC risk was increased in both the middle and tallest height tertiles compared with the shortest tertile. The age‐standardized incidence rates of CRC in males were 27.4/105 in the shortest height tertile (reference group), 35.6/105 in the middle tertile (multivariate: HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.01–1.45), and 39.0/105 in the tallest tertile (multivariate: HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.17–1.70). DM was associated with an increased CRC risk in males. The age‐standardized incidence rates of CRC were 33.7/105 in males who were not diagnosed with DM (reference group) and 40.3/105 in males living with DM (multivariate: HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.04–1.78). Education level, alcohol consumption, or physical inactivity were not statistically significantly associated with the risk of CRC in males in this study (Table 2).

CRC Risk Trends

The overall AAPC in CRC incidence adjusted for the considered risk factors was 1.7 (95% CI 0.9–2.6) in males and 1.0 (95% CI 0.1–1.9) in females (Figure 1). Figure 1 illustrates that the AAPC in CRC incidence was positive in almost all risk factor exposure groups but not always statistically significant.

Average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) in colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence adjusted for education, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, body mass index, height, and diabetes (point estimates and 95% confidence interval [95% CI]) by sex and risk factor exposure level.

In males, we found the strongest evidence of increasing incidence in males with low education levels and in current male smokers. When comparing incidence rates by male education level, the AAPC was 2.1% in low (95% CI 0.7–3.5) and 1.9% in high education levels (95% CI 0.3–3.6), without noted trends in males with middle education levels (Figure 1, Table S2). When lifestyle factors, height, and DM are not adjusted, the incidence rate of CRC has risen by 1.7% per year in males with low education levels (95% CI 0.6–2.9) and 1.2% in males with middle education levels (95% CI 0.2–2.2), while no statistically significant trend was observed in males with high education levels (Table S2). In current male smokers, the CRC incidence rate rose 2.7% per year (95% CI 1.3–4.1), while such increases were not noted in male never or former smokers (Figure 1, Table S2).

In females, we found the strongest evidence for increasing incidence in moderately active females. The incidence of CRC rose 2.8% per year in females with moderate physical activity (95% CI 1.0–4.5), without trends in CRC incidences in physically active or inactive females (Figure 1, Table S2).

Sensitivity Analysis

Restricting the sensitivity analysis to 10‐ and 15‐year follow‐up times did not change the conclusions concerning CRC risk trends (Table S3). The shorter the follow‐up time, the more pronounced the effect of current smoking on the CRC trend.

Discussion

In this study, living with overweight or obesity, having a tall body height, or living with DM were statistically significant risk factors for CRC in both males and females. Furthermore, current or former smoking increased the risk of CRC in males, whereas heavy alcohol consumption increased the CRC risk in females. CRC incidence, adjusted for major lifestyle factors, increased annually by 1.7% in males and by 1.0% in females. In the smoking‐stratified analysis, the average annual percentage increase in the incidence of CRC was especially high in current male smokers. In females, the increase in CRC incidence was especially high in those engaging in moderate physical activity.

Risk Factors

Previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [7, 10, 13, 17] along with the International Agency for Research on Cancer [18] have observed the association between smoking and an increased incidence of CRC. Like a previous cohort study from Korea, we noted statistical significance only in males [43].

A meta‐analysis conducted by the World Cancer Research Fund [11], along with other previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [7, 10, 13, 17, 19], observed a positive association between alcohol intake and CRC risk. In our study, this was statistically significant only in females with heavy alcohol consumption, while the observed HR was higher (1.61) than previously reported [11]. Compared with previous analyses, the HR in males was of similar magnitude, but did not reach statistical significance [11]. Our non‐user (reference) group included both never and former alcohol users, potentially leading to more pronounced in‐group heterogeneity in males.

Our observation regarding the association between high BMI and an increased risk of CRC agrees with previous findings. Such associations have previously been observed in systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [7, 10, 13, 14], and it has been acknowledged by the International Agency for Research on Cancer [15]. The association between obesity and increased CRC incidence is likely to be influenced by several confounding variables, since many of the factors that cause obesity, such as sedentary behavior and dietary choices, are also known risk factors for CRC [11, 13, 19, 21, 27].

In our study, DM was associated with an increased risk of CRC in both males and females, which has been confirmed in previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses [8, 9]. The results also strengthen the evidence of an association between greater body height and the risk of CRC. A meta‐analysis conducted by the World Cancer Research Fund [11] observed a 5% increase in the CRC risk for every 5‐cm increase in height, while another meta‐analysis [12] observed a 14% increase in the risk of CRC for every 10‐cm increase in height. Our study found a 24% increase in the risk for females and a 41% increase for males when comparing the tallest height tertile to the shortest tertile. The underlying mechanisms behind the association between taller individual heights and increased CRC incidences may include genetic, environmental, hormonal, and nutritional factors behind greater adult height, including larger numbers of cells and higher concentrations of insulin‐like growth factors [11, 44, 45].

Risk Trends

Although the overall incidence trend of CRC, and the trends of many risk factors for CRC are well studied, there is limited previous research on CRC incidence trends by risk factor status. Overtime changes in substances included in cigarettes, changes in physical activity assessment, and changes in risk behavior in the population may have contributed to the various incidence trends of CRC. According to a 2014 report, the prevalences of various risk factors for CRC show a decreasing trend in the Finnish population, while some, like obesity and alcohol consumption, have become more prevalent [24]. Alcohol consumption has increased and the prevalence of abstinence decreased since the early 1980s until the early 2000s in the Finnish population and has thereafter remained unchanged [24].

A previous Finnish study found that colon cancer incidence was higher in those with higher education levels and higher SES compared with those with lower education levels and lower SES [5]. In our study, education level did not statistically significantly influence the CRC risk or its incidence trends when adjusted for lifestyle factors. If adjustment for lifestyle factors was not performed, the incidence rate of CRC increased especially rapidly in males with low education levels. This suggests that lifestyle factors may be one explanation behind the difference between the incidence trends of CRC at different education levels. Smoking, for example, remains more common in less educated groups, and differences in smoking between educational groups have widened further since the late 1970s [46].

The overall CRC incidence trend has increased especially rapidly in male smokers. They likely suffer from other adverse health issues such as higher consumption of red and processed meats and correspondingly lesser intake of fibers, fruits, and vegetables [47]. While occasional and never smokers improved their dietary behaviors between 1999 and 2013, daily smokers did not, which increased the differences in dietary behaviors between daily and never smokers over time [47]. Notably, in Norway, inactivity appeared more common in male and female smokers compared with their never‐smoker peers [47]. The latency period after smoking initiation for a notable increase in CRC risk is 30–40 years [48]. The prevalence of smoking has decreased in Finnish males since the late 1970s [24, 46].

The amount of leisure‐time physical activity increased in the Finnish population since the late 1970s until the mid‐1990s, when physically active ways of traveling to work became less common, and has thereafter remained somewhat unchanged [24]. In our study, the increase in the incidence of CRC was especially high in moderately active females. Leisure‐time physical activity did not affect the CRC risk in our study. Since half of the females in our study were classified as moderately active, in‐group heterogeneity in physical activity habits remained high, whereas the groups of physically inactive and active females became more strictly defined.

Methodological Considerations

Weaknesses in our study include potential changes in risk factor exposure after the time of survey. This may cause measurement bias leading to misclassification of the exposure. In addition, individuals may underestimate their exposures, which can lead to social desirability bias. Because of these biases, our hazard ratios could be biased toward the null. In our previous sensitivity analyses for the time dependence of exposure effects, measurement bias had no notable effects between exposures and the CRC risk [32]. Further, the validity of our results may have been affected by health survey‐related response biases, since more healthy or more health‐conscious individuals more frequently participate in surveys [49, 50]. Moreover, compared with invited populations, males appear under‐represented in health survey responders [49]. Lower response rates in more recent cohorts compared with older ones may add to selection bias [32]. Adjustment for risk factors remains incomplete to an extent, since some included health studies did not provide information on alcohol, physical activity, or body height. Furthermore, we only adjusted for age, sex, social and behavioral factors, body height, and DM, while adjustments were not made, for example, for certain dietary (e.g., consumption of red meat, lack of dietary fiber), genetic (e.g., family history), or clinical (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease) risk factors. For example, the consumption of red and processed meat has increased in Finland in the past decades [5]. The incompleteness of the adjustment for risk factors should be accounted for when interpreting our results. We do not consider the possibility of chance findings due to multiple comparisons to be a notable problem here, as we mainly report risk factor‐specific trends, and the risk factors in the analysis were carefully chosen according to previous knowledge regarding their associations with CRC risk.

The large numbers of test subjects and the reasonable number of CRCs in our pooled data provided us with good statistical power for the risk factor analysis. The information used on CRC in the analysis was based on extensive and accurate registry data. Data completeness for rectal and anal cancers in the Finnish Cancer Registry was 97.4%. The proportions of morphologically verified colon cancers and rectal and rectosigmoid cancers were 95.8% and 97.1%, respectively [42]. Considering the long latency of CRC, our long follow‐up time was vital. Our results are likely generalizable overall to white, Western populations. Given the adequate representation of the Finnish population provided by the METCA project, we consider the METCA data valid. For example, the large FINRISK survey has, since 1972, been based on random population samples from various parts of Finland drawn from the National Population Information System every 5 years [35]. The participation rates in the FINRISK Study have gradually declined from 91% in males and 94% in females in 1972 to 57% in males and 67% in females in 2012, which can still be considered good response rates.

Conclusion

Even after adjustment for the major risk factors, the overall incidence of CRC appeared to continue to increase. In risk factor‐stratified analysis, the average annual percentage increase in CRC incidence was especially high in current male smokers. We further confirmed the associations of BMI, body height, and DM with elevated CRC risk. Our findings highlight a need for effective public health strategies, with special emphasis on the prevention of overweight, obesity, and smoking along with other modifiable lifestyle factors known to affect CRC risk.
