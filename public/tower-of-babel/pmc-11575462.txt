Intimate partner violence in the context of infertility: voices from women seeking assisted reproductive technology treatment and clinicians’ perspectives
Authors: Yu Fu, Xueqi Sun, Kailibinuer Kayimu, Gong Chen, Yuanyuan Wang, Fan Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interplay between infertility and intimate partner violence (IPV) is a pressing global health issue, yet qualitative evidence on this phenomenon is limited. Background

Infertility defined as “the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse,” [1] varies in prevalence globally, ranging from 3 to 30% [2–4]. In China, the prevalence of infertility ranged from 11.9 to 15.5% [5]. However, infertility transcends mere physical health issues, leading to extensive social and mental health repercussions. Although male factors have been found to account for about a half of cases of infertility, infertility disproportionately affects women [6]. Infertility can exert long-lasting adverse effects on women’s mental health, such as fear, anxiety, distress, and depression [7, 8]. The impact of infertility is further magnified by sociocultural factors as many women with infertility are confronted with discrimination, stigma, and marginalization that stem from traditional gender norms and belief, which are detrimental to women’s long-term well-being [9].

Intimate partner violence (IPV) refers to violence by a current or former intimate partners, encompassing physical, sexual, psychological or emotional, and economic abuse as well as controlling behaviors, which is a form of gender-based violence because of the disproportionate effect on women in most situations [10, 11]. Among all violence against women, 60–80% are perpetrated by their intimate partners [12]. Globally, the prevalence of physical and/or sexual IPV among ever-married/partnered women aged 15–49 years was estimated to be 13% in the past 12 months and 27% in their lifetime [10].

Given that gender-based inequality widely exists in both infertility and IPV, women with infertility are at increased risk of experiencing IPV, especially in low- and middle- income settings. A systematic review indicated that 47.2% of women with infertility had experienced IPV in their lifetime [13]. Previous qualitative studies on the consequences of infertility have revealed intense emotional stress, social stigma, marital instability, and even abuse experienced by women with infertility [14–16], providing insights into the profound psychosocial impacts of infertility in specific cultural contexts. However, current qualitative evidences are mainly from African countries, such as Rwanda [15], Mozambique [17], Nigeria [18], Ghana [19], and South Africa [14], while a few were from Asian countries like Pakistan, Iran, and Jordan [16, 20–22]. Only two previous studies on IPV against infertile women were conducted in China, reporting the past-12 month IPV prevalence of 26.9% and 10.5% in two counties, respectively [23, 24]. No qualitative studies on IPV against infertile women have been conducted in China.

In order to measure and prevent IPV, it is pivotal to explore and integrate perceptions and experiences from those women with infertility themselves on IPV definition and its phenomenal manifestation in the context of infertility. To add to the standardized etic definition of IPV, we aim to extend current knowledge on IPV by providing the emic perspectives of women with infertility [25]. The dichotomy of etic and emic perspectives has been commonly used in medical anthropology [26]. While an etic perspective represents the “outside” framework and taxonomy of IPV, such as the WHO definitions of IPV, an emic perspective, on the other hand, delves into the lenses of “insiders” which can exist in parallel to the etic [27]. In this study, emic perspective on IPV is theorized to be shaped by the equal or unequal power between genders, fertility culture, as well as other structural forces working together [28]. Characterizing the emic definition on IPV in the context of infertility aligns with overarching goal of qualitative health research to uncover in-depth meanings related to health and illness.

So far, there is no existing qualitative evidence characterizing the emic definition of IPV specific to women with infertility in China. IPV in the context of infertility can be intertwined with exacerbated shame and layered stigma, rendering it a social taboo in communities which are a stronghold of patriarchal traditions and pronatalist values [9, 20]. To circumvent this social taboo, we leveraged fertility treatment clinics as a gateway to approach women with infertility, which assures a relatively safe and trusted environment for marginalized groups to voice their views.

Furthermore, to triangulate with women’s views, healthcare practitioners’ experience with the phenomenon of IPV against infertile women is also essential for understanding the intersectionality of infertility and IPV. Moreover, their insights would help inform future facility-based programming, such as routinized screening for IPV in clinical settings [29]. Therefore, our study aims to explore the awareness and understanding of IPV among women with infertility from multiple aspects, through in-depth interviews with female patients seeking fertility treatment services, and healthcare practitioners at fertility treatment clinics, to provide for the first time a detailed qualitative account on the phenomenal manifestation of violence at the intersection of intimate partnership and infertility in China. By eliciting the views of women at the assisted reproductive technology (ART) clinic, we can gain a better understanding of the context of infertility and infertile population, laying the groundwork for future efforts in implementing IPV screening in ART settings.

Methods

This study is undertaken as part of a year-long ethnographic research project based at a reproductive medicine center in Beijing, China from July 2022 to July 2023. This center was chosen because of its high volume of patients from across the country seeking fertility treatment. During the study period, the first author performed participant observation of clinical consultation as part of the hospital staff team, in order to understand the context and build rapport with clinicians and patients in preparation for study recruitment.

The first two authors also conducted individual interviews with women attending ART outpatient clinics and with clinicians involved in infertility treatment. Extensive notes were taken from the observation and interviews and were used to help illuminate the concept of IPV in the context of female infertility. In this study, we drew on Grounded Theory methodology to guide data analysis, which entailed a three-step analytical approach, including open, axial and focused coding of interview transcription documents [30]. Finally, we sorted out the relationship between major themes through coding layer by layer, and presented the findings in the form of a theoretical framework that depicts the structure of IPV perceived by women with infertility.

Recruitment

The study was approved by the Peking University Third Hospital Medical Science Research Ethics Committee and was conducted at the outpatient clinic of the reproductive medicne center. We used convenience sampling, and female patients were recruited with the help from clinicians. Patients were given a small card with study recruitment information at the end of their clinic visit from the first author. The card did not indicate that the study was related to IPV. If patients expressed interest in participating, they were invited to a private room for detailed introduction of the study and informed consent procedure before face-to-face interview. Male partners were not permitted to enter the interview area, to ensure privacy for all participants. Clinicians were recruited and screened for eligibility at the reproductive medicine center. We used online and in-person hybrid mode for clinician interviews in order to accommodate their busy schedule.

The inclusion criteria for female patients were: (1) women who met the infertility diagnostic criteria of “a couple who has not achieved clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of routine unprotected intercourse”, or seeking other fertility treatment at the clinic; (2) the person was mentally conscious and able to understand the purpose, risks and benefits of the study; and (3) the person voluntarily participated in the study with written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria for clinicians were: (1) working in a reproductive clinical specialty for at least 3 months and having direct contact with female patients and/or their partners in their day-to-day work; and (2) volunteering to participate in this study with informed consent.

The size of the sample was determined by information saturation.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted by the first two authors. Participant observation was carried out by the first author with input from second author from July 2022 to July 2023. Prior to interviewing, participants were provided with an opportunity to seek additional information or clarification. Interviews were audio recorded and verbatim transcribed. Participants received gifts (hand lotion) or monetary reimbursement (¥200, equivalent of $28) for compensation of their time. Interviews were completed during September to October 2022.

Interviews focused on the women’s perceptions of IPV surrounding the following overarching questions. The interview questions were formulated based on a comprehensive review of existing literature on IPV experienced by women with infertility. This initial set of questions was designed to elicit in-depth insights into the women's perceptions of IPV. To ensure the relevance and clarity of the questions, we consulted with experts in the field of reproductive health and IPV. Additionally, the questions underwent a pilot testing phase, allowing us to refine them for optimal clarity and effectiveness in facilitating the interviews.

Interviews with clinicians focused on their experience of encountering patients who fell victim to IPV, and their understanding of IPV in the context of infertility treatment.

Throughout the study process, we paid close attention to the sensitivity of the questions and participants’ safety. Prior to the interview, to ensure that the questions within the interview guide would not cause harm to the participants, we tested the questions with medical professionals and lay persons. During the interview, some questions involving participants’ own experiences would inevitably elicit their negative memories. Therefore, interviewers carefully worded questions during the interview, and if the participants showed obvious mood swings, the interviewers would immediately pause the interview and provide timely reassurance to the participants. We have also ensured the privacy of the interview settings.

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using a three-step analysis method as informed by Grounded Theory. First, the first two authors carefully read the transcribed texts of the interviews and open-coded them by segmenting the information to form categories about the participants’ perceptions related to the phenomenon of IPV against women with infertility. Within each category, researchers searched for its sub-categories by line-by-line coding, dividing the data into different dimensions and carefully searching for connections between each category to form an initial coding paradigm [31].

Secondly, the first two authors performed axial coding on the basis of the initial coding paradigm: assembling the data code in a new way, identifying a central phenomenon from it, and mapping the codes around this central phenomenon.

Third, the first two authors performed selective coding, which linked all categories through a conceptual matrix and formulated theoretical structure that is grounded in the interview data and their categories. Throughout the coding process, when uncertainties arose, we discussed thoroughly and eventually reached consensus on the most appropriate coding. The first two authors and the last two authors also performed comparative analysis of each individual case to maintain a connection with the data and gain a theoretical understanding of them.

We leveraged notes and knowledge gained from the participant observation to gain a contextualized understanding of our data, and to help interpret our study results.

Research quality and trustworthiness

During the data collection process, the authors spent a significant amount of time (two months) stationed at outpatient clinics to immerse themselves in the participants’ medical processes. In addition, the authors discussed different views on the same issue during the coding process to achieve investigator triangulation and tried to present as much detail of the study as possible when writing up this study, thus further enhancing the credibility.

Transferability was supported by the detailed description of methodology and presenting participants’ narratives in the context of their life. A numbering system was used for each participant to maintain confidentiality and protect the participants' identities. Each participant was assigned a unique number that represented their identity. We demonstrated confirmability through: (a) detailed memo writing, (b) high-quality audio recordings, and (c) consistency in coding by multiple researchers to ensure accurate understanding and presentation of the data. We tried to remove our own biases on some issues through ongoing discussions during the study period, tried to be objective and impartial with the data, and were careful not to draw assumptions or conclusions based on our personal experiences or knowledge gained from prior work.

Results

A total of 37 women and five clinicians participated in interviews during the study period. Among the 37 women, 36 (97.3%) were diagnosed with infertility, and one came for an ART-related service without confirmed infertility. The mean age of the participants who provided demographic data was 33 years with a range of 20–43 years. The duration of marriage among the participants varied from 1 to 10 years. In terms of employment status, 23 participants (62.2%) were employed, while 14 participants (37.8%) were either unemployed or did not report their occupation. Regarding infertility, 89.2% of the women experienced primary infertility, and for the majority (56.8%), no clear cause of infertility was identified upon examination. Experience and awareness of IPV

All patients provided valuable insights on the definition of IPV and its potential intersectionality with infertility. Clinicians contributed their perceptions on IPV definition as well. They also referred to previous cases they encountered in clinical settings, and offered suggestions for addressing IPV in context of infertility.

When participants were asked what kind of behaviors were considered IPV to them, they demonstrated diverse perspectives around definitions of IPV. While the World Health Organization (WHO)’ s IPV definition encompasses physical, psychological, emotional, sexual, economic coercions, and controlling behaviors [10], participants’ understanding was somewhat ambiguous (Fig. 1). Their perceived types of IPV were mostly limited to physical, psychological, and verbal violence. As they described “I think intimate partner violence is a form of verbal, or more seriously, behavioral violence (physical violence)” (P12, Age 25). None of the participants mentioned sexual or economic violence during the interviews. Most women (n = 15) demonstrated limited knowledge of the common types of IPV.

Incomplete conceptualization of IPV among women with infertility

Based on such definitions, most participants (n = 23) believed that the occurrence of IPV is relatively low. Most participants’ awareness of IPV incidence came from news reports or short videos posted on social media platforms. In contrary, in reality, IPV was rarely discussed. For instance, one participant highlighted this contrast by stating:

“If you look at the Internet, I think it's pretty common. But looking around me, it's unusual” (P12, Age 25).

At the same time, we found that the perceived prevalence of IPV declines over generations and during urbanization, as one mentioned: “there may have been more of this (IPV) in our grandparents' generation, but it seems to be less common among young people now.” Another participant also explained: “I think these years are much better. Now women are different from our parents' generation and have changed a lot” (P5, Age 30). One participant also noted regional differences in the distribution of IPV incidences: “I think it's probably better in the cities than in the rural areas” (P25, Age 34).

While several doctors shared the idea that IPV is not prevalent as far as they know, other doctors mentioned that some women with infertility might often be confronted with the threat of divorce because of infertility: “I often encounter this type of women (patient) who said: ‘if I fail [this treatment cycle] again this time, I will be divorced,’ Then you will suspect that she cannot get the support from her family, especially her partner” (D3, Attending Physician).

Doctors provided more experiential understanding of IPV definition based on their interactions with patients. They stressed that emotional abuse should be considered a prevalent form of violence experienced by women with infertility. For example, a doctor suggested that: “a family member, for example, might have some views about her (women with infertility), which makes her feel uncomfortable, does this count? Stonewalling (silent treatment) or something? It can be regarded as a kind of psychological violence. And I think this will definitely happen in most cases” (D1, Associate Chief Physician).

IPV intersecting with infertility

Delving into the intersection between IPV and infertility, three subcategories have been developed from emerged codes (Fig. 2).

IPV Intersecting with infertility

Predisposition

IPV against women experiencing infertility may represent a distinct form of violence, where infertility is exploited as an excuse for aggression. Nevertheless, perpetrators of IPV often exhibit common predispositions and risk factors. One participant suggested that:

“I think for IPV and infertility, women experiencing IPV is just a phenomenon. It's not because of infertility that they face IPV. It's because a person who perpetrates violence will find various excuses to do so. Infertility is just perhaps a trigger point for this behavior” (P21, Age 34).

Context of infertility

When discussing IPV in the context of fertility issues, high levels of stress and frustration during the ART process could become a provocative factor to trigger violence in relationships for both men and women. In turn, distress caused by IPV, such as anxiety and depression, coupled with infertility, creates a vicious cycle that challenges women’s reproductive health and exerts extremely adverse effects. As mentioned by some participants:

“Originally this group of infertile women are very stressful, in the process of receiving treatment may be under a certain amount of pressure mentally and physically. It may be difficult for them to deal with the stress of infertility, and it's even harder for them to deal with IPV, so I think it's kind of a vicious cycle that just gets worse” (P6, Age 36).

Additionally, many participants suggested that women’s lack of financial independence could exacerbate women’s vulnerability to IPV. While this statement may hold for almost all women, it may especially be true for women with infertility when financial strain caused by fertility treatment costs could worsen the power imbalance:

“It’s just that after a long time [of infertility treatment], she spends money and energy, … If she is on the journey of IVF, she may have to spend money while not earning money. After a long time, maybe her husband can’t stand it, and there will always be a feeling that it’s her fault… and I think there will be a tendency to violence. At this point, I feel that many women will choose to tolerate because of her physical incompleteness” (P10, Age 31).

Some participants noted that lowering the cost of infertility treatment could have averted IPV occurrence since the financial burden caused by infertility treatment could negatively affect relationship quality, and in turn induce IPV.

“This [IPV against infertile women] is difficult to solve, because the biggest problem for IVF is actually the high expenditure. … A total of 40,000 [RMB] was spent, that is, 40,000 for one time [cycle], and 80,000 for two [cycles]. You can accept your first failure. The second failure may be particularly difficult. The third time may cause family conflicts.” (P10, Age 31).

Furthermore, toxic masculinity, which may involve blaming women for childlessness, adds another layer of complexity and potential for abuse: “It's possible that, for example, in everyday interactions, they (men) might frequently bring up this issue, and then possibly place all the blame on the woman” (P2, Age 32).

Several doctors also mentioned that IPV can greatly impact women’s emotional well-being, which is a key factor in the success of assisted reproduction. Therefore, the occurrence of IPV can easily lead to repeated failure of assisted reproduction in women, causing layered distress. One doctor noted that:

“Performing in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) is like sowing a field, your emotions are like the external environment, sometimes it is difficult to produce fruits without the cooperation of the external environment even if you are a good soil. If your mood is not good, your body will not produce enough hormones, which can make it difficult for embryo transfer to be successful. So I think IPV has a very bad effect on women [in terms of ART outcome]” (D3, Attending Physician).

Phenomenal manifestation in infertility care and services

During the interviews, emotional abuse was frequently mentioned and emerged as a particularly distressing form of violence to many women with infertility. Five participants expressed that emotional abuse was in particular unbearable and traumatizing. For instance, one participant mentioned: “I think emotional abuse is scarier (than other forms of violence)” (P1, Age 38). In addition, several participants (n = 9) mentioned that emotional violence, such as “emotional indifference”, “mocking their infertility” and “mental suppression”, were considered intolerable to them.

“In addition to verbal provocation or physical violence, I think there are a lot of things that he doesn't express verbally or physically, such as the meaningful glance or talking about something with friends or relatives. I think this is something that I don't feel directly hurt me, but actually it has hurt me deeply” (P34, Age 35).

One doctor confirmed about emotional abuse as a phenomenal manifestation of IPV in the context of infertility. As commonly observed in medical practices, IPV experienced by infertile women included not only refusing to communicate and intentionally avoiding, but also shunning collective tasks assigned to the couple during fertility treatment process:

“I think the violence of beating and scolding, within the scope of my knowledge, is rare from what I have seen, but cold violence (in Chinese, “leng bao-li”, that is, refusing to communicate, intentionally avoiding) might be more common, such as those (husbands) who are not cooperative and are not proactive [in the treatment process], then let women feel that she is alone and that there is no help on this road, no partner who encourages each other. I think there may be more of this type of violence (in the context of infertility)” (D3, Attending Physician).

While the majority of participants believed that IPV, to their understanding, still happens rarely between couples facing infertility issues, many brought up the threat of divorce or abandonment from the partners that every now and then happens to women with infertility:

“I heard others also say this, saying that some people we know that if it doesn't work this time [cycle], then it's going to lead to divorce” (P14, Age 33, 0923_03).

One participant described her witness of one of such incidences at a healthcare setting:

“Because previously, I had surgery at Hospital X (pseudonym) and afterwards, in the hospital room next to ours, there was someone who said that if you're infertile, then it means divorce” (P17, Age 35).

Additionally, the interference of the older generation may increase women’s vulnerability to IPV, especially in cultures where there is a strong emphasis on bringing offspring as a part of filial duty falling on the shoulders of a young couple:

“Regarding the in-laws, I feel that if there are those kinds of words spoken to her [infertile women], it's more common in my hometown…like if you don't have a child, the in-laws might say particularly unpleasant things, and there will be a lot of that, I think” (P22, Age 31).

Persisting son preference in fertility culture exacerbates women’s vulnerability within their household, as pointed out by one participant:

“But for many in the older generation, the mindset is that the family must have a boy. Whether they like it or not is not important; you must have a boy. This is quite a serious issue in Shanxi, including my aunt. As part of our own family, she wouldn't say this in front of her daughter-in-law, but she would say to us that if you don't give birth to a boy within 2 years of marriage, then get lost” (P8, Age 32).

Coping strategies for infertile women vulnerable to IPV

Participants were asked about strategies to cope with IPV. They were also asked that if they had a friend with infertility who was suffering from IPV, what suggestions they would give to her. While a few participants suggested that they preferred not to get involved with other people’s private business, most participants offered their opinions on potential responses to IPV, whereby three themes emerged as follow:

Dyadic level: end toxic and harmful relationship

Half of the participants (n = 19) said they would not tolerate any behavior that constituted what they perceived as violence. Most participants talked about their zero-tolerance for IPV and women should simply end their marriages or relationships once there is violence since the perpetrator cannot change and the violent incidence will happen again regardless of their fertility outcome and parity.

“My personal opinion is that there are either zero or countless times of violence, so I can't tolerate this. If it were me, I would definitely find a way to divorce, protect my family, and end this relationship” (P21, Age 34).

“If it were my best friend experiencing IPV, I would talk her out of it. The sooner they break up, the better, because [if] the violence happens now, and then he'll beat you up even after you have a baby” (P14, Age 33).

Individual level: resistance to the blaming from the onset of care seeking

Participants shared that when infertility occurs, women need to resist the stereotypical presumption about female-factor infertility, and instead one should resist the blaming for childlessness. One participant suggested that figuring out the actual reasons for infertility with medical examinations would be important for reducing IPV against infertile women since it dispels the myth that infertility is attributed to only female factor.

“First, you should go to hospital for a [fertility] examination. You can’t just say that it’s woman’s fault right away, right? Because many people actually have male-factor infertility problems, and if you finally find out that there is a problem with the women, then you have to see what kind of problem it is, because people don’t want to be sick” (P21, Age 34).

Interpersonal level: social support and professional assistance

Other common coping strategies for IPV against infertile women proposed by the participants include seeking help from family or friends, organizations, or police. As one noted:

“If this were my friend, I would be furious. I would ask her to tell her parents [the violence victimization] and seek the help from friends and families. And I would probably accompany her to [do something to] relax” (P12, Age 25).

“Maybe the Women's Federation…. I think the Women's Federation is certainly a way to protect women. Then there are the police to help. I hope that the law can provide more legal support for women…” (P25, Age 34).

In dealing with IPV against women with infertility, participants also noted that psychological interventions might be helpful for victims to cope with trauma.

“If she is really troubled [by IPV], she can come talk to me or find other people to talk to or find psychological intervention” (P 19, Age 32).

“I think you can find a therapist to talk to. Professional people to go to help her. Because you cannot change others. You can only deal with it by yourself” (P3, Age 39).

Strategies to prevent IPV from happening to women with in fertility

Three subcategories have emerged based on participants’ responses to the question of what should be done to preventively address IPV in the context of infertility and in general.

Empowering women through education, financial autonomy and independence

Women’s empowerment was brought up to reduce or prevent IPV occurrence, such as education and more financial autonomy.

“It may help to get more education and go to big cities. I think the idea of patriarchy may be less (there). You just get a better undergraduate degree, and then go to see a bigger world, go to a big city to get more experience” (P9, Age 38).

Several participants believed that education leading to independence of women, can help disentangle women’s role from motherhood, and in turn as a means to prevent IPV perpetration. For instance, one shared:

“I think it is better to strengthen education and strengthen publicity… for women’s independence… The traditional [belief] that a women’s destiny is to become a mother is not always true” (P2, Age 32).

Focus on educating men to prevent IPV perpetration

Participants expressed that strategies for addressing IPV should place more emphasis on prevention and include education programs targeting men.

“I think we need to prevent the occurrence of violence rather than to seek help after the harm has been done. We should educate male early [before the occurrence of violence]” (P34, Age 35)”.

Doctors echoed this proposal by suggesting that health education for men could potentially be beneficial for IPV reduction:

“Perhaps the most important thing is to communicate science, which is the cause of infertility, male and female factors. It may be better to make everyone get in touch with more knowledge in this area…You should make a clear diagnosis and educate him. This might be better, right? For example, if infertility is caused by male factors, some people, especially those who have strong traditional values, may reduce violence perpetration… And male-factor and female-factor infertility both account for half of the cases, but this concept is less well known to the general public” (D4, Associate Chief Physician).

The role of health system in IPV prevention

Participants discussed the role of clinicians and health system in addressing IPV against infertile women. Many participants brought up that clinicians could potentially help deliver psychological support to some extent in tandem with their medical services.

“I think, for example, maybe our doctors can provide clinical treatment and, at the same time, provide some psychological counseling, such as telling her that her situation [refers to infertility] is common, or that many people are also experiencing it….” (P25, Age 34).

Doctors suggested that incorporating IPV sensitizing and screening in infertility treatment would be beneficial for patients in terms of recognizing violence:

“I think screening might be a warning for her [female patient]. She may not have realized it was IPV at first, but she could realize that it was a red flag while she was filling out the (screening survey) form. Then after she realizes it, she will have the idea of defending her own rights” (D2, Associate Chief Physician).

However, several doctors also commented that in order for healthcare workers to engage more in IPV reduction at infertility clinics, guidelines and training are necessary:

“I think that if I can identify it [IPV] during my diagnosis and treatment, or I know how to comfort her, I think I will, but first I have to be able to identify, and I have to know the ‘diagnosis’ and ‘treatment’ process (of IPV), and what should we do if we have a diagnosis and treatment guideline. Then, I think we may need to receive certain training, and we may be able to do better” (D1, Associate Chief Physician).

Discussion

In the current qualitative study, we discovered a complex interplay between infertility experience and IPV perceptions as illustrated in Fig. 2. The narratives of the participants revealed the incomplete conceptualization of IPV among participants but also brought novel insights from their viewpoints. Notably, when asked to define IPV, none of the participants mentioned sexual or economic abuse. This might be due to the strong taboo related to sexual violence, and the lack of awareness about economic abuse within intimate partnerships. As evidenced in previous research, discussions of sexual matters, especially victimization, are stigmatized and discouraged [32]. Consequently, participants might be reluctant to disclose experiences or knowledge pertaining to sexual violence. The lack of mentioning economic violence among the participants might stem from participants’ demographic characteristics and economic backgrounds. As the recruitment was carried out in Beijing, the capital city of China, patients at our study site tended to have relatively higher level of education attainment, with a high degree of economic autonomy compared to their rural and non-care seeking counterparts, which potentially diminishes the prevalence of perceived economic violence [33]. This was evident in Table 1 where most women in this study reported currently being employed in formal jobs. Nevertheless, our findings revealed among many participants the insufficient awareness of sexual and economic abuse as subtypes of IPV. Therefore, increasing awareness of sexual and economic violence as types of IPV potentially helps women better understand their rights and protect themselves against various forms of abuse, and is thus imperative to lay a foundation of common understanding of IPV for any screening program to be effective in clinical settings.

The detailed account from women with infertility and from healthcare workers illuminated the salient features of the distinct context of infertility in China where IPV might take place. IPV against women living with infertility, manifests through psychological abuse, such as stonewalling and threats of divorce which corroborates with qualitative and quantitative evidence from other low- and middle- income countries [13, 14, 21]. While IPV against women with infertility shares certain manifestations with that against the general population, the experiences and situations of women with fertility problems are unique. Our findings further underscored that spouse’s non-compliance with and passive aggression towards ART treatment demonstrated another manifestation of IPV specific to the context of infertility. The impact of such psychological abuse, as reported by women, could lead to a sense of isolation and helplessness, which could exert an additional severe psychological burden on women with infertility. Based on these findings, we suggest that definitions of IPV in the context of infertility integrate those contextualized manifestations of violence and consider women’s narratives in defining IPV. The development of IPV screening tool for women with infertility should consider incorporating those experiential aspects as items to identify IPV among ART patients. Moreover, beyond the scope of the present study, law and regulations should consider women’s voice in drafting protective measures for women facing reproductive injustice.

Although the study mainly investigates women’s understanding and attitudes of violence perpetrated by an intimate partner, we found that other family members could exacerbate the psychological violence experienced by women with infertility through ridiculing or gossiping about women for their so-called ‘incompleteness’. Violence perpetrated by other family members not only exacerbates the trauma experienced by the victim but also reinforces harmful gender stereotypes surrounding fertility and motherhood which may also influence the partner’s perception [34]. Earlier evidence also suggested that women are challenged with stigma and criticism from their household members when infertility occurs [35]. This is especially true in East Asian context, where bringing offspring is seen as not only a critical and irreplaceable life event, but also a core filial duty of wedded couples to meet the expectations of their parents [36, 37]. Therefore, intergenerational conflicts often concur with infertility and involuntary childlessness in extended family and worsen the gender inequality within the dyadic relationship potentially leading to IPV. Therefore, structural risk factors in societal culture should be considered and intervened upon in order to prevent IPV against women with infertility. Future studies may explore possible interventions that target gendered stigma against infertility and focus on multi-generational household as analytical units [38].

Our findings highlighted several additional context-specific factors contributing to IPV against women with infertility, adding to the existing understanding of common risk factors for IPV such as alcohol use, depression and age [39]. First, mental stress and frustration associated with infertility and ART treatments are notable contributors that often provoke disagreements in intimate partnerships. Second, financial strain caused by ART treatments could give rise to this stress, creating situations where conflicts and IPV can happen. In China, up till the data collection period, ART treatments were paid out-of-pocketnationwide, which causes substantial financial strain for infertile couples [40]. To mitigate this risk factor, inclusion of ART treatments in medical insurance reimbursement may ease this tension and facilitate more equitable access to reproductive health services [41]. Last, women in our study voiced the injustice brought by the widespread myth that infertility issues are primarily and exclusively due to women’s health conditions, leads to blaming on women, causing interpersonal and psychosomatic spiral of adverse reactions. This finding adds nuances and first-person account as testimony to the central issue on etiology and blame in infertility diagnosis and treatment [9]. Regardless of female or male factors from infertility diagnosis, infertility should not be weaponized as an excuse to justify IPV in any form, because violence is a fundamental violation of human rights, irrespective of their reproductive capabilities [42].

In our study, we found a consensus among women and health practitioners around the overwhelming mental stress of patients undergoing fertility treatment, which calls for a comprehensive examination of mental health, for instance, the prevalence of stress, among ART patients in order to inform any evidence-based response in healthcare practice. Women’s empowerment and all-gender fertility education programs have been brought up by both women and clinicians as means to prevent IPV in the context of infertility, consistent with a previous study [43]. It was also suggested that incorporating IPV screening into infertility treatment workflow could potentially facilitate timely referral to specialized agencies and the monitoring of IPV occurrence. Conversely, previous literature from other countries debated about the effectiveness of routinized IPV screening at primary care settings [44]. Further, a randomized controlled trial reported no difference between the IPV screening arm versus the control arm in terms of women’s quality of life, safety planning and behavior, mental health (measured by SF-12), or anxiety [45]. Therefore, empirical evidence from China is warranted to assess the effectiveness of IPV screening at ART clinics in terms of relevant and meaningful endpoints in the local context.

Research remains scarce on effective IPV interventions specifically tailored for women with infertility, but strategies like psychosocial interventions, training and engaging healthcare providers, couples counseling may be appraised further for their effectiveness for reducing IPV [46–48]. Beyond individual-level measures, structural interventions on IPV at multiple socioecological levels have been emphasized in recent years and were reflected in our research findings [49]. In summary, our study grounded in women’s voices and clinicians’ perspectives, suggests that intervention package that entails enhancing awareness of all types of IPV, correcting false beliefs about infertility and motherhood, as well as addressing gender inequality in the social climate, could potentially tackle the root causes of IPV against women with infertility.

Limitations and strengths

Our findings should be considered with the following limitations. Firstly, conducting ethnographic fieldwork at an outpatient clinic had its advantages and disadvantages. The direct observation of large volume of patients and encounter with individual woman with infertility provided an immersed experience adding to the authenticity and credibility of the study findings. Yet, healthcare setting has its constraints such as the limited time that patients planned to spend and the biomedical atmosphere which may have inhibited participants from feeling at ease to share their feelings. Second, our participant pool primarily comprised individuals receiving fertility treatment healthcare in Beijing, which may not reflect the experiences of those in other regions or those excluded from fertility treatment for access reasons. Those women may face more severe vulnerability to IPV and could offer different viewpoints to our findings. However, as a tertiary hospital, our study site received patients referred from health facilities across the country and remains the last resort for many families seeking fertility treatment. To that end, our sample captured certain degree of heterogeneity and our findings demonstrated transferability to other settings outside of Beijing. Third, during recruitment, we did not dichotomize women into IPV victims versus non-victims, or exclude non-victims from participating. This decision was based on the rationale that a reductionism approach to define IPV at the entry point to data collection, may fail to capture the rich emic experience and definitions of violence and social suffering among women with infertility. Therefore, in our interviews, no women have explicitly revealed lived experience of physical violence victimization from intimate partners. Future studies may consider filling this gap by conducting phenomenological or narrative research focusing solely on survivors of physical abuse. Fourth, no women mentioned issues related to reproductive coercion during the interviews which remains to be explored in detail.

This research offers several novel contributions to the literature on IPV and infertility. Despite the increasing prevalence of infertility affecting up to one fourth of women wanting to become mothers, few studies have qualitatively explored infertility from both women’s and healthcare workers’ perspectives [50]. Filling these gaps is particularly necessary as reproductive health in contemporary society is characterized by the intersectionality of human experience, technology, and social meanings. Our findings extend knowledge about IPV using qualitative methodology, adding nuances to a previous systematic review on IPV prevalence in the context of infertility [13]. In terms of methodology, qualitative research confirmability was achieved through detailed memo writing, extended time in the field by the first two authors, verbatim transcription of audio recordings, and consistency in coding by multiple researchers to ensure accurate understanding and authentic presentation of the data.

Implications

Our study drawing on frontline health workers’ and women’s perspectives on IPV in the context of infertility care seeking in China provided insights into the emic understanding of this phenomenon, which complemented the recent biomedical paradigm of and reductionist approach to IPV. Practically, salient themes and suggested future practices grounded in our findings, can be leveraged to improve IPV prevention effort in China, with specific target on the context of ART care seeking and via women empowerment, male education, and health system engagement.

Conclusion

This study represents an inaugural qualitative exploration of the intersection between IPV perception and infertility experience in China, distinguished by its emphasis on firsthand accounts from women with infertility and insights from clinicians. The findings suggest that awareness about IPV still need to be enhanced among women with infertility in China. The phenomenal manifestation of IPV against women with infertility included emotional abuse such as stonewalling, threats of divorce, and non-compliance with ART treatment, reflecting the specificity of violence in ART context. In addition, several coping strategies and preventive measures were suggested by women and clinicians which should be considered when designing context-specific interventions to IPV against women with infertility.
