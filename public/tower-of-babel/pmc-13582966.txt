Integration of multiple perspectives to understand the left ventricular pressure–volume relationship
Authors: Giovanni Tonti, Gianni Pedrizzetti, Alessandro Salustri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For more than a century, the pressure–volume loop has offered a simple and elegant way to show how the heart pumps blood. In one picture, it summarizes how the heart fills, contracts, and ejects blood. However, this simplicity has a limitation: the loop treats the heart as if it were a single, uniform chamber. Therefore, it cannot reveal many important biological and mechanical processes that influence how the heart truly works. This helps explain why some patients develop symptoms or early signs of heart problems even when their pressure–volume measurements look completely normal. In this Perspective, we describe the pressure–volume relationship not as a stand-alone measure but as an emergent phenotype - the visible “end result” of many processes happening at different levels. Beneath the loop are the molecular and cellular mechanisms that control how heart muscle contracts and relaxes. Beyond the loop are differences in how various regions of the heart function, the organised patterns of blood flow inside the ventricle, and the continuous interaction between the heart and the rest of the circulatory system. We discuss how advanced imaging, flow visualization techniques, and computational modelling can add depth to pressure–volume analysis, revealing mechanisms that global, chamber-level measurements cannot show. Seen this way, the familiar loop becomes a window into an individual patient’s underlying biology, helping clinicians diagnose problems earlier and more accurately. This approach may help reveal the true mechanisms behind seemingly normal pressure–volume loops and support more precise, targeted treatments.

Introduction

The left ventricular pressure-volume (P–V) diagram represents one of the most intellectually elegant achievements in modern cardiovascular physiology. Conceptually developed from the seminal works of Otto Frank1,2 and subsequently formalized through the contributions of Suga, Sagawa, and colleagues3–8, this theoretical construct enables condensing the entire cardiac cycle dynamics into a two-dimensional representation. The counterclockwise trajectory of the loop—traversing the phases of diastolic filling, isovolumetric contraction, systolic ejection, and isovolumetric relaxation—provides immediate and quantitative information regarding external cardiac work, ventricular-arterial interaction, and intrinsic myocardial properties. However, despite its utility, the P–V loop provides an incomplete view of cardiac dynamics: the model treats the ventricle as a single compartment (a “lumped” model) and therefore cannot mechanistically discriminate regional differences in contraction, electromechanical activation, and tissue properties. In other words, the framework yields chamber-level averages that are agnostic to regional mechanical heterogeneity. Such simplifications, albeit didactically and clinically valuable for a global representation, obscure a wealth of phenomena operating both at lower (molecular, subcellular, tissular) and upper (regional, multi-chamber, systemic) scales. The result is a growing clinical paradox: patients whose symptoms and outcomes diverge sharply from their P–V metrics.

In this Perspective, we reframe the P–V loop as an emergent phenotype: the macroscopic expression generated by the collective interaction of processes operating across multiple biological scales but not uniquely predictive of the individual components that generated it. We first examine determinants “beneath” the loop, including sarcomere mechanics, calcium handling, energetic constraints, and extracellular matrix remodeling. We then address phenomena “beyond” its resolution: regional heterogeneity, intracavitary vortex dynamics, multi-chamber coupling, and autonomic modulation. We highlight that titin modifications alter diastolic stiffness independently of measured filling pressures, that disrupted vortex formation has been reported to precede adverse remodeling before ejection fraction falls, and that atrial function and ventricular interdependence introduce variability invisible to standard analysis. Integration of the P–V framework with deformation imaging, flow visualization, and computational modeling offers a path toward earlier detection of latent dysfunction and possibilities for individualized therapy.

Informative content of the P–V loop: derivable parameters and their physiological significance

Prior to analyzing the phenomena “beyond” and “beneath” the loop, we will describe which physiological quantities are fully accessible through the P–V curve analysis. Such clarification is essential for understanding the intrinsic limitations of this representation.

External work and loop area

The area enclosed by the P–V loop corresponds to the systolic stroke work (SW), namely the mechanical energy transferred from the ventricle to the blood during each cardiac cycle9–11. This quantity, expressed in joules or millimeters of mercury per milliliter (mmHg mL−1), represents the integral product of pressure and volume change during the ejection phase. Although external work accounts for only a small proportion of the total energy consumed by the myocardium, a substantial amount is expended as potential energy—linked to achieving end-systolic pressure—and as heat resulting from the thermodynamic inefficiency inherent in muscular contraction.

The energetic framework established by Hill’s foundational work on muscle mechanics12 and subsequently extended by Fenn13 provides the theoretical background for understanding myocardial efficiency. The Fenn effect—whereby energy liberation increases during muscle contraction—demonstrates that cardiac muscle, like skeletal muscle, exhibits load-dependent energetics that cannot be fully captured by simple area calculations. Contemporary investigations utilizing P–V area (PVA) analysis have refined our understanding of the relationship between mechanical work and oxygen consumption, revealing that ~25% of the total energy expenditure is converted into external work under physiological conditions, a value that reflects the combined efficiency of oxygen-to-PVA and PVA-to-work conversion11. At this point, a clarification of terminology is needed. The P–V loop area quantifies the SW, whereas the total mechanical energy transferred to the arterial conduit corresponds to the ejection work, which additionally includes the filling work represented by the area beneath the lower limb of the loop (diastolic filling). Part of this filling work is supplied by atrial contraction, which delivers a small but physiologically relevant energy increment into the ventricular structure that is subsequently handed on during ejection.

PVA and myocardial oxygen demand

The PVA represents the sum of external work (P–V loop area) and end-systolic potential energy (the triangular area comprised between end-systolic pressure-volume relationship [ESPVR], end-diatolic pressure-volume relationship [EDPVR], and the vertical line passing through the end-systolic volume). Pioneering studies by Suga demonstrated a linear relationship between PVA and myocardial oxygen consumption (MVO2) per beat9–11, making PVA a surrogate of the total myocardial energy demand. This relationship holds clinical importance in evaluating cardiac mechanical efficiency and in understanding the pathophysiology of myocardial ischemia.

The PVA-MVO2 relationship represents one of the most robust findings in cardiac energetics, showing a remarkable linearity across a wide range of loading conditions and contractile states11. The y-intercept of this relationship represents basal metabolism and excitation-contraction coupling energy, while the slope reflects the energy cost of P–V work. Importantly, this framework has been validated across species and has proven clinically useful in predicting the energetic consequences of therapeutic interventions, including pharmacological agents and mechanical circulatory support devices14–16.

ESPVR and contractility

Non-invasive, single-beat P–V loop methodologies—combining peripheral pressure measurements with imaging-derived volumes—have markedly expanded access to ventricular mechanics without preload manipulation. Classical approaches, such as the Chen method, estimate end-systolic elastance (Ees) using normalized elastance frameworks17. Subsequent developments include isovolumic pressure-based and time-varying elastance models, reflecting heterogeneous assumptions. A systematic review by Lambrecht et al. identified 24 distinct single-beat methods with variable validation and accuracy18. More recently, Marcus proposed a coupling-based reformulation deriving ESPVR from ejection fraction and ventricular–arterial interaction19. While these advances enhance feasibility and clinical applicability, they also suggest that broader adoption will require a deeper physiological understanding, because the resulting indices remain model-dependent and load-sensitive. At present, single-beat estimation techniques require rigor and caution when applied in clinical research settings.

The upper left margin of the P–V loop defines the ESPVR. The slope of this relationship, termed maximal end-systolic elastance (Ees or Emax), has historically been proposed as a relatively load-independent index of myocardial contractility3–5. Ideally, the ESPVR represents the envelope of end-systolic points obtained by varying preload under constant afterload conditions, or vice versa. Nonetheless, experimental and computational studies have demonstrated that Ees is not entirely independent of afterload, particularly under conditions of myocardial dysfunction or altered ventricular geometry20,21. Furthermore, the non-linearity of the ESPVR in various pathological conditions (dilated cardiomyopathy, acute ischemia) confounds the interpretation of this index as an unequivocal measure of intrinsic contractility. The seminal work of Burkhoff and colleagues20 elucidated the contractility-dependent curvilinearity of end-systolic P–V relations, demonstrating that the assumption of linearity represents an oversimplification that may introduce systematic errors in contractility assessment.

From a physiological perspective, the Frank–Starling mechanism is intrinsic to the measurement of the ESPVR: because the relationship is operationally constructed from the family of end-systolic points sampled across a preload reduction (for example during transient caval occlusion), each point reflects the length-dependent activation that couples instantaneous end-diastolic volume to developed pressure. The slope of Ees is therefore read precisely from the set of beats generated by the Frank–Starling relationship22. Moreover, the concept of time-varying elastance, introduced by Suga and Sagawa4,5, suggests that ventricular elastance increases from a minimal value at end-diastole to a maximum at end-systole, following a characteristic time course that is relatively preserved across different loading conditions. This framework has proven remarkably robust for understanding ventricular pump function, though recent investigations have highlighted its limitations in capturing the full complexity of cardiac mechanics, particularly during the transition phases of isovolumetric contraction and relaxation23–25.

EDPVR and ventricular compliance

The inferior margin of the P–V loop defines the EDPVR, which characterizes the passive properties of the ventricular chamber during filling. The EDPVR curve typically exhibits an exponential course: at low filling volumes, volumetric increments produce a modest increase in pressure (high compliance), whereas at higher volumes the chamber becomes progressively stiffer (reduced compliance). The position and curvature of the EDPVR reflect multiple tissular determinants: the viscoelastic properties of the extracellular matrix (collagen content and cross-linking)26,27, the intrinsic characteristics of cardiomyocytes (titin isoform and phosphorylation state)28–33, chamber geometry (wall thickness, mass-to-volume ratio), and extrinsic factors such as pericardial constraint and interventricular interaction.

The pioneering work of Mirsky and Parmley34 established the theoretical framework for assessing passive elastic stiffness, distinguishing between chamber stiffness (dP/dV) and myocardial stiffness (dσ/dε), where σ denotes stress and ε denotes strain. Contemporary understanding recognizes that diastolic properties emerge from a complex interplay between cellular and extracellular components. At shorter sarcomere lengths, titin-based elasticity is predominant, whereas at longer lengths collagen engagement becomes increasingly significant. This dual-component model has important implications for understanding the pathophysiology of diastolic dysfunction, as different disease processes may preferentially affect one component over the other.

Effective arterial elastance and ventricular-arterial coupling

The interaction between the ventricle and the arterial system can be characterized through the effective arterial elastance (Ea), defined as the ratio of end-systolic pressure to stroke volume. Geometrically, Ea corresponds to the magnitude of the negative slope of the line joining the point defined by end-diastolic volume at zero pressure on the volume axis to the end-systolic point in the P–V plane. The Ea/Ees ratio (ventricular-arterial coupling) represents a paradigm shift from an isolated assessment of ventricular or vascular function toward an integrated systems perspective21. Two distinct optima must be distinguished: SW is maximal when Ea/Ees approximates 1.0, whereas mechanical efficiency—external work per unit of myocardial oxygen consumption—peaks at lower Ea/Ees ratios, in the range of ~0.5–0.6. The commonly quoted physiological range of Ea/Ees 0.5–1.0 therefore spans, rather than identifies, these two optima. In heart failure, coupling deterioration occurs through elevation of Ea (increased vascular stiffness, neurohormonal activation) and reduction of Ees (impaired contractility), creating a mechanically unfavorable state that compounds the energetic burden on the failing heart.

The P–V loop must be conceived not as a self-sufficient measure of cardiac function, but as the emergent phenotype, i.e., the macroscopic projection generated by the collective interaction of processes operating across multiple biological scales (Fig. 1). The shape, position, and dimensions of the P–V loop result from the spaciotemporal integration of biophysical processes operating at lower dimensional scales to the global chamber representation. Systolic P–V loop function

Recently, high-resolution imaging, molecular phenotyping, and computational modeling have recently converged to reveal information concealed to the P–V loop has occurred. Sarcomere-directed pharmacology, titin-based modulation of diastolic stiffness, metabolic modulators, and image-derived surrogates of regional work and intraventricular flow are all moving from bench to bedside. In the following section, we will describe each determinant—sarcomere and myofilament mechanics, excitation–contraction coupling, bioenergetics, extracellular matrix remodeling, and mechanotransduction—and, wherever possible, the relative impact on the morphology of the global P–V loop, thereby framing both current advances and future perspectives35,36.

Sarcomere physics and myofilament mechanics

The sarcomere represents the fundamental contractile unit of the cardiomyocyte, and its biophysical properties ultimately determine the force-generating capacity of the myocardium. The dynamics of cross-bridge cycling—including attachment, power stroke, detachment, and reattachment of myosin heads to actin filaments—govern both the velocity of pressure development (dP/dt) and the peak pressure attainable during systole.

The discovery of the sliding filament mechanism by Huxley and colleagues37,38 in 1954 represented a watershed moment in muscle biology. Their observations of structural changes during contraction, subsequently refined through the proposed mechanism of force generation39, established the molecular basis for understanding cardiac contraction. The elegant experiments of Gordon et al. on the variation of isometric tension with sarcomere length in skeletal muscle provided the structural foundation for the Frank-Starling mechanism, demonstrating that force generation is maximal at sarcomere lengths of ~2.2 μm (~2.2–2.3 μm in cardiac muscle) where optimal overlap between thick and thin filaments occurs22.

Myofilament calcium sensitivity represents a critical modulator of contractility40,41. This sensitivity is influenced by multiple factors: intracellular pH (acidosis reduces troponin C affinity for calcium), the phosphorylation state of regulatory proteins (troponin I, myosin-binding protein C), sarcomere length itself, and lattice spacing between thick and thin filaments. This latter phenomenon contributes to the Frank-Starling mechanism through length-dependent activation42–45: sarcomere elongation increases the probability of cross-bridge formation, augmenting force generated for a given cytosolic calcium concentration.

Excitation–contraction coupling

The transition from an electrical event (action potential) to mechanical response (contraction) is mediated by a finely orchestrated cascade of calcium-dependent signals, known as excitation-contraction coupling (ECC). This process determines the amplitude, kinetics, and duration of the cytosolic calcium transient, parameters that translate directly into the temporal characteristics of the isovolumetric phases of the P–V loop. These mechanisms become visible in the loop through the slopes and durations of the two isovolumetric limbs: a larger and faster Ca²⁺ transient steepens the systolic pressure upstroke (higher dP/dt max) and raises peak isovolumetric pressure, whereas delayed Ca²⁺ reuptake prolongs isovolumetric relaxation (higher tau), delays mitral opening, and shifts and steepens the early-diastolic portion of the loop40. Calcium influx through L-type voltage-dependent channels triggers calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptors (RyR2)46. The efficiency of this coupling, termed “CICR gain,” is modulated by the physical proximity between L-type channels and RyR2 in dyadic structures and by the phosphorylation state of RyR2 channels themselves47,48. Alterations in CICR gain are implicated in the pathogenesis of arrhythmias and contractile dysfunction in various cardiomyopathies.

The discovery of calcium sparks by Cheng and colleagues47 established that calcium release occurs as discrete, spatially localized events that add up to produce the global calcium transient. This stochastic framework has important implications for understanding both normal beat-to-beat variability and pathological calcium handling, as alterations in spark properties (frequency, amplitude, spatial spread) can produce either enhanced or diminished contractility depending on the underlying mechanism. Termination of the calcium transient and consequent myocardial relaxation depend on calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (via SERCA2a, modulated by phospholamban) and extrusion across the plasma membrane (primarily via the sodium-calcium exchanger)40,41. Alterations in SERCA2a activity— observed in heart failure—slow the decline of the calcium transient, prolonging isovolumetric relaxation and compromising the rapid filling phase24,25. Such alterations are translated into changes in the P–V loop as an upward and leftward shift of the EDPVR—a higher pressure for any given volume—and as an alteration of the relaxation phase slope.

The molecular regulation of RyR2 has emerged as a critical determinant of cardiac function and arrhythmogenesis. The work of Marx and colleagues demonstrated that protein kinase A phosphorylation dissociates FKBP12.6 from the calcium release channel, increasing open probability and potentially contributing to diastolic calcium leak in heart failure48. Subsequent investigations by Bers49 have elaborated the macromolecular complex regulating RyR2 function, revealing a sophisticated regulatory network that integrates multiple signaling pathways to fine-tune calcium release.

Diastolic P–V loop function

Passive mechanics

A central role in passive sarcomere mechanics is played by titin, the largest protein known in the human body28. Titin acts as a molecular spring, conferring elasticity to the sarcomere and contributing significantly to myocardial diastolic stiffness50. The expression of different titin isoforms (N2B, stiffer; N2BA, more compliant) and their phosphorylation state dynamically modulate the EDPVR curve29–31. Titin hypophosphorylation, observed in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), contributes to the increased diastolic stiffness characteristic of this syndrome32. Recent advances in titin biology have revealed an unexpected complexity in its regulatory mechanisms33. Post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation by protein kinase A, protein kinase G, and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II, modulate titin-based passive stiffness in a site-specific manner. Oxidative modifications, particularly disulfide bond formation within the N2B unique sequence, further contribute to passive tension regulation under pathological conditions51,52. These findings have important therapeutic implications, suggesting that targeting titin modifications may represent a novel approach for modulating diastolic function.

Extracellular matrix and fibrotic architecture

The myocardial extracellular matrix (ECM) is not merely a structural scaffold but a dynamic compartment that profoundly influences tissue mechanical properties53–55. The collagen content, the degree of fibril cross-linking, and the spatial organization of the network—interacting non-additively—determine myocardial passive stiffness56,57 and, consequently, the position and curvature of the EDPVR. Myocardial fibrosis presents distinct patterns with different functional implications53–55. Replacement fibrosis, which substitutes necrotic cardiomyocytes as in myocardial infarction, creates non-contractile regions that reduce global ejection fraction and may serve as arrhythmogenic substrate. Diffuse interstitial fibrosis (reactive fibrosis), observed in hypertrophic remodeling and heart failure, increases diastolic stiffness more uniformly, shifting the EDPVR upward and compromising ventricular filling. The pioneering work of Weber and Brilla26 established the relationship between pathological hypertrophy and cardiac interstitium, demonstrating that fibrosis represents not merely a consequence of cardiomyocyte loss, but an active process driven by neurohormonal activation, particularly the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. This insight has had profound therapeutic implications, as interruption of these pathways has become a cornerstone of heart failure management.

Contractile reserve and myocardial bioenergetics

The heart is an organ of extraordinarily high metabolic demand, renewing its ATP pool every 10–15 seconds under basal conditions, and even more rapidly during stress14,15. The capacity for matching ATP demand and supply represents a fundamental determinant of contractile reserve and the sustainability of cardiac function over time.

Contractile reserve

Resting P–V loop analysis captures only the basal state of cardiac function, which may be insufficient for unveiling latent dysfunction or for prognostic risk stratification. Contractile reserve may be expressed as an upward/leftward shift or steepening of the ESPVR, reflected by an increase in end-systolic elastance (ΔEes) and by augmentation of preload-recruitable SW. Diastolic reserve, conversely, is assessed by the ability to accommodate increased venous return without excessive upward displacement or steepening of the EDPVR, indicating preserved chamber compliance. Failure to augment ESPVR or SW, or stress-induced upward/leftward displacement of the EDPVR, identifies impaired systolic or diastolic reserve despite apparently preserved resting loop geometry58,59.

A ventricle with ample reserve widens its loop and generates more SW at a similar end-diastolic volume when inotropic demand rises during exercise, dobutamine, or a post-extrasystolic beat, whereas exhausted reserve manifests as a fixed or flattening ESPVR under stress despite a normal resting loop. The bioenergetic substrate of this reserve—the ability to match ATP supply to abruptly increased demand—is considered next60.

Mitochondrial dysfunction—either primary (mitochondrial myopathies)61,62 or secondary (ischemia-reperfusion, chronic pressure overload)63,64—compromises oxidative phosphorylation and limits ATP availability. Such energetic limitation is translated in the P–V loop through multiple mechanisms: reduction of achievable peak pressure65 (downward shift of ESPVR), slowing of ATP-dependent active relaxation (alteration of isovolumetric phases), and the potential activation of alternative metabolic pathways with accumulation of cardiotoxic metabolites. The phosphocreatine system functions as an energy buffer, enabling rapid transfer of high-energy phosphate groups from mitochondria to utilization sites (myofilaments, ion pumps). The phosphocreatine/ATP ratio, measurable via phosphorus-31 magnetic resonance spectroscopy, represents a sensitive index of myocardial energetic state and correlates with prognosis in various forms of heart failure66,67. Cardiac metabolic flexibility—the capacity to utilize diverse substrates (fatty acids, glucose, ketone bodies, lactate) in response to systemic metabolic conditions—contributes to functional resilience. Alterations in this flexibility, observed in the diabetic heart and in heart failure, may compromise energetic efficiency and indirectly influence P–V loop morphology through modulation of contractile reserve. The emerging understanding of metabolic remodeling in heart failure has stimulated interest in metabolic modulators as potential therapeutic agents.

LV remodeling indicators

Mechanotransduction and remodeling programs

Cardiomyocytes can sense and respond to mechanical stimuli through a complex mechanotransduction apparatus. Integrins, costameric complexes, and mechanosensitive ion channels—operating as an integrated apparatus rather than parallel pathways—translate mechanical forces into biochemical signals that modulate gene expression, cellular metabolism, and ultimately the structural phenotype of the myocardium68,69. Chronic activation of hypertrophic signaling pathways (calcineurin-NFAT, MAPK, PI3K-Akt) in response to pressure or volume overload triggers geometric remodeling—concentric or eccentric hypertrophy—that alters the relationship between intraventricular pressure and wall stress (Laplace’s law). Such geometric remodeling is reflected in progressive modifications of the PV loop: ESPVR shift (alteration of intrinsic contractility), EDPVR modification (compliance change), and alteration of end-diastolic volume and pressure. These remodeling processes operate on timescales (weeks to months to years) vastly longer than individual cardiac cycles. Therefore, the “instantaneous” P–V loop represents a snapshot of a continuously evolving state, influenced by loading history and the adaptive (or maladaptive) response of the myocardium. Longitudinal assessment of P–V loop characteristics may thus provide insight into the trajectory of ventricular remodeling and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. As an example, chronic pressure overload from hypertension or aortic stenosis drives concentric hypertrophy: wall thickness increases, the chamber stiffens, and the loop narrows while the EDPVR displaces upward and leftward, and end-diastolic pressure increases, typically well before ejection fraction declines. In contrast, chronic volume overload from mitral or aortic regurgitation results in eccentric hypertrophy, with a rightward-enlarged loop and an initially rightward-shifted, flatter EDPVR35.

Beyond the P–V loop: regional phenomena not captured by a global representation

While the preceding sections explored the “in-depth” determinants of the P–V loop systolic and diastolic properties, the present section addresses phenomena that the loop conceals due to its global nature. In fact, the P–V loop model constitutes a homogeneous mathematical abstraction of a physically heterogeneous system, such that all state variables are expressed exclusively as functions of time, without dependence on spatial coordinates. Accordingly, while P–V loop models provide a description of global system dynamics, they cannot distinguish the multiscale spatial organization of fluid and tissue mechanics that underpin the complexity of cardiovascular function.

Spatial heterogeneity and regional mechanics

The global P–V loop implicitly assumes that all ventricular regions contract simultaneously and with equal magnitude. This assumption is violated in several clinically relevant pathological conditions70–72. In this sense, the regional dispersion of contractility, strain, and myocardial motion constitutes a set of hidden state variables that may explain why LVEF is preserved while symptoms worsen, i.e., the global P–V loop looks normal despite regional function impairment, leading to interventions that succeed or fail unpredictably. As an example of heterogeneity, global SW and ejection fraction may remain near-normal in patients with left bundle branch block, while segmental work is grossly redistributed and dissipated—an inefficiency invisible at the chamber-level loop, but readily detectable by segmental pressure–strain analysis73.

Integrating local (segmental) P–V loops with three-dimensional cardiac imaging could address the core limitation of the global LV P–V loop—its spatial blindness—by reconstructing regional mechanics, work and energetic inefficiency in a geometry-faithful way at defined cross-sections, while remaining clinically deployable74,75 (Fig. 2). However, this theoretical approach faces the practical difficulty of simultaneously generating multiple true segmental P–V loops in vivo. Conceptually, such a regional decomposition does not yield many miniature P–V loops but rather a family of segmental pressure–area (P–A) loops, in which regional cross-sectional area (or segmental strain) replaces global volume. The transmural distribution of function holds specific pathophysiological importance. The subendocardium, which is more vulnerable to ischemia, may exhibit early dysfunction undetectable by the P–V loop, but identifiable through strain analysis76,77. Similarly, infiltrative cardiomyopathies (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis) may produce characteristic regional dysfunction patterns that precede alterations in global function78,79.

Intraventricular fluid dynamics: vortices, pressure gradients, and hemodynamic forces

The blood within the ventricle is not a static fluid but a dynamic system with complex flow patterns that influence filling efficiency, kinetic energy transport, and mechanical wall stress80–85. The P–V loop, treating the ventricle as a compartment with uniform pressure, cannot capture these fluid-dynamic phenomena. During diastole, transmitral flow generates an anterograde vortex that develops at the ventricular apex and ascends toward the outflow tract80–82. This vortex maintains kinetic energy that is partially recovered during ejection, improving pumping efficiency. The original observation by Kilner and colleagues80 of an asymmetric flow redirection through the heart established the physiological importance of intraventricular flow organization. Alterations of vortex formation—observed in dilated cardiomyopathy, diastolic dysfunction, and after myocardial infarction—have been associated with reduced energetic efficiency and unfavorable prognosis, independently of parameters derivable from the P–V loop86–90. This evidence, however, derives largely from single-center observational cohorts, and confirmation in outcome-powered multicentre studies is still needed. The theoretical framework established by Bellhouse82 on fluid mechanics of model mitral valves and left ventricles laid the groundwork for understanding how valve geometry and ventricular shape influence filling dynamics. Pedrizzetti and colleagues81 have subsequently proposed that the vortex may serve as an early predictor of cardiovascular outcome, integrating information about chamber geometry, filling dynamics, and systolic function into a single flow-based marker. Intraventricular pressure gradients represent regional pressure differences within the ventricular cavity, generated by inertial forces of blood flow83–85. During the rapid filling phase, an apico-basal pressure gradient promotes active suction of blood into the ventricle (diastolic suction). Impairment of this mechanism, assessable via color M-mode Doppler or computational simulations, may precede alterations in globally measured filling pressures.

HDF represents an attractive recent construct quantifying momentum exchange between blood and ventricular walls83,84. Represented as a vector in the apico-basal/lateral-septal plane, HDF integrate information on chamber geometry, contraction synchrony, and flow patterns into a single vectorial descriptor. The methodological framework established by Pedrizzetti and colleagues83 for estimating intraventricular HDF from endocardial dynamics has opened avenues for clinical applications of this concept. HDF alterations have been reported in conditions of adverse remodeling, dyssynchrony, and diastolic dysfunction, often preceding modifications of conventional parameters and correlating with a propensity for progressive remodeling in exploratory cohorts73,91.

Confounding factors of interpretation

Valvular disease and wave phenomena

Interpretation of the P–V loop in the presence of valvular diseases requires caution, as alterations in valvular impedance and wave reflection phenomena may significantly confound the relationship between loop morphology and intrinsic myocardial state.

In aortic stenosis, outflow obstruction imposes a pressure overload that shifts the upper left corner of the loop upward (increased end-systolic pressure) and, over time, induces concentric hypertrophic remodeling. However, stenosis severity is not directly deducible from the P–V loop, as the transvalvular gradient depends on both orifice area and flow. Furthermore, similar P–V loops may correspond to very different myocardial states: a ventricle with compensated hypertrophy and preserved contractile function versus a ventricle with advanced fibrosis and exhausted contractile reserve92,93.

Mitral regurgitation hampers P–V loop interpretation through the presence of a low-impedance shunt (regurgitation toward the left atrium) that facilitates ejection and may mask underlying contractile dysfunction. Ejection fraction may overestimate effective myocardial function, and the P–V loop may appear normal despite progressive deterioration of intrinsic contractility.

Wave phenomena in the arterial system—wave reflections from branching points and resistance arterioles—influence the shape of the ventricular pressure waveform and, consequently, the temporal profile of the P–V loop. Separate analysis of reservoir and wave components (reservoir-wave separation) may provide information on the origin of pressure variations that the global P–V loop cannot distinguish. Wave phenomena can be quantified noninvasively through analysis of central arterial pressure waveforms obtained by applanation tonometry and transfer-function–based reconstruction, with subsequent application of reservoir–wave separation techniques.

Multi-chamber coupling and systemic interactions

The left ventricle does not operate in isolation but in constant mechanical and functional interaction with the left atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary circulation, and pericardium. Therefore, these interactions may significantly modify the P–V loop morphology without the presence of a primary left ventricular pathology.

Left atrial function is classically described as a triphasic process comprising reservoir function (blood accumulation during ventricular systole), conduit function (passive transfer during early diastole), and booster pump function (active atrial contraction during late diastole)94,95. These components play a fundamental role in modulating ventricular filling, such that alterations in atrial function may significantly impact diastolic hemodynamics independently of intrinsic left ventricular compliance96.

Ventricular interdependence, mediated by the shared interventricular septum and pericardial constraint, implies that right ventricular alterations may influence left ventricular geometry and function. A dilated right ventricle (as in pulmonary hypertension) may compress the left ventricle, shifting the septum leftward and reducing available end-diastolic volume. This phenomenon, particularly evident under conditions of low circulating volume, may dramatically alter the left P–V loop despite intrinsically preserved left ventricular function. A paradigmatic example is acute pulmonary embolism, in which abrupt right ventricular pressure overload causes septal leftward shift and reduced left ventricular filling. The resulting PV loop shows decreased end-diastolic volume and stroke volume despite preserved contractility, illustrating how ventricular interdependence can mimic left ventricular dysfunction in the absence of intrinsic myocardial impairment97.

Pericardial constraint becomes clinically relevant when pericardial fluid accumulation or pericardial thickening/calcification limits diastolic chamber expansion. Cardiac tamponade and constrictive pericarditis produce characteristic PV loop alterations—elevation and flattening of EDPVR, reduction of end-diastolic volume—that reflect external mechanical constraint rather than intrinsic alterations in ventricular myocardium98.

Temporal variability and control systems

On clinical grounds, P–V loop analysis assumes a steady state, treating a single cardiac cycle as representative of global ventricular function. This assumption may be inadequate in the presence of significant beat-to-beat variability or time-varying systemic influences. Autonomic modulation—variations in sympathetic and parasympathetic tone—simultaneously influences heart rate, contractility (inotropic effect), relaxation (lusitropic effect), and preload through capacitance vein properties99,100. These influences may produce P–V loop variations on timescales of seconds to minutes that complicate interpretation of isolated measurements. From a quantitative perspective, the beat-to-beat coefficient of variation of stroke volume and ejection fraction in patients in sinus rhythm is small—typically only a few percent—whereas in atrial fibrillation and other irregular rhythms it becomes larger and strongly dependent on the preceding R-R interval, a finding that supports averaging ejection fraction measurements over at least five cardiac cycles101. This spontaneous variability is therefore generally smaller than the deliberate, load-driven excursion induced by a preload reduction, and is best regarded as a secondary source of variability to be controlled for rather than a primary determinant of loop geometry.

Respiratory variability induces cyclic fluctuations in right and left preload through variations in intrathoracic pressure. During inspiration, increased venous return to the right heart and reduced transmitral pressure may transiently reduce left ventricular end-diastolic volume. This heart-lung interaction is amplified under conditions of hypovolemia, cardiac tamponade, or mechanical ventilation, and may be exploited diagnostically (pulse pressure variation, stroke volume variation) or confound interpretation of non-standardized measurements.

Baroreceptor reflexes and other short-term feedback mechanisms maintain pressure and flow homeostasis, continuously modulating parameters that influence the P–V loop. Attenuation of these reflexes, observed in aging, diabetes, and heart failure99 reduces response capacity to perturbations and may alter the relationship between resting P–V loop and response to stress.

Integrative framework: toward multiscale characterization

The previous discussion highlights how the P–V loop, while remaining a conceptual and diagnostic instrument of irreplaceable value, requires contextualization within a broader framework to allow accurate pathophysiological inferences and optimal clinical decisions102,103. Integration with advanced imaging

The combination of P–V loop analysis with imaging-derived parameters may overcome many of the discussed limitations. Quantitative methods are currently available which can be integrated into routine clinical practice to assess the contribution of each proposed mechanism to the resulting PV loop outcome (see Table in the Supplementary material). As an example, non-invasive myocardial work indices derived from pressure–strain loops allow bedside quantification of segmental work and have been used to guide patient selection for cardiac resynchronization therapy, providing a directly deployable route to test, in individual patients, the regional mechanics discussed above104,105.

Tissue characterization via cardiac magnetic resonance (T1/T2 mapping, extracellular volume quantification, late gadolinium enhancement) provides structural information that allows interpretation of P–V loop alterations in the context of underlying myocardial substrate54,55. Demonstration of diffuse fibrosis in a patient with upward-shifted EDPVR may identify the tissular mechanism of diastolic dysfunction; presence of a transmural scar in a coronary territory explains Ees reduction in the absence of global cardiomyopathy.

Intraventricular fluid dynamics analysis

Flow imaging methodologies—contrast echocardiography with particle image velocimetry, 4D flow magnetic resonance—and computational simulations (computational fluid dynamics) allow characterization of fluid-dynamic phenomena not captured by the P–V loop81,83–85. Integration of intraventricular pressure gradients, HDF, and vortex analysis with P–V loop parameters may open new avenues to identify patient subgroups with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms who might benefit from differentiated therapeutic approaches. As an example, pairing HDF or vortex analysis with a resting P–V loop can flag a patient whose loop-derived indices are still normal but whose diastolic suction and intraventricular flow organization are already deteriorated—identifying, for instance, early diastolic dysfunction or subclinical dyssynchrony that would justify closer surveillance or earlier device therapy rather than reassurance based on a normal loop73,81,83.

Arterial load decomposition

Effective arterial elastance represents a composite index that does not distinguish between different components of arterial load. Decomposition of aortic input impedance into its components—peripheral resistance, arterial compliance, characteristic impedance, timing and magnitude of wave reflections—might result in a more granular understanding of ventricular-arterial interaction. Such an approach may identify specific therapeutic targets (vasodilators versus agents reducing arterial stiffness) not inferable from P–V loop analysis alone. It should be acknowledged that the effective arterial elastance derived from the loop already embeds both steady and pulsatile arterial load, so the loop is not, strictly speaking, blind to pulsatility. The main value of impedance decomposition is to separate these contributions, a distinction that carries therapeutic implications: a patient whose elevated Ea is driven by high peripheral resistance (favoring use of vasodilators) differs substantively from one in whom conduit stiffening and early wave reflection predominate (favoring stiffness-directed strategies), and the single composite slope cannot resolve the two mechanisms.

Clinical implications

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)

HFpEF represents the paradigm of inadequacy of the global P–V loop as sole descriptor of cardiac function106–110. Patients with heart failure symptoms and an apparently normal P–V loop (preserved ejection fraction, normal ESPVR) present multiple alterations that escape conventional analysis: diastolic dysfunction (EDPVR shift), reduced contractile reserve (ESPVR normal at rest, but flattened during stress), regional mechanics alterations (reduced longitudinal strain), left atrial dysfunction, and inadequate chronotropism. The multiscale approach delineated in this Perspective could be the framework for HFpEF phenotypic characterization, and for identifying subgroups that might respond to targeted therapies.

Aortic stenosis

The decision for intervention in aortic stenosis cannot be based exclusively on transvalvular gradients or P–V loop morphology. Evaluation of underlying myocardial state—degree of hypertrophy, extent of fibrosis, presence of subclinical longitudinal dysfunction, contractile reserve—is crucial for operative risk stratification and for predicting reversibility of myocardial damage after valve replacement111,112. The concept of “extravalvular myocardial damage” in aortic stenosis emphasizes the necessity of integrating hemodynamic analysis with tissue characterization113.

Cardiomyopathies

Cardiomyopathy phenotyping benefits greatly from the multiscale approach. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), subcellular alterations (sarcomere dysfunction from contractile protein mutations) produce variable macroscopic phenotypes whose significance cannot be detected from the P–V loop alone114. Identification of regional fibrosis, subclinical diastolic dysfunction, dynamic outflow tract obstruction, and intraventricular fluid dynamic alterations informs arrhythmic risk stratification and therapeutic decisions115. Similarly, in infiltrative cardiomyopathies (amyloidosis), tissue characterization via magnetic resonance often precedes P–V loop alterations and permits earlier diagnosis and intervention116. In obstructive HCM, the cardiac myosin inhibitor aficamten proved superior to metoprolol monotherapy (MAPLE-HCM), whereas in symptomatic non-obstructive disease mavacamten did not improve peak oxygen uptake or health status compared with placebo (ODYSSEY-HCM), despite favorable changes in cardiac biomarkers and remodeling36. The contrast is instructive: where a loop-level abnormality—dynamic outflow obstruction—dominates the phenotype, unloading the sarcomere translates into symptomatic benefit, whereas in its absence symptom burden is driven by diastolic dysfunction, subendocardial ischemia and abnormal energetics, that is, by determinants residing beneath the loop that a pre-treatment P–V loop alone would not disclose.

Applicative perspectives

The P–V loop should be read as an emergent, chamber-level summary whose position, shape, and area are defined by determinants beneath it (sarcomere mechanics, excitation–contraction coupling, bioenergetics, and matrix remodeling) and constrained by phenomena beyond it (regional heterogeneity, intraventricular fluid dynamics, multi-chamber coupling, and reflex control). Once the “beneath” and “beyond” phenomena are understood, the P–V loop acquires direct implications for clinical practice across different contexts. The promise is greatest where biology is most heterogeneous. Patients with HFpEF, HCM, or aortic stenosis have profoundly different molecular substrates, disease trajectories, and therapeutic responses, yet they cannot be differentiated by conventional indices of left ventricular function. Computational reconstruction of the concealed multiscale organization from which the classical descriptors emerge has the potential to open new perspectives on the expression of an individual patient’s biology.

Conclusions

Toward a new paradigm of ventricular phenotyping

The historical success of the P–V loop arose from an era in which physiological variables could only be measured individually. Contemporary cardiovascular medicine is entering a fundamentally different landscape. Currently, advanced echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance, molecular imaging, computational fluid dynamics, wearable technologies, and systems biology generate multidimensional information describing the same ventricle from complementary perspectives. The challenge is therefore no longer acquiring but rather integrating all the physiological information. Future cardiovascular physiology will progressively reinterpret these classical descriptors as projections of a higher-dimensional latent biological state. Within this framework, the P–V loop represents one observable manifestation of an underlying multiscale ventricular state.
