From training to practice: a quality assurance evaluation of graduates’ experiences in a Canadian pathologists’ assistant program
Authors: Rilla J Wei, Nancy G Chan, Jina J Y Kum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPathologists’ assistants (PathAs) are allied health professionals who support surgical and anatomic pathology services through gross examination and dissection, intraoperative consultation, autopsy practice, and education. As the PathA profession in Canada continues to standardize and expand, university training programs must prepare graduates for high-stakes clinical practice while meeting evolving academic and professional expectations. Western University’s Master of Clinical Science-Pathologists’ Assistant program was the first Canadian program to receive accreditation from the National Accrediting Agency for Clinical Laboratory Sciences (NAACLS). The program has continued to evolve since 2007 as successive cohorts have entered training and practice. Despite increasing workforce demand, few published studies have examined how PathA program components align with graduate expectations and entry-to-practice experiences.MethodsThis study examined how Western University’s program supports trainees in developing as PathAs through its core curriculum of didactic courses, practical training through clinical rotations, and scholarly components. This mixed-methods evaluation used quality assurance data from the annual graduate student exit surveys and an alumni survey conducted as part of an internal program review.ResultsDescriptive quantitative analysis of Likert-scale items indicated high overall satisfaction and strong self-reported preparedness for entry to practice. Qualitative thematic analysis of open-ended responses, interpreted through the frameworks of communities of practice and professional identity formation, identified tensions between academic content and workplace applicability, site-to-site variability in supervision, and delays in timely, actionable feedback.ConclusionRetrospectively, this single-program review offers a practical, context-specific framework for aligning PathA curricula with workplace realities, with cautious relevance for other Canadian programs as the profession continues to expand.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09545-5.Keywords: Pathologists’ assistant, Program evaluation, Pathologists’ assistant education, Workforce development, Clinical education

Background

The profession of pathologists’ assistants (PathAs) emerged in 1969 with the establishment of the first PathA university-based training program at Duke University (Durham, NC) by Dr. Thomas Kinney, MD. Developed following the establishment of the first physician assistant program created by his colleague, Dr. Eugene A. Stead Jr, MD, also at Duke, Kinney described the PathA role as “an extension of the pathologist”. Unlike many allied health professions that function independently in supportive roles outside the direct practice of medicine, PathAs are trained to undertake responsibilities historically performed exclusively by physicians [1]. The scope of practice is documented by the American Association of Pathologists’ Assistants (AAPA) [2], where PathAs primarily assist pathologists in macroscopic examination (“grossing”) of surgical specimens, autopsies, and intraoperative consultations. As the profession matured, AAPA reported in 2024 that PathAs also perform a broad range of additional responsibilities, including frozen sections, administrative duties, CAP protocol inspection preparation, research, and, most commonly, teaching.

The only official route to becoming a PathA in the United States is to graduate from a National Accrediting Agency for Clinical Laboratory Sciences (NAACLS)-accredited program and pass the certification examination by the American Society for Clinical Pathology Board of Certification (ASCP-BOC). NAACLS is an international accreditation agency that accredits educational programs in clinical laboratory sciences and related healthcare disciplines in North America. There are currently four NAACLS-accredited Master’s programs in Canada: University of Alberta (Edmonton, Alberta), University of Calgary (Calgary, Alberta), University of Toronto (Toronto, Ontario), and Western University (London, Ontario) [3, 4]. Successful graduates earn a Master’s degree and are eligible to write both the ASCP-BOC examination and the Canadian Certification Council of Pathologists’ Assistants (CCCPA) certification exam [5].

As the PathA profession expands into a wider set of practical, administrative, and academic responsibilities, programs must keep pace with a scope of practice that has grown considerably more complex than in earlier training eras. However, despite the profession’s growth and continued expansion, few studies have evaluated how Canadian NAACLS-accredited programs have adapted their curricula over time or how program objectives align with students’ expectations and perceived outcomes. Existing literature documents the history and scope of PathAs but provides limited insight into the effectiveness of educational programs, the consistency of practicum experiences, and the impact of accreditation-driven reforms.

Western University provides an ideal case study for investigating how the PathA program has evolved. The program was launched in 2007, at a time when the PathA profession in Canada lacked formalized university-based training, and most practicing PathAs were “grandfathered” laboratory personnel trained on the job. Western University became the first Canadian institution to receive NAACLS accreditation in 2010, positioning it as a national leader in setting training standards and defining expectations for Canadian PathA education. Following accreditation, the program has undergone iterative, evaluation-driven refinements. The first systematic evaluation in 2014 (surveying 15 of 19 alumni) informed targeted changes. In the first year, both the Environmental Pathology and Microbiology courses showed limited relevance to surgical pathology practice, with the latter geared towards nursing students in another program. These were revised into autopsy-focused toxicology content and a pathogen-centred, case-based microbiology module co-taught by microbiologists and pathologists. Practicum feedback regarding inconsistent grossing techniques and variable preceptor support led to the introduction of standardized grossing templates, monthly didactic lectures, and mentor training workshops. Building on these reforms, the graduate education committee in 2017 removed the microbiology module altogether, replacing it with the Infectious Disease & Pathology course. By 2021, an internal self-study in preparation for accreditation renewal reported further refinements: a standalone end-of-year grossing seminar co-taught by anatomical pathologists and PathA preceptors.

As Western’s PathA program evolved into one of the largest NAACLS-accredited training programs in Canada (Fig. 1), its leadership had an undeniable influence on the profession's development in Canada [1]. Thus, evaluating its graduates’ experiences on the program’s quality of education provides timely and meaningful insight into how PathA education is responding to the changing demands of the profession.

Methods

Study context

Western University’s PathA program follows an intensive 22-month continuous curriculum that balances foundational knowledge with hands-on clinical training. The didactic first year requires students to achieve a minimum of 70% average in core courses before advancing. The second year begins with a two-month grossing orientation in the pathology laboratory at London Health Sciences Centre (LHSC). Students then complete surgical grossing and autopsy rotations at LHSC and three external placement sites. The program concludes with a two-month community hospital rotation. During rotations, students complete a supervised research project and an independent case report on a unique surgical specimen [6]. Both scholarly components are presented at the annual department-wide forum, with two students selected as delegates to the AAPA Annual Continuing Education Conference based on their case report presentations [7].

As incremental refinements have continued, they have solidified Western’s reputation as a leading PathA program. Given that this study evaluates current outcomes, it is necessary to situate the program within its trajectory of curricular refinement, illustrating how feedback and accreditation reviews have progressively shaped its present form.

Study design

This study used a convergent mixed-methods design to analyze two pre-existing quality assurance/quality improvement (QA/QI) datasets from separate program-developed instruments: the alumni survey (Supplementary Material 1) and graduate student exit surveys (Supplementary Material 2). The survey items and open-text prompts were designed and administered by the program director and departmental leadership for QA/QI purposes, independently of the present study. The original QA/QI activity was intended to support ongoing program improvement by collecting alumni and graduate student feedback on the relevance of coursework, clinical training, professional examination preparation, and transition to practice. Program leadership used this feedback to monitor whether training remained aligned with graduates’ future practice and to inform iterative curricular changes, including refinements of grossing education and clinical rotation emphasis.

In contrast, the present study reports a structured secondary analysis of anonymous data to examine graduate perceptions, program outcomes, and areas for improvement. The evaluation was guided by a constructivist epistemology, recognizing that participants’ perceived educational experiences are co-constructed through context, prior experience, and interpretation. This study was further informed by a relativist ontological stance, acknowledging that alumni and recent graduates may hold diverse and equally valid perspectives on their educational experiences.

Additional Comments

Additional Comments

Because the instruments were developed for internal QA/QI purposes rather than as standardized research measures, they were not formally piloted or psychometrically validated. However, they were retained because the instrument items aligned directly with the program domains and graduate outcomes under investigation.

Data analysis and theoretical positioning

Quantitative and qualitative datasets were analyzed separately and then integrated during interpretation and reporting. Alumni survey ratings identified broad program-level patterns. Exit survey responses were then used to contextualize these patterns, showing where graduates’ open-text accounts reinforced, expanded, or complicated the quantitative findings.

The student researcher conducted quantitative analysis of the alumni survey dataset using the above seven predefined domains. Responses were organized in Excel, and frequency and percentage distributions were calculated for each Likert-scale item (Supplementary Table 1–3). Percentages were calculated as n/N, where n represents the number of responses in a given category and N represents the number of respondents for that item. Diverging stacked bar charts were used to visualize rating distributions for Educational Experience and People and Culture. To summarize subdomains in Infrastructure and Support, 5-point Likert-scale responses were converted to deviation scores ranging from −2 to + 2 and then averaged. These scores were plotted on a radar chart as a compact visual summary for comparing subdomain patterns across different placement sites. Additional grouped bar charts displayed rating distributions for Western’s institutional infrastructure and Research Projects and Case Reports.

Open-ended responses from the exit surveys were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to thematic analysis with semantic coding [8, 9]. Responses were reviewed line by line and coded based on surface-level meaning; each comment could receive up to three semantic codes where relevant. Coding and theme development proceeded iteratively until no new codes were identified. Finalized themes were interpreted using sensitizing concepts from the literature on situated learning within communities of practice (CoP) and professional identity formation (PIF). CoP conceptualizes workplace learning as socially and contextually embedded, informing interpretation of how program structures and clinical environments shaped experiential learning [10, 11]. PIF was used to understand accounts of role internalization, professional belonging and identity development over time [12, 13]. Together, the two frameworks provided structured lenses for interpreting how program design and workplace learning shaped graduates’ expectations and perceived outcomes.

The analysis was conducted by the student researcher, who was in the second year of the program at the time of the study. This insider positionality provided contextual understanding of the curriculum, program culture, and terminology, facilitating a nuanced interpretation of participants’ feedback. However, this position was acknowledged reflexively as a potential source of confirmation bias. Several strategies were used to enhance methodological rigour and trustworthiness. First, coding decisions were cross-checked against verbatim excerpts to preserve fidelity to participants’ perspectives. Second, limited method triangulation was used by comparing different data sources across quantitative rating distributions and graduates’ open-text feedback. Because all data were survey-based and no independent coding or external validation was undertaken, triangulation served as a credibility-enhancing strategy rather than a validation procedure. Third, regular team debriefings were conducted to discuss emerging interpretations and reduce reliance on the student researcher’s individual assumptions. Finally, a coding audit trail was maintained to document analytic decisions.

Ethics reviews/consent

This project was reviewed by Western University’s Research Ethics Board, and it was determined that REB oversight is not required as the study met criteria for QA/QI (Project ID: 127,732).

First-year didactic courses

The first-year curriculum consists of a mix of undergraduate- and graduate-level courses that are pathology-specific, apart from the Anatomy & Embryology course, which is shared with the Master’s program in Anatomy and Cell Biology, Clinical Anatomy (Fig. 2). Owing to its extensive lectures and dissection hours, the Anatomy & Embryology course represents the heaviest workload, equivalent to two course credits.

Overall, first-year course ratings were generally positive or neutral, with most responses clustering at “meets expectations” or above and modest variation across courses. The three undergraduate-level courses clustered near the midpoint, suggesting that they generally met respondent expectations. Human Physiology showed 80% “meets expectations,” though interpretation is limited by the smaller response pool (N = 15), as students were exempted with transferable course credits. In the four graduate-level courses, Anatomy & Embryology received the strongest evaluations, with over 50% “above expectations” or “outstanding”. Infectious Disease and Functional Histology also performed well, showing relatively few negative responses and solid concentrations in the positive range. In contrast, Gross Seminar showed the most even distribution (SD = 1.13), with 34% positive and 24% negative ratings, indicating more heterogeneous perceptions among students.

Second-year rotations

The grossing practicum reflected a similar pattern of central clustering, with most experiences showing rightward divergence (Fig. 3). Orientation and Grossing Rotation at LHSC were mainly rated as meeting expectations. Among external sites, Mount Sinai was rated more favourably, with approximately half of the responses “above expectations”.

Autopsy rotations were consistently well-regarded, with most receiving > 60% positive ratings (Fig. 4). Autopsy rotation at LHSC received moderately positive ratings (median = 3.5), with some responses reaching "outstanding" (Fig. 4), while OFPS and Community Placements were particularly well received (median = 4; Figs. 4 and 5, respectively). In contrast, grossing and autopsy at SickKids showed the greatest variability, suggesting perceived gaps in teaching quality and/or the overall learning experience (Figs. 3 and 4).

Throughout second-year placements, students are supervised and regularly assessed on their professional and technical competencies. Structured evaluation components included Rounds (patient case review and discussion), Oral Examinations, and Competency Assessments (hands-on proficiency with different types of specimens). Students were generally satisfied with these evaluation components across autopsy and grossing, which showed predominantly positive ratings (Figs. 3 and 4).

Overall feedback from staff showed a greater leftward shift, with seven ratings of “below expectations” (Fig. 5). However, interpretation of this item requires caution due to potential ambiguity in item wording. Free-text comments suggest some students might have interpreted “staff” as the supervising PathAs they interacted with, while others interpreted it as the overall quality of routine feedback received. Overall ratings met or exceeded expectations for most items (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Some gaps do exist, and the program will continue to strive for alignment between student expectations and instructional experiences.

Effectiveness of staff in students’ training

Overall impressions of program personnel were highly positive, with all staff groups receiving ratings clustered around “meets expectations” to “outstanding”, with no “poor” ratings (Fig. 6). The Program Director, Medical Director, and Administrative Staff were rated particularly strongly, with approximately 80% of positive responses, reflecting consistently high satisfaction with academic leadership and administrative support. PathAs (non-preceptors) showed slightly more mixed feedback despite still leaning positive, suggesting greater variability in day-to-day mentorship interactions. Collectively, the results indicate broad confidence in instructional and supervisory staff, with only minor variations in perceived effectiveness across roles.

Research projects and case reports

The program’s scholarly components, including the research project and case report, were generally rated as satisfactory or better (Fig. 9). Over half of respondents indicated neutral attitudes regarding their research project and case report experiences, while approximately one-third reported satisfaction. Only 16% of respondents rated the research project as not useful, and no respondents selected the lowest two ratings for either component. Similarly, 54% agreed that their research project was useful in preparing them for a PathA career, reflecting perceived educational value (Fig. 10). Notably, no respondents rated the case report at or below 2, suggesting a more positive reception compared with the research project (Fig. 9).

Career intention & outlook

Across both datasets, findings demonstrate strong self-reported employment outcomes and early career alignment among respondents. The open-ended exit survey responses were overwhelmingly focused on entering the workforce as PathAs, echoing the quantitative findings from the alumni survey in Table 2. The consistency in findings suggests that graduates perceived the program as supporting early workforce entry through professional preparation and network support.

Immediate plan post-graduation

In the alumni survey, 81% of respondents secured employment before completing their community rotation, and nearly all were employed before convocation (Table 2). This rapid job acquisition is echoed in the exit surveys: almost every graduate (34/37, 92%) confirmed their immediate plan following graduation was to enter full-time employment as a PathA, with several noting active job search or imminent start dates.

Consistent with the alumni survey’s geographic distribution (81% Ontario; 19% other provinces), reported workplaces in the exit survey were concentrated in Ontario teaching and community hospitals (Table 2). Practice locations included LHSC, Mount Sinai Hospital (Toronto, ON), Windsor Regional Hospital (Windsor, ON), Bluewater Health (Sarnia, ON), Brightshores Health System – Owen Sound Hospital (Owen Sound, ON), Kingston General Hospital (Kingston, ON), and Peterborough Regional Health Centre (Peterborough, ON), alongside placements outside Ontario, such as Nanaimo Regional General Hospital (Nanaimo, BC) and St. Paul’s Hospital (Vancouver, BC). Forensic appointments were also frequent, with multiple graduates holding roles, including part-time and casual, at OFPS; a small number described dual appointments (e.g., an additional part-time and casual role at Markham-Stouffville Hospital (Markham, ON)).

A minority described transitional plans prior to stable employment. For example, short-term travel or studying for professional certification exams alongside initial employment (Supplementary Table 4). Collectively, these responses indicate that most graduates transition directly into relevant employment shortly after program completion, with limited interprovincial mobility reported.

Long-term career plans

Graduates’ long-term aspirations indicate sustained commitment to the profession, with strong emphasis on continued skill development, leadership, and educational contribution. The majority intended to remain in PathA practice, while nearly half anticipated progression into senior, managerial, or teaching roles. Some specifically envisioned a path to “move up in the field” into a lead PathA or laboratory manager. Frequent mentions of teaching also reflected a desire for professional contribution to clinical education (Supplementary Table 5).

Several graduates emphasized continued technical proficiency, citing goals such as performing additional autopsies, completing ASCP-BOC and CCCPA certification exams, or working with more complex specimens. Professional growth through continuing education and conference participation was also highlighted. Beyond personal development, some respondents expressed interest in advancing the profession nationally, through involvement in program development, student training, and advocacy for the PathA role. A few responses integrated personal milestones, including financial stability, work-life balance, and family development, illustrating a holistic conception of career success. Only two respondents planned to leave the PathA role to pursue a career as a pathologist (Supplementary Table 5).

In contrast with the exit surveys, the alumni survey reported that 21/32 (66%) remained in the PathA role, 4/32 (13%) were undecided, and 7/32 (22%) wanted to transition out of the profession (Table 3). This discrepancy suggests that students, immediately following graduation, are more likely to exhibit a strong commitment to the PathA field and articulate specific career development plans. Over time, however, the higher proportion of alumni wanting to transition out of the profession or remaining undecided suggests that career intentions may evolve as graduates gain seniority and encounter new professional opportunities or demands.

Program contribution to graduates’ future plans

In response to the exit survey question regarding how the graduate program contributed to future plans, graduates frequently provided positive feedback (Supplementary Table 5). They emphasized the program’s credentials, practical skills training, knowledge base, clinical exposure, and professional connections as important contributors to employment and transition into PathA practice.

Themes were developed from semantic coding of open-text responses to the question “In your opinion, how did the graduate program in Pathology help you regarding your future plans?” (N = 37) and the additional comment section, respectively (Supplementary Material 2). Four strength-focused themes described perceived program contributions to graduates’ future careers: (i) workforce readiness and employability; (ii) skills and knowledge development; (iii) clinical exposure and practical training; and (iv) credentials and identity in a professional community. Three additional themes were developed from the supplementary comment section: (v) uneven clinical support and its impact on professional identity formation; (vi) perceived misalignment between curriculum content and workplace applicability; and (vii) site-to-site variation undermining continuity of clinical learning. For each theme, n represents the number of unique participants whose comments contributed to that theme.

In aggregate, Themes 1–4 suggest that graduates perceived the program as supporting their transition into practice. The overall tone was one of strong satisfaction and appreciation, with only isolated remarks concerning program cost or calls for expanded feedback mechanisms. Additional comments (N = 22) further amplified the program’s central strengths—clinical exposure, strong faculty and preceptor support, and grounded credentials. These endorsements corroborate the program’s role as a professional “launchpad” and emphasize the social and mentoring mechanisms (preceptors, pathologists, administrators) that scaffold legitimate peripheral participation in situated learning [14]. These comments provided more heterogeneous details on perceived program contributions and effectiveness. Overall, the qualitative results reflect strong affirmation while identifying recurrent gaps in Themes 5–7 that warrant targeted improvements in curriculum structure to support participation, belonging, and identity formation.

Workforce readiness and employability (n = 20)

Respondents most frequently framed the program’s value in terms of immediate career translation. Over half of the responses described the program as providing the practical pathway to employment (e.g., “This program has created and shaped my career path and opened up many opportunities for jobs”; “Without this program, I would not be viewed as employable in hospitals”; “It opened a door to immediately start my career as a PathA”). Participants emphasized readiness for hiring processes and the ability to compete for positions across clinical settings. This corresponded to the 81% job acquisition prior to finishing community rotation (Table 2). Conceptually, employability was framed as graduates’ perceived transition from learner status to legitimate workforce participation, rather than solely job acquisition. From a PIF perspective, employability also functioned as an external marker of role legitimacy, although it should not be interpreted as objective evidence of clinical competence.

Skills and knowledge development (n = 11)

A substantial subset of responses highlighted acquisition of theoretical knowledge and professional skills through the curriculum (e.g., “It gave me the necessary skills, knowledge base, and experience to secure a position in the field”; “It prepared me to become a pathologists' assistant by teaching me the theory and the practical knowledge required for the profession”). Graduates credited the curriculum sequencing as key to developing both theoretical understanding and procedural competence. As one participant wrote, “The coursework in the first year gave me a good knowledge base before heading into the second year of practical experience”. Others highlighted that the curriculum’s design allowed an “easy transition” into employment and the ability to perform PathA duties “from day one”. The structure of classroom-to-practicum learning was widely viewed as a strength supporting seamless workforce readiness.

Clinical exposure and practical training (n = 10)

Experiential learning, including rotations, practica, grossing experience, and autopsy exposure, emerged as a distinct and highly valued element (e.g., “The program provided me with the hands-on experience necessary to perform almost all of the duties of a Pathologists' Assistant”; “The rotations were great at allowing us to develop our skills and prepare us for the profession”; “Having the option to be at leading Canadian centers like LHSC (for gross and autopsy), OFPS (for autopsy), Mt. Sinai and Sick Kids (for perinatal/pediatric autopsy) was invaluable”). Many respondents explicitly connected experiential placements with interview preparedness and immediate workplace performance.

Graduates described developing technical proficiency and adaptability, which contributed to their perceived readiness for professional practice. Clinical exposure functioned as a form of legitimate peripheral participation, facilitating progression from observation toward supervised participation in routine PathA work. This finding reinforces the centrality of authentic clinical exposure in PathA education: practical placements not only consolidate technical skills but also provide access to the social, procedural, and supervisory routines through which PathA practice is learned.

Credentials and identity in a professional community (n = 14)

Multiple responses emphasized the formal credential and immediate credibility the program conferred to employers (e.g., “It has provided me with extensive training … and the designation of being an alumnus of an accredited program”; “Gave me the certification required to work this job”; “It provided a degree, skills and the knowledge to make me a competitive applicant for PathA positions”). Credentialing contributed to PIF by providing external recognition, role legitimacy, and a sense of membership within the PathA community.

In addition, the learning environment began an early fostering of professional socialization, encouraging graduates to make use of the program’s alumni network that is close-knit, supportive, and inclusive. Respondents framed the program status and dedicated personnel as both gatekeeping mechanisms for professionalism and as enablers of future academic options (e.g., eligibility for teaching roles as preceptors). Taken together, these accounts suggest that the transition from student to professional was supported not only by technical training but also by recognition, belonging, and engagement in a PathA community.

Uneven clinical support and its impact on professional identity formation (n = 7)

This theme captures how interpersonal, pedagogical, and cultural variation across clinical placements shaped trainees’ experiences of belonging, role clarity, professional norms, and progressive autonomy. While most respondents described supportive situated learning environments, a minority reported tensions that undermined trust and belonging and, in turn, affected the perceived continuity of their learning.

Some concerns reflected transitional issues. Earlier cohorts reported limited flexibility for “time off” during second-year rotations, which respondents acknowledged has since been addressed. More substantively, several graduates perceived the program as still developing relative to long-established PathA programs in the United States, particularly with respect to training depth and consistency across sites. Variability in supervisory engagement emerged as a key issue. Some respondents noted that “…at least one teacher in each rotation site did not seem very invested or interested in teaching", leading to frustration and diminished learning. One account from the LHSC rotation described concerns about professionalism and confidentiality, including coarse language or discussion of student performance in a public, uncontrolled environment, which eroded trust in preceptors and PathA staff, as the student deemed their behaviours “extremely unprofessional and inappropriate”. Others reported instructional gaps related to professional standards, such as CAP protocols and AAPA staging guidelines, which were sometimes learned independently rather than systematically taught. While some students appreciated preceptors who balanced supervision with their own “real-world” duties, others experienced this independence as premature, revealing a tension between self-directed learning and adequate guidance. Through CoP and PIF lenses, autonomy appeared most constructive when paired with trust, role modelling, guidance, and timely feedback. When these supports were inconsistent, autonomy risked becoming unsupported independence, weakening students’ sense of belonging, legitimacy, and continuity within the professional community.

Perceived misalignment between curriculum content and workplace applicability

Applied vs. academic emphasis (n = 9)

Student feedback reflected ongoing tension between academic content and workplace applicability. While earlier issues (e.g., limited benign exposure; no pre-second-year grossing course) have been addressed, recent comments still pointed to gaps in curriculum balance and examination preparedness. Several students described the second-year practicum as “autopsy heavy”, noting that the emphasis exceeded typical Canadian PathA responsibilities. These concerns were particularly evident among the 2020 cohort, whose training coincided with COVID-19 disruptions and the initial implementation of additional case report and gross-round responsibilities. Some respondents described feeling “overwhelmed at times", particularly while balancing research, oral examinations, clinical training, and newly introduced academic tasks alongside periods of reduced access to structured teaching and in-person rotations.

Students also called for more applied teaching that integrates grossing decisions with pathology interpretation and surgical context (e.g., margins, common procedures). Several comments referenced online and visual learning tools, reflecting both post-COVID adaptations and student interest in flexible, interactive approaches, such as gross description modules or specimen atlases [15, 16]. A recurring critique was an overreliance on templates and protocols with limited explanation of underlying rationales. As one respondent noted, “Students are often quizzed on how many sections to take according to LHSC grossing protocols rather than background pathology knowledge”. Course-specific suggestions included: (i) condensing Infectious Disease content while strengthening applied safety teaching; and (ii) adding practical histology learning outside the classroom with laboratory tours to better contextualize downstream workflows.

Research project relevance to PathA practice (n = 3)

As shown in Table 4, 68% of respondents selected options in which they preferred the research project not to be mandatory (58% case report mandatory only; 10% neither mandatory). Exit survey comments provided possible explanations for this preference. Some graduates’ feedback addressed concerns about the relevance of their research projects. For example, one noted that their project “does not pertain directly” to PathA training, while another indicated that it took time away from more central learning on grossing and cancer staging protocols [17]. The preference for mandatory case reports, while implemented in the current program curriculum, did not replace the research project as an alternative to the extent some graduates desired. However, perceptions of the research project requirement may have varied depending on the focus and perceived practice relevance of each student’s project.

Site-to-site variation undermining continuity of clinical learning (n = 5)

While respondents valued the breadth of clinical exposure, several described inconsistencies in placement organization, supervision, and feedback that disrupted continuity of learning and limited opportunities to improve in real time. From a CoP lens, site-to-site variability disrupted students’ access to consistent participation, feedback, supervision, and progressive autonomy across clinical learning environments.

A first source of variability was practicum organization and communication. Some students characterized rotations as disorganized, noting that some PathAs “…did not know what to do with us when we got there” and suggesting that communication between sites could be strengthened. A second concern involved feedback processes on One45, the program’s online clinical evaluation and feedback platform [19]. Although structured evaluation was viewed positively in principle, students described feedback as delayed and therefore less actionable. One noted that they “…frequently received feedback too long after a rotation had been completed”, while others emphasized that end-of-rotation feedback left little time to implement changes, recommending weekly or biweekly check-ins.

Students also reported uneven access to teaching and educational materials during off-site placements, and unclear communication regarding additional learning opportunities. One respondent observed that some pathologist-led grossing sessions occurred only after repeated student requests, leading students to “educate each other” through peer presentations. Finally, supervisory inconsistency further complicated learning when students were paired with senior PathAs carrying substantial administrative duties, resulting in “constant interruptions” and reduced real-time guidance. Respondents recommended pairing one student with one preceptor for a defined period to enable more structured formative mentoring and longitudinal tracking of skill development.

Discussion

Across health professions, how trainees experience clinical learning is shaped not only by curriculum content but by how reliably placements provide role clarity, supervised participation, and actionable feedback, especially when learning is distributed across multiple sites. In PathA specifically, the role has evolved within broader “physician extender” workforce trends, with longstanding emphasis on workplace-based skill development and continuing expansion of responsibilities [20]. This evaluation examined how graduates perceived Western University’s PathA program in relation to classroom learning, clinical training, scholarly work, credentialing, and transition to practice. Interpreted through CoP and PIF, the findings suggest that graduates valued the program most when training supported supervised legitimate participation, progressive autonomy, timely feedback, and recognition within the professional community. Consistent with accreditation expectations that embed clinical learning, scholarship, and professional development, we focus on three program development themes: (i) clinical rotations and early professional socialization; (ii) scholarly training and its alignment with practice; and (iii) credentialing as part of ongoing professionalization [21]. We do not attempt to address every evaluation domain in equal depth.

Clinical rotations and early professional socialization

Clinical exposure can be understood as a form of legitimate peripheral participation. Students acquire not only technical competencies but also the tacit norms of communication, workflow management, and professional judgment that define everyday pathology practice [10, 11, 14]. This process also supports PIF, as learners gradually internalize a profession’s values, norms, and self-concept and begin to “think, act, and feel” like members of that profession [13].

In our results, early professional development was most visible in accounts of: (i) feeling trusted and included within the team; (ii) access to consistent role-modelling and training; and (iii) feedback that was specific and actionable enough to support progressive autonomy. However, these conditions were less consistently present at LHSC and external sites. Variability in supervisory engagement, ambiguity in learning expectations, and delayed or inconsistent feedback sometimes reduced graduates’ confidence in calibrating their performance and professionalism to the PathA role. This pattern aligns with recent qualitative work on introducing PathAs into a surgical pathology department, which described an initial “testing” period and early uncertainty that improved when role expectations and communication pathways were clarified [22].

A distinctive challenge for PathA education is that rotation experience is shaped by a small workforce, highly variable case mix, and shift-based staffing, making “who teaches”, “how often”, and “what gets observed” less predictable. In this context, variability is not only an educational problem; it is also a systems problem. When expectations are implicit or transmitted informally, students may experience the rotation as contingent on individual supervisors rather than as a program-level design. This is precisely the type of inconsistency that accreditation standards try to buffer against by requiring programs to sequence learning experiences, permit increasing responsibility only after demonstrated competence, and use evaluation systems frequently enough to provide timely indications of progress [21].

Graduates’ comments suggest several targeted, feasible improvements. First, feedback could shift from primarily summative end-of-rotation evaluations toward a more predictable formative cadence. For instance, structured weekly or biweekly check-ins and mid-rotation reviews could support a “feed-forward” system, allowing learners to implement changes during the rotation rather than after it. Second, although a strict one-to-one preceptor-student model may be difficult in settings where PathAs work rotating shifts and manage service demands, the program could consider more structured near-peer PathA teaching opportunities. Students undergoing rotations are likely to encounter newly hired or early-career PathAs who can formalize small recurring touchpoints with them. For example, brief recurring case-based discussions could help students understand the rationale behind protocols. Importantly, this can also reinforce the non-traditional educator role that many PathAs assume early in practice [2, 23], while addressing the “open issues” noted in the Pathologica study: role integration improves when responsibilities and expectations are made explicit and supported institutionally [22].

Scholarly training and alignment with practice

Workforce data suggest that PathAs’ scope remains predominantly clinical, centred around grossing, specimen documentation, and intraoperative support, while research activities are comparatively peripheral and often limited to tissue procurement or banking [23, 24]. Within this practice context, the rationale for a required scholarly component in PathA education may be less about producing “researchers” and more about cultivating evidence-informed practitioners who can interpret literature, participate in QA/QI, and communicate reasoning within clinical teams, capacities that are also reflected in NAACLS curriculum expectations and broader health professions education [21, 25]. A recent scoping review similarly identified research and scholarship as “a mainstay strategy” for building individual clinicians’ capacity across allied-health disciplines [10].

Our findings add nuance to this rationale: graduates’ perceived value of the scholarly requirement depended heavily on proximity to PathA practice. Through a PIF lens, scholarly work appeared most meaningful when students could recognize it as part of becoming a PathA, such as when projects were embedded in laboratory workflow, service priorities, quality improvement, or educational practice. When topics felt remote from day-to-day PathA responsibilities, the research project could be experienced as competing with entry-to-practice preparation rather than supporting professional development. This aligns with broader nursing and allied health literature arguing that research training supports capacity-building, but feasibility and workplace alignment shape whether clinicians experience it as beneficial or burdensome [25, 26].

A key implication for program development is to strengthen alignment mechanisms while maintaining scholarly rigour. Prioritize practice-embedded QA/QI/service topics co-developed with sites and supervisors, and explicitly frame research outputs as usable artifacts for the workplace (e.g., workflow evaluation, grossing competency tools, teaching modules) [27]. This preserves the educational intent (critical appraisal and improvement mindset) while increasing perceived relevance, an approach consistent with findings that collaborative student research can be experienced positively when projects are well supported and connected to clinical priorities [26].

Future direction: the next stage of professionalization

Findings on licensing examinations indicated a large proportion of graduates had not written either the ASCP-BOC (32%) or the CCCPA examination (39%) at the time of response (Table 5). Among those who had written or intended to write, most reported adequate preparedness, yet a meaningful minority (33% for ASCP-BOC; 16% for CCCPA) indicated insufficient readiness. Graduates’ mixed exam participation and variable perceived preparedness may reflect structural ambiguity rather than curricular deficiency alone. When certification is not uniformly required across workplaces, motivation and timing may vary even among otherwise satisfied graduates.

As the PathA profession continues to standardize in Canada, educators and program leaders should be attentive to the relationship between accreditation, certification, and emerging licensure expectations. According to AAPA, certification and ultimately licensure serve as the primary mechanism for protecting public safety, defining professional scope, and distinguishing qualified practitioners in environments where non-certified personnel may perform overlapping tasks [28]. CCCPA materials state that post-January 2025 certificate eligibility is limited to graduates of a NAACLS-accredited Master’s PathA program, with an expectation to apply and complete the process within a 5-year post-graduation window [29]. From a PIF perspective, this policy change may strengthen the connection between accredited training, certification, and graduates’ sense of professional legitimacy. Explicit program-level guidance on certification pathways may help graduates to navigate certification options and consolidate their professional identity more effectively [28].

For Western University’s program, a practical response could include three steps: (i) making certification planning visible (without making it coercive), including clear advising on recommended timelines and a “decision map” for CCCPA and ASCP pathways; (ii) integrating lighter-touch exam preparation into second-year practicum; and (iii) tracking certification examination attempts and pass rates as part of continuous program improvement.

Framed this way, credentialing becomes part of “the next stage of professionalization” rather than an additional burden. Aligning rigorous training with evolving certification expectations can strengthen workforce mobility and clarify professional identity in a still-developing Canadian PathA landscape.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the survey instruments were originally developed for QA/QI program evaluation and NAACLS accreditation purposes, rather than as formally validated research instruments. Although the surveys used were appropriate for the present study as they addressed program quality, graduate experience, perceived preparedness, and areas for improvement, the instruments were not psychometrically validated. As such, findings should be interpreted as context-specific program evaluation data, rather than as results derived from validated measures of educational quality or graduate competence.

Second, despite the high response rates (91% for the alumni survey and 88% on average for exit surveys), the survey instruments relied on self-reported outcomes from a small total number of responses, due to the limited annual cohort size of six. Measures such as satisfaction, perceived preparedness, and expectations met provide important insight into graduates’ experiences and perceived program impact; however, they are subjective and may be influenced by recall bias, response bias, and social desirability bias inherent to self-report survey methods [30, 31]. High satisfaction, perceived readiness, and rapid employment should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinical competence, workplace performance, or long-term professional success. In addition, the use of online surveys limited opportunities for probing, clarification, or deeper exploration of responses that may have been possible through interviews or focus groups. A broader 360-degree evaluation, including employers, preceptors, faculty, and workplace supervisors, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of program outcomes.

Third, although several strategies were used to strengthen methodological rigour and reduce bias during interpretation, independent coding, inter-coder reliability assessment, and external validation of themes were not undertaken. Given the student researcher’s insider position within the program, the potential influence of researcher positionality and confirmation bias cannot be excluded.

Finally, this study examined a single Canadian PathA program with a distinctive institutional and historical context. Western University’s position as Canada’s first NAACLS-accredited PathA program may have shaped graduate experiences in ways that limit direct transferability to other Canadian programs. The study period captured cohorts trained during an evolving phase of program development, during which educational support and resources, clinical placement expectations, and professional standardization of the PathA role in Canada continued to mature. Several contextual factors should, therefore, be considered when interpreting the findings in relation to other Canadian programs, including institutional history, accreditation status or timing, curriculum structure, clinical placement networks, and regional workforce pathways.

Conclusion

This review of Western University’s PathA program suggests generally positive perceived alignment between curricular intent, learner experience, and early professional transition. Quantitative findings show consistently high self-reported satisfaction across core domains, including didactic coursework, clinical rotations, supervisory support, and institutional infrastructure, with nearly 90% of alumni reporting that the program met their expectations. Qualitative data from exit surveys contextualized these trends by highlighting graduates’ appreciation for broad clinical exposure, meaningful mentorship, and an accredited credentialing structure that facilitated workforce entry.

These observations align with evidence from allied health education, where program quality is closely linked to structured workplace immersion, alignment between curriculum and real-world practice, and supportive supervisory frameworks [32, 33]. Nonetheless, as recent analyses of the North American PathA educational landscape reveal, strong graduation, employment, and certification outcomes are increasingly expected among accredited programs rather than sufficient markers of program distinctiveness and effectiveness [5]. In this context, the value of the present findings lies in their detailed account of how graduates perceived Western’s PathA curriculum, clinical training, professional identity development, and transition-to-practice preparation. Future evaluations would be strengthened by incorporating objective or externally assessed measures, such as certification examination performance, employer or preceptor feedback, workplace-based assessments, and longitudinal tracking of career progression.

For Western’s program, this raises a central question: what sets it apart in a field where foundational outcomes are already uniformly strong? One graduate captured this tension with clarity: “We should be viewed as pioneers … a learning opportunity … to make changes that could result in a more wholesome education for its students”. This sentiment reflects the symbolic weight Western carries as Canada’s first NAACLS-accredited PathA program. Its pioneering position brings both pride and pressure: not only to meet accreditation standards, but to lead in curricular refinement, consistency across clinical placements, and the maturation of PathA education within the Canadian context. Western University’s distinctiveness may lie not merely in supporting graduates’ transition into practice, but in its aspirational mandate to contribute to the developing model of a balanced, academically rigorous, and professionally integrated PathA curriculum in Canada. Its ongoing challenges and iterative improvements therefore become part of the program’s identity, reflecting the work of shaping an emerging profession while maintaining high standards of educational coherence and institutional leadership.
