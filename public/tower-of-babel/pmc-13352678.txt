Prenatal diagnosis and genetic counseling of a paternally inherited chromosome 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication in a Chinese family
Authors: Wei Wang, Lin Zhan, Lu Xu, Pei Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCopy number variants (CNVs) represent a significant source of genomic diversity, encompassing both benign and pathogenic variations. The accurate interpretation of CNVs identified during prenatal diagnosis is crucial for appropriate genetic counseling and management. Introduction

Copy number variants (CNVs) are an important source of normal and pathogenic genome variations [1]. Recent widespread application of chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) in diagnosis in China revealed many new CNVs. CMA also known as “molecular karyotyping” is a high-resolution genetic testing technology widely used in the diagnosis of genetic disorders and developmental abnormalities. With the help of bioinformatics tools, an abundant number of chromosome variations could be identified by CMA [2]. Overall, clinical significance of these new CNVs needs to be still some considerations [3–5]. The literature on 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication is rare, which is a challenge for genetic counseling. Here we report the prenatal diagnosis and genetic counseling of a paternally inherited chromosome 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication in a Chinese family.

Methods

Patients and molecular analysis

In 2024, a 35-year-old, gravida 2, para 1, woman underwent amniocentesi at 18 weeks of gestation due to advanced maternal age. Her husband was 36 years old. They have a three-year-old daughter who is currently in good health. There was no family history of birth defects or genetic diseases.

Cytogenetic analysis of the cultured amniocytes revealed a normal karyotype of 46,XY. Subsequent CMA was performed using the Affymetrix CytoScan 750 K chip, which includes 550 k non-polymorphic markers and 200 k SNP markers distributed throughout the human genome with an average marker density of approximately 1 per 4 kb. This platform enables genome-wide detection of chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletions/microduplications (CNVs), and regions of homozygosity (ROH), with a typical resolution of 100 kb for duplications and 50 kb for deletions; CNVs smaller than these thresholds are generally not reported. Additionally, the platform can identify copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity suggestive of uniparental disomy (UPD) [2, 5]. CMA on uncultured amniocytes detected a 762-kb chromosomal microduplication in the region of 5p13.3p13.2 (Uncertain Significance, VUS), which is to be reported as arr[GRCh37] 5p13.3p13.2(33,405,597_34,167,786) × 3 (Fig. 1).

CMA detected a 762-kb microduplication in the region of 5p13.3p13.2: arr[GRCh37] 5p13.3p13.2(33,405,597_34,167,786) × 3

Then we performed conventional karyotyping and CMA using the samples from the peripheral blood of three family members (the father, mother and old sister of the fetus) and Quartet whole-exome sequencing (WES) on the family. The Novaseq6000 platform (with the mode of 150-bp pair-end sequencing, Illumina, San Diego, USA), was used for sequencing the genomic DNA from this family. We aligned the sequencing reads to the human reference genome (hg38/GRCh38) using the tool of Burrows-Wheeler Aligner. Their karyotypes were normal. The Quartet-WES results showed no pathogenic gene variation but CMA and WES showed the father had the same microduplication as the fetus (Fig. 2). We performed a comprehensive physical examination of the three family members and failed to identify anything abnormal. After genetic counseling, the parents decided to continue the pregnancy. Fetal ultrasound and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 26 weeks of gestation showed no structural anomalies or intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). Following genetic counseling, the parents elected to continue the pregnancy.

The results of the family analysis revealed that the father carries the same 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication as the fetus

The infant was born at 40 weeks via spontaneous vaginal delivery. Birth weight, length, and head circumference were all within normal ranges. Apgar scores were 9/10/10. Physical examination at birth and at 6 months revealed no dysmorphic features or developmental delays. Gross motor, fine motor, and social milestones were age-appropriate.

Discussion

This region encompasses seven protein-coding genes: ADAMTS12, AMACR, C1QTNF3, RXFP3, TARS1, SLC45A2, and C1QTNF3-AMACR. According to the ClinGen database, none of these genes are currently known to be triplosensitive, and no clear dosage sensitivity has been established.

ADAMTS12 encodes a secreted metalloproteinase that degrades cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) and remodels the extracellular matrix in cartilage, tendon and tumour stroma. Up-regulation promotes tumour invasion and chemo-resistance in gastric and pancreatic cancers, whereas loss-of-function protects against cartilage erosion in osteoarthritis [6].

AMACR (α-methylacyl-CoA racemase) catalyses the racemisation of branched-chain fatty-acid and bile-acid intermediates, an essential step for mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation. Its over-expression supports lipid metabolism in colorectal and prostate cancers and is widely used as an immunohistochemical cancer marker [7].

C1QTNF3 is a secreted glycoprotein of the C1q/TNF super-family that inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis and stimulates chondrocyte proliferation. By repressing the non-canonical NF-κB pathway it exerts anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitising effects; circulating levels are inversely correlated with obesity and metabolic syndrome [8].

RXFP3 encodes a Gi/o-coupled receptor for relaxin-3 highly expressed in the CNS, where it modulates stress responses, feeding behaviour and circadian rhythm. Ligand binding decreases cAMP and downstream Ca2⁺/MAPK signalling; altered gene dosage has been tentatively linked to anxiety and metabolic traits, but no triplosensitive phenotype has been reported [9].

TARS1 (threonyl-tRNA synthetase) charges tRNA-Thr in the cytoplasm and is crucial for translational fidelity. Heterozygous missense mutations cause axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth type 2N, yet haploinsufficiency is well tolerated and no dosage-sensitive pathology has been associated with three copies; over-expression might only mildly increase global translation rates [10].

SLC45A2 is a melanosomal membrane transporter that regulates tyrosine uptake and lumenal pH, thereby determining eumelanin/pheomelanin ratio. Expression level correlates with skin/hair colour, but no pathological effect of over-expression has been documented and the gene is not considered dosage-sensitive [11].

C1QTNF3-AMACR read-through transcript: This rare fusion transcript spans C1QTNF3 and AMACR, potentially encoding a chimeric protein retaining parts of the CTRRP3 domain and the complete AMACR catalytic core. Its physiological relevance is unknown; RNA-seq databases show very low abundance compared with the individual parental transcripts and no dosage-sensitive or disease association has been reported [12].

Collectively, the current literature provides no evidence that an extra copy of any of these seven genes would confer a high probability of a clinically recognizable triplosensitive phenotype. A single prior case report described a de novo microduplication of this locus in a child; however, no additional patients with comparable microduplications have since been documented. Iourov et al. (2015) described this de novo 5p13.3p13.2 duplication in a child with developmental delay, feeding difficulties, and visual impairment [13]. However, a detailed comparison between this case and our family reveals important differences that may explain the phenotypic variability. The duplication reported by Iourov et al. spanned approximately 994-kb (chr5:33,130,008–34,124,081, GRCh37/hg19), which is larger than the 762-kb duplication observed in our family. Although both duplications overlap in the core region and both involve these seven genes: ADAMTS12, AMACR, C1QTNF3, RXFP3, TARS1, SLC45A2, and C1QTNF3-AMACR, the larger duplication includes additional genomic segments that may contain other regulatory elements or genes with potential dosage sensitivity. Furthermore, the duplication in the Iourov case occurred de novo, whereas in our family it was inherited from a phenotypically normal father. The de novo occurrence often carries a higher risk for pathogenicity compared to familial inheritance. Finally, Iourov et al. noted chromosome instability in their patient, which was not observed in our family. These differences suggest that the 5p13.3p13.2 duplication may exhibit variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance, and that the presence of additional genetic or environmental modifiers may influence clinical outcomes.

The term “chromosome 5p13 duplication syndrome” has been used to describe larger duplications encompassing the 5p13 region, often associated with developmental delay, intellectual disability, and dysmorphic features. However, these reported duplications are typically much larger (> 5 Mb) and involve numerous genes beyond the 5p13.3p13.2 interval described here [14]. The present 762-kb microduplication is significantly smaller and more discrete than the classic 5p13 duplication syndrome regions. Based on current evidence, this CNV should not be considered part of the classical 5p13 duplication syndrome. Instead, it represents a distinct, smaller CNV whose clinical significance requires individual assessment.

In the family reported here, the fetal microduplication was inherited from the phenotypically normal father, and no additional anomalies were detected during prenatal surveillance. This case posed considerable challenges in genetic counseling. As a variant of VUS, the 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication had an unclear prognosis, and the limited published data made it difficult to provide precise recurrence risk or outcome predictions. In daily practice, such uncertainty often leads to parental anxiety and complex decision-making. We addressed this by integrating multiple data sources: the normal phenotype of the carrier father, normal prenatal imaging, and the absence of pathogenic variants on WES. After thorough counseling and discussion with their physicians, the parents elected to continue the pregnancy. The infant is now 6 months old and remains clinically unaffected. Of course, we will continue to monitor his growth and development.

This case underscores the challenges in prenatal CNV interpretation, particularly for VUS. The favorable outcome highlights the importance of integrating multidisciplinary data-including parental testing, WES, and imaging-into genetic counseling. While CNVs often raise concern for severe disorders, our findings support the notion that not all variants confer significant risk.

Conclusion

We present a case of paternally inherited chromosome 5p13.3p13.2 microduplication with a normal phenotype at 6 months. This report emphasizes the need for cautious interpretation of prenatal CNVs, particularly VUS, and the value of comprehensive follow-up. Continued monitoring of the child’s development is warranted to detect late-onset effects. Combining banding karyotype analysis, CMA, WES, and imaging enhances prenatal diagnostic accuracy and informs counseling [15].
