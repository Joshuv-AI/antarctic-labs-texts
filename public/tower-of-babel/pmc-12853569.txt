Evaluation of the functional outcome measures of recurrent clubfeet after posteromedial or posterior release treated using the Ponseti method: a preliminary study
Authors: Jing Ding, Zhen-Zhen Dai, Li Zhao, Zhen-Kai Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimThe Ponsetimethod has been popularized for treating recurrent idiopathic clubfoot and, more recently, recurrent clubfoot following posteromedial (PMR) or posterior (PR) release. A retrospective study was performed to determine the functional outcome in Ponseti treatment of recurrent idiopathic clubfoot after PMR or PR. The study also aimed to determine whether clinical scoring systems (Pirani and Dimeglio scores) could be predictive for the initial numbers of casts, Achilles tenotomy, ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, relapse, and functional outcomes following the Ponseti method of treatment.MethodsThis was a retrospective study of 17 consecutive patients (27 feet) treated with the Ponseti method for recurrent clubfoot after PMR or PR. Clinical charts were reviewed for sex, laterality, age at initial visit to our institution, age at the first surgery, initial number of casts, undergoing a percutaneous Achilles tenotomy (PAT) or not, clinical presentation, Pirani and Dimeglio scores, range of motion of ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, the International Clubfoot Study Group (ICFSG) rating system, and recurrences following the Ponseti method of treatment. We conducted descriptive statistical analyses between patients who rated as excellent, or good and fair in the ICFSG rating system following the Ponseti method of treatment. Outcome and demographic data were analyzed using an independent student’s t-test for means and Fisher exact test for proportional data.ResultsDimeglio and Pirani scores were not helpful in predicting whether or not a PAT was performed (p > 0.01), ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment (p > 0.01), and relapse after initial treatment (p > 0.01). There was no statistical correlation between functional outcomes and the number of serial casts (p > 0.01), undergoing a PAT or not, (p > 0.01), ankle dorsiflexion (p > 0.01), and relapse (p > 0.01) after initial treatment. We found a statistical relationship between the initial clinical severity and the number of casts required for clubfoot correction (p = 0.005), and a significant statistical correlation between the initial scores of Pirani (p = 0.001) and Dimeglio (p < 0.001) with the final scores of the ICFSG rating system.ConclusionsPonseti treatment for recurrent idiopathic clubfoot following PMR or PR achieved a satisfactory functional outcome in most patients. The Dimeglio and Pirani scoring systems provided a prognostic value for the initial number of casts and functional outcomes for clubfeet recurring after PMR or PR treated by the Ponseti method. The better functional outcomes were attributed to less initial severity of the previously operated clubfeet.Level of evidenceLevel IV, therapeutic study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09408-y.Keywords: Clubfoot, Ponseti method, Posteromedial or posterior release, Recurrence, Functional outcomes

Introduction

Congenital clubfoot, which can be isolated or can occur as part of other disorders, is one of the most common birth defects [1]. Over the last decades, the Ponseti method for clubfoot management has been widely accepted for primary, neglected, or relapsed deformity correction, and the high success rates and superior functional outcomes have been largely reported around the world [2–5]. In 2005, a nationwide clubfoot project focusing on the expansion of Ponseti method was initiated in China [6]. Before the acceptance of the Ponseti method, surgical treatment was widely practiced, often as a posteromedial release (PMR) or posterior release (PR). PMR or PR is an invasive surgical procedure that has multiple long-term disadvantages, including premature arthritis, joint stiffness and pain [7, 8]. Additionally, there is a possibility of recurrence, as well as residual deformities, after surgical treatment of clubfoot deformity, and the solutions to these problems present daunting challenge in the field of pediatric orthopaedics. These feet are deformed and rigid, and the scarred soft tissue makes the posteromedial part of the foot contracture resistant to treatment [9]. The available treatment for recurrent clubfoot after the surgical procedure has included mastering the serial casting, repeating PMR or PR, talectomy, triple arthrodesis, supra-malleolar osteotomy, and using the circular and/or hexapold external fixators [9]. Many parents show their resistance to the recommendation for the repeated surgery for their children and are eager for the protocol of less invasive treatment after relapse. One study reported that the Ponseti method succeeded in treating clubfeet recurring after PMR and achieved full morphological correction in most patients [10]. However, it is still unclear whether complete morphological correction can represent full functional correction. At present, there is no study evaluating functional outcomes of recurrent clubfeet after PMR or PR treated using the Ponseti method. Therefore, since 2009, we began using the Ponseti technique in patients with recurrent deformities following PMR or PR and evaluated the functional outcomes at the latest follow-up.

The purposes of this research aims to explore functional outcomes of recurrent clubfeet after PMR or PR treated using the Ponseti method and its influence factors from the following aspects: (1) to evaluate the correlation between the initial Pirani or Dimeglio scores with the initial numbers of casts needed, with or without a percutaneous Achilles tenotomy (PAT), ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, relapse or not, and functional outcomes following Ponseti method of treatment, and (2) to evaluate the correlation between excellent and good or fair in the International Clubfoot Study Group (ICFSG) rating system with initial scores, initial numbers of casts, undergoing a PAT or not, ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, and relapse following Ponseti method.

Materials and methods

After institutional review board approval, we reviewed all the patients treated with the Ponseti method for recurrent clubfoot after PMR or PR from our database. The patients with idiopathic clubfoot treated by the Ponseti method all had at least one procedure of PMR or PR performed elsewhere before this treatment and came to our clinic for the further treatment. All the recurrent clubfeet were then treated with Ponseti method by a single pediatric orthopedic surgeon (L.Z.).

Exclusion criteria were as follows: the patient (1) had postural, arthrogryposis, syndromic, or neurological clubfoot; (2) had not yet completed his/her course of the Ponseti treatment in our hospital for recurrence clubfeet after PMR or PR; (3) with feet that were previously surgically treated with other than or addition to the PMR or PR, including tibialis anterior tendon transfer (TATT), Ilizarov technique, et al., and (4) lost to follow-up.

Recurrence was defined as a presentation with one of the four components of clubfoot deformities, or a combination of them, including forefoot adductus, equinus, hindfoot varus, cavus, and the need for further treatment, after initial complete correction [11, 12]. Regardless of the severity of the recurrence, we offered the same method of serial gentle manipulation and multiple casting followed by PAT when necessary, as described by Dr. Ponseti. The time interval of cast changes was 1 or 2 weeks in the older children (older than 2 years old). All casts are long leg cylindrical casts for the lower limbs. If the patient has bilateral recurrent clubfoot, we will simultaneously perform bilateral Ponseti method correction. When the deformities were completely corrected, bracing was typically prescribed for full-time use for the first 3 months and then for 14 to 16 h per day until the child was approximately 5 years old. At least one year of wearing the brace was required after complete correction in recurrent cases identified after 5 years of age. All parents were instructed on the importance of complying with the brace treatment. Beginning with the application of the brace, they were advised to return to the clinic 3 months later, then every 6 months until the age of 5 years. One or two additional years were required for follow-up after achieving correction in recurrent cases identified after the age of 5 years. The parents were advised to bring the child to our center when there was any sign of relapse.

Information obtained included sex, laterality, age at initial visit to our institution, age at the first surgery, initial number of casts, undergoing a PAT or not, clinical presentation, the score of Pirani and Dimeglio, range of motion (ROM) of ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, ICFSG rating system scores, and recurrences following the Ponseti method. All data were collected from assessments conducted prior to recurrent clubfoot after PMR or PR treated using the Ponseti method. The abbreviations of the clinical presentation were recorded, e.g. forefoot adductus (A), equinus (E), hindfoot varus (V), and cavus (C). We considered each foot an independent observation in bilateral cases because scar formation and severity in deformity differed among the involved feet. According to van Praag’s study [13], we also defined a well-corrected foot as a straight lateral border, ankle dorsiflexion of ≥ 10 degrees, and hindfoot in neutral or mild valgus after initial treatment. Besides, we assessed the ROM of ankle dorsiflexion.

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive statistical analyses between patients that rated as excellent, or good and fair in the ICFSG rating system following the Ponseti method. As the Pirani and Dimeglio scores were measured initially and the ICFSG rating system was measured at the final stage after correction, the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve was used to determine empirical cut-point estimation of the Pirani score and the Dimeglio score by excellent or good and fair in the ICFSG rating system. An independent student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test were used to compare the continuous variable data. Chi-square test or Fisher exact test were used to compare categorical variable data. The value of p < 0.01 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed at our institution with STATA/SE version 15.1 for Windows (Copyright 1985–2017 Statacorp LLC).

Results

We reviewed the records of 17 patients treated with the Ponseti method for recurrent clubfoot after PMR or PR from January 2010 to December 2014 (Fig. 1). Of 17 patients with 27 clubfeet (case shown in Figs. 2 and 3), 4 (23.5%) patients were female and 13 (76.5%) were male. There were 6 (35.3%) patients with unilateral clubfeet, and 11 (64.7%) with bilateral clubfeet. Only one patient with bilateral clubfeet had the recurrence on one side. Of the 27 feet, 13 were on the left and 14 were on the right feet. The age at the first visit was on average 6.12 years (range, 1–15 years). The analysis of the age at the first surgery (PMR or PR) included 13 patients because of missing data regarding the age at the first surgery for 4 patients. The age at the first surgery was on average 18.2 months (range, 2–100 months). The empirical cut-point estimation of Pirani score was 2.25, the sensitivity at cut-point was 0.81, and the specificity at cut-point was 0.91. The empirical cut-point estimation of Dimeglio score was 8.5, the sensitivity at cut-point was 0.94, and the specificity at cut-point was 0.64. There was no statistical difference of baseline characteristics, including sex, age, side, and follow-up.

Equinus recurred in 24 feet, while hindfoot varus recurred in 22 feet. Forefoot adduction recurred in 25 feet, while cavus recurred in 6 feet. The combination of the above four deformity components was found to recur in 6 feet. The mean initial Pirani score was 2.65 (range, 1.0 to 5.0). The mean initial Dimeglio score was 9.96 (range, 7 to 14). Four patients with bilateral clubfeet had different initial Pirani scores on the both sides, while 7 patients with bilateral clubfeet had different initial Dimeglio scores on the both sides. Patients required an average of 7.7 casts (range, 3 to 13) before PAT or upon completion of treatment. However, only one patient required a different number of casts for bilateral clubfeet. Fifteen feet did not have hindfoot equinus deformity noted after serial casting. Twelve clubfeet underwent PAT after Ponseti serial casting. After manipulation, none of the 27 clubfeet had dynamic supination with gait or underwent tibialis anterior tendon transfer (TATT). All of the 27 feet achieved a straight lateral border and a hindfoot in neutral or mild valgus position after initial treatment.

Ankle dorsiflexion ROM after initial treatment was on average 10.4 degrees (range, 5–20 degrees). Of the 27 feet, 18 (66%) feet achieving correction had equal to or more than 10 degrees of ankle dorsiflexion. All parents adhered to correct application of an abduction brace following our instruction. The average follow-up was 4.7 years (range, 3–7 years) from the initiation of brace wearing. Subsequent relapses occurred in 10 clubfeet and repeated Ponseti casting was used for complete correction for 6 recurrent clubfeet. Two patients with bilateral clubfeet had recurrence of the deformity on one side. Two clubfeet (in one patient) were corrected with external fixator after relapse. One patient (both recurrent clubfeet with EAV) gave up the additional treatment after relapse.

No patient had acute complications from the manipulation, serial casting, or surgical procedures. The mean final score in the ICFSG rating system was 8.3 (range, 3 to 24). Seven patients with bilateral clubfeet had different final ICFSG scores for each side. Eleven feet were rated as excellent (range, 0–5), while 14 feet were rated as good (range, 6–15) and 2 feet were rated as fair (range, 16–30). Therefore, we conducted descriptive statistical analyses between patients that rated as excellent, or good and fair in the ICFSG rating system following the treatment of Ponseti method.

The patient characteristics by ICFSG rating system are shown in Table 1. We observed that the initial Pirani (p = 0.001) or Dimeglio (p < 0.001) scores could be used to predict final scores in the ICFSG rating system. We observed no correlation (p = 0.037) between the number of casts and final scores in the ICFSG rating system. The final scores in the ICFSG rating system did not correlate (p = 0.130) with whether or not a PAT was performed. We also observed no correlation (p = 0.044) between ankle dorsiflexion ROM after initial treatment and final scores in the ICFSG rating system. Subsequent relapses also did not correlate (p = 1.000) with final scores of the ICFSG rating system.

Discussion

Congenital clubfoot was treated with either nonsurgical or surgical methods. Surgical procedures include PMR, PR, TATT, and Ilizarov technique. PMR or PR traditionally involved the procedures releasing the subtalar, medial, and posterior soft-tissue contractures. Many studies have shown poor long-term function after surgical correction of clubfoot [14–18]. These feet were frequently painful, rigid, and had the limited range of foot and ankle motion. Relapses were usually treated with further surgery, which was demonstrated to have the worsened long-term functional outcomes. Current conservative treatment methods for clubfoot have included the Ponseti method, French functional or physiotherapist method, Copenhagen method, and Kite method [19–25]. In many centers, the minimally invasive Ponseti technique has gradually gained acceptance as the gold standard for idiopathic clubfoot treatment due to its efficacy and safety [24]. Long-term results during the adulthood have shown that the Ponseti group has fared better than the surgery group because of the advantages such as greater strength, increased ROM observed at the physical examination and during gait, and fewer occurrence of arthritis [25]. Moreover, the Ponseti method has recently been popularized for non-idiopathic or recurrent clubfoot. Several centers have shown the Ponseti method could be applied in the treatment of clubfeet recurring after PMR and achieve full morphological correction in most patients [10, 21]. Meanwhile, we also have applied the Ponseti method in the treatment of clubfeet recurring after PMR or PR and achieved a satisfactory functional outcome in most patients.

The findings from our study showed that the Dimeglio and Pirani scores were not helpful in indicating whether or not a PAT was performed, ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment, and relapse after initial treatment. There was no statistical correlation between functional outcomes and the number of serial casts, undergoing a PAT or not, ankle dorsiflexion, and relapse after initial treatment. However, we found a statistical relationship between the initial severity and the number of serial casts required for clubfoot correction, as well as a significant statistical correlation between the initial Pirani and Dimeglio scores with the final scores of the ICFSG rating system. There was no difference between the Pirani score and Dimeglio score in predicting the number of casts, undergoing a PAT or not, ankle dorsiflexion, relapse, and functional outcomes. The scoring systems of Dimeglio and Pirani have proven track records with regard to intra-observer and inter-observer reliability, but their prognostic value remains unclear [26]. Gao R et al. [22] showed the prognostic value for both the Dimeglio score and the Pirani score remains questionable, at least during the early stages of treatment.

Although the Pirani and Dimeglio scores were originally developed to assess the severity of untreated clubfeet in infants and not designed to evaluate previously treated clubfeet [22], we chose to use Pirani and Dimeglio scores as a way to identify the severity of deformity in these previously operated feet. The scores were of some misleading impacts for assessing the mid-foot or hindfoot in previously operated clubfeet, as the palpation of the calcaneus and even the medial or posterior crease were almost scored as 0. Just as in our group’s previous study [27], perhaps a quadratic or cubic relation between the initial scores and the cast numbers, and more casts were needed when there were initial higher scores of the Pirani or Dimeglio rating system. Others have reported that an initially high Pirani score suggests the need for greater cast numbers to achieve correction and probably a need for PAT [28]. Agarwal’s study [29] also reported that the cast numbers for correction of clubfoot were influenced by both the initial Pirani score and age. However, as Chu noted [26], both the initial Pirani and Dimeglio scores had a low correlation with the cast numbers required by Ponseti management. As Jauregui’s study reported [30], Ponseti treatment statistically and clinically improved passive ankle dorsiflexion in recurrent idiopathic clubfoot. The results from our study showed a mean dorsiflexion of 10.4 degrees, with 66% achieving dorsiflexion ≥ 10 degrees after initial treatment for recurrent clubfoot following PMR or PR. However, there was no correlation between initial severity or functional outcomes and ankle dorsiflexion after initial treatment. A published study [31] noted that initial severity of idiopathic clubfeet was an outcome predictor at age 2 years. The findings from our study indicated that both Dimeglio and Pirani scoring systems were of prognostic value for functional outcomes, and the poorer functional outcomes were attributed to more initial severity of recurrent idiopathic clubfoot after PMR or PR. As reported by Goriainov [32], higher Pirani scores were associated with the late recurrence and the total Pirani score and the hindfoot contracture score were shown to be statistically significant in predicting potential relapse. However, this phenomenon was not observed in our study.

Contrary to the general belief that the older the child, the more difficult the treatment, we observed no correlation between age and final functional scores. Such a finding was also described by other studies. Surgical releasing procedures were recommended for recurrent clubfeet in children up to age 10 years [33, 34]. In our study, 3 patients (5 feet) aged more than 10 years achieved a satisfactory functional outcome, with 1 foot rated as excellent and 4 feet rated as good.

A recurrent clubfoot deformity is a universal problem that has been noted in 26% to 48% of patients undergoing Ponseti treatment [35–40]. As shown by our study, up to 37% of clubfeet recurring after PMR or PR experience relapse after initial correction with the Ponseti method. Possible causes for relapse include the inherent contractile nature of the soft tissues in clubfoot deformity [41], soft-tissue scarring [10], the details of clubfoot management [42], and a significant discrepancy between reported brace wearing rates and actual wearing rates [43]. The TATT is a crucial component of the Ponseti method and serves as an effective strategy for correcting recurrence, with favorable mid-term and long-term outcomes reported. Currently, many patients with relapse receive a TATT as a primary approach to correct the recurrent deformity [44, 45]. None of the 27 clubfeet in our study had dynamic supination with gait and none underwent a TATT. As we understand, the indication for the procedure of TATT is that the delayed supination of the forefoot happens during the swing phase of the gait cycle. In our cases, such signs did not appear, so no cases required TATT.

In our study, 17 patients (27 clubfeet) relapsed after PMR or PR, the mean age at the initiation of Ponseti treatment was 6.1 years (range, 1–15 years), the average Dimeglio score was 10 (range, 7–14), the mean number of casts required for the correction was 7.8 (range, 3 to 13), 12 feet required a PAT, and the final score in the ICFSG rating system was on average 8.3 (range, 3–24). Accordingly, as seen in our group’s previous study [46], 17 patients (25 clubfeet) relapsed after Ponseti treatment, the mean age at the beginning of repeat Ponseti method was 3.6 years (range, 13 months to 5 years), the average Dimeglio score was 9.0 (range, 8–14), the mean number of casts required for the correction was 5.4 (3 to 9), 2 feet required a PAT, and the final score in the ICFSG rating system was on average 4.6 (range, 0–9). Comparing the above data, it impressed us that the recurrent clubfeet after PMR or PR in older patients with the similar initial severity required more numbers of casts and were more likely to require the procedure of PAT for the correction of relapsed deformities and the good functional outcome, nevertheless, which were not comparable to that in treating the recurrent clubfeet following the non-surgical method. These might be due to the destruction of the ligament, capsule, and cartilage around the ankle and subtalar joints by PMR or PR [47].

Limitations of this study include the single center and the small sample size, although the patients came from across the country and were treated using Ponseti technique by an experienced orthopaedic surgeon (L.Z). This was a retrospective study, and the use of extensive surgical procedures to treat clubfoot deformity has greatly declined because of the wide adoption of the Ponseti technique. This study serves both as a historical record and as an analysis of past data illustrating the effectiveness and related factors of using the Ponseti method to treat cases of deformity recurrence after extensive soft tissue surgery. It encourages us to initially consider non-surgical treatment with the Ponseti method, even for cases with deformity recurrence following extensive soft tissue release surgery. Finally, an average follow-up of 4.7 years in our study is short, but we followed the patients until at least until the age of 7 years. We plan for an annual follow-up on the patients after Ponseti treatment.

Conclusions

In our study, the satisfactory functional outcome was obtained in most of our patients with the recurrent idiopathic clubfeet following PMR or PR by using Ponseti method. The Dimeglio and Pirani scoring systems were applied effectively to assess the initial severity of previously treated clubfeet and provided a prognostic value for the number of required of casts and functional outcomes. Although the treatment of the recurrent clubfeet from the previous surgery were somewhat less successful than in untreated or previously Ponseti non-surgically-treated clubfeet, these preliminary results are hopefully of significant value in encouraging the orthopaedic peers to apply the Ponseti method in this type of condition and may contribute to decreasing the number of patients requiring more extensive surgical procedures in the recurrent clubfeet from the previous PMR or PR surgery.
