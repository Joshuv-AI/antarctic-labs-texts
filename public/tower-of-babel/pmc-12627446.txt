Comparative analysis of convective heat transfer in novel spiral tube geometries for enhanced thermal performance
Authors: Sobhan Farhadi, Younes Shekari, Hamid Ansari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research explores the convective heat transfer performance of three configurations: a conventional spiral tube, a Spiral Flow Reverser (SFR), and a Semicircular Spiral Flow Reverser (SSFR). The study aims to determine their relative thermal efficiencies across varying Reynolds numbers (Re). A combination of numerical simulations and experimental analysis was employed to evaluate the thermal performance of the three designs. Key parameters, such as the Thermal Performance Ratio (TPR), were assessed under a range of Re to identify peak efficiency conditions. The SFR and SSFR demonstrated significantly improved heat transfer compared to the traditional spiral tube. At a Re of 17,207, the SFR achieved a TPR of 1.24, while the SSFR attained a superior ratio of 1.45. The SSFR consistently outperformed all other configurations across all scenarios, underscoring its effectiveness in enhancing heat transfer. These findings highlight the potential of spiral flow reversal and semicircular designs in optimizing heat exchanger performance.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

There are two main strategies to enhance heat transfer: passive and active methods1,2. Passive methods improve heat transfer without external power or input, typically by modifying the geometry or surface of the heat exchanger or by introducing additives to the fluid. These modifications aim to increase the heat transfer area, induce turbulence, or boost thermal conductivity. A common passive approach involves altering the shape of the heat exchanger, such as using spiral tubes instead of straight ones.

Spiral tubes, or coils, are widely used in thermal engineering applications such as heat exchangers, electronic cooling systems, and chemical reactors. They offer superior heat transfer performance and require less space compared to straight tubes. The heat transfer mechanisms within spiral tubes are more complex due to the secondary flows and the curvature-induced centrifugal forces that significantly influence the flow field. Traditional explanations for the enhanced heat transfer in spiral tubes focus on their larger surface area, compact design, and unique shape. For instance, studies have linked the increased heat transfer efficiency in spiral tubes to the presence of Dean vortices, a secondary flow pattern caused by centrifugal forces first observed by Dean in the 1920s3. These vortices, characterized by their counter-rotational configuration, can enhance fluid mixing within the tube, leading to improved heat and mass transfer4.

Several studies have explored the performance and characteristics of spiral tubes. Ho and Wijeysundera.5 studied a spiral-coil heat exchanger with horizontal finned tubes for cooling and dehumidification, developing a theoretical model validated by experiments. Their findings showed that spiral-coil heat exchangers outperform straight tube heat exchangers. Naphon and Wongwises6 investigated a spiral coil heat exchanger designed for cooling and dehumidification, using a mathematical model aligned closely with experimental data. Jayakumar et al.7 provided insights into fluid flow and heat transfer in helically coiled tubes, revealing that heat transfer is greater in helical coils than in straight pipes. Sasmito et al.8 performed computational analyses on heat transfer within in-plane spiral ducts with various cross-sections, while Dong et al.9 conducted experiments on heat transfer and flow resistance in a spiral twisted tube heat exchanger, noting enhanced performance compared to conventional designs. Anish et al.10 and Li et al.11 explored the applications of spiral coil heat exchangers in thermal storage and solar ponds, respectively, emphasizing the potential of spiral designs in renewable energy systems. Zeinali and Neshat12 and Rezaei et al.13 further examined the thermohydraulic and exergoeconomic characteristics of spiral tube heat exchangers, contributing to the understanding of their efficiency and optimization. Flow reversers represent a more recent and innovative approach to improving heat transfer in spiral tubes. Unlike standard spiral tubes, which rely on the natural flow patterns induced by their geometry, flow reversers actively change the direction of flow within the tube, leading to significant improvements in heat transfer. Saxena et al.15 introduced the Coiled Flow Inverter (CFI), a device designed to invert flow direction by altering the centrifugal force in helically-coiled tubes, as shown in Fig. 1.

Coiled flow inverter, introduced by Saxena et al.16.

This innovation showed a 20–30% performance improvement over conventional spiral tubes under smooth flow conditions, as confirmed by Kumar et al.16. Further studies by Kumar et al.17 experimentally verified that CFIs demonstrate superior heat transfer at both low and high Reynolds numbers compared to spiral tubes. Additionally, Mansour et al.18 presented a coiled flow reverser, depicted in Fig. 2, which reverses the flow direction by 180 degrees. This design further enhances heat transfer and mixing, particularly at higher Reynolds numbers.

Coiled Flow Reverser (CFR), introduced by Mansour et al.18.

Research on flow reversers highlights their ability to disrupt and enhance the natural flow patterns within spiral tubes. Studies by Kumar et al.16,17 and Mansour et al.18, Farhadi et al.19 and Kazemi et al.20 demonstrate that modifying the flow path by 90 or 180 degrees can significantly boost heat transfer. Despite the potential of flow reversers, most research has focused on their application in vertical helically coiled tubes, with limited exploration of their use in in-plane (horizontal) spiral tubes. We propose two new spiral tube geometries incorporating coiled flow reversers. The proposed SFR and SSFR are novel designs that use a periodic flow reversal mechanism to actively disrupt the flow (Fig. 3b and c). This forceful redirection intensifies fluid mixing and disrupts boundary layer development, resulting in significantly enhanced heat transfer compared to that in conventional spiral-coiled tubes, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Unlike conventional passive spiral tubes, this approach actively controls and strengthens Dean vortices throughout the tube length. The design provides a superior balance between improved thermal performance and manageable pressure drop, making it a practical and scalable solution for industrial heat exchangers and chemical reactors. While the enhancement of heat transfer in spiral tubes through passive geometric modifications is well-documented, a significant gap exists in the exploration of active flow manipulation via periodic axis reversal to intensify secondary flow effects. The distinct novelty of this work lies in the strategic implementation of a coiled flow reverser after every 360° and 180° turn, respectively—a concept previously unreported in the literature. This design forces a deliberate reorganization of Dean vortices at each junction, fundamentally disrupting thermal boundary layer development and promoting superior fluid mixing. The primary objective of this research is to quantitatively investigate and compare the thermo-hydraulic performance (heat transfer and pressure drop) of these innovative designs against a conventional spiral tube using a combined CFD and experimental approach, thereby addressing the critical gap in understanding the potential of active flow reversal as a mechanism for performance enhancement in coiled heat exchangers. Numerical methodology

The problem’s geometry is created using Ansys Design Modeler and meshed in Ansys Meshing (version 2021 R2). The simulation employs the SIMPLE algorithm and a second-order upwind discretization method. Residual criteria were set to for other parameters and for energy. To achieve mesh independence, the number of meshes was progressively augmented until discernible alterations in results ceased to be observed. Setup and procedure for experiments

Figure 6 depicts the experimental setup in the laboratory. The setup consists of a tank filled with cold water, a tube (Spiral tube, SFR, or SSFR), two pressure gauges, two thermometers, a data logger, and a water bath with a thermostatic control system to maintain a constant temperature of 50 °C. Initially, water at 25 °C is pumped from the cold-water tank and immersed into the tube within the water bath. The setup includes two pressure gauges: one before the inlet of the tube and the other after the outlet. To calculate the pressure drop in the tube, the outlet pressure is subtracted from the inlet pressure. The temperature gradient along the tube is monitored by two thermocouples placed at the inlet and outlet. All thermocouples are connected to a data logger for recording temperature data. The experimental process involves introducing cold water into the tube and analyzing pressure drop and water temperature at different points using pressure gauges and thermometers. The pump ensures a consistent water flow rate in all trials. A flowmeter, capable of adjusting the flow rate, is installed before the tube to set the desired flow and direct water into the tube. Different water flow rates are tested to investigate the impact on heat transfer and pressure drop within the tubes. After completing the experiments, the experimental results are compared with numerical results to validate the findings. This comparison is essential for assessing the accuracy of CFD simulations and identifying any differences between the two datasets.

Results and discussion

The current investigation examined the impact of reversing the axis of a spiral tube on its convective heat transfer. The study encompassed both numerical simulations and experimental inquiries. As depicted in Figs. 7 and 8, the numerical modeling of fluid movement in the spiral tube uncovered a 7 percent discrepancy in pressure drop and a 4.14 percent discrepancy in outlet temperature when compared to the experimental findings.

By comparing the numerical and experimental results for SFR, it was concluded that these results differ by 7.06 percent in pressure drop and 4.63 percent in outlet temperature. The comparison of these results is shown in Figs. 7 and 8.

Upon comparing the numerical and experimental outcomes for SSFR, it was determined that there is a 7.3 percent variance in pressure drop and a 4.02 percent difference in outlet temperature. The presentation of these comparisons is illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8. The disparities between the numerical and experimental outcomes are sufficiently minor to ensure the accuracy of the results. Furthermore, as indicated in Table 6, the numerical findings revealed that the SFR demonstrated its optimal heat transfer performance at Re of 17,207, achieving a noteworthy TPR of 1.24. Similarly, the SSFR showcased its highest heat transfer efficiency at the same Re, reaching an impressive TPR of 1.45. Notably, the SSFR consistently surpassed (Fig. 9) all other configurations in all investigated cases, underscoring its effectiveness in enhancing convective heat transfer.

Examining these contours leads to the conclusion that reversing the axis of the tube enhances the strength of Dean vortices, resulting in a more prominent secondary flow in SSFR compared to SFR, and in SFR compared to the spiral tube. Analyzing these contours reveals that the turbulence kinetic energy in SSFR surpasses that in SFR, and in turn, the turbulence kinetic energy in SFR exceeds that in the spiral tube. Consequently, the mixing in SSFR is greater than in SFR, and in SFR, it is greater than in the spiral tube. Based on Fig. 12, which displays temperature contours in the identical cross-sections as depicted in Figs. 10 and 11, it is evident (as anticipated from the prior analysis) that the temperature at the cross-section of the SSFR tube is elevated compared to that of the SFR and spiral tube. The current investigation explored the impact of reversing the axis of a spiral tube on its convective heat transfer. Both numerical simulations and experimental inquiries indicated that the SSFR exhibits the most favorable performance. However, SSFR intensifies the secondary flow and radial velocity, leading to improved heat transfer but accompanied by an increased pressure drop. Additionally, we observed that enhancing the flow rate can enhance heat transfer efficiency, yet it is concurrently linked with an elevation in pressure drop. Also, considering that SSFR takes up less space, it appears to be a more economically viable choice for use in most engineering applications than the other two tubes. The SSFR design’s key advantage stems from its 180° flow reversal cycle, which more frequently disrupts thermal boundary layers and regenerates Dean vortices compared to the SFR’s 360° cycle. This generates stronger secondary flows and higher turbulent kinetic energy, leading to markedly improved fluid mixing and heat transfer efficiency. While this results in a greater pressure drop, the significantly enhanced thermal performance and compact geometry make the SSFR particularly advantageous for applications where maximizing heat transfer in a limited space is essential. Conclusion

This study introduced and rigorously evaluated two novel heat exchanger geometries: the Spiral Flow Reverser (SFR) and the Semicircular Spiral Flow Reverser (SSFR), which implement flow reversal after every 360° and 180° of curvature, respectively. Through combined computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and experimental analysis, the following major conclusions were drawn:

The SSFR configuration consistently demonstrated superior thermal performance, achieving a maximum TPR of 1.45 at a Re of 17,207. This represents a 45% enhancement in heat transfer efficiency compared to a conventional spiral tube. The SFR geometry also showed significant improvement, with a TPR of 1.24.

The key mechanism for this enhancement was identified as the intensification of secondary flows (Dean vortices) and a substantial increase in turbulent kinetic energy—by approximately 30% in the SSFR—resulting from the forced flow reversals. This effect is vividly captured in the cross-sectional contours of axial velocity, streamlines, and temperature, which confirm more vigorous fluid mixing and efficient thermal boundary layer disruption in the proposed geometries.

This enhancement comes with a known trade-off: an increase in pressure drop due to the intensified secondary flow and associated viscous dissipation. However, the SSFR’s compact design and superior heat transfer coefficient present a strong case for its economic viability in space-constrained applications where pumping power is not the primary constraint.

The numerical model was validated against experimental data with excellent agreement, showing maximum discrepancies of 7.3% in pressure drop and 4.63% in outlet temperature, which are within the combined uncertainty of the measurements and simulations.

The promising results of this foundational study open several avenues for future research, including parametric optimization of the reversal bend radius and straight section length to reduce pressure drop while maintaining heat transfer benefits, detailed investigations into fouling and long-term performance by leveraging the high wall shear stress of the SSFR, system-level scaling from single-tube analysis to multi-tube heat exchanger bundles, and exploration of applications beyond heat transfer such as mixing intensification in chemical reactors and mass transfer enhancement in separation processes; together, these directions establish the SSFR as a highly promising geometry for advanced heat exchanger design, offering significant thermal performance improvements and a clear roadmap for future research and practical implementation.
