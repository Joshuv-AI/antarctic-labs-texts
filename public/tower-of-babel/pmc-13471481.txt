Associations of preoperative nutritional and inflammatory markers with length of stay after primary total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Haotian Yang, Yi Wang, Wei Wang, Kuike Yu, Yu Chen, Zhenlin Sun, Ran Tao, Hongdong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital length of stay (LOS) after primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is influenced by both perioperative pathways and patient-level factors. Although inflammatory markers have been linked to complication-related outcomes, the association of preoperative nutritional and inflammatory markers with routine postoperative LOS remains unclear. This study examined the associations of the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) with LOS after primary TKA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent primary TKA for osteoarthritis at a single institution in 2023. The primary outcome was index LOS, defined as days from admission to discharge. Prolonged LOS (> 8 days) was the secondary outcome. Associations were evaluated using sequential linear and logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, and operative time. Time to first ambulation was assessed separately in a sensitivity analysis because it is a postoperative variable that may lie on the early recovery pathway.ResultsAmong 464 patients, higher preoperative PNI was associated with shorter LOS in the primary adjusted model (0.42-day decrease per 5-point increase; 95% CI − 0.60 to − 0.24; P < 0.001) and with lower odds of prolonged hospitalization (OR = 0.69; 95% CI 0.55 to 0.87; P = 0.002). Higher hs-CRP showed a modest association with longer LOS (β = 0.17; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.33; P = 0.042), but was not significantly associated with prolonged LOS after adjustment. CAR was associated with both outcomes, although less consistently than PNI. In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for time to first ambulation, the association between PNI and LOS reversed direction.ConclusionsHigher preoperative PNI was associated with shorter hospitalization after primary TKA, whereas hs-CRP and CAR showed weaker or less consistent associations. The reversal observed after additional adjustment for time to first ambulation indicates that models including postoperative recovery variables should be interpreted cautiously.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-07021-w.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is being performed with increasing frequency, and recent projections suggest that the future burden of hip and knee arthroplasty will continue to rise substantially [1]. With the wider adoption of enhanced recovery pathways, shorter hospitalization and earlier discharge have become routine goals of perioperative care, making length of stay (LOS) an important measure of postoperative recovery and healthcare utilization rather than a purely administrative endpoint [2–4]. Even so, LOS remains variable after TKA, indicating that discharge readiness is influenced not only by pathway design but also by patient-related factors [2, 4].

Among these factors, preoperative nutritional status has attracted increasing attention because it can be assessed before surgery and may be modifiable [5–8]. Recent studies have shown that malnutrition, hypoalbuminemia, and related nutritional deficits are associated with worse outcomes after total joint arthroplasty, including wound complications, infection, and delayed recovery [6–9]. These findings are clinically relevant because screening and targeted optimization of nutritional risk before TKA may be feasible in routine practice and may also be cost-effective [5].

Albumin alone, however, may not fully reflect perioperative physiologic reserve, because it is influenced by systemic inflammation and other acute-phase responses [7, 8]. Composite indices may therefore be more informative. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin and lymphocyte count, has emerged as a practical marker of nutritional–immune status and has been associated with wound complications and recovery-related outcomes in arthroplasty [9–12]. Other nutrition-based indices, including the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), have also shown associations with complications and functional outcomes after total joint arthroplasty [10–12].

Inflammation-related markers may provide complementary information. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is widely used to reflect systemic inflammatory burden, and the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) has been linked to postoperative delirium, periprosthetic infection, and wound complications after arthroplasty [13–17]. However, most previous studies have focused on complication-related endpoints, whereas the associations of nutritional and inflammatory markers with routine LOS after uncomplicated primary TKA remain less clear. In addition, LOS is influenced by several perioperative and patient-level factors, including discharge disposition, frailty, comorbidity burden, and postoperative mobilization [18–21]. Against this background, the specific role of preoperative inflammatory–nutritional status in LOS warrants further study.

The present study was therefore designed to examine the associations of preoperative PNI, hs-CRP, and CAR with length of stay (LOS) and prolonged LOS after primary TKA for osteoarthritis. These markers were evaluated together because they capture related but distinct aspects of preoperative status, with PNI reflecting nutritional-immune reserve and hs-CRP and CAR reflecting systemic inflammatory burden. We hypothesized that PNI would show a more consistent association with postoperative LOS than the inflammation-based markers.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, Nantong, China. Consecutive inpatient admissions for primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) performed between January 1, 2023 and December 31, 2023 were screened using hospital admission and operative records. The study was designed to investigate whether preoperative nutritional and inflammatory markers were associated with hospital length of stay after primary TKA for osteoarthritis.

Patients were eligible if they underwent primary TKA for primary osteoarthritis during the study period and had complete admission and discharge dates available for calculation of length of stay (LOS). Because the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) requires serum albumin and lymphocyte count, patients also needed to have these preoperative laboratory measurements available for analyses involving PNI. For patients who underwent bilateral procedures during the same admission, only one knee per patient was included to maintain statistical independence; the analyzed side was defined as the first-operated knee according to the operative records. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision arthroplasty or if the indication for surgery was not primary osteoarthritis. For analyses involving high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), only patients with available preoperative hs-CRP measurements were included. After application of the eligibility criteria, 464 patients were included in the overall study cohort (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record system, laboratory information system, and perioperative documentation. Collected variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus status, operative time, and time to first ambulation. Preoperative laboratory data were obtained from the last available routine blood tests performed before surgery during the index hospitalization and included serum albumin, total lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, and hs-CRP. Several other factors that may influence LOS after TKA, including anesthesia technique, postoperative analgesia protocols, discharge destination, frailty status, and formal comorbidity indices, were not consistently documented in the medical records and therefore could not be included in the multivariable analyses.

Exposure variables

The preoperative markers of interest were PNI, hs-CRP, and CAR. PNI was calculated as serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (× 109/L). CAR was calculated as hs-CRP (mg/L) divided by serum albumin (g/L). For descriptive analyses, patients were categorized into quartiles according to preoperative PNI values. For regression analyses, PNI, hs-CRP, and CAR were treated as continuous variables. Because hs-CRP and CAR were right-skewed, both variables were natural log-transformed before regression modeling.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was LOS, defined as the number of days from hospital admission to discharge during the index TKA hospitalization; this definition included any preoperative inpatient days during the same admission. The secondary outcome was prolonged LOS, defined a priori as LOS greater than 8 days, which corresponded to the upper quartile of the LOS distribution in this cohort. This threshold was chosen to identify patients with relatively longer hospitalization within the study population.

Covariates

Covariates were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and previously reported associations with postoperative recovery after TKA. Prespecified covariates included age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and operative time. Time to first ambulation was considered separately because it occurs after surgery and may reflect the pace of early recovery rather than a conventional baseline confounder. Time to first ambulation was not included in the primary adjustment set. Instead, it was added separately in a sensitivity analysis because it is a postoperative variable that may lie on the early recovery pathway.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 31.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Distributional assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection when required. Baseline characteristics across PNI quartiles were compared using one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Association with LOS

Associations between preoperative markers and LOS were evaluated using sequential linear regression models, with LOS treated as a continuous dependent variable. Results are reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For each exposure variable (PNI, ln(hs-CRP), and ln(CAR)), three primary models and one additional sensitivity model were fitted. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and diabetes mellitus. Model 3 additionally adjusted for operative time. Model 4 further included time to first ambulation as a sensitivity analysis because this postoperative variable may lie on the early recovery pathway. This modeling strategy was used to distinguish associations involving preoperative markers from those observed after additional adjustment for a postoperative recovery variable.

Association with prolonged LOS

Associations between preoperative markers and prolonged LOS were examined using sequential logistic regression models. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. The same model structure was applied to analyses of prolonged LOS: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and diabetes mellitus; Model 3 additionally adjusted for operative time; and Model 4 further included time to first ambulation as a sensitivity analysis.

Ethics approval

According to the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, formal ethical approval was not required for this retrospective analysis of de-identified clinical data, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 464 patients undergoing primary TKA for osteoarthritis were included in the study cohort. The median age was 68 years (interquartile range (IQR), 63–73 years), 64.0% were female, and the median LOS was 7 days (IQR, 7–8 days). Patients were categorized into quartiles according to preoperative PNI (Table 1). Those in the higher PNI quartiles had higher albumin, hemoglobin, and lymphocyte levels than patients in the lowest quartile, whereas hs-CRP and CAR showed no significant differences across quartiles. Significant differences across PNI quartiles were also observed for age, sex distribution, diabetes prevalence, and time to first ambulation, while BMI and operative time were similar between groups. Overall, 21% of patients had LOS greater than 8 days and therefore met the prespecified definition of prolonged LOS. The distribution of LOS across PNI quartiles is shown in Fig. 2.

Associations of preoperative markers with LOS

In the primary analyses, higher preoperative PNI was associated with shorter LOS, whereas higher ln-transformed hs-CRP and ln-transformed CAR were associated with longer LOS (Table 2). The association between PNI and LOS remained similar after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and operative time. In Model 3, each 5-point increase in PNI was associated with a 0.42-day decrease in LOS (β = − 0.42, 95% CI − 0.60 to − 0.24; P < 0.001). Higher hs-CRP showed a modest positive association with LOS across the primary models, while CAR was also positively associated with LOS in Models 1 to 3.

Associations of preoperative markers with prolonged LOS

In the primary logistic regression analyses, higher preoperative PNI was associated with lower odds of prolonged LOS (Table 3). This association remained after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and operative time (Model 3: OR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.87; P = 0.002). Higher ln-transformed hs-CRP showed a positive but non-significant association with prolonged LOS in Model 3 (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.50; P = 0.062), whereas ln-transformed CAR remained significantly associated with increased odds of prolonged LOS (OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.58; P = 0.023).

Discussion

In the primary models, higher preoperative PNI was associated with both shorter LOS and lower odds of prolonged hospitalization, whereas hs-CRP and CAR showed weaker or less consistent associations. Within this cohort, these findings suggest that PNI was more consistently related to postoperative hospital stay than the inflammation-based markers examined in parallel.

The observed pattern is consistent with the role of albumin and lymphocyte count as markers of perioperative reserve. PNI incorporates serum albumin and lymphocyte count, thereby reflecting both nutritional substrate and immune competence rather than protein status alone [9–12]. After TKA, discharge readiness depends not only on the operation itself but also on wound condition, tolerance of rehabilitation, pain recovery, and the ability to progress through early postoperative milestones. A marker that reflects broader physiologic reserve may therefore be more closely associated with LOS. Our results are in line with recent arthroplasty studies reporting that poorer nutritional status is associated with higher complication rates and less favorable short-term outcomes [6–12].

By comparison, hs-CRP and CAR appeared to reflect part of the perioperative inflammatory burden, but neither showed the same stability as PNI when LOS was used as the endpoint. This does not diminish the importance of inflammation in arthroplasty care. Rather, it suggests that inflammation-based markers may be more informative for complication-related outcomes than for routine hospital stay. Existing literature supports this view, as CAR and related inflammatory ratios have been linked more often to wound complications, infection, and delirium than to uncomplicated postoperative LOS after primary arthroplasty [13–17]. In our cohort, the weaker and less stable associations of hs-CRP and CAR may indicate that discharge after TKA is influenced by factors beyond inflammatory activity alone.

One aspect of the analysis warrants particular attention. The inverse association between PNI and LOS remained similar across the unadjusted model and the primary adjusted models, but reversed after time to first ambulation was introduced in the sensitivity analysis. We do not interpret this finding to mean that better preoperative nutritional status prolongs hospitalization. Rather, time to first ambulation is a postoperative variable that may lie on the early recovery pathway and may therefore function differently from a conventional baseline confounder. Earlier ambulation may itself be linked to shorter hospital stay, and adjustment for this variable may have altered the observed association between preoperative PNI and LOS. [2, 15, 16, 18, 19] At the same time, alternative explanations, including overadjustment, model instability, and residual confounding, should also be considered. For these reasons, the model additionally adjusting for time to first ambulation is best viewed as a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary estimate of the preoperative association.

This interpretation also fits with the broader arthroplasty literature. Studies of early mobilization, discharge disposition, frailty, and perioperative prehabilitation consistently show that LOS is shaped by both baseline patient status and the efficiency with which postoperative recovery steps are achieved [2, 15, 16, 18–21]. Our findings extend that perspective by suggesting that nutritional–immune reserve may influence how rapidly those recovery steps can be completed. In that sense, PNI may function less as a marker of isolated nutritional deficiency and more as a simple indicator of overall perioperative resilience.

Although statistically significant, the magnitude of the association was modest and should be interpreted in the context of local discharge practices and perioperative care pathways. From a practical perspective, a low preoperative PNI may help identify patients who require closer perioperative monitoring, more individualized discharge planning, or targeted preoperative optimization. Albumin and lymphocyte count are inexpensive, routinely available, and already included in standard preoperative testing, making PNI easy to calculate without additional burden on patients or institutions [9–12]. This is particularly relevant because recent studies suggest that malnutrition screening before TKA is both feasible and potentially cost-effective [5]. Although our data do not show that improving PNI will necessarily shorten LOS, they do support the value of incorporating nutritional reserve into preoperative assessment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design limits causal inference and may reduce generalizability. LOS is shaped not only by patient characteristics but also by institutional discharge criteria, rehabilitation resources, and local care pathways. The median LOS in this cohort may also be longer than that reported in some contemporary ERAS-based arthroplasty pathways, which may limit the applicability of these findings to centers with shorter routine hospitalization. [2–4] Second, residual confounding remains possible. Important factors such as anesthesia technique, postoperative analgesia protocols, discharge destination, frailty status, formal comorbidity indices, and postoperative complications were not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be incorporated into the adjusted models. Third, hs-CRP and CAR were analyzed after logarithmic transformation, which improved model fitting but makes direct clinical interpretation less intuitive. Finally, this study cannot determine whether active preoperative nutritional optimization would shorten LOS, and that question will require prospective evaluation.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It focused on readily obtainable preoperative biomarkers, evaluated both continuous and dichotomized LOS outcomes, and directly compared nutrition-based and inflammation-based indices within the same TKA cohort. The consistency of the PNI findings across the main regression models supports the relevance of preoperative nutritional–immune reserve to early recovery after primary TKA [9–12]. Future prospective multicenter studies should examine whether nutrition-oriented risk stratification and targeted optimization can be incorporated more effectively into perioperative TKA pathways [2, 5, 9–12].

Conclusion

Among patients undergoing primary TKA for osteoarthritis, higher preoperative PNI was associated with shorter LOS and a lower likelihood of prolonged hospitalization across the primary models, whereas hs-CRP and CAR showed weaker or less consistent associations. These findings suggest that PNI may offer additional value in preoperative risk assessment, particularly when considering short-term postoperative recovery and discharge planning. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these observations and to clarify whether preoperative nutritional optimization can improve early postoperative outcomes after TKA.
