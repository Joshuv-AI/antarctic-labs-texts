Subgaleal Haematoma—A Rare Cause of Head Swelling in a School‐Aged Girl
Authors: Patrick J B Walker, Jim J Goutzamanis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning Points Subgaleal haematoma, while rare outside of the neonatal population, is an important cause of head swelling in older children.The presence of a subgaleal haematoma should prompt active exclusion of an underlying bleeding disorder, including Factor XIII deficiency if the coagulation profile, von Willebrand studies, and platelet function are normal. However, SGH can occur in the absence of a bleeding disorder.Patients with subgaleal haematoma should be closely monitored and receive prompt administration of blood products if required, to prevent coagulopathy and tissue hypoperfusion.Rarely, surgical drainage may be required; however, this runs the risk of removing the tamponade effect of blood collected in the subgaleal space.

Case Report

A previously well 7‐year‐old girl presented to hospital with increasing headache and scalp swelling, 5 days after falling and hitting her head on a wooden post while playing at school. She did not lose consciousness, did not vomit, and initially had only a mild headache. Three days later her parents noted a boggy swelling across her scalp, accompanied by an increase in headache. She did not have decreased conscious state or seizures, did not feel nauseated or vomit, and did not have any fevers or infective symptoms. Past history was significant only for an extended 48h period of bleeding following a tooth extraction several years earlier. There were no other features of abnormal bleeding, and she had no family history of bleeding disorders, including haemophilia and von Willebrand disease. Her parents were non‐consanguineous. Given the delayed presentation, a detailed assessment including comprehensive social work review was conducted to exclude possible non‐accidental injury (NAI), finding NAI to be exceedingly unlikely. Specific investigations to rule out NAI, including skeletal survey and bone scan, were not conducted, as there were no social concerns, the proposed mechanism was plausibly linked to the observed injury, and social work review did not reveal any concerning features.

On examination she had a diffuse, boggy, gravity‐dependent swelling throughout the anterior and posterior aspect of her scalp. The swelling crossed suture lines. She had slight pallor, had no other unusual bruising or petechiae, and did not have hepatosplenomegaly. Battle sign was faintly positive on the left side, but she did not have raccoon eyes or haemotympanum. Cranial, upper limb and lower limb neurological examinations were normal, and she had normal visual acuity. Her vital signs were all within normal limits, and cardiac, respiratory and abdominal examinations were unrevealing.

Her full blood count was normal, with a haemoglobin of 122 g/L, white cell count of 7.5 × 109, and platelet count of 235 × 109. Coagulation profile was normal, with an INR of 1.0, APTT of 31 s, and fibrinogen of 3.3 g/L. Electrolytes and liver function were normal. CT brain showed a large scalp haematoma covering frontal, parietal and temporal regions without skull fracture, and no intracranial haemorrhage or white matter change (Figure 1). This scan revealed blood of varying age within the haematoma, raising “suspicion for a bleeding diathesis”. MRI the following day confirmed a diffuse subgaleal collection of different ages with both acute and subacute bleeding in the midline vertex and left frontoparietal region (Figure 2). There was no evidence of vascular malformation or intracranial pathology. An ultrasound confirmed extension of her haematoma into the temporoparietal region and down into the neck.

Over the subsequent 3 days her head swelling increased markedly, associated with increased pain requiring opioid analgesia and a fentanyl PCA. Her haemoglobin dropped to 81 g/L, and her INR increased to 1.4. APTT and fibrinogen remained normal. She became symptomatic from her anaemia, and received one unit of packed red blood cells, which incremented her haemoglobin to 104 g/L. She also received 5 mg of IV vitamin K, with her INR subsequently returning to 1.0. Further testing of her coagulation function, including platelet function testing, von Willebrand screen, and Factor VIII and Factor IX, were all normal. Factor XIII was also tested, which was slightly low at 63% (normal range: > 70%).

Given her worsening pain and swelling, she was transferred to a tertiary paediatric hospital for input from the plastic surgery team. She underwent an evacuation of her subgaleal haematoma, during which 600 mL blood was drained, with no source of her bleeding identified. She remained well post‐operatively and was discharged home 4 days later.

Follow‐up investigations 4 weeks later revealed normal INR of 1.1, normal APTT of 35 s, normal platelet function and normal von Willebrand studies. Factor VIII was elevated at 163%, and Factor XIII had returned to normal at 74%. She was reviewed several weeks later by the plastic surgery and haematology teams, by which time her head swelling had improved dramatically. She was discharged to the care of her general practitioner with no ongoing health issues related to her presentation or surgical management. Her final diagnosis was traumatic subgaleal haematoma without an underlying bleeding disorder. Recurrence or further episodes of abnormal bleeding were deemed unlikely.

Discussion

Subgaleal haematoma (SGH) is an important cause of haemodynamic compromise in neonates [1], yet its relevance is rarely appreciated outside of the neonatal period. Often associated with instrumental delivery, SGH refers to bleeding beneath the galea aponeurotica of the scalp. In neonates, mortality of SGH may be as high as 11.8%, and it is frequently associated with intraventricular haemorrhage [2]. In older children and adults, however, its importance is under‐recognised. Although an important cause of head swelling among the non‐neonatal population, it is rarely appreciated outside of the context of major trauma, and reported cases in the literature are isolated to sporadic case reports [3, 4, 5]. It may be the first presentation of a bleeding diathesis but, as this case shows, it may occur in the absence of a bleeding disorder following a relatively minor traumatic event. Similarly to among the neonatal population, it involves collection of blood between the galea aponeurotica and periosteum secondary to rupture of dural sinus veins. As this collection is not contained by the cranial sutures, blood loss can be significant [3].

The incidence of SGH among neonates ranges from 1.5 to 70 per 10 000 live births, with the majority of cases occurring in the context of forceps or Ventouse deliveries [1]. Outside of the neonatal population, however, they are extremely rare, with cases mostly limited to case reports. Almost all cases are associated with major head trauma or an underlying bleeding disorder [6]. Given the uncommon nature of this type of haematoma, its presence outside of the neonatal period should trigger clinicians to think about a cause for abnormal bleeding, and clinicians should have a high index of suspicion for this type of haematoma when seeing a patient with diffuse head or scalp swelling. Owing to the bleeding source being venous rather than arterial, combined with higher total blood volume in older children compared to neonates, presentation may be delayed, as in this case. This slow progression of disease has been noted in the literature [5], and presents a diagnostic challenge for clinicians.

Children with SGH often report a previous history of abnormal bleeding, such as prolonged bleeding following dental procedures or surgeries. They may also have a family history of abnormal bleeding or consanguinity. As in neonates, examination reveals a boggy, diffuse swelling which is gravity‐dependent and not confined to suture lines. Investigations should focus on identifying dysfunction in either platelet function (FBE, platelet function studies) or the coagulation cascade (INR, APTT, von Willebrand studies and Factor levels). In cases involving a large amount of swelling, neuroimaging should be conducted to exclude intracranial haemorrhage. This may also identify a source of bleeding, such as an arteriovenous malformation. Similar to caring for neonates with SGH, haemodynamic status and haemoglobin should be monitored closely. Large bleeds should prompt consideration of whether there is a risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation, and if so, clinicians should have a low threshold to prescribe packed red cells and other blood products, including fresh frozen plasma or cryoprecipitate [7].

The presence of abnormal or unexplained bleeding with normal platelet and coagulation studies should prompt the clinician to think about Factor XIII deficiency. Factor XIII is a zymogenic coagulation factor which, upon activation by thrombin and calcium cross‐links, enables the adequate functioning of fibrin clots [5]. Congenital Factor XIII deficiency is rare, with a reported prevalence of 1 in 4 million, with higher rates among people of Middle‐Eastern background and in children born to consanguineous parents. Although homozygous Factor XIII deficiency is rare, milder, heterozygous forms of the condition are significantly more common [8]. Importantly, a low circulating Factor XIII level does not necessarily indicate underlying Factor XIII deficiency, but rather may represent a consumptive process, as can result in mild rises in INR such as that observed in our case. This has been observed in critically ill patients and is known to affect unwell children [9]. This is more common with mildly low circulating Factor XIII levels, as in our case. In such cases, the low Factor XIII level is more likely the result of, rather than cause of, bleeding. As such, Factor replacement is unlikely to be helpful.

There are limited data on surgical management options for SGH. Surgical evacuation of blood from the subgaleal space, whereas occasionally necessary to manage symptoms, risks removing the tamponade effect, creating a potential for further bleeding [1]. Surgical drainage, then, is generally only considered for cases involving uncontrollable swelling and/or pain, where conservative management approaches have failed. One case report from 2020 involving a 12‐year‐old girl with SGH secondary to hair braiding involved aspiration of 300 mL blood from the subgaleal space [4]. This case also described a positive outcome following surgical drainage, with no recurrence observed at follow‐up review. However, surgical intervention for SGH in the non‐neonatal population remains uncommon, and the decision to pursue this treatment approach should involve careful weighting of the risk of removing the tamponade effect of the haematoma (and the risk of infection) against the need to relieve symptoms or prevent secondary complications.

Subgaleal haematoma is a rare but important cause of traumatic head swelling in children. Although mostly confined to neonates soon after birth, a boggy scalp swelling which crosses suture lines should prompt clinicians to consider this diagnosis in older children, particularly following trauma, including minor trauma. Due to the uncommon nature of SGH, clinicians should actively exclude an underlying bleeding disorder, particularly in patients with a previous or family history of abnormal bleeding. Initial management should focus on maintaining haemodynamic stability and preventing coagulopathy while excluding intracranial bleeding with prompt neuroimaging and excluding an underlying bleeding disorder through coagulation testing. If symptoms progress despite conservative management, surgical drainage may be required; however, the decision to drain collected blood is complex and should involve surgical teams with experience managing extracranial haemorrhage in children.
