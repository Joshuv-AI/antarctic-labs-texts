Association between eye washing and ocular symptoms of hay fever: a mobile app-based prospective cohort study in Japan
Authors: Ken Nagino, Jaemyoung Sung, Akie Midorikawa-Inomata, Atsuko Eguchi, Shintaro Nakao, Hiroyuki Kobayashi, Takenori Inomata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge-scale analyses of the relationship between eyewash and hay fever symptoms after adjusting for factors associated with allergic symptoms have not yet been conducted. We aimed to evaluate the association between eyewash and ocular symptoms of hay fever. In this digital prospective observational cohort study, we used the AllerSearch smartphone application. Participants reported symptoms (eye itching, tearing, and redness) and their preventive behaviors such as eye washing. Multivariate regression analysis was used to determine the association; temporal changes in ocular symptoms were compared using a mixed-effects model. Of 476 included participants, 71 practiced eye washing. Eyewash group had milder symptoms than non-eyewash group (estimated difference between the two groups, 95% confidence interval): eye itching (− 0.851 points, − 1.369 to − 0.333, P = 0.001), tearing (− 0.655 points, − 1.217 to − 0.094, P = 0.022), total ocular symptoms score (− 0.840 points, − 1.481 to − 0.198, P = 0.010), and total Japanese Allergic Conjunctival Disease Quality of Life Questionnaire score (− 5.983 points, − 9.633 to − 2.333, P = 0.001). Longitudinal analysis indicated that eyewash group maintained milder ocular symptoms over time, with more symptom-free days. In conclusion, eye washing was associated with milder ocular symptoms of hay fever.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24950-4.Keywords: Allergic conjunctivitis, Eyewash, Hay fever, Mobile health, Ocular symptom, JACQLQSubject terms: Eye diseases, Conjunctival diseases, Preventive medicine

Introduction

Hay fever is the most common allergic disease, affecting almost 30 million individuals in Japan, with a steady increase in prevalence1. Allergic rhinitis, conjunctivitis, and dermatitis are characteristic symptoms of hay fever and negatively affect an individual’s quality of life (QoL), sleep hygiene, academic performance, and work productivity1–3. The current mainstay approach revolves around symptomatic treatment using antihistaminergic agents; however, in severe cases, it is difficult to achieve complete resolution of symptoms with ex post facto therapy1,4,5. Therefore, in addition to medication, avoiding exposure to causative airborne agents and daily self-care are imperative in preventing the symptoms and exacerbation of hay fever1,2,5.

Allergic conjunctivitis due to hay fever (seasonal allergic conjunctivitis) is primarily driven by exposure to airborne cedar pollen, which acts as an allergen, and its symptoms noticeably intensify during high pollen dispersion seasons6. In addition, other airborne particles, including particulate matter 2.5 (PM 2.5) and “yellow dust,” the levels of which concomitantly increase during cedar pollen seasons, may exacerbate the symptoms of allergic conjunctivitis due to hay fever7,8. To minimize prolonged exposure to these particles and symptoms by actively removing debris from the ocular surface, eyewash products are being sold commercially to the general public in Japan9. The Japanese Society of Ocular Allergology has also endorsed preservative-free eyewash use to reduce the symptoms of allergic conjunctival diseases10. Following the elucidation of its efficacy, usage, and limitations, eyewash appears to have potential as an additional self-care option for patients with hay fever, owing to its ease of use and safety profile11.

Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy of eyewash in reducing eyelid edema and ocular pruritus secondary to reactions against cedar pollen12, and in improving ocular allergic symptoms secondary to responses to air pollutants6. A previous study was conducted in a small cohort2. A large-scale analysis assessing the relationship between eyewash and hay fever symptoms, while adjusting for various factors associated with allergic symptoms, is yet to be conducted. To address this gap, we conducted a large-scale digital cohort study using AllerSearch, an in-house developed smartphone application for hay fever research2,13. Since its release in 2018, we have compiled a comprehensive dataset on hay fever, including preventive behavior (i.e., eyewash), subjective symptoms, app-based assessment of conjunctival hyperemia, and environmental data (i.e., pollen dispersion, yellow dust level, and PM 2.5 level)2,11,13–17. Owing to ease of access to a wide network of patients, the smartphone app-based data collection has shown advantages in adjusting for multiplex factors associated with hay fever. This has resulted in the compilation of a robust dataset to accurately delineate and examine the effects of eye washing on ocular allergic symptoms. This study assessed the association between eye washing as a preventive measure and hay fever symptoms using AllerSearch.

Results

Participant enrollment and characteristics

The participant enrollment process is shown in Fig. 1. Data of 6,705 individuals who downloaded the application were extracted from the database. Based on the exclusion criteria, individuals were excluded for the following reasons: lack of electronic informed consent, incomplete survey on hay fever, responded “non-hay fever” or “unknown” to the question “Do you have hay fever?” data submitted outside the study period from February to May, data submitted only once, or missing Global Positioning System (GPS) data or locations outside Japan. Thus, 476 individuals were included in the final analysis. The results of the sensitivity analysis comparing individuals with hay fever who completed all questionnaire items within the study period to those who did not fully complete the questionnaire, were non-hay fever or unknown-status individuals, or responded outside the study period are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The cohort of patients with hay fever who completed all questionnaire items during the study period was significantly older than the excluded population and had significantly higher prevalences of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, and malignant tumors, as well as significantly higher implementation rates of all preventive behaviors (Supplementary Table S1). The results of the additional sensitivity analysis comparing the included cohort with individuals excluded due to only a single data submission, lack of GPS data, or responses from outside Japan are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Compared with the excluded population, the included cohort was significantly older, and had a significantly lower proportion of females, and had a significantly higher body weight. In addition, the included cohort showed significantly higher prevalences of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and brain disease, along with significantly greater implementation rates of preventive behaviors such as the use of nasal drops, air purifiers, and the wearing of glasses and goggles (Supplementary Table S2).

Assessing the association between eye washing and subjective symptoms of hay fever and hay fever-related QoL scores using multivariate regression analysis

Table 3 presents the results of the multivariate regression analysis of the association between eyewash and hay fever-related symptoms and QoL scores, adjusted for age, sex, systemic disease, preventive behaviors other than eyewash, and pollen dispersion levels. Eye washing was negatively associated with rhinorrhea and effects of hay fever on daily life in the nasal symptom score (NSS), eye itching and tearing in the non-nasal symptom score (NNSS), total NSS, ocular symptoms score, total NNSS, and total symptom score (TSS). Eye washing was negatively associated with the following hay fever-related QoL scores and total Japanese Allergic Conjunctival Disease Standard QoL Questionnaire (JACQLQ) score: interference with study, work, or household chores; decreased ability to think; trouble with outdoor activities, such as sports and picnics; difficulty going out; difficulty in socializing with others; difficulty in talking with others or making phone calls; fatigue (tiredness); fatigue (easily tired); depression; and total JACQLQ score.

Assessing the association between eye washing and temporal changes in subjective hay fever symptoms and hay fever-related QoL scores using a mixed-effects model.

Table 4 summarizes the results of a longitudinal multivariate regression analysis using a mixed-effects model performed to assess the association between eye washing and temporal changes in subjective symptoms of hay fever and hay fever-related QoL scores, adjusted for age, sex, systemic disease, preventive behaviors other than eyewash, and pollen dispersion level. When viewed longitudinally throughout the study period, the eyewash group had substantially lower symptom scores for rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, nasal itching, sneezing, effects on daily life, total NSS , eye itching, eye tearing, ocular symptoms score, ear and/or mouth itching, and TSS than that of the non-eyewash group. The eyewash group had substantially lower QoL scores related to interference with study, work, or household chores; poor mental concentration; difficulty (inconvenience) in reading newspapers and books; trouble with outdoor activities, such as sports and picnics; difficulty going out (tending to refrain from going out); difficulty in socializing with others (tending to refrain from doing so); difficulty in talking with others or making phone calls; anxious about people around; feeling unwell; and lower total JACQLQ scores.

Overall, 470 (98.7%) responded to questions regarding subjective symptoms of dry eye disease (DED). Discussion

In this study, we used the data collected through AllerSearch, designed for hay fever research, to examine the relationship between eyewash and ocular symptoms of hay fever. The multivariate analysis indicated that individuals in the eyewash group experienced substantially milder ocular symptoms than those in the non-eyewash group. Additionally, individuals in the eyewash group reported improved long-term symptom control, as demonstrated by a higher number of symptom-free days. These findings indicate that eye washing was associated with milder ocular symptoms during the acute, exacerbative, and chronic phases.

The study showed that eyewash was associated with improvements in ocular symptoms and certain nasal symptoms of hay fever. Excluding eye itching, univariate comparisons between the eyewash and non-eyewash groups did not indicate significant differences in hay fever-related ocular symptoms. Eye washing remains a relatively niche approach used by a select group of patients with hay fever, particularly among those with more severe symptoms and higher motivation for self-care2.

Differences in basic demographic data between the eyewash and non-eyewash groups partially demonstrate the aforementioned confounding factors, as the eyewash group in this study exhibited higher goggle and ophthalmic drop usage rates than that of the non-eyewash group. Hence, a fundamental behavioral difference between the groups may be susceptible to several confounders and complex biases, which may have led to a lack of significant differences in symptoms between the groups. To mitigate these confounding factors, multivariate regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for age, sex, preventive behaviors, and pollen dispersion status. The results demonstrated negative associations between eyewash and effects of nasal symptoms on daily life, in addition to reductions in ocular symptoms scores, including eye itching, tearing, and total NNSS. These findings align with the findings of a previous study, indicating that patients experiencing both nasal and non-nasal symptoms exhibit more severe manifestations in both categories compared to those with isolated symptoms14. These results indicate that eye washing, as part of an individual’s preventive regimen, was associated with milder ocular symptoms and less severe nasal symptoms in a major portion of the hay fever population.

A longitudinal multivariate analysis of hay fever symptom dynamics over time indicated a longer period of milder ocular symptoms in the eyewash group than that in the non-eyewash group. Our longitudinal data analysis indicated a small but significant positive interaction between time and eye washing, indicating that hay fever symptoms among individuals in the eyewash group may have progressed slightly during hay fever season. However, in a concurrent analysis, the magnitude of the negative interaction between eye washing and hay fever symptoms (i.e., alleviation of symptoms) was greater than those of the aforementioned positive interaction between time and eye washing. Hay fever symptoms substantially diminishes a patient’s QoL1, and eye washing may allow patients to adequately suppress hay fever symptoms to maintain a sufficient level of QoL throughout the hay fever season. Previous studies concur that when assessing the clinical effect of an intervention against hay fever, an improvement of 0.2–0.3 points per hay fever symptom score and 0.5 points per QoL score can be considered clinically meaningful16,18. Under this accepted MCID, the improvements in hay fever symptom scores and QoL scores observed in our study associated with eyewash surpassed the MCID criteria for all items other than the total scores. These findings suggest that eye washing was associated with milder acute exacerbations of hay fever and less severe long-term symptoms in clinical practice.

In this study, the eyewash group experienced a higher frequency of asymptomatic days than that of the non-eyewash group during hay fever sessions. The multivariate evaluation adjusted for confounding factors and the number of days for which each symptom or QoL score remained zero was calculated for this part of the analysis. Considering the aforementioned MCID thresholds, a single-point increase in symptom or QoL score may clinically affect the experience of patients16,18. The increased frequency of symptom-free days observed in the eyewash group emphasizes the association between eyewash use and better QoL of patients with hay fever.

This study showed that there was no marked association between eyewash and the worsening of DED subjective symptoms. The use of eyewash containing benzalkonium chloride as a preservative may exacerbate corneal and conjunctival epithelial disease as well as DED19. Using a preservative-free eyewash for 1 month caused no substantial changes in tear film stability and ocular surface mucin, and improved the score of DED subjective symptoms9. In Japan, many recently marketed eyewashes do not contain benzalkonium chloride, which may explain why the use of eyewashes did not worsen the subjective symptoms of DED9. According to the latest Japanese guidelines for allergic conjunctival diseases, preservative-free eyewashes may be useful in washing out antigens from the ocular surface and alleviating allergic symptoms10. Therefore, using preservative-free eyewash may help reduce pollen-induced ocular symptoms without exacerbating DED.

This study has some limitations. First, the study population was restricted to smartphone users with hay fever in Japan, introducing a degree of selection bias. Additionally, the study period was between February and May, when the dispersion of cedar pollen, the major allergen causing hay fever in the Japanese population, peaked20. Therefore, this study may unevenly represent the characteristics of patients with hay fever allergic to cedar pollen, and caution regarding result generalizability may be warranted. In studies utilizing smartphones, differences in culture, geography, and digital literacy may influence the results21. For instance, while smartphones are widely used in Japan, many older adults, who make up a large proportion of the Japanese population, are less comfortable using smartphones and may be less likely to participate in studies that use smartphone applications22. Additionally, while most patients with hay fever in Japan are allergic to Japanese cedar pollen, patients in other countries may be allergic to different types of pollen. Furthermore, although smartphone penetration and digital literacy are presumed to be high in Japan, countries with lower smartphone adoption rates may have lower levels of digital literacy23. These cultural, geographic, and digital literacy differences may impact study outcomes. In the future, including a wider age range of app users, such as older adults, as well as participants living outside Japan or those with pollen allergies other than Japanese cedar, may help improve the generalizability of the research findings.

Second, data on subjective symptoms related to hay fever were directly reported by participants through validated questionnaires, and objective examination findings were not included in the analysis, which may have increased the susceptibility to recall bias. In addition, the participants in this study were those who answered “yes” to the question “Do you have hay fever?” in the questionnaire, and clinical diagnosis was not confirmed. In future studies, it will be necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of eyewash based on clinical assessment in patients with hay fever who were diagnosed at medical institutions. Third, eyewash exposure was defined in a binary manner (users vs. non-users) based on participants’ self-reported selection of “eyewash” from the list of preventive behaviors. Therefore, data on the frequency, duration, and type of eyewash use were not collected in this study. Thus, the impact of differences in the frequency, type, and duration of eyewash products used on ocular symptoms of hay fever could not be accounted for. In addition, because the timing of when participants began using eyewash was not collected in this study, we could not compare hay fever symptoms before and after the initiation of eyewash use. Because it was unclear whether eyewash use preceded or followed the onset of hay fever symptoms, the study could not determine the potential protective effects. Several eye-wash products contain preservatives (e.g., benzalkonium chloride), which have been reported to induce corneal epithelial disorders and ocular surface mucin layer dysfunction9. Disrupting ocular surface mucin homeostasis may lead to various keratoconjunctival disorders, including allergic conjunctivitis and DED, which may have affected ocular symptoms scores15. In future studies, it will be necessary to stratify participants based on the frequency, type, and duration of eye wash use to evaluate the impact of these factors. In addition, future studies evaluating the effectiveness of eye washes should be designed to include a placebo or sham control. Fourth, compared to non-eyewash users, eyewash users may have higher health awareness or medication adherence, potentially influencing the outcomes observed. To control for this potential bias, we performed multivariate analysis that adjusted for preventive behaviors, including the use of eye drops, nasal drops, oral medications, masks, air purifiers, glasses and goggles, and nasal rinses. However, unmeasured treatments or preventive behaviors not included as adjustment factors may have influenced the results. As this was an observational study, causal relationships cannot be definitively established. In future research, approaches such as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) could help to illustrate potential causal pathways among related factors. Fifth, in this study, many users were excluded during the selection of the analysis cohort from the group of individuals who downloaded the app. To evaluate the impact of these exclusions, we conducted sensitivity analyses. First, we compared individuals with hay fever who completed all questionnaire items within the study period with those who did not fully complete the questionnaire, were non-hay fever or unknown individuals, or responded outside the study period (Supplementary Table S1). Second, we compared the included cohort with individuals excluded owing to only a single data submission, lack of GPS data, or responses from outside Japan (Supplementary Table S2). The results of both sensitivity analyses showed significant differences in the prevalence of certain systemic diseases and preventive behaviors. In particular, the group excluded due to incomplete responses, non-hay fever status, or data collected outside the study period (Supplementary Table S1) exhibited significantly lower rates of all preventive behaviors. Similarly, the group excluded due to only a single data entry, lack of GPS data, or responses from outside Japan (Supplementary Table S2) showed significantly lower implementation rates of preventive behaviors such as nasal drops, air purifier use, and the wearing of glasses or goggles. Therefore, compared to the general population, the cohort included in this study may have been more health-conscious regarding hay fever and demonstrated higher adherence to preventive behaviors. Thus, both the usage rate and estimated effectiveness of eye wash may have been higher in this study than in the general population. In future studies, collecting data on the frequency of eye wash use may help minimize this type of bias and yield more generalizable results.

In this study, we found that eye washing was associated with milder ocular symptoms related to hay fever, fewer exacerbations of hay fever symptoms over time, and a higher frequency of symptom-free days in patients with hay fever. In addition, this study showed that there was no marked association between eyewash and the worsening of subjective symptoms of DED. Preservative-free eyewash products demonstrated adequate safety profiles with minimal effect on DED or tear film homeostasis. Our results indicate that eye washing was associated with adequate safety and potentially favorable clinical outcomes for patients with hay fever and chronic impairment of QoL, particularly during peak pollen seasons. In future studies, clarifying appropriate adjunctive methods to eye washing in addition to eye drop use and promoting awareness of these associations may help identify new strategies for hay fever management in patients whose symptoms are insufficiently controlled using conventional treatments.

Methods

Study design

This digital prospective cohort study was conducted between February 1, 2021, and May 31, 202313. All participants downloaded AllerSearch; they provided electronic informed consent to participate in this study. At the first launch of the app, a consent screen displaying an information sheet about this ethically approved study was shown to users. After reviewing the information sheet, users provided their consent to participate in the study by tapping the consent button. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in Brazil, 2013) and approved by the Independent Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Juntendo University (approval number: M17-0061-M02). This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE Checklist24.

AllerSearch was developed by the Department of Ophthalmology, Juntendo University Graduate School of Medicine, Tokyo, Japan. This application was released on February 1, 2018, for iOS on App Store (Apple, Inc, Cupertino, CA, USA) and on August 26, 2020, for Android on Google Play (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA). The survey questionnaire in AllerSearch was developed in consultation with the scientific committee, comprising allergy specialists, ophthalmologists, otolaryngologists, epidemiologists, and members involved in Patient and Public Involvement25.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individuals who freely downloaded and used AllerSearch were included in the study. The participants completed a hay fever questionnaire and provided their GPS location data. Individuals were excluded if they did not consent to participate in the study; did not respond to the hay fever symptom questionnaire (lacked the necessary data for analysis); answered “No,” “Cured spontaneously in the past,” “Cured completely after treatment,” or “Unknown” to the question “Do you have hay fever?“; submitted data outside the period from February to May, which represents the Japanese cedar pollen hay fever season; submitted data only once; did not provide GPS location data; or had GPS location data that indicated locations outside Japan. Individuals with missing values for variables included in the multivariate analysis were excluded using the listwise deletion method as a complete-case analysis26.

Data collection using allersearch

Participants submitted the following data through an electronic questionnaire and data collection features in AllerSearch (Fig. 3): demographic characteristics, medical history, lifestyle, hay fever symptoms, hay fever-related QoL score, preventive behavior for hay fever (including eyewash), and GPS location data14. Data on pollen dispersion levels were collected from the weather information website, tenki.jp27, operated by the Japan Weather Association, as regional data based on the GPS locations of the participants on the days they submitted their responses. Supplementary Table S7 lists the survey questions regarding demographic characteristics, medical history, preventive behaviors for hay fever, and lifestyle. Supplementary Table S8 presents the survey questions related to hay fever symptoms, and Supplementary Table S9 shows the survey questions regarding hay fever-related QoL score based on JACQLQ Domains II.

Screenshots of the AllerSearch application. Screenshots of (a) top screen, (b) hay fever symptoms survey.

Identification of preventive behavior for hay fever

Participants selected their hay fever-preventive behaviors from a predefined list in response to the question: “Please tell us which of the following hay fever-prevention methods you have been following.” The listed options were “Mask use,” “Eye drop use,” “Nasal drop use,” “Oral medication use,” “Air purifier use,” “Use of glasses and goggles,” “Eyewash,” “Nasal rinse use.”

Definition of eye wash users and non-eye wash users

Participants who selected “Eyewash” from the list of options in response to the question, “Please tell us which of the following hay fever-prevention methods you have been following,” were classified as eyewash users, whereas those who did not select “Eyewash” were classified as non-eyewash users.

Assessment of subjective symptoms of hay fever

The subjective symptoms of hay fever were assessed using a composite score that combined the NSS and NNSS (Supplementary Table S8)28,29. The NSS included items related to rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, nasal itching, sneezing, and interference with daily life28. The NNSS included items related to itchy eyes, watery eyes, reddened eyes, and itchy ears and mouth29. Each item in the NSS and NNSS was scored on a 4-point scale (0 = no symptoms, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe symptoms). The TSS (0–27 points) was calculated as the sum of the NSS and NNSS. Of the NNSS items, the sum of the itchy, watery, and reddened eye scores was calculated as the ocular symptoms score (0–9 points).

Hay fever-related QoL scores were evaluated using the app-based JACQLQ (Supplementary Table S9)15,30. A previous study demonstrated the validity and reliability of the app-based JACQLQ compared to that of the paper-based version31. The JACQLQ Domain II scores, which included 17 items related to daily activity (11 items) and psychological well-being (six items), were used to assess hay fever-related QoL in this study. Each item in JACQLQ Domain II was rated on a 5-point scale (0 = no symptoms, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe, and 4 = very severe)30. Total JACQLQ score (0–68 points) was calculated as the sum of the 17 items in JACQLQ Domain II.

Assessment of the subjective symptoms of DED

To assess the effect of eye washing on DED, subjective symptoms of DED were measured. Participants rated the frequency of ocular dryness and discomfort associated with DED on a 4-point scale (0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often, 3 = always; Supplementary Table S7).

Power analysis

As this study involved a retrospective analysis of data collected in a prospective study13, we performed a post-hoc power analysis instead of a priori sample size calculation to evaluate statistical power. G*Power (ver 3.197, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used as the analytical tool32, and the evaluation was based on a multiple regression analysis with total JACQLQ score as the dependent variable. With a total sample size of 476, 23 predictors, an effect size of 0.114, and an alpha error level of 0.05, the calculated power was 99.8%, indicating that the study had sufficient statistical power.

Statistical analysis

Participants’ characteristics were compared between the eyewash and non-eyewash groups in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. In the cross-sectional analysis, continuous variables were compared using two-tailed t-tests, categorical variables were compared using chi-squared tests, and the association between eye washing and hay fever symptoms was evaluated using a multivariate regression analysis. To evaluate potential multicollinearity affecting the multivariate regression analysis results, we calculated the correlation coefficients between eye washing and other preventive behaviors. Multicollinearity was judged to be absent when none of the pairwise correlation coefficients exceeded 0.833.

In the longitudinal analysis, temporal changes in hay fever symptoms between the eyewash and non-eyewash groups were evaluated using mixed-effects models. In the multivariate analysis, each dependent variable is reported as fully adjusted regression coefficient and 95% CI. Ordinal logistic regression was used when the dependent variable in the multivariate analysis was ordinal. Independent variables for inclusion in the multivariate models were chosen according to clinical relevance and factors previously identified as associated with hay fever2,11. The magnitude of symptom changes associated with eyewash use was evaluated using the MCID and regression coefficients obtained from the multivariate analysis. The MCID was set at 0.3 points for each symptom score and 0.5 points for each QoL score16,18. If the absolute value of the regression coefficient for each score, excluding the total scores, exceeded the MCID, the magnitude of symptom changes associated with eyewash use was considered to be clinically meaningful. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all analyses. Data were analyzed using STATA (version 18.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).
