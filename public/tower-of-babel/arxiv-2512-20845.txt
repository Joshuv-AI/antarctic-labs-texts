MAR: Multi-Agent Reflexion Improves Reasoning Abilities in LLMs
Onat Ozer, Grace Wu, Yuchen Wang, Daniel Dosti, Honghao Zhang, Vivi De La Rue

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in reasoning and program synthesis, yet enabling them to improve through experience remains a central challenge. Reinforcement learning offers one opportunity for agent improvement, but updating large models is computationally expensive and requires significant environment interaction. The Reflexion framework introduced a lightweight alternative in which an LLM agent generates natural language reflections about its failures and stores them as episodic memory that guides future attempts. Reflexion demonstrated meaningful gains on multi hop question answering and code generation without modifying model parameters. However, our replication shows that the single agent design of Reflexion leads to systematic shortcomings. The same model generates actions, evaluates its own behavior, and produces reflections, which often results in repeated reasoning errors, confirmation bias, and limited corrective feedback. To address these limitations, we reproduce Reflexion on HotPotQA and HumanEval using GPT 3.5 and GPT 4, and we analyze failure modes through detailed logging of trajectories and reflections. We consistently observe that self reflections tend to repeat earlier misconceptions and do not introduce new reasoning paths on difficult examples. Motivated by these findings, we introduce Multi Agent Reflexion (MAR), a structured multi agent extension that incorporates diverse reasoning personas and a judge model that synthesizes their critiques into a unified reflection. MAR separates the processes of acting, diagnosing, critiquing, and aggregating, which reduces shared blind spots and prevents the repeated reinforcement of earlier mistakes. On both benchmarks, MAR improves directly over Reflexion. MAR increases HotPotQA exact-match accuracy by 3 points, from 44 to 47, and improves HumanEval pass@1 from 76.4 to 82.6, a 6.2-point gain. MAR also reduces the stagnation observed in single agent Reflexion, indicating that multi agent interaction offers a simple and effective path toward more reliable LLM agents.



Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) has evolved to generate strong reasoning traces and high-quality code, but enabling them to improve through experience remains an open problem. Traditional reinforcement learning offers one mechanism for agent improvement, but updating billion-parameter policies is computationally costly and data-intensive. In-context learning
(
1
)
, where an LLM adapts using examples directly in the prompt, offers one path toward improving model performance without additional training. Building on this idea, prompting strategies such as chain-of-thought (CoT), expert-style prompting, and ReAct have been shown to enhance LLM reasoning and decision-making.

Reflexion
(
11
)
integrates these advances while adding a mechanism for the model to self-reflect on its own mistakes: after each attempt, the agent converts feedback into natural-language guidance for the next trial, analogous to how reinforcement learning algorithms use reward signals to update a policy
(
14
)
.

Reflexion reports substantial gains over baseline LLM and CoT/ReAct agents on HotPotQA
(
17
)
and HumanEval
(
2
)
, improving exact-match accuracy by roughly 20 points on HotPotQA and pass@1 accuracy by 11 points on HumanEval. We focus on these benchmarks for replication since
multi-hop question
(questions where a model must combine information across several steps of retrieveal) answering and program synthesis are the two high-impact domains where iterative self-improvement most meaningfully enhance LLM agent performance, and covers the majority of use cases.

Despite the gains of the Reflexion framework, in our replication, we consistently observed that Reflexion is vulnerable to degeneration-of-thought
(
8
)
, where the agent repeats the same flawed reasoning across iterations even when explicit failures are identified. To mitigate this limitation, we introduce
Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR)
, a framework that replaces single-agent self-critique with a structured debate among diverse persona-based critics. These multiple critics generate richer reflections that more effectively guide the agent toward improved strategies.

Related Work

Recent research explores improving LLM reasoning through self-generated feedback, iterative refinement, and multi-agent interaction. Self-critique methods such as Self-Refine and Reflexion show that verbal feedback can improve performance without updating model weights, though they often rely on a single agent with fixed reasoning patterns. Multi-agent debate introduces structured disagreement and persona diversity, helping reduce shared blind spots; our approach builds on this idea by integrating diverse critic personas into an episodic memory framework that supports more stable and transferable improvements across tasks.

Self-Generated Feedback and Iterative Refinement:

Several works explore letting LLMs critique and revise their own outputs. Self-Refine, for example, iteratively generates output, asks the model to provide targeted feedback, and then uses that feedback to produce a revised answer
(
10
)
.

Multi-Agent Debate and Divergent Reasoning:

Multi-agent debate methods instantiate multiple LLM agents that propose answers, critique each other’s reasoning, and then aggregate the resulting arguments into a final decision
(
4
)
. Recent work shows that structured disagreement can improve factual accuracy and robustness on challenging reasoning benchmarks by promoting divergent reasoning and reducing shared blind spots
(
8
)
. Other multi-agent frameworks introduce persona-driven or role-based heterogeneity among agents and coordinate their outputs through majority voting or a centralized judge model
(
7
)
.

Chain-of-Thought and Tool-Augmented Reasoning:

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting shows that LLMs can solve substantially harder reasoning problems when encouraged to generate intermediate natural-language steps before answering
(
16
)
. CoT is purely a single-pass prompting strategy. The model produces one reasoning trace and does not explicitly learn across episodes. ReAct extends this idea by interleaving reasoning tokens with actions, so that an LLM can plan, call tools or APIs, and react to new observations in text-based environments
(
18
)
.

Background

Reflexion Framework

Reflexion
(
11
)
wraps a language model in a simple loop that turns past failures into natural-language guidance for future attempts. Conceptually, the framework decomposes the agent into three prompted roles plus an episodic memory.

Actor (
M_{a}
).

The Actor is the LLM prompted to behave as the agent’s policy. It generates actions, intermediate reasoning, and final answers. Reflexion is compatible with multiple prompting styles; the original paper focuses on:

•

Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(
16
)
prompting, which encourages structured step-by-step reasoning.

•

ReAct
(
18
)
, which interleaves reasoning steps with explicit actions taken in an external environment.

Evaluator (
M_{e}
).

The Evaluator judges whether an Actor trajectory successfully solves the task. Depending on the domain,
M_{e}
may be another LLM prompt, a heuristic rule, or an external execution environment such as code unit tests. Importantly, Reflexion only requires a coarse signal, such as success/failure, not a dense numeric reward.

Self-Reflector (
M_{sr}
).

When the Evaluator indicates failure, the Self-Reflection model converts this outcome into verbal feedback. Given the failed trajectory and its evaluation,
M_{sr}
produces natural-language reflections that diagnose what went wrong, explain why the reasoning failed, and suggest strategies to try the next attempt. These “verbal rewards” play the role of a lightweight optimization signal.

Figure 1:
The Reflexion Architecture.
Actor performs an action on the environment. The evaluator gives feedback to reflector LLM, with feedback stored in short-term memory. A new action is then performed starting a new iteration.

At a high level, each failed attempt produces a new reflection that is added to memory, and the Actor’s next attempt is conditioned on this accumulated verbal history. Over multiple trials, the agent can therefore improve its decisions without any parameter updates.

Datasets

We evaluate our approach on two benchmarks that stress different aspects of LLM reasoning. HotPotQA evaluates multi-hop question answering over Wikipedia, requiring models to combine evidence across documents. HumanEval tests program synthesis by checking whether generated functions pass hidden unit tests. Together, these datasets provide complementary settings for analyzing how reflection influences both symbolic reasoning and code generation.

•

HotPotQA
(
17
)
is a large-scale question answering benchmark constructed from Wikipedia and designed to test multi-hop reasoning (questions system has to reason with information taken from more than one document to arrive at the answer. Each example provides a question, a set of candidate paragraphs, supporting facts, and a short answer. Successful solutions typically require integrating evidence from more than one source, which makes HotPotQA a natural environment for studying how Reflexion’s episodic memory and reflections influence multi-step reasoning. Following the evaluation protocol in
11
, we report Exact Match (EM) on the development set after applying standard normalization procedures such as lowercasing and punctuation removal.

•

HumanEval
(
2
)
is a benchmark for program synthesis in which each task specifies a function signature, a natural-language description of the intended behavior, and a series of hidden unit tests. A model succeeds if its generated function passes all tests when executed in a sandboxed environment. HumanEval is the domain where the original Reflexion paper observes its largest performance gains relative to GPT-4 baselines, and it provides a clear setting for examining how reflection-guided retries alter the structure and quality of code-generation trajectories.

Performance Metrics

We evaluate both the original Reflexion framework and our multi-agent extension using task-appropriate accuracy metrics. For HotPotQA, we report Exact Match (EM), and for HumanEval, we use pass@1 based on whether the generated solution passes all hidden unit tests.

•

Exact Match (EM) for HotPotQA.
EM measures whether a predicted answer exactly matches the ground truth HotPotQA answer matches after applying HotPotQA’s standard normalization steps, including lowercasing and removal of punctuation and articles.

•

pass@1 for HumanEval.
A model receives credit for a HumanEval problem if its single sampled solution passes all hidden unit tests when executed in the official sandboxed environment.

Methodology, Results, and Discussion

Methodology

We evaluate Reflexion and our extensions on two benchmarks: multi-hop question answering with HotPotQA and program synthesis with HumanEval. Across both settings, we follow the original Reflexion protocol while adding detailed logging to analyze how reasoning, evaluation, and reflection evolve across attempts.

HotPotQA Setup.

We evaluate Reflexion on the multi-hop HotPotQA benchmark by reproducing the three agent configurations examined in the original study: Baseline GPT-3.5-Turbo using the ReAct framework, and GPT-3.5-turbo using the ReAct-Reflect framework. These frameworks are run on a curated selection of 100 difficult HotPotQa questions, matching the dataset used in their paper. We run these experiments over the course of 5 trials where after each trial, the ReAct agent will try again after reflecting over previous failures.

HumanEval Setup.

For the HumanEval benchmark, we replicate the Reflexion pipeline using the official sandboxed execution environment. Each generated solution is executed against the hidden unit tests, and performance is reported using the standard pass@1 metric. Following the original Reflexion protocol, each problem receives one initial attempt followed by a single reflection-guided retry.

Discussion

In this section, we present the results of these experiments and compare our replicated performance with the findings reported in the Reflexion paper.

HotPotQA Discussion

Our replication of HotPotQA shows similar results to the improvements reported in the original Reflexion paper but the gains plateau around 45-50% even with additional reflective attempts. We note that our replication runs were ran using GPT-3.5 for cost reasons, whereas the original reflexion paper is vague in their choice of model, and this difference could be a source of discrepancy between our results. We found the EM metric often penalizes semantically correct answers, labeling them as failures due to formatting or synonym mismatches. This creates a structural limitation: even with better reasoning, the agent cannot improve beyond what EM will credit. We hypothesize that this explains the limited improvement of
MAR
over the Reflexion baseline.

HumanEval Discussion

shows that our replication confirms the main takeaway of the original Reflexion paper: reflection-guided retries meaningfully improve code correctness. Although our gains were slightly smaller, the trend clearly holds.

Inspection of logged trajectories revealed two recurring failure modes that likely contributed to the reduced improvement: (1) confirmation bias, where the single-agent Reflexion loop repeats its initial flawed reasoning because the same model acts as Actor, Evaluator, and Reflector; and (2) mode collapse, where the Actor reproduces nearly identical solutions across retries despite receiving feedback, resulting in repeated failures on the same conceptual error (see appendix A and B for illustrative examples).

These observations motivated our Multi-Agent Reflexion extension, which explicitly separates acting, evaluation, and critique across multiple personas to inject diversity and reduce these failure modes.

Extensions

In this section, we introduce
Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR)
, our extension to the Reflexion framework that replaces single-agent self-critique with a coordinated team of persona driven critics meant to address the core failure modes we observed in replication.

Motivation

LLMs frequently struggle to revise their own reasoning, and even with external feedback they tend to reinforce the same flawed chain of thought across successive attempts. This phenomenon is described as degeneration-of-thought
(
8
)
or the mental set problem
(
9
)
. A central cause of this issue is that self-reflective approaches depend on fixed internal thinking patterns. Even when agents are prompted with different personas, their underlying reasoning strategies often remain uniform. This uniformity prevents the model from exploring alternative perspectives and makes it difficult to escape entrenched errors. These limitations are reflected in the behavior of methods such as Reflexion
(
11
)
and related approaches
(
6
;
10
)
, which frequently plateau on complex reasoning tasks and can sometimes reinforce earlier mistakes instead of correcting them
(
5
;
13
;
15
)
.

In our replication of Reflexion, these limitations manifested consistently. The single-agent loop exhibited two recurring failure modes:

1.

Confirmation Bias in Self-Reflection.

The Actor frequently committed an initial reasoning error. The Evaluator, drawn from the same model family, then attempted to critique the trajectory. However, instead of isolating the root cause, the reflection often:

•

Restated the incorrect reasoning.

•

Justified the error through additional flawed logic.

•

Produced superficial edits.

2.

Mode Collapse Across Iterations.

Because the same model generated, evaluated, and revised the reasoning, the corrections tended to follow a narrow and repetitive template. In HumanEval, we observed multiple instances in which the system reproduced:

•

Identical buggy loop structures.

•

Repeated indexing mistakes.

•

The same off-by-one error.

With a single model acting as its own generator, evaluator, and critic tends to reproduce the same reasoning structure across iterations with little correction. As such, our proposed Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR) framework explicitly address these weakness by incorporating deliberately varied reasoning strategies into the reflection loop, with a judge that takes away as much meaningful conclusion as possible from each debate.

Extension: Multi-Agent Debate as Reflection

Figure 2:
The Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR) Architecture.
This high-level diagram illustrates the extension of the single-agent Reflexion framework.

Our extension replaces the single self-reflecting model in Reflexion with a group of LLM agents that each serve as distinct critics. When the Actor produces an incorrect answer, the system does not rely on a single reflection. Instead, it initiates a structured multi-agent debate in which several persona-guided critics analyze the failed reasoning from different perspectives. Each critic contributes alternative hypotheses, highlights potential flaws, and proposes corrective strategies. A debate coordinator then aggregates these perspectives into a final consensus reflection, which is appended to the Actor’s memory for the next attempt.

The Multi-Agent Reflexion loop works as follows:

1.

Actor Attempt:
the Actor first attempts the question using the same baseline model as in standard Reflexion.

2.

Evaluation:
If incorrect, the system triggers the debate module. Note that this uses the same evaluator as the original Reflexion framework.

3.

Initial Diagnosis:
The failed thoughts (scratchpad) of the actor are passed to the judge. The judge asks each persona to write a diagnosis as to why the actor failed the task.

4.

Debates:
After each persona has made a diagnosis, the judge asks whether other personas agree or disagree and refine their critiques.

5.

Consensus and Reflection
The judge synthesizes the debate into a single, actionable ”Consensus Reflection” which includes error outputs, debate logs, and consensus between debaters depending on the task. If there has been no consensus reached between the debaters, then the personas debate for another round (maximum 2 rounds).

6.

Retry
This reflection is injected into the Actor’s memory, guiding the next attempt with specific advice on how to avoid previous pitfalls.

Personas in Multi-Agent Debate

A key component of MAR is the use of intentionally diverse critic personas. We designed personas in a systemic way such that reasoning tendencies differ, inspired by the Society of Mind framework and the divergent-thinking objectives of Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) (Liang et al., 2024).

We define the following axes to vary across for persona creation. For any given dataset, we carefully craft a set of persona to cover a comprehensively cover these these axes.

•

Evidence Exploitation:
how strongly the critic demands
explicit textual support for actions.

•

Exploration:
whether the critic tries to actively pushes the debate toward alternative hypotheses.

•

Specification strictness:
how much the critic optimizes for exact compliance with the metric.

Within this design space, we instantiate the following critics
(see Appendix C for full prompts):

•

Verifier
(high exploit, low exploration, medium strictness)
chains of thought.

•

Skeptic
(low exploit, high exploration, medium strictness)
tuned to counter confirmation bias.

•

Logician
(high exploit, low exploration, high strictness)

•

Creative
(low exploit, high exploration, high exploration)

Concrete definitions of personas are given in the appendix (see appendix item C), along with exact prompt used and reasoning behind personas. As future work, we may ask an LLM to generate personas on these axes for a specific dataset.

Experimental Setup

To evaluate our Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR), our experimental configuration mirrors the structure of the original Reflexion framework where possible, differing only in replacing the single-agent reflection step with a structured multi-agent debate. We use GPT-3.5 Turbo for all agent roles (actor, debaters, and judge) which allows us to isolate the impact of persona-guided multi-agent reasoning while preserving Reflexion’s core design.

Across both HotPotQA and HumanEval, we designed our agent personas to reflect the dominant failure modes observed during replication. For HotPotQA, we selected personas focused on factual grounding, logical consistency, alternative hypothesis generation, and error checking. For code generation, we used personas mirroring real world software roles (e.g., Senior Engineer, QA Engineer, Code Reviewer) to capture algorithmic and specification considerations. Personas were engineered to disagree naturally and expose different lines of reasoning, so that debates surface diverse critiques rather than stylistic variations.

We cap debates at two rounds, based on empirical logs and prior MAD work showing that nearly all meaningful disagreements arise within the first two exchanges. Trial budgets were also kept intentionally small (five trials for HotPotQA and three for HumanEval), to keep API costs lower. While increasing the limit did yield marginal improvements, we observed sharply diminishing returns beyond these thresholds, accompanied by rapidly increasing API cost and latency. This setup therefore preserves the core design principles of Reflexion while isolating the specific contribution of multi-agent reasoning.

Takeaways

Reduced Degeneration of Thought.

Single-agent Reflexion often suffers from “degeneration of thought,” where the model repeats the same flawed reasoning across retries. Because the Actor, Evaluator, and Reflector are all the same model, its reflections tend to reinforce the original misconception rather than correct it. MAR mitigates this by introducing multiple agents with distinct reasoning styles. Their disagreements surface alternative interpretations of the specification and error trace, helping the system escape the mental-set failures that limit Reflexion.

Higher-quality revisions through aggregation.

Each MAR trial ends with a judge model that selects the most coherent and spec-aligned proposal from the debate. In practice, agents catch different issues. One may focus on specification accuracy, another on edge cases, another on logic or style. The judge synthesizes these perspectives, producing patches that are more reliable than the single-agent self-reflection loop.

Limitations

While MAR consistently outperforms single-agent Reflexion, this improvement comes with a significant computational cost. Each failed attempt triggers multiple critic responses, a structured debate, and a judge synthesis step, expanding both token usage and context length. In practice, this leads to roughly a 3x increase in API calls and latency compared to Reflexion. As a result, MAR is substantially more expensive to run, and its scalability is constrained when deployed on large benchmarks or real-world agentic systems. Reducing debate depth or dynamically selecting which personas to invoke may help mitigate these costs in future work.

Overall, the goal of Multi-Agent Reflexion is to inject structured disagreement and diverse reasoning into the reflection step, reducing confirmation bias and improving the agent’s ability to escape repeated failures.

Conclusions

In this work, we introduced Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR), a multi-agent extension of the Reflexion framework designed to address key failure modes in single-agent self-improvement. Most notably cognitive entrenchment and the degeneration-of-thought problem. Across both reasoning (HotPotQA) and program synthesis (HumanEval-Python), MAR consistently outperforms the GPT-3.5 baseline and single-agent Reflexion, demonstrating that diversified critique and structured disagreement substantially enhance error diagnosis and correction.

Looking forward, several directions appear promising: experimenting with MAD paradigms that reduce context lengths, as demonstrated by

Societal Impact

Our work highlights both the potential benefits and risks of developing self-improving LLM agents. Multi-agent debate offers a scalable way to elicit diverse reasoning and richer critiques without gradient-based training, suggesting a path toward more transparent and interpretable AI systems. However, our experiments also reveal notable societal concerns. Because our multi-agent Reflexion pipeline required roughly 300–400 API calls per task, around three times the cost of single-agent Reflexion, it substantially increases computational demand, energy consumption, and therefore environmental impact, while also raising economic and accessibility barriers. Moreover, self-refining agents that generate and act on their own critiques introduce safety and governance challenges, as emergent multi-agent behaviors may become harder to predict or align. By demonstrating both the promise and the heavy computational footprint of multi-agent reflexive systems, our work underscores the need for careful evaluation of efficiency, reliability, and the broader societal consequences of deploying increasingly autonomous LLM agents.

semantically correct. A representative example from our MAR HotPotQA runs is

Question.

"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"

All five trials are marked incorrect under EM, despite the fact that
“women’s interest magazines” is:

•

semantically identical to the core label “women interest magazine”,

•

factually correct for both
Woman’s Era

Naj

•

only missing the non-essential temporal modifier “fortnightly”,

•

and differing only in surface form (pluralization and wording).

This illustrates the broader issue mentioned in the main text: these
superficial mismatches arise even when the answer matches human expectations.
Under a more forgiving metric (e.g., F1, or a semantic-match / LLM-judge
scoring scheme), this example would be counted as correct, and the apparent
gap between MAR and the original Reflexion results would likely shrink in
favor of MAR.

Reasoning Personas for HotPotQA

Verifier

You are a Verifier. Your job is to check each claim carefully for factual correctness and internal logical consistency. For each assertion the Actor or another agent makes, ask yourself: “Is this backed by evidence or context? Could this be wrong?” Discard any reasoning steps that lack justification, ambiguous references, or unsupported assumptions. If you find an error or gap, call it out explicitly and explain why it might be wrong.

Planner

You are a Planner. You care about the high-level structure of the reasoning. Before diving into low-level details, outline a strategy: what steps to take, what subproblems to solve, and in what order. If earlier attempts failed, propose a different overall plan (alternative breakdown). Your reflections should focus on planning, not just individual mistakes.

Skeptic

You are a Skeptic. Assume earlier reasoning may have hallucinations or leaps. Critique every assumption, spec, and inference. Ask: “How do I know this is true?”, “What if the premise is wrong?”, “Is there another possible interpretation?” Your goal is to prevent overconfidence and surface plausible failure modes.

Logician

You are a Strict Logician. Evaluate whether the answer exactly matches the specification or asked question. Do not accept vague matches, implied meanings, or partially correct statements. If the requirement asks for a full definition, full proof, or exact formatting, check strictly for compliance.

Creative

You are a Creative Thinker. If conventional reasoning fails or stalls, propose unforeseen angles. Look for edge cases, trick questions, alternative interpretations, or unusual solutions. Your reflections should expand the search space rather than refine within the existing pattern.

Meta-Reflector

You are a Meta-Reflector. After seeing multiple failed attempts, reflect not just on code or reasoning errors but on the overall process. Ask: “Why did we keep failing?”, “Are we stuck in a loop of similar mistakes?”, “Should we change the memory buffer, retry policy, or strategy type?” Suggest meta-changes: different prompting style, more memory, switching reasoning mode, or abandoning this approach.
