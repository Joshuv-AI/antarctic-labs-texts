Leveraging large language models on free-text symptoms from participatory surveillance enhances pertussis forecasting in the United States
Authors: Luigi De Angelis, Samuele Tarallo, Caterina Rizzo, Autumn Gertz, Francesco Baglivo, John S Brownstein, Benjamin Rader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeclines in childhood vaccination in the U.S. have contributed to a resurgence of vaccine-preventable diseases, including a notable increase in pertussis cases. Although pertussis signals are difficult to detect due to low incidence and symptom overlap with other infections, ONM collects free-text descriptions that may contain pertussis-specific information. Background

Rapidly declining childhood vaccination rates have led to an increase in vaccine-preventable pediatric diseases in the United States (U.S) [1, 2]. Recent data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) indicate a surge in pertussis cases, with over 35,000 cases documented in 2024 and 6,600 cases in the first 3 months of 2025 [2]. This resurgence is attributed to a combination of a rebound following reduced circulation of pertussis during the COVID-19 pandemic and concurrent declines in vaccination rates [3]. This data is alarming, particularly for the pediatric population, as pertussis can be extremely severe, and sometimes fatal, among infants and young children.

In the U.S., pertussis surveillance has historically depended on the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS), a passive framework established in 1922. This traditional public health surveillance system relies primarily on case-based reporting from healthcare providers and laboratories. While essential, such systems are subject to underreporting, particularly for pertussis, which often presents with non-specific symptoms among adults and vaccinated children and is not routinely tested [4]. The current pertussis case definition, updated by the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists (CSTE) in 2020, requires a cough illness lasting at least two weeks, in the absence of a more likely diagnosis, with at least one additional characteristic symptom [5].

In parallel to traditional systems, participatory surveillance platforms have emerged as a complementary tool for tracking infectious diseases. For example, Outbreaks Near Me (ONM), collects reports of Influenza-Like Illness (ILI) symptoms from individuals in the U.S. and Canada via a brief weekly survey since 2011 (formerly Flu Near You) [6]. While these platforms have shown some promise in disambiguating the etiology of co-occurring respiratory infections, they generally focus on high-prevalence respiratory illnesses such as ILI and COVID-19 [7]. A recent systematic review of 29 global participatory surveillance systems found that most tools focused on ILI, and none on pertussis [8]. This likely reflects the disease’s low prevalence, its pediatric-specific population, the substantial overlap of its symptoms in adults with those of other respiratory infections, and the difficulty of distinguishing pertussis-specific clinical patterns within structured self-reported symptoms. Notably, participatory surveillance systems often collect free-text symptom descriptions that could capture distinctive features of diseases like pertussis (e.g., “whooping sound” or “vomiting after coughing”), but this type of data has not yet been systematically analyzed for pertussis surveillance [9].

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing (NLP) now offer the opportunity to leverage such unstructured information for disease detection and forecasting [10]. Recent work has shown that NLP methods can be used to extract clinically meaningful signals from structured and unstructured text for syndromic surveillance using social media data [11]. While LLM applications in infectious diseases are often highlighted for their use as patient-facing chatbots for tasks such as health promotion or vaccine education, their potential as programmatic NLP tools for analyzing unstructured text for surveillance is a critical, emerging application [12].

This study aims to evaluate whether pertussis forecasting in the United States can be improved by leveraging LLM tools and free-text data from the ONM participatory surveillance platform. By exploring this novel integration of LLMs into participatory surveillance, we aim to demonstrate the feasibility of repurposing data from already-collected participatory surveillance for highly prevalent respiratory illnesses to enhance forecasting of resurgent pediatric infectious diseases.

Methods

We analyzed weekly pertussis surveillance data in the United States spanning January 2022 through July 2025, integrating traditional epidemiologic sources with participatory surveillance data refined using LLMs. This timeframe was selected because pre-pandemic ONM data were unavailable and 2020–2021 were excluded due to substantial pandemic-related disruptions in reporting and we wanted to focus our evaluation under current post-pandemic surveillance conditions.

Weekly provisional case counts were extracted from the CDC’s NNDSS [13]. These reports reflect cases submitted by state and territorial health departments based on the CSTE 2020 case definition [14]. No retrospective revisions were applied; analyses were based on the case count reported during each epidemiologic week. These data were cleaned and standardized, with conversion of missing weekly case counts to zero, harmonization of state identifiers and week formats and, most notably, a missing report for Week 12 of 2023 was corrected using CDC WONDER, an online database offering a query system to extract CDC data [15]. National-level time series were constructed by aggregating state-level counts.

Self-reported symptom data were extracted from Boston Children’s Hospital’s ONM participatory surveillance system. ONM is a web-based participatory syndromic surveillance platform focused on respiratory illness. A weekly survey is sent to registered users via email or text asking how they feel. Unregistered users can submit reports directly on the website without creating an account. Registered users can also report on the health of household members. All reports start with a prompt of whether they are feeling “healthy” or “not feeling well.” Users that report they are not feeling well are asked about symptoms (pre-populated list including “other”), date of onset, type of care sought (if any), testing (including at-home), diagnosis, and prescribed medication. Weekly files from January 2022 to July 2025 were batch-processed to standardized date fields using the ISO-week standard and to calculate duration of time the participant has been experiencing symptoms, as the difference between the date of self-reported start of symptoms and the date when the survey was filed. Multiple participant entries in the same week were removed, keeping only the most recent entry for each week. Reports were retained if they described a cough lasting more than seven days and fewer than 100 days and lacked any self-reported alternative diagnosis among the following: COVID-19, influenza, RSV, common cold, or strep throat. This rule-based filter, based on cough presence, symptoms duration, and the absence of other respiratory diagnoses, is adapted following the clinical criteria of the CSTE case definition. The cutoff of one week instead of two was chosen based on evidence that, among vaccinated individuals, cough duration may be shorter, and on the idea that the purpose of our study is to detect signals of pertussis at the population level before they are officially reported [16, 17]. It should be noted that the ONM survey did not collect data in a structured format on characteristic symptoms of pertussis, such as paroxysms of coughing, inspiratory whoop, post-tussive vomiting, or apnea; however, a free-text field for self-reported symptoms was included in the survey. This filtering approach is based on a strong assumption that all the patients with cough and at least one week of symptoms are considered as possible pertussis cases, in the absence of another self-reported diagnosis of respiratory infection. This assumption is likely to lead to a low specificity of this criterion, which we cannot directly test, given that we do not have data on whether each individual in ONM was diagnosed with pertussis.

To enhance the specificity of filtered pertussis cases, we created a subset based on a two-step NLP pipeline using GPT-4 models (GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o) via the OpenAI API. This decomposed prompting strategy allowed simpler summarization tasks to be handled by a smaller model, while reserving more complex clinical reasoning for a more capable model, improving both efficiency and transparency. First, GPT-4o-mini transformed structured and free-text symptoms and demographic data into concise, anonymous clinical summaries. Second, GPT-4o evaluated these summaries to exclude cases inconsistent with pertussis. This prompt, instructed the model to exclude summaries describing symptoms highly characteristic of another illness or features contradictory to pertussis (e.g. “high fever”). Conversely, it was instructed to keep records containing key pertussis indicators, such as “whooping” or “post-tussive vomiting”. All outputs were required in JSON format and subsequently aggregated into a single JSON file for each year. Standard prompt engineering methods, such as decomposed and role-based prompting were employed [18]. The complete prompt strategy is available as supplementary material S1.

Three datasets were constructed for time-series analysis: (1) a baseline dataset using CDC-only case counts, (2) a dataset including both CDC case counts and ONM filtered cases as separate predictor variables, and (3) a dataset including both CDC case counts and post-LLM refined ONM cases as separate predictor variables. Importantly, ONM and CDC counts were not summed; rather, the ONM data served as a complementary feature alongside traditional reporting.

Each dataset was temporally aligned on a weekly basis and aggregated at the national level. Forecasting models were trained on data from 2022 to 2024 and tested on the first seven months of 2025 to simulate real-time performance. The primary target was the weekly CDC-reported pertussis case count. We employed three different forecasting models: ARIMA, XG-Boost, and linear regression (LR). Accuracy was assessed using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) over the test set. MAE was selected because it provides an interpretable measure of average prediction error in case counts, is robust to occasional reporting outliers, and avoids instability issues common to relative error metrics when weekly counts are low.

The following time-related features were extracted: year, month, week, quarter, and a boolean indicator (is_holiday) identifying weeks overlapping with major U.S. federal holidays to account for potential reporting fluctuations during these periods. These variables, together with the rolling means and standard deviations of CDC case counts computed over two- and four-week windows, were used as predictors in the time series analysis for XG-Boost and LR models.

Hyperparameter tuning was performed for each model using Bayesian optimization implemented employing Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPE) [19], to efficiently explore the search space and prioritize promising regions. Model performance was optimized using the MAE as the objective function, with a fixed random seed to ensure replicability. For each model, ARIMA, XGBoost, and Linear Regression, key hyperparameters related to lag structure, model complexity, and regularization were tuned over 2,000 optimization trials. Detailed parameter explanations and ranges are reported in the Supplementary Materials (S2).

The best-performing optimized model on the CDC-only dataset was subsequently evaluated on the time series containing ONM data. To ensure a fair comparison, the hyperparameter optimization was conducted again for this model on both the “CDC and ONM filtered” and “CDC and ONM LLM-refined” datasets. This comparative performance analysis quantified the added predictive value of incorporating participatory data in its filtered and LLM-enhanced forms, measured through a reduction in the MAE.

To evaluate the consistency of the LLM-enhanced signal and the model’s performance at finer spatial scales, we extended the analysis to the nine U.S. Census Divisions. This sub-national evaluation serves to determine if the predictive gains observed at the national level are robust across geographically diverse regions with varying pertussis incidence and reporting behaviors. We opted for Census Division-level aggregation rather than state-level analysis because the latter suffered from extreme data sparsity.

For this analysis, we adopted the same protocol used for the national level, utilizing a global XGBoost model trained across all divisions. Hyperparameter optimization was re-conducted for each of the three dataset variants (CDC-only, CDC and ONM Filtered, and CDC and ONM LLM-refined) at the sub-national level.

All the data processing and analyses, except for forecasting, were conducted using R 4.3.1. software. Time-series forecasting analysis was conducted using Python 3.12.2, with Optuna and DARTS libraries.

Results

Between January 2022 and July 2025, a total of 20,587 records were included in the final CDC dataset. The CDC’s NNDSS reported 862 pertussis cases in 2022, 2,512 in 2023, 11,276 in 2024, and 5,937 in the first seven months of 2025.

The ONM dataset yielded 836,910 reports in 2022, 833,454 in 2023, 689,938 in 2024, and 214,346 in the first seven months of 2025.

After applying the rule-based filter, 757 (0.09%) records in 2022, 637 (0.08%) in 2023, 846 (0.12%) in 2024, and 501 (0.23%) in 2025 were included in the following analyses.

Overall, after filtering, 2,741 reports were retained as possible pertussis cases. Subsequent LLM-based refinement excluded 1,784 reports, resulting in a subset of 957 LLM-processed ONM cases for forecasting integration. A summary of these results is reported in Table 1.

When comparing forecasting models trained on 2022–2024 data and evaluated on weekly pertussis case counts from the first seven months of 2025 (CDC data only), the optimized ARIMA model, characterized by an autoregressive order (p) of 5, differencing order (d) of 2, and moving average order (q) of 3, achieved a MAE of 41.82, representing the weakest predictive performance among the models tested. The optimized LR model, using two target lags (past values of the case counts) and one past covariate lag (past values of other time-related predictors), modestly improved accuracy with a MAE of 39.82. In contrast, the optimized XGBoost model substantially outperformed both, achieving a MAE of 26.65, underscoring its superior ability to capture temporal dependencies and nonlinear relationships within the CDC time series.

Given its stronger baseline performance, the XGBoost model architecture was subsequently optimized for the two extended datasets incorporating participatory surveillance data. Figure 1 visually compares the forecasts from these three distinct, separately optimized XGBoost models. The performance improved with the inclusion of participatory data. Compared to the baseline CDC-only model (MAE = 26.65), the model trained with CDC and ONM filtered reports yielded a lower forecast error (MAE = 25.60). Integrating the LLM-refined ONM data provided the highest accuracy, achieving the lowest MAE of 24.69, representing a 7.4% reduction compared to the CDC-only baseline.

In a head-to-head comparison of all three datasets, the LLM-refined model was the top performer in three divisions (East South Central, Mountain, and Pacific), while the rule-based ONM model yielded the lowest error in four other divisions (South Atlantic, West North Central, and West South Central, and New England).

Notably, the LLM-processed data reduced error compared to the CDC-only baseline in 6 of 9 divisions (66.7%), whereas the rule-based filter provided improvements in only 4 of 9 (44.4%).

Discussion

Participatory surveillance platforms have proven valuable for tracking high-prevalence ILI and COVID-19, but this approach struggles with pertussis. The disease’s lower prevalence and substantial symptom overlap with other respiratory infections in adults make it exceptionally difficult to identify from clinical data [20]. This study demonstrates the feasibility of an innovative approach integrating LLM-refined participatory surveillance data into traditional epidemiological forecasting frameworks to improve pertussis outbreak prediction in the United States. Our findings suggest that participatory data, when post-processed with LLM-based methods, can meaningfully reduce forecasting error, even for diseases like pertussis that are not the primary focus of participatory surveillance platforms.

A central consideration in our study design was the decision to use weekly provisional case counts from the CDC’s NNDSS without retroactive correction. Although final pertussis case counts are typically revised upward as jurisdictions complete their reporting, we deliberately used the unadjusted weekly counts to mirror a real-time public health forecasting context [21].

The ONM platform, originally designed for ILI and later expanded to include COVID-19 surveillance, contains a large volume of reports, many of which pertain to common respiratory viruses or include no symptoms at all [18]. Consequently, only a small fraction of reports meet criteria consistent with possible pertussis. Nonetheless, we observed an increase in both the absolute number and the proportion of ONM participants meeting the filtering criteria in 2025 (0.23%) and 2024 (0.12%) compared to previous years (0.09% in 2022, 0.08% in 2023). Considering that only 35% of ONM users who report ILI symptoms seek medical attention, it is reasonable to assume that most ONM participants with pertussis-like symptoms likewise do not seek care, reinforcing the potential of participatory surveillance as a valuable complementary source to traditional pertussis surveillance systems [22]. In addition, ONM data are processed in near real time (typically within 1–2 days), whereas CDC notifiable disease data are released with a median delay of approximately 40 days, highlighting the potential of ONM to support earlier situational awareness [23].

The sub-national analysis demonstrates that incorporating participatory surveillance generally stabilizes predictive performance. While the rule-based approach yielded the lowest absolute error in four divisions, the broad success of the LLM-enhanced model across two-thirds of the U.S. Census Divisions underscores its potential as a robust tool for cross-regional syndromic surveillance.

To date, the only comparable effort to augment pertussis forecasting with alternative data streams is a study that used Google Trends search volumes as an external regressor, achieving a 6% reduction in MAE over a 2-week forecasting horizon [24]. Our framework achieved a larger percentage reduction (7.4%) on a shorter, 1-week horizon. This gain is relevant for two reasons. First, from a modeling perspective, reducing error on short-term (1-week) forecasts typically exhibit lower baseline variance, making incremental gains more difficult to achieve. Second, from a public health standpoint, the 1-week horizon represents the most actionable window for operational response, such as localized resource allocation or targeted clinical alerts, where even modest increases in precision can help mitigate secondary transmission chains [25].

Beyond pertussis, our AI-driven framework illustrates a novel approach for capturing early population-level signals of understudied infectious diseases by leveraging existing participatory data and refining them through LLM-based contextualization. Here, we demonstrated a proof of concept: participatory surveillance data, when augmented with LLMs, can be effectively repurposed for syndromic surveillance of an underreported disease with significant syndromic overlap with common seasonal pathogens. AI tools like LLMs have the potential to unlock already collected but underused digital health data sources for real-time surveillance of a wide range of conditions, such as RSV or vector-borne diseases.

By leveraging existing participatory data sources and cloud-based LLM processing, this approach is resource-efficient and cost-effective compared with building new surveillance infrastructure. For example, the total cost of running the entire LLM-based processing pipeline for this study was less than 10 USD and required less than 10 min. With continued declines in API costs, this figure is expected to decrease further, making such analyses increasingly affordable and scalable for real-time public health applications [26]. The flexibility and scalability of this pipeline could be applied to other digital surveillance systems with minimal adaptation, such as Influenzanet, a European counterpart of ONM [27].

This study has several limitations. First, without access to laboratory confirmation or clinical validation of ONM reports, we cannot quantify the true positive rate of our possible pertussis cases. Second, while the sub-national analysis at the census divisions level demonstrated the framework’s regional robustness, data sparsity at the state level remains a significant barrier to localized forecasting. Third, our analysis relies on provisional notifiable disease data, as retrospectively corrected case counts are not available from CDC at a weekly temporal resolution and are released with substantial delays, limiting their applicability for real-time forecasting. Finally, while the superior performance of XGBoost highlights the advantages of nonlinear ensemble methods in capturing complex temporal dependencies and interactions between variables, future work could explore additional modeling approaches that may further improve performance.

Conclusions

Our findings illustrate the potential of using LLMs to enhance participatory surveillance data for forecasting diseases beyond their original scope. Integrating advanced AI tools can enable broader, more flexible disease monitoring without requiring additional data collection. This approach may be especially valuable for low-incidence conditions that are difficult to resource and track with traditional surveillance alone. Future work should explore the real-time implementation of LLM-based pipelines within participatory platforms, assess performance in decentralized or local settings, and evaluate the utility of these approaches for syndromic surveillance beyond respiratory illnesses. In addition, research should examine how output from participatory models can be best communicated to and utilized by public health authorities for timely action [28]. By embracing these innovations, public health systems can move toward a more flexible, data-driven approach to outbreak detection and preparedness.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Participatory surveillance systems have been widely used for influenza-like illness and COVID-19, but their potential for monitoring low-incidence diseases such as pertussis remains unclear. We searched for existing evidence on participatory surveillance or digital data sources applied to pertussis detection or forecasting. In October 2025, we conducted a systematic search of PubMed and Web of Science for peer-reviewed articles, using combinations of terms related to participatory surveillance (“participatory surveillance”, “crowdsourced surveillance”, “internet-based surveillance”, “citizen science”) and pertussis (“pertussis”, “whooping cough”). None of the studies described the use of participatory surveillance for pertussis detection or forecasting. Only one study used Google Trends as an alternative digital data source to improve pertussis predictions. Although several participatory platforms collect free-text symptom descriptions, no prior work has analyzed these unstructured data using natural language processing or large language models for pertussis surveillance.

Added value of this study

This study is the first to apply large language models (LLMs) to free-text symptom reports from a participatory surveillance system to enhance forecasting of a re-emerging vaccine-preventable disease. By combining rule-based filtering with an LLM pipeline that summarized and excluded reports inconsistent with pertussis, we extracted a refined set of suspected cases from Outbreaks Near Me that had never previously been used in pertussis surveillance. When incorporated into XGBoost forecasting models, these LLM-processed data improved predictive accuracy for 2025 pertussis activity, reducing the mean absolute error from 26.65 (CDC-only baseline) to 24.69. Our findings show that free-text fields within participatory surveillance platforms contain meaningful, underutilized disease-specific information that can be identified with modern AI tools. This study demonstrates that LLM-enhanced participatory surveillance can augment traditional pertussis surveillance.

Implications of all the available evidence

The available evidence shows that participatory surveillance has been underused for low-incidence respiratory diseases. Our findings suggest that free-text data already collected by these systems can be repurposed using LLMs to generate meaningful signals that complement traditional surveillance for pertussis. This low-cost and scalable approach can be adapted and extended to other underreported or emerging infections, offering a practical way to strengthen infectious disease forecasting without requiring new data collection.
