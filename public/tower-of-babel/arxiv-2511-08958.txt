Space-Efficient and Output-Sensitive Algorithms for the Longest Common Bitonic Subsequence
Md. Tanzeem Rahat, Md. Manzurul Hasan

Abstract
The longest common bitonic subsequence (LCBS) of two sequences A A and B B is the longest subsequence that increases to a single peak and then decreases while appearing, in order, in both inputs. Although LCBS naturally models “rise–fall” patterns in bioinformatics, finance and signal analysis, the only previously documented solution was the quadratic dynamic programme that needs Θ ⁡ ( n ​ m ) \Theta(nm) time and space. We show that this space barrier is not inherent: a refined rolling-row implementation evaluates the same recurrence in Θ ⁡ ( n ​ m ) \Theta(nm) time with only Θ ⁡ ( min ⁡ { n , m } ) \Theta(\min\{n,m\}) additional memory. Finally, by isolating the ℳ \mathcal{M} symbol matches and their 𝒞 \mathcal{C} bitonic-compatible pairs, we cast LCBS as a longest-path problem in a sparse DAG and solve it in O ⁡ ( ( n + m ) ​ log ⁡ n + ℳ ​ log 2 ​ ℳ ) ​ time and ​ O ​ ( ℳ ) ​ space O\bigl((n+m)\log n+\mathcal{M}\log^{2}\!\mathcal{M}\bigr)\ \text{time and}\ O(\mathcal{M})\ \text{space} , which is asymptotically faster than the quadratic baseline whenever ℳ ≪ n ​ m \mathcal{M}\ll nm . These results make exact LCBS computation practical for inputs that were previously out of reach and expose a new fine-grained complexity landscape that invites further exploration.

Introduction

Over the past half-century,
subsequence‐based similarity measures
have become indispensable tools in

At the centre of this landscape lie three classical one-sequence problems:

For two sequences, the canonical measure is the

LCS lacks truly sub-quadratic algorithms; Abboud, Backurs, and Williams

Adding monotonicity constraints gives rise to the

The LCBS problem and its significance.

Bitonicity
captures patterns that rise to a local maximum before declining—“mountain shapes” that abound in practice:
gene-expression profiles that surge and fade during a cell cycle, commodity prices that climb and correct, or heart-rate traces that accelerate and decelerate during exercise.
Detecting such a trend simultaneously in two (possibly noisy) series motivates the

LCBS combines the LCS

(i) the problem is clearly at least as hard as LCIS, whose SETH lower bounds rule out a general sub-quadratic algorithm, and

, less than quadratic

Conceptual contributions.

Beyond the raw bounds, our study delivers two insights of independent interest:

1.

Compatibility sparsification.

By isolating value-equal matches and operating only on their compatibility, we pinpoint the exact structure responsible for hardness and open a route to instance-sensitive preprocessing and multivariate lower bounds in the spirit of Bringmann and Künnemann

2.

Peak-aware linear-space scheduling.

We show how to maintain the two LCBS phases with rolling rows and light column buffers, conceptually extending Hirschberg-style ideas to shape-constrained two-sequence problems.

2.5

Algorithm

\Theta(m)

\Theta(m)

\Theta(nm)\;+\;\Theta(nm)\;+\;O(|\texttt{inc}\cap\texttt{dec}|)\;=\;\Theta(nm),

Proof.

Immediately before updating row

When the two input sequences contain only a small number

3.5
Correctness

We prove that Algorithm
5
returns the length of a
longest common bitonic subsequence
(LCBS) of
A
and
B
.
Let

V=\{(i,j)\mid A[i]=B[j]\}

be the vertex set and

E=\bigl\{(u,v)\mid u=(i,j),\,v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),\,i<i^{\prime},\,j<j^{\prime},\,A[i]<A[i^{\prime}]\bigr\}

the directed–acyclic edge set for the increasing half; the decreasing half is defined symmetrically. The DAG
(V,E)
contains a path of length
\ell

iff

\ell
is the length of an LCBS.

Organisation of the proof.

(1)

A vertex processed during the forward (resp. backward) pass sees exactly the lengths of
all
valid predecessors (resp. successors) when it is queried.

(2)

The value written to
INC[v]
(resp.
DEC[v]
) therefore equals the optimal length of a strictly increasing (resp. strictly decreasing) common subsequence ending (resp. starting) at
v
.

(3)

Combining the two arrays yields
INC[v]+DEC[v]-1
,  the length of a complete bitonic subsequence with
peak

v
.

(4)

Taking the maximum over all vertices returns the LCBS length.

We now justify each step in detail.

(1) Topological processing order.

Lemma 8
.

During the forward pass the vertices are visited in a topological order that respects
i
and
j
:
if
(u,v)\in E
then
u
is processed before
v
.
During the backward pass the mirrored
j
-rank

\widehat{r}_{J}=MAX_{J}-r_{J}+1
restores the same property for
successors
.

Proof.

The list
MATCHES
is sorted lexicographically
by
(i\uparrow,j\uparrow)
, so
u=(i,j)
precedes

v=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
whenever
i<i^{\prime}
or
i=i^{\prime}
and
j<j^{\prime}
.
But
(i,i^{\prime})=(i,i^{\prime})
with
i=i^{\prime}
cannot occur in
E

because
A[i]=A[i^{\prime}]
would violate the strict value inequality
A[i]<A[i^{\prime}]
needed for a subsequence. Hence the order is topological for
E
. Mirroring the
j
-rank in the second pass transforms the test
j^{\prime}>j
into
\widehat{r}_{J}<\widehat{r}_{J}(v)
, turning every successor into a
prefix
of the mirrored axis
and preserving acyclicity.
∎

(2) Exact dynamic-programming values.

Lemma 9
.

Immediately after the update of a vertex
v

INC[v]=1+\max_{u\to v}INC[u].

An analogous statement holds for
DEC
in the backward pass.

Proof.

Consider the forward scan. By Lemma
8
every predecessor
u
has been processed already and inserted into
\mathsf{RT}_{inc}
at key
(r_{J}(u),r_{V}(u))
. A valid predecessor additionally satisfies
r_{J}(u)<r_{J}(v)
and
r_{V}(u)<r_{V}(v)
,
therefore lies within the range rectangle
(r_{J}{<}r_{J}(v),\,r_{V}{<}r_{V}(v))
. The
RT_Query
operation returns the maximum
INC[u]
among exactly those points, and adding 1 accounts for
v
itself. No illegal edge can contribute because
(i)
equal values share the same
r_{V}
and are excluded by the strict inequality, and
(ii)
indices with
j^{\prime}\geq j
are outside the range.
∎

(3) Bitonic length of a fixed peak.

For any vertex
v
the concatenation of an optimal increasing prefix ending in
v
and an optimal decreasing suffix starting in
v
is a feasible bitonic subsequence whose length is
INC[v]+DEC[v]-1
(the vertex
v
is counted twice). By Lemma
9
each component is individually optimal, so the sum is the
longest
bitonic subsequence that uses
v
as its peak.

(4) Global optimality.

Theorem 10
.

Algorithm
5
outputs the LCBS length.

Proof.

Let
v^{\star}
be the peak of a
globally
longest LCBS
P^{\star}
.
Its length satisfies

|P^{\star}|\leq INC[v^{\star}]+DEC[v^{\star}]-1\leq\max_{v\in V}\bigl(INC[v]+DEC[v]-1\bigr).

The right-hand side is returned as
bestLen
, hence

|P^{\star}|\leq\texttt{bestLen}
.
Conversely, Step (3) shows that for every
v

there
exists
a bitonic subsequence of
length
INC[v]+DEC[v]-1
,
so
bestLen
cannot exceed the LCBS length.
Equality follows, establishing correctness.
∎

(5) Retrieval of an optimal subsequence (optional).

If each range-tree update stores the coordinate of the
maximising predecessor (resp. successor), then a standard back-tracking procedure recovers an LCBS of length
bestLen
in
O(\texttt{bestLen})
time and
O(1)
additional space, completing the constructive proof.

3.6
Complexity analysis

Theorem 11
.

Let
A[0\ldots n{-}1]
and
B[0\ldots m{-}1]
be two input sequences,
\mathcal{M}
their number of matching pairs, and
\mathcal{C}
the number of compatible edges examined by the algorithm. Each update touches
O(\log^{2}\!\mathcal{M})
range-tree nodes, so
\mathcal{C}=O(\mathcal{M}\log^{2}\!\mathcal{M})
in the worst case, but on sparse instances
\mathcal{C}\ll nm
.
The sparse-DAG algorithm computes the LCBS length in

O\!\bigl((n+m)\log n\;+\;\mathcal{C}\bigr)\text{ time}\quad\text{and}\quad O(\mathcal{M})\text{ additional space}.

Proof.

Enumerating
MATCHES
costs

O((n+m)\log n+\mathcal{M})
time.
Both range-tree passes perform one
query and one update per vertex; each costs
O(\log^{2}\!\mathcal{M})
,
so the total is

O(\mathcal{C})
.
The peak scan is linear in
\mathcal{M}
.
Range trees store
O(\mathcal{M})
points and a constant
number of keys per internal node, yielding
O(\mathcal{M})
space.
∎

4
Discussion and Conclusion

What we contributed.

We gave two complementary LCBS solvers: a
linear-space
exact dynamic programme that preserves witness reconstruction, and a
sparse-DAG
method that exploits equality structure to obtain instance-sensitive time and memory.

When to use which.

•

Linear-space DP:
Use when memory is tight but
O(nm)
time is acceptable. It computes the exact LCBS and reconstructs a witness using
O(\min\{n,m\})
extra space with negligible overhead.

•

Sparse-DAG:
Use when equalities are rare or clustered so
\mathcal{M}
(match pairs) or
\mathcal{C}
(DAG edges) satisfies
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{C}\ll nm
. Running time and memory scale with sparsity (e.g.,
O(\mathcal{C}\log n)
time,
O(\mathcal{M})
space in our implementation).

Limits and realism.

LCBS inherits SETH-based lower bounds from LCIS/LCS, so worst-case truly sub-quadratic time is unlikely. Nonetheless, the instance-sensitive view in Section
3
yields robust speedups on realistic, low-redundancy data distributions.
