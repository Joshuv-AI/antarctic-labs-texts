Association of cumulative exposure and change patterns of triglyceride–glucose index combined with novel obesity indices with glycemic transitions in prediabetes: evidence from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Chunying Wang, Yi Peng, Gui Cheng, Qianwen Duan, Wei Wang, Nan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior research indicates that the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and its derivatives are cost-effective and easily accessible tools for assessing glucose metabolism risk. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the longitudinal associations between TyG-novel obesity indices [TyG-body roundness index (TyG-BRI), TyG-chinese visceral adiposity index (TyG-CVAI), TyG-weight-adjusted waist index (TyG-WWI), TyG-a body shape index (TyG-ABSI)] and glycemic transitions in individuals with prediabetes.MethodsThis analysis used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, encompassing 2004 participants with prediabetes. Employing longitudinal data from baseline and a 3‑year follow‑up, we systematically quantified the baseline values, cumulative exposure, and change patterns of TyG‑novel obesity indices. Multiple statistical models were applied to assess the associations of these indices with glycemic transitions in prediabetes and to examine the potential mediating effect of inflammatory burden.ResultsDuring follow‑up, 306 participants progressed to diabetes and 452 reverted to normoglycemia. Compared with static baseline measurements, cumulative exposure and change patterns of TyG‑novel obesity indices strengthened their associations with glycemic transitions. Notably, TyG-BRI and TyG-CVAI exhibited the strongest associations at baseline, whereas TyG-WWI showed the most prominent performance in the analyses of both cumulative exposure and change patterns. Compared with individuals exhibiting well-controlled (low baseline with a slight increase over follow-up) or low cumulative exposure, those with poor control (high baseline and minimal change over follow-up) or the highest cumulative exposure to TyG-WWI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-CVAI faced a significantly higher risk of diabetes progression, with TyG-WWI showing the strongest association with diabetic outcomes. Conversely, elevated levels of all four TyG-novel obesity indices were associated with a reduced likelihood of reverting to normoglycemia, with the strongest inverse associations observed for TyG-BRI (both at baseline and cumulative exposure) and TyG‑WWI (cumulative exposure). For the predictive analysis of glycemic status transitions in prediabetes, the continuous net reclassification improvement demonstrated that the TyG-BRI model exhibited the greatest ability to reclassify individuals into more accurate risk categories, followed by TyG-CVAI, TyG-WWI, and TyG-ABSI. Finally, mediation analysis showed that the progression of prediabetes associated with the TyG-novel obesity indices was partially mediated (2%) by long-term inflammatory burden.ConclusionCumulative exposure and change patterns of TyG-novel obesity indices are significant factors influencing glycemic transitions in individuals with prediabetes.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03152-w.

Background

Prediabetes, a critical transitional stage in the development of diabetes, has been rising persistently worldwide in recent years and has become a major public health concern [1, 2]. According to data from the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 500 million people globally are in a prediabetic state, a number projected to keep increasing in the future [3, 4]. Numerous epidemiological studies have confirmed that individuals with prediabetes not only face a higher risk of progressing to diabetes, but are also closely associated with an increased incidence of cardiovascular events, renal impairment, and all-cause mortality [5–8]. Owing to the subtlety of its clinical manifestations, prediabetes is often overlooked, leading to a considerable proportion of affected individuals rapidly progressing to diabetes without timely intervention, thus imposing a serious disease and economic burden on both individuals and society [4]. Although guidelines recommend lifestyle modifications and weight management to delay disease progression, glycemic outcomes among individuals with prediabetes exhibit significant heterogeneity: some may revert to normoglycemia, while others deteriorate rapidly. The predictive efficacy of existing clinical indicators for glycemic transitions remains limited [9]. Therefore, developing more sensitive and specific biomarkers to enhance risk stratification among individuals with prediabetes has garnered significant research interest.

Insulin resistance (IR) represents the core pathological mechanism of metabolic disorders. It is not only closely associated with abnormal glucose metabolism but also accompanied by dyslipidemia, activation of inflammatory responses, and excessive visceral fat accumulation. As such, IR serves as a critical driver in the progression from prediabetes to divergent glycemic outcomes [10, 11]. Obesity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, is a well-established risk factor for the development of both IR and diabetes [12, 13]. The challenges posed by current assessment methods, however, are significant. The gold-standard hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp for IR is technically demanding and costly, limiting its use in large-scale clinical and epidemiological studies [14]. Similarly, accurate obesity assessment often requires expensive or complex imaging techniques, which hampers broad clinical adoption [15]. Given these limitations, the development of simple, stable, and scalable surrogate indicators for both conditions has become a major research focus. Previous studies have shown that IR surrogate indicators such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, along with classic obesity indices such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC), as well as novel obesity indices such as Weight-Adjusted Waist Index (WWI), Body Roundness Index (BRI), A Body Shape Index (ABSI) and Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI), exhibit good predictive performance for incident diabetes and cardiovascular disease [16–22]. Building on this, the development of composite indices combining IR surrogates and obesity measures has gradually garnered increasing research attention. Studies have shown that the combination of the TyG index with obesity indices significantly enhances the value of risk assessment for metabolic diseases [16, 23, 24]. However, evidence regarding the combination of the TyG index and novel obesity indicators for assessing dynamic glycemic changes in individuals with prediabetes remains extremely limited, and their longitudinal association is still unclear, a key research gap in the current field. Against the above background and knowledge gaps, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the longitudinal associations between multiple TyG-novel obesity indices (including TyG-CVAI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-WWI, and TyG-BRI) and glycemic transitions in individuals with prediabetes.

Methods

Study population and design

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort covering 28 provinces in China, using a multistage stratified sampling method. In brief, CHARLS launched its baseline survey between 2011 and 2012, initially enrolling 17,708 participants, and has since conducted continuous follow-ups. Detailed design information is provided in Additional file 1, while Fig. S1 illustrates the complete participant selection process. We used fasting blood samples and physical measurement data collected at baseline and in the third wave of the survey to construct dynamic changes in TyG-novel obesity indices and to analyze the association with glycemic transitions in prediabetes. In accordance with the study objectives and the criteria established by the American Diabetes Association [25], we excluded participants with missing baseline fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) data, those with previously diagnosed diabetes, and those with normoglycemia at baseline. Subsequently, among the identified individuals with prediabetes, we further excluded those with missing blood glucose or HbA1c data during follow-up, those with non-fasting blood samples at follow-up, and those with missing data for TyG-novel obesity indices. A total of 2,004 participants with prediabetes were ultimately included in the final analysis of this study. The complete participant screening process is presented in Fig. 1. The CHARLS study protocol has been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (No: 00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram. Data collection

Baseline information for this study included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory results, all of which were collected by uniformly trained investigators through standardized physical examinations and structured questionnaires. The questionnaire covered demographic and health behavior information (age, sex, education, marital status, birthplace, smoking, and drinking). Medical history, including hypertension, heart disease, and stroke, was obtained from self-reported physician diagnoses.

Anthropometric measurements were performed by the CHARLS survey team following standardized protocols. Height and weight were measured with participants wearing light clothing. Waist circumference was measured at the level of the umbilicus at the end of normal expiration. Based on these measurements, four obesity indices, namely WWI, ABSI, BRI, and CVAI, were calculated. All participants were required to provide venous blood samples after an overnight fast on the morning of the following day. The samples were centrally collected and cryopreserved (–80 °C) by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and subsequently shipped to testing laboratories in Beijing for biochemical assays, including FPG, HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), uric acid, and creatinine. Laboratory testing for different survey waves was performed by distinct facilities: the baseline survey tests were conducted by the You an men Clinical Center Laboratory at Capital Medical University, while the third wave was conducted by the Beijing KingMed Central Laboratory. Throughout the study period, all test results were regularly monitored by designated personnel to ensure consistent compliance with quality control standards [30]. Detailed methods are provided in Tables S1 and S2 of Additional file 2.

Study outcome

The study outcome was prediabetes and its glycemic status transitions at the third wave of the survey, including progression to diabetes and regression to normoglycemia. Based on the American Diabetes Association criteria, normoglycemia was defined as FPG < 5.6 mmol/L or HbA1c < 5.7%; prediabetes was defined as FPG 5.6–6.9 mmol/L or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%; and diabetes was defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or self-reported physician diagnosis [25].

Assessment of exposure factors

The exposure factors included both the TyG-novel obesity indices measured at baseline and the cumulative exposure levels, as well as the control status for these indices, which were assessed by integrating data from the baseline and the third survey wave. In this study, a k-means clustering method based on Euclidean distance was employed to classify the change patterns of TyG-novel obesity indices during 2012–2015. The performance of models with different numbers of clusters was evaluated using the elbow method (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). A clear inflection point in the within-cluster sum of squares reduction was observed when the number of clusters was set to 3, suggesting that further increasing the number of clusters would yield limited improvement in model fitting. Consequently, three change patterns were ultimately determined. Class 1 (good control) had a low baseline with a slight increase over follow-up. Class 2 (moderate control) showed a moderate baseline and a small rise over follow-up. Class 3 (poor control) presented with a high baseline and minimal change over follow-up (Fig. 2). The k-means method was selected for its ability to automatically identify naturally formed subgroups based on the data’s inherent variation trends, thereby avoiding bias from manually defined thresholds. This approach is also more effective at capturing the continuous changes in TyG-novel obesity indices over time and aligns well with the underlying data distribution. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5) and Empower(R) 2.0. At the outset of these analyses, missingness in the sample was assessed. Among the 2,004 participants ultimately included, the maximum missing rate for any variable was only 0.89% (Additional file 2: Table S3). Given the low overall proportion of missing data, and to preserve maximal sample information, raw data were used directly in all subsequent analyses.

Study participants were stratified and described according to the primary outcome. The presentation of study characteristics was tailored to the different types of variables (see notes in Table 1 for details). Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between the TyG index and each novel obesity index to evaluate the interrelationships among their underlying pathophysiological dimensions, thereby providing a methodological foundation for the subsequent development of composite indices.

A multivariable Cox regression model was employed in this study to analyze the associations between TyG-novel obesity indices (including baseline levels and longitudinal changes) and glycemic status transitions in individuals with prediabetes. To facilitate comparisons of effect sizes across different variables, continuous independent variables that followed a normal distribution were standardized using Z-scores, and standardized hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Covariates were selected for inclusion based on clinical significance and inter-variable correlations. To avoid multicollinearity, the model did not include the individual components that constituted the independent variables. Based on the correlation matrix of covariates (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), highly correlated variable pairs, such as uric acid and creatinine, as well as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and total cholesterol, were excluded. Additionally, we calculated the variance inflation factors for the covariates, and the results showed no significant multicollinearity among the adjusted covariates, with all variance inflation factor values below 3. The adjustment strategies for the multivariable models were as follows: Model I was adjusted for sex and age; Model II was further adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history, including education, marital status, birthplace, heart disease, hypertension, stroke, smoking status, and drinking status; Model III was further adjusted for biochemical indicators based on Model II, including LDL-C, Cr, HbA1c, and hs-CRP.

To examine potential dose–response trends, restricted cubic splines were fitted to model the relationships between TyG-novel obesity indices (including baseline levels and cumulative exposure levels) and the outcomes, with likelihood ratio tests used to assess the nonlinearity.

This study further conducted an exploratory assessment of the mediating effect of cumulative exposure to the inflammatory factor hs-CRP on the association between cumulative TyG-novel obesity indices and glycemic transitions in prediabetic individuals, which was quantified as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. Additionally, 1000 bootstrap resamplings were performed to calculate the bias-corrected significance level.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of the TyG-novel obesity indices for glycemic status transitions in prediabetes, with the area under the curve calculated. The incremental value of the TyG-novel obesity indices beyond the original TyG model was further assessed using the DeLong test and the net reclassification improvement.

To ensure the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the association between TyG-novel obesity indices and glycemic transitions in prediabetes under various a priori assumptions. First, the study population was stratified by factors such as sex, age, hypertension, heart disease, and stroke, and the associations between the aforementioned indices and glycemic transitions in prediabetes were evaluated. Meanwhile, likelihood ratio tests were used to determine whether potential subgroup differences existed. Second, to reduce interference from multiple confounding factors, relevant analyses were repeated after excluding participants with multiple comorbidities. Third, for variables with missing data, complete datasets were generated by creating dummy variables or using mean/median imputation, and the associations described above were re-evaluated based on the imputed data. In addition, to evaluate the impact of missing data imputation on the robustness of the results, we performed a complete-case analysis by including only participants with complete data for all covariates and repeated the multivariable regression models from the primary analysis. Fourth, given that the study outcomes of glycemic progression and remission in individuals with prediabetes are mutually exclusive and competing events, we further applied a competing risk model to analyze the associations of the TyG-novel obesity indices with glycemic status transitions in the prediabetic population. Fifth, diabetes and prediabetes were defined using a combination of laboratory values and self-reported diagnosis, which may introduce heterogeneity and misclassification. To mitigate potential confounding from diagnostic criteria, we performed a sensitivity analysis using only laboratory-based definitions.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

This study included 2004 participants with prediabetes: 1246 remained prediabetic during follow-up, 306 progressed to diabetes, and 452 reverted to normoglycemia. The three outcome groups differed significantly in baseline demographic, metabolic, and obesity-related profiles (Table 1). Overall, participants in the progression-to-diabetes group were the oldest and had higher proportions of hypertension, heart disease, and unhealthy lifestyle habits (e.g., drinking). They also carried a significantly greater metabolic burden, reflected in elevated levels of FPG, HbA1c, TyG index, triglycerides, hs-CRP, WC, WWI, BRI, and CVAI. In contrast, those who reverted to normoglycemia were generally younger, more often male, and demonstrated a more favorable metabolic profile across lipid, glycemic, and obesity-related parameters. The four TyG-novel obesity indices (TyG-WWI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-CVAI) also exhibited a stepwise pattern across the three groups, with the highest levels observed in the prediabetes-to-diabetes progression group and the lowest levels in the prediabetes-to-normoglycemia remission group.

Correlation between the TyG index and novel obesity indicators

The correlation analysis showed that the TyG index was significantly and positively correlated with WWI, ABSI, BRI, and CVAI. Among these, the TyG index showed the highest correlation coefficient with CVAI (r = 0.53, P < 0.001), followed by BRI (r = 0.31, P < 0.001), WWI (r = 0.16, P < 0.001), and ABSI (r = 0.06, P = 0.007). All correlation coefficients were below 0.60, indicating that the TyG index and the novel obesity indices reflect related but distinct pathophysiological dimensions, providing a theoretical basis for combining them to construct composite indices.

Associations between TyG-novel obesity indices and glycemic transitions in prediabetes

Prior to examining the associations of the TyG-novel obesity indices with glycemic status transitions, we assessed the individual associations of the TyG index and each novel obesity index separately with glycemic outcomes in the prediabetic population. The results showed that, after full adjustment for confounders, BRI and CVAI exhibited the most significant associations with glycemic status transitions in prediabetes, followed by TyG index and WWI, whereas ABSI showed no significant association (Table S4).

In the multivariable Cox regression analyses, all TyG-novel obesity indices were significantly associated with glycemic transitions in prediabetes (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model, elevated levels of TyG-WWI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-CVAI were all significantly associated with an increased risk of progressing from prediabetes to diabetes. The associations were strongest for TyG-BRI and TyG-CVAI (HRs = 1.27 and 1.28, respectively). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity Analysis 1: Further stratified analyses by population subgroups showed that the associations of TyG-novel obesity indices with glycemic status transitions in prediabetes did not differ significantly across all subgroups (Additional file 2: Table S5–S9). Sensitivity Analysis 2: Repeated analyses after excluding patients with multiple comorbidities yielded results consistent with the main findings (Additional file 2: Table S10). Sensitivity Analysis 3: In the imputed complete dataset, the results were not significantly different from those of the main analysis (Additional file 2: Table S11). Furthermore, complete-case analyses yielded results consistent with both the main analysis and the imputed analysis, supporting the robustness of the study findings (Additional file 2: Table S12). Sensitivity Analysis 4: When further accounting for competing risks, the TyG‑novel obesity indices remained positively associated with prediabetes progression and negatively associated with prediabetes remission (Additional file 2: Table S13). Sensitivity Analysis 5: When glycemic status transitions were defined using only laboratory‑based glucose measurements, the findings for the associations between TyG‑novel obesity indices and glycemic status transitions in prediabetes remained materially unchanged (Additional file 2: Table S14).

Discussion

In this nationwide prospective cohort study, we confirmed that TyG-novel obesity indices were significantly associated with glycemic transitions in prediabetes. Among the comparisons of multiple composite indices, the combinations of the TyG index with BRI, WWI and CVAI demonstrated considerable incremental value in predicting both diabetes progression and regression.

Comparison with existing evidence

In the field of dysglycemia risk assessment, numerous studies have clearly indicated that the combination of IR and obesity improves diabetes risk prediction [35–39]. Recent research has explored combining the TyG index with novel obesity indices to enhance prediction. Evidence suggests that novel obesity indices like BRI and CVAI more accurately reflect visceral fat accumulation and fat distribution changes, thus better aligning with the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases [26, 40, 41]. A large prospective study by Wang et al. found TyG-BRI to be superior to the TyG index alone for predicting metabolic cardiovascular events [42]. Similarly, Yue et al. reported that TyG-CVAI outperformed other TyG-novel obesity indices in assessing stroke and cardiovascular disease risk [43]. In contrast, Duan et al. highlighted the sensitivity of TyG-WWI to confounding factors, leading to unstable predictive performance and, in some studies, only marginal significance [44]. Collectively, these studies point to a consistent trend: novel obesity indices can compensate for the limitations of traditional BMI or WC in reflecting body fat distribution, while the TyG index, as a robust surrogate marker for IR, often yields greater predictive gains when combined with these novel indices. However, it remains unclear whether the TyG‑novel obesity indices are equally applicable to risk assessment of glycemic status transitions in prediabetes. This study was designed to address these gaps by systematically constructing composite indices from the TyG index and novel obesity measures and, for the first time, comprehensively evaluating their associations with bidirectional glycemic transitions in prediabetes across the three dimensions: baseline levels, cumulative exposure, and change patterns. These findings not only extend the application of the “IR combined with obesity” composite in dysglycemia risk assessment but also provide a more dynamic and comprehensive evidence framework, which may facilitate precise risk stratification and the development of early intervention strategies for individuals with prediabetes.

In recent years, cumulative exposure models and clustering analyses have become widely used in metabolic epidemiology. These approaches integrate multiple timepoint measurements to characterize the temporal burden and dynamic patterns of metabolic markers [45–50]. Prior work has also indicated a long-term association between TyG-classical obesity indices and diabetes risk. Li et al. reported in their cohort study that elevated TyG-WC levels significantly predicted diabetes onset [51]. Furthermore, trajectory analysis by Yang et al. confirmed that long-term rising trajectories of TyG-BMI were closely associated with deterioration in glucose metabolism [52]. Meanwhile, Han et al. documented a positive, nonlinear association between TyG-BMI and diabetes risk among individuals with prediabetes [53]. In contrast, a study by Chen et al. demonstrated an inverse, nonlinear association between the TyG index and remission from prediabetes to normoglycemia [37]. However, most of the aforementioned studies have focused on traditional “TyG-classical obesity index combinations”, with systematic longitudinal comparisons of multiple TyG-novel obesity index combinations remaining relatively insufficient [16, 49, 54–58].

In the current analysis, we observed that all TyG-related novel obesity indices were significantly associated with glycemic transitions in prediabetes. This overall trend is consistent with previous reports indicating that “TyG-obesity composite indices outperform individual metabolic indices” [17, 59–64]. More importantly, our findings demonstrated that TyG-BRI and TyG-CVAI exhibited more stable risk assessment performance at the baseline evaluation stage, which is consistent with the conclusion from relevant literature that “body fat distribution indices (e.g., CVAI, BRI) can reflect the actual risk of fat accumulation more accurately” [27, 65–67]. Furthermore, the longitudinal follow-up analysis revealed that the change patterns of TyG-WWI exhibited higher sensitivity in assessing glycemic improvement or deterioration. Existing evidence suggests that WWI better reflects sarcopenia and fat redistribution compared to BMI and WC [68–70], and our change patterns analysis further supports this metabolic interpretation. Notably, the cumulative exposure model used in this study captures long-term metabolic stress more comprehensively than single-point measurements, aligning with the viewpoint proposed in existing studies that “cumulative TyG indices outperform single measurements in assessing the risk of prediabetes or diabetes” [71].

Nonlinear association between TyG-BRI and prediabetes progression risk

In this study, most TyG-novel obesity indices demonstrated a linear dose–response relationship with glycemic transitions. However, TyG-BRI showed significant nonlinear trends in both the baseline model (P for nonlinearity = 0.014) and the cumulative exposure model (P for nonlinearity = 0.033). As indicated by the curve shape, the association between TyG-BRI and the risk of diabetes progression was relatively flat in the lower levels but increased sharply beyond a certain threshold, suggesting the existence of a distinct “acceleration point”. This characteristic may be attributed to BRI’s sensitivity to visceral fat accumulation [27, 72, 73]: low-to-moderate BRI levels largely reflect subcutaneous fat, which has a limited impact on IR. However, once BRI increases beyond a certain threshold, visceral fat accumulates rapidly, and this process, when combined with the IR reflected by the TyG index, may lead to a sharp rise in risk. Furthermore, the nonlinearity observed in cumulative TyG-BRI reinforces this threshold effect, indicating that the interaction between long-term metabolic burden and adiposity redistribution plays a critical role in the deterioration of glucose metabolism.

Other key findings

By systematically analyzing multiple TyG–novel obesity indices, this study further demonstrates that, compared with a single baseline assessment, longitudinal monitoring of metabolic indices enables more accurate identification of future glycemic transition risk in individuals with prediabetes. Although previous studies have indicated that the TyG index and body fat distribution indices are dynamically associated with dysglycemia [74, 75], few have explicitly elucidated the incremental value of longitudinal assessment over baseline assessment. Based on the findings of this study, we propose the following clinical and methodological insights for future strategies in the primary prevention of diabetes: (1) The non-static nature of IR and body fat distribution: Both IR and adipose tissue redistribution exhibit dynamic characteristics that fluctuate with metabolic stress. A single measurement captures only a transient state and cannot distinguish between temporary fluctuations and persistent metabolic burden. In contrast, longitudinal monitoring better quantifies the true extent of long-term cumulative metabolic exposure. Consequently, TyG-WWI demonstrates higher sensitivity in identifying progression risk, while TyG-BRI and TyG-CVAI can more effectively reflect early signals of homeostatic deviation at the baseline stage. (2) Threshold and decompensation phenomena: Longitudinal monitoring can capture the critical inflection point of the transition from compensation to decompensation. When TyG-BRI, TyG-WWI, or TyG-ABSI rises beyond a certain threshold, the associated risk exhibits a marked accelerating trend characterized by non-linear amplification, a change that is often undetectable by a single measurement. (3) Cumulative Effect of Inflammation and Bias Correction: Although the proportion mediated by long-term cumulative exposure to hs-CRP was modest, this study found consistent and stable associations, indicating that chronic inflammation is a non-negligible component in the pathway of metabolic deterioration [76]. In addition, repeated measurements can reduce random error and regression dilution bias, thereby improving both the reliability of risk estimation and predictive capacity [77, 78].

In the present study, predictive analyses revealed that, except for baseline TyG-ABSI, the TyG-WWI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-CVAI significantly outperformed the TyG index alone in predicting glycemic status transitions in prediabetes (DeLong P < 0.05). This result provides direct validation for the superior predictive performance of the composite strategy combining IR surrogates and novel obesity indices. This finding is consistent with the key conclusion from previous studies that composite indices outperform single indicators [16, 23, 24], further validating the scientific validity and rationale of constructing composite indices. Furthermore, regarding the differential performance among the various composite indices, continuous NRI analysis revealed a distinct stratification pattern. We postulate that the key reason for this difference may lie in the distinct biological implications of the novel obesity indices and their differential synergistic effects with IR. BRI, CVAI, and WWI mainly reflect visceral fat accumulation, which is a critical contributor to IR development [72, 79, 80]. Their synergistic interaction with the IR status captured by the TyG index enables a more comprehensive assessment of the severity of metabolic disturbances, thereby accounting for the relatively higher predictive performance of the corresponding composite indices. In contrast, ABSI mainly reflects central body fat distribution and only captures the relative relationship of waist circumference to height and BMI [81]. It fails to comprehensively capture the association between body fat distribution and metabolic burden, resulting in limited synergistic effects with IR. Consequently, the improvement in predictive performance of the composite index constructed with ABSI is less pronounced, which also provides a clear priority reference for the selection of predictive indicators in subsequent clinical practice.

Clinical and public health implications

The findings of this study have practical implications for both the clinical management of prediabetes and community-based screening. First, from a feasibility standpoint, the TyG-novel obesity indices are calculated using only routine biochemical tests (FPG, TG) and simple anthropometric measurements (height, weight, waist circumference) [16, 72, 79–81]. These parameters are already widely available in healthcare facilities at all levels, including community health examinations, without requiring additional testing costs. Therefore, these indices hold promise for implementation in resource-limited settings. Secondly, from a predictive perspective, the composite indices exhibit higher accuracy in identifying glycemic status transitions than the TyG index alone, which is consistent with previous reports [16, 23, 24, 35–39, 42, 43]. In community screening, a two-step approach could be adopted: first, obtain the basic indicators through routine tests; second, calculate the composite indices to perform refined risk stratification. This strategy would help prioritize limited intervention resources (such as intensive lifestyle interventions and close follow-up) for the highest-risk subgroups, thereby improving screening efficiency. Finally, from an intervention strategy perspective, elevated TyG‑novel obesity indices alert clinicians that patients’ risk arises from the synergistic effect of metabolic abnormalities and obesity. Therefore, interventions targeting this population may require a dual approach: focusing simultaneously on improving IR (e.g., pharmacological interventions) and reducing body weight or waist circumference (e.g., lifestyle modifications), rather than merely normalizing a single indicator. In summary, as a cost-effective and accessible composite index, the TyG‑novel obesity indices hold potential clinical applicability for risk stratification and personalized intervention in prediabetes. Further prospective interventional studies are warranted to validate their value in guiding clinical decision-making.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged and addressed in future research: (1) Although this study performed cumulative exposure assessment and change patterns analysis based on two waves of follow-up, it still lacks more frequent repeated measurements. Future studies could include additional follow-up time points to more precisely characterize the short-term fluctuation patterns and long-term risk architecture of the TyG and its related novel obesity indices. (2) Partial study outcomes were derived from participants’ self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias and the risk of case misclassification. Although relevant studies have reported low false-positive rates, such potential biases warrant cautious interpretation. (3) Given the observational design of CHARLS, this study cannot establish causality [82] or evaluate the impact of specific interventions on the dynamic changes of TyG-novel obesity indices and glycemic transitions in individuals with prediabetes. (4) Although adjustments have been made for multiple confounding factors, residual confounding may still exist, particularly from unmeasured variables like dietary patterns, lifestyle, and genetic factors [83]. (5) The study population primarily comprised middle-aged and older Chinese adults, and extrapolation to younger populations or populations in other countries requires further validation. These limitations of the present study emphasize the need for more intensive, mechanism-driven longitudinal studies in broader populations to further refine the dynamic assessment system for metabolic risk. (6) As the CHARLS database does not provide information on diabetes type, this study cannot rule out the minimal potential impact of a very small number of participants with type 1 diabetes on the results. However, given the absolute predominance of type 2 diabetes in middle-aged and elderly populations, this potential impact is likely to be very limited. (7) As the exact timing of blood sample measurements was not disclosed by the CHARLS team, the exposure duration for the independent variables in the study population may vary slightly, which could partially influence the study results.

Conclusion

In this longitudinal cohort study based on CHARLS, the dynamic changes in the TyG and its related novel obesity indices during follow-up were closely associated with glycemic transitions in individuals with prediabetes. In comparison, the combinations of baseline TyG index with BRI and CVAI (i.e., TyG-BRI and TyG-CVAI) showed the strongest associations with diabetes progression, while cumulative TyG-WWI excelled at identifying both glycemic progression and remission. These findings suggest that primary prevention strategies should integrate assessment of the TyG index with adiposity distribution measures and reinforce their dynamic monitoring during follow-up, thereby effectively enhancing the identification of high‑risk individuals and facilitating glycemic improvement.
