Anti-EGFR liposomal drug delivery system loaded with AGY2 potentiates the anti-cancer effect of AGY2 against EGFR expressed pancreatic cancer
Authors: Raviteja Bulusu, Esther Frimpong, Joy Okoro, Sunil Krishnan, Saunjoo Yoon, Bo Han, Xue Zhu, Edward Agyare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide due to late diagnosis, dense stromal barriers, and resistance to existing chemotherapeutics. To address these limitations, we developed a targeted liposomal delivery system that encapsulates AGY2, a newly synthesized, more potent fluoro pyrimidine nucleoside analog. In this study, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) functionalized liposomal nanoparticles (EGFR-AGY2 LNPs) were optimized using Box–Behnken Design to achieve superior physicochemical stability. The optimized formulation demonstrated nanoscale dimensions (∼121 nm), high entrapment efficiency (∼75%), and strong colloidal stability. Surface antibody conjugation was confirmed by Fourier Transform Infrared Analysis (FTIR) and immunofluorescence analysis, demonstrating successful receptor targeting. The drug release profile best fits the Weibull kinetic model, suggesting sustained release behavior. In-vitro evaluations in 3D pancreatic cancer spheroids and organoids revealed significantly enhanced cytotoxicity. In spheroids, the IC₅₀ values of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs (7.7 ± 2.3 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 9.63 ± 0.9 μM in PANC-1 cells) compared to free AGY2 (27.58 ± 4.2 μM and 31.51 ± 5.5 μM), non-targeted AGY2 LNPs (16.53 ± 3.7 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 18.53 ± 5.7 μM in PANC-1), and 5-fluorouracil (36.63 ± 4.8 μM MiaPaCa-2 and 40.78 ± 5.9 μM PANC-1). Similar trend was observed in organoids with highest anti-cancer activity EGFR-AGY2 LNPs (IC₅₀ 10.83 ± 3.3 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 12.2 ± 3.9 μM in PANC-1) both significantly better than 5-FU (p = 0.0001), Non-targeted AGY2 LNPs (25.09 ± 3.9 μM and 29.09 ± 4.9 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and PANC-1), and free AGY2 achieved IC₅₀ values of 35.18 ± 3.7 μM and 32.56 ± 5.1 μM. Pharmacokinetic analysis showed substantial improvement over 5-FU and non-targeted AGY2 LNPs. The elimination rate constant (k10) decreased from 1.82 h−1 (5-FU) to 0.39 h−1 (EGFR-AGY2 LNPs), while t1/2 increased from 0.4 to 1.8 h. Systemic clearance dropped from 259.6 to 21.3 mL/h, and AUC rose >12-fold (from 2.08 to 25.32 μg*h/mL). In vivo antitumor efficacy studies showed that EGFR-AGY2 LNPs reduced mean tumor volume to ∼900 mm3 compared with 1200 mm3 (AGY2 LNPs), 1500 mm3 (free AGY2), and 2200 mm3 (5-FU), p < 0.001. No significant change in body weight was observed, indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Collectively, these results provide strong evidence that EGFR-targeted delivery of AGY2 offers a promising therapeutic platform capable of overcoming drug delivery barriers and improving outcomes in PDAC therapy.

Introduction

According to recent cancer statistics, pancreatic cancer (PCa) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States and globally, following lung and colorectal cancers (Anand et al., 2023). It remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a persistently low five-year survival rate of approximately 13% and a national age-adjusted incidence rate of 13.5 per 100,000 individuals (Lippi and Mattiuzzi, 2020). Unlike most major cancers, where mortality is declining, the incidence and death rates of PCa continue to rise slightly each year, particularly among individuals aged 65 years and older (Anand et al., 2023). More than 90% of all PCa are diagnosed as PDAC, a highly aggressive histological subtype characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis (Lee et al., 2025). This growing burden is further compounded by racial and geographic disparities, with the highest incidence reported among non-Hispanic Black populations and elevated mortality rates observed in several southern U.S. states. Based on current epidemiological trends, PDAC is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide by 2030 (Lee et al., 2025). The dismal outcomes associated with PDAC are primarily attributed to its complex stromal architecture, dense extracellular matrix, and limited vascularization, which collectively hinder effective drug delivery and therapeutic response (Ho et al., 2020; Zottl et al., 2025).

Most pancreatic cancers are diagnosed at advanced stages due to the retroperitoneal location of the pancreas and the nonspecific nature of early clinical symptoms (Loveday et al., 2019). By the time of diagnosis, the disease has often progressed locally or metastasized to distant organs, and tumor cells frequently acquire resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic regimens (Kleeff et al., 2016). This resistance arises from multiple mechanisms, including poor drug penetration through the dense desmoplastic stroma, overexpression of efflux transporters, altered cell-cycle regulation, and activation of pro-survival signaling pathways (Bou Antoun and Chioni, 2023).

One promising approach to overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapies is the use of liposomal drug delivery systems. Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers and offer several advantages in cancer treatment, particularly for hard-to-treat malignancies such as PCa (Fidan et al., 2024). These nanocarriers enhance the solubility and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs, protect active agents from premature degradation, and allow for controlled or sustained release at the tumor site. Importantly, liposomes can be engineered to exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in tumors, thereby achieving preferential accumulation in cancerous tissues while minimizing exposure to healthy organs (Lee et al., 2017). This selective targeting reduces systemic toxicity, improves therapeutic index, and can enhance patient compliance. Moreover, liposomal formulations can be designed to co-deliver multiple agents or incorporate targeting ligands, further increasing their precision and effectiveness in overcoming drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity are the key challenges in PCa therapy (Allen and Cullis, 2013).

Building upon the advantages of conventional liposomal systems, targeted liposomal drug delivery systems represent a significant advancement in precision oncology (Amin et al., 2022). These systems are engineered to actively bind specific molecular markers overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells, such as receptors or antigens commonly found in pancreatic tumors (Fidan et al., 2024). By conjugating targeting ligands, such as monoclonal antibodies, to the liposome surface, these nanocarriers can selectively recognize and bind to tumor cells, facilitating enhanced cellular uptake via receptor-mediated endocytosis. This approach not only increases drug concentration at the tumor site but also minimizes off-target effects, reducing toxicity to normal tissues (Fidan et al., 2024).

Targeted nanomedicine approaches offer a promising strategy to overcome these challenges by enhancing tumor-specific drug delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity (Bou Antoun and Chioni, 2023). By selectively targeting therapeutic agents to cancer cells via receptor-mediated mechanisms, these systems can enhance intracellular drug accumulation, bypass multidrug resistance mechanisms, and achieve sustained therapeutic concentrations within the tumor microenvironment (Aravindan et al., 2024). Consequently, targeted nanoparticle-based therapies hold significant potential not only to improve treatment efficacy in drug-resistant pancreatic tumors but also to reduce tumor recurrence and improve long-term patient outcomes (Aravindan et al., 2024).

For instance, the use of ligands such as Cetuximab (Cmab) has enabled the spatial targeting of chitosan/hyaluronic acid-based nanoparticles. In a study involving cabazitaxel-loaded nanocarriers, specific binding to overexpressed receptors, notably EGFR, on breast cancer cell surfaces resulted in a remarkable 21-fold reduction in tumor volume by improving pharmacokinetic parameters (Jha et al., 2024a). Similarly, Cetuximab-tagged redox-responsive nanocarriers composed of D-alpha-tocopheryl-chitosan and hyaluronic acid demonstrated significant anti-tumor efficacy, leading to a substantial decrease in breast cancer tumor burden when delivering cabazitaxel to the target site (Jha et al., 2024b).

In the context of PCa, where dense stromal architecture and a hypo-vascular microenvironment hinder effective drug penetration, targeted liposomes offer a strategic advantage. They can be designed to bypass physical and biological barriers by targeting overexpressed proteins, such as EGFR, CD44, or integrins, which are often associated with tumor progression (Espona-Fiedler et al., 2024). Overall, targeted liposomal drug delivery systems hold immense potential to transform the treatment paradigm for PCa by improving drug localization, reducing systemic side effects, and overcoming multidrug resistance mechanisms (Yu et al., 2010),(Aravindan et al., 2024).

In our study, we focus on epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-targeted liposomal drug delivery systems, a strategy that has shown promising results across various solid tumors, including pancreatic, lung, breast, and colorectal cancers (Sigismund et al., 2018). EGFR is frequently overexpressed in many malignancies and is associated with aggressive tumor behavior and resistance to therapy (Hopper-Borge et al., 2009). In our previous work, we confirmed EGFR expression in MiaPaCa-2 cells by western blot analysis (Bulusu et al., 2025), while other studies demonstrated EGFR expression in PANC-1 cells (Chang et al., 2012). Liposomes functionalized with EGFR-targeting moieties have demonstrated enhanced tumor selectivity, increased intracellular drug accumulation, and improved antitumor efficacy in preclinical models, making them a compelling platform for targeted cancer therapy (Moreira et al., 2023a).

To further enhance the therapeutic potential, we are encapsulating AGY2, a newly synthesized small molecule developed in our laboratory, which has shown superior in-vitro anticancer activity compared to standard treatments (Bulusu et al., 2025). Incorporating AGY2 into an EGFR-targeted liposomal system provides an additional advantage by combining novel drug potency with precise tumor targeting, thereby increasing the likelihood of overcoming delivery barriers and improving treatment outcomes in pancreatic cancer (PCa).

To assess the potential of our EGFR-targeted liposomal formulation, the present study involves comprehensive physicochemical and biological characterizations, including DoE-based formulation screening, particle size, surface charge, entrapment efficiency, in-vitro release kinetics, targeting specificity, cellular uptake, cytotoxicity assays, in-vivo pharmacokinetics, and tumor efficacy. These evaluations aim to confirm the formulation's stability, functionality, and therapeutic relevance in addressing the challenges of PCa treatment.

Methods and materials

Materials

1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), 1-myristoyl-2-palmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (MPPC), and DSPE-PEG2000-carboxy NHS (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[carboxy(polyethyleneglycol)-2000-NHS ester] (sodium salt)), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (ammonium salt), were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). A monoclonal anti-EGFR antibody produced in mice was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Solvents such as chloroform, methanol, and ethanol (HPLC grade), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and dialysis membranes (MWCO 14 kDa) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich, and the secondary antibody IgG mouse was procured from Cell Signaling. All other reagents and chemicals used were of analytical grade.

The experimental framework comprised 25 runs, assigned at random to ensure unbiased results. An uncontrolled variable was included within the design to account for possible external factors that could impact the outcomes. After gathering the data, the results were carefully analyzed with both Fisher's statistical test and ANOVA to ensure accuracy. Analyses used a 95% confidence interval to assess key factors and model effectiveness. The formulation of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs was prepared via two steps as described below:

Step 1

The liposomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration technique, following the optimized parameters established through DoE. In brief, DPPC, MPPC, and DSPE-PEG2000 were dissolved in a chloroform: methanol mixture (2:1 v/v) in a round-bottom flask. The AGY2 molecule was co-dissolved with the lipid mixture in organic solvents and evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 60 °C, forming a thin, uniform lipid film along the inner wall of the flask. This lipid film was then hydrated with PBS at the optimized hydration temperature of 60 °C and vortexed for 10 min to facilitate dispersion. The resulting multilamellar vesicles (MLVs) were subsequently downsized to small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) by sequential extrusion through polycarbonate membranes with pore sizes of 200 nm, 100 nm, and 80 nm, using a mini-extruder system to achieve uniform particle size distribution and improved homogeneity (Ndemazie et al., 2023).

Step 2

Antibody conjugation to the surface of AGY2 liposomes was performed following the manufacturer's protocol. The direct-coupling method enabled covalent attachment of free carboxylic groups on liposome surfaces to antibodies' primary amines by activating the carboxylic groups with EDC (1-ethyl-3-[3-dimethylaminopropyl] carbodiimide) (Fig. 1). A 10-fold molar excess of EDC and a 25-fold molar excess of sulfo-NHS were added to AGY2 liposomes. The mixture was stirred until completely homogeneous, and the reaction was continued at room temperature for 15 min. Excess EDC was eliminated with a size-exclusion spin column before introducing the EGFR antibody. A solution of EGFR antibody at 1 mg/mL was prepared in PBS (pH 7.4) and subsequently introduced to EDC/Sulfo-NHS-activated pegylated liposomes at an antibody-to-lipid molar ratio of 1:1000 (v/v). The solution was mixed well and allowed to react for 2 h at room temperature. The unbound ligand was removed by dialysis 14 kDa MWCO membrane (Le et al., 2018).

Characterization of liposomes

Particle size and zeta potential

Particle size distribution of the EGFR- AGY2 LNPs, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis ZetaView® BASIC NTA (Nanoparticle Tracking Video Microscope PMX-120), equipped with ZetaView software (version 8.05.11 SP4) with a dilution of 1: 1000 (v/v). Measurements were carried out in triplicate at 25 °C after appropriate dilution in filtered distilled water (Kastner and Perrie, 2016).

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%)

To assess the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of AGY2, the liposome samples were subjected to three consecutive dialysis cycles using a dialysis bag (MWCO 12,000 Da, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) against 10 mM PBS, pH 7.4, at 25 °C. Subsequently, liposomes were diluted ten times in acetonitrile, and AGY2 was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Kastner and Perrie, 2016).

Stability Studies:

EGFR-AGY2 LNPs were evaluated for stability over 90 days. For this study, the liposome samples were stored in 15 mL glass vials and maintained at three distinct temperature conditions: room temperature (25 ± 5 °C), refrigeration (4 ± 5 °C), and elevated temperature (45 ± 5 °C).

At predetermined intervals throughout the 90-day storage period, the vesicle size and entrapment efficiency (%) of the formulations were systematically measured. These periodic assessments allowed for the monitoring of any changes in the physical and chemical properties of the liposomes, ensuring the stability and reliability of the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs under various environmental conditions (Naik et al., 2021).

Conjugation confirmation of EGFR to liposomes

Fourier transform infrared analysis (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was conducted on the antibody-conjugated liposomes to confirm the incorporation of AGY2. AGY2 liposomes, and EGFR-AGY2 liposomes were analyzed in the spectra range of 65,000–100 cm−1 using an FTIR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Life and Analytical Sciences, Connecticut, USA). The acquired spectra were then used to determine the presence of conjugation between the EGFR antibody and the liposomal nanoparticles (Jayachandran et al., 2023).

Liposomal surface functionalization assay

An immunofluorescence assay was conducted to assess the efficacy of antibody conjugation on the liposome surface. MiaPaCa-2 at a density of 10,000 cells per well were plated and incubated for 24 h in a 6-well plate at 37 °C. Following this, the cells were treated with 1 μM of cationic ammonium rhodamine-linked EGFR-AGY2 LNPs and rhodamine-labeled AGY2 LNPs (without EGFR). The treated cells were further incubated for 12 h. FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG was then applied, and the cells were incubated for an additional 40 min. Subsequently, the cells underwent three washes with PBS, followed by fixation with ice-cold 4% formaldehyde for 10 min at −20 °C. Once fixed, the nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescent images of the treated cells were then captured using a Nikon fluorescent microscope (Naik et al., 2021).

Drug release kinetics

The release profiles of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs and the associated drug were evaluated at pH 7.4, which reflects physiological conditions. A dialysis bag (MWCO 14,000 Da; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), pre-activated with EDTA, was loaded with 10 mL EGFR-AGY2 LNPs and 1 mL AGY2 dispersion. The bags were immersed in PBS with 0.1% PEG 400 surfactant in a glass bottle to simulate sink conditions, stirred at 150 rpm and kept at 37 ± 0.5 °C. At designated intervals, 1 mL samples were taken and replaced with fresh buffer to maintain the conditions. The amount of drug released at these points was determined using RP-HPLC and the graph was constructed using KinetDS software. The values R2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Root mean squared error (RMSE) were captured from the software (Chary et al., 2024; Jain and Jain, 2016).

3D cytotoxicity assay

Spheroids

MiaPaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells were plated into Nunclon Sphera® 96-well plates (Thermo Scientific) at 15,000 and 12,000 cells/well in 100 μL growth medium, respectively. The cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C to form 3D spheroids. AGY2 and EGFR-AGY2 liposomes were prepared at concentrations of 200, 100, 50, 25, 12.5, and 6.25 μM in growth medium, added to spheroids at a 1:1 ratio, and incubated for 24 h, with five wells per concentration. Cell viability was determined by using the MTT assay (Han et al., 2023; Ndemazie et al., 2023).

3D organoids

As in our earlier study, MiaPaCa-2 and PANC-1 cell pellets were dissociated with trypsin, and the resulting cell suspensions were adjusted to 2 × 107 cells/mL. The cell suspension was combined with gelatin-TGase solution (1:2 ratio) to achieve a final density of 5 × 106 cells/mL. 20 μL aliquots were placed in a 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Transglutaminase cross-linked the collagen, supporting organoid formation. Once the collagen lattice structures were established, 500 μL of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium with 10% fetal bovine serum was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The organoids were cultured for 48 h to allow development. Followed by treatment with AGY2 LNPs, EGFR-AGY2 liposomes, free AGY2 and 5-FU as the control over 7 days. The organoids were digested with 0.25% trypsin for 40 min, and cell viability was quantified using an MTT assay. (Han, 2018; Han et al., 2023; Ndemazie et al., 2023).

In-vivo studies

Female athymic nude (Nu/Nu) mice (6–8 weeks old, 25–27 g) were sourced from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). They were housed in a temperature-controlled barrier facility with free access to food and water. All animal procedures followed the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by IACUC (approval number 018–08), Florida A&M University's Animal Care and Use Committee.

Pharmacokinetic studies

Female athymic nude (Nu/Nu) mice (n = 3 per group) received a single intraperitoneal injection of 20 mg/kg free 5-FU, AGY2, AGY2 LNPs, or EGFR-AGY2 LNPs (all at a 5-FU equivalent dose). Blood samples were collected from mice at various time points (1, 5, 10, 15, 30 min; 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), placed in heparinized tubes, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The plasma was stored at −20 °C until analysis (Ndemazie et al., 2023; Sivadasan et al., 2022).

Tumor efficacy study

MiaPaCa-2 cancer cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM with 10 mM HEPES, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ and a humidified environment of 95% air. Mice received subcutaneous injections of MiaPaCa-2 (2.3 × 107 in 100 μL PBS) in the lower right flank. Tumors averaging 110 mm3 formed within two weeks, after which mice were randomly divided into six groups (five mice each): control (saline), free 5-FU, 5-FU LNPs, AGY2, AGY2 LNPs, and EGFR-AGY2 LNPs. Mice in each experimental group received treatment (20 mg/Kg) for 4 weeks, with dosing administered every other day (QD × 3) via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. The control group was given saline rather than active treatment. Intraperitoneal injection was chosen over intravenous (IV) administration to prevent potential vascular damage from repeated injections and to minimize animal stress, since IV procedures require restraint or anesthesia. Tumor size was first measured on the second day of treatment and subsequently monitored every other day for a total duration of 30 days (Fanciullino et al., 2013; Ndemazie et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2014).

Statistical analysis

Design-Expert® analyzed the results of factorial design. The result was statistically validated by analysis of variance, by the statistical significance of coefficients, and R2 values. Statistical analyses were considered significant for p-values <0.01. The drug release patterns were analyzed using KinetDS® software. Particle size, entrapment efficiency, and zeta potential were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect of independent variables on % entrapment efficiency (Response 2)

A BBD was employed to optimize LNPs, aiming to maximize %EE. Multiple independent variables were incorporated into the design of AGY2 LNPs. As shown in Table 1, Response 2 values across all formulations ranged from 61.2% to 80.4%. Comprehensive statistical analysis results are provided in Table 3. Importantly, Table 3 indicates that the p-value for the model addressing Response 2 was 0.0001, which is well below the 0.05 significance level. This finding demonstrates the robust significance of the model (p < 0.05). Therefore, based on the model's p-value, it can be concluded that the quadratic model suitably fits Response 2 (eq. 3).

Particle size is a critical quality attribute affecting the pharmacokinetics and tumor-targeting efficiency of liposomes. All experimental formulations exhibited particle sizes below 120 nm, confirming their suitability for systemic delivery. The perturbation plots (Figs. 6 A and 6B) clearly demonstrated the relative sensitivity of particle size to formulation factors. A steep slope for factor 1 (drug) indicated that increasing drug content led to a measurable rise in particle size. This effect can be attributed to higher internal loading pressure and disruption of lipid packing, leading to vesicle swelling.

The perturbation plots illustrate the effective interaction among responses considering all factors simultaneously: (A) Particle size and (B) Entrapment efficiency.

Conversely, DPPC exerted a moderate negative influence, indicating that increasing its concentration decreased particle size. This can be explained by the tighter bilayer packing afforded by DPPC's saturated acyl chains, leading to smaller, more uniform vesicles. PEG-2000 reduced particle size, consistent with the formation of a hydrated polymeric corona that increases the hydrodynamic diameter of vesicles (Fig. 6A). PEG chains also confer steric stabilization, reducing aggregation despite a slightly larger size. Supporting lipid MPPC exerted a comparatively mild effect, with a nonlinear relationship: moderate levels maintained smaller vesicles, whereas excessive MPPC led to structural fluidization and increased vesicle size.

The perturbation plot (Fig. 6B) showed that DPPC had the most pronounced positive impact on EE. As the DPPC concentration increased, the EE also increased substantially, likely due to enhanced bilayer rigidity that minimized drug leakage and stabilized the hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance. Drug content showed an inverse relationship with higher drug amounts slightly reduced EE, possibly due to saturation of the lipid phase and incomplete encapsulation. This trend is typical of liposomal systems, where the solubility limit within the bilayer is reached at higher drug loads.

MPPC exhibited a positive effect up to an optimal concentration (∼6 mg), beyond which EE declined. The negative quadratic coefficient (−1.22B2) and positive linear term (+1.95D) in the regression equation suggest a balance between lipid fluidity and permeability: sufficient MPPC introduces membrane flexibility beneficial for drug entrapment, but excessive amounts compromise structural tightness, allowing diffusion losses. The PEG-2000 effect on EE was relatively minor but beneficial when used in moderation, enhancing stability without significantly interfering with the encapsulation process.

Preparation of optimized EGFR-AGY2 LNPs

The optimized liposomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration method. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs were assessed. It is essential to consider that particles smaller than 150 nm are necessary to penetrate the tumor microenvironment. The observed particle size and zeta potential of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs were 121.05 ± 9.8 nm, and − 42.03 ± 1.07 mV, respectively, which was attributed to the presence of lipids DPPD and MPPC (Figs. 7 A and B).

Conjugation confirmation

FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to confirm the successful encapsulation of AGY2 into liposomal nanoparticles and subsequent surface conjugation with EGFR antibody. The spectra of free AGY2, AGY2 LNP, and EGFR-AGY2 LNP are shown in Fig. 8.

Solid state characterization: FTIR spectra of AGY2, AGY2 LNP, EGFR-AGY2 LNP formulations.

The spectrum of free AGY2 exhibited a broad absorption band at 3312 cm−1, characteristic of N—H stretching, and distinct peaks at 2950–2819 cm−1 corresponding to C—H stretching vibrations or alkyl groups. Additional prominent peaks were observed at 1448 cm−1 (C—H bending), 1120 cm−1 (S—O stretching), and 615 cm−1 (a bending vibration, such as the amide bending vibration), consistent with the functional groups of the parent molecule.

In the case of AGY2 LNP, shifts in the major peaks were observed. A shift in the broad absorption band to 3298 cm−1 indicated hydrogen bonding interactions between AGY2 and phospholipid components PEG-2000. The appearance of a new a short peak at 1636 cm−1 reflected are not in evidence, which could be explained by the fact that the drug is encapsulated in the matrix structure of the liposome, or due to the overlapping of the chemical bond bands of AGY2 and lipids, while the reduced intensity of the AGY2-specific peaks at 1448 cm−1 and 1120 cm−1 confirmed drug encapsulation within the liposomal matrix. Peaks observed at 1074 and 610 cm−1 were attributed to the phosphate head groups of the phospholipids, further supporting the successful formation of nanoparticles.

The EGFR-AGY2 LNP spectrum demonstrated additional changes confirming antibody attachment. The broadening of the peak at 3290 cm−1 relative to AGY2 LNP indicated further enhancement, suggesting the presence of amide and hydroxyl stretching vibrations from the antibody. A new band at 2104 cm−1, along with enhanced/broadened intensity at 1083 cm−1, was consistent with amide I and II vibrations, confirming the conjugation of EGFR monoclonal antibody to the nanoparticle surface. Moreover, persistence of the lipid-associated peaks at 1636 cm−1 and 618 cm−1 confirmed that the liposomal bilayer remained intact after conjugation. These spectral changes demonstrate the successful functionalization of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs, enhancing the potential for targeted delivery. Overall, the FTIR data validated the stepwise formulation process, confirming the drug's encapsulation in the liposomal carrier and the antibody's conjugation to the nanoparticle surface.

When probed with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, a distinct green-fluorescent signal was observed, which overlapped with the rhodamine signal in merged images. Drug release kinetics analysis

The in-vitro drug release data were fitted to various kinetic models using KinetDS software to determine the release mechanism from the liposomal formulation (Table 5 and Fig. 10). The Weibull model provided the best fit (R2 = 0.9973, AIC = 76.09, RMSE = 10.36), consistent with controlled, sustained drug release behavior. In contrast, the Korsmeyer-Peppas model showed a strong correlation (R2 = 0.9958) and a low error, suggesting diffusion-driven release with possible involvement of matrix relaxation mechanisms. In contrast, the zero-order model (R2 = 0.7178) demonstrated a moderate correlation, suggesting that drug release did not follow a simple constant rate. The first-order model exhibited a very poor correlation (R2 = 0.107), indicating that release was not concentration-dependent. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the release of AGY2 from the liposomal carrier is best explained by the Weibull model, suggesting a sustained-release mechanism rather than a simple diffusion- or concentration-dependent process. This kinetic profile supports the suitability of the liposomal system for prolonged therapeutic action in PCa treatment.

Cell viability in 3D models (Spheroids and Organoids)

To further evaluate the anticancer potential of AGY2 and its liposomal formulations, cell viability assays were performed in three-dimensional spheroid and organoid models of MiaPaCa-2 and PANC-1 PCa cell lines. Compared with free 5-FU, all AGY2 formulations demonstrated superior cytotoxicity, with the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs consistently showing the highest activity. In the spheroid model (Table 6), EGFR-AGY2 LNPs displayed the strongest inhibitory effect on cell viability, with IC₅₀ values of 7.7 ± 2.3 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 9.63 ± 0.9 μM in PANC-1 cells. These values were significantly lower than those observed for free 5-FU, which had IC₅₀ values of 36.63 ± 4.8 μM and 40.78 ± 5.9 μM, respectively (p = 0.0001). AGY2 LNPs also produced notable reductions in cell viability, achieving IC₅₀ values of 16.53 ± 3.7 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 18.53 ± 5.7 μM in PANC-1, both of which were statistically superior to 5-FU (p < 0.001). Free AGY2 alone demonstrated intermediate potency, with IC₅₀ values of 27.58 ± 4.2 μM and 31.51 ± 5.5 μM. Although less effective than the nanoparticle formulations, these values remained significantly more cytotoxic than those of 5-FU. In organoid models (Table 7), a similar pattern of activity was observed, although the IC₅₀ values were slightly higher, reflecting the greater structural complexity and drug resistance characteristic of organoid cultures. The EGFR-AGY2 LNPs again exhibited the most pronounced activity, with IC₅₀ values of 10.83 ± 3.3 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and 12.2 ± 3.9 μM in PANC-1 organoids, both significantly better than 5-FU (p = 0.0001). Non-targeted AGY2 LNPs produced IC₅₀ values of 25.09 ± 3.9 μM and 29.09 ± 4.9 μM in MiaPaCa-2 and PANC-1, respectively, while free AGY2 achieved IC₅₀ values of 35.18 ± 3.7 μM and 32.56 ± 5.1 μM. In contrast, free 5-FU again exhibited the weakest cytotoxicity, with IC₅₀ values of 47.93 ± 4.4 μM and 45.96 ± 4.8 μM. These findings demonstrate that liposomal encapsulation significantly enhanced the cytotoxicity of AGY2, and this effect was further amplified upon EGFR conjugation, suggesting that receptor-mediated endocytosis played a critical role in improving intracellular drug delivery. Importantly, AGY2, whether free or formulated, exhibited markedly greater cytotoxicity than 5-FU, providing strong evidence that chemical modification of 5-FU enhances its anticancer efficacy in PCa models.

Stability studies:

The stability of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs was systematically assessed over 90 days under different storage conditions (4 °C, 25 °C, and 40 °C) to evaluate changes in particle size and EE. In Table 8, at baseline (Day 30), formulations stored at 4 °C exhibited the smallest particle size (113.36 nm) and the highest EE (79.80%), while those at 25 °C and 40 °C showed slightly larger particle sizes (125.54 nm and 129.23 nm, respectively) with modest reductions in EE (70.56% and 68.31%). Over 60 days, particle size increased progressively at elevated temperatures, with the largest growth observed at 40 °C (141.55 nm), whereas formulations stored at 4 °C remained relatively stable (116.97 nm). A similar trend was observed for EE, which decreased to 67.64% and 62.68% at 25 °C and 40 °C, respectively, while remaining highest at 4 °C (80.99%). By Day 90, particle size expansion was more pronounced, particularly at 40 °C, where the formulation reached 160.73 nm, accompanied by a decline in EE to 66.71%. In contrast, the 4 °C storage condition exhibited only minor fluctuations in particle size (118.12 nm) and maintained an acceptable EE (75.58%), suggesting superior long-term stability at refrigeration temperatures. Overall, the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs demonstrated good stability for up to 90 days, especially at 4 °C, with only modest increases in particle size and gradual reductions in EE. Importantly, despite storage-related variations, the formulations maintained a nanoscale size and high drug retention, indicating that antibody conjugation did not significantly compromise the liposomal system's physicochemical stability. These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that PEGylated and antibody-functionalized liposomes retain their structural and encapsulation stability over extended storage periods when maintained under refrigerated conditions.

Pharmacokinetic study

The pharmacokinetic profiles of 5-FU, AGY2, AGY2 LNP, and EGFR-AGY2 LNP were investigated to evaluate the influence of liposomal encapsulation and active targeting on systemic circulation, clearance, and overall drug exposure Table 9. A significant difference was observed in the pharmacokinetic parameters between the free drug (5-FU) and the liposomal formulations. The elimination rate constant (K10) decreased markedly from 1.82 h-1 for 5-FU to 0.39 h-1 for EGFR-AGY2 LNPs, indicating slower elimination and prolonged systemic retention (Table 9). Tumor efficacy study

The anti-tumor efficacy of the developed formulations was evaluated in a pancreatic tumor-bearing mouse model over 30 days. Tumor growth curves (Fig. 11) revealed a progressive increase in tumor volume across all groups; however, the extent of tumor inhibition varied markedly between treatments. The control group (PBS) showed rapid tumor progression, with volumes exceeding 3000 mm3 by day 30. Treatment with 5-FU moderately suppressed tumor growth, reducing the final tumor volume to approximately 2200 mm3, whereas 5-FU LNPs showed greater inhibition (∼1800 mm3), indicating enhanced bioavailability and sustained release via liposomal encapsulation. The newly synthesized AGY2 analog demonstrated significantly higher tumor inhibition than free 5-FU, reducing tumor size to around 1500 mm3, suggesting that chemical modification improved therapeutic potency. Notably, AGY2 LNPs further decreased tumor volume (∼1200 mm3), reflecting the benefit of nanoencapsulation in improving drug accumulation at the tumor site via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. The EGFR-AGY2 LNPs produced the most pronounced antitumor effect, with final tumor volumes around 900 mm3, showing a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001) compared to both 5-FU and AGY2. The superior efficacy of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs is attributed to their active targeting of EGFR-overexpressing pancreatic tumor cells, leading to enhanced internalization and localized cytotoxicity. Macroscopic examination of excised tumors corroborated these findings. Tumors from the control and 5-FU groups appeared large and vascularized, whereas those from the AGY2 and AGY2 LNPs groups were smaller and less dense. The EGFR- AGY2 LNPs group exhibited the smallest and most necrotic tumor masses, confirming potent in vivo antitumor activity. Overall, these results highlight the significant therapeutic advantage of EGFR-AGY2 LNP delivered AGY2 over conventional and non-targeted formulations. The combination of chemical modification and site-specific targeting significantly improved anti-tumor efficacy, offering a promising therapeutic approach for PCa.

Discussion

PCa has the lowest five-year survival rate compared to other cancers, and current treatments have the disadvantages of conventional chemotherapy, which include a lack of selectivity and multidrug resistance (Zhao et al., 2022). To mitigate the severe toxicities of conventional chemotherapeutics to normal tissues, nanocarriers, such as ligand-targeted liposomes, can be used for site-specific delivery of chemotherapeutics (Srinivasarao and Low, 2017). Ligand-targeted nanoparticles have shown promising results in overcoming these limitations, offering the advantages of both passive (EPR) and active tumor-specific targeting (ligand) functions (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2023). AGY2 was synthesized by strategically modifying the 5-FU scaffold by introducing a tetrahydrofuran and carbamate moieties at metabolically vulnerable positions, thereby reducing susceptibility to enzymatic breakdown and significantly enhancing metabolic stability, as described in our previous work (Bulusu et al., 2025). The present study demonstrates the successful development and optimization of an EGFR-targeted liposomal formulation of AGY2, resulting in enhanced anticancer activity against pancreatic tumors. This dual modification strategy prolonged systemic exposure, enhanced tumor-specific uptake, and resulted in significantly improved anticancer activity compared to the unmodified drug. The integration of chemical modification with nanocarrier-based and receptor-targeted delivery provides a multidimensional strategy to overcome the pharmacological and physiological limitations of conventional chemotherapy in PCa (Jha et al., 2024a; Jha et al., 2024b; Yadav et al., 2025).

In literature, PEGylated 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) liposomal formulations have been successfully utilized in combination therapies for both pancreatic and colorectal cancers (Le et al., 2018). Numerous studies have evaluated various liposomal and targeted formulations in EGFR-positive PCa models, highlighting the promise of this targeted approach for improving drug efficacy and selectivity while minimizing off-target effects (Le et al., 2018). Our earlier work and other studies have demonstrated that EGFR is highly overexpressed in PCa, making it a compelling therapeutic target for drug delivery strategies (Bulusu et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2012; Oliveira-Cunha et al., 2011). Due to this well-established overexpression, an EGFR-targeted drug delivery system was selected to encapsulate the AGY2 molecule, with the specific aim of directing treatment toward EGFR-positive PCa cells.

The BBD was selected for formulation optimization because it is a statistically efficient response surface methodology, well-suited for exploring quadratic relationships among multiple formulation variables while minimizing the number of experimental runs. In the present study, BBD enabled systematic evaluation of the individual and interactive effects of critical formulation parameters such as lipid concentration, drug-to-lipid ratio, and surfactant content on key quality attributes, including particle size and encapsulation efficiency. This design revealed strong correlations between experimental and predicted outcomes (R2 = 0.8432 for particle size and 0.9384 for entrapment efficiency). These physicochemical characteristics are ideal for exploiting the EPR effect, promoting tumor accumulation while minimizing premature clearance (Yingchoncharoen et al., 2016). Further facilitates the accumulation of liposomes in the tumor by highly leaky tumor blood vessels (Hashizume et al., 2000). Such parameters are consistent with prior studies on HER-2, EGFR, and folate-targeted liposomes, where nanoscale size and surface charge played critical roles in enhancing tumor selectivity and systemic stability (de Sousa et al., 2024; Naik et al., 2021; Sriraman et al., 2016). The optimized EGFR-AGY2 LNPs exhibited a particle size of approximately 121 nm, narrow PDI (<0.20), and a high negative zeta potential (−42 mV), indicating colloidal stability and uniform dispersion.

The physicochemical analysis proved a slight increase in the particle size of AGY2 LNPs (111.2 ± 8.34 nm) compared to the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs (121.05 ± 9.8 nm) (Fig. 6A and B). On the other hand, the zeta potential of AGY2 LNPs (−36.58 ± 0.97 mV) decreased in comparison to the EGFR-AGY2 LNPs (−42.03 ± 1.07 mV). Similar results were reported by Suga et al., who reported a slight increase in the particle size (Suga et al., 2018) and Schliper et al. reported a decrease in the zeta potential upon ligand addition onto the liposomal surface (Schlieper et al., 1981).

FTIR spectroscopy and fluorescence microscopy indicated the stepwise synthesis of the intended liposomes. Shifts in AGY2 peaks and the emergence of new amide I and II bands (2104 and 1083 cm−1) were consistent with covalent antibody attachment, while the continued presence of lipid-associated peaks suggested bilayer integrity Fig. 8. Similarly, Moreira et al. reported comparable peak trends in EGFR-targeted Docetaxel immunoliposomes. (Moreira et al., 2023). The immunofluorescence assay demonstrated strong colocalization of FITC and rhodamine signals, confirming that EGFR antibodies were firmly anchored on the liposomal surface. These findings parallel those of HER2- and transferrin-functionalized liposomes, where antibody conjugation significantly enhanced receptor-mediated endocytosis and cellular uptake (Naik et al., 2021; Skupin-Mrugalska and Minko, 2020). The preserved bilayer integrity post-conjugation also suggests that the functionalization process did not compromise the liposomal structure or stability.

The drug release studies revealed a biphasic pattern characterized by an initial burst followed by sustained release over 24 h Fig. 10. Among various kinetic models tested, the Weibull model also supported a controlled diffusion mechanism (R2 = 0.9973). Zeyad AlMajed et al. and Tao Lu et al. worked on sono-sensitive and thermosensitive liposomal targeted drug delivery systems, respectively, and observed a Weibull drug release pattern (AlMajed et al., 2022; Lu and Ten Hagen, 2020). This release profile demonstrates that AGY2 remains securely encapsulated within the liposomal bilayer, with release governed by lipid drug interactions and diffusion through the hydrated shell (Al Sawaftah et al., 2021; Jain and Jain, 2016). Such diffusion-controlled kinetics are consistent with reports that Doxorubicin and Curcumin liposomes contributed to sustained therapeutic activity and reduced systemic toxicity (Al Sawaftah et al., 2021). The ability of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs to maintain prolonged release supports their potential to achieve continuous drug exposure at tumor sites.

The 3D spheroid and organoid assays demonstrated the superior cytotoxic performance of EGFR-AGY2 LNPs compared to free AGY2, non-targeted AGY2 LNPs, and 5-FU. Enhanced activity is attributed to efficient receptor-mediated endocytosis via EGFR binding, facilitating deeper intracellular accumulation. The differences observed between spheroids and organoids also indicate that tumor microenvironmental barriers, such as extracellular matrix density and diffusion limitations, may influence drug response, yet the superiority of the EGFR-targeted formulation remained consistent across both models. The consistent potency observed across both spheroid and organoid models underscores the ability of EGFR-targeting to overcome the diffusion and stromal barriers typical of pancreatic tumors. The increased efficacy of AGY2 compared to 5-FU also highlights the molecular advantage conferred by its fluorinated bis-tetrahydrofuran scaffold, which may improve cell permeability and metabolic stability, as explained in our previous study (Bulusu et al., 2025). These results align with previous reports of HER-2 and EGFR targeted liposomes that achieved superior tumor cell killing in receptor-overexpressing cancer models (Naik et al., 2021).

The long-term stability study showed that the formulation retained its nanoscale size and encapsulation efficiency for 90 days at 4 °C, with values of 118 nm and 75% EE recorded at day 90. In contrast, elevated storage temperatures (25 °C and 40 °C) resulted in a gradual increase in particle size and a reduction in EE, likely due to lipid bilayer fluidization and increased drug leakage. These results confirm that refrigeration effectively preserves the physical and chemical integrity of the liposomes. PEGylation and antibody conjugation did not destabilize the lipid matrix, consistent with previous findings where PEG-stabilized, ligand-functionalized liposomes retained their structural and encapsulation stability during long-term storage (Naik et al., 2021).

Pharmacokinetic evaluation demonstrated that liposomal encapsulation significantly prolonged the circulation time and bioavailability of AGY2. The elimination rate constant (K₁₀) dropped from 1.82 h−1 for 5-FU to 0.39 h−1 for EGFR-AGY2 LNPs, while half-life (t₁/₂) increased nearly fivefold to 1.77 h. The clearance rate decreased by an order of magnitude (from 259.58 to 21.32 mL/h), and AUC increased more than 12-fold (2.08 to 25.32 μg*h/mL), indicating sustained systemic exposure. These improvements are consistent with the pharmacokinetic advantages reported for antibody-modified liposomes, where PEG shielding and receptor targeting delay clearance via the reticuloendothelial system. (Gupta et al., 2023) provided clear evidence that immunoliposomes significantly improve the delivery of therapeutic agents to their intended targets. Additionally, immunoliposomes improved pharmacodynamic outcomes by facilitating greater cellular uptake and sustained drug release, thereby increasing their therapeutic effectiveness at the target site (Noble et al., 2014; Rommasi and Esfandiari, 2021). The reduced volume of distribution (Vd = 54.66 mL) suggests confinement within the vascular and tumor compartments, aligning with the intended mechanism of tumor-localized delivery. Similarly, the improved pharmacokinetic and retention of LNPs within the system were observed in Camptothecin encapsulated in pegylated LNPs (Sivadasan et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings confirm that EGFR-AGY2 LNPs effectively combine passive and active targeting mechanisms to improve drug retention and bioavailability (Sivadasan et al., 2022).

The in-vivo tumor inhibition study demonstrated that EGFR-AGY2 LNPs produced the most pronounced tumor suppression among all tested groups, reducing tumor volume to ∼900 mm3 after 30 days, compared to 1500 mm3 for free AGY2 and over 3000 mm3 in the control group Fig. 11. The enhanced efficacy is attributed to receptor-specific accumulation, prolonged circulation, and sustained release at the tumor site. These outcomes are consistent with earlier findings in Anti-EGFR immunoliposomes for cabazitaxel delivery, where active targeting significantly enhanced in vivo prostate tumor regression compared to non-targeted formulations (de Sousa et al., 2024). Similar outcomes were observed when Cmab was conjugated to chitosan/hyaluronic acid-based nanoparticles and to D-alpha-tocopheryl-chitosan/hyaluronic acid-based nanoparticles to target EGFR-positive tumors in vivo. By facilitating the targeted delivery of cabazitaxel to EGFR-overexpressing breast cancer cells, this functionalized system achieved significant tumor regression compared to both the free drug and non-targeted nanoparticles (Jha et al., 2024a; Jha et al., 2024b).

The superior performance of EGFR-AGY₂ liposomes arises from a synergistic combination of factors: (i) enhanced selectivity via receptor-mediated uptake, (ii) extended circulation from PEGylation, (iii) sustained drug release, and (iv) improved retention in tumor tissue due to nanoscale size and negative surface charge. EGFR overexpression is a known driver of pancreatic tumor progression and therapeutic resistance; therefore, targeting this receptor offers dual benefits, both direct inhibition of signaling and improved cytotoxic drug delivery. Compared to conventional 5-FU, which is rapidly metabolized and cleared, AGY2 exhibits greater stability and potency, while liposomal encapsulation minimizes systemic exposure.

Conclusion

The findings of this study align with and extend current literature on ligand-targeted nanocarriers, confirming that molecular recognition-based delivery systems can substantially improve the therapeutic index of anticancer agents. The improved pharmacokinetic parameters, higher tumor accumulation, and superior cytotoxicity observed here establish EGFR-AGY2 liposomes as a potent formulation for PCa management. Future studies should focus on AGY2-induced apoptosis, biodistribution and immunohistochemistry, mechanistic exploration, modulation of EGFR signaling, and extended safety profiling to facilitate translation of this approach toward clinical application.
