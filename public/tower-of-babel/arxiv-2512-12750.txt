Improved Concentration for Mean Estimators via Shrinkage
Antônio Catão, Lucas Resende, Paulo Orenstein

Abstract
We study a class of robust mean estimators μ ^ \widehat{\mu} obtained by adaptively shrinking the weights of sample points far from a base estimator κ ^ \widehat{\kappa} . Given a data-dependent scaling factor α ^ \widehat{\alpha} and a weighting function w : [ 0 , ∞ ) → [ 0 , 1 ] w:[0,\infty)\to[0,1] , we let μ ^ = κ ^ + 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n ( X i − κ ^ ) ​ w ​ ( α ^ ​ | X i − κ ^ | ) \widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\kappa}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa})w(\widehat{\alpha}|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|) . We prove that, under mild assumptions over w w , these estimators achieve stronger concentration bounds than the base estimate κ ^ \widehat{\kappa} , including sub-Gaussian guarantees. This framework unifies and extends several existing approaches to robust mean estimation in ℝ \mathbb{R} . Through numerical experiments, we show that our shrinking approach translates to faster concentration, even for small sample sizes.

Introduction

Let
P
be an unknown distribution over
\mathbb{R}
and let
X_{1:n}=(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\sim P^{\otimes n}
be an independent sample from
P
. Our goal is to approximate the mean
\mu:=\mathbb{E}X_{1}
through an estimator
\widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\mu}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. In this setting, a canonical choice for
\widehat{\mu}
is the empirical mean
\overline{X}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Indeed, this estimator possesses many appealing properties, such as consistency, asymptotic efficiency, and admissibility among large classes of distributions.

Nevertheless, as observed by
29
, the empirical mean presents important limitations when the underlying distribution deviates from the Gaussian. This motivated the study of robust mean estimation, whose aim is to provide good estimators under challenging settings. For instance,
11
developed a systematic treatment for location estimation to minimize the asymptotic variance under adversarial distributions.
Typically, robust mean estimators try to improve on the empirical mean by reducing the sensitivity to unlikely but extreme points in the sample, often exploiting the idea of “outlyingness”, through order statistics or distance from a good centrality estimate. Some examples are given by the following estimators:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{TM}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n-2k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k}X_{(i)},

(1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{WM}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{(i\vee(k+1)\wedge(n-k))},

(2)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{LV}^{\eta}=\widehat{\kappa}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\kappa)(1-\min(\alpha^{2}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert^{2},1)),

(3)

\alpha

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(\alpha^{2}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert^{2},1)=\eta

The trimmed (

1.1
Main results

1.1.1
Optimal concentration via shrinkage

We prove concentration for
\widehat{\mu}
under adversarial contamination; we assume that an underlying sample
X_{1:n}\sim P^{\otimes n}
is adversarially contaminated with contamination level
\varepsilon\in[0,1]

X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon):=\{Y_{1:n}:\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:X_{i}\neq Y_{i}\}\leq n\varepsilon\}

\widehat{\kappa}

\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})

\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}

\widehat{\kappa}

\delta\in(0,1)

R_{\widehat{\kappa}}(\delta):=\inf\left\{r>0:\mathbb{P}\left[|\widehat{\kappa}-\mu|>r\right]\leq\delta\right\}.

(5)

We then have the following informal version of the main result, presented in
Section

3

Theorem 1.1

(Concentration under adversarial contamination - informal statement)
.

Assume
\eta=\ln\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{3}{2}n\varepsilon
and that
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})
is computed on the contaminated sample
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}

\widehat{\mu}
given by (
4

\widehat{\kappa}
computed on an independent (possibly corrupted) sample. Further assume that
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}(\delta)\leq C_{\widehat{\kappa}}\nu_{p}

\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\mu\rvert\leq C_{w,\widehat{\kappa},\varepsilon}\left[\inf_{1<p\leq 2}\nu_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}+\inf_{p>1}\nu_{p}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\right],

1.1.2
Improved concentration rates

Indeed, our main result is more general; it guarantees concentration bounds without necessarily assuming that
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}
is conveniently bounded. For the sake of exposition, let us consider the uncontaminated case (
\varepsilon=0

3.1

Theorem 1.2

(Main result - informal statement)
.

Let
\widehat{\kappa}
be independent of
X_{1:n}
,
n
and
\delta>e^{-cn}

\eta=\ln\frac{1}{\delta}
and
\widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})

In other words, if
\widehat{\kappa}

(n,\delta)

\widehat{\mu}
offers an improvement over
\widehat{\kappa}

\widehat{\kappa}
be the empirical average of some independent sample of size

Contrastingly with respect to the result presented in

1.1.3
Breakdown point, asymptotic efficiency and affine equivariance

Beyond optimal concentration rates under mild assumptions on
\widehat{\kappa}
, our shrinkage estimator
\widehat{\mu}
also inherits other desirable properties from the base estimator. For example, if
\widehat{\kappa}
is affine-equivariant then so is
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa})
. Another desirable property that
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})
enjoys is asymptotic efficiency; fix
\eta>0
and denote
\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}_{n};X_{1:n})
for a sequence of
\widehat{\kappa}_{n}
independent of
X_{1:n}
. If we assume that
\nu_{p}<\infty
for any
p>2
and that
(\widehat{\kappa}_{n}-\mu)n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\to 0
in probability, then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\mu)\overset{d}{\to}N(0,\nu_{2}^{2})
. That is, the shrinkage estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{n}
is undistinguishable from the empirical mean in terms of asymptotic distribution. Notice that the assumption that
(\widehat{\kappa}_{n}-\mu)n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\to 0
is very weak: in particular it holds for any constant base estimator
\widehat{\kappa}_{n}
.

Finally, we also show that, under mild assumptions on
w
, the breakdown point of the estimator
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa})
, given by
(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})\mapsto\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m});X_{1:n})
,

\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}),(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\in[0,1]:\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m});X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon});X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})\rvert=\infty\right\}.

is at least as high as that of
\widehat{\kappa}
, as long as
\eta\geq n\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\kappa};Y_{1:m})
. When
\widehat{\kappa}
is taken to be the sample median of
Y_{1:m}
and
\eta\geq n/2
, this implies that
\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}),(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m}))=1/2
for every pair of samples
(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})
. The formal statements of these properties are given and proved in
Section

4
.

1.2
Related works

Estimators satisfying concentration bounds such as the ones given in (
6
) have been the focus of many recent papers in robust mean estimation. In particular, for the case
\varepsilon=0
and restricting the infima to
p=2
, these estimators are called
\delta
-dependent sub-Gaussian mean estimators.
Indeed,
7
showed that such guarantees are optimal in terms of probabilistic concentration. Recent efforts have established similar high-probability bounds. The first work to study whether estimators are sub-Gaussian in the above sense was
5
. In this paper, Catoni shows that Chebyshev’s inequality is sharp, implying that the empirical mean concentrates badly for certain distributions with finite variance. He then proposes a sub-Gaussian estimator that is constructed as a
Z
-estimator satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(X_{i}-\mu)=0
, for
\psi(x)=\phi(\beta x)
where
\beta>0
is a parameter,
\phi
is increasing and satisfies
-\ln(1-x+x^{2}/2)\leq\phi(x)\leq\ln(1+x+x^{2}/2)
. The parameter
\beta
plays the role of a scaling factor and the bounds on
\phi
enforce diminishing contributions from outliers.
5
only manages to obtain sub-Gaussian deviations assuming knowledge of an upper bound
\nu\geq\sigma^{2}
and setting
\beta\approx\sqrt{(2/(n\nu)\ln(1/\delta)}
. With no prior knowledge of
\sigma^{2}
, Lepski’s method
(
16
)
can be employed to select
\beta
, yielding an almost sub-Gaussian bound. In fact,
7
showed that, in order for an estimator to attain these guarantees without prior knowledge of
\sigma^{2}
, it needs to be
\delta-
dependent, justifying our choice of the parameter
\eta
as a function of
\delta
in
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
.

Following
5
, several papers have shown that classical estimators are also sub-Gaussian (see the survey
18
). For instance,
17
showed that the median-of-means
(
25
;
12
;
1
)
is sub-Gaussian when
P
has second moment;
4
showed that (
6
) also holds when the infima are restricted to any single
p\in(1,2)
. Recently,
26
showed the general adversarially contaminated case (
6
) for the trimmed mean (
1
), and
18
proved that the Winsorized mean (
2
) is sub-Gaussian assuming the existence of second moment.

Assuming existence of second moment,
15
obtained the optimal sub-Gaussian constant
L=\sqrt{2}
under the asymptotic regime
\big(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta},\delta\big)\to(0,0)
by employing the estimator (
3
). They do so through a reweighting scheme on top of an already sub-Gaussian base estimator
\widehat{\kappa}
, exploiting a data-dependent scaling factor analogous to the Z-estimator
\widehat{\alpha}
. More recently,
14
showed that this estimator preserves optimal concentration bounds under adversarial contamination and infinite-variance distributions under the same asymptotics and assuming
\varepsilon n\in O\big(\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\big)
. Our framework is fundamentally more general and conceptually distinct in two key aspects. First, we make no structural assumptions on the base estimator
\widehat{\kappa}
, which can be any preliminary estimate of location, and may not even be sub-Gaussian. Second, our work introduces a broad class of shrinkage functions
w(\cdot)
. This generality comes at the cost of assuming that
\widehat{\kappa}
is independent of
X_{1:n}
, which we believe to be an artifact of our proofs.

Other novel sub-Gaussian estimators have also been proposed.
20
studies concentration properties of mean estimators through their influence function under the adversarial contamination context.
21
introduce an estimator that splits the sample in
K
buckets, and use the empirical deviation
\widehat{\sigma}_{j}
and empirical mean
\widehat{\mu}_{j}
of each bucket
j=1,2,\dots,K
to construct the weighted average
\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{\mu}_{j}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{-r}/\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{-r}
for some parameter
r\geq 1
, showing that it is robust to a weaker form of contamination.

23
studies some modifications to the median-of-means construction to achieve better constants (see also
13
).

The idea of using an initial location estimate and then reweighting the sample points to obtain a better estimate is not new. W-estimators were introduced by
24
as estimators
(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}
, where the weights
w_{i}
are a non-increasing function of the normalized deviation
\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert
, and
\widehat{\kappa}
is chosen to be the sample median.
9
then generalized this idea to higher dimensions, providing breakdown point guarantees for such estimators. In fact, beyond the estimator given in (
4
), we also show that concentration properties hold for a related estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{W}
, introduced in
Section

2
.

.

The alternative estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{W}(\kappa;X_{1:n}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)|X_{i}-\kappa|)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)},

2.2
Assumptions

To obtain concentration bounds for
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
, we will assume the following four hypotheses.

Assumption 1
.

All distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
are atom-free.

This assumption ensures that there are no ties in the uncontaminated data (that is,
\left\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\right\rvert\neq\left\lvert X_{j}-\widehat{\kappa}\right\rvert
for
i\neq j
— we allow ties for the contaminated sample
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}
). This yields tight lower bounds on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa})\left\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\right\rvert)
(see
Lemma

B.1
) and makes
\mathbb{E}[w(\alpha|X_{1}|)]
continuous in
\alpha
(see the proof of
Lemma

3.4
). This assumption is relatively weak: it is not necessary if
w
is continuous and, even if that is not the case, we can consider the distribution
P^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}=X_{1}+\sigma Z
for
Z\sim N(0,1)
independent of
X_{1}
and
\sigma>0
arbitrarily small. Then,
P^{\prime}
is atom-free, with the same mean as
P
and centered
L^{p}
norm
\nu^{\prime}_{p}
bounded by
\nu_{p}+\sigma(\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{p}])^{1/p}
. Our results can then be applied to
P^{\prime}
.

Assumption 2
.

The function
w(\cdot)
satisfies
\sup_{t\geq 0}tw(t)<\infty
.

This hypothesis ensures that
w(t)
decays at least as fast as
1/t
for large
t
. Consider the function

m(\alpha):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|x|w(\alpha|x|)=\alpha^{-1}\sup_{t\geq 0}tw(t),

(10)

for
\alpha>0
. Informally,
m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})
measures the maximum contribution that a sample point can have towards the sum in (
8
). Note that Assumption
2
is satisfied whenever
w
has compact support, which is the case for some natural choices of

(see Section
5
). By Assumption
2

m(\alpha)<\infty
for all
\alpha>0
and by (
10
),
m(\alpha)
is decreasing, so lower-bounding
\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
by a positive value with high probability implies that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa})w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|)
is a sum of bounded random variables (conditioned on a high-probability event). This will be a core tool to obtain concentration bounds for
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
, relying on
m(\underline{\alpha})
where

\underline{\alpha}
is some lower bound of
\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}

Assumption 3
.

The function

satisfies

This hypothesis ensures

Assumption 4
.

The base estimator
\widehat{\kappa}
is independent from the sample
X_{1:n}
.

This assumption is used to control the empirical processes that arise in our proof, and we believe it is ultimately just a proof artifact. Nevertheless, to obtain sub-Gaussian concentration bounds, we only require
|\widehat{\kappa}-\mu|
to be of order
\nu_{p}
. In practice, this means that for a sample of size
n
, one can allocate a small subset of observations to estimate
\widehat{\kappa}
, reserving most of the data for evaluating (
8
). We empirically investigate several data-splitting strategies in
Section

6
.

3
Concentration of shrinkage estimators

We now state the main result of this paper in full generality, which provides concentration bounds for the shrinkage estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
8
). The main ingredients for the proof are discussed in
Section

3.1
, with details included in
Appendix

B
.

Theorem 3.1

(Concentration of shrinkage estimators under adversarial contamination)
.

Let
X_{1:n}\sim P^{\otimes n}
with
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
\varepsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
. Under
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, set
\eta=\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+(1+\xi)\varepsilon n
for some
\xi>0
. Assume
(1+2\xi)\varepsilon+\frac{\overline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}<1
, where
\overline{c}=16+4\xi^{-1}
. If
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})
is as in (
8
) and
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}
is as in (
5
), then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)
,
\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\mu\rvert
is at most

C_{w,\xi}\left\{\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\left(\nu_{q}+\nu_{q}^{\frac{q}{2}}R_{\widehat{\kappa}}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}+\inf_{1<q\leq p}\left(\nu_{q}+R_{\widehat{\kappa}}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\varepsilon\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\right\}

(11)

for
C_{w,\xi}>0
depending only on
w
and
\xi
.

In particular, if there exist constants
C,\delta_{\textup{min}}>0
depending only on the base estimator such that
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}(\frac{\delta}{4})\leq C\nu_{p}
for
\delta\geq\delta_{\textup{min}}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
attains for
\delta\geq\delta_{\textup{min}}\vee 4e^{-\frac{1-(1+2\xi)\varepsilon}{\overline{c}}n}
:

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}|\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\mu|>\tilde{C}_{w,\xi}\left\{\nu_{2\wedge p}\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2\wedge p}}+\nu^{p}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\right\}\right]\leq\delta,

(12)

where
\tilde{C}_{w,\xi}=C_{w,\xi}(2C+3)
.

Remark 2
.

While
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}=O(1)
is sufficient for attaining sub-Gaussian guarantees, for example, one can conclude from (
11
) that having moments
p>2
leads to an improved bound, in the sense that
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
might retain light tails even if
\widehat{\kappa}
has poorer concentration. For example, if
p=4,\varepsilon=0
, and
\widehat{\kappa}
is such that

R_{\widehat{\kappa}}(\delta)=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1/4}\right),

with an increasingly poor concentration even as
\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\to 0
, then
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
still retains sub-Gaussian rate tails.
Notice also that if
w(t)\geq\mathbf{1}_{t<1}
,
3
is satisfied for every
p>1
so, in particular, the infimum in (
11
) can be taken in
(1,2]
for the first term and in
(1,\infty)
for the second. It is under this scenario that
Theorem

1.1
holds (taking
\xi=\frac{1}{2}
). We explore choices of
w
satisfying this lower bound in
Section

5
.

Informally,
Theorem

3.1
describes the error associated to the estimation of the population mean
\mu
through the estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
. The error in (
11
) can be decomposed into two terms: one that depends on
R_{\widehat{\kappa}}
and one that does not. The latter term corresponds to the optimal order of mean estimation. The former term is associated with the quality of the base estimate combined with the contamination level and is significantly mitigated when
\delta\gg e^{-cn}
, since the term
\left(n^{-1}\ln(4\delta^{-1})\right)^{1-1/p}
is small in this regime, leaving only the error associated to the contamination
\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{p}}
. This means that for sufficiently large sample sizes,
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
may concentrate well around the mean
\mu
even if
\widehat{\kappa}
does not.
Remark

2
emphasizes the magnification of this effect as
P
presents finite moments of higher order.

We now explore the consequences of
Theorem

3.1
, exhibiting examples of shrinkage estimators with specific choices of base estimators that yield mean-estimators with optimal concentration rates. We will consider estimates computed on an independent sample
Y_{1:m}\sim P^{\otimes m}
. To account for possible adversarial contamination (with the same level
\varepsilon
) on the sample
Y_{1:m}
, we allow
\widehat{\kappa}
to take the form of
\widehat{\kappa}(f(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon))
, where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a Borel-measurable function such that
f(y_{1:m},\gamma)\in\mathcal{A}(y_{1:m},\gamma)
for every possible sample
y_{1:m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and contamination level
\gamma\in[0,1]
. Notice that
f(y_{1:m},0)=y_{1:m}
.

Example 1

(Empirical mean)
.

Assume that
\varepsilon=0
. Let
\widehat{\kappa}=\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})=\overline{Y}
. Recall that the empirical mean
\overline{Y}
satisfies
R_{\overline{Y}}(\delta)\leq\nu_{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta m}}
for

. Then, (

) holds for

,

, and
\delta_{\textup{min}}=\frac{4}{m}
. Notice that

is necessary in this case, since the empirical mean is not robust to adversarial contamination. In fact,
\sup_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{Y^{\varepsilon}};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)\to\infty
as any
Y_{i}^{\varepsilon}\to\infty
, rendering this estimator useless under adversarial contamination.

Proposition 3.2

(Concentration of empirical quantiles)

Let

for every

The proof is deferred to
Appendix

Example 2

(Empirical quantiles)
.

Let
\widehat{\kappa}=\widehat{\kappa}(f(Y_{1:n},\varepsilon))=\widehat{Q}_{\gamma}(f(Y_{1:n},\varepsilon))
be an empirical quantile of a possibly contaminated version of the sample

. It is easy to see that

. In particular, the sample median satisfies the condition

for any

. Then, if

, with probability at least

(13)

for some
C_{w,\xi,\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
w
,
\xi
, and
\varepsilon
.

. In particular, if

, then, for

, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\mu\rvert\leq C_{w,\xi}\left\{\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\nu_{q}\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}+\inf_{1<q\leq p}\nu_{q}\varepsilon^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\right\},

for some

and

Example 4

(Optimal

-dependent mean-estimators)
.

Assume that the

3.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

We now state the results that are used to prove
Theorem

3.1
.
For this section and the appendices, denote the uncontaminated shrinkage estimator based on
\widehat{\kappa}
given by
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})
as
\widehat{\mu}
. Similarly, denote
\widehat{\alpha}:=\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})
. The proof of
Theorem

3.1
follows by showing that
\widehat{\mu}
concentrates well and that the error
\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}\rvert
is small.
Theorems

3.3
,
3.4
and
3.5
are dedicated to showing the former, while
Lemma

3.6
justifies the latter. The main difficulty in proving concentration bounds for
\widehat{\mu}
is to control the dependence between the parameter
\widehat{\alpha}
and the sample
X_{1:n}
.
To that end, we will first assume that, for each
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa):=\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n})
is, with high probability, in an interval
I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
such that the shrinkage estimator’s error may be properly controlled, for fixed
\kappa
. This is done by bounding
|\widehat{\mu}(\kappa;X_{1:n})-\mu|
by
\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}|s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu|
where

s(\alpha,\kappa):=\kappa+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\kappa)w(\alpha|X_{i}-\kappa|).

The bias induced by a fixed choice of
\alpha
and
\kappa
on the weighted sum
s(\alpha,\kappa)
is

b(\alpha,\kappa):=\mathbb{E}s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu=\mathbb{E}\left(\kappa+(X-\kappa)w(\alpha|X-\kappa|)\right)-\mu.

The estimator
\widehat{\mu}
is obtained taking
\kappa=\widehat{\kappa}
and
\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}
. Formally, if it holds that
\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
with high probability for some set
I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
, then, with high probability,

|s(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa),\kappa)-\mu|\leq\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}|s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu|\leq\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}|b(\alpha,\kappa)|+\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}|s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu-b(\alpha,\kappa)|.

(14)

We may then use the concentration of
\widehat{\kappa}
in some interval
I_{\widehat{\kappa}}
along with its independence with respect to the sample
X_{1:n}
to derive a high-probability bound on the error
\lvert\widehat{\mu}-\mu\rvert
.
The upper bound in (
14
) can be thought of as a bias-variance decomposition.

Theorem

3.3
below shows that, assuming the existence of the appropriate set
I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
, we can control the variance term
\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}\left\lvert s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu-b(\alpha,\kappa)\right\rvert
in (
14
) via
\|F_{\kappa}\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}
and
m(\alpha)
(see (
10
)).
Lemma

3.4
then guarantees the existence of a useful choice of
I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
, which allows for a good bound on the bias and variance terms through
Lemma

3.5
.

Theorem 3.3

(Bias-variance decomposition)
.

Under
2
and
4
, let
I_{\widehat{\kappa}}
be a deterministic set such that
\widehat{\kappa}\in I_{\widehat{\kappa}}
with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}
and
I_{\alpha}(\kappa)
be a family of sets indexed by
\kappa\in I_{\widehat{\kappa}}
such that, for every
\kappa\in I_{\widehat{\kappa}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)\right]\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}.

(15)

Define
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}=\{x\mapsto\kappa+(x-\kappa)w(\alpha|x-\kappa|)-\mu:\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)\}
and
F_{\kappa}(x)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}}|f(x)|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\widehat{\mu}-\mu|\leq\sup_{\kappa\in I_{\widehat{\kappa}}}\left\{\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}|b(\alpha,\kappa)|+\left(2K+\sqrt{2}\right)\|F_{\kappa}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}}+\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}\frac{8m(\alpha)}{3n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right\}.

(16)

The proof of
Theorem

3.3
is achieved through a Bennett-type concentration inequality for suprema of empirical processes (
Lemma

A.3
) applied to
\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}\left\lvert s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu-b(\alpha,\kappa)\right\rvert
followed by an integration over the distribution of
\widehat{\kappa}
(
4

2

\|s(\alpha,\kappa)-\mu-b(\alpha,\kappa)\|_{\infty}=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\kappa)w(\alpha|X_{i}-\kappa|)-\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}-\kappa)w(\alpha|X_{i}-\kappa|)]\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2m(\alpha),

assuming that
\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}m(\alpha)<\infty
. The application of
Lemma

A.3
inequality relies on this last bound, but it is easy to see that the inequality (

\sup_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}(\kappa)}m(\alpha)
is infinite, although we will show that, by the construction of the appropriate set

in

3.4

A detailed proof is presented in
Appendix

B

The next lemma provides the definition of the set

satisfying (

Intuitively, we will construct sets

Lemma 3.4
.

Lemma 3.6

(Bounding the error introduced by contamination)
.

Let any
\kappa\in\mathbb{R},\eta\in(0,n),\varepsilon\in[0,1/2)
. Consider any sample
X_{1:n}
and
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)
. Then, it holds that

\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa)-\widehat{\mu}(\kappa)\rvert\leq\left(\varepsilon+\frac{2n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa)\lvert X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa\rvert)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)}{n}\right)\left(m(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa))+m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa))\right).

Proof.

We will ommit the dependence on
\kappa
throughout the proof to simplify notation. The proof follows directly by the definitions of
\widehat{\mu}
,
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
,
\widehat{\alpha}
, and
\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\kappa)w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)-(X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa)w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa\rvert)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon(m(\widehat{\alpha})+m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert\lvert w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)-w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\rvert.

In the last inequality, we used the definition
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert xw(\alpha x)\rvert=m(\alpha)
to bound the summands corresponding to the contaminated points. For the remaining term, notice that, for any
a,b\in[0,1]
, it holds that
\lvert a-b\rvert\leq a(1-b)+b(1-a)
. Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert\lvert w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)-w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\rvert
is upper-bounded by

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)(1-w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa\rvert))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)(1-w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)).

Bounding
\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert w(\widehat{\alpha}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\leq m(\widehat{\alpha})
and
\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert w(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\leq m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})
for all
i
and combining the resulting bounds, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Under
1
,
Lemma

B.1
yields, for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)
with probability
1
,

n-\eta-1-\left\lfloor n\varepsilon\right\rfloor\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w\left(\widehat{\alpha}\left(\kappa;X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\right)|X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa|\right),

and so

\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa)-\widehat{\mu}(\kappa)\rvert\leq\left(\varepsilon+\frac{2\eta+2+2n\varepsilon}{n}\right)\left(m(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa))+m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa))\right).

In addition, when
\underline{\alpha}(\kappa)<\inf_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa)\leq\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)
, it holds that the error
\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}\rvert
is bounded by
\left(6\varepsilon+\frac{4\eta+4}{n}\right)m(\underline{\alpha}(\kappa))
. Then, for
\eta
as in
Lemma

3.4
, we may use the bounds on
m(\underline{\alpha}(\kappa))
from
Lemma

3.5
to control the error introduced by contamination, concluding that, with high probability,

\sup_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}\rvert\leq\left((10+4\xi)\varepsilon+\frac{8}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)c_{w}(\nu_{p}+\lvert\kappa-\mu\rvert)\left(\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\frac{\xi}{7}\varepsilon\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}.

Theorem

3.1
then follows by combining the previous results. Details are presented in
Appendix

B
.

Properties of shrinkage estimators

In this section, we formally state and prove the desirable properties of the shrinkage estimators
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa})
presented in
Section

1.1.3
.

Proposition 4.1

(Affine-equivariance)
.

Assume
\widehat{\kappa}
depends on the sample
Y_{1:m}
and is affine-equivariant, that is,
\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t)=\lambda\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})+t
, for every
\lambda\neq 0,t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\lambda X_{1:n}+t=(\lambda X_{1}+t,\ldots,\lambda X_{n}+t)
. Denote by
\widehat{\mu}(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m});X_{1:n})
the shrinkage estimator from (
8
) applied to the base estimate
\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})
and the sample
X_{1:n}
. Then,
\widehat{\mu}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)=\lambda\widehat{\mu}(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda X_{i}+t-\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t))w(\widehat{\alpha}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)\lvert\lambda X_{i}+t-\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t)\rvert)

\displaystyle=\lambda\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})+t+\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m}))w(\widehat{\alpha}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)\lvert\lambda\rvert\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})\rvert)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m}))w(\widehat{\alpha}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)\lvert\lambda\rvert\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})\rvert)\right)+t.

It is then enough to check that
\lvert\lambda\rvert\widehat{\alpha}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t)=\widehat{\alpha}(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})
. Recall that
\widehat{\alpha}(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})
is such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\alpha>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\alpha\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})\rvert)\leq n-\eta\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\alpha>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}w\left(\frac{\alpha}{\lvert\lambda\rvert}\lvert\lambda X_{i}+t-\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t)\rvert\right)\leq n-\eta\right\}

\displaystyle=\lvert\lambda\rvert\inf\left\{\alpha>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}w\left(\alpha\lvert\lambda X_{i}+t-\widehat{\kappa}(\lambda Y_{1:m}+t)\rvert\right)\leq n-\eta\right\}=\lvert\lambda\rvert\widehat{\alpha}(\lambda(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m})+t).\qed

Proposition 4.2

(Distributional limit of shrinkage estimators)
.

Let
\eta
be fixed and take
\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}_{n};X_{1:n})
as in (
8
). Under
1
, further assume that
\nu_{p+\gamma}<\infty
for some
p>1,\gamma>0
, that
(\widehat{\kappa}_{n}-\mu)n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\to 0
in probability and that
\widehat{\kappa}_{n}
is independent from
X_{1:n}
. Then,
n^{1-\frac{1}{p}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\mu)\overset{d}{\to}D
for some distribution
D
if, and only if,
n^{1-\frac{1}{p}}(\overline{X}-\mu)\overset{d}{\to}D
.

Proof.

Consider
n^{1-\frac{1}{p}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\overline{X})
. We show that it converges in probability to zero:

\displaystyle\lvert n^{1-\frac{1}{p}}(\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\overline{X})\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\left(n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}_{n}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}_{n}\rvert)\right)(\widehat{\kappa}_{n}-\mu)+n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu)(w(\widehat{\alpha}_{n}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}_{n}\rvert)-1)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(\eta+1)n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\lvert\widehat{\kappa}_{n}-\mu\rvert+(\eta+1)n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert X_{i}-\mu\rvert,

where
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}=\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}_{n};X_{1:n})
and we used
Lemma

B.1
with
\varepsilon=0
to attain the last inequality (which holds almost surely).
To finish the proof, it is enough to show that
n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert X_{i}-\mu\rvert
converges in probability to zero. To that end, notice that

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}\left[n^{-\frac{1}{p}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert X_{i}-\mu\rvert>t\right]=1-\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left[\lvert X_{1}-\mu\rvert>tn^{\frac{1}{p}}\right]\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left[\lvert X_{1}-\mu\rvert^{p+\gamma}>t^{p+\gamma}n^{\frac{p+\gamma}{p}}\right]\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left(1-\frac{\nu_{p+\gamma}^{p+\gamma}}{t^{p+\gamma}n^{1+\frac{\gamma}{p}}}\right)^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}0.\qed

Proposition 4.3

(
\widehat{\mu}
preserves breakdown point)
.

Under
3
, let
\eta\geq n\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\kappa},Y_{1:m})
. Then
\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}),(X_{1:n},Y_{1:m}))\geq\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\kappa},Y_{1:m})
.

Proof.

We need to show that, when
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\kappa},Y_{1:m})
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon});X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m});X_{1:n})\rvert<\infty.

Denote
\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\kappa}(Y_{1:m})
and
\widehat{\kappa}=\widehat{\kappa}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{1:m})
. We use that
\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})\rvert\leq\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n})\rvert+\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})\rvert
. For the first part, we use
Lemma

3.6
and the fact that
w
is non-negative to attain the inequality

\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n})\rvert\leq\left(\varepsilon+2\right)\left(m(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}))+m(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}))\right)

We then use
3
to conclude that, for any
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle n-\eta

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n})\lvert X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa\rvert)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)-n\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq n(1-\varepsilon)-\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n})^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert^{p}.

which implies the bound

(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n}))^{-p}\leq(\eta-n\varepsilon)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(\eta-n\varepsilon)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert^{p}+n\lvert\widehat{\kappa}-\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\right).

It is then immediate that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}))^{-1}<\infty,

because
\widehat{\kappa}
has breakdown point greater than
\varepsilon
and
\eta\geq n\varepsilon^{\star}(\widehat{\kappa},Y_{1:m})>n\varepsilon
by assumption.
It follows that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}m(\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}))<\infty,

since
m(\alpha)=c_{w}\alpha^{-1}
. The bound for
m(\widehat{\alpha}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n}))
is obtained similarly.

We now focus on the term
\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})\rvert
. Its supremum is easily bounded:

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon};X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n})\rvert\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)\\
Y_{1:m}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(Y_{1:m},\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\lvert\widehat{\kappa}-\widehat{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rvert+2n\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert X_{i}\rvert<\infty.

The proof is complete by collecting the bounds. ∎

5
Examples of estimators

In this section, we show that suitable choices of
w:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
in (
8
) lead to known robust estimators or variants thereof. In particular, the theoretical results in
Section

3
can be directly applied to establish their concentration bounds.
2
and
3
are shown to hold in each example below.

Example 5

(The asymmetric trimmed mean)
.

The choice of
w(t)=\mathbf{1}_{t<1}
yields

\widehat{\mu}=\frac{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}{n}\widehat{\kappa}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-\lfloor\eta\rfloor}X_{(i)},

where
(\cdot)
is a permutation such that
|X_{(1)}-\widehat{\kappa}|\leq|X_{(2)}-\widehat{\kappa}|\leq\cdots\leq|X_{(n)}-\widehat{\kappa}|
. This estimator is similar to the classical trimmed mean, but instead of discarding the
k
largest and the
k
smallest points in the sample before averaging, it discards the ones further from the base estimate
\widehat{\kappa}
.
2
follows from
\sup_{t\geq 0}tw(t)=1<\infty
. Regardless of which
p
-th moment is available,
3
is satisfied. Thus, the estimator achieves optimal concentration in an appropriate regime of
\delta
depending on the concentration of
\widehat{\kappa}
(see
Theorem

3.1
). The classical trimmed mean
(
26
)
attains sub-Gaussian concentration rates and optimal bounds for the infinite variance scenario. In these cases it has been established that
\delta_{\textup{min}}
is of order
e^{-c^{\prime}n}
for some
c^{\prime}>0
, which is optimal. The estimator proposed in (
9
) in this case takes the form of

\widehat{\mu}_{W}=\frac{1}{n-\left\lfloor\eta\right\rfloor}\sum_{i=1}^{n-\left\lfloor\eta\right\rfloor}X_{(i)},

effectively excluding
\widehat{\kappa}
from its explicit formula and using it only to guide the reweighting scheme.

Example 6

(Winsorized mean)
.

If
w(t)=1\wedge t^{-1}
, then

\widehat{\mu}=\widehat{\kappa}+\frac{1}{n}\left(M(n_{+}-n_{-})+\sum_{|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|\leq M}X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\right)\text{ and }\widehat{\alpha}^{-1}=M,

(18)

where
n_{+}=\left|\left\{i:X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}>M\right\}\right|
,
n_{-}=\left|\left\{i:X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}<-M\right\}\right|
and
M
is the (unique) solution of

n-\eta=\left|\left\{i:|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|\leq M\right\}\right|+\sum_{|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|>M}\frac{M}{|X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}|}.

(19)

The resulting estimator is the same regardless of
p
. It corresponds to the Winsorized mean (
11
), but with the threshold
M
chosen via (
19
).
3
follows directly for all
p\in(1,2]
and, since
\sup_{t\geq 0}tw(t)=1<\infty
,
2
holds. This function attains the slowest possible decay in the sense that
\limsup_{t\to\infty}tw(t)\in(0,\infty)
and any decay of a slower order would violate
2
.

Example 7

(Lee and Valiant’s estimator
15
)
.

Taking
w(t)=(1-t^{p})_{+}
yields the estimator
\widehat{\mu}
such that
(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\mu})
solves

\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(\alpha^{p}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert^{p},1)=\eta,\\
\mu=\widehat{\kappa}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa})(1-\min(\alpha^{p}\lvert X_{i}-\widehat{\kappa}\rvert^{p},1)).\end{cases}

15
introduced this estimator for the case
p=2
and showed that if
\widehat{\kappa}
is sub-Gaussian, then
\widehat{\mu}
is sub-Gaussian with asymptotically optimal constants as
\big(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta},\delta\big)\to(0,0)
. In our framework, this estimator satisfies
3
with equality and
2
follows for every
p\in(1,2]
since

\sup_{t\geq 0}tw(t)=\sup_{t\geq 0}t(1-t^{p})_{+}=p(p+1)^{-(p+1)/p}<\infty.

Thus, our main result ensures that
\widehat{\mu}
attains sub-Gaussian concentration even under weaker assumptions on the base estimator
\widehat{\kappa}
: we require only that its error is
O(1)
with high probability (see
Example

2
).

Beyond well-studied estimators, our framework also facilitates the search for new ones with possibly different desirable properties. For example, we may look for shrinkage estimators that do not shrink large sample points as aggressively as the trimmed mean but more aggressively than the Winsorized mean. One way to do this is by tuning the
w
function, as it determines the shrinkage associated with each sample point.

Example 8

(Fast decay)
.

A possible choice of estimator that shrinks large sample points polynomially would entail choosing
w(t)=(1+t^{p})^{-1}
. It satisfies
2
and
3
for all
p\in(1,2]
since
\sup_{t\geq 0}t(1+t^{p})^{-1}\leq 1
and
(1+x)^{-1}\geq(1-x)_{+}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that this choice of
w
interpolates between
Example

6
and
Example

7
in the sense that
(1-x^{p})\leq\frac{1}{1+x^{p}}\leq 1\wedge x^{-1}\text{ for all }x\geq 0.
In fact, we observe empirically that this interpolation also takes place in terms of performance, as is detailed in the appendix.

7
Discussion

This paper formalizes the intuition that downweighting sample points far from a location estimate enhances robustness in mean estimation through improvement of concentration rates, in particular achieving sub-Gaussian behavior in certain
(n,\delta)
regimes. Unlike most works in robust statistics that focus on a single estimator, our results apply to a broad class of mean estimators. Robust mean estimation underpins various statistical tasks — including uniform mean estimation
(
21
)
, estimation under arbitrary norms
(
19
)
, regression
(
2
)
, and covariance estimation
(
27
)
— yet the computational tractability of existing methods remains a significant challenge. This limitation has motivated research into computationally efficient alternatives
(
6
;
8
;
10
)
. We believe that the flexibility in choosing
w
could lead to robust procedures with improved computational properties. Furthermore, our experiments reveal that different choices of
w
excel in distinct regimes of sample size and confidence level
1-\delta
, exhibiting varying sensitivities to the base estimator. Thus, a refinement of our results beyond order bounds could guide the selection of
w
for specific applications.

Useful Lemmata and Definitions

Lemma A.1

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean zero and bounded by
1
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\geq\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}}+\frac{1}{3n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Definition A.2

(VC-subgraph dimension)
.

Given a class of functions
\mathcal{F}
mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
, the VC-subgraph dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest integer
d
such that there exist
d
pairs in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}

(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{d},y_{d})
for which the set of subgraphs
\left\{\left\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)>y\right\}:f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
can realize all
2^{d}
possible labelings. That is, for every subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
, there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f(x_{i})>y_{i}\text{ if and only if }i\in S.

Lemma A.3

(Concentration of suprema of empirical processes under VC assumptions)
.

Let
P
be a distribution over a set
X
and
\mathcal{G}
be a class of integrable functions
g:\textbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|g-Pg\|_{\infty}\leq L
for some
L>0
. Let
G(x)=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|g(x)|
be the envelope of
\mathcal{G}
and assume
\mathcal{G}
is a VC-subgraph class with dimension
d
. Assume also that exists a countable
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{G}
such that every
g\in\mathcal{G}
is the
P
-pointwise a.s. and
L_{1}(P)
limits of functions
(h_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}
. For every
\delta\in(0,1)
, it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
, that

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\left(\widehat{P}_{n}-P\right)g\right|

\displaystyle\leq K\|G\|_{L_{2}(P)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\left(\sigma^{2}+2LK\|G\|_{L_{2}(P)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)}+\frac{L}{3n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq 2K\|G\|_{L_{2}(P)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}}+\frac{4L}{3n}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

where
K>0
is an absolute constant and
\sigma^{2}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}P(g-Pg)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is pointwise
P
-a.s. and
L_{1}(P)
limit of functions in
\mathcal{H}
we have

\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\left(\widehat{P}_{n}-P\right)g\right|=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\left(\widehat{P}_{n}-P\right)h\right|\text{ a.s. }

On the countable family
\mathcal{H}
, apply Bousquet’s version of Talagrand’s inequality
(
3
, Theorem 2.3)
and use the entropy bound on the expectation of the empirical process (
Lemma

A.5
).
∎

A.2

A.5

Definition A.4

(Covering number)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space. Given
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the metric
d
, denoted by
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},d,\varepsilon)
, is the smallest number of elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
such that for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists some
x_{i}
satisfying
d(x,x_{i})\leq\varepsilon
. In other words,
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},d,\varepsilon)
is the minimal number of points required to form an
\varepsilon
-net that covers
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the given metric.

Lemma A.5

(Integral entropy bound)
.

Let
X,X_{1},...,X_{n}
be i.i.d.,
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables with distribution
P
and
\mathcal{F}
a family of real functions define in
\mathcal{X}
. Assume
\mathcal{F}
is VC-subgraph with dimension
d
and an envelope function
F
(that is,
|f(x)|\leq F(x)
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
).

\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}f(X)\right\rvert\leq K\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(P)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}

where
K>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

A symmetrization argument followed by an application of Dudley’s theorem (see the proof of Theorem 8.3.23 in
30
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}f(X)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{2\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})}}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(P)}\int_{0}^{2}\sup_{Q}\sqrt{\ln\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)},\varepsilon\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)})}d\varepsilon,

(20)

where the supremum is taken over distributions
Q
. We proceed by using that
F
is an envelope of
\mathcal{F}
to bound (proof in
Lemma

A.6
)

\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)}\leq 2\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{\textup{sg}(f)}-\mathbf{1}_{\textup{sg}(g)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q^{\prime})}.

It then follows that

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)},\varepsilon\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q)})\leq\mathcal{N}(\{\mathbf{1}_{\textup{sg}(f)}:f\in\mathcal{F}\},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L_{2}(Q^{\prime})},\varepsilon/2)\leq\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right)^{C^{\prime}d},

where the last inequality follows by
(
30
, Theorem 8.3.18)
and
\textup{sg}(f):=\{(x,t)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)>t\}
. Plugging this inequality into (
20
) concludes the proof.
∎

.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions from

to
\mathbb{R}
and

be its envelope. Let

be any distribution over
\mathcal{X}
. Then, for any

Lemma B.1
.

Assume
X_{1:n}\sim P^{\otimes n}
with
P
atom-free. Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the following inequality holds almost surely for all
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)

Proof.

For the purpose of this proof, denote
\widehat{\alpha}=\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})
Define

S_{X^{\varepsilon}_{1:n}}(\alpha):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\alpha|X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa|).

The upper bound follows directly by the definition of
\widehat{\alpha}
and right-continuity of
w
:

S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}\left(\widehat{\alpha}\right)=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow\widehat{\alpha}}S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}(\alpha)\leq n-\eta

We proceed by proving the lower bound.
Assume that
S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}\left(\widehat{\alpha}\right)<n-\eta-1-\left\lfloor n\varepsilon\right\rfloor
, with positive probability. By definition of
\widehat{\alpha}
,
\lim_{\alpha\uparrow\widehat{\alpha}}S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}(\alpha)\geq n-\eta.
Thus,

\lim_{\alpha\uparrow\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)}S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}(\alpha)-S_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}}(\widehat{\alpha})>1+\left\lfloor n\varepsilon\right\rfloor

and
\widehat{\alpha}
is a discontinuity of
\alpha\mapsto w(\alpha\left\lvert X_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\kappa\right\rvert)
for at least
2+\left\lfloor n\varepsilon\right\rfloor
different indices. Since
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\neq X_{1:n}
holds for at most
\left\lfloor n\varepsilon\right\rfloor
indices, then there are at least two different indices

such that
\widehat{\alpha}
is a discontinuity of
\alpha\mapsto w(\alpha\lvert X_{l}-\kappa\rvert)
for
l\in\{i,j\}
.

\mathcal{D}
be the set of discontinuities of

, which we know to be countable since
w
is non-increasing. We will now show that, because
P
has no point mass, the event

D_{k,l}:=\left\{(\widehat{\alpha}\left\lvert X_{k}-\kappa\right\rvert,\widehat{\alpha}\left\lvert X_{l}-\kappa\right\rvert)\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right\}

has probability zero, where
k\neq l
. Denote
\mathcal{D}/\mathcal{D}:=\left\{\frac{t_{1}}{t_{2}}:(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right\}
, which is well defined since

(

is defined on

, which implies that
\widehat{\alpha}
is a discontinuity of
\alpha\mapsto w(\alpha\left\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\right\rvert)
for at most one index almost surely.
∎

\displaystyle\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\nu_{q}^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(\sup_{\kappa\in I_{\widehat{\kappa}}}m(\underline{\alpha}(\kappa))^{1-\frac{q}{2}}+R^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\nu_{q}^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(c_{w}^{1-\frac{q}{2}}(\nu_{q}+R)^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\left(\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\frac{\xi}{7}\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}}+R^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\underline{c}}}((c_{w}\vee 1)+1)\left(\nu_{q}+\nu_{q}^{\frac{q}{2}}R^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}},

where we used
\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\frac{\xi}{7}\varepsilon\leq 1
and that
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\leq 0
in the last inequality.
We conclude that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}
,
\lvert\widehat{\mu}-\mu\rvert
is at most

C_{w,\xi}^{\prime}\left\{\inf_{1<q\leq 2\wedge p}\left(\nu_{q}+\nu_{q}^{\frac{q}{2}}R^{1-\frac{q}{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}+\inf_{1<q\leq p}\left(\nu_{q}+R\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\varepsilon\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\right\}

for some
C^{\prime}_{w,\xi}>0
depending only on
w
and
\xi
.

The conclusion (
11
) is then proved by using the bound on the error

\sup_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}\rvert\leq\inf_{1<q\leq p}\left(6\varepsilon+\frac{8}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}\right)c_{w}(\nu_{q}+R)\left(\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\frac{\xi}{7}\varepsilon\right)^{-\frac{1}{q}},

given by
Lemma

3.6
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.2
.

The proof follows by a simple application of Bernstein’s inequality. Let
F^{-}(x)=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}<x]\text{ and }Q^{-}_{t}=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F^{-}(x)\leq t\}
. It holds that
F^{-}(Q^{-}_{t})=t
for
t\in(0,1)
.

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\kappa}\geq Q_{t}^{-}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}\geq Q_{t}^{-}}\geq 1-\gamma\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}\geq Q_{t}^{-}}-(1-t)\geq-\gamma+t\right]

Take
t
to be
\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\kappa}\geq Q_{t}^{-}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}\geq Q_{t}^{-}}-(1-t)\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\right]\leq\frac{\delta}{2},

as long as
\delta\geq 2e^{-\frac{7}{10}n}
.
By Markov’s inequality, we can bound
Q_{t}^{-}
as follows:

\displaystyle 1-t=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\geq Q_{t}^{-}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu_{p}^{p}}{\lvert Q_{t}^{-}-\mu\rvert^{p}},\text{ if $Q_{t}^{-}\geq\mu$ }

\displaystyle t=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}<Q_{t}^{-}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu_{p}^{p}}{\lvert Q_{t}^{-}-\mu\rvert^{p}},\text{ if $Q_{t}^{-}<\mu$ }

implying that
Q_{t}^{-}\leq\mu+\nu_{p}\left[\left(\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\wedge\left(1-\gamma-\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\right]^{-1/p}.
In particular, if
\delta
is greater than
2\exp\left\{-\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}n}{4}\right\}
, then
\widehat{\kappa}-\mu\leq\nu_{p}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma\wedge(1-\gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
.
We provide a lower-bound similarly: let
F(x)=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\leq x],Q_{t}=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x)\geq t\}
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\kappa}\leq Q_{t}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq Q_{t}}\geq\frac{n-1}{n}\gamma\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{X_{i}\leq Q_{t}}-t\geq\frac{n-1}{n}\gamma-t\right].

Taking
t=\gamma-2\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}
leads to
\mathbb{P}[\widehat{\kappa}\leq Q_{t}]\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
as before. Using Markov’s inequality again yields the bound
Q_{t}\geq\mu-\nu_{p}\left[\left(\gamma-2\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\wedge\left(1-\gamma+2\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\right]^{-1/p}
. If
\delta\geq\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma^{2}n}{16}\right\}
,
\widehat{\kappa}-\mu\geq\nu_{p}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma\wedge(1-\gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
holds with high probability. Combining the lower and upper bounds, when
\delta\geq 2e^{-\frac{((1-\gamma)\wedge\gamma)^{2}n}{16}}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\lvert\widehat{\kappa}-\mu\rvert\leq\nu_{p}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma\wedge(1-\gamma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{\gamma}\nu_{p}\qed

Finally, we show that the normalized shrinkage estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{W}
defined in (
9
) behaves as the non-normalized one:

Proposition B.3
.

Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\kappa;X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}_{W}(\kappa;X_{1:n})\rvert\leq m(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n}))\frac{n-S}{n},
where
S
is defined by
S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa)\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)
.

Proof.

By the definitions of
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{W}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{W}(\kappa;X_{1:n})-\widehat{\mu}(\kappa;X_{1:n})\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n})\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)-\kappa-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\kappa)w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n})\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{S}-\frac{1}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert w(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n})\lvert X_{i}-\kappa\rvert)\leq m(\widehat{\alpha}(\kappa;X_{1:n}))\frac{n-S}{n}.\qed

It immediately follows that the concentration guarantee provided for
\widehat{\mu}^{\varepsilon}
also holds for
\widehat{\mu}_{W}^{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\mu}_{W}(\widehat{\kappa};X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon})
under the adversarial contamination scenario (with different constants), since
S\geq n-\eta-1
almost surely (
Lemma

B.1
),
\inf_{X_{1:n}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{A}(X_{1:n},\varepsilon)}\widehat{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(\kappa)\geq\underline{\alpha}(\kappa)
with high probability (
Lemma

3.4
), and
m(\underline{\alpha}(\kappa))\leq c_{w}(\nu_{p}+\lvert\kappa-\mu\rvert)\left(\frac{\underline{c}}{n}\ln\frac{4}{\delta}+\frac{\xi}{7}\varepsilon\right)^{-\frac{1}{p}}
(
Lemma

3.5
).
