EZH1 Inhibition attenuates apoptosis and promotes regeneration for liver repair in acute liver failure
Authors: Ying You, Feng Gu, Meihua Mei, Ningxin Tan, Xiao Cong, Peidong Chi, Yili Chen, Junqi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute liver failure (ALF) is a life-threatening clinical syndrome characterized by massive hepatocyte death and insufficient regenerative response. However, the epigenetic mechanisms that disturb the balance between cell death and regeneration remain largely unclear.Materials and methodsWe integrated transcriptomic data from public ALF patient cohorts, a CCl₄-induced murine ALF model, and an APAP-induced murine ALF model. Bioinformatic analyses included single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). The functional role of EZH1 was validated using EZH1 knockout mice, and liver injury was evaluated by serum biochemistry, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry.ResultsIntegrated transcriptomic analysis revealed concurrent activation of apoptotic and proliferative pathways in ALF (P < 0.001). WGCNA identified EZH1 as a key hub gene strongly correlated with both apoptosis and proliferation (cor > 0.5, P < 0.001). Patients with low EZH1 expression exhibited significantly reduced apoptotic signaling (P < 0.05) and enhanced regenerative signatures (P < 0.001). In vivo, Ezh1 knockout markedly alleviated liver injury, as indicated by reduced serum ALT and AST levels (P < 0.01), decreased levels of GSH (P < 0.05) and MDA (P < 0.01), and fewer TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells (P < 0.01), accompanied by increased Ki67- (P < 0.01) and PCNA-positive cells (P < 0.05).ConclusionsOur findings identify EZH1 as a pivotal epigenetic regulator promoting hepatocyte apoptosis in ALF. Targeted inhibition of EZH1 may offer a promising therapeutic approach to restore the balance between hepatocyte death and regeneration, thereby facilitating liver repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04463-0.

Introduction

Acute liver failuIntroductionre (ALF) is a severe clinical syndrome characterized by rapid loss of hepatic function, coagulopathy, and encephalopathy, resulting in extremely high mortality [1, 2]. It can be triggered by viral infection, toxins, or drugs such as carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄), which is widely used as an experimental model of ALF [3, 4]. The pathological process involves extensive hepatocyte death and metabolic dysfunction, ultimately leading to liver failure [5].

Epigenetic regulation has emerged as a key determinant of liver injury and regeneration [6]. The polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) mediates histone H3K27 methylation through its catalytic subunits EZH1 and EZH2, thereby controlling transcriptional silencing [7]. Although EZH2 has been extensively characterized as an oncogenic and pro-inflammatory factor in liver diseases, its homolog EZH1 has attracted growing attention for its distinct and often complementary roles. Unlike EZH2, EZH1 possesses unique chromatin-binding dynamics and sustains gene repression under low-proliferative or stress conditions, implying a specialized function in tissue repair and regeneration. Clinically, elevated EZH1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma, highlighting its broader pathophysiological significance beyond tumor biology [8].

Our previous studies have revealed that EZH1 plays potential roles in immune modulation and ferroptosis regulation [9, 10]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which EZH1 governs hepatocyte fate and regeneration during ALF remain poorly understood.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the mechanistic role of EZH1 in hepatocyte survival and regeneration during ALF through comprehensive integrative transcriptomic and experimental analyses.

Material

Animal experimental design

Ezh1 knockout (Ezh1⁻/⁻) mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (strain: C57BL/6 N, catalog number KOCMP-14055-Ezh1-B6N). Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 N mice were purchased from the Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. All mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the Laboratory Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, with ad libitum access to food and water, controlled temperature and humidity, and a standard 12-hour light/dark cycle.

Mice were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence: (1) WT control, (2) WT + CCl₄, (3) Ezh1⁻/⁻ control and (4) Ezh1⁻/⁻ + CCl₄. The sample size for each group was set at n = 6 based on power analysis, confirming that this sample size provides 80% power to detect a significant effect at α = 0.05. All histological evaluations and subsequent data analyses were carried out by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

To induce ALF, mice in the CCl₄ groups received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of CCl₄ (1 mL/kg, dissolved in corn oil). This specific dosage was selected based on previous studies [11] that have demonstrated it reliably produces a robust but sub-lethal hepatocellular injury, allowing for the clear observation of both pathological damage and subsequent compensatory regenerative responses over a 24-hour period. The control groups received an equal volume of corn oil vehicle via i.p. injection. To minimize bias, the investigators responsible for outcome assessments (e.g., histopathological scoring, serum biochemical analysis) were blinded to the group allocation throughout the experiment.24 h post-injection, mice were euthanized for sample collection. Blood was collected for serum separation. The left lobe of the liver was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis. The remaining liver tissues and serum were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for further use. All animal experiments were approved by the Clinical Research and Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Cell culture and treatment

AML12 cells (normal mouse hepatocytes) were cultured in DMEM medium (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100IU/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin) and placed in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37℃. AML12 cells were divided into five groups: control group (PBS), APAP group (10mM APAP) and rescue group (10mM APAP + 10 µM DS3201 (EZH1 inhibitor)). Each group was incubated with corresponding drugs for 24 h.

Histopathology and lesion score

Specimens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The samples were dehydrated by alcohol gradient and cleared in xylene, and finally embedded in paraffin. The wax block was sliced continuously (thickness 4 μm). They were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) dye, then observed and photographed under a microscope. To evaluate liver injury [12], inflammation is scored by lymphocyte infiltration in liver lobe: rare foci scored 1 point; up to 5 foci scored 2 points; 3 points for >5 foci. Balloon degeneration of hepatocytes was marked by swelling of hepatocytes, rounding of cell membrane, thinning of cytoplasm, and even clumping of cytoplasm contents. Rare cells became ballooned and scored 1 point; a small group of cells became ballooned and scored 2 points; A large number of cells became ballooned and scored 3 points, especially when they were seen beyond centrilobular zones. The score of central lobular necrosis is 1 point for only zone 3 of hepatocyte necrosis; except for hepatocyte necrosis in zone 3, occasional bridging necrosis was seen scored 2 points; 3 points for obvious bridging and confluent necrosis. The parenchymal necrosis showed spotty necrosis or apoptosis in area 2 (periportal vein area) and area 1 (gland area). 1 point for ≤ 1 foci; 2 points for 5–10 foci; 3 points for ≥ 10 foci.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval via heat-induced epitope retrieval. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies, including TUNEL (using the DAB (SA-HRP) TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit, Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G4305), Ki67 (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, GB111499-100,1:300), and PCNA (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, GB11010,1:300). After washing, the sections were incubated with an HRP-labeled secondary antibody. Finally, the slides were mounted with neutral gum. The positive expression of antigens was quantified using ImageJ.

Determination of biochemical indicators

The mouse serum was thawed, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was taken and placed in a refrigerator at 4℃. ALT/AST detection solution was added to 96-well plate and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min. Serum samples were added to a 96-well plate, mixed evenly and incubated at 37℃ for 60s. The absorbance changes of each tube were detected at 340 nm for 1–3 min, and the ALT and AST values were calculated.

Detection of GSH and MDA

Liver tissue was ground in a high-speed low-temperature freeze grinder to obtain supernatant, and the contents of GSH (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G4302) and MDA (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, G4305) in liver tissue were detected by kit.

Western blot

The liver tissue of mice was lysed by protein lysate, and quantified by Western blot (WB). After electrophoresis, electrotransformation, sealing, primary antibody incubation, PBST washing, and ECL chemiluminescence development; Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imager.RT The antibodies used were as follows:β-actin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, GB11001,1:1000), Caspase3 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 9664,1:1000), Bcl2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 2870,1:1000), Bax (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 2772,1:1000), EZH1(Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab263961,1:1000), Histone H3(Abcam, Cambridge, UK, EPR17785,1:500).

Cell death status was detected by Calcein/PI double staining

AML12 cells at the logarithmic growth phase were prepared into a single-cell suspension at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵/mL and seeded into 6-well plates. Cells were cultured under standard conditions in a cell incubator for 24 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of Calcein/PI working solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well. Collection and processing of RNA-seq data

Three publicly available gene expression datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): the human acute liver failure dataset (GSE38941), the mouse CCl₄-induced acute liver failure dataset (GSE167033), and the mouse APAP-induced acute liver failure dataset (GSE167032). Prior to further analysis, all datasets were processed using robust quantile normalization followed by log₂(n + 1) transformation.

Differential gene expression analysis using Limma

Differential expression analysis of the human HIRI dataset (GSE38941) was performed using the Limma package (version 3.40.6) in R. Genes with a p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change (|log₂FC|) > 1 were considered differentially expressed (DEGs).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software (version 3.0) to identify significantly enriched biological pathways. The analysis was based on the KEGG pathway gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt). Gene expression profiles and phenotype labels were used as input.The minimum and maximum gene set sizes were set to 5 and 5000, respectively, and 1,000 permutations were conducted to assess statistical significance. Enrichment was evaluated using the Normalized Enrichment Score (NES), nominal p-value, and false discovery rate (FDR).

Integrated assessment of cell death pathways and immune response using SsGSEA

Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was employed to quantify the activity of predefined biological pathways in individual samples. The analysis was performed using the GSVA R package (version 4.2.1).In this study, gene sets corresponding to cell death-related pathways were constructed based on reference [14], while gene sets for 24 categories of immune cell-related pathways were developed according to reference [15].

The ssGSEA algorithm transforms the gene expression matrix into a pathway enrichment score matrix, where each score represents the relative abundance of a pathway’s gene set in a single sample. The enrichment scores were calculated using a non-parametric Kolmogorov-Smirnov-like random walk statistic. To assess the significance of differences in pathway activity between ALF and normal control groups, a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. A false discovery rate (p value) of < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Weighted gene Co-Expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA R package (version 4.2.1) to identify co-expressed gene modules and explore their associations with clinical traits. The analysis included 12,452 genes from a merged dataset comprising ALF and normal samples (n = 24). To enhance network robustness, genes with low expression variance (standard deviation ≤ 0.25) were filtered out, retaining 9,860 genes for downstream analysis. A scale-free topology network was constructed using a soft-thresholding power of β = 26, which was the lowest value achieving a scale-free topology fit index (R²) > 0.85. Based on this threshold, a weighted adjacency matrix was generated and transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Gene modules were identified using average linkage hierarchical clustering with a minimum module size of 30 genes. A dynamic tree-cutting algorithm (deepSplit = 2, mergeCutHeight = 0.25) was applied, yielding 17 distinct co-expression modules. Genes not assigned to any module were grouped into the “grey” module and excluded from further analysis. Module eigengenes were calculated as the first principal component of each module. Correlations between module eigengenes and sample traits (apoptosis and proliferation scores) were evaluated using Pearson correlation, with statistical significance assessed based on p-values. For key modules, the relationship between Gene Significance (GS, absolute correlation between a gene and a trait) and Module Membership (MM, correlation between a gene and its module eigengene) was analyzed. Modules with a significant module–trait relationship (p < 0.01) and strong GS–MM correlation were considered biologically relevant. Within these modules, genes with MM > 0.8 and GS for apoptosis > 0.2 were defined as core (hub) genes. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of core genes was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape (version 3.10.1).

Statistical analysis and data visualization

The sample sizes were determined based on established standards in the field and our laboratory’s prior experience with comparable models, which have consistently demonstrated sufficient power to detect biologically meaningful differences.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.2.1). Data visualization was performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R packages including ggplot2. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For two-group comparisons with normally distributed data and equal variances, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. If normality or homoscedasticity assumptions were violated, the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was used. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was employed when data met normality and homoscedasticity criteria, followed by Dunnett’s test for comparisons against a single control group or Tukey’s test for all pairwise comparisons. The Bonferroni method was applied to correct for multiple testing where appropriate. If ANOVA assumptions were not satisfied, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bioinformatics analyses, including differential expression (limma), functional enrichment (clusterProfiler, org.Hs.eg.db), gene set retrieval (msigdbr), and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), were performed using custom R scripts.

Result

ALF induces increased apoptosis and compensatory cell proliferation

To investigate the primary cell death mechanisms in ALF, we analyzed three publicly available GEO datasets: the human ALF dataset (GSE38941) and two mouse ALF datasets (GSE167033 and GSE167032). Enrichment analysis of cell death pathways across these datasets consistently revealed a significant upregulation of apoptosis in the ALF group compared to the control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A–C). Given the close relationship between apoptosis and cell proliferation, as well as the potential of ALF to trigger compensatory liver regeneration [16], we performed an enrichment analysis of cell proliferation pathways across these datasets. The results showed a significantly higher enrichment score of cell proliferation pathways in the ALF group compared to controls, indicating activation of compensatory proliferation following liver injury (P < 0.001, Fig. 1D).

We next characterized the immune landscape in ALF using two complementary approaches. First, the abundance of 24 immune cell types was quantified using ssGSEA, revealing a significant increase in multiple subpopulations in ALF, including neutrophils (P < 0.01), cytotoxic cells (P < 0.0001), and T cells (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E). EZH1 as a potential regulator of apoptosis and proliferation in ALF

To investigate the potential role of EZH1 in hepatocyte survival and regeneration during ALF, we analyzed both the human (GSE38941) and mouse (GSE167033) RNA-seq datasets. In the human ALF dataset, EZH1 expression was significantly upregulated compared to healthy controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A) and exhibited strong positive correlations with apoptosis and proliferation scores (cor > 0.5, P < 0.001, Fig. 4B). In the mouse ALF model, EZH1 expression showed a consistent upward trend, although the change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4C). Further analysis revealed that EZH1 expression progressively increased with worsening histopathological severity in human ALF samples (P < 0.001, Fig. 4E). By contrast, although EZH2 expression was elevated relative to healthy controls, no significant difference was observed between patients with submassive and massive hepatic necrosis (P < 0.001, Figure S1C). These results suggest that EZH1, rather than EZH2, may play a more critical role in regulating hepatocyte survival and regenerative responses during advanced liver injury.

Consistent with these findings, EZH1 expression positively correlated with serum ALT (cor > 0.5, P < 0.001) and AST (cor = 0.41, P = 0.042) levels in ALF patients (Fig. 4F). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further demonstrated that EZH1 exhibited excellent diagnostic performance in distinguishing ALF patients from healthy controls, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.0 and an optimal cut-off value of 7.84 (Fig. 4G).

Collectively, these observations indicate that EZH1 may serve as a pivotal regulator of apoptosis and compensatory proliferation in ALF and could represent a potential therapeutic target.

Differential expression analysis using the Limma algorithm identified 791 up-regulated and 314 down-regulated genes in EZH1High versus EZH1Low samples (adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| >1, Fig. 5D). Targeted Inhibition of EZH1 effectively attenuated ALF and significantly reduced inflammation and oxidative stress

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of EZH1 in ALF, we generated EZH1 knockout (Ezh1⁻/⁻) mice and confirmed hepatic deletion of EZH1 by qPCR and Western blot (P < 0.001, Figure S1A–B). ALF was induced via intraperitoneal injection of CCl₄ at 1 mL/kg.

Histopathological analysis revealed that the WT CCl₄ group suffered severe liver injury, characterized by central venous congestion, hepatocyte swelling, partial nuclear dissolution, and significant inflammatory cell infiltration, resulting in high histological necrosis scores. To examine the relationship between EZH1 and EZH2, hepatic expression was quantified by qPCR after CCl₄ administration. Both EZH1 (P < 0.001) and EZH2 (P < 0.001) were significantly upregulated in WT livers, indicating coordinated PRC2 activation under acute injury. However, EZH2 expression remained unchanged in Ezh1⁻/⁻ mice after CCl₄ exposure (P < 0.001, Figure S1D–E), highlighting a potential regulatory interplay between EZH1 and EZH2 during hepatic injury.

Compared with the WT CCl₄ group, the Ezh1⁻/⁻ CCl₄ group exhibited a significant increase in Ki67-positive (P < 0.01) and PCNA-positive cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7D–E). While Pcna levels were reduced in the WT CCl₄ group (P < 0.05), Ezh1⁻/⁻ CCl₄ mice showed a marked restoration of its expression (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7F).

In vitro, APAP stimulation in AML12 cells significantly upregulated the expression of pro-apoptotic genes (Parp, P < 0.05; Caspase8, P < 0.05; Caspase7, P < 0.001; Caspase3, P < 0.001; Caspase9, P < 0.001; and Bax, P < 0.001). In contrast, treatment with DS3201 markedly attenuated the APAP-induced upregulation of these pro-apoptotic genes (Caspase3, P < 0.01; Caspase9, P < 0.05; Bax, P < 0.01; Caspase7, P < 0.01; and Parp, P < 0.05), while significantly enhancing the expression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl2 (P < 0.01). Moreover, T2AA, a PCNA inhibitor, suppressed cell proliferation, reversed the DS3201-mediated inhibition of pro-apoptotic gene expression, and reduced the expression of anti-apoptotic genes (P < 0.05, Fig. 7H).

Consistent with these findings, APAP treatment also led to the downregulation of Pcna in AML12 cells, resulting in diminished cell proliferation. DS3201 counteracted this inhibitory effect by restoring Pcna expression (P < 0.05), thereby promoting cell proliferation. However, T2AA abrogated the protective effect of DS3201 by preventing the restoration of Pcna expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 7G).

In conclusion, These results indicate that EZH1 exerts a protective effect in ALF by inhibiting apoptosis and promoting hepatocyte proliferation, potentially via PCNA regulation.

Discussion

ALF is a devastating clinical syndrome characterized by massive hepatocyte loss and a futile compensatory regenerative response [1, 2, 17, 18]. While the activation of proliferative pathways is a recognized feature, the relentless pace of cell death, particularly apoptosis, ultimately dictates disease progression [19, 20]. Our multi-cohort transcriptomic analysis not only reaffirms apoptosis as the predominant driver of hepatocyte loss in ALF but, more importantly, positions the epigenetic regulator EZH1 as a novel and pivotal orchestrator of this pathological process.

A central finding of our study is the profound imbalance between cell death and regeneration. Despite concurrent elevations in proliferation markers across datasets, the compensatory response was insufficient to counteract the extensive apoptosis. This suggests that therapeutic strategies aiming solely to boost proliferation may be inadequate; instead, concurrently suppressing key drivers of hepatocyte death holds greater promise.

To further dissect the molecular landscape of ALF, we performed KEGG pathway analysis, revealing enrichment of PPAR signaling and fatty acid degradation pathways in ALF liver tissue. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that PPAR activation can mitigate apoptosis through modulation of lipid metabolism [21–23]. Notably, differential expression analysis between EZH1High and EZH1Low groups revealed similar enrichment, indicating a potential link between disrupted lipid homeostasis and apoptotic hepatocyte death. While causality cannot be definitively established in this study, these results suggest that metabolic dysregulation may contribute to hepatocyte vulnerability in ALF.

Beyond metabolic alterations, the immune microenvironment constitutes another critical dimension of ALF pathology. Our analysis confirmed increased immune infiltration in ALF livers, including CD45⁺ lymphocytes, dendritic cells, T cells, neutrophils, and macrophages [17, 18, 24]. Most immune cell types positively correlated with both apoptosis and proliferation levels, implying that hepatocyte death not only directly drives liver injury but may also exacerbate inflammation by promoting immune activation. Therefore, targeting apoptotic pathways may simultaneously attenuate liver damage and modulate the immune microenvironment.

Within this multifaceted pathological network, EZH1 emerges as a novel and central player. As a core component of the PRC2, EZH1 regulates gene expression through trimethylation of H3K27me3, thereby modulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [25, 26]. While the epigenetic regulator EZH2 has been extensively implicated in liver injury, with studies demonstrating its targeting alleviates acute liver failure by mitigating ferroptosis and improves outcomes in infection-induced liver injury [27–33], the role of its paralog, EZH1, has remained obscure. Our study reveals a critical, non-redundant role for EZH1 in ALF pathogenesis. During cellular development, EZH2 expression is mainly restricted to proliferating cells and declines during later stages, whereas EZH1 expression progressively increases and persists in differentiated cells [26]. We observed that while EZH2 levels plateaued during moderate to severe injury, EZH1 expression escalated with worsening pathology. WGCNA analysis identified EZH1 as a key mediator of chromatin compaction in ALF, and its overexpression correlated with a protective phenotype characterized by reduced apoptosis and enhanced hepatocyte proliferation. This distinctive expression pattern, together with EZH1’s inherently stronger chromatin-compacting ability, suggests that late-stage liver injury may involve a shift in epigenetic dominance from EZH2 to EZH1.

Enrichment analyses further linked EZH1 activity to key ALF-response pathways, including drug metabolism and DNA damage repair [18], highlighting its potential as an independent and essential therapeutic target. Interestingly, EZH1 knockout did not trigger compensatory upregulation of EZH2; instead, EZH2, which is normally upregulated under liver failure conditions, returned to near-normal levels following EZH1 deletion, suggesting a potential mutual regulatory mechanism in this stress environment. The molecular basis of this interaction and its precise roles during hepatitis or liver regeneration remain unexplored and warrant further investigation.

In the field of liver research, a previous study has reached a conclusion that seemingly contradicts ours. The study pointed out that EZH1 and EZH2 play redundant roles in maintaining liver homeostasis and regeneration. Double knockout of EZH1 and EZH2 resulted in a complete loss of the H3K27me3 mark, which impaired hepatocyte proliferation and survival, leading to periportal fibrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and defective regeneration following chronic chemical injury or partial hepatectomy [34]. First, the disease contexts differ fundamentally: whereas Bae et al. employed chronic CCl₄ exposure or partial hepatectomy models—processes involving repeated damage cycles, progenitor activation, and fibrotic remodeling [35–38]—our study examines acute liver failure, a setting dominated by rapid hepatocyte death, oxidative stress, and sterile inflammation [39, 40]. Second, the genetic interventions are distinct. Complete dual knockout of EZH1/EZH2 ablates H3K27me3 and disrupts PRC2 activity, impairing hepatocyte maturation and homeostasis [34, 41]. In contrast, our selective inhibition of EZH1 preserves EZH2 function and baseline H3K27me3, enabling targeted derepression of pro-regenerative genes without compromising core PRC2 activity. Thus, while total PRC2 loss is detrimental to liver homeostasis, selective EZH1 inhibition in acute injury exerts a protective and regenerative effect—consistent with other reports of context-dependent benefits of partial EZH1/2 inhibition [10, 27–33]. Our results thereby refine the understanding of EZH1 in liver repair, highlighting its role not as redundant to EZH2, but as a tractable target in acute hepatic failure.

Our current study directly complements and extends our previous finding that EZH1 protects against sepsis-associated liver injury by activating NRF2 to suppress ferroptosis [10]. Here, we demonstrate in a model of drug-induced acute liver failure that EZH1 also confers protection, but primarily through the inhibition of apoptotic pathways. This critical distinction reveals that EZH1 modulates distinct cell death programs in a context-dependent manner, thereby significantly expanding the mechanistic understanding of its hepatoprotective role. The consistent protective effect across etiologically distinct models strongly reinforces EZH1’s potential as a broad-spectrum therapeutic target and provides a compelling rationale for developing selective EZH1 inhibitors.

We acknowledge several limitations. The scarcity of human ALF samples limits the depth of clinical correlation in available datasets. As ALF etiology is diverse, the universality of EZH1’s regulatory role across different causes requires further validation. Finally, our use of a systemic EZH1 knockout model, while informative, cannot fully exclude contributory effects from extra-hepatic tissues on the observed phenotype.

In conclusion, our study is the first to position EZH1 as a critical regulator within the molecular landscape of ALF, orchestrating apoptosis, proliferation, immune infiltration and metabolic pathways. These novel findings pave the way for the development of EZH1-targeted therapies that could rebalance hepatocyte survival and regeneration while modulating immune responses (Fig. 8). As such, EZH1 is emerging as a promising therapeutic target to alleviate liver injury and improve regenerative outcomes in ALF patients.
