TempRetinex: Retinex-based Unsupervised Enhancement for Low-light Video Under Diverse Lighting Conditions
Yini Li, Nantheera Anantrasirichai

Abstract
Videos inherently contain rich temporal information that provides complementary cues for low-light enhancement beyond what can be achieved with single images. We propose TempRetinex, a novel unsupervised Retinex-based framework that effectively exploits inter-frame correlations for video enhancement. To address the poor generalization of existing unsupervised methods under varying illumination, we introduce adaptive brightness adjustment (ABA) preprocessing that explicitly aligns lighting distributions across exposures. This significantly improves model robustness to diverse lighting scenarios and eases training optimization, leading to better denoising performance. For enhanced temporal coherence, we propose a multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss to enforce multiscale similarity between consecutive frames, and an occlusion-aware masking technique to handle complex motions. We further incorporate a reverse inference strategy to refine unconverged frames and a self-ensemble (SE) mechanism to boost the denoising across diverse textures. Experiments demonstrate that TempRetinex achieves state-of-the-art performance in both perceptual quality and temporal consistency, achieving up to a 29.7% PSNR gain over prior methods. Code is available at https://github.com/XXXX/TempRetinex .



1
Introduction

Low-light enhancement refers to the improvement of visual quality in images or videos captured under poor illumination conditions. A common strategy is to increase camera ISO to capture more photons, but high ISO settings inevitably introduce amplified sensor noise. Thus, low-light enhancement must not only address color correction but also the effective suppression of sensor-induced noise, while preserving fine details. The process is important not only for better visualization but also to support various computer vision tasks
[
1
]
.

Recent deep learning-based methods have achieved remarkable performance in low-light enhancement
[
12
,
27
]
, but remain limited in several aspects
[
22
]
. A key challenge is the scarcity of high-quality paired video datasets, which constrains supervised approaches that require extensive ground truth. Many methods also fail to fully exploit the rich temporal information contained in the video, merely extending single-image processing techniques to individual frames, which tends to introduce undesirable flickering artifacts. In practice, the dynamic adjustment of camera parameters (e.g. ISO, aperture, and exposure) under varying lighting conditions introduces notable heterogeneity in video characteristics. Training models with these data is challenging, as they often struggle to converge to an optimal solution. Moreover, limited training data reduces generalizability, leading to suboptimal performance in real-world scenarios.

Video-specific low-light enhancement methods remain relatively limited. Existing unsupervised approaches typically follow two strategies: processing frames independently
[
26
]
or simultaneously inputting adjacent frames into the network
[
13
]
. To utilize sufficient temporal information, Zero-TIG
[
10
]
made initial progress by introducing temporal feedback. However, this method lacked explicit temporal constraints through loss functions and did not incorporate mechanisms to handle motion occlusions. To further improve performance, this paper presents
TempRetinex
, an enhanced unsupervised learning framework that builds upon the Retinex theory. In addition, we introduce a multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss function and incorporate a self-ensemble mechanism into TempRetinex. The key innovations include:

•

We propose an Adaptive Brightness Adjustment (ABA) preprocessing based on image statistics that performs contrast correction to normalize videos captured under varying lighting conditions into consistent intensity distributions. This improves the generalizability of illumination and reflectance estimation and stabilizes training, leading to better denoising performance.

•

We introduce a new multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss function that enforces multiscale similarity between current and historical output frames. An occlusion-aware mask is integrated to effectively handle motion artifacts in complex scenes.

•

To enhance robustness across diverse textures without increasing model complexity, we incorporate a lightweight self-ensemble (SE) strategy.

•

We propose a novel strategy,
reverse inference
, which leverages information from subsequent frames through backward propagation to refine unconverged frames and involve more temporal information.

•

Extensive experimental results show that TempRetinex achieves superior performance in both enhancement quality and temporal consistency compared to existing methods, while maintaining strong adaptability to real-world low-light video scenarios.

Related work

2.1
Unsupervised Low-light Video Enhancement

Recent unsupervised low-light enhancement methods have achieved promising results without relying on paired training data. Zero-DCE
[
4
]
introduced a foundational zero-reference learning framework by formulating enhancement as image-specific curve estimation, guided by non-reference losses for spatial consistency and exposure control. Subsequent works have extended this paradigm in multiple directions. SGZSL
[
26
]
incorporated semantic guidance to preserve critical image content, while EnlightenGAN
[
7
]
advanced adversarial learning with attention-guided dual discriminators that separately evaluate global and local structures. Physics-inspired methods have shown particular promise: RUAS
[
17
]
and Zero-IG
[
18
]
both build upon Retinex theory, with the former leveraging neural representations for robust decomposition and the latter introducing illumination guidance for joint denoising and adaptive enhancement. SCI
[
14
]
proposed stagewise optimization with self-calibrated modules to accelerate convergence, though it neglects denoising. Building on classical S-curve enhancement
[
23
]
, LightenFormer
[
13
]
developed a lightweight pixel-wise estimation network with spatial-temporal refinement, marking an important step toward video-specific enhancement. More recently, UDU-Net
[
28
]
unrolled the optimization process into spatial and temporal sub-networks, integrating expert retouching priors and human perception feedback for exposure control, while employing optical flow to improve inter-frame consistency.

2.2
Unsupervised Denoising

In unsupervised denoising, Noise2Noise
[
9
]
first demonstrated clean image recovery from noisy pairs, while Neighbor2Neighbor
[
5
]
extended this to single images via spatial subsampling. ZS-N2N
[
15
]
further improved practicality by generating training pairs through fixed-filter convolutions of the input image. More recently, AP-BSN
[
8
]
developed single-image solutions using blind spot networks with asymmetric pixel-shuffle downsampling, effectively breaking noise correlations while preserving image details. These advancements highlight the growing trend toward unified frameworks for real-world image restoration.

3.1
Adaptive Brightness Adjustment

This section first analyzes the limitations of existing networks in generalization across varying illumination conditions, then elaborates on the motivation for introducing Retinex-based decomposition as a preprocessing step, and finally details our proposed solution.

Generalization problem due to diverse brightness.
As shown in
Fig.

5
(top), images of the same scene captured at 10% and 20% brightness exhibit markedly different histograms. Real-world applications require networks to generalize across arbitrary lighting conditions. Although supervised methods can achieve good generalization, they depend on large annotated datasets covering the continuous spectrum of illumination which is impractical.

Most unsupervised methods, on the other hand, directly enhance low-light images without explicitly accounting for such differences in intensity distributions, and thus generalize poorly. In general, these methods perform well when trained and tested at the same illumination level. However, in real-world scenarios, inference must handle diverse brightness inputs, and they fail to maintain consistent brightness, as shown in
Fig.

5
, where outputs are underexposed when the input is very dark and overexposed when the input is brighter than the training data mean. This weakness arises from the substantial domain shifts across illumination levels and the absence of ground-truth supervision to correct deviations, which causes the network to overfit local subsets of features rather than learning a mapping robust across the exposure spectrum
[
25
,
21
,
16
]
.

ABA preprocessing.

To address the above problem, we propose an efficient and robust preprocessing method based on image statistical characteristics and Retinex theory. The Retinex theory points out the reflectance represents the inherent properties of objects and is independent of the lighting conditions. This implies that the statistical distribution of the reflectance should theoretically exhibit consistent characteristics across different brightness levels. Therefore, prioritizing the reflectance estimation—rather than directly decomposing illumination—proves more intuitive and effective. Our ABA preprocessing explicitly aligns intensity distributions under different exposures to similar distributions. The images
R_{0}
, generated from inputs with varying brightness as shown in
Fig.

5
(c) and (d), are then used as the initial reflectance and inputted to RE-Net for further refinement and enhancement.

The pipeline of our ABA preprocessing is as follows: Firstly, we calculate the grayscale value
v
at which the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the image reaches a threshold
C
. Here
v
is regarded the maximum valid brightness under current illumination. Secondly, we derive the histogram amplification coefficient
\gamma
as

\gamma=C\cdot 0.8/v,

(2)

where 0.8 is a safety factor to prevent overexposure. Lastly, the initial reflectance is generated by

R_{0}=\gamma\cdot I.

(3)

This pipeline requires no additional training and relies solely on simple image processing operations, ensuring computational efficiency while significantly improving input consistency for subsequent network processing.

3.2.1
Enhancement Module

As illustrated in
Fig.

1
, this module consists of four core parts: the low-light denoising network (LD-Net), the ABA preprocessing, the reflectance estimation network (RE-Net), and the reflectance denoising network (RD-Net). The subnetwork architectures are shown in
Fig.

6
.

The input image
I
is initially processed by LD-Net, which employs three
3\times 3
convolutional layers to generate a preliminary denoised image
I_{LD}
. This step effectively mitigates noise interference in subsequent illumination estimation. Rather than directly utilizing
I_{LD}
for Retinex decomposition, we innovatively introduce the ABA preprocessing described in
Section

3.1
to process
I_{LD}
into an initial reflectance
R_{0}
with a relatively normalized distribution.

Inspired by the stage-wise optimization strategy in
[
17
,
14
]
, RE-Net adopts a convolutional network with residual structure for iterative refinement, following the update equations:

\mathcal{F}(R_{k}):\begin{cases}u_{k}=\mathcal{G}(R_{k}),\\
R_{k+1}=R_{k}+u_{k},\end{cases}

(4)

where
\mathcal{G}
denotes the convolutional mapping layers,
k
represents the iteration index, and
u_{k}
indicates the residual term at stage
k
. In our implementation, we set
k=1
, and
R_{0}
as the initial estimate for the RE-Net’s optimization process. The Retinex decomposition is computed as
Eq.

5
:

\begin{split}&R_{RE}=\mathcal{F}_{RE}(R_{0}),\\
&S_{RE}=I_{LD}\;\oslash\;R_{RE},\end{split}

(5)

where
\oslash
denotes element-wise division and
\mathcal{F}_{RE}
is the estimation function. Notably, the
R_{RE}
here corresponds to an ideal reflectance with sensor noise
N
, whereas the
S_{RE}
can be regarded as noise-free due to the smoothness constraint imposed on
S_{RE}
as described in
Section

3.3
.

Finally, the optimized reflectance
R_{RE}
and illumination
S_{RE}
are concatenated along the channel dimension and fed into RD-Net (as shown in
Fig.

6
). To enhance denoising performance and robustness to diverse textures, we introduce the self-ensemble (SE) strategy into the CNN architecture. Specifically, the CNN network processes four geometrically transformed versions of the input image: original, horizontally flipped, vertically flipped, and 90° rotated images. These four variants share weights within the same convolutional network, ensuring no additional parameters are introduced. The final denoised output
R_{RD}
is obtained by averaging the inversely transformed results of these four processed variants.

3.2.2
Temporal Feedback Module

To accurately describe the temporal feedback mechanism, we employ superscript
t
to denote timestep, where
I^{t}
represents the current frame input and
I^{t-1}
represents the previous frame input, and so on. Image restoration is essentially an ill-posed problem, that is, a single input may correspond to multiple reasonable outputs. When processing video frames independently, as each frame may converge to its own local optima, the combination of these solutions often leads to obvious inter-frame flickering due to the lack of temporal constraints. To this end, we propose an improved temporal feedback module inspired by
[
10
]
, achieving inter-frame information fusion through optical flow alignment technology.

The module first establishes motion estimation between current and historical frames. Considering that noise in the raw input
I^{t}
would degrade the accuracy of motion estimation, we utilize the denoised
I^{t}_{LD}
as a reference image. Unlike
[
10
]
using histogram equalization for brightness consistency, we perform histogram matching (HM) on
I^{t}_{LD}
to align its intensity distribution with
R^{t-1}_{RD}
following
Eq.

6
, where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the HM method. The processed image
\hat{I}^{t}_{LD}
and
R^{t-1}_{RD}
are then fed into optical flow estimation
\mathcal{Q}^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}
(we employ a pretrained RAFT
[
20
]
network in this paper) to compute the optical displacement map
OF^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}
as shown in
Eq.

7
.

\hat{I}^{t}_{LD}=\mathcal{M}(I^{t}_{LD}\,|\,R^{t-1}_{RD}).

(6)

OF^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}=\mathcal{Q}^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}(R^{t-1}_{RD},\;\hat{I}^{t}_{LD}).

(7)

Based on the
OF^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}
, the module performs bilinear interpolation warping on the reflectance component
R^{t-1}_{RD}
and illumination component
S^{t-1}_{RD}
, obtaining aligned results
R^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD}
and
S^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD}
. For the first frame in a sequence, historical components are initialized as zero vectors. These temporal features are then channel-wise concatenated with current frame features for joint optimization in both RE-Net and RD-Net.

3.3
Loss Functions

Since our method is unsupervised learning, a set of non-reference loss functions plays an essential role. We follow the loss functions from
[
10
]
and further introduce a novel multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss for a more comprehensive loss function system.

For LD-Net, we adopt the same optimization strategy as
[
15
]
by decomposing the noisy image into two subimages through downsamplers
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Given
\mathcal{F}_{LD}
as the noise prediction function, and
I
as
I_{LD}
in short, the residual loss
L_{res1}
and consistency loss
L_{cons1}
are defined as
Eq.

8
and
Eq.

9
.

\displaystyle L_{res1}

\displaystyle=||G_{1}(I)-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(G_{1}(I))-G_{2}(I)||^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle+||G_{2}(I)-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(G_{2}(I))-G_{1}(I)||^{2}_{2}\,.

(8)

\displaystyle L_{cons1}

\displaystyle=||G_{1}(I)-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(G_{1}(I))-G_{1}(I-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(I))||^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle+||G_{2}(I)-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(G_{2}(I))-G_{2}(I-\mathcal{F}_{LD}(I))||^{2}_{2}\ .

(9)

For RE-Net, we introduce three optimization objectives to refine both reflectance and illumination. Concretely, the global brightness constraint
L_{glob}
regulates the mean intensity of
R^{t}_{RE}
to approach a predefined upscaling factor
\alpha
:

L_{glob}=||R^{t}_{RE}-\alpha I^{t}_{LD}||^{2}_{2},

(10)

where
\alpha=Y_{H}\cdot Y_{L}^{-1}
,
Y_{L}
represents the mean value of the luminance of
I^{t}_{LD}
, while
Y_{H}
is the mean value of the luminance of normal brightness images.

The pixel-wise adjustment loss
L_{pix}
establishes nonlinear mapping relationships between different intensity levels to achieve pixel-wise brightness adjustment.
L_{pix}
is depicted in
Eq.

11
, where the scaling factor
\lambda
is set to
\alpha^{-1}0.7^{-\alpha}
according to
[
10
]
.

L_{pix}=||S^{t}_{RE}-\lambda(\alpha I^{t}_{LD})^{\alpha}||^{2}_{2}.

(11)

Based on the prior assumption that illumination should be continuous,
L_{s}
applies TV regularization to ensure spatial continuity of the illumination:

L_{s}=(|\nabla_{x}S^{t}_{RE}|+|\nabla_{y}S^{t}_{RE}|)^{2}+\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}|S^{t}_{RE,i}-S^{t}_{RE,j}|,

(12)

where
\nabla_{x}
and
\nabla_{y}
represent horizontal and vertical gradient operators, and
w_{i,j}
are Gaussian-weighted coefficients for a 5×5 neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(i)
around pixel
i
.

For RD-Net, we employ the same downsampling strategy along with loss functions
L_{res2}
and consistency loss
L_{cons2}
to train the denoising performance for
R^{t}_{RD}
. The illumination consistency loss
L_{ill}
as in
Eq.

13
, minimizes the mean square error between
S^{t}_{RD}
and
S^{t}_{RE}
to maintain stability before and after denoising.

L_{ill}=\|S^{t}_{RD}-S^{t}_{RE}\|_{2}^{2}.

(13)

Following
[
18
]
, we incorporate
L_{var}
and
L_{color}
to preserve texture and color fidelity in
R^{t}_{RD}
, while their interactive denoising loss
L_{inter}
further enhances performance. Implementation details of these components are available in the original work.

Multi-scale Temporal Consistency-aware Loss.
To address the potential inconsistency from single-frame image restoration converging to different local optima, we propose a new multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss
L_{mtc}
to further strengthen the cross-frame coherence. The core concept of
L_{mtc}
is to minimize the L1 loss between current reflectance
R^{t}_{RD}
and warped reference
R^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD}
. Considering that optical flow estimation can only model 2D pixel displacement but fails in handling spatial structural changes in depth dimension, we introduce an occlusion mask mechanism inspired by UDU-Net
[
28
]
, which is computed by the exponent of square error between
R^{t}_{RE}
and
R^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD}
:

M=exp(-\omega(||R^{t}_{RE}-R^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD}||_{2}^{2})),

(14)

where
\omega
is empirically set to 100. Additionally, it has been well illustrated in
[
19
]
that downsampled sequences exhibit reduced motion blur. Therefore, we employ a spatial pyramid structure for multiscale optimization of the loss function. The complete formulation of
L_{mtc}
is expressed as:

L_{mtc}=\sum_{i=1,2,3,4}\mathcal{D}_{i}(M\odot(R^{t}_{RD}-R^{(t-1)\rightarrow t}_{RD})),

(15)

where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
denotes bicubic downsampling operation at level i,
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.

In summary, the total loss function is defined as
L_{total}=L_{res1}+L_{cons1}+L_{glob}+L_{pix}+L_{s}+L_{res2}+L_{cons2}+L_{ill}+L_{inter}+L_{var}+L_{color}+L_{mtc}
.

3.4
Reverse Inference Strategy

Theoretically, the temporal feedback module essentially forms a causal closed-loop filter that incorporates historical frame information. As characteristic of filtering systems, it requires a convergence window for stabilization, and cannot utilize future frame during standard online processing, which is necessary for low-latency applications. For offline video enhancement, leveraging both past and future frames yields significant benefits. Considering these reasons, we develop a reverse inference strategy that processes the video sequence both forward and backward during inference, then averages the results. This optional strategy requires no network modifications or retraining and remains compatible with online forward-only processing.

4
Experiments

4.1
Implementation Details

We evaluated our method on two datasets: BVI-RLV
[
11
]
and DID
[
3
]
. The BVI-RLV dataset provides pairs under 40 dynamic scenes, each scene recorded at 10% and 20% brightness along with their corresponding normal light (100% brightness) GT videos. All videos in BVI-RLV dataset are stored in HD-resolution, in PNG format at 25 fps. The DID dataset offers a diverse collection of real-world scenes with challenging illumination variations. In our work, we adopted the 413 HD-resolution video pairs from this dataset, all stored in JPG format. As for the
Y_{H}
values, we set 0.3 for BVI-RLV dataset, 0.5 for DID dataset, accounting for their inherent brightness differences.

All models were implemented on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. We employed the Adam optimizer with
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
, the weight decay of
3\times 10^{-4}
, and the learning rate of
5\times 10^{-5}
. The RAFT network weights are from the pretrained model on the Sintel dataset
[
2
]
. To accelerate optical flow computation during training, input images were downsampled by
3\times
in both spatial dimensions, while maintaining original resolution during testing.

5
Conclusion

This work presents a novel unsupervised framework, TempRetinex, for low-light video enhancement. An ABA preprocessing module is proposed by dynamic contrast stretching, improving the generalizability across diverse lighting conditions. In addition, we design a multi-scale temporal consistency-aware loss function that integrates occlusion-aware masks, ensuring temporal consistency between video frames. To achieve better denoising performance, the SE and reverse inference strategies are introduced. Both qualitative and quantitative experiments demonstrate SOTA performance without requiring paired training data.
