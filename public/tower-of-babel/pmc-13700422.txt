Clinical characteristics of infections due to Staphylococcus lugdunensis compared to other staphylococcal infections
Authors: Souad Bou Harb, Reem Mansour, Lea Al Zoghby, Alicia Khazzeka, Ayah Matar, Youssef Zoughaib, Zeina A. Kanafani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus lugdunensis is a coagulase-negative staphylococcal species with virulence characteristics resembling those of Staphylococcus aureus. Despite its increasing recognition as a clinically significant pathogen, data comparing its clinical outcomes with other staphylococcal infections remain limited. This study aimed to compare the clinical characteristics, outcomes, and factors associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes among patients with S. lugdunensis, S. aureus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis infections.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at the American University of Beirut Medical Center between January 2019 and December 2023. Adult patients with S. lugdunensis infections were identified and matched by site of infection to patients with S. aureus and S. epidermidis infections. Demographic, clinical, microbiological, treatment, and outcome data were collected. Bivariable analyses and multivariable logistic regression were performed to identify factors independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome, defined as a composite of in-hospital mortality and major infection-related complications.ResultsA total of 301 patients were included: 101 with S. lugdunensis, 103 with S. aureus, and 97 with S. epidermidis infections. Skin and soft tissue infections were the most common presentation of S. lugdunensis (72%), followed by urinary tract infections (16%), bacteremia (8%), and respiratory infections (4%). Compared with S. aureus, S. lugdunensis infections were associated with lower rates of persistent bacteremia (0% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.025) and higher rates of acute renal failure (8.9% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.028). No significant outcome differences were observed between S. lugdunensis and S. epidermidis infections. Overall in-hospital mortality rates were 7.9%, 5.8%, and 7.2% for S. lugdunensis, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis, respectively. In multivariable analysis, S. aureus infection was associated with lower odds of unfavorable clinical outcome than S. lugdunensis (aOR 0.31, 95% CI 0.13–0.75), whereas no significant difference was observed between S. lugdunensis and S. epidermidis. Diabetes mellitus (aOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.27–5.82) and hospitalization within the previous 30 days (aOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.26–3.06) were independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome.ConclusionsS. lugdunensis demonstrates clinical characteristics and outcomes overlapping those of S. aureus and S. epidermidis. Prompt recognition and appropriate management remain essential to optimize clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Staphylococcus lugdunensis (S. lugdunensis), first identified in 1988 by Freney et al., is a Gram-positive, catalase-positive staphylococcus [1, 2]. It is classified as a coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) due to the absence of free coagulase production [2]. The species was named after Lugdunum; the Latin name of Lyon, the French city where it was first discovered [3]. Although S. lugdunensis is part of the normal skin flora [4], it has emerged as an intriguing human pathogen, responsible for a wide range of infections [3]. These include skin and soft tissue infections as well as severe conditions such as osteomyelitis, prosthetic joint infections [5], bacteremia, endovascular infections [6], infective endocarditis [7], endometritis [8], endophthalmitis [9], central nervous system infections [10] and urinary tract infections [11].

Compared to other CoNS, S. lugdunensis is associated with a more virulent clinical course, closely resembling Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infections [4]. For example, several virulence factors identified in S. aureus are also present in S. lugdunensis including heat-stable δ-like hemolysin, which enables staphylococci to escape phagocytic killing, and O-acetyltransferase (OatA) which protects them against lysozyme-mediated killing [12]. Although it has its unique characteristics, staphylococcus lugdunensis can occasionally be misidentified in clinical microbiology laboratories as other staphylococcal species [13]. For example, its coagulase-negative status often prompts comparison with Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis), although the two differ in virulence [13]. Moreover, the membrane-bound form of coagulase may lead to confusion with S. aureus, highlighting the importance of accurate species-level identification [13, 14].

Despite its clinical importance, no specific treatment guidelines exist for S. lugdunensis infections. Current management relies mainly on antibiotic susceptibility results, which may be inadequate in invasive infections such as bacteremia [15, 16]. Although recent studies, including those by Palumbo et al. and Lefèvre et al., have further characterized the clinical spectrum and invasive manifestations of S. lugdunensis [17, 18], comparative studies evaluating clinical outcomes relative to both S. aureus and S. epidermidis remain limited. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate and compare the clinical characteristics and outcomes of infections caused by S. lugdunensis, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis.

Methods

Study population and design

This study was conducted at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), a major tertiary care hospital in the Middle East, using a retrospective cohort study design. Adult patients treated for S. lugdunensis infections between January 2019 and December 2023 were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic databases and included in the study. Patients were categorized into four groups based on the site of infection, determined by cultures: wound, urine, respiratory, and blood. Controls with S. aureus and S. epidermidis infections were selected to achieve an approximately 1:1:1 distribution with the Staphylococcus lugdunensis cohort according to the site of infection. Exact matching was not possible for respiratory infections because no eligible S. epidermidis respiratory infections were identified. In addition, two initially excluded S. aureus cases were subsequently confirmed to represent true infections following detailed chart review and were therefore included in the final cohort, resulting in 103 S. aureus patients. Patients who were only colonized with S. lugdunensis (positive culture with no active infection) or who did not receive treatment were excluded. For all three study groups, infection was confirmed based on microbiological findings together with the treating Infectious Diseases physician’s clinical assessment, as documented in the medical record. For S. epidermidis bloodstream infections, only clinically significant bacteremia was included, typically requiring at least two positive blood culture sets together with compatible clinical findings, along with the treating Infectious Diseases physician’s assessment. Each patient was included in the study only once.

Collected data included patients’ demographics (BMI), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, chronic pulmonary disease, HIV infection, immunosuppression including the presence and type of malignancy and recent steroid use within the past 30 days from positive culture, renal insufficiency with or without hemodialysis, and the Charlson comorbidity index), hospitalizations within the past 30 days from positive culture, any intensive care unit (ICU) stay within the past 30 days from positive culture, recent (within 24 h from positive culture) or previous (within the past 30 days from positive culture) antibiotic use, complications following the S. lugdunensis infection, and outcomes at hospital discharge. Renal insufficiency was defined as documented chronic kidney disease or the need for hemodialysis, as recorded in the electronic medical record. Antibiotic susceptibilities for oxacillin, glycopeptides, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), erythromycin, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines were retrieved from microbiology reports, along with the primary antibiotics prescribed for each infection.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables and outcomes. Bivariable analyses were performed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and the independent samples t-test for continuous variables. To identify factors independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using unfavorable outcome as the dependent variable. Unfavorable clinical outcome was defined as a composite endpoint comprising in-hospital mortality or the occurrence of one or more major infection-related complications, including sepsis, septic shock, persistent or progressive infection, persistent bacteremia, recurrent infection, acute renal failure, ICU admission, or respiratory failure. Covariates were selected based on their clinical relevance and their association with unfavorable clinical outcome in bivariable analysis (p < 0.20). Variables entered into the final multivariable model included infecting organism, renal insufficiency, hospitalization within the preceding 30 days, and diabetes mellitus. The infecting organism was entered as a categorical variable, with Staphylococcus lugdunensis as the reference category. Renal insufficiency and diabetes mellitus were included as dichotomous variables (yes/no), whereas hospitalization within the preceding 30 days was categorized as 0–2 days, 3–5 days, and > 6 days and entered as an ordinal variable. To minimize overfitting given the relatively small number of unfavorable outcomes, the multivariable model was restricted to these clinically relevant covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because matching was performed to obtain comparable distributions of infection sites rather than as an individually matched-pair design, standard logistic regression analysis was performed without accounting for matching.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the American University of Beirut (IRB ID: BIO-2022-0065). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Patient characteristics

We identified 101 patients with S. lugdunensis infections at our institution. These were matched by site of infection to 103 patients with S. aureus infections and 97 patients with S. epidermidis infections (after exclusion of respiratory samples), yielding a total of 301 patients.

The most common site of S. lugdunensis infection was skin and soft tissue (SSTI) (n = 73, 72%), followed by urine (n = 16, 16%), blood (n = 8, 8%), and sputum (n = 4, 4%). The baseline characteristics of patients in each group are represented in Table 1. The mean age was 50.68 ± 20.2 for S. lugdunensis, 55.63 ± 19.9 years for S. aureus and 52.9 ± 20.74 years for S. epidermidis, with no statistically significant differences between the groups. Similarly, there were no statistically significant differences in Charlson Comorbidity Index scores between the three groups.

Compared to patients with S. aureus infections, those with S. lugdunensis infections were less likely to be males (50.5% vs. 66%, p = 0.025), and less likely to have previous hospital stays more than 2 days (10.9% vs. 37.9%, p < 0.001). Compared with S. epidermidis infections, patients with S. lugdunensis were more likely to have a prior history of infective endocarditis (5.9% vs. 0, p = 0.015) and more likely to have received antibiotics within the 24 h before culture positivity (19.8% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.002).

Antibiotic resistance and antibiotic treatment

Antibiotic resistance rates of the S. lugdunensis cohort were as follows: resistance to TMP-SMX and oxacillin was low (2% and 7%, respectively), and no glycopeptide resistance was observed. Resistance to clindamycin, erythromycin, and tetracyclines was 14%, 13% and 11%, respectively.

Regarding treatment, the most frequently prescribed primary antibiotics for S. lugdunensis were beta lactams, followed by fluoroquinolones. Similarly, beta-lactams were most commonly used primary antibiotics for infections caused by S. aureus and S. epidermidis, followed by glycopeptides (Fig. 1). The mean duration of therapy was 11 days for S. lugdunensis infections, 7 days for S. aureus infections, and 9.5 days for S. epidermidis infections.

Outcomes

The outcomes of patients with S. lugdunensis, S. aureus and S. epidermidis infections are summarized in Table 2. Compared with S. aureus, patients with S. lugdunensis were less likely to have persistent bacteremia (4.9% vs. 0%, p = 0.025), and more likely to develop acute renal failure (9% vs. 2%, p = 0.028). No statistically significant differences in outcomes were observed between S. lugdunensis and S. epidermidis infections. Namely, the overall mortality rate was 7.9% with SL, 5.8% with SA and 7.25% with SE with no statistically significant differences between the three groups.

Factors associated with unfavorable clinical outcome

Overall, unfavorable clinical outcome occurred in 20 of 101 (19.8%) patients with Staphylococcus lugdunensis infection, compared with 11 of 103 (10.7%) patients with Staphylococcus aureus infection and 13 of 97 (13.4%) patients with Staphylococcus epidermidis infection.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, the infecting organism was independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome overall (p = 0.031). Compared with S. lugdunensis, S. aureus infection was associated with significantly lower odds of unfavorable clinical outcome (aOR 0.31, 95% CI 0.13–0.75; p = 0.010), whereas S. epidermidis infection was not significantly different from S. lugdunensis (aOR 0.54, 95% CI 0.24–1.23; p = 0.142). Hospitalization within the previous 30 days (aOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.26–3.06; p = 0.003) and diabetes mellitus (aOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.27–5.82; p = 0.010) were also independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome. Renal insufficiency showed a borderline association with unfavorable clinical outcome (aOR 2.40, 95% CI 0.99–5.81; p = 0.052) (Table 3).

Discussion

The demographic and clinical features of patients with S. lugdunensis infections in our study resembled those with S. epidermidis and S. aureus. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) did not differ significantly between groups. While S. aureus infections were more frequent in males (66% male), consistent with epidemiologic studies reporting a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1.5 for bacteremia and an odds ratio of 2.4 for SSTIs in males [19], S. lugdunensis infections were evenly distributed between sexes. The male predominance observed in S. aureus infections may be explained by anatomical and microbiota differences, as well as behavioral factors [20, 21].

In our cohort, SSTIs were the most common presentation, consistent with prior reports [1, 22], including the recent cohort by Palumbo et al., emphasizing S. lugdunensis as an important cause of skin and soft tissue infections [18]. This parallels the infection profile of S. aureus and reflects their similar clinical outcomes [23]. Other reported manifestations include urinary tract infections (16%), bacteremia (8%), and endocarditis (8%) [14, 22, 24].

Our findings suggested that patients with S. lugdunensis infections were less likely to experience persistent bacteremia compared with S. aureus (0% and 4.9% respectively). This difference may reflect differences in pathogenic mechanisms between the two organisms, although persistent bacteremia is also influenced by several clinical factors, including source control, timely administration of effective antimicrobial therapy, and host characteristics [25, 26]. The greater propensity of S. aureus to cause persistent infection has been attributed to multiple virulence mechanisms, including immune evasion and biofilm formation [23, 27, 28]. Ainoda et al., in a study of 2,478 patients with bacteremia in Japan, reported seven cases of S. lugdunensis bacteremia, including one methicillin-resistant case that resulted in death within 30 days [29]. Similarly, Liu et al., in a review of 67 cases of S. lugdunensis infective endocarditis between 1988 and 2008, reported a mortality rate of 38.8% [30]. A retrospective study by Non & Santos found that the fraction of infective endocarditis in patients with S. lugdunensis bacteremia was similar to those with S. aureus bacteremia [13]. In recent studies, Yukuwa et al. reported that S. lugdunensis infections exhibit more complex clinical outcomes than S. aureus. More recently, Lefèvre et al. confirmed the clinical significance of S. lugdunensis infective endocarditis in a large international multicenter cohort, further emphasizing its potential to cause severe invasive disease and poor clinical outcomes [17]. Another study comparing infective endocarditis outcomes between S. lugdunensis and S. aureus found that the mortality rate for S. lugdunensis cases (20%) was significantly higher than S. aureus (9%) and other coagulase negative staphylococci (7%) [14]. These findings reinforce the need for continued investigations into the virulence and resistance mechanisms of S. lugdunensis [31].

An additional finding in our study was the higher incidence of acute renal failure in patients with S. lugdunensis compared to S. aureus. This may be related to infection site–specific pathogenic mechanisms, suggesting a need to better understand the renal complications associated with this organism. In contrast, no statistically significant differences in outcomes were observed between S. lugdunensis and S. epidermidis in our study despite their differing epidemiological profiles.

An unexpected finding of our study was that S. aureus infection was associated with lower odds of unfavorable clinical outcome compared with S. lugdunensis infection. One possible explanation is that S. aureus is universally recognized as a major pathogen, often prompting earlier diagnostic evaluation and more aggressive therapeutic management. In contrast, although S. lugdunensis can cause severe invasive infections, its clinical significance may be underestimated, potentially delaying optimal management. This hypothesis is supported by the recent study by Webb et al., in which S. lugdunensis bacteremia was more frequently considered a contaminant despite demonstrating mortality and infective endocarditis rates comparable to those of S. aureus [32]. However, treatment-related variables, including the timing of appropriate antimicrobial therapy and source control, were not evaluated in our study, and therefore this explanation remains speculative. Residual confounding, differences in patient characteristics, and the relatively small sample size may also have contributed to the observed association. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing S. lugdunensis as a clinically significant pathogen and warrant further investigation in larger prospective studies.

Recent hospitalization was independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome in our cohort. This finding likely reflects the increased complexity of patients with recent healthcare exposure, who may have a greater burden of comorbidities, repeated healthcare contacts, or more severe underlying illness, all of which may contribute to poorer clinical outcomes.

Lastly, antibiotic resistance remains an important aspect of S. lugdunensis epidemiology. Overall, resistance rates remain low, and it remains susceptible to most anti-staphylococcal agents. In our cohort, the methicillin resistance rate was 7%, which is consistent with previously reported international rates. Palumbo et al. reported generally favorable antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, including low methicillin resistance and preserved susceptibility to glycopeptides, supporting the continued effectiveness of these agents for the treatment of S. lugdunensis infections [18]. Nevertheless, higher methicillin resistance rates have been reported in some geographic regions, including Taiwan, highlighting the importance of continued antimicrobial surveillance to detect emerging resistance and guide treatment strategies [33].

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding. Second, the predominance of skin and soft tissue infections in our cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings to more invasive S. lugdunensis infections. Third, despite using a composite unfavorable clinical outcome to better capture disease severity, the relatively small sample size limited the number of variables that could be included in the multivariable model and may have reduced the statistical power to detect additional associations. Fourth, treatment-related factors, including the timing of appropriate antimicrobial therapy and adequacy of source control, were not evaluated and may have influenced clinical outcomes. Finally, larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to further characterize the clinical behavior and outcomes of S. lugdunensis infections. Furthermore, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was analyzed only for the S. lugdunensis cohort. As susceptibility data were not systematically collected for the comparator groups, we were unable to compare resistance profiles or account for methicillin resistance in the analyses, which may have influenced treatment strategies and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Although historically considered a less virulent coagulase-negative staphylococcus, Staphylococcus lugdunensis has emerged as a clinically significant pathogen with clinical manifestations and outcomes comparable to those of Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis. In our cohort, S. aureus infection was associated with lower odds of unfavorable clinical outcome than S. lugdunensis, while diabetes mellitus and recent hospitalization were independently associated with unfavorable clinical outcome. These findings highlight the importance of prompt recognition and appropriate management of S. lugdunensis infections. Further multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and better define the factors influencing clinical outcomes.
