Amide-engineered copper nitride for shortcut-pathway-locked electroreduction of concentrated hydroxymethylfurfural
Authors: Ju Huang, Jianqiu Zhu, Bowen Liu, Chencheng Dai, Zhimin Chen, Hao Wu, Gang Li, Shichao Du, Jian-Qiang Wang, Zhiyu Ren, Zhichuan J Xu, Honggang Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving high selectivity in the electrocatalytic hydrogenation (ECH) of concentrated 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) to 2,5-dihydroxymethylfuran (DHMF) remains a challenge due to competitive adsorption at high HMF concentrations, which limits active-site availability and suppresses *H generation. Regulating the balance between *H generation and HMF adsorption is therefore essential for maintaining selectivity under practical conditions. Here we show a surface amide-functionalized copper nitride catalyst (Ami-Cu3N) featuring dual-nitrogen active sites. The catalyst achieves >99% DHMF selectivity and >98% Faradaic efficiency, with a production rate of 63.4 mmol cm-2 h-1 at 500 mA cm-2 under concentrated HMF conditions. Mechanistic studies indicate that the combined roles of lattice nitrogen (promoting water dissociation) and amide nitrogen (modulating HMF adsorption) lower the energy barriers for both *H formation and HMF hydrogenation, facilitating high activity and selectivity. In addition, a coupled electrolysis system enables simultaneous production of value-added products at both electrodes. These findings provide design principles for electrocatalysts that enable selective biomass conversion at high reaction rates.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

2,5-Dihydroxymethylfuran (DHMF), a biodegradable, multifunctional diol precursor widely used in synthesizing polyethers, polyurethanes, and polyamides, commands a market price 3–5 times higher than that of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) and is experiencing rapidly growing global demand1,2. Conventional DHMF production depends on energy-intensive hydrogenation under high temperature and pressure, which diminishes its economic and environmental appeal3,4. In contrast, electrocatalytic reduction of biomass-derived HMF using green hydrogen, generated from water electrolysis powered by renewable electricity, offers a sustainable route under mild conditions5,6. Nevertheless, at industrial-level concentrations (>100 mM), achieving high-selectivity electrosynthesis of DHMF remains challenging, primarily due to insufficient control over the electrocatalytic hydrogenation (ECH) pathway and the impacts of competing reactions.

Direct electroreduction via concerted proton-electron transfer (CPET) suffers from inefficient proton transfer and aggregation of reactive intermediates (e.g., ∙C–OH radicals), which promotes dimerization and undermines selectivity7. In contrast, the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) mechanism employs surface-adsorbed hydrogen (*H) to hydrogenate adsorbed HMF directly, suppressing dimerization and offering enhanced pathway control. However, under high HMF concentrations relevant to practical operation, the L-H pathway still competes with the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), as strong competitive adsorption between HMF and water limits H availability at the catalyst surface, thereby reducing hydrogen utilization efficiency8. More critically, at elevated HMF concentrations, maintaining sufficient surface HMF coverage necessitates strong adsorption, while the increased reactant flux intensifies competition for interfacial proton-electron pairs, exacerbating proton-transfer limitations and favoring the re-emergence of CPET-driven side reactions. Therefore, a central catalyst design challenge lies in simultaneously ensuring a sufficient surface H supply and selective HMF adsorption under high reactant concentrations, while suppressing CPET and HER, to enable efficient and selective DHMF production from concentrated HMF feedstocks.

Recent efforts to improve ECH performance have focused on increasing the surface coverage of active *H species through strategies such as incorporating electron-rich metals, constructing heterogeneous interfaces, and designing bimetallic sites9,10. While these approaches exhibit promising ECH activity at low HMF concentrations, they often suffer from inadequate selectivity and Faradaic efficiency (FE) under high concentrations of HMF (>100 mM), limiting their practical utility11. An ideal ECH catalyst must simultaneously facilitate *H generation and improve HMF adsorption12. Copper-based catalysts are particularly promising13. They can effectively generate *H via water dissociation (H2O → *H + OH-), comparable to noble metals like Pt or Ru, while their relatively high hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔG*H > 0) suppresses HER, thereby maintaining a high *H coverage14,15. Further incorporation of nitrogen into Cu to form copper nitrides enhances the metal’s d-electron affinity, which promotes water dissociation and sustains a high *H supply16. Equally critical is the enrichment of HMF at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Molecular modification, especially the introduction of nitrogen-containing functional groups, enables precisely tuning of the catalyst’s electronic structure and local microenvironment, markedly enhancing HMF adsorption17,18. Such modifications generally induce surface charge redistribution, creating localized nucleophilic regions (e.g., Mδ+–Nδ- motifs), which strengthen interaction with the electrophilic carbonyl group of HMF19. Therefore, by rationally designing the nitrogen-coordination environment around Cu centers, an adaptive synergy between *H generation and HMF adsorption can be achieved, leading to high activity and selectivity under industrially relevant high-concentration conditions.

Building on these insights, we synthesized a surface amide-functionalized copper nitride catalyst (Ami-Cu3N/CF) via synchronous surficial amide functionalization and nitridation. The lattice N in Cu3N promotes *H generation and coverage, while the surface amide groups enhance HMF adsorption through microenvironment modulation. The adjacent dual-nitrogen sites facilitate rapid *H-HMF binding, achieving dynamic balance during electrocatalytic hydrogenation. Under high-concentration HMF conditions (100–1000 mM), Ami-Cu3N/CF demonstrates high catalytic stability and selectivity, with a FE for DHMF consistently maintained above 90%; the selectivity for DHMF exceeds 99%, thereby showing competitive performance compared to previously reported systems. Mechanistic studies combining experimental and theoretical approaches reveal a synergistic multi-nitrogen effect. Furthermore, a coupled electrolysis system for concurrent DHMF (cathode) and 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA; anode) production reduces the cell voltage compared to conventional OER-coupled HMF reduction, demonstrating improved energy efficiency and practical potential.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterizations of Ami-Cu3N/CF

The synthesis and phase evolution pathway of Ami-Cu3N/CF is schematically depicted in Fig. 1a, b. A pretreated Cu foam (CF) substrate was oxidized to form Cu(OH)2 nanowires (Cu(OH)2/CF), which underwent coordination corrosion with BTC to yield a BTC-Cu metal-organic framework (BTC-Cu/CF). Subsequent thermal annealing under NH3 induced simultaneous nitridation and in-situ amide functionalization, producing Ami-Cu3N/CF. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of Ami-Cu3N/CF (Fig. 1b) shows distinct Cu3N peaks (PDF# 86-2283) alongside those of the metallic Cu substrate, with positions matching those in Cu3N/CF prepared without coordination corrosion. The scanning electron microscope (SEM) image reveals that Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits an octahedral-derived framework structure derived from BTC-Cu/CF, contrasting the nanowire morphology of its Cu(OH)2/CF precursor (Figs. 1c, d and S1). This octahedral-derived framework features a rugged surface adorned with numerous nanocubes. Conversely, control samples Cu3N/CF (NH3 nitridation) and Cu/CF (H2 reduction), synthesized without BTC’s coordinating-corrosive effect, largely retain a nanowire-like morphology, albeit with rougher or more curved surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 2). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of exfoliated Ami-Cu3N (Fig. 1e) confirms nanocubes (10–50 nm) embedded on the octahedral-derived frameworks. Higher-magnification TEM images show that these nanocubes consist primarily of porous carbon that embeds numerous small nanoparticles, attributed to incomplete decomposition of BTC-Cu organic ligands, as supported by thermogravimetric analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3). The high-resolution TEM image (Fig. 1f, g) shows a spacing of 0.38 nm, corresponding to the (100) interplanar crystal spacing of Cu3N. Furthermore, the energy dispersive spectrometer elemental mapping reveals that C, N, and Cu elements are distributed in Ami-Cu3N nanocubes (Fig. 1h). Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits the largest Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface area (2.62 m2 g−1) compared to Cu3N/CF (1.45 m2 g−1) and Cu/CF (0.89 m2 g−1), attributed to BTC-derived porosity, which facilitates reactant diffusion and exposure of active sites (Supplementary Fig. 4)20.

The surface composition and valence states of Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF, and Cu/CF were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). High-resolution Cu 2p XPS spectra and corresponding Cu LMM Auger spectra (Fig. 2a, b) collectively indicate that Ami-Cu3N/CF consists predominantly of Cu+ species (Cu 2p3/2: 930–938 eV; Cu 2p1/2: 950–958 eV), with minor Cu2+ contributions21. The Cu 2p peaks of Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibit a slight negative binding energy shift (0.05 eV) compared with those of Cu3N/CF. Cu3N/CF exhibits higher Cu2+ content, whereas Cu/CF is dominated by metallic Cu0. The ubiquitous presence of Cu2+ across all catalysts is attributed to surface passivation from ambient air exposure22. Crucially, the carbonaceous layer derived from amide functionalization mitigates oxidation of Cu+, enabling partial retention of the Cu+ valence state in Ami-Cu3N/CF. The electronic and coordination structures of Ami-Cu3N/CF were characterized using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). Figure 2c displays the normalized Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra for Ami-Cu3N, Cu3N, BTC-Cu, and reference samples. The absorption edge energies of Ami-Cu3N and Cu3N lies between those of Cu2O and Cu foil, indicating Cu valence states in them intermediate between +1 and 023. Moreover, Cu K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Fig. 2d) reveal similar primary peaks at ∼1.25 and ∼2.2 Å for Ami-Cu3N, Cu3N, and BTC-Cu, corresponding to Cu–N/O and Cu–Cu scattering paths24. Nitridation-driven conversion of BTC-Cu to Ami-Cu3N alters the Cu coordination environment (Supplementary Table 1), yielding a significant reduction in the first-shell coordination number (CN) for Cu–N/O (BTC-Cu: CN = 4.64; Ami-Cu3N: CN = 1.32). Moreover, Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits further reduced CNs in both first and second coordination shells compared to Cu3N and Mix-Ami-Cu3N, with a long Cu–N–Cu bond length (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). This indicates that, in addition to the intrinsic Cu–N and Cu–Cu bonding in Cu3N, the N species from amide groups in the external carbon layer are additionally coordinated to Cu sites in Ami-Cu3N. Wavelet transform EXAFS analysis further confirms this; specifically, the Cu–N–Cu signal in Ami-Cu3N/CF is stronger than that in Cu3N/CF. It maybe benefits from the formation of the –CO–NH–Cu3N linkage, which proceeds via the reaction of the -COOH group in BTC-Cu with NH3 during nitridation, is energetically favorable, as evidenced by a reaction energy (ΔE) of −0.214 eV (Supplementary Fig. 7)25. The above findings indicate that the synthesized Ami-Cu3N exhibits a proximal multi-nitrogen coordination environment.

The presence of –CO–NH– groups in Ami-Cu3N/CF is confirmed by comparing the Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and high-resolution N 1s XPS spectra between Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF. The FTIR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 8) of Ami-Cu3N/CF highlights the amide II band (1541 cm−1, C–N stretching vibration and N–H in-plane bending of a –CO–NH– group), amide I band (1663 cm−1, C=O stretching vibration in a secondary amide group), and C–N bond (stretching vibration at 1400 cm−1 and flexural vibration at 703 cm−1)26,27. The N 1s XPS spectrum of Ami-Cu3N/CF (Fig. 2f) reveals a distinguished peak at 400 eV, assigned to the amide nitrogen (N–C=O), absent in Cu3N/CF. Both catalysts show peaks at 397.4 eV (Cu–N) and 399.0 eV (N–H). Deconvolution of the O 1s XPS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 9) yields peaks at 531.2 eV (amide O, –NH–C=O) and 532.6 eV (carboxyl O, HO–C=O). The C 1s XPS spectrum deconvolutes into peaks at 289.0 eV (C=O), 286.7 eV(C–N), 285.5 eV (C–O), and 284.4 eV (C–C)28. The N K-edge XANES spectrum of Ami-Cu3N/CF (Fig. 2g) further corroborates the successful formation of amide bonds. The spectrum shows four distinct peaks: a strong pre-edge peak N1 (~398.6 eV) from M3d–N2p covalency, minor features N2/N3 (~399.4/400.6 eV) from amino groups and nitrogen dopants within carbon layer, and peak N4 (~405 eV) from the N 1s core level to C–N σ* states29–32. This assignment validates the chemical transformation from BTC to the amide-linked product. The XPS valence state spectrum (Fig. 2h) results from the cumulative contributions of the electronic structures of all atoms in the sample. The relative intensities follow the order Ami-Cu3N/CF <Cu3N/CF <Cu/CF, indicating distinctions in their electronic configurations. Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits a relatively higher electron density near the Fermi level compared to the other samples33–35.

Electrochemical hydrogenation of HMF

Preliminary electrochemical evaluation in a three-electrode system shows that Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF preferentially catalyze the electrocatalytic hydrogenation of HMF (HMF ECH) over HER, in contrast to Cu/CF (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Moreover, Ami-Cu3N/CF also shows notably enhanced HER activity compared to Cu3N/CF, which can be attributed to the facilitated dissociation of H2O promoted by the amide functionalization. Subsequent chronoamperometry experiments conducted at −0.05 VRHE monitored by ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy track HMF depletion and concomitant DHMF formation (Supplementary Fig. 12), while quantitative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis establishes their inverse concentration correlation over time (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Fig. 13). Given the absence of detectable gaseous carbon-containing products, the remaining charge was primarily attributed to HER after accounting for all quantified liquid-phase products. Accordingly, the FE toward H2 was estimated from charge balance as 100%–∑FEliquid. This enhanced HMF ECH selectivity is attributed to the synergistic effect of the amide-Cu3N synergy, as evidenced by the control experiments (lacking nitridation and amide functionalization) (Supplementary Fig. 14). Amide-adsorbed Cu3N/CF exhibits higher current density, DHMF yield, and FE than unfunctionalized Cu3N, yet remains less active than Ami-Cu3N/CF (Supplementary Fig. 15), likely due to insufficient interfacial contact.

The HMF ECH performances of Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF are systematically evaluated in a divided flow cell equipped with a Nafion-212 membrane, using a nickel foam as the anode and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode (Fig. 3a). Catalysts are loaded into the flow cell with 1.0 cm2 geometric area. The anodic compartment contains 100 mL of 0.5 M PBS, while the catholyte comprises 100 mL of 0.5 M PBS with 200 mM HMF. Chronopotentiometry measurements are conducted at a total charge of 3859 C (theoretical 2e– reduction of HMF to DHMF, assuming 100% FE) across current densities ranging from −100 to −600 mA cm−2. Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits enhanced performance (Figs. 3c and S16), achieving higher FEDHMF and HMF conversion than Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF. At −100 mA cm−2, the potential of Ami-Cu3N/CF drops to 26 mV while maintaining >98.0% FEDHMF. Ami-Cu3N/CF maintains >90% FEDHMF and >90% HMF conversion across a broad current range (−100 to −600 mA cm−2), indicating improved performance relative to the control catalysts. Full-duration chronopotentiometry (Supplementary Fig. 17) validates that the charge-normalized comparison reflects robust steady-state catalysis rather than transient deactivation. Unlike the decaying control electrodes, Ami-Cu3N/CF sustains a stable potential across all current densities, indicating that its performance is associated with a robust, amide-stabilized active phase. At high cathodic current densities (−600 mA cm−2), a slight decline in FEDHMF (~90%) is observed. Additional concentration-dependent and hydrodynamic control experiments (Supplementary Fig. 18) indicate that interfacial mass transport becomes a limiting factor under these conditions. Enhancing reactant supply via improved convection or concentration effectively mitigates this limitation and restores DHMF selectivity. This combination of high activity at low potential and effective HER suppression represents an important step toward practical implementation. Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits competitive mass-specific productivity compared to reported catalysts, while maintaining near-quantitative DHMF yield and FE (Supplementary Table 5). To verify that the enhanced performance stems from intrinsic catalytic properties rather than potential-induced bias under galvanostatic control, we conducted potentiostatic evaluations at fixed thermodynamic driving forces (Supplementary Fig. 19). Notably, the matched-potential potentiostatic comparison provides a critical control for excluding protocol-dependent selectivity bias. Even under identical thermodynamic driving forces (−10 to −50 mVRHE), Ami-Cu3N/CF maintains near-quantitative DHMF yield and FE, showing improved performance relative to Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF. This result demonstrates that the enhanced hydrogenation performance of Ami-Cu3N/CF is intrinsic to its amide-engineered interfacial structure rather than an artifact of the galvanostatic testing protocol. The much higher overpotentials required by Cu/CF further exacerbate selectivity loss at high current densities. Under such strongly cathodic conditions, accelerated water reduction leads to increased HER contribution, thereby diverting *H away from productive hydrogenation pathways. In contrast, Ami-Cu3N/CF operates at much milder potentials, where the interfacial structure effectively sustains selective HMF hydrogenation while suppressing parasitic HER. These matched-potential results therefore provide direct evidence that the catalyst-dependent selectivity originates from intrinsic interfacial chemistry rather than from differences in the operating potential imposed by galvanostatic testing. Control experiments using bare Cu foam show HER dominated behavior with minimal DHMF formation, indicating that the high selectivity of Ami-Cu3N/CF originates from the engineered active interface rather than the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 20). The apparent turnover frequency (TOF), normalized to electrochemically accessible Cu sites derived from surface oxidation voltammetry, was used to compare the intrinsic HMF ECH activity of the catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 21). Despite the conservative nature of this normalization (which counts the total accessible Cu reservoir), Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits a markedly higher TOF (17.2 s−1, at −0.35 VRHE) than Cu3N/CF (10.1 s−1) and Cu/CF (1.5 s−1), confirming its enhanced intrinsic activity associated with nitrogen-modified Cu sites36.

High-concentration HMF ECH is crucial for industrial applications. Evaluation across 20–1000 mM HMF demonstrates that, unlike Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF, which lose activity at higher concentrations, Ami-Cu3N/CF maintains nearly constant DHMF yield and FE (≈99%) with a productivity of 63.4 mmol cm−2 h−1 at 1000 mM and >98% HMF conversion (Figs. 3d and S22). At identical current densities, the cathode potential of Ami-Cu3N/CF remains remarkably stable against varying HMF concentrations, in sharp contrast to the significant potential drift observed for Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF (Supplementary Table 3). This invariance confirms that the FE evolution is not an artifact of potential fluctuation but is intrinsically grounded in concentration-dependent kinetics, where higher substrate coverage effectively suppresses the competing HER. Under 200 mA cm−2 constant current (Fig. 3e), HMF depletes quantitatively (>99% conversion) with concurrent DHMF accumulation to maximum yield. In 200 mM electrolyte, near-complete conversion occurs within 3 h (Figs. 3f and S23), and Ami-Cu3N/CF retains >98% DHMF yield and >99% selectivity over 90 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 24), confirming high stability. This performance is competitive with previously reported benchmarks (Supplementary Table 5)2,11,37–57. To further decouple intrinsic catalyst stability from potential-induced degradation, we additionally performed long-term stability tests under matched-potential conditions (Supplementary Fig. 25). Under the same applied half-cell potential, Ami-Cu3N/CF still exhibits more stable performance than Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF, indicating that its enhanced durability is not solely a consequence of operating at milder galvanostatic potentials, but also reflects the intrinsic robustness of Ami-Cu3N/CF. Furthermore, substrate scope studies (Supplementary Figs. 26–28, and Supplementary Table 6) confirm broad applicability for aldehyde electroreduction, achieving enhanced yields, selectivity, and FE toward furfuryl, benzyl, and 4-methoxybenzyl alcohols. The comprehensive performance results indicate that the multi-nitrogen coordination environment of Ami-Cu3N regulates *H and substrate adsorption dynamics, enabling efficient and selective aldehyde hydrogenation.

Capitalizing on the bifunctional efficacy of Ami-Cu3N/CF, we further assembled a proof-of-concept paired electrolysis system. By coupling cathodic HMF hydrogenation with anodic HMF oxidation (Supplementary Figs. 29–31), thereby circumventing the energy-intensive oxygen evolution reaction, we illustrate a generalizable strategy for maximizing energy efficiency in biomass valorization. As this configuration serves primarily to demonstrate system-level feasibility rather than intrinsic catalytic metrics, detailed performance data and a preliminary techno-economic analysis are presented in Supplementary Figs. 32–38.

Origin of Ami-Cu3N/CF’s high activity for HMF ECH

By integrating quasi-in-situ XRD and XPS tests with post-reaction crystalline phase, microstructure, and atomic-level analysis, we established that the catalyst evolves via a partial, self-limited electrochemical reconstruction mechanism (Supplementary Figs. 39–48). Importantly, additional high-bias XPS comparisons further show that this self-limiting behavior is not merely a consequence of the mild operating window, but reflects an intrinsic resistance to deep metallization conferred by amide functionalization. Under reaction-relevant cathodic potentials, the partial reduction of Cu+ to metallic Cu0 is thermodynamically inevitable; thus, the emergence of Cu0 represents an intrinsic response to the reductive environment required for high-rate HMF hydrogenation. Crucially, however, unlike unfunctionalized Cu3N, which suffers from deep reduction, the amide-functionalized catalyst retains a dominant Cu+ character. Quantitative XPS analysis shows that the surface amide coverage (RAmi-N/Cu–N) rapidly reaches a stable plateau, confirming that these groups act as robust ligands to inhibit excessive metallization. XAS evidence further supports this interfacial model. The post-reaction EXAFS spectrum requires a dual-shell fit consisting of Cu–N and Cu–Cu contributions, indicating the coexistence of residual nitride coordination and newly formed metallic Cu species. The fitted Cu–Cu bond length (2.70 Å) is longer than that of bulk Cu metal (2.54 Å) and close to the Cu–Cu distance in the Cu3N lattice (2.73 Å), which is consistent with a closely coupled Cu/Cu3N interfacial structure formed after reconstruction. Complementary N K-edge XANES and Quasi-in-situ FTIR confirm the integrity of the amide linkages (–CO–NH–) during this process (Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48). Time-dependent inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry analysis also reveals a dynamic reconstruction process characterized by transient Cu dissolution followed by spontaneous re-deposition (Supplementary Fig. 49, and Supplementary Table 9). Ultimately, the bulk electrolyte remains virtually free of dissolved Cu species after stabilization. Taken together, the quasi-in-situ XPS, XAS, and XRD results show that amide functionalization suppresses deep reduction of Cu3N and promotes a self-limited reconstruction toward an Ami-Cu/Cu3N heterostructure. Importantly, the enhanced operational durability of Ami-Cu3N/CF should not be attributed to a single factor alone. Rather, amide functionalization confers both (i) structural stabilization that limits reconstruction depth and prevents full metallization, and (ii) enhanced intrinsic activity that allows the catalyst to operate at substantially milder cathodic potentials. These two effects synergistically contribute to the observed long-term stability. Thus, under operating conditions, the catalyst evolves into a robust amide-functionalized Cu/Cu3N heterostructure, which is inferred from the post-reaction characterizations to represent the steady-state active surface. This experimentally inferred end-state, rather than the dynamic reconstruction pathway itself, was subsequently used as the structural basis for the DFT analysis.

To elucidate the DHMF formation pathway over Ami-Cu3N/CF, in-situ FTIR spectroscopy was employed to monitor HMF adsorption and conversion behavior. As shown in Figs. 4a and S50, three key intermediates are identified: *H (around 2053 cm−1), −C=O (near 1650 cm−1), and the furan ring (1014, 1072, 1153, 1453 and 1515 cm−1)58–61. No discernible peaks are observed under open-circuit potential (OCP) conditions, whereas characteristic bands appear upon applying more negative potentials. Hydrogenation of HMF commences at −0.1 VRHE on Ami-Cu3N/CF. As the potential became more negative, the *H band exhibits a blue shift, indicating weakened adsorption caused by competitive HER. The peaks associated with the furan ring remain unchanged across all potentials, indicating negligible adsorption of the furan ring on the catalyst. Cu3N/CF shows a similar trend but with significantly weaker *H and HMF adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 51). During electrolysis at −0.5 VRHE (Fig. 4b, c), *H and C=O intensities first increase then decrease after 250–300 min, corresponding to *H consumption and C=O desorption. These results indicate that active *H species are involved in the hydrogenation process, while HMF adsorbs primarily via the −C=O group, implying that hydrogenation occurs selectively at the carbonyl group. Additionally, in-situ FTIR confirms the enrichment of −COOH groups from HMF on the Ami-Cu3N/CF surface.

To elucidate the hydrogen source in HMF ECH, isotope labeling experiments were conducted. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Fig. 4d–f and S52) of the products in PBS/H2O display DHMF signals, while rapid H−D exchange at the −OH group is confirmed by signal disappearance upon D2O addition. The spectrum obtained from PBS/D2O shows deuteration at three hydrogen positions, demonstrating that water serves as the hydrogen source. Quasi-in-situ EPR using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as a trap detects ∙H species on Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF, and Cu/CF in electrolyte under potential, confirming water-derived ∙H generation, while no signal is observed in the absence of voltage (Figs. 4g and S53)62,63. CV analysis reveals distinct *H desorption features at 0.56, 0.35, and 0.03 VRHE for Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF, and Cu/CF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 54). Notably, Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits the most pronounced anodic peak, indicative of a substantially enriched *H reservoir. To probe the mechanistic role of this reservoir, we introduced tert-butanol (TBA) as a site-blocking agent. The progressive attenuation of the *H-related peak upon TBA addition, coupled with the concomitant suppression of HMF reduction kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 55), provides compelling evidence that surface-adsorbed *H is the indispensable active species driving the hydrogenation, consistent with an L-H pathway (Supplementary Fig. 56).

OCP measurements were used to monitor adsorption behavior changes within the Helmholtz layer after HMF introduction. As shown in Fig. 4h, upon injection of HMF, the OCP of Ami-Cu3N/CF drop markedly by 1.31 V, substantially larger than that of Cu3N/CF (1.01 V) or Cu/CF (0.18 V). This indicates stronger HMF adsorption on Ami-Cu3N/CF, suggesting that the amide-functionalized surface facilitates HMF adsorption, a crucial precondition for its efficient subsequent conversion. To further quantify HMF enrichment on the catalyst surface, quartz crystal microbalance measurements were conducted. Quartz crystal microbalance measurements further quantify this enrichment: upon exposure to 20 mM HMF, Ami-Cu3N/CF exhibits approximately two- and twelve-fold higher mass increases than Cu3N/CF and Cu/CF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 57), confirming the elevated adsorption capacity arising from the amide-functionalized carbon layer. The contact angles for Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF, and Cu/CF are 118.9°, 138.8°, and 147.9°, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 58), further supporting the improved affinity of the amide-functionalized surface toward HMF in aqueous medium. Spectroscopic evidence from concentration-dependent FTIR reveals a systematic red-shift of the amide N–H stretching band upon HMF introduction (Supplementary Fig. 59). This vibrational perturbation confirms the formation of interfacial hydrogen bonds (−NH…O=CHMF), demonstrating that the surface amide groups function as chemical anchors to facilitate the preferential enrichment of HMF. The electrochemical reduction of HMF on Ami-Cu3N/CF is proposed to proceed via the mechanism illustrated in Fig. 4i. Water is activated and dissociated into *H at the Cu/Cu3N heterojunction interface, accompanied by electron transfer; subsequently, two *H species react with the adsorbed carbonyl group of HMF on the amide-functionalized carbon layer, producing DHMF via a L-H pathway. Experimental analyzes reveal that lattice nitrogen in Cu3N strongly promotes water dissociation, accelerating *H generation, while surface amide groups regulate the local interfacial microenvironment through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. This cooperative modulation enhances HMF adsorption and stabilized *H species, collectively demonstrating that the multi-nitrogen coordination environment plays a pivotal role in enabling high ECH activity.

The kinetics of HER and HMF ECH were evaluated using Tafel analysis. As shown in Fig. 5a, the Tafel slopes for HER are 146, 170, and 151 mV dec−1 for Ami-Cu3N/CF, Cu3N/CF, and Cu/CF, respectively, indicating that the Volmer step is the rate-determining step (RDS) for all catalysts64. Upon addition of HMF, the Tafel behavior varies significantly among the samples. For Cu/CF, the slope increases from 151 to 192 mV dec−1, suggesting a less favorable HMF ECH pathway compared to HER. In contrast, Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF exhibit similar Tafel slopes under HMF ECH (118 and 154 mV dec−1), implying that the Volmer step remains the RDS. Isotopic experiments using H2O/D2O electrolytes (Figs. 5b and S60–S62) are conducted to further probe the RDS. For HER, Cu/CF displays a significant kinetic isotope effect (KIE) with a 127 mV cathodic shift at 20 mA cm−2, whereas Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF show smaller shifts (92 and 52 mV), confirming that Cu3N facilitates water dissociation and promotes the Volmer step. During HMF ECH, the KIE values for Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF (119 and 72 mV) closely match those in HER, supporting a L-H mechanism involving adsorbed *H species. In contrast, Cu/CF exhibits a smaller KIE decrease (114 mV) during HMF ECH, implying a shift of the RDS toward a CPET pathway. Reaction-order analysis with respect to HMF concentration further distinguished these mechanisms. For Ami-Cu3N/CF, a negative reaction order (Supplementary Fig. 60) indicates competitive adsorption between HMF and H2O, consistent with the L-H mechanism. Cu3N/CF maintains a negative reaction order up to 100 mM, but the slope approaches 0.56 beyond this concentration, suggesting limited *H availability and a transition toward Eley-Rideal (E-R) behavior. Cu/CF, however, shows a positive order of 0.95, consistent with a predominant E-R pathway. Notably, the concentration-dependent rate analysis in Supplementary Fig. 63 was performed under matched-potential potentiostatic conditions (−0.35 VRHE) for all three catalysts in an H-cell, so that the observed differences in apparent reaction order reflect catalyst-dependent concentration responses under the same electrochemical driving force. Product analysis (Supplementary Fig. 64) reveals that C−C coupling is promoted on Cu/CF and emerges on Cu3N/CF at high HMF concentrations, but is effectively suppressed on Ami-Cu3N/CF, indicating that amide functionalization inhibits radical-mediated coupling and stabilizes the selective L-H hydrogenation pathway65–67.

Operando electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to interrogate the hydrogen supply and consumption dynamics during HMF ECH (Fig. 5c–h, Supplementary Figs. 66–72, Supplementary Tables 10–12)68,69. Relevant reaction steps are summarized in Scheme S2. It should be noted that the assignment of equivalent circuit elements to specific elementary steps (e.g., Volmer or Heyrovsky) is subject to inherent model dependence. Therefore, the circuit model presented here is interpreted as a semi-quantitative framework to deconvolute the impedance features and track the kinetic trends across different potentials, rather than as an absolute mechanistic determination. Under HER conditions, the Bode plots exhibit two distinct relaxation features: a low-frequency response attributed to the relaxation of surface coverage (*H formation/Volmer step) and a mid-frequency feature associated with interfacial charge transfer (Heyrovsky step). To quantify the coupled dynamics, we extracted the characteristic relaxation time constant (τ) from the fitted CPE parameters, serving as a kinetic fingerprint for the coverage-dependent Volmer-to-L-H sequence (Supplementary Fig. 70). Notably, upon HMF introduction, τ is selectively and strongly modulated within the low-overpotential window (−0.10 to −0.30 VRHE) for Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF. This behavior signals a regime where *H generation and its consumption via HMF hydrogenation are tightly coupled, specifically, the *H-mediated L-H step becomes kinetically rate-determining. Consistent with this τ signature, HMF addition predominantly reshapes the medium-to-low frequency impedance response for the nitride-based catalysts (Fig. 5f, g). The fitted resistance parameters indicate that while the *H formation pathway remains robust, the interfacial reduction resistance (Rct) increases in the presence of HMF at low overpotentials (−0.10 to −0.45 VRHE). This effectively implies that the surface *H reservoir is diverted from the HER pathway toward HMF hydrogenation, leading to a suppression of parasitic HER, in line with the enhanced FEDHMF observed (Fig. 3d). In stark contrast, Cu/CF exhibits a negligible τ response and a decrease in resistance upon HMF addition, pointing to a distinct ECH trajectory less reliant on a stable *H reservoir, consistent with a concerted CPET-type mechanism. The significantly lower polarization resistance for Ami-Cu3N/CF relative to its counterparts further corroborates its enhanced capability for facilitating rapid interfacial electron delivery and efficient *H utilization. It follows that HMF reduction proceeds via divergent mechanisms: whereas Ami-Cu3N/CF and Cu3N/CF facilitate HMF hydrogenation through an L-H pathway, the reaction on Cu/CF follows a distinct direct electroreduction mechanism involving CPET, yielding a radical intermediate that may either be hydrogenated to DHMF or participate in C−C coupling (Supplementary Fig. 73). PBS was selected not merely for bulk pH control, but for its enhanced capability in modulating interfacial proton dynamics (Supplementary Figs. 74–78). While control experiments in borate buffer (BBS) resulted in a loss of HMF-response and sluggish kinetics, PBS enables rapid interfacial charge transfer. XPS analysis confirms that phosphate species adsorb at the interface without acting as sacrificial reactants, thereby functioning as a stable proton-management scaffold that sustains the *H coverage required for the coupled L-H mechanism.

Mechanistic studies of HMF ECH to DHMF

Guided by the post-reaction structural characterizations and the experimentally identified self-limited reconstruction behavior, we constructed a static Ami-Cu/Cu3N interfacial model to represent the inferred steady-state active motif, in which amide groups are selectively ligated at the Cu–Cu3N boundary. This model was used to analyze the electronic structure and reaction energetics of the reconstructed interface, rather than to simulate the dynamic reconstruction process itself. Using this representative motif (Supplementary Figs. 79–84 and Supplementary Data 1), we deciphered the cooperative mechanism by which the heterojunction architecture and ligand functionalization synergistically minimize the energy barriers for HMF ECH. The adsorption energies of HMF on Ami-Cu/Cu3N, Ami-Cu3N, Ami-Cu, Cu/Cu3N, Cu3N and Cu were compared. As shown in Fig. 6a, the introduction of lattice-N (Cu3N) alone improves adsorption relative to metallic Cu (−1.81 eV vs. −1.78 eV) by creating polar Cu–Nlatt motifs that enhance basal affinity for the polar HMF substrate. Building on this, amide-functionalized catalysts (Ami-Cu/Cu3N, Ami-Cu3N, Ami-Cu) exhibited significantly more negative adsorption energies (ΔEad = −2.35, −2.11, and −1.83 eV, respectively). This hierarchy confirms a dual-site synergy, that is, lattice-N constructs a receptive active platform, while amide groups function as geometric anchors to maximize substrate capture. Electron localization function analysis revealed localized electrons at unsaturated Cu sites, with Ami-Cu/Cu3N showing the strongest electronic localization (Figs. 6b and S85). This is complemented by significant electron redistribution across the amide-functionalized heterojunction (Fig. 6c), creating a highly active electronic landscape. Further electronic analysis (Supplementary Figs. 86 and 87) reveals that the amide functionalities induce a distinct polarization of the HMF carbonyl bond. Corroborated by Bader charge transfer data, this polarization arises from amide-mediated hydrogen bonding, which geometrically stabilizes the adsorption and electronically pre-activates the substrate for efficient hydrogenation.

Two distinct electron transfer pathways were identified for aldehyde group hydrogenation: the L-H and CPET mechanisms. For the L-H pathway, Ami-Cu/Cu3N exhibited the lowest activation energy (−2.11 eV) for the first hydrogenation step at the oxygen site (Figs. 6d and S88), initiating ECH via formation of an alkyl radical. In the second hydrogenation step, Ami-Cu/Cu3N showed a reaction barrier of 0.69 eV for DHMF* formation, lower than that of Cu/Cu3N (0.76 eV) and significantly lower than Cu3N (1.04 eV) and Cu (1.10 eV). This demonstrates that nitrogen incorporation, through both Cu3N and amide functionalization, synergistically reduced the thermodynamic energy barrier for keto radical hydrogenation. The highest barrier in the second step constitutes the potential-determining step (PDS) for all catalysts. To examine the sensitivity of the reaction thermodynamics to electrode polarization, an auxiliary charged-slab analysis was performed by varying the total electron number in the simulation cell. The methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Information. Cathodic polarization induces a general stabilization of the reaction intermediates (Supplementary Table 14). Crucially, this energetic shift is uniform across the Ami-Cu/Cu3N, Ami-Cu3N, and Ami-Cu models. Consequently, neither the relative stability order of the intermediates nor the identity of PDS is altered by the applied potential (Supplementary Fig. 89). This confirms that the mechanistic conclusions and the activity trends established herein are robust against potential-dependent field effects. Due to keto radical coupling, the L-H mechanism requires a higher barrier for BHH formation, making it more unfavorable on Ami-Cu/Cu3N, consistent with their enhanced ECH efficiency toward DHMF. In contrast, for the CPET pathway (Supplementary Figs. 90 and 91), Cu exhibited lower energy barriers for direct DHMF formation, suggesting Cu prefers CPET, a finding corroborated by experimental results.

The influence of amide functionalization on electronic structure was further probed via density of states (DOS) calculations. As shown in Fig. 6e, the Cu/Cu3N heterostructure exhibits a higher density of Cu 3d states near the Fermi level compared with Cu3N and metallic Cu, indicating enhanced electrical conductivity that facilitates charge transfer during electrocatalysis. Upon amide functionalization (Ami-Cu/Cu3N, Ami-Cu3N, and Ami-Cu), the d-band centers (εd) of all catalysts shift upward toward the Fermi level relative to their unmodified counterparts, suggesting strengthened interactions between the catalysts and adsorbates. Physically, this upshift originates from the amide-induced inductive effect (Supplementary Fig. 87), which creates an electron-deficient environment at the Cu sites. Upon HMF adsorption, the d-band center shifts to lower energy due to orbital stabilization. The Ami-Cu/Cu3N system retains a relatively higher d-band center compared to the control samples, indicating enhanced electronic coupling between adsorbed *HMF and the active sites. Mechanistically, the adsorption of HMF on Cu/Cu3N induces electron transfer and orbital mixing, pushing the anti-bonding states above the Fermi level and increasing the population of unpaired electrons (holes) in the d-band (Fig. 6f). Furthermore, amide functionalization enhances HMF adsorption on the catalyst surface, further optimizing the electronic state of the Ami-Cu/Cu3N system. This modulation facilitates the depopulation of Cu-adsorbate anti-bonding orbitals (labeled as “Less-filling” in Fig. 6f), thereby strengthening intermediate adsorption and stabilizing the catalytic interface. Ami-Cu/Cu3N also possesses a decisive electronic advantage for hydrogen activation and supply. Lattice-N sites serve as the primary reservoir for *H generation, and they are electronically tuned by the neighboring amide groups. Although Amide-N does not act as an independent hydrogen-activation site, it exerts a long-range electronic modulation that upshifts the interfacial Cu d-states. This strengthens the Cu−H interaction at adjacent Cu–Nlatt motifs, facilitating water activation. Consequently, the Gibbs free energy for H adsorption (ΔG*H) is optimized to 1.34 eV on Ami-Cu/Cu3N, enhanced to both pristine Cu3N (1.67 eV) and Cu (1.76 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 92). Collectively, these findings elucidate a sophisticated dual-nitrogen synergy. The lattice-N skeleton establishes a fundamental polar thermodynamic platform for basal reactivity, which is distinctively potentiated by amide-N functionalities. Acting as a bifunctional modulator, the amide group orchestrates a concerted mechanism, simultaneously locking the HMF substrate via geometric hydrogen bonding and fine-tuning the electronic structure to optimize *H supply, thereby minimizing the energetic span for efficient ECH.

Discussion

In summary, a heterogeneous catalyst, denoted as Ami-Cu3N/CF, featuring surface amide functionalization on a Cu3N substrate, was successfully developed. This catalyst achieves both high FE and ECH activity under elevated HMF concentrations. The synergistic interaction between copper nitride and the amide groups enhances the efficient generation of *H and targeted HMF adsorption, thereby finely tuning the local microenvironment during the ECH process. Notably, the Ami-Cu3N/CF catalyst accomplishes HMF electroreduction with a FE exceeding 98% at an operating potential of merely −26 mV, and even at an HMF concentration of 1000 mM, the FE for DHMF remains above 90%. Moreover, the dual-electrode flow electrolytic cell lowers the cell voltage and energy consumption, thereby improving the economic feasibility of biomass upgrading and demonstrating significant practical potential. This work provides a foundational understanding and a design blueprint for cooperative active sites, paving the way for advanced electrocatalytic systems in renewable chemical synthesis.

Electrode preparation

Pretreatment of the Cu foam (CF)

The purchased Cu foam (CF) was firstly cut into 3 × 4 cm2. The CF was sonicated in acetone for 15 min to remove impurities on the surface. After washing with water, the CF was transferred into the 0.5 M HCl for 1 min to remove the oxide layers on the surface, rinsed with distilled water for several times to remove any additional HCl.

Synthesis of Cu(OH)2/CF

Cu(OH)2 nanowire arrays were grown on Cu foam by surface chemical oxidation in an alkaline medium. The Cu(OH)2/CF were fabricated by immersing the thoroughly cleaned Cu foam into a solution containing 0.12 M NaOH and 0.01 M ammonium persulfate ((NH4)2S2O8) at 5 °C temperature for 30 min without disturbance. The chemically oxidized Cu(OH)2/CF were gently rinsed with DI water and then dried at room temperature.

Synthesis of Cu/CF

The contrast Cu/CF was obtained by heating the as-formed Cu(OH)2/CF at 400 °C for 3 h in H2 atmosphere at a ramping rate of 2 °C min−1.

Synthesis of Cu3N/CF

The contrast Cu3N/CF was obtained by heating the as-formed Cu(OH)2/CF at 325 °C for 2 h in NH3 atmosphere at a ramping rate of 5 °C min−1.

Synthesis of BTC-Cu/CF

The octahedral BTC-Cu particles, used as MOF precursors, were synthesized according to previous research with some modifications. Briefly, a 3 × 4 cm2 Cu(OH)2/CF was placed in 500 mL of methanol solution containing 8.6 g C6H3(COOH)3 and kept at room temperature for 12 h. The resulting BTC-Cu/CF washed several times with methanol and finally dried in an oven at 60 °C.

Synthesis of Ami-Cu3N/CF

The contrast Ami-Cu3N/CF was obtained by heating the as-formed BTC-Cu/CF at 325 °C for 2 h in NH3 atmosphere at a ramping rate of 5 °C min−1. The catalyst loading on CF was determined by gravimetric analysis before and after catalyst growth, yielding an average metal-containing catalyst (Supplementary Table 2).

Electrode preparation

The as-prepared Cu-based materials were directly used as self-supported electrodes without additional binders. The geometric area of the working electrode was precisely defined (typically 1 cm2 unless otherwise specified). Catalyst loading was determined based on the mass difference before and after synthesis.

Characterizations

The morphology and phase composition of as prepared catalysts were studied by SEM (Hitachi S-4800 at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV), TEM (JEM-2100 at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV), and XRD (Bruke D8 diffractometer using Cu Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å) radiation with accelerating voltage 40 kV). The chemical state was characterized by XPS, performed on VG ESCALAB MK II using a Mg Kα (1253.6 eV) achromatic X-ray radiation. Center of gravity calculation for the valence band spectra is conducted as ∫N(ε)εdε∫N(ε)dε in the range −5~10 eV, where N(ε) is the density of states. The BET equation was used to calculate the specific surface area. The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests of radicals were carried out on the Bruker EMX plus model spectrometer. The Cu K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy was collected in the transmission mode using inhouse laboratory-based X-ray absorption spectrometer (Super XAFS). All XAFS data were analyzed using the program Demeter70. For all samples, the EXAFS oscillations were extracted from the normalized XAFS spectra by subtracting the atomic background using a cubic spline fit to k3-weighted data, where k is the photoelectron wave number. The χ(k) functions were then Fourier transformed into R-space. The Fourier-transform window was in the k range 3–11 Å−1. Cu, CuO, Cu2O and Cu3N single crystals were measured simultaneously for energy calibration of Cu K-edge. Soft XAS were collected at the Taiwan Light Source (TLS) beamline BL-11A of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC). The N-K spectra were obtained using the total electron yield (TEY) mode. Scanning Kelvin probe (SKP) measurement (SKP5050 system, Scotland) was performed in ambient atmosphere, and a gold electrode was used as the reference electrode. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Pyris Diamond thermogravimetric/differential thermal analyzer (TG/DTA) at a constant dry inert atmosphere flow rate of 10 °C min−1. In-situ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurement utilized a hemicylindrical CaF2, and the spectra were collected by Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 FTIR. FTIR optical accessory was produced by Shanghai Linglu Co. Ltd. Quasi-in-situ XRD, XPS and FTIR were conducted to investigate the structural and chemical evolution of catalysts after HMF ECH. In detail, the electrode after electrolysis for different HMF ECH time was immediately removed from the cell, rinsed with deionized water or ethanol to remove residual electrolyte and adsorbed species, and dried under inert gas flow prior to characterization. The total air exposure time was minimized to suppress additional oxidation or reconstruction. While slight surface oxidation during transfer cannot be completely excluded, the dominant features observed by quasi-in-situ XRD and XPS are considered representative of the reconstructed catalyst state established under reaction conditions. The content of Cu leaching after HMF ECH was determined by an inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES).

Theoretical calculations

All the DFT calculations were performed by the projector-augmented-wave method implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP). The generalized gradient approximation of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof was applied to describe the exchange-correlation potential. The energy cutoff for the plane wave was set as 400 eV. The conjugate gradient algorithm was employed to predict the lowest energy position based on the energy and strain changes at each step. The convergence thresholds for optimization were 1 × 10−5 eV in energy and 0.01 eV Å−1 in force. Monkhorst–Pack mesh was used for k-space samplings with set as 3 × 3 × 3 k-points sampled for the optimization of all structures and 3 × 3 × 1 k-points sampled for the computation of electronic structure. The water solvation effect was also considered by using VASPsol. Transition states and energy barriers were calculated using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (Cl-NEB) method.

DFT calculations

The climbing-image nudged elastic band (Cl-NEB) is a method for finding saddle points and minimum energy paths between known reactants and products. The method operates by optimizing a series of intermediate images along the reaction pathway. Each image finds the lowest energy possible while maintaining equal spacing to neighboring images. This constrained optimization is done by adding spring forces along the band between images and by projecting out the component of the force due to the potential perpendicular to the band.

Construction of DFT models

To elucidate the reaction mechanism at the atomic scale, the catalyst models were constructed to represent the experimentally inferred steady-state surface motifs identified by post-reaction characterizations (XRD, HRTEM, and XPS), rather than to simulate the dynamic electrochemical reconstruction process itself. Specifically, the heterostructure model was built by interfacing a Cu(111) slab with a Cu3N(100) slab to capture the local Cu/Cu3N boundary formed after the self-limited reconstruction observed experimentally. Pristine Cu3N and Cu were modeled using stoichiometric Cu3N(100) and Cu(111) surfaces, respectively, corresponding to the primary crystallographic facets identified by XRD. Based on the experimental evidence that amide groups act as persistent surface ligands under reaction conditions, amide molecules were placed selectively at the Cu–Cu3N phase boundary to represent the local anchored configuration inferred for the reconstructed interface. This interfacial model was therefore used as a static, experimentally constrained descriptor of the post-reconstruction active motif for analyzing electronic modulation, adsorption behavior, and reaction energetics. A vacuum layer of 15 Å was applied to avoid periodic interactions, and the bottom atomic layers were fixed while the interface and adsorbates were fully relaxed.

Physical basis and implementation of the charged-slab approach

To evaluate the sensitivity of reaction thermodynamics to electrode polarization, we performed auxiliary calculations using the charged-slab approach. The physical basis and computational details are as follows.

The electrode potential (U) in an electrochemical cell is fundamentally linked to the electronic chemical potential (μe) of the interface. In our DFT model, we modulate μe by explicitly varying the total number of electrons (Nelect) in the simulation cell. This process shifts the Fermi level (EF) and the work function (Ф) of the slab, effectively mimicking the charging of the electrode surface under bias: U ∝ Ф/e + constant. Calculations were performed by adjusting the NELECT parameter (e.g., NELECT = NELECT0 + 0.5 e or +1.0 e). To maintain the requirements of periodic boundary conditions and avoid electrostatic divergence, a uniform compensating background charge (Jellium background) was automatically introduced by VASP. This background charge serves as a first-order approximation for the counter-ions in the electric double layer (EDL), ensuring that the perturbation arises solely from the modification of the surface electronic state.

We emphasize that this charged slab approach does not rigorously correspond to a constant-potential electrochemical model, nor does it explicitly account for solvent or electric double-layer effects. In addition, as is well known for charged slab calculations under periodic boundary conditions, the total energies may exhibit slow convergence with respect to vacuum thickness due to interactions with the compensating background charge. Therefore, the present analysis is intended as a semi-quantitative sensitivity probe, aimed at assessing whether moderate surface charging can qualitatively alter the relative stability of reaction intermediates and the identity of the potential-determining step, rather than providing quantitatively accurate potential-dependent energetics.
