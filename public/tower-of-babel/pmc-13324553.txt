Evaluation of the protective effect of direct peritoneal resuscitation on lung injury in a controlled hemorrhagic shock model in rats
Authors: Zafer Akıncı, Özhan Özcan, Kıvanç Öncü, Yunus Katırcı, Ömer Rıdvan Tarhan, İsmail Sarbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are severe complications following hemorrhagic shock, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Direct peritoneal resuscitation (DPR) has been proposed to improve microcirculation and reduce organ damage, but its effects on lung injury have not yet been fully explored. Does direct peritoneal resuscitation with peritoneal dialysis fluid (PDF) reduce lung injury in a controlled hemorrhagic shock model in rats? In this randomized experimental study, 32 male Wistar albino rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 per group). Group I served as the control group, while Groups II, III, and IV underwent hemorrhagic shock. Group III received peritoneal resuscitation with saline, and Group IV received PDF. Lung tissue samples were harvested after 24 h to assess histopathological damage and inflammatory markers; Interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels. DPR with PDF significantly attenuated lung injury compared to saline-treated or hemorrhagic shock-only groups. Interstitial polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNL) infiltration and alveolar septal thickening were reduced in the DPR group. Additionally, IL-6 levels were elevated in the DPR group, suggesting a potentially enhanced localized inflammatory response, while no significant differences were found in IL-10 and TNF-α levels. Direct peritoneal resuscitation with PDF was effective in reducing lung injury in rats subjected to hemorrhagic shock by improving microcirculatory function and modulating the inflammatory response. However, the elevated IL-6 levels suggest further investigation is needed to understand the long-term implications of this inflammatory response.

Introduction

Shock is a life-threatening condition characterized by systemic dysfunction in the circulatory system, leading to inadequate organ perfusion and oxygenation1. This results in a failure to meet the body’s metabolic demands, with clinical manifestations such as hypotension, hypothermia, oliguria, hyperlactatemia, and disturbances in various inflammatory markers, including interleukin-10 (IL-10), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), caspase-3, and interleukin-6 (IL-6)1–3. Restoring adequate tissue perfusion as quickly as possible is essential to prevent irreversible tissue injury and organ failure. This requires effective reestablishment of microcirculatory hemodynamics during acute shock management4.

The standard treatment for hypovolemic or distributive shock involves intravascular volume replacement with intravenous administration of crystalloid solutions and blood products4,5. However, prolonged hypoperfusion, even when corrected, can induce a severe systemic inflammatory response. This is particularly damaging to splanchnic organs, such as the intestine and liver, which are highly susceptible to ischemia-reperfusion injury4–7. Persistent vasoconstriction in the intestinal microcirculation can exacerbate hypoperfusion and ischemia, leading to further organ damage despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are major complications associated with hemorrhagic shock. ALI is characterized by respiratory insufficiency, presenting with tachypnea, cyanosis, decreased lung compliance, and diffuse alveolar infiltrates on chest X-ray8. ARDS is a critical early complication following resuscitation, characterized by non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, increased capillary permeability, and hypoxemia, which collectively contribute to oxygen deficiency and respiratory acidosis8,9. The condition is exacerbated by systemic hypoperfusion and intestinal vasoconstriction, which predispose the lungs to progressive dysfunction and eventual failure10–12. The incidence of ARDS in severely injured trauma patients remains high, with mortality rates often ranging between 10% and 50%, causing long-term physical and cognitive impairment in survivors13,14. Biomarkers, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, play a critical role in the pathophysiology of ALI/ARDS and can help in diagnosing, risk stratifying, and targeting therapies15–18.

Direct peritoneal resuscitation (DPR) with a hypertonic peritoneal dialysis (PD) solution has been shown to improve microcirculation and prevent vasoconstriction in the intestines during hemorrhagic shock19 Studies have demonstrated that hypertonic PD solutions induce visceral vasodilation, unlike isotonic solutions, suggesting that DPR could preserve blood flow to vital organs under conditions of hypoperfusion20. DPR has been demonstrated to improve microcirculation in experimental hemorrhagic shock models, and it shows promise for clinical use in patients with hemorrhagic or septic shock, as well as in those requiring an open abdomen after damage control laparotomy21.

The aim of this study is to investigate the hypothesis that direct peritoneal resuscitation with peritoneal dialysis fluid (PDF) will reduce lung damage following hemorrhagic shock in rats subjected to a hemorrhagic shock model.

Materials and methods

All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Süleyman Demirel University Faculty of Medicine (18.10.2010/08). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Sample size was determined by an a priori power analysis and further justified by sample sizes reported in comparable experimental studies.

Animal preparation and randomization

A total of 32 male Wistar albino rats, weighing between 200 and 300 g and aged 16 weeks, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Research Center of Süleyman Demirel University, Turkey, and were included in this study. Prior to the experiment, the rats were maintained under standardized conditions, including a temperature-controlled environment (22–25 °C), humidity (55% ± 5%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The animals had ad libitum access to standard rat chow and water.

The rats were allocated randomly into four groups (n = 8 per group) using a simple randomization method. Each rat was numbered from 1 to 32, and these numbers were sealed into opaque envelopes. A staff member, blinded to the experimental groups, drew numbers to assign the rats to their respective groups.

Anesthesia and induction of hemorrhagic shock

The rats were administered anesthesia with 90 mg/kg of intraperitoneal ketamine hydrochloride and 10 mg/kg of xylazine to ensure adequate anesthesia and analgesia. Subsequently, a tail vein cannula was inserted into each animal for fluid administration.

After stabilization for 30 min, a vertical cervical incision was made. The common carotid artery was carefully isolated and cannulated for arterial monitoring.

Hemorrhagic shock was established by gradually withdrawing blood (2 mL every 3 min) from the carotid artery catheter, using a 2-mL syringe prefilled with 0.2 mL of ACDA solution. Blood withdrawal was performed until the mean arterial pressure (MAP) dropped to 35–40 mmHg, which typically required about 13 min22.

At the end of the experimental protocol, the rats were humanely euthanized by decapitation under deep anesthesia, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and ARRIVE 2.0 recommendations.

Lung tissue collection and biochemical analysis

Twenty-four hours after resuscitation, a median sternotomy was performed to expose the thoracic cavity, and both lungs were resected for analysis. The right lung was placed in 10% formaldehyde for histopathological examination, while the left lung was stored at −80 °C in Eppendorf tubes for biochemical analysis. Measurements were performed according to the instructions using appropriate rat kits for IL-6 (ELISA, Raybiotech, Catalog No. ELR-IL6-001, Georgia, USA), IL-10 (ELISA, Invitrogen, Catalog No. KRC0101, Nivelles, Belgium), and TNF-α (ELISA, Invitrogen, Catalog No. KRC 3011, Nivelles, Belgium). Parameter measurements were conducted at the Microbiology Laboratory of SDU Faculty of Medicine.

Histopathological examination of lung tissue samples

Lung tissue samples were taken and subjected to routine processing before being embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 5 micrometers were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined under a light microscope. The examination was performed in a blinded manner by a single pathologist at the Pathology Laboratory of SDU Faculty of Medicine. The lung tissue samples of the groups were classified based on the four-point scale (0–3 scoring system), assessing interstitial PMNL infiltration, intra-alveolar PMNL infiltration, and alveolar septal thickness, ranging from normal lung tissue to severe lung tissue damage (Table 1)23–25.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed with SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, VERSION 20.0,Armonk, NY; IBM Corp.). Conformity to the normal distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables according to groups. Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the non-normally distributed data. ANOVA was used to compare the means of three or more groups when the data is normally distributed, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests where appropriate. Analysis results were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum – maximum) for quantitative data and as frequency (percent) for categorical data. While the mean value was based on the independent two-sample t-test and repeated analysis of variance, the median values were based on the Mann-Whitney U and Friedman tests. Level of significance was considered as P < 0.05.

Results

The levels of IL-10, IL-6, and TNF-α, as immune biomarkers, were compared across the groups. There were no statistically significant differences in IL-10 levels between the groups, indicating that the anti-inflammatory response remained consistent across treatments (Fig. 3a).

IL-6 levels in the Hemorrhagic Shock (HS) group were lower than the Control group, but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.069). The HS and Saline Fluid Resuscitation (SF) groups showed similar IL-6 levels (p = 0.45). However, IL-6 levels were significantly higher in the DPR group compared to both the HS group (p < 0.001) and the SF group (p < 0.001), suggesting a potentially enhanced localized inflammatory response following DPR treatment (Fig. 3b).

TNF-α levels were slightly elevated in the DPR group compared to the HS group, although this increase was not statistically significant (p = 0.174). No significant differences in TNF-α levels were observed between the other groups (Fig. 3c).

Lung tissue samples were examined histopathologically to assess the extent of lung injury, including interstitial PMNL infiltration, intra-alveolar PMNL infiltration, and alveolar septal thickening.

The HS group exhibited significantly higher interstitial PMNL infiltration compared to the Control group (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the HS and SF groups (p = 0.668). However, the DPR group demonstrated a significantly lower infiltration score compared to both the HS group (p = 0.009) and the SF group (p = 0.049), indicating a protective effect of DPR on lung tissue (Fig. 4a).

The HS group had a significantly higher intra-alveolar PMNL infiltration score compared to the Control group (p = 0.013). There were no significant differences between the SF and DPR groups in terms of intra-alveolar infiltration, suggesting that DPR did not notably affect this parameter (Fig. 4b).

Alveolar septal thickness was significantly increased in the HS group compared to the Control group (p < 0.001), indicating structural alterations in the lung tissue due to shock. The DPR group showed a marked reduction in alveolar septal thickening compared to both the HS group (p = 0.003) and the SF group (p = 0.003). This finding suggests that DPR may alleviate septal thickening and potentially improve lung function in the context of hemorrhagic shock (Fig. 4c).

Overall, histopathological assessments confirmed that DPR mitigated lung injury in rats subjected to hemorrhagic shock. The Control group displayed no significant PMNL infiltration or increase in alveolar septal thickness, consistent with healthy lung tissue. Both the HS group and the Saline-treated group exhibited intense PMNL infiltration and increased septal thickness, indicating significant lung injury. In contrast, the DPR group demonstrated mild PMNL infiltration and no increase in alveolar septal thickness, underscoring the protective effect of DPR on lung tissue (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, a controlled hemorrhagic shock model was established in rats, resulting in significant histopathological lung injury. The findings demonstrate that peritoneal resuscitation using PDF significantly reduced lung injury in these animals, while lavage with an equivalent amount of normal saline and intravenous Ringer’s lactate solution did not provide similar protection.

Clinically, ARDS remains a critical early complication following resuscitation from hemorrhagic shock, contributing to severe oxygen deficiency and respiratory acidosis26. This secondary pulmonary compromise is heavily driven by intestinal vasoconstriction and splanchnic hypoperfusion, which systemically predispose the lungs to progressive failure27. The clinical burden of this cascade is substantial, as mortality rates in severely injured trauma patients range between 10% and 50%28. Preventing this progression is therefore vital, and as our histopathological evaluations demonstrate, this secondary damage is largely mediated by local inflammatory cell recruitment.

Previous studies have shown that PMNL infiltration in lung tissue is a significant determinant of ARDS following hemorrhagic shock29. In the present model, a statistically significant increase in interstitial and intra-alveolar PMNL infiltration, alveolar septal thickness, and cumulative lung injury scores was found in the hemorrhagic shock group compared to controls, confirming the occurrence of ARDS.

PDF induces visceral vasodilation due to its hypertonicity and the presence of lactate, glucose, and glucose degradation products30. Studies examining the microcirculatory effects of PDF have shown rapid dilation of visceral arterioles upon exposure to hypertonic fluid, which is not observed with isotonic solutions31. These findings suggest that hypertonic solution infusion into the abdomen during states of low intestinal blood flow may help maintain perfusion to vital organs. This resuscitation technique, known as DPR, has been shown to preserve endothelial cell function and prevent significant visceral edema compared to conventional resuscitation methods32. However, translating this adjunctive therapy to broader clinical practice presents a significant limitation. Existing clinical literature and case reports on DPR predominantly involve trauma patients who already require an open abdomen or have an intra-abdominal drain in place following damage control laparotomy21. In instances of hemorrhagic shock originating from extra-abdominal sources (e.g., severe extremity or thoracic trauma), the clinical justification, safety, and ethical viability of inserting an abdominal drain solely to initiate DPR warrant rigorous consideration, as the procedural risks may outweigh the microcirculatory benefits.

In our study, DPR with PDF resulted in significantly improved histopathological outcomes compared to standard fluid resuscitation. Specifically, interstitial PMNL density and alveolar septal thickness were significantly lower in the DPR group. However, it must be acknowledged as a limitation that lung injury severity was evaluated relying solely on semiquantitative histopathological scoring systems. Although these structural assessments are critical, the absence of physiological respiratory data, such as arterial blood gas analysis or ventilatory mechanics, limits our ability to confirm whether this structural preservation translates to functional pulmonary improvement. Furthermore, the relatively limited sample size inherent to controlled animal experiments restricts external validity.

Hemorrhagic shock and subsequent resuscitation often lead to ARDS, which is mediated by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)33. Inflammatory products from gut or liver leukocytes, such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and other cytokines, further contribute to ARDS progression34.

An interesting finding of the present study was the increased IL-6 levels observed in the DPR group, while TNF-α levels did not differ significantly between groups. The observed elevation of IL-6 is likely multifactorial. On one hand, it is consistent with previous findings indicating that pro-inflammatory mediators are key players in transfusion-related immune modulation following hemorrhagic shock. Elevated plasma levels of IL-6 and IL-8 have been identified as significant markers in distinguishing pulmonary transfusion reactions, such as TRALI, from other complications35, and high IL-6 concentrations are negatively associated with the efficacy of red blood cell transfusions36. In this context, the initial hemorrhagic event may act as a ‘first insult’ that creates a pre-existing inflammatory state, increasing susceptibility to immune activation upon the introduction of blood products. On the other hand, specifically in the context of DPR, intraperitoneal fluid administration itself may transiently stimulate inflammatory signaling within the peritoneal microenvironment, further contributing to the increased circulating IL-6 levels.

Crucially, despite these elevated IL-6 levels—which are traditionally associated with adverse pulmonary outcomes—the DPR group in our study demonstrated improved histopathological lung findings. This divergence suggests that IL-6 elevation during the early phase of injury may not be purely harmful. Instead, it may reflect an adaptive, context-dependent response to tissue stress and immune activation, contributing to immune regulation and tissue repair rather than propagating acute lung injury37,38. However, since measurements were obtained only at a single 24-hour time point, the temporal dynamics of these cytokine responses remain unclear, and longer observational studies are required to fully elucidate this mechanism.

Despite this, the overall protective mechanism of DPR may still involve improved splanchnic microcirculation, leading to reduced systemic inflammatory mediator release, and potentially a dilutional effect of PDF on local cytokine concentrations; however, further studies are needed to clarify these mechanisms. IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, showed no significant differences between groups, indicating that DPR’s protective effects may primarily be driven by mechanisms other than anti-inflammatory cytokine upregulation, such as microcirculatory preservation and reduced neutrophilic infiltration. Because our analysis was limited to a 24-hour observation period, the longer-term effects of DPR on pulmonary injury and systemic inflammation remain uncertain and should be explored in future studies.

Future studies should prioritize incorporating physiological respiratory measurements, such as arterial blood gas analysis and lung compliance tests, to validate functional outcomes. Furthermore, investigations spanning longer observation periods with serial cytokine sampling and larger experimental cohorts are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the temporal inflammatory dynamics and translational potential of DPR.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the inherent limitations of an animal model dictate that our findings cannot be directly generalized to human clinical practice. Second, our experimental design utilized a single 24-hour observation window. Consequently, the long-term temporal dynamics of cytokine responses remain unclear, contributing to the uncertainty regarding the interpretation of elevated IL-6 levels as either a purely harmful inflammatory marker or an adaptive tissue response. Third, the study focused primarily on histopathological and biochemical parameters, lacking physiological respiratory measurements or functional respiratory outcomes, which are essential to fully evaluate the clinical therapeutic benefits of DPR on lung function. Fourth, despite adherence to the principles of Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement (3Rs), the relatively limited sample size inherent to controlled animal experiments restricts the statistical power and external validity of our findings. Finally, regarding clinical applicability, the justification and feasibility of abdominal drain insertion for DPR in hemodynamically unstable patients require careful evaluation, as such invasive procedures during hemorrhagic shock carry inherent risks. Despite these limitations, our findings provide a valuable foundation for future translational research.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this exploratory animal study, it can be concluded that peritoneal resuscitation using PDF significantly reduces structural lung injury in a controlled hemorrhagic shock model in rats. The reduction in histopathological lung damage, as evidenced by lower interstitial PMNL density and decreased alveolar septal thickness, suggests that DPR plays a protective role in mitigating acute lung injury. Despite the elevated IL-6 levels in the lung tissue, DPR appears to attenuate the progression of structural lung damage, although the mechanisms underlying this effect remain uncertain. These findings suggest that DPR may reduce structural lung injury following hemorrhagic shock in this experimental model; however, further studies integrating physiological respiratory outcomes and longer observation periods are required before translational conclusions can be drawn. The protective mechanism of DPR may involve improved splanchnic microcirculation, leading to reduced systemic inflammatory mediator release, and potentially a dilutional effect of PDF on local cytokine concentrations; however, further studies are needed to clarify these mechanisms.

This study supports the potential of DPR as a viable adjunctive treatment in managing ARDS following hemorrhagic shock. However, further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying these effects, particularly the role of inflammatory cytokines and the long-term implications of DPR on pulmonary function. Additionally, translating these findings from animal models to clinical practice will require careful consideration of the differences between species and the complex nature of human trauma and shock.
