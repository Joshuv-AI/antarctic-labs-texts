The effect of peer-supported mobile orientation on nursing students’ clinical stress and sense of belonging: an experimental study
Authors: Yeter Kurt, Çiğdem Gamze Özkan, Çiğdem Yalçin, Aleyna Semiz Büyükaltay, Sümeyye Atalar, Ayvaz Büyükaltay, Havva Öztürk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical practice environments are a major source of anxiety and stress, especially for nursing students who are experiencing their first clinical experience. Peer Mentoring reduces the stress associated with early clinical experiences and helps students overcome challenges by developing a sense of belonging and participation.AimThe study aimed to assess the impact of peer support, delivered through a mobile orientation application, on nursing students’ clinical stress and sense of belonging during their clinical practice.MethodThe study was an experimental study with a control group and was conducted with a total of 89 s-year nursing students (43 experimental and 46 control) who were interning at a university hospital in Trabzon. Data were collected in October-December 2022 using the student information form, the student opinion form, the Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students, and the Perceived Insider Status Scale as pre-test and post-test. In the study, the HEMMEN mobile application was utilized for the experimental group, while the control group followed the routine method.ResultThere was no statistically significant difference between the pre-test scores of the experimental and control groups regarding the stress and belonging levels they perceived before the internship (p > 0.05). The post-test scores of the experimental group regarding the stress levels perceived after the internship were statistically higher than the control group (p < 0.05). The belonging levels perceived after the internship were evaluated, and no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups (p > 0.05). Additionally, the experimental group reported satisfaction with the HEMMEN application, stating that it was easy to use, facilitated relationships in the clinic and that the peer counselors were understanding, supportive, and provided helpful feedback.ConclusionThe HEMMEN application effectively reduced the stress levels of nursing students and served as a valuable tool in enhancing their clinical experiences.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04043-3.

Introduction

Clinical practice in nursing education provides students with the opportunity to tackle real-world challenges, integrate theoretical knowledge from coursework, and apply it to patient care, all while refining their psychomotor and intellectual abilities [1]. Moreover, clinical experiences are widely regarded as a fundamental component of nursing education, contributing to both the development of practical skills and the establishment of a professional identity [2].

From the start of their education, nursing students encounter stressors that impact their academic performance and overall quality of life [3]. Clinical practice environments, although essential for the development of professional knowledge and skills, are also considered significant sources of stress [4]. Research has identified clinical practice settings as a major source of anxiety and stress, particularly for nursing students undergoing their first clinical experience [5, 6]. Indeed, studies assessing the stress levels of nursing students during clinical practice have reported that those entering clinical practice for the first time experience moderate to high stress levels [7, 8].

Stress is a significant psychosocial factor influencing students’ academic performance and overall well-being during their education [9]. The presence and intensity of stress can impact learning and cognition either positively or negatively. While moderate stress levels can motivate students, excessive stress may hinder learning, impair reasoning and abstract thinking, and lead to academic failure [5, 7]. Research in this area has shown that high stress levels negatively affect students’ academic success and make it difficult for them to assess patient needs accurately [6, 10]. Students often experience high stress on their first day of clinical practice [7], which can lead to anxiety, migraines, and other stress-related symptoms [11]. Several factors have been identified as contributors to increased stress, including fear of making mistakes, harming patients, facing negative feedback, high expectations from instructors, adjusting to hospital routines, and a lack of self-confidence [4, 7].

One of the most significant factors influencing the integration and acceptance of new nursing students in clinical settings is the sense of “belonging” [12]. Belonging, a crucial element for acceptance and adaptation, refers to an individual’s perception of being part of an institution [13]. Developing a sense of belonging within a professional group in the clinical environment plays a vital role in shaping students’ nursing identity [14]. Research has shown that students who feel a sense of belonging experience healthier social and cognitive development, which helps to reduce stress and anxiety [11, 15], improve relationships with healthcare staff [16, 17], and boost self-esteem [16], confidence, motivation [18, 19], academic success [20] and overall satisfaction [21]. In contrast, students who lack a sense of belonging often exhibit decreased motivation, lower self-confidence, and impaired learning in the clinical environment [12]. Recent studies highlight the effectiveness of mobile-based peer mentoring programs in reducing clinical stress and enhancing students’ sense of belonging, particularly in hybrid and online learning environments [22–24].

In complex and dynamic clinical settings, students may struggle to accurately identify patient needs, which can undermine patient trust and negatively affect the nurse-patient relationship, ultimately hindering the effectiveness of nursing services [10]. It is well documented that nursing students often report clinical practice as the most stressful aspect of their education. Many students, particularly those entering the clinical environment for the first time, frequently experience intense anxiety related to fears of making mistakes, harming patients, or receiving negative feedback. In this context, it is clear that nursing students must adapt to the clinical education environment to achieve effective learning outcomes [25]. Current research emphasizes that structured peer mentoring and digital support tools can mitigate stress and facilitate integration into clinical environments, even during disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic [26–28]. However, research on stress factors and coping strategies reveals that the factors influencing students’ ability to cope with stress are often underemphasized [29]. This highlights the need for increased support to help students manage clinical stress and foster a sense of belonging. Identifying the levels and causes of stress that students experience on their first day and throughout clinical practice, as well as providing guidance through strategies to manage this stress [30], are essential steps in reducing stress in the clinical setting. Mentoring programs, among the methods of guiding in clinical settings [24], have also been shown to reduce stress for nursing students. At this stage, peer mentoring—a support system based on shared responsibility and mutual benefit—can be particularly effective in helping students orient themselves to the clinical environment and reduce clinical stress. Peer support encourages individuals to challenge themselves while learning to cope with others. Through the social and emotional relationships established with peers, students can better understand themselves and others, thereby improving their ability to handle stress and adapt to school and clinical environments. The most important role of peer mentors is to guide and encourage nursing students as they navigate challenges in clinical practice and peer support is often more effective than support provided by instructors [31].

Peer support, which encourages students to take responsibility for their learning and motivates them, can be provided face-to-face and online [32]. In educational environments like schools and hospitals, it is not always possible for students to always communicate face-to-face with their peers. With the integration of new technologies, e-mentoring offers a flexible alternative, and sometimes a complementary option, to traditional face-to-face mentoring [33]. Mobile communication tools, which offer a flexible environment independent of time and location, can provide real-time guidance and feedback during clinical practice. Peer support delivered through online platforms, such as phones, computers, and mobile applications, allows students to receive instant feedback on areas where they may need further clarification [34].

Mentoring, successfully applied in nursing education, is highly beneficial for students receiving peer support [35]. It helps first-year students overcome challenges by fostering a greater sense of belonging and participation [36], bridging the gap between theory and practice, and reducing the stress associated with early clinical experiences [37]. Despite the critical role of clinical practice in nursing education, first-year students frequently face high levels of stress and struggle to develop a sense of belonging in clinical environments, which can negatively affect their learning, adaptation, and professional identity formation. Although traditional peer mentoring programs are beneficial, they are often limited by time, location, and mentor availability. There is a scarcity of research evaluating mobile e-mentoring applications as flexible, real-time support tools for first-year nursing students, particularly during short clinical internships. This study therefore aims to address this gap by investigating whether a peer-supported mobile application can effectively reduce clinical stress and enhance students’ sense of belonging, providing evidence for innovative strategies to improve early clinical experiences in nursing education. This study was designed to evaluate the impact of peer support provided through a mobile orientation application on nursing students’ clinical stress and their sense of belonging during clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Type of research

The research is an experimental study with a control group.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of second-year students enrolled in the Nursing Department at a Faculty of Health Sciences located in the Eastern Black Sea region during the 2021–2022 academic year (N = 178). Second-year students, who were participating in their first clinical practice due to the pandemic, attended the Internal Medicine and Surgical Nursing courses for hands-on clinical experience in the hospital setting. A power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size for the study. With a 95% confidence interval, a 5% significance level, and an estimated effect size of 0.671, it was determined that at least 43 students would be needed in the experimental group and 46 in the control group, resulting in a total of 89 s-year nursing students. To prevent interaction between groups, students were pragmatically assigned to either the experimental group (43 students participating in internal medicine clinical practice) or the control group (46 students participating in surgical clinical practice). No formal randomization method was employed due to technical constraints of the mobile application, the need to maximize participation during the pandemic, and limitations in available devices, which constitutes a potential selection bias. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and instructors was not possible, which is another limitation of the study.

Inclusion criteria for the study required students to be enrolled in the second-year Internal Medicine Nursing or Surgical Diseases Nursing courses, both of which involved their first clinical practice due to the pandemic. Additionally, participants needed to have an Android smartphone compatible with the HEMMEN application (version 4.0 or higher).

Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate in the study, enrollment through a vertical transfer exam, lack of fluency in Turkish as a native language, and previous completion of the Internal Medicine or Surgical Diseases Nursing courses.

Based on these criteria, a pre-survey was conducted with students from both Group A and Group B to assign them to either the experimental or control group. A preliminary questionnaire was administered to identify students who owned mobile phones with Android operating systems version 4.0 or higher, which was required for the use of the HEMMEN application. The questionnaire was distributed and collected manually. Based on the results, it was determined that students enrolled in the internal medicine nursing course had a higher number of Android phones, making them suitable for inclusion in the experimental group. It should be noted that no formal randomization method was employed, which introduces a potential selection bias. This pragmatic allocation was necessary due to technical constraints of the mobile application, the need to maximize participation during the pandemic, and limitations in available devices. Due to various factors, such as 26 students having iOS devices, 11 students declining participation, 3 being foreign nationals, 2 being vertical transfer graduates, and 4 being absent, 46 out of 89 students were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 43 students were included in the experimental group. In the control group, 46 students who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were selected.

Data collection tools

The data were collected using the Student Information Form, the Student Opinion Form, the Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students and the Perceived Insider Status Scale.

The student information form

This form was developed specifically for this study by the researchers. It included 9 questions regarding student nurses’ demographic characteristics, reasons for choosing nursing, readiness for clinical internship, and experiences that might cause anxiety [7, 21, 36, 37] (Supplementary file 1).

The student opinion form

This form was also developed by the researchers to evaluate student opinions on the HEMMEN mobile application and peer support [24, 36, 38–40] (Supplementary file 2).

The Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students Originally developed by Sheu et al. in Chinese, the scale comprises 6 sub-dimensions and 29 items [43]. Responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “4 - Very stressful for me” to “0 - Not stressful for me.” The sub-dimensions of the scale assess stress related to: lack of professional knowledge and skills (items 2, 7, 11); taking care of patients (items 1, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 19); assignments and workload (items 3, 9, 13, 17, 21); teachers and nursing staff (items 5, 16, 18, 20, 25, 27); the clinical environment (items 15, 23, 26); and peers and daily life (items 22, 24, 28, 29). The total score ranges from 0 to 116, with higher scores indicating higher stress levels [43]. The scale’s validity and reliability were verified by Karaca et al. [41] with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.67 to 0.93 and a two-week test-retest reliability of 0.96. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.95. Permission to use the scale was obtained from the authors.

The perceived insider status scale

The scale was developed by Stamper and Masterson (2002) to measure an individual’s perceived sense of belonging within an organization [13]. It consists of 6 items, with items 3, 4, and 6 being reverse-coded. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale, where responses range from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree. Its validity and reliability were established by Mirap (2008) [42], with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient between 0.79 and 0.93 [43]. In the present study, the scale’s Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.79. Permission to use the scale was obtained from the authors.

The mobile clinical internship orientation program (HEM-MEN)

The HEMMEN, Mobile Clinical Internship Orientation Program, was developed by the researchers in collaboration with a software expert to support nursing students during their first clinical practice. The app is designed to reduce clinical stress, facilitate faster orientation, and enhance students’ sense of belonging in the clinical environment.

HEMMEN provides real-time guidance and peer support throughout the internship. The app content is organized into six key modules:

Hospital/Clinic Information: Overview of the institution, departments, and key rules.Clinical Functions: Explanation of ward routines, patient care processes, and daily responsibilities.Internship Rules and Expectations: Guidelines on behavior, reporting, and professional standards.Clinical Team Introduction: Profiles of mentors, nurses, and other staff to foster familiarity and communication.Ongoing Guidance: Step-by-step instructions and checklists to support daily clinical tasks.Resources and Q&A: Access to relevant documents, videos, and the ability to ask questions directly to peer mentors.

Students in the experimental group received training on how to navigate and use the app before starting their clinical practice. During the internship, students could submit questions, receive peer feedback, and track their progress in real time, allowing for immediate support in response to challenges encountered in the clinical setting. The control group did not have access to the app and received only standard guidance from course instructors.

Data collection

Before the clinical practice of the Internal Medicine and Surgical Nursing courses, students in both the experimental and control groups attended a general internship information meeting led by the course lecturers. During the meeting, general information about the clinical practice was provided, and students’ questions were addressed. Following this session, a separate meeting was held specifically for the experimental group, during which the HEMMEN mobile clinical orientation application was downloaded to their smartphones, and training on its use was provided before the internship. The control group only participated in the general information meeting and received no further intervention. After the internship information meeting, students from both groups completed the Student Information Form and the Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students. At the end of the first day of clinical practice, the Perceived Insider Status Scale was administered to the experimental group via the HEMMEN mobile app, while the control group completed it using Google Forms. Throughout the internship, the experimental group used the HEMMEN app, which provided peer support during their clinical experience. In contrast, the control group maintained contact with the lecturer responsible, as is typical during internships, but did not use the mobile app or receive peer support.

At the conclusion of the clinical internship, both groups were reassessed using the Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students and the Perceived Insider Status Scale, while the Student Opinion Form was administered only to the experimental group. This entire process, including data collection, was conducted between October and December 2022.

Data evaluation

The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which revealed that the data did not follow a normal distribution. To describe the demographic characteristics of the students, number, percentage, and mean tests were used, and the chi-square test was applied to compare these characteristics. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the pre-test and post-test scores between groups, while the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests were used to analyze the pre-test and post-test scores within groups. The findings were evaluated at a 95% confidence interval with a 5% significance level.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Prior to the study, approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Karadeniz Technical University Health Sciences Scientific Research Ethics Committee (dated November 22, 2019, Decision No: 24237859-825) and institutional permission was obtained from Karadeniz Technical University Faculty of Health Sciences Dean’s Office (29.03.2019 Decision No:63582098-299). Our study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [44]. Participants were informed about the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and their consent was obtained for the Informed Consent Form. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study. Voluntary participants were included in the study. Volunteers who wanted to participate in the study were asked to fill out an informed consent form, which included the purpose and method of the study and the volunteers’ rights regarding the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to participate in the study.

Limitations of the research

The limitation of the study is that it was carried out only with first-year students studying in the nursing department of a university in the experimental and control groups, and six senior students as mentors. Another limitation of the study is that the personal characteristics of the mentor students selected from among the senior students who volunteered to participate in the study and who have previously completed clinical internships in different clinical settings, may have influenced the research results. Additionally, the study included only students with Android-compatible smartphones, as the HEMMEN application was not available on iOS devices. Due to technical constraints and the need to maximize participation during the pandemic, students with Android devices were pragmatically assigned to the experimental group. This allocation method may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Finally, although pre-surveys and assignments were conducted manually with attention to confidentiality and voluntary participation, this process may also have affected student responses. Future studies should consider cross-platform compatibility and randomized allocation to enhance external validity and minimize bias.

Results

The descriptive characteristics of the students in the experimental and control groups in clinical practice for the first time are as follows: The experimental group had an average age of 20 ± 0.6 years (18–29), with 76.7% being female and 60.5% being graduates from normal/Anatolian high schools. Additionally, 81.4% were not members of any university club, 51.2% had not attended hospital orientation training and 79.1% had participated in school orientation training (Table 1).

The control group had an average age of 19.78 ± 0.91 years (18–23), with 73.9% being female. A larger proportion (73.9%) graduated from normal/Anatolian high schools. Similarly, 82.6% were not members of any university club, but 89.1% reported participating in hospital orientation training, and 87% in school orientation training (Table 1).

Descriptive characteristics of nursing students

The stress levels of the students were analyzed across two measurements. In the first measurement, the experimental group had a mean stress score of 2.70 ± 0.71, while the control group had a mean stress score of 2.56 ± 0.71. When the second measurement was taken, the mean stress score of the experimental group had decreased to 2.27 ± 0.80, whereas the control group’s score was 2.50 ± 0.87. A comparison of the stress scores within each group between the first and second measurements revealed a statistically significant reduction in stress levels for the experimental group (t = 3.442, p = 0.001). In contrast, no significant change was observed in the control group’s stress levels over the same period (t = 0.598, p = 0.553). When comparing the stress levels between the experimental and control groups, no statistically significant difference was found in either the first measurement (t = 0.882, p = 0.380) or the second measurement (t=-1.247, p = 0.216) (Table 2).

The belonging levels of the students were also evaluated. In the first measurement, the experimental group had a mean belongingness score of 3.40 ± 0.59, compared to 3.48 ± 0.58 for the control group. In the second measurement, the experimental group’s score increased slightly to 3.48 ± 0.58, while the control group’s score decreased to 3.26 ± 0.71. When the belongingness scores were compared within the groups between the first and second measurements, no statistically significant difference was found in either the experimental or control groups (Table 2).

Regarding the opinions of students in the experimental group regarding the HEM-MEN application, several positive findings emerged. 71.7% of students expressed satisfaction with the sharing of notes and resources related to their course 75% were pleased with the opportunity to share various emotions related to their internship experiences, 76.7% of students were satisfied with the platform for sharing challenges, and 76.7% appreciated the solutions offered when they faced difficulties with course-related assignments. Additionally, 65% of the students indicated that the HEMMEN application clarified their questions, 66.7% stated that it boosted their confidence, and 71.7% found the time allocated for using the mobile application to be sufficient, with the same percentage reporting that it encouraged learning and critical thinking. Another 71.7% mentioned that it facilitated positive relationships with clinicians, 65% felt that the content of the program met their expectations, 73.3% believed the mobile program was designed to be user-friendly. Regarding peer support, 76.7% of students noted that their peer counselors were knowledgeable, 80% found them to be understanding and supportive, 81.7% said they provided feedback, and 65% reported that the counselors helped them improve their clinical skills (Table 3).

Discussion

The study examined the impact of the HEMMEN application, designed to provide real-time guidance and feedback during clinical practice, on nursing students’ perceived stress and sense of belonging in the clinical environment. Findings indicated that the peer mentoring facilitated by the HEMMEN application significantly reduced the stress perception among nursing students. Current literature supports this observation, suggesting that peer support and mentoring effectively diminish anxiety and stress levels in students [14, 22–24, 45]. Specifically, research indicates that mentoring practices employed in nursing schools can alleviate students’ anxiety in clinical learning settings [24], foster a more conducive learning environment [46], enhance their adaptation to the nursing profession [47], facilitate socialization within the clinical context [48] and improve their coping mechanisms for stress [39]. In a related study, first-year nursing students who received peer support from fourth-year students reported enhancements in motivation, academic performance, and social connections following the mentoring experience [47]. While numerous studies in the literature assess the effects of face-to-face peer mentoring in clinical nursing education [24, 39, 48], there is a notable scarcity of research on the application of e-mentoring [49, 50]. This gap appears to be specific to nursing; however, evidence suggests that virtual mentoring and technological support are increasingly utilized to assist new graduates in transitioning to professional life in fields such as medicine, business, and pharmacy [51]. Castillo-Segura et al. (2021) found that electronic peer mentoring during laparoscopic surgeries enhanced both the performance and learning of trainees in a medical education setting [52]. Positive outcomes from e-learning and virtual mentoring have also been reported to assist graduate doctors in transitioning to independent practice [51]. In line with these findings, the current study supports the notion that peer-provided social and academic support, peer communication, and clinic adaptation positively impact students’ clinical learning, ultimately reducing their overall stress levels.

One of the critical factors influencing the quality of the clinical learning environment, both positively and negatively, is the sense of clinical belonging. A positive perception of belonging is particularly important for students entering the clinic for the first time, as their experiences in this environment can significantly shape their views on the nursing profession [53]. Previous literature indicates that peer mentoring is especially effective in fostering a sense of belonging and participation, particularly for first-year students, helping them overcome challenges [36]. This non-significant result may be explained by several contextual factors. First, the limited duration of the clinical internship did not provide students with sufficient time to build sustained and meaningful connections with their clinical environment and peers. Belonging is a gradual process that often requires continuous interaction with the clinical culture, mentors, and institutional support mechanisms. Second, the absence of active involvement from the host institution may have restricted opportunities for students to perceive themselves as integrated members of the clinical team. Prior studies emphasize that institutional engagement and endorsement are essential in creating an environment where students feel truly accepted and valued [54, 55]. Finally, belonging is closely related to group cohesion and collective identity formation; given the short internship period, students may not have had the opportunity to develop a deeper sense of cohesion within their clinical groups. Additionally, the fact that the health institution where the internship was not involved in the mentoring program may have influenced the results. The host organization plays a crucial role in supporting the mentoring environment and evaluating the program itself [54]. When the host institution actively engages in such programs, its organizational culture can contribute significantly to the development of e-mentoring relationships [55]. Another factor affecting the sense of belonging is “cohesion.” [15] Students may not have had sufficient time to fully develop a sense of clinical belonging during the short internship period. The first year of university education is often a challenging time for students as they navigate the orientation and adaptation processes [56]. It is expected that developing a sense of belonging to both the university and clinical environments will take time. Therefore, institutions and educators need to monitor and support students through these adaptation processes. It is also important to note that the study’s limitation to Android-compatible devices may have influenced the sample composition, potentially introducing selection bias. This constraint, combined with the short duration of clinical practice and moderate initial levels of belonging, could help explain why the intervention did not result in a statistically significant increase in students’ sense of belonging, despite positive qualitative feedback. Recognizing these factors provides a nuanced understanding of the findings and highlights areas for future research.

Electronic or online mentoring, supported by technology and accessible anytime and anywhere, enhances role modeling as well as the psychosocial and professional skills of individuals. Studies on e-mentoring in nursing education’s clinical practice highlight its significant impact on the professional development of nursing students and their clinical performance [49, 50]. E-mentoring, as a hybrid approach that connects mentors and mentees, fosters creative, supportive, and impactful relationships [57]. In this study, students’ positive evaluations of the HEMMEN program align with the literature. Most students expressed satisfaction with the program’s benefits, viewing it as a key support in their clinical education. The findings are consistent with previous research, indicating that peer-supported mobile applications promote collaboration among students and encourage active participation in the learning process. The fact that students reported their questions were answered, their confidence increased, and the peer-supported HEMMEN program was both easy to use and had sufficient time allocated for it indicates that the program was effectively designed and user-friendly. However, although students’ qualitative feedback emphasized satisfaction, increased confidence, and perceived support, these positive perceptions did not fully align with the quantitative findings, particularly regarding the sense of belonging. This divergence suggests that while the intervention was perceived as beneficial in the short term, belonging—as a deeper construct—may require longer exposure, stronger institutional engagement, and broader integration into the clinical environment to demonstrate measurable improvement.

Furthermore, peer-supported mobile programs were found to enhance students’ clinical practice skills and foster positive relationships within the clinical setting. This finding aligns with similar studies in the literature. For instance, an e-mentoring program for senior nursing students was shown to improve their clinical skills and boost their self-confidence [49]. These results suggest that peer-supported mobile programs can be effective tools for enhancing nursing students’ success and confidence in clinical education. However, the current findings also highlight the need for further research with longer intervention durations and stronger institutional involvement to fully capture the potential impact of e-mentoring on dimensions such as belonging and integration into the clinical community. Future studies should therefore explore how extending program duration, fostering organizational engagement, and combining qualitative and quantitative evaluations can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of peer-supported e-mentoring. In this context, the positive experiences students had with their peer mentors highlight the importance of peer-supported mobile programs in improving both the effectiveness of mentoring and students’ success in clinical education processes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the HEMMEN peer-mentoring mobile application effectively reduced nursing students’ perceived stress during clinical practice. The program was positively evaluated by students, who reported satisfaction with its usability and supportive features. However, the intervention did not significantly improve students’ sense of belonging in the clinical setting. This finding suggests that while peer-supported mobile applications can be valuable tools for stress reduction and clinical adaptation, belonging—as a deeper construct—may require longer-term and institutionally integrated interventions.

Future implementations should therefore consider designing long-term, cross-platform mobile orientation and mentoring programs that actively involve host institutions. Such approaches could provide broader applicability, foster stronger integration into the clinical community, and enhance both students’ sense of belonging and overall success in clinical education.
