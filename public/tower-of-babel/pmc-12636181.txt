Improving osteoarthritis diagnosis and management in the primary care setting: a concise educational tool for primary care providers
Authors: Tessalyn Morrison, Luke Giangregorio, Emily Hadley-Strout, Jeanne Gosselin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis, and its prevalence is increasing. We developed an educational tool to improve primary care provider (PCP) comfort with diagnosis and management of non-inflammatory arthritis.MethodsA pilot survey of PCP comfort with OA informed the creation of a two-page educational tool which was introduced at 28 clinics within the University of Vermont Health Network. The tool’s utility was assessed with pre- (n = 94) and post-intervention (n = 32) surveys. We conducted a chart review of adult patients diagnosed with OA at new patient rheumatology visits between 2020 and 2022.ResultsOA was the principal diagnosis at 9% of new patient rheumatology visits. Among 390 patients diagnosed with OA, 22 (5%) returned to rheumatology clinic within 6 months. Patients traveled on average 31 miles (SD ± 30) and 40 min (SD ± 31) one-way to reach new patient appointments. Pre-intervention, clinicians were least comfortable knowing when to refer to rheumatology and most comfortable diagnosing OA. Post-intervention, there were improvements in total comfort with OA management and when to refer to orthopedics. Half of clinicians (50%) used the tool in practice, 63% in medical education and 43% in shared decision making. Post-intervention, 34% expected a reduction in rheumatology referrals and 9% reported placing fewer referrals already.ConclusionsA small percentage of patients with OA require ongoing rheumatologic care, with patients traveling a significant distance for evaluation. The introduction of a concise educational tool for PCPs improved comfort managing non-inflammatory arthritis with anticipation of fewer referrals to rheumatology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-025-00595-8.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common type of arthritis worldwide, affecting nearly 15% of the global adult population, and is expected to increase in prevalence alongside upward trends in obesity and an aging population [1]. Patients with OA may experience chronic joint pain that worsens with use, progressive loss of range of motion, and bony swelling and joint instability that can eventually limit their ability to participate in meaningful activities. While OA is considered a non-inflammatory arthritis, it is a source of significant debility, affecting both the physical and mental well-being of the patient [2]. Management of OA is coordinated initially by primary care providers (PCPs) with a focus on shared decision making given the range of interventions spanning multiple domains. The American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) Osteoarthritis guidelines for both internists and rheumatologists stress non-pharmacologic approaches, including behavioral, psychosocial and physical interventions (e.g. bracing and taping), in addition to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), centrally acting agents, intra-articular glucocorticoid injections and ultimately joint replacement, depending on the joint involved [3]. While research evaluating agents such as methotrexate is underway, to date, there are no disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) approved for the treatment of OA [4]. While none of the conservative treatments used for osteoarthritis require a rheumatologist, rheumatologists may be involved in diagnosis if there is concern for an alternate inflammatory pathology that would require ongoing rheumatologic care.

Joint pain is ubiquitous in the primary care setting and it can be challenging for PCPs to fully address these concerns, including a tailored workup and management plan, given significant time constraints and multiple patient complaints [5, 6] It is important, where possible, to differentiate non-inflammatory arthritis, such as OA, from inflammatory arthritis in the primary care setting to avoid potentially costly delays in appropriate care, reduce the burden on the patient of unnecessary specialist visits, and to conserve limited rheumatology resources. In rheumatology clinics, including our own at the University of Vermont Medical Center (UVMMC), there is a significant volume of referrals for non-inflammatory joint pain. A previous study within our network evaluated inappropriate referrals to rheumatology, finding that increased care capacity correlated with a reduction in proportion of appropriate referrals, which prompted initiatives to develop electronic consults (eConsults) and a call for educational tools to help guide primary providers toward more appropriate referrals [7]. Referrals often lack the necessary information for effective triage, such as complete documentation of arthralgia history, exam findings and appropriate work-up [8, 9]. In another study of our rheumatology patient population, Thoms et al. found that only 26% of all new patients evaluated at UVMMC rheumatology for joint pain were ultimately diagnosed with inflammatory arthritis, with only 20.7% of new inflammatory arthritis patients seen within six weeks of referral [9]. PCP educational programs, whether self-paced audiovisual modules or educational workshops, have been found to increase adherence to appropriate treatments, but have not yet been shown to improve diagnostic accuracy, the quality of referrals, or reduce referrals to rheumatology [10–13].

The goal of our quality improvement project was to implement a concise educational tool to improve PCP comfort in differentiating non-inflammatory from inflammatory arthritis, to support management of OA in the primary care setting, and help PCPs identify patients who would benefit from specialty care. We created and distributed a paper tool to PCPs in our network that highlights key patient history and physical exam findings, potential laboratory testing and imaging, and brief descriptions of the central features of different types of arthritis. As a rural health network, we also sought to describe the burden experienced by patients seeking rheumatologic evaluation for OA to highlight the opportunity for centralizing OA management in the primary care setting. While not directly assessed, we hope additional benefits of the educational tool and project surveys will include improvements in the quality of referral documentation and the identification of further barriers to initiating OA management in the primary setting, respectively.

Methods

The goal of this quality improvement project was to design and distribute an educational tool for PCPs to allow them to better differentiate non-inflammatory arthritis, such as osteoarthritis, from inflammatory arthritis, and to identify patients who may benefit from specialty care. This project involved a chart review to understand the scope of patients seen with osteoarthritis at their first new patient visit in rheumatology, a pilot survey to inform design of the educational tool, pre-intervention surveys prior to tool distribution, and post-intervention survey after distribution of the tool.

Chart review

The first phase of the project was a chart review of all adult patients seen in rheumatology clinic for a new patient visit at UVMMC or a network affiliate, Central Vermont Medical Center, who received a principal diagnosis of OA between 2020 and 2022. We reviewed the time from referral to new patient visit, the estimated distance driven to the visit site based on zip code, PCP practice site, referring provider type (MD, DO, NP, PA or ND) and whether they were seen for a follow up appointment within six months. For patients with follow-up appointments, two authors (TM and LG) reviewed the patient chart to determine their eventual primary diagnosis.

Pilot survey

We administered a pilot survey to all PCPs working within UVMHN between April 1, 2023, and May 1, 2023, to understand baseline comfort with providing OA care and to help identify any knowledge gaps to be addressed with the tool. We asked providers how comfortable they were with assessing rheumatologic symptoms, diagnosing OA, treating OA, knowing when to refer patients to orthopedic surgery and rheumatology, and asked in which areas providers wanted more information (see Suplementary Materials).

Educational tool intervention

We developed a two-sided printed handout with information about non-inflammatory and inflammatory arthritis, including information on presentation, diagnosis, work-up and referrals, based on the results from a pilot survey of PCPs assessing which areas of OA management they were least comfortable with (see Supplementary Materials). The tool was designed by TM and then edited by JG (rheumatology) and EHS (primary care). We (TM, JG, or LG) introduced the tool to 27 out of 28 total UVM health network primary careclinics during virtual meetings (Zoom or Microsoft Teams) and for one clinic, we mailed the tool with a letter of introduction. Prior to the introduction of the tool, we distributed a pre-intervention survey to PCPs (March 1, 2024, to May 3, 2024) about comfort with managing OA. At two months following introduction and distribution of the tool, we administered a post-intervention survey assessing change in comfort with managing OA post-intervention and how they believe the tool changed their practice. The surveys used in this study were novel, designed to assess the impact of our specific educational intervention, and had not been used in previous research (see Supplementary Files: Provider Surveys).

Statistical analysis

Survey data was collected using REDCap and analysis was performed using Excel 2016, R (version 4.4.3), and ArcGIS (version 10.8.2). Distance and time required to travel to clinic in either Burlington or Berlin, Vermont was calculated using ArcGIS and R using the nearest point on a road in the geographic center of a zip code. Mann-Whitney rank sum tests were performed on pre- and post- survey data, using an alpha cutoff of 0.05.

Ethics approval

Permission to review patient charts was obtained from the University of Vermont Medical Center institutional review board (STUDY #00002447).

Results

Chart review

Between 2020 and2022, there were 390 new patients seen with a principal diagnosis of OA (9% of all new patient visits): 103 in 2020, 134 in 2021 and 153 in 2022. Despite an observed 51% increase in the number of patients diagnosed with OA over the study period, the proportion of these patients among all new patient visits was stable: 10% in 2020, 8% in 2021, and 9% in 2022. Most patients were referred by physicians (55% MD, 5% DO), while 22% were referred by a nurse practitioner (NP), 17% by a physician assistant (PA), and 1% by a naturopathic doctor (ND). Patients were most commonly referred by a PCP (81% by any PCP, 50% by the patient’s listed PCP), while 8% were referred by orthopedics, and 2% by dermatology. Over half of referrals were from providers outside of the network (55%).

Of the 390 new patients diagnosed with OA, 22 (5%) were seen for follow-up in rheumatology clinic within six months: 2 had previous rheumatologic disease (1 with Sjogren’s disease, 1 with sarcoidosis), 2 were diagnosed on a subsequent visit with a spondyloarthropathy, 3 with crystalline arthropathies, and 1 with rheumatoid arthritis. Using zip code as an approximation, patients spent an average of 40 min (SD ± 31 min; range 5 min–3.5 h) driving 31 miles (SD ± 30 min; range 1.5–228 miles) one-way to reach a new appointment. In total, this sample of patients traveled 12,095 miles for 262 h to reach their appointments.

PCP surveys

We delivered three provider surveys to a total of 230 clinicians at 28 clinics across the UVMHN: a pilot survey prior to design of the educational tool (n = 33, 14% response), a pre-intervention survey (n = 94, 41%), and a post-intervention survey (n = 32, 14%) (Table 1). From the pilot survey, we identified that clinicians were least comfortable with knowing when to refer to rheumatology and in assessing for rheumatologic symptoms and were most comfortable with diagnosing OA and knowing when to refer to orthopedic surgery. These results informed the design of our educational tool and were consistent with the results of the formal pre-intervention survey (Table 2).

Data from the post-intervention survey, administered two months after introduction and distribution of the tool, showed stability or an increase of median score in all areas of comfort with managing OA, with a significant increase in total comfort managing OA (p = 0.04) and knowing when to refer patients to orthopedic surgery (p = 0.04) (Table 2). While not statistically significant, we saw a trend towards increased comfort in when to refer to rheumatology (8% improvement in median score, p = 0.13).

We observed some trends that suggest the educational tool may have different effect levels depending on training and experience, though sample sizes were too small to make statistical comparisons. Clinicians with less than 5 years of experience showed greater improvements in overall score with use of the tool compared to those with more than 5 years (12% versus 6%). Compared to physicians, advanced practice providers had similar levels of comfort with managing osteoarthritis but demonstrated greater knowledge gains with knowing when to refer to rheumatology (20% among NP and PA, versus 0% among physicians) and in assessing for rheumatologic symptoms (20% for APPs versus 0% for physicians).

In assessing utility and satisfaction with the tool two months after introduction and distribution, 50% of clinicians report using the tool in practice, 75% were moderately or very satisfied with the tool, and 31% recommended the tool to a colleague (Table 3). Clinicians found additional utility in the tool as an educational resource for learners (63%) and to improve shared decision making (41%). Regarding how the tool has changed their practice, 9% of PCPs reported fewer referrals to rheumatology post-intervention and 34% anticipated placing fewer referrals in the future (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature examining the barriers to managing osteoarthritis in primary care settings. We found that PCPs reported the least comfort in knowing when to refer patients to rheumatology and evaluating patients for inflammatory symptoms. This is in line with previous studies showing that primary care clinicians face challenges including lack of familiarity with referral protocols, applying OA management guidelines with an emphasis on shared decision making, often due to time constraints, and managing patient expectations, all in the face of growing provider burnout [5, 6] The current project highlights these challenges in a health network serving a rural population, which is an understudied demographic to date [14]. Educational interventions have shown promise in addressing these gaps. Previous educational outreach programs and tools have improved adherence to treatment guidelines [12, 13], exercise therapy referrals [11], and patient education [10], but were unable to impact referrals to orthopedics [10, 13] or rheumatology [14]. Our project showed improvement in overall comfort managing osteoarthritis, comfort with knowing when to refer to orthopedic surgery and anticipation of fewer referrals to rheumatology with use of the tool. In line with other studies, we found the current educational tool may be particularly helpful for early career clinicians [12]. Additionally, we demonstrated the potential value of the tool as a teaching aid for learners in the clinic, which has not been observed in previous studies.

While not the primary objective of this quality improvement project, it was important to include some measure of referral patterns and utilization of specialty care, as well as the burden of accessing that care on the patient. The small proportion (5%) of patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis that required ongoing rheumatologic care highlights an area for significant improvement in multiple stages of the referral process, from PCP evaluation to referral template design, screening and triage. This project focused on the first step in this process, the demand side of the health care equation, which is often neglected with a focus on supply, but is an essential piece to address if we are to improve access to rheumatologic and other specialty care, especially in rural areas. In a recent review of efforts to support equitable access to OA care, only one of the interventions took place in a rural area [14]. For our rural health network, we chose to estimate the cost of unnecessary referrals as the distance and time traveled by patients to reach their new patient appointments. We felt that distance traveled and time spent by patients to reach their appointment was a patient-centered approach to estimating the impact of specialty care needs among patients in rural areas. Patients in our rural health network diagnosed with OA at their first visit traveled significant distance and spent many hours to reach rheumatology appointments, a burden that could be reduced. We do not expect that eliminating all unnecessary referrals is possible, and we were unable to study the tool’s direct impact on referral rates as multiple departmental interventions were occurring simultaneously. However, with our general goal of reducing unnecessary referrals, we found it encouraging that 43% of clinicians reported that the tool had already changed their referral practices, or they anticipate referring fewer patients to rheumatology.

The current project has several limitations. Our findings likely underestimate the true volume of OA visits as we focused on patients diagnosed with OA at the initial new patient visit, excluding those who may have required additional testing or rheumatology follow-up to confirm the diagnosis or better characterize the extent of OA. We chose to focus on this narrower subset of patients with OA as this population in particular may benefit most from PCP education and avoidance of rheumatology referral in the first place. A previous study within our own network supports this demonstrating that only 26% of those referred to rheumatology for joint pain were eventually diagnosed with an inflammatory arthropathy [10]. Given the timing of our project, we suspect that the referral volume, along with the availability and completion of follow-up visits, may have been impacted by operational changes during the COVID pandemic. We utilized self-reported data on comfort with managing OA, which may have overestimated pre-intervention provider knowledge and therefore obscured changes in post-intervention outcomes. Our survey data were also not matched, which reduced our ability to detect change in knowledge and may have been affected by who responded to either survey based on their baseline knowledge, interest in OA, or affinity towards the tool. We chose to use unmatched samples to invite broader engagement with the tool anticipating low survey responses, though for future studies interested in optimizing the tool or estimating its impact, we would suggest using matched samples or focus groups. We did not study the tool’s direct impact on referral counts as multiple departmental interventions were occurring simultaneously, which would make it difficult to isolate the impact of the tool within the timeframe of the study. While we attempted to introduce the tool in a standardized way, there was variability between presenters and variability in adoption within each clinic which likely influenced its overall effectiveness.

Conclusions

Our quality improvement project involved the development, distribution, and evaluation of an educational tool to help PCPs differentiate between inflammatory and non-inflammatory arthritis. Based on our experience, we suggest that future interventions focus on integration of educational tools into the EHR and e-consult systems, adapting educational tools to address PCP sub-groups (e.g. advanced practitioners, early career clinicians), tailoring efforts towards educational remediation based on referral patterns, and including patients as partners in development and evaluation of the tool. Broader distribution of the tool to other healthcare settings such as urgent care and orthopedics may help further refine referrals to rheumatology. Ensuring the availability of this tool to PCPs outside of our network who have less access to specialty care, e-consults and enhanced referrals would likely improve healthcare equity. As artificial intelligence expands within the medical field, it may also be worth adapting components of this tool for personalized education for clinicians or as an aid in the process of referring patients when there is diagnostic uncertainty. Previous research conducted within our network has shown that initiatives focused on supply alone may not address declining access, growing wait times, and inequitable access to rheumatology [1]. Further work is needed to understand provider, patient, and system level barriers to the management of non-inflammatory arthritis in the primary care setting and utilization patterns of rheumatology services to inform future interventions targeting demand. As part of a multi-pronged approach, such educational interventions can play a pivotal role in optimizing rheumatologic and primary care.
