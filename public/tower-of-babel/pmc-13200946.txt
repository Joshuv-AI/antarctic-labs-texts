Metabolic score for insulin resistance and cancer mortality: Effect modification by hypertension in a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Huanrong Wang, Hua Bai, Wenhui Wang, Dan Yang, Fanfan Zhou, Meifeng Yin, Kai Li, Yunxia Guo, Ruijun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) is associated with cardiovascular mortality, but its association with cancer-specific mortality remains unclear. This retrospective cohort study included 17,347 adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999 to 2018. METS-IR was calculated based on fasting glucose, triglycerides and other indicators, with follow-up until December 31, 2019. Weighted Cox models and other analyses were performed. The results showed that each 1-unit increase in METS-IR was associated with a 3% higher risk of cancer-specific mortality (P = .01), presenting an approximately linear dose–response relationship; METS-IR remained associated with cancer mortality risk when analyzed as a categorical variable. Subgroup analyses revealed a significant interaction between hypertension and METS-IR in relation to cancer mortality, and stratified analyses further indicated that hypertension status acted as an effect modifier of this association. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Higher METS-IR was independently associated with increased risks of cancer mortality in the US population, with hypertension significantly amplifying this association.

1. Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite substantial advances in cancer prevention, early detection, and therapeutic strategies, its global incidence continues to rise, and survival outcomes remain highly heterogeneous across populations and tumor types.[1,2] In the United States, gains in cancer survival have been partially offset by the growing burden of population aging, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction (factors that are now firmly established as major determinants of both cancer risk and prognosis).[1,3] Among these, insulin resistance represents a central pathophysiological hallmark of metabolic dysfunction, mechanistically linking obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) to a pro-tumorigenic milieu characterized by hyperinsulinemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and dysregulation of insulin/insulin-like growth factor signaling. This altered metabolic environment promotes cellular proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis, thereby facilitating tumor initiation and progression.[4–6]

A substantial body of epidemiological evidence has demonstrated that metabolic syndrome, hyperinsulinemia, and T2DM are associated with increased incidence and poorer survival across multiple malignancies, including colorectal, pancreatic, hepatic, endometrial, and postmenopausal breast cancers.[1,5,6] However, accurate assessment of insulin resistance in large-scale population studies remains challenging. The hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp, although considered the gold standard, is limited by its technical complexity, invasiveness, and high cost. Surrogate indices such as the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, which rely primarily on fasting insulin and glucose levels, have been widely adopted but suffer from important limitations, including tissue specificity and reliance on a narrow metabolic spectrum, rendering them insufficient to comprehensively capture systemic metabolic dysregulation.[7] More recently, the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) has been proposed as a practical laboratory-based alternative that integrates fasting glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and body mass index (BMI) to estimate insulin resistance. METS-IR has demonstrated good agreement with reference methods[8] and has been shown to predict incident T2DM, cardiovascular disease, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease across diverse populations.[9–11] Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that METS-IR reflects broader metabolic risk beyond glycemic abnormalities and is associated with cardiovascular outcomes and all-cause mortality.[12] However, data regarding the association between the METS-IR and cancer mortality remain extremely scarce.

Hypertension often coexists with insulin resistance and induces cardiovascular disease through endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.[13,14] These mechanisms can also promote carcinogenesis and tumor progression through dysregulation of cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune function.[15] Given this close biological link, hypertension is likely to influence the association between insulin resistance and mortality risk. While hypertension is widely recognized as a cause of death, few epidemiological studies have explored whether it affects the relationship between insulin resistance-related indicators and cancer mortality. Most previous studies have only considered hypertension as a confounding factor, without considering its potential role as an effect modifier. Therefore, it remains unclear whether individuals with both insulin resistance and hypertension have a higher risk of cancer death compared to those without hypertension.

In summary, we conducted a nationally representative cohort study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 1999 to 2018, combined with mortality records. This study had 2 objectives: first, to explore the association between METS-IR and all-cause mortality and cancer mortality in the U.S. adult population; and second, to examine whether hypertension affects these associations. By analyzing a large-sample database, this study aimed to provide strong evidence for the predictive value of METS-IR and to elucidate the combined effects of insulin resistance and hypertension on cancer mortality risk.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

The NHANES, administered by the National Center for Health Statistics, uses a complex, stratified, multistage probability design to generate a nationally representative sample of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. Each 2-year cycle comprises a household interview followed by a physical examination in a mobile examination center (MEC). The present analysis followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

We constructed a retrospective cohort of adults aged ≥20 years who participated in any of the ten NHANES cycles administered between 1999 and 2018. Participants were excluded if they could not be linked to the National Death Index (NDI); lacked data required to calculate the METS-IR index (fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-C, or BMI); were pregnant at examination; or had missing covariate information. Study flow chart. NHANES = the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, METS-IR = metabolic score for insulin resistance.

2.2. METS-IR assessment

In this study, the METS-IR index was used as the exposure variable and was calculated using the following formula: METS-IR = Ln (2 × fasting glucose [mg/dL] + fasting triglycerides [mg/dL]) × BMI (kg/m2)/Ln (HDL-C [mg/dL]). Fasting glucose and triglyceride levels were measured after an 8.5 hours fasting period using an automated biochemical analyzer. The BMI (kg/m2) was derived by dividing the weight (kg) of the individual by the square of their height (m).

2.3. Mortality ascertainment

Mortality follow-up was ascertained through probabilistic linkage with the the NDI as of December 31,2019. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any underlying cause. Deaths attributable to malignant neoplasms were identified using ICD-10 codes C00-C97. Each participant was assigned a vital-status code (0 = alive, 1 = deceased) for survival analyses. Median follow-up time among decedents was 110 months.

2.4. Covariate assessment

On the basis of prior literature, we adjusted for the following potential confounders: age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, family income, waist circumference, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and baseline cancer history. Race/ethnicity was self-reported as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, or other. Marital status was dichotomized into living alone (never married, separated, divorced, and widowed) versus living with a partner (married or living with partner). Educational attainment was categorized as <9 years, 9–12 years, or >12 years. Family income was divided into 3 levels according to the poverty income ratio (PIR): low income (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium income (PIR > 1.3 and ≤3.5), and high income (PIR > 3.5).[16] Smoking status was defined as never (<100 lifetime cigarettes), former (≥100 cigarettes and currently abstinent), or current (≥100 cigarettes and currently smoking). Alcohol consumption was considered present if participants reported consuming ≥12 alcoholic drinks in any single year. Hypertension was recorded if the mean of 3 seated blood-pressure readings yielded systolic ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic ≥90 mm Hg, or if participants self-reported a physician diagnosis or current antihypertensive medication use, consistent with International Society of Hypertension criteria. Diabetes mellitus was identified when any of the following applied: fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), HbA1c ≥6.5%, or self-reported physician diagnosis with concurrent use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents.[17] Dyslipidemia was defined according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria: total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, HDL-C ≤40 mg/dL (men) or ≤50 mg/dL (women), LDL-C ≥130 mg/dL, or self-reported high cholesterol with lipid-lowering therapy.[18] Baseline cancer history was ascertained by affirmative response to the question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you had cancer?”

2.5. Statistical analysis

This study was conducted as a secondary analysis of publicly available data from the NHANES. In accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines, all analyses incorporated sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units to account for the complex, multistage probability sampling design. Failure to apply these design variables may lead to biased estimates and inflated type I error rates; therefore, survey-specific procedures were consistently applied.

Data were derived from both the household interview and the MEC components of NHANES. Because METS-IR related measurements were obtained during the MEC examination, fasting subsample weights were used as recommended. Sampling weights were calculated as follows: for participants surveyed between 1999 and 2002, weights were calculated as 2/10 multiplied by the 4-year fasting weight; for participants surveyed after 2002, weights were calculated as 8/10 multiplied by the 2-year fasting weight.

Follow-up time was calculated from the date of MEC examination to the date of death or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Mortality data were obtained through linkage with the NDI, which is updated every 4 years; the most recent follow-up was available through December 31, 2019.

Categorical variables are presented as unweighted counts with weighted percent-ages, whereas continuous variables are expressed as weighted means with standard errors. Differences across METS-IR categories were evaluated using weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables and weighted linear regression for continuous variables. Weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models accounting for the NHANES complex sampling design were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cancer-specific mortality and all-cause mortality, both per unit increase in METS-IR and across METS-IR quartiles. To control for potential confounding, 3 progressively adjusted multivariable models were constructed. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, marital status, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, family income, BMI, and waist circumference. Model 2 further adjusted for smoking status and alcohol consumption. Model 3 additionally adjusted for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and history of cancer. Restricted cubic spline regression was applied to examine potential dose–response relationships between METS-IR and mortality outcomes. The restricted cubic spline models were adjusted for the same covariates included in Model 3 to assess both nonlinear associations and overall trends while maintaining model comparability.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed using weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models stratified by age (≤60 vs >60 years), sex (men vs women), smoking status (never vs former or current), alcohol consumption (never vs former or current), BMI (≤27.92 vs >27.92 kg/m2), waist circumference (≤98 vs >98 cm), hypertension (no vs yes), diabetes (no vs yes), hyperlipidemia (no vs yes), and cancer history (no vs yes). Continuous variables such as age were dichotomized according to cutoff values reported in previous literature,[18] whereas BMI and waist circumference were dichotomized based on their median values. Interaction effects were formally tested using likelihood ratio tests. For covariates demonstrating significant interaction effects in subgroup analyses, stratified analysis was further conducted to explore potential underlying mechanisms.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. First, to reduce potential reverse causality, participants who died within 2 years of enrollment were excluded. Second, individuals with diabetes mellitus were excluded, given that diabetes is frequently accompanied by disordered glucose metabolism and is itself a recognized risk factor for cancer development, progression, and mortality. Third, missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation with 5 imputations, and analyses were repeated using the imputed datasets.

Because the sample size was determined entirely by data availability, no a priori statistical power calculation was performed. All analyses were conducted using Free Statistics software (version 2.3; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). A two-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analyses were performed between November and December 2025.

2.6. Standard protocol approval, registration, and patient consent

Ethics approval was obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Committee, and participants provided written informed consent. The secondary analysis did not require additional institutional review board approval.

3. Results

3.1. Study population

The selection process for the study cohort is summarized as follows: a total of 21,888 participants aged over 20 years were initially identified from the 1999 to 2018 National Health and NHANES. After excluding 1181 individuals due to missing follow-up data (n = 35), missing METS-IR data (n = 587), and pregnancy (n = 559), 20,707 participants remained eligible for further analysis. Following the exclusion of participants with missing covariates, the final analytical cohort consisted of 17,347 participants (Fig. 1).

3.3. METS-IR and mortality

By the end of follow-up, 2575 all-cause deaths and 613 cancer deaths were identified (Supplementary Table 1), with a median follow-up period of 110 months. Table 2 shows the results of the multivariate analysis. When METS-IR was treated as a continuous variable, higher METS-IR was independently associated with an increased risk of cancer mortality (Model 3: HR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, P = .01). 3.4. Subgroup analyses

Elevated METS‑IR was significantly associated with increased cancer mortality across multiple prespecified subgroups (Fig. 3). The strongest associations were observed in participants aged ≤60 years (Q4 vs Q1: HR 6.13, 95% CI 2.18–17.25) and in males (Q4 vs Q1: HR 3.74, 95% CI 1.62–8.63). Statistically significant positive associations were also evident among current smokers, former/current drinkers, individuals with lower BMI or waist circumference, those with hyperlipidemia, those without diabetes, and those without a history of prior cancer. Except for hypertension status, no significant interaction effects were observed in any subgroups stratified by sex, age, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, waist circumference, diabetes status, hyperlipidemia, or prior cancer status.

3.5. Modifying effect of hypertension

The subgroup analysis above showed a significant interaction between METS-IR and hypertension status with cancer mortality. To further analyze the modifying effect of hypertension, the entire population was divided into 2 groups based on whether they had hypertension, and Cox multivariate analysis was performed separately (Table 3). We adjusted for confounding factors such as sex, age, marital status, race, education level, family income, BMI, waist circumference, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and tumors. The results showed that in the hypertension group, METS-IR was associated with cancer mortality, whether as a continuous or categorical variable. Furthermore, compared to the lowest quartile (Q4 vs Q1), the HR for cancer mortality was higher in hypertensive patients than in the general population (P = .002 vs P = .004), indicating that hypertension exacerbated the adverse association between insulin resistance and cancer mortality.

The results of sensitivity analyses are summarized in Table 4. After excluding participants who died within 2 years of follow-up, for each per-unit increase in METS-IR, the adjusted HR for cancer mortality was 1.03 (95% CI, 1.00–1.06; P = .05). Participants in the highest METS-IR quartile had roughly a 2-fold greater risk of cancer death versus the lowest quartile (HR = 2.12; 95% CI, 1.09–4.10; P = .03). After excluding participants with baseline diabetes, for each per-unit increase in METS-IR, the adjusted HR for cancer mortality was 1.05 (95% CI, 1.02–1.08; P < .001). Compared to the lowest quartile of METS-IR, participants in the highest quartile had an adjusted HR for cancer mortality of 2.42 (95% CI, 1.33–4.39; P < .01). Of the 20,707 participants, covariates were missing for 3360 (16.2%). We performed multiple imputations of missing covariates for the 20,707 participants. 4. Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort, we observed a robust and independent association between METS-IR and cancer-specific mortality. Notably, this association remained statistically significant after comprehensive adjustment for potential con-founders and was consistently preserved across multiple sensitivity analyses, under-scoring the stability of the findings. Furthermore, within the observed exposure range, METS-IR exhibited an approximately linear dose–response relationship with cancer-specific mortality, such that higher METS-IR levels were associated with progressively increased cancer-related death risk. In contrast, the association between METS-IR and all-cause mortality followed a nonlinear pattern. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that, with the exception of individuals with hypertension, the effect of METS-IR on cancer-specific mortality was largely consistent across major sociodemographic and behavioral strata, including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, waist circumference, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and cancer history. Collectively, these findings support the central conclusion of this study: METS-IR, a comprehensive and readily available index of insulin resistance, reflects systemic metabolic risk, and elevated levels are associated with increased cancer-specific mortality, with hypertension exacerbating this risk.

One of the key strengths of METS-IR lies in its accessibility and integrative nature. By incorporating fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-C, and body mass index, METS-IR simultaneously reflects dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and obesity (core components of metabolic dysfunction). An expanding body of evidence has demonstrated the predictive value of METS-IR for incident T2DM and cardiometabolic events,[19,20] and recent studies have further linked METS-IR to nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and liver fibrosis.[11] In addition, several cohort studies have reported significant associations between METS-IR and cardiovascular mortality as well as all-cause mortality.[12,21] A prospective study has also shown that elevated serum insulin levels and insulin metabolism-related markers are associated with increased lung cancer risk, with greater degrees of insulin resistance conferring higher risk.[22] Our observation of a linear association between METS-IR and cancer-specific mortality extends these findings and is biologically plausible, as tumor progression is known to exhibit a dose-dependent response to the metabolic milieu. Insulin-like growth factor-1, as key growth factors, activate tyrosine kinase-mediated signaling cascades that promote tumor cell proliferation, while the cytokine dysregulation accompanying metabolic syndrome may further facilitate tumor progression and survival.[23] By comparison, the nonlinear (J-shaped) relationship observed between METS-IR and all-cause mortality likely reflects individuals with very low METS-IR levels may be more susceptible to chronic catabolic states, which increase vulnerability to death from various causes. As METS-IR increases from low levels, all-cause mortality risk may initially decline; however, beyond a critical inflection point, severe insulin resistance and dyslipidemia may directly precipitate diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other fatal complications, thereby driving mortality risk upward. This interpretation is consistent with the findings reported by Su et al[19] and highlights the importance of distinguishing cause-specific mortality from all-cause mortality and of applying flexible modeling strategies when evaluating metabolic risk predictors.

To further explore heterogeneity in survival outcomes, we conducted subgroup analyses. Elevated METS-IR was significantly associated with higher cancer-specific mortality across most subgroups, and this relationship was largely independent of age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, waist circumference, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and cancer history. In participants younger than 60 years, cancer-specific mortality increased across higher METS-IR quartiles compared with the lowest quartile, whereas this association was not observed in older individuals. Interestingly, a recent study examining METS-IR in relation to cardiovascular mortality reported a similar age-dependent pattern.[24] Moreover, elevated METS-IR emerged as a risk factor for cancer-specific death among men, smokers, former or current drinkers, individuals with lower BMI or waist circumference, and those without hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, or cancer at baseline. Multiplicative interaction analyses indicated a significant interaction between METS-IR and hypertension in relation to cancer-specific mortality risk; further stratified analysis revealed that hypertension significantly impacted the association between METS-IR and cancer death. This may suggest that improving hypertension status in clinical practice could help improve risk stratification.

The robustness of our findings was further supported by multiple sensitivity analyses. Excluding participants who died within 2 years of enrollment minimized potential reverse causality arising from preclinical disease. Given that diabetes is associated with increased risks of several malignancies, including prostate, breast, and colorectal cancers, and is itself a major determinant of cancer mortality,[25] we additionally excluded participants with baseline diabetes. Finally, multiple imputation was applied to address potential bias due to missing covariate data. Across all sensitivity analyses, the association between METS-IR and cancer-specific mortality remained statistically significant, reinforcing the credibility of the observed relationship.

This study has several notable strengths. First, we leveraged a large, nationally representative sample of U.S. adults and applied rigorous survey weighting procedures, enhancing the generalizability of our findings to the broader population. Second, we comprehensively adjusted for a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical confounders and conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to test the stability of the results. Third, we conducted detailed subgroup and stratified analyses to explore potential effect modifications and examined the association between METS-IR and cancer death in detail.

This study has some limitations. The observational nature of the study excludes explicit causal inferences. Although we adjusted for numerous known confounding factors, database limitations prevented us from completely excluding confounding factors such as tumor stage and cancer treatment regimen. Furthermore, METS-IR was obtained based on a baseline questionnaire, making it impossible to obtain dynamic METS-IR. Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies to dynamically monitor METS-IR and investigate whether lifestyle or pharmacological interventions can significantly reduce cancer-related mortality.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a higher METS-IR is independently associated with an increased risk of cancer-specific death, and this association exhibits a linear dose–response relationship, the robustness of which was confirmed by multiple sensitivity analyses. Subgroup and stratified analyses revealed that hypertension status may modify this association. The differential association between METS-IR and all-cause mortality highlights the complexity of the influence of metabolic factors on multiple causes of death. While these results support METS-IR as an epidemiological indicator of cancer death risk and its potential clinical applicability, prospective validation and intervention assessments are still needed to determine whether insulin resistance can reduce cancer mortality.
