Descend or Rewind? Stochastic Gradient Descent Unlearning
Siqiao Mu, Diego Klabjan

Abstract
Machine unlearning algorithms aim to remove the impact of selected training data from a model without the computational expenses of retraining from scratch. Two such algorithms are “Descent-to-Delete” (D2D) and “Rewind-to-Delete” (R2D), full-batch gradient descent algorithms that are easy to implement and satisfy provable unlearning guarantees. In particular, the stochastic version of D2D is widely implemented as the “finetuning” unlearning baseline, despite lacking theoretical backing on nonconvex functions. In this work, we prove ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) certified unlearning guarantees for stochastic R2D and D2D for strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex loss functions, by analyzing unlearning through the lens of disturbed or biased gradient systems, which may be contracting, semi-contracting, or expansive respectively. Our argument relies on optimally coupling the random behavior of the unlearning and retraining trajectories, resulting in a probabilistic sensitivity bound that can be combined with a novel relaxed Gaussian mechanism to achieve ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) unlearning. We determine that D2D can yield tighter guarantees for strongly convex functions compared to R2D by relying on contraction to a unique global minimum. However, unlike D2D, R2D can achieve unlearning in the convex and nonconvex setting because it draws the unlearned model closer to the retrained model by reversing the accumulated disturbances.



1
Introduction

Machine unlearning algorithms aim to remove the influence of specific data from a trained model without retraining from scratch. First introduced in
[
5
]
, machine unlearning has attracted significant attention in recent years, driven by heightened concerns over user privacy, data quality, and the energy expenditures of training massive deep learning models such as large language models (LLMs). Regulatory pressures also play a role: provisions in the European Union’s General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR), the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), and the Canadian Consumer
Privacy Protection Act (CPPA), protect a user’s ”right to be forgotten” by requiring that individuals be able to request removal of their personal information, whether stored in databases or retained by models
[
36
]
. Since it is impractical to retrain from scratch every time a user requests removal of their data, we desire cost-saving methods for ”forgetting” information from models after training.

Generally, a machine unlearning algorithm aims to produce a model that appears as if it was trained without the unlearned data. This raises the question: how do we mathematically characterize the extent to which a model has truly unlearned? A rich line of work investigates “certified unlearning” algorithms, which are theoretically guaranteed to return model weights that are probabilistically indistinguishable to the weights obtained from retraining from scratch
[
19
,
17
]
. This is typically achieved by first bounding the distance between the weights after unlearning and after retraining, and then injecting appropriately calibrated Gaussian noise, a technique from differential privacy (DP) known as the Gaussian mechanism
[
14
]
. Such provable unlearning guarantees can be a powerful alternative to empirical metrics of unlearning, which can be unreliable or misleading
[
39
,
43
]
.

However, many existing certified unlearning algorithms have limited practicality for modern deep learning settings. The vast majority are either second-order methods that require access to the Hessian (or Hessian vector products)
[
41
,
33
,
36
]
or first-order methods that require computing the full gradient
[
31
,
9
,
30
]
. Both settings are computationally intractable for large-scale models, which are typically trained with
stochastic gradient descent
(SGD) algorithms. While a couple recent works are able to show certified unlearning with stochastic gradients, they either prove a weaker version of certified unlearning
[
27
]
or only apply to convex functions
[
8
]
. To reflect realistic practices, we desire SGD-based certified unlearning algorithms for
nonconvex
functions.

With this goal in mind, we revisit two existing first-order certified unlearning algorithms: Descent-to-Delete (D2D), designed for (strongly) convex loss functions, and Rewind-to-Delete (R2D), designed for nonconvex functions. Both algorithms perform learning via full-batch gradient descent (GD). Upon unlearning, both algorithms perform additional GD steps on the loss function of the retained dataset, but D2D “descends” from the original trained model, whereas R2D “rewinds” to an earlier saved checkpoint during training before performing the unlearning steps. Even though D2D is only theoretically supported for GD on strongly convex functions, its stochastic version is the basis for the “finetuning” unlearning baseline method, which is implemented in
virtually all
unlearning papers despite the fact that it underperforms on deep neural networks. The authors of
[
30
]
argue that rewinding, instead of descending, is a more appropriate baseline method for nonconvex settings, but their analysis does not apply to stochastic gradients. Therefore, establishing the unlearning properties of the stochastic versions of these two methods is an important open question.

In this work, we prove that SGD versions of D2D and R2D do achieve
(\epsilon,\delta)
certified unlearning and we mathematically characterize their privacy-utility-complexity tradeoffs under looser assumptions than the original works. We first examine R2D with projected SGD (PSGD), for which we can achieve clean guarantees with minimal assumptions on strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions. On an unbounded domain, i.e. without projection, we can also prove certified unlearning for SGD R2D for strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions, assuming that the second moment of the noise is relatively bounded and the loss is finite at initialization. Finally, we prove certified unlearning for the SGD version of D2D on strongly convex functions.

To analyze stochastic R2D, we leverage the contractive properties of gradient systems to carefully track the divergence between the training trajectory and the retraining trajectory. By characterizing SGD on the original loss function as
biased
or
disturbed
SGD on the loss function of the retained data samples, we can achieve noise bounds that reflect the contractive, semi-contractive, and expansive properties of strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions respectively. For stochastic D2D, we also treat it as biased SGD, but strongly convex functions have additional favorable properties allow the bias to be “folded into” the classic convergence analysis, as long as the proportion of unlearned data is small enough. This approach is completely different from the original proof in
[
31
]
, which considers a highly constrained setting where the function is both strongly convex and Lipschitz continuous.

Our approach also relies on a key coupling argument; to achieve a minimal bound on the distance between unlearning and retraining, we can control the coupling between the two randomized SGD trajectories, yielding a probabilistic bound with respect to their joint distribution. In particular, with only a first or second moment bound, we can obtain a tail bound that can be combined with a novel
relaxed Gaussian mechanism
to yield
(\epsilon,\delta)
indistinguishability at a small cost to the dependence on
\delta
. This technique allows a more flexible analysis of SGD algorithms, for which worst-case sensitivity bounds, required by the classic Gaussian mechanism, are challenging to obtain without heavy-handed measures such as bounded gradient assumptions or clipping.

Our work reveals deeper insights into the difference between “rewinding” and “descending.” On strongly convex functions, D2D yields tighter probabilistic bounds than R2D. However, D2D is not guaranteed to be more efficient than retraining from scratch if the initial point is sufficiently close to the global minimum. In contrast, R2D will
always
be more efficient than retraining from scratch. In fact, for strongly convex functions, the number of unlearning iterations
K
is
better than sublinear
in
T
, the number of training iterations, for constant noise; in fact, it converges to a constant for arbitrarily large
T
, implying a
potentially infinite computational advantage

T-K
for R2D unlearning.

Our proof techniques are novel to the field of certified unlearning and DP, and they allow us to establish privacy-utility-complexity tradeoffs for SGD-R2D and SGD-D2D, which are easy to implement and are “black-box,” in that they only require noise injection at the end and do not require special algorithmic procedures during training. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We prove
(\epsilon,\delta)
certified unlearning for R2D with projected SGD on strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex loss functions, under minimal assumptions.

•

We prove
(\epsilon,\delta)
certified unlearning for R2D with SGD
without projection
on strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex loss functions, assuming that the second moment of the noise is relatively bounded and the loss is finite at initialization over all possible data samples.

•

We prove
(\epsilon,\delta)
certified unlearning for D2D with SGD on strongly convex functions, using a novel proof approach that circumvents the limiting assumptions of the original work.

Analyses

We first discuss general assumptions required for this work. We assume that the loss function
\ell
is differentiable and Lipschitz smooth (Assumption
1
), a standard requirement for the analysis of SGD algorithms.

Assumption 1
.

For all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
\ell(z;\theta)
is differentiable in
\theta
and Lipschitz smooth in
\theta
with constant
L>0
such that for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lVert\nabla\ell(z;\theta_{1})-\nabla\ell(z;\theta_{2})\rVert\leq L\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\rVert.

In addition to nonconvex functions, we analyze the cases of convex and strongly convex functions, defined below in Assumptions
2
and
3
.

Assumption 2
.

For all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
\ell(z;\theta)
is convex in
\theta
such that for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\ell(z;\theta_{1})\geq\ell(z;\theta_{2})+\langle\nabla\ell(z;\theta_{2}),\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\rangle.

(5)

Assumption 3
.

For all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
\ell(z;\theta)
is strongly convex with parameter
\mu
such that for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\ell(z;\theta_{1})\geq\ell(z;\theta_{2})+\nabla\ell(z;\theta_{2})^{T}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})+\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\rVert^{2}.

Now we broadly describe our proof approach. Our analysis relies on carefully tracking the (P)SGD trajectories on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
and bounding the outputs of training on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
and unlearning on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
. Specifically, we have that

•

\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
represents the learning iterates on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
, starting from
\theta_{0}
,

•

\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
represents the learning iterates on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D^{\prime}}}
, where
\theta^{\prime}_{0}=\theta_{0}
,

•

\{\theta^{\prime\prime}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{K}
represents the unlearning iterates on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, where for D2D we have
\theta^{\prime\prime}_{0}=\theta_{T}
and for R2D we have at
\theta^{\prime\prime}_{0}=\theta_{T-K}
.

The goal of our work is to bound the distance between
\theta^{\prime}_{T}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}_{K}
. As highlighted in the introduction, our analysis relies on a key coupling argument, which enables the application of a
relaxed Gaussian mechanism
. Basically, we observe that the definition of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-unlearning (Definition
2
) involves only the
marginal
distributions of
\theta^{\prime}_{T}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}_{K}
, we are free, for the purpose of analysis, to consider any coupling of these marginals that is convenient; in particular, we choose a coupling that minimizes the expected distance between them. Like in DP, unlearning involves comparing against a hypothetical output that may not be realized in practice: for DP, the point of comparison is the algorithm output on a dataset with one sample replaced, and for certified unlearning, we compare against the output of retraining from scratch. Therefore, we only need to show that the real output— the output of the algorithm called on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
— is probabilistically indistinguishable from a valid instance of the hypothetical output.

This rationale allows us to couple the randomization (the “coin flips”) of the learning and unlearning algorithms to minimize the distances between
\theta_{t}
and
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
, as well as
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}_{t}
. Specifically, we can carefully choose the batches sampled at each time step so that they coincide as much as possible. We obtain a sensitivity bound on
\lVert\theta^{\prime}_{T}-\theta^{\prime\prime}_{K}\rVert
that holds with high probability with respect to the chosen joint distribution; in our work, we combine Markov’s inequality with first or second moment bounds, which are easier to obtain from standard analysis of SGD. We can roll the probability of failure into the
(\epsilon,\delta)
guarantee of the
relaxed Gaussian mechanism
, described in the lemma below.

Lemma 1
.

(Informal) Let
A
and
U
denote randomized algorithms that map datasets to model parameters. For any fixed dataset
\mathcal{D}
, let
x=A(\mathcal{D})
and
y=U(\mathcal{D})
denote their random outputs, where their randomness is due to the internal coin flips of the algorithm (e.g., mini-batch sampling). Let
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
be draws from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
where
\sigma\geq\frac{\Sigma\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}}{\epsilon}
. Suppose
\lVert x-y\rVert\leq\Sigma
with probability
1-\delta
, where the probability is taken over the internal randomness of
A
and
U
. Then
x
and
y
are
(\epsilon,2\delta)
-indistinguishable.

In particular, the “algorithmic randomness” for SGD refers to the mini-batch indices sampled from a fixed distribution (e.g., uniformly from
\{1,...,n\}
), where this distribution does not depend on the actual data values. Like for the classic Gaussian mechanism, we require that the bound
\Sigma
also does not encode information about the specific dataset, thereby preventing data leakage. We emphasize that the relaxed Gaussian mechanism differs from
(\epsilon,\delta,\gamma)
-
random differential privacy

[
21
,
34
]
, which considers whether
(\epsilon,\delta)
-privacy holds with probability
1-\gamma

with respect to the sampling of the dataset
from a well-behaved data distribution.

Our analysis of the R2D framework relies on characterizing unlearning as
biased
or
disturbed
SGD on a gradient system. Specifically, we can characterize the SGD iterates on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
as biased SGD iterates on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, where the
unlearning bias
or disturbance at each step is the difference between the gradients on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
.
Upon unlearning, we perform
unbiased
SGD on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
. Our results on strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions align with nonlinear contraction theory
[
28
,
37
]
that predicts that
\theta_{t}
and
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
will stay close for perturbed contracting systems (strongly convex functions), diverge linearly on perturbed semi-contracting systems (convex functions), and diverge exponentially otherwise (nonconvex functions). Like in the original R2D proof
[
30
]
, rewinding reverses the accumulation of these disturbances, drawing the unlearning trajectory closer to the retraining trajectory. Moreover, rewinding allows us to completely eliminate the impact of noise during unlearning by coupling. However, because the contraction analysis only holds with respect to a Riemannian metric, we can only achieve a first-moment sensitivity bound for the relaxed Gaussian mechanism.

In contrast, the analysis of D2D relies on additional “tricks” available for the analysis of biased gradient descent on strongly convex functions. By relying on the existence and attractivity of the global minimum of strongly convex functions, we can leverage the faster convergence of biased SGD algorithms to achieve a tighter second-moment bound that has a better dependence on
\delta
.

4.1
PSGD-R2D: Rewinding with Projected SGD

We first consider projected SGD algorithms, for which we can achieve clean unlearning guarantees with minimal assumptions. In particular, in this setting the gradient of the loss function is uniformly bounded over
\mathcal{C}
. This allows us to uniformly bound the unlearning bias without a bounded gradient assumption or clipping.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the loss function
\ell
satisfies Assumptions
1
, and within the closed, bounded, and convex set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient of
\ell
is uniformly bounded by some constant

such that for all

and

\lVert\nabla\ell(z;\theta)\rVert\leq G.

We implement PSGD-R2D (Algorithms 1 and 2 with projection) with

\sigma=\frac{\Sigma\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}}{\epsilon\delta}.

Then PSGD-R2D is an
(\epsilon,2\delta)
-unlearning algorithm for the following choices of

•

For general functions, we have

\Sigma=\frac{2Gm((1+\eta L)^{T}-(1+\eta L)^{K})}{nL}.

•

If
\ell

\Sigma=\frac{2\eta Gm(T-K)}{n}.

\eta\leq\frac{\mu}{L^{2}}
and
\gamma=\sqrt{1-\eta\mu}
we have

\Sigma=\frac{2\eta Gm(\gamma^{K}-\gamma^{T})}{n\mu}.

Our approach relies on coupling the trajectories
\theta_{t}
and
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
, and uniformly bounding the bias with

. Theorem

highlights the privacy-utility-complexity tradeoff of PSGD-R2D, through the relationship between
\Sigma
,
\epsilon
and
K
. We observe that for all settings,
\Sigma
decreases to zero as
K
increases from zero to
T
, and it decays exponentially for strongly convex functions and linearly for convex functions. For general nonconvex functions,
\Sigma
still decreases to zero as
K
goes to
T
, but at a slower rate. This reflects the contractive, semi-contractive, and expansive properties of gradient algorithms on strongly convex, convex, and nonconvex functions respectively.

For strongly convex functions, we achieve
K
that is
better than sublinear
in
T
for constant noise; in fact, it converges to a constant for large

, implying that
T-K
, the computational advantage of unlearning, is potentially
infinite

Corollary 2.1
.

If
\ell
is
\mu
-strongly convex (satisfies Assumption

), then
\Sigma
is uniformly upper bounded as follows.

\Sigma\leq\frac{2\eta Gm(1-\gamma^{T})}{n\mu}.

(6)

In addition, for constant
\Sigma
we have

K=\frac{\log(\frac{n\mu\Sigma}{2m\eta G}+\gamma^{T})}{\log(\gamma)}\leq\frac{\log(\frac{n\mu\Sigma}{2m\eta G})}{\log(\gamma)}.

Proof.
See Appendix
B.5.1
.

We note that
\frac{n\mu\Sigma}{2\eta G}+\gamma^{T}\leq 1
, so the equation of

is overall positive. For small

increases at an approximately linear rate, but as

converges to a constant value. This implies that for a given level of of privacy
(\epsilon,\delta)
and noise, the number of unlearning iterations required is uniformly upper bounded even if the number of training iterations

4.2
SGD-R2D: Rewinding with SGD

Now we consider rewinding without projection. A key challenge of analyzing SGD unlearning algorithms on an unbounded domain is that the noise and unlearning bias may also be unbounded. To control the noise, we first require a reasonable assumption that the second moment of the stochastic gradient is relatively bounded, as in Assumption
4
.

Assumption 4
.

For all datasets
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim\mathcal{Z}
, let
g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)
represent the gradient estimator constructed from a batch
\mathcal{B}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
uniformly sampled with replacement. Then the second moment of the gradient estimator is relatively bounded with constants
B,C\geq 0
as follows:

\mathbb{E}[\lVert g_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)\rVert^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}[\lVert\nabla\ell(z;\theta)\rVert^{2}]\leq B\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\rVert^{2}+C,\qquad\text{for all }\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(7)

For finite-sum problems (
1
), Assumption
4
is satisfied by strongly convex loss functions and functions that satisfy the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality, as established in
[
26
,
16
,
18
]
. In particular, for batch sampling with replacement, the constant
B
can be computed from
L
and the batch size
b
, and the constant
C
can be estimated from how close the model is to interpolation, where perfect interpolation implies
C=0
.

To handle the unlearning bias, we observe that it can be bounded by the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between the empirical distributions of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, denoted as
P_{\mathcal{D}}
and
P_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
respectively. Specifically, we show (Lemma
19
) that

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\rVert^{2}=

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}}[\nabla\ell(z;\theta)]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_{\mathcal{D}}}[\nabla\ell(z;\theta)]\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(P_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\|P_{\mathcal{D}})\;\cdot\;\mathrm{Var}_{P_{\mathcal{D}}}(\nabla\ell(z;\theta)).

This convenient bound allows us to exploit existing conditions on the noise like Assumption
4
.

Finally, when we leverage the above rationale, we note that the accumulated bias and noise effects during training can be upper bounded by the loss at initialization. To achieve a dataset-independent result, we require the following assumption that the loss is finite at initialization for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Assumption 5
.

For all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\ell(z;\theta)\geq 0
. Moreover, the loss function is bounded at initialization
\theta_{0}
by some constant
\ell_{\theta_{0}}
, such that for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have
\ell(z;\theta_{0})\leq\ell_{\theta_{0}}
.

Assumption
5
is satisfied if the underlying data distribution
\mathcal{Z}
is closed and bounded.

With the above stipulations, we are able to leverage a similar proof structure as in Section
4.1
to achieve provable SGD-R2D unlearning on unbounded domains as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that the loss function
\ell
satisfies Assumptions
1
,
4
, and
5
and we implement SGD-R2D (Algorithms 1 and 2 without projection) with

\sigma=\frac{\Sigma\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}}{\epsilon\delta}.

Then SGD-R2D is an
(\epsilon,2\delta)
-unlearning algorithm for the following choices of

•

For general functions, we have

\Sigma=\frac{(1+\eta L)^{T}-(1+\eta L)^{K}}{L}\left(3B\left(\frac{2}{\eta}\ell_{\theta_{0}}+L\eta C(T-K)\right)(\frac{3n-m}{n-m})+6(\frac{4n-3m}{n-m})C\right)^{1/2}.

If

is convex (satisfies Assumption

If
\ell
is

-strongly convex (satisfies Assumption

and

4.3
D2D: Descending with SGD

Finally, in this section, we prove that SGD-D2D achieves certified unlearning. We first show that during training, the biased SGD iterates
\{\theta_{t}\}
converge to a neighborhood of
\theta^{*^{\prime}}
, the global minimum of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, and we then show that the unbiased SGD iterates
\{\theta^{\prime\prime}_{t}\}
converge closer to
\theta^{*^{\prime}}
. We note that this differs from the original D2D proof, where Lipschitz
continuity
and strong convexity are combined to show that
\theta^{*^{\prime}}
is close to the global minimum of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
. When we exit this highly constrained setting, this property no longer holds.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that the loss function
\ell
satisfies Assumptions
1
,
3
,
4
, and
5
. Let
\eta\leq\frac{1}{BL}
and
\frac{m}{n}<\frac{1}{6B+1}
. Let

T=K+\frac{\log(\ell_{\theta_{0}})-\log(\frac{5C}{4B\mu})}{\log(\frac{1}{1-\eta\mu/2})}.

Then SGD-D2D is an
(\epsilon,2\delta)
-certified unlearning algorithm with

\sigma=\frac{\Sigma}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1.25/\delta)}{\delta}},

where

\Sigma^{2}=\frac{5C}{\mu^{2}B}((1-\frac{\eta\mu}{2})^{2K}+2(1-\frac{\eta\mu}{2})^{K})+\frac{4L\eta C}{\mu^{2}}.

Proof.
See Appendix
B.7
.

Theorem
4
shows that SGD-D2D can achieve certified unlearning as long as the proportion of unlearned data is small enough, which allows the unlearning bias to be “folded into” the standard unbiased SGD analysis, yielding linear convergence to the global minimum. The noise decays
exponentially
with the number of unlearning iterates
K
. This mirrors the original D2D result in
[
31
]
, but the stochastic setting yields an
O(\eta)
constant term at the end which cannot be eliminated. Moreover, without the bounded gradient/Lipschitz smoothness assumption of
[
31
]
, the computation advantage of unlearning,
T-K
, depends on the initialization; in fact, if
\theta_{0}
happens to be close enough to the optimum of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, then unlearning may not be more efficient than retraining from scratch.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we prove certified unlearning guarantees for (P)SGD-R2D and SGD-D2D. Our analysis demonstrates that we can achieve a tighter dependence on
\delta
for D2D-SGD, due to a second moment bound obtained from all trajectories coming close to the same unique minimum. SGD-R2D, which relies on the underlying contractivity of the gradient system, has a looser dependence on
\delta
because these properties only hold under Riemannian metrics that satisfy the triangle inequality, leading to a first-moment bound. However, SGD-R2D is always more computationally efficient than retraining, and for strongly convex functions it can have a potentially infinite computational advantage.

The next steps include computing utility and generalization guarantees, as well as developing experiments to compare various algorithms. In addition, there are several potential future directions of this work. First, we note that other certified unlearning algorithms, such as
[
8
]
and
[
27
]
, follow a D2D-like framework with clipped or noisy gradient updates, instead of vanilla SGD. Combining their approaches with R2D instead could yield improved guarantees, especially on nonconvex functions. Second, our results for PSGD rely on sampling
with replacement
to achieve tighter bounds. Whenever a sample from
Z
is selected during the generation of
\theta_{t}
, we can replace it with an i.i.d. sample from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
for producing
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
, maintaining a “valid” run of
\theta^{\prime}_{t}
. However, if sampling occurs without replacement, then drawing one sample affects the probability of the next. Developing the corresponding theory for sampling without replacement, which is more commonly implemented in practice, would be a useful future direction.

Appendix A
Additional Discussion of Related Work

Differential Privacy.

Differential privacy (DP)
[
13
]
is a mathematical framework for privacy that leverages noise injection to limit the impact of the inclusion or exclusion of any one data sample on the output of the algorithm.

Definition 3
.

[
14
]
A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
with domain
\mathbb{N}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all
S\subset Range(\mathcal{M})
and for all adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{|\mathcal{X}|}

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in S]+\delta.

A common DP technique is the Gaussian mechanism, which scales Gaussian noise to the
deterministic
global sensitivity, a worst-case bound on how much the algorithm output shifts when the underlying dataset is changed
[
14
]
. This technique has been applied to privatizing deterministic functions such as dataset queries or full-batch gradient descent, as well as
randomized
algorithms such as SGD
[
1
]

[
40
]

[
42
]

[
6
]
. To bound the sensitivity, the well-known DP-SGD algorithm
[
1
]
clips the gradient at each step to minimize the impact of any single data sample, and the black-box SGD algorithm
[
40
]
requires a uniformly bounded gradient.

Biased SGD.

Our work relies on interpreting the certified unlearning problem as a form of biased or disturbed gradient descent. There are two complementary viewpoints in the literature. First, several works analyze biased SGD from a classic optimization perspective to achieve convergence to a minimum or stationary point. Typically, these works establish minimal viable assumptions on the bias or noise to achieve convergence. For example,
[
11
]
summarizes biased SGD results for nonconvex and strongly convex (or PL) loss functions. In addition,
[
22
]
considers bandit convex optimization with absolutely bounded bias and on a bounded convex domain, and
[
12
]
considers biased SGD schemes that satisfy a specific “memory-biased” property, like SARAH and SVRG. In our work, we utilize the groundwork laid in
[
2
]
to show unlearning for D2D on strongly convex functions.

The second viewpoint of biased SGD is through the lens of nonlinear contraction theory
[
28
]

[
37
]
, characterizing the bias at every step as disturbances to a noisy gradient system. The trajectory stability, which determines how far a gradient trajectory will diverge when disturbed, can be used to bound the distance between SGD training on two different loss functions. We leverage well-known properties of these systems to analyze stochastic R2D unlearning.

), if we know
\ell

\mu
-strongly convex, we know that

\langle\theta-\theta^{\prime},\nabla\ell(z;\theta)-\nabla\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})\rangle\geq\mu\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}.

We have

\displaystyle\lVert\theta-\eta\nabla\ell(z;\theta)-\theta^{\prime}+\eta\nabla\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})\rVert^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}-2\eta\mu\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}+\eta^{2}L^{2}\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-2\eta\mu+\eta^{2}L^{2})\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eta\leq\frac{\mu}{L^{2}}}{\leq}

\displaystyle(1-\eta\mu)\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert^{2}.

Finally, to prove the last part (
16
), we have

\displaystyle\lVert T^{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}_{\eta}(\theta)-T^{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}_{\eta}(\theta^{\prime})\rVert=

\displaystyle\lVert\theta-\eta\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}|}}\sum_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\nabla\ell(z;\theta)-\theta^{\prime}+\eta\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}|}}\sum_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\nabla\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}|}}\sum_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\theta-\nabla\ell(z;\theta)-\theta^{\prime}+\nabla\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})\right]\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}|}}\sum_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\lVert\theta-\nabla\ell(z;\theta)-\theta^{\prime}+\nabla\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{D}|}}\sum_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\lVert T^{z}_{\eta}(\theta)-T^{z}_{\eta}(\theta^{\prime})\rVert\leq\gamma\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert.

∎

Lemma 16
.

Suppose there exists
\gamma>0
such that
\lVert T^{z}_{\eta}(\theta)-T^{z}_{\eta}(\theta^{\prime})\rVert\leq\gamma\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Suppose
w_{t}

.

Proof.

In the following, we provide an explicit proof that does not require any prior knowledge for easy understanding. However, we note that the result ultimately follows from defining the empirical distributions of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
as
P_{\mathcal{D}}
and
P_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, and applying Lemma
23
.

Define
w(x)
as follows

w(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{n-m}&x\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\\
0&x\in\mathcal{D}\backslash\mathcal{D}^{\prime}.\\
\end{cases}

(27)

Then we can write
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\theta)
in terms of
w(z_{i})
for all
z_{i}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
as follows,

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})w(z_{i}).

Then for

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\theta)\rVert^{2}=

\displaystyle\lVert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})w(z_{i})\rVert^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})(1-w(z_{i}))\rVert^{2}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\theta)\rVert^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})\rVert^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-w(z_{i}))^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})\rVert^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}(m+(n-m)(1-\frac{n}{n-m})^{2}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}}[\lVert\nabla\ell(\theta;z_{i})\rVert^{2}]\frac{m}{n-m}.

The theory underlying Lemma
19
is that we use the second moment bound to write the difference in terms of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between
P_{\mathcal{D}}
and
P_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, where
w(x)
represents a discrete version of the Radon Nikodym derivative
\frac{dP_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}}{dP_{\mathcal{D}}}
. The
\chi^{2}
-divergence then evaluates to
\frac{m}{n-m}
.

∎
