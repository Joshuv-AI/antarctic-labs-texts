Evaluating KLHDC8A as a biomarker for glioma: impact on survival and proliferation through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway
Authors: Yu Tian, Xiaofei Hu, Xiaoling Wang, Youcai Huang, Junfeng Hao, Yu Guo, Chao Zhu, Yanyang Tu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glioma is one of the most common malignant tumors and shows a high metastasis rate and poor prognosis. KLHDC8A has been implicated in several cancers, but its role in glioma and its potential as a biomarker remain unclear. We conducted a pan-cancer analysis of KLHDC8A using multiple databases and assessed its expression levels, survival associations, and diagnostic utility. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate KLHDC8A’s prognostic and diagnostic potential. Additionally, we investigated KLHDC8A's role in glioma cell proliferation and its involvement in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Our pan-cancer analysis revealed significant dysregulation of KLHDC8A across various tumors. Elevated KLHDC8A expression was associated with poor overall survival (OS) in cancers including ACC, KIRP, LGG, and STAD. Disease-specific survival (DSS) analysis indicated worse outcomes in ACC, KIRP, and LGG with high KLHDC8A levels. ROC curve analysis demonstrated KLHDC8A's strong diagnostic potential for glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and lower-grade gliomas (LGG), with AUC values of 0.920 and 0.748, respectively. In glioma patients, high KLHDC8A expression correlated with shorter OS and DSS in LGG, while no significant correlation was observed in GBM. Multivariate Cox regression confirmed KLHDC8A as an independent prognostic factor for OS and DSS in LGG. Knockdown experiments in glioma cell lines U251 and U87 showed that KLHDC8A silencing led to reduced cell proliferation and colony formation, which was associated with downregulation of key proteins in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Overall, KLHDC8A is a promising biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis of LGG, and its high expression is linked to poor survival outcomes. The gene's involvement in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway underscores its role in glioma progression, highlighting KLHDC8A’s potential as a therapeutic target and diagnostic marker, particularly for LGG.

Introduction

Gliomas are a major type of central nervous system tumors, accounting for approximately 30% to 40% of all brain tumors [1]. Gliomas have a wide age distribution, but the majority of cases occur in adults, particularly between the ages of 45 and 70 [2]. Gliomas can also be seen in children and adolescents, especially low-grade gliomas. Familial genetic disorders such as Neurofibromatosis and Li-Fraumeni syndrome are associated with the development of gliomas [3, 4]. The incidence of gliomas can vary significantly across different regions, with generally higher rates observed in developed countries. These geographic differences may be attributed to a variety of factors, including environmental influences, lifestyle habits, and disparities in healthcare resources. Additionally, certain racial or ethnic groups may exhibit varying incidence rates, further contributing to the overall landscape of glioma prevalence [5, 6]. For instance, studies have indicated that the incidence of certain types of gliomas may be lower among African populations compared to higher rates observed in Caucasian populations. Patients diagnosed with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) have not experienced significant improvements in their survival rates despite undergoing conventional treatments such as surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy. The optimal methods for identifying and treating adult gliomas remain a topic of considerable debate [7, 8]. Molecular studies on human cancers over the last decade have shed light on the intricate genetic changes linked to glioma's onset and progression [9, 10]. Combining phenotypic and genotypic classifications of gliomas enhances diagnostic accuracy and precision, potentially leading to improved prognoses and individualized treatment plans. To advance targeted therapies, it is essential to identify reliable biomarkers. While several indicators have been discovered, there is an increasing need for additional dependable markers to guide clinical decision-making in glioma treatment.

One protein that is up-regulated in cancer is KLHDC8A, which encodes a kelch domain-containing protein. The Kelch superfamily, which comprises 63 genes that code for proteins and 3 genes that do not, is comprised of proteins that contain the Kelch domain [11]. KLHDC8A is associated with various biological activities, including cellular development, differentiation, and stress responses. While its specific roles are still under investigation, it is thought to play a significant part in cellular regulation and signaling pathways [12, 13]. KLHDC8A is expressed in various tissues, with levels varying across different cell types and biological contexts. Its expression profile offers valuable insights into its functional significance in diverse biological settings. Research indicates that KLHDC8A may play a role in cancer; altered expression levels have been associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis in certain malignancies. For instance, elevated KLHDC8A expression correlates with decreased overall survival and disease-specific survival in glioma patients. Additionally, KLHDC8A is being investigated for its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in various cancers [14–16]. In addition, high expression of KLHDC8A in ovarian cancer and its prognostic value was also confirmed [17]. However, little is known about the role of KLHDC8A or its molecular mechanism in glioma formation.

In this study, we conducted a pan-cancer analysis to investigate the role of KLHDC8A in tumor progression across various cancer types. We performed Kaplan–Meier survival analyses and ROC curve evaluations to assess KLHDC8A’s prognostic value, with a particular focus on lower-grade glioma (LGG) and glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). Additionally, we conducted in vitro experiments to examine the effects of KLHDC8A knockdown on glioma cell proliferation and its impact on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.

Methods

Data collection and preprocessing

To explore the potential association between KLHDC8A expression and clinical prognosis of glioma patients, we retrieved data from several databases: CGGA, TCGA and GTEx. The raw expression data were downloaded, and normalization was performed using R (version 4.3.0). Specifically, expression levels from the GTEx and TCGA databases were log2-transformed and adjusted for batch effects using the sva package to ensure comparability across different platforms. Missing values in the clinical and expression data were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors algorithm implemented in the impute package in R. We analyzed KLHDC8A expression across various glioma subtypes and conducted differential expression analysis between normal and glioma tissues using the limma package. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was employed to determine statistical significance by adjusting p-values. Expression matrices for both healthy and cancerous tissues can be found in these databases.

Pan-cancer analysis of KLHDC8A expression

The pan-cancer expression profile data were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), which includes normalized RNA-Seq data from TCGA across multiple cancer types. Expression data for KLHDC8A was extracted for all available tumor types, along with corresponding clinical metadata such as overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). To assess KLHDC8A expression across different cancer types compared to normal tissues, we conducted Wilcoxon rank sum tests and signed rank tests. This non-parametric approach was chosen to accommodate the potentially non-normal distribution of gene expression data. For several cancer types, we evaluated the expression levels in tumor and normal tissues, applying the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to adjust for significance discrepancies. We constructed Cox proportional hazards regression models for overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) to examine the prognostic value of KLHDC8A. These models were adjusted for relevant clinical covariates, including age, gender, tumor stage, and treatment status. Separate models were fitted for each cancer type to explore cancer-specific associations.

Prognostic analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were created to evaluate the predictive importance of KLHDC8A expression in glioma patients. According to the median KLHDC8A expression level in the glioma group, patients were categorized as either "high" or "low" expression. In order to compare the two groups' survival results, including OS, the log-rank test was employed. At p < 0.05, a significant level was established. To quantify the relative risk of death associated with greater KLHDC8A expression, hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were included with the Kaplan–Meier analysis. Using Cox proportional hazards models, the HR was estimated. Additional evaluation of KLHDC8A expression's predictive potential in glioma prognosis was conducted using ROC curve analysis. Evaluation of KLHDC8A expression as a predictor of overall survival after five years was done using the ROC curve. To evaluate the discriminatory power of the model, we calculated the area under the ROC curve (AUC), where values closer to 1.0 indicate better sensitivity and specificity. The independence of KLHDC8A as a predictive factor was confirmed through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses.

Cell culture and cell transfection

We got the normal astrocyte cell line, human glioma cell lines TG905, LN308, T98, and U251 from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Once received, every cell line was grown in DMEM (Gibco, Rockville, MD, USA) with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). For the best possible cell growth, the incubation temperature was 37 °C and the humidity level was 5% CO₂. The shRNAs that target KLHDC8A were manufactured by Jikai Gene in Shanghai, China. An online RNA interference (RNAi) design tool was used to validate the specificity and low off-target effects of the shRNAs, which were designed to target conserved areas of the KLHDC8A mRNA. Stable transfection and cell selection under puromycin selection pressure are made possible by cloning the shRNA sequences into the lentiviral vector pcDNA-EF2-puromycin. As a control for all tests, the same lentiviral vector was also used to clone a non-targeting scrambled shRNA gene. The Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to transfect U251 and U87 cells with lentiviral vectors carrying KLHDC8A-specific shRNAs or scrambled control shRNAs, according to the manufacturer's procedure. After 48 h of transfection, the lentiviral particles were collected, filtered, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR assay

The TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used to extract total RNA from astrocytes and glioma cell lines (TG905, LN308, T98, U87, U251) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To lyse the cells, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added per well after cell harvesting from the growth plates. Two hundred microliters of chloroform were added after homogenization, and the samples were spun at 12,000 times the force of gravity for fifteen minutes at 4 °C. The RNA-containing water phase was cautiously gathered, and 500 µL of isopropanol was added to precipitate the RNA. The RNA pellet was resuspended in water that did not contain RNase after being washed with 75% ethanol following centrifugation. PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) cDNA synthesis was performed according to the manufacturer's procedure. Twenty microliters of RNAse-free water were added to four and a half milliliters of 5 × PrimeScript buffer, one microliter of PrimeScript RT enzyme mix, one microliter of random hexamers, and one microliter of oligo(dT) primers. The enzyme was inactivated at 85 °C for 5 s after the reaction had been incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The cDNA that was produced was then frozen at -20 °C until it could be used again in real-time PCR. An ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR equipment (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used to conduct real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) with the SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). A 20 µL reaction volume was used for the reactions, which included 1 µL of cDNA template, 10 µL of 2 × SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward and reverse primers (10 µM each), and 8.2 µL of nuclease-free water. The comparative Ct (2^ − ΔΔCt) approach was used to do relative quantification of KLHDC8A expression. Fold changes in gene expression were calculated relative to the control (normal astrocyte cells), and Ct values for KLHDC8A were adjusted to GAPDH as an internal control. For determination of the expression of KLHDC8A, the following primers were used: forward, 5′-ATGGAGGTGCCTAACGTCAAG-3′, reverse 5′- CCGTTGTCGTCACATCCCC-3′; for GAPDH: forward 5′-GTCAAGGCTGAGAACGGGAA-3′, reverse 5′-AAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCTC-3′.

Cell proliferation assay

In vitro cell proliferation tests were conducted using the U251 and U87 cell lines to assess the effect of KLHDC8A knockdown on glioma cell proliferation. Complete DMEM (supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin) was used to resuspend trypsinized U251 and U87 cells after 36 h of transfection. To guarantee uniform seeding density in all experimental wells, the cell density was calibrated to 3 × 104 cells per well using a hemocytometer. 100 µL of single-cell suspensions were added to each well of 96-well plates. Three independent platings were performed on each treatment group (shKLHDC8A and scrambled control) to guarantee statistical reliability. For as long as 96 h, the plates were kept in an incubator with a humidifier and 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after seeding, the CCK-8 reagent was used to evaluate cell proliferation at 24-h intervals. Each well was supplemented with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution at each time point, and the cells were left to incubate at 37 °C for an extra four hours. Each well's optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) after the 4-h incubation with CCK-8. One way to measure cell proliferation over time is by looking at the optical density (OD) measurements, which are directly related to the number of viable cells. Proliferation curves for cells were created using the OD values measured at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. Relative cell proliferation rates were presented as a percentage of the mean OD value at 24 h after triplicate wells were measured.

Colony formation assay

A colony formation experiment was conducted to evaluate the clonogenic potential and long-term proliferative capacity of U251 and U87 cells after KLHDC8A knockdown. Cells from U251 and U87 were transfected for 36 h, then trypsinized, hemocytometer counted, and resuspended in full DMEM (10% FBS). With a final amount of 2 mL DMEM, 500 cells per well were seeded into 6-well plates for both the shKLHDC8A and scrambled control groups. To make sure that each cell could grow into its own distinct colony, a low seeding density was used. For 10 days, the cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO₂. In order to keep the cells growing at their optimal rate, the medium was changed every three days. The cells were given permission to multiply and cluster into colonies, which were characterized as groups of 50 cells or more. Gently aspirating the medium and washing the cells twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove remaining media was done after 10 days of culture, when noticeable colonies had formed. The next step was to fix the cells at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After the colonies were fixed, they were stained for 20 min with a 0.5% crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in order to see them. Because crystal violet attaches to DNA, counting and identifying colonies is a breeze. To remove any excess stain, the wells were delicately rinsed with distilled water until the background became clear and the stained colonies remained. At room temperature, the plates were let to dry naturally. We used a digital camera (Olympus Inc.) attached to a microscope to take pictures of the dyed colonies, and then we numbered them by hand.

Western blot assay

After 48 h of transfection, U251 and U87 GBM cells were analyzed for shRNAs targeting KLHDC8A or control shRNAs that were scrambled. After rinsing with cold PBS, cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China), which also contained protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich). To guarantee full lysis, cell lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min while being sometimes vortexed. To remove cell debris, the lysates were spun at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Then, the liquid that remained after centrifugation, which included total proteins, was collected. We followed the manufacturer's instructions to determine the protein concentrations using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit from Beyotime, China. The absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) after 10 µL of each protein sample was combined with BCA reagent. A standard curve was made using bovine serum albumin (BSA) standards to determine protein concentrations. The proteins were denatured by boiling 30 µg of protein per sample with 5 times the volume of loading buffer at 95 °C for 5 min. Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was used with a 10% polyacrylamide gel to separate the protein samples. Until the dye front reached the bottom of the gel, electrophoresis was performed at a constant voltage of 100 V for around 1.5 h. Using a wet transfer technique, the proteins were moved from the gel onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA) after separation. The transfer was carried out in a transfer buffer containing 25% methanol, 25% tris, and 192% glycine at 4 °C for 1 h at 100 V. A short wash in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) and blocking were performed on the PVDF membrane following transfer. After preparing 5% skim milk in TBST, the PVDF membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature with careful shaking to avoid non-specific antibody binding. To get rid of any leftover blocking solution, the membrane was rinsed three times with TBST. Primary antibodies specific to the following proteins were added to the membrane and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. These antibodies were from Cell Signaling Technology and were used at a ratio of 1:1,000 for β-catenin, CDK4 and CDK6, respectively. GAPDH was added at a ratio of 1:5,000 from Beyotime. To achieve the highest level of binding specificity, the antibodies were diluted in TBST that included 1% BSA. The membrane was washed three times with TBST, with each wash lasting 10 min, after being incubated overnight. One hour of incubation at room temperature followed the primary antibody incubation, and then the membrane was treated with secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (anti-rabbit IgG, 1:1,000; Beyotime, China). To eliminate any unbound antibodies, the membrane was washed three times with TBST, with each wash lasting 10 min, after the secondary antibody incubation. A Millipore, USA-based enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent was used to identify the bound antibodies. The Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, USA) was used to visualize the signal after the membrane was incubated with the ECL solution for 1 min. The strength of the bands dictated the adjustment of exposure periods, which were then used to collect and preserve photos for analysis. To conserve valuable samples, we used partial membranes (not full-length) in our experiments and cropped the membranes to focus on the target protein region.

Statistical analysis

The means ± SDs are used to express the study measurements, and each assay was conducted three times. We used Student's t test to find statistical differences between two groups and one-way ANOVA to find statistical differences among several groups. When comparing multiple groups, we first ran a one-way ANOVA and then used Tukey's post hoc test to look at how the groups stacked up against each other when comparing them in pairs. In order to determine if there was a statistically significant difference between the group means, we used a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) that controlled for type I error and multiple comparisons. An area under the curve (AUC) was computed in ROC curve analysis to evaluate the accuracy of KLHDC8A expression in predicting patient outcomes. The AUC values were used to display the calculated sensitivity and specificity as well. The statistical studies were carried out using the following software: R (v.4.0.3), RStudio (v.1.3.1093), SPSS version 20.0, GraphPad Prism 8.0, And Microsoft Excel 2016. To be deemed statistically significant, a P value had to be lower than 0.05.

Results

Pan-cancer analysis of KLHDC8A

Figure 1A, B showed the results of our pan-cancer investigation of KLHDC8A, which we conducted to investigate its possible role in tumor progression. We discovered that KLHDC8A showed dysregulation at a high level in numerous tumor types. There was an upregulation of KLHDC8A expression in several malignancies, suggesting a possible function for this gene in tumor biology. Tumors with increased or decreased KLHDC8A expression were highlighted in box plots that visualized the expression levels of KLHDC8A in various cancer types. To assess the effect of KLHDC8A expression on overall survival in different cancer types, a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted. Figure 1C showed that in various malignancies, including ACC, KIRP, LGG, and STAD, elevated KLHDC8A expression was linked to poor overall survival. The statistical significance of differences between the groups with high and low KLHDC8A expression was assessed using log-rank tests, after survival curves had been constructed. Similarly, we looked at disease-specific survival rates in relation to KLHDC8A expression. For ACC, KIRP, and LGG, a lower DSS was associated with higher KLHDC8A expression (Fig. 1D). The data was presented as Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and to find out how KLHDC8A affected DSS after controlling for other variables, the researchers used Cox proportional hazards regression models. Using ROC curve analysis, we assessed KLHDC8A's diagnostic utility in distinguishing tumor from normal tissues. There was reasonable diagnostic performance for LGG, as shown by the ROC analysis, which yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.748 (Fig. 1E). The ROC curve demonstrated that KLHDC8A could be a useful biomarker for LGG diagnosis. The AUC for KLHDC8A was 0.920, indicating a strong diagnostic potential for GBM (Fig. 1F). Given its high AUC value, KLHDC8A shows promise as a reliable diagnostic marker for GBM due to its significant ability to differentiate GBM specimens from normal tissues. The results of the pan-cancer analysis underscore the critical role of KLHDC8A dysregulation across various tumor types, its association with poor prognosis, and its diagnostic utility, particularly in LGG. The prognostic value of KLHDC8A in glioma patients

In order to assess OS and DSS among glioma patients with high and low KLHDC8A expression, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was employed. We divided the patients into two groups according to their median KLHDC8A expression level. Both LGG and GBM were represented by survival curves. Figure 2A showed that compared to LGG patients with low KLHDC8A expression, those with high KLHDC8A expression had noticeably shorter OS and DSS. Patients with LGG who have elevated KLHDC8A levels do not have a good prognosis, since the median survival durations were significantly shorter in the high-expression group. For LGG patients, time-dependent ROC curve analysis was performed to further assess the prognostic usefulness of KLHDC8A. The ROC curves showed that KLHDC8A expression was highly predictive of survival at one, three, and five years (Fig. 2B), with statistically significant AUC values. The AUC values demonstrated that KLHDC8A successfully separated patients into groups according to their long-term survival chances. Neither OS nor DSS were significantly correlated with KLHDC8A expression in GBM patients (Fig. 2C). These results raise the possibility that KLHDC8A is not a good predictive marker for GBM compared to LGG. The purpose of this study was to employ multivariate Cox regression analysis to identify any associations between KLHDC8A expression and OS or DSS in LGG and GBM patients. Figure 2D and 2E showed that the results show that KLHDC8A expression is a predictor of OS and DSS in LGG patients. This finding highlighted the potential clinical value of KLHDC8A for LGG patient prognosis stratification. Further evidence for KLHDC8A's predictive usefulness was found in analyses of glioma samples from the CGGA database. Figure 3A showed that glioma patients with low KLHDC8A expression had a better overall survival rate compared to those with high KLHDC8A levels. Figure 3B showed that KLHDC8A had strong predictive performance for one-, three-, and five-year survival when analyzed using ROC curves. The results show that KLHDC8A can be used to predict how long a patient will live. Several clinical variables including KLHDC8A were found to be significant risk factors for survival in the univariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 3C). Figure 3D demonstrated that, after adjusting for various factors, multivariate Cox regression analysis identifies KLHDC8A as an independent predictor of overall survival. Analysis of the CGGA data revealed significant associations between KLHDC8A expression and several clinical characteristics, including age, tumor grade, chemotherapy status, IDH mutation status, histology, and 1p/19q codeletion status(Fig. 3E). These connections indicate that KLHDC8A plays a role in multiple aspects of glioma pathogenesis and may influence prognosis and treatment. In lower-grade glioma (LGG), KLHDC8A is associated with poorer survival outcomes and serves as a standalone prognostic marker. The findings of this comprehensive study underscore KLHDC8A's substantial predictive relevance. However, its applicability in GBM remained limited. Knockdown of KLHDC8A suppressed the proliferation of glioma cells via regulating Wnt/β‐catenin pathway in vitro

We evaluated KLHDC8A expression across a range of glioma cell lines to identify those exhibiting the highest expression levels. The analysis indicated significantly elevated mRNA and protein levels of KLHDC8A in five glioma cell lines, as illustrated in Fig. 4A. Notably, U251 and U87 cells displayed the highest KLHDC8A expression levels, which led to their selection for subsequent functional studies. To investigate the functional role of KLHDC8A, we utilized short hairpin RNA (shRNA) plasmids to achieve targeted knockdown of KLHDC8A in U251 and U87 cells. The efficiency of knockdown was confirmed through qPCR and Western blot analysis, demonstrating a reduction in KLHDC8A expression exceeding 60% (Fig. 4B). Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. U251 and U87 cells with KLHDC8A knockdown displayed significantly reduced proliferation compared to control cells. Absorbance measurements at 450 nm indicated a decrease in cell viability in the knockdown groups (Fig. 4C). To further assess the impact on cell proliferation, we conducted a colony formation assay. U251 and U87 cells with KLHDC8A knockdown exhibited fewer and smaller colonies compared to the control cells, indicating impaired long-term cell growth (Fig. 4D). Given the observed impact on cell proliferation, we investigated the potential involvement of KLHDC8A in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This pathway is known to regulate cell growth and proliferation through its downstream targets [18–20]. Western blot analysis was performed to measure the levels of key proteins involved in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including β-catenin, CDK4, and CDK6. Silencing of KLHDC8A resulted in a notable decrease in the expression of these proteins (Fig. 4E). The knockdown of KLHDC8A in glioma cells led to a significant suppression of cell proliferation, which is associated with the downregulation of key components in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Discussion

Surgical excision, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy are some of the current clinical treatments for gliomas. The main method is surgery, which tries to remove the tumor as much as possible while keeping the nervous system functioning normally [21, 22]. Post-surgical radiation therapy is employed to eliminate residual cancer cells, often in combination with chemotherapy, particularly for malignant gliomas such as glioblastoma. Targeted therapies, such as bevacizumab, aim to inhibit tumor angiogenesis and slow tumor growth. Meanwhile, immunotherapy is still being investigated but shows promising potential. Additionally, clinical trials provide opportunities for exploring new treatment options [10, 23–25]. Regarding prognosis, glioblastomas generally have a poor prognosis, with an average survival time of about 12–15 months [26, 27]. Low-grade gliomas tend to have a better prognosis, particularly when detected early and successfully surgically resected, with survival times potentially extending to 5 years or more [28, 29]. High-grade gliomas, on the other hand, usually have a worse prognosis, with significantly shorter survival times compared to low-grade gliomas. Clinical biomarkers play a crucial role in the diagnosis and treatment of gliomas. The methylation status of O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) is a crucial biomarker for predicting the response to temozolomide; however, it is only effective for a subset of patients. Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations are associated with a more favorable prognosis, particularly in low-grade gliomas, although they are less prevalent in high-grade gliomas [30]. 1p/19q codeletion is common in diffuse low-grade gliomas and is linked to a better prognosis, but is not applicable to high-grade gliomas [31–33]. Genetic mutations such as K-ras and B-raf help identify the biological characteristics of the tumor and guide targeted therapy, though their clinical application is still under study [34, 35]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers can aid in monitoring tumor progression or recurrence but involve invasive procedures and may be less sensitive in early stages. Thus, more sensitive biomarkers were important for glioma patients.

Aa a newly identified tumor-related gene, the expression and function of KLHDC8A was rarely reported in glioma. Lee et al. reported that identified KLHDC8A as a novel epigenetically driven oncogene in glioblastoma through superenhancer landscape analysis. KLHDC8A promotes the assembly of primary cilia by binding to CCT, thereby activating the Hedgehog signaling pathway. Targeting KLHDC8A demonstrates significant antitumor effects on GSCs, and a combined therapeutic strategy targeting Aurora B/C kinases and the Hedgehog signaling pathway shows enhanced therapeutic efficacy [13]. Zhu et al. identified KLHDC8A as a key oncogene in high-grade gliomas, with its expression significantly linked to poor prognosis. Functional assays revealed that KLHDC8A knockdown inhibits glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and promotes apoptosis. KLHDC8A overexpression enhances tumorigenesis through ERK and P38 MAPK activation, which can be reversed by specific inhibitors, and its expression is stimulated by lactate produced from glycolysis, highlighting KLHDC8A as a promising prognostic marker and therapeutic target [14]. Although they reported KLHDC8A as an oncogene in glioma progression, its function and mechanisms in glioma remained largely unclear. In this study, the pan-cancer analysis of KLHDC8A revealed its significant dysregulation across multiple tumor types, with notably increased expression in certain cancers. High KLHDC8A levels were associated with poor overall survival in cancers such as adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), LGG, and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and with reduced disease-specific survival in ACC, KIRP, and LGG. Diagnostic evaluation showed KLHDC8A had a moderate performance in distinguishing LGG from normal tissues, with an AUC of 0.748, and exhibited strong diagnostic capability for glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), with an AUC of 0.920. These findings highlight KLHDC8A's potential as a valuable biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, especially in GBM, underscoring its significant role in tumor progression and its promise in clinical applications. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that high KLHDC8A expression is associated with significantly shorter OS and DSS in patients with LGG. Time-dependent ROC curve analysis further validated KLHDC8A's strong prognostic value for predicting one-year, three-year, and five-year survival in LGG. In contrast, KLHDC8A expression did not demonstrate a significant correlation with OS or DSS in GBM. Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed KLHDC8A as an independent prognostic factor for LGG, with supporting data from the CGGA indicating its association with various clinical features. These findings establish KLHDC8A as a significant prognostic marker for LGG, where high expression correlates with poorer survival outcomes and serves as an independent predictor of prognosis. The strong prognostic value in LGG is supported by multiple analyses, including time-dependent ROC curves and multivariate Cox regression. However, KLHDC8A’s limited utility in GBM suggests that its role might be specific to certain glioma subtypes. The reason why KLHDC8A does not demonstrate strong prognostic value in GBM may be related to the high heterogeneity and complex molecular characteristics of GBM. GBM and LGG differ significantly in terms of molecular subtypes, tumor microenvironment, genomic instability, and immune evasion mechanisms, which could influence the role of KLHDC8A in different types of gliomas. Additionally, the higher malignancy of GBM, often accompanied by more complex signaling pathways and multiple mutations, may obscure the significance of KLHDC8A as an independent prognostic marker. The correlations with clinical features, such as age and tumor grade, further underline KLHDC8A’s involvement in glioma pathology. Overall, KLHDC8A shows promise as a valuable biomarker for LGG diagnosis and prognosis, which could inform patient stratification and treatment strategies in clinical practice.

The Wnt/β-catenin system controls cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and stem cell self-renewal, making it an important biological signaling route [36, 37]. The binding of Wnt signaling molecules to the Frizzled receptors and co-receptors LRP5/6 activates this pathway. The process of β-catenin's breakdown is halted by this interaction, which causes it to build up in the cytoplasm and eventually go into the nucleus [38–40]. In order to activate genes associated with the cell cycle, proliferation, and stem cell functions, β-catenin interacts with Tcf/Lef transcription factors within the nucleus. The formation of different types of cancers is tightly linked to the abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [41–43]. For instance, colon cancer patients with APC gene mutations have persistent activation of this pathway, which aids in tumor development and advancement. On top of that, this route is associated with cancer stem cells, which are involved in carcinogenesis and resistance to drugs; these cells can self-renew and differentiate in multiple directions; and they are frequently controlled by Wnt signaling. When gliomas activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway abnormally, β-catenin tends to accumulate in the tumor, which boosts its ability to activate transcription in the nucleus and may promote the growth and survival of glioma cells. Glioma stem cells, which are linked to the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, can self-renew and play a role in tumor medication resistance and recurrence [44–46]. Glioma stem cell activity, and by extension, treatment and prognosis, could be affected by modulating this route. This study demonstrated that glioma cell growth was significantly reduced upon knockdown of KLHDC8A. Our focus was on the relationship between KLHDC8A expression and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in order to investigate the possible mechanism of KLHDC8A's oncogenic functions in the progression of gliomas. The role of KLHDC8A in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which controls cell growth and proliferation, was examined in the study. Key proteins in this pathway, including β-catenin, CDK4, and CDK6, had their expression significantly reduced when KLHDC8A was silenced, according to the data. According to the results, KLHDC8A probably regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, making it essential for the growth and proliferation of glioma cells. The fact that β-catenin, CDK4, and CDK6 levels were found to drop when KLHDC8A was silenced suggests that KLHDC8A might have a favorable role in regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. This control may entail enhancing the stability and activity of β-catenin, which triggers the production of genes that are crucial for the progression and proliferation of the cell cycle, as downstream targets. Knocking down KLHDC8A significantly reduces cell growth, suggesting it may be a therapeutic target for gliomas. The activity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway can be influenced and glioma growth can be controlled by adjusting KLHDC8A levels. The significance of these results highlights the need for additional research into the function of KLHDC8A in gliomas and its possible use as a therapeutic target, especially in developing methods to stop the abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway seen in these tumors.

This article has several limitations. Firstly, although the study investigates KLHDC8A’s involvement in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, it does not explore the underlying molecular mechanisms in depth. Additional functional studies are necessary to elucidate the precise role of KLHDC8A in glioma progression. Secondly, the absence of animal experiments in our study limits a comprehensive understanding of KLHDC8A's role in glioma progression. Animal models can provide biological validation within a physiological context, thereby enhancing the assessment of KLHDC8A's function and its potential therapeutic effects. Therefore, future research should focus on validating the role of KLHDC8A in appropriate animal models to further our understanding of its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target.

Conclusion

Our pan-cancer analysis reveals that KLHDC8A is dysregulated across various tumor types, with high expression linked to poor overall and disease-specific survival in several cancers, including LGG. In gliomas, KLHDC8A expression correlates with adverse prognosis, particularly in lower-grade gliomas (LGG), where it acts as an independent prognostic marker. Functional studies show that KLHDC8A knockdown in glioma cell lines suppresses cell proliferation through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These results underscore KLHDC8A’s potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, especially for LGG. Future research should focus on validating KLHDC8A’s role in GBM and exploring its therapeutic potential, including targeted interventions to modulate KLHDC8A activity in gliomas.
