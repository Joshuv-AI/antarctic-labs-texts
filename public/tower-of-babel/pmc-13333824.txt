Influence of acid structure and acidity strength on thermochemical, in-situ nitrogen generation
Authors: Shakiba Sharifi Tashnizi, Behnam Sedaee, Jahan B. Ghasemi, Yasin Rigi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sodium nitrite and ammonium chloride are commonly used thermochemicals in the oil field, mostly for in-situ nitrogen (N2) generation—a developing technique for enhancing oil recovery and eliminating blockages. Additionally, the kinetics of this reaction, particularly when activated with acids, has been extensively studied. In this work, the effects of five acids—acetic, citric, monochloroacetic, dichloroacetic, and trichloroacetic acids—on thermochemicals were studied, and the impact of several parameters on sodium nitrite (NaNO2) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) was examined, such as concentration, temperature, and pH. Experimental results show that acetic acid produced the maximum volume of nitrogen, generating up to 20% more gas than other acids at 82 °C and 3.39 mol/L concentration. Furthermore, the concentration of reactants strongly affected the gas production rate, showing a near-linear relationship. The reaction’s efficiency improved at elevated temperatures, with optimal gas generation between 70 and 90 °C. Additionally, pH control within the studied range minimized by-products NO and NO2. Due to its optimal molecular structure and catalytic properties, Acetic acid enhances the efficiency of N2 generation, making it promising for oilfield operations and reservoir enhancement. The study also highlights that the acid’s strength and structure play important roles in the reaction, with the structure being a key factor as a catalyst.

Introduction

In situ gas generation technology has become a popular way to improve oil recovery, help maintain reservoir pressure1,2 and improve formation properties (e.g., eliminating blockages)3–5. There are typically two types of commonly utilized in-situ gas generation systems: those that generate CO26 and those that generate N27,8. Shell Company was the first to propose an in-situ N2 generation system. Conventionally, N2 is created through redox processes involving sodium nitrite (NaNO2) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl)3. It is an extremely exothermic, irreversible, and manageable process. Consequently, this process can be employed in a unique and intriguing category of systems known as fused chemical reactions, which can be delayed through physical or chemical methods7. The reaction can be ignited by temperature acting as a catalyst9. Chemical fusion reactions offer diverse uses, including drug release control10, agricultural fertilizer control11, oil industry deposit remediation7,12,13, and reservoir enhancement14. Fused chemical reactions are particularly relevant to the oil industry7,13,15,16. They have been employed for scenarios like clearing hydrate below the ocean floor17 and boosting the formation’s energy through the impressive release of gas and heat18. As deeper offshore oil wells are drilled, the deposition of paraffin, asphaltene, and hydrates becomes more severe due to the frigid temperatures of the ocean floor7,15. The generation of heat and nitrogen in a system offers a viable solution for eliminating mud-induced formation damage in heavy oil reservoirs12,19.

In an aqueous solution, both sodium nitrite (NaNO2) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) demonstrate significant solubility alongside the resulting substance, NaCl. Gas generation leading to an increase in formation temperature is likely to enhance the radial penetration of acid fluid, resulting in the cooling effect often experienced during matrix stimulations. Following matrix acid stimulation treatments, field tests in Northern Brazil displayed a notable surge in oil production from the producing wells20. The usage of the NaNO2/NH4Cl system effectively raised the formation energy in a low-permeability setting, resulting in enhanced oil production in the Jianghan oilfield3.

The presence of H+ in the N2 generation system significantly influences the rate, degree of reaction, and thermal properties of the system21. To enhance the performance of in-situ N2 generation systems, hydrochloric acid, and citric acid are added as additives to increase the reaction rate, resulting in higher temperatures and greater gas volume. Singh et al.13 and Nguyen et al.22 viewed the NaNO2–NH4Cl system as a chemical fusion reaction, utilizing acid-filled capsules in their approach. Various chemicals, such as methyl formate and tetrachloroethane3, have been commonly utilized to enhance the system by producing H+ in specific circumstances. Despite the advantages of using H+, the presence of this material in an uncontrolled manner leads to an increase in the intensity of the reaction rate and the occurrence of side reactions. This ultimately results in the production of by-products such as NO and NO2. To optimize the catalytic effect of H+, it is crucial to accurately determine the concentration boundary of H+ that promotes the main reaction23.

Dusenbury et al.’s24 findings indicate NOx is not expected to be generated by the NaNO2/NH4Cl system at pH levels between 1 and 6, according to their analysis of kinetics and mechanism. Researchers examined how pH, temperature, and reactant concentrations affected the reaction kinetics between NH4+ and NO2− in aqueous solutions. The rate of reaction experiences a significant boost when the pH of the solution drops, driving the reaction forward not by altering the reaction pathway but by modifying the concentrations of ammonia and nitrogen trioxide9. Developing remediation strategies requires a comprehensive knowledge of the reaction kinetics, mechanisms, and key rate parameters like pH, reactant concentration, and temperature25.

Despite the growing application of in-situ nitrogen generation in enhanced oil recovery, most previous studies have focused on individual parameters such as temperature or concentration in isolation, often neglecting their interdependent effects. Moreover, the influence of acid structure and acidity strength on the efficiency and selectivity of the reaction has received limited attention. This study addresses this gap by systematically evaluating the impact of five different organic acids under controlled experimental conditions using a statistically optimized design. The goal is to understand better how acid-catalysed thermochemical reactions can be fine-tuned for maximum nitrogen output, minimal by-products, and optimal kinetic profiles26.

Although several studies have investigated the NaNO2/NH4Cl system, most prior research has focused on individual parameters such as temperature, concentration, or pH evaluated in isolation. This approach overlooks the interdependent nature of these variables under real operational conditions. Additionally, previous works have rarely examined how the molecular structure and intrinsic acidity strength of organic acids influence the reaction kinetics, selectivity, and overall nitrogen yield. To the best of our knowledge, no study has simultaneously evaluated the combined effects of acid structure, acidity strength, and reaction parameters using a statistically optimized design. Therefore, the present work introduces a comprehensive, multi-parameter evaluation of five structurally distinct organic acids using a CCD-based RSM approach, providing new insights into catalyst selection and reaction optimization for in-situ nitrogen generation.

Results and discussion

Recently, in the oil industry, in-situ gas generation technology proved highly effective for enhancing oil recovery (EOR) and reforming formations. In-situ nitrogen gas generation reduces the viscosity of paraffin-rich crude oil, enhances matrix acidification, and increases formation energy. Consequently, the reaction between ammonium chloride and sodium nitrite is a thermochemical reaction that is widely used and is the most important for in situ nitrogen gas production in this industry. The high exothermic reaction results in the generation of remarkable heat and gas, providing a major advantage in this thermochemical process. For this reason, the experiments were designed to thoroughly investigate the effects of key variables, including concentration, temperature, and pH, and the influence of different catalysts (acids) on the thermochemical reaction.

Experimental design for N2 optimization

The preliminary studies, based on previously reported works in the literature, showed that certain physical variables (factors) drastically affect the efficiency of the acid-catalytic N2 generation reaction. The selected variables included temperature, concentrations of thermochemicals, and the type and pH of the acid. There are at least two general methods for optimizing several levels of the influenced variables. The first is called “one at a time,” which has limitations, especially when intercorrelated variables prevent the identification of optimum levels with a reasonable number of experiments. The other method is experimental design, which is widely used across most science and engineering fields. In contrast to the former, it can handle intercorrelations with a very small set of experiments. One of the most important and broadly used experimental design approaches is response surface methodology (RSM), which is divided into many disciplines, such as central composite design (CCD). Despite the high acidity strength, trichloroacetic acid, dichloroacetic acid, and monochloroacetic acid demonstrated lower performance compared to acetic acid. The possible mechanism behind the observed order can be formalized by two factors, the acid strength (pKa’s) and the varying pH of the whole chemical reactants (thermochemical and the composition of the used acid to control the pH). The impact of the two mentioned factors was reflected in the reaction rate and the produced volume of nitrogen gas.

It is surprising to mention that, regarding the observed order of the acidity strength on the rate of the reaction and in part on the released volume of N2, although acetic acid is stronger than citric acid, its impact is more favourable than all other acids on the N2 production. This trend is due to that acetic acid has a simpler molecular structure, better solubility, more precise pH control, lower by-product formation, and faster diffusion rate. These characteristics enhanced the efficiency of the reaction, resulting in greater nitrogen production when acetic acid is used as a catalyst. Moreover, these phenomenal properties can be attributed to the better compatibility of acetic acid with the experimental conditions.

The quantitative effects of the important variables were investigated by performing experimental measurements based on the designed experiments. The results of these studies are discussed in the next sections.

Gas-producing tests with acids

To determine the optimal conditions for nitrogen gas production in the thermochemical reaction, the five acids were used as catalysts. Nguyen et al. found through their studies on the reaction kinetics and mechanisms that uncontrolled pH can lead to side reactions, producing byproducts like NO and NO2. Their research indicated that keeping pH levels between 3 and 5 is crucial to preventing NOx formation and reducing pollutants8. All tested acids were applied under the same conditions, and their results were compared. The basis for comparing these five acids is the quantity of the amount of nitrogen generated in a constant time interval and the rate of injection of the acids. When AA was used, results demonstrated that both the production of nitrogen gas and the reaction rate were positively correlated with increasing thermochemical concentrations and higher temperatures. Specifically, at a constant thermochemical concentration, a significant increase in nitrogen output was noted at 82 °C compared to 58 °C. This efficiency showcases AA’s effective catalysis in generating N2 under elevated thermal conditions. Experiments with CA exhibited similar positive trends concerning concentration and temperature increments. However, the total nitrogen output was consistently lower than that observed with AA, suggesting less efficient hydrolysis of CA. Interestingly, the initial N2 generation rate with CA was slower but accelerated after a few minutes, contrasting with AA’s steadier kinetics. DCAA followed a similar pattern to the aforementioned acids, with nitrogen release increasing as thermochemical concentration and temperature rose. With TCAA, the system displayed a unique behaviour where the nitrogen generation rate was higher at the start due to the acid’s high acidity and greater release of H+ ions. This rapid initial reaction is attributed to the presence of more chlorine atoms in TCAA, aiding in faster hydrolysis. However, over time, this rate diminished, possibly due to degradation or stability issues, reflecting the trade-offs associated with using highly acidic catalysts. The superior performance of acetic acid compared to stronger halogenated acids can be explained by its molecular characteristics and catalytic behaviour. While acids such as monochloroacetic, dichloroacetic, and trichloroacetic release H+ more rapidly due to higher acidity, this often leads to excessively fast initiation, localized hot spots, and undesirable side reactions. In contrast, acetic acid provides a more controlled release of H⁺, allowing steadier reaction kinetics and improved selectivity toward N2 rather than NOx by-products. Its simpler molecular structure enhances solubility and diffusion into the reaction medium, enabling a more uniform catalytic effect. These factors collectively result in higher nitrogen yield and more stable reaction rates when acetic acid is used, demonstrating that molecular structure—not only acidity strength—is critical in optimizing thermochemical nitrogen generation.

The pH substantially affected the reaction between sodium nitrite and ammonium chloride in nitrogen production. Effect of temperature

As it is expected, temperature has a significant effect on the kinetics and efficiency of the chemical reaction and physical transformations. By increasing the temperature, the reaction rate accelerates because the molecules gain more kinetic energy. This enables more energetic collisions between molecules, hastening the reaction. Simply put, at elevated temperatures, the reaction occurs more swiftly, and nitrogen gas is released sooner.

Temperature can also influence the reaction equilibrium. As this reaction is exothermic (releases heat), raising the temperature can shift the equilibrium toward the reactants (sodium nitrite and ammonium chloride), decreasing nitrogen gas production. In other words, at very high temperatures, less gas may be produced. When the temperature drops, the equilibrium shifts toward the products (nitrogen gas, water, and salt), and the reaction rate slows down. Hence, the ideal temperature for this reaction is one where the reaction rate is high and nitrogen gas production is maximized. Generally, this reaction is most effective at higher temperatures to balance both rate and equilibrium considerations.

One of the very important aspects of the use of thermochemical reactions is the fast kinetics of the production of N2. Since a chemical reaction occurs and we measure a physical variable over time—specifically, the volume of nitrogen gas released—this can be classified as a kinetic reaction. The rate of reaction depends on time, and as time progresses, the system eventually reaches equilibrium, at which point no further reaction takes place.

The optimized reaction profiles, nitrogen gas volume versus time, of thermochemicals with different catalyst acids are shown in Fig. 5. The volume-time profile data can be fitted to different reaction kinetics. As the first-order reaction mechanism has several advantages, including simple interpretation of the results and the ease of experimental control, the obtained data is first modelled by this type of kinetic mechanism. A typical representation of the N2 production is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NaNO_{2} + NH_{4} Cl \to N_{2} \uparrow + NaCl + 2H_{2} O$$\end{document}

The influence of the acid on the NaNO2/NH4Cl system is governed not only by its macroscopic acidity but also by molecular-level electronic and structural effects that control proton delivery and intermediate stability. Nitrogen generation proceeds via protonation of nitrite to form nitrous acid (HNO2), followed by its decomposition; however, the rate and selectivity of this sequence are highly sensitive to acid strength. In chloroacetic acids, successive chlorine substitution introduces strong − I electron-withdrawing effects that stabilize the conjugate base and markedly lower the pKa, resulting in rapid and excessive protonation of nitrite. This over-acidification accelerates uncontrolled HNO2 decomposition and promotes competitive oxidative pathways leading to NOx (particularly NO2), thereby reducing selectivity toward N2.

Acetic acid, by contrast, provides a moderate and kinetically regulated proton activity, while its small, monocarboxylic structure enables efficient hydrogen-bonding interactions with nitrite and ammonium ions. These interactions facilitate stepwise proton transfer and transient stabilization of reaction intermediates, allowing acetic acid to function effectively as a proton-shuttle catalyst rather than a simple stoichiometric acid. Although citric acid exhibits a comparable pKa, its larger, polycarboxylic framework increases solution structuring and restricts molecular mobility, limiting the effective availability of protons for rapid HNO2 turnover. Consequently, acetic acid uniquely balances acidity, molecular size, and electronic neutrality, leading to controlled N2 evolution rates, reproducible gas volumes, and minimized side-reaction pathways.

The volume-time profiles are shown in Fig. 2 of the previous sections. To approve the kinetic mechanisms, each profile was fitted to the first order and, in some cases, to the higher order kinetics. The privilege one was selected by statistical quality parameters like correlation coefficient R2, root mean squares (RMS), randomness of the residuals, and visual inspection of the fitted model. For nonlinear least squares of the volume-time profiles for each kinetic equation, we used the Solver add-ins of Microsoft Excel 2021, and the RMS was set as an objective function. Conclusion

This study underscores the pivotal role of acid catalysts, with a particular focus on acetic acid, in augmenting nitrogen gas production during thermochemical reactions between sodium nitrite and ammonium chloride. The experimental findings demonstrate that reaction temperature, pH, and reactant concentration are critical factors that significantly influence the efficiency of nitrogen generation. Among the acids tested, acetic acid emerged as the most effective, delivering the highest yield and fastest reaction rate. This superior performance is attributed to its simpler molecular structure, optimal solubility, and lower by-product formation.

The study reveals that ideal reaction kinetics occurred at elevated temperatures, where nitrogen gas production was directly proportional to the concentration of thermochemical reagents. These insights are vital for optimizing in-situ nitrogen generation processes employed in oil recovery and reservoir enhancement. By carefully adjusting variables such as pH, Concentration, and temperature, the efficiency of nitrogen production can be substantially enhanced, thereby reducing formation damage risks and maximizing oil extraction.

Furthermore, the research provides a foundation for developing more efficient catalysts and optimized conditions for large-scale oil industry applications. The primary aim was to explore the influence of various factors on gas production volume and establish the kinetics equation for the NaNO2/NH4Cl system. This led to the creation of an acid-assisted in situ nitrogen generation system, using several acids selected as catalysts based on pH tracking.

The comprehensive evaluation of gas generation capabilities and energy enhancement potential yielded key findings:Among the variables measured, concentration exerted the most significant effect on nitrogen production efficiency.While pH values had a minor impact on selecting an acid as a catalyst, the molecular structure of the acid played a crucial role. This emphasizes the need for precise selection based on acid structure for optimal catalysis efficiency.

Unlike traditional assumptions that stronger acids always enhance gas generation, our findings reveal that controlled acidity release, diffusion behaviour, and structural compatibility can significantly improve reaction efficiency. These insights provide a new framework for designing optimized in-situ N2 generation fluids and offer practical guidance for field-scale applications in stimulation, blockage removal, and formation-energy enhancement.
