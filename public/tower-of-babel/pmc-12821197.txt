Differences in the clinical course of COVID-19 in children and adolescents hospitalized in the 2023/2024 and 2024/2025 seasons – a retrospective real-world study
Authors: Krystyna Dobrowolska, Małgorzata Pawłowska, Dorota Zarębska-Michaluk, Barbara Szczepańska, Przemysław Ciechanowski, Małgorzata Sobolewska-Pilarczyk, Izabela Zaleska, Maria Pokorska-Śpiewak, Robert Flisiak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis multicenter retrospective study was designed to track changes in the clinical presentation of COVID-19 in children that occurred in the last two infectious seasons, and to identify risk factors for severe disease progression.MethodsThe analysis covered all consecutive children hospitalized due to COVID-19 during the 2023/2024 and 2024/2025 infectious seasons in Polish pediatric centers.ResultsOf the 366 children included in the study, 203 were hospitalized in the first period analyzed and 163 in the second. In both seasons, about three-quarters of the population were children under 2 years of age, boys were hospitalized more often than girls and more than a one-fourth had comorbidities. Oxygen saturation (SpO2) upon admission was significantly lower in the 2023/2024 season. Children in unstable clinical condition with SpO2 ≤ 95% were admitted to hospital statistically more often in the first period analyzed. Eleven patients were admitted in unstable clinical condition with SpO2 < 90%, nine of whom were hospitalized during the period 2023/2024. Nine of them were aged one year or younger, and seven had comorbidities. Oxygen supplementation was used significantly more often in the first period analyzed, high-flow therapy was used in three children hospitalized in the 2023/2024 season, while one child required mechanical ventilation in the 2024/2025 season.ConclusionsWith each season of Omicron dominance, the course of COVID-19 in pediatric patients is becoming milder. Special attention should be paid to the youngest children and those with comorbidities, as the disease may progress more rapidly and lead to severe course in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Since the emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in late 2019, the epidemiology of infections has evolved with the appearance of new variants of concern (VOCs), characterized by unique properties of transmission, infectivity, virulence, and severity of the disease they cause. However, regardless of the variant that dominated during successive waves of the pandemic, the clinical picture of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the pediatric population was significantly milder than that in adults, with a much lower percentage of patients with symptomatic infections, including those requiring hospitalization [1–3]. Among those who required hospital treatment, disease severity and mortality among children were also much lower than those among adults, but the exact age-dependent differences varied according to the predominant variant [4, 5]. Although the detailed mechanism resulting in a milder course and better prognosis in children has not been definitively established, lower levels of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor expression and a weaker binding affinity between ACE2 receptors and viral spike proteins are thought to contribute [6]. Age-related differences in the innate and adaptive immune response, higher lymphocyte counts, higher levels of immunoglobulins (Ig) M, G, and interferon, and lower levels of interleukin (IL) 6 and 10 during SARS-CoV-2 infection also account for the milder clinical picture and lower risk of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in SARS-CoV-2-infected patients than in older patients [7]. Importantly, children and young people have fewer comorbidities, which, in adults, are associated with a higher risk of severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and death.

Among all the VOCs identified, the most recent is the Omicron variant, which was dominant as of early 2022. It is more contagious, has a greater ability to evade the immune response, and is less likely than previous variants to cause severe or critical disease, which has been well documented in adult populations [8]. Although almost four years have passed since its emergence, no further variants have been identified to date, whereas Omicron is constantly evolving and subvariants are emerging [9]. With the advent of subsequent Omicron subvariants, further changes in the clinical picture of SARS-CoV-2 infections have been observed both in adult and pediatric populations [10–13].

This mitigation of SARS-CoV-2 infection was due to the evolution of Omicron, the widespread availability of vaccines against COVID-19 updated to subsequent strains of the virus, and the development of natural immunity after previous infections resulted in an decrease in the epidemic threat by the World Health Organization (WHO) in mid-2023 [14]. Since then, no SARS-CoV-2 variants have met the VOC criteria. However, the virus continues to cause infections around the world, and people with more severe COVID-19 are being hospitalized and dying. Thus, there is a continuous need to monitor the epidemiological situation and the changing clinical picture of COVID-19, and research into new forms of prevention and therapy is still ongoing [15]. This is reflected in the still published results of analyses in the adult patient population, while the number of reports of studies conducted in the pediatric population is limited [16].

To fill this gap, we designed a multicenter retrospective study from routine clinical practice to analyze children and adolescents hospitalized for COVID-19 to track changes in clinical presentation that occurred in the 2023/2024 and 2024/2025 seasons in a country where the vaccination rate in the pediatric population is significantly lower than the European average [17].

Methods

Data collection

The data for the study were retrospectively gathered through the SARSTer-ped database, a Polish project managed and supported by PTEiLChZ (Polish Society of Epidemiology and Physicians of Infectious Diseases), a scientific and professional society of infectious disease physicians that obtains funding for research from various sources as part of its statutory activities. The strength of the SARSTer program lies in its multi-center nature, encompassing major infectious disease centers, which is significant due to the lack of centralized collection and analysis of clinical data managed by government institutions. A weakness of this program organization is the voluntary nature of participation by centers, which means the entire patient population could not be included. The project aims to collect observational data on hospitalized pediatric patients diagnosed with COVID-19 and involves five Polish pediatric centers specializing in infectious diseases. The study included all pediatric patients hospitalized during the analyzed periods in the centers participating in the study, without applying any exclusion criteria. The parameters entered into the database included demographic and clinical characteristics such as sex, age, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, concomitant medications, saturation at baseline, and coinfections, including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza virus. Both RSV and influenza virus coinfections were diagnosed with positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results. BMI-for-age percentiles were calculated, and sex-adjusted centile charts were used. For the assessment of children aged 0–5 years, WHO charts were used, whereas older children were evaluated via OLA and OLAF centile charts. Baseline laboratory parameters, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, procalcitonin (PCT) levels, white blood cell (WBC) counts, lymphocyte counts, absolute neutrophil counts (ANC), platelet (PLT) counts, interleukin-6 levels, D-dimer levels, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity, were also analyzed. At admission to the hospital, patients were assigned to one of the following categories on the basis of the presence of symptoms and oxygen saturation (SpO2) when they were breathing room air: (1) asymptomatic, (2) stable symptomatic with SpO2 >95, (3) unstable symptomatic with SpO2 91–95%, (4) unstable symptomatic with SpO2 ≤ 90, and ARDS. This classification is based on the recommendations of the PTEiLChZ, which were regularly updated in the years 2020–2025 and consistently used in subsequent publications resulting from the SARSTer project, enabling comparisons between subsequent periods of the pandemic [18, 19]. Information on medications, such as antivirals, immunomodulators, antibiotics, and low-molecular-weight heparin, used during the hospital stay was also collected.

Study population

A total of 366 pediatric patients were included in the study. They were divided into two groups (G) based on the two periods of hospitalization: April 2023–March 2024 (period 1) and April 2024–March 2025 (period 2). At that time, the dominant Omicron subvariants in Poland, according to GISAID data, were XBB.1.5 and HK.3 (2023/2024) and XEC, KP.3.1.1. and LP.8.1 (2024/2025) [20]. The diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2 infection was made on the basis of positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or antigen test results.

Endpoints

Endpoints were defined as the duration of hospitalization, the duration of oxygen therapy, the need for oxygen therapy, the need for high-flow oxygen therapy, the need for mechanical ventilation, and 28-day mortality. These endpoints were compared between the two populations.

Subgroup analysis

An additional, more in-depth analysis was carried out to assess the baseline clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, and clinical course of disease of patients who, at the time of admission to the hospital, presented in an unstable clinical state with saturation ≤ 90% or with acute respiratory distress syndrome.

Ethics

The study was performed per the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Jan Kochanowski University (16/2025). The need for consent to participate was waived by an Ethical Committee due to the retrospective design of the study. The patients were not exposed to the experimental interventions. Treatment was carried out according to the label and the latest national guidelines. Personal data protection principles were in effect.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via Statistica v. 13 (StatSoft, Tulsa, Oklahoma, United States). Continuous data were checked with the Shapiro‒Wilk test, which did not meet the Gaussian distribution with a nonnormal distribution, and are presented as medians and interquartile ranges. Group comparisons were conducted via the nonparametric Mann‒Whitney test. Categorical data are expressed as numbers and percentages. Differences between the groups were evaluated with the Pearson χ2 test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Regardless of the period analyzed, boys more frequently required hospital treatment than girls did. In both groups, over 50% of the children were under two years of age (76.35% vs. 72.39% in G1 and G2, respectively). Comorbidities were reported by about one-fourth of the children in both periods. Among them, the neurological and respiratory system disorders were the most common (Table 1). No comparison between the analyzed seasons has reached a significant difference.

Coinfection with RSV and influenza were documented only in the first analyzed season, in 10 (4.9%) and 2 (0.98%) children respectively. RSV coinfection was diagnosed in 4 children in stable condition and saturation > 95%, in 6 children with more severe presentation (2 in unstable condition with saturation ≤ 95%, 3 with saturation ≤ 90% and one child with ARDS.), and the difference was significant with p-value < 0.001. Coinfection with influenza virus was confirmed in two children in stable condition with saturation > 95% (no significance was reached).

At admission to the hospital, the median blood oxygen saturation was significantly lower in the 2023/2024 period than in the 2024/2025 period (p < 0.01). The majority of patients were stable with SpO2 > 95% (87.19% vs. 97.54%, respectively). Unstable clinical conditions with reduced blood oxygen saturation (≤ 95%) were reported statistically more often in the 2023/2024 time period (12.32% vs. 2.43%) than in the 2024/2025 time period.

Regardless of the clinical condition and period, the most common symptoms were cough, fever, and dyspnea (Fig. 1).

The laboratory parameters were comparable between the periods. Only median D-dimers and ALT activity were slightly elevated in group 1; however, both remained within a normal range (Table 1).

A more detailed analysis of eleven patients who were admitted to the hospital with unstable clinical conditions and SpO2 < 90% revealed that most of them were hospitalized in 2023/2024 (9 children), and only two were treated in 2024/2025. Nine children were one year old or younger. Comorbidities were reported in five patients (45.5%), and inflammatory parameters were relatively low. None of the children were treated with antivirals, while dexamethasone was used in more than half of them (54.5%). All patients received symptomatic treatment with β2-mimetics, cholinolytics, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (Table 2). Children in the youngest age groups required parenteral hydration.

Antibiotics were used in two out of nine children in 2023/2024 and in all children during the 2024/2025 period. High-flow oxygen therapy was administered only in the first group (2023/2024) and lasted 7 days (a boy, 5 months old) or 6 days (a girl, 1 year old). The boy was exceptionally burdened by the presence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, while the girl at the time of admission already suffered from purulent otitis and was additionally diagnosed with RSV co-infection during COVID-19. CRP was normal in both children, and in both cases, dexamethasone and oxygen therapy were administered. No antiviral treatment was used.

Mechanical ventilation was necessary for one patient—a boy suffering from severe myofibrillar myopathy who was hospitalized during the 2024–2025 period. He presented with fever and dyspnea. Both the CRP and PCT levels were within normal ranges. Mechanical ventilation was started after one day of hospitalization and continued for 24 days. Any attempt at cessation was unsuccessful. The treatment consisted of cefuroxime, budesonide, and salbutamol. After discharge, the child was eligible for the home ventilation program (Table 2).

Overall, one child from group 1 was treated with remdesivir, whereas one patient from group 2 received nirmatrelvir/ritonavir. In neither case was statistical significance achieved. Dexamethasone was used more often in group 1 (p = 0.54), as was budesonide inhalation (p = 0.53). Antibiotic treatment was initiated in a comparable number of children in both periods analyzed and was the result of bacterial superinfections (Table 1).

Endpoints considered for the whole cohort included the time of hospitalization, which was comparable between the two groups, as most of the children were discharged after four days (Table 3).

Discussion

According to available literature data, children are less likely than adults to require hospitalization due to COVID-19, as they have a lower risk of developing severe [21]. Several innate mechanisms seem to be at play, such as cellular immunity, which develops in children at a rapid rate after the initial disease and is linked to a less severe response to reinfection than in adults [22]. In a recently conducted matched study between Delta and Omicron pediatric patients, the latter was associated with a significantly lower risk of developing moderate or severe infection [23]. Similarly, over 90% of patients hospitalized during Omicron predominance in 2022 had mild disease (compared with 84.2% during Delta dominance) [23]. We also noted a strong prevalence of children under the age of 2 years, regardless of the period analyzed and our results mirror those obtained through studies conducted in Poland since the start of the pandemic in March 2020 20. This predominance may stem from the fact that children under the age of 6, compared with older groups, have greater odds of developing moderate or severe illness [16, 23], and both newborns and infants are at risk for severe COVID-19, thus increasing the chance for hospital referral [22]. Additionally, comorbidities, reported in more than 25% of cases, are known to increase the risk of mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic in both adults and children [21], which may influence the final decision for hospitalization. Although we did not collect such information, it cannot be ruled out that older children had been vaccinated or had already had one or more natural SARS-CoV-2 infections, which resulted in natural immunity, reducing the risk of severe disease [22]. It should be mentioned that vaccination coverage in Poland in all age groups, including children, was significantly lower than the values for Europe. Data at the end of 2023 documented a lack of vaccination in three-quarters of the pediatric population and third booster was administered in 0,0% in this age group [17].

The significantly greater percentage of children in an unstable clinical state with blood oxygen saturation less than or equal to 95% during the 2023/2024 period than in the 2024/2025 period confirms the mitigation of the clinical course of COVID-19 over time [16]. In a multicenter retrospective study conducted in China, children infected and hospitalized during the Omicron variant dominance (2022/2023) were characterized by a milder course of the disease, presented with fewer symptoms and recovered faster than children hospitalized at the start of the pandemic [22]. Patients in our cohort reported various symptoms, the most common of which were cough, fever, diarrhea, and shortness of breath. Yue et al. noted a similar distribution of symptoms in a large Chinese pediatric cohort of over 4,000 patients infected with Omicron during 2022. However, many patients (13.5%, 610 patients) were reported to suffer from convulsions during infection, and five patients were diagnosed with acute necrotizing encephalitis [23]. This damage to the nervous system is possible because the ACE-2 receptor is present in the endothelium of arterial and venous vessels and in the smooth muscle cells of arterial vessels but also plays a significant role as a protective and regulatory agent of inflammation in the central nervous system [13]. In our study, we recorded fever-induced seizures in single children in both periods analyzed, but there were no cases of acute necrotizing encephalitis.

We conducted a separate analysis of patients in unstable clinical conditions whose blood oxygen saturation was less than 90% (9 children in G1 and 2 children in G2). Both figures are higher than those reported for children hospitalized in 2022/2023 11. The severity of the disease in 2023/2024 observed in our study may be related to the young age of the cohort (7 out of 9 children were less than 1 year old) compared with older children; infants (< 1 year) are at increased risk of admission to intensive care and death [16, 24]. In addition, coexisting cardiac, neurological, and pulmonary conditions, present in more than half of the population, regardless of age, increase the risk of intensive care and death [16, 24]. Interestingly, unlike adults, male sex does not affect the severity of the disease, which was confirmed in our study, as the numbers of girls and boys with unstable clinical conditions were comparable [24]. Although 11 children were in unstable clinical condition at the time of admission to the hospital, only one child developed ARDS and required mechanical ventilation (a 3-month-old boy from group G2 suffering from severe myofibrillar myopathy). One child already had ARDS at the time of admission—a 5-week-old boy from group G2—was underweight and had no known disease. The results are comparable to those presented in the study by Pokorska-Śpiewak et al., as only one child required mechanical ventilation in the late phase of Omicron (July 2022–April 2023) [11].

The decreasing severity of COVID-19 over time was described in a study comparing three periods: the dominance of the alpha variant (T1), delta (T2), and Omicron (T3) in Detroit, United States, where at the beginning of the Omicron variant’s dominance, 18.8% of children developed severe disease (hospitalization in intensive care, mechanical ventilation, multisystem inflammatory syndrome, myocarditis, death), whereas 36.2% developed severe disease during the alpha variant dominance period and 27.4% during the delta variant dominance period. There was also a significant decrease in the number of cases of multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) from 17.4% (Alpha) to 6.4% (Omicron), p < 0.001 25. In a prospective study conducted in Krakow between 2020 and 2023 involving 116 pediatric patients diagnosed with MIC-S, the number of diagnoses was highest during the period when the alpha variant was dominant (74 children, 63.79%) and gradually decreased during the delta phase (27 children, 23.28%) and Omicron (15 children, 12.93%) phases, with boys being more common in all periods (60.8% in Original/Alpha, 74.1% in Delta) except for Omicron (40%) 26. No cases of MIS-C were reported in our cohort, which can be considered further evidence of the decreasing pathogenicity of the Omicron variant.

However, one should note that it is not only the genetic variability of the SARS-CoV-2 virus that influences the observed mitigation of COVID-19 over time. It is also impacted by immunity resulting from previous infections and vaccine-induced protection, although in Poland the latter is insufficient compared to the European average.

Several limitations need to be addressed. First, our study was retrospective in design, which is always linked to possible bias in data entry and loss of data. We did not perform SARS-CoV-2 genome sequencing to establish the exact subvariant of Omicron causing the infection. The data on vaccination were scarce and unreliable, which is why we excluded them from the analysis. Without access to an objective source of information on vaccinations, we were unable to verify the parents’ statements. No follow-up after discharge from hospital was carried out, so we do not have data on long COVID or post-infection complications in the analyzed pediatric population,. It is also important to mention the potential underreporting of mild cases not requiring hospitalization, as well as lack of socioeconomic data that could influence access to healthcare. And finally, the assessment of the impact of laboratory parameters on clinical severity and disease outcomes was not performed because data were available only for some of the patients.

The multicenter nature of our study can be considered an undeniable strength, as it allows for a generalization of the results. It is also one of the few analyses available that assesses the current course of COVID-19 in the pediatric population. Although SARS-CoV-2 infection has become more common and milder, it can still pose a threat to the health of patients, especially children, and the disease course should be monitored.

Conclusions

With each year and each new subvariant of Omicron, the course of COVID-19 in pediatric patients has decreased. Special attention should be given to children with multimorbidity, especially those with cardiological, pneumological, and neurological disorders, as in this population, the illness can progress more rapidly and result in a more serious clinical state and hospitalization.
