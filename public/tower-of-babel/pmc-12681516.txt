Perceptions, attitudes, and practices influencing management of obesity in Ireland–The Awareness, Care and Treatment in Obesity maNagement, (ACTION) Ireland study
Authors: Fiona Curran, Leona Ryan, Susan Birney, Karen Gaynor, Maura Murphy, Jean O’ Connell, Gráinne O'Donoghue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData is lacking comparing perceptions of people living with obesity (PWO) and health care professionals (HCPs) towards obesity management in Ireland, where 30 % of the population live with obesity. The ACTION-Ireland study aimed to explore perceptions, attitudes, practices, and barriers to effective obesity management in Ireland, using data from PWO and HCPs comparable to international data sets.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional online survey conducted among adults living with BMI ≥30 kg/m2 and HCPs managing PWO.ResultsA total of 504 PWO and 159 HCPs completed the survey (August–December 2024). Most PWO (75 %) and almost all HCPs (98 %) believe obesity is a chronic disease. While 86 % of HCPs believe they have responsibility to support PWO, 82 % of PWO believe they are completely responsible for weight management.PWO waited on average eight years (SD 9.7) from first weight concern to first weight discussion with their HCP. HCPs most initiated obesity conversations when the patient had (70 %) or were at risk of (63 %) obesity complications. Lifestyle interventions were most frequently discussed and recommended by HCPs (physical activity 72 %, diet 67 %), while fewer recommended prescription medication (34 %), referral to obesity specialists (23 %) or for bariatric surgery (24 %). Although 88 % of PWO had attempted weight loss, only 10 % used prescription medication, 4 % visited an obesity specialist, and 2–4 % had bariatric surgery.Many HCPs expressed concern about obesity medication; side effects (60 %), long term safety (48 %), lack of knowledge (46 %), cost (79 %), and preference for lifestyle changes (35 %). Importantly, 81 % of HCPs believe society and the healthcare system are not meeting PWO needs.ConclusionsDespite strong consensus that obesity is a disease, disparities remain regarding treatment of and responsibility for obesity management. This dataset highlights opportunities to improve education, management and system level support while identifying inequities and barriers in obesity care.

Background

Obesity is a multifactorial, chronic relapsing non-communicable disease marked by an abnormal and/or excessive accumulation of body fat that presents a risk to health [1]. It is well established that obesity increases the risk for other diseases and represents a major public health concern [[1], [2], [3]]. In the past two decades, obesity has caused 3.7 million deaths, and 129,000,000 disability adjusted life years (DALYs), with related global DALY rates rising over 15 %, making it one of the fastest growing health risks [4].

In Ireland, the proportion of adults with self-reported body mass index (BMI) above 25 kg/m2 is 50 % among females and 63 % among males [5]. True rates are likely higher, as self-reported BMI typically underestimates prevalence by 5–15 % with earlier measured data reporting 60 % of females and 70 % of males with BMI classified obesity [6]. These figures are projected to rise by 17 % and 12 % by 2050 [4], along with parallel increases in associated metabolic, cardiovascular and other non-communicable diseases.

Obesity classification systems based on disease impact and progression continue to emerge in response to evolving phenotypes and its rising prevalence [3,[7], [8], [9]]. These systems enable identification of those most severely affected, support targeted clinical decision making, and are increasingly embedded in guidelines and service planning [10,11]. Nonetheless, body mass index (BMI), as a surrogate for adiposity, remains a broad screening tool for weight-related health risks and a key measure in public health to assess population level risks [9,12]. Therefore, for simplicity the term obesity or people with obesity (PWO) is used throughout this paper.

Ireland has adopted a tiered model of obesity care [6], similar to systems used in the UK and other Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries [13]. This includes general public health promotion (tier 1), primary care and pharmacological interventions (tier 2), specialized multidisciplinary teams (tier 3), and bariatric surgery (tier 4) [6]. However, disparities in access to services persist across different regions, and obesity is still frequently under-addressed in clinical settings [6,14,15].

Several factors contribute to this gap in care, including lack of education and training for healthcare professionals (HCPs) on causes and treatment of obesity, and the debate about whether obesity should be classified as a disease or merely a risk factor leads to uncertainty and inconsistency in clinical approaches [16]. Furthermore, public understanding of obesity is often shaped by oversimplified media narratives that place blame on individuals [17]. This stigma overlooks obesity's complex nature and hinders progress in both prevention and treatment [18]. Identifying and addressing these misconceptions is essential to improve care and reduce additional social, psychological and health burden of stigmatization for people living with obesity.

Improving obesity care requires deeper insight into how the disease is understood and managed. Large national and multinational Awareness Care and Treatment In Obesity maNagement International Observation (ACTION-IO) studies have explored perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors of both people living with obesity and HCPs and identified potential barriers to effective treatment [7,13,[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. However, these studies did not include Irish data and were conducted predominantly among physicians, rather than the wider multidisciplinary team of HCPs. Hence, there is a need to explore how PWO and HCPs in Ireland perceive and manage the condition, to better inform Irish obesity care and generate insights that are both nationally and internationally informative.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore perceptions, attitudes, practices, and barriers to effective obesity management in Ireland, using data from PWO and HCPs comparable to international data sets.

Methods

University College Dublin Human Research Ethics Committee granted study approval (HREC LS-24-43 and LS-LR-24-238) and all respondents provided electronic informed consent prior to study participation.

Study design and participants

The ACTION-Ireland study was a cross-sectional study that collected data via an online survey in the Republic of Ireland from PWO and HCPs. Eligible people with obesity were individuals aged 18 or older residing in Ireland with a self-reported BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, participation in intensive fitness or body-building programs, significant unexplained weight loss in preceding six months or declined to provide information on occupation of the highest household earner, used as a proxy for income in Ireland.

Eligible HCPs were practicing clinicians directly involved in patient care and recognized as core members of the multidisciplinary team in Ireland's Model of Care for Obesity [6]. Thus, HCPs were eligible at any career stage, working in any sector or specialty, from the following disciplines: clinical psychology, dietetics, medicine, medical social work, nursing, occupational therapy, pharmacy, or physiotherapy.

Survey development

Two questionnaires, one for PWO and one for HCPs (available in supplementary file Appendix A), were developed by the research team, based on the ACTION-IO [19]. The surveys were modified to ensure questions were relevant to study objectives, the Irish context, contemporary obesity management, and to ensure people first language. Additionally, both surveys were reviewed by Public and Patient Involvement (PPI) partners to ensure appropriateness, relevance, comprehensiveness and contextual fit. Survey items on both surveys were presented in the same order for each respondent, however, within particular sets, items were displayed alphabetically, categorically, chronologically, or randomly to reduce bias. Participants could withdraw from the survey at any point; however, responses already saved could not be deleted due to inherent survey anonymity.

Procedures

Data were collected via two separate online surveys. The PWO survey was administered by a market research company (RED C Research, Dublin, Ireland) in August–September 2024. This utilized their existing database of 40,000 individuals to recruit a nationally representative sample, balanced by gender, age and socioeconomic status. The market research company was responsible for survey programming, utilizing their proprietary software, and for data collection. The respondents had given permission to be contacted by the company for research purposes. All respondents first completed screening questions to determine eligibility; subsequently, only those with a BMI of ≥30 kg/m2, determined by self-reported height and weight, proceeded to the full survey. Respondents were blinded to the specific study aims but were informed that the survey focused on weight management and their health care experiences in managing weight. Respondents were compensated for their time at market research company rates.

Healthcare professional data were collected using purposive and snowball sampling through collaborative HCP networks, via professional bodies and social media, from October–December 2024. The HCP survey was administered via an anonymous online survey using SurveyMonkey. The research team, comprising members from HCP disciplines and gatekeepers to the Health Service Executive (HSE), Irelands national public health care provider, distributed survey links via e-mail to their professional networks, professional bodies and on social media. All respondents completed initial screening questions to determine eligibility, subsequently, only eligible respondents proceeded to the full survey. HCP respondents were not compensated for their time.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included attitudes and beliefs about obesity, its causes, impact, and management strategies; history of weight loss and management, healthcare interactions, initiation of weight management conversations, obesity treatments; perceived responsibility and motivation for obesity management, frequency of diagnosis and follow-up appointments; and sources of information and support. These measures were assessed using yes/no responses, 5-point Likert scales, numeric responses, or single/multiple item selection, as appropriate.

Analysis

Sample size was not determined via a formal statistical power analysis but was pragmatically based on the reported data for BMI classified obesity in Ireland [6,11] and comparison with similar published ACTION-IO studies [13,21]. Considering these factors, a sample of size 400 PWO and 150 HCPs (15–40 per profession) was deemed optimal to balance statistical power recruitment feasibility and cost effectiveness. Analysis of anonymous data was conducted by the research team using excel (for Microsoft 365 MSO version 2410). All completed PWO surveys were included in the full analysis set. For HCPs both completed and partially completed surveys were included in the analysis with statistics reported by number of respondents or total sample as appropriate and indicated in the results. Since this research was exploratory in nature, the objective was to describe and summarize the observed data. Therefore, no inferential or subgroup statistical analyses were conducted. Instead data were summarized using univariate descriptive statistics (means, medians, frequencies, proportions).

Results

Study population and characteristics

The ACTION-Ireland survey was completed by PWO and HCPs in Ireland. A total of 1955 PWO and 235 HCPs completed the screening questions. Eligibility rate post-screening was 25 % (507/1995) for PWO, and 85 % (200/235) for HCPs. Final valid sample was 504 PWO, all of whom completed, and 159 HCPs (completed 70 % (n = 112) or partially completed 30 % (n = 47)).

Perceptions of obesity and its health impact

Most PWO (75 %) and almost all HCPs (98 %) recognized obesity as a chronic disease [Fig. S1]. The majority of PWO (90 %) and HCPs (86 %) agreed that obesity has an extreme or significant impact on overall health similar to other chronic conditions, including diabetes (PWO 89 %, HCPs 86 %), chronic obstructive airway disease (COPD) (PWO 89 %, HCPs 90 %), cancer (PWO 86 %, HCPs 96 %), and stroke (PWO 90 %, HCPs 97 %). PWO tended to rate the impact of all chronic diseases on health more extremely (5 on Likert scale) than did HCPs. [Fig. S2].

In contrast, HCPs rated the impact of obesity higher (significant or severe) than PWO on physical health (91 % vs 76 %), psychological health (92 % vs 79 %), and level of independence (68 % vs 62 %). Fewer respondents in both groups (64 %, 51 %) rated its impact on social relationships as significant or severe. [Fig. S3].

Weight management responsibility and motivation

While the majority of PWO (86 %) and HCPs (94 %) agree that achieving 5–10 % weight loss would be beneficial, perceptions of who is responsible for weight loss and management differed markedly between groups. The majority of PWO (82 %) felt solely responsible for weight loss, whereas only 12 % of HCPs held this view. In contrast, 86 % of HCPs felt responsible to actively support weight management, compared to just 30 % of PWO who believed HCPs have this responsibility. Twenty percent of PWO believe HCPs cannot help them with weight management, but only 4 % of HCPs hold this belief. Conversely, 40 % of HCPs believed PWO cannot lose weight on their own, compared to 26 % of PWO. Most PWO were not happy with their current weight (76 %), over half (56 %) described their weight as excess, while 40 % stated they have obesity. [Table S2, Fig. S4]. Most agreed that they know what they need to do to lose weight (60 %) and could lose weight if they set their mind to it (61 %), but a quarter were past the point where they could lose weight alone (26 %), and few (18 %) believed weight loss or maintaining weight loss is easy. Similarly, only 22 % of HCPs agreed that PWO could lose weight through willpower alone, and fewer believed that weight loss (4 %) or maintenance of weight loss (6 %) is easy for PWO. Many HCPs were motivated to support PWO with weight management (72 %), consider it a valuable use of their time (74 %) and actively help PWO make healthy lifestyle changes (88 %). Beliefs about weight loss and regain

Most PWO (88 %) had attempted weight loss at least once (mean attempts n = 8), but maintenance was very low, with only 10 % of PWO maintaining ≥3 % weight loss for one year, and only 4 % maintaining ≥10 % weight loss [Table S1]. Most common reasons PWO cited for regain were difficulty staying motivated (49 %) and difficulty following the eating plan after weight loss (45 %). Half felt helpless managing their weight and 42 % felt factors outside their control affected their weight [Figs. S5 and S6].

PWO-HCP weight discussions

Among PWO, 57 % had engaged in a weight-related discussion with a HCP within the past five years, 96 % of whom experienced a delay in discussion initiation. The mean delay between initial weight concerns and first discussion was eight years. PWO were on average 38 years old when they first discussed their weight concerns with their HCP. The mean age of first weight concern for all respondents was 32.4 years (SD 15.8), but many (23 %) reported experiencing weight issues from childhood [Table S3].

Half (49 %) of HCPs stated that they typically start the weight discussion, with their patients initiating the discussion 43 % of the time. [Fig. S7]. Conversely, 59 % of PWO reported starting the discussion, while 39 % stated that it was HCP initiated. Some PWO (16 %) do not want their HCP to discuss their weight, but most do (84 %), albeit with varying frequency. [Fig. S8].

The predominant reasons for HCPs addressing obesity were because the patient has (70 %) or is at risk of (63 %) ORCs. Other reasons included patients’ mental health (44 %), BMI (42 %), and perceived receptiveness to discussion around weight (39 %). [Fig. 3]. However, PWO often avoided discussions because they believe weight management is their own responsibility (40 %), or they already know what they need to do to lose weight (36 %). Less than half of HCPs routinely discuss weight impact (44 %), or weight management (43 %), during appointments with PWO, with fewer routinely discussing ORCs (31 %), or obesity treatments (25 %). [Fig. S10]. HCPs believe that PWO become motivated to manage weight when a family member (77 %) or they themselves (70 %) experience a medical event, or when they want to feel better physically (75 %). Thirty percent of HCPs believe that experiences of weight bias motivate patients to focus on weight management. [Fig. S11].

Of the 57 % of PWO who discussed weight with a HCP in the last five years, three quarters found it helpful (very/extremely helpful (28 %), somewhat/a little helpful (58 %)), while 17 % found it not at all helpful [Fig. S12]. Many felt either supported (38 %), hopeful (28 %), or motivated (26 %) after the discussion, but some felt embarrassed (17 %), shamed (11 %), blamed (5 %) or offended (4 %). [Fig. S13]. Two thirds of all PWO agreed that they felt comfortable discussing weight with their HCP, that the HCP listened carefully and understood difficulties in weight management. [Fig. S14]. Eighty percent of PWO had discussed (50 %) or would consider discussing (30 %) weight with their general practitioner (GP), compared to dietician (9 %, 14 %), nurse (12 %, 8 %), or other HCPs (all <10 %) [Fig. S15].

Treatment goals, outcomes, approaches, and effectiveness

Although 86 % of PWO believed weight loss of 5–10 % would provide health benefits, their average target for weight loss was 19 % (SD 10.3) and ideally, they would prefer to lose 23 % (SD-11.0). [Table S4]. Top outcomes set by HCPs for PWO were to improve mental health and wellbeing (61 %), improve existing health conditions (55 %), reduce stress (50 %), and reduce risks associated with obesity (46 %). [Fig. S16]. Whereas for PWO, top goals included to improve QOL (39 %), lose any weight (39 %), have more energy (35 %), improve mental health and well-being (31 %), and reduce risks associated with obesity (27 %). [Fig. S17].

The most frequent weight management strategies tried by PWO were physical activity increase (61 %), calorie reduction (46 %), specific diet programs (e.g. commercial weight-loss programs) (42 %), exercise tracking (40 %), and improved eating patterns (e.g. regular meals) (37 %). In contrast, clinical interventions were far less frequently tried: 13 % had visited a dietician, 10 % used prescription obesity medication and only 4 % had consulted an obesity specialist, undergone behavioral/psychotherapy or received bariatric surgery. [Table S5]. PWO most frequently reported information sources for weight management were weight loss programs (37 %), followed by family and friends (33 %), HCPs (33 %), medical websites (32 %), and smartphone apps (31 %), but fewer used dieticians (18 %), personal trainers (13 %), peer support (7 %), or social media (3–17 %) (Fig. S18).

HCPs most reported discussing general physical activity (65 %), improving dietary patterns (53 %), and referring patients to dieticians (49 %). Perceived effectiveness of treatment approaches

Similarities and disparities existed in perceived effectiveness of interventions. Across both groups, fewer than one third of respondents rated weight management interventions as effective, except for a general increase in physical activity (PWO 36 %, HCPs 41 %). Structured exercise programs (PWO 11 %, HCPs 10 %), and tracking interventions (PWO 17 %, HCPs 11–14 %) were selected less frequently.

Marked disparities emerged in perceptions of clinical interventions. HCPs were considerably more likely than PWO to view clinical referrals as effective, particularly dietician referral (29 % vs 2 %), behavioral or psychotherapy (15 % vs 1 %), pharmacotherapy (31 % vs 5 %), bariatric surgery (16 % vs 1 %). Further, perceived effectiveness was substantially higher among HCPs who engaged with these treatments or referrals, for example, prescription medication (90 %), bariatric surgery (69 %), behavioral psychotherapy (59 %), referral to an obesity specialist (50 %), physical activity (63 %), and improved eating patterns (60 %). [Fig. S19].

Similarly, quality of life interventions such as sleep and stress management, were considered effective by HCPs (20–22 %), more so than by PWO (4–5 %). Conversely, a greater proportion of PWO expressed confidence in dietary interventions, such as specific diet programs and elimination diets (both 16 % vs 4 % HCPs). [Table S4].

Diagnosis and follow-up care

Among PWO who discussed weight with HCPs, almost half (44 %) received a formal obesity diagnosis. [Fig. S20]. While 37 % of HCPs typically record an obesity diagnosis, only 23 % routinely inform patients of the diagnosis and 40 % do not inform the patient at all. [Figs. S21 and S22]. Follow-up scheduling was limited with 42 % of HCPs reporting no follow-up scheduled, and for those who scheduled follow-up (58 %) only 45 % report that PWO mostly keep their appointment. [Figs. S23, S24, S25]. Fewer PWO (24 %) reported a follow-up being scheduled, of whom 97 % attended or planned to attend. Fifty-nine percent of PWO who had not discussed obesity, would like their HCP to schedule a weight-related appointment. [Table S5].

Prescription medication and bariatric surgery

Most HCPs (68 %) believed there are good options for prescription obesity medication, and that patients would like a prescription (65 %), but three quarters (79 %) believed medication cost is a barrier. Many are concerned about medication side-effects (60 %), long-term safety (48 %), lack knowledge about available medications (46 %) or believe patients should prioritize lifestyle changes (35 %) instead of medication and/or surgery. Most PWO are also concerned about medication side effects (64 %), would rather manage weight without medication (67 %), or believe they should focus on lifestyle changes instead (60 %). Almost half would like to be prescribed medication (43 %), but 46 % believe cost is a barrier. [Fig. S26].

Regarding bariatric surgery, most PWO (81 %) had safety concerns, would rather lose weight with diet and exercise (80 %), and believed this would mean a permanent lifestyle change (76 %). However, only 64 % of HCPs were aware of PWO safety concerns, while 76 % agreed that the wait time was too long compared to 32 % PWO. Only 5 % of HCPs believe that bariatric surgery is the easy way out, compared to 37 % of PWO. [Fig. S27].

Barriers to obesity care, multidisciplinary team (MDT), and specialist access

Importantly, 81 % of HCPs believed that society and the healthcare system is not meeting the needs of PWO, compared to 54 % of PWO. HCPs almost unanimously agreed that lack of access to specialist services and to dietetics/psychology (92 % each), patients’ mental health/emotional state (91 %) and other health conditions (90 %) are barriers to care. More than 80 % also cited lack of understanding of obesity as a disease, cost of medication, physical inactivity and poor eating patterns are barriers to effective management. PWO proportionately rate individual factors such as unhealthy eating (67 %), lack of exercise (65 %), and motivation (55 %) as barriers, with fewer rating their understanding of obesity (16 %). Discussion

The ACTION-Ireland study provides the first comprehensive nationally representative dataset on perceptions, attitudes and practices influencing obesity management among people living with obesity and a multidisciplinary sample of healthcare professionals in Ireland. Findings identify considerable alignment in recognition of obesity as a chronic disease, but persistent discrepancies in perceived responsibility, communication dynamics, treatment approaches, and deficits in system level support. Largely reflecting international findings, these insights highlight context-specific barriers, critical in informing policy and practice to improve obesity care within the Irish healthcare system.

Almost all Irish HCPs (98 %) and a significant majority of PWO (75 %) identify obesity as a chronic disease. This recognition, particularly by PWO, may indicate increasing global awareness, being higher than reported in previous ACTION-IO studies, such as Israel (95 % HCPs, 70 % PWO) [21], the UK (88 % HCPs, 68 % PWO) [25], South Korea (81 % HCPs, 78 % PWO) [22], Spain (93 % HCPs, 59 % PWO) [23] and France (89 % HCPs, 63 % PWO) [26]. Notably, this disease recognition is much higher than reported in another large global study of HCPs (25 %) and healthcare decision-makers (29 %) [16]. Recognizing obesity as a chronic disease is an essential foundation for implementing chronic disease models of care. However, this recognition does not yet translate to obesity management and practice, which remains focused on generic lifestyle advice with limited integration of MDT specialist care, pharmacotherapy, or bariatric surgery. This gap between recognition and clinical implementation suggests that further efforts are required to embed into practice, the model of care for obesity [6] and obesity management guidelines [11]. Barriers to equitable healthcare access in Ireland [27], particularly for obesity care [14,15], may reflect broader systemic, economic and political challenges identified globally [28], suggesting that further efforts are needed to ensure equitable access to obesity care worldwide.

A central finding of this study is the mismatch in perceived responsibility for weight management, whereby 86 % of HCPs believe they have a role in supporting PWO, but 82 % of PWO consider themselves solely responsible for weight. This self-reliant view is consistent with findings from previous research [29] and other national cohorts [13,22,26], and reflects internalized stigma shaped by sociocultural narratives of individual blame [[30], [31], [32]]. Notably, only 12 % of HCPs support this self-directed perspective highlighting a disconnect in how responsibility is shared and communicated during clinical encounters.

This internalization delays help seeking behavior [33,34], as the ACTION-Ireland data reveals that PWO waited an average of eight years from onset of weight concern to the first discussion with a HCP. This delay represents a critical missed opportunity for early intervention and is above the mean delay observed across ACTION studies [19]. This is further compounded by delayed access to specialized obesity care, particularly in Ireland, where waiting times exceed two years from referral to first consultation. To be effective, obesity care requires a paradigm shift toward shared responsibility and timely, collaborative management, supported by public education and HCP initiated engagement. Assertively and empathetically conveying that obesity requires collaborative long-term management, rather than individual blame or responsibility, may enhance PWO engagement.

These findings highlight a mutual hesitation to engage in obesity-related discussions, further delaying care. Approximately one third of both groups expressed discomfort raising the topic, and only 56 % of PWO reported discussing weight with an HCP in the past five years, despite a shared recognition of obesity's health impact. However, when these conversations occurred, they were perceived positively by most PWO, who found them helpful, respectful, and supportive. This challenges the concern that initiating such discussions may cause offence. Moreover, most PWO (84 %) preferred their HCP to proactively initiate weight discussion, suggesting that appropriately framed conversations are both acceptable and valued. Enhanced communication and structured care pathways could therefore, improve engagement [35]. Equipping HCPs with stigma-free communication strategies, such as motivational interviewing and weight-neutral language, may address this discomfort and empower HCPs to initiate these conversations with confidence and competence [11,[35], [36], [37]].

Perceptions of clinical referrals as ineffective from PWOs' perception warrant investigation, especially given the low uptake of professionally-led physical, nutritional, and psychological interventions. Such perceptions may reflect dissatisfaction with the accessibility, quality, or outcomes of services, but may also stem from internalized beliefs about personal responsibility, unrealistic weight loss expectations, or misconceptions of obesity as a condition with a simple ‘eat less’ cure. Moreover, generic lifestyle approaches such as change in eating pattern, calorie reduction, or physical activity were the most reported weight management approaches used by PWO, and most frequently discussed with HCPs (reported by both HCPs and PWO), with low uptake of rehabilitative exercise programs or input from dieticians. This highlights the importance of communicating to both PWO and HCPs, the chronic, relapsing nature of obesity, increasing awareness of evidence-based, professionally-led, treatment approaches, and tailoring health care to the expectations and needs of PWO.

Despite evidence supporting obesity pharmacotherapy and bariatric surgery efficacy [[38], [39], [40]], generic lifestyle interventions remain the most frequently recommended and tried approaches in Ireland. Generic physical activity and dietary changes were recommended by 72 % and 67 % of HCPs respectively compared to 34 % recommending prescription medications and 24 % suggesting bariatric surgery. This conservative approach appears to influence patient behavior, resulting in underutilization of these evidence-based treatments. Costs were a greater concern for HCPs than PWO, but both expressed concerns about medication and surgery safety. In the ACTION-Ireland study, HCPs highlighted lack of knowledge of these treatments, consistent with a study of obesity management training among Irish HCPs [41]. These findings align with global data [13,20,22] although uptake of pharmacological and surgical treatments is lower in Ireland than other countries revealing a need for urgent upskilling of HCPs in the safety, efficacy and appropriateness of pharmacological and surgical treatments. Additionally, public-patient education to address misconceptions about treatment options, alongside expanded access to and reimbursement for appropriately prescribed evidence-based treatments, is necessary to reduce inequity and improve patient outcomes.

The psychological burden of obesity emerged as a major theme in this Irish cohort, with anxiety/depression reported by over one third of PWO, yet few HCPs report routinely referring patients for psychological support (25 %). Most PWO cite difficulty maintaining motivation and effort, and feelings of helplessness as contributors to weight regain. These experiences, coupled with societal stigma, form a powerful psychological barrier to effective obesity care [42]. Integrating psychologists into multidisciplinary obesity care teams and providing PWO access to behavioral therapy and emotional support is warranted.

Similar to a large retrospective American study [43], less than half of PWO who had discussed weight with their HCP had a formal diagnosis of obesity recorded. Furthermore, among the 37 % of HCPs that typically documented obesity, only 23 % of them routinely informed patients of the diagnosis. This practice may explain PWOs self-perception, with 56 % identifying as having excess weight rather than obesity, despite 80 % living with at least one HCP-diagnosed ORC. This lack of formal diagnosis needs to be addressed as it limits access to services and reinforces the perception that obesity is not a medical condition that requires early and comprehensive care [44].

Follow-up care was similarly lacking; while 58 % of HCPs reported scheduling follow-up, only 45 % said PWO attended. In contrast, 97 % of PWO who reported a scheduled follow-up said they had attended or planned to attend, suggesting HCPs perception of non-attendance may be unfounded. These inconsistencies highlight the need for consistent structured follow-up embedded within chronic disease management frameworks. Clear diagnosis, shared decision making and proactive scheduling inform PWO that their condition is being taken seriously [45,46]. These essential steps in chronic disease care models are yet to be extended to obesity management and embedded in clinical practice.

The findings from ACTION-Ireland highlight an urgent need to align recognition of obesity as a chronic disease with accessible, evidence-based care and international best practice [3,9,10,47,48]. This reflects findings from the ACTION-IO studies, and endorses the recommendation that recording and communication of obesity diagnosis and improved access to follow up referrals and evidence based treatments within healthcare systems will support effective obesity care by promoting greater discussion, diagnosis, and direction [46]. This requires enhanced professional education for HCPs, aligned to obesity competency frameworks [32,49,50] and targeted to address gaps for individual disciplines on the MDT [41,51]. In the Irish context, particular focus on the pillars of effective obesity management, behavioral, pharmacological and surgical interventions [10,11] similar to contemporary international approaches [1,2,9,10,47], is required alongside improved empathetic communication strategies to reduce stigma and encourage patient engagement, aligned with international findings [[52], [53], [54]] and consensus approaches [55,56]. In parallel, expansion of specialist services and referral pathways, integration of psychological support across all tiers of care, public health campaigns and policy reforms are essential to address treatment costs, expand access and promote a broader understanding of obesity as a complex multifactorial disease.

Strengths and limitations

The ACTION-Ireland study provides the first Irish dataset derived from a nationally representative sample of people living with obesity, offering a robust and generalizable understanding of obesity-related perceptions and experiences among the Irish public, comparable to international findings. A further strength is the dual perspective, capturing experiences and attitudes from both PWO and a diverse group of HCPs. The use of internationally benchmarked and contextually adapted survey instruments allows for meaningful comparison with global ACTION-IO datasets while addressing the specific realities of the Irish healthcare system. The inclusion of a broad range of HCP disciplines, from medicine, nursing and allied health professions, provides a comprehensive view of current clinical practices. Furthermore, involvement of public and patient partners in survey development ensured cultural sensitivity, relevance, and consistent use of people first language, thereby enhancing the quality and acceptability of the data collection process.

However, limitations must also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design captured perceptions at a single time point limiting causal inference. Self-reported data, particularly relating to BMI status and clinical practices, may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Similarly, self-reported sociodemographic data may underrepresent individuals with obesity, particularly those with severe obesity or from lower sociodemographic groups. While recruiting the HCP cohort through national snowball sampling enabled inclusion of multiple disciplines across practice settings, it may have introduced sampling bias towards those with greater interest in obesity care. Additionally, HCP survey attrition, potentially due to survey length, repetitiveness of questions and lack of participant compensation, may have further limited representation. A representative sample of HCPs would have further strengthened findings and enhanced generalizability; however, no accessible national database of HCPs exists in Ireland to support representative sampling.

Conclusion

The ACTION-Ireland study revealed persistent gaps in diagnosis and clinical communication about obesity, in perceived responsibility for its management and in uptake of effective treatment. Similar to international findings, it revealed a health system where recognition of obesity as a disease has yet to be matched by accessible, effective treatment pathways, including evidence-based multidisciplinary care. Addressing communication barriers, underutilized treatments, and systemic deficiencies is vital.
