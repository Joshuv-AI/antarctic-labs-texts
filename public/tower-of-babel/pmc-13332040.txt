Mutational profile of atypical fibroxanthoma and pleomorphic dermal sarcoma further expands the spectrum of genomic alterations in rare cutaneous neoplasms
Authors: Elisabetta Caprini, Giovanni Luca Scaglione, Claudia Scarponi, Martina Morelli, Stefania Madonna, Carla Marani, Valeria Sebastiani, Enrico Scala, Cristina Albanesi, Siavash Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtypical fibroxanthoma (AFX) and pleomorphic dermal sarcoma (PDS) are rare cutaneous mesenchymal tumours sharing clinical and histopathological features. Compared to AFX, PDS has an increased risk of local recurrence and metastasis. A precise diagnosis is critical to ensure proper clinical management and follow-up. AFX and PDS show a similar genetic background, but also a heterogeneous pattern of different molecular abnormalities still poorly investigated due to the rarity of these tumours. Multiple data from different institutions and geographical areas, facilitate the identification of molecular alteration/s of valuable diagnostic and/or sub-classification power. We investigated the DNA profile of 32 AFX and PDS samples using a custom targeted Next-Generation Sequencing panel including 228 cancer genes. We confirm a common pattern of gene mutations affecting TP53, CDKN2A and NOTCH1. Differences appeared in less frequently detected genes (e.g. TSC2) and in NF2 harbouring novel genetic alterations. Integrating our results with published datasets of AFX and PDS mutation profiles we observed a divergent distribution of alterations in genes signalling through angiogenic pathway (KDR, PDGFRB), DNA damage response (ATR), cellular migration/metastasis (DDR2, CDH1). These differences do not reach statistical significance, and histopathological evaluation remains the diagnostic gold standard, however, they offer valuable insights into the pathogenesis of these tumours.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50665-1.Keywords: Atypical fibroxanthoma, Pleomorphic dermal sarcoma, Next-generation sequencing, NF2, KDR/PDGFRB, ATRSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Atypical fibroxanthoma (AFX) and pleomorphic dermal sarcoma (PDS) are morphologically similar mesenchymal neoplasms of the skin, classified in the “Tumours of Uncertain Differentiation” chapter of the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification1,2. Both entities most commonly arise on chronically sun-damaged skin of the head and neck region in elderly male patients, and share overlapping clinical, histopathological, and immunohistochemical features3,4. Immunohistochemical analysis is essential in excluding histological mimickers such as melanoma, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, and leiomyosarcoma; tumour cells in AFX and PDS typically lack expression of epithelial, melanocytic, and myogenic markers, however, smooth muscle actin may show positivity. Both tumour types consistently demonstrate immunoreactivity for vimentin, CD10, CD68, and α1-antitrypsin5,6. Histological evaluation remains the cornerstone of the differential diagnosis. Unlike AFX, PDS is characterized by infiltrative growth, necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, and/or perineural infiltration, features that require deep incisional biopsy for adequate assessment. Nonetheless, these distinguishing features are not always unequivocal, and well-demarcated AFX lesions may be misclassified as PDS in the absence of definitive infiltrative behaviour7,8.Clinically, PDS carries a higher malignant potential, with greater propensity for local recurrence, distant metastasis, and disease-specific mortality, underscoring the necessity for accurate diagnosis and vigilant clinical management9.

A major limitation in advancing diagnostic and prognostic stratification of these tumours is their rarity. Epidemiological data indicate a prevalence of 0.24% for AFX among over 42,000 diagnosed skin tumours10 with tumours’ incidence rates reported between 0.2 and 0.59 per 100,000 individuals11,12. This scarcity substantially restricts the statistical power and generalisability of molecular and clinical studies.

Recent advances in molecular pathology have revealed a shared mutational landscape between AFX and PDS, particularly involving ultraviolet-induced mutations in TP53 and frequent alterations in CDKN2A and NOTCH1/2 13–16. These tumours are characterized by a high mutational burden and similar DNA copy number variations, suggesting a common pathogenesis. However, considerable molecular heterogeneity exists, and discrepancies between studies are often attributed to methodological differences and limited cohort sizes17–19.

Given these limitations, the accumulation of multicentric data and expansion of sample sizes are imperative to deepen the molecular characterization of these rare entities. Such efforts are likely to enhance diagnostic precision and potentially enable the development of clinically relevant molecular subclassifications.

In the present study, we investigated the genomic profiles of 32 AFX and PDS samples using a custom-designed, capture-based Next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel encompassing 228 cancer-related genes (SOPHiA GENETICS, Switzerland). Our findings corroborate previously reported mutational patterns, thereby validating our sequencing approach. Furthermore, we identified novel genetic alterations with potential pathogenic relevance. When integrated with published mutational datasets, our results demonstrated a divergent distribution of genetic events, offering novel insights into the pathogenesis and molecular diversity of AFX and PDS.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Genomic data were extracted from Formalin Fixed Paraffin Embedded (FFPE) samples of 16 AFX and 16 PDS patients, respectively. Slices from primary tumours, collected between 2019 and 2022 in the Anatomic Pathology Department of IDI IRCCS, were subjected to manual tumour macro-dissection and DNA extraction. From 10 to 50 ng of genomic DNA was extracted for each sample and DNA libraries were prepared according to SOPHiA DNA Library Preparation Kit II (SOPHiA GENETICS S, Switzerland). Quality controls of the DNA library were performed using the Agilent Fragment Analyzer capillary electrophoresis system.

Ethical approval

The research was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol underwent review and approval by the IDI–IRCCS’s committee, with approval number IDI-IRCCS CE 686/1, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Next generation sequencing (NGS)

DNA libraries were prepared, pooled and analysed following SOPHiA DDM™ Custom Solid Tumor Solution v2 (STS) target sequencing protocol (SOPHiA GENETICS S, Switzerland). Two NGS runs were performed on a NextSeq500 Illumina platform using Illumina MiSeq® v3 (2 × 300 bp) cartridge. In each run 16 samples, eight PDS and eight AFX, were pooled together and analysed. SOPHiA DDM™ Custom STS v2 covers 217 genes (Supplementary Table S1) associated with solid tumours. This panel includes protein-coding exons for all transcripts of a given gene, with the addition of +/-5 bp flanking into the intron and including 5’ UTR exons that contain coding sequence. Additionally, hotspots regions were also analysed in the following genes: ABL, BRAF, CSF3R, CTNNB1, EGFR, IDH1, IDH2, JAK2, KRAS, MPL, NRAS. All the genetic variations were mapped on GRCh37/hg19 human genome assembly using canonical transcripts.

Bioinformatics analysis

At the end of each run, Fastq files were collected and uploaded on the SOPHiA DDM software for mapping (bam files) and variant calling (vcf files) steps. At this stage raw sequence data underwent quality control to filter and remove low-quality reads. Additionally, adapter removal and trimming were performed to ensure the integrity of the data. The SOPHiA DDM™ Platform was used for detecting, annotating and pre-classifying multiple types of genomic alterations in one unique experiment within its dedicated pipeline. Then, we collected 32 vcf files annotated by SOPHiA DDM v4 software, and data were processed using an ad hoc somatic bioinformatics filtering pipeline. As reported in Supplementary Table S2, after checking data integrity (step1) and selecting variants with depth of coverage of at least 100x (step 2), all variants classified as benign and/or likely benign by ClinVar (step 3–5) were discarded. The remaining 49,216 variants were further filtered out if the allele frequency was lower than 5% (step 6). Subsequently, SOPHiA DDM pipeline filters were applied (step 7) and intronic, synonymous as well as intergenic (step 8–10) variants removed. Lastly, population filters (MAF < 0.05) from 1000 Genomes and ExAC were applied in steps 12–13. Of the initial 168,982 (100%) records collected from 32 FFPE samples, 2030 (1.17%) records were retained at the end point of the filter chain. Downstream analyses revealed these 2030 records were representing 1233 unique variants.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ samples were grouped according to their histological diagnosis of AFX and PDS. For each tumour of our cohort, the number of variants classified by ClinVar as pathogenic/likely pathogenic and of unknown significance were counted. When multiple combined datasets’ analyses were performed, all the identified variants were included in the calculation. Furthermore, if longitudinal tumour samples were available, only the patient’s sample carrying all the identified alterations was included. The statistical difference between the number of mutations per gene found in AFX and PDS samples was evaluated by two-sided Fisher’s exact test and the estimates of odds ratio applying 2-way contingency table analysis available at https://statpages.info/ctab2x2.html.

Results

Study cohort

The AFX cohort included 16 representative AFX tumours from 16 patients and the PDS cohort included 16 patients with 16 tumours. All specimens were adequately sampled and evaluated histologically. Diagnoses were established by routine histopathological examination supported by a panel of immunohistochemical markers, including CD10, CD68, S100, SOX10, Melan-A, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), cytokeratins (AE1/AE3), cytokeratin 5/6, cytokeratin 8/18, cytokeratin 14, high-molecular-weight cytokeratin (34βE12), p63, p40, smooth muscle actin (SMA), desmin, and caldesmon. For the purposes of this study, all cases were independently reviewed and the diagnoses confirmed by three expert pathologists (VS, CM, and SR). AFX and PDS demonstrated similar morphological features. Tumours were composed of irregularly arranged spindle to epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and marked nuclear atypia and pleomorphism. Mitotic activity was brisk, including atypical mitotic figures. Prominent solar elastosis was commonly present in the adjacent dermis. Lesions that were well circumscribed and confined to the dermis, without tumour necrosis, perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, or subcutaneous invasion, were classified as AFX. Tumours exhibiting one or more of these adverse histological features were classified as PDS (Table 1). Finally, adequacy of material was considered for tumour DNA extraction. Most of the patients with AFX (13/16, 81%,) and PDS (13/16, 81%) were male. The median age at diagnosis was 83 years (range: 65–98 years) in patients with AFX and 86 years (range: 68–96 years) in patients diagnosed with PDS. Most AFX and PDS primary tumours arose on the skin of head and neck (30/32, 94%) mainly represented by the scalp (21/30, 70%) followed by forehead (3/30, 10%), ear (2/30, 7%), cheek (2/30, 7%), parietal area (1/30, 3%) and neck (1/30, 3%). Two PDS tumours, outside of the head/neck district, were located on chest and shoulder, respectively (Table 1). Histological and immunohistochemical images for CD10, SMA, and CD68 of representative AFX and PDS samples from our cohort are shown in Fig. 1.

Targeted NGS analysis of AFX and PDS tumours

The targeted panel used to investigate the genetic data of 16 AFX and 16 PDS samples included biomarker genes associated with various solid tumours. Its design covered all the protein-coding exons of 217 genes and the hotspot regions from additional 11 genes; furthermore, it detected gene amplification events of the 217 protein-coding genes and microsatellite instability (MSI) status in 6 unique loci (Supplementary Table S1).

After quality check and filtering pipeline (Supplementary Table S2), we retrieved a total of 198 genes of which, 152 (76.8%) were found to be mutated in both AFX and PDS groups, whereas 22 (11.1%) carried solely by AFX tumours and 24 (12.1%) exclusively by PDS samples (Supplementary Tables S3-S5). We firstly investigated the distribution of pathogenic and/or likely pathogenic variants as per ClinVar assignment, as well as likely oncogenic Copy Number Amplification (CNA), as classified in OncoKB™ 20, across the 32 tumour samples. For more consistency of copy number data, we selected high-level amplifications defined as having CNA values equal or greater than 4. In this manner we found 89 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants and seven oncogenic amplifications affecting 37 genes (Supplementary Table S6). The most frequently mutated genes were TP53 and CDKN2A altered in 78% and 31% of samples, respectively. Specifically, the distribution of these mutations between the two subgroups showed that 81% of AFX (13/16) and 75% of PDS cases (12/16) were affected by TP53 alterations, while 19% of AFX (3/16) and 44% of PDS (7/16) harboured CDKN2A mutations (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S7). We also observed TSC2 mutations in 16% of cases (Fig. 2A), with truncating and splice site mutations identified in 25% of AFX (4/16) and a missense mutation in one PDS case (1/16, 6%). A lower mutation rate affected NF2, NOTCH1 and PIK3CA. Although no significant differences were observed between subgroups, NF2 showed two truncating mutations in 3 PDS samples (19%), but no alterations in AFX (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Tables S6-S7). Less than 3 mutated samples carried pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in the other genes (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S7). Our NGS target panel did not support copy number loss analysis which may result in an underestimate of some gene mutation frequencies, like TP53 and CDKN2A, in our cohort with respect to other studies. We observed few CNA mainly affecting PDS samples (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S6). Interestingly, one PDS sample carrying PDGFRA amplification harboured also KIT and KDR CNA. Closely located on chromosome 4q, PDGFRA and/or KIT amplification events have been found also by other groups in AFX and PDS17,18,21. Promoter mutations of TERT were also frequently reported in both subgroups of tumours16,19; we were unable to compare this finding within our patients’ cohort as our target panel did not fully covered TERT promoter region, however, our analysis detected one oncogenic CNA in one AFX case. To further characterize these tumours, we enlarged the mutation profile analysis adding also the variants of unknown significance. We considered this approach useful for discovery purposes despite the possible occurrence of random passenger mutations. We identified a total of 217 genetic variants and 10 amplification events affecting 77 genes (Supplementary Table S8). The most frequently affected gene remained TP53, showing a slight increase in AFX (88% of mutated samples), followed by CDKN2A (38% of AFX, 50% of PDS) and NOTCH1 (31% AFX, 13% PDS) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S9) confirming their established role in both tumour entities15,16,18,19,21. The inclusion of variant of unknown significance increased mutation rates also for NF2, predominantly affected in PDS (31%) compared to AFX (6%), TSC2, increasing the proportion of AFX affected samples to 31%, and TSC1 disproportionately affecting AFX (25%) with respect to PDS (13%) (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S7-S9). No additional alterations were found for PIK3CA showing oncogenic mutations in 6% of AFX and in 13% of PDS (Fig. 2B) already reported by others at various occurring frequencies15,16,19,21. One HRAS activating mutation and one missense mutation of unknown significance were present only in PDS samples (Fig. 2A, B), similarly to what previously found also in other studies14–16. In our analysis we found 40 more genes affected only by variations of unknown significance, mostly occurring at low frequency. Among them we found a single PDS sample carrying a missense mutation in NOTCH2 gene (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Tables S8-S9), which, conversely, was found frequently mutated in both AFX and PDS by some authors16,19.

MSI analysis indicated a stable status in all analysed tumours, except for one PDS case, which displayed microsatellite instability with a low confidence level (Supplementary Table S10). We found an overall mutational load of 81.0 (66.7–91.7) [p50 (p25-p75)] nonsynonymous variants per mega base (Mb), assuming ~ 0.6 Mb targeted genomic space, with higher average median value of 91.1 (69.6–102.0) mut/Mb in PDS with respect to AFX displaying 72.5 (66.2–75.0) mutation/Mb. These findings are in line with previous reports18,19.

Collectively, the data achieved with our custom targeted NGS cancer panel confirm previous findings which identified TP53, CDKN2A and NOTCH1 as the most frequently altered genes in both AFX and PDS, as well as other common mutations occurring at lower frequency. Some gene alterations showed a prevalence in our groups of AFX (e.g. TSC2) and PDS (e.g. NF2) not observed in prior investigations. The small numbers of analysed samples and several technical/analytical differences may account for the variable occurrence and distribution of some gene alterations. Similarly to former studies, we did not find any statistically significant difference between AFX and PDS, even when variants of unknown significance were considered.

Integration of AFX and PDS mutation profiling datasets

Considering the rarity of AFX and PDS cases among cutaneous neoplasms, the identification of molecular features that may distinguish them is mostly limited by the small sample size and the large genetic homogeneity. We reasoned that, increasing the number of AFX and PDS tumours, may help to overcome these constraints and identify biologically relevant alterations with or without a known functional impact. As unknown variations have an unclear effect on protein activity, this approach has an exploratory role aimed at providing some hints into the still largely unknown disease pathogenesis. To this end, we combined our dataset with previously published NGS based molecular profiles of genetic alterations performed in AFX and/or PDS. The following studies were selected because the complete data output was available: (1) the dataset of 13 AFX of Griewank, KG et al. 16 assayed with the MSK-IMPACT multi gene panel, here indicated as D1, (2) the sample groups of 10 AFX and 13 PDS analysed by Ak, M. et al. with the FoundationOne®CDx test19, defined as D2 and (3) the whole-exome sequencing of 28 PDS tumours performed by Klein, S. and colleagues18 named as D3 (see Supplementary Table S11 for details about each dataset and assay). We then searched the above-mentioned datasets for mutated genes that were common to our cases (representing the D4 dataset) and distributed in a balanced and representative number of additional AFX and PDS. The largest intersection was identified between D2 and D4 datasets, yielding 62 commonly mutated genes across 26 AFX and 29 PDS that were evaluated for significant differences in the mutation rate using two-sided Fisher’s exact test and odds ratio estimates. Although not significant, we found tendency values for KDR (p = 0.0605) and PDGFRB (p = 0.0822), both showing higher odds (3.04 and 4.57 times, respectively) to be altered in PDS rather than in AFX tumours. Conversely, ATR shows the lower odds (0.28 times) to be observed in PDS rather than in AFX with a tendency towards significance (p = 0.0729) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S12). No mutation clustering within specific functional domains were observed for any of the protein identified (Fig. 3B). Further cross-dataset integrations identified eleven common mutated genes within a total of 29 AFX and 29 PDS and seven mutated genes shared by 39 AFX and 44 PDS (Supplementary Tables S13-S14). Despite the large number of samples, no significant differently mutated genes were identified, but the odds ratio trend indicates that DDR2, CDH1, FGFR1 and MET were more likely to be found mutated in PDS, while NBN, FGFR2 and BTK displayed the opposite trend (Fig. 4A, B). These results suggest that, although genetically related, AFX and PDS display some differences representing possible clues of a diverse molecular pathogenesis.

Discussion

Our targeted NGS panel analysis, performed in a series of 32 AFX and PDS patients, identified TP53 and CDKN2A as the genes most frequently affected by inactivating mutations in both groups. These findings are in line with previous studies showing, also, that both genes displayed a UV-mutation pattern consistent with the sun-exposed skin anatomical localization13,15–19,21. Among the less frequently affected genes, we detected NOTCH1 which, however, showed a substantial increase in the mutation rate when also variants of unknown significance were considered. This approach aligned our study with other investigations performed with targeted NGS large gene panels16,19. With respect to these studies, the major difference concerned NOTCH2, affected by a mutation of unknown significance only in one PDS sample, while it was frequently found in both AFX (30–42% of cases) and PDS (23–49% of samples). Mutational profiling studies may interrogate diverse databases for cross-referencing somatic alterations which may explain these discrepancies, as well as other minor differences. Indeed, all the identified alterations, regardless their functional impact, present in our sample group, compared with the D2 dataset of Ak, M. et al.. 19, revealed similar frequencies in the most commonly mutated genes (see Supplementary Table S12).

In our series HRAS mutations were confined to two PDS samples, supporting the very low mutational frequency, which excludes its utility as differential diagnostic marker14–16. Other previously reported altered genes such as COL11A1, CSMD3, FAT1, DNHD1, RTN1, RTL1, ZBTB7A, NCKAP5L and FAM200A could not be investigated as these genes were not part of our NGS panel16,18,22. Among less commonly altered genes, TSC2 — and to a lesser extent TSC1— carried pathogenic mutations predominantly in AFX, suggesting a prominent role of mTOR signalling pathway in this entity similarly to what observed in other rare mesenchymal tumours23. On the other hand, NF2 carried truncating mutations exclusively in PDS representing a novel finding of this study. Although16NF2 has been included in previously used NGS cancer panels, its alteration in PDS had not been previously described. Beyond its established role in neurofibromatosis type 2 24, NF2 is also a tumour suppressor gene implicated in various tumours, such as mesothelioma, schwannomas, and meningioma25. Interestingly, NF2 genetic alterations have more frequently been observed in biphasic and sarcomatoid, rather than epithelioid-like, histological subtypes of malignant pleural mesothelioma, suggesting that NF2 inactivation might be involved in epithelial–mesenchymal transition26. Additionally, several lines of evidence indicate a close relationship between RAS and NF2, with the latter being able to inhibit the RAS-mediated signalling pathway25. Intriguingly, at physiological level, NF2 regulates epidermal development playing key roles in epithelial adhesion and polarity27. Its prevalence in PDS might contribute to the more aggressive behaviour of this entity compared to AFX.

Our study, consistent with previous observations, has shown that AFX and PDS display a similar pattern of frequent functionally relevant mutations in several critical genes (e.g. TP53, CDKN2A, NOTCH1), suggesting they share a common profile of oncogenic driver events. However, some differences, between AFX and PDS, have emerged analysing all identified gene alterations, without regards to their functional impact19, indicating that, besides the putative driver mutations, further oncogenic events might differentiate the pathogenesis of AFX and PDS. The rarity of these tumours often limits the statistical power of individual cohorts. Thus, we integrated our dataset with three published cohorts, including all genetic variants irrespective of their functional effects, enabling the analysis of a larger combined samples’ group. Using two-sided Fishers’ exact test and odds ratio plots, we tried to prioritize their relevance in the disease pathogenesis. No significant differences in mutation frequencies emerged between AFX and PDS, indicating that none of the evaluated alterations can be considered potential discriminative biomarkers. However, KDR and PDGFRB displayed a tendency towards higher mutation rates in PDS. Both genes are known to be involved in the angiogenesis of various solid tumours28 and also in some sarcoma subtypes such as angiosarcoma and osteosarcoma29. Particularly, in osteosarcoma KDR alterations co-occurred with KIT and PDGFRA as we also have observed in a PDS case of our cohort (Fig. 2) suggesting a signalling dysregulation through these three receptor tyrosine kinase genes located at the 4q12 locus29. Alterations of genes regulating the PDGFR pathways were already described in previous studies17,18,21. Further investigations, therefore, are required to establish if angiogenic pathways may represent a pathogenetic mechanism involved in PDS tumour growth and invasiveness. Also, the simultaneous amplification or high co-expression of KIT/PDGFRA/KDR should be clarified to ascertain their potential clinical utility. Conversely, ATR alterations showed a trend towards higher frequency in AFX (Fig. 3). ATM and ATR encode important sensors of DNA damage upstream of TP53 that are central to the DNA damage response signalling pathway. They both are critical for double-stranded DNA breaks (DSB)-induced checkpoint responses and DSB repair, but they have non-redundant roles in these processes30,31. Unlike ATM, ATR is activated by a broad spectrum of DNA damage through a single-stranded DNA-dependent mechanism32, suggesting that distinct types of DNA damage and/or different DNA damage signalling pathway response might be elicited in AFX and PDS.

Among genes identified through broader dataset integration, Discoidin Domain Receptor 2 (DDR2) and CDH1, also known as E-cadherin, appeared more likely to be mutated in PDS rather than AFX (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, DDR2, co-expressed with PDGFRB/A, distinguishes PDS lineage-specific genes, indicating it is a functionally active gene in this entity18. DDR2 is a receptor tyrosine kinase that mediates activation of several downstream signalling pathways, such as the MAPK and PI3K pathways33–35 and its activation or expression has been involved in metastasis of various cancer types36–39. Alterations of CDH1 have been found by Ak M. et al.. in 5/13 PDS samples (38%), but not in AFX cases (0/10)19. CDH1 promotes the signalling cascade leading to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition40 and its inactivation enables cell detachment, increases cellular motility, contributing to cancer progression41,42. On the other hand, BTK (Bruton’s agammaglobulinemia tyrosine kinase) showed a trend towards higher mutation rates in AFX (Fig. 4B). Although primarily associated with B-cell malignancies43 novel oncogenic BTK isoforms have been discovered in solid tumours44,45 with aberrant expressions across multiple cancer types45–51 warranting further exploration of its role in AFX. The absence of statistical significance across all integrated analyses reflects the inherent limitations of studying rare tumours with relatively small cohorts and heterogeneous methodologies. Validation in larger independent cohorts — ideally through standardised sequencing approaches — will be essential to confirm these trends and to determine their biological and clinical relevance.

Conclusions

Collectively, our NGS analysis of over 200 genes confirms previous mutational profiling studies, demonstrating that AFX and PDS share common genetic alterations, most frequently affecting TP53, CDKN2A, and NOTCH1. We observed potential differences between AFX and PDS in some less frequently altered genes, particularly TSC2 and NF2, which warrant further investigations to establish if they may represent molecular subtypes of clinical relevance.

By combining multiple datasets of mutational profiles, we improved the representativeness of both AFX and PDS. The differences observed—particularly if considering somatic alterations of unknown significance—did not reach statistical significance, limiting their utility as genetic biomarkers for distinguishing the two malignancies. However, these alterations have not been thoroughly investigated in AFX and PDS. Notably, genes involved in angiogenic signalling (KDR, PDGFRB), DNA damage response (ATR), cellular migration, and metastasis (DDR2, CDH1) show marked differences in the distribution of DNA alterations between the two groups. Further studies are needed to determine whether these differences reflect distinct pathogenetic mechanisms with diagnostic or therapeutic relevance in clinical practice.

According to our data and previous studies, molecular analysis, along with immunohistochemical marker evaluation, is not helpful for the differential diagnosis between AFX and PDS in a clinical setting. Among all immunohistochemical and molecular criteria, histopathological features, such as margin status, depth of tissue invasion, presence of necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion, remain the primary and only reliable parameters for distinguishing PDS from AFX.

Limitations of the study

The major weakness of our study concerns the heterogeneity of NGS platforms used to compile the different datasets. This necessarily limited the analyses to selected gene lists that, although robust due to the larger numbers of samples and different NGS approaches, do not investigate all the possible sources of somatic DNA changes. Therefore, further explorative whole-exome, whole-genome and/or transcriptomic studies might give more comprehensive results. The lack of available paired normal tissue in our study is another considerable limit. Although most germline variants have been excluded by SNP databases filtering, we cannot exclude the possibility that occasional germline variants or clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) associated mutations were interpreted as somatic mutations. Given the small cohort of our patients represented by elderly individuals, CHIP-associated variants may potentially contribute to the detected mutations, especially in genes known to be affected in age-related clonal haematopoiesis (e.g. TET2 and ASXL1)52. Despite the limitations of the study, the results advocate for larger, multi-centre studies to validate these findings and integrate them with clinical data and functional investigations.
