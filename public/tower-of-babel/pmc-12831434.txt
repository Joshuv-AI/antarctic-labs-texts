Network dynamics of metabolic disease continuum in older adults: a 7-year longitudinal cohort study in China
Authors: Guoqing Xu, Qingxian Song, Meng Jia, Yingni Yu, Shu Su, Jinning Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic diseases co-occur as multimorbidity rather than presenting in isolation. This study aims to estimate the evolution and patterns of multimorbidity networks centered on metabolic disease continuum (MDC) among old adults to understand the accumulation over time and identify factors associated with multimorbidity.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal study involving individuals aged ≥ 65 years who underwent examinations at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between 2016 and 2022. Network analysis was applied to analyze the multimorbidity patterns over time between 6 MDCs (including diabetes, osteoporosis, Vitamin D deficiency, obesity, fatty liver, and hyperuricaemia) and 20 other non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of association for all diseases. Kaplan–Meier curves, univariate Cox regression, and multivariable Cox regression were used to evaluate the risk of multimorbidity and its risk factors in patients with MDCs.ResultsAmong the 10,052 participants, MDC-centered multimorbidity networks became more interconnected over time. Hypertension showed the strongest and progressively increasing correlation with MDCs (male: r = 0.177 to 0.209; female: r = 0.189 to 0.220). Patients with MDCs had a higher risk of developing fatty liver (first examination: OR = 5.21, 95% CI: 4.52–6.00; last examination: OR = 8.87, 95% CI: 7.28–10.81). In addition, diabetes (HR = 1.322, 95% CI: 1.245–1.403), vitamin D deficiency (HR = 1.394, 95% CI: 1.107–1.754), obesity (HR = 1.793, 95% CI: 1.601–2.007), and fatty liver (HR = 1.308, 95% CI: 1.224–1.398) were associated with an increased cumulative risk of multimorbidity. Among patients with MDCs, female sex (HR = 1.156, 95% CI: 1.108–1.205), older age (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.001–1.007), higher BMI (HR = 1.067, 95% CI: 1.059–1.074), higher education (HR = 1.084, 95% CI: 1.036–1.135), salty diet (HR = 1.353, 95% CI: 1.276–1.435), and moderate drinking (HR = 1.218, 95% CI: 1.080–1.373) further increased multimorbidity risk.ConclusionsMDC-centered multimorbidity networks became increasingly interconnected, with hypertension consistently showing the strongest association. Specific metabolic disorders (diabetes, Vitamin D deficiency, obesity, fatty liver, and hyperuricaemia) and demographic-behavioral determinants (female, older age, higher BMI, drinking and salty diet) significantly accelerated multimorbidity progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06882-w.

Introduction

Chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) including diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic respiratory disease and cancer are the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. Approximately 41 million people die from NCDs each year, and three-quarters of these occur in low- and middle-income countries [2]. It is estimated that by 2030 the most common NCDs will incur cumulative global economic losses of around $47 trillion [3, 4]. The rising prevalence and fragile health-care systems without universal coverage exacerbate the financial burden on households in low- and middle-income countries [2, 5, 6].

Given the growing recognition that chronic diseases interact rather than occur in isolation, recent research has emphasized the need to explore their shared mechanisms and overlapping pathways. The Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework was introduced to describe the close and interdependent relationships among metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and kidney dysfunction [7, 8]. Within this framework, metabolic abnormalities are considered the earliest and most modifiable factors in the disease process.

These abnormalities collectively form the metabolic disease continuum (MDC), which includes hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, and hyperuricemia. These metabolic disorders rarely occur in isolation but rather coexist often coexist and develop sequentially [9], forming a network of multimorbidity that increases the risk of CVD and other serious complications [10, 11], thereby increasing both disability and mortality burdens [12, 13]. Globally, more than one-third of people with chronic disease live with two or more conditions, and the multimorbidity prevalence rises with age [9, 14]—about 65% in those aged 65 and older [15, 16]. According to recent reports, the prevalence of all major metabolic diseases has increased from 2000 to 2019, with hypertension contributing the highest mortality and disease burden [17, 18]. Therefore, in the context of global population ageing, early identification of patterns of MDC is of critical importance for reducing individual suffering and optimizing public-health resources.

Multimorbidity develops sequentially as individuals accumulate diagnoses [19]. However, longitudinal research examining the progression and risk factors of MDC-centered multimorbidity among older adults remains limited. This study aims to investigate the temporal evolution and structural patterns of MDC-centered multimorbidity networks, and to identify key determinants associated with disease accumulation. These insights may inform prevention strategies and support healthcare planning, in alignment with the Healthy China 2030 policy framework [20].

Method

Method

Study design and participants

A single-center, retrospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted to examine MDC-centered multimorbidity patterns among older adults in southwest China. Data was collected from the Health Management Center, Affiliated Second Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. including all the examination records from 2016 to 2022. Two independent databases were used: (1) demographic and lifestyle information, and (2) clinical diagnostic. Demographic and lifestyle characteristics included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), education, smoking, drinking, dietary patterns, and exercise frequency. All lifestyle information was obtained through self-reported questionnaires.

Participants aged ≥ 65 years were included. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete demographic and lifestyle information, (2) missing diagnostic information, or (3) missing identification information.

Procedures and outcomes

Disease status was determined by combining physician diagnoses, standardized clinical criteria and disease history. Disease history was obtained by asking participants whether they had ever been diagnosed with any chronic condition. Based on the determined disease status for each participant, diseases with a prevalence of ≥ 0.1% in the cohort (≥ 30/30,052 participants) were selected and grouped into 26 chronic disease categories which were further organized into four major subgroups: (1) 4 cardiovascular diseases continuum, including hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD), dyslipidemia and arteriosclerosis; (2) 3 chronic respiratory diseases, including emphysema, pulmonary fiber, and chronic pharyngitis; (3) 6 MDCs, including diabetes, osteoporosis, Vitamin D deficiency, obesity, fatty liver, and hyperuricaemia; and (4) 13 other common chronic diseases, including liver cyst, renal cyst, thyroid nodule, prostate diseases, polypoid lesions in the gallbladder, intrahepatic calcification lesion, abnormal liver function, gallstone, sinus bradycardia, pulmonary nodules, thyroid cyst, cataract and kidney stone disease (Table S1).

Records were merged using a unique examination code, and participant identity was tracked via ID number. Repeated visits per individual were calculated accordingly.

The primary outcome was to identify MDC-centered multimorbidity patterns and their evolution over time. The secondary outcome was to explore the associations between multimorbidity occurrence and demographic or lifestyle factors.

Statistical analyses

Multimorbidity network construction

Multimorbidity networks were constructed to depict associations among chronic diseases, particularly highlighting core MDC-centered conditions. Edges represented statistically significant Spearman correlations (p < 0.05) between diseases, with color indicating direction (pink: positive; green: negative) and thickness indicating strength. Similarly, the size of nodes represent the degree, which is the number of related nodes obtained through cluster analysis of node subsets using Cytoscape software (v3.7.2). The core diseases are defined as nodes with a degree of at least 10 in each sub-group. Finally, the multimorbidity network is visualized using Gephi software (v0.1.0).

Odds ratio (OR) analysis for multimorbidity in patients with MDCs

Odds Ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated to evaluate the association between MDCs and other MDCs or other NCDs. Each MDC was treated as an exposure, and the co-occurrence with one or more diseases from four main disease categories (cardiovascular, respiratory, other MDCs, and miscellaneous conditions) was considered as the outcome. The exposure groups were recognized as risk factors if the ORs and its 95% CI exceeded 1. ORs were also calculated for the associations between the first and last examinations to assess changes in the risk of disease co-occurrence over time. Sensitivity analyses were performed using multivariable logistic regression adjusting for demographic and lifestyle characteristics.

Survival analysis of multimorbidity in patients with MDCs

To assess the risk of multimorbidity progression in patients with MDCs, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to estimate the risk of co-occurrence of each individual MDC with other diseases, including both MDCs and other NCDs. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI were calculated for each MDC to the occurrence of other diseases during the study period. Person years of follow-up were calculated from baseline to the date of the occurrence of multimorbidity other than MDCs, or end of study period, whichever came first. The Kaplan-Meier curves (KM-curves) showed the cumulative hazard of survival and occurrence of developing additional diseases at each follow-up time for patients with or without MDCs.

Multivariable cox regression analysis for risk factors for the development of other NCDs in patients with MDCs

Multivariable Cox regression was used to explore the impact of demographic and lifestyle factors on the development of other NCDs in individuals with one or more MDCs. Factors such as sex, age, BMI, education level, dietary habits, smoking, drinking and exercise habits were included in the model to estimate their effect on the risk of developing other NCDs. HRs and 95% CIs were computed for each factor.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1. All tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 34,156 case reports met the study inclusion criteria. After excluding records with missing ID values or incomplete electronic medical records (n = 3,840), 30,496 valid records remained. These were grouped by ID and name, identifying 10,057 unique patients. Following the exclusion of patients without disease information (n = 5), the final analytic cohort included 10,052 participants. A detailed flowchart of participant selection is presented in Fig. 1.

Table 1 summarizes the basic demographics and behavioral characteristics of the 10,052 participants at the first examination. Among them, 78.67% of the participants suffered from MDCs. Compared to patients without MDCs, those with MDCs were older (with MDCs: 68.00 [64.00, 74.00] years, without MDCs: 67.00 [64.00, 72.00] years), shorter (with MDCs: 159.50 [153.50, 166.00] cm, without MDCs: 161.00 [155.00, 166.50] cm), heavier (with MDCs: 63.00 [56.00, 70.30], without MDCs: 55.40 [50.60, 61.00]), and the number of visits was lower (with MDCs: 3.00 [2.00, 4.00], without MDCs: 3.00 [3.00, 4.00]) (All p < 0.001). Behavioral data displayed lifestyle differences among participants in variables such as dietary habit, drinking, smoking, and disease history.

Network patterns of MDC-centered multimorbidity

Figure 2 showed sparsely multimorbidity correlation networks, retaining disease nodes with degree ≥ 4. Core diseases were defined as nodes with degree ≥ 10. For males, the core diseases shifted from arteriosclerosis (degree = 17) and prostate diseases (degree = 15) at the first examination to renal cyst (degree = 17) and prostate diseases (degree = 15) at the last. For females, arteriosclerosis (degree = 17) and MDCs (degree = 16) were the leading core diseases initially, whereas by the last examination the centrality shifted shifting to MDCs (degree = 16) and dyslipidemia (degree = 13).

Compared to the first examination, the correlation of MDC-Centered Multimorbidity network increased over time, indicating that individuals with MDCs are more prone to developing other related conditions concurrently, regardless of gender. In males, significant increases in the association of hypertension (r = 0.177 for first versus r = 0.209 for last) and prostate disease (r = 0.097 for first versus r = 0.151 for last) with MDCs was observed. In females, hypertension (r = 0.188 for first versus r = 0.220 for last), dyslipidemia (r = 0.118 for first versus r = 0.155 for last), and renal cysts (r = 0.072 for first versus r = 0.104 for last) also exhibited increases in association with c MDCs.

Associations and cumulative risk of multimorbidity development in patients with MDCs

Associations between MDCs and multimorbidity development

Table 2 reflected the strength of association for each MDC with the co-occurrence of other diseases. In general, the risks of developing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, other chronic metabolic diseases, and other diseases in the first examination were significantly higher for individuals with each metabolic disease compared to those without it. Notably, within MDCs, each condition exhibited the highest risk of coexisting with other MDCs, except for osteoporosis and Vitamin D deficiency. For instance, individuals with fatty liver have a 5.21-fold higher risk (95% CI: 4.52–6.00) of developing other MDCs compared to those without fatty liver. In addition, individuals with fatty liver also have a higher risk of developing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 2.63–3.46) as well as other diseases (OR = 2.28, 95% CI: 2.18–2.81). Similar results were also found in the last examination.

Odds ratios for MDC co-occurrence with cardiovascular diseases continuum, chronic respiratory diseases, other MDCs, and other diseases at first and last physical examinations

Compared with first examination, patients with osteoporosis demonstrate a markedly increased risk for chronic respiratory diseases (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15-1.58), other MDCs (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.38 – 1.93), and other diseases (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.10-1.63) at last examination (Table 3). Similarly, patients suffering from obesity have a considerably higher incidence of cardiovascular (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05 – 1.29]) and chronic respiratory diseases (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09 – 1.30), as well as other MDCs (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.16 – 1.34). Furthermore, individuals with hyperuricaemia are at a heightened risk for chronic respiratory diseases (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.14 – 1.53). Those with fatty liver are more susceptible to both chronic respiratory diseases (OR = 1.23, 95% CI:1.08 – 1.39) and MDCs (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.75 – 2.77).

Cumulative risk of multimorbidity in patients with MDCs

After over 2000 days of follow-up, Univariate Cox Regression and Survival Analysis revealed the association between MDCs and the cumulative risk of multimorbidity progression. As detailed in Table S3 and Fig. 3, diabetes (HR = 1.322; 95% CI: 1.245–1.403), Vitamin D deficiency (HR = 1.394; 95% CI: 1.107–1.754), obesity (HR = 1.793; 95% CI: 1.601–2.007), fatty liver (HR = 1.308; 95% CI: 1.224–1.398), and high blood uric acid (HR = 1.350 ; 95% CI: 1.272–1.432) were significantly associated with an elevated risk of developing additional diseases compared with normal status.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of these findings, a sensitivity analysis using multivariate logistic regression adjusting for demographic and lifestyle covariates was conducted. As shown in Table S2, the results were generally consistent with the unadjusted ORs reported in the primary analyses. Diabetes, obesity, fatty liver, and hyperuricaemia remained significantly associated with the cardiovascular disease continuum (adjusted ORs ranging from 1.33 to 1.77, all p < 0.05). Osteoporosis was persistently associated with increased risks of chronic respiratory diseases (adjusted OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.32–1.69) and other diseases (adjusted OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.19–1.63). Fatty liver (adjusted OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.94–2.51) and hyperuricaemia (adjusted OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.30) also showed robust associations with other diseases.

Risk factors for the development of other NCDs conditions in patients with MDCs

The Multivariate Cox Proportional Hazards Regression model analysis identified several risk factors for the development of other NCDs in patients with MDCs. Female (HR = 1.156, 95% CI: 1.108–1.205), older age (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.001–1.007), higher BMI (HR = 1.067, 95% CI: 1.059–1.074), higher education level (HR = 1.084, 95% CI: 1.036–1.135), salty diet (HR = 1.353, 95% CI: 1.276–1.435), and moderate drinking (HR = 1.218, 95% CI: 1.080–1.373) were associated with an increased risk of multimorbidity progression. In contrast, exercising three or more times per week significantly reduced the risk of developing additional NCD conditions (HR = 0.867, 95% CI: 0.837–0.899) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study revealed a high prevalence of common MDCs among older adults in Southwest China and demonstrated that individuals with MDCs experienced a substantially higher progression rate of multimorbidity during follow-up, especially for hypertension. Several demographic and lifestyle factors, including female sex, older age, higher BMI, higher education level, salty diet, and moderate drinking, were associated with increased risk of multimorbidity.

Multimorbidity network analysis indicated that the MDC-centered Multimorbidity network became more interconnected over time, suggesting that patients with MDCs were increasingly likely to develop additional related conditions. This strengthening of inter-disease connections is consistent with shared pathophysiological mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction [21–23]. These mechanisms interact synergistically and can disrupt multiple organ systems, increasing susceptibility to multimorbidity. Hypertension emerged as the most strongly connected condition across both genders and became more central over time, aligning with previous network studies identifying hypertension as a key metabolic hub [24].

An interesting and novel finding was the increasing correlation between MDCs and renal cysts among female participants. Although it is often viewed as benign and age-related, recent evidence links multiple or large renal cysts with metabolic syndrome and systemic metabolic dysregulation [25, 26]. This suggests that renal cysts in female MDC patients may warrant closer metabolic evaluation rather than being dismissed as incidental.

Beyond network patterns, our longitudinal analyses showed that MDCs increased both cross-sectional co-occurrence and the cumulative hazard of developing additional chronic diseases. Fatty liver had a higher risk of developing other MDCs and cardiovascular. This observation aligns with prior evidence that fatty liver represents a systemic metabolic dysfunction and is a key driver of insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and low-grade inflammation, which collectively increase cardiovascular and metabolic risk [27]. By altering glucose and lipid metabolism, disrupting adipokine signaling, and inducing hepatic and systemic inflammation, fatty liver creates a biological environment conducive to the onset of obesity, hyperuricaemia, dysglycaemia, and other MDCs [28–31]. Consistently, in our study, obesity, hyperuricaemia, diabetes, and vitamin D deficiency were also associated with increased cumulative hazards for developing multimorbidity, reflecting shared metabolic pathways that drive multimorbidity accumulation [31–33]. These findings suggested early identification of specific MDCs could enable targeted preventive strategies to slow or prevent the progression toward multimorbidity. And screening for fatty liver, diabetes, vitamin D status, and hyperuricaemia may be warranted in at-risk populations.

Risk factor patterns in our study were broadly consistent with previous literature. Female sex, older age, higher BMI, drinking, and salty diet increased the risk of multimorbidity, whereas exercising three or more times per week significantly reduced the risk [34–36]. The higher risk observed in women may reflect biological differences, including hormonal and metabolic characteristics [37, 38], as well as biological factors such as hormonal differences, metabolic characteristics, and immune response [39]. Women also tend to utilize healthcare services more frequently, which may lead to earlier diagnosis and contribute to the observed higher risk [40]. Interestingly, higher educational attainment was associated with increased risk of further NCD development among patients with MDCs. This finding contrasts with most prior studies, which report that lower education is linked to higher multimorbidity risk [41, 42]. Previous study suggests that highly educated individuals generally have greater health awareness and healthcare utilization, leading to earlier detection and diagnosis of chronic conditions, which may explain the observed elevated risk [43]. Evidence from Mendelian randomization studies indicates that BMI and smoking partially mediate the education–multimorbidity association, with approximately 20% of educational inequality explained by BMI and about 32% explained by BMI combined with smoking [44]. Therefore, the observed increased multimorbidity risk among highly educated individuals likely reflects a combination of factors, including diagnostic bias and lifestyle-related mediators, rather than a direct causal effect of education itself. In summary, these findings underscore the role of lifestyle interventions, including weight control, healthier diet, limited alcohol consumption, and regular physical activity, in slowing the progression of multimorbidity among patients with MDCs, with important consequences for public health and clinical management.

Several limitations should be noted in the study. First, only selected disease groups were captured due to the structure of the medical examination reports, and detailed severity indicators (e.g., graded glucose or uric acid measures) were unavailable. Second, the network analysis aggregated all MDCs into a single node to visualize overall multimorbidity patterns. Although this improves interpretability, it does not reflect heterogeneity among individual MDCs. Our disease-specific analyses partially address this limitation, and future work could incorporate more comprehensive network models. Third, the analysis relied on baseline characteristics without capturing changes over time, such as evolving lifestyle behaviors. Finally, as a retrospective study, missing data, loss to follow-up and small sample sizes in certain subgroups (e.g., vitamin D deficiency) may affect estimate stability. Despite this, our study is among the first to apply longitudinal network analysis to characterize the evolution multimorbidity patterns of MDCs, providing meaningful insights for targeted prevention and disease management in older adults.

Conclusion

Hypertension remained the most influential comorbidity, with its association strengthening over time. Diabetes, vitamin D deficiency, obesity, fatty liver, and hyperuricaemia markedly increased the risk of multimorbidity progression. Among individuals with MDCs, female sex, older age, higher BMI, alcohol consumption, and a high-salt diet further elevated this risk. These findings highlight the need for integrated prevention strategies that target key metabolic disorders and modifiable lifestyle factors to slow the progression of multimorbidity in aging populations.
