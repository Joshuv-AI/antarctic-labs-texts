Barriers and facilitators to the utilisation of point-of-use water treatment systems during and immediately following flood-related disasters: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Rebecca Johnson, Sophie E Shank, Pamela M Aaltonen, Ernest R Blatchley, Lindsey Payne, Vicki Simpson, Afsan Bhadelia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroduction The United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goal 6 seeks to ensure universal access to safe drinking water by 2030, but vast inequities in access exist, especially among vulnerable communities including limited resource, rural, disaster-affected areas. Flood disasters, exacerbated by the climate crisis, hinder the ability of individuals and families to meet essential drinking water needs and increase their susceptibility to waterborne illnesses. Point-of-use (POU) water treatment is an effective solution for water-insecure populations during and immediately following flood emergencies. However, an initial literature search identified knowledge gaps surrounding implementation of POU water systems. This scoping review aims to synthesise published evidence between January 2015 and July 2025 on barriers and facilitators to utilisation of POU water treatment systems during and immediately following flood-related disasters. The findings will inform efforts to promote resilience and agency among water insecure communities, specifically by equipping them with actionable knowledge on sustainable access to safe drinking water.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will be guided by the work of Arksey and O’Malley and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews. Search terms will be identified through an iterative process using the PICOT method and Boolean logic. Four databases—Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science and Google Scholar—with the addition of grey literature from UN agencies and non-governmental organisations focused on water-related issues will be searched. Two independent reviewers will apply a priori eligibility criteria to select studies. Conflicts will be resolved through discussion and a third independent reviewer absent agreement between the first two reviewers. Cohen’s kappa statistic will be calculated to assess inter-rater reliability. Data extraction will be guided by predefined data points, and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research will guide evidence synthesis through a solution-based approach.Ethics and disseminationInstitutional research ethics review is not required because no human subjects are involved. Findings will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication, a policy brief, conference presentations and infographics for use by organisations serving flood disaster impacted communities.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Public health, Gastrointestinal infections, Implementation Science

Introduction

Global crisis around access to clean water

Globally, 2.2 billion individuals still lack access to safe drinking water.1 While recognised as a United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goal (SDG), specifically SDG 6.1 related to clean water, progress on this complex problem remains limited.2 Preventable deaths occur due to a lack of safe water, sanitation and hygiene practices with diarrhoea being the most commonly reported problem, accounting for over a million deaths globally per year.3 An estimated 91% of diarrhoeal cases could be prevented with adequate access to safe drinking water.3 Point-of-use (POU) water treatment alongside other protective factors can help mitigate preventable waterborne illnesses and related mortality.4 This issue is exacerbated when natural disasters such as flooding decrease availability and access of safe drinking water to at-risk and vulnerable populations.

While community-based water treatment systems provide widespread access to clean water through the economies of scale, flood damage can render these systems non-functional.5 Based on contextual considerations, this review focuses on POU water treatment systems to build agency and resilience among individuals affected by flood-related disasters. It is important to distinguish POU water treatment systems, which are considered to be POU when the user treats the water immediately prior to consumption, from a point-of-entry (POE) system in which water is treated before it enters a structure, and community-based systems that clean and store large batches of water for on-demand access at scale.6 While literature often uses terminology such as household water treatment (HWT), household drinking water treatment and POU water treatment interchangeably, this study uses the term POU water treatment to specify treatment of water by the end user immediately prior to consumption and not attached to a structure or treatment of water on entry into a structure.

Additionally, flooding can provide increased risk of both pathogenic and chemical contamination of the water supply system. However, the interventions to combat these two very different sources of contamination can be vastly different, making it difficult to include both in one single study of this nature.7 As such, this study will focus on pathogenic causes of contamination only and chemical causes of contamination will not be included, presenting this as a limitation.

Climate crisis and flood disaster issues

Flood disasters are the most prevalent of all natural disasters and are increasing in number.8 9 Climate change and global population growth are contributing to the increase in flood-related disasters.10 Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are at disproportionately higher risk in the event of flood disasters due to structural vulnerabilities in preparing for and withstanding large-scale disasters.11 Adequate, timely and affordable access to safe drinking water is essential to build resiliency. However, during and immediately following flood disasters, many existing water treatment modalities will not be operational, and a decentralised method is critical for safe and equitable access to those most in need.5 Flooding not only causes contamination of well water, but also local surface waters from rainfall and flooding, or due to sewer overflow.5 Additionally, transportation of water to affected areas is fraught with issues from the ensuring trucked water is potable to the logistics of travel on potentially impassable roads and amidst scarce or non-existent electricity to treat the water.5

Study rationale

Floods are a major public health concern, generating morbidity and mortality due to enteric waterborne illnesses.8 An estimated age-standardised 1244 per 100 000 disability-adjusted life-years were attributed to unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene worldwide in 2019. While the disease burden has decreased considerably since 1990, children and the elderly still face an unnecessary and inequitable share of the burden.12 The lack of coordinated public health response to combat infectious diseases following flood events complicates overall response.9 The dearth of evidence-based solutions for individuals to access safe drinking water during and after flood disasters is a challenge.13 Few studies cover POU water treatment and those that do are generally in stable settings or as a part of broader development efforts.14 For example, a review on the barriers and facilitators of HWTs does not consider flood-based disaster settings.15 The inability to find research relating to POU water treatment systems in emergency settings has been recognised.16 This review aims to map and synthesise any available evidence on barriers and facilitators to the use of POU water treatment systems during and immediately following flood disasters to inform future research.

Methods and analysis

A scoping review will be guided by Arksey & O’Malley,17 the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis,18 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA) framework.19 A scoping review, as opposed to a systematic review, matches the goal of descriptive synthesis of available evidence on a research area in order to map it and identify knowledge gaps.20 As appraisal of methodology and bias is not undertaken in scoping reviews, it will not be undertaken for the present review.21 Covidence software will be used for the collection and review of the literature. A preset scoping review protocol developed using a template created by the Health Justice and Systems Innovation lab at Purdue University will be implemented. The information sources and databases as well as search terms will be selected based on major themes of the work related to flooding, water treatment, POU and disasters using Boolean logic to reinforce the search criteria. Specifically, the following steps as identified below will be taken sequentially.

Step 1: defining the research question

The primary research question is: ‘What are the facilitators and barriers to the effective utilisation of POU water treatment systems among individuals or households experiencing a flood-related disaster, especially in LMICs?’ This review is part of broader research which will use findings of the present study to develop a decision-making tool for individuals facing flood disasters in the Americas and more specifically Spanish-speaking countries. The design of this protocol is also informed by the focus of the broader research. The research question was derived after several focused preliminary literature reviews designed to query safe drinking water procurement by individuals during and post flood disasters using the population, intervention, comparison/control, outcome, time (PICOT) framework for research question formulation.22 Further, based on gaps identified in the literature,14 16 it was determined that identification of all available literature related to the research question was essential. While evidence from all settings is of relevance, evidence on POU water treatment from low-to-middle income countries is of particular importance due to vulnerabilities in disaster preparedness and response in these settings.

The scoping review focuses on safe drinking water access (free from pathogenic contaminants) during and immediately after a flood-related disaster and considers the timeframe to begin at the flood event and last until individuals have the stable ability to access safe drinking water within humanitarian guidelines. Though rare, some documentation exists for providing insight into a timeline for restoration of access to safe drinking water post flood disasters.23 24 Sphere guidelines detail the minimum standards for humanitarian response and SDG 6 clearly indicate that safely managed water should be available within 30 min and free from contamination.25 26 However, the literature notes variability in the time required to restore access to safe drinking water during or after flood disasters.27 Specifically, examples indicate that temporary solutions have been necessary for 2 months or more.28 29 This is impacted by the duration of the flood event. For example, in one study on the effects of a severe flash flooding event in Bangladesh, residents reported flooding lasting from 2 to over 11 days with most flooding lasting 4–10 days. Of the residents in this study, 92% stated they lacked access to safe drinking water.24 Listing a specific time frame would be arbitrary given the range of factors impacting response time and ultimately may not capture the core aim—restoration of stable access to safe drinking water. Stable access is defined as consistent and uninterrupted access to safe drinking water beyond the minimum standard required by Sphere guidelines.

With these parameters in mind, this study will use the conceptual boundary of any time frame required to ensure immediate water access in alignment with humanitarian guidelines both during a flood event and after until stable access is restored.

Step 2: identifying relevant studies and grey literature

The search approach is iterative in nature, uses guidance from a university librarian to ensure literary consistency and confirm relevance of data repositories needed to capture the breadth and depth of information for a scoping review, and maximises quality through early consultation of experts with relevant technical and methodological knowledge.30 31 Specifically, the authors of this article bring multidisciplinary expertise in the fields of disaster preparedness, engineering, nursing and public health.

To ensure success and appropriateness of design, an initial review of major databases was conducted to confirm the relevance of proposed data sources. A limited search was initially conducted to identify relevant keywords focused on the primary concept of safe drinking water; flood-related disaster setting; POU water treatment options to ensure access to safe drinking water; the timeliness, affordability, accessibility and efficiency of POU systems; and public health implications. A few of the initial terms used included: flood, “water-related disaster” point-of-use, “water treatment”, access, timel*, afford*, quality, waterborne, assess*, barrier and facilitator. Key terms identified through the iterative process included synonyms used by respective databases with the appropriate Boolean logic to map concepts to search terms as detailed below.

Application of the PICOT framework is associated with better reporting quality.22 Hosseini et al32 note the importance of the use of PICOT for studies on interventions considering effectiveness and flexibility of concept parameters. As such, PICOT provided a systematic process for search term mapping and selection (table 1; online supplemental appendix A presents sample search).22 The population defined in this study focused on individuals or households experiencing a flood disaster. The intervention chosen was the POU water treatment system. A real-life counterfactual, not using any POU water treatment was treated as the comparison or control group, particularly as POE or community-based water treatment systems are unlikely to be functioning in a disaster where water treatment is needed. Community-based systems are likely to have been damaged or contaminated during a large flood disaster. It is important to note, these large, centralised water systems are often reliant on electricity which also has the potential to be unavailable during or after a flood-related disaster due to power outages.5 As such, a direct water treatment comparison is neither practical nor appropriate. The anticipated outcome is effective implementation, as measured by accessibility, acceptability, efficiency, timeliness, affordability and quality of POU water treatment systems to ensure improved access to safe drinking water (table 1).25 33 Lastly, the time horizon for the review is the 10-year window between 2015 and 2025 to align with adoption of the SDGs in 2015 and literature published since that focuses on SDG 6 on clean water and sanitation.

The search will be conducted in Scopus (for its breadth of coverage and availability of articles), PubMed (for its focus on health sciences), Web of Science (all databases) (for its broad reach and focus on health sciences) and Google Scholar (because of its inclusion of grey literature and broad coverage across disciplines). Primary, secondary and tertiary sources will be included.34 The specific search strategy for the largest database to be searched is included in online supplemental appendix A, including exact terms, dates of search and results.34

Key grey literature will be captured to ensure inclusion of information that is not published in indexed journal articles. Beyond Google Scholar, UN and non-governmental organisation (NGO) resource repositories of agencies working on water related issues and following the scope of safe drinking water and the hazards of floods will also be searched. All grey literature will stem from this source unless included in the results from the Google Scholar search. The rationale for limiting the search to these sources is feasibility, including the volume of results and related time required to assess the breadth and depth of grey literature available and the knowledge that Google Scholar includes grey literature in search results. Additionally, with the focus of this study on addressing SDG 6, the identified sources are expected to report on major guidelines and implementation efforts worldwide, especially high-level information of relevance.

Literature in both English and Spanish will be considered accounting for the focus of the broader research, which this scoping review informs. The addition of a second language in the search serves to reduce language bias and increases the chances of an equitable representation of the literature.32 In instances where an article is presented in two languages, the original language will be reviewed for the purposes of the review to maintain consistency in screening. Google Scholar does not have a formal extraction process, creating difficulties in exporting title, abstract and full-text content. Moreover, the first 10 pages of results (or 100 hits) are expected to identify the most relevant information. As such, Google Scholar results will be manually searched and restricted to the first 10 pages. A snowball citation search of articles included from Google Scholar (studies meeting eligibility criteria during full-text review) will be conducted to reduce selection bias. Once the search is performed across included databases, results will be imported into Covidence, a web-based platform for management and review of systematic and scoping review results.

Step 3: study selection

Screening of abstracts and titles from the database searches will be undertaken by two independent reviewers with disagreements resolved through discussion and as needed, by a third independent reviewer if agreement cannot be reached between the first two independent reviewers.34 The reviewers will follow the established eligibility criteria to identify articles for full text review. The primary researcher and reviewer will train the secondary reviewer to ensure inter-rater reliability or consistency in screening. The Cohen’s kappa statistic for title and abstract review and full text review will be reported.

Eligibility criteria include studies focused on implementation and utilisation of POU water treatment systems. The criteria are based on piloting the search and screening process with joint review of the first 25 articles in Covidence and reevaluation of criteria for appropriateness and effectiveness. Step 4: data charting

Predefined data points and areas of information will guide the extraction process in Covidence. Data points will include but not be limited to; citation details, study design, data collection methods, geographic location, POU water treatment system used, barriers, facilitators, disease outcome data, study setting (urban/rural), country income level (World Bank classification) and to assess the effectiveness of implementation, the dimensions of acceptability, efficiency, access, timeliness, quality and affordability of POU water treatment systems will be used as indicators of success. Defined measurement of each dimension include the following: acceptability (user-defined acceptability), efficiency (as it relates to the ability of safe water to be procured in a quantity needed within the specified 30-minute time frame), access (defined by user-reported ease of access), timeliness (ability to procure safe drinking water within humanitarian guidelines), quality (pH, Escherichia coli, turbidity measurements that meet the WHO guidelines),35 affordability (self-reported ability to afford innovation). The aim of the data charting will be to capture descriptive information about the selected studies, defining features of study design (eg, dependent and independent variables), and classification of research findings to effectively complete step 5. Data charting will also be iterative with initial extraction and refinement after research team review. Variables for which source-specific information is extracted will be defined, noting related assumptions, and corresponding data will be presented in the online supplemental appendix of the publication reporting results. Synthesis of results will include the source of the data and how it relates to the research aims. Moreover, a PRISMA flow chart will detail the number of sources reviewed at each stage and specify the reason for exclusions.

Step 5: applying the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research framework to synthesise and report results

Step 5 will be guided by Reardon36 on data analysis using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), a determinants framework in implementation science. Specifically, it can help identify the barriers and facilitators to increasing delivery of a previously implemented innovation which is of importance to the present study.37 The CFIR framework allows facilitators and barriers to be grouped into one of five domains—innovation (the innovation we are studying such as POU water treatment systems), outer setting (external factors), inner setting (internal factors), individuals: roles and characteristics (who are they and what are their characteristics), and the implementation process (components, features or strategies for implementation).37 The CFIR framework will help synthesise actionable information on critical factors that determine success or failure of the POU water treatment systems during or immediately following a flood disaster.

To identify gaps in the literature all five CFIR domains along with the associated constructs and subconstructs will be used to provide the greatest breadth and depth to this scoping review. These domains along with their corresponding constructs and subconstructs will be used to create a codebook that supports identification of common themes within the domains. This includes 8 constructs in the innovation domain, 10 constructs in the setting domain, 11 constructs in the inner setting domain, 2 subdomains and related constructs in the individual domain and 9 constructs in the implementation process domain. Data will be extracted from the study articles and categorised into these five domains and their corresponding constructs. table 3 outlines the CFIR domains and constructs to be used. Using the CFIR Implementation Research Worksheet and the CFIR Constructs and Definitions Worksheet as a guide, the definition of each construct has been created relevant to the research being conducted and table 3 is reflective of this.36 38 39 The CFIR has been constructed specifically to align these domains as a framework for matching them to either barriers or facilitators.38 Similarly, the extraction process will then take relevant information gained from included studies and allow this data to be categorised into the appropriate construct and domain along with its alignment with being either a barrier or facilitator.38

Discussion

There has been global progress on access to safe drinking water over the past two decades; however, further progress is impeded by social and structural determinants that generate inequities, including those related to interconnected SDGs such as SDG 5 on gender equality.1 The World Bank recently launched the Water Forward initiative that is seeking to improve ‘water security for 1 billion people by 2030’. It is linked with national compacts and commitment to priority-setting reforms and investment to achieve this goal.40 The present review seeks to close the gap between the depth of knowledge we have on POU water treatment systems and the individual use of these technologies during or immediately after a flood emergency, considering lived experience with flood-related disasters and individual level vulnerabilities impacting implementation. This research may help improve health outcomes by bridging technological capabilities and identifying strategies for successful and sustainable adoption of these technologies during and shortly after flood disasters. While the focus of this review is on flood-related disasters, findings are highly transferable to other disaster contexts, offering actionable insights to improve deployment of POU and other individual-level health interventions in settings where centralised infrastructure has collapsed and is non-functional or is unavailable. The review results can serve as a foundation for developing effective, tailored implementation strategies that enhance feasibility and cultural acceptability across varied contexts.
