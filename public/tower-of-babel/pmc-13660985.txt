KSHV K15 genetic heterogeneity is associated with Kaposi sarcoma aggressiveness through modulation of the host inflammatory microenvironment
Authors: Yu Zhang, Peng Wang, Jingzhan Zhang, Yunying Wang, Tingting Li, Yuan Ding, Xiaojing Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kaposi sarcoma (KS) exhibits profound clinical heterogeneity, yet current risk assessment remains constrained by anatomical staging systems that lack universality across diverse KS variants. This study characterized the functional impact of Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) K15 genotypes (M or P) on clinical aggressiveness in a retrospective cohort of 156 patients from Xinjiang, China. To enable unified analysis, we operationalized disease extent into a harmonized binary framework—categorized as localized or disseminated—by integrating anatomical criteria from the ACTG and Krigel classifications. Using causal mediation analysis to quantify the “Genotype–Inflammation–Edema” association, we identified the K15 M-genotype (72.8%) was independently associated with a hyper-inflammatory phenotype. This revealed a Viral–Clinical Paradox: despite significantly lower HIV coinfection rates compared to P-carriers (10.3% vs. 27.5%; P = 0.009), M-carriers exhibited an elevated Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and severe clinical edema. Mediation analysis confirmed that the SII significantly mediated 29.0% (P = 0.009) of the genotype-associated effect on edema. To translate these biological insights into clinical utility, a six-factor prognostic nomogram was developed using LASSO regression, maintaining an events-per-variable ratio of 11.2. The model demonstrated robust discrimination (Concordance Index, 0.761) and significantly outperformed traditional anatomical observation (AUC, 0.830 vs. 0.563; P = 0.003). Risk stratification successfully identified distinct subgroups, separating patients with rapid progression (median progression-free survival: 11.0 months) from those with favorable outcomes (40.0 months; P < 0.0001). Our results suggest that the K15 M-genotype is associated with increased KS severity via intrinsic inflammatory activation, independent of host immunodeficiency. Integrating viral genotyping into clinical assessment may shift KS management from anatomical observation toward biology-driven precision oncology, identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from early systemic or anti-inflammatory interventions.

Introduction

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a complex angioproliferative malignancy caused by the Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV) [1]. Its clinical spectrum is remarkably heterogeneous, varying from indolent, slowly progressing cutaneous patches commonly seen in classic KS to aggressive, rapidly fatal visceral dissemination often associated with HIV coinfection [2]. In the modern era of widespread use of antiretroviral therapy, the classical paradigm linking KS progression to HIV-induced immunodeficiency has been fundamentally challenged. A significant subset of patients with an effectively reconstituted immune system experience rapid disease progression. This clinical observation highlights a potential “Viral-Clinical Paradox”, where aggressiveness persists despite immune reconstitution—suggesting that disease progression is associated not solely with host immunodeficiency but also with host genetic environmental factors and viral determinants, including K15 heterogeneity and viral load [3].

Current paradigms in oncology have largely shifted toward precision medicine, where molecular subtyping dictates management, as has been successfully implemented in breast cancer and melanoma [4–6]. However, risk stratification for KS remains constrained by the reliance on anatomical staging systems such as the AIDS Clinical Trials Group (ACTG) and Krigel classifications [7–9]. Although valid for assessing the physical tumor burden, these frameworks are fundamentally limited because they capture the anatomical manifestations of the disease rather than its underlying biological factors. Consequently, they often fail to reliably predict individual outcomes, leaving clinicians without robust tools to distinguish between patients who require immediate systemic chemotherapy and those who are suitable for accumulation-sparing observation. There is a critical and unmet need for accessible biomarkers that reflect underlying pathogenic factors to guide precision management.

Tumor-promoting inflammation is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer, particularly in virus-driven malignancies [10]. The genetic heterogeneity of KSHV is a potent but understudied determinant of clinical variability; however, its direct contribution to disease severity remains unproven [11,12]. The K15 gene, located at the right end of the viral genome, encodes a multi-transmembrane signaling protein that exists as two highly divergent alleles: the prototype (P) form and minority (M) form [11–13]. These alleles share less than 33% amino acid homology in their cytoplasmic signaling domains, suggesting that they may use distinct molecular interactors relevant to pathogenesis. While the P-form has been extensively characterized for its ability to recruit Src-family kinases and TRAFs to activate pro-inflammatory and angiogenic pathways (NF-κB, MAPK) [14–16], the functional consequences of the M-form remain poorly understood in clinical settings. Despite this structural divergence, both variants are thought to converge in inflammatory signaling cascades. However, whether the M genotype is associated with a distinct clinical phenotype, specifically “inflammation-associated” aggressiveness, remains a critical knowledge gap, as most prior studies have been limited to descriptive molecular epidemiology without clinical correlation.

The Xinjiang region of China, a historical hub on the Silk Road, offers a unique window into this viral diversity owing to the high prevalence of KS and the co-circulation of distinct viral genotypes rarely seen in Western cohorts. Although the geographical prevalence of the K15 genotype has been previously reported, data from Asia are limited and fragmented [11,12,17–22]. We hypothesized that the K15 M-genotype may not merely be a neutral geographic marker but an associated factor in a specific “inflammatory phenotype” that observationally explains this viral-clinical paradox. This study aimed to (1) characterize the distribution of K15 genotypes in the largest single-center KS cohort in Asia, (2) elucidate the potential biological pathways linking viral genotypes to clinical edema using causal mediation analysis, and (3) develop and validate a biologically informed prognostic nomogram to shift risk stratification from an anatomical stage to a biology-driven model.

Results

Baseline characteristics and distinct clinical phenotypes of KSHV K15 genotypes

A total of 156 patients with KS were enrolled. After quality control and genotyping, 147 patients were included in the final analysis (K15M, n = 107 [72.8%]; K15P, n = 40 [27.2%]; Fig 1A). Although baseline demographics were comparable across groups (Table 1), distinct clinical profiles were observed (S1 Table). Notably, a divergence between HIV status and inflammatory burden was identified; despite a significantly lower prevalence of HIV coinfection in K15M carriers than in K15P carriers (10.3% vs. 27.5%, P = 0.009), the K15M group exhibited significantly elevated systemic inflammatory indices, including SII, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and neutrophil counts (all P < 0.05; Figs 1B and 1C). Clinically, this profile was associated with a higher incidence of lower limb edema in K15M carriers (37.4% vs. 20.0%, P = 0.045). Conversely, mucosal involvement was significantly less frequent in the K15M group (5.6% vs. 17.5%, P = 0.044; Fig 1D).Furthermore, the proportional distribution of primary therapeutic modalities showed no significant difference between the K15P and K15M groups (P = 0.104, S2 Table), confirming that the subsequent survival analysis was not systematically biased by unbalanced therapeutic interventions.

Multivariable analysis of genotype and phenotype

Multivariate logistic regression identified K15M carriage as an independent predictor of specific clinical phenotypes. Importantly, this association remained robust after adjusting for tumor stage and demographic factors (S1 Fig). Specifically, K15M carriers had 2.6-fold increased odds of lower limb edema (Model B: aOR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.07–6.37, P = 0.034). Furthermore, this genotype strongly predicted a high inflammatory burden, including elevated SII (aOR = 5.43, P = 0.001), NLR, and PLR. Notably, these associations persisted regardless of the CD4 + T-cell count adjustment, suggesting that the viral genotype exerts a pathogenic effect independent of the patient’s baseline immunodeficiency.

SII mediates K15M-associated edema via a genotype-dependent dose-response pathway

The results of causal mediation analysis implicated SII as a significant partial mediator linking K15M to edema (Fig 2A and S3A–C Tables). Although the direct effect of the genotype remained significant, the indirect pathway via SII was statistically robust (ACME P = 0.009), accounting for 29.0% of the total effect. To rigorously validate the directionality of this association, we performed a reverse mediation analysis (hypothesizing Edema → SII). Unlike the forward model, this reverse pathway failed to reach statistical significance (P = 0.075), supporting the hypothesis that inflammation may be associated with an upstream position in this association rather than a downstream consequence. Furthermore, the dose–response analysis revealed a distinctive genotype-dependent biologic gradient (Figs 2B and 2C). In K15M carriers, the risk of edema increased linearly with elevating SII (OR=1.58 per 500-unit increase, P = 0.025); no such relationship was observed in K15P carriers (P = 0.271), reinforcing that the inflammation–edema link is intrinsically tied to the viral genotype.

K15M genotype as an independent prognostic factor for PFS.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that K15M carriers experienced significantly inferior progression-free survival (PFS) (Log-rank P = 0.017; Fig 3A). In the multivariate Cox model, the K15M genotype retained a substantial hazard ratio (aHR = 1.87; Fig 3B). Although the P-value was borderline (P = 0.052), sensitivity analyses under varying censorship scenarios confirmed the robustness of this risk estimate (Fig 3C; S4 Table). Subgroup stratification revealed a stage-dependent pattern; the deleterious effect of K15M was most pronounced in localized disease (hazard ratio (HR) = 4.32, P = 0.009; Fig 3D and S2 Fig). Additionally, statistical interaction tests confirmed that age (using the sensitivity-validated 55-year cut-off; S3 Fig) did not confound the impact of the viral genotype (P for interaction = 0.555).

To assess whether the above associations were attributable to clinical subtype heterogeneity, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to Classic KS patients (n = 115; K15P, n = 25; K15M, n = 90). The inflammatory signature of K15M was consistently observed in this subgroup. K15M carriers exhibited significantly higher SII (P = 0.003) and NLR (P = 0.006) than K15P carriers. Associations with edema (aOR = 2.27, P = 0.304) and PFS (aHR = 1.44, P = 0.297; S4 Fig) remained directionally consistent but did not reach statistical significance, likely attributable to the limited K15P reference group (n = 25). The persistent inflammatory associations (P < 0.01) in this Classic KS subgroup do not support subtype-driven confounding as the primary explanation (S5 Table).

Variable selection and hybrid model construction

Based on the optimized cut-off points derived from Maximally Selected Rank Statistics (MaxStat) (S6 Table), we implemented a systematic feature selection framework. In the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)Cox regression, cross-validation identified core predictors with nonzero coefficients among the 12 candidates (S5C and S5D Figs). The final model (Table 2) utilized a hybrid strategy: the K15 genotype and visceral status were forced into the model as biological anchors, whereas the CD4 count, PCT level, LMR, and NLR were selected based on LASSO strength. Notably, the non-linear prognostic association of baseline CD4 count was characterized using restricted cubic splines, identifying distinct high-risk phenotypes (S5E Fig). The SII was excluded to prevent collinearity redundancy (variance inflation factor < 1.10).

Validation and clinical utility of the nomogram

Based on these six predictors, a visual prognostic nomogram was constructed (Fig 4A). The nomogram demonstrated strong discriminative ability with a C-index of 0.761 (95% CI: 0.704–0.818) and excellent calibration (Fig 4B). Internal validation via bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) yielded a corrected C-index value of 0.743. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis confirmed sustained accuracy over 1, 2, and 3 years (areas under the ROC curve [AUCs] > 0.80; S5F Fig). Subsequently, we benchmarked the nomogram against existing prognostic determinants. The proposed nomogram (AUC = 0.830) significantly outperformed both anatomical stage (AUC = 0.563, P = 0.003) and visceral involvement alone (AUC = 0.530, P < 0.001; Fig 4C). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 156 KS patients from Xinjiang, we observed that the K15M genotype is significantly associated with aggressive disease manifestations—including elevated systemic inflammation, edema, and reduced progression-free survival—despite lower HIV coinfection rates. These findings support a viral–clinical paradox, wherein disease severity appears dissociated from host immunodeficiency. While previous molecular epidemiological studies have mapped the global distribution of K15 alleles [11,12,17–22], they have primarily focused on geographic prevalence. We describe a clinically relevant association: K15M carriers manifest a hyperinflammatory profile independent of traditional HIV-related immunosuppression. This observation challenges the dogma that aggressive KS is solely a consequence of host immune collapse and aligns with the hypothesis that the M-genotype may influence host signaling pathways to sustain systemic inflammation.

Historically, the Xinjiang region, a conduit of the Silk Road, represents a unique intersection of genetic diversity, where divergent viral strains co-circulate. The predominance of the M type (72.8%) in our cohort suggests that distinct evolutionary pressures may have been selected for this proinflammatory variant in Central Asian populations. This enrichment parallels that observed in classic KS cohorts from Japan and Taiwan and suggests host genetic factors may shape viral genotype distribution [19,22]. This geographic specificity underscores the limitations of generalizing prognostic models derived from Western cohorts to regions with distinct viral molecular epidemiology, highlighting the need for localized genomic surveillance.

A pivotal contribution of this study is the resolution of this paradox through causal mediation analysis to link viral genotype with clinical phenotype. Mechanistically, the biological plausibility of our statistical findings lies in the structural divergence of the K15 protein. K15 is a multi-pass transmembrane protein whose signaling capacity resides in its C-terminal cytoplasmic tail, which contains motifs (such as sources of SH2- and SH3-binding sites) that recruit Src-family kinases and TRAF adaptors to activate the NF-κB and MAPK pathways [23–25]. While the predominant P-type has been characterized to mobilize these pathways via specific tyrosine-based motifs, the M-type exhibits substantial sequence divergence (<33% homology) in this signaling domain [26,27]. Specifically, the M-type cytoplasmic tail exhibits reduced dependence on the canonical YEEV motif for NFAT activation [26], a feature that may permit constitutive signaling in diverse host microenvironments. We hypothesize that the M-type cytoplasmic tail may also harbor structurally distinct motifs that confer enhanced affinity for inflammatory adaptors or lack negative regulatory phosphorylation sites present in the P-type. Such structural variance would result in constitutive, unchecked signaling output. This aligns with our clinical observation that the M genotype is associated with sustaining high-grade systemic inflammation (SII). Our finding that SII mediates 29.0% of the genotype’s effect on edema is consistent with a pathogenic hierarchy: the viral signaling domain may be associated with the onset of the cascade, systemic inflammation may amplify it, and vascular instability (edema) may represent the clinical outcome. This model is biologically plausible given the structural divergence of the K15 M-allele [26,27] but requires experimental validation. This is consistent with a potential feed-forward loop in which tumor-induced cytokines promote vascular permeability, facilitating stromal remodeling favorable for tumor progression.

This biological distinction has profound therapeutic implications, potentially paving the way for a “genotype-informed” therapy for KS. Currently, treatment guidelines are largely standardized across viral genotypes. However, our findings suggest that K15M carriers, who exhibit a distinct, likely NF-κB-driven inflammatory profile, may represent a subgroup with potential sensitivity to anti-inflammatory interventions. For these high-risk patients, traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy can be augmented with agents targeting the inflammatory axis, such as immunomodulators or specific cytokine inhibitors. While prospective trials are required, the K15M status could serve as a potential companion diagnostic tool to identify candidates for “anti-inflammatory” adjuvant strategies.

Translating these biological insights into clinical practice, we constructed a six-factor prognostic nomogram. In the model design, we balanced the mechanistic understanding with statistical stability; although SII was identified as a key mediator, it was excluded from the final nomogram to avoid multicollinearity, with NLR and LMR selected instead for their superior predictive independence. This validated nomogram represents a significant step toward precision medicine. Unlike the anatomical stage alone, which relies primarily on physical tumor distribution and may overlook the biological heterogeneity of early-stage disease, our model integrates viral genotyping with routine markers. With a concordance index of 0.761 and superior net clinical benefit demonstrated by DCA, this tool may help clinicians identify “high-risk localized” patients who may warrant early systemic intervention rather than the standard “watchful waiting” strategy.

This study has some limitations. First, this single-center retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and the rarity of KS precluded an external validation cohort. The cohort showed marked subgroup imbalance, with K15M and classic KS being predominant—a pattern reported in other classic KS-endemic regions [19–22]. To address this, we confirmed model stability via internal bootstrapping (5,000 replicates) and subgroup analyses, and the event-per-variable ratio (11.2) exceeded the recommended threshold (>10) for multivariable modeling. Furthermore, to mitigate the potential confounding effect of clinical subtype heterogeneity, a dedicated sensitivity analysis confined to Classic KS patients (n = 115) confirmed that the core inflammatory associations of K15M (SII and NLR, both P < 0.01) were preserved independently of KS subtype, although clinical endpoints (edema and PFS) did not reach statistical significance due to the limited K15P reference group (n = 25) in this subgroup (S4 Fig, S5 Table).Second, a small proportion (5.8%) of viral genotypes remained unclassified. This may reflect either (i) recombination events between P and M alleles—a phenomenon documented in other herpesviruses—or (ii) the presence of K15 variant(s) not covered by our PCR primers, such as the reported N-type (or non-P/M) alleles [21]. Future whole-genome sequencing will be needed to resolve this ambiguity. Third, this study lacks baseline epidemiological data on the prevalence of K15 genotypes in the general Xinjiang population or in asymptomatic KSHV carriers, and therefore we cannot directly demonstrate relative enrichment of K15M among KS patients in this region. Population-based epidemiological surveys will be required in the future to formally validate this observation. Fourth, the assessment of edema in this study was performed using qualitative rather than quantitative operational definitions. As with any retrospective observational study, residual unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded by available covariates. The results of the causal mediation analysis should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory, biologically plausible association rather than a definitive causal inference. Finally, our evidence linking the K15M genotype to a hyper-inflammatory phenotype relies on clinical inflammatory indices, statistical associations, and causal mediation analysis, rather than direct wet-lab experimentation. While archival plasma was utilized for viral DNA genotyping—a process involving highly stable nucleic acids—these long-term retrospective samples lacked the strictly standardized pre-analytical handling necessary for sensitive protein-level cytokine arrays. As a result, running direct molecular assays to biologically prove the exact inflammatory markers induced by K15M in this historical cohort would be prone to significant artifacts. Thus, although our statistical findings are biologically plausible and conceptually aligned with existing in vitro studies of K15 signaling variations [25,27], future investigations employing strictly controlled prospective cohorts and transcriptomic profiling (RNA sequencing) are essential to confidently map the precise downstream cytokine cascades driving this phenotype.

In summary, the KSHV K15M genotype was significantly associated with KS aggressiveness in this cohort, suggesting a proinflammatory pathway independent of host immunodeficiency. We validated a genotype-integrated nomogram that significantly outperforms anatomical staging. While these findings warrant prospective evaluation, they provide a strong rationale for incorporating K15 genotyping into the clinical assessment of KS in regions with high M-genotype prevalence, particularly to identify patients who may benefit from early anti-inflammatory strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the analysis, and patient data were anonymized prior to access. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (ΚΥ2024101008). The individual in the clinical photograph (S8 Fig) has given written informed consent (as outlined in PLOS consent form) to publish this image under a Creative Commons license.

Study population and design

This retrospective cohort study included 156 patients with histopathologically confirmed KS who were admitted to Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (January 2007–June 2025). The hospital is a tertiary center serving a diverse population in Northwest China; thus, the study cohort is representative of the population and viral epidemiology of the region. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed KS; (2) availability of pretreatment peripheral blood of sufficient quality for genomic DNA extraction; and (3) ascertainable clinical outcomes with ≥6 months follow-up, except for patients who reached primary endpoints (progression or death) earlier. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete clinical data preventing accurate staging or outcome assessment; and (2) age < 18 years. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for observational reporting [28] and the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines for prognostic models [29] (S1 and S2 Files).

KSHV genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using a Polymeric Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (MF061–04; Mei5 Biotechnology Co, Ltd, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. K15 genotyping was performed using highly sensitive subtype-specific nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays targeting distinct variable exons of KSHV K15. The complete list of primer sequences based on previous protocols [11,19,30] is shown in S7 Table. To ensure assay validity, the PCR runs included strict negative (water) and positive controls. Amplicons were visualized via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, with genotypes assigned based on the band size: 285 bp for the P-type and 370 bp for the M-type (S7 Fig). Assay specificity was further validated by Sanger sequencing of representative products (GenBank reference NC_009333.1) [21]. Samples failing to amplify with either primer set after three independent attempts were classified as “Unclassified” and excluded from genotype-specific analyses.

Outcomes, definitions

To address the absence of a universal staging system encompassing all KS variants, we operationalized disease extent into a harmonized binary framework to enable unified analysis. Patients were stratified independently of etiology based on phenotypic severity, integrating anatomical criteria from the Krigel and ACTG classifications [7,8]. Localized disease was defined as the presence of cutaneous lesions with or without non-bulky regional lymphadenopathy or minimal (non-nodular) oral involvement (equivalent to Krigel I/II and ACTG T0). Disseminated disease was characterized by visceral involvement, extensive oral disease, tumor-associated edema, and ulceration (equivalent to Krigel III/IV and ACTG T1). Given the retrospective nature of the historical EMR data, tumor-associated edema was operationalized as a binary clinical variable (present vs. absent). Tumor-associated edema was defined qualitatively as visually apparent, non-physiologic tissue swelling secondary to KS lesions relative to the patient’s normal baseline or contralateral limb, rather than by standardized quantitative metrics. Representative anonymized clinical photographs illustrating typical KS-associated edema in this cohort are provided in S8 Fig. Given the 18-year span and resultant therapeutic heterogeneity, specific treatment regimens were not included as covariates. Instead, to evaluate potential treatment-induced bias between virological groups, primary interventions were categorized into three main tiers (systemic or combined therapy, local therapy, and no active treatment) and independently compared between K15 genotypes. This strategy prioritizes isolating intrinsic viral and host drivers for initial risk stratification, independent of subsequent interventions. The primary endpoint, PFS, was defined as the time from diagnosis to progression (RECIST 1.1), death, or last follow-up. A validated 24-month censoring interval was applied to account for irregular surveillance.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U and Chi-square tests. Optimal survival cut-offs for inflammatory indices were determined using MaxStat to maximize stratification, as standard ROC curve analysis lacked discriminative power (S5A and S5B Figs). For age, sensitivity analysis supported a clinical threshold of 55 years (S3 Fig) over the MaxStat-derived 69 years. Non-linear relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (S5C Fig). The “Genotype–Inflammation–Edema” association was investigated using causal mediation analysis. Model selection employed a hybrid framework: (1) LASSO Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation (S5D and S5E Figs); and (2) forced inclusion of genotype and visceral status. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (global test P = 0.164, with all individual covariates P > 0.05). Collinearity was assessed via VIF. The final model maintained an EPV of 11.2. Performance and incremental utility were evaluated using the C-index (bootstrapped 5,000 times), calibration plots, time-dependent ROC (S5F Fig), DCA, NRI, and IDI. Consistency was verified across subgroups (S6 Fig). To evaluate the potential influence of KS clinical subtype on the observed genotype–phenotype associations, a pre-specified sensitivity analysis was conducted in the Classic KS subgroup (n = 115 with available K15 genotyping data), examining inflammatory indices (Wilcoxon rank-sum test), lower limb edema (univariable and multivariable logistic regression), and PFS (multivariable Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analysis), with the same covariate adjustment structure applied to the primary analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.5.2), and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant.
