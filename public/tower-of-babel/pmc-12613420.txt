Digital nudges and gamification: how the China state promotes citizens to manage chronic diseases in neighborhoods
Authors: Ronghui Yang, Yanchao Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic diseases pose a growing threat to public health and have raised widespread social concern in China. In response, the Chinese government has rolled out chronic disease management campaigns at the community level to curb disease incidence. However, low public participation constrains their effectiveness. Although campaign-style mobilization has been widely studied, less is known about how digital nudges, particularly gamification, enhance citizen engagement in Chinese communities.MethodsTo address this gap, we conducted in-depth interviews with sixty Chinese stakeholders to examine how the Chinese state nudged individuals toward proactive chronic disease management using gamified mechanics.ResultsWe identify four core gamification elements, including dramatization, level-up progression, rewards, and socialization, that function as digital nudges. These gamified interventions can significantly reduce participants’ cognitive burden, amplify their perceived benefits of self-care, enhanced individual agency, and foster supportive social networks. However, the sustainability of these gamified interventions is undermined by misaligned reward structures, standardized level-up progression systems, and unresolved intra-community conflicts.ConclusionsTo overcome these obstacles, we propose implementing culturally attuned reward mechanisms, dynamically tailored challenge pathways, and robust online community governance to foster enduring public engagement. By revealing China’s neighborhood-level digital nudge approaches, this research not only enriches global understanding of gamified chronic disease management but also provides transferable design principles for public health interventions worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25381-6.

Introduction

According to the World Health Statistics 2021, chronic diseases caused approximately 41 million deaths globally, with nearly 10.64 million deaths occurring in China—accounting for 91.0% of national mortality [1]. In response to this public health burden, the Chinese central government unveiled the 14th Five-Year Plan for National Health in 2022, explicitly elevating chronic disease prevention and management to a top public health priority [2]. Local governments subsequently committed substantial financial investments, technological infrastructure, and policy support to neighborhood-level chronic disease management programs. However, low public engagement has impeded effective chronic disease management in communities [3]. This disengagement is evident in low participation for health screenings organized by local authorities, poor adherence to medical recommendations, limited uptake of preventive behaviors, and reluctance to share personal health data with community health managers [4]. Consequently, chronic disease management infrastructures remain underutilized, bureaucratic formalities proliferate, and social resources are squandered, undermining overall public health outcomes [5].

To address these challenges, China has traditionally employed campaign-style governance and top-down policy directives to spur public engagement in chronic disease management. In line with governmental priorities, scholars have explored diversified social mobilization strategies to promote public engagement. These include exerting political pressure on local cadres to secure compliance with government-led health initiatives [6], deploying media campaigns that foster individuals’ positive perceptions of chronic disease management [7], and invoking collective identity by emphasizing the role of healthy citizens in national prosperity and social cohesion [8]. However, in post-modern China, such campaign-style approaches often yield unintended consequences. Prolonged bureaucratic tweaking generates psychological reactance among both officials and the public, thereby eroding the overall effectiveness of mobilization‐based governance. Moreover, one‐size‐fits‐all tactics fail to reduce individuals’ cognitive burdens or enhance their ability to manage chronic diseases [9]. Yet many patients still perceive self-management as a burden, imposing stringent lifestyle constraints that require substantial time and effort for minimal benefit, and force them to overcome deep-seated behavioral inertia. By relying primarily on political mobilization to secure public compliance with governmental initiatives rather than emphasizing empowerment, tailored incentives, and a reduction in cognitive load, traditional campaign‐style governance has struggled to engender sustained, proactive participation in chronic disease self‐care. These shortcomings highlighted the need for more individualized, engaging, and supportive approaches.

In response, community health practitioners in China are increasingly adopting digital nudges instead of campaign approaches. These nudges include such as health behavior check-ins in virtual communities, point‐based incentive systems that converted individual self‐care activities into redeemable credits, smartphone notifications for behavior reminders and personalized recommendations, online health education, gamification, and social encouragement [10]. Among these, gamified interventions have garnered particular attention. By weaving game elements into individuals’ daily health routines, gamification enhances engagement, motivation, and adherence through playful challenges and reward structures [11]. Existing scholarship has examined a diverse array of nudge strategies for promoting health behaviors, such as defaults, framing, reminders, feedback loops, environmental cues, social norms, personalization, and monetary incentives, and has documented attendant risks, including behavioral relapse following reward withdrawal, alert fatigue, comparison anxiety, manipulation concerns, and over-reliance on environmental changes [12–17]. However, few studies have analyzed digital nudging specifically through the lens of gamified interventions. In practice, community health workers have implemented a range of online gamified programs, for example, Sugar Smash (糖果粉碎), interactive movement events such as Samba de Amigo (快乐桑巴), Online Garden Tour (线上游园), Baitang Dazhan (白糖大战), and Guardians of Cardiovascular Health (心血管护卫队), to increase public engagement in chronic disease prevention and self-management. Although gamification has been extensively investigated in Western settings, its implementation, contextual adaptations, and effectiveness within China remain underexplored [18].

Informed by these analyses above, this study investigates how neighborhood health managers nudge citizens to actively manage their chronic diseases through the lens of gamified intervention. Specifically, It addresses three questions: (1) how gamified chronic-disease management activities are organized within Chinese neighbourhood contexts, (2) how such interventions affect individuals’ proactive health behaviours and self-management, and (3) what strategies can optimize the implementation of gamified chronic-disease interventions. Theoretically, this study goes beyond prior work that treats gamification as a mere motivational tool. It develops an integrated framework linking gamification, nudge theory, and institutional governance to examine how gamified interventions shape individual health-behavior change and neighborhood health governance. Correspondingly, we conceptualize gamification as a specific, practice-oriented manifestation of nudges. It functions as an operational form of choice architecture through which neighborhood health managers design and deploy gamified interventions to align individual motivations with public health goals and to promote active, sustained chronic-disease self-management. Practically, this study seeks to offer a gamified chronic disease management pathway for both domestic and global public health initiatives.

Methods

Research design

In this study, we employed a qualitative approach to examine the gamified chronic disease management and its impact on individuals’ proactive health behaviors. Two authors, both experienced in semi-structured interviews and in chronic disease management research, conducted in-depth interviews with Chinese stakeholders. Data were gathered between September 2024 and January 2025 via face-to-face meetings, telephone conversations, and WeChat video calls. Prior to each interview, participants were briefed on the study’s objectives, methods, anticipated outcomes, and their rights and responsibilities. Oral informed consent was obtained, and strict measures were taken to protect participant anonymity.

Data collection

The data collection involved identifying, recruiting, and interviewing participants, followed by the construction of detailed transcripts. We identified potential participants based on two inclusion criteria: (1) first-hand experience with gamified chronic disease management (as participants or organizers), and (2) ability to describe activity organization, risk perceptions, or risk responses. Accordingly, the sample comprised three subgroups: residents who had participated in gamified chronic disease management activities; staff from community health service centers (a statutory grassroots unit directly subordinate to district/county health commissions, functioning as a government actor within China’s public health system and representing the frontline administrative implementer of gamified interventions) who organized such activities; and managers of online chronic disease self-management groups. Recruitment proceeded via snowball sampling, starting from known contacts and expanding through referrals to maximize diversity and reach thematic saturation [19]. Ultimately, 60 individuals from Beijing, Guangzhou, Shanghai, Wuhan, and Changsha were interviewed: 15 community health service center staff, 40 residents living with conditions such as lumbar disc herniation, hypertension, or diabetes, and 5 online chronic disease self-management group administrators (see Appendix 1). Interviews followed a structured outline examining organizational processes of gamified interventions and their implications for personal proactive health behaviors (see Appendix 2). At the participants’ request, interviews were no audio-recorded; interviewers reconstructed detailed transcripts immediately after each session and sent them to participants via WeChat for verification and correction. Only verified transcripts were used for analysis.

Data analysis

We conducted an inductive thematic analysis using NVivo 12. The first author (Coder 1) manually coded the full set of transcripts and developed an initial hierarchical codebook (Codebook_1). An external expert in qualitative methods and public health (Coder 2) independently coded 20% of transcript to assess reliability. Inter-coder agreement was calculated using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.88), exceeding the conventional threshold for good agreement [20]. Coders discussed discrepancies iteratively until consensus was reached; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by the senior investigator. The reconciled scheme comprised three thematic domains: (1) core gamification elements (dramatization, socialization, level-up progression, and rewards); (2) impacts on proactive health behavior (immersion, reduced cognitive burden, empowerment, and continuity of engagement); and (3) identified vulnerabilities (insufficiently personalized challenges, intra-community conflict, inequitable reward structures, and weak intrinsic motivation) (see Appendix 3).

We further examined interactions between codes and themes across three nested layers to clarify when and why these co-occurrences emerge. First, code-theme interactions captured context-dependent linkages. For example, the sub-code tangible rewards (Rewards) positively connects to affected motivation when redemption was readily accessible (e.g., seniors immediately exchanging points for daily necessities), but produced negative effects, such as disheartenment and attrition, when thresholds were unrealistic (e.g., requiring six months of perfect adherence for a low-value reward). Second, code-code interactions within gamified mechanics revealed synergistic complementarities: scenarization (immersive contextual cues) and role play (skill-based “boss” challenges) often co-occurred to reduce task tedium and supply behavioral purpose; each element was less effective if implemented in isolation. Third, theme-theme interactions illuminated higher-order dynamics that produced distinct adherence trajectories; level-up progression combined with positive motivational feedback generated a virtuous cycle in which small, incremental rewards sustained engagement and unlocked subsequent stages of progression. By contrast, poorly designed reward structures could produce a vicious cycle in which unattainable incentives reduced patients’ motivation and participation. These analytical layers clarify the situational “when” (contextual conditions) and “why” (mechanisms) that determine whether gamification elements support sustained self-management. In the Results section, we illustrate each theme and key interaction dynamics with representative interview excerpts to ground these insights in empirical data.

Results

This section describes the core elements of gamified chronic disease management and their impact on individual proactive health.

Gamified mechanics in chronic disease management

We identified four primary gamified mechanics: dramatization, level-up progression, rewards, and socialization.

Dramatization

Dramatization integrates narrative structures, role-play, and game-like missions into digital health interventions to reframe self-care tasks as an engaging, story-driven journey [20, 21]. Conceptually, dramatization functions as a framing nudge, casts users as protagonists within a compelling storyline, transforming otherwise mundane health activities into purposeful quests. Dramatization enhances patients’ intrinsic motivation, strengthens their adherence to self-management regimens, and facilitates the acquisition and consolidation of chronic disease management skills.

Scenarization denotes the deliberate construction of immersive, context-sensitive environments that heighten emotional engagement and sustain attentional focus during self-management tasks. By simulating meaningful scenes, scenarization transforms repetitive activities into temporally-bounded journeys that transiently remove participants from quotidian routines while keeping behavior directed toward self-care objectives. Practically, scenarization employs sensory and symbolic affordances that modulate affect and arousal. Relaxation-oriented designs (e.g., iconic visual backdrops, naturalistic soundscapes, harmonious color palettes) reduce stress and foster sustained engagement (ID 1–3). Tension-driven designs (e.g., constricted visual frames, ambiguous figures, salient auditory cues) induce mild urgency to sharpen focus and investment (ID 4–11). Together, these manipulations operate through affective priming and attentional gating. By increasing the perceived value and salience of self-care tasks, they raise the likelihood of repeated engagement.As one interviewee said,

Previously, managing my chronic condition meant endlessly repeating the same rehab exercises, which made me feel tedious and dull. Now I feel like I’m leveling up and battling monsters. It’s both fun and exhilarating (ID 4).

Role-play assigns users a persona within the narrative scaffold, enabling them to enact goal-directed behaviors as empowered agents (e.g., “Health Guardian” or “Wellness Explorer”) [22]. Conceptually, role-play leverages processes of identification and agency: by allowing users to adopt a health-oriented identity and to practice decision-making in low-stakes, simulated contexts, role-play strengthens self-efficacy, supports procedural learning, and increases intrinsic motivation. In gamified chronic-disease management, role-play supports three core mechanics (ID 5–9). First, interactions with non-player characters (NPCs) provide scripted dialogues that deliver tailored information and scaffold decision rules. Second, “boss battles” or complex scenario challenges require users to apply acquired self-management knowledge in problem-solving contexts, thereby promoting transfer and skill consolidation. Third, exploration–discovery mechanics invite users to navigate virtual spaces, collect informational artifacts, and uncover clues—practices that structure progressive learning and reward knowledge accumulation. Collectively, these mechanics convert passive repetition into active, learner-centred engagement that reinforces both competence and retention.

Level-up progression

Level-up progression denotes a structured framework in which users advance through successive stages or levels by completing predefined, health-related tasks [23]. In community settings, level-up systems act as a simplification nudge and a choice-architecture technique that breaks complex medical knowledge into digestible, ordered steps. By doing so, they enable patients to progressively build self-care competencies [24]. This approach is particularly relevant for older adults and other groups with constrained information-processing capacity. Level-up designs achieve this by decomposing complex concepts into sequential, graduated stages. Doing so lowers cognitive burden, improves comprehension, and matches task difficulty to users’ evolving abilities, which together foster mastery and sustained motivation in chronic disease self-management. Two core design strategies underpin level-up progression in digital chronic-disease systems: gradient difficulty scaling and task decomposition.

Gradient difficulty scaling structures multi-tiered challenges whose frequency and complexity increase in accordance with users’ demonstrated competencies. This design principle seeks to maintain participants within an “optimal challenge” zone by ensuring that each successive level aligns with their evolving abilities (ID 14–16). Initiating engagement with simpler tasks minimizes users’ frustration and cognitive overload, while the gradual introduction of more complex challenges sustains their motivation and mitigates boredom over time. Effective implementation of gradient difficulty scaling entails two core components. First, eligibility screening establishes clear participation criteria, such as contraindications or health status thresholds, to ensure safety engagement and protect vulnerable subgroups (ID 17–24). Second, hierarchical challenge design organizes tasks into progressive levels that build cumulatively upon prior achievements. For example, a clinician in the community health service center argued that,

In our gamified program for patients with lumbar disc herniation, we designed a three-level learning challenge. At the first level, patients start by learning the basics knowledge like daily care precautions. The second level lets them watch and practice rehabilitation exercises through short instructional videos, then upload their own practice clips for feedback. By the third level, they’re confident enough to create personalized care plans that fit their health conditions and daily routines. (ID 20)

Task decomposition conceptualizes chronic disease management as a structured learning process in which complex behavioral competencies are segmented into discrete, achievable subtasks. By minimizing cognitive burden and enhancing individuals’ sense of attainability and mastery, this approach fosters incremental self-efficacy and sustained behavioral engagement (ID 25–28). Implementing task decomposition requires translating overarching management goals into granular, contextually grounded actions that align with users’ everyday capacities and routines (ID 29–33). For example, in a core stability training program for individuals with lumbar disc herniation, a gamified chronic disease management system embodies task decomposition by structuring behavioral learning into three graded stages. The initial phase develops proprioceptive awareness and foundational muscle control; the intermediate phase reinforces neuromuscular activation through simplified standing exercises; and the advanced phase consolidates skill proficiency via plank-based training to enhance spinal stability. By converting a cognitively demanding rehabilitation routine into a series of achievable learning modules, task decomposition enhances patients’ self-efficacy, maintains their engagement, and promotes durable behavioral change in chronic disease management (ID 34–36).

Rewards

Rewards function as incentive mechanisms that reinforce participants’ engagement in chronic disease self-management. As an incentive-alignment nudge, they enhance individuals’ perceived benefits of self-care and sustain their motivation throughout the behavior change process [25]. By reinforcing target behaviors, rewards can improve patients’ medical adherence and promote long-term engagement in chronic disease management. Within intervention practices, three primary reward types are commonly identified: tangible, virtual, and emotional incentives.

Tangible rewards deliver immediate, real-world benefits, such as discounted medications, gift cards, or health-related equipment, that participants can directly redeem [26]. In community-based programs, these rewards are typically operationalized through a health-points system, where participants accumulate points by engaging in preventive or health-promoting activities such as registering for family doctor services, completing online health education modules, donating blood, or participating in health screenings. Accumulated points can then be exchanged for health-related products (e.g., nutritional supplements, wearable devices) or services (e.g., physical examinations, specialist consultations) (ID 41–44). By linking individual participation to tangible outcomes, such systems align personal motivation with collective public health goals, fostering a culture of sustained engagement and shared well-being (ID 38–40). As one staff of the community health service center explained,

The health points system has proven highly attractive to economically sensitive groups, especially older adults. By accumulating points over time and redeeming them for products or services, many old people remain actively engaged in managing their chronic diseases (ID 40).

The health points system has proven highly attractive to economically sensitive groups, especially older adults. By accumulating points over time and redeeming them for products or services, many old people remain actively engaged in managing their chronic diseases (ID 40).

Virtual rewards employ symbolic incentives, such as badges, titles, or levels, within digital health platforms to sustain user engagement in health-promoting behaviors. These rewards are typically auto-generated upon task completion and carry both hierarchical and symbolic significance. For instance, in a digital health management system, the Bronze badge “Beginner in Health Management” signifies initiation into basic self-care routines; the Silver badge “Self-Disciplined Health Manager” represents consistent engagement and growing competence; and the Gold badge “Health Commander” acknowledges exemplary leadership in personal and community health promotion. Beyond their motivational value, virtual rewards also enhance users’ social visibility and peer reinforcement. When users share their health achievements on social media, they often receive recognition and encouragement from peers, which amplifies feelings of accomplishment and strengthens social connectedness (ID 45–48). This mechanism transforms individual self-care practices into socially validated achievements, thereby deepening users’ identification with healthy lifestyles and reinforcing their commitment to long-term chronic disease management. As one participant reflected,

When I earned the Gold badge and shared it online, my friends congratulated me. It made me feel proud and motivated to keep going. I wanted to maintain that image of being someone who really manages their health well. (ID 48)

When I earned the Gold badge and shared it online, my friends congratulated me. It made me feel proud and motivated to keep going. I wanted to maintain that image of being someone who really manages their health well. (ID 48)

In digital gamified systems for chronic disease management, emotional rewards deploy visual, auditory, and narrative feedback to cultivate users’ intrinsic motivation and mitigate the affective fatigue associated with routine self-care. Celebratory animations (e.g., confetti, starbursts), victory sounds, and personalized congratulatory messages convert otherwise mundane actions, such as seven consecutive days of blood-pressure monitoring, into emotionally salient experiences (ID 50–52). For example, upon reaching a weekly target a user may encounter an animated banner, upbeat music, and a message reading, “You’ve hit your target for seven days in a row. Amazing work!” Such positive affective feedback strengthens the emotional bond to the target behavior, enhances self-esteem, and creates a reinforcing loop that supports habit formation and sustained adherence to long-term disease management (ID 53–55).

Socialization

Socialization converts individual chronic disease management practices into collective action by embedding social interaction, group participation, and interpersonal networks within intervention design [27]. Functioning as a normative nudge, socialization mitigates the isolation of self-management, builds collective capacity for disease control, reinforces proactive health behaviors, and sustains long-term medical adherence [28, 29]. In community practice, health workers operationalize socialization by empowering local leaders to convene peer-support groups, recruiting volunteers to deliver health education and encouragement, codifying community covenants that articulate shared expectations, and organizing regular group activities that strengthen mutual accountability. These strategies generate the social infrastructure that enables members to feel supported, observed, and responsible for one another’s health behaviors. In gamified chronic disease management programs, socialization leverages mechanisms of cooperation, conformity, and benign competition within online groups to amplify engagement, normalize self-care practices, and promote durable participation.

Cooperation within virtual health communities operates through three interrelated mechanisms: knowledge sharing, emotional support, and peer monitoring. First, members exchange experiential and practical knowledge of disease management, bridging gaps in formal medical guidance that is often overly technical or inaccessible. By integrating fragmented online information with lived experience, participants accelerate collective learning and enhance practical problem-solving. Second, empathic listening, encouragement, and companionship mitigate the isolation, frustration, and anxiety commonly associated with chronic disease flare-ups. Third, peer monitoring addresses behavioral inertia by providing reminders, positive reinforcement, guidance, gentle corrective feedback, and tailored recommendations (ID 56–59). This multilayered mutual support not only strengthens individual self-efficacy in disease management but also distributes accountability across the group, cultivating collective resilience and sustained engagement in health-promoting behaviors. As one resident described,

Although I knew chronic disease self-care was important, I could never stick to a daily routine, and always found reasons to skip today and start again tomorrow, but the cycle persisted. Since joining the community’s self-management group, I have formed meaningful friendships and eagerly participated in the daily online health check-ins. Whenever I missed a day, a member promptly reached out to ask if I’ m okay. That attentive support made me feel valued and cared for, and has significantly improved my consistency (ID 57).

Social conformity describes the processes by which individuals alter behaviors, attitudes, or beliefs to align with perceived majority norms under peer influence. Within online self-management communities, facilitators operationalize conformity through role modeling and behavioral visibility to guide hesitant or resistant members toward consistent self-care routines. Role modeling elevates exemplary self-managers by disseminating their success narratives, thereby providing salient behavioral exemplars for others to emulate. Behavioral visibility requires routine public check-ins (e.g., balanced-meal photos, exercise logs), transforming private practices into observable social signals that imply “everyone else is doing it,” and thus framing these routines as correct, desirable, and socially endorsed (ID 12, 25). Online community managers also institutionalize mild social accountability by publishing participation rosters that make compliance visible and noncompliance salient. This strategy leverages normative pressure, where failure to conform risks negative labeling (e.g., lazy, undisciplined) and potential social exclusion, to reinforce adherence (ID 57–59). Conceptually, these practices harness normative influence and social identity processes. By shifting salient reference groups and increasing the visibility of pro-health norms, interventions increase behavioral conformity, strengthen group identity around healthy practices, and thereby support sustained adherence. As one member recounted,

At first, I resisted the daily health check-ins [in the online group]. But when managers posted my WeChat alias for missing a check-in, I felt intense psychological pressure, afraid of being seen as lazy or undisciplined. I began checking in like everyone else, and soon it became a habit (ID 59).

Competition within online chronic disease self-management communities is cultivated through leaderboards and time-limited challenge events. Leaderboards convert accumulated health-points, numerical aggregations of target behaviors such as blood-pressure checks or exercise, into weekly rankings that community managers publish to publicly recognize top performers (ID 41–44). By making performance visible, leaderboards activate social comparison processes: upward comparisons and observable progress motivate individuals to increase effort and close performance gaps. As one resident remarked, “seeing my neighbor leap from 12th to 8th place ignited my competitive drive. I resolved to surpass him.” (ID 41). Challenge events are time-bounded contests in which individuals or teams compete on concrete health objectives (e.g., daily step counts, dietary logging, medication adherence). The combination of temporal urgency, public recognition, and shared team identity accelerates behavior change, generates enjoyment and camaraderie, and cultivates collective responsibility for self-management. Together, leaderboards and challenges instantiate a structured, friendly rivalry that leverages social comparison and group belonging to sustain engagement and mutual accountability in chronic disease management (ID 37, 49, 60).

Implications for individual proactive health: playfulness, empowerment and continuity

Gamified chronic disease management in communities has generated subtle yet profound impacts on individual proactive health. On the one hand, gamified mechanics, such as dramatization, level-up progression, rewards, and socialization, reduce individuals’ perceived burden of chronic disease self-management, enhance their motivation and capabilities, mitigate individual behavioral inertia, and foster sustained public engagement. On the other hand, simplistic or poorly calibrated progression systems, misaligned incentives, and unresolved intra-community conflict can produce disengagement, norm erosion, and opportunistic behaviours that undermine collective purpose. Constructing a resilient ecosystem for community-based chronic disease management thus requires three interlocking design responses. Algorithmically driven, personalized progression systems should dynamically adapt challenge to individual competence. Complementing this, reward architectures must be calibrated to participants’ effort and local preferences, and community cohesion should be reinforced through formalized governance and active norm stewardship.

Community health practitioners implement gamified interventions as structured nudges to promote residents’ proactive engagement in chronic disease management. These interventions operate through four interrelated mechanisms. First, dramatization, conceived as a framing nudge, leverages narrative scenarios and immersive design to reconfigure repetitive self-care tasks as meaningful trajectories.This narrative modulation reduces cognitive load, elevates perceived task value, and strengthens affective engagement and sustained motivation for self-management. Second, level-up systems function as a simplification nudge, operationalizing task decomposition and pedagogical scaffolding to reduce cognitive complexity and make clinical competencies more tractable for users. By segmenting complex skills into graduated learning modules, these systems support incremental mastery, reinforce perceived competence, and thereby bolster self-efficacy in chronic disease self-management. Third, reward systems function as both incentive-alignment nudges and social-signaling mechanisms. By integrating tangible benefits, symbolic badges, and affective feedback, they enhance the instrumental and social value of self-care, sustaining motivation through a combination of extrinsic rewards and internally valued reinforcers. Fourth, socialization, functioning as a normative nudge, mobilizes cooperation, competition, and normative influence to shift disease management from an individual burden to a collective enterprise. Through peer support, social accountability, and shared goals, interventions reshape reference groups and salient norms, reducing behavioral inertia and strengthening communal commitment to health (ID 4–9). As a resident reflected,

I failed to actively manage my hypertension when I was doing it alone. Now I manage it by watching videos, completing daily health check‑ins with my group, and chatting with others. It improved my health, gave me emotional support, and even let me earn material rewards. Why would I not participate?” (ID 5).

It improved my health, gave me emotional support, and even let me earn material rewards. Why would I not participate?” (ID 5).

Nevertheless, the absence of personalized challenges undermines long‑term engagement. When challenge content is homogeneous and the number of levels is limited, participants encounter neither sufficient difficulty nor novelty. Consequently, tasks are completed quickly and interest wanes. Without dynamic features—such as adaptive difficulty calibration, additional tasks, or narrative expansion—repeat participation becomes monotonous, eroding perceived progression and diminishing intrinsic motivation (ID 17–19).

Despite initial engagement, the absence of personalized challenges undermines long-term participation. Homogeneous content and a limited number of levels produce an insufficient difficulty–novelty gradient, creating a mismatch between task demands and users’ evolving competencies. This can attenuate users’ perceived competence and autonomy, accelerating task completion and reducing interest. Without dynamic mechanisms, such as adaptive difficulty calibration, incremental task additions, or narrative branching, perceived progression stalls, intrinsic motivation decays, and repeat participation becomes monotonous (ID 17–19). One participant reported,

After completing it twice, I realized it was always the same content, no real challenge and no fresh experience. I immediately lost interest (ID 19).

Inappropriate reward structures can critically undermine public motivation for chronic disease management. Incentives that are perceived as arbitrary (for example, distributed by random lottery rather than contingent on effort) erode distributive and procedural fairness, reducing perceived instrumentality and prompting disengagement. A skewed effort–reward ratio—where substantial participant effort yields minimal value—triggers expectancy and equity concerns that diminish motivation. Overreliance on tangible rewards also neglects basic psychological needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness (per self-determination theory), so intrinsic motivation collapses once material incentives cease. Finally, delayed or absent feedback disrupts reinforcement contingencies (operant conditioning), weakening the perceived link between behavior and outcome and eroding the sense of achievement (ID 29–33). As one resident complained,

Despite diligently accumulating health points in line with the community health center’s guidance, l failed to redeem even the lowest-value reward after two months. This experience disheartened me. They encouraged us to participate, yet never provide any tangible rewards. Health points are like a mirage—l could see them but never attain anything (ID 30).

Despite diligently accumulating health points in line with the community health center’s guidance, l failed to redeem even the lowest-value reward after two months. This experience disheartened me. They encouraged us to participate, yet never provide any tangible rewards. Health points are like a mirage—l could see them but never attain anything (ID 30).

Interpersonal conflict within online communities attenuates members’ sense of belonging and degrades the social capital that underpins effective chronic disease management. Unmoderated disputes, often arising from divergent health beliefs or contested care practices, produce affective dissonance and social exclusion, reducing group identification and undermining participants’ propensity to perform prosocial behaviors such as emotional support or experiential sharing. Declining identification weakens norm internalization and reciprocity, thereby eroding both instrumental and affective support capacities. The net effect is a diminished collective resilience and a fracture in the social accountability mechanisms that sustain long-term self-care adherence (ID 53–58). As one resident observed,

Our group, originally intended to foster cooperation and emotional connection, has become a public forum for petty disputes among neighbors. Without mediation, many have grown disillusioned, and some have even left the community (ID 57).

Addressing these challenges demands an integrated framework that simultaneously amplifies challenge adaptability, safeguards reward integrity, and institutionalizes robust online community governance. First, adaptive challenge systems should deploy algorithmic personalization and diversified level-up architectures, such as individualized item banks, difficulty calibration, and branching narratives, that align task demands with evolving user competence and preserve a salutary novelty–challenge gradient, thereby sustaining flow and progressive mastery. Second, reward architectures must foreground instrumentality and procedural fairness by tying incentives transparently to verifiable effort and outcomes, providing immediate reinforcement, and embedding intrinsic motivators (e.g., mastery badges, meaningful milestones, and competence-affirming feedback) to protect autonomy and internalized motivation when extrinsic rewards wane. Third, resilient governance structures, anchored by explicit community norms, moderator capacity-building, transparent participation rosters, and formalized conflict-resolution pathways, preserve social capital, prevent norm erosion, and maintain reciprocal support networks. Together, these interlocking design elements reconcile short-term engagement mechanics with long-term adherence goals by harmonizing adaptive pedagogy, equitable reinforcement contingencies, and social-institutional safeguards that sustain collective efficacy in chronic disease self-management.

Discussion

In this section, we present the main findings, outline the study’s theoretical contributions to gamified health interventions, and highlight practical implications for grassroots public-health practice.

Main findings

This study examines how community health practitioners deploy gamification strategies and digital tools to nudge residents toward active chronic-disease self-management. We identify four core gamification elements, dramatization, level-up progression, rewards, and socialization, and describe how each contributes to engagement and adherence. Dramatization embeds routine chronic disease self-care tasks within immersive narrative frameworks, transforming otherwise tedious activities into purpose-driven storylines that increase the meaning of tasks and participants’ motivation. Level-up mechanics decompose complex chronic disease management behaviours into scaffolded, tiered modules that guide participants’ incremental skill acquisition. Rewards raise participants’ perceived value of self-care by combining tangible incentives, symbolic badges, and emotional feedback. Socialization introduces cooperation, competition, and normative influence, shifting self-management from a solitary responsibility to a shared community endeavour. Despite these strengths, several barriers undermine sustained public participation in chronic disease management. Homogeneous challenge designs reduce the novelty in level-up systems and depress long-term engagement. Opaque or unfair reward architectures erode participants’ motivation. Unresolved conflicts within online communities weaken social support networks and, consequently, collective adherence. To promote durable public engagement, interventions should implement adaptive, personalized challenge pathways, transparent effort-based rewards, and robust community governance that strengthens participant cohesion and trust.

Theoretical contributions

To articulate this study’s theoretical contributions, we situate our findings within the existing literature and identify four core advances. First, we extend frameworks for gamification mechanics by integrating contextual and psychological dynamics. Second, we reconceptualize chronic-disease self-management by reframing the dominant “pain” narrative as a “play” narrative. Third, we synthesize nudge theory and gamification to specify micro-behavioral pathways. Fourth, we situate gamification within state-led public health governance to distinguish institutionalized interventions from non-state alternatives.

Global interest in chronic disease management has spurred extensive research into gamified interventions. These studies primarily examined the requisite infrastructure, core mechanics, and implementation risks. According to Sardi et al., effective gamified systems require both software components (e.g., active video games and web platforms), and hardware elements (e.g., game controllers, computers, robots, and virtual reality devices) [30]. Within these ecosystems, key gamification mechanics included reward structures, instant feedback, socialization, and level-up progression. Reward typically combined digital assets (e.g., badges, trophies, medals) with tangible incentives (e.g., vouchers, health services) to reinforce participants’ task completion or level advancement [31–33]. Chen et al. emphasized that instant feedback, delivered via visual pop-ups or auditory notifications, kept participants informed of their chronic disease self-management progress and alerted them to critical status changes [34]. Socialization mechanisms both facilitated participant collaboration and the sharing of public experience, and fostered healthy competition through leaderboards, motivating users to surpass their peers [35–37]. Level-up progression mechanics scaffolded challenges of gradually increasing complexity [38, 39]. Despite demonstrable gains in enjoyment, prosocial behaviors, and proactive health management, many game applications of chronic disease management faltered due to non-personalized challenges, overly complex feedback channels, and counterproductive social comparisons [40–42].

Building upon these findings, our study identifies core gamification elements, such as level-up progression, rewards, and socialization, that align with established drivers of user engagement and reaffirm their central role in supporting chronic disease self-management. We further advance existing frameworks in two theoretically grounded ways. First, contextual immersion and narrative dramatization reduce participants’ perceived burden and cognitive load by embedding gamified experiences in participants’ everyday life. Second, algorithmic recommendation systems can dynamically calibrate incentives and difficulty to user ability and effort, thereby mitigating incentive attenuation. Meanwhile, strengthening community management can sustain resilient social support structures for chronic disease self-care. Third, synthesizing nudge theory and gamification, clarifies how micro-behavioral pathways reconfigure choice architecture. Dramatization functions as a framing nudge, recasting self-care from a burdensome obligation into a voluntary, mission-driven activity and thereby increasing perceived autonomy and engagement; level-up progression acts as a simplification nudge by breaking complex, long-term goals into small, sequentially attainable steps, which lowers cognitive barriers to initiation and maintenance; rewards constitute an incentive-alignment nudge, increasing the perceived benefits of adherence and rebalancing the subjective cost–benefit calculus; socialization operates as a normative nudge: peer check-ins, leaderboards, and community challenges activate descriptive and injunctive norms, converting solitary self-management into socially reinforced practice. Taken together, these elements show that gamification elevates digital nudging beyond the discrete tools emphasized in prior literature (e.g., pop-up reminders, default settings) by reframing nudges as a contextualized, bundled, system-level intervention anchored in community-based chronic-disease self-management [43]. This coordinated chain of framing, simplification, incentive and normalization that both extends and deepens the theoretical and practical scope of digital nudging in public health contexts.

In community-based chronic disease care, gamification does more than increase superficial engagement. It reconceptualizes the conventional “pain narrative” of chronic-disease self-management as an engaging “playful narrative” enacted across three interconnected dimensions. Cognitively, gamification alleviates patients’ perceptions of coercion by reframing their identity and self-care task meaning. Patients are recast as proactive “health guardians”, and routine self-care tasks are embedded in immersive storylines that convert self-care requirements into purpose-driven missions. This framing reduces patients’ cognitive resistance to chronic disease self-management and promotes goal-directed behavior. Emotionally, gamified features mitigate patients negative sentiments toward chronic disease self-care, such as anxiety, shame, and isolation, by providing social support and frequent positive feedback (e.g., badges, milestones). These elements replace frustration with a sense of achievement and sustain motivation. Behaviorally, level-up progression breaks long-term objectives into small, attainable steps, reducing monotony and lowering the effort required to begin and continue self-care. Social affordances, for example, leaderboards and group missions, convert solitary activities into shared practices that reward participation. These mechanisms shift chronic disease self-management from a burdensome obligation to an engaging, socially supported practice.

Situated within a state-led public health governance framework, gamification can attenuate citizens’ psychological reactance to top-down campaigns by reframing participation as a voluntary, intrinsically meaningful choice. When institutional actors replace blunt mandates with culturally attuned, incentive-compatible game mechanics, they more effectively align macro-level policy objectives with micro-level behavioral change. This depoliticization pathway reduces public resistance to policy directives and thereby increases grassroots legitimacy and uptake of state-initiated chronic-disease interventions. State-led gamification is analytically distinct from non-state interventions. Commercial or NGO-driven designs typically emphasize individual motivation through generic mechanics and rely on short-term extrinsic rewards or consumer interest, often remaining detached from formal public-health infrastructures. By contrast, state-led gamification is explicitly dual-oriented. It is designed both to modify individual health behaviors and to advance population-level governance goals. Its deployment is integrated with existing public-health institutions and services and is adapted to local administrative contexts rather than following one-size-fits-all commercial templates. Collectively, these mechanisms explain how state-led gamification produces micro-level behaviour change while concurrently advancing macro-level public-health governance outcomes, offering a theoretically grounded template for aligning policy mandates with citizen engagement.

Practical implications

To optimize community-based gamified, chronic-disease management, this study yields four actionable recommendations. First, we recommend designing personalized progression pathways driven by adaptive algorithms. Level-up content and challenge sequences should be matched to individual characteristics. For example, older adults with limited digital literacy should receive simplified modules, whereas patients with well-controlled conditions could be offered more advanced self-management tasks. Tailoring pathways should reduce the homogeneity of current programs and preserve novelty over time, thereby supporting sustained engagement. Second, we recommend structuring reward systems according to transparent, effort-based principles. Incentives should be explicitly tied to observable engagement, for example by awarding higher health points for consistent daily check-ins. Redemption rules and thresholds must be communicated clearly and updated in real time to reduce participants’ perceptions of unfairness and mitigate their disillusionment. Third, we suggest strengthening community governance to enhance social cohesion. Community health staff should be trained in conflict resolution and group facilitation to improve their capacity to manage participant interactions in online chronic disease self-management groups. Formalizing peer-support roles, such as health mentors, will help onboard and retain new participants. In addition, integrating gamified socialization into routine public-health services. for example, linking group-challenge completion to free chronic-disease screenings, can institutionalize social support networks and promote long-term adherence. Fourth, we recommend that intervention narratives foreground playfulness rather than punitive or fear-based messaging. Program designers should use motivational, story-driven framing to set salient, positively valenced goals that resonate with participants. Such framing can reduce anxiety, foster positive affect, and bolster sustained engagement in chronic-disease self-care.

Limitations of the research

This qualitative study deepened our understanding of gamified chronic disease management in Chinese communities. Nonetheless, this research must be considered against the background of its limitations. First, participants were recruited through an informal snowball sampling approach rather than a systematic sampling method, primarily to accommodate China’s highly relationship-oriented social context. Although this approach risks potential sampling bias, the data collection process adhered to the principle of data saturation, ensuring that no new themes emerged in the later stages of interviews. Second, this study focused on the general landscape of gamified chronic disease management in China without stratifying participants by condition severity, disease type, or age. As Blok and Valley suggest, gamified interventions may vary significantly depending on the disease type, condition severity and patients’ ages [44, 45]. Future research should therefore explore tailored gamification strategies for subgroups defined by chronic condition severity (e.g., mild, moderate, or severe hypertension/diabetes), participants’ age (e.g., middle-aged vs. older adults), and disease type (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, or chronic respiratory diseases) to generate more targeted insights and effective intervention models for chronic disease management in China.

Conclusions

In the digital era, the Chinese government has increasingly deployed gamification mechanisms to nudge citizens to engage in chronic disease management programs. Gamified interventions that integrated dramatization, level-up progression, rewards, and socialization have successfully recast self-care from a monotonous obligation into a compelling, immersive experience. Although these strategies have demonstrably enhanced users’ motivation, competence, and sustained participation, their long-term impact has been undermined by homogeneous level-up designs, unfair reward systems, and unresolved intra-community conflicts. To address these challenges, future interventions should implement adaptive, user-specific challenge pathways, establish transparent and fair reward systems, and institute robust online community governance to bolster participants’ perceived fairness and strengthen social cohesion. These enhancements are critical for maximizing the durability and effectiveness of gamified chronic disease management initiatives.
