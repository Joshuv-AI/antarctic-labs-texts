Tooth-surface plaque microbiome and different levels of oral disease burden among dentate older adults living in long-term care
Authors: Lina Julkunen, Muhammed Manzoor, Kaija Hiltunen, Riitta KT Saarela, Kaisu Pitkälä, Pirkko J Pussinen, Päivi Mäntylä
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to explore the composition of the tooth-surface plaque (subgingival with marginal supragingival) microbiome in dentate older adults residing in long-term care (LTC) facilities, stratified by clinically assessed oral disease burden (ODB). A total of 196 LTC residents aged ≥62 years underwent oral examinations and microbial sampling from each dentate quadrant. Microbial profiling was performed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing.ResultsParticipants were more frequently categorized into Moderate (n = 95, 48%) than Low (n = 32, 16%) or High (n = 69, 35%) ODB groups. Those with High ODB were oldest and had lowest number of remaining teeth. Alpha diversity did not differ between the ODB groups, whereas beta diversity analysis revealed significant differences between groups (Bray-Curtis: P = 0.005; weighted Unifrac: P = 0.025). The Low and Moderate ODB groups were enriched with both commensals and disease-associated genera, such as Ottowia, Lactococcus, Pseudoramibacter, and Anaeroglobus. High ODB group exhibited an increased abundance of genera linked to both oral and systemic diseases, including Cardiobacterium, Leptotrichia, Stomatobaculum, and Pseudopropionibacterium. Among ODB groups, periodontitis was a stronger determinant of oral microbiome composition than caries, whereas caries had a stronger effect on bacterial diversity.ConclusionThese findings indicate a progressive shift toward a dysbiotic oral microbiome with increasing ODB.Keywords: Oral health, oral microbiome, 16S rRNA sequencing, dysbiosis, periodontitis, dental caries

Introduction

Older adults, particularly those residing in long-term care (LTC) facilities, often live with dementia, experience functional impairments, and rely on assistance with daily activities such as oral hygiene and eating. This dependence directly affects their oral health. The typical biofilm-mediated diseases, such as dental caries, gingivitis and periodontitis, result from microbial plaque build-up as a consequence of poor oral hygiene [1].

The oral microbiome is diverse, comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses, and differs depending on the location in the oral cavity [2,3], and can translocate to various sites in the human body [4]. The oral microbiome changes throughout life and is influenced by factors such as aging, systemic health and diseases, cognition, medication, and oral dryness [5–8]. Microbiota diversity can fluctuate and dominant species may shift in response to dietary changes, medication, genetic factors and lifestyle factors, potentially leading to dysbiosis [9]. Frail older adults have been found to have lower gut microbiota diversity [10], and frailty may also affect the composition and abundance of microbiota in saliva [11].

Oral infections, frailty, vulnerability, and immunocompromise are common among LTC residents, leading to several severe inflammatory conditions affecting tissues in other parts of body such as brain, gut, heart, and lungs [12,13]. Aspiration pneumonia is a typical cause of death for LTC residents, and poor oral health can increase the risk for it [14,15]. Additionally, oral microbes have been linked to heart diseases, like endocarditis and atherosclerosis, stroke, diabetes, and general infections (sepsis) [16–19]. Immunosenescence (aging-related deterioration of the immune system) and inflammaging (chronic low-grade inflammation associated with ageing) are significant phenomena among frail older adults [20–22]. Immune system's function is closely associated with morbidity and mortality in the older people [22]. Furthermore, miRNAs are linked to biological aging and age-related diseases, as well as periodontitis. miRNAs regulate gene expression and play a crucial role in the development of systemic diseases [23]. In periodontitis, miRNAs are involved in disease development and tissue destruction, and they regulate periodontal soft and hard tissue remodellation [24,25]. The link between periodontitis and Alzheimer's disease (AD) may be related to pathogens associated with periodontitis or neuroinflammation caused by proinflammatory mediators [26].

Very little is known about the oral microbiome composition of older adults living in LTC, and data on how oral health status associates oral microbial composition is lacking. This study aimed to determine the associations between common oral biofilm diseases, specified as different levels of oral disease burden (ODB), and the tooth-surface plaque microbiome (subgingival with marginal supragingival contributions) composition among older adults living in LTC.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The population of this cross-sectional study consisted of residents in LTC in Helsinki, Finland, who had previously participated in the nutritional study with clinical examinations (n = 550). The total number of participants in the oral examination was 393, all of whom were included in the FINORAL (Finnish oral health studies in older adults) study [27,28]. Figure S1 shows the exclusion criteria in detail. Excluded were individuals in need of prophylactic antibiotics before examination (n = 47), those having major deficiencies or who completely refused the clinical examination (n = 35), and individuals who were deceased between the end of the nutritional study and the beginning of FINORAL study (n = 75). For the current study, the edentulous individuals (n = 104) and the individuals who did not have enough information for asymptotic dental score (ADS) calculations (n = 80) were excluded. A dentate person was defined as having at least one tooth or root remnant in the oral cavity. In addition, participants without a microbiome sample (n = 12) or with insufficient microbiome reads (n = 1) were excluded, which resulted in the final sample of 196 participants.

The Ethics Committee of the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa approved the study protocols (Register number HUS/968/2017). The project adhered to the guidelines set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Belmont Report to ensure the safety of human research subjects. All participants or their proxies in case of moderate-to-severe dementia gave written consent. Participation was voluntary.

Data collection

The head nurse of the department filled in a questionnaire regarding study participants' demographic characteristics (age, sex, education, smoking). Personal medical data and antibiotic use during one year before examination were obtained from medical records. Two qualified dentists conducted the clinical oral examination. In the intraoral examination, the number of teeth and the open caries lesions (crown or root caries) were examined; the plaque index (PIE) (0−4, from 0 = no visible plaque to 4 = whole tooth covered with plaque) and the gingival index (GI 0−3, 0 = no inflammation to 3 = severe gingival inflammation) were registered as a highest score for each tooth and calculated as the mean value for the whole dentition according to the modified Silness & Loe index [29]; the periodontal pocket probing depth (PPD) measurements from 4 sites (mid- and distobuccal, mid- and mesiolingual) were registered as the deepest PPD for each tooth (<4 mm, 4−5 mm, ≥6 mm); and bleeding on probing (BOP) as yes/no for each tooth. Radiographic imaging was not possible during the examination.

After the intraoral examination, the microbial samples were collected from the deepest or the most representative periodontal pocket in each quadrant where teeth were present. The samples were taken with a sterile curette with a single pull from the base of the periodontal pocket to coronal direction. During the sample collection, the saliva ejector was not available. Instead, cotton rolls/pads were used, if possible, and only the most abundant supragingival plaque accumulation was removed. Thus, the samples comprised both subgingival and supragingival plaque. The samples were pooled in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific®) containing PCR grade water (Roche®) and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

The ODB was described by ADS [27,30,31], which was modified for the purpose of this study as earlier described by Julkunen et al. 2021 [27]. The variables included in ADS are presented in Table S1. For statistical analyses, all the participants were divided in three ODB groups according to the calculated score of the ADS [30]. The groups were ODB Low (ADS 0-2) n = 32, Moderate (ADS 3-4) n = 95, and High (ADS 5-9) n = 69.

Sample processing and microbiota profiling

Sample processing, DNA extraction, and 16S rRNA gene amplification methods are described in detail elsewhere [5,32]. Briefly, DNA was extracted from plaque samples using the FastDNATM Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals). PCR amplifications targeted the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene using previously reported primers [33] on an ARKTIK thermal cycler (Finnzymes/Thermo Scientific). Paired-end sequencing was performed using Illumina MiSeq (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at the DNA Sequencing and Genomics Laboratory, Institute of Biotechnology, University of Helsinki, Finland. Primer trimming and read merging were performed using Cutadapt (v. 2.8) [34], and mothur pipeline (v. 1.45.2) [35]. Chimeric reads were filtered using UCHIME. Sequences were aligned with the Silva reference database (v. 132) [36] and taxonomically assigned using the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD v. 15.1) [37]. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity using the average-neighbour algorithm in the mothur pipeline (v1.45.2). Singleton and rare OTUs (<10% of samples) were removed prior to downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

The categorical variables included in the ADS-calculation were reported as numbers and percentages (%), and the continuous variables as means and standard deviations (SDs). Significance of the trends between groups were tested using Linear-by-linear Association for categorical and Jonckheere-Terpstra test for continuous variables. The statistical analyses were performed in the R software version 4.2.2 and IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0.2.0. The level of statistical significance was set to P < 0.05.

For microbiome analysis, raw count data were filtered to remove samples with fewer than 1,000 reads, and the data were rarefied to the minimum library size. To identify the core microbiome, taxa were defined as ‘core’ if present in ≥90% of samples and with a relative abundance of ≥0.001%. Analyses of alpha and beta diversity were conducted on the fully filtered dataset.

Alpha diversity, including the Shannon and Inverse Simpson indices, was calculated using the phyloseq R package (v1.42.0) and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and weighted UniFrac distances calculated from relative abundance data, followed by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and PERMANOVA (adonis2) with 9999 permutations in the vegan package (v2.6.4).

Microbiome data were then transformed to relative abundances and subjected to a centred log-ratio transformation for downstream analyses. Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2) [38] was applied to detect differential taxa at the phylum, genus, and species levels between the ODB groups with false discovery rate (FDR) control, adjusting for age and sex. Additionally, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) [39] was used to identify differentially abundant taxa between the ODB groups using counts per million (CPM) normalisation. Taxa were filtered using Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests (P < 0.05), and the most discriminative taxa were identified using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) with a threshold ≥4. An UpSet plot was used to visualise the shared and unique core taxa among the different ODB groups.

To disentangle periodontal from caries-driven effects, we derived separate burden scores from the ADS components: a periodontal burden score (sum of gingivitis and deepened pockets; range, 0–4) and a caries burden score (sum of crown/root caries and root remnants; range, 0–5). Alpha and beta diversities were assessed across these groups.

In all groups, the majority were women. Education, smoking, partner status, type of residence, and health related variables considered were at similar levels across the groups.

Diversity analysis of tooth-surface plaque microbiome

Alpha diversities indices (Shannon and Inverse Simpson) did not differ significantly between the ODB groups (Figure 2a, b), whereas the microbial community composition (beta diversity) did (Bray-Curtis: R² = 0.018, F = 1.76, P = 0.005; Weighted Unifrac: R² = 0.017, F = 1.63, P = 0.025; Figure 2c, d). These differences were not driven by unequal multivariate dispersion (Table S3). The significance of the differences was not attenuated after adjusting for age and sex (Table S3).

Composition of the tooth-surface plaque microbiome

After filtering a total of 10 phyla, 87 genera and 372 OTUs were identified in the microbial samples. The circular plot (Figure 3) shows the distribution and interconnectedness of OTUs at the phylum level. The microbiota mainly consisted of five phyla: Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, Fusobacteriota, and Pseudomonadota, with minor proportions of Saccharibacteria (TM7), Spirochaetota, Synergistota, Absconditabacteria (SR1), and Chloroflexota. The most common bacterial genera in all groups were Veillonella, Prevotella, Streptococcus, Campylobacter, Capnocytophaga, Fusobacterium, Selenomonas and Actinomyces (Figure 4).

Circular plot showing the distribution of OTUs at the phylum level. Node sizes reflect relative abundance, and distinct colours indicate different ODB categories.

Stacked bar plots depicting the mean relative abundance of bacterial genera across ODB groups, highlighting distinctive community compositions.

In unadjusted analysis, the ODB Low presented a relatively higher abundance of the phylum Actinomycetota (P = 0.013), whereas the ODB High showed a relatively higher abundance of Chloroflexota (P = 0.035) than the other ODB groups (Table S4). The relative abundances of genera Actinomyces (P = 0.015), Fusobacterium (P = 0.009), Haemophilus (P = 0.002), Leptotrichia (P = 0.015) and Parvimonas (P = 0.011) differed significantly between ODB groups (Table S5).

Among the 372 identified OTUs, 272 were core species present in at least 90% of the samples. The UpSet plot (Figure 5) illustrates their distribution among the different ODB groups. From a total of 272 core species, 204 (75.0%) were common across all ODB categories. Thus, only 4 (1.5%) OTUs were unique to ODB Low, 24 (8.8%) to ODB Moderate, and 10 (3.7%) to ODB High (Table S6).

UpSet plot showing unique and shared core OTUs among ODB categories. The main bars represent the number of OTUs (species) in each intersection, while the side bars indicate the total number of core OTUs (species) within each group.

Comparison of microbial biomarkers

LEfSe analysis showed the enrichment of Actinomycetota (phylum), Actinomyces (genus), and Actinomyces_unclassified and Neisseria macacae (species) in ODB Low. Fusobacterium nucleatum subsp. vincentii, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Prevotella denticola, and Prevotella oris were enriched in ODB High (Figure S2 and Table S7).

However, when taking the confounders into account in ANCOM-BC2 analyses across ODB groups, different taxonomic profiles were revealed (Table S8). Compared with ODB High, the Low and Moderate ODB groups were enriched with both commensal and oral disease-linked genera Lactococcus, Pseudoramirabcter, Anaeroglobus, Lautropia, Lactobacillus, and Neisseria. Species observed in ODB Low and Moderate having the highest fold changes included Saccharibacteria [G-1] bacterium HMT 349, Leptotrichia sp. HMT 225, Treponema lecithinolyticum, and Anaeroglobus geminatus. In contrast, ODB High exhibited increased abundance of dysbiosis-associated and periodontitis-linked taxa, including genera Cardiobacterium, Stomatobaculum, and Pseudopropionibacterium. The species enriched in ODB High with largest fold changes included caries-associated Leptotrichia sp. HMT 498 and Lactobacillus casei, and health-associated Lactobacillus salivarius (Figure 6 and Table S8).

Sensitivity analyses separating periodontal and caries burden

To disentangle the contributions of periodontal disease and caries to the ODB and microbiome associations, we performed sensitivity analyses separately for periodontal components and caries burden. Alpha diversity did not differ significantly among the periodontal scoring. For increasing caries burden, Shannon and Inverse Simpson diversity decreased (P = 0.007; Figure S3). Beta diversity analysis revealed significant differences in microbial community composition between periodontal burden scores (Bray–Curtis: R² = 0.026, F = 1.69, P = 0.003; weighted UniFrac R² = 0.025, F = 1.65, P = 0.006; Figure S4). In contrast, no differences were found between the caries burden scores.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we investigated how the clinically assessed ODB is reflected in the tooth-surface plaque microbiome of 196 dentate LTC residents. The relative abundance of several periodontitis-associated taxa increased with higher ODB. While overall species richness (alpha diversity) did not differ significantly between categories, microbial community composition (beta diversity) differed, with significant differences in microbial taxon abundances across all taxonomic levels. Sensitivity analyses suggested that periodontitis is the stronger determinant of the oral microbiome composition, while caries only associated with diversity reduction. Additionally, there is a clear transition from a commensal-dominated microbiome in Low/Moderate ODB to a dysbiosis-enriched, pathogen-associated community in High ODB, highlighting potential microbial markers of ODB.

At the phylum and genus levels, the relative abundance of several taxa, including Actinomycetota, Actinomyces, Haemophilus, Leptotrichia, and Parvimonas, differed between ODB groups. Overall, bacterial communities showed high similarity regardless of ODB level, as shared species accounted for approximately three quarters of the total 372 species across the groups, with the largest set observed in ODB Moderate and the smallest in ODB Low.

We applied ANCOM-BC2 for differential abundance analysis among three ODB groups to address the taxon-specific bias inherent in pairwise comparisons [38]. In total, 24 taxa differed between ODB Low and Moderate, 11 taxa between ODB Low and High, and 34 taxa between ODB Moderate and High. In ODB Low and Moderate, both commensal and oral disease -associated genera, including Lactococcus (family Streptococcaceae), Pseudoramirabcter, Anaeroglobus, Lautropia, Lactobacillus, and Neisseria, were enriched, while the ODB High group showed increased abundance of dysbiosis-associated and periodontitis-linked genera, including Leptotrichia, Prevotella., Cardiobacterium, Saccharibacteria, and multiple species of Aggregatibacter, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella, suggesting a shift toward an inflammatory, disease-associated microbial profile enriched with opportunistic species [40].

All the aforementioned taxa associated with ODB High are anaerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria and, in addition to periodontal diseases, have been linked to various systemic health conditions. Stomatobaculum longum has been associated with lymph node metastasis in oral squamous cell carcinoma [41], while Cardiobacterium and Aggregatibacter can cause endocarditis [16]. Genus Leptotrichia, belonging to the family Lacnospiracheae, are Gram-negative facultative anaerobic or anaerobic bacteria that possess lipopolysaccharides and ferment carbohydrates, producing lactic acid involved in tooth decay [42]. Leptotrichia has previously been associated with gingivitis [43,44] but more recently Leptotrichia are considered opportunistic pathogens, implicated in a variety of human diseases, including appendicitis, pneumonia, mucositis, and sepsis [42]. Saccharibacteria (TM7) are ultrasmall and live on the surface of their host bacterium, potentially influencing oral microbial ecology by modulating oral microbiome structure and functionality through effects on the host bacterium's physiology [45].

Prevotella spp. were also associated with ODB High in the LEfSe analysis. In the oral cavity, Prevotella participates in the complex process of biofilm formation and has been linked to poor oral hygiene [46,47], gingivitis and periodontitis [48]. It plays a role in the early and middle stages of biofilm development, creating conditions that favour later colonisers [49,50]. Oral Prevotella has also been associated with systemic and organ-related infections like endocarditis [51].

Associations with tooth hard tissue status were less pronounced in the microbiome than those related to periodontal status, although the prevalence of caries increased linearly across the ODB groups from Low to High. The taxa Lactobacillus and Lactobacillus sp. were enriched in ODB High and ODB Moderate, together with the previously mentioned Leptotrichia, which may be involved in tooth decay [42]. Root caries is prevalent in older adults and often co-occurs with marginal periodontitis, especially when daily oral hygiene is compromised [52]. Lactobacilli can create an acidic environment sufficient for root surface caries development even in the absence of S. mutans [53] and have been identified as unique inhabitants of dentine in root caries lesions [54]. The polymicrobial nature of periodontitis and dental caries has been suggested to exert synergistic effects [55]. Despite their different etiologies, both periodontitis and caries are driven by feedforward loops between the microbiota and host factors (inflammation and dietary sugars, respectively) that favour the emergence and persistence of dysbiosis.

To our knowledge, no previous studies have compared oral microbiomes and the burden of oral clinical biofilm induced disease findings among dentate older adults residing in LTC. Our participants represented the most vulnerable segment of the population in terms of functional capacity, and all were on multiple medications. The limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design. In addition, detailed information on recent antibiotic use was lacking. Our analysis focused exclusively on bacteria, excluding other microbial communities. The immunologic status of participants was unknown, and their nutritional intake was not considered. Some participants may have been removable denture users. The number of participants with low ODB levels was relatively small, although this is consistent with existing evidence that oral health among LTC residents is often poor [1,56]. Microbial samples were collected from the deepest or most representative periodontal pocket in each quadrant where teeth were present, together with biofilm from the supragingival marginal area, providing broader insight into tooth surface microenvironments. Samples were collected from one to four teeth, depending on the number of dentate quadrants in each subject. This variation, along with differences in sampling depth determined by each subject's periodontal condition, may have introduced some bias into the pooled samples. Although our sensitivity analyses of alpha and beta diversity suggest that microbial community differences are primarily driven by periodontal burden, the sampling method and the composite nature of ODB indicate that some taxa could also be influenced by caries-related ecology.

Beyond local oral effects, it is important to recognise that bacterial translocation from diseased oral cavities into the bloodstream via inflamed tissues, ingestion into the gastrointestinal tract, or aspiration into the respiratory system may pose a significant health risk. Oxidative stress caused by chronic inflammatory diseases, such as periodontitis, can lead to neuroinflammation and cognitive decline [57]. In addition, several pathogens associated with periodontitis can induce endotoxemia, which stimulates oxidative stress and promotes excessive production of reactive oxygen species. This cascade results in systemic inflammation and inflammatory responses, potentially contributing to the development of systemic diseases and conditions [12,57]. These effects may substantially impair overall health, particularly in frail and vulnerable older adults with compromised immunity. Poor oral health is also a well-established risk factor for aspiration pneumonia in older populations, especially when combined with dysphagia, diminished cough reflex, and cognitive decline [14,15].

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that as oral health deteriorates, the oral microbiome progressively shifts toward a dysbiotic composition, with clear enrichment of periodontal and acidogenic pathogens. These changes may contribute to the progression of oral diseases such as periodontitis and root surface caries, and consequently, exert detrimental effects on overall health. This study provides valuable insights into the oral health of older adults in LTC and reinforces the importance of maintaining oral health throughout life.
