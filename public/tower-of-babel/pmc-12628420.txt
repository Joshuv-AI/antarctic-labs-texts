A long non-coding RNA SCAMP1 induces pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma progression through miR-106a-5p/AGK signaling
Authors: Xiyao Du, Yunlong Cai, Peng Kuang, Mo Zeng, Yingli Huang, Shanshan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA) participate in regulation of gene expression and biology manipulation and altered lncRNA expression associated with cancer development and progression. The lncRNA SCAMP1 expression was aberrant and changed various cancer malignant behaviors. This study assessed SCAMP1 expression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) for association with clinicopathological parameters and survival of patients and then explored the underlying molecular events. The data revealed that SCAMP1 expression was significantly upregulated in PDAC tissues, which was associated with a larger tumor size and tumor de-differentiation as well as poor survival of patients. Knockdown of SCAMP1 expression reduced tumor cell growth, invasion, epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT), and improved sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in vitro and inhibited tumor cell xenograft growth in nude mice. At gene levels, SCAMP1 was able to target miR-106a-5p to in turn upregulate acylglycerol kinase (AGK) expression and promote PDAC malignant behaviors in vitro. The data from the current study demonstrated an oncogenic SCAMP1 activity in PDAC. Further study will investigate SCAMP1 as a tumor biomarker and novel target in control of PDAC clinically.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01943-1.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, a significant healthy burden, is the seventh most common cause of cancer-related deaths in the world and an overall 5-year survival rate is about 10% [1, 2]. Histologically, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for more than 90% all pancreatic cancer cases [1, 2]. The incidence and mortality rates of PDAC have been rising annually, with a worldwide incidence rate of about 4.2 per 100,000 and are projected to become the second leading cause of cancer deaths by 2030 [3, 4]. Classical treatments such as surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy (including gemcitabine and capecitabine, or the pyrimidine analog 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)) have been widely used and are responsible for only a modest improvement in survival [5]. The overall survival for metastatic pancreatic cancer is still less than 12 months [6]. Various genetic and clinical factors contribute to the dismal prognosis of PDAC, most of which are diagnosed at the very late stages of disease, rapid progression of disease with atypical symptoms, and insensitive to conventional therapy [1, 2]. Various mechanisms, such as gene mutations involved in critical metabolic pathways and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) that modulate the expression of genes implied in cell behavior, play vital roles in PDAC therapeutic resistance [7]. Thus, further study of pancreatic cancer biology, pathogenesis, and molecular mechanism could lead to identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for early detection and clinical control of this now deadly disease.

To date, a great number of studies have conducted to investigate aberrant expression and functions of the protein-coding genes in pancreatic cancer and significantly improved the understanding of pancreatic cancer for early diagnosis and effective control [8, 9]. However, protein-coding genes only account for 1.5% of human genome sequences, and these genes are far from fully explanation of human carcinogenesis and tumor progression [10]. To further understand human diseases, different studies of a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) have significantly attracted our attention and research interest and lncRNAs are an important novel class of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) with ≥ 200 nucleotides in length [11]. Total number of predicted lncRNA genes in humans has been already exceeded 10,000 in 2017 studies [12]. LncRNAs play critical roles in regulating gene expression at multiple levels through various mechanisms, including acting as molecular scaffolds to provide a platform for facilitating interactions among various molecules, serving as decoys by binding to proteins or other RNAs to prevent them from interacting with their target sites, and participating in chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional processing of other RNAs [13]. Continuous advancement of research has shown that lncRNAs can regulate diverse biological processes and are implicated in various diseases, particularly cancer. Alterations in lncRNA expression and their mutations could contribute to cancer development and progression [14, 15]. A growing number of lncRNAs have been linked to the deadly pancreatic cancer. For example, expression of an exosomal long non-coding RNA LINC01133 was increased in PDAC tissue specimens and significantly associated with later PDAC stages and poor prognosis [16]. LncRNA PVT1 induced gemcitabine resistance of pancreatic cancer [17], while hypoxia-induced LncRNA-BX111 also enhanced PDAC metastasis and progression in vitro [18]. LncRNA SCAMP1 is located at chromosome 5 in humans, and recent studies have indicated that altered SCAMP1 expression enhanced malignant behaviors of breast cancer [19], ovarian cancer [20], pediatric renal cell carcinoma [21], and glioma [22]. Moreover, SCAMP1 was verified to be a key lncRNA and associated with prognosis in pancreatic cancer [23]. However, to date, the true functions and the molecular mechanisms of SCAMP1 in PDAC are still not well documented.

This study further assessed SCAMP1 expression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) for association with survival of patients and then explored the underlying molecular alterations. Upon completion of this study, it is expected to provide novel information regarding SCAMP1 as a novel tumor biomarker and molecular target for control of PDAC clinically.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

This study contained 63 cases of paired of primary PDAC and their matched adjacent benign pancreatic tissues from surgical tumor resection patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Nanchang, Jiangxi, China) between December 1, 2017, and July 1, 2019. These patients had not received any chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery. Fresh tumor and normal tissues were collected from these patients in the surgery room and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in − 80 °C. Clinicopathological characteristics of patients were collected from patients’ medical records (Table 1). This study was authorized by the Ethics Committee of the First affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (The ethics permission number of #08-076), and all patients provided a consent form before enrolled into this study. Cell lines and culture

Human PDAC Capan-1, AsPC-1, SW1990, BxPC-3, and Panc-1 cell lines and normal HPDE6-C7 human pancreatic duct epithelial cells were originally obtained from The Cell Bank of the Chinese Science Academy (Shanghai, China). The Capan-1, AsPC-1, SW1990, and Panc-1 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, while HPDE6-C7 cell line was maintained in keratinocyte serum-free medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and BxPC-3 cells were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium-1640 (RPMI-1640) (Hyclone) with 10% FBS.

Cell transfection

SCAMP1 short‐hairpin RNA (shRNA) and the non-targeting shRNA, having been cloned into pGPU6/GFP/Neo vector, and the full‐length SCAMP1 cDNA, having been cloned into pIRES2‐EGFP were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The miR-106a-5p mimics and inhibitor, their respective non‐targeting sequences were also obtained from GenePharma. Scramble siRNA, siRNAs for AGK, and expression vectors for AGK were synthesized and obtained from GenePharma as well. To manipulate their expression in PDAC Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells, cells were grown and transfected with these constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 48 h following the manufacturer’s instructions. After that, cells were harvested for the following experiments.

Tumor cell viability and transwell invasion assays

The above gene-manipulated cells were then subjected to tumor cell viability CCK-8 and Transwell invasion assays according to our previous study [24].

Colony formation assay

The above gene-manipulated cells were seeded into 6-well cell culture plates with a density of 1000 cells/well. Cells were treated with 5-FU with indicated concentrations and then grown at 37 °C for 14 days with medium refreshment for every 3–4 days. After that, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) thrice and then fixed in ice-cold methanol for 10 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 10 min at the room temperature. After washed with tap water, the number of colonies with 50 cells or more was counted under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The experiment was in triplicate and reproduced at least for three times.

Nude mouse tumor cell xenograft assay

Our animal protocol was authorized by our Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the First affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (approval #12-83) and followed the Animal Laboratory Care and Use Guidelines [25]. The reporting of this study conforms to ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [26]. Twenty NOD/SCID mice were purchased from Center of Experimental Animal of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Guangzhou, China) and cared in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) “barrier” facility with controlled temperature and humidity by alternating 12-h light and dark cycles. The mice received SPF mouse chow and were allowed to drink sterile water ad libitum. Stably sh-Scramble or sh-SCAMP1-transfected Panc-1 cell sublines were cultured and then subcutaneously injected into mouse right flanks for 1 × 107 cells per injection. The size of the tumor cell xenografts was monitored every 2 days for 4 weeks. At the end of experiment, the mice were sacrificed, and the xenografts were resected and weighted.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular RNA was isolated from tissues or cell lines with the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and then reversely transcribed into cDNA with a TransScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturers’ protocols. After that, qPCR was amplified with the resulted cDNA samples to assess level of SCAMP1 and AGK mRNAs using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster city, CA, USA) on the ABI7500 system (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences of SCAMP1 were 5′-TTCGACAGTAACCCGTTTGC-3′ and 5′-ATTAGGCATCTTCACACCGC-3′; U6 primers were 5′-GCTTCGGCAGCAGCACATATACTAAAAT-3′ and 5′-CGCTTCACGAATTTGCGTGTCAT-3ʹ; AGK primers were 5′-CCTGACACCATCAGCAAAGG-3′ and 5′-CTCCGGGATAAGCAAAGTGC-3′; and GAPDH primers were 5′-CAGGGCTGCTTTTAACTCTGGT-3′ and 5′-GATTTTGGAGGGATCTCGCT-3′. The relative SCAMP1 or AGK level was normalized to the control U6 and GAPDH, respectively (n = 3), using the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted with the radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer) and quantified according to our previous study [21]. After that, protein samples were separated in sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels and transferred onto the PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, USA). After that, the membranes were incubated in 5% skimmed milk powder-TBS solution at the room temperature for 1 h and then with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Next, the membranes were washed in Tris-based saline-Tween 20 (TBS-T) solution thrice and incubated in secondary antibody solutions at the room temperature for 1 h and then washed with TBST for three times and visualized using the ECL Plus Detection Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The first antibodies were an anti-E-cadherin (Cat. #ab1416; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, at a dilution of 1:1000), anti-N-cadherin (#ab18203; Abcam, 1:1000), anti-vimentin (Clone D21H3; Cat. #5741, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 1:1000), anti-AGK (Cat. #GTX107413; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA, 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (Cat. #GTX627408; GeneTex, 1:1000).

Subcellular fractionation assay

Subcellular isolation of RNAs in Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cell lines was assessed with the Cytoplasmic and Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen, Thorold, ON, Canada) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, PDAC cell lines were cultured in 6-well dishes and subjected to isolation of subcellular fractions and RNA isolation using the kit. After that, RNA samples from both nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were used for qRT-PCR analysis. The experiments were set to be duplicate and reproduced for at least three times.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

We performed the RIP assay using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit from Millipore (Burlington, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s suggestions. The immunoprecipitation reaction was conducted using the magnetic beads conjugated with an anti-Argonaute 2 (Cat. #ab32381; Abcam) at a dilution of 1:50 or mouse IgG (Cat. #ab150077; Abcam; 1:100) as a control from PDAC cells. After that, expression of SCAMP1 mRNA and miR-106a-5p in the immunoprecipitation samples was assayed using qRT-PCR.

Luciferase gene reporter assay

Wild‐type or mutated SCAMP1 or AGK sequences with predicted binding sequences of miR-106a-5p were subcloned into the pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter gene expression plasmid (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) as SCAMP1 wild‐type (SCAMP1-WT), AGK wild-type (AGK-WT), SCAMP1 mutant (SCAMP1‐MUT), or AGK mutant (AGK-MUT). The mutated cDNA sequences were constructed using the wide-type sequences with a site-direct mutagenesis kit (Takara, Dalian, China). After DNA sequencing confirmation, these vectors were co-transfected into PDAC cells for 48 h using miR-106a-5p mimics or control mimics with help of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). After that, total cellular protein was extracted and then subjected to detection of changed luciferase activity with the dual‐luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega) following manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was assayed at least three times, and the data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD; n = 3) and statistically analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The correlation of SCAMP1 expression with clinicopathological parameters was analyzed with the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, while comparison of the averaged two or more groups of data was evaluated with the Student's t-test and the one-way analysis of variance, respectively. A P value equal to or less than 0.05 was indicated as statistical significance.

Results

SCAMP1 is upregulated in PDAC and associated with poor prognosis in PDAC patients

LncRNA SCAMP1 is found to be dysregulated in multiple types of cancer, including pancreatic cancer [19–23]. However, the expression pattern and its impact on the malignant behavior of PDAC remain largely unknown. Therefore, SCAMP1 caught our attention, and we firstly assessed the level of SCAMP1 expression in 63 paired PDAC and normal tissue specimens using qRT-PCR. The data showed that SCAMP1 level was significantly upregulated in PDAC tissues vs. that of the normal tissues (Fig. 1A). To associate SCAMP1 expression with clinicopathological features and survival of PDAC patients, we divided these 63 cases into high vs. low SCAMP1 level using the median SCAMP1 level. We revealed that SCAMP1 level was associated with larger tumor size and tumor de-differentiation (Table 1) and poorer overall survival than patients with low SCAMP1 expressed PDAC (5.184 ± 0.53 months versus 7.123 ± 0.46 months; P = 0.04; Fig. 1B). Furthermore, our KM-Plotter database data (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) also confirmed the prognostic role of SCAMP1 in PDAC (Fig. 1C). In addition, SCAMP1 level was also high in PDAC cell lines (Fig. 1D). Inhibition of PDAC malignant phenotypes after SCAMP1 expression knockdown in vitro and in vivo

We next investigated the oncogenic activity of SCAMP1 in PDAC by performed a series of functional assays. According to the endogenous SCAMP1 levels in these five PDAC cell lines, Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells were chosen for overexpression and knockdown assays. As shown in Fig. 2A, SCAMP1 expression was significantly knocked down or overexpressed in these cell lines, respectively. We then performed cell viability CCK-8 (Fig. 2B) and colony formation assays (Fig. 2C) and found that the proliferation rates of Panc-1 cells and BxPC-3 cells were significantly higher in SCAMP1-overexpressing tumor cells than in the control, whereas the proliferation rates were reduced after knockdown of SCAMP1 expression (Fig. 2B, C). Furthermore, our Transwell tumor cell invasion assay revealed that downregulation of SCAMP1 expression inhibited cell invasion capacity, whereas SCAMP1 overexpression enhanced cell invasion capacity vs. that of control cells (Fig. 2D). At the gene level, level of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins was also altered; i.e., SCAMP1 overexpression cells showed a higher expression level of N-cadherin and vimentin than control cells, but a lower expression level of E-cadherin than control cells (Fig. 2E). ﻿In contrast, knockdown of SCAMP1 expression had the opposite results in Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 2E).Fig. 2Inhibition of PDAC cell malignant phenotypes upon knockdown of SCAMP1 expression. Besides, we conducted the nude mouse PDAC cell xenograft assay, and our data showed that knockdown of SCAMP1 expression led to a slower growth rate and smaller average volume of Panc-1 cells xenograft tumors than in scramble shRNA-manipulated cells xenografts (Fig. 2F–H). Our current findings imply that knockdown of SCAMP1 expression suppressed PDAC cell malignant phenotypes in vitro and in vivo, whereas SCAMP1 overexpression had an opposite effect on PDAC cells. Furthermore, it was unclear whether SCAMP1 has any functional role in conferring resistance to 5-FU. Hence, cell viability CCK-8 (Fig. 2I) and colony formation assays (Fig. 2J) in 5-FU (1ug/mL) treated Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells were performed. Interestingly, the results revealed that the increased expression levels of SCAMP1 were correlated with less sensitive to 5-FU treatment and vice versa, suggesting that SCAMP1 plays a critical role in the chemoresistance of PDAC cells.

SCAMP1 as a miR-106a-5p sponge in PDAC cells

We next determined the distribution of SCAMP1 in the PDAC cells, and our data showed that SCAMP1 was mainly expressed in PDAC cell cytoplasm (Fig. 3A). Since lncRNA usually acts as a sponge of miRNA to regulate gene expression [22], we searched and analyzed the online database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) and revealed that miR-106a-5p was predicted to be a potential target of SCAMP1 (Fig. 3B). To confirm the binding between SCAMP1 and miR-106a-5p, we performed the luciferase gene reporter assay. As shown in Fig. 3C, transfection with miR-106a-5p mimics into Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells dramatically decreased the luciferase activity of SCAMP1-WT plasmid, whereas the luciferase activity of SCAMP1-MUT plasmid was not affected. Knockdown or upregulation of miR-106a-5p expression was able to significantly affect level of SCAMP1 expression (Fig. 3D). We utilized human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK-293 T) cells to perform the RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay. Our data showed that both endogenous SCAMP1 and miR-106a-5p were preferentially enriched in Ago2‑containing miRNAs than in control IgG immunoprecipitates (Fig. 3E). Acylglycerol kinase (AGK), a target of miR-106a-5p

We further determined the protein target of the SCAMP1/miR-106a-5p axis in PDAC cells by searched the online database of TargetScan and found that AGK is one of the best potential targets of miR-106a-5p (Fig. 4A). We performed the luciferase gene reporter assay to validate this prediction and found that indeed, miR-106a-5p mimics was able to reduce the luciferase activity of AGK-WT, whereas AGK-MUT showed no notable changes in luciferase activity in Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 4B). Besides, miR-106a-5p overexpression resulted in a significant decrease of AGK mRNA and protein levels in Panc-1 and BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 4C, D). miR-106a-5p antagonization of SCAMP1 tumor-promoting activity in PDAC cells by regulation of AGK expression

We further investigated the functions of AGK in PDAC cells and found that AGK overexpression promoted BxPC-3 cell invasion, whereas downregulation of AGK expression inhibited Panc-1 cell invasion in vitro (Fig. 5A). Besides, the regulation of AGK expression in PDAC cells can affect the fluctuation of EMT-related proteins, which indicated that AGK promoted the EMT process of PDAC cells (Fig. 5B). Then, we further detect whether miR-106a-5p/AGK axis was involved in SCAMP1-mediated PDAC progression. Thus, a series of rescue experiments were performed, i.e., our Western blot analysis of tumor cell xenografts showed that level of AGK protein was downregulated in shSCAMP1-infected tumor cell xenografts (Fig. 5C). AGK expression was also significantly downregulated in SCAMP1-knocked down Panc-1 cells; however, this reduction was reversed after co-transfection with miR-106a-5p inhibitors (Fig. 5D). SCAMP1 overexpression resulted in increase in AGK expression in BxPC-3 cells; however, such a SCAMP1-mediated enhanced AGK expression was partly inhibited in SCAMP1 overexpression plus miR-106a-5p mimics co-transfected BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 5D). In addition, the oncogenic function of SCAMP1 overexpression in enhancing tumor cell viability and invasion was attenuated after miR-106a-5p overexpression (Fig. 5E–G). Discussion

LncRNA, a class of non-protein-coding transcripts, plays a key role in regulation of various biological functions in human body and altered lncRNA expression or functions associate with the development of human diseases, including cancers [27]. To date, there are only a few lncRNAs that have been functionally and mechanically annotated in human diseases. In the current study, we focused on interpreting the function and its underlying mechanism of lncRNA SCAMP1 in PDAC progression. We found that SCAMP1 expression was upregulated in PDAC tissue specimens and cell lines, suggesting that lncRNA SCAMP1 may have cancer‐promoting effects in PDAC. Moreover, high SCAMP1 expression was associated with a larger tumor size, poorer tumor differentiation, and shorter overall survival of PDAC patients. In addition, knockdown of SCAMP1 expression inhibited PDAC cell proliferation, invasion and EMT phenotype in vitro, and PDAC cell xenograft formation and growth in nude mice. In conclusion, the data from our current study indicate that SCAMP1 is an oncogene or at least possesses oncogenic activity in PDAC. Thus, further study will evaluate SCAMP1 as a biomarker for PDAC diagnosis and prediction of prognosis and/or as a novel target for developing anti-PDAC therapy.

Functionally, lncRNA is able to serve as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to regulate miRNA expression and impose further post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression in cells [28]. Indeed, miRNA is another type of non-coding RNAs with 18–22 nucleotides in length [29]. Similar to lncRNA, miRNA molecule does not directly code a protein, but it can post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression in cells by directly binding to the 3'-untranslated region (UTR) of targeting mRNAs [30]. For example, alteration of miR-106a-5p expression was associated with cancer development and progression [31–33], although the downstream target genes of miR-106a-5p and its biological functions in PDAC have not yet been fully elucidated. LncRNAs can directly bind to miRNA molecules for subsequently competitive sequence‐specific interactions with mRNA targets [34, 35]. For example, lncRNA RHPN1-AS1 was able to competitively bind to the miR-345-5p 3'-UTR to downregulate miR-345-5p expression and then upregulate YAP1 expression in cholangiocarcinoma progression [36]. LncRNA TRPM2-AS induced retinoblastoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion capacity through the ceRNA function in regulation of miR-497 expression and, therefore, targeting WEE1 expression [37]. Moreover, lncRNA BLACAT2 was able to promote gastric cancer progression by targeting the miR-139b-5p/METTL3 signaling [38]. LncRNA SCAMP1 could act as the similar way in regulation of gene expression. For instance, SCAMP1 was reported to enhance ovarian cancer cell invasion capacity and angiogenesis after targeting of the miR-137/CXCL12 axis [20]. SCAMP1 could also associate with renal cell carcinoma tumorigenesis by controlling miR-429 expression and miR-429 downstream genes [21] or promoted glioma cell malignant behaviors by acting as a molecular sponge of miR-499a-5p to further regulate NLRC5 expression [22]. Expression and regulation mechanism of SCAMP1 in PDAC have not been reported before. In the current study, we revealed upregulated SCAMP1 expression and association with advancement of PDAC and poor survival of patients. Functionally, we revealed that SCAMP1 was able to target miR-106a-5p by binding to the miR-106a-5p 3'-UTR site, and miR-106a-5p was reported to be an oncogene in some human cancers but a tumor suppressor in other cancers [39]. A previous study showed that miR-106a-5p expression was high in 5-FU-resistant colorectal cancer tissues and cell lines, and miR-106a-5p expression associated with colorectal cancer progression by enhancement of tumor cell 5-FU resistance and metastasis [40]. However, miR-106a-5p expression inhibited renal cell carcinoma cell migration and invasion capacity by targeting PAK5 expression [32]. The contradictory function of miR-106a-5p in different human cancers could indicate that the downstream target of miR-106a-5p is important to regulate the functions of miR-106a-5p in different human cancers. To date, there is no report of miR-106a-5p in pancreatic cancer. Our current study is the first to show SCAMP1 as a ceRNA to sponge miR-106a-5p in order to promote pancreatic cancer cell malignant phenotypes.

AGK, initially identified in Sengers syndrome due to AGK gene mutation [41], functions to phosphorylate monoacylglycerol and diacylglycerol for lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) production [42]. Additional studies showed AGK overexpression as a key oncogene in various human cancers [43, 44]. For example, AGK was overexpressed in early-staged cervical squamous cell cancer and associated with poor survival of patients [45] and promoted breast cancer cell proliferation and tumorigenicity by suppression of the FOXO1 transcription factor [46], although AGK might help to maintain the metabolic state and immune responses of CD8 + T lymphocytes [47]. Another previous study indicated that AGK could be a novel target in cancer therapy [48]. In our previous study, we revealed that AGK expression was high in gastric cancer, and the YAP1-AGK-positive feedback loop could facilitate gastric cancer development [49]. In the current study, our bioinformatics analysis and luciferase reporter assay results both demonstrated that AGK could be as a direct target of miR‑106a‑5p, and the later was targeted by SCAMP1, i.e., AGK expression was significantly downregulated in SCAMP1-knocked down cells, and this reduction was partially rescued by transfection with miR-106a-5p inhibitors in PDAC cells. However, the oncogenic function of SCAMP1 was attenuated by co-transfection with miR-106a-5p mimics in PDAC cells. Thus, we speculated that SCAMP1-induced PDAC progression could be through directly targeting of the miR-106a-5p/AGK signaling pathway.

However, our current study is just proof of principle and lots of more need to be clarified before clinical application of SCAMP1, miR-106a-5p, and AGK as biomarkers, novel targets, and key genes in PDAC. Our current study also has had some limitations; for example, we detected SCAMP1 expression for association with survival of PDAC patients, but we did not associate patients with treatment types and duration as stratifying factors for SCAMP1 expression and prognosis. We did not investigate whether the SCAMP1/miR-106a-5p/AGK axis regulates PDAC progression via some certain signaling pathways. We also did not assess expression of three genes in PDAC tissues for better understanding of their expression and regulations in PDAC.

In summary, the results of the current study provided a novel insight into function of the lncRNA SCAMP1 in PDAC. Detection of SCAMP1 expression could be further evaluated as a tumor biomarker for PDAC diagnosis and predication of prognosis. SCAMP1 expression was associated with a larger tumor size, poorer tumor differentiation, and shorter overall survival of PDAC. Moreover, SCAMP1 also sponged miR-106a-5p to downregulate miR-106a-5p expression and, in turn, upregulate AGK expression in PDAC development and progression. Our future study will assess SCAMP1 as a prognostic marker for PDAC patients and targeting of the SCAMP1-miR-106a-5p-AGK axis as a novel therapeutic strategy for PDAC patients.
