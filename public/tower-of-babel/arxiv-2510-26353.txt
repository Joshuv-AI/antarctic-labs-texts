Abstract
. Financial forecasting increasingly uses large neural network models, but their opacity raises challenges for trust and regulatory compliance. We present several approaches to explainable and reliable AI in finance. First , we describe how Time-LLM, a time series foundation model, uses a prompt to avoid a wrong directional forecast. Second , we show that combining foundation models for time series forecasting with a reliability estimator can filter our unreliable predictions. Third , we argue for symbolic reasoning encoding domain rules for transparent justification. These approaches shift emphasize executing only forecasts that are both reliable and explainable. Experiments on equity and cryptocurrency data show that the architecture reduces false positives and supports selective execution. By integrating predictive performance with reliability estimation and rule-based reasoning, our framework advances transparent and auditable financial AI systems.



1.
Introduction

Artificial intelligence has transformed financial forecasting, but its reliance on opaque neural network architectures raises concerns for trust, accountability, and regulatory compliance.
While accuracy remains a central performance metric
(
Isufaj et al. 2025
;
Sharma et al. 2025
;
Feuerriegel and Prendinger 2016
;
Mai et al. 2024
)
, financial stakeholders require more: they need to know both
when
and
why
a prediction or trade should be acted upon. A wrong forecast in trading can translate directly into monetary loss, and in high-stakes environments, the cost of false positives is particularly severe.

Legislation such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, Article 22, 2018, Recital
71) and the EU AI Act asserts the right for explainability of decisions, and even to challenge them. This calls for a suitable explanation mechanism that is understandable to human users. Rules encoding the data
(market conditions, price history, technical signals etc.) are a natural candidate, as they can be intuitively
understood. Non-profitable trades are unavoidable in trading; yet, the system needs to be able to reveal its
decision rules and how they were applied.

Explainable AI (XAI) has provided an interesting approach to mitigate these concerns.
(
Carta et al. 2021
)
develops different feature selection strategies in an applied financial setting to predict the next-day returns for stocks.

(
Ohana et al. 2021
)
utilizes SHAP values to predict strong crash-risk, and simple date-by-date explanations of which features drove the model.
(
Yeo et al. 2025
)
offers a systematic review on XAI in finance.

In this paper, we propose some approaches to explainability in financial time series forecasting.

•

Time-LLM
(
Jin et al. 2024
)
, where
Prompt-as-Prefix
interacts with the model’s time series forecaster, by concatenating prompt vectors with the series embedding. The prompt is human-readable, so it’s easy to audit why the model leaned one way.

•

Meta-labeling
(
De Prado 2018
)
, a kind of “Corrective AI”, where a primary model makes a forecast and a secondary model acts as a
reliability estimator
.
Alternatively,
symbolic reasoning
can encode domain rules for accept/abstain decisions, providing transparent, rule-based justifications that can be inspected by practitioners and regulators.

Reliability Estimation Model as Corrective AI

Corrective AI
(
Pik et al. 2025
)
in financial forecasting is an approach where a one model is making a prediction and another model estimates the trustworthiness of the primary model.

This kind of decision decoupling has been introduced by Lopez de Prado’s meta-labeling concept
(
De Prado 2018
)
. Here, a primary model
M1
predicts the direction of the price action (up or down), whereas a secondary model
M2
estimates the reliability of the primary model’s prediction.

1

Such approach is important in high-stake decision-making environments (healthcare, automated driving, trading) with low tolerance for false positives (FPs), i.e., classifying with a wrong label
(

;
Rabanser et al. 2025
)
. In the context of trading, a FP means that our model suggests that, for instance, the price of the asset will go up, while in reality it is not.

5.
Knowledge-based Reasoning

While foundation models and neural predictors provide strong forecasts, they remain opaque and may fail under shifting market regimes. To address this, one could complement them with a symbolic, rule-based reasoning layer
(
Beck et al. 2018
;
Eiter et al. 2023
;
Le-Phuoc et al. 2021
)
that governs whether forecasts should be accepted, rejected, or deferred. Unlike purely neural corrective models, this layer is grounded in formally defined trading rules and domain knowledge (e.g., candlestick setups, support/resistance conditions). These rules produce human-legible justifications for execution decisions, increasing both operational trust and regulatory compliance, in line with recent calls for explainability in financial AI
(
Yeo et al. 2025
)
.

The reasoning layer thus goes beyond filtering unreliable forecasts: it provides an explanation mechanism that can reveal why a prediction was endorsed or rejected, and under what market conditions. Explanations can be framed in intuitive financial terms (“the trade was vetoed because volatility exceeded the safe threshold”) rather than in opaque statistical metrics.

The integration of knowledge-based reasoning will transform the meta-labeling framework
(
De Prado 2018
)
into a neurosymbolic system
(
Eiter et al. 2023
)
, where execution requires both statistical confidence and logical justification. In doing so, the approach enhances transparency, trust, and robustness in financial AI applications.

As a simple illustrative example, we show the reasoning with a bottoming-tail candlestick detector. The predicate

bottoming_tail_candle

holds only if subordinate conditions about relative low, candle size, volume, and tail/body geometry are met over a recent window (e.g., 90 days). Each sub-predicate is a measurable criterion; violating any invalidates the rule.

) and evaluated online, producing a traceable accept/reject rationale.

6.
Conclusions

The approaches discussed in this paper are aimed to contribute to develop robust and verifiable socio-technical systems, and to move from black-box systems to ones that are transparent, understandable, and reliable. They support transparent decision-making in high-stake applications, including
finance, energy systems, and climate modeling. In that, explainability is a key issue.
Beyond technical gains, the presented approaches contribute to governance and regulatory compliance by offering transparent, auditable decision rationales.

Future work will explore richer rule-based reasoning, adaptive evaluation under regime shifts, and integration with real-world trading workflows.
