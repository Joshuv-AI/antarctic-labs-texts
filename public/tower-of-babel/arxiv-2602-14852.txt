Abstract
. Quantifying the degree of dissimilarity between two probability distributions on a finite metric space X X is a fundamental task in Computer Science and Computer Vision. A natural dissimilarity measurement based on optimal transport is the earth mover’s distance (EMD), also known as the Kantorovich metric or Wasserstein-1 metric. We denote the metric space of probability measures on X X equipped with the earth mover’s distance as EMD ⁡ ( X ) \EMD(X) , called the earth mover’s space. A key technique for analyzing this metric – pioneered by Charikar [ Cha02 ] and Indyk and Thaper [ IT03 ] – involves constructing low-distortion embeddings of EMD ⁡ ( X ) \EMD(X) into the Lebesgue space L 1 L_{1} . The best upper bound for the distortion of an embedding of EMD ⁡ ( X ) \EMD(X) with | X | = n |X|=n into L 1 L_{1} is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) . This result follows from a combination of Charikar’s work (which builds on [ KT02 ] ) and the seminal result by Fakcharoenphol, Rao, and Talwar [ FRT04 ] . Moreover, it is well known that expander graphs yield a matching lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) for L 1 L_{1} -distortion, showing that the upper bound can be tight. It became a key problem to investigate whether the upper bound of O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) can be improved for important classes of metric spaces known to admit low-distortion embeddings into L 1 L_{1} . In the context of Computer Vision, grid graphs — especially planar grids — are among the most fundamental. Indyk posed in [ MN11 , Problem 2.17] the related problem of estimating the L 1 L_{1} -distortion of the space of uniform distributions on n n -point subsets of ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . The Progress Report in [ MN11 ] , last updated in August 2011, highlighted two key results: first, the work of Khot and Naor [ KN06 ] on Hamming cubes, which showed that the L 1 L_{1} -distortion of EMD ⁡ ( { 0 , 1 } n ) \EMD(\{0,1\}^{n}) is of the order n n , and second, the result of Naor and Schechtman [ NS07 ] for planar grids, which established that the L 1 L_{1} -distortion of EMD ⁡ ( { 0 , … , n } 2 ) \EMD(\{0,\dots,n\}^{2}) is Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\sqrt{\log n}) . Our first result is the improvement of the lower bound on the L 1 L_{1} -distortion of EMD ⁡ ( { 0 , … , n } 2 ) \EMD(\{0,\dots,n\}^{2}) to Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega\left(\log n\right) , matching the universal upper bound up to multiplicative constants. The key ingredient allowing us to obtain these sharp estimates is a new Sobolev-type inequality for scalar-valued functions on the grid graphs. Our method is also applicable to many recursive families of graphs, such as diamond and Laakso graphs. We obtain the sharp distortion estimates of log ⁡ n \log n in these cases as well.

1.3.
The Basic Idea of the Proof

The techniques used in the proof of the main step, Theorem
2.1
, resemble the techniques used in many lower distortion bounds. We mean the well-known method based on Poincaré inequalities (see
[
Ost13
, Chapter 4]
). Let us remind the basic idea.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. Suppose that our goal is to estimate the infimal distortion of embeddings of
X
into
Y
from below. To achieve this, we find a subset
U
of
X
, arrays of real numbers
\{a_{uv}\}_{u,v\in U}
and
\{b_{uv}\}_{u,v\in U}
, and
p\in[1,\infty)
, such that, on one hand,

(1.5)

\sum_{u,v\in U}a_{u,v}(d_{X}(u,v))^{p}\leq\sum_{u,v\in U}b_{u,v}(d_{X}(u,v))^{p},

and, on the other hand, for any mapping
f:X\to Y

(1.6)

\sum_{u,v\in U}a_{u,v}(d_{Y}(f(u),f(v)))^{p}\geq D\sum_{u,v\in U}b_{u,v}(d_{Y}(f(u),f(v)))^{p}.

The inequalities above are called
Poincaré inequalities
. It is not difficult to conclude that inequalities (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) imply that the distortion of any map of
X
into
Y
is at least
D^{1/p}
.

We use a similar method. We also observe that because of the linearization equality (
1.3
), we may assume that
f
is linear. Because of this, we may, in addition, assume that
f
is noncontractive and
\|f\|\leq
distortion
\,+\varepsilon
.

To achieve the optimal distortion rate, in our versions of inequalities (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), we had to mix exponents
1
and
2
on one side. Because of this, we call the obtained inequality ‘Sobolev inequality’ (let us recall that in the classical Poincaré inequality exponents in both sides are the same, but in the classical Sobolev inequality the exponents are mixed).

We work with the case in which the metric space
G
is a weighted graph,
G=(V,E,w)
, with a positive weight
w
and the shortest path distance
d_{G}
satisfying
d_{G}(u,v)=w(uv)
for each edge
uv
. Furthermore, we assume that there is another positive function

E
called

, satisfying

\sum_{uv\in E(G)}th(uv)d_{G_{n}}(uv)=1.

Our analogues of the Poincaré inequalities are the following. For some graphs

we find index sets
\mathcal{M}=\sqcup_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{M}_{k}
and systems
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(G)
of transportation problems, such that for certain matrices
\{\theta_{s}(t)\}_{s,t\in\mathcal{M}_{k}},\theta_{s}(t)=\pm 1
, and numbers

and
C
(independent of

), we have (here

and
e^{-}
are endpoint vertices of the oriented edge

)

(1.7)

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{k}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\left\|\sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\theta_{s}(t)\mu_{t}\right\|^{2}_{{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt}\right)^{1/2}\geq m\cdot\alpha\sum_{e\in E}\|\delta_{e^{+}}-\delta_{e^{-}}\|_{{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt}th(e),

and on the other hand, for any linear noncontractive map
f:{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)\to\ell_{1}^{d}
, we have

(1.8)

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{k}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\left\|f\left(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\theta_{s}(t)\mu_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\ell_{1}^{d}}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\sum_{e\in E}\|f(\delta_{e^{+}}-\delta_{e^{-}})\|_{\ell_{1}^{d}}th(e).

As for the Poincaré inequalities above, it is easy to see that the distortion estimate
c_{1}({\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(G))\geq\frac{m\alpha}{C}
follows.
Note that in the argument below, it is convenient to use slightly different notation. The inequality (

) follows eventually from (

) and the inequality (
1.8
) can be found inside (

).

To prove the main results of the paper, it remains to observe that for some families

of such graphs, the parameter

grows as needed, while the quotient

2.2.
Reduction to Simply Connected Sets

In this subsection, we provide a very useful reduction that allows us to verify Sobolev-type inequalities only for indicator functions of sets that are connected and have a connected complement.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected, finite, directed graph and
A\subseteq V
. The
edge-boundary
of
A
, denoted
\partial_{E}A
, is the set of all edges having one endpoint in
A
and the other in
A^{c}
. Obviously,
\partial_{E}A=\partial_{E}A^{c}
. Let
\nu
be a fully supported positive measure on
E
. For
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the gradient
\nabla f:E\to\mathbb{R}
by
\nabla(f)(uv):=f(v)-f(u)
. We define the
(1,1)
-Sobolev (semi)norm of
f
by

\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}:=\|\nabla f\|_{L_{1}(\nu)}=\sum_{uv\in E}|f(v)-f(u)|\nu(uv).

Note that when
f=\mathsf{1}_{A}
for some
A\subseteq V
,
\|\mathsf{1}_{A}\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=\nu(\partial_{E}A)
.

Since
G
is connected and
\nu
is fully supported,
\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=0
if and only if
f
is a constant function. Thus, the set of functions
V\to\mathbb{R}
modulo constant functions becomes a normed space when equipped with the Sobolev norm. We denote this normed space by
W^{1,1}(\nu)
.

To prove the desired reduction, we use a characterization of the extreme points of
B_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
found in
[
Ost05
]
, where
B_{W}
denotes the closed unit ball of a normed space
W
. We refer to a set of vertices
A\subseteq V
as
simply connected
if
A
and
A^{c}
are connected. We also use the comment on
[
Ost05
, bottom of p. 501]
(stating that the proof for edge-weighted graphs goes along the same lines).

Theorem 2.2

(
[
Ost05
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Suppose
\nu
is a positive measure on
E
. Then the equivalence class of a function
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
is an extreme point of
B_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
if and only if
\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=1
and there exists a simply connected subset
A\subseteq V
such that the restriction of
f
to
A
is a constant function and the restriction of
f
to
A^{c}
is a constant function.

Theorem 2.3

(Reduction to Simply Connected Sets)
.

Suppose that
\nu
is a positive measure on
E
. Let
|||\cdot|||
be a seminorm on
\mathbb{R}^{V}
with
|||\mathsf{1}_{V}|||=0
, and let
C\in[0,\infty)
be a constant. If
|||\mathsf{1}_{A}|||\leq C\|\mathsf{1}_{A}\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
for all simply connected sets
A\subseteq V
, then
|||f|||\leq C\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
for all
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Assume that
|||\mathsf{1}_{A}|||\leq C\|\mathsf{1}_{A}\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
for all simply connected sets
A\subseteq V
. Let
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
be a function. If
f
is a constant function, then
|||f|||=0
by assumption, and so the conclusion holds trivially. Assume, then, that
f
is not constant. By homogeneity of the seminorms on each side of the inequality, we may then assume that
\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=1
. By Carathéodory’s convex hull theorem, there exist finitely many extreme points
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq B_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}
and positive scalars
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=1
and
f\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i}
(modulo constant functions). By Theorem
2.2
, there exist simply connected subsets
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
V
and scalars
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
g_{i}\equiv a_{i}\mathsf{1}_{A_{i}}
(modulo constant functions) for each
i\in\{1,\dots m\}
. Then

f\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}a_{i}\mathsf{1}_{A_{i}}
(modulo constant functions). Then, since both the seminorm
|||\cdot|||
and the Sobolev norm vanish on constant functions, we have

\displaystyle|||f|||

\displaystyle=|||\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}a_{i}\mathsf{1}_{A_{i}}|||\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}|a_{i}|\,|||\mathsf{1}_{A_{i}}|||

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}|a_{i}|\,\|\mathsf{1}_{A_{i}}\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=C\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\|g_{i}\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}=C=C\|f\|_{W^{1,1}(\nu)}.\qed

We begin with a simple lemma stating that the transportation cost norm is approximately additive when the supports of the measures involved are sufficiently separated. This is intuitively clear, and one will frequently encounter closely related estimates of this type in the transportation cost literature. Once we’ve established the lemma, condition
(C

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space and
M
a finite collection of nonzero signed measures on
X
, each having 0 total mass, such that

•

\sup_{\mu\in M}{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\mu))\leq A
and

•

\inf_{\mu^{\prime},\mu\in M,\mu^{\prime}\neq\mu}\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm dist}\hskip 0.28436pt(\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\mu),\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\mu^{\prime}))\geq B^{-1}

for some
A,B<\infty
. Then for any function
g:M\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Big\|\sum_{\mu\in M}g(\mu)\mu\Big\|_{\TC}\geq(AB)^{-1}\sum_{\mu\in M}|g(\mu)|\|\mu\|_{\TC}.

Proof.

Denoting
g(\mu)\mu
by
\tau
, we can write the desired inequality as

(3.1)

(AB)\left\|\sum_{\tau\in M^{\prime}}\tau\right\|_{\TC}\geq\sum_{\tau\in M^{\prime}}\|\tau\|_{\TC}

Let

\sum_{\tau\in M^{\prime}}\tau=a_{1}(\delta_{x_{1}}-\delta_{y_{1}})+a_{2}(\delta_{x_{2}}-\delta_{y_{2}})+\dots+a_{n}(\delta_{x_{n}}-\delta_{y_{n}})

be an optimal transportation plan (recall that our space is finite). It is easy to see that we may assume that
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are contained in
\cup_{\tau\in M^{\prime}}\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\tau)
. If each pair
(x_{i},y_{i})
is inside the support of some
\tau\in M^{\prime}
, the inequality (
3.1
) is immediate with
AB
replaced by
1
.

If some pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
are split and their elements belong to the supports of different
\tau
’s, we change the transportation plan in the following way. For each
\tau
, let
P_{\tau}=\{i:~x_{i}\in\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\tau),y_{i}\notin\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\tau)\}
and
N_{\tau}=\{i:~y_{i}\in\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\tau),x_{i}\notin\hskip 0.56917pt{\rm supp}\hskip 0.28436pt(\tau)\}
. Since
\tau(X)=0
, we get that
\sum_{i\in P_{\tau}}a_{i}=\sum_{i\in N_{\tau}}a_{i}
. Thus, we can match the corresponding availabilities and needs, and obtain a modified plan that, for each
\tau
, matches its availabilities with its needs. The cost of the resulting plan is at most a factor of
AB
times the cost of the original plan, because each distance

from the supports of different
\tau
is at least
B^{-1}
, and from the support of the same
\tau
is at most
A
. On the other hand, the cost of the resulting plan cannot be less than the sum of optimal plans for each of
\tau
. The inequality (
3.1
) follows.

The thickness function we take on

3.3.1.
Graph and Planar Topology

We now collect some basic facts about general graphs, as well as facts specific to the topology of planar grids. Since these latter facts can fail quite badly in graphs such as trees and cycles (whose transportation cost spaces do embed into
L_{1}
with uniformly bounded distortion), they can be seen as important metric-topological reasons for the distortion
c_{1}(\TC(Gr_{n}))
being large.
We begin with the elementary facts holding in any graph.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be any finite unweighted graph,
d
the associated shortest path metric in
G
, and
{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt
the associated diameter, allowing the value
\infty
in the event that
G
is not connected. Let
A\subseteq V
be connected. Then the following are true.

(1)

{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A)+1\leq|A|
.

(2)

If there exist
r>0
and an
r

S\subseteq A
(meaning
d(u,v)\geq r
for all
u,v\in S,u\neq v
) with
|S|\geq 2
, then
r|S|\leq 3|A|
.

We do not claim that the constant 3 in the second item above is optimal. This proposition plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, by way of (
3.3
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

The first item is even more obvious than the second: the cardinality of
A
bounds the cardinality of any chosen path in
A
(which exists since
A
is connected), and a path can be chosen such that its cardinality bounds
{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A)+1
.

We now prove the second item. For each
s\in S
, let
B_{r/3}(s)
denote the subset of
A
consisting of all those
a\in A
that can be joined to
s
by a path that is completely contained in
A
and that has length at most
r/3
. Since
S
is
r
-separated, the triangle inequality implies that the sets
\{B_{r/3}(s)\}_{s\in S}
are pairwise disjoint. Moreover, we have the cardinality bound
|B_{r/3}(s)|\geq r/3
. This is due to the following fact: since
|S|\geq 2
and
A
is connected, for any
s\in S
, we can choose
s_{0}\in S\setminus\{s\}
and a path
P\subseteq A
joining

to
s_{0}
. We must have that

|P\cap B_{r/3}(s)|\geq r/3
. This cardinality bound together with the pairwise disjointness implies

.
∎

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, we define the
symmetric vertex-boundary
of
A
as
\partial_{V}A:=\bigcup_{uv\in\partial_{E}A}\{u,v\}
.
Obviously,
\partial_{V}A=\partial_{V}A^{c}
and

(3.2)

|\partial_{V}A|\leq 2|\partial_{E}A|.

We now give the two essential lemmas concerning the topology of planar grids and sketch their proofs. The first is the graph-theoretical version of the classical result from planar topology that simply connected subsets of the 2-dimensional sphere have connected boundary. Recall that when
A
is a subset of the vertex set of a graph, we have defined
A
to be simply connected if
A
and
A^{c}
are both connected.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
A\subseteq V(Gr_{n})
is simply connected, then
\partial_{V}A
is connected.

Proof.

For each
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in V(Gr_{n})
, let
\bar{x}:=[x_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2},x_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}]\times[x_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2},x_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.,
\bar{x}
is the closed
1\times 1
square centered at
x
. For
A\subseteq V(Gr_{n})
, let
\tilde{A}:=\mathrm{int}\left(\bigcup_{x\in A}\bar{x}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\mathrm{int}(U)
denotes the interior of
U
as a subset of the topological space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We also introduce
\tilde{A^{c}}:=\mathrm{int}\left(\bigcup_{x\in A^{c}}\bar{x}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We get two disjoint subsets of the square
S:=[-\tfrac{1}{2},2^{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}]\times[-\tfrac{1}{2},2^{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}]
. It is clear that the only parts of the square
S
which are not in
\tilde{A^{c}}\cup\tilde{A}
are (1) The boundary of
S
, (2) The closed line segments between the squares belonging to different regions,
\tilde{A^{c}}
and
\tilde{A}
.

We form a graph
B
in which the closed line segments between the squares belonging to different regions,
\tilde{A^{c}}
and
\tilde{A}
, are edges, and their endpoints are vertices. It is important to observe that the degrees of the vertices in this graph can be either
1
,
2
, or
4
, and the degree
1
occurs if and only if the vertex is on the boundary of
S
.
The statement about the vertices in the boundary of
S
is clear. Let us consider a vertex that is not in the boundary of
S
. Edges from it are perpendicular to the line segments joining the vertices of the grid belonging to different sets of
\tilde{A^{c}}
and
\tilde{A}
. This is why the degree of the vertices of
B
, which are not in the boundary of
S
, can be either
2
or
4
.

By a version of the Euler theorem (on Euler tours and trails, see
[
BM08
, Section 3.3 and Exercise 3.3.5]
), the graph
B
is an edge-disjoint union of paths and cycles, and paths can only join different vertices of the boundary.

Now we consider the graph
\widehat{B}
, in which, in addition to
B
, we include all line segments of the boundary of
S
. We get a planar graph. By the Euler formula,
n-m+\ell=c+1
, where
n
is the number of vertices,
m
is the number of edges,
\ell
is the number of faces, and
c
is the number of connected components. Since the graph has three faces,
\tilde{A^{c}}
,
\tilde{A}
, and the outer face, we get
n-m=c-2
.

Consider two cases: (a) There is a path among the paths and cycles, (b) There are only cycles.

In both cases, the planar graph obtained by picking one path in case (a), one cycle in case (b), and omitting further paths and cycles already has three faces. Therefore, in order to show that there are no further paths and cycles, it suffices to show that adding any of them increases the number of faces.

Case (a). Adding a path of length
k
adds at most
k-1
vertices,
k
edges, and does not change the number of components of
\widehat{B}
; therefore, it increases the number of faces. Adding a cycle. There are two subcases, in one of them the cycle is a new component in
\widehat{B}
. In this case, we add
k
vertices,
k
edges, and
1
component, so the number of faces has to increase by
1
. In the second subcase, we add more edges than vertices without changing the number of components, so the number of faces should increase.

Case (b). We need consider adding a cycle only, but this can be done exactly as in the previous paragraph.

The last step: let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be edges of the only path/cycle in the partition of
B
described above, in their natural order. Then edges of
Gr_{n}
crossing
e_{k}
and
e_{k+1}
either have a common vertex (if
e_{k}
and
e_{k+1}
are perpendicular) or their vertices are vertices of a grid square (if the edges are on a straight line). All of these vertices are in
\partial_{V}A
. In either case, we get connectivity.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
A\subseteq V(Gr_{n})
with
A,A^{c}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\min\{{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A),{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A^{c})\}\leq 2{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(\partial_{V}A)
.

Proof.

Color vertices of
A
blue and of
A^{c}
yellow. The result is immediate if there are two opposite multicolored sides of the grid. In fact, in this case,
{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(\partial_{V}A)=2^{n}
. On the other hand, it is clear that
\max\{{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A),{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A^{c})\}\leq 2^{n+1}
.

So, assume that each pair of opposite sides has one unicolored side, say,
S_{1}
for one pair and
S_{2}
for the other pair. Since
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have a common vertex, the color of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
should be the same. Assume without loss of generality that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are both blue.

Now, consider the smallest rectangle containing all yellow vertices. Let
m
be its longest side. Consider the two opposite shortest sides of this rectangle. They contain yellow vertices, and their straight-line extensions necessarily contain blue vertices. Therefore
{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(\partial_{V}A)\geq m
. On the other hand, since

is contained in this rectangle, we have
{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(A^{c})\leq 2m
.
∎

4.

4.1.
Definitions and Basic Concepts

In the remaining part of this paper, it will be convenient to follow closely the terminology for graphs introduced by Serre
[
Ser03
, §2]
. Namely, graphs
G
have finite vertex sets
V(G)
and directed edge sets
Y(G)\subseteq(V(G)\times V(G))\setminus\{(u,u):u\in V(G)\}
. The set
Y(G)
contains each edge in both directions, meaning
uv\in Y(G)\iff vu\in Y(G)
. When we need to pick a direction on each edge, we call it
a choice of an orientation
, the resulting subset of
Y(G)
is denoted
E(G)
.

When
e=(u,v)\in Y(G)
, we denote
u
by
e^{+}
,
v
by
e^{-}
, and
(v,u)
by
\bar{e}
. A sequence of vertices
\{u_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq V(G)
is a
directed path
for the orientation
E(G)
if
(u_{i-1},u_{i})\in E(G)
for every
i\in\{1,\dots k\}
and is a
path
if
(u_{i-1},u_{i})\in Y(G)
for every
i\in\{1,\dots k\}
. A sequence of edges
\{e_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq E(G)
is a
directed edge path
if
e_{i-1}^{+}=e_{i}^{-}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots k\}
and is an
edge path
if
e_{i}\in\{f_{i},\bar{f_{i}}\}
for some directed edge path
\{f_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
.

We consider a special class of graphs called
st
-graphs. Classes of graphs similar to the one we consider have been studied under the same name before, but the exact definition can vary from author to author. For us, an
st
-graph
is a graph
G
, as defined above, having two distinct vertices, a
source
vertex
s_{G}
and
sink
vertex
t_{G}
, equipped with a
weighted

geodesic metric

d_{G}:V(G)\times V(G)\to[0,1]
, an orientation
E(G)
, and a
thickness function

th_{G}:E(G)\to(0,1]
satisfying the axioms
(a)-(c)
below. Note that the assumption that
th_{G}
takes positive values implies that every edge
e\in E(G)
belongs to the introduced below
\gamma_{i}
for at least one
i\in I
.

(a)

d_{G}(s_{G},t_{G})=1
,

(b)

every directed path in orientation
E(G)
is a geodesic,

(c)

there exists a finite index set
I
and a collection of (not necessarily distinct) directed edge paths
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i\in I}
from
s_{G}
to
t_{G}
in
E(G)
such that
th_{G}(e)=\frac{|\{i\in I:e\in\gamma_{i}\}|}{|I|}
for all
e\in E(G)
.

We overload notation and write
d_{G}:Y(G)\to[0,1]
to denote the
length function

d_{G}(e):=d_{G}(e^{-},e^{+})
. Being a geodesic metric, the values of
d_{G}
on

are completely determined by its values on

.

For
A\subseteq V(G)
, we let
\partial_{G}A
denote the
edge boundary
of
A
, meaning the set of all edges
e\in E(G)
for which
|\{e^{-},e^{+}\}\cap A|=1
. The next proposition contains two fundamental structural results about
st
-graphs.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G
be an
st
-graph. Then the following facts hold.

(1)

\sum_{e\in E(G)}th_{G}(e)d_{G}(e)=1
.

(2)

If
A\subseteq V(G)
and
|\{s_{G},t_{G}\}\cap A|=1
, then
\sum_{e\in\partial_{G}A}th_{G}(e)\geq 1
.

Proof.

For (1), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(G)}th_{G}(e)d_{G}(e)=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\frac{|\{i\in I:e\in\gamma_{i}\}|}{|I|}d_{G}(e)=\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{e\in\gamma_{i}}d_{G}(e)=1.

For (2), let
A\subseteq V(G)
with
|\{s_{G},t_{G}\}\cap A|=1
. For each of the directed edge paths
\gamma_{i}
, there must exist at least one edge
\gamma_{i}\cap\partial_{G}A
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\partial_{G}A}th_{G}(e)=\sum_{e\in\partial_{G}A}\frac{|\{i\in I:e\in\gamma_{i}\}|}{|I|}=\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{e\in\partial_{G}A\cap\gamma_{i}}1

\displaystyle\geq 1.\qed

A fundamental operation on the class of
st
-graphs is edge replacement.

Definition 4.2

(Edge Replacement)
.

Suppose that
G,H
are
st
-graphs and
e\in E(G)
. We form the
edge replacement

st
-graph
G\cup_{e}H
by replacing
e
with
H
in the following way:

•

The vertex set is
V(G\cup_{e}H):=V(G)\sqcup V(H)/\sim
, where
\sim
is the equivalence relation generated by
e^{-}\sim s_{H}
and
e^{+}\sim t_{H}
. The composition of the inclusion map and the quotient map gives an injection from each of
V(G)
and
V(H)
into
V(G\cup_{e}H)
. We identify
V(G)
as a subset of
V(G\cup_{e}H)
in this way, without making reference to the inclusion and quotient maps. On the other hand, we will use special notation for the injection of
V(H)
into
V(G\cup_{e}H)
; the image of
v\in V(H)
is written as
e\oslash v
.

•

With this identification, the source and sink vertices are defined by
s_{G\cup_{e}H}=s_{G}
and
t_{G\cup_{e}H}=t_{G}
.

•

When
e^{\prime}=(u,v)\in E(H)
, we write
e\oslash e^{\prime}
to denote the ordered pair
(e\oslash u,e\oslash v)\in V(G\cup_{e}H)\times V(G\cup_{e}H)
and
e\oslash E(H)
to denote
\{e\oslash e^{\prime}\}_{e^{\prime}\in E(H)}
. The directed edge set

is defined to be
(E(G)\setminus\{e\})\cup(e\oslash E(H))\subseteq V(G\cup_{e}H)\times V(G\cup_{e}H)
. Note that the mapping
E(H)\ni e^{\prime}\mapsto e\oslash e^{\prime}\in e\oslash E(H)
is bijective and that the two subsets
E(G)\setminus\{e\}
and
e\oslash E(H)
are disjoint.

•

The geodesic metric is defined on edges by
d_{G\cup_{e}H}(e^{\prime})=d_{G}(e^{\prime})
if

and
d_{G\cup_{e}H}(e\oslash e^{\prime})=d_{G}(e)d_{H}(e^{\prime})
if
e^{\prime}\in E(H)
.

•

The thickness function is defined by
th_{G\cup_{e}H}(e^{\prime})=th_{G}(e^{\prime})
if

and
th_{G\cup_{e}H}(e\oslash e^{\prime})=th_{G}(e)th_{H}(e^{\prime})
if

.

Remark 4.3
.

The informal germ of this construction was implicit in many papers, the earliest we know is

Proposition 4.4
.

For all
st
-graphs
G,H
and edges

, the edge replacement graph

satisfies the axioms
(a)-(c)

Proof.

Let
G,H
be
st

in

Let
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i\in I_{G}}
and

of
st

Now let

\displaystyle th_{G\cup_{e}H}(e^{\prime})

\displaystyle=th_{G}(e^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{|i\in I_{G}:e^{\prime}\in\gamma_{i}|}{|I_{G}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|(i,j)\in I_{G}\times I_{H}:e^{\prime}\in\gamma_{i}|}{|I_{G}||I_{H}|}

\displaystyle th_{G\cup_{e}H}(e^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{|i\in I_{G}:e\in\gamma_{i}|}{|I_{G}|}\frac{|j\in I_{H}:e^{\prime\prime}\in\gamma_{j}|}{|I_{H}|}

4.2.
Elementary Developments

Now, we turn our attention to

st
-graphs that can be obtained by a sequence of iterative edge replacements by “elementary”
st
-graphs. Specifically, let us say that an
st
-graph
H
is
elementary
if it consists of a single directed path from
s_{H}
to
t_{H}
with all edges having thickness 1, or it consists of two directed paths from
s_{H}
to
t_{H}
, each containing at least three vertices, that share only their endpoint vertices
\{s_{H},t_{H}\}
in common and all edges having thickness
\frac{1}{2}
.
Note that the edges of an elementary
st
-graph are permitted to have different lengths. Elementary graphs of the first type described above are called
st
-paths
, and those of the second type are called
st
-cycles
. An
st
-graph is
trivial
if it is an
st
-path with only 2 vertices. We say that a sequence of
st
-graphs
(G^{(n)})_{n=0}^{\infty}=(V^{(n)},E^{(n)})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is an
elementary development
if

•

G^{(0)}
is a trivial
st
-path with
E^{(0)}=\{(s,t)\}
and

•

for each
n\geq 0
, there exists an edge
e\in E^{(n)}
and an elementary
st
-graph
H
(depending on
n,e
) such that
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}\cup_{e}H
.

Note that, although an elementary development formally involves an infinite sequence of graphs, we allow the case that the sequence stabilizes and
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}
for all sufficiently large
n
, which occurs when the
st
-graph
H
with
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}\cup_{e}H
is trivial. In this way, the definition of elementary development we give can accommodate finite sequences.

Fix an elementary development
(G^{(n)})_{n=0}^{\infty}=(V^{(n)},E^{(n)})_{n=0}^{\infty}
. We write
(\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V},d)
to denote the direct limit of the sequence of metric spaces
(V^{(n)},d_{G^{(n)}})
. That is,
\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
is the increasing infinite union
\bigcup_{n\geq 0}V^{(n)}
, and

denotes the metric on
\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
that restricts to
d_{G^{(n)}}
on each
V^{(n)}
. This metric is well-defined by Proposition
4.5
(2). We emphasize here that
\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
is a countable set (being the countable union of finite sets), and
(\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V},d)
is generally not complete.

Let
n\geq 0
. If
G^{(n+1)}\neq G^{(n)}
, then the edge
e\in E^{(n)}
and
st
-graph
H
for which
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}\cup_{e}H
are uniquely determined (up to isomorphism, in the case of
H
) by
G^{(n)}
and
G^{(n+1)}
because
e
is the unique element of
E^{(n)}\setminus E^{(n+1)}
, and
H
is isomorphic to the subgraph
G_{e}
induced by the union of all directed paths in
G^{(n+1)}
from
e^{-}
to
e^{+}
, with
s_{H}
identified with
e^{-}
,
t_{H}
identified with
e^{+}
, and
d_{H}
identified with the restriction of
\frac{d}{d(e)}
to
G_{e}
.

Define
\mathcal{E}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}E^{(n)}
. Note that these unions are in general neither disjoint nor increasing, as, for each
n\geq 0
with
G^{(n+1)}\neq G^{(n)}
, there is an edge
e\in E^{(n)}
for which
E^{(n)}\setminus\{e\}\subseteq E^{(n+1)}
and
e\not\in E^{(n+1)}
. Let
th:\mathcal{E}\to(0,1]
denote the function that restricts to
th_{G^{(n)}}
on each
E^{(n)}
. This is well defined since if
e\in E^{(n)}\cap E^{(n+1)}
, then
th_{G^{(n+1)}}(e)=th_{G^{(n)}}(e)
. For each
e\in\mathcal{E}
, we let
n_{e}
denote the greatest
n\geq 1
such that
e\in E^{(n-1)}
(allowing the possibility that
n_{e}=\infty
). Let
m\geq 0
with
G^{(m+1)}\neq G^{(m)}
. As previously explained, there is a unique edge
e^{(m)}\in E^{(m)}
for which there exists an
st
-graph
H^{(m)}
with
G^{(m+1)}=G^{(m)}\cup_{e}H^{(m)}
. Let
\prec
be the strict partial order on
\mathcal{E}
generated by the relation
e^{(m)}\oslash e\prec e^{(m)}
for all such
m\geq 0
and all
e\in E(H^{(m)})
. We let
\preceq
denote the corresponding partial order on
\mathcal{E}
(

if

or

). Then

has the structure of a rooted tree. The

is

since it satisfies
(s,t)\succeq e
for all

, and the tree structure owes to the fact that for each

, the
ancestor set

\mathcal{E}_{\succ e}:=\{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}:e^{\prime}\succ e\}
is totally ordered (that is, for any two distinct elements

and
e^{\prime\prime}
of it either
e^{\prime}\prec e^{\prime\prime}
or
e^{\prime\prime}\prec e
). We say that

are

if
e_{1}\not\preceq e_{2}
and
e_{2}\not\preceq e_{1}
. We turn the reader’s attention to the fact that we mean the root is a maximal element in the tree (in many sources, it is required to be minimal).

Lemma 4.8

(Bottleneck)
.

Suppose
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
are incomparable. Suppose that
x_{1},x_{2}\in\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
are descendant vertices of
f_{1},f_{2}
, respectively, by which we mean vertices of the form
x_{1}\in\{e_{1}^{-},e_{1}^{+}\}
and
x_{2}\in\{e_{2}^{-},e_{2}^{+}\}
for some
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
with
e_{1}\preceq f_{1}
and
e_{2}\preceq f_{2}
. Then for every
n\geq 0
with
x_{1},x_{2}\in V^{(n)}
, every path in
G^{(n)}
starting at
x_{1}
and ending at
x_{2}
must have nonempty intersection first with
\{f_{1}^{-},f_{1}^{+}\}
and then with
\{f_{2}^{-},f_{2}^{+}\}
.

Proof.

To see this, it suffices to use the observation: the only descendant vertices of
f_{1}
which are ends of edges
e
that do not satisfy
e\preceq f_{1}
are
\{f_{1}^{-},f_{1}^{+}\}
(and likewise for
f_{2}
).
∎

For
e\in\mathcal{E}
, we define the
descendant set

\mathcal{E}_{\preceq e}
to be
\{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}:e^{\prime}\preceq e\}
. For
n\geq 0
, we define the
n
th descendant edge set
of

to be the subset
E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}:=E^{(n)}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\preceq e}
.

Lemma 4.9

(Incomparability Implies Disjointness)
.

If
E^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
is a pairwise incomparable subset, then for every
n\geq 0
, the collection
\{E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}\}_{e\in E^{\prime}}
of subsets of

is pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

It is easy to see, for example, by induction,
that descendants of incomparable edges are incomparable. ∎

We need to single out those edges in
\mathcal{E}
that were replaced by an
st
-cycle at some stage of the development. Specifically, we define
\mathcal{E}_{cyc}
to be the set of all
e\in\mathcal{E}
such that there exists
n\geq 0
an
st
-cycle
H
with

. It follows immediately from the definitions that
\mathcal{E}_{cyc}\subseteq\{e\in\mathcal{E}:n_{e}<\infty\}
.

Lemma 4.10

(Disjoint Descendants)
.

For all

, the collection
\{E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in\mathcal{E}_{cyc}\\

is pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

Note that, for all

with

, if

, then

are incomparable. This is because, if

).
∎

4.3.
Perimeter Measure

Let
A\subseteq\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
be any subset (
A
is permitted to be infinite). The
edge boundary
is the subset
\partial A=\{e\in\mathcal{E}:|\{e^{-},e^{+}\}\cap A|=1\}
. The
perimeter measure
of
A
is defined on each subset
E^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
by

\Per_{A}(E^{\prime}):=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}\cap\partial A}th(e)\in[0,\infty].

Along with Lemma
4.12
, the next lemma is one of the two most important results concerning the geometry of developments. These lemmas should be seen as the two basic ingredients going into the Sobolev Inequality for Sets (Lemma
4.14
).

Lemma 4.11

(Monotonicity of Perimeter)
.

For all
A\subseteq\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
,
e\in\mathcal{E}
, and
n\geq 0
, we have the inequality

\Per_{A}(E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e})\geq\Per_{A}(E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}).

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
,
e\in\mathcal{E}
, and
n\geq 0
. If
E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}=\emptyset
(which happens if and only if
e
does not appear until after the
n
th stage of the development), then the inequality is trivially true, and thus we may assume that
E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}\neq\emptyset
.

We will prove that, for all edges
e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}

(where
des
stands for ‘descendant’),

(4.1)

\Per_{A}(E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}})\geq\Per_{A}(\{e_{des}\}).

Before establishing (
4.1
), let us see how it implies the conclusion. Assume that (
4.1
) holds for every
e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}
. Then since the set
E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e}
is the disjoint union of the collection of sets
\{E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}}\}_{e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}}
(this is straightforward from the definitions), we have that

\displaystyle\Per_{A}(E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}}\Per_{A}(E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Per(edes)}}{\geq}\sum_{e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}}\Per_{A}(\{e_{des}\})

\displaystyle=\Per_{A}(E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}),

which is the desired inequality. We now prove (
4.1
).

Let
e_{des}\in E^{(n)}_{\preceq e}
. There are two cases: (a)
e_{des}
was not replaced at stage
n+1
, meaning
e_{des}\in E^{(n+1)}
, and (b)
e_{des}
was replaced at stage
n+1
, meaning
e_{des}\not\in E^{(n+1)}
. In case (a), we have that
E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}}=E^{(n)}_{\preceq e_{des}}
, and so (
4.1
) is satisfied tautologically.

Assume, then, that we are in case (b). Then
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}\cup_{e_{des}}H
for some
st
-graph
H
, and

(4.2)

E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}}=e_{des}\oslash E(H)

by definition. There are two subcases to consider: (i)
e_{des}\not\in\partial A
and (ii)
e_{des}\in\partial A
. In case (i), the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is 0, and so the inequality is trivially satisfied.

Now assume that (ii)
e_{des}\in\partial A
. Then, by definition,

(4.3)

\Per_{A}(\{e_{des}\})=th(e_{des}).

By Proposition
4.5
(3), the subgraph of
G^{(n+1)}
induced by
e_{des}\oslash V(H)
is naturally identified (as a directed graph) with
H
, and under this identification,
e_{des}^{-}
corresponds to
s_{H}
and
e_{des}^{+}
to
t_{H}
. Let
A_{H}
denote the subset of
V(H)
identified with
A\cap(e_{des}\oslash V(H))
. Then
|\{s_{H},t_{H}\}\cap A_{H}|=1
since
e_{des}\in\partial A
. Hence, by Proposition
4.1
(2),

(4.4)

\sum_{e^{\prime\prime}\in\partial_{H}A_{H}}th_{H}(e^{\prime\prime})\geq 1.

Then we have

\displaystyle\Per_{A}(E^{(n+1)}_{\preceq e_{des}})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:des(edes)}}{=}\Per_{A}(e_{des}\oslash E(H))

\displaystyle=\sum_{e^{\prime}\in(e_{des}\oslash E(H))\cap\partial A}th(e^{\prime})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Prop }\ref{prop:edgerepgeometry}(3)}{=}\sum_{e^{\prime\prime}\in\partial_{H}A_{H}}th(e_{des}\oslash e^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e^{\prime\prime}\in\partial_{H}A_{H}}th(e_{des})th_{H}(e^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:A_H}}{\geq}th(e_{des})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Per=th}}{=}\Per_{A}(\{e_{des}\}),

establishing (
4.1
).
∎

We define for any subset
A\subseteq\hskip 0.28436pt{\rm V}
the
total perimeter
of
A
to be the number

(4.5)

\Per(A):=\sup_{n\geq 0}\Per_{A}(E^{(n)})\in[0,\infty].

4.4.
A Sobolev Inequality on
st
-Graphs

We now introduce the measures appearing on the left-hand side of the Sobolev inequality (
2.2
). Fix

, and let

such that
G^{(n+1)}=G^{(n)}\cup_{e}H
for some

u_{X}
is the uniform probability measure on
X
. Note that no measure
\mu\in{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)
with norm
\leq 1
can assign to any point mass more than
r^{-1}
, because the mass at that point must be transported a distance at least
r
in any transportation plan. On the other hand,
u_{X}
assigns mass
\frac{1}{n}
to every point. Therefore,
g(\mu)
is indeed a probability measure on
X
.

Furthermore, it is easy to see that

\EMD(g(\mu),g(\nu))=\frac{r}{n}\|\mu-\nu\|_{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt.

Therefore,
H:B_{{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)}\to L_{1}
defined as
H=\frac{n}{r}\cdot F\circ g
is a non-contractive map with
\|H\tau-H\sigma\|_{L_{1}}\leq D\|\tau-\sigma\|_{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt
.

We now utilize the following special case of Bourgain’s Discretization Theorem (see
[
Bou87
]
,
[
GNS12
]
, and
[
Ost13
, Chapter 9]
for the full statement and proof of Bourgain’s Discretization Theorem).

Theorem 5.1

(
[
GNS12
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

For every
\beta>0
and every finite-dimensional Banach space
V
, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for all
\delta
-nets
N_{\delta}
in
B_{V}
, there exists a linear isomorphic embedding of
V
into an
L_{1}
-space with distortion at most
c_{1}(N_{\delta})+\beta
.

The estimates for the mapping
H
imply that
c_{1}(N_{\delta})\leq D
for each
\delta>0
and
\delta
-net
N_{\delta}\subseteq B_{{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)}
. Therefore, applying Theorem
5.1
, we get that
{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)
linearly isomorphically embeds into an
L_{1}
-space with distortion
\leq D+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. Then
[
BL00
, Theorem F.2(i)]
implies that there is some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X)
linearly isomorphically embeds into
\ell_{1}^{m}
with distortion
\leq D+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}
. Hence, we conclude
c_{1,{\rm lin}}({\rm TC}\hskip 0.56917pt(X))\leq D+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}<c_{1}(\EMD(X))+\varepsilon
, completing the proof.

Recall that
M_{2}^{s}
is the subset of
\EMD(\mathbb{R}^{2})
consisting of uniform distributions on
s
-point subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

c_{1}(M_{2}^{s})=\Omega(\log s)
.

Proof.

Let
G_{n}
denote the
n\times n
grid graph, with unit edge lengths. We think of
G_{n}
as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
\ell_{1}
-metric. Let
\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
denote the subset of
\EMD(G_{n})\subseteq\EMD(\mathbb{R}^{2})
consisting of probability measures
\mu
such that
\mu(\{x\})\in(\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,1]
for all
x\in G_{n}
. It is easy to see that the closure of
M_{2}^{s}
in
\EMD(\mathbb{R}^{2})
contains
\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
, and hence that
c_{1}(M_{2}^{s})\geq c_{1}(\tilde{M}_{2}^{s})
. Indeed, let
\mu\in\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose that
\mu
is supported on the points
x_{1}\dots x_{k}\in G_{n}
. For each
x_{i}\in\{x_{1}\dots x_{k}\}
, let
m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[1,s]
such that
\mu(\{x_{i}\})=\frac{m_{i}}{s}
. For each
x_{i}\in\{x_{1}\dots x_{k}\}
, choose “satellite” points
y_{i}^{1},\dots y_{i}^{m_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
x_{i}
such that

•

the collection
\{y_{i}^{j}\}_{i=1,j=1}^{k,m_{i}}
is distinct and

•

\|x_{i}-y_{i}^{j}\|<\varepsilon
for all
i\leq k
and all
j\leq m_{i}
.

Then it is easy to see that the cardinality of the set
\{y_{i}^{j}\}_{i=1,j=1}^{k,m_{i}}
is
s
and that the uniform distribution on this set has EMD-distance to
\mu
at most
\varepsilon
.

It is also easy to see that
\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
is
\frac{2n^{3}}{s}
-dense in
\EMD(G_{n})
. Indeed, given
\mu\in\EMD(G_{n})
, obtain
\mu_{1}
by rounding up each value
\mu(\{x\})
to the nearest number
\mu_{1}(\{x\})
in
(\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,1]
. The value of
\mu
at any of the
n^{2}
points changes by at most
\frac{1}{s}
. However, it is generally the case that we rounded up too much so that
\mu_{1}
is not a probability measure. We know that
\mu_{1}(G_{n})\in(\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z})\cap[1,1+\frac{n^{2}}{s}]
, so that the remainder
R=\mu_{1}(G_{n})-1
belongs to
(\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,\frac{n^{2}}{s}]
, which implies
sR\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,n^{2}]
. Choose any collection of
sR
points
x_{1}\dots x_{sR}\in G_{n}
, and define
\mu_{2}
by
\mu_{2}(\{x\})=\mu_{1}(\{x\})-\frac{1}{s}
for
x\in\{x_{1},\dots x_{sR}\}
and
\mu_{2}(\{x\})=\mu_{1}(\{x\})
for
x\not\in\{x_{1},\dots x_{sR}\}
. Then we have that

•

\mu_{2}
takes values in
(\frac{1}{s}\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,1]
,

•

\mu_{2}
is a probability measure, and

•

|\mu(\{x\})-\mu_{2}(\{x\})|\leq\frac{1}{s}
for any
x\in G_{n}
.

These imply that
\mu_{2}\in\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
and
\|\mu-\mu_{2}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{s}
. Then we use the estimate
\|\mu-\mu_{2}\|_{\TC}\leq{\rm diam}\hskip 0.56917pt(G_{n})\|\mu-\mu_{s}\|_{\rm TV}\leq 2n\frac{n^{2}}{s}
to conclude
\frac{2n^{3}}{s}
-density.

Let
N_{\delta}
be a maximal
\delta
-separated subset of
B_{\TC(G_{n})}
, with
\delta>0
to be chosen later. By the formula (
5.1
), there exists a scaled isometric embedding
g:B_{\TC(G_{n})}\hookrightarrow\EMD(G_{n})
with scaling factor
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
. Hence,
g(N_{\delta})
is a maximal
\frac{\delta}{n^{2}}
-separated subset of
g(B_{\TC(G_{n})})
. Let
\pi:g(N_{\delta})\to\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
be a nearest neighbor projection. Since
\tilde{M}_{2}^{s}
is
\frac{2n^{3}}{s}
-dense in
\EMD(G_{n})
and since
g(N_{\delta})
is
\frac{\delta}{n^{2}}
-separated, if we assume that
\frac{2n^{3}}{s}\leq\frac{1}{3}\frac{\delta}{n^{2}}
, then
\pi
will be a 6-biLipschitz embedding. Towards this end, we take
\delta=\frac{6n^{5}}{s}
and consequently get
c_{1}(\tilde{M}_{2}^{s})\geq\frac{1}{6}c_{1}(g(N_{\delta}))=\frac{1}{6}c_{1}(N_{\delta})
. By
[
GNS12
, Corollary 1.4]
, if
\frac{6n^{5}}{s}=\delta=O(\frac{1}{n^{5}})
, then we have that
c_{1}(N_{\delta})\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{1}(\TC(G_{n}))
, which equals
\Omega(\log n)
by Theorem
1.1
. Put another way, if
s=\Omega(n^{10})
, then
c_{1}(N_{\delta})=\Omega(\log n)
. In summary, if
s=\Omega(n^{10})
, then

c_{1}(M_{2}^{s})\geq c_{1}(\tilde{M}_{2}^{s})\geq\frac{1}{6}c_{1}(g(N_{\delta}))=\frac{1}{6}c_{1}(N_{\delta})=\Omega(\log n).

This implies
c_{1}(M_{2}^{s})=\Omega(\log s)
.
∎
