Findings Interpretation Concerns Regarding the Emulated Target Trial of SGLT2 Inhibitors vs GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in Psoriatic Arthritis [Response to Letter]
Authors: Fu-Shun Yen, Shiow-Ing Wang, Chii-Min Hwu, Kai-Yang Chen, Chih-Cheng Hsu, James Cheng-Chung Wei
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We sincerely thank Dr. Anastasios Lymperopoulos and Madyson G. Leiker for their interest in our study and for their thoughtful comments regarding the potential mechanisms underlying the effects of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) on psoriatic arthritis (PsA).1,2 We appreciate the additional mechanistic evidence they have highlighted, particularly regarding the direct immunomodulatory and local joint effects of GLP-1 RAs. These perspectives complement our discussion and provide a more balanced understanding of the potential biological effects of both drug classes on inflammatory arthritis and PsA.

We agree that the mechanistic interpretation of our findings should be approached cautiously. Our study was an observational cohort study using real-world data and was not designed to investigate molecular or cellular mechanisms. Accordingly, the biological pathways discussed in our article were proposed primarily to provide potential explanations for the observed epidemiological association rather than to establish biological causality. Mechanisms less directly related to our findings may therefore not have been discussed in detail. Moreover, compared with GLP-1 RAs, relatively limited experimental evidence is currently available regarding the direct effects of SGLT2is on inflammatory arthritis and PsA, which constrained our ability to provide equally comprehensive mechanistic comparisons between the two drug classes.3

We therefore welcome the additional evidence presented by Lymperopoulos and Leiker regarding the potential anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1 RAs, including modulation of immune cells and the IL-23/IL-17 pathway, as well as their potential direct effects on chondrocytes and fibroblast-like synoviocytes.1 These observations emphasize that the effects of GLP-1 RAs on inflammatory arthritis may extend beyond weight reduction and systemic metabolic improvement.

At the same time, caution is warranted when extrapolating findings from cellular, tissue, or animal studies to clinical outcomes in humans.4 The biological effects observed in experimental models may not necessarily translate into differences in incident PsA at the population level.5 Conversely, an epidemiological association observed in a real-world database cannot establish the underlying biological mechanism or demonstrate a direct pharmacological effect.6 Our finding that SGLT2i initiation was associated with a lower risk of incident PsA than GLP-1 RA initiation should therefore be interpreted as a comparative clinical association rather than evidence that SGLT2is have intrinsically superior anti-inflammatory or joint-protective properties.

Importantly, evidence regarding the effects of both SGLT2is and GLP-1 RAs on autoimmune diseases, inflammatory arthritis, and PsA remains limited.7–10 We hope that our findings, together with the mechanistic considerations raised by Lymperopoulos and Leiker, will stimulate further investigation. Future translational and prospective studies integrating clinical outcomes with immunological, metabolic, synovial, and molecular assessments are needed to clarify whether and how these agents influence the development and progression of PsA and other immune-mediated inflammatory diseases.

We again thank Lymperopoulos and Leiker for their constructive comments, which broaden the mechanistic perspective of our findings and contribute to a more balanced interpretation of the potential roles of SGLT2is and GLP-1 RAs in PsA.
