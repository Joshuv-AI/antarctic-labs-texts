Multimodal Transformer Fusion of PTR‐ToF‐MS Volatiles and Targeted Non‐Volatile Metabolites Enables Data‐Driven Grading of Baimudan White Tea
Authors: Dandan Zhang, Suk‐Hwan Hong, Xiaojing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tea quality ultimately reflects sensory attributes that arise from chemical composition and processing. Baimudan white tea is commercially graded into Special and Grades I–III under the national standard GB/T 22291‐2017 [1, 2], and previous studies have reported grade‐associated differences in both volatile fingerprints and non‐volatile metabolite profiles [3, 4, 5]. Sensory panels remain the practical standard for grade assignment, but panel‐based evaluation can be time‐consuming and may vary with panel experience and testing conditions [6]. This motivates complementary, instrument‐based approaches that quantify grade‐relevant chemical signatures to support standardized quality control.

Instrumental analysis can quantify chemical signatures linked to aroma and taste. Proton‐transfer‐reaction time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (PTR‐ToF‐MS) enables rapid headspace VOC fingerprinting relevant to white tea aroma chemistry [7, 8, 9, 10]. In parallel, high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and amino acid analysis quantify key non‐volatile metabolites (catechins, caffeine, amino acids and soluble sugars) that contribute to taste and mouthfeel [11]. Although both volatile and non‐volatile profiles differ across Baimudan grades [12, 13], most published studies analyze each modality separately and rely on linear chemometrics or unimodal models. As a result, potential nonlinear interactions between aroma‐ and taste‐related chemistry may be underutilized for robust grade prediction.

Multimodal data fusion aims to integrate complementary chemical information from heterogeneous sources. In food science applications, combining spectroscopy, chromatographic data, and other analytical modalities can improve classification accuracy and support more comprehensive interpretation [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Deep learning further enables the modeling of nonlinear relationships within high‐dimensional chemical datasets with limited manual feature engineering [21, 22, 23]. Hyperspectral imaging–based deep‐learning models have also been applied to tea cultivar classification and quality prediction tasks [24, 25, 26]. However, studies that jointly model PTR‐ToF‐MS VOC fingerprints and targeted non‐volatile metabolite measurements for Baimudan grade prediction remain limited. This gap motivates a unified multimodal framework that can preserve within‐modality structure while learning cross‐modality interactions.

In this study, we developed a two‐stream multimodal Transformer that integrates volatile profiles obtained by PTR‐ToF‐MS with targeted non‐volatile metabolite concentrations quantified by HPLC and amino acid analysis to predict the official grades of Baimudan white tea. On an independent prediction set, the model achieved high predictive performance (accuracy = 0.958; macro‐F1 = 0.958), and repeated stratified five‐fold cross‐validation within the training set demonstrated stable performance across splits. We further combined attention‐based fusion with SHAP‐based interpretation to identify grade‐discriminative chemical drivers spanning aroma VOCs, amino acids, soluble sugars, and polyphenol/caffeine‐related markers. These results show that integrating PTR‐ToF‐MS VOC fingerprints with targeted non‐volatiles via a two‐stream Transformer can support instrument‐assisted Baimudan grading while retaining chemically interpretable drivers.

Materials and Methods

Tea Samples and Grading

A total of 120 Baimudan white tea samples were obtained from Fujian Tea Import and Export Co. Ltd. (Fuzhou, Fujian, China). The samples originated from core white tea production areas in Fujian Province and were produced from the same tea cultivar, Camellia sinensis cv. Fuding Dabai. All samples were harvested during the spring season of 2023 to minimize varietal, seasonal, and agronomic variability.

According to the Chinese national standard GB/T 22291‐2017, the samples were classified by the supplier into four commercial grades: Special, Grade I, Grade II, and Grade III (30 samples per grade). This grading standard evaluates appearance, aroma, liquor color, taste, and infused‐leaf characteristics.

After acquisition, the samples were vacuum‐sealed in aluminum foil bags and stored at −20°C to minimize oxidative and volatile degradation. Prior to instrumental analysis, the samples were equilibrated to room temperature (approximately 22°C) for 30 min. Moisture content was maintained within the range of 3%–5% to ensure consistency in subsequent chemical measurements.

Sensory‐Related Archival Data and Ethical Statement

No additional sensory panel or consumer testing was conducted for this study. The sensory subscores used in Figure S5 were provided by the supplier as archival records from routine commercial grading performed according to GB/T 22291‐2017.

These archival records contained no personal identifiers. Accordingly, the use of these sensory‐related archival records was exempt from ethics committee approval, and informed consent was not required.

PTR‐ToF‐MS Analysis of Volatile Profiles

Headspace VOC measurements of dry tea samples were conducted using a PTR‐ToF‐MS 1000 mass spectrometer (Ionicon Analytik GmbH, Innsbruck, Austria). In this instrument, trace volatile compounds released from the tea leaves undergo non‐dissociative proton transfer from H3O+ reagent ions, forming protonated molecular ions [M + H]+, which are subsequently detected in the time‐of‐flight mass analyzer. A small proportion of fragment ions may also be generated under these conditions.

The instrumental conditions of the drift tube were set as follows: drift voltage of 600 V, drift temperature of 60°C, and drift pressure of 2.3 mbar, yielding an E/N value of approximately 130 Td. The drift tube flow was maintained at ~50 sccm, and mass spectra were acquired across the m/z 33–300 range with a resolving power of ~5000. For each measurement, 2.0 g of intact dry tea leaves were placed into 20‐mL crimp‐top headspace vials and equilibrated at 80°C for 20 min to facilitate reproducible VOC release. Immediately after equilibration, the vial headspace was introduced into the drift tube through a heated transfer line (80°C), and each sample was scanned for 30 s. Three independent replicates were prepared for each tea sample, and each replicate was measured in triplicate. The averaged spectrum was used for data analysis. Replicate spectra were averaged to yield a single volatile feature vector per tea sample (n = 120 total samples).

Raw spectra were subjected to baseline correction, dead‐time adjustment, peak alignment, and total ion current (TIC) normalization. All calibrated m/z channels within the acquisition range were retained to preserve full spectral information for downstream deep‐learning analysis. Tentative VOC identifications were assigned by comparing the observed m/z values with theoretical [M + H] + values derived from molecular formulas and previously reported PTR‐MS databases. The resulting assignments are summarized in (Table S5).

Analysis of Non‐Volatile Compounds

Non‐volatile metabolites, including amino acids, catechins, caffeine, flavonols, and soluble sugars, were quantified to construct the non‐volatile feature vector for each sample. Tea infusions were prepared by steeping 2.0 g of leaves in 100 mL of boiling deionized water for 5 min, followed by filtration through a 0.45‐μm membrane. Amino Acids

Free amino acids (e.g., theanine, proline, and valine) were quantified using an automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi L8900 series) following GB/T 23193‐2008. Results were expressed as mg/g dry weight.

Catechins, Caffeine, and Flavonols

Catechins (EGCG, ECG, and EGC), caffeine, and flavonols (quercetin, myricetin, and kaempferol) were quantified using HPLC according to GB/T 8313‐2018. Analyses were performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a diode‐array detector and a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). A gradient elution was applied using water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). Detection was conducted at 278 nm with external calibration curves (R 2 > 0.99).

Soluble Sugars

Soluble sugars were quantified using the anthrone colorimetric method, a standard procedure for determining soluble sugar content in tea.

Multimodal Deep Learning Fusion Model

To model nonlinear interactions between volatile features and targeted non‐volatile metabolites, a Transformer‐based multimodal deep learning framework was constructed (Figure 1). Each chemical modality was independently encoded using a dedicated attention‐based encoder stream and subsequently fused for final grade prediction.

Multimodal Transformer architecture for Baimudan white tea quality grading.

Input Representation

Each sample was represented by two standardized chemical feature vectors: a volatile feature vector derived from PTR‐ToF‐MS full ion‐intensity profiles (268 dimensions) and a non‐volatile metabolite vector (34 dimensions) comprising quantified concentrations of amino acids, catechins, caffeine, flavonols, and soluble sugars.

All inputs were z‐score normalized using parameters from the training set.

Model Architecture

Each modality was processed through an independent Transformer encoder stream. Input vectors were first projected into a 64‐dimensional latent space using learnable linear embeddings with sinusoidal positional encoding. Each encoder consisted of two Transformer layers (8 attention heads and 128‐unit feedforward networks), with dropout (0.1) and layer normalization applied throughout.

The output representations from the two modality‐specific encoders were aggregated by mean pooling and concatenated to generate a unified multimodal chemical embedding.

The fused embedding was passed through a fully connected layer (128 ReLU units), followed by a softmax classifier predicting four grade categories: Special, Grade I, Grade II, and Grade III. Model optimization used the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001) with categorical cross‐entropy loss. Early stopping (patience = 10) was applied based on validation loss on an internal validation split of the training set. The data splitting and evaluation strategy are described in Section 2.5.3.

Model Training and Evaluation

To evaluate generalization performance under limited sample size, the dataset (n = 120; 30 samples per grade) was first stratified into a training set (n = 96; 24 samples per grade) and an independent prediction set (n = 24; 6 samples per grade). The prediction set was completely held out and not used for model training, hyperparameter selection, early stopping, or data normalization.

Within the training set (n = 96), stratified five‐fold cross‐validation was performed with three repeats (15 validation runs in total) to assess robustness to different data splits. In each fold, 80% of the training samples were used for model fitting and 20% for validation. Models were trained for a fixed number of epochs, and fold‐wise accuracy and macro‐F1 scores were recorded to quantify training‐set stability.

After cross‐validation, the final model was retrained on the full training set (n = 96) using the selected hyperparameters and evaluated exclusively on the independent prediction set (n = 24). Classification performance was quantified using accuracy, precision, recall, macro‐F1 score, and confusion matrix.

For cross‐validation, z‐score normalization parameters were fitted on the training portion of each fold and applied to the corresponding validation fold to avoid information leakage. Because the training set size (n = 96) is not divisible by five, validation folds contained 19 or 20 samples (difference ≤ 1) under stratified splitting.

Embedding Visualization

To visualize how the fused multimodal representation separates tea grades, the concatenated fused embedding vectors (output of the fusion module) for all samples were projected into two dimensions using t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE, scikit‐learn). The visualization used perplexity = 20, n_iter = 600, method = “exact,” and random seed = 7. Points were colored by the ground‐truth grade; misclassified samples were marked based on the corresponding model predictions for the training and prediction splits, respectively. The t‐SNE axes are arbitrary and unitless.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.9.

Results and Discussion

Model Training and Convergence

The training dynamics of the multimodal Transformer model are illustrated in Figure 2. During the first 15 epochs, both training and validation loss decreased sharply, indicating effective feature learning across the two chemical modalities. The validation loss reached a plateau near epoch 20, while the training loss continued to decline modestly thereafter. Early stopping was triggered at epoch 22 after 10 epochs without further improvement in validation loss, preventing overfitting and ensuring stable convergence.

Training convergence and cross‐validation robustness of the multimodal Transformer model. (A) Training and validation loss curves over epochs. (B) Repeated stratified five‐fold cross‐validation accuracy within the training set (three repeats). Each line represents one repeat; each point represents one fold.

This convergence behavior suggests stable optimization and limited overfitting during training. The final validation loss (≈0.19) remained close to the training loss, indicating no obvious overfitting under the applied early‐stopping strategy. The Transformer encoder design—featuring two self‐attention layers per modality with 8 attention heads and 128‐unit feedforward networks—provided sufficient depth to model nonlinear interactions between volatile and non‐volatile chemical features.

As shown in Figure 2A, the smooth loss curves and small train–validation gap and low, stable validation loss indicate stable optimization prior to downstream evaluation.

Stratified five‐fold cross‐validation within the training set showed broadly consistent performance across folds (Figure 2B and Table S1). Across 15 validation runs (3 repeats × 5 folds), the model achieved an average accuracy of 0.979 ± 0.026 and macro‐F1 of 0.977 ± 0.028, suggesting that performance was not driven by a single favorable partition.

Overall Classification Performance

Model performance was evaluated on the independent prediction set (n = 24; 6 samples per grade), which was held out during training and model selection. The multimodal Transformer achieved an overall accuracy of 0.958 (23/24) and a macro‐averaged F1 score of 0.958 (Figure 3A and Table 2). Only one Special‐grade sample was predicted as Grade I, while all samples from Grade I, Grade II, and Grade III were correctly classified. Comparative results against unimodal baselines and early fusion are reported in Table S2. Conceptually, the superior performance of the two‐stream multimodal model may be attributed to its ability to preserve modality‐specific information before fusion. PTR‐ToF‐MS volatile fingerprints and targeted non‐volatile metabolite profiles differ in chemical meaning, dimensionality, and feature structure; therefore, separate encoder streams can learn aroma‐related and taste/metabolite‐related discriminatory patterns within each modality, while the fusion layer captures complementary cross‐modal interactions. In contrast, volatile‐only and non‐volatile‐only baselines use only partial chemical information, whereas early fusion may combine heterogeneous variables before modality‐specific representations are fully learned.

Feature Attribution and Interpretability

To elucidate how the multimodal Transformer model distinguished tea grades, we combined SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), modality‐specific self‐attention heatmaps, and grade‐wise chemical visualization. Figure 4A summarizes the top‐20 chemical variables ranked by mean absolute SHAP value (mean|SHAP|), highlighting that both volatile markers (PTR‐ToF‐MS m/z features) and non‐volatile metabolites contributed to grading decisions. Key discriminative signals spanned aroma‐related volatile ions (putatively annotated to phenylacetaldehyde, ethyl acetate, γ‐nonalactone, nonanal, linalool, and linalool oxides) as well as taste‐related non‐volatiles (e.g., valine, total soluble sugars, ECG, EGCG, and caffeine). Representative literature supporting the sensory/taste assignments of these markers is summarized in Table 3, while the grade‐wise statistics are reported in Table S3. Consistent with Table S3 and the four‐ring mechanistic summary (Figure 4B), high‐grade teas (Special + Grade I) were characterized by higher levels of floral/sweet‐associated VOC ions and sweet/umami‐related non‐volatiles, whereas low‐grade teas (Grade II + Grade III) showed enrichment of green/woody VOC ions (e.g., 1‐octen‐3‐ol, cis‐3‐hexen‐1‐ol, and [E]‐2‐hexenal) and selected flavonol‐related variables (e.g., kaempferol). Additional aroma‐associated VOC ions within the top‐20 set included benzaldehyde, phenylethyl alcohol, methyl salicylate, ionone‐like ions, and furan‐related signals (m/z 69) (Tables 3 and S3). Notably, galloylated catechins (EGCG/ECG) and caffeine also ranked among the top features and were higher in the high‐grade group in this dataset (Table S3). Because these compounds are commonly associated with bitterness/astringency, this pattern may reflect batch‐specific raw‐material or processing characteristics and could be sensorially moderated by concurrently elevated amino acids and soluble sugars.

These SHAP‐derived patterns were consistent with grade‐wise chemical fingerprints. Radar profiles of representative volatile and non‐volatile markers (Figure S1) highlighted distinct enrichment trends across grades. Spearman correlation analysis further linked the top chemical markers with sensory Aroma and Taste scores, while also revealing strong collinearity among sensory subscores within the evaluation system (Figure S5). Because these sensory subscores were supplier‐provided archival records from routine commercial grading rather than results from an independently designed sensory panel in this study, the sensory‐correlation analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and supportive. The observed associations were used to provide contextual interpretation of model‐highlighted chemical features and should not be regarded as causal evidence or definitive sensory markers. Further targeted sensory‐directed validation would be required to confirm the direct contribution of individual volatile or non‐volatile markers to specific sensory attributes. For example, high‐grade teas tended to show higher signals for floral/sweet‐associated putative VOC markers (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde and lactone/ester‐related features such as γ‐nonalactone and ethyl acetate), whereas low‐grade teas showed relatively higher levels of green‐note/woody putative VOC markers (e.g., 1‐octen‐3‐ol, cis‐3‐hexen‐1‐ol, and [E]‐2‐hexenal). At the non‐volatile level, amino acids and total soluble sugars contributed strongly to high‐grade differentiation, while flavonol‐related variables (e.g., kaempferol) were more associated with lower‐grade groups (Table S3). Sensory/taste annotations and representative literature support for these markers are summarized in Table 3.

Attention heatmaps from the Transformer encoders (Figure S2) provided additional mechanistic insight. The volatile encoder showed localized attention on subsets of VOC‐related m/z channels, including ions putatively corresponding to aldehyde‐, monoterpene‐related, and lactone/ester markers (Table 3), whereas the non‐volatile encoder highlighted amino acids, catechins, and sugar‐related indices. This attention localization suggests that the model captured chemically meaningful relationships rather than spurious patterns.

Prediction confidence was assessed using the top‐1 probability and Shannon entropy (Table S4 and Figure S3). Model probability calibration was additionally evaluated using a reliability diagram, indicating that predicted top‐1 probabilities were broadly well aligned with empirical accuracy (Figure S4). Across all 120 samples, most predictions were made with high confidence (79% showed top‐1 probability > 0.95), whereas misclassified samples exhibited reduced confidence and elevated entropy, supporting the use of probability/entropy thresholds for practical screening or rejection in an automated grading pipeline. Such confidence estimates may enable the implementation of reject‐option strategies in automated grading scenarios.

Conclusions

This study developed a multimodal Transformer model that integrates volatile and non‐volatile chemical data to predict the official quality grades of Baimudan white tea. In benchmark comparisons, the multimodal fusion model outperformed unimodal and early‐fusion baselines (Table S2). The model achieved 0.958 accuracy and 0.958 macro‐F1 on an independent prediction set (n = 24), while stratified five‐fold cross‐validation within the training set demonstrated stable performance across data partitions, supporting the robustness of the multimodal framework.

SHAP‐based feature attribution and attention visualization indicated that grading decisions were grounded in chemically meaningful signatures. High‐grade teas were associated with floral/sweet aroma markers (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde and lactone/ester‐related VOC features) together with sweet/umami‐related non‐volatiles such as amino acids and soluble sugars, whereas low‐grade teas showed enrichment of green/woody volatiles and selected flavonol‐related markers (e.g., kaempferol). Overall, these attribution results are consistent with literature‐supported sensory descriptors and supplier‐provided sensory grading records: high‐grade samples were enriched in putative floral/sweet VOC ions and sweet/umami‐related amino acids and soluble sugars, whereas lower grades showed higher green/woody VOC ions and kaempferol (Table S3). These sensory‐related interpretations should be regarded as exploratory associations rather than causal sensory validation.

Model confidence analysis further supports potential practical deployment: most samples were predicted with high top‐1 probabilities, whereas ambiguous or misclassified cases exhibited reduced confidence and elevated entropy. This suggests that probability‐ or entropy‐based rejection rules could be considered for automated screening. Nevertheless, the practical scope of the proposed framework should be interpreted within the boundaries of the current study design. Specifically, the dataset comprised 120 Baimudan samples collected from one harvest period, namely the spring 2023 harvest. Therefore, broader external validation using independent samples from additional harvest years, producers, geographic origins, and processing batches will be necessary to further evaluate model generalizability, transferability, and robustness. Within these current boundaries, the multimodal Transformer provides an interpretable way to integrate PTR‐ToF‐MS VOC fingerprints with targeted non‐volatile measurements for instrument‐assisted grade prediction of Baimudan white tea.

Data S1: Supporting Information

Figure S1: Grade‐specific radar fingerprints of representative volatile and non‐volatile chemical markers. Radar plots illustrate the grade‐wise mean profiles of selected volatile compounds (top row) and non‐volatile constituents (bottom row) across the four official grades of Baimudan white tea (Special, Grade I, Grade II, and Grade III). Each line represents the profile of a specific grade, and each radial axis represents an individual chemical marker (compound names labeled around the perimeter).For each marker, values were normalized across the four grades prior to visualization (per‐marker normalization), allowing relative enrichment patterns to be compared within each compound rather than absolute concentration differences across compounds. Larger radii therefore indicate that a given marker is relatively higher in that grade compared with the other grades. Abbreviations: ECG, epicatechin gallate; EGCG, epigallocatechin gallate.

Figure S2: Modality‐specific self‐attention maps of the multimodal Transformer. (A) Attention matrix of the volatile‐profile encoder (m/z features as tokens). (B) Attention matrix of the non‐volatile encoder (quantified compounds and summary indices as tokens). Color intensity denotes relative attention strength between feature pairs. The maps correspond to a representative sample and are averaged across attention heads. Attention weights reflect learned dependency structure within each modality and do not imply direct biochemical causation.

Figure S3: Prediction confidence and uncertainty stratified by correctness. (A) Distribution of the top‑1 predicted probability (model confidence) for correctly classified versus misclassified samples. (B) Distribution of the Shannon entropy of the predicted class‐probability vector (model uncertainty) for correctly classified versus misclassified samples. Each point denotes one sample prediction; boxplots summarize the median and interquartile range. As expected, correct predictions tend to show higher confidence and lower entropy, whereas misclassifications are associated with reduced confidence and increased uncertainty, supporting the use of probability/entropy‐based thresholds for practical screening or rejection in an automated grading pipeline (Table S4 provides the sample‐level values).

Figure S4: Calibration of top‑1 prediction confidence (reliability diagram).A reliability diagram was used to evaluate whether the model's top‑1 predicted probability (confidence) matches the empirical accuracy. Samples were binned by confidence (e.g., 10 bins on [0,1]); each point shows the mean confidence (x‑axis) and the fraction of correct predictions within that bin (y‑axis). The dashed diagonal indicates perfect calibration (confidence = accuracy). Interpretation: points below the diagonal indicate over‑confidence (predicted probabilities are too high), while points above the diagonal indicate under‑confidence.

Figure S5: Chemistry–sensory association and sensory‐score collinearity (A) Spearman rank correlations (ρ) between the model‐identified top chemical features (Top‑20 markers) and sensory Aroma and Taste scores. (B) Pairwise Spearman correlations among sensory subscores (Appearance, Aroma, Taste, Infusion color, Infused leaf, and Weighted score), illustrating collinearity within the sensory evaluation system. Asterisks indicate statistical significance after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH‑FDR) correction (q < 0.05).Correlations reflect associations (often driven by grade‐related covariation) and do not imply causation. Sensory subscores were generated by Fujian Tea Import and Export Co. Ltd. as part of routine commercial grading according to GB/T 22291‐2017; no additional sensory evaluation was conducted for this study. As no human participants were recruited and no personal data were collected, this analysis was exempt from ethics committee approval. Note: This sensory‐correlation analysis was conducted as an exploratory analysis to support interpretation of the associations between model‐highlighted chemical features and supplier‐provided sensory subscores. The observed correlations do not establish causal relationships and should not be considered definitive sensory markers without further targeted sensory validation.

Figure S6: Grade‐wise mean Z‐scores of the top‐20 discriminative chemical features. Heatmap summarizing the mean feature‐wise Z‐scores (standardized across all samples for each feature) for the top‐20 chemical variables selected for model interpretation. Columns represent the four commercial grades (Special, Grade I, Grade II, Grade III), and rows represent chemical features (volatile markers and non‐volatile constituents). Warm colors indicate relatively higher‐than‐average abundance for a given grade, whereas cool colors indicate relatively lower‐than‐average abundance. The up/down arrows denote the expected directionality (enriched in high‐grade or low‐grade samples) consistent with the four‐ring mechanistic summary.
