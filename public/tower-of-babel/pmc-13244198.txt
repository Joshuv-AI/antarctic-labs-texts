Pulmonary rehabilitation is a cornerstone of chronic respiratory disease (CRD) management. Recommended by all international guidelines, it reliably improves physical endurance, decreases breathlessness and enhances health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [1–4]. Nevertheless, one of the most persistent paradoxes remains unresolved: despite receiving an intervention of proven efficacy, a substantial proportion of patients – often between a third and half – fail to achieve clinically meaningful benefit [5–7]. This phenomenon, termed “nonresponse”, has been documented repeatedly across a range of functional outcomes, patient-reported measures and even peripheral muscle strength [8, 9]. Qualitative research has further demonstrated that nonresponse is not a neutral outcome: patients who do not improve report symptoms of distress, hopelessness and disengagement from their rehabilitation journey [10].

Conventionally, this variability has been ascribed to factors such as disease burden, comorbidities or baseline functional capacity. Lung function accounts for only a small fraction of outcome variance and improvements in exercise performance do not necessarily translate into reduced symptoms or greater wellbeing [11]. Recent studies suggest that identifying and addressing extrapulmonary treatable traits – such as fatigue, anxiety, depression, deconditioning and impaired balance – may increase the likelihood of a positive response to pulmonary rehabilitation, including achieving minimal important differences in key clinical outcomes [12].

However, this line of research has only partially addressed the broader behavioural and psychological landscape. While concepts such as fear of breathlessness, avoidance patterns, psychological distress and coping responses are increasingly recognised in CRD management, they have rarely been investigated systematically as determinants of nonresponse [10, 13]. This discrepancy hinders our capacity to comprehend the reasons behind patients’ significant variations in their engagement with pulmonary rehabilitation and why standardised programmes persist in yielding heterogeneous outcomes.

In this context, the study by Kuss et al. [14], published in this issue of ERJ Open Research, provides a critical piece of the puzzle by examining whether personality traits may help explain nonresponse to pulmonary rehabilitation in individuals with CRDs. In a large, multicentre cohort of 719 individuals undergoing inpatient pulmonary rehabilitation, the authors investigated whether specific personality traits and their threshold scores could predict nonresponse to key clinical outcomes of pulmonary rehabilitation, including HRQoL, exercise capacity and dyspnoea. Their findings indicate that higher neuroticism and lower agreeableness are associated with an increased likelihood of nonresponse in HRQoL. Crucially, however, no threshold value for any trait showed sufficient accuracy to distinguish responders from nonresponders. This clarifies two key points: personality traits do shape variability in pulmonary rehabilitation outcomes, but they cannot serve as categorical predictors or screening tools. Their clinical relevance lies instead in the continuous dispositional patterns that influence how patients engage with rehabilitation – shaping expectations, emotional responses and ultimately perceived benefit.

Another significant line of research has examined clinical phenotypes associated with differential response to pulmonary rehabilitation. In a cohort of 835 patients with COPD, Al Chikhanie et al. [15] used hierarchical clustering to identify four subgroups based on demographics, lung function, oxygen requirements and walking performance. The response rates exhibited marked variation across the clusters. Individuals who were older, more severely affected, oxygen-dependent and with very low baseline 6-min walking test distance were substantially more likely to be nonresponders. The findings demonstrate that patient heterogeneity is structured and that specific clinical profiles are systematically less likely to benefit from pulmonary rehabilitation.

However, this approach also reveals a major limitation. Even within these clinical clusters, nonresponse remained common and no phenotype reliably predicted improvement. This reinforces that clinical stratification alone cannot explain why pulmonary rehabilitation benefits some individuals but not others. Dimensions beyond disease severity – such as psychological dispositions, coping patterns, beliefs and expectations – may be necessary to understand and personalise the rehabilitative process.

Taken together, these findings underscore a critical point: pulmonary rehabilitation is effective but not equally effective for everyone. Outcome variability reflects not a failure of the intervention but differences in how well the programme aligns with individual patients. This alignment depends not only on lung physiology and clinical parameters, but also on the psychological dispositions that shape how patients perceive breathlessness, engage with effort, regulate emotion and adopt behavioural change.

Recognising these dispositional differences is not about labelling patients or excluding them from treatment. Instead, it signifies the initial phase of a more precise, person-centred model of pulmonary rehabilitation, wherein individual characteristics significantly influence the delivery of rehabilitation, thereby enhancing the probability of patient responders.

Beyond traditional psychosocial models: lessons from musculoskeletal rehabilitation

Insights from musculoskeletal rehabilitation illustrate a clear parallel. Psychosocial “yellow flags” – such as fear, anxiety and catastrophising – have long been used as screening tools to identify patients at risk of poor recovery, yet they capture only a narrow dimension of the psychological factors that shape engagement with rehabilitation. A recent review has therefore argued for a broader conceptual framework, drawing on personality psychology and the Big Five model. Importantly, these traits are not proposed as predictive thresholds or screening instruments; rather, they offer a richer, continuous lens through which to understand the dispositional tendencies that influence motivation, emotional regulation and adherence, and through which rehabilitation can be more effectively tailored [16].

Pulmonary rehabilitation now stands on the threshold of a similar evolution. Chronic breathlessness, like chronic pain, is a multidimensional experience shaped not only by impaired physiology but by prior learning, cognitive appraisal and affective interpretation. Thus, personality offers a plausible and empirically grounded explanation for why patients with similar lung function may experience and respond to pulmonary rehabilitation in markedly different ways.

Toward personality phenotyping: a new frontier for personalised pulmonary rehabilitation

What recent research makes increasingly clear is that personality is not merely a “psychological factor”: it may represent a clinically meaningful rehabilitation phenotype. In contrast to traditional yellow flags, which typically refer to specific psychological risk factors, personality traits exert a broader influence across multiple processes involved in rehabilitation. Personality traits shape how patients perceive breathlessness, anticipate difficulty, engage with exercise, adhere to home routines, respond to feedback and maintain behavioural change [17–19]. These traits act as stable – but modifiable – patterns of cognitive, emotional and behavioural functioning that directly influence the mechanisms through which pulmonary rehabilitation exerts its benefits [20].

This perspective reframes the role of personality entirely. Importantly, the aim is not to classify patients into personality “types” – something the present study shows is neither feasible nor desirable – but to recognise continuous patterns that inform how rehabilitation can be individually optimised. As such, personality phenotyping offers a structured, evidence-based way to identify distinct engagement profiles – those who need more emotional scaffolding, those who require behavioural structure, those who respond best to collaborative goal setting and those who benefit from strategies that recalibrate maladaptive expectations of breathlessness.

In a field searching for ways to personalise care, personality offers a clinically meaningful lens – not for stratifying patients into categories, which this study clearly shows is not feasible, but for understanding continuous dispositional tendencies that can guide how rehabilitation is delivered.

Clinical implications: what could change now?

Personalisation in pulmonary rehabilitation does not require redesigning programmes but delivering the same intervention calibrated to psychological dispositions that influence engagement and response. Growing evidence suggests that psychological characteristics, including personality and affective profiles, influence functional outcomes and engagement in pulmonary rehabilitation and related respiratory conditions [21–23]. From predictors to modifiers: a vision for future research

Personality traits have long been considered stable and, therefore, outside the clinician's sphere of influence. However, emerging evidence, including longitudinal research in CRD, shows that learning, therapeutic experiences and behavioural changes such as sustained physical activity, can slow or even reverse declines in key traits like conscientiousness and extraversion [24]. More broadly, research in personality psychology indicates that personality traits are amenable to change through targeted interventions and therapeutic experiences [25, 26], and that such changes are associated with favourable outcomes, including increases in life satisfaction [27]. These findings raise the possibility that pulmonary rehabilitation itself – or adjunct behavioural interventions embedded within it – could contribute to beneficial personality change; for example, by reducing neuroticism or strengthening conscientiousness, potentially lowering the risk of nonresponse. This reframes the clinical challenge: the goal should not be merely to predict nonresponse, but to prevent it.

A call to action

If pulmonary rehabilitation is to realise its full potential, the field must integrate physiological, psychological and dispositional factors into a coherent personalised model of care – a necessary evolution, not a weakness. The lungs may limit breath but the person decides how breath is lived. Recognising this truth is the path toward more responsive, equitable and humane pulmonary rehabilitation.

The study by Kuss et al. [14] reminds us that pulmonary rehabilitation is not delivered to lungs alone but to people whose experiences, dispositions and expectations matter. Integrating personality into pulmonary rehabilitation is not a luxury: it is a step toward more effective, humane and personalised respiratory care.
