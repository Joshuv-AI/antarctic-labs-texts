Blood eosinophil count and clinical characteristics according to etiology in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Jeong Hyeon Seong, Hee-Young Yoon, Ye Jin Lee, Hyonsoo Joo, Joo Hun Park, Seong Yong Lim, Sang Yeub Lee, Hyoung Kyu Yoon, Kwang Ha Yoo, Chin Kook Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous condition with multiple etiologies. We evaluated whether clinical characteristics differ according to COPD etiology.MethodsPatients with infection as the sole etiology were classified as the infection-related group (COPD-I, Group I), whereas those with any smoking-related etiology were assigned to the smoking-related group (COPD-C, Group C).ResultsOf 873 patients included, 41 were classified as Group I and 832 as Group C. A significantly higher proportion of patients in Group I had a blood eosinophil count < 100 cells/µL compared to Group C (44.4% vs. 27.8%; p = 0.031). The inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) prescription rate was lower in Group I compared to Group C (25.7% vs. 41.5%; p = 0.063). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that Group I was independently associated with a lower likelihood of ICS use (p = 0.018). In addition, Group I exhibited a significantly higher residual volume to total lung capacity ratio (48.88% vs. 43.28%; p = 0.030), indicating greater air trapping and small airway involvement.ConclusionsThese findings support the notion that infection-related COPD (COPD-I) represents a clinically distinct phenotype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04382-0.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global health concern and remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Although cigarette smoking has traditionally been recognized as the principal risk factor for COPD development, recent studies indicate that COPD is a heterogeneous disorder with multifactorial etiologies extending beyond smoking. Contributing factors include genetic susceptibility, early-life events, such as in utero exposures, previous respiratory infections, and harmful environmental exposures [1, 2].

To capture this etiological heterogeneity in more detail, the Lancet Commission recently proposed a novel classification framework for COPD encompassing five distinct subtypes based on predominant risk factors: Type 1 (COPD-G, genetic), Type 2 (COPD-D, developmental), Type 3 (COPD-I, infection-related), Type 4 (COPD-C, cigarette smoking-related), and Type 5 (COPD-P, pollution- or environmental-related) [3]. This etiotype-based model has been integrated into the 2023 update of the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report, which underscores the importance of a more individualized approach to COPD diagnosis and management [4, 5].

Despite the recent adoption of this etiological classification, few studies have investigated differences in clinical characteristics or treatment patterns among the various COPD subtypes. Blood eosinophil count (BEC) has emerged as a valuable biomarker for predicting responsiveness to inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapy [6, 7]; however, data on the distribution of eosinophil counts and ICS prescription practices across etiological subtypes remain limited. In particular, patients with infection-related COPD—defined by a history of respiratory infections such as tuberculosis—may exhibit distinct immunologic profiles and therapeutic responses compared to those with smoking-related COPD [8–10].

Therefore, we classified Korean patients with COPD using the new etiological framework and compared BECs and ICS prescription rates between infection-related COPD (COPD-I only) and smoking-related COPD (COPD-C and its combinations). By elucidating these differences, we aimed to enhance understanding of phenotype-specific features and inform individualized therapeutic strategies for COPD management.

Methods

Study population

This prospective, observational, nationwide cohort study utilized data from the Korean COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS) cohort, a multi-center registry of patients with COPD diagnosed by pulmonologists at 54 tertiary and university-affiliated hospitals across South Korea. Enrollment began in 2012 and remains ongoing. Participants aged ≥ 40 years with South Korean nationality and a post-bronchodilator (post-BD) ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 s to forced vital capacity (FEV₁/FVC) < 0.7 were included. Participants were enrolled regardless of smoking history. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Konkuk University Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to study participation.

Distribution of patients according to the new classification criteria

Patients were classified into five etiological groups based on the Lancet Commission classification and the GOLD framework, which identify five major risk factors for COPD development [3, 4]. Type 1 (COPD-G) included patients diagnosed with α₁-antitrypsin deficiency, confirmed by α₁-antitrypsin protein phenotyping or genotyping. Type 2 (COPD-D) comprised patients who experienced a respiratory event before the age of 19 years that could have affected lung development; at enrollment, participants were asked about previous lung diseases and the age at diagnosis, and this information was used for classification. Type 3 (COPD-I) included patients with a history of respiratory infections, such as tuberculosis or bronchiectasis. Past tuberculosis was defined as the presence of tuberculosis sequelae on chest radiography or computed tomography (CT) or a self-reported history of tuberculosis at enrollment, whereas bronchiectasis was identified based on radiologic findings on chest radiography or CT. Type 4 (COPD-C) included patients with a smoking history of ≥ 10 pack-years. Type 5 (COPD-P) included patients with a history of exposure to indoor, outdoor, or occupational air pollution. All etiological factors were assessed at cohort enrollment through standardized questionnaires and medical history review, using the same methodology applied in a previous study to define COPD etiotypes [11].

For the purposes of this study, the study population was divided into two groups based on the proposed etiological classification system. The infection-related group (Group I) included patients classified as Type 3 (COPD-I), with no overlap with other COPD types. The smoking-related group (Group C) included patients for whom smoking-related COPD (Type 4) was a contributing factor, excluding those with COPD-I (Type 3). This group comprised patients with Type 4 (COPD-C), Type 4 and 5 (COPD-C and P), and Type 2, 4, and 5 (COPD-D, C, and P).

Measurement of clinical data

Clinical data were collected at enrollment and during regular follow-up visits. Baseline characteristics included age, sex, education level, smoking history, exposure to air pollution, area of residence, biomass fuel exposure, occupational history, duration since COPD diagnosis, duration of COPD treatment, current medications for COPD, weight, and height. Comorbidities included myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, osteoporosis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, hyperlipidemia, thyroid disease, and inflammatory bowel disease. Past medical history encompassed bronchiectasis, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, COPD, tuberculosis, measles, pertussis, pneumonia, asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and drug allergies. Pulmonary function testing included pre- and post-BD spirometry, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and lung volume measurements using body plethysmography. Symptom burden and quality of life were assessed using the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale, and the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire-C [12]; additionally, the presence of chronic bronchitis symptoms was recorded. Functional capacity was evaluated using the 6-min walk distance, and additional assessments included chest radiography, electrocardiography, CT of the chest, and laboratory testing [13]. Exacerbation history, including prior events, frequency of emergency room visits, and intensive care unit admissions, were documented. Patients were managed by their pulmonologists in an observational study design and were followed at least every 6 months, with exacerbation data recorded at each visit [14]. Moderate exacerbations were defined as events prompting unscheduled outpatient visits requiring systemic corticosteroids and antibiotics, whereas severe exacerbations necessitated emergency department visits or hospitalization. Acute exacerbation data were collected prospectively throughout the study period. Pulmonary function tests were conducted using standardized equipment in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) guidelines, with predicted values adjusted for the Korean population [15]. Lung volume measurement was performed according to ATS/ERS guidelines, using body plethysmography [16]. Total lung capacity (TLC), residual volume (RV), functional residual capacity, and the TLC/RV ratio were recorded. DLCO was measured using the single-breath technique with standardized methods recommended by ATS/ERS and commercially available equipment. Predicted values were calculated using a Korean reference formula [17]. Medication use was documented at enrollment and at each 6-month follow-up visit. Inhaled therapies were classified as long-acting beta-agonists (LABA), long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), ICS, ICS/LABA combinations, LABA/LAMA combinations, or triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance. Fisher’s exact test was applied when > 20% of cells had expected frequencies < 5. The proportion of patients prescribed ICS was examined using binary logistic regression. Unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) were first calculated via univariate analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression to derive adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariate models were adjusted for age, sex, post-BD FEV₁ (% predicted), and CAT score. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution of patients with COPD based on the new classification criteria

Of 3,371 COPD patients registered in the Korean COPD Subgroup Study database between April 2012 and November 2024, 1,849 were excluded due to insufficient data for etiological classification. The remaining 1,522 patients were classified as follows: 14, 602, 1304, and 1,041 patients with Type 2 (COPD-D), Type 3 (COPD-I), Type 4 (COPD-C), and Type 5 (COPD-P), respectively. No patients were classified solely as Type 2 (COPD-D), and some patients were classified across multiple types (Types 2–5).

Of these, 41 patients (3%) had only Type 3 (COPD-I), 296 patients (19%) had only Type 4 (COPD-C), and 86 patients (6%) had only Type 5 (COPD-P). For subsequent analyses, patients were grouped into two categories (Fig. 1): the infection-related group (Group I: COPD-I only), comprising 41 patients (4.7%), and the smoking-related group (Group C: COPD-C, CP, DCP), comprising 832 patients (95.3%).

The mMRC dyspnea scale scores ranged from 1.34 to 1.43, and CAT scores ranged from 13.29 to 15.10 across the groups. More than 80% of patients were classified as GOLD grade 2–3. Mean BECs were 276.69/mm³ for Type 2 (COPD-D), 222.72/mm³ for Type 3 (COPD-I), 236.38/mm³ for Type 4 (COPD-C), and 236.55/mm³ for Type 5 (COPD-P). The proportions of patients who experienced moderate-to-severe exacerbations in the previous year were 42.9%, 43.9%, 40.9%, and 40.4% for Types 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively.

Pulmonary function test results for each COPD type are also summarized in Table 1. The post-BD mean forced vital capacity (FVC) ranged from 3.10 to 3.35 L. Post-BD predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (post-BD FEV₁, % predicted) was 45%, 55%, 57%, and 57% for Types 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. The post-BD FEV₁/FVC ratio was 44%, 50%, 50%, and 51% for Types 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. The residual volume to total lung capacity ratio (RV/TLC) was 44%, 45%, 44%, and 44% for Types 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively.

Table 2 presents the characteristics of patients in the infection-related group (Group I: COPD-I only) and the smoking-related group (Group C: COPD-C, CP, DCP). The mean age was 66.51 years in Group I and 69.23 years in Group C. Group C was predominantly male (97.4%), whereas the proportion of males in Group I was significantly lower (53.7%, p < 0.001). Group I had a higher proportion of never-smokers (75.6%, p < 0.001). In addition, smoking exposure differed between the groups, with Group C reporting a mean of 45.27 pack-years (p < 0.001).

Pulmonary function testing revealed that post-BD FVC (% predicted) was 77% in Group I and 82% in Group C, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.064). Post-BD FEV1 (% predicted) was 58% in Group I and 59% in Group C (p = 0.807). The post-BD FEV1/FVC ratio was significantly lower in Group C (51%) than in Group I (55%) (p = 0.039). TLC (L) was significantly greater in Group C compared to Group I (p = 0.003). However, TLC (%) was not different between the two groups. The RV/TLC ratio was significantly lower in Group C compared to Group I (p = 0.030).

No significant differences in symptom burden were observed between the groups, as assessed by the mMRC dyspnea scale (p = 0.549) and the CAT score (p = 0.790). There were no significant differences in mean BEC levels between Group I and Group C (p = 0.254). However, the proportion of patients with eosinophil counts < 100 cells/µL was significantly higher in Group I (44.4%) compared to Group C (27.8%) (p = 0.031). Group I exhibited a trend toward a lower rate of ICS prescription (25.7%) compared to Group C (41.5%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.063; Fig. 2). The prevalence of a history of moderate-to-severe exacerbations was comparable between the two groups (Group I: 41.5%; Group C: 39.2%; p = 0.772).

Binary logistic regression analysis: proportion of patients with ICS prescription rates by group

Table 3 presents the results of the binary logistic regression analysis for ICS prescription rates between the two groups. In the univariate analysis, Group I exhibited a trend toward lower ICS prescription rates compared to Group C (OR = 0.487, 95% CI 0.225–1.055; p = 0.068). After adjustment for potential confounders, this association became statistically significant. When adjusted for age and sex, the AOR was 0.309 (95% CI 0.122–0.783; p = 0.013). Further adjustment for post-BD FEV₁ (% predicted) yielded an AOR of 0.297 (95% CI 0.117–0.757; p = 0.011), and additional adjustment for CAT score resulted in an AOR of 0.322 (95% CI 0.126–0.822; p = 0.018), indicating that ICS prescription rates were significantly lower in Group I compared to Group C.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare COPD-I and COPD-C directly using the etiological classification proposed by the Lancet Commission [3]. Several clinical differences were identified between the two groups, most notably the significantly lower BEC in the COPD-I group. This novel finding, not previously reported, may have important clinical implications.

Although the precise pathogenesis of infection-related COPD remains unclear, prior studies suggest that airflow obstruction following pulmonary infections, such as tuberculosis, may result from structural lung injuries including bronchiectasis, extracellular matrix degradation mediated by matrix metalloproteinases, and bronchiolar narrowing [18]. Furthermore, small airway dysfunction is frequently observed in patients with a history of tuberculosis [19]. Consistent with these findings, Group I in our study demonstrated a significantly higher RV/TLC ratio compared to Group C.

Patients with tuberculosis-destroyed lungs have been reported to experience frequent exacerbations and accelerated decline in lung function, with a mean FEV₁ decline of 38.24 mL/year [20]. Although the mechanisms underlying exacerbations in COPD-I are not fully elucidated, chronic bacterial colonization—commonly observed in post-tuberculosis bronchiectasis—may play a significant role. Notably, low BEC (< 100 cells/µL) has been significantly associated with bronchiectasis and chronic bacterial infection [21]. These findings suggest that the low BEC observed in our COPD-I group may reflect underlying infectious airway pathology. The percentage of BEC < 100 in COPD-IC was 22.5% (Table S1) in this study, which was significantly lower than that in Group I (44.4%). However, when compared with Group C (27.8%), there was no significant difference. These results may suggest that cigarette smoking is a more important component than infection in contributing to BEC. However, causal or mechanistic interpretation should be avoided since the data were based on an observational cohort study. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate this result.

These structural and pathological differences may account for the observed treatment patterns. In our study, Group I exhibited a significantly lower rate of ICS prescriptions compared to Group C. This likely reflects clinical concerns regarding the increased risk of pneumonia and bacterial colonization associated with ICS use in patients prone to chronic infection. Furthermore, current GOLD guidelines do not recommend ICS therapy for patients with BEC < 100 cells/µL [4]. As all patients in the KOCOSS cohort were managed by pulmonology specialists, the lower ICS prescription rate in Group I likely represents appropriate, evidence-based clinical decision-making tailored to patient-specific risk factors.

Our study has several limitations. First, the number of patients classified as Group I was relatively small (n = 41), limiting robustness. This small sample size limited the statistical power, the stability of estimates, and the reliability of subgroup conclusions. Also, Group C includes heterogeneous combinations (COPD-C, CP, DCP), which may dilute comparisons. This group heterogeneity affected the interpretation of the results. Second, etiological classification relied in part on patient-reported history (e.g., prior tuberculosis or environmental exposures). This introduces recall bias and possible misclassification of COPD etiotypes. Third, 54.9% of patients (1,849/3,371) were excluded due to insufficient data for classification, which may introduce selection bias and limit external validity. Excluded patients may represent individuals with less complete clinical evaluation or follow-up, which could affect the generalizability of our findings. The impact of missing data on the results was not fully explored. Future studies using more complete datasets or applying imputation methods may help address this limitation. Fourth, serological testing to exclude secondary causes of bronchiectasis (e.g., connective tissue diseases or immunodeficiency) was not performed. However, in Korea, post-infectious causes, such as tuberculosis, measles, and pertussis, remain the most common etiologies of bronchiectasis, whereas immune-mediated causes are relatively rare [22, 23]. Fifth, this was not a clinical trial but an observational study. ICS prescription differences may reflect physician preference, confounding factors, and clinical bias. Thus, causal or mechanistic conclusions should be strictly avoided.

Despite these limitations, our study provides direct evidence that COPD-I differs meaningfully from COPD-C in both pathophysiology and clinical management. These findings emphasize the importance of a personalized, etiology-informed approach to COPD management, extending beyond traditional symptom- or pulmonary function–based classifications. In particular, tailored treatment strategies that consider BEC, the presence of bronchiectasis, and chronic bacterial colonization are likely to be more effective in patients with COPD-I.

Conclusions

Our study is the first to demonstrate that patients with COPD-I (Group I) exhibit significantly lower BEC compared to patients with COPD-C (Group C). These differences likely reflect distinct underlying disease mechanisms and heightened clinical concern for infection risk. Our findings support the notion that COPD-I represents a clinically and pathophysiologically distinct phenotype. These findings emphasize the value of incorporating etiological classification into COPD management and provide a foundation for developing more personalized, mechanism-based treatment strategies. However, the findings are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and any potential clinical implications should be interpreted with caution.
