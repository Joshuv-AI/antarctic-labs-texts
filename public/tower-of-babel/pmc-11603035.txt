Erosion behavior of Al–SiC and Al–WC MMCs via powder metallurgy under solid particle impingement
Authors: Manvendra Yadav, L. A. Kumaraswamidhas, Ashish Kumar, T. M. Yunus Khan, Umair Baig
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the erosion behavior of Al–SiC and Al–WC metal-based composites in a solid particle impingement environment developed through powder metallurgy. Micro-sized powders of SiC and WC were utilized in different weight proportions (0–10 wt%) for fabricating the composite samples. The angle of impingements of 30–90° and impact velocity of 30–90 m/s were used for solid particle erosion testing. Microstructure was characterized by XRD, SEM, and EDS. The results of the ANOVA test indicate that impact velocity is the most significant factor, followed by reinforcement content. SiC composite with reinforcement content of 2.5 and 5 wt% showed ductile erosion behavior at lower angles of impingements and brittle behavior at higher angles of impingements. On the other hand, 10 wt% of SiC indicated a semi-ductile erosion behavior. The reinforcement content is 5 and 10 wt % of WC showed a semi-ductile erosion behavior, whereas 2.5 wt% of WC showed a mix of ductile and brittle behavior. At a 30° angle of impingement, SiC (10 wt%) provides the best erosion performance, whereas, at 90° (i.e., at normal), WC (10 wt%) performs better than all other composites. SiC (5 wt %) comes out to be the best performer at 45° of the angle of impingement.

Introduction

Metal Matrix Composites (MMCs) are increasingly established as critical materials in a plethora of applications, which include automotive components, aerospace parts, and marine applications. The inherent properties of MMCs, such as higher stiffness, improved strength, better fatigue strength, superior performance at elevated temperatures, etc., are responsible for their increasing applications1. Metallic materials alone are not able to achieve these properties up to the extent that is obtained through their composites. The reinforcement of metal matrix with ceramic particulates results in improved thermal and superior mechanical properties2. Ceramics-based MMCs are generally developed in the form of powders, palates, or whiskers3. Ceramic reinforcements can enhance the erosion performance of composites by controlling the microstructure4. Aluminum-based MMCs are most commonly preferred when it comes to utilization in lightweight applications. A discontinuous type of reinforcement is generally utilized for producing aluminum composites so that the essential mechanical and tribological properties are certainly achieved5,6. Different types of reinforcing materials have been used for the development of aluminum composites, which include TiO2, TiC, SiC, WC, Al2O3, ZrO2, and others7,8.

The term “erosion” is frequently used in engineering applications to describe material deterioration. Solid particle erosion wear, an abrasive wear procedure, results from the repetitive impact of only stiff, solid, and microscopic particles9. A substrate-losing material results in a shortened durability in the actual environment compared to the rated life10,11. The loss of material is a significant issue in various industrial applications, including compressors, gas nozzles, particulate-carrying valves, boiler tubes, gas turbines, and mining equipment. The deterioration of a material brought on by an individual or many erodent impacts occurs either continuously or intermittently during service. The ductile and brittle behavior of the target material can affect the erosion mechanism in various materials. With ductile materials like metals, erosion happens as a result of plastic deformation, in which the material scratches off the targeted surface due to the impact of the erodent. In the case of brittle materials, cracks are developed on the surface due to the impingement of erodent particles. Lateral cracks cause the loss of material from the brittle surface. The loss of material due to erosion wear depends on different factors, which include the nature of impingement material, the behavior of substrate material, and others12.

Erosion wear characteristics of several traditional and metal matrix composite materials have been investigated on the basis of various performance parameters by many researchers over the years. These parameters include angle of impingement13–15, size of the particle16–18, impact velocity19,22, feed rate23, hardness24,25, thermal conditions26,30, and type of microstructure26–28. Among all the performance parameters mentioned above, impact velocity and angle of impingement are the key parameters that significantly affect the erosion performance of materials. Erosion wear of ductile materials is significant at lower angles of impingements (10°–30°), whereas, for brittle materials, it occurs around 90°17–20. SiO2 and Al2O3 are most commonly used as the erodent particles for investigating the erosion wear of MMCs17–19,28–31. The most common range of impact velocity and angle of impingement used by the researchers are 20–150 m/s and 15–90°, respectively.

Chand and Chandrasekhar32 investigated aluminum base hybrid MMC through the reinforcement of BN and B4C via powder metallurgy. The erosion performance of this composite was enhanced with the increasing B4C content. In another study, sillimanite-reinforced aluminum composite was developed through the stir casting method with reinforcement content of 5 and 10 wt%33,34. Results showed that 5 wt% sillimanite had improved erosion behavior than that of 10 wt%. Mudasar and Mohamed35 carried out an experimental investigation of Al2O3 and SiC-reinforced aluminum composite developed through a casting approach. It was observed that the hardness of the composite was significantly improved with increasing Al2O3. Patnaik and co-workers36 found that maximum erosion wear occurs at 60 °C for TiO2-reinforced aluminum-zinc alloy composite. The investigation of a TiB2-reinforced aluminum composite revealed that the tensile rate of erosion was maximum at an angle of impingement of 30° and minimum at 90°37.

Although significant studies are available on the erosion performance of aluminum composites reinforced through a variety of reinforcing materials, the analysis of SiC and WC with aluminum composite has not been explored much so far. Additionally, the development of aluminum-based MMC via the powder metallurgy route is minimal. By investigating the erosion behavior of Al–SiC and Al–WC MMCs in solid particle impingement conditions, researchers can generate valuable data for material selection, design optimization, and performance prediction in various industrial applications such as aerospace, automotive, and marine sectors, ultimately leading to more efficient and reliable systems.

Experimental plan

In the current research, two different composites of aluminum (Al–SiC and Al–WC) were fabricated through powder metallurgy. Aluminum powder, having average particle size of 20.14 µm and purity of 99%, was utilized as the metal matrix, whereas SiC and WC powders, having average particle size of 49.30 µm and 39.64 µm respectively, were used as the reinforcements for developing the proposed composites. Figure 1 displays the SEM images of the powder, while Fig. 2 illustrates the particle size distribution of (a) Pure-Al, (b) SiC, and (c) WC, respectively. Four different wt% of 0, 2.5, 5, and 10% were used for developing the composites. The mixture of powders was prepared by using the ball milling operation. This operation was conducted for 60 min by maintaining a ball/powder ratio of 10:1. The possibility of accumulation in the reinforcing materials was avoided by utilizing the Toluene solution. The powder compaction was conducted to prepare the samples with size 25 × 25 × 5 mm. A compaction pressure of 780 MPa was exerted for 5 min in a Compression Testing Machine to get the homogeneous compaction of the powders. Then, sintering of the compacted samples was carried out for 1 h at 550 °C. Vickers hardness testing of the prepared composite samples was done by applying the load of 100 g for 10 s. Figure 3b shows the developed composite sample. Alumina particles having 106 μm average size were used as erodents for the wear testing of MMCs as per ASTM standard G76. The abrasive particles were supplied along with compressed air and passed via a tungsten carbide nozzle having a 2 mm diameter. A range of impact velocities of 30, 50, 70, and 90 m/s were used to strike the erodent particles on the developed MMCs. Effective cleaning of samples with Acetone and air drying was done to ensure they were dirt-free. The wear rate caused due to erosion was measured by adopting the weight loss approach. The total mass of the erodent particles was obtained by taking the product of mass flow rate and total test time.

In this research work, the experiments are designed as per the L16 array of Taguchi methodology. Various constraints and their levels are presented in Table 3. Different combinations of the levels were used as inputs, and the corresponding outputs were calculated in the form of abrasive wear rate for SiC and WC-reinforced Al composites. Then, the outputs were converted into S/N ratios to analyze the data. S/N ratios have different characteristics depending upon the desired outputs. In this study, the focus is on minimizing the rate of erosion, and therefore, the smaller-the-better characteristic of the S/N ratio, i.e., (S/N)s, is used. Result and discussion

The impact of the angle of impingement on the rate of erosion

The rate of erosion shows a complex relationship with the angle of impingement. In the case of ductile materials, the highest rate of erosion is achieved at 15–30°, and the minimum wear occurs at 90°. This scenario is precisely reversed in the case of the brittle materials39. The erosion wear of ceramic-based metal composites mainly depends on the ductile or brittle nature of composites and also on the erosion conditions40. In the case of SiC (2.5%), a similar rate of erosion was obtained at angles of impingement of 30°, 45°, and 90° whereas the lowest rate of erosion was observed at 60°. SiC (2.5%) shows a ductile behavior between 30 and 60° and a brittle nature from 60 to 90°. For SiC (5%), a maximum and minimum rate of erosion is observed at 30° and 45°, respectively. It shows a ductile behavior from 30 to 45°, and after 45°, it shows brittle behavior up to 90° of impingement angle. In the case of SiC (10%), the highest erosion is obtained at 60° and the lowest at 90°. Since the peak is obtained between 45 and 60°, it is an indication of semi-ductile behavior. Considering all SiC composites together, it can be observed from Fig. 7 that the maximum rate of erosion among SiC composites is obtained with SiC (10%) at the angle of impingement of 60° and the minimum rate of erosion is observed for SiC (2.5%) at the angle of 60°. Among all angles of impingements, SiC (10%) is the only composite that provides the minimum rate of erosion at two instances, i.e., for 30° and 90°. Thus, it can be understood that SiC (10%) performs better than the other SiC composites.

In the case of WC (2.5%), the maximum rate of erosion was obtained at an angle of impingement of 30°, whereas the lowest rate of erosion was detected at 60°. WC (2.5%) shows a ductile behavior between 30 and 60° and a brittle nature from 60 to 90°. For WC (5%), a maximum and minimum rate of erosion is found at 45° and 60°, respectively. It shows a semi-ductile behavior as the peak is observed at 45° of the angle of impingement. In the case of WC (10%), the highest and lowest erosion is obtained at 60° and 90°, respectively. Since the peak is obtained at 60°, it is an indication of semi-ductile behavior. Considering all WC composites together, it can be observed from Fig. 7 that the maximum rate of erosion among WC composites is obtained with WC (10%) at the angle of impingement of 60° and the minimum rate of erosion is observed for WC (10%) at the angle of 90°. Among all angles of impingements, WC (2.5%) is the only composite that provides the minimum rate of erosion at two instances, i.e., for 60° and 90°. Thus, it is evident that WC (2.5%) provides better erosion performance in comparison to the other WC composites.

The erosion performance of all developed composites is presented together in Fig. 7. It is interesting to observe that maximum and minimum rates of erosions are provided by the same composite, i.e., by WC (10%). The composites WC (5%) and SiC (2.5%) show a pattern of increase → decrease → increase corresponding to the variation in the angle of impingements as 30° → 45°, 45° → 60°, 60° → 90°. Some drastic variations in the rate of erosions are observed against the variation in the angle of impingements. It includes a reduction of 91% in the rate of erosion of WC (10%) when the angle of impingement varies from 60 to 90°. Another reduction of 73% was observed with WC (5%) with variation in the angle of impingement from 45 to 60°.

It can be observed from Figs. 5 and 6 that the increment in the rate of erosion becomes more rapid at higher impact velocities for most of the developed composites. The maximum rate of erosion for each composite occurred at the impact velocity of 90 m/s. At this velocity, steady-state wear due to erosion increased by 15 folds for pure Al, 1.22, 2.76, and 2.24 times for 2.5, 5–10 weight % of SiC, respectively. At the same time, a 2.58, 4, and 9.67 times increment was witnessed for 2.5, 5, and 10 wt% of WC, respectively. In some cases, it was detected that the erosion rate reduced with increasing impact velocities. It was observed that the rate of erosion reduced at a velocity of 50 m/s for SiC (2.5%) and at a velocity of 70 m/s for SiC (10%). Such discrepancies in the rate of erosion occur in metal composites. The rest of the composites showed the typical wear behavior in which the rate of erosion is the direct function of the impact velocity. With each increment of impact velocity by 20 m/s, the variation in the rate of erosion was found within a range of 0.58–4 times for all the developed compositions of the composites.

The least square regression approach was utilized to find the velocity exponent values of all the composites tested at an angle of impingement of 60° and Table 4 depicted the corresponding results. Table 4 clearly mentions that the majority of the experimental data fit with the satisfactory regression levels (R2 > 0.75)20 except for 2.5 and 5 weight % of SiC and 2.5 weight % of WC composite specimens. A similar observation was also made during the steady-state analysis of these composites. The best data fit was obtained with 5 wt% of WC composite having a regression value of 0.9334. The velocity exponent value of 1.9718 indicates a ductile erosion wear behavior for WC (2.5%). Analysis of the worn-out surface of the metal composites

The surface characteristics of eroded composite samples were analyzed through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The samples with maximum and minimum distorted surfaces were selected for SEM analysis. The erosion behavior of SiC (10%) and WC (10%) composites indicates that maximum erosion occurs at smaller angles, while minimum erosion is observed at the normal angle of impingement. This suggests a ductile erosion behavior, as described by Hutchings41. SEM image of the Al–SiC (10%) composite at 45° and 50 m/s is shown in Fig. 14a. Three different wear modes, viz. plowing, crater, and crack, were observed in this case, as shown in Fig. 14a. A significant plowing shows that the Al–SiC (10%) composite undergoes ductile erosion. The cracks propagated and ultimately formed the craters. The majority of the erosion phenomenon was caused by plowing and crater formation, which was also verified during steady-state analysis. The SEM image of the Al–SiC (10%) composite tested at an angle of impingement of 90° and impact velocity of 50 m/s is shown in Fig. 14b. Fractures at different locations were observed apart from shallow craters. The impact of erodent particles in the expected direction caused the formation of craters. The initial cracks propagated to form the fractures. Under the testing conditions of 90° and 50 m/s, wear phenomena such as plowing and platelet formation were absent. Instead, the loss of material was primarily attributed to crater formation and fractures. This particular case at 90° and 50 m/s exhibited minimal erosion wear, a conclusion further confirmed during steady-state analysis15.Fig. 14SEM images of the worn-out surface at a velocity of 50 m/s for (a) Al–SiC (10%) at an angle of impingement 45° and (b) Al–SiC (10%) at an angle of 90°.

Conclusion

This study investigates the erosion performance of Al–SiC and Al–WC composites, which were produced through powder metallurgy. The steady-state analysis was conducted for the developed composite samples to investigate their erosion behavior under different angles of impingements and impact velocities. The experiments were designed with the help of Taguchi methodology, and the most significant parameters affecting the performance of the fabricated composites were identified through ANOVA analysis. Further, the surface morphology of the developed composites was studied to understand better the erosion phenomenon occurring in the composites. The increasing content of the reinforcing materials improves the hardness of the developed metal composites. Al–WC composites had better hardness in comparison to that of Al–SiC composites. No foreign elements were identified during the XRD analysis of reinforcing materials. Also, no formation of secondary phases was visible, which indicates that proper mixing of powders was carried out. EDS analysis verified that the reinforcing materials were homogeneously distributed within the composites. SiC composite with reinforcement content of 2.5 and 5 wt% showed ductile erosion behavior at lower angles of impingements and brittle behavior at higher angles of impingements. On the other hand, 10 wt% of SiC indicated a semi-ductile erosion behavior. The reinforcement content is 5 and 10 wt % of WC showed a semi-ductile erosion behavior, whereas 2.5 wt% of WC showed a mix of ductile and brittle behavior. At a 30° angle of impingement, SiC (10 wt%) provides the best erosion performance, whereas, at 90° (i.e., at normal), WC (10 wt%) performs better than all other composites. SiC (5 wt %) comes out to be the best performer at 45° of the angle of impingement. Impact velocity significantly influenced the erosion performance of the composites. Most of the developed composites showed good data fitting and 5 wt % of WC composite had the best data fitting with a regression value of 0.9334. The outcomes of the ANOVA analysis indicated that the most significant factor for both SiC and WC composites was impact velocity. The influence of the angle of impingement on erosion performance is moderately significant despite being the least prominent factor. The SEM analysis of the worn-out samples showed the presence of various wear phenomena, which include ploughings at oblique impact angles as well as cracks, deep craters, and fractures at standard impact angles.
