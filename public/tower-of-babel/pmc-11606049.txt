Genome-wide identification and characterization of the LRX gene family in grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) and functional characterization of VvLRX7 in plant salt response
Authors: Kai Liu, Xiujie Li, Chaoping Wang, Yan Han, Ziguo Zhu, Bo Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeucine-rich repeat (LRR) extensins (LRXs), which are cell wall-localized chimeric extensin proteins, are essential for the development of plants and their resistance to stress. Despite the significance of these genes, an extensive genome-wide analysis of the LRX gene family in grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is lacking.ResultsWe here detected 14 grapevine LRX genes and classified them into four groups through phylogenetic analysis. Then, their physiological and biochemical properties and gene/protein structures were analyzed. According to synteny analysis, tandem and segmental duplications have appreciably affected the expansion of the grapevine LRX gene family. On investigating tissue-specific expression profiles and cis-regulatory elements, we observed that VvLRXs likely serve as regulators of both the growth of grapevines and their responses to various environmental stresses. Salt stress treatments induced the expression of several VvLRXs, and VvLRX7 expression was the most significantly upregulated. Furthermore, VvLRX7 expression was positively correlated with the salt tolerance of grape rootstocks. VvLRX7 overexpression in Arabidopsis markedly enhanced its salt tolerance.ConclusionThis study provides a general understanding of the characteristics and evolution of the LRX gene family in grapevine. VvLRX7 may function as a positive regulator of plant’s response to salt stress. These findings offer a basis for future studies on the function of grapevine LRXs and their role in improving salt stress tolerance in grapevine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11087-3.Keywords: Genome-wide analysis, Leucine-rich repeat extensins, Grapevine, Gene expression, Gene function

Background

Cell wall is a unique and complex structure present in plant cells but absent in animal cells. It shapes each cell and shields the cells against biological and abiotic stresses [1, 2]. This dynamic structure is primarily composed of polysaccharides such as pectin, hemicellulose, and cellulose, which are augmented by a small contingent of proteins [3]. Although these proteins are present in a minor proportion, they are crucial for regulating cellular communication, embryogenesis, and responses to injury [4, 5]. Extensins (EXTs), exhibiting Ser-Pro2–n repeats, are initial structural proteins of the cell wall [6, 7]. They predominantly facilitate cell wall loosening and expansion, thereby representing the sole class of cell wall proteins that induce in vitro cell enlargement and overall cell wall growth [8, 9]. Leucine-rich repeat (LRR) extensins (LRXs), a special group within EXTs, have highly conserved LRR and EXT domains [5]. The LRR domain facilitates a spectrum of protein–protein interactions, extending from small peptides to large proteins. The EXT domain likely participates in the positioning and stabilization of these cell wall proteins [10, 11]. In Arabidopsis, 11 AtLRXs were cataloged into two distinct clades based on their expression patterns: class I is found in vegetative tissues (AtLRX1–AtLRX7) and class II in pollen (AtLRX8–AtLRX11) [5, 12]. Class I AtLRXs play pivotal roles in cell wall construction in root hairs [13, 14], and class II AtLRXs are integral to orchestrating the maintenance of cell wall integrity during pollen tube growth [15].

Grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is among the oldest fruit crops that is widely cultivated globally [16]. Grape berries can be eaten fresh and are used for producing wine and juice, and as dried fruits and are thus of substantial economic importance [17]. Approximately 7% of the Earth’s land surface, or approximately 1 billion hectares, currently encounters the problem of salinization, and the affected area is continuously expanding. Soil salinization has become one of the greatest environmental challenges of agricultural production [18–21]. Salt is the key source of abiotic stress affecting fruit crops such as grapevine. Salt stress can lead to nutritional imbalance, ionic toxicity, osmotic stress, and oxidative damage, which thus restricts the growth, yield, and fruit quality of fruit trees [18, 20, 22, 23]. Improving the salt tolerance of grape varieties and rootstocks through selective breeding and using grape resources with an inherent salt tolerance ability are critical strategies for expanding cultivation in medium and low saline-alkali soils and enhancing grape yield and quality under such challenging conditions. However, molecular mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in grapevine remain poorly understood. The extended duration of breeding cycles has also hampered the breeding of high-salt-tolerant varieties and rootstocks.

Recent in-depth studies have highlighted the substantial contributions of LRXs to plant growth and development. Of the Arabidopsis thaliana LRXs expressed in vegetative tissues, AtLRX1/2 are crucial for root hair formation. Root hairs in the lrx1 single mutant were malformed, whereas those in the lrx2 single mutant maintained a normal appearance [13]. Notably, root hairs of the lrx1lrx2 double mutant have more severe abnormalities than those of the lrx1 single mutant alone, which suggests a synergistic effect of LRX1/2 on root hair morphology [14]. AtLRX3/4/5 single mutants typically exhibit no notable phenotypic changes. By contrast, the lrx3lrx4lrx5 triple mutant shows stunted growth, compromised cell and vacuole formation, and increased anthocyanin accumulation [24–26]. Furthermore, A. thaliana LRXs specifically expressed in pollen (AtLRX8–11) are necessary for regulating pollen germination and tube elongation. The quadruple mutants lrx8–lrx11 exhibit reduced male fertility, decreased seed formation, and inhibition of pollen tube development, which highlights the critical roles of these proteins in reproduction [27, 28]. LRXs not only play an pivotal role in regulating plant growth and development but also participate in stress response, especially salt stress response. In Arabidopsis, the RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR (AtRALF) peptide directly binds to FERONIAs (AtFERs) and AtLRXs [29]. Under salt stress, the mature AtRALF22 peptide is liberated because of S1P protease, which subsequently initiates AtFER internalization through endosomal mechanisms. Comparable phenotypic manifestations, including growth inhibition and increased salt stress sensitivity, were observed in the lrx345 triple mutant, fer mutant, and AtRALF22/23-overexpressing plants. These findings support the hypothesis that the interaction among AtLRX, AtRALF, and AtFER forms a crucial signaling pathway that translates cell wall signals into mechanisms coordinating plant growth and salt stress responses. This thus underscores their pivotal roles in plant adaptability and stress management [26]. However, studies investigating the regulatory role of LRXs in stress resistance in plants, particularly in woody plants such as grapevine, remain limited.

Based on previous study results, we propose that LRXs in grapevine are crucial for modulating salt stress response. The LRX gene family in species such as A. thaliana [30], tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum) [31], maize (Zea mays) [32], rice (Oryza sativa) [30], loblolly pine (Pinus taeda), and norwegian spruce (Picea abies) [33] have been extensively analyzed, but the functions of these genes within the grapevine genome remain largely unexplored. To enhance our understanding of the roles of LRX genes in grapevine, a genome-wide search was conducted to identify these genes. In total, 14 full-length LRX genes were successfully detected within the grapevine genome. Using comprehensive bioinformatics methods, we analyzed the physicochemical attributes, gene/protein structures, chromosomal locations, evolutionary relationships, and tissue-specific expression profiles of these genes. Additionally, using qRT-PCR, VvLRX expression profiles were assessed after salt treatment. The functionality of VvLRX7 was specifically explored through transgenic approaches. These insights advance our comprehension of the functions of the LRX gene family in grapevine, thereby underscoring the potential roles of this gene family in salt stress response mechanisms.

Results

VvLRX gene identification

To identify potential LRXs in the grapevine genome (PN_T2T) [34], Arabidopsis LRX protein sequences were used as a reference, and a BLASTP search was conducted. This initial search yielded approximately 50 candidate proteins. The presence of conserved LRR and extensin domains was confirmed using SMART and NCBI–CDD tools. Ultimately, 14 VvLRXs were identified (Fig. S1), a number higher than those found in Arabidopsis, rice, maize, and tomato (Table S2). These 14 grapevine LRX genes were designated VvLRX1–VvLRX14 based on their chromosomal locations.

The molecular weights of the 14 VvLRXs varied from 29.84 kDa (VvLRX13) to 152.84 kDa (VvLRX11), and their lengths varied from 275 (VvLRX13) to 1388 amino acids (VvLRX11). Additionally, the isoelectric points of these proteins varied from 5.47 (VvLRX4) to 9.90 (VvLRX12) (Table 1).

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses of VvLRXs

To examine the evolutionary and phylogenetic relationships among 11 AtLRX and 14 VvLRX proteins, we constructed an unrooted phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1a). According to the analysis results, the LRX proteins were classified into four primary groups (I–IV). Groups I and II each contained three proteins, and groups III and IV each comprised four proteins. This classification differs from that in most species, where the LRX proteins are typically categorized into two groups.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses of LRXs. a Phylogenetic relationship between LRXs from grapevine and Arabidopsis. Arabidopsis LRXs are represented by a green triangle, and grapevine LRXs are indicated by purple circles. b Duplication events and chromosomal distribution of VvLRXs. Segmental duplicated genes are linked by red lines, and tandem duplicated genes are linked by blue lines

VvLRXs are distributed across six grapevine chromosomes, although not uniformly (Fig. 1b). Specifically, one gene was located on chromosome 5; two genes each on chromosomes 9 and 12; and three genes each on chromosomes 3, 11, and 18. Notably, more genes were positioned in the middle regions of the chromosomes than at the proximal or distal ends. To understand the evolutionary mechanisms of VvLRXs, duplication events were analyzed. Based on sequence homology, six VvLRXs (42.86%) formed three tandem duplication pairs, while another six VvLRXs (42.86%) formed three segmental duplication pairs (Fig. 1b, Table S3).

Conserved motif and gene structure analyses of VvLRXs

The conserved motifs and gene structure of VvLRXs were examined using GSDS 2.0 and MEME, respectively. The relationship between evolution, conserved motifs, and gene structure was analyzed by constructing a NJ phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2a), consistent with the findings in Fig. 1a. Conserved protein motifs must be identified for understanding evolutionary processes. Three conserved motifs were identified within the VvLRXs, with lengths of 21, 15, and 21 amino acids, respectively (Fig. 2b and Fig. S2). Members within the same group had similar motif locations and distributions. The positions and distributions of motifs 1 and 2 were similar across groups I–III. However, motif 3 was significantly less prevalent in group III compared with groups I and II. Group IV sequences completely lacked motif 3, and motif 1 was significantly more abundant in group IV than in groups I–III.

Motifs 1, 2, and 3 are depicted by blue, red, and green squares, respectively. c Gene structure of VvLRXs. UTRs and CDSs are represented by red and green squares, respectively, and black lines indicate introns

Additionally, exon/intron distribution is a critical aspect of the gene structure. Therefore, the structural characteristics of the 14 VvLRXs were analyzed, including the location, number, and length of their exons and introns (Fig. 2c). Members within the same subfamily exhibited a similar gene structure, reinforcing the results of the grapevine LRX classification. Groups I and II each had 1 exon. Group III had 1–2 exons and group IV had 2–4 exons. Moreover, the length of all group IV genes was clearly greater than that of groups I–III. All members of groups I and III had both 3′-UTR and 5′-UTR regions, whereas only a few members of groups II and IV had both UTRs. Gene structure variations inevitably lead to functional differences among the genes.

Cis‑regulatory element analysis of VvLRXs

To predict the transcriptional properties and functions of VvLRXs, cis-regulatory elements in the promoter sequences of these genes were analyzed using PlantCARE. Five hormone-related elements, including auxin-, MeJA-, gibberellin-, salicylic acid-, and abscisic acid-responsive elements, were identified. Additionally, five putative cis-elements associated with stress responses and seven cis-elements associated with plant growth and development were detected (Fig. 3a). The most common cis-acting elements in groups I–IV were anaerobic induction elements, MeJA-responsive elements, abscisic acid-responsive elements, and salicylic acid-responsive elements, respectively. Furthermore, the number of cis-acting elements related to plant growth and development, stress response, and hormones differed among the different groups. Group IV had the highest number of plant growth- and development-related cis-elements, group I had the highest number of stress response-related cis-elements, and group III had the highest number of hormone-related cis-elements (Fig. 3b). VvLRXs have various types and number of cis-acting elements, which suggests that they have different biological functions.

Tissue-specific expression profile of VvLRXs

For a more comprehensive understanding of the potential functions of VvLRXs, the expression levels of these genes across 21 organs or tissues at various grapevine developmental stages were examined by referring to the BAR database [35] (Fig. 4). In general, VvLRXs are constitutively expressed across almost all examined tissues. The VvLRX expression profiles varied among different groups in various tissues and organs. VvLRXs in groups I and II exhibited broader expression across various tissues and organs than those in group III. Notably, VvLRX5 and VvLRX7 in group I demonstrated the highest expression levels across multiple tissues and organs. Additionally, even same group members differed in their expression profiles. For example, among the four genes in group IV (VvLRX6/8/9/11), VvLRX11 exhibited significantly higher expression across different tissues and organs than the other three genes. Based on these data, we hypothesize that VvLRXs are comprehensive regulators of grapevine growth and its response to environmental stresses.

Expression profiles of VvLRXs after salt stress

Building on the aforementioned findings, numerous putative hormone- and stress-related cis-acting elements were identified in the VvLRX promoters. Additionally, a study demonstrated that AtLRX3/4/5 are vital for the salt tolerance of Arabidopsis [26]. To explore the response of VvLRXs to salt stress, the expression levels of these genes were assessed in the leaves of grape rootstock at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after salt treatment. Significant accumulation of Cl − was recorded in the leaves of salt-treated vines at 7 days whereas Na + accumulated only at 15 days (Fig. 4). The sampling time points were selected based on previous studies, which indicate that grapevine leaves subjected to salt treatment (0–48 h) accumulate a certain amount of Na+ and Cl− during this period [37, 38]. Since 5BB is commonly used in grapevine cultivation and exhibits moderate salt tolerance among grape rootstocks [39], it was chosen as the experimental material to investigate the expression profiles of VvLRXs after salt stress. VvLRX1/4/5/7/8/10/11/14 expression significantly increased over time, with VvLRX7 exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation (Fig. 5). Salt treatment also induced VvLRX2/3/13 expression, although the changes were not statistically significant. Interestingly, VvLRX12 expression first increased and then decreased, whereas VvLRX6 expression first decreased and then increased.

Expression profiles of VvLRXs in different grape rootstocks after salt stress

Salt tolerance varies widely among grape rootstocks. By evaluating the physiological and morphological characteristics, and antioxidant enzyme activities of 10 common grape rootstocks under salt stress, studies have been able to identify the intolerant genotypes Beta, 101 − 14, and 5BB, and the tolerant genotypes 1103P, 520 A, and 3309 C [39]. VvLRX1/4/5/7/8/10/11/14 expression was significantly upregulated in 5BB under salt stress (Fig. 5). Since key physiological parameters showed more pronounced changes at 48 h compared to earlier time points of salt treatment according to previous studies [37, 38], we selected 48 h as the time point to investigate the expression of these eight VvLRXs in the leaves of different grape rootstocks. According to the results, only VvLRX7 expression was positively correlated with the salt tolerance of the different grape rootstocks (Fig. 6). Given that grape rootstocks are primarily used for grafting in grapevine production and that VvLRX7 is highly expressed in grapevine roots, we further examined the VvLRX7 expression level in the roots of different grape rootstocks at various time points after salt treatment. The results indicated that the expression profiles of VvLRX7 in both leaves and roots were largely consistent after salt treatment. However, VvLRX7 expression in the roots peaked at 12 h after treatment and remained elevated, which contrasted with the continuous upregulation observed in the leaves following salt exposure (Fig. S3).

VvLRX7 overexpression enhanced salt tolerance in Arabidopsis

As previously noted, VvLRX7 was the most significantly upregulated VvLRX gene in grape rootstock leaves at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after salt treatment. Additionally, among VvLRX genes, the expression level of only VvLRX7 was positively correlated with the salt tolerance of different grapevine rootstock genotypes. VvLRX7 also shared high homology with AtLRX3/4/5, which are essential for salt tolerance in Arabidopsis. VvLRX7 may be a key regulator in the salt stress response of plants. To investigate the role of VvLRX7 in the salt stress response, 12 VvLRX7 overexpression (VvLRX7-OE) Arabidopsis lines were generated using an Agrobacterium-mediated method. qPCR confirmed that the VvLRX7 mRNA expression level in the leaves of these transgenic lines increased (Fig. 7a). The lines with the highest VvLRX7 expression (#2, #7, and #11) were selected for further experiments. To determine whether VvLRX7 overexpression affected salt tolerance in Arabidopsis, #2, #7, and #11 were grown in the presence of 150 mM NaCl. All VvLRX7-OE lines had significantly higher germination and survival rates than the wild-type (WT) lines (Fig. 6b, c, d), which suggest that VvLRX7 allows higher rate of seed germination and improves survival under salt stress.

Discussion

LRX proteins are cell wall-localized chimeric extensins. They are essential for modulating plant development and plant stress resistance [24, 40, 41]. Comprehensive studies have systematically cataloged the LRX gene family in several species, including A. thaliana [30], tomato (L. esculentum) [31], maize (Z. mays) [32], rice (O. sativa) [30], loblolly pine (P. taeda), and norwegian spruce (Picea abies) [33]. However, the LRX gene family in grapevine (V. vinifera. L) had not been genome-wide analyzed before this study. Therefore, a genome-wide identification of this gene family in grapevine was conducted, and 14 VvLRX genes were detected. Then, the physicochemical properties, gene/protein structures, chromosomal locations, evolutionary relationships, tissue-specific expression profiles, and expression responses of these genes under salt stress conditions were characterized. Moreover, the role of VvLRX7 in augmenting salt tolerance in plants was specifically investigated. The study findings lay a foundation for further functional analysis of VvLRX genes.

LRX genes of higher plants are broadly categorized into two distinct clades: one chiefly expressed in vegetative tissues and the other in pollen grains and tubes [5]. In the current study, phylogenetic analyses and sequence alignments revealed that VvLRXs can be grouped into four categories (Fig. 1a), a classification deviating from the typical grouping observed in most species [42]. This difference underscores that species-specific characteristics influence the gene family organization [43, 44]. The function of the grapevine LRX gene family might be different from that of the LRX gene family previously identified in other species. In grapevine, groups III and IV of VvLRXs exhibited more heterogeneous compositions than groups I and II (Fig. 1b). This diversity suggests that groups III and IV may be evolutionarily older, offering extended periods for gene duplication and structural rearrangement. Notably, the LRX gene family in grapevine has expanded to 14 VvLRXs, which is more than the numbers reported in Arabidopsis (11 AtLRX genes), rice (8 OsLRX genes), maize (2 StLRX genes), loblolly pine (0 PtLRX genes), and norwegian spruce (2 PaLRX genes) (Table S2). Tandem and segmental duplications are vital for gene family expansion and the adaptive responses of plants to environmental fluctuations [45, 46]. Approximately 50% VvLRXs were located within duplicated genomic blocks (Fig. 1b, Table S3). This finding highlights the significance of these duplication processes in promoting the diversification of the VvLRX gene family in grapevine. Furthermore, our analysis indicated that the number of LRX genes and genome size across species were not positively correlated (Table S2). For instance, despite having a significantly larger genome (~ 2300 Mbp), Z. mays [47] has fewer LRX genes than A. thaliana (genome size: ~135 Mbp) [48].

The structural features of various gene families offer insights into their evolutionary patterns [49, 50]. By contrast, conserved motifs shed light on their putative protein functions [51, 52]. In the current study, the motif arrangement and gene structure of VvLRXs changed with a change in group (Fig. 2, Fig. S2). This suggested that the gene function of different VvLRX groups might have evolved over time to help organisms adapt to environmental changes. Furthermore, groups I–III VvLRXs were more similar in the gene structure and conserved domains than group IV VvLRXs (Fig. 2, Fig. S2). Functional studies of LRX genes in Arabidopsis have indicated that proteins with similar gene structures and conserved domains tend to play analogous regulatory roles [30]. Therefore, groups I–III VvLRXs may have somewhat similar functions. Cis-acting elements, such as inducible elements, enhancers, and promoters, play a crucial role in modulating plant growth, development, and stress responses by activating or inhibiting specific gene expression [53, 54]. We here identified numerous cis-acting elements related to plant growth, development, stress responses, and hormones within VvLRX promoters (Fig. 3). The number and types of these elements varied among different VvLRXs, thereby potentially contributing to the diverse regulatory roles of these genes in the growth, development, and environmental stress tolerance of grapes. Tissue-specific expression is a key factor for predicting the function of genes [55, 56]. Comprehensive data on the tissue-specific expression of LRX genes in grapevine are currently lacking. Based on the data of other studies, we propose that VvLRXs are constitutively expressed in all tissues tested in grapevine (Fig. 4), suggesting that these genes have diverse functions in the growth and stress resistance of grapevine. Additionally, many genes within the same group (e.g., VvLRX10/14), and even gene pairs (e.g., VvLRX12/13), exhibited different tissue-specific expression patterns, which was attributable to subfunctionalization occurring between gene pairs [57].

LRX gene functions in Arabidopsis have been well characterized, with a specific subfamily having a specific function. AtLRX genes have broad regulatory roles in plant growth and development. Among the vegetatively expressed AtLRXs, AtLRX1/2 are essential for forming cell walls in root hairs [13, 14]. AtLRX3/4/5 also regulate cell wall formation. The lrx3lrx4lrx5 Arabidopsis triple mutant exhibits stunted growth, broader rosette leaves with defects in cell–cell adhesion in the epidermal layer, cell and vacuole growth defects, and increased anthocyanin accumulation [24–26]. The pollen-expressed AtLRXs (AtLRX8/9/10/11) work synergistically to maintain cell wall integrity in the pollen tube and to ensure pollen germination [5, 12]. AtLRX3/4/5 are known to be vital for plant salt tolerance, with the lrx345 triple mutant and fer mutant plants being hypersensitive to salt stress [26, 29]. In our current study, VvLRX7 was found to share the highest homology with AtLRX3/4/5 (Fig. 1a), and its expression was strongly induced under salt stress (Fig. 5). These results suggest that VvLRX7 plays a role in the responses of grapevine to salt stress. We then cloned VvLRX7 and obtained 12 VvLRX7-OE Arabidopsis lines. The lines with the highest VvLRX7 expression (#2, #7, and #11) were selected to verify salt tolerance (Fig. 7a). VvLRX7-overexpressing Arabidopsis lines exhibited increased tolerance to NaCl (Fig. 7b, c, d)), indicating that VvLRX7 positively regulates the plant’s response to salt stress. Interestingly, VvLRX7 was significantly upregulated in both leaves and roots of grape rootstocks after salt treatment, with a more rapid upregulation observed in the roots than in the leaves (Fig. S3). Grape roots detect sodium earlier than the leaves after salt exposure [58]. This suggests that VvLRX7 may be involved in the early signaling of salt perception.

In summary, this study performed an extensive analysis of the LRX gene family in grapevine. qRT-PCR was performed to examine VvLRX expression after the salt treatment. We then investigated the specific function of the VvLRX7 gene by using transgenic technology. The findings provide a reference for future studies on the function of the LRX gene family in grapevine and other plant species.

Conclusion

In current study, 14 full-length LRX genes were identified in grapevine and classified into four distinct groups. Synteny analysis revealed that segmental and tandem duplications contributed to the expansion of the grapevine LRX gene family. Analysis of cis-regulatory elements and tissue-specific expression patterns suggested that VvLRXs may play roles in various aspects of grapevine growth and resistance to environmental stresses. Additionally, several VvLRX genes were upregulated in the leaves of grape rootstocks after salt treatment, and VvLRX7 overexpression in Arabidopsis significantly enhanced its salt tolerance. These findings provide a more comprehensive understanding of the functions of LRX genes in grapevines and highlight their potential applications in enhancing plant stress tolerance.

Materials and methods

Identification of grapevine LRX genes

The amino acid sequences of Arabidopsis LRXs were obtained from TAIR1 [59] and served as references for identifying grapevine LRX proteins by conducitng BLAST [60] searches in Phytozome22 and Winberige3 databases. The Vitis vinifera PN_T2T genome [34] was used here. Sequences with an e-value of < 1e⁻30 were selected for further analysis. Using NCBI-CDD and SMART, the potential VvLRX sequences were analyzed for confirming the existence of LRR and extensin domains. The size, molecular weight, and isoelectric point of LRXs in grapevine were predicted using the Expasy ProtParam tool4.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

To create an unrooted phylogenetic tree based on the alignment of the complete amino acid sequences of LRX proteins from Arabidopsis and grapevine, the neighbor-joining (NJ) method was employed by using MEGA-7 software. The Clustal W program allowed sequence alignments, and bootstrap values were derived from 1000 replicates to ensure reliability [61]. Using MCScanX [62], gene duplication patterns were analyzed to conduct synteny analysis. Chromosome locations were identified by referring to the Winberige website, and chromosomal positions and synteny relationships were visualized using TBtools [63].

Gene structure and conserved motif analyses

The Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS)5 was used for analyzing the exon–intron structures of the 14 VvLRXs [64]. Visualizations were produced using TBtools. Conserved motifs within the 14 VvLRXs were identified using MEME [65]6, with motif widths of 6–200 amino acids.

Promoter analysis

Using TBtools, the promoter region, identified as 2-kb sequences upstream of individual VvLRXs, was extracted from the Winberige database. Cis-acting elements within these sequences were identified using PlantCARE7 and visualized using TBtools.

Tissue-specific expression profile analysis

Tissue-specific VvLRX expression data were obtained from the BAR database8 [36]. The Vitis vinifera v2.1 genome [36] was used here. These data included gene expression profiles across major grapevine organs, such as roots, stems, leaves, buds, flowers, fruits, seedlings, and pollen. The FPKM values for all genes were subjected to quality control. Normalized log2 expression values were converted, and a corresponding heatmap was generated using TBtools software.

Plant material

Sixteen-month-old potted grape rootstocks, namely 5BB (V. berlandieri × V. riparia), Beta (V. riparia × V. labrusca), 101 − 14 (V. riparia × V. rupestris), 1103P (V. berlandieri × V. rupestris), 520 A (V. berlandieri × V. riparia), and 3309 C (V. riparia × V. rupestris), were purchased from Shandong Zhichang Agricultural Science and Technology Development Co., Ltd, Rizhao, China. The grape rootstocks with similar leaf areas, leaf numbers, and plant heights were selected. The leaf area ranged from 700 to 1000 cm2, the number of leaves ranged from 20 to 30, and the plant height ranged from 50 to 70 cm. These rootstocks were maintained in a greenhouse at the Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Jinan, China; 36°42′N, 117°4′E). The greenhouse had a day/night temperature regime of 25–28 °C/5–10 °C, a relative humidity of 55–65%, and an average daylight duration of 12 h. The rootstocks were raised in plastic pots with a base diameter of 20 cm, a top diameter of 28 cm, and a height of 20 cm. The pots were filled with a potting mixture of sand, peat, and soil (1:1:3 ratio (v/v/v)). To induce salt stress for the experiment, the rootstocks were irrigated with 150 mM NaCl solution. For the gene expression analysis, the newly emerged and still shining leaves from the 5BB rootstock were sampled at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after the treatment. Each time point represents a stage of salt treatment with three replicates. Similarly, the newly emerged and still shining leaves and root tip from 520 A, 1103P, 3309 C, 101 − 14, and Beta rootstocks were sampled at 48 h after the treatment. The treatment was repeated three times. Three rootstocks were used as biological replicates, with 3–5 leaves sampled from each plant. All samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

qRT-PCR validation

SnapGene software was used to design qRT-PCR primers (Table S1). Total RNA was extracted using the Quick RNA isolation Kit (ZH120) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Residual DNA in the RNA samples was removed through digestion by using the TURBO DNAfree Kit™ (Ambion). RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. The PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) from TaKaRa was used for cDNA synthesis. TB Green Premix DimerEraser (TaKaRa) was used as a fluorescence labeling agent. The internal reference gene selected was VvUBI [20]. qRT-PCR was performed on a CFX96™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). A 25-µL reaction mixture was prepared by mixing 2 µL template cDNA, 1 mM forward and reverse primers, and 12.5 µL of TB Green Premix DimerEraser. The PCR program included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s.

Primer specificity was assessed based on the presence of a unimodal dissociation kinetics curve. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were tested for each sample. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative gene expression levels [66].

Vector construction and transgenesis

The overexpression vector was constructed by amplifying the 2259-bp coding sequence (CDS) of VvLRX7 from the cDNA of 5BB grape stock leaves. Gene cloning was confirmed through sequencing. Using the ABclonal MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (ABclonal Technology), the CDS was inserted into the plant expression vector PCambia1300. The recombinant PCambia1300-VvLRX7 vector was subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105. Table S1 presents the list of amplification primers used. The A. thaliana seeds (Col-0 ecotype) were provided by Prof. Caixia Zhang (Institute of Pomology, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Xingcheng, Liaoning). Transgenic A. thaliana plants harboring the 35 S::LRX7 expression cassette were generated through Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation [67].
