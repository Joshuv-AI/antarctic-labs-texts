Self-Concordant Perturbations for Linear Bandits
Lucas Lévy, Jean-Lou Valeau, Arya Akhavan, Patrick Rebeschini

Abstract
We study the adversarial linear bandits problem and present a unified algorithmic framework that bridges Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) and Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader (FTPL) methods, extending the known connection between them from the full-information setting. Within this framework, we introduce self-concordant perturbations, a family of probability distributions that mirror the role of self-concordant barriers previously employed in the FTRL-based SCRiBLe algorithm. Using this idea, we design a novel FTPL-based algorithm that combines self-concordant regularization with efficient stochastic exploration. Our approach achieves a regret of O ⁡ ( d ​ n ​ ln ⁡ n ) O(d\sqrt{n\ln n}) on both the d d -dimensional hypercube and the Euclidean ball. On the Euclidean ball, this matches the rate attained by existing self-concordant FTRL methods. For the hypercube, this represents a d \sqrt{d} improvement over these methods and matches the optimal bound up to logarithmic factors.



1
Introduction

We study the problem of
online optimization
, where an agent selects actions sequentially based on the information available from previous steps. The loss of each action varies over time and is unknown to the agent at the moment of decision. The goal is to minimize the
regret
, defined as the difference between the agent’s cumulative loss and that of the best fixed action in hindsight. Two standard feedback models are considered: in the
full-information setting
, the losses of all actions are revealed after each round, whereas in the
bandit setting
, only the loss of the chosen action is observed. While these settings share similarities, the latter typically requires additional algorithmic considerations.

In both settings, two prominent families of algorithms have been widely studied:
Follow-the-Regularized-Leader
(FTRL) and
Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader
(FTPL)
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020
)
.
In the full-information setting,
Abernethy et al., 2014
;
Abernethy et al., 2016
introduced a general framework,
Gradient-Based Prediction Algorithm
(GBPA), which encompasses both FTRL and FTPL and enables a unified analysis.
They also showed that every instance of FTPL can be expressed as an instance of FTRL. Note that the converse does not hold.

Many algorithms for adversarial bandits adapt techniques from the full-information setting but rely on estimators of the loss function gradient rather than its exact value. Such estimators must be constructed from limited feedback, which makes
exploration
, deliberately selecting potentially suboptimal actions to gather information, essential
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
)
. Stochastic exploration mechanisms include playing from a fixed exploration distribution with small probability
(
Auer et al., 2002
)
, or stochastically shifting the solution given by FTRL
(
Flaxman et al., 2005
)
.
An important instance of FTRL, the
Self-Concordant Regularization in Bandit Learning
(SCRiBLe) algorithm was introduced by
Abernethy et al., 2012
in the context of linear bandits, where each action is represented by a feature vector and the loss is a linear function of that vector. This instance relies on self-concordant barriers, a special class of convex functions extensively studied by
Nemirovskii, 1996
for their role in interior-point methods.
These functions diverge to infinity at the boundary of their domain, at a rate controlled by their self-concordance barrier parameter, while maintaining smoothness with respect to the local norm defined by their Hessian. Such properties make them well adapted to the geometry of the action set and yield a favorable trade-off between exploration and exploitation.

SCRiBLe attains a regret bound of
O(d^{3/2}\sqrt{n\ln n})
for arbitrary convex body action sets
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
n
the number of rounds. When the action set is the
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball, the bound improves to
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
, while for the hypercube no improvement over
O(d^{3/2}\sqrt{n\ln n})
can be achieved.
Moreover, whenever a closed-form expression of the barrier is available, which holds for many natural convex sets, the algorithm admits an efficient implementation with per-round computational complexity polynomial in
d
.

For linear bandits,
Dani et al., 2007
established a minimax lower bound of
\Omega(d\sqrt{n})
on the regret for arbitrary convex bodies.
Later,
Bubeck et al., 2012
proposed two FTRL-based algorithms:
Exp2
, which achieves regret
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
in the general case, matching the lower bound up to logarithmic factors, and
OSMD
, which attains improved bounds of
O(d\sqrt{n})
for the
d
-dimensional hypercube and
O(\sqrt{dn\ln n})
for the Euclidean ball. However, Exp2 is based on a particular preprocessing of the action set, and is not computationally tractable for arbitrary convex bodies in practice.
This motivates the study of whether algorithms based on self-concordant barriers can achieve comparable regret guarantees while offering a more broadly tractable implementation.

Our first contribution
is the introduction of
Bandits-GBPA
, a unifying framework for linear bandit algorithms that includes both FTRL- and FTPL-based approaches. Bandits-GBPA extends the GBPA framework of
Abernethy et al., 2014
;
Abernethy et al., 2016
, originally developed for the full-information setting, to the bandit setting.
Unlike in the full-information setting, this framework does not imply the inclusion of FTPL within FTRL, but it provides a conceptual structure allowing a common analysis.

Building on this framework,
our second contribution
is
Self-Concordant FTPL
(SC-FTPL), an algorithm that incorporates both self-concordant regularization, and an FTPL sampling scheme. A central element of our approach is the identification and analysis of a family of perturbation distributions that we call
self-concordant perturbations
. These distributions replicate, within an FTPL framework, the local smoothness properties that self-concordant barriers provide in FTRL, as previously exploited by
Abernethy et al., 2012
in the SCRiBLe algorithm, while defining a sampling scheme enabling more exploration.

Our third contribution
is to construct self-concordant perturbations for specific action sets, and to analyze their impact on SC-FTPL.
When the action set is the
d
-dimensional hypercube, we show that SC-FTPL produces lower-variance loss vector estimates, and achieves a regret of
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
, matching the minimax lower bound established by
Dani et al., 2007
up to a logarithmic factor. This represents an improvement of a factor
\sqrt{d}
over SCRiBLe and matches the performance of OSMD. When the action set is the Euclidean ball however, SC-FTPL achieves suboptimal regret in
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
, matching the performance of SCRiBLe. Finally, we provide a computational complexity analysis of our algorithm in those two instances and show it attains a per-round complexity linear in
d
.

2.1
Online Linear Optimization

We now formalize the
Online Linear Optimization
(OLO) framework, informally described in the introduction. An OLO instance is a sequential game between a learner and an adversary. It is defined by an
horizon

n\in\mathbb{N}
and an
action set

K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In each round
t\in[n]\coloneq\{1,...,n\}
, the learner chooses, possibly at random an action
a_{t}\in K
. Concurrently, the adversary reveals a loss function
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, the learner receives some feedback and suffers loss
\langle y_{t},a_{t}\rangle
. So the learner can choose the action
a_{t}
based on the history of previous feedback, but without knowing
y_{t}
.
There are two distinct cases regarding the feedback received by the learner. In the
full-information
setting, the whole loss vector
y_{t}
is revealed to the learner at the end of round
t
, while in the
bandits
setting, only the punctual loss
\langle y_{t},a_{t}\rangle
is revealed.

We consider that the adversary is
oblivious
, meaning that the loss functions are chosen by the adversary before the game starts, possibly with the knowledge of the learner’s policy. We also suppose that
K
is a convex body and that losses are bounded: for all loss vectors
y
and action
a\in K
,
|\langle y,a\rangle|\leqslant 1
.

The quantity of interest is the difference between the learner’s cumulated loss and the cumulated loss of the best action in-hindsight. We define the learner’s
regret with respect to some competitor
u\in K
as

R_{n}(u)\coloneq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle y_{t},a_{t}\rangle\Big]-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle y_{t},u\rangle\,,

(1)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness in the actions of the learner.
The learner’s
regret
is then defined as

R_{n}\coloneq\sup_{u\in K}R_{n}(u)
. A minimal goal for a good learner would be to achieve sublinear regret for all sequence of loss vectors
(y_{t})_{1\leqslant t\leqslant n}
.

2.2
Self-Concordant Barriers

Self-concordant barriers are a class of functions originally introduced in optimization as a central tool for interior-point methods. A detailed treatment of these functions and their applications in this context is given in
Nemirovskii, 1996
. Interestingly, their analytical properties make them particularly adapted to FTRL regularization.
In this section, we define self-concordant barriers and present key properties. Subsequently, in Section
5
we show how these properties support their use as FTRL regularizers.

Definition 1

(Self-Concordant Barrier)
.

Let
K
be a convex set with non-empty interior and
\mathcal{R}:\operatorname{int}K\to\mathbb{R}
. The function
\mathcal{R}
is a
self-concordant function
on
K
if

•

it is three times continuously differentiable and convex,

•

for all sequence
(x_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{int}K^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
x_{n}\to x\in\partial K
,
\mathcal{R}(x_{n})\to+\infty
, and

•

for all
x\in\operatorname{int}K
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|D^{3}\mathcal{R}(x)[h,h,h]|\leqslant 2(D^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)[h,h])^{3/2}
.

Let
\vartheta\geqslant 0
.
\mathcal{R}
is a
\vartheta
-self-concordant barrier
if it is a self-concordant function and if for all
x\in\operatorname{int}K
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|D\mathcal{R}(x)[h]|\leqslant\sqrt{\vartheta\;D^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)[h,h]}
.

We have denoted
D^{k}\mathcal{R}(x)[h_{1},...,h_{k}]
the
k
-th derivative of
\mathcal{R}
at
x
applied to the directions
h_{1},...,h_{k}
.

The following lemma shows how
\mathcal{R}
defines a local geometry via its Hessian. This local geometry stretches
K
, so that the Dikin ellipsoid centered in
x
, the unit ball for the local norm, is always inside
K
. Finally, Equation (
2
) provides a local Bregman smoothness bound for
\mathcal{R}
that holds inside the Dikin ellipsoid.
1
1

1

As a comparison, a function
f
is
{\lambda}
-smooth with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
if and only if we have
B_{f}(x,y)\leqslant\frac{{\lambda}}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\operatorname{dom}f
.

Lemma 1

(Local Norm and Dikin Ellipsoid)
.

Let
K
be a convex body,
\mathcal{R}
be a self-concordant barrier on
K
and
x\in\operatorname{int}K
.
Then, the Hessian of
\mathcal{R}
in
x
is positive definite, so we can define the local norm in
x
as
\|y\|_{x}\coloneq\|y\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We also define the open unit Dikin ellipsoid:

W(x)\coloneq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y-x\|_{x}<1\}\,.

Then
W(x)\subset K
and for all
y\in W(x)
,

B_{\mathcal{R}}(y,x)\leqslant\rho(\|y-x\|_{x})

(2)

where
\rho(t)\coloneq-\ln(1-t)-t
.

The next lemma controls the rate at which
\mathcal{R}
goes to infinity close to the boundary by a logarithmic function of
1-\pi_{x}
, which informally measures the distance to the boundary of
K
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a
\vartheta
-self-concordant barrier on
K
with
K
a convex set with non-empty interior and
\vartheta\geqslant 0
. Then for all
x,y\in\operatorname{int}K
,

\mathcal{R}(y)-\mathcal{R}(x)\leqslant-\vartheta\ln(1-\pi_{x}(y))\,.

The proof of these two lemmas can be found in
Nemirovskii, 1996
.
Concerning their existence, it is well-known how to construct self-concordant barriers for several important convex bodies, including polytopes and the Euclidean ball. More generally, self-concordant barriers are guaranteed to exist for all convex sets:

Proposition 3

(
Chewi, 2023
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body. Then there exists a
d
-self-concordant barrier on
K
.

3
Main Contributions

In this section we present our main contribution, the
Self-Concordant Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader
(SC-FTPL) algorithm. We briefly introduce self-concordant perturbations, describe the SC-FTPL algorithm, and state its regret guarantees on two canonical action sets.
In the rest of the paper, we develop the construction of SC-FTPL in more detail, highlight its connections and contrasts with existing approaches, and provide intermediary results.

Definition 2

(Self-Concordant Perturbation)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex set with non-empty interior,
\vartheta\geqslant 0
, and
\mathcal{D}
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebsegue measure.
\mathcal{D}
is a
\vartheta
-self-concordant perturbation

K
if there exists a
\vartheta
-self-concordant barrier on
K
,
\mathcal{R}
, such that, for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\nabla\mathcal{R}^{*}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]
. In this case, we say that
\mathcal{D}

replicates
the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
.

The SC-FTPL algorithm is defined in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

1:

A
\vartheta
-self-concordant perturbation
\mathcal{D}
for

and a learning rate
\eta>0
.

2:

Set
\hat{Y}_{0}=0
.

3:

for

t\in[n]

do

4:

Sample
\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
independently from the past.

5:

A_{t}=\argmin_{a\in K}\langle a,\eta\,\hat{Y}_{t-1}-\xi_{t}\rangle
.

6:

Receive punctual loss
\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
.

7:

Compute estimator
\hat{y}_{t}=Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
, where
Q_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[A_{t}A_{t}^{\top}]
.

8:

Update
\hat{Y}_{t}=\hat{Y}_{t-1}+\hat{y}_{t}
.

9:

end

for

SC-FTPL selects actions according to an FTPL scheme where the perturbation distribution
\mathcal{D}
is designed to replicate the properties of self-concordant barriers used in SCRiBLe. As we see in Section
5

When the action set

is either the hypercube
[-1,1]^{d}
or the unit Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leqslant 1\}
, we can construct explicit self-concordant perturbations and derive the following regret bounds for SC-FTPL:

(name=Regret of SC-FTPL on the Hypercube,restate=regrethypercube)

be the

\frac{n}{\ln n}\geqslant 24\,d
. Then, SC-FTPL on the hypercube with perturbation distribution
\mathcal{D}
and learning rate
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln n}{n}}

4
Gradient-Based Prediction

We introduce the Bandits-GBPA framework, which will allow us to analyze both self-concordant perturbations and self-concordant regularization as part of a unified framework, and thus derive regret bounds for SC-FTPL. We first present the unified GBPA framework introduced by
Abernethy et al., 2014
in the full-information setting, and then define the Bandits-GBPA algorithm.

4.1
Follow-the-Leader Style Algorithms

Let
Y_{t}\coloneq\sum_{s=1}^{\top}y_{s}
be the cumulative loss for all
t
. At each time
t\in[n]
, Follow-the-Leader style algorithms choose the action
a_{t}
by solving an optimization problem on
K
involving
Y_{t-1}
and a regularization term.

Follow-the-Regularized-Leader
(FTRL) adds regularization via a penalty function
(
Abernethy et al., 2008
)
. Given a convex function
\psi:K\to\mathbb{R}
called the
regularizer
2
2

2

In the literature, the regularizer is usually scaled by a
learning rate

\eta>0

(
Orabona, 2025
)
. The tuning of the learning rate is crucial in the analysis of FTRL in order to enjoy sublinear regret. However, for the sake of brevity, we do not include it in this first part and simply consider that it is included in the regularizer
\psi
.

, at time
t
, FTRL selects the action

\displaystyle a_{t}=\argmin_{a\in K}\langle a,Y_{t-1}\rangle+\psi(a)\,,

(3)

where ties are resolved arbitrarily.

Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader
(FTPL) adds regularization via a stochastic perturbation
(
Kalai and Vempala, 2005
;
Cohen and Hazan, 2015
)
. Given a probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
called the
perturbation distribution
, at each time
t\in[n]
, FTPL selects the action

a_{t}\in\argmin_{a\in K}\langle a,Y_{t-1}-\xi_{t}\rangle=\partial\phi_{K}(-Y_{t-1}+\xi_{t})\,,

(4)

where
\phi_{K}
is the support function of
K
,
\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
is independent from the past, and ties are resolved arbitrarily.

In the full-information setting, these two procedures have been shown to be part of a more general framework called
Gradient-Based Prediction Algorithm
(GBPA) by
Abernethy et al., 2014
;
Abernethy et al., 2016
. In GBPA, we give ourselves a differentiable function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\operatorname{Im}\nabla\Phi\subseteq K
, called a
potential
. At each step
t\in[n]
, the action played by GBPA is
a_{t}=\nabla\Phi(-Y_{t-1})
.
The key result from
Abernethy et al., 2014
is that the class of GBPA algorithms and FTRL are actually equal. This implies that every instance of FTPL can be described as an instance of FTRL:
let
\mathcal{D}
be an integrable perturbation distribution and define for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Phi(\theta)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]\,.

(5)

This is well-defined because
\xi
is integrable,
\phi_{K}
is sublinear and differentiable almost everywhere. Then, one can show that for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla\Phi(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]\,.

(6)

Therefore, the action played by FTRL with regularizer
\Phi^{*}
is the expected action played by FTPL with perturbation
\mathcal{D}
.
Note that the two algorithm are not strictly identical, FTPL is a stochastic algorithm while FTRL is deterministic.

In general, the converse does not hold, as not every potential
\Phi
can be expressed as
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]
for some random vector
\xi
. Therefore, the relation between FTPL and FTRL is a strict inclusion in the full-information setting.
Hofbauer and Sandholm, 2002
show that the function
\psi:x\mapsto=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ln(x_{i})
defined on the probability simplex
\Delta^{d}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\|x\|_{1}=1\}
does not admit a representation of the form of Equation (
5
).

4.2
Gradient-Based Prediction for Linear Bandits

We are now ready to present
Bandits-GBPA
, extending GBPA from the full-information setting to the linear bandit setting. In full information, GBPA relies only on a gradient-based update rule. In the bandit case, however, the learner observes only the scalar loss incurred at the chosen action. Thus, Bandits-GBPA needs two additional ingredients:

1.

a
sampling scheme
, which randomizes the chosen action so that exploration is possible, and

2.

an
estimation scheme
, which constructs an estimate of the unobserved loss vector from the observed scalar feedback.

Formally, an instance of Bandits-GBPA is specified by a differentiable potential function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and such that
\operatorname{Im}\nabla\Phi\subseteq K
, a sampling scheme
S:K\to\mathcal{P}(K)
, where
\mathcal{P}(K)
is the set of probability distributions on
K
, and an estimation scheme
E:\mathbb{R}\times K\times K\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which maps the observed scalar loss, the sampled action, and the expected action to an estimator of the loss vector.
3
3

3

The names
sampling
and
estimation schemes
come from
Abernethy et al., 2012
, which introduces the idea of designing a bandit algorithm by adding these two steps to a full-information algorithm.

The resulting procedure is summarized in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

1:

A differentiable potential function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\operatorname{Im}\nabla\Phi\subseteq K
, a sampling scheme
S:K\to\mathcal{P}(K)
and an estimation scheme
E:\mathbb{R}\times K\times K\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2:

Set
\hat{Y}_{0}=0
.

3:

for

t\in[n]

do

4:

Let

.

5:

Sample action
A_{t}\sim S(x_{t})

6:

Observe punctual loss

7:

.

8:

Update
\hat{Y}_{t}=\hat{Y}_{t-1}+\hat{y}_{t}

9:

end

To establish regret guarantees, we require an unbiasedness condition for the sampling and estimation schemes. This is formalized in the following definition.

Definition 3

Let
S:K\to\mathcal{P}(K)

(S,E)

\mathbb{E}_{A\sim S(x)}\left[A\right]=x\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\mathbb{E}_{A\sim S(x)}\left[E(\langle y,A\rangle,A,x)\right]=y\,.

u\in K
,

R_{n}(u)\leqslant\Phi^{*}(u)-\min_{K}\Phi^{*}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[B_{\Phi}(-\hat{Y}_{t},-\hat{Y}_{t-1})\big]\,.

This result is standard in the bandit literature, a similar, though not identical, statement can be found in
Bubeck and Cesa-Bianchi, 2012
. A full proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

5
From Self-Concordant Regularization to Self-Concordant Perturbations

In this section, we contextualize SC-FTPL by examining related algorithms and the key properties of self-concordant perturbations. We begin with a review of the SCRiBLe algorithm, which inspired our approach, and then discuss the design choices underlying SC-FTPL, in particular its sampling scheme.

5.1
Self-Concordant Regularization in Bandits Learning

The
Self-Concordant Regularization in Bandits Learning
(SCRiBLe) algorithm, introduced by
Abernethy et al., 2012
,
can be described as a particular algorithm in the Bandit-GBPA framework.
Concretely, an instance of SCRiBLe is defined by a
\vartheta
-self-concordant barrier
\mathcal{R}
on
K
, with
\vartheta\geqslant 0
and a learning rate
\eta>0
.
Its potential is given by
\Phi=(\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathcal{R})^{*}
. For
x_{t}\in K
, The sampling distribution
S(x_{t})
is defined as the uniform distribution over the set of the
2d
poles of the Dikin ellipsoid
W(x_{t})
, defined as

\{x_{t}\pm{\lambda}_{i}^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}

where
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i}
are the sets of eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})
.
After observing the punctual loss
\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
, we compute the unbiased estimator

\displaystyle E(\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle,A_{t},x_{t})

\displaystyle=d\,\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})(A_{t}-x_{t})\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle\,.

Thus, the self-concordant barrier
\mathcal{R}
plays a dual role in SCRiBLe: first as the potential through its Fenchel conjugate, and second as the basis of the sampling scheme via its Hessian. The properties of the Dikin ellipsoid stated in Lemma
1
ensure that
A_{t}\in K
, while simultaneously providing sufficient exploration to construct an accurate estimator of
y_{t}
.

For an optimal learning rate
\eta
, SCRiBLe achieves regret

R_{n}=O(d\sqrt{\vartheta n\ln n})
, where
\vartheta
is the self-concordance parameter. From Proposition
3
, every convex body admits a
\vartheta\leqslant d
barrier, so the general bound is
R_{n}=O(d^{3/2}\sqrt{n\ln n})
. For the Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
, which admits a 1-self-concordant barrier, the regret improves to
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
.

5.2
Self-Concordant FTPL

SC-FTPL uses the same potential as SCRiBLe but employs a different sampling scheme. Whereas SCRiBLe samples from the poles of the Dikin ellipsoid, SC-FTPL uses the sampling naturally induced by FTPL, which is inherently randomized. Concretely, at round
t\in[n]
, SC-FTPL draws
\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is a self-concordant perturbation, and plays

A_{t}=\argmin_{a\in K}\langle a,\eta\,\hat{Y}_{t-1}-\xi_{t}\rangle\,.

The unbiasedness of this sampling scheme follows directly from the definition of self-concordant perturbations.

Because SC-FTPL uses the same potential as SCRiBLe, it inherits some of its properties: the local smoothness and barrier properties of self-concordant functions are the main tools we use to control regret. The next theorem makes this dependence explicit.

Theorem 7

(name=,restate=SCregretbound)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body,
\eta>0
, and
\mathcal{D}
be a
\vartheta
-self-concordant perturbation for
K
, such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)^{\top}]
is non-singular for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then SC-FTPL with learning rate
\eta
and perturbation
\mathcal{D}
has its regret bounded by

R_{n}\leqslant\frac{\vartheta\ln n}{\eta}+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{y}_{t}\|_{t}^{2}]+2\,,

provided that
2\eta\|\hat{y}_{t}\|_{t}\leqslant 1
almost surely for all
t\in[n]
, with
\|\cdot\|_{t}=\|\cdot\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(a_{t})^{-1}}
where
\mathcal{R}
is the self-concordant barrier replicated by
\mathcal{D}
.

The regret decomposition of Theorem
7
follows from Lemma
6
. We then use Lemma
1
to bound the Bregman divergence term by a quadratic term and Lemma
2
to controls the rate at which
\mathcal{R}
goes to infinity close to the boundary. The detailed proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Theorem
7
, which also holds for SCRiBLe, shows that the regret is controlled by two quantities: the self-concordance parameter
\vartheta
and the sum of the variances of loss vector estimators
\hat{y}_{t}
measured in the local norms
\|\cdot\|_{t}
.
Thus, improving regret reduces to designing sampling and estimation schemes that produce small
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{y}_{t}\|_{t}^{2}]
.

Motivations for FTPL Sampling

In SCRiBLe, the variance of the local norm of the estimator is upper-bounded by
d^{2}
, which yields the suboptimal regret bound of
O(d\sqrt{\vartheta\,n\ln n})
. The goal of SC-FTPL is therefore to reduce this variance via different sampling and estimation schemes. Informally, the variance of the loss vector estimator is tied to how well the sampling scheme explores by spreading actions across all directions. In the extreme case, if no stochastic exploration is introduced, the learner cannot form an efficient estimator of the loss vector and sublinear regret is impossible
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006
)
. Therefore, a good sampling scheme has to make a good use of the available space in
K
around
x_{t}
.

The Dikin ellipsoid sampling used by SCRiBLe does not use optimally the space available, an intuition provided by
Bubeck and Eldan, 2015
.
In particular, if
x_{t}
is close to the boundary of
K
, then the Dikin ellipsoid
W(x_{t})
shrinks, because it must remain centered on
x_{t}
and inside
K
. By contrast, the FTPL sampling mechanism selects actions by solving a linear program on
K
, the action
A_{t}
is thus an extreme point of
K
. This boundary-oriented behavior allows FTPL sampling to exploit the full available geometry of
K
and, as we show later for specific sets, can yield substantially lower variance in the local norm and correspondingly sharper regret bounds.

SC-FTPL Estimation Scheme

Let us now turn to the estimation scheme. The goal is to construct an unbiased estimator
\hat{y}_{t}
of
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given only the observed feedback
\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
where
A_{t}
is a drawn from a known distribution
S(x_{t})
is known. Beyond unbiasedness, we also aim to control the variance of the estimator. A natural candidate, introduced by
Bubeck et al., 2012
, is

\hat{y}_{t}=Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}\langle A_{t},y_{t}\rangle\quad\text{with }Q_{t}=\mathbb{E}[A_{t}A_{t}^{\top}],

(7)

where the expected value is taken conditional on the past
t-1
rounds. By construction, this estimation scheme is unbiased.

However, for
\hat{y}_{t}
to be well-defined,
Q_{t}
must be non-singular. In Section
6
, we will show that for the hypercube and the Euclidean ball, one can explicitly choose self-concordant perturbations that ensure
Q_{t}
remains non-singular at every round.

Heavy-Tailed Nature of Self-Concordant Perturbations

A distinctive feature of self-concordant perturbations is their heavy-tailed nature:

Proposition 8

(name=,restate=heavytail)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body and
\mathcal{D}
be a self-concordant perturbation for
K
. Then
\mathcal{D}
does not have a finite first moment.

The proof, relying on the unboundedness of self-concordant barriers, is given in Appendix
B
. This observation explains why we define self-concordant perturbations through the identity

\nabla\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\cdot+\xi)]

rather than through the potential-level identity
\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\phi_{K}(\cdot+\xi)]
used in
Abernethy et al., 2014
.
Since
\mathcal{D}
lacks a finite first moment, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]
may fail to exist, while
\mathbb{E}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]
is always well-defined.

6
Analysis of SC-FTPL for Two Specific Action Sets

In this last section, we study SC-FTPL on two particular action sets: the hypercube and the unit ball. For each of them, we construct a self-concordant perturbation and detail the steps that lead to the regret bound established in Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
. Finally, we provide a time complexity analysis of SC-FTPL in both of these cases.

6.1
Hypercube Case

Let
K
be the hypercube
[-1,1]^{d}
. The following function is a
d
-self-concordant barrier

\mathcal{R}:x\in(-1,1)^{d}\mapsto-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ln(1-{x_{i}}^{2})

(8)

The gradient of the conjugate of
\mathcal{R}
is

\nabla\mathcal{R}^{*}(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+{\theta_{i}}^{2}}-1}{\theta_{i}}\Big)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}

for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while the gradient of the support function is given by

\nabla\phi_{[-1,1]^{d}}(\theta)=(\operatorname{sgn}(\theta_{i}))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}

for all
\theta\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{d}
.
Both of these vector-valued function are component-wise separable, i.e. their
i
-th coordinate only depends on the
i
-th coordinate of
\theta
. Using this separability, we can construct a perturbation replicating
\mathcal{R}
on
[-1,1]^{d}
:

Proposition 9

(name=,restate=hypercubedensitymargins)
.

There exists a probability distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose marginal distributions are independent and have probability density function

f:t\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\frac{\sqrt{1+t^{2}}-1}{2\,t^{2}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}}\,.

This is a
d
-self-concordant perturbation for
[-1,1]^{d}
.

A full proof is given in Appendix
C
. Note that this distribution is heavy-tailed, since
f(t)\sim\frac{1}{2}t^{-2}
as
t\to\pm\infty
, in line with Proposition
8
.
We now show that the estimator is well-defined and that its local norm can be efficiently bounded:

Proposition 10

(name=,restate=hypercubeybounds)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the
d
-self-concordant perturbation defined in Proposition
9
and
\mathcal{R}
be the self-concordant barrier defined in (
8
).
Consider Algorithm
1
run with perturbation distribution
\mathcal{D}
and any learning rate
\eta>0
. Then, for all
t\in[n]
, the estimator
\hat{y}_{t}
is well-defined and satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\|\hat{y}_{t}\|^{2}_{t}]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}d\quad\text{and}\quad\|\hat{y}_{t}\|^{2}_{t}\leqslant 3d\;\;\text{a.s.}

where
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\cdot]
is the expectation conditionally on the first
t-1
rounds.

The proof is mainly computational and is provided in Appendix
C
. Crucially, the bound of the variance of the local norm of the estimator improves by a
2d
factor over the SCRiBLe’s
d^{2}
bound. This confirms that, on the hypercube, SC-FTPL achieves more accurate loss vector estimation.

Combining this result with Theorem
7
yields Theorem
4
, which bounds the regret of SC-FTPL on
[-1,1]^{d}
by
d\sqrt{2n\ln n}+2
for an optimal choice of
\eta
. This improves over SCRiBLe by a factor of
\sqrt{d}
and matches the lower bound established by
Dani et al., 2007
up to logarithmic factors.

Complexity Analysis

At each round
t
, SC-FTPL performs three main steps: sample
\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
, solve the linear program
A_{t}=\argmin_{a\in K}\langle a,\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}-\xi_{t}\rangle
, and compute the estimator
Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
.
Sampling from
\mathcal{D}
reduces to drawing
d
i.i.d. random variables. Using inverse transform sampling with the generalized inverse of the cumulated distribution function of the
\xi_{i}
being

F^{-1}:t\in(0,1)\mapsto\frac{1-2t}{2\,t\,(t-1)}\,

each coordinate can be generated in constant time.
So the overall sampling step requires
O(d)
operations. The linear program over the hypercube admits the closed-form solution
A_{t,i}=\operatorname{sgn}(\xi_{t,i}-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1,i})
for each coordinate
i\in[d]
, which can be computed in constant time per coordinate, yielding
O(d)
complexity.

To compute the estimator, we first compute
x_{t}=\nabla\mathcal{R}^{*}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})
, which is separable across coordinates and therefore requires
O(d)
time. From the proof of Proposition
10
in Appendix
C
,
Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}
admits the closed form

Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}=\Big(\frac{-1}{1+\alpha}\frac{x_{t,i}}{{1-x_{t,i}^{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{x_{t,j}A_{t,j}}{1-x_{t,j}^{2}}+\frac{A_{t,i}}{{1-x_{t,i}^{2}}}\Big)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}

where
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{t,i}^{2}/({1-x_{t,i}^{2}})
.
This allows computing
Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}
efficiently: we first evaluate
\alpha
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{t,j}A_{t,j}/(1-x_{t,j}^{2})
in
O(d)
, and then each coordinate of
Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}
can be computed in constant time.

Combining all steps, SC-FTPL on the hypercube has a total per-round complexity of
O(d)
.
For comparison, both SCRiBLe and OSMD enjoy a per-round complexity in
O(d)
when the action set of the hypercube.
4
4

4

For SCRiBLe, when the action set is the hypercube, we have access to closed forms for the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})
. This allows to attain a per-round complexity in
O(d)
.

6.2
Euclidean Ball Case

Let us now consider the case where the action set is the unit Euclidean ball
K=\mathbb{B}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leqslant 1\}
. The following function is a 1-self-concordant barrier on
\mathbb{B}^{d}

\mathcal{R}:x\in\operatorname{int}\mathbb{B}^{d}\mapsto-\ln(1-\|x\|^{2}).

(9)

In this case, both
\mathcal{R}
and
\phi_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}=\|\cdot\|
are spherically invariant, i.e. are functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that only depend on the norm of their argument. Using this property, along with computations detailed in Appendix
D
, we can construct a perturbation replicating
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathbb{B}^{d}
:

Proposition 11

(name=,restate=sphereyexistence)
.

There exists a probability distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose probability density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure is

f:\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{\Gamma(d+\frac{1}{2})}{2\pi^{d/2}\,\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\,(1+t\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2})^{-d-\frac{1}{2}}\,dt\,.

It is a 1-self-concordant perturbation for
\mathbb{B}^{d}
.

We then derive almost-sure and in expectation bounds on the estimators
\hat{y}_{t}
of SC-FTPL run with such a perturbation distribution. These bounds are stated in the following proposition:

Proposition 12

(name=,restate=sphereybound)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the
1
-self-concordant perturbation defined in Proposition
11
and
\mathcal{R}
be the self-concordant barrier of (
9
). Consider Algorithm
1
run with perturbation distribution
\mathcal{D}
and any learning rate
\eta>0
. Then, for all
t\in[n]
, the estimator
\hat{y}_{t}
is well-defined and satisfies

1.

\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[||\hat{y}_{t}||_{t}^{2}]\leqslant\frac{3}{2}d^{2}
,

2.

||\hat{y}_{t}||_{t}^{2}\leqslant d^{2}\eta\,\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert+4d^{2}
almost surely, and

3.

\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[||\hat{y}_{t}||^{2}]\leqslant d^{2}\eta\,\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert+2d^{2}
.

where
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\cdot]
is the expectation conditionally on the first
t-1
rounds, and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{t}
is the norm defined in Theorem
7
by
\|\cdot\|_{t}=\|\cdot\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})^{-1}}
.

From these bounds, we are able to derive Theorem
5
, bounding the regret of SC-FTPL run on
\mathbb{B}^{d}
:

\regretsphere
*

The complete proofs of Proposition
12
and Theorem
5
are provided in Appendix
D
.

Compared to Proposition
10
, which provides bounds on
\hat{y}_{t}
in the hypercube setting, Proposition
12
is weaker in two aspects.
First, we were unable to establish an
O(d)
bound on the variance of the local norm of
\hat{y}_{t}
. Instead, our analysis yields a quadratic dependence on
d
, matching the variance bound of the SCRiBLe estimator. It remains unclear whether a linear bound in
d
can be achieved. We do not provide a lower bound here, and determining whether the obtained rate is tight remains an open question.
This quadratic growth directly leads to the
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
regret bound in Theorem
5
. On the Euclidean ball, the variance of the SC-FTPL estimator thus coincides, up to constant factors, with that of SCRiBLe, yielding no improvement in the rate of achieved regret. In contrast, on the hypercube, a
d
factor reduction in estimator variance between SCRiBLe and SC-FTPL translated into a
\sqrt{d}
factor improvement in regret.

Second, our results suggest that a uniform almost-sure bound on the local norm of the estimator is unlikely to hold. In the hypercube case, such a uniform bound ensured that
\eta\lVert\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}\leqslant 1/2
, a key condition enabling to make use of the local smoothness properties of self-concordant barriers. To address this limitation, the proof of Theorem
5
requires additional care.
We first show that the event
E=\{\lVert\eta\,\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}\leqslant 1/2,\,\forall t\in[n]\}
holds with high probability, using the second and third inequalities from Proposition
12
as well as Ville’s inequality. We then bound the regret conditionally on
E
, introducing an appropriate stopping time and using Doob’s optional stopping theorem to derive a regret decomposition similar to Theorem
7
. The final additive term in the regret bound of Theorem
5
corresponds to the probability that
E
fails to hold. Although this final term is of lower order than
d\sqrt{n\ln n}
, it may still be significant for small values of
d
.

Finally, we provide a complexity analysis of SC-FTPL on
\mathbb{B}^{d}
in Appendix
D
and show that it has a per-round complexity linear in
d
. We follow the same proof scheme as for the hypercube by showing that both sampling from
\mathcal{D}
and computing
Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}
can be carried out efficiently.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced Self-Concordant FTPL, an FTPL-based for adversarial linear bandits that incorporates properties of self-concordant barriers. This was done by defining self-concordant perturbations, a class of distributions that replicate their regularization effect. When the action set is the hypercube or the Euclidean ball, we have exhibited self-concordant perturbations and provided a complete analysis of SC-FTPL, proving that it yields regret bounds in
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
in both cases.

Our results leave several questions open:

•

Tightness of the regret bound.
We have shown that SC-FTPL on the Euclidean ball achieves a suboptimal regret rate of
O(d\sqrt{n\ln n})
, stemming from the
d^{2}
dependence of the variance of the local norm of the loss estimator. Whether this rate is tight remains an open question.

•

Existence of self-concordant perturbations for general convex bodies.
It remains unclear whether such perturbations exist beyond specific domains. A promising direction is to investigate the entropic barrier, a universal self-concordant barrier defined for any convex body
(
Bubeck and Eldan, 2015
)
, and determine whether it systematically admits a corresponding perturbation distribution.

•

Extending self-concordant perturbations beyond linear losses.

Hazan and Levy, 2014
showed how self-concordant regularization can be used for convex bandits. It would be natural to explore whether the perturbation-based perspective developed here can yield similar benefits in that setting.

\kappa\in(0,1)
, and define
u^{\prime}=\kappa x^{*}+(1-\kappa){u}
. Then
\pi_{x^{*}}(u^{\prime})=(1-\kappa)\pi_{x^{*}}(u)\leqslant 1-\kappa
. By Lemma
2
, it follows that

\mathcal{R}(u^{\prime})-\mathcal{R}(x^{*})\leqslant-\vartheta\ln\kappa\,.

We now decompose the regret as

\displaystyle R_{n}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u^{\prime}+u^{\prime}-u\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=R_{n}(u^{\prime})+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle y_{t},u^{\prime}-u\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{R}(u^{\prime})-\mathcal{R}(x^{*})}{\eta}+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[B_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t},-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})]+\kappa\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle y_{t},x^{*}-u\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{\eta}\vartheta\ln\kappa+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[B_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t},-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})]+2n\kappa\,,

(13)

where the first inequality applies (
12
) to
u^{\prime}
.
Since (
13
) holds for any
\kappa\in(0,1)
, taking
\kappa=\frac{1}{n}
gives

R_{n}(u)\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta}\vartheta\ln n+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[B_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t},-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})]+2\,.

(14)

It remains to bound the Bregman divergence term. A key property of self-concordant functions is that if
\mathcal{R}
is self-concordant, then so is
\mathcal{R}^{*}

(
Nemirovskii, 1996
, Property 2.VII)
. Hence, by Lemma
1
,

B_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(y,x)\leqslant\rho(\|y-x\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(x)})\,,

(15)

for all
x,y\in K
satisfying
\|y-x\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(x)}<1
, where
\rho(t)=-\ln(1-t)-t
. We claim that
\rho(t)\leqslant t^{2}
for
t\leqslant 1/2
. Let
f(t)=t^{2}-\rho(t)
for
t<1
. Then

f^{\prime}(t)=2t-\frac{1}{1-t}+1=\frac{t(2t-1)}{(t-1)}\,.

For all
t\leqslant 1/2
, we have
f^{\prime}(t)\geqslant 0
, hence
f(t)\geqslant f(0)=0
, which proves
\rho(t)\leqslant t^{2}
for
t\leqslant 1/2
. Applying this inequality to (
15
) yields

B_{\mathcal{R}^{*}}(y,x)\leqslant\|y-x\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(x)}^{2}\,,

for all
x,y\in K
with
\|y-x\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(x)}\leqslant 1/2
.
Since
\|\eta\hat{y}_{t}\|_{t}\leq 1/2
almost surely for all
t\in[n]
,
we can apply the above bound in (
14
), obtaining

\displaystyle R_{n}(u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta}\vartheta\ln n+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\|-\eta\hat{Y}_{t}+\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})}^{2}]+2

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}\vartheta\ln n+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{y}_{t}\|_{t}^{2}]+2\,,

where the last equality uses
\|\cdot\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}^{*}(-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1})}=\|\cdot\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})^{-1}}=\|\cdot\|_{t}
.
∎

B.2
Proof of Proposition
8

\heavytail

*

The proof of Proposition
8
makes use of the following lemma from
Bertsekas, 1973
that justifies exchanging the gradient and expectation operators, i.e.,
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\phi(\theta+\xi)]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla\phi(\theta+\xi)]
.

Lemma 13

(
Bertsekas, 1973
)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
\xi
be a random vector on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose distribution is absolutely continuous w.r.t the Lebesgue measure. Assume that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{E}|h(x-\xi)|<\infty
and define
H(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(x-\xi)]
. Then
h
is differentiable almost everywhere,
H
is differentiable everywhere and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla H(x)=\mathbb{E}[\nabla h(x-\xi)]\,.

Proof of Proposition
8
.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
\mathbb{E}\|\xi\|<+\infty
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}
. For all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}|\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)|\leqslant\big(\|\theta\|+\mathbb{E}\|\xi\|\big)\sup_{x\in K}\|x\|<+\infty\,.

From Lemma
13
, it follows that for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla\mathbb{E}[\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]=\nabla\mathcal{R}^{*}(\theta)\,.

So there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)]+C\quad\text{for all $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

The support function
\phi_{K}
is
L
-Lipschitz with
L=\sup_{x\in K}\|x\|
, so for all
\theta,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\phi_{K}(\theta+\xi)-\phi_{K}(\theta)\geqslant-L\|\xi\|\,.

Taking the expectation over
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}
yields

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\theta)-\phi_{K}(\theta)\geqslant-L\,\mathbb{E}\|\xi\|+C\,,\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(16)

For all
x\in K
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\nabla\mathcal{R}(x))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(x),x\rangle-\mathcal{R}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\phi_{K}(\nabla\mathcal{R}(x))-\mathcal{R}(x)\,.

Hence
(\mathcal{R}^{*}-\phi_{K})(\nabla\mathcal{R}(x))
goes to
-\infty
when
\mathcal{R}(x)
goes to
+\infty
.
Because
\mathcal{R}
is unbounded from above on
K
, this directly contradicts (
16
). So
\mathcal{D}
does not have a finite first moment.
∎

Note that the only properties of
\mathcal{R}
we have used are the strong convexity and the unboundedness. Hence, this property is far more general: for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
replicating a strongly convex function
\mathcal{R}
unbounded on
K
,
\mathcal{D}
does not admit a finite first moment.

C.2
Proof of Proposition
10

\hypercubeybounds

*

Note that the norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{t}\}_{t}
are the norms defined in Theorem
7
, with
\|\cdot\|_{t}=\|\cdot\|_{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x_{t})^{-1}}
for all
t\in[n]

Proof.

Let
t\in[n]
. For brevity, denote by
A
the action chosen by SC-FTPL at time
t
,
x=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[A]
and
Q=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[AA^{\top}]
.

Step 1: Computing the expression of
Q^{-1}
.

Let
i\neq j\in[d]
. Since the coordinates
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{j}
of the perturbation are independent, it follows that

Q_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}[A_{i}A_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]\mathbb{E}[A_{j}]=x_{i}x_{j}\,.

Because the action
A
is the solution of a linear program over
[-1,1]^{d}
, we can take
A\in\mathrm{extr}([-1,1]^{d})=\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, the set of extreme points of the hypercube. Hence, for all
i\in[d]
,
A_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
almost surely, and therefore

Q_{i,i}=\mathbb{E}[A_{i}^{2}]=1\,.

Consequently,

Q=xx^{\top}+\operatorname{Cov}(A)\,,\quad\text{where }\operatorname{Cov}(A)=\operatorname{diag}((1-x_{i}^{2})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}).

Since
x\in\nabla\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=(-1,1)^{d}
almost surely, the diagonal matrix
\operatorname{Cov}(A)
is non-singular.
A direct computation shows that

Q^{-1}\Big(\frac{-1}{1+\alpha}(\operatorname{Cov}A)^{-1}xx^{\top}+I_{d}\Big)(\operatorname{Cov}A)^{-1}\,,

(17)

where

\alpha\coloneq x^{\top}(\operatorname{Cov}A)^{-1}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{{x_{i}}^{2}}{1-{x_{i}}^{2}}\,.

Step 2: Bounding the local norm variance.

Let
y
denote the loss vector and
\hat{y}=Q^{-1}A\langle y,A\rangle
the loss vector estimator. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[\langle y,A\rangle A^{\top}Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}A\langle y,A\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[A^{\top}Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}A\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[\operatorname{tr}(AA^{\top}Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1})\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}\right)\,,

(18)

where the inequality uses the boundedness of the losses, the second equality uses the cyclic invariance of the trace, and the last follows from linearity of expectation.
For all
x_{i}\in(-1,1)^{d}
, we have

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)=\operatorname{diag}\!\left[\frac{2(1+x_{i}^{2})}{(1-x_{i}^{2})^{2}}\right]_{i=1}^{d},

from which
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}
follows by inversion.
Substituting the closed-form expressions of
Q^{-1}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}
into (
18
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1-x_{i}^{2}}{1+x_{i}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{d}{2}\,.

Step 3: Bounding the local norm almost surely.

We now derive an almost-sure bound on
\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant A^{\top}Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}A

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}A_{i}A_{j}[Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}[Q^{-1}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}Q^{-1}]_{i,j}\,.

Plugging in the closed-form expressions of
Q^{-1}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}(x)^{-1}
yields

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant d}\Big(\frac{-1}{1+\alpha}\frac{x_{i}x_{k}}{1-x_{i}^{2}}+\delta_{i,k}\Big)\frac{1}{1-x_{k}^{2}}\frac{(1-x_{k}^{2})^{2}}{2(1+x_{k}^{2})}\Big(\frac{-1}{1+\alpha}\frac{x_{k}x_{j}}{1-x_{k}^{2}}+\delta_{k,j}\Big)\frac{1}{1-x_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}}{1-x_{i}^{2}}\Big)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{1+x_{k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}}{1-x_{i}^{2}}\Big)\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{x_{k}}{1-x_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1+a_{k}^{2}}\,.

where
\delta_{i,j}=\bm{1}\{i=j\}
is the Kronecker delta.

Using
\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\frac{1}{1+x_{i}^{2}}\leq 1
for all
i
, and applying the triangle inequality, we obtain

\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{i}|}{1-x_{i}^{2}}\Big)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}{x_{k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{i}|}{1-x_{i}^{2}}\Big)\sum_{k=1}^{d}|x_{k}|+\frac{d}{2}\,.

(19)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\sum_{k=1}^{d}|x_{k}|\leqslant\sqrt{d\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{|x_{i}|}{1-x_{i}^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{1-x_{i}^{2}}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}^{2}}}=\sqrt{\alpha(d+\alpha)}\,.

Plugging theses inequalities into (

) gives

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d+\alpha}{2\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{d(d+\alpha)}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}^{2}}+\frac{d}{2}\,.

Finally,

and hence

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}\|_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant d+\sqrt{2}d+\frac{d}{2}\leqslant 3d\,.\qed

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle=\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\lVert\hat{y}_{t}\rVert^{2}]+2\langle y_{t},\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+d^{2}\eta\,\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert+2d^{2}+2\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+3\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert+2d^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{9n}{4}+2d^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+4n\,,

(32)

where the first inequality follows from the third bound of Proposition
12
and from the fact that
\lVert y_{t}\rVert\leqslant 1
, the second and fourth inequalities follow from the assumption that
\frac{n}{\ln n}\geqslant 2d^{2}
and the third inequality follows from the AM-GM inequality. Define

\displaystyle M_{t}\coloneq\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{-t}(\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}+4n^{2})\,.

The sequence
(M_{t})_{t}
is a positive stochastic process, adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t}
, with
M_{0}=4n^{2}
. From (
D.3
), we have that for all
t\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[M_{t}]\leqslant\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{-t}\Big(\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}+4n+4n^{2}\Big)=M_{t-1}\,.

Thus,
(M_{t})_{t}
is a supermartingale. Therefore, by Ville’s inequality, for all
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant n-1}M_{t}\geqslant\varepsilon\Big]\leqslant\frac{4n^{2}}{\varepsilon}\,.

Moreover, for all
t\in[n]
, it holds that

\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}=(1+\frac{1}{n})^{t}M_{t}-4n^{2}\leqslant e\,M_{t}\,.

Combining this last inequality with (
D.3
), we get

\displaystyle E\supseteq\Big\{\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant n-1}M_{t}\leqslant\frac{d^{2}}{8e}\Big(\frac{n}{\ln n}\Big)^{3}\Big\}\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t<n}\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}>\frac{d^{2}}{8}\Big(\frac{n}{\ln n}\Big)^{3}\Big]\leqslant\frac{32\,e}{d^{2}}\frac{(\ln n)^{3}}{n}\,.

(33)

Step 2: Bounding the regret
R_{n}
.

In order to bound the regret using similar arguments as in Theorem
7
, we introduce an appropriate stopping time and invoke Doob’s optional stopping theorem to bound the regret conditionally on
E
. Define the random variable

\tau\coloneq\inf\Big\{t<n:\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}>\frac{d^{2}}{8}\Big(\frac{n}{\ln n}\Big)^{3}\Big\}\wedge n\,.

Because
(\hat{Y}_{t})_{t}
is a stochastic process adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t}
,
\tau
is a stopping time with respect to the same filtration.
For all
u\in K
, we can decompose the regret of SC-FTPL w.r.t
u
as

R_{n}(u)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u\rangle\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=\tau+1}^{n}\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u\rangle\Big]\,.

(34)

We now proceed to bound each term of the right-hand side of (
34
). For the second term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=\tau+1}^{n}\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\tau<n)\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}|\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u\rangle|\Big|\tau<n\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big[\sup_{0\leqslant t<n}\lVert\hat{Y}_{t}\rVert^{2}>\frac{d^{2}}{8}\Big(\frac{n}{\ln n}\Big)^{3}\Big]\;2n

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{64\,e}{d^{2}}\,\ln^{3}n\,,

(35)

where the second inequality follows from the boundedness of vectors
y_{t}
and the last inequality follows from (
33
). Let

B_{t}\coloneq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\langle y_{s},A_{s}-u\rangle-\langle\hat{y}_{s},x_{s}-u\rangle\,.

The process
(B_{t})_{t}
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t}
and for all
t\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[B_{t}]=B_{t-1}+\langle y_{t},\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[A_{t}-u]\rangle-\langle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\hat{y}_{t}],x_{t}-u\rangle=B_{t-1}\,.

Therefore,
(B_{t})_{t}
is a martingale with
B_{0}=0
. Thus, by Doob’s optional stopping theorem,
\mathbb{E}[B_{\tau}]=0
, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\langle y_{t},A_{t}-u\rangle\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\langle\hat{y}_{t},x_{t}-u\rangle\Big]\,.

Moreover, for all
t\leqslant\tau
, we have that
\lVert\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert^{2}\leqslant\frac{d^{2}}{8}(\frac{n}{\ln n})^{3}
, and thus
\lVert\eta\,\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, we can bound
\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}[\langle\hat{y}_{t},x_{t}-u\rangle]
following the arguments from the proofs of Lemma
6
and of Theorem
7
, which yield

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\langle\hat{y}_{t},x_{t}-u\rangle\Big]\leqslant\frac{\vartheta\ln n}{\eta}+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}^{2}]+2\,.

(36)

Note that Theorem
7
could not have been applied directly, as we needed the bound
\lVert\eta\,\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
to hold almost-surely. Also, conditioning the expectation in the regret definition by the event
E
would not have enabled to use the unbiasedness of
A_{t}
and
\hat{y}_{t}
, which justifies the need for the stopping time argument we have developed. Combining (
D.3
) and (
36
), we have that

\displaystyle R_{n}(u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\vartheta\ln n}{\eta}+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}^{2}]+2+\frac{64\,e}{d^{2}}\,\ln^{3}n

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\ln n}{\eta}+\frac{3}{2}\,\eta\,n\,d^{2}+2+\frac{64\,e}{d^{2}}\,\ln^{3}n\,,

where the last inequality follows from the value of the self-concordance parameter
\vartheta=1
and from the bound on
\mathbb{E}[\lVert\hat{y}_{t}\rVert_{t}^{2}]
from Proposition
12
. Finally, plugging the value of
\eta
and taking the supremum over all
u\in K
, we have

R_{n}\leqslant d\sqrt{6\,n\ln n}+2+\frac{64\,e}{d^{2}}\,\ln^{3}n\,.\qed

D.4
Complexity Analysis of SC-FTPL on the Euclidean Ball

We establish that SC-FTPL on the Euclidean Ball with the self-concordant perturbation
\mathcal{D}
introduced in Proposition
11
, has a per-round complexity in
O(d)
.

Proof.

At each round
t\in[n]
, SC-FTPL performs the following three steps:

1.

sample
\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
,

2.

solve the linear program
A_{t}=\argmin_{a\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}\langle a,\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}-\xi_{t}\rangle
, and

3.

compute the estimator
\hat{y}_{t}=Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
.

Step 1: Sampling step.
Let
U\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
V
be a random variable on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution function

F:s\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}(\lVert\xi\rVert\leqslant s)\,.

Then we have

UV\sim\mathcal{D}

Hence, one can sample from
\mathcal{D}
by independently sampling
U
and
V
and setting
\xi_{t}=UV
. The multiplication of a scalar and a
d
-dimensional vector requires
O(d)
operations.

To generate
U\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, note that if
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
the normal distribution with mean
0
and covariance matrix
I_{d}
, then

\frac{N}{\lVert N\rVert}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

Thus, we can sample from
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in
O(d)
time.

The density of
V
satisfies

\displaystyle p_{V}(s)

\displaystyle=S_{d-1}\tilde{f}(s)s^{d-1}\,.

Since
p_{V}
can be evaluated up to any desired precision independently of
d
, sampling
V
incurs constant time in
d
.
Overall, the sampling step thus requires
O(d)
operations.

Step 2: Linear program step.

The linear minimization over the Euclidean ball admits the closed-form solution

A_{t}=\frac{\xi_{t}-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}}{\lVert\xi_{t}-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}\rVert}\,,

which can be computed in
O(d)
time.

Step 3: Estimation step.

From Lemma
14
we have

Q_{t}^{-1}=\frac{d-1}{K(\|\theta_{t}\|)}\,(I_{d}-p_{\theta_{t}})+\frac{1}{1-K(\|\theta_{t}\|)}\,p_{\theta_{t}}\,,

where
\theta_{t}=-\eta\hat{Y}_{t-1}
,
p_{\theta_{t}}=\frac{\theta_{t}\theta_{t}^{\top}}{\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert^{2}}
, and

K(x)=S_{d-2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{d}\varphi}{r^{2}+x^{2}+2rx\cos\varphi}d\varphi\,\tilde{f}(r)r^{d+1}\,dr\,.

The scalar
k_{t}=K(\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert)
can be computed numerically up to any desired precision independently of
d
. Then, we have

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}=\frac{d-1}{k_{t}}A_{t}+\Big(\frac{1}{1-k_{t}}-\frac{d-1}{k_{t}}\Big)\frac{\langle\theta_{t},A_{t}\rangle}{\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert^{2}}\theta_{t}\,.

(37)

So
\hat{y}_{t}=Q_{t}^{-1}A_{t}\langle y_{t},A_{t}\rangle
can be computed in
O(d)
time.

Combining all steps, SC-FTPL on the Euclidean ball enjoys a per-round complexity of
O(d)
, similarly to the hypercube case. For comparison, the OSMD algorithm analyzed by
Bubeck et al., 2012
also has a per-round complexity in
O(d)
on the Euclidean ball.
∎
