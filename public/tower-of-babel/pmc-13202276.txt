Demineralization of Camel Dentin for Dental Tissue Engineering
Authors: Zohaib Khurshid, Paul R Cooper, Abdul Samad Khan, George Dias, Javed Mazher, Syed Faraz Moin, Jithendra Ratnayake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDemineralisation of dentin effectively removes the crystalline hydroxyapatite phase while preserving the collagen matrix and associated bioactive proteins. This process results in an osteoinductive scaffold with favourable biological properties, making demineralised dentin a promising naturally derived biomaterial for dentoalveolar bone and orthopaedic bone tissue regeneration.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the structural and elemental characteristics of camel dentin matrix after demineralisation using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and citric acid (CA) at various concentrations at 24 hours, with the goal of assessing its suitability as a biomaterial for bone grafting.MethodsCamel dentin granules were extracted after removal of enamel and cementum and treated with EDTA and citric acid for 24 hours. Post-treatment, samples were analysed using scanning electron microscopy to assess morphological changes, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy for elemental composition, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy for molecular characterisation. The degree of demineralisation was compared across groups.ResultsScanning electron microscopy analysis revealed increased porosity and surface roughness following acid treatment, indicating demineralisation. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in calcium and phosphorus levels, confirming mineral loss. Fourier-transform infrared spectra confirmed efficient demineralisation, evidenced by reduced phosphate and carbonate bands and enhanced Amide I to III peaks, indicating increased exposure of the collagen framework. Raman spectra showed diminished intensity of phosphate and carbonate peaks in treated samples, further supporting successful demineralisation. EDTA treatment for 24 hours yielded the most pronounced structural and chemical alterations.ConclusionDemineralisation of camel dentin using EDTA and citric acid effectively alters its morphology and elemental composition. The optimised demineralisation protocol provides a promising approach for developing future demineralised dentin-derived graft materials from animal sources with potential osteoinductive properties.Key words: Camelus dromedarius, Dentin, Demineralisation, Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, Citric acid, Bone tissue engineering

Introduction

Oral tissues can be lost due to trauma or diseases such as dental caries, gingivitis, periodontitis and cancer.1 Depending on clinical need, these losses are rehabilitated or restored by regenerative procedures, dental materials and dental bone grafts. Regenerative dentistry is an evolving field within dentistry that focuses on restoring and regenerating damaged or lost tissues in the oral cavity.2 This includes techniques aimed at restoring enamel, dentin, dental pulp and periodontal tissues (such as gingiva, periodontal ligament and alveolar bone).2,3 Contemporary regenerative materials comprise natural or synthetic resorbable grafts that serve as scaffolds to support cell attachment, infiltration and the regrowth of hard and soft tissues.4,5 Among naturally derived materials, grafts such as autografts (harvested from the patient), allografts (from human donors) and xenografts (from animal sources) are commonly employed in clinical applications.4,6 In addition, a variety of synthetic materials have been synthesised for use as bone graft substitutes, including calcium phosphate-based materials such as hydroxyapatite and tricalcium phosphate, as well as bioceramics. These substitutes offer advantages such as biocompatibility and scaffold support for tissue growth, making them viable alternatives to traditional grafts in dental and orthopaedic procedures.4, 5, 6

Dentin is the calcified tissue layer of the tooth located beneath the outer covering of the enamel.7 It constitutes the bulk of the tooth and has a tubular structure, with 33% organic material, consisting primarily of collagen (90% type 1), as well as 10% ground substance and proteins, including enzymes, proteoglycans and growth factors.8 The organic matrix comprises a reservoir of several bioactive and regenerative molecules. These include insulin-like growth factors, transforming growth factors (TGFs), bone morphogenetic proteins and basic fibroblast growth factor, along with noncollagenous protein, such as dentin phosphoprotein.8 Additionally, dentin possesses high permeability due to its abundance of dentinal tubules, which extend from the pulp tissue to the dentino-enamel junction in the crown and further down to the dentino-cemental junction in the root. These tubules exhibit regional variations in both density and diameter. Due to its intricate structure, dentin can provide support as a scaffold for tissue regeneration.9,10

Dentin has a similar chemical composition to bone and is increasingly recognised as a valuable source for bone graft materials.11 Demineralised dentin has been derived from a range of sources, such as humans, sheep, pigs and cows.9,10,12 Human-derived dentin, which can be from a tooth of the donor (autogenic) or another individual (allogenic) has proven to be effective in serving as a regenerative material in dentistry. However, it exhibits some drawbacks.9,13 Firstly, if derived from the donor’s own tooth, the procedure may involve a traumatic surgical extraction.14 Secondly, limitations of allogenic, such as disease transmission, are drawbacks in the dental and bone graft market, as sources may be limited.4 Animal teeth, which are normally discarded, have been shown to be a source of bioactive materials for bone tissue regeneration.10,15 Using various chemical processes, dentin can be demineralised to generate a tubular organic matrix, rich in the growth factors and proteins, consequently having significant regenerative potential.16 The demineralised dentin serves as a scaffold for the application of stem cells, which can be seeded onto or into the matrix.11,17 When used as a graft in a bone defect, this matrix supports cell attachment, proliferation and differentiation, ultimately facilitating the regeneration of dental tissues such as dentin, pulp or periodontal tissues.18, 19, 20 Several demineralising agents have been used for the production of demineralised dentin, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), hydrochloric acid and citric acid (CA) or nitric acid which removes a major part of the crystalline apatite whilst maintaining the majority of the collagen type I and noncollagenous proteins such as (Dentin Sialoprotein, Dentin Matrix Protein-1 [DMP-1], Osteopontin [OPN] and Bone Sialoprotein [BSP]).20 Consequently, this results in an osteoinductive scaffold containing matrix components and bioactive molecules.10,16 These demineralising agents facilitate the dissolution of calcium ions present in the mineralised structure weakening the dentin's mineral matrix, leading to exposure of the underlying collagen matrix. Animal teeth provide an environmentally sustainable potential source of demineralised dentin for use in dental procedures due to their structural resemblance to human teeth and availability in certain regions of the world. In the Middle East, camels (Camelus dromedarius) are used as a source of food and milk, for transportation and as a cultural symbol. Dromedaries have 34 permanent teeth, including tusk-like canines used for crushing fibrous plants. Large flat molars in the back provide a powerful grinding action, while a tough dental pad replaces upper incisors for stripping vegetation. These features collectively enable the camel to efficiently consume and digest coarse forage.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the demineralisation of dentine matrix (DM) extracted from C. Dromedarius teeth using EDTA and CA and evaluate its physicochemical properties, which would possess osteoinductive properties conducive to the development of dental scaffolds for oral tissue regeneration. Following demineralisation, the samples were subjected to detailed protein content analyses, as well as chemical and morphological analyses. This project aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) by advancing regenerative bone therapies, SDG 9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure) through development of a novel biomaterial platform and SDG 12 (responsible consumption and production) by transforming camel tooth waste into a high-value bone graft substitute.

Materials and method

Preparation and demineralisation of dentin granules

The study methodology was approved by the ethical committee of the Deanship of Scientific Research (DSR), King Faisal University, Saudi Arabia (KFU-REC-2025-AUG –ETHICS2995). Camel (C. dromedarius) teeth were obtained from a local slaughterhouse in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. Upon collection, the pulp tissue, enamel and cementum were carefully removed from the teeth using a diamond bur and were subsequently disinfected by 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Merck, Darmstadt) as described previously.21 Subsequently, the camel dentin samples were stored in phosphate-buffered saline containing Pen/Strep (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4°C to facilitate decontamination. Once thoroughly cleaned, the teeth were ground into a powder using a commercial coffee mill (Figure 1). Throughout the grinding process, continuous cooling with liquid nitrogen (LN₂) was employed to prevent denaturation of the matrix proteins. After grinding, the camel dentin powder was washed 3 times with deionised (DI) water to ensure removal of any residual contaminants. Following this, the dentin powder was dried using a vacuum suction machine (Hawach Scientific) and then stored at −20°C until further use.

Camel tooth processing including removal of enamel and dental pulp and extraction of the dentin powder for further investigation. (A) Initial extraction of camel tooth showing remaining soft tissue. (B) Cleaned teeth weighed prior to processing. (C) Sectioning of teeth into small dentin blocks after removal of enamel and pulp. (D) After thorough washing of dentin blocks stored at −20°C. (E and F) Grinding of dentin under continuous liquid nitrogen cooling to prevent protein denaturation, followed by collection of processed dentin granules.

A total of 50 g of camel dentin was extracted from 8 teeth for further analysis. For demineralisation with EDTA (Modern Medical Equipment), a range of concentrations (5%, 10% and 17%) of EDTA were used for 24 hours (groups: E-1, E-2 and E-3). Similarly, for demineralisation with citric acid (Fisher Scientific), a range of concentrations (5%, 10% and 15%) were employed (groups CA-1, CA-2 and CA-3) for 24 hours. The 24-hour demineralisation period was selected to ensure sufficient removal of the inorganic hydroxyapatite phase while preserving the organic collagen matrix and associated bioactive proteins within the dentin matrix.20,22, 23, 24 Previous studies have shown that extended demineralisation durations enhance mineral dissolution and facilitate the exposure of matrix-bound growth factors, which are essential for the osteoinductive potential of demineralised dentin matrices.25,26

Protein content estimations

Following demineralisation, the dentin was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes to separate the supernatant from the residual dentin powder. The clear supernatant was carefully aspirated with a micropipette and transferred to a fresh sterile tube for protein analysis assayed for protein estimation. The Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Novagen) was used for all experimental groups described above (reference). When the treatment time for each reagent was complete, the supernatant was immediately transferred to a fresh tube and 10 μL in duplicate was used for protein estimation. The residual tooth powder was collected after treatment, underwent 2 washes with deionised water and was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for all samples, which were then stored at −20 °C.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to analyse the chemical and structural changes in camel dentin samples following demineralisation with varying concentrations of EDTA and citric acid.27,28 Dried dentin samples were finely grounded into powder and analysed using a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer with attenuated total reflectance mode. The powdered samples were placed directly on a diamond crystal window and spectra were recorded over the range of 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 8 cm⁻¹, averaging 32 scans per sample. Characteristic absorption bands corresponding to phosphate (PO₄³⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻) and the collagen-related amide I, II and III regions were identified to assess changes in mineral and organic composition. Data processing and peak assignment were performed using Origin software.

Micro-Raman analysis

Finely powdered demineralised camel dentin samples were utilised in micro-Raman spectroscopy to examine the impact of chelation and acidic-based demineralisation treatment on the structural and compositional characteristics of the hydroxyapatite phase.29,30 Raman spectra were obtained using a He-Cd gas laser with a fixed laser power of 300 mW, employing the confocal configuration of the Raman spectrometer (Horiba Labram Evolution-2). The laser's excitation wavelength was set to 442 nm and Raman signals were collected through a 50× objective lens with a 50 µm confocal aperture. Data acquisition and spectrum plotting were conducted using Labspec-6 software (Horiba). It is to be noted that the Raman data is collected at the smaller confocal hole size, whilst the samples are excited at a lower laser power, for which a numerical density filter of 25% is used, combined with a larger integration time (100s) to avoid bioluminescence-related interference in the spectra.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis

The surface morphology and elemental composition of demineralised camel dentin were analysed using a SEM (JEOL JSM-2300) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector (Oxford Instruments), operated at 15 kV and 15 mA. Prior to imaging, the samples were sputter-coated with palladium and mounted on SEM stubs for analysis.31

Statistical analysis

Protein content analysis with Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) protein assay were performed in triplicate (n = 3 per group) and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 27.0; IBM Corp.). Differences in protein content among treatment groups were evaluated using 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Because homogeneity of variances was not assumed, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using the Games–Howell test. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The stacked FTIR spectra of camel dentin samples untreated and treated with varying concentrations of EDTA (5%, 10% and 17%) and citric acid (5%, 10% and 17%) revealed progressive changes in the spectral features associated with the organic matrix, particularly collagen. Increasing chemical agent’s concentration led to a progressive reduction of phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and carbonate (CO₃²⁻) peaks, accompanied by enhancement of collagen-related Amide I, II and III bands. These spectral changes indicate gradual mineral loss and exposure of the organic matrix, with citric acid showing slightly stronger demineralising effects than EDTA (Figure 3).

Figure 4 presents the full-range FTIR spectra (4000-500 cm⁻¹) of camel dentin after 24 hours treatment with varying concentrations of EDTA (5%-17%) and citric acid (5%-15%), compared with untreated dentin. The spectra show concentration-dependent reductions in phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and carbonate (CO₃²⁻) bands, indicating progressive dissolution of the mineral hydroxyapatite phase. Correspondingly, collagen-related Amide I (∼1650 cm⁻¹), Amide II (∼1550 cm⁻¹) and Amide III (∼1250 cm⁻¹) peaks became more prominent, reflecting increased exposure of the organic matrix. Citric acid produced a more pronounced attenuation of mineral bands than EDTA, confirming its stronger demineralising capability.

EDTA, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid; FTIR, Fourier-transform infrared.

Figure 5 presents the magnified FTIR spectra of camel dentin after 24 hours demineralisation using EDTA at concentrations of 5%, 10% and 17% and CA at concentrations of 5%, 10% and 15%, focusing on the amide, phosphate and carbonate regions. In both treatments, the Amide I (∼1650 cm⁻¹), Amide II (∼1550 cm⁻¹) and Amide III (∼1250 cm⁻¹) bands attributed to the C=O stretching and N–H bending vibrations of collagen became progressively more prominent with increasing concentration, indicating greater exposure of the organic matrix as mineral content decreased. Conversely, the phosphate (PO₄³⁻) bands near 1090 to 1030 cm⁻¹ and 603 to 565 cm⁻¹, associated with the hydroxyapatite phase, showed a marked reduction in intensity, confirming gradual dissolution of the mineral component. A distinct carbonate (CO₃²⁻) band around 1410 to 1450 cm⁻¹, representing B-type carbonate substitution within the apatite lattice, was also detected in both EDTA- and CA-treated samples. The intensity of this band decreased with higher chelating-agent concentrations, indicating the removal of carbonate groups along with phosphate ions from the hydroxyapatite lattice. The decrease in both phosphate and carbonate peaks was more pronounced in citric acid–treated samples than in those treated with EDTA, highlighting the stronger chelating and demineralising potential of citric acid. Overall, these spectra confirm that both EDTA and citric acid induce concentration-dependent demineralisation of camel dentin, characterised by progressive mineral loss (phosphate and carbonate) and enhanced exposure of the collagen-rich organic matrix.

Citric acid (CA) treated demineralised camel dentin behaved differently when compared to EDTA treatment. SEM analysis of camel dentin treated with 5% CA for 24 hours revealed partial surface demineralisation characterised by the presence of abundant needle-like or plate-like crystalline deposits, likely representing calcium citrate by-products that obscured many dentinal tubule openings (Figure 8). In contrast, treatment with 10% CA resulted in a denser accumulation of crystalline calcium citrate deposits, with minimal visibility of tubule orifices. The surface was heavily masked and dentin granules showed mild erosion, suggesting deeper mineral chelation and limited organic matrix accessibility. The 15% CA group exhibited the most pronounced effects, showing extensive rosette-like crystal growth and significantly clearer tubule exposure. High-magnification SEM images revealed open, well-defined tubule lumens partially surrounded by elongated crystalline structures. The crystalline surface features observed in SEM are tentatively attributed to calcium–chelate complexes formed during demineralisation; however, this interpretation remains speculative and would require additional phase-specific analytical techniques for definitive confirmation.

Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis

Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis of camel dentin treated with varying concentrations of EDTA and citric acid (CA) for 24 hours revealed distinct patterns in elemental composition and demineralisation behaviour. In the EDTA-treated groups, 5% EDTA preserved substantial mineral content (Ca: 40.60%, P: 18.67%) with a Ca/P ratio of 2.72, indicating partial demineralisation and moderate phosphate leaching. At 10% EDTA, calcium levels increased considerably (48.55%), while phosphorus levels decreased (13.11%), resulting in the highest Ca/P ratio of 4.30, suggesting surface calcium retention or preferential phosphate extraction. At 17% EDTA, both calcium (21.83%) and phosphorus (14.04%) declined, lowering the Ca/P ratio to 1.66 and indicating extensive mineral loss and exposure of the organic matrix, accompanied by elevated carbon (21.13%) and magnesium content. Notably, magnesium deposition showed a concentration-dependent increase with EDTA treatment, peaking at 17%.

In the CA treated groups, 5% CA produced moderate mineral depletion (Ca/P: 2.16), while 10% CA led to the most aggressive phosphate removal (P: 5.23%) and peak carbon accumulation (31.37%), reflected in a Ca/P ratio of 3.16 and significant collagen exposure, potentially masked by calcium citrate deposition. At 15% CA, calcium and phosphorus levels partially rebounded (Ca: 18.17%, P: 7.98%) with a Ca/P ratio of 4.03, indicating demineralised but more compositionally balanced surface. Magnesium levels generally declined with increasing CA concentration. Although decreasing Ca/P ratios reflect progressive mineral extraction, reduced mineral content alone does not necessarily translate into superior biological functionality. Over-demineralisation may compromise matrix architecture and structural stability. Accordingly, scaffold optimisation requires regulated mineral–organic phase balance rather than complete mineral removal25 (Figure 9).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to successfully demineralise camel dentin for future dental tissue engineering applications. The primary objective of this study was to optimise the demineralisation protocol for producing camel demineralised dentin matrix using EDTA and CA as chelating agents. Our findings demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent relationship between the applied demineralising agents and the protein content released in the supernatants. EDTA, a widely used chelating agent in endodontics, exhibited a gradual and controlled demineralisation pattern. Increasing EDTA concentrations from 5% to 17% led to a moderate elevation in protein release consistent with its selective chelation of calcium ions while largely preserving the organic matrix. These results align with prior reports highlight EDTA's ability to demineralise dentin while minimising collagen denaturation.32 In contrast, citric acid induced a markedly stronger and concentration-dependent protein extraction, with protein content increasing from approximately 245 µg at 5% to over 440 µg at 15%. This enhanced efficiency is attributable to citric acid’s lower pH and more aggressive decalcifying action, which rapidly disrupts calcium–phosphate bonds within the dentin matrix.33 The increased protein release observed at higher citric acid concentrations (CA-15) may indicate enhanced exposure of matrix-bound proteins; however, it may also reflect partial collagen denaturation or nonspecific matrix disruption. However, such extensive demineralisation may compromise matrix integrity. Marshall et al.34 reported surface collapse and collagen destabilisation following prolonged exposure to acidic demineralising agents, highlighting potential risks associated with excessive mineral removal. Additional studies have shown that citric acid can significantly increase dentin surface roughness and may leave residual crystalline deposits, necessitating careful post-treatment rinsing to avoid adverse effects. Conversely, EDTA has been shown to better preserve collagen ultrastructure while facilitating the release of biologically active growth factors, including transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β1), fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which are critical for regenerative applications.35 Excessive demineralisation may compromise structural integrity and result in unintended matrix breakdown, suggesting that optimal scaffold preparation requires a balance between mineral removal and preservation of the organic matrix. As total protein quantification does not differentiate between functional growth factor release and structural matrix breakdown, the elevated protein levels in CA-15 should be interpreted cautiously. In our study, the differences in protein extraction profiles between EDTA and CA reinforce the idea that reagent choice must balance effective demineralisation with preservation of dentinal matrix bioactivity. Thus, EDTA appears more appropriate for applications requiring gradual mineral removal and bioactivity preservation, while CA may be better suited for protocols targeting rapid matrix dissolution and growth factor harvesting.

In this study, the degree of demineralisation of camel tooth dentin induced by EDTA and CA was quantified using FTIR spectroscopy. FTIR analysis of camel dentin treated with varying concentrations of EDTA and citric acid revealed a clear concentration-dependent demineralisation effect. Both agents induced a progressive reduction in mineral content, as indicated by the decreasing intensity of phosphate-associated peaks and the relative enhancement of the Amide II and III bands, signifying increased organic matrix exposure. Notably, 17% EDTA resulted in pronounced collagen exposure and partial matrix disruption, while 15% citric acid induced similar, though slightly less intense, effects. The FTIR peak positions observed in the present camel dentin study closely correspond to those reported for human and bovine dentin.11,36, 37, 38 Characteristic phosphate bands (∼1090-1030 cm⁻¹, 603-565 cm⁻¹) and amide peaks (Amide I ∼1650 cm⁻¹, Amide II ∼1550 cm⁻¹, Amide III ∼1250 cm⁻¹) appeared at similar wavenumbers. In addition, the carbonate peaks (ν₃ CO₃ ≈ 1405-1450 cm⁻¹) diminished markedly, signifying the loss of carbonate-substituted apatite during demineralisation.36,39 The consistent reduction in phosphate and carbonate intensities, along with enhancement of the amide bands, confirms that the chemical response of camel dentin to chelating agents follows a comparable demineralisation pattern to that of other mammalian dentin.36,38,39 Together, these findings support the potential of camel dentin as a bioactive and structurally resilient substrate for dental bone tissue engineering.

Ratio of Raman intensities of the collagen peaks such as amide -I, II or III peaks to that of the sharpest PO43- characteristic peak, which is related to symmetric stretching mode of tetrahedron of oxygen atoms surrounding the phosphate atom, is an excellent indicator of the dentin demineralisation.40,41 It is interesting to note that the ratio (Iγ/Ic)is found to increase much more rapidly during the citric acid demineralisation, increasing to 1.2 for 10% CA concentration. Whilst for EDTA treatment the ratio is ∼ 0.55 for 10% EDTA concentration. This trend aligns with Tsuda et al.40, who observed that Raman spectral features of human dentin, including amide and phosphate bands, are sensitive to chemical treatments. Their findings confirmed that NaOCl treatment removed organic content (loss of amide peaks) and elevated the carbonate-to-phosphate band ratio due to increased CO₃ incorporation (1072 cm⁻¹). It is well known that during any demineralisation of dentin first the carbonate (CO32-) phase is formed in the hydroxyapatite lattice replacing the PO4 sites. We have found that the intermediate carbonate phases are readily formed even at lower CA concentrations, the carbonate to phosphate mineral peak ratio (Ie/Ic)was 1.33 at 5% CA, paving the way for more rapid demineralisation. The Raman analysis revealed that CA-15 exhibited a lower amide-to-phosphate peak ratio (0.55) compared with CA-10 (1.2), suggesting that excessive citric acid concentration may induce partial alteration or destabilisation of collagen-associated peptide structures. While moderate demineralisation (eg, CA-10) appears to enhance organic matrix exposure, higher acid concentration may lead to over-demineralisation and potential structural modification of the collagen framework. In contrast, the EDTA treatment has shown a slower formation of intermediate CO32- phase in the HA lattice. The mineral peak ratio (Ie/Ic)slowly increased from 0.11 to 0.15 following increasing EDTA from 5% to 17%, leading to slower carbonate intermediation in demineralisation process. Consequently, during our study, the lower concentration of CA was found to be more effective in dentin demineralisation.

The SEM findings demonstrate that increasing EDTA and citric acid concentrations enhance dentinal tubule exposure, with 10% and 17% EDTA providing optimal microstructural features for regeneration, while 15% citric acid showed improved patency; however, higher concentrations in both treatments may compromise mechanical integrity and result in crystal accumulation. The observed structural damage at higher EDTA and citric acid concentrations is most likely due to over-demineralisation, leading to excessive dissolution of peritubular and intertubular dentin minerals. Prolonged or high-concentration chelation disrupts the collagen–mineral interface, weakens the collagen scaffold and can promote microstructural collapse and crystal reprecipitation on the surface. This highlights the need for controlled concentration and exposure time to balance effective mineral removal while preserving dentin’s structural integrity and bioactivity. Notably, SEM observations of dense surface deposits in CA-15 samples likely represent surface precipitates rather than preserved organic matrix integrity. Therefore, the presence of surface precipitates should not be interpreted as evidence of improved collagen preservation. These findings collectively indicate that controlled demineralisation is critical to maintaining structural balance between mineral removal and organic matrix stability. These data underscore the need for controlled application and post-treatment rinsing to preserve structure and bioactivity. Enhanced dentinal tubule exposure and partial demineralisation increase the bioavailability of matrix-bound growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins and TGF-β, which are known to promote angiogenesis, cell migration and osteogenic cell proliferation.

EDX analysis of human dentin typically revealed calcium (Ca) as the main element (50-70 wt%), followed by phosphorus (P, 15-20 wt%) and oxygen (20-30 wt%) due to the presence of hydroxyapatite. Carbon, mainly from the collagen matrix, generally remains below 10%, while sodium (Na) and magnesium (Mg) are found in trace amounts (<1%).42 In our study, 24-hour EDTA treatment of camel dentin at 5% and 17% concentrations produced Ca/P ratios of 2.72 and 1.66, respectively, values consistent with previously reported ranges for demineralised human and bovine dentin (Ca/P ≈ 2).11,26,43 These results align with the findings of Unverdi Eldeniz et al.44, who observed reduced Ca and P levels and altered Ca/P ratios in human dentin following treatment with EDTA and CA, particularly under aggressive citric acid treatment conditions. Similarly, Anastasiadis and Nassar32 reported that EDTA preserved mineral integrity and induced minimal surface degradation compared with stronger acids. Furthermore, Farzad et al.45 demonstrated that EDTA-conditioned human dentin blocks exhibited near-complete surface loss of Ca and P, with EDX spectra dominated by organic elements such as carbon (55.1%) and nitrogen (26.6%). These data align with our camel dentin EDX profile at higher EDTA concentrations, suggesting a comparable demineralisation response. Holiel et al.46 further reported partial demineralisation of EDTA-treated human dentin matrix, with high Ca (55.8%) and P (40.7%) retention, supporting its regenerative potential in pulp-capping applications. The observed variations in Ca/P ratios and elemental composition between camel and human dentin may stem from biological and environmental factors. Dietary differences, such as the consumption of mineral-rich desert vegetation by camels, as well as ecological adaptations to arid environments, likely influence dentin mineralisation.21 Although 17% EDTA resulted in the lowest Ca/P ratio, reduced mineral content alone does not equate to superior osteoinductive potential. Over-demineralisation may compromise matrix integrity; thus, balanced mineral–organic modulation is critical for functional scaffold design. Overall, EDTA treatments yielded a more controlled and progressive demineralisation profile with clear organic matrix exposure at higher concentrations, whereas CA treatments induced rapid phosphate depletion, increased surface carbon and potential mineral reprecipitation and post-treatment protocols to preserve dentin bioactivity.

The production of biomaterials from camel dentin can significantly contribute to SDG.47 Firstly, by utilising camel dentin as a biomaterial, which is a by-product of the meat and dairy industry in Saudi Arabia, the project exploits a renewable resource, thereby reducing waste and maximising resource utilisation. This aligns with the goal of sustainable resource management. Secondly, the use of camel dentin reduces the reliance on synthetic materials, which often have a significant environmental footprint. By doing this, the project can potentially mitigate environmental degradation and pollution. Thirdly, by utilising camel dentin, a resource abundant in Saudi Arabia, mass production of such a biomaterial could support the growth of local industries, particularly in the healthcare sector, thus promoting the development of local industries as outlined in Saudi Vision 2030.48 Lastly, the biomaterial derived from camel dentin has potential applications in dental tissue engineering. Overall, the demineralised camel dentin can be incorporated into carriers such as alginate, hyaluronic acid, collagen, silk fibroin, photocurable gelatin and polymers to produce biomaterials in forms of hydrogels, scaffolds or malleable putty’s for drug delivery.49, 50, 51, 52 These carriers not only improve handling properties but also support osteogenic activity and bone regeneration.17,51,52 Recently, Chen et al.49 embedded granular human DDM into a photo-curable silk fibroin/hyaluronic acid hydrogel, creating a mouldable, stable scaffold. The composite showed high cytocompatibility, enhanced osteogenesis in vitro and superior bone regeneration in rat and beagle models, outperforming DDM alone.49 We can hence expect to develop a dental bone graft putty with demineralised camel dentin will be suitable tooth-derived xenograft. The production of scaffolds derived from camel dentin not only has the potential to advance healthcare practices but also contributes significantly to the broader SDGs. A limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size (n = 3 per group), which may restrict the statistical power and generalisability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and further studies with larger sample sizes are recommended to validate the observed trends. Despite the promising physicochemical characteristics demonstrated in this study, clinical translation requires further investigation. Future studies involving cytocompatibility assays, inflammatory response evaluation and preclinical animal models will be critical to establish safety and translational feasibility.

Conclusion

Physicochemical characterisation of camel dentin following EDTA and citric acid demineralisation demonstrated substantial mineral removal, treatment-dependent protein release and preservation of the collagen framework, supporting its suitability as a candidate scaffold material. Higher citric acid concentrations resulted in greater protein extraction but were associated with more aggressive demineralisation and potential structural compromise. In contrast, 17% EDTA for 24 hours provided a balanced profile of effective mineral removal, moderate protein release and matrix preservation, representing the most controlled condition among those tested. These findings suggest that controlled EDTA-based demineralisation offers a favourable physicochemical foundation for camel dentin–derived graft materials. Further biological validation through targeted growth factor analysis and in vitro and in vivo studies is required to confirm functional regenerative performance. Future investigations should focus on quantifying the release of bioactive growth factors, including TGF-β1, FGF-2 and VEGF and evaluating biological performance through in vitro cell culture assays and in vivo animal models. Such studies are essential to validate the regenerative potential and clinical applicability of demineralised camel dentin as a novel biomaterial for dental bone grafting and pulp tissue engineering.
