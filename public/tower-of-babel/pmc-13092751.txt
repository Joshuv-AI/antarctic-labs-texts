Visceral adiposity and insulin resistance in polycystic ovarian syndrome – A cross-sectional, observational study
Authors: Anushri Sridharan, Arun Guddeti, Sai Ramya Vasamsetti, Sanjay Kumar, Sayyad Aleem, Sravanthi Mashetty, Lalitha AV Alekya, KVS Hari Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is the pathogenic mechanism responsible for polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) and excess of visceral adipose tissue (VAT).ObjectivesWe examined VAT and IR using the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) in patients with PCOS and controls.Study designIn this cross-sectional, observational study, we included 74 patients newly diagnosed with PCOS and a similar number of age- and weight-matched healthy women as controls. Haematological, biochemical, and hormonal parameters were estimated from a fasting blood sample taken during the follicular phase, and VAT was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA). The results were analyzed using relevant statistical methods, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsThe study participants (n = 148) had a mean age of 23.6 ± 3.7 years, body weight of 59.9 ± 11.6 kg, and a body mass index (BMI) of 25.2 ± 3.2 kg/m². Among the PCOS patients, 25 had a normal BMI and 49 were obese. The VAT (grams) was comparable between patients (484.3 ± 218.2) and controls (439.9 ± 212.8) (p = 0.1591). The percentage of fat in the trunk/legs was higher in PCOS patients (0.9 ± 0.1) than controls (0.87 ± 0.08) (p = 0.0329). Correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation of 17-hydroxy progesterone (17OHP) with many adiposity parameters in both groups (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe VAT was similar in PCOS and controls, and HOMA showed significant correlation with VAT. 17 OHP correlated with many metabolic and hormonal parameters in both groups. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm our findings.

Introduction

Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) is a hormonal and metabolic disorder of heterogeneous origin with a wide range of systemic consequences [1]. Obesity and PCOS share a bidirectional relationship, and the majority of patients with PCOS are obese. Obesity causes hyperinsulinemia, which may further promote the progression of PCOS [2]. Obesity is denoted by excess of adipose tissue deposited in either subcutaneous or visceral compartments. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is linked to increased risk of metabolic, cardiovascular disorders and methods to estimate VAT include computed tomography (CT), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [3]. DEXA is a widely available, reliable method for estimating VAT and can also be used to assess regional fat distribution, including android and gynoid fat distribution [4].

Metabolic risk in PCOS patients is directly linked to increased VAT in addition to the underlying global obesity. Many authors have examined regional fat distribution and its correlation with metabolic and hormonal parameters in patients with PCOS [5], [6]. Barber et al. have showed that the regional fat distribution is similar between patients with PCOS and body mass index (BMI) matched control women despite differences in the underlying insulin resistance [7]. Studies conducted in Caucasians have shown differences in regional fat distribution between PCOS patients and controls [8]. South Asians have a different phenotype, and whether regional fat differences exist in patients from India remains unclear. Despite numerous studies, it is still uncertain whether obese PCOS patients have a different metabolic risk profile compared to lean PCOS patients [9], [10], [11]. Hence, we conducted this study with a primary objective of comparing the VAT between patients with PCOS and BMI matched controls. Our secondary objectives were to investigate the differences in regional fat distribution and correlation between the metabolic and hormonal parameters in patients with PCOS versus controls.

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted this cross-sectional, observational study at a tertiary-level referral hospital in India from June to December 2024. All women (aged 18–35 years) with a new diagnosis of PCOS were included. We excluded patients with known thyroid and metabolic disorders, those on long-term medications (glucocorticoids, thyroxine, estrogen, testosterone, and vitamin D), and individuals with systemic disorders (chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, hypogonadism, and Cushing’s syndrome) affecting VAT. Age- and BMI-matched healthy women with normal menstrual history, either accompanying the patients or volunteering from hospital nursing staff, served as the control group. All participants were informed about the study's objectives and procedures. The local ethics committee approved the protocol (523-A/35/1/2019 – Admin dated 26th Aug 2023), and all participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Sample size

The required sample size was estimated assuming a case–control ratio of 1:1, with a 95% confidence level and 80% statistical power. Based on these assumptions, the calculated sample size was 74 participants per group. This sample size is comparable to previously published studies evaluating body composition parameters in women with PCOS [12].

Study measures

A fasting blood sample after an overnight fast of minimum 12 h was collected from each participant between 0800 and 0900 hr and analyzed for hematological, biochemical, and hormonal parameters. All samples were collected between the 2nd and 5th day of either a spontaneous or progesterone withdrawal-induced menstrual cycle. Samples were checked for glucose, insulin, lipid parameters, thyroid hormones, luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), testosterone, prolactin (PRL), and 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17OHP). IR was assessed by the HOMA method using fasting glucose and insulin levels [13]. Hormone levels were estimated by the chemiluminescence method and the intra-assay and inter-assay variability in our laboratory were below 2% and 4% respectively.

The body composition parameters such as VAT, total fat mass (TFM), total body fat (TBF), android/gynoid ratio (AGR), and percentage fat mass (%FM) were measured by DEXA using the Hologic Horizon Wi S/N 306382 M (Hologic® Inc., Massachusetts, USA; coefficient of variation 0.55%). This machine is calibrated for body composition according to NHANES standards and was calibrated daily with the manufacturer-provided phantom device. It was also periodically reviewed by service engineers for quality assurance. All DEXA scans were performed by the same operator to reduce operator-dependent variability. Ultrasonography was conducted transabdominally by the same operator to measure ovarian volume and endometrial thickness.

Study definitions

The diagnosis of PCOS and categorization into four phenotypes (A to D) was based on the Rotterdam criteria [14]. Obesity or overweight was diagnosed based on BMI according to Asian guidelines, with a BMI of more than 25 kg/m2 classified as obesity and between 23.5 and 24.9 kg/m2 as overweight [15]. Any participant with elevated 17OHP (> 2 ng/mL) was eliminated from the study because the ACTH stimulation test for confirming late onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia (LOCAH) was not routinely available at our institute.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data are presented as the number of patients (percentage) or as mean (standard deviation). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Considering the heterogeneity in obesity status among women with PCOS, participants were further stratified based on BMI into lean and obese categories according to Asian BMI criteria, and subgroup analyses were performed accordingly. Spearman’s correlation test was used to evaluate correlations between body fat distribution parameters and hormonal or metabolic variables. In addition, binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent variables associated with the presence of PCOS. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism Software, Version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Discussion

Principal findings

Our study showed that VAT was similar in patients with PCOS and controls, whereas the percentage of fat in the trunks and legs was higher in the PCOS group. De Melo et al. have shown that PCOS patients have higher central adiposity and VAT compared to controls despite having similar total body fat [16]. The discrepancy observed in our sample could be due to the lower mean age of our participants and differences in the phenotype of PCOS in comparison to other published report from India [17]. Ramessur et al. demonstrated that the total fat mass is similar in patients with PCOS, and linear regression analysis showed that PCOS patients had higher visceral fat than peripheral fat [18]. Our data also indicated that the percentage of fat in the trunk is greater than in the legs, and this central adiposity could be a key pathogenic factor driving metabolic complications in PCOS.

We analyzed VAT using DEXA alone and did not compare the same with other methodologies like CT or MRI. DEXA may have certain known limitations including reduced accuracy, overestimation of the VAT in patients with high BMI, technical challenges and lack of validation across all ethnicities and age groups [19], [20]. DEXA calculates visceral fat as the total fat between the top of the pelvis and a point 20% of the distance towards the base of the skull minus the fat located just beneath the skin on both sides of the abdominal cavity, which is an indirect estimate of the subcutaneous fat using a system software program. However, changes in the body dimension and varying amount of the subcutaneous fat on the dorsal and ventral surfaces may affect the accuracy of the VAT estimation by DEXA [21].

Comparative analyses

To further account for the heterogeneity of obesity in PCOS, we performed BMI-stratified analyses comparing lean and obese PCOS with corresponding control groups. Body composition parameters were largely comparable within BMI strata, although obese women with PCOS demonstrated a higher trunk-to-leg fat ratio, suggesting relatively greater central fat distribution in this subgroup. Similar patterns of regional adiposity in PCOS have been reported in recent studies evaluating DXA-based body composition [16]. This may be because alterations in body composition require a prolonged period of hormonal imbalance. Binary logistic regression analysis further demonstrated that elevated LH and fasting blood glucose were independently associated with PCOS, while HDL showed an inverse association. Increased ovarian volume on ultrasonography also remained a significant predictor of PCOS in the model. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the combined hormonal, metabolic, and ovarian morphological features underlying the pathophysiology of PCOS [22].

Our data showed a positive correlation between HOMA IR and VAT in both the PCOS and control populations. Hyperinsulinemia drives increased androgen synthesis in the ovarian theca cells and also contributes to visceral fat accumulation through its action on lipoprotein lipase [23]. Another interesting observation from our study is the strong correlation of 17 OHP with many metabolic parameters and VAT in both the PCOS and control groups. 17 OHP is an important intermediate in the androgen biosynthetic pathway and mediates many of its actions by binding to the glucocorticoid receptor [24]. 17 OHP transactivates the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) present in the hepatocytes which further leads to upregulation of the gluconeogenesis [24]. 17 OHP also serves as a direct marker of the underlying IR with increased levels seen in patients with type 2 diabetes and PCOS [25]. Rosenfield et al. demonstrated that 17 OHP is an excellent surrogate marker for increased bioactive androgens in women with PCOS and when stimulated by gonadotropin, provides an excellent predictor of ovarian hyperandrogenism [26]. Identifying antagonists to 17 OHP could pave the way for new options in managing the metabolic complications of PCOS. Other studies evaluating 17 OHP levels also showed similar findings to ours [27]. The metabolic abnormalities, including dyslipidemia and IR, were more pronounced in the PCOS group than in controls, as expected in the disease's pathogenesis.

Phenotypic and ethnic variations

PCOS is divided into four phenotypes based on endocrine and metabolic abnormalities. The study by Pluta et al. showed that VAT was the same across different PCOS phenotypes [28]. Phenotype A was the most common type in our study population, and we did not evaluate VAT in each phenotype due to the small sample size. There are many PCOS phenotypes based on obesity (obese, lean), presence or absence of hyperandrogenism, or insulin resistance, among others. Identifying these phenotypic characteristics helps in selecting the appropriate medications for patients with PCOS. Keyif et al. studied the effect of insulin resistance and dysfunctional adiposity indices in patients with PCOS and reported that they provide valuable information about the underlying metabolic risks in these patients [29]. Rosenfield et al. have showed that the excess abdominal obesity is a common feature in both typical and atypical PCOS due to ovarian hyperandrogenism resulting from enhanced responsiveness to the circulating gonadotropins [30]. This has paved way for classifying the PCOS into reproductive and metabolic subtypes.

Previous reports have shown several racial and ethnic differences in the hormonal and metabolic features of the PCOS [31]. South Asian and Mediterranean women have higher hirsutism than Caucasian women due to differences in the genetic expression of the 5 α reductase enzyme [32]. The metabolic abnormalities are also different amongst the patients with PCOS of different ethnicities [33]. In addition, the metabolic abnormalities are more common in adolescent PCOS and the cardiovascular risk may persist the same or increase in the adulthood [34], [35]. Our data pertains only to Indian women and mostly in the second and third decade of life and prospective observation of this cohort could give more information regarding the future cardiometabolic complications.

Clinical and research implications

Our study has several implications for managing patients with PCOS. First, visceral adiposity in PCOS is comparable to that of the control population. Second, the role of 17OHP should be expanded beyond merely ruling out LOCAH. Lastly, early identification of the associated metabolic dysfunction in PCOS is essential to prevent morbidity. However, the role of 17OHP should be considered with caution in the absence of a multivariate analysis. Recent reports have suggested that PCOS and obesity disrupt the reproductive function either synergistically or independent to each other [36]. Clinicians caring for the PCOS patients and researchers in this area should focus on evaluating the individual contribution of the visceral adiposity and hormonal dysfunction in the pathophysiology of PCOS helping in devising better management strategies.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include assessing VAT in newly diagnosed PCOS, including an age- and BMI-matched control group, and using the DEXA technique to measure VAT, which is a reliable diagnostic tool in clinical practice. The limitations include a small sample size, failure to evaluate other adiposity measures, cross-sectional design with failure to establish the causality, lack of comparing the DEXA data with the CT or MRI and selection of a convenient sample from the attendants and hospital staff which may not be a true representation of the population. We derived the controls from a convenient sample which may induce a selection bias. Moreover, the sample is derived from the same ethnic group of the patients which may have familial clustering of the risk factors for PCOS [37]. We did not control for the variation in the diet and physical activity amongst our study population which could affect the results. Our study findings, being derived from the Indian population may not be applicable to other ethnicities.

Conclusions

To conclude, the VAT was similar in patients with PCOS and controls, and 17OHP showed a significant correlation with adiposity markers in both groups. Further research with a larger number of patients, longitudinal design and adipose tissue assessment using different methods is necessary to confirm the findings observed in our study.
