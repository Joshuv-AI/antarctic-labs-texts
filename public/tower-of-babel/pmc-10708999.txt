Photocatalytic CO2 reduction of 2D/0D CoAl-LDH@Cu2O catalyst with p-n heterojunction
Authors: Zhiyao Wu, Xiyang Wang, Shuang Deng, Xupeng Qin, Qilin Han, Yu Zhou, Yanqiu Zhu, Nannan Wang, Chunlin He, Yimin A. Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryLayered double hydroxides (LDHs) are widely used in catalytic field, especially in photocatalysis, benefiting from the ultrathin 2D structure and luxuriant surface functional groups. However, the wide band gap and low utilization rate of solar spectrum affect their photocatalytic performance. Herein, we integrated n-type CoAl-LDH with p-type Cu2O nanoparticles to construct a p-n heterojunction with a strong built-in electric field, which can prevent photoinduced electron-hole pairs from recombination as well as facilitate charge transfer. With the X-ray photoelectron spectroscope and in situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, we confirmed the charge transfer under light illumination complying with the type II-scheme mechanism and analyzed the intermediates during photocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR). The highest yields reached 320.9 μmol h−1 g−1 for CoAl-LDH@Cu2O-60 (LC-60) under 1 h light irradiation, which was about 1.6 times than the pristine CoAl-LDH. The sample also exhibited excellent stability which maintained 84.1% of initial performance after 4 circulations.

Introduction

Unrestrained utilization of fossil fuel accelerates the development of society and technology but exacerbates the heavy metal pollution1 and climate change which is due to the green-house gas emission, CO2 in particular. Lots of methods have been brought into investigate for converting CO2 into value-added chemicals such as CO, CH4, and CH3OH etc.2 Wherein, electrocatalytic reduction and photocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide3,4 are deemed as the most promising methods, due to their environment friendly and easy to operate virtues. Photocatalytic CO2 reduction, using sunlight as the driving force to excite electrons to react with CO2, which meets with mild reaction conditions, low cost and sustainability. However a few problems hinder its large-scale application, such as low solar utilization rate, high dissociation energy of CO2 (750 kJ mol−1),5 fast recombination of photoinduced electron-hole pairs, weak redox potential, and severe self-photocorrosion throughout photocatalytic process.6

2D materials, due to their ultrathin structure which possess considerable specific surface area and benefits the photoinduced charge separation and transform are widely utilized in CO2 photoreduction. For example, Zhu et al.7 successfully prepared hydroxyl-modification monolayer black phosphorus (M-BP-OH) using liquid nitrogen assisted exfoliation method. The ultrathin 2D structure (∼0.62 nm) provides possibilities for high efficiency charge transfer and enlarges the band gap to an appropriate value, which causes a more negative conduction band (CB) to promote photocatalytic CO2 reduction. The CO production rate for M-BP-OH is 112.6 μmol h−1 g−1, which is 4 times higher than that of bulk BP (25.3 μmol h−1 g−1). Layered double hydroxides (LDHs), which have a common formulation of [M2+1-xM3+x(OH)2]y+(Az−)·nH2O, where M2+ is usually consist of divalent metal ions such as Cu2+、Co2+、Mg2+、Ni2+ etc. M3+ represents trivalent metal ions, including Al3+、Cr3+、Fe3+ etc. An− represents a charge-balancing anion, generally is the CO3.8 According to their ultrathin 2D structure, richness surface functional groups(-OH), strong alkalinity and satisfactory light harvesting ability in visible light region, CO2 adsorption or charge transfer efficiency, LDHs deliver a noteworthy answer for carbon dioxide photocatalytic reduction reaction (CO2RR).9,10

However, low solar energy utilization rate and serious photoinduced electrons recombination behavior create a hassle in front of large-scale application. To solve these issues, lots of strategies in material preparation and modification are performed. Vacancy engineering is a frequently used method in catalyst modification, especially 2D materials which provide possibilities for the creation of ideal vacancies benefitting from their large specific surface area. Zhu et al.11 introduced O vacancies on Ln2O3, the O vacancies reformed the band structure causing a more negative CB which could enhance the reduction capability of the photogenerated electrons. The optimized VO-Ln2O3 reaches a productivity of CO for 63.3 μmol h−1 g−1, which is about 3 times higher than that of Ln2O3. Element doping is also been widely applied in optimization, Zhou et al.12 successfully introduced O element in g-C3N4 creating the red g-C3N4 with the oxygen bridge structure (CSCN). With the help of O bridge structure, the light absorption range has been broadened and the charge separation efficiency has been improved. The CSCN exhibits a productivity of CO of 28.5 μmol h−1 g−1. Heterojunction is widely favored due to the convenient, feasible, and strong controllability features in catalyst preparation procedure. What’s more, the hybrid catalyst can combine the advantages of each original samples, such as narrower band gap, enhanced solar energy utilization rate and builds robust charge force which prevents photogenerated electrons from recombining between heterojunction interface. Zhu et al.13 in situ grew Co2P on the surface of black phosphorus (BP), thus Co2P could lower the CO2 activation energy barrier and a robust Mott-Schottky heterojunction was established which prevented the photoinduced charges from recombination.

Cu element is abundant on earth and copper-based catalysts have exhibited extraordinary performance according to photocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR). Herein, we grew p-type copper-based catalyst (Cu2O) on the interlayer surface of n-type CoAl-LDH. This p-n heterojunction builds robust internal electrical field which could accelerate the charge transfer further enhance the CO2RR efficiency. With the addition of TEOA as sacrificial agent and Ru(bpy)3Cl2·6H2O as photosensitizer, the highest yield for LC-60 reached 320.9 μmol h−1 g−1. in situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS) measurements provided evidence for the conversion pathway of CO2 to CO and type II-scheme charge transfer route in the CO2RR.

X-ray diffraction was applied to get insight into crystal phase of as-prepared catalysts. As shown in Figure S1, evident peaks at 29.6°, 36.4°, 42.3°, 61.3°, 73.5°, and 77.3° are attributed to the characteristic diffraction peaks of the (110), (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) facets of Cu2O according to the standard card (JCPDS No. 05–0667).14 For bare CoAl-LDH, diffraction peaks at 11.5°, 23.2°, 34.6°, and 38.7° are considered to be the (003), (006), (012), and (015) peaks which matched well with that of standard CoAl-LDH card (JCPDS No. 51-0045)15 indicating the successful preparation of CoAl-LDH. However no obvious peaks have been detected for Cu2O even at the strongest (111) peak at 36.4°, which is on account of the tiny loading amount of Cu2O (Figure 1B).16 Interestingly, the intensity of all characteristic peaks diminished compared to the pristine, which may be due to the depletion of lattice spacing between layers by the introduction of Cu2O nanoparticles.17 FTIR was applied to get insight into chemical bonds and chemical groups. As shown in Figure 1C, the broad bands locating at approximately 3480 and 1640 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of O-H bond containing in host layers and interlayer and bending vibration of H2O.18,19 The peak at approximately 1360 cm−1 is associated with the stretching ν3 antisymmetric stretch of carbonate ion (CO32−) attributed to CoAl-LDH, and the peaks around 750 cm−1 are considered as the ν2 out-of-plane bend of M-OH and M-O vibrations mode.20 While after adsorption of Cu2+ from Cu (OAc)2 as well as loading Cu2O nano particles, the vibration band at 3480 cm−1 shifted to higher wavenumber which indicated a strong chemical interaction between Cu2O and CoAl-LDH in the heterojunction,21 specially through the surface -OH groups which can adsorb and stabilize the Cu2+ for further crystallization to Cu2O, while no characteristic absorption band attributed to Cu-O bond at 630 cm−1 was detected due to the low loading of Cu2O referring to X-ray diffractometer (XRD) results.

The unique morphology of CoAl-LDH and their hybrid samples were confirmed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) and transmission electron microscope (TEM). Pristine CoAl-LDH showed the flower-like morphology which was composed of massive 2D nanoplates with quite thin thickness which possess the diameter ranging from a few to over ten μm (Figure 2A). After Cu2O hybridizing, the smooth surface of single CoAl-LDH nanoplate appeared coarsely and the Cu2O nanoparticles anchored uniformly on the surface of LDH can be discovered. The particles were quite small and highly dispersed in the LDH single nanosheet, this 2D/0D morphology may supply considerable active sites for photocatalytic CO2 reduction (Figures 2B and 2C). TEM images confirmed the flower-like morphology self-assembled of ultrathin 2D nanosheets (Figure 2D) coupled with Cu2O nanoparticles (black pellets) ranging from 10 to 20 nm (Figure 2E). Clear grain boundaries were identified by the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) between the interface of CoAl-LDH and Cu2O, which illustrated the fine heterojunction had been established. The distinctive lattice spacings of 0.24 and 0.26 nm were gained ascribed to (111) lattice plane of cubic crystal structured Cu2O and (012) plane of CoAl-LDH, respectively (Figure 2F).15,22 Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis showed a homogeneously dispersant of Cu (red), Al (green), Co (bule), and O (bright green) elements on the surface of CoAl-LDH nanosheets (Figure S2), which verified the successful preparation of CoAl-LDH@Cu2O hybrids, and the results showed the Cu element take 2.2% of mass percentage of the LC-60 samples, which is close to the theoretical calculation of 2.7%, the slight loss may be caused by the preparation and washing process. Surface composition and chemical states

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy is a sensitive tool to detect the chemical composition and surface oxidation state for catalysts. All elements containing in as-prepared samples were detected as shown in XPS survey spectrum (Figure S4), which was consistent with the EDS result and further indicated the successful synthesis. High resolution XPS spectrum was involved for each sample, O 1s orbit was separated into 531, 531.6, and 532.15 eV (Figure 3C), which are attributed to defect sites with low oxygen coordination,24 hydroxyl bonding, and adsorbed oxygen, respectively,25 the situation of O 1s orbit for LC-60 is almost the same as CoAl-LDH, except for the slight component decrease of defect sites with low oxygen coordination. For Cu2O, binding energies at 530, 530.55, and 531.75 eV are usually associated with lattice oxygen donated as Cu-O, lattice hydroxyl and adsorbed oxygen, respectively.26 As for Co element in CoAl-LDH and LC-60, the peaks at 781.0 and 797.0 eV are deemed as Co3+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 for CoAl-LDH, the peaks at 782.9 and 798.5 eV indicates Co2+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 (Figure 3A). While slight shift toward higher binding energy of Co element was observed for LC-60, specifically peaks at 781.1 and 797.2 eV are attributed to Co3+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, 783.1 and 799.0 eV are deemed as Co2+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 (Figure 3A). Two satellite peaks located at 787.2 eV and 803.1 eV are indexed to Co2+ and Co3+ of CoAl-LDH and satellite peaks located at 787.3 and 803.3 eV are corresponding to Co2+ and Co3+ of LC-60 (Figure 3A).27 However the ratio of Co2+/Co3+ changed slightly from 1.03 to 0.85 after Cu2O modification, which may possess intimate relevancy with the defect sites with low oxygen coordination.28 As shown in Figure S5, Al element in CoAl-LDH has a main 2p peak located at 74.45 eV attributed to Al3+ which shifted toward higher energy at 74.55 eV after Cu2O was introduced. Chemical valence of Cu exists mainly as Cu+ before and after growth, which is located at 932.3 and 931.8 eV of 2p3/2 orbit for pristine Cu2O and the LC-60, Cu+ 2p1/2 orbit is identified as 952.1 and 951.8 eV for these two samples (Figure 3B).29 There is trace amount signal of Cu2+ which was separated into 933.4 and 933.3 eV of 2p3/2 for Cu2O and LC-60 while 954.3 and 954.0 eV of 2p1/2 for these two references in Cu 2p XPS, which is the result of rapid oxidation during preparation for analysis due to the quite tiny particle size.30 Besides the increased binding energy of Co, Al elements after combined with Cu, an obvious shift toward lower binding energy for Cu 2p XPS spectrum (Figure 3B) as well as the lower kinetic energy of Cu LMM Auger spectrum (Figure S6) have attracted attention, indicating the electrons transfer from CoAl-LDH to Cu2O when these two different semiconductors contacted each other. X-ray absorption spectrum (XAS) is an efficient tool to detect the surface oxidation state and chemical bond situation due to its sensitive detectability, here, soft X-ray adsorption near edge spectrum (XANES) was applied to draw a sight into surface oxidation state of the as-prepared samples. Figure 3D shows the XANES of Co L2,3 edge, each sample exhibited the characteristic peaks of mixture states of Co2+ and Co3+.33 Intensity of Co2+ to Co3+ decreased while coupling with CoAl-LDH, indicating the existence of charge transfer along with the establishment of the heterojunction, which is correspond to the XPS semi-quantitative result. The more positively charged Co may act on lowing charge transfer barrier during CO2RR thus enhanced the photocatalytic efficiency. When it comes to Cu, whose L2,3 XANES showed the characteristic peaks of Cu+ with a certain amount of Cu2+ (Figure 3E), the peaks intensity of L-edge which represents the electron transfer from occupied 2p orbit to the unoccupied 3d states33 went down as well as the position moved toward lower energy after the combination of CoAl-LDH with Cu2O. The lower energy suggests the existence of more occupied 3d orbits which could enhance the electrons density. We could get a conclusion that during the process of hybridization, electrons would transfer from n-type CoAl-LDH to the p-type Cu2O, thus the puissant interface electric acting force has been established, which is conformed to the XPS results. O K-edge XANES in Figure 3F emerges distinguish between copper oxide and LDHs, which possess an intensity peak for Cu2O and brod peaks for LDHs. Peak A at 528.4 eV for Cu2O is associated with hybridization between Cu 3d and O 2p, peak C for LDHs is deemed as the overlapping of Co 3d and O 2p, while peak C is considered as the hybridization between Al 3p and O 2p and the surface adsorbed oxygen species. Peak D is attributed to the covalent of Cu 4sp and O 2p for Cu2O and the covalent of Co 4sp/Cu 4sp and O 2p34,35 for LDHs. With increasing the Cu2O proportion, the intensity of peak A gradually increased along with moving toward higher energy, thus we can get a conclusion that the electron transfer from CoAl-LDH to Cu2O injecting certain vitality to the lattice oxygen of CoAl-LDH and Cu2O.36

Photocatalytic performance for CO2 reduction

As displayed in Figure 5A, photocatalytic activity of CO2 reduction reaction was performed with TEOA as holes sacrifice agent and Ru(bpy)3Cl2·6H2O as photosensitizer, while without sacrifice agent and photosensitizer, bare CoAl-LDH showed poor photocatalytic properties. Under this reaction system, CoAl-LDH only got CO and H2 gaseous product which exhibited a yield of 191.85 μmol h−1 g−1 for CO, different samples regulated through various composite ratio gave rise to the yields of 231.4、320.9 and 235.9 μmol h−1 g−1 for LC-55、LC-60 and LC-65, respectively. Besides, signal of H2 was detected for each sample, which are 70.4, 130.5, 216.3, and 113.9 μmol h−1 g−1 for CoAl-LDH, LC-55, LC-60 and LC-65, respectively. Obviously, the variation tendency of yields stayed consistent with that of CO productivity, only trace amount CH4 of 2.12, 2.34, 2.7, and 2.23 μmol h−1 g−1 for CoAl-LDH, LC-55, LC-60, and LC-65 were detected leading to the eximious selectivity for CO of 59.4% for LC-60. With the introduction of Cu2O, photocatalytic properties gradually enhanced which was due to the increased active sites and p-n heterojunction boosting charge transfer. However it began to go down at the Cu2O weight ratio increased from LC-60 to LC-55, which may be on account of excessive Cu2O would aggregate and further occupy the CO2 active sites which acting as the recombination centers of photoexcited charge carriers.25 On the other hand, Cu2O exhibited poor activity in this reaction system which got the production of CO for 0.4 μmol h−1 g−1 and CH4 for 0.42 μmol h−1 g−1 only. We also explored the CO2 reduction performance under visible light region, for the main gaseous products, CoAl-LDH exhibited a productivity for CO of 159.3 μmol h−1 g−1 and for H2 of 61.18 μmol h−1 g−1. As displayed in Figure 5C, after 4 cycles of experiments, the LC-60 still maintained excellent photocatalytic reduction activity toward CO2 photoreduction, which maintained of 84.1% of the initial test in the cycle experiment. The characteristic peaks for XRD patterns are same as the pristine LC-60 indicating the crystal structure was maintained after long time testing and the TEM of LC-60 after 4 cycles also shows the iconic 2D nanosheets (Figures S10A and S10B). In order to eliminate the influence of foreign carbon-containing organic matter, TEOA and photosensitizer to make sure that CO products only came from CO2RR, a series of control experiments and isotope analysis experiment were applied. We implemented photocatalytic reduction of CO2 experiments at the conditions without TEOA, photosensitizer and catalysts, using Ar as the reactant gas and under dark environment, respectively, as shown in Figure 5B. The results gave an outcome that the CO gas could only be acquired through CO2 photocatalysis by photocatalyst under light illumination with the presence of holes sacrifice agent and in photosensitizer.

Isotope analysis experiment was explored using 13CO2 as the only reactant gas, the mass spectra in Figure 5D shows obvious peaks of 13CO (m/z = 29) and 13CH4 (m/z = 17) after irradiating the LC-60 under the 13CO2 atmosphere, got the isotope analysis and contrast experiments into consideration, we verified it was the photogenerated electrons which reduced CO2 to CO.

Photoelectrical properties measurement

UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) was applied to investigate the light response property and band edge information, which all make contribution to catalytic ability. Traditional CoAl-LDH has an intense absorption peak at UV region, with an apparent shoulder at about 330 nm, corresponding to an optical band gap of 4.04 eV estimated from the intercept of the tangent to the transferred curves from Kubelka-Munk plot (αhυ = A(hυ- Eg)n/2)41 (Figures 6A and S12). As shown in Figure 6A, intrinsic absorption bands for CoAl-LDH at UV region are attributed to the ligand-metal charge transfer (LMCT) from the O2− to the Co2+, the metal-metal charge transfer (MMCT) peak usually appears at 264–318 nm21 showed no signal because d0 electron configuration of Al3+ does not show absorption bands in the UV-vis spectra.42 The absorption band seated in visible region exhibits a broad range from 390 to 780 nm (Figure 6A), which mainly contains d-d transitions of octahedral Co2+ within the CoAl-LDH layer at 450, 490, and 530 nm, the d states of Co2+ are split into t2g and e.g., orbitals in an octahedral environment. However, the absorption band of d-d transitions in low spin of octahedrally coordinated Co3+ possesses much less intensity at 630 nm in comparison with that of Co2+ which is on account of the insignificant content.25,42 With Cu2O amount increasing, adsorption band enhanced especially at the region from 260 to 480 nm and the absorption edge red-shift associated with the gradually shrank band edge43 of 3.46, 3.63, and 3.77 eV for LC-55, LC-60, and LC-65 (Figure S12), respectively, based on the Kubelka-Munk plot (αhυ = A(hυ- Eg)n/2).41 The gradually reduced band gap was exactly corresponding to the enhanced light harvesting ability in visible region. We further explored the charge transfer and separation efficiency for each CoAl-LDH@Cu2O references using steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy through 380 nm excitation energy.44 It is generally known that low PL intensity always associated with better photoinduced electron-hole pairs separation efficiency. LC-60 showed the lowest PL intensity, thus, it indicates the best effective separation ability of photogenerated electron and holes between CoAl-LDH and hybrids (Figure 6B), which benefits from the robust internal electric field induced by p-n heterojunction keeping electron-hole pairs from recombination.45 Transient photocurrent responses was carried out with a 40 s interval between light on and off, LC-60 unfolded the highest photocurrent response among all tested samples (Figure 6C), which is an intuitionistic evidence for its rapid separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs ability.46 Electrochemical impedance spectrum (EIS) of all samples were measured to understand the electrochemical impedance of charge transfer (Figure 6D), the smallest radius shown at Nyquist plots for LC-60 verified the highest conductivity for charge transfer.47 All phenomenon points to the same result that the formed p-n heterojunction inhibited the charge recombination and promote the separation of charge carriers in LC-60 which strengthens the photocatalytic activity.48

Possible photocatalytic mechanism

The Mott-Schottky tests were performed under the frequency of 500 Hz and tested for three times, the positive slope for CoAl-LDH (Figure 7A) and negative for Cu2O (Figure 7B) of Mott-Schottky curves pointed to a conclusion that CoAl-LDH is n-type semiconductor, Cu2O is p-type semiconductor conversely.49 After integrating, an evident V-shaped profile (Figure S13)50 of LC-60 in Mott-Schottky curves was observed. Thus, the high energy internal electric field was constructed through p-n heterojunction caused by big difference in Femi levels between two kinds of catalysts.51 Flat band potentials (Efb) were calculated to be −0.67 and 0.93 eV (vs. Ag/AgCl) for CoAl-LDH and Cu2O which were −0.47 and 1.13 eV (vs. NHE) (Figures 7A and 7B). Efb is quite close to CB for n-type semiconductors, which is about 0.1 eV positive than CB,52 then the CB energy of CoAl-LDH was calculated to be −0.57 eV. Valence band (VB) edge energy were confirmed by the formulation of VB = CB + Eg,53 thus VB of CoAl-LDH was verified as 3.47 eV. While for p-type semiconductors, Efb is 0.1 eV negative than the valence band (VB),54 thus the VB energy of Cu2O was calculated to be 1.23 eV, and CB of Cu2O was −0.69 eV according to the formulation aforementioned. Flat band potential is frequently equivalent to Fermi level for semiconductors, because the Fermi level of CoAl-LDH is more negative than that of Cu2O, charge migration would occur between these two components.55 Up till now the clear mechanism of established p-n heterojunction of as-prepared samples are revealed as Figure 7D. After contacted, electrons in n-type CoAl-LDH would transfer to the p-type Cu2O, thus a stable built-in electric field was established which made contribution to prevent charges from recombination and could promote electron-hole separation under light irradiation.56Figure 7Band structure analysis and possible photocatalytic CO2 reduction mechanismMott-Schottky curves of (A) CoAl-LDH, (B) Cu2O.(C) XPS of Co element after several tests.(D) Schematic diagram of charge transfer of p-n CoAl-LDH@Cu2O heterojunction.(E) Proposed mechanism of photocatalytic reduction of CO2 into CO by the CoAl-LDH@Cu2O.

Charge transfer between CoAl-LDH and Cu2O may comply with two mechanisms that is type II-scheme mechanism or Z-scheme transfer mechanism, these two kinds of situations own distinctive outcome.44 The essence of photocatalytic reduction is the migration of photoinduced electrons reacting with CO2, which happened at the interface of catalysts. XPS spectrum is a forceful surface tool to monitor this process. XPS of Cu and Co elements for LC-60 was conducted after several photocatalytic tests. As seen in Figure 7C, binding energy for Co 2p3/2 gradually decreased from the initial ca. 781.1 eV–780.7 eV and from ca. 797.3 eV–796.9 eV for Co 2p1/2 finally of the samples tested after 4 cycles. On the contrary, Cu element got an increase in binding energy, which evidenced that the electrons transferred from Cu2O to CoAl-LDH under light irradiation (Figure S14). Thus, the charge transfer mode can be described as that under light illumination, the photoexcited electrons on the CB of Cu2O would transfer and pass through the internal electrical filed getting to the CB of CoAl-LDH ultimately while the h+ would be left on the VB of Cu2O. Thus, we can draw a conclusion that a type II-scheme charge transfer mode may occur on the CoAl-LDH@Cu2O composition, during photoreduction of CO2 upon CoAl-LDH@Cu2O. The photogenerated electrons on CB of CoAl-LDH would reduce CO2 to CO and the h+ left on the VB of Cu2O would oxidize TEOA conversely.57 While Ru(bpy)3Cl2·6H2O in this system not only increased the light harvest ability but also would be excited and react with TEOA to produce [Ru(bpy)3]+ under light irradiation, thus extensive electrons would be supplied to the catalysts from the reductive [Ru(bpy)3]+.44,58 The CB of CoAl-LDH and Cu2O are −0.57 and −0.69 eV which meet with CO2 reduction potential to CO of −0.52 eV and to CH4 of −0.24 eV,59 therefore the CO2 photoreduction mechanism of LC-60 can be illustrated in Figure 7E.

To get insight into the photocatalytic process during CO2RR upon CoAl-LDH and its hybrid samples, in situ FTIR was conducted to investigate the intermediates during CO2RR, the test was carried out for 60 min, and the image of the equipment was shown at Figure S15. The first 10 min was under dark condition with a CO2 pumped rate of 6 mL/min as the adsorption stage. After that, simulated sunlight was lightened on to get the last 50 min spectrum acquisition. The signal intensity of crucial intermediate COOH∗ was gradually appeared at 1629, 1630, and 1628 cm−1 for CoAl-LDH, LC-60 and Cu2O with increasing irradiation time, which plays key role in further converting into CO or CH4.9 Subsequently, the signal of CO∗ which is the final intermediate for CO generation was detected at 2076 cm−1 for CoAl-LDH, Cu2O, and LC-60.60 Peaks at 1418 cm−1 for Cu2O nanoparticles (Figure 8). 1363 and 1415 cm−1 for CoAl-LDH (Figure 8A) and 1362 cm−1 for LC-60 are considered as symmetric stretching of HCO3− (Figure 8B), which were resulted from the reaction of adsorbed H2O and CO2.61 Peaks at 1294 and 1231 cm−1 attributed to the bidentate carbonate (b-CO32-) for CoAl-LDH and LC-60, 1539 and 1540 cm−1 are in accord with monodentate carbonate groups (m-CO32-) (Figures 8A and 8B).62 Interestingly a pivotal peaks located at 1080 cm−1 attributed to CHO∗ which is deemed as the key intermediate for going step forward protonation gathering CH4, emerged for the Cu2O nanoparticles with irradiation time increasing. This not only evidenced its productive ability for CH4 but is also in good accord with the CO2RR tests results.63 Nevertheless, this unique peak didn’t appear in neither CoAl-LDH nor LC-60, which confirms its eximious selectivity toward CO during CO2RR rather than CH4 (Figures 8A–8C). Conclusions

We chose n-type CoAl-LDH combined with p-type Cu2O to construct a p-n heterojunction which possessed a powerful built-in electric field getting a considerable performance toward CO2 photoreduction, the highest yields reached 320.9 μmolg−1h−1 after 1 h irradiation for LC-60. Mott-Schottky curves made ample evidence for the construction of p-n heterojunction, and a series of photoelectric characterization such as PL and electrochemical impedance tests has also provided a profile of the distinguished electron transfer and separation ability induced by the p-n heterojunction. Charge transfer mode was verified using XPS, after 4 cycles, the binding energy of Co elements decreased the situation of Cu elements was on the opposite, indicating the electron transfer from Cu2O to CoAl-LDH under light illumination, which was comply with the type-II scheme. In situ FTIR test confirmed the prominent CO∗ intermediate during CO2RR upon the LC-60 occupy the main component rather than CHO∗, which gave evidence for the high selectivity of CO. This work provided a worth referencing method to design an efficient photocatalyst upon CoAl-LDH toward CO2 photoreduction.

Limitations of the study

Besides the ultraviolet light and visible light, there are infrared light which takes considerable part in sunlight. Our research focus on the CO2 photoreduction under the ultraviolet and visible light region simulated by Xe lamp. However, in the real nature environment, all regions of the light should be taken into account.

Method details

Synthesis of COAl-LDH

CoAl-LDH was prepared through the one-pot hydro-thermal method, firstly, Co(NO3)2·6H2O (1.5 mmol), Al(NO3)3·9H2O (0.5 mmol), urea (5 mmol), and ammonium fluoride (2 mmol) were dispersed in deionized water (60 mL) further treated with ultrasonic processing for 10 min, and stirred under 500r/min for 20 minutes to get an evenly dispersed solution, then the solution was transformed into a 100 mL PTFE liner interlinked into a stainless steel reaction kettle to react in an oven at 100°C for 24 h. Finally, the pink precipitate was wash with deionized water and absolute ethyl alcohol through centrifugation for several times and dried at 60°C for 12 h in an vacuum drying oven.

Synthesis of LC-X and Cu2O nanoparticles

5 mg of cupric acetate was dissolved in 25 mL deionized water to construct a Cu2+ solution. To synthetize the LC-X samples which X indicated the additive amount of CoAl-LDH, 55, 60 and 65 mg as prepared CoAl-LDH were dispersed in cupric acetate solution through ultrasonic for 20 min to be uniformly dispersed, and further stirred under 500r/min for 30 minutes to make sure that Cu2+ could attach to the CoAl-LDH, the obtained solution was labeled as solution A. Solution B was prepared by mixing 16 mg NaOH and 14 mg ascorbic acid (AA) in 20 mL deionized water. Then the LC-x was prepared by adding the solution B to solution A drop by drop under stirring, after completely reacting, final samples were gotten by centrifugation and further washed with deionized water and absolute ethyl alcohol for several times, finally dried at 60°C for 12 h in a vacuum drying oven. Cu2O nanoparticles was fabricated using same method but without adding CoAl-LDH.

Catalyst characterizations

The crystal structure was detected by the X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku D/MAX 2500 V, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) with the Cu Ka radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm, 40 kV and 100 mA). The microstructure and lattice arrangements were investigated by scanning electronic microscopy (SEM, Sigma 300, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and transmission electronic microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). The elemental distribution of the samples was detected by using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) of Oxford Instrument. The elemental microanalysis and atom binding states were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS, ESCALAB 250XI, Waltham, USA) with an Al Kα radiator. These XPS spectra were corrected by using C 1s spectra with the calibration energy of 284.8 eV. The infrared spectra further provided information of surface functional groups and chemical bonds through a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR, Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The photoelectric performance of as prepared samples was estimated using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660D, Chenhua Instrument, China) with a three-electrode system. Pt and Ag/AgCl electrodes were selected as the counter and reference electrodes, while 5 mg of catalyst coated on pure uorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glasses (1 ∗ 1 cm2) acted as working electrodes, with 0.5 M Na2SO4 as the electrolyte solution. Transient photocurrent responses spectroscopy, EIS and Mott-Schottky curves were tested under the open circuit voltage and Mott-Schottky curves were tested using 500 Hz and performed three times to reduce errors. A full spectrum Xe lamp (300 W, PLS-SXE300, Perfect light, China) was adopted as the light source deposited 10 cm away from the testing system to realize the transient photocurrent responses spectroscopy experiment. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS, UV-3600Plus, SHIMADZU, Japan) were applied to investigate the light-harvesting ability of the catalysts. Photoluminescence spectra (PL, FL3C-111 TCSPC, HORIBA, Japan) was obtained to study the recombination degree of photoinduced electrons and holes upon different samples. Co K-edge and Cu K-edge XAFS is measured in the HXMA and SXRMB beamlines of Canadian Light Source (CLS). These data are collected at room temperature under air atmosphere. Soft XAS measurements were carried out at spherical grating monochromator (SGM) beamline at Canadian Light Source under vacuum condition. The photon energy step of Co L-edge, Cu L-edge, and O K-edge XAS spectrum was set at 0.1 eV N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and CO2 adsorption isotherms (Autosorb iQ-C-MP, Quantachrome Instruments, USA) were measured to test investigate the BET surface areas and the adsorption capacity of CO2 under 298 K.

In-situ FTIR was used to analyze the reaction intermediates during CO2RR. Each sample was firstly purged with high purity Ar gas (99.999%) for 30 min at room temperature to discharge interference and collect the background, then high purity CO2 (99.999%) with water vapor was introduced with the flow rate of 6 mL/min and obtained by 64 cumulative scans at room temperature with a resolution of 8 cm−1 for each scan, and all spectral data were recorded. After 10 min CO2 adsorption, 300 W Xenon lamp (PLS-FX300HU, Perfectlight, China) was turned on and the background measured by subtracting pure Ar gas from the spectrum obtained from the test was the catalyst test data.

Catalytic tests

10 mg of photocatalyst was dispersed uniformly in a quartz reactor with the triethanolamine (TEOA), acetonitrile and DI water inside, which adhered to a ratio of 1:1:3, further 3 mg Tris (2, 2′-bipyridine) dichlororuthenium (II) hexahydrate (Ru(bpy)3Cl2·6H2O) was added as the photosensitizer. A 300 W Xe arc lamp (PLS-SXE300D, Beijing Perfectlight), used as the UV–vis light source (320 nm < λ < 780 nm), was placed at a distance about 10 cm from the reactor. Before photocatalytic test, the reactor was pumped with pure CO2 for 30 min under the flow rate of 80 ml/min, further illuminating for 1 h to test the CO2RR performance. During the photocatalytic test, the ambient temperature was kept around 25°C through a cooling water external connection system. After each 2 hours photocatalytic test, the used sample was washed with DI water and ethanol for several times through vacuum filtration, and dried at 60°C for 12 h at vacuum drying oven to get activity recover, this procedure was carried out for four times to measure the stability of catalysts.

After test, gas products were collected using a peristaltic pump and detected by a gas chromatograph (GC-2014C, Shimadzu, Japan). Isotopic labeling test with the only input gas of 13CO2 was performed to determine the carbon source in the products by an assembly of mass spectrome and gas chromatography (MS-5975 and GC-7920).

The formulation of gaseous yield is listed as following:

Yield =C×V22.4×m×h μmol·h−1 g−1. Where c represents the gas concentration (ppm), V represents the volume of reactor (mL), m represents the dosage of catalysts (mg) and h represents the irradiation time (hours).
