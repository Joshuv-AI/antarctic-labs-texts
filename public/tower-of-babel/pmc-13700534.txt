Integrative multi-omics of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma reveals immune alterations and lactylation-driven mechanisms in metastatic progression
Authors: Yanan Pang, Ye Gao, Guobin Liu, Jingxiang Zhao, Han Lin, Yilin Wang, Lei Xin, Wei Wang, Yan Bian, Yunfei Jiao, Qiuxin Li, Zengjun Li, Zhaoshen Li, He Huang, Luowei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is highly lethal owing to its early and aggressive metastasis, yet the underlying multi-layer molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, limiting therapeutic advances.MethodsWe performed integrative multi-omics profiling (including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and lactylome) on 99 matched primary tumor, metastatic lymph nodes, and normal esophagus tissue samples from 36 patients with metastatic ESCC. Molecular changes and cross-omics interactions associated with the metastatic cascades were characterized. Key findings were validated by immunohistochemistry and survival analysis in an independent cohort of 116 ESCC patients. Further functional and mechanistic studies were conducted using in vitro and a tail-vein experimental lung metastasis model.ResultsOur analysis revealed pronounced disturbance in immune-related pathways closely associated with metastatic progression and poor prognosis. Intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) was identified as a critical factor of ESCC invasion and metastasis. Additionally, lysine-lactylated proteins were predominantly involved in cytoskeleton, focal adhesion and glycolysis pathways during ESCC metastasis. Integrative analysis revealed that the lactylome interacts with the genome and transcriptome through both cis- and trans-regulatory mechanisms, primarily in immune and metabolic pathways. Notably, lactylation of phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) at residue K251 enhances its interaction with actin gamma 1 and promotes metastatic phenotypes. ICAM1 and PGAM1 K251 lactylation represent two independent metastasis-associated mechanisms identified from the same multi-omics dataset.ConclusionsThis integrative multi-omics characterization provides a comprehensive resource for metastatic ESCC research and identifies K251-lactylated PGAM1 as a candidate prognostic biomarker and mechanistic lead warranting further preclinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02755-x.Keywords: Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma, Metastasis, Multiomics Profiling, Proteomics, Lysine Lactylation

Introduction

Esophageal cancer ranksseventh in global cancer-related mortality, with 510,716 estimated new cases and 445,129 deaths annually [1]. The prognosis remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate below 20% [2]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) accounts for ~ 85% of all esophageal cancer cases, especially in high-risk countries, particularly China [3, 4]. The high mortality of ESCC is predominantly attributable to its high invasive and metastatic potential, yet the underlying molecular changes are incompletely understood, limiting the development of effective therapies. Metastatic ESCC arises from the accumulation of genetic mutations and subsequent transcriptomic and proteomic alterations that collectively confer malignant phenotypes, such as uncontrolled proliferation, enhanced migration, and invasion [5]. While multi-omics studies have characterized ESCC [6–8], the molecular regulatory interplay underlying metastasis remains unclear. Moreover, as a novel regulation layer in cancer progression, the role of post-translational modifications (PTMs) in ESCC metastasis remains largely unexplored.

A hallmark of cancer metabolism is the Warburg effect, which leads to the aberrant lactate accumulation [9]. Beyond serving as a metabolic byproduct, lactate also functions as a signaling molecule that associates with PTMs, notably lysine lactylation (Kla), thereby influencing gene expression and cellular function [10]. For instance, lactylation of adenylate kinase 2 (AK2) at lysine 28 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation by orchestrating metabolic alterations [11]. Specifically, the functional impact of Kla, particularly its role in orchestrating the invasion and metastasis of ESCC progression, has not been systematically investigated.

Previous ESCC studies have predominantly employed single-omics profiling or focused on isolated biological processes [5, 12], leaving a gap in systems-level understanding of metastasis. Existing multi-omics studies have mainly focused on defining static molecular portraits and subtyping biomarkers, rather than on elucidating dynamic, metastasis related molecular characteristics [6–8]. To address this, we performed a combined multi-omics analysis of 99 samples from 36 metastatic ESCC patients, encompassing genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and lactylomic profiling. Our analysis revealed coherent immune pathway dysregulation at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels, which was critically linked to ESCC metastatic progression and patient prognosis. We identified intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) as a critical participant of ESCC invasion and migration in vitro and in vivo. Notably, we also uncovered that Kla-modified proteins were predominantly involved in cytoskeleton, focal adhesion and glycolysis pathways during ESCC metastasis. Particularly, lactylation at the K251 site of PGAM1 promotes ESCC metastasis by enhancing its interaction with ACTG1. Although PGAM1 is primarily recognized for its glycolytic activity, our findings underscore its non-enzymatic contribution to cancer metastasis [13]. Taken together, this multi-omic resource and the associated mechanistic leads offer a foundation for future functional and preclinical studies in metastatic ESCC.

Results

Multi-omics profiling of metastatic ESCC

We performed multi-omics profiling of matched primary ESCC tumor tissues (T), normal adjacent tissues (N), and lymph node metastatic tissues (L) from 36 patients without prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy (Fig. 1A). Their clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table S1. Within this cohort, 77.8% of patients were male, and the majority (80.6%) were in stage III according to the 8th American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system. Our analysis identified 21,389 RNAs, 8,364 proteins, and 7,172 Kla sites on 2,309 proteins, 21,100 copy number variations (CNVs) and 3,720 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) (Fig. S1A, Table S2−6). A case-by-case plot showed the number of detected RNAs, proteins, and Kla sites in each sample (Fig. S1B). Comparative analysis revealed that group T exhibited increased molecular complexity relative to groups N and L with higher number of both proteins and Kla sites. Whole exome sequencing (WES) showed that primary tumors harbored a greater burden of point mutations and CNVs compared to matched lymph node metastases. Frequently altered genes in the genomic landscape included OBSCN, NOTCH1, SHANK2, AHNAK, TP53, etc. (Fig. S1C-D). Differential analysis of somatic variations between groups T and L showed that NOTCH1 amplifications and TP53 missense mutations/indels were significantly more prevalent in Group T (Fig. 1B and S1E), indicating potential clonal evolution during metastasis. These distinct genomic characteristics might shape the metastatic transcriptome and proteome of ESCC.

Quality control confirmed high reproducibility across the transcriptomic, proteomic, and lactylomic datasets (Fig. S2A), with principal component analysis (PCA) showing robust separation among all three groups (Fig. S2B). Next, differential expression analysis revealed substantial molecular alterations including differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), and differentially expressed Kla sites between groups. Compared with group N, groups T exhibited 3,048 DEGs, 1,301 DEPs, and 433 differentially Kla sites. Group L showed even more extensive transcriptomic and proteomic changes, with 5,940 DEGs, 1,636 DEPs, and 272 differentially Kla sites (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR < 0.05, Fig. 1C). The expression of actin alpha 1 (ACTA1) showed significantly upregulation in both groups T and L at the transcriptome level, while myosin light chain 1 (MYL1) was downregulated at the proteome level. Elevated ACTA1 is linked to poor prognosis in oral squamous cell carcinoma due to its vital role in the movement, structure, and integrity of cancer cells [14]. However, although MYL1 promotes metastasis and correlates with poor prognosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma by interacting with EGF/EGFR [15], it tends to be a protective factor in our cohort. We further examined the spatial relationship between differential Kla sites and known protein active sites. The lactylated residues ALDOA K230, ALDOC K230, NIPEPL1 K272 and CISD1 K55 were found to directly coincide with (0 Å from) their respective active sites (Fig. 1D), strongly suggesting that lactylation may directly modulate their protein function. This is consistent with prior findings that ALDOA K230/322 lactylation attenuates DDX17 binding and promotes the stemness of liver cancer stem cells [16], implying a potentially conserved and critical role for Kla in cancer progression.

To delineate pathway-level alterations, we applied gene set variation analysis (GSVA) to identify dysregulated pathways in groups T and L at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels and further evaluated their cross-omics correlation. Notably, pathways related to lysosome, spliceosome, protein export, auto-immune disorders, Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, and antigen processing and presentation showed high concordance between RNA and protein levels, which were mostly immune-associated (Fig. 1E, F). In contrast, metabolic pathways (e.g., lipoic acid, amino acid, and glycometabolism) exhibited poor correlation, suggesting potential regulation by processes other than the central dogma. Specially, this might be exemplified by the widespread lactylation events we identified, which are likely driving metabolic rewiring in ESCC progression. Collectively, our multi-omics profiling provides a global snapshot of ESCC metastasis, implicating intertwined roles for genomic variations and lactylome modifications in regulating gene expression.

Immune dysregulation was involved in metastasis and predicted prognosis

Given the functional discrepancies observed between the transcriptomes and proteomes in ESCC, and considering the central role of proteins in cellular processes including tumorigenesis and metastasis, we prioritized the analysis of DEPs across the three groups. The overlap of specific DEPs across groups was visualized using an upset plot (Fig. S3A). The proteomic profiles effectively distinguished both groups T and L from group N. Subsequent enrichment analysis of these DEPs identified significant involvement in processes critical to cancer progression, including focal adhesion, extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interaction, actin cytoskeleton regulation, phagosome function, and antigen processing and presentation coupled with B/T cell receptor signaling (Fig. S3B-D). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks for down- and up-regulated proteins in groups T and L were constructed using the STRING database (Fig. S3E-F). Core modules of down-regulated proteins were enriched in pathways related to muscle contraction, and RHO GTPases activated PAKs pathways, processes implicated in cancer cell motility. Conversely, core modules of up-regulated proteins were involved in phagosome function, leukocyte trans-endothelial migration, and antigen processing and presentation, highlighting the engagement of immunity pathways in metastasis (Fig. S3G, H).

To gain a global perspective on ESCC metastasis, we integrated transcriptomics and proteomics data for further analysis. Correlation analysis between DEGs and DEPs in group T vs. L comparison revealed that immune-related pathways, including hematopoietic cell lineage, B cell receptor signaling, and cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), were consistently up-regulated and displayed a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.79, p < 2.2e-16, Fig. 2A). CAMs comprise integrins, the immunoglobulins superfamily (IgSF), selectins, and cadherins. Among these, IgSFs members are pivotal in transmitting immune signals to effector cells, and their dysregulation is closely linked to tumor metastasis [17]. Furthermore, assessment of the ESCC microenvironment using transcriptomic data revealed a significant enrichment of B and T cells in Group L compared to group T (Fig. 2B). Correspondingly, GSVA indicated elevated activity of both CAMs and B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathways in group L (Fig. 2C-D). Notably, the activity level of the BCR pathway was a significant prognostic factor for metastatic ESCC patients specifically in group L (p = 0.0019), whereas no such association was observed in group T (p = 0.37) (Fig. 2E-F). We further constructed an immunogenetic prognostic model by applying LASSO regression for feature selection followed by Cox regression analysis. This model exhibited excellent performance in predicting patient outcomes (p < 0.0001, Fig. S3I). This exploratory model requires further independent external validation before clinical application. To reconstruct the metastatic trajectory of ESCC, we performed trajectory inference analysis and aimed to identify key immunity-related genes with dynamic expression patterns along the normal-primary tumor-lymph node metastasis (N-T-L) axis (Fig. 2G-H). Key immune-related proteins were screened to construct a PPI network, highlighting central hub proteins such as ICAM1, LYN, and ITGB2 (Fig. 2I). Functional enrichment analysis of these hub proteins revealed their association with pathways including tertiary granule lumen, integral components of plasma membrane, and integrin complexes (Fig. 2J). Consistently, the protein expression levels of ICAM1, ITGB2 and LYN were significantly elevated in both groups T and L compared to group N (Fig. 2K-M). These molecules closely involved in inter-cellular adhesion and communications may help cancer cells interact with and thus adapt to or reshape the new microenvironment in the metastatic organ. We acknowledge that the immune signatures detected in metastatic lymph nodes by bulk transcriptomic and proteomic analyses may partly reflect the lymphoid organ context rather than a purely tumor-intrinsic program. Spatial or single-cell approaches would be needed to further resolve tumor-cell versus microenvironmental contributions.

ICAM1 promoted ESCC metastasis both in vitro and in vivo

Given its central role in immune cell adhesion, we sought to define the biological function and clinical relevance of ICAM1 in ESCC. Immunohistochemistry revealed increased ICAM1 expression in both primary tumors and metastatic lymph nodes compared to normal tissues (Fig. 3A). This finding was further validated in an independent cohort of 116 ESCC patients, where high ICAM1 expression in the tumor tissues was associated with poor overall survival (OS, p = 0.012) and progression-free survival (PFS, p = 0.004) (Fig. 3B, S4A). Notably, in stage T1-2 ESCC patients, high ICAM1 was further associated with a higher incidence of lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3C).

To assess its functional role, ICAM1 expression was stably knocked down in KYSE150 and KYSE30 cell lines (Fig. S4B). ECM adhesion assays demonstrated that ICAM1 down-regulation significantly impaired the adhesion of ESCC cells to collagen I and fibronectin (FN) (Fig. 3D). Consistently, wound healing and transwell assays revealed that reduced ICAM1 expression markedly decreased the migration and invasion capacities of ESCC cells (Fig. 3E-F). In a tail-vein injection experimental lung metastasis model, mice injected with sh-ICAM1 KYSE150 cells developed fewer lung metastasis nodules compared to the control group (Fig. 3G-H). Collectively, these results indicate that ICAM1 promotes ESCC metastasis and represents a potential prognostic biomarker and candidate target.

Impact of CNVs on protein expression in ESCC

As mentioned above, the interplay between the transcriptome and proteome has been established for many genes in ESCC. We next sought to elucidate the direct relationship between genomic variations and the proteome in ESCC, which remains unclarified. CNVs represented the predominant form of genomic alteration in our cohort. Chromosome-level analysis revealed higher genomic instability (G-score) and a greater burden of CNVs in group T than group L (Fig. 4A). Recurrent amplifications in group T included regions such as 3q11.1, 3q29, 5p11, 5p15.1, 11q13.3, consistent with known oncogenic loci in ESCC [6, 18]. Notably, group L exhibited distinct CNV profiles, with specific amplifications at 16q11.1 and 17p11.1. Functionally, genes affected by significant CNVs in group T were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways (Fig. 4B), whereas in group L they were associated with regulation of the actin cytoskeleton and PI3k-Akt signaling pathways (Fig. 4C). CNVs may influence mRNA and protein abundance through both cis- or trans-regulatory modes [19]. We characterized the impact of CNVs on the protein abundance in both modes (Fig. S5A-B). Functional enrichment of CNV-correlated proteins revealed differential patterns between groups. (Fig. S5C-D). These results suggest that CNVs exert differential functional impacts during ESCC progression, predominantly modulating metabolic pathways in primary ESCC tissues and shifting to influence cell signaling and structural remodeling in metastasis.

We further identified 245 and 133 significant CNV-protein correlations (cis effects) in groups T and L, respectively. Among these, 13 overlapping proteins were significantly differentially expressed between the two groups (Fig. 4D). Notably, dedicator of cytokinesis 9 (DOCK9) exhibited high CNVs and elevated protein expression, with high expression linked to prolonged survival in ESCC patients (Fig. S5E). Multi-omics correlation analysis revealed strong CNV-RNA, CNV-protein, and RNA–protein associations in group L (Fig. S5F-I). A combined analysis of cis- and trans- effect of CNVs using GSVA indicated that CNV-correlated proteins were predominantly enriched in metabolic processes (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) notably pinpointed several components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, including NDUFA9, NDUFS6, NDUFC2, and NDUFA12, which were significantly enriched within the CNV-protein interaction datasets (Fig. 4F). We further assessed the CNV levels and RNA–protein concordance for mitochondrial respiratory chain components (Fig. 4G). The results revealed a discordance between transcriptional and proteomic expression in both groups T and L, indicating a role for post-transcriptional, translational or post-translational regulation in maintaining their biological functions. Specifically, proteins such as ATP6AP1, ATP6V1H, CYC1, NDUFA12, and NDUFA9 appeared to be modulated by CNVs mostly in trans- mode. Among them, NDUFA12 is a key accessory subunit of Complex I. Its elevated CNV and protein levels were associated with poor prognosis (Fig. S5J-K). Overall, our integrated multi-omics findings underscore that widespread trans-acting CNV alterations disproportionately affect metabolic proteins in ESCC.

Lactylome characterization associated with ESCC metastasis

The correlation analysis of metabolic protein alterations with CNVs and mRNA expression suggested the involvement of post-translational regulation. Kla, which plays a well-established role in modulating protein function, stability, subcellular localization, enzyme activity, and interactions, represented a compelling mechanism to explore. Therefore, we next characterized the lactylome to investigate its role in ESCC molecular alterations and metastasis. First, we evaluated the clinical relevance of Kla using IHC staining and observed elevated pan-Kla levels in both primary ESCC tumors and matched metastatic lymph nodes (Fig. 5A). High pan-Kla expression was independently associated with worse OS (HR = 1.945, 95% CI 1.255–3.013, p = 0.0029) and PFS (HR = 2.307, 95% CI 1.522–3.497, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Lactylome profiling identified many differentially Kla sites in both groups T and L (Fig. S6A-B). Functionally, Kla-modified proteins in group T were enriched in the Ras signaling pathway and antigen processing and presentation, while glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and actin cytoskeleton regulation were up-regulated in both groups T and L (Fig. S6C). Quantitatively, Kla changes were significantly correlated across groups T and L (R2 = 0.35, p < 2.2e-16, Fig. 5C), yet pan-Kla levels were generally higher in group T. Moreover, group T exhibited a greater number of up-regulated Kla sites than in group L (153 vs. 42, Fig. 5D).

To dissect the dynamics of Kla variations during ESCC metastasis, we performed clustering analysis using the mfuzz algorithm. This yielded six distinct functional clusters (Fig. 5E), which could be further aggregated into three major patterns based on the sample group with the highest Kla level as clusters 1 and 2 peaked in group N, clusters 5 and 6 peaked in group T, and clusters 3 and 4 peaked in group L. The cadherin-binding pathway was enriched across five clusters, yet the number of Kla sites on cadherin-related proteins remained largely unchanged across groups (Fig. S6D), indicating that Kla site abundance did not directly align with functional enrichment. Pathways including focal adhesion, cytoskeleton and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis were specifically enriched in cluster 3&4, pointing to a potential role in metastasis. Meanwhile, expression analysis of Kla-related writers and erasers revealed no consistent pattern across groups N, T, and L (Fig. S6E).

We analyzed the sequence motifs flanking Kla sites using the icelogo tool (Fig. 5F). In clusters with peak lactylation in group T, the motifs were characterized by an overrepresentation of positively charged lysine (K) at positions 1, 2, 5, 8, 12, and 13, alongside negatively charged alanine (A) at positions 3, 4, 6, 9, and 10, or isoleucine (I) at position 11. In contrast, motifs from clusters peaking in group L predominantly featured negatively charged glutamic acid (E) at positions 3, 6, 8, 10, and 11. Glycine also exhibited a characteristic preference near Kla sites within the clusters showing the highest lactylation level. This indicates that Kla modification patterns are stage-specific during ESCC progression. Further analysis of protein domain features revealed that clusters 3 and 4 (Table S7) possessed a distinctive domain composition, including actin, ANX, coiled_coil_region filament and myosin_tail_1, which significantly differed from other clusters (Fig. 5G, S6F). Among proteins with the highest number of Kla sites, X-ray repair cross complementing 6 (XRCC6) was predominant in clusters 5 and 6, while caldesmon 1 (CALD1) was most prominent in clusters 3 and 4 (Fig. 5H). XRCC6 (encoding Ku70) has been reported to interact with YAP and regulate PARP1 ubiquitination, thereby affecting genome stability and tumorigenesis [20]. CALD1 is also implicated in tumor progression and cisplatin resistance [21]. Further structural analysis revealed that proteins in cluster 4 exhibited the lowest solvent accessibility (Fig. 5I), suggesting their Kla sites may be internally located and more likely to influence protein conformations. As identified by high-resolution mass spectrometry in the discovery cohort, several protein Kla sites enriched in group L, including PGAM1.251, ANXA3.66, DCPS.10, were associated with patient prognosis (Fig. 5J). Among these, some Kla sites (e.g., ALDOC.42, HSPA1B.507, HSPA8.512, and PGAM1.251) mapped to proteins involved in purine ribonucleoside triphosphate metabolism, underscoring a link between lactylation-related alterations and metastasis (Fig. 5K). Notably, PGAM1 is a glycolytic mutase whose phosphorylation at tyr26 is known to confer metabolic advantages in cancer [22]. Collectively, these results implicate sequence- and domain-specific lactylation in ESCC metastasis as well as their potential functional pathways and mechanisms.

Kla connected genomics with proteomics primarily in immunity and metabolic pathways

To further elucidate the crosstalk between PTMs and the genome, we analyzed correlations between Kla and genomic variations in ESCC. Specifically, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, carbon metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis were consistently enriched for CNV-correlated lactylated proteins in both groups T and L (Fig. 6A). In contrast, these CNV-associated genes were enriched in pathways such as alcoholism, ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. S7A). We then applied a complementary algorithm to systematically map associations between somatic gene mutations and Kla-modified proteins in both groups T and L (Fig. S7B). This analysis identified 289 Kla-modified proteins whose correlation with gene mutations was conserved across both groups T and L. PPI network analysis of these overlapping proteins revealed a set of central hub proteins (Fig. 6B-C). Pathway enrichment analysis of these hub proteins highlighted glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, amino acid biosynthesis, RNA transport, spliceosome, and carbon metabolism (Fig. 6D). Conversely, the mutated genes associated with Kla-modified proteins in Fig. S7B were mainly involved in cytoskeleton in muscle cells, ABC transporter, ECM-receptor interaction, and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Kla interacts with genetic variations to influence proteins within metabolic pathways in either cis- or trans- modes.

Consistent with the established role of histones in maintaining genomic stability [23], we hypothesized that histone lactylation might correlate with genomic alterations. Indeed, lactylation at HIST1H4A K32, K80, and K92 correlated with CNV genes enriched chromatin-related pathways, including nucleosomes, chromosomal regions, and CENP − A containing chromatin (Fig. 6F). We further observed that NDUFS6 expression was significantly correlated with Kla at HIST1H2BD K6 (R2 = 0.41, p = 0.0018) and DDX46 K1025 (R2 = 0.45, p = 0.00094) in group T (Fig. 6G-H). This links histones lactylation and spliceosome proteins to mitochondrial metabolism, potentially representing a novel regulatory layer in tumor progression.

We next examined how Kla influences transcription factors (TFs), which are key proteins controlling gene expression and metastasis [24]. We found TF activities correlated more strongly with their Kla levels than with protein expression (Fig. 6I). As exemplified by signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), lactylation at residue K652 correlated more robustly with the expression of downstream RNAs (e.g., STAT2) than with its own protein expression (Fig. 6J-K). Furthermore, RNAs were ranked by their correlation with lactylation at STAT1 K652. These correlated transcripts were predominantly involved in immune and metabolic pathways (Fig. 6L). Collectively, these results offer deeper insight into how lactylation interacts with gene regulation across the central dogma, particularly within immunity and metabolic pathways.

PGAM1 K251 lactylation fostered ESCC metastasis by interacting with ACTG1

As shown in Fig. 5E and J, elevated Kla levels at several protein sites in cluster 3 and 4, including PGAM1, were peaked in the L group and associated with poor prognosis. PGAM1 was also involved in glycolysis and cytoskeleton pathways alterations. PGAM1 is a pivotal glycolytic enzyme that fuels energy metabolism in various tumors [25]. In addition to its established role in metabolic rewiring, PGAM1 can also promote tumor metastasis by interacting with cytoskeletal proteins such as actin alpha 2 and ACTG1 [13, 26]. Considering the relevance of PGAM1 with patient prognosis, metastatic process, and glycolysis and cytoskeleton pathways, we chose to further study the functional role and mechanisms of PGAM1 lactylation in ESCC progression. The prioritization criteria and the ranking of candidate lactylation sites, including PGAM1 K251, are provided in Supplementary Table S7. We first established KYSE150 and KYSE450 cell lines with stable PGAM1 knock-down, followed by rescue with the indicated vectors (Fig. S8A). The results demonstrated that WT PGAM1 underwent lactylation, and its Kla levels were elevated following treatment with sodium lactate (Nala) or a high dose of glucose (Fig. 7A, Fig S8B). To validate the lactylation sites in our lactylome analysis (K113 and K251), we constructed mutants of PGAM1 (K113R, K251R, and 2KR of both) and assessed their Kla levels upon Nala treatment. As predicted, the K251R mutation substantially reduced PGAM1 lactylation to a level comparable to that of the 2KR mutant, identifying K251 as the principal lactylation site (Fig. 7B, Fig. S8C), a result consistent with mass spectrometry (MS) detection (Fig. 7C). PGAM1 K251 lactylation does not substantially alter its enzymatic activity (Fig. S8D), suggesting that the pro-metastatic effect is possibly mediated by non-enzymatic activity. Protein–protein interactions are usually modulated by conformational changes or sequence variations. We found that both PGAM1 K113R and K251R mutations augmented their binding to ACTG1 (Fig. 7D). To specifically mimic lactylation, we further constructed K113la and K251la mutants carrying unnatural lactylation. Notably, both mimetic mutations strengthened PGAM1-ACTG1 interaction, with the effect being more pronounced for K251la (Fig. 7E). These results suggest that the lactylation at K113 and K251 may produce a functional effect akin to a sequence mutation. A similar pattern was observed for the lactylation-mimetic mutant of AK2 at K28 [11]. Confocal microscopy imaging of F-actin and G-actin (primarily ACTG1) in PGAM1 wild-type and K251R mutant KYSE150 cell lines was performed, confirming that Nala treatment increases F-actin formation and F-actin/G-actin ratio, while such effects were diminished in K251R mutant cell lines (Fig. S8E).

To define the functional impact of PGAM1 lactylation on ESCC metastasis, we used KYSE150 and KYSE450 cells with stable PGAM1 knockdown, which were then rescued with either PGAM1 WT or PGAM1 K251R mutant, with or without 10 mM Nala (thus forming four groups: transfected PGAM1 WT or K251R, Nala treated or untreated). Next, cell adhesion, wound healing, transwell migration and invasion assays were performed across these conditions. Compared to untreated cells, Nala treatment significantly enhanced the migration, invasion, and adhesion (to collagen I and FN) of ESCC cells expressing WT PGAM1. However, the same Nala treatment induced no significant functional changes in cells expressing the PGAM1 K251R mutant (Fig. 7F-I, S8F-I). Collectively, these results indicate that lactylation at PGAM1 K251 specifically promotes the metastatic potential of ESCC cells in vitro. In addition, we established an ESCC experimental lung metastasis model via tail vein injection using the same four cell groups. Results showed that re-overexpression of WT PGAM1 in PGAM1-deficient ESCC cells without treatment with Nala resulted in decreased lung metastasis in mice compared with Nala (10 mM) treatment. Furthermore, overexpression of the K251R mutant in PGAM1-deficient ESCC cells did not lead to significant changes in metastasis, regardless of Nala treatment (Fig. 7J-K). Overall, we demonstrate that lactylation of PGAM1 at K251 is a critical factor for ESCC metastasis. Consequently, we suggest that therapeutic interventions aimed at the PGAM1 K251 could be developed for treating metastatic ESCC.

Discussion

Building upon and extending existing multi-omics framework for ESCC, we have established a comprehensive resource for metastatic ESCC by profiling genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and lactylomic alterations in a cohort of 36 metastatic ESCC patients. Our analyses revealed distinctive metastatic molecular features, including immune and lactylation-related alterations, especially a candidate mechanism of PGAM1 K251 lactylation-ACTG1 axis. Moreover, cross-omics validation and correlation analyses indicated that alterations across these molecular layers are interconnected, which may collectively contribute to metastasis.

As a major form of genetic alteration, CNVs are known to promote tumorigenic phenotypes such as proliferation, invasion, metastasis, apoptosis, and senescence, etc [27]. The 13q32.1 locus amplification, for instance, is a known genetic alteration implicated in ESCC susceptibility and treatment outcomes [28]. We further delineated CNV patterns that differed between groups T and L. Specific amplifications (e.g., 3q11.1 and 5p15.1 in group T; 16q11.1 and 17p11.1 in group L) and deletions (e.g., 8p23.1 and 8p23.2 in group T; 15q11.2 and 14q32.33 in group L) were recurrently identified. These genomic alterations may drive invasive and metastatic phenotypes by dysregulating key oncogenes and tumor suppressors. We found the NOTCH1 gene was notably amplified in the majority of group T and approximately 40% of group L. While NOTCH1-p.C702fs is known to promote ovarian cancer metastasis and can be targeted by inhibitor LY3039478 [29], its special role in ESCC metastasis remains to be fully elucidated. Given that dysregulated Notch signaling was found to promotes tumor angiogenesis through the NOTCH1–USP5–STAT3 axis, its amplification in our cohort suggests a potential role in driving ESCC metastasis [30]. TP53 gene was also the most frequently mutated gene in our cohort. Additionally, recurrent mutations were detected in genes such as TTN, DNAH5, PCLO, etc., though their special functional implications in ESCC remain uncharacterized. In general, group T exhibited a higher burden of point mutations and CNVs than group L, suggesting that ESCC invasion and migration are likely driven by alternative factors, such as dysregulated mRNAs/proteins expression, epigenetic modifications, PTMs, or RNA–protein interactions. Given the abundance of Kla sites identified in both groups T and L, we propose that genomic variations functionally intersect with widespread protein lactylation, synergistically driving metastatic gene expression in ESCC.

Combined transcriptomic and proteomic analysis showed strong RNA–protein concordance for immunity genes, whereas metabolic genes exhibited weaker correlations, suggesting differential regulatory mechanisms between these functional categories. We also observed that metastatic ESCC exhibits disrupted immune pathway and a marked B and T cells enrichment in metastatic lymph nodes. This finding contrasts with the prevailing notion that metastatic nodes typically harbor low levels of cytotoxic T cells infiltration [31]. Moreover, immunity pathways such as B cell receptor, CAM, ECM-receptor interaction, and focal adhesion were significantly activated in group L. We identified ICAM1, a member of the IgSF, as a prognostic marker in ESCC, with functional data suggesting its role as a potential contributor of metastasis both in vivo and in vitro. Our IHC analyses suggest that ICAM1 is possibly tumor intrinsic, promoting metastasis via intercellular adhesion signaling. However, these data were obtained from whole-tissue lysates and bulk sequencing. Although we have adopted rigorous criteria of tumor purity > 50%, the observed B-cell, T-cell, BCR, and CAM signals may still partly reflect the anatomical composition of lymph nodes. Spatial and single-cell transcriptomics would be ideal to definitively dissect the contributions and interactions of tumor cells versus the microenvironment.

Additionally, proteins associated with gene CNVs exhibited distinct functional enrichment between groups. They were primarily linked to metabolic pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species in group T, suggesting a role in sustaining tumor bioenergetics and oxidative stress adaptation. Group L showed enrichment in the Ras signaling pathway and regulation of actin cytoskeleton, implying enhanced migratory capabilities at this stage. While metabolic reprogramming is a distinct hallmark of ESCC [32], the precise mechanisms by which it fuels metastatic spread are not yet fully clarified. Recurrent CNVs identified in our cohort were significantly enriched in key signaling pathways, including rap1, Ras, and PI3k-Akt, which are known to transduce signals through downstream protein phosphorylation. We further assessed the coherence between CNVs and protein abundance. This analysis identified 13 proteins whose expression was consistently altered in both groups T and L, representing putative cis-regulatory products of their corresponding CNVs, which may play convergent roles across ESCC stages. Additionally, while recurrent CNVs affected other mitochondrial respiratory chain genes, their RNA and protein expression levels were poorly correlated. Collectively, our analysis delineated a dysregulated regulatory network in metastatic ESCC and positioning CNVs as associated with specific proteomic alterations. Our analysis revealed that CNVs had a limited impact on the expression of metabolic genes, with minimal concordance between mRNA and protein levels. This implies that the high energy demands of ESCC cells may be met not through gene dosage changes, but rather via faster-acting mechanisms such as enzymatic conformational changes or PTMs. We therefore propose that diverse PTMs, particularly Kla, could critically modulate metabolic protein activity, revealing previously unrecognized regulatory mechanisms. We identified extensive and aberrant protein lactylation Kla in both groups T and L, compared to group N. PTMs like Kla substantially diversify the proteome and represent promising biomarkers and candidate targets [33]. Based on the dynamics of Kla during ESCC metastasis, we categorized Kla patterns into 3 major categories. Notably, proteins with elevated Kla levels were predominantly enriched in metabolic pathways. This enrichment helps resolve the observed transcriptome-proteome discordance in metabolism and underscores the functional importance of Kla in driving cancer metastasis.

Specifically, we identified lactylation at the K251 site of the metabolic enzyme PGAM1 as associated with poor ESCC prognosis. This modification enhanced its binding to ACTG1, thereby driving ESCC cell invasion and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. Targeting K251 lactylation of PGAM1 therefore represents a candidate avenue for preclinical investigation. Structural studies are needed to determine whether lactylation alters PGAM1 conformation or allosterically modulates cytoskeletal binding. While PGAM1 K251 lactylation enhances ACTG1 binding and promotes F-actin organization, whether ACTG1 is absolutely required for the metastatic phenotype remains to be formally established. Given the essential role of ACTG1 in cytoskeletal integrity and cell migration, conventional knockdown or knockout approaches may confound phenotypic interpretation. Specifically blocking the interaction between PGAM1 and ACTG1, such as peptide competition or the use of interaction-defective PGAM1 mutants, would be suggested for future studies. Moreover, PGAM1 K251 lactylation was identified by high-resolution mass spectrometry in our discovery cohort, independent validation using targeted proteomics (e.g., PRM) or a site-specific antibody in a larger cohort is required before clinical translation. Pharmacological intervention targeting PGAM1 K251 lactylation was not developed in this study. PGAM1 K251 lactylation is therefore nominated as a candidate dependency for future drug discovery. As for in vivo models, our study primarily used mouse tail‑vein injection, which models hematogenous dissemination and lung colonization rather than spontaneous lymphatic metastasis. We therefore interpret our in vivo findings as evidence of metastatic colonization potential rather than as a direct validation of lymphatic metastasis mechanisms. Although our human discovery cohort data support the clinical relevance of PGAM1 K251 lactylation, future studies using orthotopic esophageal implantation, patient‑derived xenografts, or genetically engineered mouse models are needed to more fully model the clinical metastatic route.

Our combined analysis reveals that Kla on histones, TFs, and spliceosome proteins defines a previously unrecognized regulation layer in ESCC biology. Collectively, our findings elucidate how Kla correlates with gene expression across the central dogma, particularly within metabolic pathways. A major challenge in cancer research remains the mechanistic interpretation of multi-layered regulatory networks, particularly in establishing causal relationships between molecular variations. Future efforts should aim to systematically integrate diverse PTMs forms to elucidate their collective impact on tumor biology. Our finding that K251 lactylation alters PGAM1 function offers a starting point for such detailed mechanistic investigation.

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first matched multi-omic dataset (including lactylome) for metastatic ESCC. However, our findings may not generalize to treated, unresectable, recurrent, or geographically diverse ESCC. Independent validation in external multi-omic cohorts is a future priority. In summary, our study established a comprehensive proteogenomic resource for metastatic ESCC, delineating key factors of metastasis through systematic multi-omics integration. Notably, both immune disturbance and the lactylation of metabolic or cytoskeletal pathway proteins are associated with metastasis, possibly through parallel pathways. Functional and mechanistic studies highlight the potential of ICAM1 and PGAM1 K251 lactylation as candidate targets in metastatic ESCC, warranting further preclinical validation.

Methods

Patient samples

Sample acquisition and clinical information

Thirty-six patients with ESCC with lymph node metastasis who underwent surgical esophagectomy at Changhai Hospital and Shandong Cancer Hospital, China, between September 2021 and February 2022 were recruited. None of the patients had received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy before surgery. Adjacent normal tissues, primary tumor tissues, and lymph node metastases were grouped for proteogenomic analysis. Tumor staging of these samples was determined according to the AJCC TNM staging system (the eighth edition), based on surgical pathology specimens. The clinical follow-up period of the patients was at least 2 years. The clinical features of the patients included are summarized in Table S1. We recruited another cohort of 116 patients with ESCC with survival outcomes from Changhai Hospital, which was part of the CMECC cohort for one clinical study (registry number: NCT06410677), for further IHC analysis. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples of surgically resected ESCC tissues were obtained from the Pathology Department of Changhai Hospital.

Sample processing

Clinical samples were processed at Changhai Hospital and Shandong Cancer Hospital. After surgery, the tumor tissues, adjacent normal esophagus tissues (> 3 cm away from the cutting edge), and lymph node metastases were collected and labeled (with date of collection, patient ID, and sample type). For quality control and tumor purity assessment, a small portion (approximately 2–3 mm3) of each primary tumor and lymph node metastasis specimen was immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. H&E-stained sections were prepared and independently reviewed by two pathologists. Only samples in which > 50% of the tissue area was estimated to contain tumor cells were enrolled for multi-omics analysis. Samples with tumor purity below this threshold were excluded. The remaining tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min after surgery and then stored at −80 °C at Changhai Hospital until shipped to PTM Bio. (Hangzhou, China) for subsequent nucleic acid and protein extraction.

Cell lines

Cell lines, including KYSE30 (catalog number: ACC-351, from the DSMZ), KYSE150 (catalog number: TCHu236, from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture) and KYSE450 (catalog number: ACC-387, from the DSMZ), were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI1640, catalog number: L220KJ, BasalMedia) medium, while HEK293T (catalog number: SCSP-502, purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture) cell line, was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Catalog number: L110KJ, BasalMedia) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, catalog number: WS500T, Ausbian) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, catalog number: PWL062, meilunbio), at 37 °C in 5% CO2 incubator. All cell lines were verified by short tandem repeat testing and tested for mycoplasma.

Mice

Six -week-old BALB/c Nude female mice (GemPharmatech, Strain# D000521) were bred under specific pathogen-free conditions and used for tail vein injection metastasis exploration. Mice were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. Every 3–5 mice were housed in the same type of cage with a 12-h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 20–24 °C and a humidity of 40–60%. Clean water and food were provided ad libitum.

Genomic and transcriptomic sample preparation and data acquisition

Genomic DNA and total RNA extraction

After resection, every recruited patient with ESCC provided a minimum of 20 mg of tissue, including adjacent normal tissue, primary tumor, and lymph node metastasis. Genomic DNAs and total RNAs were extracted using a HiPure Universal DNA/RNA Kit(Cat#D3018/Cat#R4130-03) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Equalbit 1 × dsDNA HS Assay Kit was used to determine the extracted DNAs, and the OD260/OD280 values were measured using a NanoDrop2000. For library preparation, at least 300 ng of high-quality DNA (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.0) was sufficient. NanoDrop8000 and an Agilent Bioanalyzer were used to quantify and assess the quality of the extracted RNAs. RNA samples were selected for sequencing only if they met the quality criteria and had an RNA integrity number score ≥ 7.

Whole-exome sequencing

Library construction

WES libraries construction was achieved by using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme) following the standardized protocol. We further achieved the repairment of 5’ and 3’ hanging fragments, 3’ adenylation, and total DNA fragmentation by FEA Enzyme Mix V2. Purified fragments were ligated with indexed adapters using DNA ligase. The adapter-ligated DNA fragments were amplified using VAHTS HiFi Amplification Mix, and the products were further purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads. Finally, the constructed libraries were quantified using Qubit DsDNA HS kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

In-solution hybrid selection, amplification, and sequencing

Illumina Nextera Exome Kit was utilized for hybridization and exome capture in accordance with the standardized protocol. DNA libraries were cluster amplified using exclusion amplification chemistry and flow cells, which were also provided by Illumina. Flow cells were further sequenced by synthesis chemistry and analyzed using RTA v.2.7.3. Size distribution of the sequenced libraries was analyzed using Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100. To meet the coverage criteria for all libraries, the entire exome library pool was sequenced into paired 76 cycles operation. Index reads were performed with two 8 cycles across the required lanes.

The libraries were then pooled via paired end runs on an Illumina HiSeq PE150 with a 200 × coverage depth of the input libraries. To remove adaptors and low-quality sequences, the raw sequencing data were further demultiplexed and converted into fastq files. All experiments were performed by Rongxiang Biotech. Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA-seq library construction

First, mRNAs were enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads in RNA samples that passed the quality control. The obtained mRNAs fragments were broken into short segments by adding fragmentation buffer. Then, the first strand of cDNAs was synthesized using these segments as templates and random hexamer primers. The second strand of cDNAs was synthesized by adding buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA Polymerase I. The products were further purified using the QIAQuick PCR kit and eluted with ultrapure water. Double-stranded cDNAs were then subjected to end repair, tailing, and ligation using sequencing adapters. Finally, the target-sized fragments via gel electrophoresis and amplified using PCR.

RNA sequencing

Different qualified libraries were pooled in terms of the effective concentration and appointed output data size. The libraries were then sequenced according to the basic principle of sequencing-by-synthesis. The first is the addition of fluorescent-labeled DNA polymerases, dNTPs, adapter primers, and other necessary components to the flow cells. During the extension process of the complementary strands, fluorescently labeled dNTPs were added, and the corresponding fluorescence was released. The fluorescence signals were then captured by a sequencer, and the optical signals were converted to sequencing peaks using the related software. Finally, the target sequences were obtained with reads. These experiments were performed by Rongxiang Biotech. Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Furthermore, the obtained reads were mapped to the reference genome hg38, and QC steps were conducted. We were concerned with the total mapped read number, number of mRNAs and rRNAs, and transcripts of the housekeeping genes. The expected expression patterns were confirmed using clustered samples according to their expression levels in different groups.

WES and RNA-seq data processing

Genome alignment

The WES and RNA-Seq data were harmonized using NCI Genomic Data Commons (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/gdc-data-processing). The reference genome used was hg38 (GRCh38.d1.vd1).

Somatic mutations calling and CNV analysis

Somatic mutations calling from WES tumor, lymph metastasis nodes, and adjacent normal matched BAMs were performed with the SomaticWrapper following the filters as: 1) both group T and L VAF ≥ 0.01 and group N VAF ≤ 0.02; 2) in both group T and L, read depth ≥ 14 and in group N, read depth ≤ 8; 3) the indel length was less than 100 bp; 4) all mutations must be called by more than 2 callers and be exonic. Otherwise, all SNVs and indels were called from samples in our cohort using MuTect v1.1.7 in SomaticWrapper. We annotated the variants using the Ensemble VEP, including frameshift and non-frameshift indels, stop gains, and synonymous or non-synonymous SNVs. Genes with obvious mutation frequencies were evaluated using MutSigCV (v1.41).

BAM files were used to analyze CNVs and further processed with BIC-seq2 to call the CNVs. Normalized sequencing data from groups T, L, and N were compared. Genome regions with the copy number ratio of ≥ 0.58 were further termed as losses or gains. GISTIC (v2.0.23) was used to identify arm-level and significant CNVs in our cohort.

RNA-seq data analysis and mRNA quantification

The adaptors of the RNA-Seq raw reads were trimmed and the quality of the RNA-seq data was assessed using FastQC 0.11.7. All raw reads were then mapped on-to the reference genome, GRCh38, using HISAT2 2.2.1. Mapped reads were assembled into genes or transcripts using StringTie (v1.3.4d). The relative abundances of these genes and transcripts were further quantified using normalized metrics as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. Next, genes with low expression levels were removed using edgeR package 3.34.1.

Sample preparation and data collection of proteomic and lactylomic

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion

Liquid nitrogen was used to grind the tissue samples into cell powder, which was then transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. Thereafter, 4 times the volume of lysis buffer, including 3 μM TSA, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 1% Triton X-100, and 50 mM NAM, was added to the Ep tubes and sonicated on ice for 3 min. By centrifugation at 12,000 g and 4 oC for 10 min, the debris was removed, and the supernatant was collected into new Ep tubes. Their concentrations were determined using bicinchoninic acid method following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For protein precipitation, TCA was added to samples slowly to the final concentration as 20% (m/v). After incubating the samples at 4 oC for 2 h, the precipitates were collected via centrifugation at 4500 g and 4 oC for 5 min. The precipitates were washed with pre-cooled acetone. The precipitated proteins were redissolved in 200 mM TEAB and dispersed via ultrasonication. At a mass ratio of 1:50 (trypsin-to-protein), trypsin was added to the samples to digest the proteins overnight. All samples were reduced by 5 mM dithiothreitol at 37 oC for 60 min and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide at 20–22 oC for 45 min in the dark. Finally, all the digested peptides were desalted using SPE columns.

Affinity enrichment

NETN buffer (1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0, 50 mM Tris–HCl) were used to dissolve tryptic peptides. The dissolved peptides were incubated at 4 oC with pan-Kla antibody beads (Cat:#1404, PTM Bio) overnight with gentle shaking to enrich the peptides with Kla modification. The samples were further washed four times with NETN buffer and then ultrapure water 2 times. Next, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid was used to elute the pan-Kla antibody-bead-bound peptides. The peptides were combined, dried, and desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore) for further processing.

LC–MS/MS analysis

The bound peptides were dissolved in solvent A composed of 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The samples were then loaded onto reversed-phase analytical columns. Solvent B was prepared by diluting formic acid (0.1%) in acetonitrile. The peptides were separated on a NanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics) using solvent B at a flow rate of 450 nL/min. Next, these peptides were subjected to capillary analysis using timsTOF Pro (Bruker Daltonics) with 1.65 kV electrospray voltage. The scanning range was set from 100 to 1700 m/z to analyze the precursors and fragments. Parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) was selected as the operating mode for timsTOF Pro. The precursor charge states selected for fragmentation were 0 to 5. Ten PASEF-MS/MS scans were acquired in each cycle. For proteomic and lactylomic analyse, the dynamic exclusion was set at 30 and 24 s, respectively.

Proteomic and lactylomic data processing

Database search

The MS data were processed using MaxQuant (v.1.6.15.0). The SwissProt database, which was concatenated with the reverse decoy, was used to search for tandem mass spectra. The selected cleavage enzyme was trypsin/P with no more than 2 missing cleavages of the proteome and 4 for the lactylome. In addition, the mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm in the first search, 20 ppm in the main search processes for the precursor ions, and 20 ppm for the fragment ions. Lactylation of Lys was the variable modification, and carbamidomethyl of Cys was the fixed modification used in our study. FDR was set as < 1%.

Quantitative and repeatability analysis

After searching the selected databases, the raw LC–MS/MS datasets were converted into matrices, including the LFQ intensity of both proteins and peptides. The modified peptides intensity (I) was centralized and transformed into relative quantitative values (R). Moreover, the formula was listed as i for samples and j for proteins/modified peptides: Rij = Iij/Mean(Ij). In our cohort, the relative quantitative values of the proteins were divided by the relative quantitative values of the Kla peptides to eliminate the impact of lactylation. The PCA method was adopted to further evaluate the quantitative reproducibility, and if the biological replicates aggregated together, it indicated good repeatability.

Differential analysis and functional classification

Samples from groups T, L, and N were further selected for comparison according to the FC calculation using the median intensity of each protein. To conduct function, pathway annotation, and enrichment analyses, the DEPs were processed using ClusterProfiler (v4.6.2). Subcellular locations and structures were annotated using SMART [34].

PPI network

All DEPs and differentially expressed Kla sites were searched using STRING (v11.0) to construct the PPI networks. Interactions with a confidence score > 0.7 were fetched as high confidence. All networks were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.1).

Motif analysis

The motif features of Kla sites were analyzed using iceLogo with a background of the human proteome [35]. In general, the motif sequences comprised 13 amino acids upstream and downstream of the identified Kla sites.

Integrated analysis

Impacts of CNVs on RNAs and proteins expression levels

GISTIC2 was used to identify significant CNVs with the increased threshold set to 0.4, and the confidence level set to 0.95. Other parameters were set to their respective default values. This analysis was performed on all CNVs data for all chromosomes. Next, all genes with quantifiable CNVs, mRNAs, and protein expression, and Kla-modified proteins were filtered. Finally, the correlation coefficients between them were calculated and genes with significant CNVs cis- or trans-effect by CNV-mRNA or CNV-protein correlations were filtered (FDR < 0.05). Furthermore, the filtered gene sets were used for enrichment analysis to identify concerned biological processes [36].

GSVA

GSVA is an unsupervised and nonparametric analysis method suitable for discovering how various gene-enriched clusters differ from each other. GSVA was performed on the transcriptomic and proteomic data of all samples in groups T, L, and N. To assign the signaling pathway changing scores for every gene cluster, the “GSVA” package was selected (v1.46.0). Gene clusters were identified from molecular signature databases. A t-value of > 1 was considered significant. The correlations of the pathway-changing scores were analyzed between the transcriptomic and proteomic data for every sample.

Pseudo-time analysis

Pseudotime analysis was conducted with the SCORPIUS package (v1.0.8) to describe the immunity proteins developmental trajectories in ESCC metastasis. In addition, features of immune proteins whose expression levels changed with ESCC progression were identified using the draw_trajectory_plot. Thus, dynamic changes in vital proteins associated with ESCC metastasis could be captured.

Clustering of Mfuzz expression patterns

Based on their dynamic expression profiles, the fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm (Mfuzz package, v2.64 in R) was utilized to classify differentially expressed Kla-modified proteins. The input data contained all scale intensity values of the six clusters. After standardization, each Kla site was assigned to a specific cluster based on its expression pattern. Enrichment analysis was conducted for further inference.

Mutation impact on transcriptome, proteome, and lactylome

The cis- and trans-effects of genes with significantly somatic mutations were explored at the transcriptomic, proteomic, and lactylomic levels. After excluding silent mutations, all samples were stratified into mutation-positive and WT groups for every target gene by systematically removing missing values. Meanwhile, molecular feature differential analysis was performed for RNAs, proteins, or Kla sites between all groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with a minimum demand of three samples/comparison groups. In terms of cumulative values, the statistically significant features that matched the threshold of FDR less than 0.05 were ranked. Pathway enrichment analysis was also performed using GSEA implemented in R.

Solvent-accessible surface area and distance location analysis

The structures of the targeted proteins from AlphaFold were obtained to analyze their solvent-accessible surface areas. The filtering criteria were structures with an average per-residue confidence metric of over 70 and a length of less than 2,700 aa could be selected. In addition, the distance assignments and relative solvent accessibility values of Kla sites were calculated using DSSP 4.0 [37]. The Kla sites were classified as hidden or exposed depending on whether their average relative solvent accessibility was below 20% [38].

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed by the log-rank test. The coefficient values were calculated through Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, and p value < 0.05 represented a significant difference. The study endpoints were OS and PFS. Moreover, clinical variables whose univariate analysis had p < 0.05 were selected to perform Cox regression multivariate analysis. R package “survival” and R software 4.2 were used for survival statistical examination.

IHC staining and scoring

To evaluate the expression levels of ICAM1 and Pan-Kla in ESCC tumors, lymph node metastasis, and adjacent normal tissues, IHC was performed using anti-ICAM1 antibody (1:200, Servicebio, Cat# GB14040-50) and anti-L-Lactyl Lysine antibody (1:50, PTM Bio Inc., Cat# PTM-1401RM). IHC slides were processed with an automated Leica Bond staining system, in accordance with a standardized protocol. The average integrated optical density (IOD) values were calculated using ImageJ v7.0.0.3 for IHC scoring. Based on the IOD values, the 116 cases in this study were defined as having high or low expression in a 1:1 ratio.

qRT-PCR

Total RNAs was extracted with the HiPure Universal RNA Mini Kit (Magen, Cat #R4130-03) and then reverse transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript II First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). Next, qRT-PCR was performed following the normal procedures, and the relative gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin expression.

Plasmids, transfection, infection, and stable cell lines construction

KYSE150, KYSE 450 or HEK 293 T cell lines were seeded in six-well plates (catalog number: TCP010006, BIOFIL) until the density reached 80–90%. The indicated plasmids were transiently transfected with the Hieff trans-liposomal transfection reagent (catalog number: 40802ES03, Yeasen). After transfection for 24 h, the cells were cultured in PBS or 10 mM L-sodium lactate (CAS number: 867–56-1, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h before collection.

The human DNA sequences of ICAM1 and PGAM1 were obtained from the NCBI database. shRNAs against ICAM1 (5’-caaccaatgtgctattcaa-3’) and PGAM1 (5’-caggttctcagtctaagcta-3’) were cloned into pGIPZ and UNI-PCDH-FLAG-Hygro-5’ vector was used to construct the PGAM1 WT or K251R plasmids. Transfection, infection, and stable cell line construction were performed as described below. Briefly, HEK 293 T cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 50% on the day before transfection. The indicated plasmids containing shRNAs were then co-transfected against ICMA1/PGAM1 or rescued PGAM1 with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 and envelope plasmid pMD2.G using the same transfection reagents. After transfection of 48 h and 72 h, the lentiviral supernatants were collected and filtered using a 0.22 μm filter (catalog number: SLGP033RB, Sigma-Aldrich). Both KYSE150 and KYSE450 cells were infected with the virus for 12 h until the fresh medium was replaced. Puromycin (CAS number: 58–58-2, Yeasen) was used at the final concentration of 3 μg/mL to select the stable cell lines after infecting the virus for 48 h.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

After transfection for 48 h, both KYSE150 or KYSE450 cells were harvested and washed twice with pre-cooled PBS for immunoprecipitation (IP). Samples were lysed with IP buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1% Triton X-100, and 150 mM NaCl) at 4 oC for 30 min with rotation. The cell lysates were then centrifuged at 16,000 g and 4 oC for 10 min. Then, 10% of the supernatant was collected as the input sample. Furthermore, the remaining supernatants were incubated at 4 oC under rotation for 2 h with 4 μL anti-Flag antibody-conjugated agarose beads (A2220-5ML, Sigma-Aldrich). The treated beads were washed three times with pre-cooled IP buffer. Finally, proteins captured by the treated beads were denatured through heating at 95 oC for 5 min directly.

Next, we separated the IP and input samples through sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred them onto a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. After blocking at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies, including pan-Kla (catalog number: PTM-1401RM, PTM-BIO), Flag, PGAM1, β-actin, and HA (catalog number: 20543–1-AP, 16,126–1-AP, 66,009–1-Ig and 51,064–2-AP, all purchased from Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies were washed and the secondary antibodies (catalog number:115–035-003 targeted mouse and 111–035–144 targeted rabbit, Jackson ImmunoResearch) were added for secondary incubation. The indicated protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit on a Tanon 4600 system.

Site-specific incorporation of Kla into PGAM1

Kla was incorporated into PGAM1 by employing genetic code expansion, which could induce acylation modifications at specific sites on the indicated proteins. Human PGAM1 cDNAs were cloned into the eukaryotic expression vector, pFlag-cmv-3’. The K113/K251 sites of PGAM1 were mutated from a codon encoding lysine to an amber-mutated termination codon (TAG). Moreover, lactyl-lysine was recognized by the plasmid pNEU_hMb_KlaRS and incorporated into TAG. Next, both the pFlag-cmv-3’-PGAM1 K113TAG or pFlag-cmv-3’-PGAM1 K251TAG plasmids were transfected with pNEU_hMb_KlaRS together into HEK293T cells. The cells were treated with 2 mM lactyllysine and collected for further analysis after 48 h.

Immunofluorescence staining of F-actin and G-actin

KYSE150 cells stably expressing PGAM1-WT or PGAM1-K251R, with or without 10 mM sodium lactate treatment, were seeded on coverslips. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated phalloidin for F-actin, and with anti-G-actin antibody followed by Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated secondary antibody for G-actin. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) with a 63 × oil immersion objective.

Cell adhesion assay

First, rescue-stable KYSE150 and KYSE450 cell lines were treated with PBS or 10 mM L-sodium lactate for 24 h. In addition, the 96-well plates (catalog number: TCP010096, BIOFIL) were coated with 5 μg/cm2 collagen I and FN for 2 h at room temperature. Next, 5 × 104 KYSE150 and 1 × 105 KYSE450 cells were seeded in coated 96-well plates and cultured for 2 h. To remove non-adherent cells, the 96-well plates were washed three times with PBS. There was no need to wash the plates for the control groups because the total number of cells was measured to calculate the cell adhesion rate. Finally, cell counting kit-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance (A) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax M5e Microplate Reader (San Jose, CA, USA).

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Transwell chambers (catalog number: 3422, Corning) were used for the migration and invasion assays. For the migration assays, the rescue-stable KYSE150 and KYSE450 cell lines were resuspended with or without 10 mM L-sodium lactate in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. Next, 250 μL cell suspensions containing 3 × 04 KYSE150 or 5 × 105 KYSE450 cells were added into the upper chambers of each inserted transwell. The bottom chambers were covered with 1 mL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20% FBS. After 48 h, all the chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (catalog number: E672002-0500, Sangon Biotech) for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (CAS number: 548–62-9, Titan) for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed three times with PBS. After drying, images were captured using a microscope (CKX53; OLYMPUS). ImageJ v7.0.0.3 was used to count the cells that migrated to the bottom chambers. Besides, the upper chambers were coated with Matrigel (Cat# 356,234, Corning) at 37 °C for 2 h to perform the invasion assays. The cells were cultured for 72 h before measurement, and all other steps were the same.

Wound healing assay

Rescue stable KYSE150 and KYSE450 cell lines were seeded in 6-well plates. Next, the wound lines were scratched with a 200 μL sterile plastic tip when the cell monolayer was formed. After scratching, floating cells were removed using PBS. These cells were further cultured in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium containing PBS or 10 mM L-sodium lactate. Images were taken at 0, 24 or 36 h. ImageJ v7.0.0.3 was also used to evaluate the wound healing areas.

PGAM1 activity assay

PGAM1 enzymatic activity was measured using the Phosphoglycerate Mutase Activity Assay Kit (BioVision, K2007) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, KYSE150 cells expressing Flag-tagged PGAM1 (WT, K251R and K251la) were lysed with cold PGAM lysis buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants and standards were incubated with the reaction mix in a 96‑well plate. The assay couples PGAM1‑mediated conversion of 3‑phosphoglycerate to NADH oxidation, monitored by absorbance decrease at 340 nm over 10 min at 37 °C. Each assay was performed in triplicate with three independent experiments.

ESCC metastasis mouse model and in vivo imaging

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Changhai Hospital. Female BALB/c nude mice aged 6 weeks were selected to establish mouse models following institutional guidelines. Mice were randomly assigned to each experimental group using computer‑generated random numbers. Around 1 × 106 luciferase-expressing and treated ESCC cells (with the indicated groups) were resuspended in PBS for each mouse and injected via the tail vein. For the lactate-treated group, sodium lactate (120 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally once daily from the day of cell injection. The status of each mouse was monitored weekly using an IVIS-100 Imaging System. No exclusion criteria were predefined, and all mice that reached the experimental endpoint were included in the final analysis. Humane endpoints were established in accordance with institutional animal care protocols, including a tumor burden exceeding 10% of body weight, signs of severe distress, or an inability to access food/water. At the experimental endpoint (4 weeks post-injection, or earlier if humane endpoints were reached), mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. Lungs were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and paraffin-embedded. Serial sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation. The investigator who quantified lung metastases was blinded to group allocation throughout the analysis. Sample size was determined based on a pilot experiment (n = 5 per group) in which a 50% reduction in metastatic nodule count was detected (α = 0.05, power = 0.8), yielding a required sample size of 5 mice per group.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Correlation tests were performed using Spearman’s rank test unless specially indicated. Continuous variables, if normally distributed, were compared using a student t-test or ANOVA. Otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskall-Wallis tests were performed. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the association between unordered categorical variables. p values were adjusted for FDR in multiple comparisons by the Benjamini & Hochberg method. All statistical analyses were performed using R software 4.2 and Python 3.7.
