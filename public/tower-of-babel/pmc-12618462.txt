Hyperconnected amorphous oxide networks under compression
Authors: Sung Keun Lee, Elias El Ghazaoui, Jin Jung Kweon, Seoyoung Lee, Jae-Hyeon Parq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrreversibly densified oxide glass under extreme deformation exhibits unexpected softening with plasticity, while the glass network typically becomes rigid as it densifies. These distinct mechanical responses in amorphous networks remain puzzling at the atomic level. Understanding this behavior requires knowledge of how the network entangles and connects under high pressure. Here, our measurements on densified amorphous oxides under irreversible densification via magnetic resonance spectroscopy provide evidence of enhanced network entanglement and, consequently, hyperconnectivity, as highlighted by an increase in highly coordinated Al’s and their spatial proximity. The results reveal that configurational diversity in prototypical amorphous Al2O3 under irreversible densification is more prominent than those of other complex oxide glasses, reaching hyperconnected under much lower pressures. In general, attainment of configurational diversity at lower pressures is promoted by increasing field strength of non-network cations in glasses under irreversible densification. The enhanced connectivity with increasing densification allows us to postulate the origin of mechanical responses in glass networks. Particularly, attainment of hyperconnectivity under lower pressures may promote network flexibility during deformation. This conceptual protocol enables control of dual mechanical responses in glasses under extreme stress, guiding the discovery of super-hard densified glasses for technological innovation and accounting for the weakening of hyperconnected glasses in planetary interiors.Subject terms: Glasses, Solid-state NMR, Structure of solids and liquids, Mineralogy, Phase transitions and critical phenomena

Introduction

Glasses under compression at high pressure experience irreversible deformation after pressure release, which permanently increases the glass density and modifies the thermo-mechanical properties1–4. The nature of the irreversible structural evolution of oxide glass is required to properly infer glass deformation during indentation-induced densification and/or shear flow under contact loading (e.g.5,6, and references therein). Topological network’s adaptation (i.e., bond-angle, length, ring-size distributions, and cage-structure) has often been attributed to such irreversible densifications (e.g.,7–14). Furthermore, high-pressure characteristics in both short- (i.e., cation coordination number) and medium-range structures (structural changes beyond the 2nd coordination environments) could partly remain in decompressed glasses3,15,16, while portion of the higher-pressure structural signatures is indeed relaxed10,17,18 (see supporting information, S1). Particularly, the preserved, highly coordinated[5,6]Al’s in the decompressed oxide glasses tends to increase with increasing peak pressure, contributing to the permanent increase in glass density [see3 and references therein]. Therefore, identifying such structural changes during irreversible densification offer insights into the mechanical responses1,5,19–25, for example, extensive experimental studies unveil the link between the mechanical properties and their short-range structures under lower-pressure compression (below 2 GPa) and near the glass transition temperatures [e.g.,2,20,26–28]. While such relationships under much higher-pressure conditions remain to be established, earlier theoretical studies also suggested the importance of the role of highly coordinated Si as well as the prevalence of the edge-sharing topology on the mechanical responses of oxide glasses (e.g.,29,30). These structural transitions under irreversible densification could also provide atomic insights into anomalous pressure-induced decrease in their sound wave velocities and shear and bulk modulus in silicate glasses (i.e., mechanical weakening) [See31–33 and references therein].

While oxide glasses are often expected to deform brittlely, recent reports revealed an anomalous ductility in prototypical amorphous Al2O3 (a-Al2O3)34,35, the vital component of electronic devices and technologically important glasses, including those thin glasses used for cell phone covers1,23,24,36–38. Irreversibly densified a-Al2O3 may also have technological applications, as its resistance to crack and chemical corrosion could improve upon pressurization. The detailed structural transformation in a-Al2O3 with distinctive Al-O bond and network topology under pressures at room temperature could reveal structural origin of the ability of glasses to avoid usual brittle fracturing. Whereas transformation in a-Al2O3 has been explored via theoretical classical molecular dynamics (MD) studies [e.g.,39,40], and its 1 atm structure was experimentally revealed [e.g.,36; S2], no experimental study on the structure of a-Al2O3 at high pressures is available; unlike other prototypical oxide glasses such as SiO2, B2O3, and GeO2 that have been extensively studied under pressure, high-pressure structure of a-Al2O3 has not been experimentally probed. This is partly because of the difficulty in synthesis, as traditional melt-quenching does not form glasses. Nevertheless, the amorphous phase was achieved through thin-film deposition37,41 and via sol-gel synthesis37,42,43, providing a unique access to probe the detailed pressure-induced structural transitions in a-Al2O3. Despite unique synthesis paths for a-Al2O3, its structure does not vary substantially with synthesis methods (S244–46; for the difference in nature of amorphous oxides and oxide glasses formed by melt-quenching). The structure of a-Al2O3 under irreversible densification could provide a key to the puzzle of dual mechanical responses in non-crystalline amorphous oxides.

As the glass undergoes densification, entanglement of the amorphous network may evolve18,47. The connectivity among cationic species may increase upon densification. Here, we introduce the term hyperconnectivity to describe the increase in overall connectivity and entanglement in network. As the nature of entanglement in 3-dimenstional glass network is expected to be complex, to visualize such entanglement in a simple 2-dimension network, we use the Seoul metropolitan subway network (Fig. 1A) where the number of transfer stations (i.e., at least two subway lines intersect) increases, with increasing number of subway line (n), along with the number of total stations (Figs. S1 and S3). Increasing n corresponds to an increase in network density (or pressure), demonstrating a pressure-induced increase in network entanglement (Fig. 1B). In line with this analogy, densification in amorphous network increases the coordination number of the constituent atoms [e.g., from [4](Si, Al) to [5,6](Si, Al), or [5,6]T, were T refers to Si and Al] (e.g.,13,47–54 and references therein). Under low pressure conditions, the spatial proximity between highly coordinated species is low, corresponding to a lower degree of network entanglement. The higher degree of entanglement and thus, hyperconnectivity in networks is characterized with larger fractions of highly coordinated cations and an increase in their proximity (e.g., [5,6]T…..[5,6]T fraction)55; while the full quantification remains to be established (as it depends on the types of the networks), the hyperconnectivity in the current study specifically refers to a state of an entangled network where the fractions of high-pressure configurations generated upon external compression (with respect to 1 atm) and/or the proportion of any equivalent high-energy structural units (e.g., [5,6]Al) are sufficiently high, so that the proximity between these units and those without perturbation (e.g., [4]Al) integrated over the 3rd coordination shells (within medium-range structures) is substantial ( ~ 30-40%) (See S3 for detailed definition). As a network approaches hyperconnectivity, network topology under densification can also be accompanied by a smaller bond angle distribution and diminished ring size, collapse of cages, and the reduced free volume (i.e., topological contraction). We refer to these changes collectively, as an enhanced network entanglement (see S3). The pressure-driven evolution of the degree of entanglement may be linked to the network flexibility in a-Al2O3, while experimental confirmation remains.

While probing the hyperconnectivity in glasses has been experimentally challenging, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) has revealed the coordination environment of constituent nuclides under compression3,49–51,56,57. Particularly, 2-dimensional double quantum (DQ) MAS NMR has been effective in revealing the relative inter-proximity among coordinated species in the medium-range scale in glasses at 1 atm58–60 and under compression55 (see S2 for earlier NMR studies for Al environments in a-Al2O3 at 1 atm). To gain an understanding of permanent densification and the structural origins of network flexibility, we explore network hyperconnectivity and the structural changes that accompany irreversible densification in a-Al2O3 upon compression up to 12.4 GPa using NMR (See Methods; S4), identifying what makes a-Al2O3 under irreversible compression unique, so that its mechanical responses are largely different from other oxide glasses. Together with structural transitions in other complex oxide glasses, we discuss the atomistic origins of the enhanced rigidity and plasticity of the densified glasses under irreversible compression.

Results and discsussion

Pressure-driven evolution in Al coordination environments in amorphous Al2O3

The structural transitions in deformed a-Al2O3 are revealed in the 27Al magic angle spinning (MAS) NMR spectra both at 1 atm and under varying peak pressures at room temperature (Fig. 1C, D) (S3); our NMR spectral acquisition was also performed for the samples at ambient pressure after decompression. At 1 atm, peaks at ~60 ppm and ~30 ppm corresponding to [4]Al and [5]Al are observed. A minor peak for [6]Al (at ~5 ppm) is also shown, consistent with earlier results (S2)36,61. As the pressure at which decompression commences increases, above ~5 GPa, the spectral intensity near the [6]Al peak increases, and the [4]Al peak intensity decreases. While the change is apparently substantial from ~5 to ~10 GPa, the observable Al coordination environments became less pronounced above 10 GPa. Two-dimensional triple quantum (3Q) MAS NMR spectra with clearly-resolved [4,5,6]Al further revealed a pressure-driven decrease in [4]Al and the growth of [6]Al in decompressed a-Al2O3 (Fig. 1E). The isotropic projection of the spectra also confirms that a dramatic increase in the [6]Al fraction prevails from increasing the peak pressure up to ~10 GPa (Fig. 1F, G), identifying its previously unknown transition path under densification.

Pressure-induced increase in network entanglement in a-Al2O3

In addition to the densification-driven changes in the Al coordination number, our first application of 27Al double quantum (DQ) NMR to the medium-range glass structures at high pressure reveals the evolution of the inter-proximity among distinct Al sites in a-Al2O3 under permanent densification (Fig. 2) (S5), as the DQ MAS NMR is capable of revealing structural correlations beyond the 2nd coordination shells. The spectra display multiple correlation peaks corresponding to [4,5,6]Al…..[4,5,6]Al. These peaks stem from both short-range oxygen clusters, such as [m]Al-[2,3]O-[n]Al37, and those beyond the 2nd coordination environment, [m]Al…..[n]Al (Fig. 2A), thereby reflecting inter-connectivity among distinct Al coordination environments in a medium-range scale. Under low-pressure conditions, the proximity among cations is dominated by the prevalence of the correlation peaks with less-coordinated Al (e.g., [4,5]Al…..[4,5]Al). With increasing pressure, the proximity among highly coordinated Al ([5,6]Al…..[5,6]Al) increases (Fig. 2B, C). The presence and the pressure-driven rise of these off-diagonal peaks due to [5,6]Al…..[5,6]Al are reminiscent of the entangled network. Therefore, spectral features provide unambiguous experimental evidence for the achievement of enhanced network entanglement and thus, hyperconnectivity in densified glass. The presence of the multiple correlation peaks also indicates that the formation of a cluster consisting exclusively of a specific [n]Al environment is unlikely, indicating enhanced configurational disorder beyond the 2nd coordination environments (S5).

The extent of permanent densification in a-Al2O3

The Al fractions for a-Al2O3 identify the pressure-driven structural adaptation of the network (S6); Fig. 3A shows the variation in [4,5,6]Al fractions in irrevocably densified a-Al2O3. The fractions at 1 atm are [4]Al ( ~ 50%), [5]Al ( ~ 45%), and a minor fraction of [6]Al ( ~ 5%) [Table S136]. Below 4-5 GPa, the pressure-induced change in the populations of [4,5,6]Al is minor. As the peak pressure increases, the total [5,6]Al fraction increases from ~54% (at 5 GPa) to ~60% (at 10 GPa). At 12.4 GPa, the Al coordination is saturated with a maximum [5,6]Al fraction of ~62%. In our earlier high-pressure studies of more complex aluminosilicate glasses15,47, the fractions of both [5,6]Al increase at the expense of [4]Al. As for a-Al2O3, the change in the fraction of [5]Al is minor, while a decrease in [4]Al leads primarily to an increase in the fraction of [6]Al. This densification path for a-Al2O3 may arise from the difference in the role of [5,6]Al between a-Al2O3 and those in other oxide glasses (S7)62. While it is useful to extend the peak pressure conditions for a-Al2O3 at much higher pressures, it is evident that [5.6]Al fraction does not vary above ~12.4 GPa15 (Fig. 2).

Quantification of permanent densification in amorphous oxides

To explore the chemical control of the densification behaviors in diverse glasses, the pressure-driven changes [n]Al in a-Al2O3 are compared with those in other chemically complex amorphous oxides, such as Mg3Al2Si3O12 glass3 and Ca-aluminate glass (Ca13Al7, CaO:Al2O3 = 13:7)15. We also added [n]Al variation in two complex model basaltic glass (CMAS-I, CaO:MgO:Al2O3:SiO2 = 25:16:9:50 mol%; CMAS-II, CaO:MgO:Al2O3:SiO2 = 21.5:11.5:18.4:48.6)63 (Figs. 3B and S7, Table S1 for the densities of studied materials). The observed trends may well be grouped into multiple pressure spans with characteristic responses; in the initial densification under low-pressure conditions, decompression of the glass results in a negligible increase in the [5,6]Al fraction, and/or any compression-induced coordination changes is primarily reversible, corresponding to elastic network response upon compression. As the peak pressure reaches above 3–5 GPa, the irrevocable Al speciation change is prevalent within a narrow transition pressure range to ~10 GPa. Beyond this intermediate pressure zone, the Al coordination environments remain invariant, although peak pressure further increases. The observed reproducibility of the single erfc function for all the glasses highlights the commonality of such multi-stage structural adaptation under irreversible densification (Fig. 3B). Nevertheless, the pattern of a-Al2O3 is unique, stemming from much smaller initial [4]Al fraction at 1 atm (~50%) than those in other oxide glasses ( ~ 95%).

To delve further into such a composition dependence, the [5,6]Al fractions were normalized with respect to their maximum values (Fig. 3C): X5,6Al(p)¯= [X5,6Al(p)-X5,6Al1atm]/ [X5,6AlMAX-X5,6Al1atm])3. While the X5,6Al(p)¯ shows similar trends for in all the glasses, the dispersion in the trends is confirmed by the differing α and p0.5, highlighting the impact of glass composition. In Fig. 3D, the pressure is shifted to p’ = p-p0.5. This shift better distinguishes the effect of the difference in composition on the α value. The change in XnAl in a-Al2O3 is most dramatic (with the smallest α), while other complex glasses show much less dramatic changes. Figure 3E displays the normalized fractions with respect to the normalized pressure (p/2p0.5) during the cycle of compression-decompression. All the Al site statistics of the oxide glasses are described effectively using the erfc model that accounts for elastic response below p/2p0.5 of ~0.2 and irrevocable and dramatic plastic deformation under below p/2p0.5 varying from ~0.2 to ~0.8, above which the hardening of the network is expected, while the exact pressure-conditions for the attainment of plasticity could exhibit a characteristic composition dependence.

Chemical control of permanent densification

The current results show that the type of cations controls the nature of irreversible glass densification. The distinct densification behaviors result from the difference in the field strength of the constituting cations (CFS, charge/r2, where r is the ionic radius); [i.e., Mg3Al2Si3O12 glass (CFS = 3.86), 13CaO.7Al2O3 (C13A7, CFS = 2.04), model basaltic glass (CMAS-I; average CFS = 2.75), CMAS-II glass (average CFS = 2.67), and a-Al2O3 (CFS = 10.3)]; here, for glasses with more than two cations, the average CFS (=∑Xi*CFSi, where Xi refers to the mole fraction of cation i) is used. Furthermore, as [5,6]Al in Al-rich oxide glasses at 1 atm could play a role of non-network former62, the CFS for highly coordinated Al in a-Al2O3 was considered. This inclusion leads to a clear demonstration of the effect of the CFS on the Al coordination number (S7); the relationships among the CFS, p0.5 and α were unveiled (Fig. 3F–H), quantifying the impact of the type of cation on the nature of irrevocable densification. In a-Al2O3, our erfc model with α of ~ 3 GPa and p0.5 of ~6.1 GPa describes the Al coordination changes. These values are much smaller than those of other glasses (Table S1). As displayed in Fig. 3G, the transition pressure (p0.5) for Ca aluminate glass is greater than that for a-Al2O3. Therefore, field-strength cations, such as Al3+ and Mg2+, result in a smaller p0.5 (compared to Ca2+), inducing coordination changes at much lower pressure conditions (S7, Table S1). In general, p0.5 decreases with increasing CFS (from Ca2+ to Mg2+ to Al3+) and the glasses with a higher CFS are more susceptible to pressure-driven changes in the Al coordination transformation upon permanent densification. While the high field strength cations are known to promote the [5,6]Al formation50, the current results highlight that such effects are also prevalent for the glasses undergoing irreversible densification.

A similar trend is found for α, which decreases with increasing CFS (Fig. 3G); a-Al2O3 (CFS = 10.3) has a remarkably smaller α than those in other complex glasses. As a larger α results in a wider and more gradual pressure range for irreversible structural transitions, Al coordination transformation involving a higher CFS tend to promote a more dramatic change within narrow transition zones. Both α and p0.5 decrease with increasing CFS, confirming that p0.5 and α are positively correlated (Fig. 3H). The observations establish previously unknown correlation between the CFS and the [n]Al fractions in irreversibly decompressed glass (Table S1).

a-alumina during compression vs. irreversible densification

Because the acquisition of NMR results for a-Al2O3 during compression is currently unattainable, the pressure-driven Al environments are calculated theoretically (S4). The calculated coordination environments at 1 atm are consistent with the experiments (Fig. 4A) and earlier ab initio results38,65; despite differences in synthesis conditions (i.e., experimental sol-gel synthesis vs. rapid quenching via ab initio simulations), our ab initio results at 1 atm including Al coordination statistics ([4]Al 44.0%; [5]Al 47.2%; [6]Al 8.8%) show similarity to those from the experimental results ([4]Al 50.3%; [5]Al 44.3%; [6]Al 5.4%)36,37,66. The network structures of a-Al2O3 comprising of a single type of cation provide an unequivocal evidence of network entanglement and its pressure-driven increase due to an enhanced proximity among species (Figs. 4B and S10).

Our simulations during compression reveal a gradual increase in both [5]Al and [6]Al species over a wide range of pressures (Fig. 4)39,40. This in-situ trend differs from that under irreversible densification, where the primary pressure-induced change is an increase in [6]Al within a narrow pressure span (Figs. 3 and S8). The Al coordination environments in a-Al2O3 at peak pressure is, therefore, partly relaxed to those at ambient pressures. Meanwhile, the [5,6]Al species formed at high pressures are partly quenchable to 1 atm during decompression, displaying a three-step response to pressure (Fig. 4C). In order to delve into the decompression-induced change in [5,6]Al fractions, we explore the structures of decompressed a-Al2O3 with distinct peak pressures at 17.4 GPa (above P0.5 and saturation pressure of 12.4 GPa) and 5.5 GPa (below P0.5) (Fig. S8) using ab initio simulations. The results confirm that below P0.5, the [5,6]Al fractions are mainly relaxed back to those 1 atm, while decompression from higher pressure results in clear permanent densification. In detail, upon decompression from 17.4 GPa, the [6]Al fraction decreases, which mainly increases [4]Al species, while the fraction of [5]Al may not significantly change. In contrast, decompression from low pressure condition of 5.5 GPa, both [6]Al and [5]Al species tend to decrease, forming [4]Al. The results indicate that the structural relaxations during decompression have distinct mechanisms involving Al coordination transformations (Fig. S9; See S8). The simulation results also confirm that the irreversible topological changes are more prominent for the glass network compressed under higher pressure conditions, particularly above P0.5 [See S8 for detailed topological evolutions, such as bond-angle and length distributions in a-Al2O3 during compression and decompression via ab initio MD simulations]. Evolutions in polyhedral proximity ([n]Al-[n]Al) in a-Al2O3 during compression and decompression via ab initio simulations are also shown in S8. The trend is generally consistent with the experimental results (Fig. 2) where the proximity involving highly coordinated Al (e.g., [5,6]Al-[5,6]Al) increases with increasing peak pressure.

The differing Al statistics characterize the distinct structural transition paths during compression and those formed during irreversible decompression, resulting in the emergence of hysteresis in the Al environment. The quenched high-pressure structures, even at room temperature, provide an efficient route to synthesize plastically deformed glasses, without the need for thermal perturbation, and also account for the chemically distinct glass deformation behaviors (S8).

Hyperconnectivity and mechanical response of glass networks under permanent densification

The pressure-induced coordination transformation and an enhanced entanglement enable inference of mechanical responses of the non-crystalline oxides. Under low pressures, the formation of highly coordinated species by network entanglement increases configurational and topological diversity; while there are several different ways to count configurational diversity (e.g., mixing of cationic species, oxygen species, and/or topological distributions) (S8), the configurational diversity in a-Al2O3 stems mainly from the increased statistical distribution of [4,5,6]Al’s (S9.1 for model description)47. Enhanced configurational disorder with the improved network connectivity may result in a more flexible network mobility, enhancing its tendency to deform (S9.1)47. Meanwhile, at much higher pressures (with dominant fraction of highly coordinated species), significant entanglement under a larger degree of densification makes the network inevitably rigid. Such polarizing responses of a network are the first key to the puzzle of the seemingly incompatible mechanical behaviors of amorphous oxides when subject to compression. Second, we showed that the chemical impact on the degree of irreversible glass densification can be quantified with α and p0.5. Specifically, glass containing non-network formers with larger CFS tends to produce highly coordinated Al at lower pressures (smaller p0.5) (S9. Fig. S8). Such a glass also exhibits a smaller α, exhibiting more abrupt pressure-induced irreversible coordination changes than those with lower CFSs. The formation of highly coordinated Al in a-Al2O3 is promoted over other glasses, characterized with lowest transition pressure over relatively narrower transition span.

These experimental findings, along with structural adaptation unique to a-Al2O3 shine light on a view to understand the evolution of glass properties during deformation; the way that a-Al2O3 network is entangled differs substantially from those of other oxide glasses as the oxygens in a-Al2O3 links more than 2 Al’s, forming triply coordinated oxygen tricluster ([3]O) at 1 atm37. Furthermore, a-Al2O3 is topologically more entangled at lower pressures as evidenced by the lowest p0.5 and α among the studied glasses, which contributes to a more flexible network rearrangement. Our experimental results (Figs. 1–3), along with those simulation results (Figs. S10 and 4) confirm that the configurational diversity in a-Al2O3 is much more prominent than those of other aluminate and aluminosilicates (Fig. S17). In general, a network with larger fractions of highly coordinated species at lower pressure conditions exhibits higher degree of network entanglement, potentially reaching hyperconnected at much lower pressures (S9-S10). The contrasting differences in configurational diversity with pressure, and distinct pressure conditions for the attainment of hyperconnectivity account for the difference in their network flexibility; specifically, pronounced configurational diversity and attainment of hyperconnectivity at much lower pressure conditions may make a-Al2O3 more flexible at lower pressure conditions (Fig. 4), thereby accounting for its unique mechanical response of a-Al2O3 under stress.

Under low-pressure conditions (yet, above the elastic-plastic transition pressure), a sufficient degree of network flexibility achieved by an enhanced proximity and configurational disorder under compression promote plastic deformation during indentation (Fig. 4D, pressure-ΔX[5,6]Al span shaded in yellow; Fig. S15). Under further densification, within the pressure-ΔX[5,6]Al range shaded in green (Fig. 4D), a gradual decrease in its volume rigidifies the glass network. In other glasses, such a degree of network entanglement with larger fractions of highly coordinated cations and [3]O formation could emerge, but at much higher-pressure conditions (e.g., 10-30 GPa or higher, S10)37,67 as also inferred from a much larger p0.5. Under such pressure conditions, network lose flexibility due to an increase in glass density and an intense entanglement. The attainment of ductility will thus be hidden, inducing brittle fracture under indentation. Additional studies remain to fully quantify the current postulates (S9 for discussion regarding the topological contributions, such as the pressure-induced non-monotonic changes in Al-O bond length).

Among recent theoretical models [e.g.,1,4,23,35], bond-switching activities of Al have been attributed to the glass ductility. While the direct link to these earlier models remains to be identified, both the current and earlier models depend partly on the nature of [n]Al-O bonds under stress; our model provides a complimentary view to glass properties via structural evolution of [n]Al-O bonds experiencing irreversible-reversible densification under compression. Furthermore, recent classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of a-Al2O3 indicate that an increasing fraction of edge-sharing topology is expected to promote dynamic event between adjacent polyhedra, thereby enhancing plasticity29. Pioneering classical MD simulations of structure and mechanical responses of a-SiO2 shows the pressure-induced increase in its ductility, stemming from the enhanced formation of [5]Si and its conversion to [4]Si under deformation30. When it comes to the critical roles of highly coordinated species at high pressure on enhanced glass ductility, our current protocol with hyperconnectivity complies well with these earlier studies. Meanwhile, the current postulate generalizes the link based on hyperconnectivity that can be applied to any non-crystalline network regardless of composition and pressure conditions. Furthermore, it conceptualizes the irreversible densification of non-crystalline oxides with a single framework where the achievement of enhanced network entanglement under lower pressure conditions could promote mechanical softening under deformation and vice versa. Moreover, chemical impact on irreversible densification behaviors can now be quantified primarily with the cation field strength of the constituent elements in glasses. Therefore, the breakthrough within the current manuscript lies not merely in introducing the hyperconnectivity. Rather, our results reveal how the hyperconnectivity in amorphous oxides is attained and controlled—particularly in response to densification and composition, enabling us to infer why the mechanical responses of a-Al2O3 largely differ from those of other oxide glasses. Further in-situ measurements of the elastic properties would provide important insights into the pressure-driven evolution of the mechanical softening-hardening in a-Al2O3 during densification.

Implications and summary

Under compression, oxide glass often exhibits a decrease in elastic modulus (i.e., mechanical-softening), while the glass network typically becomes rigid as it densifies. These dual responses in amorphous networks rely on knowledge of how the network entangles and connects under pressure. Densification of non-crystalline oxides at high pressure grants a unique access to infer material properties under deformation. In this study, precise measurement of the structural changes in glasses under compaction-decompression cycle, highlighted by the first observation of short- and medium-range structures of a-Al2O3 at high pressure (S12), revealed that their transitions under stress are achieved through an enhanced entanglement and network hypoconnectivity. Configurational diversity in prototypical amorphous Al2O3 under irreversible densification is more prominent than those of other complex oxide glasses, reaching hyperconnected under much lower pressures. The impact of chemical composition on configurational diversity in the deformed glass network is quantified. At lower pressures, increased entanglement makes the network flexible, whereas at higher pressures, hyperconnectivity combined with densification rigidifies the network. Attainment of sufficient hyperconnectivity under lower pressures is the key to network flexibility.

As the quenched high-pressure structures within the hyperconnected networks, even at room temperature, provide a route to synthesize plastically densified glasses, the conceptual progress with hyperconnectivity under irreversible densification could be potentially useful to develop a strategy for discovering diverse densified glasses mechanically strengthened for technological applications. The current conceptual framework could also be applied to diverse complex glasses (e.g., boroaluminosilicates) under compression where the pressure-induced changes in the proximity among multiple network formers (e.g., B, Si, along with Al) need to be quantified. The conceptual progress may be extended to describe the degree of flexibility of any complex network (e.g., wireless network, magmatic melts in planetary interiors) under extreme stress, regardless of size and the type of networks, allowing us to experimentally assess deformation in these hyperconnected and entangled networks; for example, as Al2O3 is the most primordial oxide created from pre-solar grains, potentially under successive shock compression, the current structural transitions under compaction have implications for the impact processes during the dim beginning of rocky planets. The mechanisms can describe how complex planetary amorphous oxides deform under compression and exhumation, accounting for the mechanical weakening of hyperconnected planetary glasses under irreversible densification in Earth interiors (S13).

Methods

Synthesis

a-Al2O3 at 1 atm was synthesized from thermal degradation of Al-lactate hydrous sol-gels at 723 K for ~30 min [see S4 for the synthesis details]; the detailed synthesis methods for 1 atm a-Al2O3 have been provided in earlier literatures37. Briefly, Al-lactate (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in distilled water, resulting in ~0.145 M solution. The solution was dried to form white powders that were subsequently heated from 423 to 723 K. The detailed characterization of the 1 atm-products is available elsewhere37. ~20 mg of a-Al2O3 was inserted in a Pt-capsule (with an inner diameter of ~ 2 mm) and then sealed by welding the edges. The capsule is then loaded in a large-volume multi-anvil press using a 14/8 assembly (1100 ton) and compressed with varying peak pressures up to 12.4 GPa for 1 hr at ambient temperature and then decompressed (see Figure S3 for detailed the design and dimension of the 14/8 assembly and multi-anvil press used for the synthesis). Other binary Ca-aluminate and complex oxide glasses up to ~12 GPa were also synthesized using identical experimental procedures (see Table S1).

NMR spectroscopy

The acquisition of the 27Al MAS, DQMS, and 3QMAS NMR spectra of a-Al2O3 were performed on a Bruker-600 NMR spectrometer (14.1 Tesla) at 156 MHz, using a 3.2 mm triple-resonance probe. We used an rf single pulse with length of 0.25 μs (with B1 field of ~110 kHz, and flip angle for solid of ~30 degree), ensuring proper spectral quantification of Al sites in a-Al2O3, and a recycle delay of 1 s for 1D MAS, a fast amplitude modulation pulse sequence for 27Al 3QMAS NMR [see37,47,50,55 and references therein], and BR221 recoupling scheme for 27Al DQ NMR spectra, with 20 kHz spinning frequency55,59 (S5; Fig. S2). See S4 for the details of NMR conditions.

Ab initio simulations

Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations were performed to calculated of a-Al2O3 structure under pressure up to 18 GPa using the Vienna ab initio simulation package68,69. a-Al2O3 cluster consisted of 200 atoms (with 80 Al’s and 120 O’s) in a cubic supercell. The projected augmented plane-wave basis under local density approximation were adopted during calculations with the cutoff energy of plane-wave basis set of 400 eV. The cutoff energy of 520 eV is used for pressure calculation. The MD time step and equilibration time step are 1 fs. a-Al2O3 cluster was melted at 6000 K for 2 ps and cooled isovolumetrically to 3000 K with the cooling rate of 1 K/fs. Equilibration time is 5 ps, yielding a molten Al2O3 configuration at 3000 K. The melts were quenched to 300 K at the 1 K/fs quench rate. The configurations are then equilibrated for 5 ps with data accumulation of 1 ps70,71. The structures from 1 atm to ~17.4 GPa were equilibrated by decreasing volume at room temperature (S4 for further simulation details). Following decompression protocol was applied in our ab initio simulations of decompressed structures; the structure was relaxed under peak pressures at 17.4 GPa (as an exemplary high-pressure glass above the saturation pressure of ~ 12 GPa) and 5.5 GPa (a pressure condition below P0.5 where [5,6]Al’s formed under compression are expected to be mainly relaxed back to its 1 atm structures) (S8).

During production of this paper, we learned of an earlier paper that introduced the term “hyperconnected” to describe glass networks72. While the terminology is used to describe different structural features, we acknowledge that the term “hyperconnectivity” was introduced previously with respect to glass networks.
