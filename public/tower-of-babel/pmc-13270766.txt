Soybean oil fatty acid aromatic amides as dual-functional inhibitors and antimicrobial agents: synthesis, characterization, thermal stability, and performance evaluation
Authors: Nasrin K Dadashova, Vagif M Abbasov, Durna B Aghamaliyeva, Nizami I Mursalov, Avtandil H Talybov, Nushaba M Alieva, Ayaz M Mammadov, Taha Alqahtani, Yayesew Amlaku Zerihun, Magdi E A Zaki, Saida F Ahmadbayova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present study, novel amide derivatives of soybean oil fatty acids were synthesized via amidation of hydrolyzed fatty acids with aniline and benzylamine, yielding aromatic amides with structurally distinct amine moieties. These compounds were designed as potentially effective and biodegradable corrosion inhibitors. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization of the amides was performed using FT-IR and ¹H/¹³C NMR spectroscopy, while their thermal stability was assessed through thermogravimetric (TG) derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) and differential thermal analysis (DTA). The corrosion inhibition performance of the synthesized compounds was investigated using electrochemical techniques in CO₂-saturated 1 wt% NaCl solution. Linear polarization resistance (LPR) measurements were employed to study the time dependence of the corrosion rate, whereas potentiodynamic polarization (Tafel) curves were recorded to determine the electrochemical parameters of the corrosion process. The LPR results indicated that the inhibition efficiency of carbon steel increased with increasing inhibitor concentration, reaching 93–96% at 100 ppm. The inhibition mechanism was further elucidated by examining the surface morphology of mild steel using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM images revealed that inhibitor-treated samples exhibited a smoother and less damaged surface compared to the uninhibited system, indicating the formation of a protective film. Adsorption behavior followed the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, and the calculated Gibbs free energy of adsorption (− 39 to − 42 kJ mol⁻¹) suggested strong and spontaneous adsorption with a dominant chemisorption contribution. The antimicrobial activity of the compounds was evaluated against sulfate-reducing bacteria (Desulfovibrio desulfuricans) as well as Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli and coliforms). The benzylamine-derived fatty acid amide demonstrated the highest activity, achieving complete inhibition of D. desulfuricans growth at 50 mg L⁻¹. The results indicate that soybean oil fatty acid amides exhibit dual functionality, combining anticorrosive and antimicrobial properties, and may serve as promising environmentally friendly inhibitors for corrosion protection in oil-field environments affected by microbial activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01814-0.Keywords: Soybean oil, Aniline, Benzylamine, Amide, Corrosion, Inhibitor, Sulfate-reducing bacteria

Introduction

Corrosion of carbon steel, which arises under the intensive operating conditions of oil and gas production, processing, and transportation systems, remains one of the most critical technological and economic challenges faced by modern industry. Continuous exposure to aggressive media leads to accelerated degradation of metallic structures, diminished equipment reliability, and considerable economic losses. Consequently, the development and implementation of highly efficient, durable, and environmentally benign strategies for protecting metallic materials constitute an urgent and important objective in contemporary corrosion science and engineering [1–5].

Natural gas and crude oil contain aggressive components such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S), carbon dioxide (CO2), and water. The continuous flow of these substances through pipelines and well equipment leads to the development of intensive corrosion processes on the internal surfaces of metals. Variations in temperature and pressure, changes in fluid composition, and the gradual souring of wells further accelerate material degradation. As a result, the mechanical properties of the metal—such as strength, ductility, and hardness—deteriorate, wall thickness decreases, and in some cases, complete equipment failure may occur [6].

The most effective and economically justified method of protecting metals against corrosion is the use of corrosion inhibitors. It is well established that such compounds contain aromatic or heterocyclic fragments, as well as functional groups incorporating oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, or sulfur atoms, which possess donor–acceptor capabilities and ensure strong adsorption onto the metal surface [7–9].

An ideal corrosion inhibitor should be cost-effective, easy to apply, environmentally benign, and highly efficient even at low concentrations [10–18]. Inhibitors reduce the corrosion rate by retarding both the cathodic and anodic reactions [19–21]. Depending on their mechanism of action, inhibitors can be classified into three types: anodic, cathodic, and mixed-type inhibitors [20]. Anodic inhibitors decrease the rate of the anodic reaction by forming a passive film on the metal surface, thereby preventing its dissolution. Cathodic inhibitors reduce the rate of the cathodic reaction by adsorbing onto the metal surface to form a protective layer or by competing with the oxidizing agent for electrons. Mixed-type inhibitors can influence both anodic and cathodic reactions by forming a protective layer on the metal surface that decreases the anodic dissolution rate, while simultaneously competing with the oxidizing agent for electrons, thereby reducing the cathodic reaction rate [22, 23].

In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to the development of biodegradable and environmentally benign inhibitors derived from renewable natural resources [24–26]. Fatty acids and their derivatives extracted from vegetable oils represent an accessible, renewable, and cost-effective raw material for producing “green” corrosion inhibitors, owing to their low toxicity, biodegradability, and amphiphilic properties [27–32].

Soybean oil, one of the most widely used vegetable oils, is a complex mixture of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids-primarily oleic, linoleic, and linolenic acids [33]. These acids serve as a convenient chemical platform for the synthesis and modification of organic compounds with targeted functional groups, enabling the production of surface-active, thermally stable, and corrosion-resistant materials.

Although fatty acid-based corrosion inhibitors derived from vegetable oils have been extensively reported, the majority of existing studies primarily emphasize inhibition efficiency without establishing a comprehensive correlation between molecular structure, adsorption behavior, thermal stability, and biological activity. Furthermore, many previous investigations rely on model compounds synthesized from individual fatty acids, which do not fully reflect the compositional complexity of industrial feedstocks derived from natural oils.

Amide derivatives of fatty acids, synthesized through reaction with aliphatic or aromatic amines, are of particular interest as multifunctional materials that combine high adsorption capacity, surface activity, and enhanced thermal stability [34, 35]. The incorporation of aromatic amines, such as aniline and benzylamine, imparts a π-electron system to the molecule, capable of donor–acceptor interactions with the metal surface, which enhances adsorption and facilitates the formation of more robust protective films [36].

In this context, the presence of delocalized π-electron systems in aromatic amide structures enables strong donor–acceptor π–d interactions with the vacant d-orbitals of iron atoms. In addition, the amide functional group provides coordination sites through the lone pair electrons of nitrogen and oxygen atoms, resulting in synergistic adsorption mechanisms that enhance film compactness, resistance to desorption, and thermal robustness under aggressive operating conditions.

Thermal analysis methods such as TG, DTG, DTA are widely applied in modern corrosion science to evaluate the thermal behavior and stability of organic inhibitor molecules. These techniques provide insights into decomposition stages, mass-loss profiles, and stability thresholds of organic compounds under elevated temperatures commonly encountered in industrial environments, including oil and gas production systems [37, 38]. Understanding the thermal stability of potential inhibitors is essential, as many organic molecules undergo structural degradation at high temperatures, which can significantly reduce their protective efficiency and adsorption capability on metal surfaces [39].

Electrochemical techniques, including linear polarization resistance (LPR), potentiodynamic polarization measurements (Tafel curves), and adsorption isotherm modeling, are widely used to investigate the corrosion inhibition performance of inhibitors in CO₂-containing saline environments. Such media simulate conditions typical of oil-field systems, where the simultaneous occurrence of electrochemical and chemical processes accelerates the degradation of metallic materials.

In CO₂-saturated saline solutions, the corrosion inhibition efficiency is commonly evaluated using LPR measurements, which enable accurate determination of the corrosion rate (CR) and its variation over time. In addition, potentiodynamic polarization (Tafel) analysis provides valuable information regarding the anodic and cathodic reaction mechanisms and the kinetic parameters of the corrosion process in the presence of inhibitors.

Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is frequently employed to examine the surface morphology of metal specimens after exposure to corrosive environments. SEM analysis allows visualization of surface damage, corrosion products, and the formation of protective films resulting from inhibitor adsorption.

Therefore, the combined application of LPR measurements, Tafel polarization curves, and SEM surface analysis provides a comprehensive approach for evaluating the corrosion inhibition mechanism, adsorption behavior, and protective film formation of inhibitors under CO₂ corrosion conditions relevant to oil and gas environments [40–42].

Adsorption studies based on models such as Langmuir, Temkin, and Freundlich help elucidate the nature and strength of inhibitor interactions with steel surfaces, allowing researchers to distinguish between physisorption- and chemisorption-dominated mechanisms reported in the literature [43–45].

In parallel with electrochemical corrosion, microbiologically influenced corrosion has emerged as a serious challenge in the oil and gas sector. Sulfate-reducing bacteria, particularly Desulfovibrio species, are known to accelerate metal deterioration through the production of biogenic hydrogen sulfide, the formation of corrosive biofilms, and direct electron uptake from the metal surface [46–48].

Therefore, the development of multifunctional organic compounds possessing both anticorrosive and antimicrobial properties has become an area of increasing scientific interest. Numerous studies have shown that compounds featuring amide, amine, and aromatic functionalities can exhibit dual inhibition behavior by adsorbing onto steel while simultaneously disrupting microbial metabolic pathways [49–51].

While the concept of dual-functional inhibitors has been previously introduced, systematic studies integrating corrosion inhibition, adsorption thermodynamics, thermal stability, and antimicrobial activity within a single experimental framework remain limited, particularly for aromatic fatty acid amides operating in CO₂-containing environments.

In this study, amides of soybean oil fatty acids were synthesized using aniline and benzylamine, and their structures were characterized using IR and NMR spectroscopy. The thermal stability of the synthesized compounds was examined using TG, DTG, and DTA analyses, while their corrosion inhibition performance was evaluated through electrochemical methods. Additionally, the antimicrobial activity of these aromatic amide derivatives was investigated to assess their potential as multifunctional agents for mitigating both electrochemical and microbiologically influenced corrosion.

Experimental part

Materials

For the synthesis of the amide compounds and the subsequent development of corrosion inhibitors, the following reagents were employed: commercial-grade soybean oil (Azersun Holding), aniline, and benzylamine (both obtained from Sigma-Aldrich).

Synthesis of the inhibitors

The inhibitors were synthesized using soybean oil as the primary raw material. Soybean oil is a complex mixture of various fatty acids. The oil was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis using a 10% NaOH solution for 6–7 h at a temperature of 80 °C. This process yielded the sodium salts of fatty acids, which were subsequently acidified with a 15% hydrochloric acid solution to obtain the free fatty acids.

Amidation of the obtained fatty acids with aniline and benzylamine in a 1:1 molar ratio was carried out at a temperature range of 160–180 °C for 2 h, following the procedure described in [52]. As a result, soybean oil fatty acid amides were successfully synthesized .

Physical and chemical properties of fatty acids and fatty acid amides of soybean oil

The fatty acid composition of soybean oil was determined by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) following conversion of lipids into their corresponding fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). Prior to GC–MS analysis, the oil sample was subjected to a methylation step to obtain FAME derivatives.

The GC–MS analysis was performed using a gas chromatograph coupled with a mass spectrometer equipped with an HP-5MS Ultra Inert capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min⁻¹. The injector temperature was set to 250 °C, and 1 µL of the sample solution was injected in split mode (1:40).

The oven temperature program was as follows: the initial temperature was 60 °C (held for 1 min), then increased at 10 °C min⁻¹ to 180 °C, followed by a slower increase at 1 °C min⁻¹ to 230 °C, and finally increased at 10 °C min⁻¹ to 270 °C, where it was held for 2 min.

The oven temperature program was optimized to enhance the resolution of major fatty acids, particularly C18:1 and C18:2. The mass spectrometer operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with a scan range of m/z 50–550. The ion source and transfer line temperatures were maintained at 230 °C and 280 °C, respectively. Overall, the fatty acid compositions calculated for the pure oils used in this study are close to the values reported in the literature (Table 1) or fall within the corresponding reported ranges [53, 54]. Therefore, the obtained amides should be considered as a mixture of fatty acid amide derivatives reflecting the natural fatty acid composition of soybean oil (Fig. 1S).

The synthesized aniline amide of soybean fatty acids (AASOA) was obtained as a light-yellow waxy solid at room temperature, whereas the benzylamine amide (BASOA) appeared as a light-brown highly viscous material.

Spectroscopic analysis

The composition and molecular structure of the synthesized fatty acid amides derived from vegetable oils were investigated using infrared (IR) spectroscopy. The IR spectra were recorded on an ALPHA FT-IR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with a ZnSe crystal, over the wavenumber range of 600–4000 cm− 1.

Structural elucidation of the synthesized compounds was further confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker Fourier 300 MHz spectrometer, using deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) as the solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference standard.

Thermal analysis

The thermal stability of the synthesized amides, prepared from amines and soybean oil fatty acid mixtures, was assessed using simultaneous thermal analysis (STA), comprising TG, DTG, and DTA. Experiments were conducted on a NETZSCH STA 449 F3 Jupiter instrument under a nitrogen atmosphere using a corundum (Al2O3) crucible. The heating rate was set at 20 °C/min, the temperature range extended from 25 to 650 °C, and the sample mass was 6.4 mg.

Electrochemical testing methods

The effect of the synthesized compounds on the kinetics of steel corrosion was investigated using an ACM Instruments Gill AC potentiostat (No. 1197). In the experiments, a cylindrical C1018 mild steel rod with an exposed surface area of 7.9 cm2 was used as the working electrode. The chemical composition of the C1018 steel was as follows: 98.81–99.26% Fe, 0.14–0.20% C, 0.60–0.90% Mn, 0.04% P, and 0.05% S.

Electrochemical measurements were conducted for 5 h at 50 ℃, and the electrochemical data were automatically recorded by the potentiostat every 15 min and transferred to a computer for further analysis. The inhibiting performance of the synthesized compounds was evaluated in a 1 wt% NaCl aqueous solution continuously saturated with CO2 and maintained at 50 ℃. During the experiments, the solution was continuously stirred using a magnetic stirrer to ensure homogeneity throughout the tests. The inhibitor was introduced at the beginning of the second hour to allow the solution to become fully saturated with CO2 and to establish stable corrosion conditions. This approach enabled evaluation of the corrosion kinetics of steel in the uninhibited medium and allowed a clearer assessment of the change in corrosion rate after inhibitor addition.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were performed to observe and evaluate the surface morphology of steel samples exposed to a 1% NaCl solution saturated with CO₂. During the experiment, the steel specimens were immersed for 5 h in both inhibitor-free medium and in a medium containing of inhibitor, after which surface micrographs were obtained. The morphological characteristics of the steel surface were examined using a PHILIPS XL-30 scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Determination of bactericidal activity

All antibacterial experiments were carried out in triplicate to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the obtained results. The concentration of biogenic hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria was determined by iodometric titration, and the results are reported as mean values ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD, n = 3).

The bactericidal properties of the synthesized amides against sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) were evaluated in a nutrient medium of the following composition (g/l): NH4Cl – 1.0; K2HPO4 – 0.5; MgSO4·7H2O – 2.0; CaSO4 – 1.0; calcium lactate – 2.6. A mixed enrichment culture of SRB dominated by Desulfovibrio desulfuricans was used in the experiments.

The duration of incubation was 14 days at a temperature of 32 °C, both in the presence and absence of inhibitors. Throughout the incubation period, the viable cell counts and the concentration of biogenic hydrogen sulfide (H2S) in the culture medium were monitored daily.

Thermal analysis

TG, DTG, and DTA curves of the aniline and benzylamine amides synthesized from soybean oil fatty acids exhibit a multistage thermal degradation behavior. The nature and parameters of decomposition were found to strongly depend on the type of the amino group present in the molecule.

According to the TG analysis of the aniline amide (Fig. 4), the decomposition occurs in two main stages. The first stage, observed within the temperature range of 222.3–292.8 °C, is accompanied by a mass loss of about 10.75% and is characterized by moderate intensity. The main stage of decomposition takes place between 348 and 430 °C, with a maximum rate of mass loss of − 20.98%/min at 391.6 °C. The total mass loss reaches 66–77%, indicating incomplete degradation of the compound.

The DTA curve shows a distinct endothermic peak at 272.8 °C, while additional signals at 300.3 °C and 358.8 °C confirm the thermal nature of the observed processes.

Overall, the results of the thermal analysis demonstrate that the aniline amide of soybean oil fatty acids possesses high thermal stability and undergoes a multistage decomposition process, which is largely determined by the structural characteristics of the aromatic amino group.

The benzylamine amide of soybean oil fatty acids (Fig. 5) exhibits a higher initial thermal stability compared to the aniline amide, with the first decomposition stage commencing at 255.7 ˚C (versus 222.3 ˚C for the aniline amide). However, this stage is associated with a more pronounced mass loss of 26.26%, which may indicate a comparatively less stable amide linkage. The main decomposition occurs within the range of 330–430 ˚C, showing two distinct DTG peaks, indicative of a multistep degradation process involving both aliphatic and aromatic fragments of the molecule. The total mass loss reaches 96.91%, confirming the complete breakdown of the organic structure. The DTA curves corroborate the TG data: the aniline amide of soybean oil fatty acids exhibits a single intense exothermic peak at approximately 391.6 ˚C, whereas the benzylamine amide displays two separate exothermic events, supporting the presence of a complex, multistage degradation mechanism.

Thus, the aniline amide demonstrates lower mass loss and more uniform decomposition, leaving a significant residue (20–30%), while the benzylamine amide undergoes almost complete thermal degradation. These differences can be attributed to the steric and electronic effects of the benzylamine moiety, which influence both the stability of the amide bond and the mechanism of thermal decomposition.

TG/DTG curve analysis indicates that the thermal degradation of the aniline and benzylamine amides of soybean oil fatty acids proceeds through a multistage mechanism. The initial stage of decomposition begins in the temperature range of approximately 220–260 °C and can be attributed to the disruption of weak intermolecular interactions as well as the initial degradation of the amide functional group. In the subsequent temperature interval (approximately 320–430 °C), the main mass loss occurs, which is associated with the cleavage of the amide bond (–CONH–) accompanied by fragmentation of the long aliphatic chains derived from fatty acids. At higher temperatures, further degradation involves the decomposition of residual hydrocarbon fragments and the partial breakdown of aromatic structures. The presence of aromatic fragments may contribute to the formation of thermally stable residues, which is consistent with the residual mass observed particularly in the case of the aniline amide.

The comparative analysis of the electrochemical parameters obtained from Tafel polarization measurements shows that the addition of the aniline amide (AASOA) and benzylamine amide (BASOA) of soybean oil fatty acids significantly reduces the corrosion current density (Icorr) of carbon steel in CO2-saturated 1 wt% NaCl solution. For both inhibitors, a systematic decrease in Icorr values is observed with increasing inhibitor concentration, which can be attributed to the adsorption of inhibitor molecules on the metal surface and the subsequent formation of a protective adsorbed film.

In the uninhibited solution, the corrosion current density of carbon steel is 1.8886 × 10− 4 A/cm2. Upon addition of AASOA, this value decreases significantly with increasing concentration and reaches 6.2556 × 10− 6 A/cm2 at 100 ppm, corresponding to an inhibition efficiency of approximately 96%. In the case of BASOA, the Icorr value at 100 ppm is 1.4139 × 10− 5 A/cm2, providing an inhibition efficiency of about 93%.

The comparative analysis indicates that AASOA provides lower corrosion current densities and therefore exhibits higher inhibitory performance than BASOA. This difference can be attributed to the structural features of the inhibitor molecules. In the AASOA molecule, the aromatic ring is directly conjugated with the amide group, which enhances the electron density available for interaction with the metal surface and promotes stronger adsorption. In contrast, in the BASOA molecule the presence of a methylene (–CH2–) group between the aromatic ring and the amide group partially reduces electron delocalization, resulting in a slightly lower inhibition efficiency.

Furthermore, the shift in corrosion potential (Ecorr) values in the presence of both inhibitors is relatively small, indicating that AASOA and BASOA act as mixed-type inhibitors, suppressing both the anodic metal dissolution and the cathodic hydrogen evolution reactions.

SEM investigation

Figure 9 presents SEM micrographs of the mild steel surface under different conditions in CO2-saturated 1 wt% NaCl solution. The clean surface shown in Fig. 9(a) exhibits a smooth and uniform morphology with no visible signs of corrosion. This surface represents the initial state and serves as a reference for comparison. In the absence of inhibitor (Fig. 9(b)), the steel surface undergoes severe corrosion damage. The surface appears rough and heterogeneous, characterized by numerous pits, microcracks, and the accumulation of corrosion products. This clearly indicates intensive metal dissolution under CO2 corrosion conditions.

In the presence of AASOA at a concentration of 10 ppm (Fig. 9(c)), a noticeable improvement in surface morphology is observed. The extent of corrosion damage is reduced; however, the surface cannot be considered fully protected. Residual pitting and surface irregularities are still present. This behavior can be attributed to the partial adsorption of inhibitor molecules on the steel surface, leading to the formation of a discontinuous protective film.

When the inhibitor concentration is increased to 100 ppm (Fig. 9(d)), the steel surface becomes significantly smoother and more homogeneous. Pitting and other corrosion defects are almost completely eliminated, and a compact, uniform protective film is clearly observed. This indicates effective adsorption of AASOA molecules on the metal surface, resulting in the formation of a stable and protective barrier layer.

Overall, the SEM results demonstrate that increasing the concentration of AASOA enhances the degree of surface protection, confirming its high inhibition efficiency. These findings are in good agreement with the electrochemical measurements and adsorption isotherm analysis.

These results clearly demonstrate that the synthesized soybean oil fatty acid amides possess superior antibacterial performance and strong potential as multifunctional inhibitor–bactericides for controlling microbiologically influenced corrosion.

Moreover, unlike many petroleum-based inhibitors, the amides synthesized from soybean oil originate from renewable raw materials and contain hydrolysable amide and ester fragments, which are generally considered to enhance biodegradability. Although a detailed economic and biodegradation assessment was beyond the scope of the present work, the obtained results suggest that these compounds represent promising “green” alternatives to conventional commercial biocides.

Conclusion

Two aromatic amides derived from soybean oil fatty acids, namely AASOA and BASOA, were successfully synthesized and characterized by FT-IR, 1H/13C NMR, and thermal analysis. Electrochemical studies showed that both compounds effectively inhibited the corrosion of mild steel in CO2-saturated 1 wt% NaCl solution, with inhibition efficiency increasing with concentration and reaching 96% for AASOA and 93% for BASOA at 100 ppm. Polarization results indicated that both inhibitors acted as mixed-type inhibitors, while adsorption studies revealed spontaneous adsorption following the Langmuir isotherm with a dominant chemisorption contribution.

SEM observations confirmed the formation of a protective film on the steel surface in the presence of the inhibitors, especially at higher concentration. In addition, both compounds exhibited pronounced antibacterial activity against sulfate-reducing bacteria, and BASOA also showed strong activity against E. coli and coliform bacteria. Overall, the obtained results demonstrate that soybean oil fatty acid amides are promising multifunctional compounds with both anticorrosive and antimicrobial properties and may be considered as environmentally friendly candidates for application in oil-field corrosion control.
