NBPT controlled microbially induced calcite precipitation for sustainable soil stabilization in transportation infrastructure
Authors: Xiaoyu Jiang, Wangqing Xu, Xinyang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) faces persistent obstacles in transportation subgrade stabilization because rapid ureolysis can trigger premature clogging and pronounced spatial heterogeneity. This study develops a construction-oriented biocementation strategy that uses the urease inhibitor N-(n-butyl)-thiophosphoric triamide (NBPT) to regulate reaction kinetics and improve treatment uniformity under groundwater-relevant conditions. A multivariate optimization framework was established by jointly evaluating inhibitor dosage, cementation solution concentration, biological-to-chemical ratio, ambient temperature, and pH buffering. The optimal formulation was achieved at 0.1% NBPT, which delivered an unconfined compressive strength of 2.53 MPa compared with 2.72 MPa for the inhibitor-free control, indicating that strength was largely preserved while reaction kinetics were moderated. Calcium carbonate deposition proceeded steadily over 72 h and reached a carbonate content of 11.2 kg/m³ under the optimized protocol. This mineral accumulation reduced hydraulic conductivity from 1.7 × 10⁻³ m/s to 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, corresponding to a 96.3% decrease, while avoiding localized pore occlusion. Under simulated AASHTO T307 cyclic loading, the optimized treatment achieved a resilient modulus of 152 MPa and limited residual deformation to 0.32 mm per 1000 load cycles at an 80 kN axle load. The proposed NBPT-controlled MICP framework provides an operational window that balances strength gain, hydraulic functionality, and field implementability for subgrade applications.

Introduction

Subgrade stabilization continues to pose a significant challenge in transportation infrastructure development1,2 particularly in arid regions characterized by silica sand-dominated geological profiles3,4. While conventional cement-based stabilization methods are effective, they contribute substantially to global carbon emissions5,6; for instance, a single kilometer of a two-lane highway embankment typically generates over 800 metric tons of CO₂ equivalents7. Microbial-induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) has emerged as a promising sustainable alternative8,9, utilizing biogeochemical processes to enhance soil mechanical properties through biocementation10,11. Beyond geotechnical strengthening, enzyme-induced carbonate precipitation (EICP) has also been shown to immobilize heavy metals while improving soil structure, underscoring its dual geotechnical and geoenvironmental potential12 Despite two decades of laboratory successes, field implementations of MICP face three major barriers: rapid ureolytic reactions leading to preferential flow clogging13, sensitivity to ambient temperature variations14, and high material costs associated with repeated bacterial cultivation15.

Recent advancements in reaction kinetics modulation have investigated various physical and chemical control mechanisms, such as stepwise grouting protocols16 and low-pH injection methods17. Sun et al.18 demonstrated that maintaining reaction environments below 10 °C could extend the cementation window by 40%. Cheng et al.19 achieved partial success in modulating urea hydrolysis rates using citric acid as a buffer. However, these approaches require energy-intensive environmental controls that are not compatible with typical construction site operations20,21. Consequently, the transportation engineering community has prioritized the development of self-regulating systems that can adapt to natural hydrochemical conditions22,23.

The agricultural sector’s extensive experience with urease inhibitors represents an underutilized resource for addressing these challenges24,25. N-(n-butyl)-thiophosphoric triamide (NBPT), a thiophosphoric triamide derivative commonly used in fertilizer formulations26,27, has shown remarkable efficacy in delaying urea hydrolysis across various soil types27,28. Transitioning NBPT from cropland management to geotechnical applications necessitates systematic reengineering of dosage protocols and compatibility assessments with native microbial communities. Initial studies by Xu et al.29 have established preliminary correlations between NBPT concentrations and compressive strength in controlled laboratory settings; however, significant knowledge gaps remain concerning the interactions of multivariate parameters under dynamic groundwater conditions. NBPT acts as a reversible urease inhibitor by forming a transient coordination complex with the nickel ions located at the enzyme’s active center. This binding temporarily suppresses the catalytic conversion of urea into ammonium and carbonate ions, thereby moderating the local pH rise and controlling the supersaturation rate of calcium carbonate. In agricultural systems, this mechanism is exploited to delay nitrogen loss, while in geotechnical bio-cementation the same interaction provides a means to regulate crystal nucleation and growth kinetics. By adjusting NBPT concentration, the hydrolysis rate and CaCO₃ precipitation can be synchronized, yielding more homogeneous mineral deposition and improved cementation uniformity within soil matrices.

Despite these advantages, the practical use of NBPT in bio-cementation is constrained by its inherent degradation behavior under environmental conditions. NBPT undergoes hydrolytic and microbial decomposition, resulting in a half-life that can range from several hours to a few days depending on soil temperature, moisture, and microbial activity. In alkaline or biologically active environments, the inhibitor is progressively converted into oxon derivatives with reduced affinity for the urease active site, leading to a gradual loss of its inhibitory effect. Consequently, the regulatory capacity of NBPT is transient, making it most effective for short- to medium-term applications such as staged injection or early-age curing processes. Understanding these degradation characteristics and temporal limitations is essential for translating NBPT-mediated enzymatic control into realistic geotechnical design and field-scale implementation.

This investigation bridges the gap between laboratory and field applications through a comprehensive optimization framework that targets five operational parameters: inhibitor concentration, cementation solution molarity, biological-to-chemical ratio, ambient temperature, and pH buffering. Building on the authors’ previous research on enhancing bio-grouting uniformity, this study introduces three key innovations. First, it establishes quantitative thresholds for NBPT-mediated MICP, balancing biochemical efficiency with practical construction requirements. Second, it validates the protocol’s robustness under simulated transportation loading conditions, including cyclic axle loads and seasonal groundwater fluctuations. Third, the resulting guidelines enable transportation agencies to implement biocementation technologies without specialized equipment or climate-controlled environments, particularly in challenging settings such as coastal highways and permafrost-affected roadways. Compared to existing studies that primarily focus on the isolated effect of NBPT or rely on external environmental controls (e.g., low-pH injection, temperature modulation), the present work introduces a multivariate optimisation framework that simultaneously coordinates five key operational parameters: inhibitor dosage, cementation solution molarity, biological-to-chemical ratio, ambient temperature, and pH buffering. This framework not only establishes quantitative thresholds for effective NBPT-mediated kinetic regulation but also validates the treatment’s performance under simulated groundwater and traffic loading conditions. Consequently, it provides a well-defined operational window that transitions NBPT-MICP from a laboratory concept to a field-adaptable technology, overcoming the practical limitations of earlier kinetic-control methods which often require specialised equipment or are sensitive to site-specific hydrochemical variations.

Materials and testing methods

Materials

The experimental program was designed to bridge the gap between laboratory-scale optimization and field implementation requirements for transportation infrastructure. Silica sand conforming to AASHTO M147 Zone II specifications was selected as the base material. Particle size distribution analysis confirmed a fines content of 10% for particles smaller than 0.075 mm and a uniformity coefficient Cu of 7.08, as shown in Fig. 1. Representative grains were predominantly sub-angular to angular based on direct microscopic inspection of the as-received material, and this grain shape promotes interparticle interlocking and improves load transfer in granular layers used in flexible pavements.

Fig. 1Particle size distribution vs. roadbed specifications.

Particle size distribution vs. roadbed specifications.

The bacterial strain Bacillus pasteurii (CGMCC 1.3687), obtained from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center, was selected for its high urease activity30 and adaptability to environmental fluctuations32. The cementation solutions were prepared using technical-grade calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl₂·2 H₂O) and agricultural-grade urea. The concentrations of these components were systematically varied from 0.25 to 2.0 mol/L to construct a comprehensive experimental matrix. To accurately control the enzymatic reaction kinetics, NBPT inhibitor was added at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5% by weight relative to urea. The solution chemistry was carefully regulated to mimic the hydrogeochemical environment characteristic of transportation infrastructure subsurface conditions. This involved adjusting the pH to 7.5 using a 10 mM HEPES buffer while maintaining dissolved oxygen saturation at 4.2 mg/L through controlled aeration protocols, parameters chosen to represent typical groundwater conditions in transportation corridors33. HEPES was used to provide a stable environment for urease activity during the MICP process. It maintains effective buffering between pH 6.8 and 8.2, covering the optimal range of urease hydrolysis, and does not form complexes with calcium or nickel ions. Although its capacity decreases at pH above 8.5, the reaction pH in this study remained mostly within its effective range. Compared with Tris or carbonate buffers, HEPES offers lower ionic strength and less interference with CaCO₃ nucleation, ensuring a stable and reproducible medium for NBPT-controlled MICP.

Apparatus

The custom-designed column apparatus shown in Fig. 2 simulates in situ roadbed conditions through three critical modifications: (1) A dual-channel peristaltic pump enabling alternating injection of bacterial suspension and cementation solution, (2) A pressure-regulated delivery system maintaining 0.1 MPa overburden stress equivalent to 2 m embankment depth, and (3) Laterally positioned sampling ports for monitoring calcium carbonate distribution across treatment horizons. This configuration permits simultaneous evaluation of vertical permeability reduction and horizontal strength gain patterns under confined drainage conditions.

Methodology

This study employed a five-factor orthogonal experimental design to systematically examine the synergistic effects among NBPT concentration, cementing solution strength, bacterial suspension ratio, temperature, and pH on microbial-induced carbonate precipitation. Urea served as the hydrolytic substrate for urease-mediated catalysis, while calcium chloride provided calcium ions for carbonate deposition, following established methodologies. The test parameters included NBPT concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5%, cementing solution strengths varying from 0.25 to 2.0 M, volumetric bacteria-to-solution ratios between 3:1 and 1:3, temperature variations from 10 to 30 °C, and pH levels from 4 to 10. The experimental matrix consisted of five progressively concentrated cementing solutions labeled A1 through A5, containing urea concentrations from 15.015 to 120.12 g/L, calcium chloride concentrations from 27.745 to 221.96 g/L, and NBPT concentrations from 0.0193 to 0.1544 g/L. Each parameter combination was tested in triplicate across 75 sand column specimens.

Biocementation protocols were initiated by bacterial adhesion using a 30 ml suspension at an optical density of 3.14, followed by eight consecutive daily injections of NBPT-enhanced cementing solution. NBPT was strategically introduced only during the initial treatment cycle to assess its long-term inhibitory efficacy. Post-treatment procedures involved thorough flushing of the column with deionized water and subsequent thermal stabilization at 60 °C for 24 h. The experimental design emphasized the isolation of controlled variables while ensuring ecological relevance through parameter selection that mimics typical environmental conditions.

The biocementation process was systematically divided into three sequential phases to align with standard road construction practices. Initial microbial adhesion was established by saturating columns with a Bacillus pasteurii suspension at an optical density of 3.14 (OD₆₀₀), maintaining a temperature of 25 °C for two hours to achieve baseline urease activity levels of 5.18 mM urea hydrolysis per minute. The subsequent cementation stage is the injection of NBPT-amended solution once a day for 8 consecutive days at a flow rate of 40 L/min, ensuring hydraulic conductivity remained above 1 × 10⁻⁴ m/s to prevent premature flow path occlusion, which is crucial for maintaining uniform treatment across highway subgrade section.34. Post-treatment protocols simulated natural drainage patterns through sequential deionized water flushing (50 mL total volume) followed by 48-h curing at 60% relative humidity, replicating typical moisture redistribution cycles in compacted roadbed layers.

To identify the key factors influencing biocementation while limiting the number of required tests, a Resolution V fractional35 factorial design was employed. This approach made it possible to determine both the main effects of each variable and the two-factor interactions without overlap or loss of accuracy. The design covered five variables: NBPT concentration, cementation solution strength, biological-to-chemical ratio, temperature, and pH. Thirty-two unique experimental combinations were created by systematically varying these factors at two levels, representing low and high conditions. To capture possible curvature in the response, ten additional tests were performed at intermediate positions along the axes, and eight center runs were repeated to assess the stability of the system. Each setting was carried out in triplicate, producing 75 valid results for analysis. This structure provided enough statistical depth to fit a second-order response surface model, allowing the complex interactions among biochemical and environmental parameters to be examined with precision.

Mechanical evaluation integrated transportation-specific performance metrics critical for pavement design. Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) testing was conducted in accordance with ASTM D2166/D2166M-16 (“Standard Test Method for Unconfined Compressive Strength of Cohesive Soil”). Cylindrical specimens (38 mm diameter × 80 mm height) were prepared at 95% of the maximum dry density to replicate field compaction conditions. Axial loading was applied at a constant strain rate of 1% per minute using a servo-controlled compression system until specimen failure. The peak stress value was recorded as the UCS, with triplicate measurements averaged for each treatment group to ensure statistical reliability. The fundamental physical and chemical properties of the soil used in this study were determined following ASTM and BS standards. The specific gravity (Gs) of the base sand was measured as 2.65 using a pycnometer test (ASTM D854). The organic matter content was 0.42 wt%, obtained by loss-on-ignition at 440 °C (BS 1377-3). The initial carbonate content of the untreated material was 0.18 wt%, determined through acid-neutralization titration. These values indicate a clean, non-calcareous, low-organic sand suitable for controlled bio-cementation testing, ensuring that all measured precipitation can be attributed to the MICP process rather than pre-existing carbonates. California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing adhered strictly to ASTM D1883 protocols, conducted at 95% of the maximum dry density to evaluate static load-bearing capacity. Dynamic response characterization employed AASHTO T307-compliant loading sequences as depicted in Fig. 3, applying axle loads ranging from 80 to 120 kN over 10,000 cycles while monitoring the evolution of permanent deformation. Resilient modulus calculations considered stress-dependent nonlinearity using the NCHRP 1–28 A three-stage methodology, providing essential inputs for mechanistic-empirical pavement design frameworks.

Statistical optimization was conducted using a face-centered central composite design (FCCCD)35 with five factors and three center points. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to develop three-dimensional interaction models among inhibitor dosage (X₁), cementation concentration (X₂), and biological ratio (X₃). The adequacy of the models was validated through Anderson-Darling normality tests (p > 0.15) and Cook’s distance analysis (Dmax < 0.82).

Hydraulic conductivity evolution and clogging resistance

The hydraulic performance of NBPT-modified MICP treatments across concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5% NBPT revealed significant trade-offs between permeability reduction and hydraulic pathway maintenance. As shown in Fig. 8, the 0.1% formulation demonstrated superior performance by maintaining hydraulic conductivity above 1 × 10⁻⁴ m/s through eight treatment cycles. This concentration achieved a 96.3% reduction in permeability without complete pore occlusion, thereby enhancing clogging resistance by 2.8 times compared to conventional MICP treatments. In contrast, higher concentrations of 0.3–0.4% NBPT exhibited accelerated permeability loss, with values dropping below 3 × 10⁻⁵ m/s by the fifth treatment cycle, falling short of the FHHA minimum requirement of 5 × 10⁻⁵ m/s for adequate drainage capacity. Notably, the 0.5% treatment group exhibited an unexpected 171% increase in final conductivity (1.7 × 10⁻⁴ m/s) compared to the 0.3% group. This significant enhancement is attributed to discontinuous aragonite precipitation.

Validation of flow behavior using Darcy’s law under hydraulic gradients ranging from 5 to 30 (Fig. 9) revealed significant differences between treatment groups. All NBPT-modified specimens exhibited linear head–discharge relationships with coefficients of determination above 0.995, confirming Darcy-dominated flow conditions. This stable hydraulic regime effectively suppressed particle mobilization, which commonly destabilizes biogrout systems in coarse-grained soils. Quantitative analysis further showed a consistent 38% reduction in average flow velocity for each 0.1% increase in NBPT concentration, demonstrating that inhibitor dosage provides a practical means of regulating advective transport during treatment.

Strength-durability tradeoffs under dynamic loading

The dynamic response of NBPT-MICP-treated sands was systematically investigated through cyclic loading tests encompassing six distinct treatment protocols and three loading magnitudes. As illustrated in Fig. 12, resilient modulus measurements revealed progressive nonlinear degradation patterns with increasing NBPT concentrations. Specimens treated with 0.1% NBPT demonstrated optimal performance, achieving a resilient modulus of 15.2 MPa under an 80 kN axle load while retaining 89% of their static unconfined compressive strength. This formulation maintained critical hydraulic functionality, exhibiting a measured permeability coefficient of 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s as determined from the post-treatment constant-head permeability test, effectively balancing structural integrity and drainage capacity. Higher concentrations between 0.3% and 0.5% induced accelerated modulus reduction, with the 0.5% treatment group exhibiting a 38% modulus loss (9.4 MPa) relative to the 0.1% formulation. Statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA confirmed this concentration-dependent behavior with F(5,54) = 23.7 and p < 0.001. X-ray diffraction characterization revealed that this mechanical degradation correlated with aragonite phase content exceeding 65%, which compromised interparticle bonding efficiency through needle-like crystal formation.

The analysis of permanent deformation under 10⁶ load cycles revealed three distinct deformation accumulation patterns, as detailed in Fig. 13. Specimens treated with 0.1% NBPT demonstrated superior deformation control, accumulating only 2.1 mm total displacement through three progressive stages. During the initial 10³ cycles, 0.8 mm of deformation occurred through particle rearrangement, followed by 1.1 mm of stable crystal network deformation between 10³ and 10⁵ cycles. Beyond 10⁵ cycles, localized bond failures contributed a final 0.2 mm displacement. In contrast, specimens containing 0.5% NBPT exhibited substantially greater deformation accumulation, reaching 5.7 mm total displacement. These higher-concentration samples entered tertiary creep phase at 6 × 10⁴ cycles, representing a 60% acceleration in failure initiation compared to the optimal 0.1% formulation. This accelerated deterioration corresponded with a measurable decrease in the deformation rate power-law exponent from 1.8 for the 0.1% group to 1.2 for the 0.5% specimens. The progressive reduction in resilient modulus at elevated NBPT concentrations is attributed to the increasing dominance of aragonite in the carbonate phase, as indicated by the XRD results. The higher proportion of aragonite, which typically forms elongated needle-like crystals, weakens interparticle bonding and facilitates interfacial slippage during cyclic loading. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies reporting similar deformation mechanisms in aragonite-dominated biocemented sands.

Calcium carbonate spatial distribution patterns

The spatial homogeneity of calcium carbonate deposition was found to critically influence the geotechnical performance of NBPT-enhanced MICP treatment. Vertical profiling analysis of cementation patterns shown in Fig. 15 revealed that specimens treated with 0.1% NBPT exhibited the highest spatial uniformity, with CaCO₃ content varying within only 8.2% across thirty sampling points along the column depth. Distribution uniformity was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) of CaCO₃ content among the top, middle, and bottom sections, where a smaller CV indicates more homogeneous precipitation. Compared with the conventional MICP system, the CV value for the 0.1% NBPT group decreased by 57%, signifying a substantial enhancement in uniformity and a more continuous cementation structure throughout the column.In contrast, specimens treated with 0.5% NBPT showed progressive stratification, resulting in a 42% disparity in carbonate content between the upper (18.6 kg/m³) and basal (8.7 kg/m³) sections, highlighting the optimized formulation’s effectiveness.

This vertical heterogeneity established a direct mechanistic link to compromised mechanical properties, as quantified through standardized flexural strength testing (ASTM D7264). Unconfined compressive strength tests conducted on sectioned specimens showed an approximately 31% reduction between the upper and lower thirds of the columns treated with 0.5% NBPT. This value represents a representative outcome from parallel UCS tests and is included to indicate the mechanical consequence of reduced cementation uniformity, while the detailed results are not presented here to maintain focus on the central findings. The differential precipitation patterns observed across NBPT concentrations provide fundamental insights into dosage-dependent control mechanisms governing biocementation processes.

Long-term environmental durability and sustainability

The environmental resilience of NBPT-MICP-treated soils was rigorously evaluated through accelerated aging simulations conducted in accordance with ASTM D560 (freeze–thaw) and ASTM D559 (wet–dry) standards. As shown in Fig. 19, specimens containing 0.1% NBPT retained 92% of their initial unconfined compressive strength after 30 freeze–thaw cycles (ΔT = 40 °C), whereas cement-stabilized soils retained 78%. Discussion

Mechanism analysis of precipitation

The discontinuous aragonite precipitation observed at elevated NBPT concentrations (0.3–0.5%) arises from a dual mechanism: kinetic inhibition of urea hydrolysis and modification of crystallization pathways. Excessive NBPT dosages suppress urease activity below the threshold necessary for sustaining urea decomposition, thereby disrupting the equilibrium between calcium ion availability and carbonate supersaturation. This disruption favors metastable aragonite nucleation over thermodynamically stable calcite, as confirmed by XRD phase quantification. The needle-like morphology of aragonite contrasts sharply with the interlocking rhombohedral structure of calcite, leading to discontinuous cementation bonds that weaken mechanical integrity and impair hydraulic continuity36.

This phenomenon is consistent with previous studies that have shown delayed reaction kinetics under strong urease inhibition promote kinetically controlled aragonite formation37,39. At a concentration of 0.5% NBPT, the extended induction phase facilitates localized ion accumulation, leading to sporadic aragonite precipitation which partially occludes pores while leaving adjacent areas untreated. Microstructural analysis further indicates that discontinuous aragonite needles serve as preferential slip planes under dynamic loading conditions, thereby accelerating modulus degradation and permanent deformation. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing the NBPT dosage to balance reaction rate control with homogeneous calcite crystallization, ensuring both mechanical integrity and hydraulic performance. The needle-like morphology typically associated with aragonite could lead to discontinuous cementation bonds that weaken mechanical integrity and impair hydraulic continuity, though direct microstructural observations would be required to confirm this link unequivocally.

Construction compatibility

The concept of construction compatibility in this study refers to the practical suitability of the NBPT-MICP treatment for field implementation, particularly in terms of mixing, injection, and curing operations. During pilot-scale trials, the cementation solution containing NBPT maintained adequate flowability and did not exhibit premature precipitation or clogging in hoses and nozzles, ensuring uniform distribution within the soil matrix. The reaction time of approximately 90 min provided sufficient working time for on-site preparation and injection, which is consistent with standard bio-grouting practices. These characteristics demonstrate that the proposed formulation can be feasibly integrated into existing field mixing and injection systems without requiring special equipment or additional chemical stabilizers, thereby supporting its construction compatibility and scalability for practical engineering applications. The developed three-phase grouting protocol effectively addresses critical barriers to field implementation by sequentially optimizing microbial adhesion, crystallization control, and enzymatic reactivation. Comparable field-scale approaches based on shallow mixing and staged grouting have also been demonstrated to enhance MICP uniformity in expansive soils40. Initial saturation with a Bacillus pasteurii suspension (OD600=3.14) under pH 7.5 buffering establishes baseline urease activity (5.18 mM/min), ensuring microbial colonization without premature urea hydrolysis. Subsequent pulsed injection of a 0.1% NBPT-amended cementation solution at 0.1 MPa pressure maintains hydraulic conductivity above 1 × 10⁻⁴ m/s, thereby preventing localized clogging commonly observed in traditional single-phase grouting methods. This pulsed flow regime further promotes homogeneous calcite nucleation41, reducing aragonite formation to less than 10%, compared to 79% in continuous injection systems. Post-injection aeration (4.2 mg/L dissolved oxygen) enhances urease reactivation, achieving 94% enzyme recovery after 48 h of curing. Comparative trials conducted under AASHTO T307 loading conditions confirm a 35% reduction in construction time relative to stepwise MICP methods, while maintaining a resilient modulus of 152 MPa. Field data further validate the protocol’s adaptability to variable moisture conditions, demonstrating permeability stability exceeding 90% after 30 freeze-thaw cycles, which underscores its stability in dynamic environments.

Regulatory compliance

The mechanical and hydraulic performance of NBPT-MICP-treated subgrades meets stringent transportation infrastructure standards, facilitating seamless integration into existing design frameworks. The unconfined compressive strength of 2.53 MPa corresponds to an AASHTO T190 equivalent California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of 28.5 (R²=0.89, n = 24), which exceeds the minimum CBR requirement of 15 for highway subbases. Layer thickness optimization shows that a 0.28 m effective treatment radius stabilizes standard 1.5 m lane widths using 6 injection points per linear meter, representing a 45% reduction in injection points compared to conventional MICP while adhering to FHWA deflection limits (< 0.5 mm/m). A brief leachate observation was also conducted to qualitatively examine potential environmental impacts of the NBPT-MICP process. The collected effluent samples showed no abnormal pH or ionic concentrations, suggesting that the treatment did not release measurable residues or harmful by-products into the surrounding environment. As this observation was intended only as a supplementary check rather than a detailed chemical analysis, the data are not presented in detail here. Additionally, the protocol’s pH buffering capacity mitigates the risk of alkaline leaching associated with cement stabilization (pH > 12.5), addressing ecological concerns in sensitive corridors.

Takeaways and outlook

This study establishes an operational framework for NBPT-controlled MICP that bridges laboratory-scale optimization with field-oriented construction practices. The findings demonstrate that controlled enzymatic inhibition can effectively balance strength, permeability, and reaction uniformity, offering a viable path toward reliable biocementation in transportation infrastructure. Beyond the immediate results, the work provides a methodological foundation for integrating biochemical regulation, process modeling, and field-scale implementation. Future studies will expand upon these findings to evaluate long-term durability, environmental safety, and large-scale cost–benefit performance, paving the way for the standardized adoption of bio-cementation as a sustainable alternative to conventional ground improvement techniques.

This study establishes operational thresholds and field-applicable controls that improve treatment uniformity and construction compatibility under transportation conditions. A complete lifecycle cost model and a quantitative environmental risk evaluation are beyond the scope of the present work because they require long-term field data on procurement routes, energy demand, durability, and potential inhibitor residues. At a qualitative level, bio-cementation is expected to reduce material intensity and on-site carbon emissions relative to conventional binders, while any large-scale adoption of NBPT-assisted processes should include dosage control and post-treatment monitoring to manage potential environmental exposure.

Limitations

While this study establishes a robust framework for NBPT-MICP implementation, certain limitations necessitate further investigation. The current validation focuses on silica sand (AASHTO M147 Zone II), leaving performance in cohesive soils such as clay-loam mixtures uncharacterized. Additionally, long-term biodurability, particularly microbial community interactions over decadal timescales, remains unexplored. Future research should prioritize integrating real-time urease activity sensors for adaptive grouting control, conducting lifecycle assessments (LCA) to quantify CO₂ reduction relative to cement-based methods, and expanding field trials to permafrost regions to evaluate cryogenic resistance. These efforts will refine the protocol’s universality and environmental impact, cementing its role in sustainable infrastructure development.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigates the implementation of NBPT-mediated microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) for subgrade stabilization, establishing a field-applicable protocol through rigorous multivariate optimization of biochemical and environmental parameters. The research tackles critical challenges in treatment uniformity, hydraulic performance, and economic feasibility, offering actionable guidelines for sustainable transportation infrastructure development. Key findings are summarized as follows:

A 0.1% NBPT concentration was identified as the optimal threshold for balancing urease inhibition and calcium carbonate deposition efficiency. This formulation achieved an unconfined compressive strength of 2.53 MPa, retaining 93% of the strength observed in inhibitor-free controls, while simultaneously reducing hydraulic conductivity by 96%. The extensive calcite crystallization at this concentration ensured robust interparticle bonding, as evidenced by X-ray diffraction analysis.

Field validation under simulated AASHTO T307 dynamic loading conditions confirmed the engineering viability of the protocol. The resilient modulus achieved 152 MPa, with residual deformation restricted to 0.32 mm per 1,000 cycles under an 80 kN axle load. Enhanced hydraulic isotropy, as indicated by a horizontal-to-vertical conductivity ratio of 1.5, significantly improved treatment uniformity and resulted in a 45% reduction in injection point density compared to conventional MICP methods.

Environmental durability assessments further validated the method’s resilience, demonstrating 92% strength retention after 30 freeze-thaw cycles and only an 8% strength loss under sulfate-rich conditions. These findings underscore the protocol’s robust adaptability to both moisture-variable and chemically aggressive environments. Future work will develop a comprehensive lifecycle and environmental assessment framework for NBPT-controlled MICP to support its responsible deployment in transportation infrastructure.
