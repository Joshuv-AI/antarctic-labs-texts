An ultra-wideband Vivaldi antenna development for brain microwave imaging
Authors: Mohsen Bakouri, Abdulrahman Alqahtani, Bakheet Awad Alresheedi, Md Samsuzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article presents a comprehensive analysis of a tiny, rectangular, and exponentially tapered slotted ultra-wideband (UWB) Vivaldi antenna developed explicitly for brain microwave imaging. The antenna comprises seven symmetric rectangular slots carved on both sides of the rear radiative wings of a tapered slot antenna. The antenna’s total dimensions, which encompass the ground layer, are 0.39λ × 0.33λ × 0.007λ while operating at a lower frequency of 1.35 GHz. The simulated impedance bandwidth (IBW) is observed to have a range of frequencies between 1.35 and 5.50 GHz, with a fractional bandwidth (FBW) of 121.17%, where the magnitude of |S11|< − 10 dB. On the other hand, the measured IBW spans from 1.4 to 6.4 GHz, exhibiting an FBW of 128.20%. The antenna has a gain of 9.99 decibels isotropic (dBi) and possesses directional radiation properties, along with a front-to-back ratio (FBR) exceeding 30 dB. The analysis conducted in the time domain demonstrates little pulse distortion and a group delay of less than one nanosecond, indicating good performance. Additionally, the system exhibits a high-fidelity factor of 96.26% and maintains linearity in the transmission phase (S21). Both simulation and experimental findings demonstrate the efficacy of the antenna for microwave-based imaging.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide1. In the UK, stroke ranks as the fourth leading cause of death, with over 100,000 cases reported annually, resulting in a societal cost of approximately £26 billion per year2. Stroke occurs when the brain is deprived of oxygen and nutrients, leading to severe brain dysfunction and, in extreme cases, death3. There are two main types of strokes: ischemic and hemorrhagic. Hemorrhagic strokes result from the rapid rupture of a blood vessel in the brain, causing blood to pool and exert pressure on surrounding brain tissue. In contrast, acute ischemic strokes are caused by blood clots obstructing brain blood vessels4. Both types exhibit similar symptoms, including severe headache, limb weakness, and difficulties in speaking and eating. However, each type requires specific and timely treatment, typically within three to four hours of symptom onset5,6. Therefore, it is crucial to differentiate between these types of strokes promptly. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Computed Tomography (CT), and Positron Emission Tomography (PET) are commonly used for stroke diagnosis. While these imaging techniques provide accurate images of the brain, they have limitations. MRI cannot be used on patients with metal biomedical implants, CT has an ionizing effect, and PET requires the injection of radioactive materials. Furthermore, these modalities are expensive, time-consuming, and non-portable3,7. As a result, there is a need for an alternative imaging method that is safe, fast, inexpensive, portable, and capable of accurate brain disorder detection.

In recent years, microwave imaging (MWI) has garnered significant attention in medical diagnostics, particularly for breast and brain tumor detection, early heart failure detection, and health monitoring. MWI is an affordable, compact, portable, and non-ionizing method1,5,8. This technique leverages variations in the dielectric properties of different tissues in the body. Studies have shown that alterations in these tissues, such as the presence of cancerous cells or lack of oxygen, can change their dielectric properties, which can be exploited by the MWI method9. The fluids in human body tissues all have different electrical properties, with cancer cells containing significantly more water than healthy cells. Damaged cells accumulate more ions and free radicals, deteriorating their dielectric properties10. Compared to traditional imaging methods, MWI produces high-quality brain images and can be used for continuous brain monitoring. Its affordability and acceptable image quality make it a viable option for brain imaging in rural areas and low-income nations. Most microwave-based brain stroke detection methods utilize either microwave radar or microwave tomography11. Recent MWI devices and prototypes include the Stroke Finder developed by Chalmers University, which consists of 12 triangular patch antennas mounted on a plastic helmet operating at 0.8 to 1.5 GHz, and a system proposed by the University of Queensland involving 16 tapered slot antennas operating from 1 to 4 GHz11,12.

The performance of the antenna arrays is crucial to the success of the microwave imaging system. The frequency band used in this technique varies depending on the application, ranging from 0.3 to 11 GHz13. For instance, frequencies from 1 to 11 GHz are used for breast imaging, while frequencies from 1 to 4 GHz are used for human head imaging14–17; or up to 16 GHz for Internet of Things applications in intelligent healthcare18. In brain imaging, lower-frequency microwaves are used to ensure effective signal penetration due to the high permittivity materials in brain tissue19,20. However, lower frequencies result in decreased image resolution21. Thus, a broad frequency range encompassing both lower and higher frequencies is necessary to optimize signal penetration depth and image resolution, making UWB antennas valuable for microwave imaging systems in medical applications. Numerous UWB antenna designs have been proposed for microwave imaging of head injuries10,22–29. For example, a small wearable antenna designed to detect brain cancer in the 2.4–2.5 GHz frequency range achieved a maximum specific absorption rate (SAR) of 0.3877 W/kg, though its bandwidth was narrow at only 0.1 GHz23. Another design was a small 3D UWB antenna with a fractional bandwidth of 67%, operating between 1.1 and 2.2 GHz22. A study by24 demonstrated a compact textile antenna with a broad frequency range of 1.13 to 4.88 GHz and a maximum gain of 4.39 GHz. However, this antenna sends signals omnidirectionally, leading to inefficient signal use. A recent study suggested a wide-band antenna with an operational bandwidth of 1.82 GHz and a peak gain of 5 dBi25. Another study proposed a UWB antenna design with a 2.37 GHz frequency bandwidth, a peak gain of 5.95 GHz, and a fidelity factor exceeding 90%10. A recently conducted study developed a Vivaldi antenna with a size of 65 × 65 × 1.6 mm3. This antenna had a 1.44 GHz bandwidth ranging from 2.35 GHz to 3.79 GHz, with a fractional bandwidth of 46.9%, a maximum gain of 7.35 dBi and a maximum SAR value of 0.0013 W/kg per 10 g28. Another study has introduced two miniaturized Vivaldi antennas with specific frequency ranges. Antenna 1 has dimensions of 30.2 × 44.4 × 5.76 mm3 and operates within a frequency range of 2.65–4.2 GHz, corresponding to a fractional bandwidth of 45.26%. Antenna 2 has dimensions of 30.32 × 60 × 5.76 mm and operates within a frequency range of 1.9–5.4 GHz, corresponding to a fractional bandwidth of 95.9%. The peak realized gain of antenna 1 ranges from 1.9 to 5.2 dB, while the peak realized gain of antenna 2 ranges from 1.5 to 5.5 dB. Additionally, the antennas have a radiation efficiency above 80% within the resonant bandwidth27. A recent study presented a new slotted monopole patch antenna design that achieved a very wide bandwidth of 2.4 GHz, from 1.3 GHz to 3.7 GHz, for a fractional bandwidth of 96%26. This wide bandwidth antenna also maintained high performance, with a peak gain over 6 dBi and efficiency over 90%. Safety was also addressed, and the specific absorption rate (SAR) value for the antenna was kept below 0.1409 W/kg over the full usable frequency range. Therefore, this antenna achieved exceptional bandwidth while retaining good power, efficiency and SAR control.

Despite these advancements, a clear research gap remains. Existing designs often compromise between bandwidth, gain, efficiency, and safety, with no single design achieving optimal performance across all parameters. This study aims to address this gap by developing a novel UWB antenna design that optimally balances these factors, providing a safer, more efficient, and high-performance solution for microwave imaging of head injuries and strokes. Therefore, in this research introduces a novel exponentially tapered slotted UWB Vivaldi antenna designed for head microwave imaging applications. The antenna operates within a frequency range of 1.4–6.4 GHz, boasting a fractional bandwidth (FBW) of 128.20%. Additionally, it achieves a substantial gain of 9.99 dBi and exhibits directional radiation properties. Impressively, the antenna also possesses a high-fidelity factor of 96.26%. These characteristics demonstrate the antenna’s suitability for medical microwave imaging, particularly for detecting brain injuries. The rest of the article is organized as follows. “Antenna design methodology and optimization process” section covers the design and dimensions of the antenna along with the results of the parametric analysis conducted to evaluate its performance. “Results and discussions” section presents the results and discussion related to the antenna and a comparison with previous studies. Finally, “Conclusion” section provides a conclusion to the article.

In this work, the proposed configuration was developed based on imaging system-driven parameters, unlike traditional Vivaldi aperture antennas where the aperture parameters are mainly adjusted to broaden the impedance range. The slot geometry is engineered to reshape surface current distribution, improve forward radiation concentration toward the head phantom, and enhance time-domain signal fidelity. These modifications are intended to optimize near-field penetration and reduce multipath interference, which are critical factors in microwave brain imaging. Consequently, the design objective extends beyond bandwidth optimization to include imaging-oriented electromagnetic performance enhancement. Antenna design methodology and optimization process

Antenna design layout

The Vivaldi antenna is a wave propagation antenna that maintains a consistent current magnitude but varies in phase. Due to its exponentially tapered slot structure, the Vivaldi antenna has highly directed radiation properties and a decent gain. The peak value is relatively large compared to the pulse envelope. However, the balance between dielectric properties, size, and bandwidth must be carefully considered. Figure 1 shows the final design of the modified Vivaldi antenna, which is printed on a 1.57 mm substrate with a dielectric permittivity of 4.3. The antenna’s geometry includes an insulating medium, metallic ground plane, circular radiator, and feeding microstrip line. The structural dimensions of the antenna are listed in Table 1. Seven slots of symmetrical lengths are included to achieve a broad bandwidth and multiple resonances. The reflection coefficient of the antenna is unaffected by slot width, and all the identical rectangular slots are set to be 2 mm based on expected antenna properties, especially bandwidth and gain. The end of the microstrip coupler line is connected to a circularly slotted radiator, which reflects incident energy to the microstrip coupler line. The circular slotted radiator’s radius significantly influences the operating frequency, and the impedance can be adjusted by varying the radius. The redesigned Vivaldi antenna has three segments within its tapering slot line in the ground plane. Firstly, the bottom section (a) features a square slot to match the transmission line’s impedance. Secondly, the middle section (b) comprises a rectangular slot for coupling electromagnetic waves from the transmission line. Lastly, the top area involves an exponentially tapered slot, which can be divided into two parts: (c) propagation and (d) radiation, as depicted in Fig. 1c. The propagation phase is used to guide the electromagnetic waves toward radiation. In contrast, the radiation phase occurs when the slot width is around half the wavelength corresponding to the higher frequencies, causing the signal to radiate. As the slot width widens gradually, different antenna sections transmit at different frequencies, marked as four distinct sections in Fig. 1c. The modified Vivaldi antenna proposed here is powered through a 50 SMA connection, which has a dielectric permittivity of 2.08 and a conductivity of 0.000462 S/m. Design evaluation of the proposed antenna

Figure 2a shows the reflection coefficient for the modified Vivaldi antenna design at different stages of progression. Analyzing this helps understand how the back radiator slot affects the antenna performance. For the antenna without any slot (unslotted [A]), the reflection coefficient is 1.9 GHz to 5.4 GHz. In this case, the input impedance does not match properly, resulting in several frequency bands (2.2–2.7 GHz, 3.1–3.8 GHz and 4.7–5.1 GHz) with poor performance within the ultra-wide operating bandwidth. For the antenna with slots on the right wing (right wing slotted [B]), the reflection coefficient is 1.8 GHz to 5.4 GHz. However, this still has multiple stop bands (poor performance bands) that ultimately reduce the effective impedance bandwidth. The addition of symmetrical, rectangular slots to the left wing (left wing slotted [C]) disrupted the typical route of surface current, resulting in the ability to generate multiple resonance modes and lower frequencies shifted backward. This broadened the functional band from 1.5 to 5.48 GHz, with the reflection coefficient reaching close to the − 10 dB level at 3.5 GHz and 4.2 GHz. The extra slot further increased the current flow, allowing for more higher-order modes to be created in addition to the basic one, leading to a larger operating band of 1.5–5.48 GHz with multiple resonance points. Finally, the addition of slots to both the left and right wings of the ground structure (proposed [D]) altered the current flow, allowing the antenna to operate over a wide frequency range of 1.35–5.50 GHz. The larger effective slot area in the back-radiator extended the current path, leading to an impedance bandwidth below − 10 dB and an upper-frequency shift towards, and a lower-frequency shift backward compared to the left-wing slotted structure. This increased the overall impedance bandwidth by almost 500 MHz. The aim of adding slots to both sides in order to achieve a longer electrical length and a lower initial frequency, as lower frequencies have longer wavelengths, which can penetrate into biological tissue more effectively. The addition of slots causes a shift in the typical flow of current, resulting in the emergence of an elevated current mode that enhances directionality. The integration of slots concurrently minimizes side-lobe levels, boosts main-lobe gain, and corrects the squinting effect, leading to improved radiation characteristics. The slots enable the major radiation lobe to hold the most emitted energy, and the edges of the slots reinforce the radiation properties by controlling current distribution along the lateral edges. By examining how current flows and is distributed on the surface of an antenna, we can demonstrate how using slots can improve its performance in terms of impedance bandwidth, multiple resonance points, and radiation polarization. In Vivaldi antennas, the exponential ground layer acts as the main radiator and experiences a significant reversal current. The distributed current on the ground is driven by placing a slot directly below the antenna’s radiating patch, creating a loop around the slotting area. This causes significant polarization in the radiation pattern, which enhances both the antenna’s bandwidth and polarization. Figure 3 illustrates the current distribution throughout the antenna surface for each improvement stage at 3 GHz. Initially, the current is maximized toward the interior borderline of the exponentially tapered ground layer. However, in the second, third, and final stages, the slotted ground plane improves current flow and spreads it properly throughout the structure, emitting extra minor fields. The rectangular slots on the ground layer obstruct the typical path of the surface current, allowing the design to achieve a functional range of less than − 10 dB. Figure 4 demonstrates how the inner diameter, D, affects the reflection coefficient and gain of an antenna. When the D values are decreased to 22 mm and 24 mm, the physical slot area of the top radiator decreases, while all other parameters stay the same. As the slot becomes more restricted, the current route grows narrower, resulting in an amplification of the inductive effect. Consequently, an impedance mismatch occurs, leading to the emergence of notch bands at 3.5 GHz and 4.5 GHz with reflection coefficients beyond − 10 dB. These notch bands reduce the effective impedance bandwidth (IBW) within the operational range. Increases in D beyond the prescribed value by 2 mm do not impact the first four resonance frequencies. Nevertheless, the 5th resonance frequency and following higher frequencies undergo a backward shift, resulting in a decrease in the overall impedance bandwidth. As a result, the effective range of impedance is reduced by 400 MHz. When the diameter (D) of the antenna is set to 26 mm, the antenna is able to attain a maximum bandwidth of 4.15 GHz. This bandwidth covers the frequency range from 1.35 GHz to 5.50 GHz. Furthermore, the maximum gain is attained when D is equal to 26 mm. An exhaustive examination of different substrate materials was carried out to identify the most appropriate substrate layer. The evaluation criteria primarily considered the reflection coefficient (S11) and gain, as depicted in Fig. 5a and b. The graph in Fig. 5a shows that the FR-4 (lossy) substrate material achieved the best S11 below − 10 dB for the lower frequency range. Despite the higher bandwidth of 15 MHz achieved using Rogers RT5880 and Rogers RT5870 materials, the antenna had a subpar reflection coefficient beyond the − 10 dB reference level due to impedance mismatch, resulting in the absence of a lower frequency band. The FR-4 epoxy substrate material has a dielectric constant of 4.3, which leads to a lower starting frequency when compared to other substrate materials. As a result, there was a decrease in the frequency range and the presence of several resonances at around -35 dB. FR-4 epoxy is not only more cost-effective but also more readily accessible compared to Rogers, Taconic, and other PCB materials. Furthermore, the maximum gain increase in Fig. 5b exhibited almost comparable reactions across all substrate materials. After taking into account all of these parameters, it was found that the FR-4 (lossy) substrate material had excellent properties and a satisfactory level of amplification with a comparatively broader range of frequencies. Results and discussions

Surface current and field distribution

The proposed antenna was analyzed to observe how the concentration of surface current changes with frequency and affects the resonance frequency within its active functional range. Multiple resonance points were examined, and Fig. 6a illustrates the frequency-dependent distribution of surface current at two different resonance points: 3 GHz, and 4.7 GHz. The highest current density was found at lower frequencies in the circular ring or patch and just below the patch radiators. As the frequency increased, the current spread out towards the exponentially tapered edge, and this edge current had a significant impact on resonance at 3 GHz. At higher frequencies, such as 4.7 GHz, the highest current was found in the right-slotted region, along with a large amount of current along the circular patch. The significant current discussed earlier is responsible for producing various resonance modes and achieving impedance bandwidths of less than − 10 dB at higher frequencies. The proposed antennas near electric field (E-field) and magnetic field (H-field) distributions are analyzed at 3 GHz and 4.7 GHz, as shown in Fig. 6b and c, respectively. The magnitudes of the E-field and H-field indicate that their maximum strength is in the end-fire direction. The rectangular slots in the antenna design enhance the E-field and H-field radiation in the end-fire position, thereby maximizing the antenna’s directivity. Frequency domain analysis

To verify the accuracy of the antenna simulation, the designed antenna was created using a photolithographic technique. A snapshot of the fabricated prototype, including top and rear views and experimental measurement data, is presented in Fig. 7a–d. Figure 8a presents both the simulated and experimental S11 outputs of the proposed antenna. All antenna measurements were taken using the N9925A vector network analyzer, while nearfield statistics such as gain, radiation patterns, and efficiency were measured using the UKM Satimo-StarLab measuring tool. The simulated impedance bandwidth, where S11 is less than − 10 dB, was between 1.35 GHz and 5.50 GHz, with an FBW of 121.17%. The measured impedance bandwidth was between 1.4 and 6.4 GHz, with an FBW of 128.20%. The average gain was found to be similar in both simulation and measurement, with simulated results indicating 6.90 dBi and measured results indicating 6.40 dBi across the entire functional range. The maximum gain, as simulated, was achieved at 4.8 GHz with a value of 9.76 dBi, while the maximum gain during measurement was found to be 9.99 dBi at 4.3 GHz. Figure 8b shows that the experimentally measured gain of the antenna ranges from 2 dBi to 9.99 dBi across the entire bandwidth. To determine the antenna’s radiation efficiency, the observed gain, calculated directivity, and reported reflection coefficient are considered. The radiation efficiency is calculated using the formula Rad_Eff = Ḡ/Ḋ (1—|ℾ|2), where |ℾ| represents the reflection coefficient, Ḡ represents the gain, and Ḋ represents the directivity32,33. As a result, the intended antenna has a maximum radiation efficiency of over 93% and an average of 77% (based on simulation) across the entire operating band, as shown in Fig. 8c. For the fabricated prototype, the radiation efficiency is more than 90%, with an average of 69% (measured). The slight difference between the simulated and experimental results is due to the simulation considering ideal material characteristics, such as perfect and ideal conductors. In reality, copper material over FR-4 substrate material has resistive properties, resulting in losses and reflections. These factors cause increased reflection loss in the measured outcomes. Additionally, fabrication defects can alter the inductive or capacitive properties of the antenna, resulting in minor variations in its performance, such as reflection coefficient, gain, and efficiency. Moreover, the experimentally measured results include calibration errors from the vector network analyzer and losses in cables and connectors. While there is some fluctuation between the simulation and experimental results due to these factors, it may be insignificant since imaging applications are not entirely dependent on a specific frequency resonance.Fig. 7A snapshot of Antenna prototype after fabrication. Although FR-4 is characterized by a higher dielectric loss tangent compared to specialized microwave substrates, the achieved peak gain is primarily governed by antenna directivity rather than substrate efficiency alone. The traveling-wave nature of the Vivaldi structure supports strong end-fire radiation, while the optimized taper profile enhances effective aperture and front-to-back ratio. It should be noted that the reported peak gain occurs at the higher end of the operating band, where radiation efficiency improves and the impact of the dielectric loss is comparatively reduced.

Figure 9a–c demonstrate the proposed antenna’s radiation behavior within anechoic chambers. Both experimental and simulation results indicate stable directional radiation along the XZ plane within the operating range. The lower and upper frequencies exhibit stable main lobes in the end-fire direction, which is advantageous in microwave imaging applications. The X-pole value (cross-polarization) is also better than 20 dB in the XZ plane for 2 GHz, 3 GHz, and 4.7 GHz. Figure 9 shows that cross-polarization has a lower value than co-polarization in both simulated and experimental data. Time domain analysis

In UWB-IR (Ultra-wideband Impulse Radio), relatively short pulses (in the nanosecond range) are transmitted, covering an extensive frequency range. As a result, dispersion significantly impacts the pulses since they are so narrow. Therefore, the received pulse (Rx) will never be identical to the transmitted pulse (Tx). However, the receiver must be able to detect the transmitted pulse. So, a time-domain investigation is done to predict the signal waveform, modulation features, and signal distortions for the Tx/Rx waveform, fidelity factor (FF), group delay (GD), and S21 phase linearity of the transmitted pulse. Fidelity factor (FF) is a critical parameter for UWB and radar antennas, as it is related to S-parameters (S11 and S21), which are evaluated during antenna measurements. The amplitude of the S11 is essential for excellent matching, and the phase linearity of the S21 is crucial in a pulse communication scheme. The antenna’s transmitted wave must be distortion-free in a pulse communication network. One can determine the percentage of similarities between impulses by comparing the reference and received signals. This is done using the fidelity factor, which considers the antenna’s magnitude and phase transfer function. The value of the maximum cross-correlation between the two normalized signals (i.e., reference and received) determines the fidelity factor. If the fidelity factor value is 1 or 100%, the received signal is identical to the signal, indicating no distortion or dispersion occurred during transmission. A value of 0 indicates that the received pulse significantly differs from the transmitted pulse at the transmitter-antenna port. The required FF value to accurately identify an intended pulse varies depending on the application. Still, a pulse with more than 0.5 or 50% distortion is estimated to be practically indistinguishable35.

The fidelity factor (FF) represents the correlation between transmitted and received UWB pulses and provides an indication of waveform distortion introduced by the antenna. In microwave imaging systems, spatial resolution depends on the effective usable bandwidth and preservation of pulse shape. Consequently, improved fidelity contributes indirectly to enhanced spatial resolution and tumor localization accuracy.

Relevance of antenna gain to brain microwave imaging

In microwave brain imaging, the useful information is contained in the scattered field produced by dielectric contrasts inside the head42. Because head tissues are lossy and the skull introduces strong reflections, the received scattered signal can be weak relative to clutter and coupling artifacts. A higher antenna gain in this work corresponds mainly to improved end-fire directivity toward the head, which increases the effective radiated power delivered into the phantom and enhances the received backscattered field. In a multistatic imaging configuration, this improves the link budget and increases the measurable scattering contrast in the S-parameters between normal and tumor cases, thereby facilitating more reliable tumor localization. The time-domain metrics (group delay stability and fidelity factor) further support imaging by reducing waveform distortion and preserving the effective bandwidth used in delay-based reconstruction.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel UWB Vivaldi antenna with enhanced performance for brain microwave imaging. The antenna, designed to operate within the 1–4 GHz range, ensures superior tissue penetration and image resolution, showcasing a measured fractional S11 bandwidth of 128.20%, a peak gain of 9.99 dBi, and a front-to-back ratio exceeding 30 dB. The unidirectional radiation characteristics and over 93% radiation efficiency further highlight its potential. Experimental validation on a lossy FR-4 substrate confirms strong agreement between simulated and measured results, with time-domain analysis indicating minimal signal loss, reduced delays, and high fidelity, achieving a fidelity factor above 88% across various combinations. The parametric analysis underscores the critical impact of design parameters like patch diameter and substrate material on S11 results, demonstrating the sensitivity of current propagation and impedance matching to slot size changes. Additionally, the antenna array configuration, integrated with a 3D Hugo head model in CST simulations, effectively differentiates between normal and tumor-affected tissues, indicating its capability for detailed imaging of unwanted tissues. The specific absorption rate (SAR) analysis confirms compliance with IEEE standards, ensuring safe levels of electromagnetic absorption. Despite its promising results, the study does not address potential limitations such as the effect of different tissue types on performance or the integration challenges within a complete imaging system. Future research should focus on optimizing the antenna for various biological tissues, exploring alternative substrate materials, and developing a prototype imaging system to validate real-world efficacy. These advancements will further solidify the antenna’s potential for biomedical imaging applications.
