Morphodynamic cellular changes prior to and during cell fusion related to osteoclast formation
Authors: Lei Li, Qianfeng Xiang, Wei Ji, X Frank Walboomers, Jeroen JJP. van den Beucken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoclasts (OCs) play an important role in bone remodeling and bone resorption. Furthermore, OCs are considered to initiate the process of osteoinduction by stimulating the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs). OCs are large, multi-nucleated cells that originate from mononuclear precursors via cell fusion. Despite progress in understanding the molecular pathways involved, the dynamic morphological changes that precede and accompany fusion remain poorly defined. This study aims to characterize the spatiotemporal morphodynamics of osteoclast precursor cells (OCPs) during RANKL-stimulated differentiation, and identify structural features associated with fusion potential. Murine macrophages (RAW264.7 cells) were used as an in vitro model to study osteoclastogenesis. OCPs fusion and formation were induced by using 50 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL). Time-lapse holotomographic live-cell recordings were performed immediately after RANKL addition over 72 h using a Nanolive 3D cell explorer-fluo microscope, enabling continuous, label-free visualization of individual cell dynamics. Quantitative morphometric analysis was conducted using refractive index (RI)-based measurements with EVE analysis software to assess parameters including form factor, eccentricity, compactness, and cell extent. Finally, OCPs underwent progressive morphological remodeling prior to fusion, exhibiting increased elongation and shape irregularity. Multiple fusion phases were captured, including mono–mononuclear, mono–multinuclear, and multi–multinuclear fusion events. Notably, tunneling nanotubes-like membrane tethers (TNTs) frequently appeared before and during fusion and were associated with mediate directional movement and intercellular contact. Mononuclear cells displaying larger and more irregular morphology were more likely to participate in TNT-mediated fusion with multinucleated osteoclasts. Our findings demonstrate the predictive value of cell morphology in osteoclastogenesis and suggest that TNTs facilitate fusion coordination. This dynamic, single-cell-level perspective offers new insights into the morphodynamic characteristics of osteoclastogenesis and supports the notion that modulation of cell morphology may serve as a potential strategy to regulate osteoclast differentiation and function.

Introduction

Osteoclasts (OCs) are specialized multinucleated bone resorbing cells that act coordinated with osteoblasts (OBs) to maintain bone homeostasis through sustainable remodeling[1]. OCs have also been implicated as initiators of the process of osteoinduction following their stimulatory effect on mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs)[[2], [3], [4], [5]]. These multinucleated cells originate from the monocyte/macrophage lineage and form via tightly regulated processes involving precursor proliferation, cell-cell fusion, and functional activation[6,7]. The osteoclastogenesis process is primarily governed by two essential cytokines: macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), which promotes the survival and proliferation of osteoclast precursor cells (OCPs)[8], and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-Β ligand (RANKL), which drives their differentiation and fusion into mature OCs[9].

Osteoclasts originate from embryonic erythro-myeloid progenitors during development, while their maintenance after birth relies on the fusion of circulating blood monocytes[6]. The maturation of osteoclasts usually begins with the fusion between mononuclear cells into multinucleated cells, which subsequently may exhibit a bone resorption function[10]. Cell fusion determines the number of nuclei within osteoclasts, which has shown to be critically related to the osteoclast's bone resorption capacity: large osteoclasts with more nuclei have demonstrated significantly higher bone resorptive activity than smaller osteoclasts with less nuclei[11,12]. The fusion process involves dramatic morphological remodeling, including membrane reorganization, cytoskeletal realignment, and intracellular content mixing [10,13]. These events are regulated by a complex network of molecular signals, including those downstream of RANKL, M-CSF, and fusion-specific mediators, such as DC-STAMP[9,[14], [15], [16]]

Cell morphology is closely interlinked with the fusion capacity and efficiency of osteoclast precursors during their differentiation into multinucleated mature osteoclasts [17,18]. Several morphological features, such as cytoskeletal arrangement, cell elongation, membrane dynamics, and cell spatial location, are critical for precursor alignment and fusion, and they positively influence and reflect the development of cell-cell fusion events[13,19]. In particular, tunneling nanotubes-like membrane tethers (TNTs) have been shown to mediate direct intercellular communication and cytoplasmic exchange, promoting OCPs fusion[20]. Similarly, filopodia function as pre-fusion sensors guiding directional contact, while podosomes stabilize adhesion zones and coordinate actin cytoskeleton rearrangement[21]. Despite significant advances in genetic or molecular characterization of osteoclast differentiation and precursors fusion, most previous studies have relied on static, endpoint assays lacking the temporal resolution needed to track individual cells through the complete course of osteoclastogenesis[20,22]. As a result, the spatiotemporal behavior of OCPs—including their motility, cell-cell interactions, fusion events, and fate determination—remains unclarified. Understanding these dynamics is critical to improving our knowledge of osteoclastogenesis and identifying new targets for treating bone diseases and promoting bone regeneration.

Herein, to investigate whether specific morphological characteristics occur at different stages during the dynamic process of osteoclast formation, we performed high-resolution live-cell imaging using the Nanolive 3D Cell Explorer system to dynamically capture the complete process of osteoclastogenesis in RAW264.7 murine macrophage cells. This approach enabled us to track individual cells over time, monitor their 3D shape, movement, and fusion status, and construct a comprehensive temporal dynamics of osteoclast formation. We quantitatively analyzed morphometric parameters including cell area, form factor, eccentricity, and cell extent, as well as cell fate decisions (fusion vs. non-fusion). Our results reveal new insights into the morphodynamic landscape of osteoclast precursor fusion and the heterogeneity of differentiation outcomes. This real-time, single-cell-level perspective advances our understanding of osteoclast biology and may aid in identifying cellular or molecular bottlenecks that limit complete differentiation. This could contribute to the development of more targeted therapies for bone disorders associated with OC dysfunction or to promote bone tissue regeneration.

Materials & methods

Cell culture and osteoclast differentiation

The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 (ATCC® TIB-71™) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA; RRID: CVCL_0493). This cell line was authenticated within the three years preceding experimental use and regularly screened for mycoplasma contamination; all cultures tested negative for mycoplasma throughout the study. RAW 264.7 cells were selected due to their widespread and well-established use as an in vitro model for osteoclast differentiation and function [23]. The use of this cell line is therefore appropriate for the aims of the present study and does not affect the validity or conclusions of the manuscript.

Cells were cultured in minimum essential medium α (α-MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37°C in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells/cm2 and medium was refreshed every 3 days. When cell confluence reached 80%, cells were passaged using trypsin. Osteoclast differentiation was induced using 50 ng/mL RANKL (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, USA).

Live-cell recording

To visualize the dynamic process of osteoclast differentiation, live-cell imaging was conducted using a label-free holotomographic microscope (3D Cell Explorer, Nanolive SA, Lausanne, Switzerland). The Nanolive imaging system captures 3D refractive index (RI) tomograms of live cells without the need for fluorescent labeling, enabling continuous monitoring with minimal phototoxicity. RAW264.7 cells were seeded at the density of 2 × 103 cells/cm2 in μ-Dish 35 mm, high ibiTreat (ibidi GmbH, Germany). After overnight attachment, osteoclastogenesis medium (containing 50 ng/mL recombinant murine RANKL) was added to initiate osteoclast differentiation, and time-lapse recordings were started immediately. During acquisition, the dish was kept in the chamber mounted on the microscope stage with controlled culture condition (37°C with 5% CO2 and controlled humidity). This allowed the cells to be continuously monitored with osteoclastogenesis medium refreshments every 3 days. Raw RI data acquisition and 3D rendering reconstruction were performed with STEVE software (Nanolive SA, Switzerland). Then the resulting recordings, tomographic images, and digital staining data were exported for analysis. Recordings were made at intervals of 5 min per frame, and quantitative image analysis was conducted using EVE Analytics (Nanolive SA, Switzerland) and ImageJ (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA), focusing on cell morphology, fusion events, and temporal progression of osteoclastogenesis.

The EVE software automatically performs single-cell segmentation and parameter quantification based on refractive index (RI) data, enabling detailed morphometric tracking at the single-cell level throughout the differentiation process. Morphological parameter description.

Fusion events were identified by continuous time-lapse tracking of individual cells, requiring sustained cell-cell contact followed by disappearance of the membrane boundary and persistent transition from mono-to multinucleated cells in the reconstructed holotomographic images. Temporal changes in these parameters were analyzed across days 1, 2, and 3 (w/- RANKL stimulation) to construct a morphodynamic profile of osteoclastogenesis. Quantitative data were further correlated with TRAP activity and cytoskeletal architecture to explore associations between morphological remodeling and functional differentiation outcomes.

TNT-like membrane tethers were morphologically distinguished from regular cell protrusions based on their (i) connection to 2 spatially separated cells, (ii) incomplete flattening against the culture surface, (iii) an aspect area >10 (length-to-width), and (iv) relatively uniform diameter.

Tartrate-resistant acidic phosphatase (TRAP) staining

To confirm osteoclast differentiation, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed using a commercial TRAP staining kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in the 24-well plate at the density of 2000 cell/cm2 with medium containing 50 ng/ml RANKL. Cells were harvested for cytochemical staining at days 1, 2, 3 and 7. Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature. Then, the fixed cells were incubated with TRAP staining solution containing naphthol AS-BI phosphate and Fast Red Violet LB salt in the presence of 50 mM sodium tartrate for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. The TRAP-positive multinucleated cells were observed using a light microscope (Leica, Germany).

Fluorescence staining

To investigate the morphological and functional characteristics of osteoclast differentiation, phalloidin (F-actin) and ELF97 fluorescence staining[26] as employed to visualize the cytoskeletal organization, nuclei, and TRAP enzymatic activity during osteoclast differentiation. RAW264.7 cells seeded at 2000 cell/cm2 density were cultured in Lab-Tek® 8-well chamber slides (Nunc® Lab-Tek® II Chamber Slide™ systems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), which allowed for high-resolution fluorescence imaging without the need for sample transfer. Overnight, cells were cultured in medium supplemented with or without 50 ng/ml RANKL. Then, the cells were fixed and processed for staining at days 1, 2, 3, and 7.

For actin cytoskeleton visualization, cells were gently washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature. Thereafter, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. After that, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated phalloidin (Invitrogen, USA; 1:50 dilution in PBS with 1% BSA) for 60 min in the dark. After washing with PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:500 dilution) for 5 min and samples were mounted with a mounting medium.

To detect TRAP enzymatic activity, ELF97 phosphate substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Breda, Netherlands) was applied according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were incubated in TRAP reaction buffer containing ELF97 (1:50 times dilution in acid phosphatase solution) at 37°C in the dark. The enzymatic reaction produced a stable fluorescent precipitate, and then the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss AxioCam MRc5 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microimaging, Germany) equipped with appropriate filter sets for DAPI (blue), Alexa Fluor 594 (red), and ELF97 (green) fluorescence channels.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as mean and standard deviation (±SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used. For multiple group comparisons across different timepoints or treatments, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test to adjust for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

OCPs undergo morphological changes prior to cell fusion

Fig. 1A schematically shows the procedures followed to monitor osteoclastogenesis of RAW264.7 cells either dynamically using live cell imaging (3D Cell Explorer) or statically using cytochemical staining of fixated cells. For all procedures, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells/cm2 and stimulated with 50 ng/mL RANKL to induce osteoclast differentiation after overnight cell attachment. We started with recording cell morphology at 12 h intervals during osteoclasts differentiation (Fig. 1B and Supplemental Information Video 1). The holotomographic live-cell images were reconstructed from RI data, and illustrate the progressive morphological transformation of osteoclast precursors. At the start of the recordings, cells exhibited a round and compact morphology. As differentiation progressed, especially after 48 h, cells formed more protrusions and extended filopodia-like structures, adopting an increasingly spindle-shaped appearance. These protrusions reflect enhanced membrane activity and intercellular communication, often preceding cell-cell fusion. To quantify these morphological changes over the initial 72 h, we used quantitative analysis of multiple morphological parameters extracted from all cells within the imaging field at each time point (every 1 h; Fig. 1C); values represent the mean value per field and reflect tendency over time. This quantitative analysis showed a gradual decrease in cell form factor and cell extent, indicating that cells became more irregular and anisotropic in shape, with increased protrusions and less circular profiles. Concomitantly, cells showed a gradual increase in cell compactness and eccentricity, suggesting that cells became more elongated and structurally complex. Additional analyses of other morphological parameters (Supplemental Information Fig. S1) showed that cell area and perimeter also increased significantly over time, consistent with membrane spreading. Mean RI value, reflecting the average RI value of single cells in the field of view, decreased over time. The average dry mass density value, indicating the dry mass (e.g. proteins, nucleic acids, lipids) per unit cell area, slightly decreased. The granularity value, which reflects the distribution of organelles within the cell, was relatively stable. These morphometric trends reflect the dynamic cytoskeletal remodeling (i.e. spreading) and membrane plasticity associated with osteoclast precursor alignment, and intercellular contact formation prior to eventual cell-cell fusion. RANKL stimulation is the inducer of significant morphological changes

Since RAW264.7 cells intrinsically are adherent cells, we used comparative morphological evaluations of cells with and without RANKL stimulation at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells/cm2 in μ-dish and after overnight attachment, cells were either or not stimulated with RANKL (50 ng/ml) over this initial period. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, holotomographic live-cell imaging was performed on five fields per condition, followed by quantitative analysis using EVE and imageJ software. More than 300 individual cells were analyzed per sample to ensure statistical robustness. Fig. 2B and Supplemental Information Fig. S2 shows violin plots for the comparison of major morphological quantitative parameters between RANKL-stimulated and control cells. For RANKL-stimulated cells, both cell form factor and cell extent significantly decreased at 48 and 72 h (p < 0.0001) compared to controls, suggesting that cells became increasingly irregular and less circular. This corresponds with the formation of protrusions and elongation observed in live cell imaging videos, and is consistent with morphological changes preceding fusion. Additionally, cell compactness and eccentricity increased significantly for RANKL-stimulated at 48 and 72 h (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.01, respectively) compared to controls, indicating that cells developed more elongated, anisotropic shapes with increased structural complexity. Cytochemical stainings confirm successful osteoclast formation

To confirm osteoclast differentiation, three parallel approaches were performed to visualize cytoskeletal organization, TRAP enzymatic activity, and multinucleation. F-actin staining (Fig. 3A) revealed distinct morphological changes in the RANKL-stimulated cells. At 48 h, cells began to display pronounced cytoplasmic extensions and actin-rich peripheral structures. These structures were absent in controls, which maintained a more rounded cell morphology with limited changes in the actin cytoskeleton. ELF97 fluorescence staining (Fig. 3B) provided a sensitive result of TRAP enzymatic activity. At 48 h, and even more obviously at 72 h, RANKL-stimulated cells exhibited apparent bright yellow/green fluorescent precipitates localized within multinucleated cells, whereas controls were devoid of such signal. Classical TRAP staining (Fig. 3C) confirmed these findings at the cytochemical level. OCPs form multinucleated osteoclasts via different fusion patterns

To illuminate the spatiotemporal dynamics of osteoclast precursor cell fusion into multinucleated osteoclasts, we tracked multiple representative fusion events. These events were observed at different times from RANKL-stimulation.

Cell-cell and multinucleated cell-cell fusion at early time points after initiating RANKL-stimulation (48 h after RANKL stimulation)

To further evaluate the sequence and hierarchy of OCPs fusion, time-lapse holotomographic live-cell imaging was used to capture multistep fusion events over long periods. As shown in Fig. 4A (see video in Supplemental Information Video 2), a representative example was observed in which multiple mononuclear cells (cells 1, 2, 3, 4) progressively fused to form a large multinucleated cell. Initially, two mononuclear precursors (cells 1 and 2) approached and fused, forming a binucleated cell. This binucleated cell then underwent additional fusion events with other mononuclear cells (cells 3 and 4), with each fusion step increasing the nuclear number and cell size. Further, this cell suddenly fused with another large multinucleated osteoclast, generating a giant multinucleated cell with a significantly enlarged cytoplasmic volume. Notably, a TNT structure (Fig. 4A; yellow arrow) was observed between the two multinucleated cells prior to their fusion, indicating potential pre-fusion communication or membrane guidance. Cell-cell fusion after prolonged RANKL-stimulation (96 h after RANKL stimulation)

As shown in Fig. 5, several mononuclear precursors (labeled as cells 1, 2, and 3) and a multinucleated cell (cell 4) underwent sequential fusion events over a 4-h period. At the beginning, a tunneling nanotube (TNT)-like membrane tether was observed connecting cells 3 and 4, indicating early intercellular communication and a pattern of pre-fusion alignment. Shortly afterward (after 20min), cells 1 and 2 migrated toward cell 3 and fused sequentially, forming a multinucleated cell (cell 5). Interestingly, this fusion followed cell-cell contact along the side of the TNT-like membrane tether, suggesting that fusion-machinery is not only present at the tip of TNT-like membrane tethers. Then cell 4 moved directed toward this newly formed cell 5, which had 3 nuclei, under TNT-mediated and pulled guidance. Then the fusion between cell 5 and cell 4 resulted in a large multinucleated osteoclast containing five distinct nuclei, clearly visible in the RI images. Finally, this multinucleated cell fused into another larger multinucleated cell. Interestingly, immediately after fusion, the resulting cellular morphology shifted from a dense, compact state to a more spread and loosely structured morphology, which was consistent with an increase in cell area and cellular cytoplasmic volume. To characterize the morphology of early-stage fusion between mononuclear OCPs, representative fusion events between mononuclear cells were captured and quantitatively analyzed. As shown in Fig. 6A, two mononuclear cells gradually fused over a 15-min period to form a binucleated cell. The fusion process was visualized in both 2D RI images and 3D-rendered reconstructions. Discussion

In this study, we employed label-free holotomographic live-cell imaging combined with quantitative morphological analysis to investigate the dynamic process of OCPs fusion during RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis using RAW264.7 cells. Our objective was to characterize the spatiotemporal morphological changes and identify structural features associated with cell-cell fusion during osteoclast differentiation. Through long-term real-time imaging and fluorescent validation assays, we captured detailed morphodynamic trajectories of individual cells and fusion events. The results highlight that OCPs exhibit progressive morphological remodeling prior to fusion events, including form factor, cell extent, compactness and eccentricity, which reflected changes in cytoskeletal architecture and membrane dynamics. Multiple fusion phases, including mono-mononuclear, mono-multinuclear, and multi-multinuclear fusions were captured and tracked, revealing stepwise nuclear accumulation. Furthermore, individual cells that eventually underwent TNT-mediated fusion showed morphological differences from non-fuser cells, particularly regarding form factor and cell extent, suggesting that morphology predicts fusion competency.

Our findings demonstrate the progressive morphological remodeling of OCPs prior to fusion. We observed a consistent decrease in form factor and cell extent, along with increases in compactness and eccentricity prior to the OCPs fusion. These alterations indicate a transition toward more elongated and structurally irregular cell shapes. These changes are closely associated with dynamic remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton, reflecting active cytoskeletal reorganization, a prerequisite for efficient fusion[27,28]. This morphological transition is also associated with enhanced membrane ruffling, protrusive activity, facilitating pre-fusion alignment, and intercellular contact[29]. RANKL-stimulated macrophages exhibited more membrane protrusions, which are known to enhance the probability of cell-cell interaction and promote subsequent fusion events [30,31]. Actin-rich peripheral structures like filopodia and podosomes have been shown to mediate these processes, playing critical roles in pre-fusion alignment, membrane protrusion, and adhesion zone remodeling during osteoclastogenesis[27,32,33]. This can provide contact and exchange information or substances for cell fusion. Studies suggest that actin-driven projections like filopodia and TNTs serve as physical platforms for directional contact and intercellular exchange[34]. The absence of filopodia can prevent the close membrane apposition necessary for fusion [35]. These findings support the view that cytoskeletal remodeling and morphological plasticity are not merely byproducts of differentiation but are necessary processes for establishing fusion preparation. The structural characteristics like eccentricity, reduced form factor, and cell extent, could serve as early predictors of fusion events.

Our study visually confirmed multiple fusion scenarios in osteoclastogenesis, including mono-mononuclear, mono-multinuclear fusions, and multi-multinuclear fusions. These successive fusion events promote gradual nuclear accumulation and cell enlargement, reflecting the sequential stages of osteoclast maturation. This is consistent with previous findings that mononucleated OCPs fuse not only with other mononucleated OCPs but also with multinucleated cells [36]. Fusion initiation requires stable adhesion and close membrane contacts mediated by cell adhesion molecules, including integrins and cadherins, which anchor cytoskeletal elements and facilitate prefusion complexes[37]. Our time-lapse imaging further revealed that OCP fusion is initiated by membrane protrusions and physical contact between cells, which then progress toward complete membrane merging and cytoplasmic integration. However, further investigation is needed to understand the mechanisms that regulate this contact and fusion. Importantly, once multinucleation is established, the resulting osteoclasts were more likely to engage in subsequent fusion events with other mononuclear or multinucleated cells. This finding is consistent with those reported by Søe et al. They found that more than 60% of fusion events occur between mononuclear and multinucleated cells[38]. During fusion, smaller cells tend to move toward larger cells, and mononuclear cells tend to move toward multinuclear cells. This corroborates findings of previous studies showing that osteoclast fusion is based on the heterogeneity of its fusion partners, suggesting a more mature osteoclast prefers to fuse with a less mature pre-osteoclast [28,38]

Notably, we frequently observed TNT-like membrane tethers preceding or accompanying fusion events, indicating their potential regulatory role. TNTs are dynamic, actin-rich peripheral structures that mediate intercellular communication by facilitating the transport of vesicles, organelles, and regulatory proteins([30]; Dufrancais et al., 2025). Although our results are based on experiments using the murine cell line RAW264.7, it has been shown that TNT-like membrane tethers are also actively formed during RANKL-stimulated osteoclastogenesis and serve as conduits for signal exchange and membrane coordination among fusing partners in rat primary osteoclast precursors. [39], which validates our methodological choice. Still, this cell line has been reported to not be fully homogenous, but rather heterogenous regarding the existence of subclones with fast(er) formation of osteoclast following RANKL stimulation [40]. TNT-like membrane tethers are also considered crucial in initiating OCP fusion[30]. Live-cell imaging in our study revealed that TNT-like membrane tethers guided a mononucleated cell to move towards larger, multinucleated cell. It also guided a smaller, multinucleated cell to move towards larger one, or two cells of the same size to move closer to each other. This directional migration may reflect heterogeneity in cytoskeletal dynamics or fusion competence among cells. This phenomenon is possibly driven by heterogeneity in fusion preparation or cytoskeletal signaling. TNTs connect not only mononuclear precursors but also bridge multinucleated osteoclasts, indicating a broader role in inter-osteoclast communication and coordination[20]. Importantly, DC-STAMP, a critical regulator of osteoclast fusion, has been shown to be transferred via TNTs, highlighting their importance as signaling highways in the pre-fusion phase [39]. These findings support the hypothesis that TNT-like membrane tethers are active modulators of fusion timing, partner selection, and coordination.

The process of cell fusion involves the reorganization of the cytoskeleton, which determines cell shape and organelle organization[34]. Gardner et al. reported that the phasic cytoplasmic extension or retraction. cell morphology is dynamically regulated during RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis [41]. Sun et al. demonstrated that osteoclast fusion is more likely to be found in areas of low substrate traction force and high intercellular connectivity. This highlights the influence of physical and morphological factors on the dynamics of cell -cell fusion [17]. Dynamic protrusions such as podosome and filopodia are driven by cytoskeletal remodeling and have been implicated in both migration and adhesion during pre-fusion stages [21]. The requirement for actin-rich peripheral protrusions to initiate cell–cell fusion has been found in several model systems, including osteoclast fusion [27,42]. We further analyzed the morphological parameters of individual cells, which reflected the change of OCPs in morphological characteristics. Consistent with previous findings, in our study, RANKL-stimulated cells exhibited more protrusions and irregular shapes prior to fusion, suggesting that morphological irregularity and increased protrusions enhance the chances of cell-cell contact, connection establishment and ultimately fusions.

Levaot et al. introduced the concept of “fusion founders” that were capable of initiating fusion events [43], and prior to fusion, the founders and followers had already exchanged information via the cytoplasm. However, they discovered that the cells remained round until they adhered to one another. Similarly, we did not observe any significant morphological differences between the fusion partners in the case of fusion between mononucleated cells. However, the resulting binucleated cells showed significant changes in form factor and cell compactness. Moreover, the formed multinucleated cells gradually changed from a compact state to a spread state, demonstrating a decrease in cell density. This reflects cellular organelles remodeling, rearrangement of F-actin, and changes in multinucleated cell functions. TNTs were reported to appear before OCP fusion [44]. In this study, the mononuclear cells involved in TNT-mediated fusion events displayed increased protrusions and irregular morphology. This suggests that the protrusions and irregular morphology make the OCPs easier to establish TNT connections between cells prior to fusion, in preparation for future fusions.

This study presents an application of label-free holotomographic live-cell imaging to visualize the spatiotemporal dynamics of OCPs fusion during RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation. The use of the Nanolive 3D Cell Explorer system enables non-invasive, real-time monitoring of live RAW264.7 cells with minimal phototoxicity, capturing continuous morphological transitions at high resolution over extended timeframes. This method significantly improves on traditional endpoint analysis and static imaging methods, which often fail to analyze dynamic fusion behavior or early morphological changes. The visual recording of sequential fusion events, particularly through TNT-mediated pathways, provide new insights into the phases, movement model, and subcellular structure changes of osteoclast fusion. Furthermore, quantitative morphological analysis using EVE software allowed us to quantitatively characterize the dynamics of OCPs morphology during the fusion. By analyzing multiple structural parameters, including cell area, perimeter, form factor, eccentricity, and dry mass density, we clarified morphological characteristics associated with osteoclast differentiation and fusion preparation. The parallel experiments of fluorescence-based cytoskeletal and TRAP enzymatic staining provided critical validation of osteoclast differentiation. Our findings will enable us to control the osteoclasts differentiation and maturation by regulating OCP morphology. Methods of achieving this include changing the surface properties of biomaterials [23,45] or biological factors to promote a particular morphology. However, there are still some limitations. Although we tracked a large number of cells over time, the number of complete, clearly traceable fusion events remained limited due to field-of-view constraints and tracking complexities in areas of crowded cells. Further, despite being able to continuously image live-cells to allow us to distinguish fusion events by persistent cell-cell contact, disappearance of the intercellular boundary, and stable transition to multinucleated cells, we acknowledge that RAW264.7 cells possess phagocytic activity. Therefore, while the observed events are consistent with osteoclast precursor fusion and are supported by subsequent TRAP, ELF97, and F-actin analyses, we cannot completely exclude rare phagocytic or cell-in-cell events without complementary membrane- or cytoplasm-specific fusion markers. Moreover, while morphological metrics suggest pre-fusion behavior, mechanistic insights into molecular pathways (e.g., DC-STAMP or OC-STAMP signaling) remain indirect. Quantitative thresholds for predicting fusion competency based on morphology (e.g., eccentricity or form factor) were not definitively established. Future studies can be conducted based on a large number of cell fusion events to predict cell fate using machine learning.

Conclusions

In summary, this study employed label-free holotomographic live-cell imaging combined with quantitative morphometric analysis to dynamically track OCPs fusion during RANKL-stimulated differentiation. We demonstrated that at different stages prior to and during cell fusion, OCPs exhibit progressively distinct morphological characteristics. Furthermore, individual cells participating in different fusion patterns also display varying morphological features. The progressive morphological remodeling, characterized by reductions in form factor and cell extent, and increases in compactness and eccentricity, occurs prior to and likely facilitates fusion events. Time-lapse videos showed the process of cells gradually fusing to form multinucleated cells, including the sequential accumulation of nuclei and the gradual enlargement of osteoclasts. TNT-like membrane tethers were frequently observed establishing intercellular connectivity and guiding cell movements prior to and during fusion, indicating their vital role in RANKL-stimulated osteoclasts maturation. These findings provide valuable insights into the morphodynamic characteristics of osteoclastogenesis and highlight cell morphology as a predictor of fusion potential.
