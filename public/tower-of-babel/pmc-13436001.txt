Neurological complications induced by checkpoint inhibitors: characterising the clinical spectra
Authors: Anne Merel van Wylick, Martin van den Bent, Bart C Jacobs, Maarten Titulaer, Jeroen Kerstens, Esther Brusse, Ron H J Mathijssen, Daphne W Dumoulin, Astrid A M van der Veldt, Marjolein Geurts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely recognition of immune checkpoint immune-related neurological adverse events (irNAEs) is critical given their potential severity, yet remains challenging due to limited clinical experience. This study investigates clinical presentation and management of irNAEs from a neurological perspective.MethodsWe retrospectively identified all patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) at Erasmus MC Cancer Institute, Rotterdam, The Netherlands between 2017 and 2024. Patient records were analysed by a neurologist for clinical and treatment of irNAE post-ICI initiation.ResultsOf 3176 ICI-treated patients, irNAE was diagnosed in 76 cases (2.4%). Peripheral syndromes occurred in 55 (70%), central in 21 (30%) patients. Classification into distinct disease entities was challenging due to remarkable overlap in affected neurological structures. Median onset of irNAE was 8 weeks after ICI initiation (range 1–104 weeks); 70% developed within 18 weeks. IrNAE-related mortality was 13%, observed only in the first 18 weeks. Fewer non-small cell lung cancer patients developed irNAE (OR, 0.36; 95% CI 0.16 to 0.8; p=0.012) compared with other tumour types. Males (OR, 1.78; 95% CI 1.1 to 2.90; p=0.019) and PD1/CTLA4 combination therapy (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.28 to 3.46, p=0.004) were associated with increased irNAE incidence. Glucocorticoids were given in 64% of patients; 14% received immunosuppressive therapy beyond steroids. Treatment response varied widely, both clinically and temporally.ConclusionThis retrospective single-centre study confirms irNAEs are infrequent complications of ICI. The distinct symptom profile, with substantial overlap within affected neurological structures, underscores the need for neurological expertise in irNAE care. While most develop within 18 weeks of treatment, late-onset cases occur. Mortality occurred only in early-onset cases.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are monoclonal antibodies targeting checkpoint proteins, such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4; key regulators of T-cell activity. By blocking these pathways, ICIs enhance anti-tumour immunity and have transformed treatment standards across many tumour types.1 2 Conversely, ICI may disrupt self-tolerance, leading to immune responses against self-antigens and to immune-related adverse events (irAEs).3 Immune-related neurological adverse events (irNAEs) are considered rare, and data on clinical presentation, treatment strategies and disease course are limited. International guidelines provide little specific neurological guidance,4 5 despite the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with irNAE.

This study aimed to (1) characterise neurological irNAE, focusing on clinical characteristics and course, timing, and patient characteristics and (2) describe treatment approaches, thereby identifying gaps in current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational study of all patients treated with pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab, avelumab or their combinations, between 1 January 2017 and 24 March 2024 for oncological indications at Erasmus MC Cancer Institute, Rotterdam, The Netherlands via pharmacy dispensing data, excluding newer agents not widely used at the time. Patients receiving subsequent ICIs were classified by the first ICI. In case of irNAE, they were classified according to the ICI they were receiving at time of symptom onset. Medical records of all these patients and who consulted a neurologist post-ICI initiation were reviewed by one investigator (ie, AMvW) to identify irNAE cases; uncertain diagnoses were reviewed by a second investigator (ie, MG). Patients were classified as having irNAE if they met both of the following mandatory criteria: (1) alternative causes (eg, tumour progression, metastases, infection) were reasonably excluded and (2) neurological symptoms emerged after ICI initiation without preceding similar complaints. A third criterion—durable improvement or stabilisation was observed following corticosteroids or ICI cessation—was considered supportive but not mandatory, and applied when relevant.

Collected variables included demographics (age, sex), tumour type, ICI regimen, neurological presentation, timing of onset, performed diagnostic modalities, treatment regimen and outcome. Patients were included until March 2024. Continuous variables were delineated using a median and IQR, categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using χ² or Fisher’s exact test (in case of small numbers). Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare two groups of non-normally distributed data. Variables identified with p<0.10 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression, adjusted for sex, certain tumour types and ICI class (PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4 or PD-1/CTLA-4). Time to neurotoxicity was visualised, without further analysis of potential determinants. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 without correction for multiple testing.

Results

During the study period, 3176 individual patients received one or more ICIs at our institution. Patients had a median age of 70 years (IQR 62–77) and comprised 1728 males (54%). Pembrolizumab was most frequently administered (44%), 21% received a combination of two ICIs. Table 1 summarises patient and tumour characteristics. Of this cohort, 1104 patients consulted a neurologist for varied reasons. 76 patients (2.4%) of all ICI-treated patients developed irNAE, of whom 55 (72%) were male, with a median age of 66 years (IQR 60–77). Median time from ICI initiation to symptom onset was 8 weeks (IQR 4–25, range 0–104 weeks). 70% of irNAE occurred within the first 18 weeks, 30% presented later, up to 104 weeks after treatment initiation. This is shown in figure 1, illustrating the temporal distribution of irNAE over time. Seven patients (9%) developed irNAE after cessation of ICI (range 4–24 weeks). Peripheral irNAEs occurred in 55 (70%) patients, central in 21 (30%), with no significant difference in median time to onset between groups (12 vs 7 weeks; p=0.32). Myositis–myasthenia–cardiomyositis occurred in 16 patients (median onset 5 weeks), whereas other toxicities occurred in 57 patients (median onset 10 weeks), without a statistically significant difference in time to onset (p=0.145 after correction for multiple testing). Median time to follow-up was 15.3 weeks (range 0.9–266 weeks).

Meningo-encephalo-myelitis overlap syndrome (n=19; table 2).

Meningo-encephalo-myelitis overlap syndrome (n=19; table 2).

This group includes meningitis-like, encephalitic and myelitic presentations. Predominantly meningitis-like presentations (n=6) featured grade 3 headache in five cases (83%), somnolence in one (17%) and fever in three, with temperatures up to 40.4°C. Only one patient fulfilled the classic meningitis triad (headache, fever, altered mental status). Two patients (34%) exhibited focal neurological deficits—diplopia and ataxia—indicating encephalitic overlap.

MRI-brain, performed in all patients, revealed abnormalities in one case (i.e. asymmetric supratentorial pachymeningeal enhancement). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (n=6) revealed pleocytosis in all patients (median 44 cells/µL, range 8–470), with a median lymphocytic percentage of 90.5% (range 13%–100%). Protein and glucose levels were elevated in four (median 1.15 g/L, range 1.04–1.50 g/L; reference 0.18–0.58 g/L; and median 4.9 mmol/L, range 4.0–8.5 mmol/L; reference 2.5–3.7 mmol/L, respectively). Pathology, viral PCRs (including Herpes Simplex Virus 1 and 2 (HSV-1/2), Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV), Epstein Barr Virus (EBV) and enterovirus), and routine bacterial cultures (aerobic/anaerobic and Gram stain) were negative in five; in one patient, there were no viral panel or cultures performed. All six patients improved, four with corticosteroids; two after ICI discontinuation.

Among the predominantly encephalitic phenotype (n=10), nine patients fulfilled the Graus criteria7 (normally used for autoimmune encephalitis (AIE)) for possible AIE, of which three ultimately adhered to criteria for definite AIE. One patient did not meet the criteria for possible AIE, due to the absence of subacute onset of working memory deficit, altered mental status or psychiatric symptoms. This patient exhibited focal neurological signs that responded to high-dose prednisolone, and was therefore considered as encephalitis. Seven had atypical features (cauda syndrome, myelitis, hemiparesis or prodromal symptoms (headache, fever, somnolence)), reflecting overlap with the meningitic and myelitic phenotypes.

Brain (n=7) and spinal MRI (n=3) showed no abnormalities. CSF analysis (n=9) revealed pleocytosis in seven cases (median 26/µL (range 1–434/µL)). In five patients, pleocytosis consisted of 100% lymphocytes, and three patients had insufficient cell count for a differential count. The remaining two patients had lymphocytic percentages of 68% and 34%, respectively. Five cases had an elevated protein (median 0.59 g/L, range 0.15–2.06), and elevated glucose in seven (median 4.3 mmol/L, range 3.2–7.2; serum reference unavailable in two cases). CSF viral PCRs (which includes the same panel as mentioned before), cultures (n=7), and cytology (n=8) were negative. In the remaining two patients leptomeningeal metastasis was considered unlikely based on clinical evolution.

Serum and CSF screening was performed in eight patients. This included a standardised paraneoplastic panel (comprising anti Hu, Ri, Yo, CV2, Ma1/2, amphiphysin and Tr). Depending on the clinical phenotype, additional neuronal surface antibodies were tested. Anti-neural antibodies were detected in three patients (LGI1, Ma2, glycine receptor antibodies); one showed borderline CV2 antibodies in CSF only. All antibody-positive patients met the Graus criteria for definite AIE.

Treatment involved ICI discontinuation and high-dose methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days), followed by tapering oral prednisone (1–2 mg/kg/day). Two patients received additional intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Seronegative patients responded well, achieving full recovery without relapses. Response of seropositive patients depended on the identified antibody. Three patients died; due to infection, tumour progression, limited treatment response and patient’s wish to discontinue treatment.

Moving along the continuum, three patients had a myelitic-like phenotype (n=3). MRI spine revealed longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis, with T2-hyperintense lesions over >3 vertebral segments in two patients. CSF analysis in one patient showed pleocytosis (128 leucocytes/µL, 98% lymphocytes), normal protein (0.57 g/L) and elevated glucose (4.6 mmol/L). Treatment involved ICI discontinuation and high-dose methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days), followed by oral prednisone (2 mg/kg/day for 9 months). One patient partially recovered (ambulant, but posterior column dysfunction), the other died from tumour progression with minimal recovery.

A third patient presented with tetraparesis, followed by fluctuating consciousness, hallucinations and chorea 21 days post-ICI, illustrating encephalitic overlap. MRI spine demonstrated diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement, despite repeated negative CSF cytology. CSF revealed pleocytosis (maximum 43 × 10⁶/L leucocytes/µL, 100% lymphocytes), elevated protein and glucose (1.92 g/L, 5 mmol/L, respectively). Treatment involved ICI discontinuation, high-dose methylprednisolone (2 mg/kg) and subsequently IVIg due to limited response. Consciousness and motor function improved after 9 days post-IVIg, but treatment was discontinued due to tumour progression and patient’s choice.

Myasthenia–myositis–cardiomyositis overlap syndrome (n=16; table 2).

Myasthenia–myositis–cardiomyositis overlap syndrome (n=16; table 2).

All patients presented within 3 months of ICI initiation, exhibiting varying degrees and patterns of muscular weakness. Fluctuating symptoms, suggestive of myasthenic involvement,8 were reported in 9 (56%) cases. One patient experienced chest pain indicating cardiac involvement. 11 patients (69%) met the Lyon criteria for cardiomyositis,9 supported by elevated troponins and two minor criteria. Biochemical tests (creatine kinase (CK), troponin T and CK-MB) were performed in 94%. One patient, tested 1.5 months after symptom onset, had normal biomarkers. CK was elevated in the other 15 patients, ranging from 320 to 26 094 U/L (median 2642; upper limit of normal (ULN) <171), troponin T from 21 to 3180 ng/L (median 742; ULN <14 ng/L), and CK-MB from 16.8 to 215 µg/L (median 65; ULN <7.6 µg/L). Anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) and muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibodies were tested in 12 patients, detecting five anti-AChR positives (31%) and no anti-MuSK. Myositis blot testing (n=5) identified anti-Ku and anti-Mi-2B antibodies in one patient each. Electromyography (n=4) revealed no neuromuscular transmission defects; one patient demonstrated low compound muscle action potentials consistent with myositis. Neostigmine testing (n=5) improved symptoms in one patient.

ECG (n=15) showed abnormalities in six: conduction defects (n=4), ST changes (n=1) and arrhythmias (n=1), all with troponin elevation. Cardiac MRI (n=7) was unremarkable. One patient was postmortem diagnosed with necrotising myositis.

ICI was discontinued in all patients. Most (75%) received methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3–5 days) followed by oral prednisone (1–2 mg/kg/day). Two patients commenced with oral steroids (1 mg/kg). Treatment was intensified in 62.5% due to insufficient lab response despite clinical stabilisation. Table 1 summarises additional immune suppressive treatment regimens. Clinical outcomes varied with recovery over several months. Six patients died: two from cardiomyositis with multiorgan failure despite steroids and plasma exchange, one from infection and three from tumour progression.

Cranio-radiculo-polyneuropathy overlap syndrome (n=34; table 2).

Cranio-radiculo-polyneuropathy overlap syndrome (n=34; table 2).

Seven patients presented with predominantly cranial neuropathy (n=7); four with an additional asymmetrical polyneuropathy. The facial nerve was most commonly affected (n=4), followed by the trigeminal (n=2), vestibulocochlear and abducens nerves (n=1 each). MRI was normal except one with asymptomatic trigeminal nerve enhancement. CSF (n=4) revealed pleocytosis in four (6–51 cells/µL, 100% lymphocytic differentiation in 2 patients, one patient with 94% and one patient with no additional differentiation due to 6 cells total), elevated protein in two (up to 1.1 g/L) and elevated glucose in one patient (4.5 mmol/L). All patients ceased ICI and improved with glucocorticoids (up to 1 mg/kg) over several weeks.

Predominantly polyneuropathy occurred in 21 patients. Prior chemotherapy and other pre-existing risk factors for developing polyneuropathy were present in 19% of patients. In all cases, neurological symptoms emerged after initiation of ICI therapy with subsequent clinical improvement when applicable (in case of additional treatment with corticosteroids).

The clinical syndrome was typically sensory-dominant: paresthesias (57%), hypesthesias (29%) and neuropathic pain (38%). Motor (10%) and autonomic symptoms (19%) were less common. In 71%, impaired proprioception and osteotendinous reflexes occurred. Autonomic dysfunction was confirmed in 9.5%. EMG (n=5) showed sensorimotor axonal neuropathy in three patients. CSF (n=6) revealed pleocytosis in three patients (6, 50 and 53 cells/µL), elevated protein in two (0.62 and 0.72 g/L), increased glucose in three (3.8, 3.8 and 3.9 mmol/L) supporting clinically unexpected radicular involvement. Of note, the patients with 50 and 53 cells/µL both had a 100% lymphocytic distribution. MRI (n=6) was unremarkable. Extensive laboratory screening (n=18) excluded other causes. Treatment consisted of ICI discontinuation (29%, n=6), eight patients received corticosteroids (two with concurrent systemic irAEs). Most patients (76%) improved although four (19%) had persistent symptoms requiring neuropathic pain medication.

Polyradiculopathy (n=6) featured dermatomal sensory loss and radicular leg pain with mild motor weakness in one patient. Deep tendon reflexes were reduced or absent in three cases. Contrast-enhanced spine MRI (n=1) showed nerve root enhancement. CSF (n=6) revealed pleocytosis in five patients (median 41, range 10–78 cells/µL), elevated protein (median 0.88, range 0.52–2.03 g/L) in six; elevated glucose in three (without corresponding serum glucose levels, one patient with diabetes mellitus). Three patients had a 100% lymphocytic distribution; the other two patients had 97% and 96%, respectively. EMG, performed in one patient because of clinical deterioration, showed features of a demyelinating polyneuropathy. Five patients developed polyradiculopathy during ICI treatment and improved to mRS 0–1 after cessation of ICI and treatment with corticosteroids (60 mg/day to 2 mg/kg). One patient developed complaints 24 weeks after cessation of CPI. Alternative causes including leptomeningeal disease, infection and paraneoplastic syndromes were excluded with CSF analysis and the patient recovered fully with corticosteroids (60 mg/day). Two patients relapsed; one died because of lack of improvement and one due to the patient’s choice to stop treatment.

Three additional patients presented with motor deficits and muscle atrophy, clinically suggestive of plexopathy; two also experienced radicular pain and sensory deficits possibly also to be classified in the cranio-radiculo-polyneuropathy spectrum. MRI of both spine and plexus (n=3) revealed no abnormalities. EMG (n=1) showed denervation in C5–C6 muscles. CSF analysis (n=1) was normal. ICI was interrupted in two patients; one recovered on discontinuation, the other, who already received immunosuppressants for concurrent systemic irAE, developed plexopathy 24 weeks after cessation of ICI. Exclusion of alternative causes was limited, including leptomeningeal disease and infection, given clinical recovery. The third patient continued ICI because of mild symptomatology.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive neurological characterisation of immune-related neurotoxicity in patients treated with ICIs. Rather than describing isolated syndromes, as outlined in the current consensus definitions for neurologic irAEs by Guidon et al,10 our findings highlight that many patients exhibit overlapping features within the central or peripheral compartment. We delineate; next to the already widely recognised myasthenia-myositis-cardiomyositis, two other clinical overlap spectra: meningitic-encephalitic-myelitic, cranio-radiculo-polyneuropathy overlap syndromes, reflecting shared etiological mechanisms rather than distinct entities. These observations extend prior reports of the well-characterised myasthenia-myositis-cardiomyositis overlap syndrome11 not only in the neuromuscular effector, but also within the peripheral and the central nervous system. All three spectra identified in our cohort represent overlapping presentations rather than discrete entities. This convergence may result from shared immune checkpoint expression across anatomically distinct tissues. Checkpoint molecules are expressed in the PNS on Schwann cells, resident macrophages (including microglia, perivascular and meningeal macrophages) in the CNS, and on skeletal muscle fibres and cardiomyocytes, as shown in the human protein atlas.12 This suggests that local loss of immune tolerance may provoke anatomically contiguous yet clinically distinct inflammatory syndromes. Recognition of these overlaps can refine both diagnosis and management by emphasising immune pathophysiology over classical topographical boundaries.

A second key finding is the biphasic temporal distribution of irNAE onset. Approximately 70% occurred within 18 weeks, characterised by higher morbidity and mortality,13 while delayed presentations (30%) had a milder course. Despite more aggressive immunosuppression, early-onset irNAE had a higher mortality, suggesting a more severe course. This finding has been described for systemic irAE,13 but is less known in irNAE. This temporal distinction may hold clinical relevance: high-risk patients—particularly men and patients receiving combination therapy—might benefit from intensified neurological monitoring during the initial treatment phase. Whether pre-treatment antibody screening or earlier intensified corticosteroid therapy could mitigate early severe toxicity remains an open research question.

The delayed irNAE, arising 18 weeks after ICI initiation (or even following therapy cessation), may reflect secondary immune activation triggered by a second hit (eg, infection, vaccination). In this hypothesis, the initial priming of the immune system by ICI requires an additional stimulus to reach the threshold for clinical neurotoxicity. This distinct mechanism might explain the observed milder phenotype in delayed-onset cases. Understanding the immunological differences between early and delayed irNAE could guide more individualised surveillance and management strategies.

Only half of patients received corticosteroids, despite guideline recommendations, reflecting limited awareness of the condition and its management,4 5 especially in the early years of ICI therapy. A contributing factor is that traditional neurological diagnostic tools seem to have lower sensitivity in irNAE compared with primary autoimmune disorders. For instance, MRI in encephalitis or myelitis may be normal despite clinically severe disease, and electromyography or biochemical testing can be inconclusive in suspected myasthenic-myopathic-cardiomyopathic presentations. Similarly, CSF analysis may reveal nonspecific findings. Awareness of these atypical findings14 is essential to avoid underdiagnosis and delayed treatment.

Male sex and combination immunotherapy15 16 were associated with higher irNAE incidence, consistent with general irAE data, though not previously established for neurological irAE. Interestingly, NSCLC patients experienced fewer irNAEs, possibly due to shorter ICI exposure after tumour progression following first-line treatment, limiting time for irNAE development. This warrants further investigation.6

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design precludes causal inference. Additionally, mild irNAEs may be underreported due to referral bias. Though one of the largest cohorts, small subgroup sizes reduced power for subtype-specific analyses. Because multiple exploratory comparisons were performed, p values were not corrected for multiple testing, which may increase the risk of type I error. Diagnosis, treatment and diagnostic work-up—particularly in mild cases—were physician-determined and confirmed by one investigator, introducing potential bias and limits diagnostic standardisation. Finally, early deaths post-irNAE sometimes complicated differentiation between irNAE and cancer progression.

Despite these limitations, this large real-world cohort illustrates that irNAEs often transcend traditional neuroanatomical boundaries.17 This classification may improve diagnosis and foster research into shared immune mechanisms. Clinically, these findings underscore the importance of early neurological assessment to facilitate the prompt recognition of prodromal syndromes—typical for encephalitis-like phenotypes—where the differential diagnosis requires fast analysis. Furthermore, this highlights the need for multidisciplinary collaboration and the inclusion of neurologists in guideline development, consistent with the challenges and recommendations outlined by Willis et al18 regarding the complex and overlapping presentations of neurologic irAEs.

Future research should aim to validate these clinical spectra and explore biomarkers that distinguish early from delayed irNAE, so optimal immunosuppressive strategies could be defined. Integration of neurological monitoring into oncological care pathways could substantially improve patient outcomes as ICI use continues to expand across tumour types.

Clinical implications

Recognising neurological irAEs as part of overlapping immune-mediated spectra encourages earlier consultation of neurologists, tailored monitoring in high-risk patients, and collaborative care models between oncology and neurology. Adopting a spectrum-based approach, which reflects the overlapping neurological presentations observed, can further strengthen structured recognition and management of irNAE. Embedding these principles into future guidelines could meaningfully improve patient safety and outcomes as checkpoint inhibitors continue to expand across cancer care.
