Cellular Senescence and SASP in Bladder Ageing: Mechanisms and Emerging Interventions
Authors: Junfeng Yan, Danjie Zhang, Fan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBladder dysfunction is a prevalent age-related condition that severely impairs quality of life in older adults and commonly manifests as lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including frequency, urgency, incontinence, and nocturia. Cellular senescence, characterized by irreversible cell-cycle arrest accompanied by persistent metabolic activity, is increasingly recognized as a contributor to age-associated tissue degeneration. Senescent cells secrete a diverse array of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix-remodeling enzymes, collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which can disrupt tissue homeostasis through autocrine and paracrine signaling. Yet the bladder remains relatively understudied, and evidence linking cellular senescence and SASP to bladder dysfunction is fragmented and predominantly preclinical. Key uncertainties concern cell-specific SASP sources, causality, bladder-specific biomarkers, and validation in human tissues. To address this gap, this review critically integrates and evaluates bladder-specific evidence across ageing-related dysfunction, radiation-induced bladder injury, chemotherapy-induced cystitis, and interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome, while distinguishing direct mechanistic evidence from indirect or pathway-based associations. We further synthesize emerging senescence-targeted strategies, including senolytics and senomorphics, and highlight the context- and cell-dependent nature of therapeutic responses. By defining the current strength and limitations of the evidence and identifying key translational priorities, this review provides an integrated framework for understanding how cellular senescence and SASP may contribute to bladder dysfunction and for guiding the development of mechanism-based, biomarker-informed interventions.

Introduction

Bladder dysfunction is a multifactorial condition that frequently affects the elderly, manifesting as lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), such as urinary frequency, urgency, incontinence, and nocturia. Recent epidemiological studies have consistently shown that the prevalence of LUTS increases significantly with advancing ageing across diverse populations. The Japan Continence Survey (2023) reported LUTS in over 80% of adults aged ≥40, while a multinational Asian study showed prevalence rates exceeding 60% in those aged ≥40 and nearly 70% in individuals over 60. Similarly, in the United States, approximately 38% of women report LUTS-related bother, with symptom severity closely associated with age.1–3 Although ageing is a major contributing factor, LUTS also occur in patients with other pathological conditions, such as radiation cystitis and interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS).4 These symptoms are associated with substantial reductions in quality of life and impose a considerable burden on global healthcare systems.5,6 Effective and durable treatments remain limited, in part because the underlying mechanisms are incompletely understood.

The accumulation of senescent cells is closely associated with numerous age-related functional impairments and chronic diseases.7 Cellular senescence is induced by a variety of stressors, including telomere shortening, DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of tumor suppressor pathways, and chronic inflammation.8 As one of the hallmarks of ageing, senescence is characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest and the secretion of a complex mixture of pro-inflammatory factors collectively referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).9 Cellular senescence has been implicated in obesity-related metabolic disorders, chronic pulmonary diseases, cardiovascular ageing, and neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease.10–14 Attention has therefore shifted toward senescence in urogenital disorders, which are common in older adults. Previous studies have reported elevated senescent cell burden in kidney diseases and LUTS.15–18 Given the widespread involvement of senescence across multiple organ systems, understanding its specific impact on individual organs is crucial. Among the various organs affected by ageing, the bladder is particularly susceptible to functional deterioration due to its complex physiological role involving urine storage and periodic expulsion, which requires finely tuned neuromuscular coordination. Despite its clinical significance, the bladder has been relatively understudied in ageing research compared to other organ systems, and the specific contribution of SASP to bladder dysfunction remains unclear.

Current evidence regarding cellular senescence and the SASP in bladder dysfunction remains uneven across the preclinical and clinical domains. Preclinical studies, mainly using aged, metabolic, or experimentally injured rodent models, suggest that the accumulation of senescent cells and SASP-associated inflammatory signalling is accompanied by urothelial damage, fibrosis, and impaired bladder function. However, much of this evidence remains observational, and the cell-specific sources and causal contribution of the SASP have not been fully established. Clinical evidence is considerably more limited and largely indirect, consisting primarily of age-associated LUTS epidemiology and the detection of inflammatory mediators that overlap with canonical SASP components in bladder disorders. Direct validation in human bladder tissues, clinically applicable bladder-specific senescence biomarkers, and trials directly evaluating senescence-targeted therapies are still lacking. This imbalance highlights a substantial translational gap between experimental findings and human bladder disease.

Against this uneven evidence base, this review examines how cellular senescence and SASP may contribute to bladder dysfunction across ageing, radiation injury, chemotherapy-induced cystitis, and interstitial cystitis. It then considers senolytics, senomorphics, and other emerging interventions in light of the strength and limitations of bladder-specific evidence.

Methods

A targeted literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE and updated in August 2026. Search terms combined bladder-related concepts, including “bladder”, “urinary bladder”, “urothelium”, “detrusor”, “cystitis”, “interstitial cystitis”, and “bladder pain syndrome”, with terms related to cellular senescence and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), including “cellular senescence”, “SASP”, “p16”, “p21”, “SA-β-galactosidase”, “senolytic”, and “senomorphic”. English-language publications with abstracts available for review were considered. The titles and abstracts of retrieved publications were reviewed for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Reference lists of key original studies and relevant reviews were also examined to identify additional publications. Priority was given to original human, animal, ex vivo, organoid, and in vitro studies involving bladder tissue or bladder-derived cells. Studies focused exclusively on bladder cancer or tumor-cell senescence were excluded from the non-neoplastic bladder synthesis. Reviews and studies conducted in non-bladder tissues were used only when they provided relevant mechanistic or therapeutic context. For relevant studies, information on study design, experimental model or population, bladder tissue or cell type, senescence- and SASP-related markers, structural or functional bladder outcomes, interventions, and major limitations was considered. Evidence was interpreted according to bladder specificity and the extent of senescence/SASP validation. Because of substantial heterogeneity in study designs, experimental models, and outcome measures, the findings were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively. The reporting approach was informed by relevant items of the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews.19

Results of the Literature Review

The selected literature encompassed three principal domains: the biological mechanisms of cellular senescence and SASP; evidence linking senescence and SASP-associated processes to bladder ageing and dysfunction; and emerging strategies targeting senescent cells or their secretory phenotype. The bladder-specific evidence was derived predominantly from animal models, bladder-derived cells, ex vivo tissues, and organoid systems, whereas direct evidence from human bladder samples and clinical intervention studies remained limited. The available studies varied considerably in disease context, experimental design, senescence and SASP markers, interventions, and bladder-related outcomes. The synthesis therefore proceeds from core senescence biology to disease-specific bladder evidence and, finally, therapeutic implications.

Cellular Senescence and SASP

Cellular senescence is a highly conserved cellular stress response elicited by various stimuli, including telomere attrition, persistent DNA damage response (DDR), oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oncogene activation.20–25 A hallmark of senescence is its irreversible arrest of the cell cycle. Historically, the concept of cellular senescence was first described in cultured somatic cells undergoing serial passaging, a process termed replicative senescence.26 Subsequent studies have demonstrated that senescent cells can be detected in various tissues and across different stages of life in vivo.27,28 Transient induction of senescence plays beneficial physiological roles, including the preservation of tissue homeostasis, facilitation of wound healing and regeneration, and the suppression of malignant transformation.29–31 By contrast, chronic senescent-cell accumulation is associated with tissue ageing and a broad range of age-related diseases, including disorders of the genitourinary system.32,33

Interpreting these disease associations requires a brief account of the pathways that establish and maintain senescence. Two major tumour suppressor pathways are critically involved in the initiation and maintenance of cellular senescence: the p53/p21CIP1 and p16INK4A/pRb pathways. Upon persistent activation of the DNA damage response (DDR) triggered by cellular stressors, including oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, these signalling cascades inhibit the activity of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), resulting in hypophosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein (pRb). This, in turn, suppresses E2F-mediated transcription of pro-proliferative genes, thereby enforcing cell cycle arrest and the senescent phenotype.34 Accordingly, elevated expression of p53, p16INK4A, and p21CIP1 serves as a robust indicator of cellular senescence in both in vitro and in vivo contexts.

Cell-cycle arrest is only one component of the senescent phenotype; the SASP can also reshape surrounding tissue. Despite their stable cell cycle arrest, senescent cells remain metabolically active and secrete a diverse array of bioactive factors that reshape the local microenvironment and disrupt tissue homeostasis.35 Initial investigations into SASP centered on pro-inflammatory mediators, such as interleukins (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8), chemokines (eg, MCP-1, MCP-2), matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-3), growth-regulated oncogenes (GRO-α, -β, -γ), and insulin-like growth factor-binding proteins (IGFBPs).36–42 Subsequent studies have broadened the SASP profile to encompass both protein and non-protein signaling molecules, including coagulation factors, ceramides, bradykinin, extracellular matrix components, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and prostaglandins.43,44

The SASP exhibits pronounced heterogeneity that varies across cell types, tissue environments, and senescence-inducing stimuli. This contextual complexity renders the identification of universal biomarkers and conserved regulatory pathways particularly challenging.45 High-throughput proteomic analyses have begun to resolve this complexity by cataloguing soluble and exosomal SASP factors across cell types and senescence models. Several candidate biomarkers—such as GDF15, STC1, and SERPIN family members—were found to overlap with ageing-associated plasma proteins, offering valuable indicators for assessing senescent cell burden, stimulus origin, and tissue specificity.45 SASP composition also changes over time. Meta-analyses indicate that the secretome evolves during senescence progression.46 In late-stage senescence (>30 days), five proteins—PAI-1, vimentin, galectin-1, IGFBP4, and IGFBP7—consistently appear across diverse experimental models, suggesting their potential as conserved markers. Conversely, early-stage senescence (1–3 days) is marked by proteasomal dysfunction (eg, UCH deubiquitination, RUNX1 signaling). Metabolic shifts toward glycolysis (eg, GAPDH and PKM upregulation), coupled with enhanced inflammatory signaling (eg, IL-1/4/12/13, NF-κB), dominate the later phase, and stimulus-specific profiles (eg, extracellular matrix remodeling during replicative senescence) further reflect the contextual diversity of the SASP. These temporal and stimulus-specific patterns help explain why the SASP can support transient tissue repair yet promote chronic inflammation when it persists. This heterogeneity has also prompted efforts to improve senescent-cell detection and classification. Saul et al introduced the curated “SenMayo” set of 125 validated SASP-associated genes.47 Its performance has surpassed prior gene sets in tracking senescence progression and predicting responsiveness to senolytic therapy. The validity of SenMayo has been demonstrated in both human skeletal tissue samples and mouse models subjected to genetic or pharmacologic clearance of senescent cells. Additional gene sets, including CellAge and SeneQuest, have been proposed, but inter-dataset overlap remains low due to the high degree of heterogeneity among cell types and inducing stressors.48 A composite plasma-based biomarker score—known as the SASP index—has also been developed to quantify systemic senescence burden through weighted profiling of circulating factors.49 The limited overlap among these tools argues for multi-omic strategies that distinguish cell- and stimulus-specific signatures from more conserved senescence pathways. Such integration may improve biomarker sensitivity and clinical relevance.

Beyond their diagnostic utility, SASP components exert direct effects on the tissue microenvironment and actively participate in disease pathogenesis through diverse biological mechanisms. These secreted factors can reinforce the senescent state in an autocrine manner by sustaining growth arrest, or act on neighboring cells via paracrine signaling to induce secondary senescence and modulate immune responses—a phenomenon referred to as paracrine senescence.50 Through these autocrine and paracrine effects, the SASP links cell-intrinsic arrest to tissue-level responses.

The balance between repair and pathology depends on the duration and location of SASP activity. Transient, localised signalling can support tissue repair and regeneration. For instance, senescent fibroblasts at wound sites secrete platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA), which accelerates wound closure and facilitates post-injury skin healing.51 Similarly, SASP factors from senescent hepatic stellate cells exert anti-fibrotic effects and contribute to liver homeostasis.52,53 In contrast, persistent SASP accumulation in aged or chronically damaged tissues often drives pathological remodeling. In photoaged or intrinsically aged skin, SASP-mediated extracellular matrix degradation compromises elastin and collagen integrity, leading to structural deterioration.54,55 Muscle stem cells exposed to conditioned media from senescent fibroblasts exhibit reduced regenerative potential and upregulation of fibrotic markers, reflecting a shift toward tissue dysfunction.56 While cellular senescence acts as a tumor-suppressive mechanism, emerging evidence indicates that SASP can paradoxically promote tumorigenesis under specific conditions. In the prostate, senescent fibroblasts secrete matrix components and SASP factors that foster a tumor-permissive microenvironment,57 while also promoting epithelial dedifferentiation and tumor expansion both in vitro and in vivo.58 Co-culture studies further demonstrate that senescent fibroblasts can stimulate the proliferation of premalignant and malignant epithelial cells and promote tumor formation through secreted factors.59 These opposing effects complicate both causal interpretation and therapy. They also make cell type, tissue context, and timing essential when extrapolating general SASP biology to the bladder.

Cellular Senescence and SASP in Bladder Dysfunction

SASP in Bladder Ageing

Following the systemic impact of senescence on various tissues, the bladder is no exception. With advancing age, nearly all organ systems exhibit functional decline, and the progressive accumulation of ageing-associated phenotypes ultimately impairs organ integrity (Figure 1). Age-related deterioration in urine storage and coordinated voiding contributes to lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), a common syndrome in older adults of both sexes. LUTS are characterized by reduced bladder compliance, diminished detrusor contractility, and impaired neuromuscular coordination within the bladder wall, collectively increasing the risk of urinary retention and incontinence. Urodynamic studies have demonstrated that ageing is associated with decreased bladder capacity, increased occurrence of involuntary contractions, reduced urinary flow rate (particularly in men), diminished urethral pressure (notably in women), and elevated post-void residual volume.60 Consistent structural and functional alterations have also been documented across complementary rodent models of bladder ageing. Naturally aged female rats exhibit detrusor hypertrophy and altered voiding function,61 whereas Klotho hypomorphic mutant mice, a genetic premature-ageing model, show increased micturition frequency, reduced voided volume, and impaired neurogenic bladder contractility.62 Representative animal models used to investigate bladder ageing and senescence-associated changes are summarized in Table 1. Despite the high prevalence of bladder dysfunction in the elderly—often manifesting as detrusor underactivity and bladder wall fibrosis—the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood and have received limited investigation. Klee et al reported an age-dependent accumulation of senescent cells in the urothelium of Wistar rats.18 Moreover, ageing-related bladder dysfunction has been mechanistically linked to mitochondrial damage-induced activation of the NLRP3/IL-1β inflammasome, a process associated with increased collagen deposition, elevated p21 expression, and impaired bladder contractility, particularly in female rats.64 In diabetes-associated bladder dysfunction, senescence within the urothelium exacerbates SASP-driven inflammation and oxidative stress, potentially accelerating disease progression.18 Together, these observations link urothelial senescence and inflammatory signalling to age-related bladder dysfunction, but do not yet define the responsible cell populations or causal sequence.

A central mechanism contributing to SASP-driven chronic inflammation is the impaired clearance of senescent cells. Lysosomes, essential for degrading intracellular debris, damaged organelles, and post-mitotic waste, also play a critical role in regulating SASP accumulation and inflammatory resolution.69 Lysosomal dysfunction has been implicated in several ageing-related neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, where impaired degradation pathways contribute to pathogenic inflammation.70,71 Similar lysosomal abnormalities have been identified in the urothelial cells of aged bladders, including lysosomal accumulation and diminished acid phosphatase activity, suggesting compromised lysosomal function.63,65 Mitochondrial dysfunction, closely linked with lysosomal health, exacerbates oxidative stress, sustains DNA damage responses, and further amplifies SASP secretion via ROS-mediated signaling.72,73 At the same time, SASP-associated factors may amplify inflammation through innate immune pathways. Joshi et al demonstrated that senescent bladder epithelial cells exhibit increased expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-6, which in turn activate the caspase-1/IL-1β/gasdermin D (GsdmD) axis. This cascade ultimately results in pyroptosis-like epithelial injury and exfoliation, thereby compromising urothelial barrier integrity.65 Pharmacological intervention using D-mannose has been shown to suppress the expression of NLRP3, GsdmD, and IL-1β in bladder urothelial cells, reduce SASP-related urinary inflammatory cytokines, and attenuate epithelial shedding. These data connect inflammatory-pathway modulation with reduced epithelial injury, although they do not establish which effects are senescence- or SASP-dependent. D-mannose should therefore be viewed as a mechanistic probe and candidate uroprotective intervention rather than evidence that SASP targeting restores bladder function.

Organoid models add a tractable cellular layer to this evidence. Parab et al established mouse bladder epithelium-derived organoids from young and aged animals and showed that aged organoids recapitulated several features of urothelial ageing, including increased SA-β-galactosidase activity, DNA damage, oxidative stress, lysosomal and mitochondrial abnormalities, and distinct metabolic reprogramming. Supplementation with nicotinamide or D-mannose reduced oxidative stress and DNA damage and restored mitochondrial integrity in aged organoids, providing a tractable experimental platform for investigating metabolism-associated urothelial ageing and candidate interventions.74 However, because this study did not directly identify a cell-specific SASP or demonstrate that functional rescue depended on the elimination of senescent cells, it primarily strengthens the cellular ageing and metabolic-stress evidence rather than establishing SASP-mediated causality.

Importantly, recent evidence also indicates that cellular senescence in the bladder is heterogeneous and not uniformly detrimental. Al-Naggar et al identified polyploid superficial umbrella cells that expressed multiple senescence-associated features across the mouse lifespan. These cells were resistant to the senolytic combination of dasatinib and quercetin, and the authors proposed that their senescence-like state may contribute to urothelial integrity and maintenance of the urine–blood barrier. Moreover, dasatinib and quercetin did not reverse the cystometric alterations induced by a high-fat diet.66 These observations challenge a uniformly pathogenic interpretation of bladder senescence and suggest that the biological consequences and therapeutic susceptibility of senescent cells depend on cell identity, differentiation state, and the initiating stress.

Taken together, the available bladder-specific studies support an association between cellular senescence, SASP-associated inflammatory signalling, impaired cellular homeostasis, urothelial injury, and age-related bladder dysfunction. The study by Joshi et al provides particularly relevant evidence that modulation of ageing-associated cellular stress and SASP-related pathways can reduce urothelial inflammation and epithelial shedding. Nevertheless, the current evidence does not support a uniform model in which all senescent bladder cells are necessarily pathogenic or therapeutically expendable. Most studies remain preclinical, several rely on a limited set of senescence markers, and direct causal attribution of bladder functional decline to a defined SASP-producing cell population remains uncommon. Future studies should therefore combine multiparametric senescence profiling with spatial or single-cell identification of the responsible cell populations, longitudinal functional assessment, and cell type-specific genetic or pharmacological interventions. Such approaches will be required to distinguish maladaptive senescence from potentially homeostatic senescence and to define which bladder cell populations can be targeted safely.

SASP in Radiation- and Chemotherapy-Induced Bladder Injury

Radiotherapy remains an important component of pelvic cancer treatment but can cause acute and chronic bladder injury. Early manifestations include urinary frequency, dysuria, nocturia, and hematuria, whereas persistent or high-dose exposure may lead to fibrosis, reduced bladder capacity, and detrusor dysfunction.75 Radiation damages the urothelial barrier and promotes oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, thereby facilitating progressive tissue injury and extracellular-matrix remodelling.76 Earlier support for the involvement of cellular senescence and SASP in radiation cystitis was derived predominantly from studies of irradiated non-bladder tissues, in which persistent DNA damage, senescence-associated inflammation, and matrix remodelling were associated with chronic organ injury.77–80 More recently, direct bladder-specific evidence has emerged. Using a longitudinal mouse model of bladder-targeted irradiation, Mota et al characterised acute, latent, and chronic phases of radiation cystitis and identified persistent upregulation of Cdkn1a/p21 across disease progression. The latent phase was characterised by altered immune regulation, including reduced IL-27 and shifts in granulocyte- and lymphocyte-enriched populations, whereas the chronic phase showed increased SASP-associated inflammatory proteins and matrix-remodelling mediators that coincided with progressive structural and functional bladder deterioration.68

This study substantially strengthens the biological link between radiation-induced senescence-associated signalling, immune dysregulation, SASP-associated inflammation, and chronic bladder remodelling. Nevertheless, persistent p21 expression and the detection of SASP-overlapping mediators do not, by themselves, establish the identity of the responsible senescent cell populations or prove that senescence is the causal driver of functional decline. Cell type-specific senescence mapping and interventions that selectively eliminate senescent cells or suppress their SASP will be required to determine whether this pathway is necessary for the progression of radiation cystitis.

Intravesical chemotherapy can cause chemical cystitis, characterized by urinary urgency, frequency, dysuria, hematuria, and pelvic discomfort.81,82 A proposed mechanism involves disruption of the glycosaminoglycan layer, increased urothelial permeability, and subsequent inflammatory responses,83 providing a biological overlap with other chronic inflammatory bladder disorders such as IC/BPS. Although direct evidence linking chemotherapy-induced cystitis to cellular senescence or a senescent-cell-derived SASP is currently lacking, this barrier–inflammation phenotype may provide a relevant context in which senescence-associated mechanisms warrant further investigation. GAG-replenishment strategies have shown variable clinical effects,84,85 while a multicomponent formulation containing quercetin was associated with symptomatic improvement after intravesical chemotherapy.86 These observations do not establish a senolytic or SASP-mediated mechanism, but they highlight the potential value of investigating senescence-associated inflammatory pathways as future therapeutic targets in chemical cystitis.

The evidence for cellular senescence and SASP differs substantially between radiation- and chemotherapy-induced bladder injury. Direct preclinical evidence now associates radiation cystitis with persistent senescence-related signalling, immune dysregulation, SASP-associated inflammation, extracellular matrix remodelling, and bladder functional decline. By contrast, evidence for cellular senescence or SASP in chemotherapy-induced cystitis remains predominantly indirect. Although a quercetin-containing multicomponent formulation was associated with symptom improvement after intravesical chemotherapy, this clinical observation does not demonstrate that the effects were mediated by senolytic activity or SASP suppression. Radiation and chemotherapy-induced bladder injury should therefore be presented as biologically related but evidentially distinct contexts, with causal and clinical validation remaining necessary in both settings.

SASP in Interstitial Cystitis

Interstitial cystitis (IC), also referred to as hypersensitive bladder (HSB), is a chronic non-infectious inflammatory disorder of the bladder, clinically characterized by urinary urgency, frequency, and chronic pelvic pain or discomfort.87 A population-based cohort study from Denmark reported the diagnostic age range for IC/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) to span 16–88 years, with a median age of 53 years.88 Similarly, East Asian studies report comparable prevalence and diagnostic age ranges, with Japan, South Korea, and China showing rates between 0.027% and 100 per 100,000, and a median onset age of 50–55 years—slightly younger in China (~45 years), possibly due to regional diagnostic differences.89 Although the aetiology of IC remains uncertain and treatment options are limited, inflammatory mediators are prominent in the disease microenvironment. Direct evidence establishing cellular senescence or a senescent-cell-derived SASP as a causal mechanism in IC/BPS remains lacking. Several inflammatory mediators elevated in the urine or bladder tissue of patients with IC/BPS, including IL-1β, IL-8, MCP-1, and CXCL10,90,91 overlap with components commonly reported in the SASP, and similar inflammatory profiles have been reproduced in experimental models.92 However, these mediators are not specific to cellular senescence and may also be produced by activated immune cells, injured urothelial cells, or other non-senescent cell populations.

Recent cell-model studies have provided additional evidence that senescence-associated signalling pathways may intersect with IC/BPS pathology. Wang et al showed that a Cdk1/p53/p21 feedback loop regulated urothelial permeability and was accompanied by increased IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in an IC/BPS cell model.93 A subsequent study implicated the same regulatory loop in altered autophagy and inflammatory signalling, with combined p21 silencing and Cdk1 inhibition increasing autophagic activity and reducing inflammatory mediator release.94 These findings identify p53/p21-associated cell-cycle and stress-response pathways as potentially relevant to IC/BPS. Nevertheless, neither study established stable and irreversible cell-cycle arrest using multiple complementary senescence markers, nor did either demonstrate that the inflammatory mediators originated specifically from senescent cells. They should therefore be interpreted as senescence-associated pathway evidence rather than direct evidence of a pathogenic SASP.

Pharmacological observations require similar caution. Apigenin ameliorated IC-like abnormalities in rats while reducing IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β1, and iNOS and increasing antioxidant activity.95 Although apigenin has demonstrated senescence-modulating effects in other organ systems,96,97 its therapeutic effects in the IC model cannot be attributed specifically to the suppression of cellular senescence or SASP without direct mechanistic validation.

Accordingly, the available evidence supports only a hypothesis-generating association between SASP-related inflammatory signalling and IC/BPS, rather than a demonstrated causal relationship. Future studies should combine human bladder tissue analysis with multiple senescence markers, spatial identification of SASP-producing cell populations, and parallel assessment of urinary SASP candidates. Cell type-specific experimental models and interventions that selectively eliminate senescent cells or suppress SASP production will also be required to determine whether cellular senescence is a driver of IC/BPS or merely accompanies chronic bladder inflammation.(Figure 2). Targeting SASP to Restore Bladder Function

The heterogeneity described above has direct implications for senotherapy. Current pharmacological approaches fall into two broad categories: senolytics, which selectively induce apoptosis in senescent cells to reduce their tissue burden, and senomorphics, which modulate SASP expression and pro-inflammatory signalling without killing the cells98 (Figure 3). In 2015, Kirkland et al first introduced a senolytic combination of dasatinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) and quercetin (a flavonoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties), which effectively reduced senescent cell burden and SASP components in both naturally aged and progeroid mice.99 Quercetin alone has demonstrated therapeutic effects in bladder disease models, alleviating inflammation in IC by decreasing MPO, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and inhibiting mast cell degranulation and tissue injury.100 Additionally, it protects against bladder ischemia–reperfusion injury by attenuating oxidative stress and apoptosis.101 Bladder-specific studies of dasatinib plus quercetin, however, do not show uniform senolytic efficacy. Al-Naggar et al reported that superficial polyploid umbrella cells expressing multiple senescence-associated features were resistant to dasatinib and quercetin. The treatment also failed to reverse high-fat-diet-induced cystometric abnormalities, suggesting that either these cells were not the principal drivers of dysfunction or that their senescence phenotype was not susceptible to this senolytic regimen.66 In a separate study of 25-month-old male and female Diversity Outbred mice, Kasturi et al identified sex-specific differences in protein-homeostasis pathways within the aged bladder. Females exhibited greater activation of the PERK arm of the unfolded protein response, whereas males showed more efficient endoplasmic-reticulum-associated degradation and autophagic flux. Dasatinib and quercetin did not broadly alter endoplasmic reticulum stress or autophagy markers but selectively increased endoplasmic-reticulum-associated degradation markers, uroplakin expression, and urothelial thickness in aged females.67 These findings suggest a sex- and pathway-dependent effect on urothelial homeostasis rather than a general reversal of bladder ageing.

Collectively, the available bladder studies indicate that responses to dasatinib and quercetin depend on the senescent cell type, biological sex, tissue context, and outcome assessed. Neither study established widespread clearance of senescent bladder cells or demonstrated consistent restoration of LUTS-related functional outcomes. Future studies should therefore include cell type-specific measures of senescent-cell depletion, both sexes, cystometric and voiding outcomes, and assessment of potential adverse effects on differentiated urothelial cells with homeostatic senescence-like features.

Fisetin has demonstrated senolytic activity in ageing models and protective effects in diabetes-associated renal injury.102 Other senolytic compounds include inhibitors of the Bcl-2 family, p53, and HSP90 proteins.103 Notably, the Bcl-2 inhibitor ABT-263 significantly reduced senescent cell burden in spinal cord injury models, leading to diminished scarring, suppressed inflammation, and improved bladder function.104 While senolytics represent a rapidly evolving therapeutic class, their application in age-related LUTS remains largely unexplored.

Mechanistic overlap complicates this classification. Here, agents that induce senescent-cell death remain classed as senolytics, whereas those that primarily suppress SASP secretion without affecting cell viability are discussed as senomorphics. Senomorphics shift the therapeutic emphasis from cell elimination to SASP suppression. These agents primarily target key pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway.105 In radiation cystitis models, the NF-κB inhibitor thalidomide has been shown to attenuate chronic bladder inflammation by downregulating SASP-related cytokines and restoring epithelial barrier integrity via the upregulation of E-cadherin and β-catenin.106,107 Similarly, metformin attenuated oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in a rat model of bladder ischemia–reperfusion injury.108 A range of natural compounds—including resveratrol, apigenin, kaempferol, curcumol, boswellic acid, and caftaric acid—also exhibit SASP-modulatory activity, and are key bioactive constituents in various traditional medicinal formulations.95,109–111 Among these pathways, the JAK/STAT cascade has garnered particular attention. As a conserved signaling axis involved in immunity, inflammation, and cellular homeostasis, it is prominently activated in senescent cells and regulates SASP expression. In the context of interstitial cystitis, JAK/STAT activation has been linked to heightened inflammatory signaling and urothelial dysfunction.112,113 Pharmacologic inhibition of JAK/STAT, such as with ruxolitinib (a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor), significantly reduces systemic inflammation and improves metabolic and functional outcomes in senescent animal models.114–116 Additionally, this pathway plays a role in fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition in the urethra and prostate, potentially contributing to LUTS development in the elderly.117 These findings collectively underscore the promise of JAK/STAT-targeted senomorphics as a therapeutic strategy to modulate SASP and mitigate lower urinary tract dysfunction associated with ageing.

Taken together, the heterogeneous therapeutic responses described above raise several challenges for clinical translation. The lack of bladder-specific biomarkers makes it difficult to distinguish pathogenic senescent cells from potentially homeostatic senescence-like populations and to identify appropriate therapeutic targets and treatment windows. In addition, systemic senolysis or prolonged suppression of SASP-related pathways may cause off-target effects or interfere with physiological immune and tissue-repair functions. Finally, most bladder-specific evidence remains preclinical, and clinically meaningful efficacy, optimal treatment regimens, and long-term safety remain to be established in human studies.

Beyond senolytics and senomorphics, novel therapeutic strategies have begun to explore the modulation of cellular senescence through immunological and physical modalities. These emerging approaches aim not only to complement pharmacologic interventions but also to enhance the selective clearance or suppression of senescent cells via immune activation or mechanical stimulation, thereby offering additional avenues for senescence-targeted therapy.

Immunosenescence—characterized by functional decline and remodeling of the immune system—is a hallmark of the ageing process and is often accompanied by chronic, low-grade inflammation, a phenomenon termed “inflammaging”.118 Recent studies have shown that senescent cells upregulate ligands for several inhibitory immune checkpoint receptors. Notably, increased expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) has been consistently observed in various senescent cell types both in vitro and in vivo.119,120 This upregulation suppresses immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells, fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and promotes fibrosis and tissue injury. Together, these processes establish a positive feedback loop of “senescence–immune evasion–chronic inflammation” that accelerates tissue ageing and the progression of age-related diseases.121 To counteract this, a range of immunotherapeutic strategies has been developed to selectively identify and eliminate senescent cells. Engineered T cells—such as uPAR-CAR T and NKG2D-CAR T cells—have demonstrated the ability to specifically recognize and eradicate senescent cells. Similarly, senescence-targeted vaccines, including those against CD153 and GPNMB, have shown encouraging preclinical efficacy. Moreover, immune checkpoint blockade agents (e.g, anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies) have been proposed to restore T-cell surveillance and facilitate senescent cell clearance.122,123 Direct evidence in bladder ageing is limited, so selectivity in other tissues should not be assumed to translate into bladder benefit. Safety, delivery, and clinically relevant functional outcomes remain the immediate tests.

A different line of investigation concerns non-pharmacological modalities such as low-intensity extracorporeal shock wave therapy (LiESWT). Preclinical studies have shown that LiESWT significantly attenuates lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation and fibrosis in the bladder wall of rodent models, reduces tactile allodynia, and improves overall bladder function.124 These effects are thought to be mediated through enhanced angiogenesis, modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways, and promotion of tissue remodeling. Whether these effects involve senescence-specific pathways remains uncertain, and clinical efficacy, treatment parameters, and safety have not been established in older patients with bladder dysfunction.

Immune-based and physical modalities therefore broaden the therapeutic landscape without yet resolving the central questions of mechanism, selectivity, and safety. These limitations carry directly into the clinical priorities outlined below.

Conclusion

Cellular senescence and the SASP represent a promising but not yet clinically validated mechanistic axis in bladder ageing and dysfunction. Current bladder-specific evidence is strongest in ageing-related dysfunction and radiation-induced bladder injury, whereas the contribution of senescence to chemotherapy-induced cystitis and IC/BPS remains largely indirect or pathway-based. Future research should therefore move beyond demonstrating the presence of senescence-associated markers and establish which bladder cell populations undergo maladaptive versus potentially homeostatic senescence, when these states emerge during disease progression, and which cell-specific SASP programmes directly drive urothelial barrier dysfunction, fibrosis, inflammation, and functional decline. Addressing these questions will require multiparametric senescence profiling integrated with single-cell or spatial approaches, longitudinal functional assessment, and validation in human bladder tissues. Parallel studies of urinary and tissue-derived SASP signatures will also be important for developing bladder-specific biomarkers that can identify disease-relevant senescence, monitor its evolution, and potentially stratify patients for senescence-targeted interventions.

From a clinical perspective, the immediate priority is not the routine application of senotherapies, but the rigorous translation of mechanistic findings into safe and biomarker-guided interventions. Senolytics, senomorphics, immunomodulatory strategies, and other emerging approaches should be evaluated in well-characterised and sex-inclusive models and, ultimately, in carefully designed human studies incorporating clinically meaningful LUTS, cystometric and voiding outcomes, treatment durability, and long-term safety. Particular attention should be paid to defining therapeutic windows and avoiding indiscriminate elimination of senescent cells that may retain physiological roles in urothelial homeostasis, as well as off-target consequences of systemic senolysis or prolonged SASP suppression. In treatment-related bladder injury, senescence-targeted approaches must additionally demonstrate that bladder protection can be achieved without compromising oncological efficacy. Establishing causal biomarkers, responsive cell populations, and clinically relevant therapeutic windows will be essential for determining whether targeting senescence can move the management of age-related LUTS and chronic bladder injury from predominantly symptom-directed treatment toward mechanism-based and precision-guided intervention.
