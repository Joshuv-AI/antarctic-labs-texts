NBI With Optical Magnification Shows Good Interobserver Agreement in Diagnosing H. Pylori Gastritis
Authors: Vijosh V. Kumar, K. G. Sabu, P. Javed, K. V. Vivek Kumar, Jaseem Ansari, Kavitha Rangan, Adila A. K. Parveen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo study the interobserver agreement for diagnosing Helicobacter pylori gastritis using narrow band imaging (NBI) with magnification.MethodsThis prospective study recruited patients who underwent gastroscopy for dyspepsia in the Department of Gastroenterology, Aster MIMS Hospital, Kannur.361 patients were included in the study. The gastroscopy was performed using high‐definition white light endoscopy (WLE) and NBI with magnification. Histopathology and rapid urease test were used to detect HP infection. Endoscopy videos were analyzed by three trained endoscopists who were blinded to each other. NBI patterns were classified into four types (Types 1, 2A, 2B, and 3). Interobserver variability was examined using Kappa Statistics.Results164 of 361 patients had HP infection (45.42%). Of 361 people, 199 had Type 1 (55.12%), 54 Type 2a (14.95%), 65 Type 2b (18%), and 43 Type 3 cases (11.91%). There was good interobserver agreement with a kappa value of 0.730 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.693–0.768). 87% of HP‐negative patients had Type 1 NBI pattern. 79.5% of type 2A and 89.2% of Type 2b pattern were HP positive. The Type 1 pattern identified normal gastric mucosa with 84.75% sensitivity, 88.32% specificity and 87.4% negative predictive value. Type 2 and Type 3 NBI patterns had high positive predictive value and specificity for HP infection.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates excellent interobserver agreement among experienced endoscopists in using NBI to identify gastric mucosal patterns associated with HP infection. While biopsies remain essential for comprehensive evaluation of gastric pathologies, our findings suggest that NBI, with appropriate training and validation, may have the potential to reduce the need for biopsies in specific cases where the primary concern is HP infection. The good level of interobserver agreement seen in our study is encouraging and suggests that NBI has the potential to be a reliable tool for diagnosing HP infection.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection is now generally acknowledged as primary risk factor for peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer [1, 2]. In India, data on the prevalence of HP infection in the general population are scarce; most reports are hospital‐based [3]. According to available data, the prevalence of HP in the Indian population ranges between 30% and 50% [4, 5, 6]. HP infection can currently be diagnosed using a rapid urease test (RUT), histology, urea breath test (UBT), or stool antigen test [7]. RUT is the most widely used modality as it is easily available. Histopathology, while regarded as the gold standard, is infrequently employed. All these tests except UBT and stool antigen test are invasive modes of investigation.

Narrow‐band imaging (NBI) is an endoscopic imaging technique that enables in‐depth visualization of the mucosal microscopic structure and capillaries in the superficial mucosal layer [8]. When used in conjunction with magnifying endoscopy, NBI allows for clear visualization of microscopic structures of the superficial mucosa and their capillary patterns.

A regular arrangement of collecting venules (RACs), which can be visualized using magnifying NBI is considered a hallmark of normal gastric mucosa and is often absent in the presence of HP infection [9]. To predict histological chronic gastritis, Tahara et al. observed the gastric corpus via magnified NBI endoscopy, which enabled visualization of the micro mucosal pattern. The normal pattern consists of a honeycomb‐like subepithelial capillary network (SECN) and the presence of RAC. Typically, HP‐induced gastritis is characterized by polygonal, swollen mucosa with enlarged crypt openings. Magnifying endoscopy has sensitivities and specificities for HP infection ranging from 93.8% to 100% and 82.2% to 96.2%, respectively [10]. Anagnostopoulos et al. described four distinct NBI patterns. Type 1 was associated with normal mucosa, Types 2 and 3 with HP infection, and Type 4 with the presence of gastric atrophy. Their research demonstrated a high degree of sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value [11].

In this prospective, longitudinal study we investigated the frequency of gastric mucosal patterns using magnifying NBI endoscopy in participants with and without HP infection, as well as their relationship to biopsy status and rapid urease test results.

High‐Definition Endoscopy With Optical Magnification and NBI System

High‐resolution endoscopes typically magnify images 30–35 times. Zoom endoscopes utilize a movable lens within the endoscope tip that allows for adjustment of the focal length. This optical zoom function brings the target area closer while maintaining high image resolution, unlike digital zoom which simply enlarges the image. While conventional endoscopes can achieve 1.5× to 2× electrical magnification, zoom endoscopes can magnify up to 150 times, depending on the display size. In this study, we used Olympus (Olympus EVIS X1‐EZ 1500, Olympus Medical Systems Corp, Tokyo, Japan) which provides high‐resolution imaging with optical zoom capability up to 125× magnification [12]. This, combined with the enhanced visualization provided by NBI, allowed for detailed examination of the microvasculature and mucosal patterns of the stomach [12, 13].

Endoscopist and Interobserver Variability Assessment

We initially performed a standardized evaluation of the entire stomach using conventional white light endoscopy to exclude obvious lesions. We then carefully scanned the entire gastric mucosa of the antrum and body using NBI to identify visible lesions and abnormal patterns.

Video recordings of these examinations were captured for subsequent analysis. The video recordings were analyzed by three experienced endoscopists who were blinded to each other's observations and to the RUT and histopathology results. These endoscopists had experience of 3, 3, and 5 years, respectively, in image enhanced endoscopy. Our data analyst compiled and anonymized the video recordings, removing any identifying information or clinical data, before distributing them to the endoscopists for independent review. The anonymized video recordings were randomly distributed to the three endoscopists. Each endoscopist independently reviewed and categorized the NBI patterns observed in the videos. The endoscopists were given 2 weeks to review and analyze the video recordings.

These endoscopists were trained to identify various NBI patterns with a minimum of 3 years of experience using NBI. Prior to the study, they were informed of the specific NBI patterns and classifications to be employed, as described by Tahara et al. [10, 14] NBI patterns were divided into “normal” and “abnormal” mucosal patterns.

Type 1 NBI, or the normal pattern, has a uniform appearance with a regular arrangement of collecting venules (RAC), a normal gastric pit pattern, and no visible signs of inflammation (Figure 1).

Type 2 and Type 3 patterns are considered abnormal. Type 2 is distinguished by its poorly defined vascular pattern, dilated pits, and mild to moderate inflammation. We further subdivided Type 2 into Types 2A and 2B. Type 2A (Figure 2) is characterized by an edematous polygonal mucosal surface, a poorly defined vascular pattern, and a dilated gastric pit pattern. Type 2B (Figure 3) is characterized by an edematous polygonal mucosal surface, a poorly defined vascular pattern with hyperpigmented areas surrounding dilated gastric pits, and moderate inflammation. Type 3 (Figure 4) pattern is defined by variably elongated gastric pits, a poorly defined vascular pattern, and severe inflammation. Diagnosis of H. pylori Infection

If histopathological examination revealed HP bacteria (defined as the presence of at least one HP organism in any biopsy specimen under Giemsa stain) or if a positive RUT resulted in a color change from yellow to pink within 20 min, indicating urease activity, we made a diagnosis of HP infection. We used RUT Dry Test kits manufactured by a local manufacturer (Gastro Cure Systems), following the manufacturer's instructions. We took five biopsy samples in accordance with the Sydney protocol and sent them for histological analysis. We used two additional samples for on‐site RUT. We processed the samples for histological analysis after placing them in a 10% formalin solution. We used both hematoxylin and eosin and Giemsa stains to identify HP.

An in‐house pathologist, blinded to the endoscopic findings, evaluated all biopsy specimens. We used updated Sydney classification for histological examination [15]. We graded the variables including HP, neutrophils, mononuclear cells, glandular atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia as absent, mild, moderate, and marked. Increased numbers of mononuclear cells (lymphocytes and plasma cells) led to the diagnosis of chronic gastritis. We defined intestinal metaplasia as the replacement gastric mucosa by metaplastic columnar absorptive cells and goblet cells with intestinal morphologic features, both along the surface epithelium and in rudimentary glands [16, 17].

Data Collection, Measurement of Outcomes, and Statistical Analysis

A research assistant collected data using standardized data collection forms. These forms included information obtained from patient interviews, medical records, endoscopic procedure reports, and pathology reports. We recorded and transferred patient demographics, endoscopy findings (NBI patterns), and biopsy and histology reports to a secure, password‐protected computerized database. We calculated the sample size using Cochran's formula, assuming an estimated 50% prevalence for HP infection, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. We analyzed the data using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Depending on the data distribution, we summarized continuous data as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range. We summarized categorical variables using frequency and percentages. We used Fleiss' kappa to assess interobserver agreement for the categorization of gastric mucosal patterns using NBI. Each endoscopist independently categorized the NBI patterns for all participants. We then compared these categorizations to calculate the Fleiss' kappa. We used the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate, to compare the distribution of NBI patterns between participants with and without HP infection. To assess the diagnostic performance of each NBI pattern for identifying HP infection, we constructed 2 × 2 contingency tables comparing each pattern with the gold standard diagnosis (histology and RUT). We then calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value for each pattern. For all tests, a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnosis of H. Pylori Infection

The histopathological analysis revealed a positivity rate of 20% (73 out of 361), with 15% (54 out of 361) exhibiting HP in both the antrum and body of the stomach. Similarly, RUT was positive in 157 cases (43.5%). Histopathology found 16% of HP in the antrum and 18% in the body. If either biopsies or RUTs were initially positive, we diagnosed our subjects with HP infection. We detected HP infection in 45% of 361 samples (164 out of 361).

Association Between NBI and H. pylori Infection

There is a positive and statistically significant correlation between NBI patterns and HP infection status. 87.4% of HP‐negative patients exhibited Type 1 NBI pattern. Type 2 and Type 3 patterns were more prevalent among HP cases. Patterns of Type 2A were observed in 79.6% of HP infections. Patterns of Type 2B were observed in 89.2% of HP infections. Discussion

Changes in the endoscopic appearance of the stomach in relation to HP infection are the subject of ongoing research, and several distinguishing characteristics have been identified that can predict naive, positive, and eradicated HP status [14]. Although more research is required in this area, these characteristics can also be indicative of precancerous lesions [18].

However, the correlation between abnormal NBI patterns and HP infection has received only limited research. Several studies have discussed NBI patterns, but none have validated them in a sizable cohort or investigated interobserver variability.

Yagi et al. first reported the association between RAC and normal gastric mucosa [9]. Their pioneering work demonstrated that the presence of RAC, as visualized by magnifying endoscopy, is indicative of a healthy gastric mucosa free from HP infection. Glover et al. [14] subsequently corroborated this observation.

Our findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating the utility of magnifying NBI for predicting HP infection and assessing gastric mucosal changes. The study by Tahara et al. [10], which included 106 patients, found that NBI patterns correlated with HP infections, histological severity of gastritis, and the presence of intestinal metaplasia. They used magnifying NBI to visualize gastric mucosal patterns and classified them into four distinct types. Their study reported HP infection‐positive ratios of 7.5%, 92.9%, 94.5%, and 66.7% for normal and Types 1, 2, and 3 patterns, respectively. We attribute the differences in these ratios to advancements in magnification technology, including higher resolution endoscopes and improved image processing, as well as our larger sample size. Tahara et al. also reported a sensitivity of 95.2% and specificity of 82.2% for Types 1, 2, and 3 in detecting HP positivity, which is comparable to our results. However, our study builds on that one by looking at agreement between multiple endoscopists, using a modified Tahara classification with a Type 2 subdivision, and studying a larger, more clearly defined group of people.

The study by Anagnostopoulos et al. [11] found that a four‐type NBI classification could effectively differentiate between normal mucosa and HP‐associated gastritis. The appearance of the SECN, collecting venules, and gastric pits served as the basis for this classification [11]. They reported a high sensitivity (92.7%) and specificity (100%) for the Type 1 pattern in predicting normal gastric mucosa. Similarly, Types 2 and 3 patterns had a 100% sensitivity and 92.7% specificity for predicting HP infection. While their study used a four‐type classification, our study employed a modified Tahara classification with three main types and a further subdivision of Type 2. Our study's high interobserver agreement may have resulted from this modification, which enabled a more detailed characterization of mucosal patterns. However, the fact that our sample size was 361 patients, as opposed to 95 patients in the Anagnostopoulos study, enhances the significance of our findings. The study by Anagnostopoulos et al. found kappa values of 0.864 for agreement between observers and 0.913 for agreement within observers when predicting normal gastric mucosa, HP gastritis, and gastric atrophy. These kappa values are comparable to our findings; we observed a Fleiss kappa of 0.730 for interobserver agreement in categorizing gastric mucosal patterns. This consistency across studies further supports the notion that NBI, with proper training, can be a reliable tool for assessing gastric mucosa. Both our study and previous research have observed high interobserver agreement, which is crucial for the clinical application of NBI.

We found that the NBI pattern has a significant correlation with the HP infection status (p < 0.001). There was a negative correlation between the type 1 pattern and HP infection, with 87.43% of Type 1 NBI patients lacking HP infection. We found a significant association between HP infection and the presence of a Type 2 or Type 3 NBI pattern, with 84.8% of HP‐positive patients exhibiting the Type 2 pattern and 88.4% of HP‐infected patients displaying the Type 3 NBI pattern. The Type 2B pattern was found to be associated with HP infection in 89.2% of cases. The Type 2 and Type 3 patterns demonstrated high specificity and positive predictive value for HP infection detection. The main strength of this study is that all the NBI patterns showed a satisfactory level of interobserver agreement. This study did not assess intra‐observer variability. The future of endoscopy will involve the use of artificial intelligence, such as deep‐layer convolutional networks. The fact that magnifying NBI can diagnose HP with an accuracy of more than 80% suggests that AI could improve this even further, reaching 90% or more. This paper describes a new concept of using NBI to rule out HP infection, as the diagnosis of HP during endoscopy currently requires a biopsy or RUT. Main limitation of our study is its single‐center design. Further large‐scale, multicentric studies are needed to validate these findings and establish the role of NBI in routine clinical practice. NBI magnification is readily available as a built‐in accessory in most modern endoscopes. The financial implications of using NBI for HP detection are similar to those of classifying colonic polyps, as it doesn't require any additional equipment or disposables. In our experience, the additional time required for the NBI examination is minimal, especially with increasing familiarity with the technique. However, adequate training in NBI interpretation is essential for accurate and consistent diagnoses.
