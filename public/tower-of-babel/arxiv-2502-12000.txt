The Complexity of Dynamic LZ77 is Θ ~ ​ ( n 2 / 3 ) \tilde{\Theta}(n^{2/3})
Itai Boneh, Shay Golan, Matan Kraus

Abstract
The Lempel-Ziv 77 (LZ77) factorization is a fundamental compression scheme widely used in text processing and data compression. In this work, we investigate the time complexity of maintaining the LZ77 factorization of a dynamic string. By establishing matching upper and lower bounds, we fully characterize the complexity of this problem. We present an algorithm that efficiently maintains the LZ77 factorization of a string S S undergoing edit operations, including character substitutions, insertions, and deletions. Our data structure can be constructed in O ~ ​ ( n ) \tilde{O}(n) time for an initial string of length n n and supports updates in O ~ ​ ( n 2 / 3 ) \tilde{O}(n^{2/3}) time, where n n is the current length of S S . Additionally, we prove that no algorithm can achieve an update time of O ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 − ε ) O(n^{2/3-\varepsilon}) unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis fails. This lower bound holds even in the restricted setting where only substitutions are allowed and only the length of the LZ77 factorization is maintained.

Introduction

Lempel-Ziv 77 (
LZ77
)
[
ZL77
]
is one of the most powerful and widely used compression techniques, forming the backbone of formats like gzip and PNG.
The

S

Both types of phrases can be encoded by at most two integers, allowing the compression size of

In practice,
LZ77

[
Mah
]
.
Its influence spans a diverse range of file formats, such as PNG, PDF, and ZIP, highlighting its versatility and efficiency.
Moreover,
LZ77
plays a crucial role in modern web infrastructure, being embedded in virtually all contemporary web browsers and servers
[
AFF+19
]
.

In this work, we investigate the maintenance of the

1.2
Technical Overview

We provide a high-level discussion of the algorithm of
Theorem

1.2
and the lower bound of
Theorem

1.3
.
For this overview, we discuss only the task of reporting
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
after each update, focusing on substitution updates.
Even for this restricted problem, all the machinery of our general algorithm is required (and this is also the setting of the lower bound).

An important concept in many
LZ77
algorithms (e.g.,
[
FHSX03
,
CI08
,
CIS08
,
CII+09
,
KKP13b
,
BKKP16
,
CC19
,
Ell23
,
BCR24
,
KK24
]
) is the Longest Previous Factor (
\mathsf{LPF}
).
In a string
S
, the longest previous factor of an index
i
, denoted
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)
, is the maximum
\ell
such that
S[i..i+\ell)
is not the leftmost occurrence of itself in
S
.
Formally,

\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)=\max\{\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)\mid j\in[1..i)\}

where
\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)
is the length of the longest common prefix of
S[i..n]
and
S[j..n]
.
Notice that
r_{i}=i+\max\{\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i),1\}
is exactly the starting point of the
(x+1)
th phrase in
\text{LZ77}(S)
if the
x
th phrase starts at index
i
(and
S[i..r_{i})
is a phrase in the
LZ77
factorization).
A related useful definition is
\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(i)
, which is the rightmost index
j\in[1..i)
that is a previous occurrence of
S[i..r_{i})
(hence serving as a witness for
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)
).
Following the approach of the semi-dynamic
LZ77
algorithm
[
BCR24
]
, we employ the concept of the
\mathsf{LPF}
-tree.
The
\mathsf{LPF}
-tree of a string
S
, denoted
\mathcal{T}_{S}
, has the indices of
S
as nodes, where the parent of
i
in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
is
r_{i}
.
It is easy to see that the depth of node
1
in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
is exactly
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
.
Thus, our task reduces to maintaining
\mathcal{T}_{S}
while supporting queries for the depth of a node.
We achieve this in
\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
time by carefully analyzing how the values of
r_{i}
change when an update is applied to
S
.

We define four relevant types of indices in
S
:
super-light
,
light
,
L
-heavy
, and
R
-heavy
.
We show that any index
i
that does not belong to any of these types necessarily has its
r_{i}
unchanged as a result of the update, and therefore retains the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
before and after the update.
We then provide separate algorithms for updating the parents of indices of each type, with a single algorithm handling both
L
-heavy and
R
-heavy indices.

Characterizing indices whose
\mathsf{LPF}
values change after an update.

In the following paragraphs, we introduce four types of indices that, together, cover all indices that change their
\mathsf{LPF}
value as a result of the update. We emphasize that, for now, we are not concerned with the task of finding or even computing the new
\mathsf{LPF}
values of these indices. Instead, we focus solely on proving that these sets indeed cover all possible indices
i
such that
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)\neq\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(i)

First, let us assume that
S[z]
is being substituted, resulting in a new string
S^{\prime}
.
Let us fix an integer parameter
m
(to be set later to
n^{1/3}
), and let
i
be an index in
S
.
We focus on characterizing the set of indices whose
\mathsf{LPF}
value is reduced as a result of the update, i.e.,
\{i\mid\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)>\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(i)\}
(the characterization of the indices whose

The initial observation needed to characterize this set is that the update at
z
must have disrupted the maximal equality that led to the
\mathsf{LPF}
of

in
S
.
Formally, let
j=\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(i)
.
The value
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)
arises from the equality
S[i..i+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i))=S[j..j+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i))

or

The types we define correspond to these cases, and also roughly correspond to the distance between

and
z

Super light indices are simply defined as the indices

Let us consider a non-super light index

Notice that since

In particular, this means that

Importantly, both occurrences of

We use the occurrences of

This gives rise to the following definition.

Updating super-light and light indices.

We proceed to show how to treat each type of indices, and properly update their parents in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
efficiently.
We use top trees to represent
\mathcal{T}_{S}
throughout the algorithm.
Similarly to
[
BCR24
]
, we represent the
\mathsf{LPF}
-tree using dynamic trees in a manner that allows us to query for the depth of a node in
\tilde{O}(1)
time and apply link-cut updates in
\tilde{O}(1)
time.
This representation also allows for ”interval” updates.
That is, given an interval of integers/nodes
[a..b]
such that all nodes in
[a..b]
have the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
, and a destination node
v
, set the parent of all nodes in
[a..b]
to be
v
.
A key tool used in our algorithm is a data structure that given an index
i
, computes
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i)
in a dynamic string
S
.
We show that there is an algorithm supporting this functionality with update and query time of
\tilde{O}(1)
(
Lemmas

2.8
and
3.11
)
3
3

3

Note that a dynamic
\mathsf{LPF}
data structure immediately implies the upper bound of

. Using such data structure, one can compute the

from scratch on query time in
\tilde{O}(|\text{LZ77}(S)|)
time.
.
Given such data structure, treating super-light indices is straight-forward.
The algorithm simply iterates over every index

, and computes
\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(i)
.
Then, the algorithm cuts
i
from its parent and links it to

.
This takes

To treat light indices, we develop a tailored tool that allows their identification by definition.
Specifically, we provide a data structure that, given substrings

and

in

The update and query time of this data structure are both

.
Using this tool, we can efficiently identify all light indices.
The algorithm iterates over every pair

and finds

(and of

) in

If
i_{a,b}
exists, the algorithm proceeds as in the super-light case: it computes
\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(i_{a,b})
and sets the parent of
i_{a,b}
accordingly.
This takes
\tilde{O}(m^{2})
time.

Updating Heavy indices.

We are left with updating
L
-heavy and
R
-heavy indices, which is the main technical challenge of the algorithm.
We discuss the manner in which we update all
L
-heavy indices,
R
-heavy indices are treated in a completely symmetric manner.
Let us focus on a single occurrence of
M_{L}
at index
i
that exists both in
S
and in
S^{\prime}
, i.e.,
S[i..j]=S^{\prime}[i..j]=M_{L}
for
j=i+m-1
.
As a subroutine, our algorithm finds all
L
-heavy indices
h
such that
S[i..j]
is a witness to them being
L
-heavy, i.e.,
S[h..h+\min(\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(h),\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(h)))
contains
S[i..j]
.
We use the following helpful fact.

Fact 1

(cf.
[
BCR24
, Lemma 19]
)
.

For every
a<b\in[n]
, it holds that
a+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(a)\leq b+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(b)
.

It follows that there exists some
\ell_{\min}
such that
h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(h)>j
if and only if
h\geq\ell_{\min}
.
Therefore, using our data structure for
\mathsf{LPF}
queries, we can binary search for the threshold value
\ell_{\min}
.
The indices
[\ell_{\min}..i]
are exactly the set of indices
h
such that
S[i..j]
is contained in
S[h..h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(h))
.
We can apply the same binary search in
S^{\prime}
to obtain an interval
[\ell^{\prime}_{\min}..i]
that contains exactly all indices such that
S[h..h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(h))
contains
S[i..j]
.
By definition,
[\max(\ell_{\min},\ell^{\prime}_{\min})..i]
is exactly the set of
L
-heavy occurrences with
S[i..j]
being a witness for their
L
-heaviness.
By doing the above for every occurrence of
M_{L}
, we can identify all
L
-heavy occurrences.

Two challenges emerge when advancing with this approach.
Firstly,
M_{L}
may have a lot of occurrences (possibly
\Omega(n)
), and this approach clearly takes at least as much time as the number of
M_{L}
occurrences in
S
.
Second, and more critical: unlike in the previous cases, there are possibly a lot (
\Omega(n)
) of
L
-heavy indices (even if there are only
O(1)
occurrences of
M_{L}
).

As a warm-up, consider the case in which
M_{L}
is aperiodic.
This immediately removes the first issue, as the number of occurrences of an aperiodic string of length
m
in a string of length
n
is bounded by
O(n/m)
.
Now, get an interval of indices for every one of the
O(n/m)
occurrences of
M_{L}
, and the union of these intervals is exactly the set of all heavy indices.
Let us focus on one interval
[\ell..i]
of
L
-heavy indices with the witness
M_{L}=S[i..j]
.
In order to properly set the parents of all of these indices, we need to gather more information regarding the
\mathsf{LPF}
values of the indices in
[\ell..i]
both in
S
and in
S^{\prime}
.
To do this, we use the notion of
critical indices
.
An index
c\in[\ell..i]
is critical (in
S^{\prime}
) if it has
c+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(c)>(c-1)+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(c-1)
.
If we collect all critical indices
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{t}
in
[\ell..i]
, we essentially have a partition of
[\ell..i]
into
t+1
intervals
I^{\prime}_{1},I^{\prime}_{2},\ldots I^{\prime}_{t+1}
which we denote as
\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S^{\prime}}
.
These intervals have the property that, within each interval, all indices
h
share the same value of
h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(h)
; that is, they have the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S^{\prime}}
.
The critical indices can be found in
\tilde{O}(t)
time as follows.
Let
x_{k}=c_{k}-1+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(c_{k}-1)
(initially,
x_{0}=i+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(i)
).
The next critical index
c_{k+1}
can be found by looking for the minimal
h\in[\ell..i]
such that
h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(h)=x_{k}
.
This can be done using a binary search, employing the
\mathsf{LPF}
data structure at every step.
Now, the next
x_{k+1}
can be found using another
\mathsf{LPF}
query.
This way, the algorithm finds
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{t}
while spending
\tilde{O}(1)
time per critical index, for a total of
\tilde{O}(t)
running time.

The next natural step is to apply interval updates to
\mathcal{T}_{S}
.
Namely, for each interval
I^{\prime}_{k}=[a..b]
implied by the critical indices, set the parent of all nodes in
[a..b]
to
a+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(a)
.
Notice that this update may be invalid: the interval update has a precondition, requiring that all vertices in the moved interval
[a..b]
have the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
.
To comply with this condition, we also find critical indices in
S
in the same manner, and obtain another partition
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
which we denote as
\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}
.
Combining the partitions
\mathcal{I}^{S^{\prime}}_{i}
and
\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}
we obtain a single refined partition
\mathcal{I}_{i}
such that for every interval
[a..b]\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
in the refined partition, all indices
h
have both
h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(h)=a+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(a)
and
h+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(h)=a+\mathsf{LPF}_{S^{\prime}}(a)
.
Therefore, we can apply a move interval operation on every interval in
\mathcal{I}_{i}
and it would be both correct (due to all indices in the interval having the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S^{\prime}}
) and valid (due to all indices in the interval having the same parent in
\mathcal{T}_{S}
).
With this, we have updated all heavy occurrences that correspond to the occurrence of
M_{L}
in
S[i..j]
in
\tilde{O}(|\mathcal{I}_{i}|)
.
With this, the update of the parents of all
L
-heavy indices is concluded.

Bounding the running time for the heavy indices.

We are left with the task of analyzing the running time of the algorithm.
It is relatively easy to show that
|\mathcal{I}_{i}|\in O(n/m)
.
On a high level, each critical index
c_{x}
implies some equality
S[c_{x}..c_{x}+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{x}))=S[\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(c_{x})..\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(c_{x})+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{x}))
.
In particular, since
S[c_{x}..c_{x}+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{x}))
contains
S[i..j]=M_{L}
, there is some occurrence of
M_{L}
aligned with
S[i..j]
in
S[\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(c_{x})..\mathsf{LPFpos}_{S}(c_{x})+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{x}))
.
Denote this occurrence as
o_{x}
, we claim that there is no pair of two critical indices
c_{x},c_{y}
that have
o_{x}=o_{y}=o
.
Intuitively, if both
c_{x}
and
c_{y}
align
S[i..j]
with
S[o..o+m-1]
, the length of the remainder of their right agreements with their corresponding
\mathsf{LPFpos}
past
j
should be the same, as it depends solely on
\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,o)
.
This is a contradiction to the fact that
c_{x}+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{x})\neq c_{y}+\mathsf{LPF}_{S}(c_{y})
for every two critical indices.

Since there are
O(n/m)
occurrences of
M_{L}
(recall that we consider now the case where
M_{L}
is aperiodic), and each critical index in
\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}
corresponds to a unique occurrence, we have that
|\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}|\in O(n/m)
.
Due to the same arguments, we also have
|\mathcal{I}^{S^{\prime}}_{i}|\in O(n/m)
and therefore
|\mathcal{I}_{i}|\in O(n/m)
.
The running time of the algorithm for updating
L
-heavy indices is
\sum_{S[i..j]=S^{\prime}[i..j]=M_{L}}\mathcal{I}_{i}
.
According to the bound above this sums up to
O(\sum_{S[i..j]=S^{\prime}[i..j]=M_{L}}O(n/m))=O((n/m)^{2})
.
It follows from this analysis that
the total running time for updating the parents of all indices is
\tilde{O}((n/m)^{2}+m^{2}+m)=O((n/m)^{2}+m^{2})
.
Sadly, this expression is balanced when
m=\sqrt{n}
and yields an
\tilde{O}(n)
running time.
This is not an improvement over simply computing
\text{LZ77}(S)
from scratch after every update.
Our final running time follows from a tighter, and rather surprising, bound on the sum of all critical indices (see
Section

4
).
That is, we show that
\sum_{S[i..j]}|\mathcal{I}_{i}|\in O(|\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(M_{L})|\log^{2}n)=O((n/m)\log^{2}n)
(where
\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(M_{L})
is the set of occurrences of
M_{L}
in
S
).
The proof of this bound relies on a heavy-path decomposition of suffix trees and carefully crafted charging arguments.
This immediately improves the running time algorithm to
\tilde{O}(n/m+m^{2})
, which yields the desired
\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
running time by selecting
m=n^{1/3}
.

The periodic case.

In the case where
M_{L}
is periodic, the above analysis is not sufficient, as
|\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(M_{L})|
can not be bounded by
O(n/m)
.
Via a novel analysis of the behavior of
\mathsf{LPF}
and
\mathsf{LPFpos}
, we manage to show that it is always sufficient to run the above algorithm on a subset
A
of
O(n/m)
occurrences of
M_{L}
, and it is still guaranteed that all
L
-heavy indices are properly updated.
These occurrences of
A
have the property that
\sum_{i\in A}|\mathcal{I}_{i}|\in O(n/m)
, which allows us to recreate the running time of
\tilde{O}(n^{2/3})
in the general case.

Lower Bound

We provide an evidence for the optimality of our algorithm by showing a reduction from the Orthogonal Vectors problem to dynamic
LZ77
maintenance.

Orthogonal vectors.

We consider the following variant of the Orthogonal Vectors problem.
Initially, a set
A
of
d
-dimensional Boolean vectors (for some
d
which is sub-polynomial in
n
) is given for preprocessing, and then a set
B
is queried.
The goal is to decide if there are vectors
\mathbf{v}\in A
and
\mathbf{u}\in B
such that
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0
, where
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}=\bigvee_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{v}[j]\wedge\mathbf{u}[j])
denotes the boolean inner product of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
.
It was proved by
[
AVW21
]
that no algorithm can solve this problem with polynomial preprocessing time and
O(n^{2-\varepsilon})
query time, where
n=|A|=|B|
and
\varepsilon>0
, unless SETH is false.

Reduction from orthogonality to
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
.

In this overview, we draw a connection between the size of
\text{LZ77}(S)
and vector orthogonality.
The complete reduction appears in
Section

7
.
Consider a string
S
that consists of three parts,
S=D\#G_{B}(\mathbf{u})\#G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
where every
\#
represents a fresh symbol.
We construct the components of
S
using strings over alphabet that contains
\{0_{i},1_{i}\mid i\in[d+1]\}
.
Additionally, the alphabet contains unique symbols, each occurring at most once in
S
and represented as
\#
.
For a vector
\mathbf{v}\in A
, we define

G_{A}(\mathbf{v})=\bigodot^{d}_{i=1}\begin{cases}0_{i}0_{i}&\mathbf{v}[i]=0\\
1_{i}1_{i}&\mathbf{v}[i]=1\end{cases}\,,

i.e., each coordinate
i
is encoded by two consecutive occurrences of
\mathbf{v}[i]
using the alphabet letter dedicated to this coordinate.
The first part of
S
, called the
dictionary
, consists of

D=\bigodot^{d}_{i=1}0_{i}0_{i}\,\,\#\,\,0_{i}0_{i+1}\,\,\#\,\,0_{i}1_{i+1}\,\,\#\,\,1_{i}0_{i+1}\,\,\#\,\,1_{i}1_{i+1}\,\,\#.

In words,
D
contains a set of two
0
s for every possible coordinate, and for every
(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{2}
, and coordinate number
i\in[d]
,
x_{i}
followed
y_{i+1}
.
Notice that these sequences naturally occur in
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
on transitions between coordinates.

Intuition for the dictionary.

Let us provide some intuition for the definition of
D
.
The length we are about to analyze is the number of
LZ77
-phrases in
\text{LZ77}(S)
that cover
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
, denoted as
L_{\mathbf{v}}
.
One functionality of
D
is that it forces
L_{\mathbf{v}}
to be at most
d+1
.
In particular, if
S=D\#G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
the phrases covering
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
have the following structure.
Let
i^{\prime}\in[d]
be the first coordinate such that
\mathbf{v}[i^{\prime}]=1
.
The first
i^{\prime}-1
phrases would be
0_{1}0_{1},0_{2}0_{2},\ldots,0_{i^{\prime}-1}0_{i^{\prime}-1}
(each is a substring occurring in
D
).
Once the
i^{\prime}
coordinate is met, there is no substring
1_{i^{\prime}}1_{i^{\prime}}
anywhere in
D
or elsewhere in
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
, so the next phrase would have to be
1_{i^{\prime}}
from
D
(as a partial word of
1_{i^{\prime}}0_{i^{\prime}+1}
, for instance).
This leaves one trailing
1_{i}
symbol to be covered by the next phrase.
From this point on, there will always be one trailing symbol that is not covered by the previous phrase.
Specifically, the rest of the phrases would be of the form
\mathbf{v}[i]_{i}\mathbf{v}[i+1]_{i+1}
, covering the trailing letter of the
i
th coordinate from the previous phrase and failing to cover the next
(i+1)
th coordinate completely within the same phrase.
It can be easily verified that no phrase longer than the ones discussed here are available at any point throughout the
LZ77
scan of
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
.
This leads to
d+1
phrases for a non-zero vector
\mathbf{v}
.
Specifically, there are
i^{\prime}-1
phrases before the
i^{\prime}
th coordinate, followed by the phrase
1_{i^{\prime}}
, then,
d-i^{\prime}
phrases that cover the trailing symbol of the previous phrase and the first symbol of the next coordinate, and finally a last phrase
\mathbf{v}[d]_{d}
to cover the trailing symbol of the last coordinate.
We have shown that the
L_{\mathbf{v}}=d+1
if
S=D\#G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
.
Notice that
D
also allows the
LZ77
factorization to ’skip’ over
0
coordinates in one step, and that the factorization of
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
transitions is forced to use phrases with trailing symbols only once it encounters a
1
coordinate.
Clearly, introducing
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
between
D
and
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
(regardless of its exact content) can only decrease the number of phrases covering
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
, as
L_{\mathbf{v}}
could use substrings of
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
in addition to substrings of
D
(and itself).

B
’s gadget.

We define
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
as follows,

G_{B}(\mathbf{u})=\bigodot^{d}_{i=1}\begin{cases}0_{i}0_{i}\#&\mathbf{u}[i]=1\\
1_{i}1_{i}\#&\mathbf{u}[i]=0\end{cases}

In particular,
G(\mathbf{u})
contains the substring
1_{i}1_{i}
if and only if
\mathbf{u}[i]=0
.
Now, we claim that in
S=D\#G_{B}(\mathbf{u})\#G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
, the value of
L_{\mathbf{v}}
is
d
if
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0
and
L_{\mathbf{v}}=d+1
otherwise.
It should be easy to see that if
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0
, then
L_{\mathbf{v}}=d
.
The
0_{i}0_{i}
sub-words in
D
allows skipping over a coordinate
i
in
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
with
\mathbf{v}[i]=0
with a single phrase, and for every
i
such that
\mathbf{v}[i]=1
, orthogonality implies that
\mathbf{u}[i]=0
, and therefore the substring
1_{i}1_{i}
is in
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
, which again allows skipping over a coordinate gadget in one step for a total of
d
steps to cover all of
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
.
For the other direction, if there is some first index
i^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{u}[i^{\prime}]=\mathbf{v}[i^{\prime}]=1
, the phrases preceding the
i^{\prime}
th coordinate will be able to skip a coordinate each, but when the
i^{\prime}
th coordinate would be reached, there is no occurrence of
1_{i^{\prime}}1_{i^{\prime}}
to copy, and the remaining phrases would have to carry a trailing symbol as discussed before.

Construction for the set
A
.

Let us describe a natural, yet
faulty
construction, that builds upon the previous discussion.
Given sets
A
and
B
of vectors, build
S=D\#G_{B}(\mathbf{1})\#\bigodot_{\mathbf{v}\in A}G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
.
Initialize dynamic data structures for reporting
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
and
|\text{LZ77}(D\#G_{B}(\mathbf{1})\#)|
.
Notice that
\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in A}L_{\mathbf{v}}
is exactly the difference between the length of the
LZ77
factorization of these two strings.
(We highlight that this is an abuse of notation, as
L_{\mathbf{v}}
is the number of phrases in
\text{LZ77}(S)
covering
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
, and here
L_{\mathbf{v}}
is in a wider context where multiple
G_{A}(\cdot)
gadgets are present in
S
.)
Now, for every vector
\mathbf{u}\in B
, modify
G_{B}(\mathbf{1})
to be
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
using
O(d)
substitutions.
It follows from our previous analysis that if
\mathbf{u}
is not orthogonal with any vector of
A
,
\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in A}L_{\mathbf{v}}=|A|(d+1)
, and otherwise the sum would be strictly smaller.
Let
|A|=|B|=n
, and to avoid clutter, let us assume that
d\in\tilde{O}(1)
.
The above discussion concludes in an algorithm that reports if there is a pair of orthogonal vectors in
A
and
B
using a dynamic
LZ77
data structure on a string of length
\tilde{O}(n)
by applying
\tilde{O}(n)
updates.
It follows from
[
AVW21
]
that assuming SETH, the running time of each update can not be
O(n^{1-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

It should be clear that the above reasoning is problematic, as it contradicts
Theorem

1.2
.
The main problem is that the intricate interaction between
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
and
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
is overshadowed by the interactions between the many copies of
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
included in this reduction.
In particular, if
A
contains a vector with
\mathbf{v}[i]=1
for some coordinate
i
, all vectors
\mathbf{w}
that have their gadget
G(\mathbf{w})
appear to the right of
G(\mathbf{v})
in
S
can ’skip’ the
i
th coordinate by copying from
\mathbf{v}
, regardless of
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
.
To address this issue, we need to make sure that any gadget
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
can not use the gadgets preceding it in
S
other than
G_{B}(\mathbf{u})
and
D
in any meaningful way.
To achieve this, on a high level, we have to introduce a larger version of
G_{A}(\mathbf{v})
on each subsequent concatenation, which results in
|S|\in\tilde{O}(n^{1.5})
.
Now,
[
AVW21
]
suggests that it is impossible that all of the
\tilde{O}(n)
updates have a running time of
O(n^{1-1.5\varepsilon})=O(|S|^{2/3-\varepsilon})
, which proves
Theorem

1.3
.

1.3
Open Problems

We note that the data structures of
Theorems

1.2
and
1.4
use
\tilde{O}(n)
words of space.
A major open problem for future research is whether one can design a data structure with the same runtime guarantees as in
Theorem

1.2
, or at least with sublinear update and query times, while using only
\tilde{O}(|\text{LZ77}(S)|)
space.
The main obstacle to improving the space usage lies in our reliance on the
\mathsf{LPF}
-tree (see below), which inherently requires
\Theta(n)
space.
Any positive resolution of this problem would therefore require the development of an alternative set of tools.

While we focused on developing a data structure with polylogarithmic query time, it may be interesting to explore different trade-offs between query and update times for a dynamic Lempel-Ziv data structure.
In particular, one may consider the opposite end of the query–update trade-off spectrum, aiming to design an algorithm with polylogarithmic update time.
While
Theorem

1.4
states that
\tilde{O}(1)
update time and
\tilde{O}(|\text{LZ77}(S)|)
is possible, it remains an open problem to develop a data structure with
\tilde{O}(1)
update time and guaranteed
O(n^{1-\varepsilon})
query time (even for highly non-compressible strings).

Another interesting direction is to study other compression schemes in dynamic settings. How efficiently can other variants of Lempel-Ziv (LZ78
[
ZL78
]
, LZ-END
[
KN10
]
, LZ-SE
[
SNYI25
]
, or LZ without overlaps
[
KKN+17
]
) be maintained dynamically?
Similar questions can be asked about entirely different compressibility measures, such as the run-length encoding of the Burrows-Wheeler Transform
[
BW94
]
and substring complexity
[
CEK+21
]
.

2
Preliminaries

Integers notations.

We use range notation to denote consecutive ranges of integers.
For integers
i,j
denote
[i..j]=\{i,i+1,\ldots,j\}
and
[i..j)=[i..j-1]
(if
j<i
,
[i..j]=\emptyset
).
Denote
[i]=[1..i]
.
We sometimes deal with arithmetic progressions of integers.
We represent a set
A=\{a,a+d,a+2d,\ldots,b=a+d\cdot(|A|-1)\}
by the triplet
(a,b,d)
.
We call
d
the
difference
of the arithmetic progression
(a,b,d)
.

Strings.

A string
S
of length
|S|=n
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is a sequence of symbols,
S=S[1]S[2]S[3]\ldots S[n]\in\Sigma^{n}
.
We denote a substring
S[i]S[i+1]\ldots S[j]
by
S[i..j]
.
We say that
S[i..j]
is a substring that starts at index
i
and ends at index
j
.
We call a substring that starts at index
1
a prefix of
S
, and a substring that ends at index
n
a suffix of
S
.
We denote the reversed string of
S
as
S^{R}=S[n]S[n-1]\ldots S[1]
.
Let
\bigodot
denote
concatenation (in increasing order of the running index).

For a pattern
P
, we denote
\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(P)=\{i\mid S[i..i+|P|-1]=P\}
.
We call an index
i\in\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(P)
an occurrence of
P
in
S
, and we say that
P
occurs in
S
if
\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(P)\neq\emptyset
.

For two strings
S
and
T
, the length of longest common prefix of
S
and
T
is denoted by
\mathsf{LCP}(S,T)=\max\{\ell\mid S[1..\ell]=T[1..\ell]\}
.
The length of the longest common suffix of
S
and
T
is denoted by
\mathsf{LCS}(S,T)=\mathsf{LCP}(S^{R},T^{R})
.

For a string
S
of length
n
and two indices
i,j\in[n]
, we denote
\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)=\mathsf{LCP}(S[i..n],S[j..n])
and
\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,j)=\mathsf{LCS}(S[1..i],S[1..j])
.

We say that an integer
p\geq 1
is a period of
S

for every

.
We say that

is the period of

if it is the minimal period of

We say that

.
Otherwise, we say that

is aperiodic.
A run in a string

is a maximal periodic substring.
Formally,
R=S[i..j]
is a run if

is periodic with period
p

, and
S[j+1]\neq S[j+1-p]
(if one of the indices is undefined, the inequality is considered satisfied).
We use the following fact regarding runs.

Fact 2

(
[
BGS24
, Lemma 8]
)
.

Let
S
be a string, and
p\in[|S|]
. Every index
i
in

intersects with at most two runs with period
p
.

The following is a well known fact.
For completeness, we prove it in
Appendix

C
.

Fact 3
.

Let
S[1..n]
be a periodic string with period
p
.
Then for
x\neq S[n-p+1]
the string
Sx
is aperiodic.
Symmetrically, for
x\neq S[p]
, the string

is aperiodic.

The following is a well-known fact regarding strings and periodicity.
It follows directly from the periodicity lemma (
[
FW65
]
).

Fact 4
.

Let

and

be two strings, let

, and let

be two consecutive occurrences of

in

It must hold that either

, or

.
In particular, if

is aperiodic then

.

In particular,

suggests the following structure of occurrences of a string.

Fact 5

For a string

with period

and a string

with period

For a string

An edge-labeled tree (over an alphabet

Each node

Notice that for every two nodes

A

is an edge-labeled tree that is derived from a set of strings as follows.
We define the trie of a set
\mathcal{S}
of strings recursively.
The trie of an empty set

is a single root vertex.
Let

be the trie of a set
\mathcal{S}
of
t\geq 0
strings.
For a string

, let
A=\argmax_{A\in\mathcal{S}}\mathsf{LCP}(S,A)
.
Let

be the node in

with

.
The trie of

is obtained by adding a path from

of length

with the labels

on the edges.
Notice that the label of the node at the end of this path is

.
Also notice that if
\mathsf{LCP}(S,A)=|S|
, nothing is added to

.
In words, the trie

of

is the minimal edge labeled tree such that for every string

in
\mathcal{S}
there is a vertex

with

.

For two strings

let

and

be the nodes in

such that

and

.
It holds that

LZ77
.

The Lempel-Ziv
[
ZL77
]
factorization is obtained by the following algorithm.
Given a string
S
, output a sequence of
phrases
, denoted
\text{LZ77}(S)
.
The algorithm starts by initializing a left to right scan with
i=1
and an empty sequence
P
of phrases.

At every iteration, if
i=n+1
, the algorithm halts and returns the sequence
P
.
Otherwise, the algorithm adds a new phrase as follows.
If
S[i]
is a new character, i.e.,
\mathsf{Occ}_{S[1..i-1]}(S[i])=\emptyset
, add
S[i]
as a phrase to
P
and continue with
i\leftarrow i+1
.
Otherwise, let
j
be an index in
[i-1]
that maximizes
\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)
.
The algorithm appends
(j,\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j))
to
P
as a new phrase and continues with
i\leftarrow i+\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)
.

We denote the final sequence of phrases obtained from applying the above procedure on
S
as
\text{LZ77}(S)
.

2.1
Dynamic Strings

Edit operations.

In this paper, we develop and analyze algorithms over a dynamic string
S
over alphabet
\Sigma
that undergoes
edit operations
.
An edit operation on
S[1..n]
can be one of three options listed below, each resulting in a new string
S^{\prime}
.

1.

Substitution:
Represented as a pair
(i,\sigma)\in[n]\times\Sigma
.
Results in
S^{\prime}=S[1..i-1]\cdot\sigma\cdot S[i+1..n]
.

2.

Deletion:
Represented as an index
i\in[n]
.
Results in
S^{\prime}=S[1..i-1]\cdot S[i+1..n]
.

3.

Insertion:
Represented as a pair
(i,\sigma)\in[n+1]\times\Sigma
.
Results in
S^{\prime}=S[1..i-1]\cdot\sigma\cdot S[i..n]
.

A dynamic algorithm receives as inputs a sequence of operations, each represented by the type of the edit operation (substitution, deletion, or insertion) and an index or a pair representing the operation.
When describing our algorithms, we abstain from providing a direct implementation for the substitution update, as it can be implemented using a pair of deletion and insertion.

Dynamic indices.

Even though edit operations are very local, a single operation may ’shift’ a large number of indices.
For instance, when applying a deletion at index
i
, every index
j\geq i
in
S
becomes the index
j-1
in
S^{\prime}
.
This phenomenon is problematic for any data structure that stores indices of the text, as every index
j>i
stored implicitly in the data structure should be modified.
These ’shifts’ also introduce clutter when making statements about strings with dynamic indices.

Therefore, we represent the indices of
S
not as explicit numbers but as nodes in a dynamic tree.
Specifically, we store a balanced search tree
T_{I}
with
n
nodes, such that every node
v\in T_{I}
stores the size of the sub-tree rooted at
v
as auxiliary information.
The
i
th node in the in-order traversal of
T_{I}

is
the
i
th index of
S
.
Note that due to the auxiliary information, one can find the
i
th node in
O(\log n)
time given
i
, and also, given a node, one can find the index
i
corresponding to it in
O(\log n)
time.
When an index is deleted (resp. inserted) from
S
, we delete (resp. insert) the corresponding index (node) from
T_{I}
, which automatically shifts all other indices.

With this framework, when discussing an index
i
of a dynamic string, we actually refer to the node in
T_{I}
representing
i
rather than the actual numeric value
i
.
This introduces an additional multiplicative factor of
O(\log n)
whenever our algorithm accesses an index of the dynamic text.
To avoid clutter, we ignore this dynamic index implementation when describing algorithms and instead assume that indices are stored explicitly.

Dominant Extension Points

In this section, we present the concept of Dominant Extension points.
The combinatorial properties of dominant extension points proven in this section play a key role in the runtime analysis of our algorithm.

Let
S
be a string and let
I\subseteq[n]
be a set of indices in
S
.
For
i\in I
, let
I_{i}=I\cap[1..i-1]
be all the indices of
I
that are smaller than
i
.
For every
j\in I_{i}
let
p_{i,j,S}=(\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,j),\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j))
and let
\mathsf{Extensions}_{I,i,S}=\{p_{i,j,S}\mid j\in I_{i}\}
.
Let
\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)=\mathsf{ND}(\mathsf{Extensions}_{I,i,S})
be the set of non-dominated points in
\mathsf{Extensions}_{I,i}
.
Formally, for a set of
2
-dimensional points
P
, the set of non-dominated points of
P
is
\mathsf{ND}(P)=\{(x,y)\in P\mid\nexists_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in P\setminus\{(x,y)\}}x^{\prime}\geq x\textit{ and }y^{\prime}\geq y\}
.
When the string is clear from context we omit it from the notation.

Notice that
|\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)|\leq|\mathsf{Extensions}_{I,i,S}|\leq|I_{i}|
and therefore the sum
\sum_{i\in I}|\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)|
is bounded by
O(|I|^{2})
.
In the next lemma we prove a tighter upper bound on this sum.

Lemma 4.15
.

\sum_{i\in I}|\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)|=O(|I|\log^{2}n)

Proof 4.16
.

Let
T_{R}
be the trie over the suffixes
\mathcal{S}_{R}=\{S[i..n]\mid i\in I\}
.
Let
T_{L}
be the trie over the prefixes
\mathcal{S}_{L}=\{S[1..i]^{R}\mid i\in I\}
.
In
T_{R}
(resp. in
T_{L}
) the node corresponding to
S[i..n]
(resp. to
S[1..i]^{R}
) is called
i_{R}
(resp.
i_{L}
).
The size of each string in
\mathcal{S}_{R}
or in
\mathcal{S}_{L}
is at most
n
, so the total size of
T_{L}
and
T_{R}
is
O(n^{2})
and in particular
\log|T_{L}|,\log|T_{R}|\in O(\log n)
.

For a direction
d\in\{L,R\}
, let
\mathsf{LCA}^{d}_{i}=\{\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{d}}(i_{d},j_{d})\mid p_{i,j}\in\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)\}
.
Recall that
0pt_{T_{R}}(\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{R}}(i_{R},j_{R}))=\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)
and
0pt_{T_{L}}(\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{L}}(i_{L},j_{L}))=\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,j)
.
Also, notice that by definition of non-dominated points, there are no two points
p_{i,j},p_{i,j^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)
with
\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j)=\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,j^{\prime})
or with
\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,j)=\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,j^{\prime})
.
This implies that the depths of elements
\mathsf{LCA}^{L}_{i}
are distinct and the depths of elements in
\mathsf{LCA}^{R}_{i}
are distinct.
This means that there are no two points
p_{i,j},p_{i,j^{\prime}}\in\mathsf{DExt}_{I}(i,S)
with
\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{L}}(i_{L},j_{L})=\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{L}}(i_{L},j^{\prime}_{L})
or
\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{R}}(i_{R},j_{R})=\mathsf{LCA}_{T_{R}}(i_{R},j^{\prime}_{R})
.

We make use of the following well-known fact.

Fact 6

(Heavy path decomposition)
.

A rooted tree with
n

, such that every node-to-root path intersects

heavy paths.

We assume that

and
T_{R}
are partitioned into heavy paths.
For every

we denote by
\pi^{L}_{i}
and
\pi^{R}_{i}
the paths from

to the root of
T_{L}
and from
i_{R}
to the root of

, respectively.
Denote by
H^{L}_{i}
and

the heavy paths that intersect with
\pi^{L}_{i}
and with
\pi^{R}_{i}

We start by presenting some tools.

Sequence of critical indices.

Given a string
S
and two indices
i<j
we define the sequence
L_{S}(i,j)=(\ell_{i,0},\ell_{i,1},\ldots)
.
Intuitively, the elements of
L_{S}(i,j)
are critical indices in which the value
x+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(x)
changes, limited to the range in which this value exceeds
j
(in decreasing order).
Formally, let
\ell_{\min}
be the minimal index in
[1..i]
such that
\ell_{\min}+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell_{\min})>j
.
If
\ell_{\min}
does not exist,
L_{S}(i,j)=(\ell_{i,0})=(i)
; otherwise, we define an element
\ell_{i,k}\in L_{S}(i,j)
recursively.

\ell_{i,k}=\begin{cases}i&\text{if }k=0,\\
\min\{i^{\prime}\in[\ell_{\min}..i]\mid i^{\prime}+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i^{\prime})=i+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i)\}&\text{if }k=1,\\
\min\{i^{\prime}\in[\ell_{\min}..i]\mid i^{\prime}+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i^{\prime})=\ell_{i,k-1}-1+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell_{i,k-1}-1)\}&\text{if }k>1.\end{cases}

The following observation follows from the monotonicity of
x+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(x)
values (
Lemma

3.10
).

Observation 6.24
.

x\in L_{S}(i,j)\setminus\{\ell_{i,0}\}
if and only if
x+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(x)>x-1+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(x-1)
and
x+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(x)>j
.

|L_{S}(i,j)|
is the number of defined elements in
\{\ell_{i,k}\}
.
We sometimes omit the subscript
i
from
\ell_{i,k}
and simply write
\ell_{k}
when
i
is clear from context.

Lemma 6.25
.

There is an algorithm that given
i
,
j
, and access to the data structure of
Lemma

3.11
, computes
L_{S}(i,j)
in
\tilde{O}(|L_{S}(i,j)|)
time.

Proof 6.26
.

Recall that due to
Lemma

3.10
, the values
j+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(j)
are monotonically increasing.
This property allows us to find, given a value
x
and a range
[a..b]
, the minimal index
c\in[a,b]
with
c+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(c)-1=x
(or with
c+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(c)-1\geq x
) by employing a binary search.
Since
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(c)
can be found in
\tilde{O}(1)
time for an input vertex

using

3.11
, this binary search is executed in
\tilde{O}(1)
time.

The lemma follows by applying this procedure
|L_{S}(i,j)|+1
times as follows.
First, find
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i)
.
Then, find
\ell_{\min}
which is the minimal index in
[1..i]
with
\ell_{\min}+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell_{\min})-1\geq j
.
If
\ell_{\min}
does not exist, return

.
Otherwise, find
\ell_{i,1}
as the minimal element in
[\ell_{\min}..i]
with
\ell_{i,1}+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell_{i,1})=i+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i)
.
Notice that
\ell_{i,1}
always exists since
i\in[\ell_{\min}..i]
.
As long as
\ell_{k}
exists,
\ell_{k+1}
can be found straightforwardly according to the definition of
L_{S}(i,j)
by applying the binary search procedure.
When the algorithm finds that
\ell_{i,k}
is undefined, the algorithm reports
L_{S}(i,j)
.

We say that an interval

is

-clean
if for all

, it holds that
i+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(i)=j+\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(j)

A sequence of intervals

is

-clean if every interval

is
S
-clean.
If

is both

-clean and

-clean, we say that

is clean.

Recall that the last element of
L_{S}(i,j)
is
\ell_{\min}
(unless
L_{S}(i,j)=(i)
), and that
L_{S}(i,j)
has a decreasing order.
Let

be the sequence of intervals
[\ell_{\min}=\ell_{|L_{S}(i,j)|-1},\ell_{|L_{S}(i,j)|-2},\dots,\ell_{1},\ell_{0}=i]

Obviously,
R_{S}(i,j)
partitions

.
By

3.10
, there is an index

it holds that

.
It holds that for every

We present an algorithm for updating the parents of all indices that are

An algorithm for updating all

Correctness.

We show that for every index

-heavy index, the algorithm updates the parent of

in

to

Let

-heavy index.
Since

Recall that

Similarly, we have that

We also have

It follows that when the algorithm processes

Since all intervals in

Time complexity.

We use
Lemma

2.7
to find all occurrences of

in
\tilde{O}(n/m)
time.
Then, we find

and

using

and

Finally, we merge and sort

The total time complexity is therefore

We observe the following connection between

Proof 6.28

(Proof of

.

Let

We showed that

The following claim will be useful for us.

Claim 8
.

\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,o)=i-\ell+1

{claimproof}

Recall that

.
This already implies that
\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,o)\geq i-\ell+1

Assume to the contrary that

\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell-1)=\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}_{S}(\ell)+1

We therefore have that

This is a contradiction to the minimality of
\ell
.

We proceed to prove that the point
p_{i,o}=(\mathsf{LCS}_{S}(i,o),\mathsf{LCP}_{S}(i,o))
is a non-dominated point in
\mathsf{Extensions}_{\mathsf{Occ}_{S}(M_{L}),i}

Assume to the contrary that there is an occurrence

.
Notice that the following equality holds:

This is a contradiction to the maximality of

Thus, we show that

The algorithm.

.

We will show how to solve the problem of

and

Overview.

Let
A
be a set of
n
Boolean
d=d(n)
-dimensional vectors.
The string
S
consists of three components, which we refer to as the dictionary, the vector, and the matrix.
The dictionary is defined as a function of
n
, independent of the vectors in
A
or
B
.
The matrix represents all vectors in
A
, while the vector component at any given time represents a single
\mathbf{u}\in B
.
Our goal is to encode
A
and some
\mathbf{u}\in B
such that if all vectors in
A
are non-orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}
, then
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
attains a predetermined value.
Conversely, if there exists at least one
\mathbf{v}\in A
such that
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0
, then
|\text{LZ77}(S)|
is strictly smaller.
We introduce a representation that encodes each
\mathbf{v}\in A
separately.
The number of phrases in
\text{LZ77}(S)
contributed by the encoding of
\mathbf{v}
is
d+1
if
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0
and
d+2
otherwise.
(In particular, this allows the algorithm not only to detect the existence of an orthogonal pair but also to count the number of such pairs.)
Finally, transitioning the representation from one vector in
B
to another requires at most
O(d)
updates.
Thus, iterating over all vectors in
B
involves at most
O(d\cdot n)
updates.

The reduction.

The alphabet we use contains the symbols
0_{j}
,
1_{j}
, and
2_{j}
for every
j\in[d]
. In addition, it includes a large number of unique characters, which we denote by the symbol
\#
. Each occurrence of
\#
refers to a distinct symbol that appears exactly once in the strings we define.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sqrt{n}
is an integer number.
We first explain how to represent the set
A
.
For any
i\in[1..n]
let
(i_{1},i_{2})\in[0..\sqrt{n}]\times[0..\sqrt{n}-1]
be the two unique integers such that
i=i_{1}\cdot\sqrt{n}+i_{2}
.
We start by encoding of a single entry in a vector of
A
.
Let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots.\mathbf{v}_{n}
be the vectors of
A
in some arbitrary order and let
\mathbf{v}_{i}[j]
denote the
j
th entry of
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.

\mathbf{v}_{i}[j]
is represented by

\mathsf{ACoordinate}(i,j)=\begin{cases}0_{j}{}^{3\sqrt{n}+i_{1}}2_{j}{}^{i_{2}}&\mathbf{v}_{i}[j]=0\\
1_{j}{}^{3\sqrt{n}+i_{1}}2_{j}{}^{i_{2}}&\mathbf{v}_{i}[j]=1\end{cases}.

The representation of the
i
th vector of
A
is obtained by concatenating the representation of all its coordinates by

\mathsf{AVector}(i)=\bigodot_{j=1}^{d}\mathsf{ACoordinate}(i,j).

Finally, to represent the set
A
we concatenate the representations of all the vectors.
We use
\#
to denote a unique character that appears only once in
S
(every time we use
\#
it means a completely new symbol).
Thus,

\mathsf{Matrix}(A)=\bigodot_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathsf{AVector}(i)\#\right).

The dictionary is made up of two types of gadgets.
The types, called
skip-
0
and
skip-two-halves
gadgets, defined for every
j\in[d],i_{2}\in[0..\sqrt{n}],(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{2}
as

\mathsf{Skip\text{-}}0(j)=0_{j}{}^{4\sqrt{n}}2_{j}{}^{\sqrt{n}}

\mathsf{Skip\text{-}}2\mathsf{\text{-}halves}(j,i_{2},x,y)=x_{j}{}^{2\sqrt{n}}2_{j}{}^{i_{2}}y_{j+1}{}^{2\sqrt{n}}.

We define the dictionary as

\mathsf{Dictionary}=\left(\bigodot_{j=1}^{d}\mathsf{Skip\text{-}}0(j)\#\right)\cdot\left(\bigodot_{j=1}^{d}\bigodot_{i_{2}=0}^{\sqrt{n}}\bigodot_{(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\mathsf{Skip\text{-}}2\mathsf{\text{-}halves}(j,i_{2},x,y)\#\right).

A coordinate of a vector
\mathbf{u}\in B
is encoded by

\mathsf{BCoordinate}(\mathbf{u},j)=\begin{cases}1_{j}{}^{4\sqrt{n}+1}2_{j}{}^{\sqrt{n}}&\mathbf{u}[j]=0\\
1_{j}{}^{2\sqrt{n}}{\#}1_{j}{}^{2\sqrt{n}}2_{j}{}^{\sqrt{n}}&\mathbf{u}[j]=1\end{cases}.

We emphasize that the encoding of a 0-coordinate in

, which slightly differs from the intuition presented in

Thus, a vector
\mathbf{u}\in B

\mathsf{BVector}(\mathbf{u})=\bigodot_{j=1}^{d}\mathsf{BCoordinate}(\mathbf{u},j)\#.

As described above, the dynamic string
S(\mathbf{u})
when testing a vector
\mathbf{u}\in B
is a concatenation of the three main gadgets:

S(\mathbf{u})=\mathsf{Dictionary}\cdot\mathsf{BVector}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathsf{Matrix}(A).

Appendix A
Implementation of a Dynamic
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-tree using Top Trees

In this section, we describe how to implement the data structure presented in
Section

3
with
\tilde{O}(1)
update and query times.
To do that, we reproduce the proof from
[
BCR24
]
with some minor changes.
Our minor changes are required to obtain a worst-case running time (compared to the amortized running time obtained by
[
BCR24
]
), and to support
\mathsf{LevelAncestor}

We replace link-cut trees with top trees
[
AHdLT05
]
, as they support the same operations as link-cut trees, but top trees guarantee
\tilde{O}(1)
worst-case time complexity.
The operations supported by top trees are as follows.

1.

insert a single node to the forest.

2.

remove a leaf from a tree.

3.

attach a root to another node as a child.

4.

detach a node from its parent.

5.

add a weight
x
to a node and all of its descendants.

6.

get the weight of a given node.

7.

get the
i
th ancestor of a node.

As in
[
BCR24
]
, we use Red-Black (RB) trees, that are balanced search trees supporting the following operations.

1.

Insert an element to an RB tree.

2.

Delete an element from an RB tree.

3.

Join two RB trees
T_{1},T_{2}
into a single RB tree containing the values of both, under the promise that the maximal value in
T_{1}
is smaller than the minimal value in
T_{2}
.

4.

Given a value
c
and an RB tree
T
, split
T
into two RB trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
such that
T_{1}
contains all values smaller than
c
and the rest of the values are in
T_{2}
.

All these operations cost
\tilde{O}(1)
time.

The following observation is useful.

Observation A.51

(
[
BCR24
, Observation 23]
)
.

A collection of RB trees on
n
nodes can be simulated using top trees.
The cost of every operation on an RB tree is then
O(\log^{2}n)
.

The indices of the string
S
are stored as nodes of one top tree
U
as follows.
For every node
i
of the
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-tree, the children of
i
are stored in an RB tree
T_{i}
, where the root of the RB tree is linked to the node
i
(i.e.,
i
is the parent of the root of
T_{i}
in
U
).
The edges of
U
between nodes
i
and the root of
T_{i}
are called
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-edges, and all other edges of
U
(i.e., edges inside
T_{i}
for some
i
) are called internal edges.
We consider internal edges of
U
as edges of weight 0, and
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-edges of
U
as edges of weight 1.
These weights preserve the property that the distance between
i
and every child of
i
in the
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-tree is
1
in
U
(as it is in the
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}
-tree).
We preserve the invariant that the weight of every node

in
U
is exactly the sum of the (imaginary) edge weights from the root to

in the
\mathsf{LPF}^{\prime}

\mathsf{Insert}
,
\mathsf{Link}
, and
\mathsf{Delete}
are simple top tree operations, and cost

In the

.
This is done in
\tilde{O}(1)

A.51
(using split and join operations on both

In addition, the weight of these nodes should be updated.
This is done in

calls to get-weight and add-weight.
Therefore,

\tilde{O}(1)
time.
Moreover,
\mathsf{GetDepth}
is implemented in

by returning the weight of the node in

Finally,

, which could take
O(2^{i+1})
time to write and store if done at the time of the split.
This can be de-amortized in the same manner.

Recall that the data structure maintains dynamic partition of intervals for every
i\in[\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1]
.
Each partition is stored as a balanced search tree with delta representation
[
KM
]
, storing the starting indices of the intervals.
We make the following observation.

Lemma B.55
.

For every
a\leq b\in[|S|]
, there is a set
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
of
O(\log n)
intervals in
\cup_{i=1}^{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1}\mathcal{P}^{i}
such that
\cup_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{a,b}}I=[a..b]
.
The set
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
can be found in
\tilde{O}(1)
time given
a
and
b
.

Proof B.56
.

We describe a greedy algorithm for finding
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
.
The algorithm initializes an empty list
L
and an index
k=a
and runs the following procedure until a termination condition is met.

1.

If
k>b
, return
L
as
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
.

2.

Find the maximal
i
such that the interval
I^{i}_{j}
of
\mathcal{P}^{i}
containing
k
has
I^{i}_{j}\subseteq[a..b]
.

3.

Append
I^{i}_{j}
to
L
and set
k\leftarrow b^{i}_{j}+1
.

First, we claim that
I^{i}_{j}
is well defined for every
k\in[a..b]
.
This follows from the fact that all intervals in
\mathcal{P}^{0}
are of length exactly 1, therefore the interval
I^{0}_{k}=[k..k]
is a feasible candidate for
I^{i}_{j}
.
It follows that the algorithm terminates.
It should be clear that at the beginning of every iteration of the algorithm, the list
L
already contains a set of intervals covering exactly
[a..k-1]
, so when the algorithm terminates the list
L
indeed satisfies
\cup_{I\in L}I=[a..b]
.

Every iteration of the algorithm is implemented in
\tilde{O}(1)
time by querying each of the search trees of
\mathcal{P}^{i}
for the interval containing
k
.
It is left to prove that
|L|\in O(\log n)
- notice that this also leads to the running time of the algorithm being
\tilde{O}(1)
.

Let
k_{t}
be the value of
k
at the beginning of the
t
-th iteration.
Similarly, let
i_{t}
be the maximal
i
value found in Step
2
of the algorithm in the
t
-th iteration, and
I_{t}=[a_{t}..b_{t}]
be the interval added to
L
in the
t
-th iteration.
We claim that for every
i\in[\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1]
,
i
can appear in the sequence
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{|L|}
at most 16 times - the bound on
|L|
directly follows from this claim.

Let us fix some
i\in[\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1]
.
Assume to the contrary that there exist 17 iterations,
t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{17}
, of the algorithm in which
i_{t}=i
.
Notice that for every
x<y\in[17]
we have
I_{t_{x}}\neq I_{t_{y}}
.
It follows from
k_{t_{y}}\geq k_{t_{x+1}}>b_{t_{x}}
, i.e.,
I_{t_{x}}
does not contain
k_{t_{y}}
.
It immediately follows that the claim is correct for
i=\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1
as
|\mathcal{P}^{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil+1}|\leq 2
.

Let us assume that
i\in[\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil]
.
Recall that the length of any two consecutive intervals in
\mathcal{P}^{i}
is least
2^{i}
.
Therefore,
k_{t_{9}}>k_{t_{1}}+2^{i+2}
and
k_{t_{17}}>k_{t_{9}}+2^{i+2}
.
Implying
k_{t_{9}}>a+2^{i+1}
and
k_{t_{9}}<b-2^{i+1}
.
Let
I^{i+1}_{j}=[a^{\prime}..b^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{P}^{i+1}
be the interval in
\mathcal{P}^{i+1}
that contains
k_{t_{9}}
.
Recall that the length of
I^{i+1}_{j}
is less than
2^{i+2}
.
It follows from
k_{t_{9}}>a+2^{i+1}
that
a^{\prime}>a
and it follows from
k_{t_{9}}<b-2^{i+1}
that
b^{\prime}<b
.
Hence
I^{i+1}_{j}\subseteq[a..b]
, a contradiction to
i_{t_{9}}=i
.

We now show how to answer a query given the partitions, and the data structure
Lemma

2.6
.
First, we apply
Lemma

B.55
to obtain a set
\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
of intervals from the partitions such that the union of the intervals is exactly
S[i_{T}..j_{T}]
.
For every
I^{i}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
, we can access entries of the suffix array of
S^{i}_{j}=S[a^{i}_{j}..b^{i}_{j}]
in
\tilde{O}(1)
time, and we can query the
\mathsf{LCP}
between indices in
S^{i}_{j}
and
i_{P}
via the data structure of
Lemma

2.6
in
\tilde{O}(1)
time.
It follows from
Observation

B.53
that we can report if there is an occurrence of
P=S[i_{P}..j_{P}]
in
S^{i}_{j}
in
\tilde{O}(1)
time.
We do this for each of the
\tilde{O}(1)
intervals of
\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
for a total running time of
\tilde{O}(1)
and report that there is an occurrence of
P
in
T=S[i_{T}..j_{T}]
if one of the applications of
Observation

B.53
finds an occurrence.

Notice that this is insufficient: there may be an occurrence of
P
in
T
that is not contained in any of the intervals of
\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
.
We observe that every such ’missed’ occurrence must contain an endpoint of an interval in
\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
.

We complement our algorithm with the following procedure.
For every
x\in\cup_{[a..b]\in\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}}\{a,b\}
(i.e., each endpoint of an interval of
\mathcal{I}_{i_{T},j_{T}}
) we find a representation of all occurrences of
P
in
S[\max\{x-|P|,i_{T}\}..\min\{x+|P|,j_{T}\}]
using an internal pattern matching query to the data structure of
Lemma

2.6
.
This adds another factor of
\tilde{O}(1)
to the time complexity.
It is easy to see that each occurrence of
P
that contains an endpoint of an interval is found in this way, thus concluding the algorithm and proving
Lemma

2.8
.

Appendix C
Proofs of Technical Lemmas

For two integers
p,q
, we denote as
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)
the greatest common divisor of
p
and
q
.
In the proofs presented in this section, we use the periodicity lemma of Fine and Wilf
[
FW65
]
.

Fact 19

(Periodicity Lemma
[
FW65
]
)
.

If a string
S
has a period
p
and a period
q
such that
|S|\geq p+q-\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)
, it holds that
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)
is a period of
S
.

Proof C.57

(Proof of
Fact

3
)
.

We prove the first statement, the second statement can be proven in a symmetrical manner.
Recall that
S
is periodic, so
p\leq|S|/2
.
Assume to the contrary that
Sx
is periodic with some period
q\leq(|S|+1)/2
.
Since
x\neq S[n-p+1]
, and therefore
x\neq S[n-p+1]=S[n-i\cdot p+1]
for every positive integer
i
such that
n\geq i\cdot p
, it must hold that
p
is not a divisor of
q
.
This implies that
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)<p
.
Since
S
is a substring of
Sx
, it holds
q
is a period of
S
as well.
Since both
p
and
q
are periods of
S
, and
p+q-\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)\leq|S|
it follows from
Fact

19
that
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q)<p
is also a period of
S
.
This is a contradiction to
p
being the minimal period of
S
.

Proof C.58

(Proof of
Lemma

2.7
)
.

We simply maintain the data structure of
Lemma

2.6
.
Upon a query for
T
and
P
, for every integer
i
we define
T_{i}=T[i_{T}+i\cdot|P|..\min(i_{T}+i|P|+2|P|-1,j_{T})]
.
It can be easily verified that every occurrence of
P
in
T
is contained in
T_{i}
for some
i\in[0..|T|/|P|)
.
We query for
\mathsf{IPM}_{S}(P,T_{i})
for every
i\in[0..{|T|}/{|P|})
, this yields a set of
O(|T|/|P|)
arithmetic progressions that together represent all occurrences of
P
in
T
.
To obtain
\mathsf{Clusters}_{T}(P)
, attempt to merge clusters of occurrences from adjacent
T_{i}
’s by checking the fist occurrence of the cluster in
T_{i}
is at distance exactly
p
from the last occurrence of
T_{i-1}
.
This can be easily done in
O(|T|/|P|)
time.

Proof C.59

(Proof of
Lemma

2.9
)
.

We describe an algorithm for finding the first occurrence of
P=[i_{P}..j_{P}]
in
T=[i_{T}..j_{T}]
, an algorithm for finding the last occurrence can be constructed in a similar way.
We use the data structure of
Lemma

2.8
.
First we check whether
P
occurs in
T
.
If not, return null.
Otherwise, binary search for the minimal index
k\leq j_{T}
such that
T[i_{T}..k]
contains an occurrence of
P
and return
k-|P|+1
.
