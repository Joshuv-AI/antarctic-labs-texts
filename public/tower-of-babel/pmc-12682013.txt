Protocol for screening anti-amyloidogenic compounds in cultured mammalian cells
Authors: Emma Lacroix, Sandra Keerthisinghe, Evgenia A Momchilova, Timothy E Audas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA functional form of amyloid aggregate can be rapidly induced by various environmental stressors, yielding subnuclear structures called amyloid bodies (A-bodies). Here, we describe a cell-based high-throughput assay to identify anti-amyloidogenic compounds capable of impairing heat-induced A-body formation in adherent cell lines. We describe steps for generating reporter cell lines, optimizing scoring algorithms, screening compound libraries, and analyzing results. This assay can be expanded to various systems to identify compounds with both anti- and pro-amyloidogenic effects.Subject areas: cell culture, high-throughput screening, microscopy

Before you begin

Amyloid aggregation is a common feature of numerous human neurodegenerative and systemic disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease, AL amyloidosis, and prion-based infections.1 These structures can form within the brain or vascular tissue, damaging the surrounding cells, and having a negative impact on patient health.2,3 Thus, identifying compounds that can impair the biogenesis of amyloid aggregates could generate therapeutic opportunities to combat these pathological conditions.

The protocol below describes the specific steps to perform a high-throughput image-based screen for anti-amyloidogenic compounds in adherent mammalian cells (MCF-7). Using stress-induced subnuclear aggregates with amyloid biophysical characteristics as a biological system (A-bodies), the effects of small molecule compounds can be assessed on aggregate biogenesis.4 Formation of these structures is measured by detecting the presence of a small GFP-tagged peptide. This marker molecule possesses high fibrillation propensity and a strong affinity/specificity for A-bodies,5 allowing this fluorescently-tagged protein to be readily incorporated into these amyloid structures under a variety of cellular conditions. MCF-7 (human breast adenocarcinoma) cells were used in this protocol, as this adherent mammalian cell line is large and flat, making it ideal for this image-based analysis. However, this protocol could be easily adapted to study other adherent cell lines, as the data from this protocol has been validated in additional cell types, including U-87 MG (human glioblastoma), HeLa (human cervical adenocarcinoma), and PC-3 (human prostate adenocarcinoma).

Innovation

This protocol describes a screening platform to assess the anti-amyloidogenic effects of compound libraries in an adherent tissue culture cell line setting.4 Given the role of amyloid formation in various neurological and systemic diseases, several screening assays have been developed to aid in the identification of therapeutics that moderate amyloid fibrillation.6,7,8 While some of these strategies have identified potential hit compounds,9,10 many assess fibrillation in vitro and do not account for cytotoxicity, membrane impermeability, or reduced biological half-life of the candidate compounds. The approach described here uses an in vivo platform, which naturally excludes small molecules that are not suitable for use in a cellular setting. Additionally, this in vivo screening could prove to be more expansive, as it enables the detection of direct and indirect anti-amyloidogenic compounds. Small molecules that bind cellular proteins or alter molecular pathways associated with amyloid fibrillation would be undetectable by the in vitro techniques, but readily identified using this screening strategy. As the methodology to induce A-body formation is simple and rapid,5,11,12 these cellular aggregates represent an ideal setting to study intracellular amyloidogenic events, though alteration of the reporter construct could allow the assay to target specific pathological proteins or different biomolecular condensates. Together, these features highlight the versatility of this cellular system, which could be adapted to facilitate the screening of diverse compound libraries and various membrane-less organelles.9

Generate a reporter cell line

Timing: 2–3 weeks

To detect A-body formation, we generated an MCF-7 cell line stably expressing a GFP-tagged peptide that is specifically targeted to these stress-inducible structures (Figure 1). Alternative adherent cell lines or GFP-expressing reporter constructs could be easily employed to expand the scope of the results generated.

Expected outcomes

This protocol details the screening methods used to identify compounds that regulate the formation of heat-induced A-bodies. Using this approach, we generated a list of small molecules that impaired A-body formation and, by assessing the protein targets of these compounds, we identified a cellular signaling cascade that regulates amyloidogenesis in mammalian cells.4 This work serves as a proof-of-concept that this screening approach can be used to probe the cellular mechanisms governing physiological amyloid formation, but it could also easily be adapted to gain insight into additional aggregation-associated phenomena. The A-body reporter cell line we generated could be useful for screening compounds that: 1) disrupt A-body formation in response to additional stressors, 2) impair A-body disassembly, or 3) alter kinetic rates of amyloid aggregation/disaggregation. The use of additional libraries could also yield informative results about compounds or cellular factors that regulate physiological or pathological amyloidogenesis (e.g., FDA approved drugs, natural compounds, siRNA libraries). For example, the MCF-7 ATS-GFP reporter cell line could be used to screen libraries for compounds that induce de novo amyloid aggregation, potentially generating a class of chemical agents that we have colloquially termed “amyloidogens” (i.e., chemicals or substances that cause or promote amyloidogenic events and/or pathological amyloidosis). Changing the reporter molecule to one of the pathological proteins that have been shown to aggregate within the A-bodies, such as β-amyloid (1–42), Serum Amyloid A, or SOD1(A4V),13 would also generate some intriguing results. Here, compound screens could be performed that are more specifically targeted to neurodegenerative and systemic amyloidosis. This screening approach could also be adapted to study any proteinaceous foci that form intracellularly (e.g., stress granules, nuclear speckles, paraspeckles, PML bodies),14,15,16,17,18 by simply generating a reporter cell line expressing a marker for the structure of interest. Together, this work could have broad implications in pre-clinical drug development, by identifying anti-amyloidogenic candidates or generating mechanistic insight into potential amyloidogenic side effects of established compounds.

Limitations

This protocol is effective with MCF-7 cells, and we adapted elements of it for use in additional adherent mammalian cell types (U-87 MG, PC-3, and HeLa). However, it would not be possible to screen non-adherent cells lines with this approach. Screens of this nature are also limited by the libraries employed, as compound libraries only address targetable cellular factors, and many classes of molecules will be under-represented (e.g., some transcription factors). Alternatively, some cellular factors may be over-represented because they are desirable or common therapeutic targets.

Another important limitation of this screen is the lack of replication, as the entire screen was only performed once. As such, all hits must be thoroughly validated for statistical significance, either by re-screening plates treated with the “hit” compounds in triplicate (same protocol as above, screening only “hit” compounds rather than re-screening the entire library), or by purchasing a subset of the compounds and manually testing them in triplicate.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Varying intensity of control conditions between plates (related to Setup Step 8).

The intensity of A-bodies is proportional to the duration of the stress treatment, and sensitive to temperature fluctuations. For this reason, it is ideal to avoid opening the incubator during treatment, in order to maintain a constant temperature. However, the automated fixation process generally occurs one plate at a time, and the automated incubator door re-opens each time a plate is placed inside for heat-shock treatment or removed for fixation. This results in non-uniform stress treatment times due to fixation-induced delays. To avoid over/under representation of specific plates during analysis, consider implementing the following mitigations.

Potential solution

•Reducing batch size: Grouping plates into smaller treatment batches can yield more uniform treatment times, and therefore more uniform intensity outcomes.

Reducing batch size: Grouping plates into smaller treatment batches can yield more uniform treatment times, and therefore more uniform intensity outcomes.

•Using a “per-plate” hit threshold: Performing hit identification on a “per-plate” basis and setting the hit identification threshold based on individual plate controls.

Using a “per-plate” hit threshold: Performing hit identification on a “per-plate” basis and setting the hit identification threshold based on individual plate controls.

•Alternative controls: Using a small-molecule to induce the positive control phenotype (lack of A-bodies) would allow for all wells to be fixed together, instead of using the more time-consuming process of fixing only specific rows within the plate.

Alternative controls: Using a small-molecule to induce the positive control phenotype (lack of A-bodies) would allow for all wells to be fixed together, instead of using the more time-consuming process of fixing only specific rows within the plate.

Problem 2

Low Z′ factors, below the threshold for a viable screen (related to Setup step 10g).

Hits can only be identified if there is a sufficient difference between the two control phenotypes to distinguish between them with the chosen algorithm. If these phenotypes are not distinct or the algorithm cannot distinguish between them, the Z′ factor will be low, and there is a greater chance to obtain false positives and false negatives.

Potential solution

•Optimize scoring algorithm: If Z′ is low, but there is a clear phenotypic difference between both control settings, then the cell-scoring algorithm likely needs to be optimized. Factors such as foci number, diameter, and intensity can all be altered manually, based on the control wells in each plate. Use the “line measure” tool to assess the range of each parameter in control wells and adjust the scoring algorithm metrics accordingly.

Factors such as foci number, diameter, and intensity can all be altered manually, based on the control wells in each plate. Use the “line measure” tool to assess the range of each parameter in control wells and adjust the scoring algorithm metrics accordingly.

•Optimize control conditions: If Z′ is low, and there is not a clear phenotypic difference between the control settings, then the control conditions should be optimized further. For heat-stress, A-body intensity can be altered by temperature, cell confluence, cell passage number, and treatment time. There can also be temperature gradients within some incubators. We suggest using temperature probes to check the precise temperatures on different shelves within the heat-shock incubator and adjust plate placement accordingly.

Problem 3

Cells are being sloughed off during fixation (related to Setup step 8).

The addition of methanol can cause cells to detach from the plate if it is added too forcefully.

Potential solution

•Adjust plate washer pin depth/location: The depth of the pins within the well can be lowered, and the pins can be aligned towards one side of the plate to allow fixative to be added along the edge of the well.

•Ensure methanol is ice-cold: Warm methanol causes cells to detach more readily.

Ensure methanol is ice-cold: Warm methanol causes cells to detach more readily.

•Optimize plate coating: Various plate coatings could be used to aid cell adherence. In this protocol, poly-D-lysine was effective for our use, however various other coatings (e.g., collagen, concanavalin A, poly-L-lysine) could be implemented instead.

Problem 4

Incorrect scoring due to cell death, or image quality (related to step 7g and step 8).

In our assay, the use of an intracellular fluorescent marker can cause false positives when the cells die as a result of the compounds combined with the stress treatment. Here, cells may “ball-up” and appear as bright green foci, causing them to be scored as A-body positive despite the lack of A-bodies. Alternatively, fields that have been imaged out-of-focus, with debris, or other image artifacts may be scored incorrectly (Figure 4).

Problem 5

Too many/not enough hits (related to step 11).

Hits are identified based on a user-determined threshold. This threshold can be set in a variety of ways, yielding different results.

Potential solution

•Adjust hit identification threshold: If hits are called that do not pass visual inspection, the threshold for hit identification may need to be adjusted. This threshold can be set in a variety of ways. Here, we used a per-plate threshold of 3 standard deviations below the mean of the negative control. This threshold can also be set as the top X percentage of the phenotype, or by setting a blanket threshold for all plates in the screen (e.g., less than 60% of cells have A-bodies = hit). This threshold can be optimized during the pilot phase of the screen, by implementing various hit thresholds and manually assessing which parameter most accurately matches the visual inspection.
