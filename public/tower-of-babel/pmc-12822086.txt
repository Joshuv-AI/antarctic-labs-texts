The impact of transcranial direct current stimulation on neuromuscular control during directional change
Authors: Haoyang Wang, Hongxiang Zhang, Junhui Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ability to change direction is crucial in soccer; however, evidence regarding acute intervention methods remains limited. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), a noninvasive brain modulation technique, has already been shown to enhance muscle function and athletic performance. Still, its influence on athletes’ change-of-direction skills—especially at different turning angles—has not been fully clarified. In this study, thirteen male collegiate soccer players were divided into two groups: an experimental group receiving real stimulation and a control group receiving sham stimulation. The experimental group underwent twenty minutes of bilateral anodal tDCS over the primary motor cortex at 2 mA. tDCS significantly improved 45° COD performance (p = 0.027), while no changes were observed at 90°. Electromyography results provided further detail: during 45° turns, the integrated EMG values of the vastus lateralis increased significantly (p = 0.021), and during 90° turns, the root mean square values of the vastus medialis also rose significantly (p = 0.047). Altogether, these findings suggest that tDCS can act as an effective acute intervention to improve athletes’ agility in direction changes, offering a practical edge for performance enhancement. This study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR290251, October 15, 2025).

Introduction

Change-of-direction (COD) ability is defined as the capacity to rapidly and accurately redirect the body’s trajectory along a predetermined path. As a core technical skill in soccer, basketball, and other invasion sports, COD exerts a decisive influence on competitive performance. Although soccer may appear to be moderate in intensity, COD tasks demand high neuromuscular activation and precise coordination. It demands that athletes—while moving at high speed—execute abrupt yet precise directional shifts without compromising postural equilibrium or stability. Match statistics reveal that elite footballers perform more than 700 COD maneuvers per game [1], while basketball players may exceed 1,000 [1]. Empirical evidence indicates that COD performance is among the principal factors distinguishing elite from sub-elite athletes [2, 3], with its efficacy governing the success of attacking penetrations, defensive positioning, and tactical.

COD performance is governed by multiple determinants. Lockie reported that linear sprint speed and lower-limb strength are primary predictors of COD ability in female soccer players [4]. Rouissi et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between dynamic balance and COD outcomes [5], whereas García-Pinillos et al. found that athletes with higher flexibility scores outperformed their less-flexible peers in COD tests [6].

Conventional training approaches primarily enhance COD performance through long-term strength, speed, and sport-specific technical interventions [7–9]. Recently, tDCS has attracted attention in the athletic domain because it a non-invasive, efficient, and cost-effective technique. Accumulating evidence shows that tDCS can modulate cortical excitability [10, 11], optimize functional connectivity within brain networks [12, 13] and regulate spinal motor-neuron recruitment [14], thereby augmenting athletic outcomes such as strength [15, 16], balance [17], and agility [18]. For example, Vargas et al. [19] found that transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) can significantly increase the isometric contraction force of the quadriceps femoris in female soccer players. Although these findings suggest that tDCS holds promise for improving COD ability, several investigations have failed to detect performance benefits. For instance, Cogiamanian et al. [20] applied 1.5 mA tDCS over the right M1 for 10 min in healthy participants but observed no significant increase in maximal voluntary isometric contraction. Few studies have directly examined the immediate effects of tDCS on lower-limb neuromuscular control during COD tasks.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the immediate effects of tDCS on lower-limb neuromuscular control during soccer-specific change-of-direction (COD) tasks. By analysing COD completion time, lower-limb electromyographic activation characteristics and muscle-synergy patterns, we sought to elucidate how tDCS optimises COD performance and the underlying neuromuscular regulatory mechanisms. It was hypothesized that anodal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) applied over bilateral primary motor cortices (M1) would shorten change-of-direction (COD) completion time and modify muscle synergy patterns compared with sham stimulation.

Materials and methods

Experimental participants

Prior to participant recruitment, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power to estimate the required sample size for detecting repeated-measures effects with a statistical power of 0.80 and an alpha level of 0.05. Previous tDCS studies targeting neuromuscular or motor-performance outcomes have commonly reported medium effect sizes (0.38–0.55) in repeated-measures designs, as demonstrated by Lu et al. [21] and Alemán et al. [22]. Based on this empirical evidence, an effect size of 0.38 was adopted for the present analysis, which indicated that a minimum of 15 participants would be needed for within-subject comparisons. Considering the 15–20% attrition rate typically observed in similar experimental protocols, approximately 34 candidates were initially screened. Ultimately, thirteen male collegiate athletes met al.l eligibility criteria and completed the full experimental procedures. Although the final sample size was slightly below the minimum required sample size of 15, it was close to the estimated threshold, and the potential limitations associated with the relatively small sample size were acknowledged in the Discussion section.

Between April and May of 2025, thirteen male collegiate athletes were recruited from Wuhan Sports University. Their average age was 20.33 ± 0.50 years, with a mean height of 179.33 ± 3.33 cm and body weight of 76.56 ± 7.65 kg. They had between six and ten years of training experience, typically practicing three to five times per week, with each session lasting about 120 min. All participants were right-leg dominant and had not experienced any lower-limb injuries in the six months leading up to the study. Prior to the experiment, they were fully informed in writing about potential risks and safety guidelines associated with the testing procedures and provided signed bilingual consent forms. The study employed a simple pre- and post-intervention design, with the intervention consisting of bilateral M1 transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS). Following stimulation, participants were fitted with wireless surface electromyography sensors and performed change-of-direction tasks at 45° and 90°. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Sports University (Approval No. 2025101) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Throughout the intervention, no participant reported discomfort or adverse events, suggesting that the applied tDCS protocol was both safe and well tolerated in this group of young athletes.

Experimental instruments

The Halo Sport2 was used for tDCS intervention. In this experiment, a stimulation intensity of 2 mA and a stimulation duration of 20 min were selected, with the stimulation site being the bilateral primary motor cortices (M1) [23].

Wireless surface electromyography was recorded with the U.S.-made Delsys Trigno™ system. Following the electrode-placement guidelines of Hermens et al. [24], wireless sEMG sensors were positioned precisely on each participant. Consistent with previous work [25], signals were collected from the vastus lateralis (VL), rectus femoris (RF), vastus medialis (VM), biceps femoris (BF), semitendinosus (ST), tibialis anterior (TA), and gastrocnemius lateralis (GL) at a sampling frequency of 2,000 Hz.

Additional equipment included a synchronization unit, infrared timing gates, alcohol swabs, wet wipes, a razor, scissors, kinesiology tape, EMG adhesive interfaces, moisturizer, athletic shoes, and shorts.

Experimental protocol

tDCS was applied using the Halo Sport 2, an intelligent neurostimulation headset developed by the U.S. neurotechnology company Halo Neuroscience. This non-invasive device delivers a constant, low-intensity direct current to stimulate the cerebral cortex and modulate cortical neuronal activity. It incorporates three foam electrodes, each with an area of 24 cm², which transmit 2 mA of direct current through the scalp to bilaterally regulate the excitability of the motor cortices.

During the intervention phase, tDCS was delivered with the Halo Sport 2 headset. Before fitting the device, the three foam electrodes were rinsed with cold water or soaked in 0.9% saline to ensure optimal conductivity. The target region was the bilateral primary motor cortices (M1). At the onset, current intensity was ramped up to 2 mA over 30 s and then maintained for 20 min. Thus, the present study employed 2 mA for 20 min over bilateral M1; the safety and feasibility of this specific montage have been confirmed in previous research [23]. The bilateral M1 configuration was selected to simultaneously enhance interhemispheric coordination between lower-limb representations, given that change-of-direction movements require synchronized bilateral control of stance and swing legs. Unlike unilateral or cerebellar protocols, which primarily modulate localized excitability or error correction, bilateral stimulation targets both hemispheric motor areas to optimize symmetrical motor output and coordination efficiency during complex bipedal actions.All tests were performed within 5 min after tDCS to assess immediate effects.

This study measured EMG indices during directional changes before and after tDCS intervention. The testing method was designed in reference to the experiments conducted by Falch et al. [26] and Schreurs et al. [27]. The directional change test included 45° and 90° left turns, primarily executed by the participants’ dominant foot (right foot). The specific testing procedure was as follows: Participants started from a standing position with their front foot 20 centimeters away from the timing gate, first sprinted 5 m in a straight line, then turned left, and finally sprinted another 5 m to the finish line. Infrared timing gates were set up at both the starting and finishing points to measure the time taken for the directional change [26]. As shown in Fig. 1, the testing setup consisted of an integrated monitoring platform with infrared timing gates, cameras, and a synchronous data collection system. Each participant began at the starting line, sprinted 5 m straight ahead, then executed either a 45° or 90° change of direction as instructed, and continued another 5 m to the finish. Infrared timing gates were placed 20 centimeters in front of the starting line and at the finish line to precisely capture the time required to complete the run. Both the 45° and 90° trials were performed three times, and the best result was used for subsequent EMG analysis [28]. To minimize order effects, the testing sequence was randomized across participants, and a five-minute rest period was given between blocks to reduce fatigue. Throughout the trials, cameras recorded the movement process, while the synchronous collector and computer integrated the data, ensuring that EMG signals and performance outcomes were aligned in time.

Phase segmentation

The present study mainly employed a 3D motion capture system (SIMIMO-TION, Germany). Five high-speed cameras were positioned around the change-of-direction (COD) area to record the athletes’ COD process and identify the changes in different COD phases. The study divided the entire 45° and 90° COD running process into three consecutive phases: Push-off phase (A ~ B): From the beginning of the support step during the change of direction to the maximum knee extension angle of the support leg. Folding phase (B ~ C): From the maximum knee extension angle of the support leg to the maximum knee flexion angle. Landing buffer phase (C ~ D): From the maximum knee flexion angle of the support leg to the complete foot contact with the ground when the leg is pressed down. As shown in Fig. 2, stages A, B, C, and D illustrate different movement states of the supporting leg during a change-of-direction run. Stage A corresponds to the instant when the supporting leg first contacts the ground, which can be seen as the starting point of the push-off preparation. Stage B represents the moment when the leg is fully extended, reaching its maximum stretch, marking the end of the push-off phase. Stage C shows the leg gradually bending from full extension to its maximum flexion, serving as a critical point in the folding phase. Finally, stage D indicates that the supporting leg continues to touch the ground after bending, until the whole foot is fully planted, completing the cushioning landing. The cameras in this system had a shooting frequency of 100 Hz [29]. The data collected were processed by a low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency set at 15 Hz [30].

Data processing

Preprocessing of EMG signals

The raw EMG signals were first processed with a band-pass filter of 10–500 Hz and then full-wave rectified. The rectified signals were enveloped using a 4th -order low -pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz [31]. The amplitude of the signals was normalized by dividing by the maximum amplitude value of each channel, so that the amplitude range of all channels was unified [32]. The time axis was normalized to the range of 0%–100% by linear interpolation, making signals of different lengths comparable in time [32]. This preprocessing pipeline, which consisted of band-pass filtering, rectification, low-pass smoothing, and normalization, ensured that the processed EMG matrix retained temporal integrity and cross-muscle amplitude comparability before being subjected to non-negative matrix factorization for synergy extraction.

Statistical analysis

In this study, SPSS 21.0 software was used for statistical analysis. First, the K-means clustering algorithm was applied to cluster the synergies before and after the experiment. On this basis, Pearson correlation analysis (with a threshold set at r = 0.6) was used to classify the extracted synergies. Synergies with r >0.6 were grouped together to identify muscle synergy types [39]. In the pre - and post - difference analysis, since the synergies involved by individuals before and after the experiment were not completely consistent, only the synergies that existed both before and after were selected for paired analysis. The specific methods are as follows: if the data conform to a normal distribution, paired - samples t - test is used to compare the differences of each index between the two groups; if the data does not conform to a normal distribution, Wilcoxon signed - rank test is used for comparison. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with differences considered statistically significant.

Directional change time and EMG indices

At the 45° direction change angle, there was a significant difference in time before and after the intervention (P = 0.027), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = −1.07), indicating that tDCS produced a substantial improvement in performance. At the 90° change of direction angle, there was no significant difference in time before and after the intervention (P = 0.140); however, the effect size was moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.46), suggesting that although the statistical significance was not reached, tDCS may still have had a meaningful practical influence on performance at this angle. Results of muscle synergy extraction for 45° directional change run

45° reference synergies

The study extracted three reference muscle synergies, and there were differences among the three reference muscle synergies in terms of their main activation times. Before tDCS, the muscle synergy structure characteristics of the 45° change-of-direction sprint showed that synergy 1 mainly involved the push-off phase, synergy 2 mainly involved the folding phase, and synergy 3 mainly involved the folding and landing buffering phase (see Fig. 3). Before tDCS, the muscle activation patterns of the three synergies exhibited distinct differences. In synergy 1, the weight of VL was the highest, and the muscles BF, RF, and GL also had relatively high contributions during the push-off phase. Discussion

In this study, we explored the impact of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) on lower-limb muscle control in soccer players during 45° and 90° change-of-direction (COD) runs. Our analysis focused on change-of-direction time, electromyographic indicators, and muscle synergies extracted using non-negative matrix factorization to uncover the underlying mechanisms of tDCS effects. The results revealed that the effects of tDCS were notably dependent on the angle. Specifically, in the 45° COD task, change-of-direction time was significantly reduced after stimulation (P = 0.027), while no comparable change was observed in the 90° task (P = 0.140). This difference likely reflects the varying complexity and motor demands of the tasks. Smaller-angle changes, such as the 45° turn, require more precise neuromuscular control, whereas larger-angle changes, like the 90° turn, demand greater overall force coordination and higher braking forces, which may reduce their sensitivity to short-term neural modulation. These findings are consistent with previous research in soccer and other sports, where sharper change-of-direction (COD) angles necessitated greater deceleration and re-acceleration forces, while larger angles involved more global force coordination [40, 41].

At the electromyographic level, neither root mean square (RMS) values nor integrated electromyography (iEMG) data showed significant changes (P > 0.05), indicating that the benefits of tDCS were not simply due to increased muscle activation. Rather, they seemed to be related to improved coordination strategies. This conclusion is consistent with the findings of Falch et al. [26, 42], who noted that smaller COD angles depend on more precise activation patterns, while larger angles involve broader activation profiles. When we further examined muscle synergy patterns, we observed specific adjustments in the 45° condition. In the W matrix, synergy 1 showed a significant decrease in the weight of the biceps femoris (BF, P = 0.041), while the contribution of the lateral gastrocnemius (GL) increased significantly (P = 0.043). This redistribution likely reduced unnecessary co-activation of antagonistic muscles, while enhancing ankle plantarflexion power, thereby improving movement efficiency and stability [41]. In the H matrix, synergy 3 showed a significant timing shift after stimulation (P = 0.020), with its activation peak occurring later and being more concentrated in the landing phase. This adjustment allowed the hamstrings to provide more precise eccentric control, improving ground-contact stability and potentially reducing injury risk by eliminating premature bursts of activity. These angle-dependent effects align with previous research examining the combined use of tDCS and motor training. For example, Song and Yim [43, 44] found that when tDCS was paired with plyometric exercises, it enhanced agility, balance, and lower-limb coordination, suggesting that tDCS can influence neuromuscular strategies during complex movements. Similarly, Wang et al. [45, 46] observed that tDCS improved muscle synergy timing and spatial patterns during soccer-specific tasks, such as penalty kicks, leading to more stable force transmission and better control during the support phases. From a neurophysiological standpoint, anodal tDCS over the primary motor cortex (M1) is known to increase cortical excitability by depolarizing neuronal resting membrane potentials, which can transiently enhance corticospinal output and motor unit recruitment efficiency [47, 48]. This cortical modulation increases the responsiveness of corticospinal pathways, potentially enhancing the synchronization of motor-unit firing and intermuscular coordination. Such modulation may facilitate improved temporal coordination across synergistic muscles, resulting in refined intermuscular control without necessarily increasing overall muscle activation amplitude. These mechanisms could explain the observed performance improvements in the 45° task, where fine-tuned motor coordination rather than global strength output plays a critical role. In contrast, in the 90° COD task, no significant changes were observed in either the W or H matrix. This suggests that more complex tasks require broader neuromuscular coordination, and a single session of tDCS may not be sufficient to induce significant neuromuscular reorganization in the short term. Additionally, the high braking and re-acceleration demands at 90° may rely on integrated trunk–hip–knee coordination, which involves multisite neural regulation extending beyond M1, such as premotor and cerebellar circuits [49]. Hence, the limited effect of M1 stimulation at this angle supports the task-specific and region-dependent nature of tDCS modulation. Furthermore, the responsiveness to tDCS may vary depending on athletes’ training level and fatigue state. Well-trained athletes often exhibit ceiling effects in motor efficiency, whereas fatigued or less-trained individuals may show greater susceptibility to cortical modulation. This factor should be controlled in future studies to ensure that interindividual variability does not obscure the true efficacy of tDCS. Taken together, these findings suggest that tDCS does not completely restructure muscle synergies but rather fine-tunes specific aspects of movement during critical phases while maintaining the overall central control framework. This selective adjustment mechanism may be the main reason for the observed performance enhancement in the 45° COD task, highlighting the task-specific and phase-selective nature of tDCS effects.

However, it is important to note several limitations of this study. The sample size was relatively small, and the participants were exclusively male soccer players, with no sham-control condition included. These factors limit the interpretability and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the absence of baseline reproducibility testing and the lack of direct neurophysiological measurements (such as motor evoked potentials or cortical excitability indices) prevent definitive conclusions about the causal neural mechanisms underlying the observed effects. The use of a commercial stimulation device (Halo Sport 2) without finite element modeling of current distribution also introduces potential uncertainty regarding the effective stimulation of bilateral M1 regions. Future studies should recruit larger and more diverse samples, including female athletes and participants from various sports, and employ randomized sham-controlled or crossover designs with follow-up assessments. Consideration of fatigue and training status is essential to isolate tDCS effects. Integrating electrophysiological measures (e.g., MEP, EEG, fNIRS) can verify cortical modulation, while longitudinal or multi-session interventions can clarify the persistence of effects. Systematic comparison of bilateral, unilateral, and cerebellar tDCS protocols may reveal the optimal montage for task-specific neuromuscular enhancement [50].

Overall, the present study provides preliminary evidence that anodal tDCS over bilateral M1 can acutely enhance motor coordination efficiency during 45° change-of-direction tasks by refining the temporal and spatial structure of muscle synergies. Previous studies suggest that tDCS effects may last 30–60 min, indicating its potential use before training or competition.While the observed improvements appear promising, they should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size and methodological constraints. Continued research with rigorous experimental designs will help clarify the robustness, neural basis, and practical application of tDCS in sports performance and rehabilitation.

Conclusions

tDCS reduced 45° change-of-direction (COD) completion time by approximately 3.2% (p = 0.027, d = −1.07) by optimizing lower-limb neuromuscular synergy, yet it has no effect on 90° cutting performance, demonstrating its angle specificity and central fine-tuning advantage, and thus serving as an effective neuro-augmentation tool for improving cutting performance at selected angles.

From a practical perspective, anodal tDCS over bilateral primary motor cortices (M1) at 2 mA for 20 min may be applied as a short-term neuromodulatory intervention before training or competition, considering individual variability in responsiveness.
