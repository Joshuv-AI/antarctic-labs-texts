Particle, kinetic and hydrodynamic models for sea ice floes. Part I: Non-rotating floes
Quanling Deng, Seung-Yeal Ha

Abstract
We introduce a comprehensive modeling framework for the dynamics of sea ice floes using particle, kinetic, and hydrodynamic approaches. Building upon the foundational work of Ha and Tadmor on the Cucker-Smale model for flocking, we derive a Vlasov-type kinetic formulation and a corresponding hydrodynamic description. The particle model incorporates essential physical properties of sea ice floes, including size, position, velocity, and interactions governed by Newtonian mechanics. By extending these principles, the kinetic model captures large-scale features through the phase-space distribution, and we also present a hydrodynamic model using the velocity moments and a suitable closure condition. In this paper, as an idea-introductory step, we assume that ice floes are non-rotating and focus on the linear velocity dynamics. Our approach highlights the role of contact forces, ocean drag effects, and conservation laws in the multiscale description of sea ice dynamics, offering a potential pathway for the improved understanding and prediction of sea ice behaviors in changing climatic conditions.

Introduction

The dynamics of sea ice play a pivotal role in Earth’s climate system, influencing ocean circulation, heat exchange, and ecological processes in polar regions. The behavior of sea ice is complex, shaped by interactions between individual ice sheets/floes and environmental forces such as atmospheric wind, ocean currents, and temperature gradients. To better understand and predict these dynamics, a variety of modeling approaches have been developed, ranging from continuum hydrodynamic models
[
13
,
32
,
36
]
to particle (discrete element) models
[
12
,
29
,
34
,
43
,
45
]
.

The accurate simulation of sea ice dynamics is critical for understanding polar climate systems, particularly in the Marginal Ice Zone (MIZ), where sea ice transitions to open ocean. In such regions, the ice cover is highly fragmented and best described as a collection of interacting floes rather than as a continuous medium. Traditional continuum-based models
[
48
,
32
,
47
,
19
,
38
]
, while successful in capturing large-scale behaviors of compact ice packs, rely on the continuum assumption.
However, continuum assumption may break down at both small scale, where individual floe interactions are dominant, and large scale, where floe heterogeneity and fracture patterns become significant.
In contrast, particle-based models
[
34
,
60
,
29
,
14
]
represent sea ice as a discrete entity and offer a more physically consistent framework for capturing the granular nature of floe dynamics, including collisions, fracturing, and ridging. These models are particularly well-suited for studying MIZ processes, where the mechanical and thermodynamic behaviors of individual floes play a pivotal role in determining the evolution of the ice cover. Developing and refining particle-based simulations is thus essential for bridging different scales and improving predictive capabilities in sea ice modeling
[
3
,
21
,
37
]
.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of multiscale approaches in capturing the essential features of sea ice dynamics. Particle models, such as those inspired by the Cucker-Smale flocking dynamics, offer an analytical framework for understanding interactions at the particle level while enabling the derivation of continuum models via the corresponding kinetic equation
[
26
]
. These models incorporate fundamental physical properties such as mass, position, and velocity, and account for forces including inter-floe contact forces and environmental drag. Building on this foundation, kinetic formulations provide a bridge to hydrodynamic descriptions from particle descriptions, allowing for the integration of large-scale features into a cohesive particle-continuum multiscale framework
[
15
]
.

Kinetic theory provides a foundational framework for bridging small-scale particle dynamics and large-scale continuum descriptions in complex systems.
Originating from the statistical mechanics of gases, kinetic equation models the evolution of one-particle distribution function in phase space, and it has been successful in the derivation of fluid equations such as the Navier–Stokes and Euler equations
[
9
,
22
,
23
,
53
]
.
A central analytical tool in this transition is the BBGKY hierarchy (Bogoliubov–Born–Green–Kirkwood–Yvon,
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
40
,
61
]
), which systematically connects the Liouville equation to limiting marginal distribution functions. By incorporating appropriate closure assumptions and scaling limits, such as the Boltzmann–Grad limit, one can obtain kinetic equations like the Boltzmann equation, from which hydrodynamic limits can be derived via the Chapman–Enskog and Hilbert expansions, moment methods, or entropy-based approaches.
In recent decades, kinetic theory has been extended beyond classical gases to a wide range of interacting particle systems, including flocking models
[
25
,
26
,
28
,
49
]
and granular media
[
16
,
17
,
33
]
.

In sea ice floe dynamics, kinetic theory provides a powerful framework for bridging the gap between discrete floe-level interactions and continuum-scale descriptions of sea ice dynamics and rheology. In this work, we initiate a rigorous systematic investigation into the application of kinetic theory to sea ice modeling by introducing a particle-to-continuum pathway that captures the multiscale nature of floe dynamics.
As a foundational step, we consider a simplified set-up involving non-rotating and colliding cylindrical floes.
Cylindrical floes are widely used in discrete element modeling (DEM; see, for example,
[
12
,
29
]
).
This idealized model allows us to focus on the essential features of floe interactions while establishing a rigorous mathematical framework for the development of a continuum hydrodynamic model.
We consider ocean drag forces and assume one-way coupling that the ocean drags the floes but the floe does not impact the ocean. We ignore atmospheric drag forces for simplicity. They can be included, and the derivations remain in a similar fashion to the case of ocean drag.
We adopt distribution laws for initializing the floes, namely power-law distributions
[
1
,
52
,
46
]
for floe sizes and gamma distributions
[
7
,
59
]
for floe thickness.
Evolution models for the floe size distribution
[
50
,
35
]
and for the floe thickness distribution
[
58
,
57
]
can be incorporated into the particle framework to account for processes such as melting and refreezing.
Using a particle model
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
41
]
as the foundation and kinetic theory as a tool, we derive a Vlasov-type kinetic model and the corresponding hydrodynamic model.
With the particle model and an assumption that the particle number is sufficiently large, we derive the kinetic description based on the distribution function in phase space consisting of the radius, thickness, displacement, and velocity of sea ice floes. Then, the equations of lower-order moments are derived by integrating phase space, which captures the large-scale features of the particles and serves as the coarse-scale continuum model.
This approach highlights the influence of contact forces, drag effects, and conservation laws on the multiscale dynamics of sea ice, paving the way for more realistic and predictive models in future.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the particle model for colliding and non-rotating sea ice floes in an idealized setting. It is often referred to as the discrete element model (see Sec.
2.1
). Then we establish several results for the asymptotic behavior of the dynamics. In Section
3
and Section
4
, under the assumption of a large number of floes, we develop the Vlasov-type kinetic model, followed by further development for large scales of the dynamics captured by a hydrodynamic model. In both kinetic and hydrodynamical models, we study the asymptotic behavior of total momentum and energy. In Section
5
, we present several numerical simulations in various settings to verify our theoretical findings for the particle model. Finally, Section
6
is devoted to a brief summary of main results and some remaining issues for future work.

2
Particle description for ice floes

In this section, we first present an idealized particle model for sea ice floes following recent works
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
29
]
, and then we introduce the LaSalle invariance principle, which serves as a tool for the establishment of an asymptotic result on floe velocities.
Our main result is the relaxation of floe velocities to ocean velocity (see Theorem (
2.7
)) in Section
2.3
, which establishes the dynamics of the total momentum and energies of the particles in the entire system.

2.1
The particle model

Consider non-rotating and colliding ice floes with the geometry of cylinders.
Given a system of
n
floes,
we denote by
r^{i}
the radius and
h^{i}
the thickness (or height) of the
i
-th floe with
i\in[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.
The radius and thickness characterize the floe size.
In a realistic sea ice floe setting (particularly in marginal ice zones), the floe size follows a power law distribution
[
55
]
, while the floe thickness distribution usually follows a Gamma distribution (see, for example,
[
7
]
for the Arctic region and
[
59
]
for the Antarctic region).
The mass of the
i
-th floe is
m^{i}=\rho_{ice}\pi(r^{i})^{2}h^{i}
, where the constant
\rho_{ice}
is the density of sea ice floes. The floe position is denoted by
\bm{x}^{i}=(x^{i},y^{i})^{T}
and the floe velocity is
\bm{v}^{i}=(u^{i},v^{i})^{T}
.
Lastly, let
\bm{u}_{o}=\bm{u}_{o}(x,y)
be the given ocean surface velocity.
The governing equations of non-rotating-colliding sea ice floe dynamics are given by Newton’s equations:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\bm{v}^{i},\quad i\in[n],\\
m^{i}\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|=:\bm{F}^{i},\end{aligned}

(2.1)

where
|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}|
is the
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
\alpha^{i}=\pi\rho_{o}(2C_{v,o}r^{i}\cdot D^{i}+C_{h,o}(r^{i})^{2})
is the drag coefficient and
\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}
(in default,
i\neq j
; we assume
\bm{f}_{c}^{ii}=0
for notational simplicity) specifies the contact force.

\rho_{o}
is ocean density,
D^{i}
is the ice-floe draft (the part of the ice floe below the water surface),
C_{v,o}
is ocean vertical drag coefficients, and
C_{h,o}
is ocean horizontal drag coefficients. By default, we set
D^{i}=0.9h^{i}.

The equations (
2.1
) describe the dynamics of each flow particle.
They are the equations of motion following Newton’s law.
We scale the summation of the contact force by the factor of
1/n
to apply the mean-field theory for deriving the kinetic and hydrodynamic models.
For a system with a fixed number of floes, this scaling factor is understood as a factor of the mass and drag coefficient.
Herein, for simplicity, we consider non-rotating ice floes and assume normal contact forces.
The floe contact force is nonzero when two floes are in contact, i.e., when

\delta^{ij}:=|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|-(r^{i}+r^{j})<0,\quad i\neq j,\quad i,j\in[n].

We follow the Hertz contact theory
[
31
,
51
]
and adopt a simple model for the normal contact force (see the supplementary material of
[
29
]
):

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}:=\Big(\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}+\kappa^{ij}_{2}(\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\Big)\bm{n}^{ij},\quad\kappa^{ij}_{1}:=\frac{\pi}{4}E_{e}h^{ij}_{e},\quad\kappa^{ij}_{2}:=\beta\sqrt{\frac{5\pi}{4}E_{e}h^{ij}_{e}m^{ij}_{e}},

(2.2)

where
\bm{n}^{ij}:=\frac{\bm{x}^{j}-\bm{x}^{i}}{|\bm{x}^{j}-\bm{x}^{i}|}
is the unit normal vector pointing from the center of floe
i
to floe
j
,
E_{e}
is the effective contact modulus,
h^{ij}_{e}:=\min\{h^{i},h^{j}\}
is the effective contact thickness (part of the thickness in contact),
m^{ij}_{e}:=\frac{m^{i}m^{j}}{m^{i}+m^{j}}
is the effective mass, and

\beta:=\frac{\ln e_{r}}{\sqrt{\ln^{2}e_{r}+\pi^{2}}}<0.

(2.3)

Here
e_{r}
is the restitution coefficient in
[0,1)
and system parameters
\kappa^{ij}_{1},\kappa^{ij}_{2},h^{ij}_{e}
and
m^{ij}_{e}
depend on the pairs of floe
i
and
j
and
E_{e}
is constant for all pairs.
Details are referred to equations (1), (8), (20), and (26) in the supplementary material of
[
29
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

The floe contact model is, in general, more complicated than (
2.2
).
Herein, we consider a simple model to initiate the derivation of kinetic and hydrodynamic descriptions.
The restitution coefficient for ice floes is the measure of kinetic energy loss during floe collisions. The coefficient is usually a positive real number between 0 and 1. A value of 0 indicates a perfectly inelastic collision, while a value of 1 indicates a perfectly elastic collision. The typical values of
e_{r}
for sea ice are between 0.1 and 0.3; see
[
42
]
. In ice floe studies, values between 0 and 1 are often employed; see, for example
[
30
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

The present model (
2.1
) is an idealized floe particle model that captures the main feature of floe–floe collisions and ocean drag forces. Environmental effects can be included. For example, atmospheric wind forcing can be modeled as an additional drag term acting on the floes, with direction and magnitude determined by wind fields
[
12
,
29
]
. Thermodynamic processes such as melting or freezing can be incorporated through source terms that modify floe thickness and size distributions
[
56
,
58
,
52
]
. These extensions would enable the model to capture the influence of changing climatic conditions on floe evolution and sea ice rheology. Herein, including these extensions will make the model much more complicated, and we consider this idealized model for the simplicity of the rigorous development of particle-to-continuum models. The development of the extensions is subject to future work.

2.2
The LaSalle invariance principle

In this subsection, we recall the LaSalle invariance principle
[
39
,
27
]
to be used in later sections.
Consider the following Cauchy problem for the continuous dynamical system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\begin{cases}{\dot{\bm{x}}}=\bm{F}(\bm{x}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:=(0,\infty),\\
\bm{x}\Big|_{t=0}=\bm{x}_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Herein, we abuse the notation
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for generality. In the sequel, we denote the solution for the Cauchy problem (
2.4
) by
\varphi_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})
, and we call
\{\varphi_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\}
the orbit issued from
\bm{x}_{0}
. Next, we introduce the definition of
\omega
-limit set and recall its basic properties.

Definition 2.3
.

The
\omega
-limit set of
\bm{x}_{0}
, denoted by
\omega(\bm{x}_{0})
, is defined as the collection of all limit(accumulation) points of the orbit
\{\varphi_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\}_{t\geq 0}
:

\omega(\bm{x}_{0}):=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~\exists~\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\quad\mbox{such that}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{t_{n}}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{x}\right\}.

Then, it is well known that the
\omega
-limit set is closed, positively invariant, and connected. If the orbit
\{\varphi_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\}
is uniformly bounded in
t
, then it must have a limit point by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem. Thus,
\omega(\bm{x}_{0})
is nonempty.

Now, we are ready to state the LaSalle invariance principle, which asserts the asymptotic stability of an equilibrium point to (
2.4
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that the vector field
\bm{F}
and a nonempty open set
\Omega
satisfy the following conditions:

1.

\bm{F}
is locally Lipschitz continuous vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

Let
L:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable functional such that its orbital derivative along the flow generated by (
2.4
) is nonpositive:

{\dot{L}}(\bm{x})=\nabla L\cdot F(\bm{x})\leq 0\quad\mbox{for all $\bm{x}\in\Omega$}.

3.

{\omega(\bm{x})}
is bounded and contained in
\Omega
.

Then, the trajectory
\phi_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})
approaches to the largest invariant set contained in
\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~\dot{L}(\bm{x})=0\}
.

2.3
Asymptotic behavior

In this subsection, we study the asymptotic behavior of the particle model (
2.1
) using the energy estimate and the LaSalle invariance principle in Proposition
2.4
. We assume that the mass of each floe does not change over time (thus, no melting, freezing, or fracturing). First, we define the floe moments:

\displaystyle M_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i},\qquad\bm{M}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{v}^{i},\qquad M_{2}=M_{2,\bm{v}}+M_{2,\bm{x}},

(2.5)

\displaystyle M_{2,\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2},\qquad M_{2,\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{1}(\delta^{ij})^{2},

where
M_{2,\bm{x}}
,
M_{2,\bm{v}}
, and
M_{2}
represent total normal strain energy (the fraction
\frac{1}{4}
is due to the repetition), total translational kinetic energy,
and total energy, respectively. By definition,
\kappa^{ij}_{1}
depends on the thickness of the floe pairs; thus,
\kappa^{ij}_{1}
is not a uniform constant and is not factored out of the summation in
M_{2,\bm{x}}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

(Propagation of velocity moments)

Let
(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i})
be a global solution to the system (
2.1
). Then, the following assertions hold.

1.

The first and second velocity moments satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(i)~\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{M}_{1}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|.\\
&(ii)~\frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}|(\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\cdot\bm{v}^{i}.\end{aligned}

(2.6)

2.

If
\alpha^{i}=0,\forall~i\in[n],
then there exist positive constants
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
such that

M_{2}(t)\geq M_{2}(0)e^{-A_{0}t}+\frac{A_{1}}{A_{0}}|\bm{M}_{1}(0)|^{2}\Big(1-e^{-A_{0}t}\Big).

Proof.

(i) For the first assertion, we first note that the relations

\delta^{ij}=\delta^{ji},\quad\bm{n}^{ij}=-\bm{n}^{ji},\quad i,j\in[n],\quad i\neq j

to see the anti-symmetry of floe-floe contact force:

\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}=-\bm{f}_{c}^{ji},\quad i,j\in[n].

(2.7)

Now, we use (
2.1
), (
2.5
), and (
2.7
) to find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{M}_{1}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\frac{{\,\rm d}}{{\,\rm d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{v}^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|.\end{aligned}

For the second assertion, we take an inner product of
\eqref{eq:dem}_{2}
with
\bm{v}^{i}
and sum up the resulting equations over all
i\in[n]
to get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2,\bm{v}}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}\cdot\bm{v}^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\Big)\cdot\bm{v}^{i}\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}+\kappa_{2}(\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\Big)\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot\bm{v}^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\cdot\bm{v}^{i}\\
&=:{\mathcal{I}}_{11}+{\mathcal{I}}_{12}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\cdot\bm{v}^{i}.\end{aligned}

(2.8)

Below, we estimate the terms
{\mathcal{I}}_{1i},.~i=1,2
one by one.

\bullet
Case A.1: We use
\eqref{eq:dem}_{1}
and the relations:

\delta^{ij}=\delta^{ji},\quad\bm{n}^{ij}=-\bm{n}^{ji}

to rewrite

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{11}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{1}\Big(|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|-(r^{i}+r^{j})\Big)\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{1}\Big(|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|-(r^{i}+r^{j})\Big)\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{j}}{{\,\rm d}t}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{1}\Big(|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|-(r^{i}+r^{j})\Big)\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot\frac{{\,\rm d}(\bm{x}^{j}-\bm{x}^{i})}{{\,\rm d}t}\\

(2.9)

where we used the following identity:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}\delta^{ij}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\frac{{\,\rm d}}{{\,\rm d}t}\big(|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|-(r^{i}+r^{j})\big)=\frac{{\,\rm d}}{{\,\rm d}t}\sqrt{(x^{j}-x^{i})^{2}+(y^{j}-y^{i})^{2}}\\

(2.10)

\bullet
Case A.2: Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{12}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}\Big(((\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij})\bm{n}^{ij}\Big)\cdot\bm{v}^{i}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}\Big(((\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij})\bm{n}^{ij}\Big)\cdot\bm{v}^{j}\\

(2.11)

In (
2.8
), we combine (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) to arrive at the desired estimates.

(ii) Suppose that

\alpha^{i}=0,\quad\forall~i\in[n].

Then, the relation
(
2.6
)
reduces to

\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{M}_{1}}{{\,\rm d}t}=0,\quad\frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}\big((\bm{v}^{j}-\bm{v}^{i})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)^{2}.

(2.12)

On the other hand, by
(
2.2
)
and
\beta<0
as in (

), we have

\kappa^{ij}_{2}=\beta\sqrt{\frac{5\pi}{4}E_{e}h^{ij}_{e}m^{ij}_{e}}<0.

Together with (

\frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}\leq 0,\quad\mbox{i.e., total energy is dissipative.}

Now, we use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the boundedness of the mass of floe particles to see that there exists a positive
A_{0}
as the floe particle radii, thickness, and masses are fixed for a fixed system with
n
floes (recalling
\kappa^{ij}_{2}<0
for all pairs of colliding floes) such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\big((\bm{v}^{j}-\bm{v}^{i})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)^{2}\\
&\geq-\frac{1}{2n}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\bm{v}^{j}-\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2n}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(|\bm{v}^{j}|^{2}+|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}-2\bm{v}^{i}\cdot\bm{v}^{j}\Big)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2n}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)\Big(2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}-2\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{v}^{i}\Big|^{2}\Big)\\
&=-\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{v}^{i}\Big|^{2}\\
&=-\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big){\mathcal{I}}_{21}+\frac{1}{n}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big){\mathcal{I}}_{22}.\end{aligned}

(2.13)

Next, we estimate the terms
{\mathcal{I}}_{2i},i=1,2,
one by one.

\bullet
(Estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}_{21}
): Note that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{21}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m^{i}}{m^{i}}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\min_{i}m^{i}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2}\Big)=\frac{2}{\min_{i}m^{i}}M_{2,\bm{v}}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{\min_{i}m^{i}}M_{2}.\end{aligned}

(2.14)

\bullet
(Estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}_{22}
): We use (
2.12
) to see

{\mathcal{I}}_{22}=\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m^{i}}{m^{i}}\bm{v}^{i}\Big|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{v}^{i}\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}}|\bm{M}_{1}(0)|^{2}.

(2.15)

We combine (
2.13
), (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}&\geq-\frac{2\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)}{\min_{i}m^{i}}M_{2}+\frac{1}{nM_{0}^{2}}\Big(\max_{i,j}|\kappa^{ij}_{2}|\Big)|\bm{M}_{1}(0)|^{2}\\
&=:-A_{0}M_{2}+A_{1}|\bm{M}_{1}(0)|^{2}.\end{aligned}

Then, Grönwall’s lemma
[
24
]
yields the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Similar to the result
[
26
]
for the Cucker-Smale particle model, if the drag force is absent (i.e.,
\alpha^{i}=0
), Proposition
2.5
tells that the total energy
M_{2}
is monotonically decreasing with a lower bound.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose that the given ocean surface velocity is constant:

\bm{u}_{o}^{i}=\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}:~\mbox{constant},\quad\forall~i\in[n],

and let
(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i})
be a global solution to the system (
2.1
). Then, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}\|=0,\quad\forall~i\in[n].

Proof.

Due to the translational invariance of system (
2.1
), we may assume that

\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}\equiv 0.

In this setting, the system (
2.1
) becomes

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\bm{v}^{i},\quad i\in[n],\\
\displaystyle m^{i}\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}-\alpha^{i}\bm{v}^{i}|\bm{v}^{i}|.\end{cases}

Then, we claim that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\bm{v}^{i}(t)=0.

(2.16)

Proof of (
2.16
)
: It follows from Lemma
2.5
that

\frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}\big((\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left|\bm{v}^{i}\right|^{3}.

Note that for all pairs of colliding floes,
\kappa^{ij}_{2}<0.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}M_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}=0\quad&\iff\quad(\bm{v}^{j}-\bm{v}^{i})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha^{i}\left|\bm{v}^{i}\right|^{3}=0,\quad\forall~i,j\in[n]\\
&\iff\quad\bm{x}^{i}:~\mbox{arbitrary},\quad\bm{v}^{i}=0\quad\forall~i\in[n].\end{aligned}

Hence we have

S=\Big\{(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n},\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}^{4n}}:~{\dot{M}}_{2}=0\Big\}=\Big\{(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n},0,\ldots,0)\Big\}.

Now, we take
M_{2}
as the Lyapunov functional for Proposition
2.4
to see that the flow converges to the largest invariant subset of
S
. Thus, we have the desired estimate.
∎

3
From particle to kinetic description

In this section, we first recall the formal derivation from the particle model to the kinetic model as the mean-field approximation of the particle model with
n\gg 1
.
We assume that the number of particles involved in the particle system (
2.1
) is sufficiently large so that it becomes meaningful to use the mean-field approximation via the one-particle distribution function to describe the overall effective dynamics of the original system.

3.1
Kinetic model for ice floe dynamics

We first rewrite the floe particle model (
2.1
) as

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\bm{v}^{i},\quad i\in[n],\\
\displaystyle\frac{{\,\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\,\rm d}t}=\frac{1}{m^{i}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\Big]=:\frac{\bm{F}^{i}}{m^{i}},\\
\displaystyle\frac{dr^{i}}{dt}=0,\quad\frac{dh^{i}}{dt}=0,\end{cases}

(3.1)

where we assume that the floe sizes and thicknesses do not change in time. In what follows, we adopt the method of the BBGKY hierarchy to derive a kinetic equation for the one-particle distribution function over the generalized phase space
\mathbb{R}_{\bm{x}}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{\bm{v}}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

\bullet
Step A (Derivation of the Liouville equation for
n
-particle distribution function): First, we define
n
-particle distribution function over the
n
-particle phase space
\mathbb{R}^{4n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}
:

F^{n}=F^{n}(t,\bm{x}^{1},\bm{v}^{1},r^{1},h^{1},\cdots,\bm{x}^{n},\bm{v}^{n},r^{n},h^{n}),

(3.2)

for
(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i},r^{i},h^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Note that the
n
-particle probability density function
F^{n}
is symmetric in its phase variable in the sense that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &F^{n}(t,\cdots,\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i},r^{i},h^{i},\cdots,\bm{x}^{j},\bm{v}^{j},r^{j},h^{j},\cdots)\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt=F^{n}(t,\cdots,\bm{x}^{j},\bm{v}^{j},r^{j},h^{j},\cdots,\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i},r^{i},h^{i},\cdots).\end{aligned}

(3.3)

Then,
F^{n}
satisfies the Liouville equation on the generalized
n
-particle phase space:

\partial_{t}F^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\dot{\bm{x}}^{i}F^{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot(\dot{\bm{v}}^{i}F^{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{r^{i}}(\dot{r}^{i}F^{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{h^{i}}(\dot{h}^{i}F^{n})=0,

(3.4)

where
\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(~~)
and
\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot(~~)
denote the divergences in
\bm{x}^{i}
and
\bm{v}^{i}
-variables, respectively.
By using (
3.1
), the above system can be also written as

\partial_{t}F^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{v}^{i}\cdot\partial_{\bm{x}^{i}}F^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big)=0.

(3.5)

\bullet
Step B (Derivation of equation for
j
-particle distribution function): For notational simplicity, we set

\bm{z}:=(\bm{x},\bm{v},r,h),\quad d\bm{z}^{j}=d\bm{x}^{j}d\bm{v}^{j}dr^{j}dh^{j},\quad j\in[n],\quad{\,\rm d}E^{n:j}=\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}d\bm{z}^{i}.

Then, we introduce
j
-marginal distribution function
F^{n:j}
by the integration of (
3.2
):

F^{n:j}(t,\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{j},\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{j},r^{1},\ldots,r^{j},h^{1},\ldots,h^{j}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}F^{n}{\,\rm d}E^{n:j}.

Next, we derive an equation for
F^{n:j}
. For this, we rewrite (
3.5
) as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}F^{n}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{i}F^{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big)-\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{j}F^{n})\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big).\end{aligned}

(3.6)

Now, we integrate the Liouville equation (
3.6
) over

(\bm{x}^{j+1},\cdots,\bm{x}^{n},\bm{v}^{j+1},\cdots,\bm{v}^{n},r^{j+1},.\cdots,r^{n},h^{j+1},\cdots,h^{n})

to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}F^{n:j}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{i}F^{n}){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&-\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{j}F^{n}){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&-\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&=:{\mathcal{I}}_{21}+{\mathcal{I}}_{22}+{\mathcal{I}}_{23}+{\mathcal{I}}_{24}.\end{aligned}

(3.7)

In the next lemma, we estimate the terms
{\mathcal{I}}_{2i},~i=1,\cdots,4
, one by one.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
F^{n}
be a global solution to (
3.4
) which decays to zero sufficiently fast at
|\bm{x}^{i}|=\infty
and
|\bm{v}^{i}|=\infty
. Then, we have the following estimates:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(i)~{\mathcal{I}}_{21}=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\bm{v}^{i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}F^{n:j},\quad{\mathcal{I}}_{23}=0,\quad{\mathcal{I}}_{24}=0.\\
&(ii)~{\mathcal{I}}_{22}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big)F^{n:j}\Big]\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt-\frac{n-j}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\bm{f}_{c}^{i(j+1)}F^{n:j+1}\Big)d\bm{z}^{j+1}\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{n:j}\Big).\end{aligned}

Proof.

(i) It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{21}=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{i}F^{n}){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}\cdot(\bm{v}^{i}F^{n:j}).\end{aligned}

On the other hand, we use the divergence theorem and decay condition of
F^{n}
at infinity to find

{\mathcal{I}}_{23}=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{I}}_{24}=0.

(ii) Now, we use (
3.3
) to see that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{22}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\bf F^{i}}{m^{i}}F^{n}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{F^{n}}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&\hskip 5.69046pt-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{n}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&=:{\mathcal{I}}_{221}+{\mathcal{I}}_{222}.\end{aligned}

(3.8)

Below, we estimate the terms
{\mathcal{I}}_{22i},i=1,2,
one by one.

\diamond
Case B.1 (Estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}_{221}
): We use (
3.3
) to find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{221}&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{F^{n}}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&\hskip 5.69046pt-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{F^{n}}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big)F^{n:j}\Big]\\
&-\frac{n-j}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\bm{f}_{c}^{i(j+1)}F^{n:j+1}\Big)d\bm{z}^{j+1}.\end{aligned}

(3.9)

\diamond
Case B.2 (Estimate of
{\mathcal{I}}_{222}
): For
i\leq j
, since
\bm{v}^{i}
is independent of
z^{j+1},\cdots,z^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{I}}_{222}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4(n-j)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2(n-j)}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{n}\Big){\,\rm d}E^{n:j}\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{n:j}\Big).\end{aligned}

(3.10)

In (
3.8
), we combine (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) to find the desired estimate.
∎

In (
3.7
), we use Lemma
3.1
to find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}F^{n:j}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\bm{v}^{i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}F^{n:j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\bm{f}_{c}^{ik}\Big)F^{n:j}\Big]\\
&-\frac{n-j}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\bm{f}_{c}^{i(j+1)}F^{n:j+1}\Big)d\bm{z}^{j+1}\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{n:j}\Big).\end{aligned}

For a fixed
j
, let
n\to\infty
and we assume that the exists a limit
F^{j}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{n:j}=F^{j}\quad\mbox{in suitable sense}.

Then, formally as
n\to\infty
, the limit
F^{j}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F^{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\bm{v}^{i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}^{i}}F^{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{m^{i}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|F^{j}\Big)\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{v}^{i}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m^{i}}\bm{f}_{c}^{i(j+1)}F^{j+1}\Big)d\bm{z}^{j+1}=0.\end{aligned}

(3.11)

Note that the dynamics of
F^{j}
in (
3.11
) depends on
F^{j+1}
. In particular, for
j=1
, we remove the superscript for simplicity and set

F:=F^{1},\quad\bm{x}:=\bm{x}^{1},\quad\bm{v}:=\bm{v}^{1},\quad\bm{u}_{o}=\bm{u}_{o}^{1},\quad\alpha:=\alpha^{1},\quad m:=m^{1}.

Then, it satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}F+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\alpha\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m}\left(\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right|F\Big)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{m}\bm{f}_{c}^{12}F^{2}\Big)d\bm{z}^{2}=0.\end{aligned}

(3.12)

\bullet
Step C (Formal derivation of kinetic equation for one-particle distribution function): We assume the “molecular chaos assumption" (see, for example,
[
54
]
) by setting

F^{2}(t,\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})=F(t,\bm{z})\otimes F(t,\bm{z}^{*}).

(3.13)

Finally, we substitute the ansatz (
3.13
) into (
3.12
) to get the kinetic equation for
F
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\frac{\alpha}{m}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\big(\left(\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right|F\big)\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big[\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\bm{f}_{c}^{12}F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}\Big)F(t,\bm{z})\Big]=0.\end{aligned}

(3.14)

Note that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{f}_{c}^{12}&=\kappa_{1}\chi(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*}))\bm{n}+\kappa_{2}\big((\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}\big)\bm{n},\\
\alpha&=\pi\rho_{o}(2C_{v,o}r\cdot D+C_{h,o}r^{2}),\quad m=\rho_{ice}\pi r^{2}h,\end{aligned}

(3.15)

where
\bm{n}=\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})=\frac{\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|}
for notational simplicity and
\chi
is a function defined as

\chi(\xi)=\begin{cases}\xi,\quad\text{when}\ \xi<0,\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3.16)

In what follows, we use handy notation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\gamma_{o}:=\gamma_{r,h,o}:=\frac{\alpha}{m},\quad\gamma_{\bm{n}}:=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m},\quad\gamma_{\bm{v}}:=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{m},\quad\bm{f}[F]=\bm{f}_{o}+\bm{f}_{c}[F],\\
&\bm{f}_{c}[F]=\bm{f}_{c,\bm{n}}[F]+\bm{f}_{c,\bm{v}}[F],\quad\bm{f}_{o}:=\gamma_{o}\left(\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}\right|,\\
&\bm{f}_{c,\bm{n}}[F](t,\bm{z}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\gamma_{\bm{n}}\chi(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*}))\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*},\\
&\bm{f}_{c,\bm{v}}[F](t,\bm{z}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\gamma_{\bm{v}}[(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})]\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}.\end{aligned}

(3.17)

Finally, we substitute (
3.15
) and (
3.17
) into (
3.14
) to find the Vlasov-McKean equation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}F+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big[\bm{f}_{o}F\Big]+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big[\bm{f}_{c,\bm{n}}[F]F\Big]+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot\Big[\bm{f}_{c,\bm{v}}[F]F\Big]=0,\end{aligned}

(3.18)

or equivalently,

\partial_{t}F+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F)=0.

(3.19)

Note that the equation (
3.19
) is Galilean invariant.

3.2
Macroscopic behavior of the kinetic model

From now on, as long as there is no confusion, we suppress
t
-dependence in
F
:

F(\bm{z})\equiv F(t,\bm{z}),\quad\bm{z}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}}.

Next, we define the energy functional as follows.

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}_{K}+\mathcal{E}_{P},\quad\mathcal{E}_{K}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|^{2}F(\bm{z}){\,\rm d}\bm{z},\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{8}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}}\gamma_{\bm{n}}\Big(\int_{0}^{|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*})}\chi(\eta)d\eta\Big)F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}d\bm{z}^{*}.\end{cases}

(3.20)

With the definition (
3.16
) in mind, the functional
\mathcal{E}_{P}
becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}:=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{8}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}}\gamma_{\bm{n}}\chi(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*}))^{2}F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}d\bm{z}^{*}\geq 0.

(3.21)

Then, it is easy to see that component functionals in
\mathcal{E}
are also nonnegative, hence the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
is nonnegative. In the following two lemmas, we establish the temporal evolution of
\mathcal{E}_{K}
and
\mathcal{E}_{P}
as follows.

Lemma 3.2

(Macroscopic behavior)
.

&\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|^{3}F(\bm{z}){\,\rm d}\bm{z}\Big)^{\frac{2}{3}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}F(\bm{z}){\,\rm d}\bm{z}\Big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|^{3}F(\bm{z}){\,\rm d}\bm{z}\Big)^{\frac{2}{3}}.\end{aligned}

(3.46)

Finally, we use (
3.45
) and (
3.46
) to find the desired estimate:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{K}(t)=0.

∎

Herein, we used the a priori compact support assumption. While we do not have proof that this compact assumption is valid for all times, such assumptions are commonly adopted in kinetic theory to ensure well-posedness and boundedness of moments (see, e.g.,
[
54
]
).

Remark 3.6
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|F(\bm{z})d\bm{z}\leq\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}F(\bm{z})d\bm{z}}=\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}_{K}(t)}.

Then, we use Theorem
3.5
and the above relation to find

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|F(\bm{z})d\bm{z}=0.

4
From kinetic to hydrodynamic description

In this section, we discuss the hydrodynamic description of the collective behaviors of many ice floes. For this, we derive hydrodynamic observables from the kinetic model (
3.19
) under a suitable closure condition:

\partial_{t}F+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\bm{v}F)+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F)=0.

(4.1)

For notational simplicity, we simply set

\bm{z}:=(\bm{v},r,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2},\quad d\bm{z}:=d\bm{v}drdh.

4.1
Hydrodynamic model for ice floe dynamics

We first introduce hydrodynamic observables corresponding to the velocity-radius-thickness averages of the one-particle distribution function
F={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F(t,\bm{x},\bm{z})}
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\rho(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}Fd\bm{z},\quad&\mbox{local mass density},\\
\displaystyle(\rho\bm{u})(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\bm{v}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z},\quad&\mbox{local momentum density},\\
\displaystyle(\rho E)(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z},\quad&\mbox{local energy density},\\
\displaystyle(\rho e)(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z},\quad&\mbox{local internal energy density},\\
\displaystyle({\rho E}_{K})(t,\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{2}(\rho|\bm{u}|^{2})(t,x),\quad&\mbox{local kinetic energy density}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Then, we use

\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}+\bm{u}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\bm{u}|^{2}+(\bm{v}-\bm{u})\cdot\bm{u}

to see the following relation:

\displaystyle\rho E

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{v}-\bm{u}|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|\bm{u}|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z}+\bm{u}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}(\bm{v}-\bm{u})F{\,\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=:\rho e+\rho E_{K}.

We also define the stress tensor
P=(p_{ij})
and heat flux

as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p_{ij}&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}(v_{i}-u_{i})(v_{j}-u_{j})F{\,\rm d}\bm{z},\quad\forall~i,j=1,2,\\
q_{i}&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}(v_{i}-u_{i})\|\bm{v}-\bm{u}\|^{2}F{\,\rm d}\bm{z}.\end{aligned}

Macroscopic behavior of the hydrodynamic model

5.1
Forward Euler scheme for the particle model

For
T\in(0,\infty)
, we divide the finite time interval
[0,T]
into
N_{t}
uniform time-step
\Delta t=T/N_{t}
. The discrete time points are denoted as
t_{l}=l\,\Delta t
for
l=0,1,\dots,N_{t}
. Let

X^{i}_{l}:=\bm{x}^{i}(t_{l})\quad\mbox{and}\quad V^{i}_{l}:=\bm{v}^{i}(t_{l})

denote the numerical solution of the position and velocity of particle
i
at time
t_{l}
. Let
U^{i}_{l}:=\bm{u}_{o}(t_{l},\bm{x}^{i})
denote the ocean velocity in the position
\bm{x}^{i}
at time
t_{l}
. Starting from initial conditions
X^{i}_{0}=\bm{x}^{i}(0)
and
V^{i}_{0}=\bm{v}^{i}(0)
, the forward Euler scheme updates the solution as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X^{i}_{l+1}&=X^{i}_{l}+\Delta t\,V^{i}_{l},\quad l=0,1,\dots,N_{t}-1,\\
V^{i}_{l+1}&=V^{i}_{l}+\frac{\Delta t}{m^{i}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}(t_{l})+\alpha^{i}(U^{i}_{l}-V^{i}_{l})|U^{i}_{l}-V^{i}_{l}|\right].\end{aligned}

(5.1)

The first equation
\eqref{eq:dem_fe}_{1}
updates the position using the current velocity, while the second equation
\eqref{eq:dem_fe}_{2}
updates the velocity using the force
\bm{F}^{i}(t_{l})
. Note that the forward Euler method is an explicit time integration scheme, where the right-hand side of the ODE is evaluated at time
t_{l}
and used to advance the solution to time
t_{l+1}
. The forward Euler scheme is explicit and first-order accurate in time. The local truncation error is
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
, and thus the global error is
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
, i.e.,

|X^{i}_{N_{t}}-\bm{x}^{i}(T)|=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t),\qquad|V^{i}_{N_{t}}-\bm{v}^{i}(T)|=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t).

(5.2)

We require a sufficiently small time-step
\Delta t
to ensure accuracy and numerical stability, and we adopt this method here for simplicity and computational efficiency.
Other numerical methods may be applied to solve the floe system.
The first-order accuracy of the numerical scheme ensures that the discretized simulation of our continuous-time particle model captures the essential qualitative features of sea ice floe dynamics described by (
2.1
). While higher-order schemes may offer improved precision, the current level of accuracy is sufficient to reproduce key behaviors of the ice floe dynamics as well as to validate the theoretical findings established in Section
2

Momentum and energy in a discrete setting

In this subsection, we study the temporal evolution of total momentum and energy and compare them with analytical results in Lemma

2.7

First, we define the discrete velocity moments as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{M}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}V^{i},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{2}=\mathcal{M}_{2,V}+\mathcal{M}_{2,X},\\

For the temporal evolution of total momentum, we use (

where the differential

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\,\rm d}\mathcal{M}_{2}}{{\,\rm d}t}&=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\kappa^{ij}_{2}\big((V^{i}_{l}-V^{j}_{l})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)^{2}\\
&\hskip 5.69046pt+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big(\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}+\kappa^{ij}_{2}(V^{i}_{l}-V^{j}_{l})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)\bm{n}^{ij}\cdot(V^{i}_{l+1}-V^{i}_{l})\\
&\hskip 5.69046pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\alpha^{i}(U^{i}_{l}-V^{i}_{l})|U^{i}_{l}-V^{i}_{l}|\Big)\cdot(V^{i}_{l+1}+V^{i}_{l}).\end{aligned}

(5.5)

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have introduced a theoretical framework for modeling sea ice floe dynamics, integrating particle, kinetic, and hydrodynamic approaches. Building on the Cucker-Smale particle models, we have derived the Vlasov-type kinetic description and a corresponding hydrodynamic formulation, enabling a systematic transition from discrete floe interactions to large-scale dynamics. In an idealized setting, we focused on non-rotating floes with linear velocity dynamics under oceanic drag forces, incorporating empirical laws for floe size and thickness distributions. Total momentum and energy are analyzed. Numerical simulations confirmed the potential of this particle-continuum approach in capturing key sea ice behaviors in a multiscale setting.

Future research directions include incorporating rotational dynamics, atmospheric drag, thermodynamic effects such as melting/refreezing, floe fracturing, and more advanced contact laws in 3D.
Another direction for future work is to investigate floe dynamics in regimes with turbulent ocean fields.
In numerical simulation, developing high-order numerical schemes may further enhance accuracy and stability.
It remains unclear whether the forward Euler scheme preserves the energy dissipation property—that is, whether the right-hand side of (
5.5
) remains negative in the absence of ocean drag forces. Developing numerical schemes that rigorously capture this dissipative behavior, ideally with a guaranteed positive lower bound, remains an open problem.
This is an interesting open question and will be left for future work.
Additionally, validation against observational data will be crucial for assessing model reliability. Advancing these aspects will contribute to a deeper understanding of polar ice behavior with the potential for more accurate modelling and prediction for climate science and oceanography.

A reduced kinetic model for floe dynamics

We derive a kinetic model for floe dynamics where floe size and thickness distributions are provided as empirical probability density functions.
In particular, according to observational data, the floe size distribution satisfies a power law
[
55
]
:

P(r)=a\frac{\kappa^{a}}{r^{a+1}},\quad r>0,

(A.1)

where
r
is the diameter of the floe, and
a
and
\kappa
are parameters. On the other hand, the floe thickness follows the Gamma distribution whose density function is given as follows (see
[
7
]
):

\Gamma(h)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\theta^{k}}h^{k-1}e^{-\frac{h}{\theta}},\quad h>0,

(A.2)

where
k
and
\theta
are the shape and scale parameters, respectively. These are both common choices in practice. With this in mind, we introduce a conditional probability density function

F(t,\bm{x},\bm{v},r,h)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f(t,\bm{x},\bm{v}|r,h)}P(r)\Gamma(h).

(A.3)

We substitute the above ansatz (
A.3
) into (
3.18
) to derive a kinetic equation for conditional distribution function
f
:

\partial_{t}f+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[f]f)=0,
