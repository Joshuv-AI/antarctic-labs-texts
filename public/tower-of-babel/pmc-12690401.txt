Tracheobronchial Aspergillosis Mimicking Pseudotumoral Lesions in a Rare Presentation of a Known Disease
Authors: Cinthya Castillo Gamboa, Francisco Hernández Rodríguez, Yessika Medrano Hernández, Eduardo X Porras Rosales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTracheobronchial aspergillosis (TBA) is an uncommon form of invasive Aspergillus infection that can closely mimic endobronchial malignancy, particularly when presenting as an obstructive pseudotumoral lesion. We report the case of a 78‐year‐old immunocompetent male who presented with hemoptysis, progressive dyspnea, and an endobronchial mass initially suspected to represent bronchogenic carcinoma. Bronchoscopy revealed a tumor‐like obstructive lesion, and both bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) culture and bronchial biopsy culture grew Aspergillus spp. based on characteristic microscopic morphology. Histopathologic examination demonstrated septate hyaline hyphae with clear evidence of tissue invasion, confirming invasive fungal disease. The patient experienced marked clinical and endoscopic improvement following systemic voriconazole therapy and bronchoscopic intervention, with near‐complete resolution of the lesion. This case highlights the need to consider fungal etiologies in the differential diagnosis of endobronchial masses and emphasizes the essential role of early bronchoscopy, microbiological cultures, and histopathologic evaluation in establishing a timely and accurate diagnosis.Keywords: differential diagnosis, endobronchial mass, immunocompetence, lower respiratory tract infections, tracheobronchial aspergillosis

Introduction

Aspergillus species are ubiquitous environmental fungi capable of acting as opportunistic pathogens, most commonly affecting individuals with impaired immunity. Infection typically begins after inhalation of airborne conidia; once these spores reach the distal airways, they may produce a spectrum of clinical syndromes depending on the host's immune status [1]. Several innate defense mechanisms protect against infection, including mucociliary clearance within the airways, phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages and neutrophils, early T‐cell responses, and activation of the complement cascade [2, 3].

TBA is an uncommon invasive form of Aspergillus infection confined to the tracheobronchial tree [4]. Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis typically occurs in profoundly immunocompromised hosts—such as those with hematologic malignancies, prolonged neutropenia, or solid‐organ transplantation—where early diagnosis and antifungal therapy significantly improve survival [5]. In contrast, the clinical relevance and optimal management of TBA in immunocompetent individuals remain poorly defined due to its rarity in this population. We report a rare pseudotumoral form of TBA mimicking bronchogenic carcinoma, illustrating the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of this presentation.

Case History/Examination

A 78‐year‐old male farmer from Matagalpa, Nicaragua, with no significant surgical or family history and no history of smoking, reported occasional exposure to biomass smoke (approximately 3 h per year). His medical history was remarkable only for hypertension, controlled with losartan 100 mg once daily.

He presented to the emergency department in May 2023 with a three‐month history of moderate exertional dyspnea and intermittent nonproductive cough. These symptoms were accompanied by episodes of hemoptysis and retrosternal chest pain, rated 3/10 on the visual analog scale, sharp in character, and non‐radiating. Three weeks before admission, he developed dysphonia and worsening hemoptysis, expectorating approximately 100 mL of bright red blood daily, prompting hospital evaluation.

On physical examination, vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 120/70 mmHg, heart rate 80 bpm, respiratory rate 19 breaths per minute, temperature 37°C, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Cardiopulmonary examination revealed decreased right‐sided respiratory movements, diminished thoracic expansion and vocal fremitus, dullness to percussion, and markedly reduced breath sounds and voice transmission in the right lung field.

Given the clinical suspicion of right lung atelectasis secondary to endobronchial obstruction and significant hemoptysis, laboratory and imaging studies were performed. Additional tests performed on admission included C‐reactive protein (CRP) and D‐dimer, both of which were within normal limits. HIV testing (fourth‐generation ELISA) was negative; a CD4+ T‐cell count was not performed because there were no clinical, epidemiological, or historical features suggestive of impaired cellular immunity. These findings made bacterial pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and immunodeficiency less likely and supported the decision to prioritize evaluation of an obstructive endobronchial process.

Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an endobronchial lesion causing over 50% obstruction of the right main bronchus (Figure 1), suggesting a presumptive neoplastic etiology. The patient subsequently underwent bronchoscopy for further evaluation.

Computed tomography (CT) scan showing an endobronchial mass causing near‐complete obstruction of the right main bronchus.

Bronchoscopy revealed a tumor‐like lesion in the lower third of the trachea, causing approximately 90% luminal obstruction and originating from the right main bronchus (Figure 2). Additional findings included bilateral chronic inflammatory changes and scant hyaline secretions. Bronchial lavage, brushing, and biopsy of the lesion were obtained for cytological, microbiological, and molecular analyses, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis PCR (GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra).

Bronchoscopic view showing a pseudotumoral lesion partially obstructing the right main bronchus.

Cytological examination and Papanicolaou staining of the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) revealed reactive respiratory epithelial cells without evidence of malignancy. GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra testing was negative for M. tuberculosis in both BAL and tissue samples. The galactomannan antigen level in BAL fluid was 0.63 ng/mL. Histopathologic analysis of the bronchial biopsy demonstrated septate, hyaline hyphae with acute angle branching, characteristic of Aspergillus species (Figure 3).

Histopathological section of bronchial biopsy stained with hematoxylin and eosin, showing septate hyaline hyphae consistent with Aspergillus spp. (oil immersion, ×1000).

Fungal growth consistent with Aspergillus spp. was obtained from both the BAL and the bronchial biopsy. Macroscopic colony morphology showed a velvety, greenish surface typical of the genus, while microscopic examination with lactophenol cotton blue stain demonstrated smooth conidiophores arising from a basal foot cell, a globose terminal vesicle partially covered by uniseriate phialides, and chains of round conidia—morphological characteristics diagnostic of Aspergillus spp. at the genus level. Species‐level identification and antifungal susceptibility testing were not available at our institution; therefore, management followed guideline‐recommended therapy for invasive aspergillosis. β‐D‐glucan testing was not performed because this assay is not routinely available at our institution. Integrating the borderline BAL galactomannan value, the characteristic culture morphology, the presence of invasive hyphae on histopathology, and the pseudotumoral endobronchial appearance, the findings were diagnostic of invasive TBA.

The treatment was initiated with intravenous voriconazole (6 mg/kg every 12 h on Day 1, followed by 4 mg/kg twice daily) for 21 days. Interventional bronchoscopy was performed without complications. Although clinical, radiologic, and bronchoscopic features initially suggested a neoplastic process, a multidisciplinary approach led to the definitive diagnosis of pseudotumoral TBA.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial clinical and radiologic findings suggested an endobronchial neoplasm, particularly bronchogenic carcinoma, as the leading differential diagnosis due to the obstructive lesion and hemoptysis. Other considerations included endobronchial tuberculosis, carcinoid tumor, and foreign body aspiration. However, cytological analysis revealed no malignant cells, and GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was negative in both BAL and tissue samples. The presence of septate hyaline hyphae on histopathology, along with a positive galactomannan assay, confirmed the diagnosis of pseudotumoral TBA. These findings underscored the importance of integrating microbiologic, molecular, and histologic data to differentiate fungal airway infections from malignant or granulomatous processes.

Conclusion and Results

Following initiation of systemic antifungal therapy with voriconazole and interventional bronchoscopy, the patient demonstrated rapid clinical improvement. Hemoptysis resolved within the first week of treatment, with progressive reduction of dyspnea and complete functional recovery thereafter. Follow‐up bronchoscopy performed 4 weeks after treatment initiation demonstrated marked mucosal healing and near‐complete resolution of the obstructive pseudotumoral lesion. A repeat chest CT scan at 3 months showed full re‐expansion of the right main bronchus with no residual mass and only minimal post‐inflammatory changes.

The patient completed a 12‐week course of voriconazole without adverse effects and remained asymptomatic at 6 months of follow‐up. This case underscores the potential for full recovery when pseudotumoral TBA is promptly recognized and managed through coordinated multidisciplinary intervention, highlighting the critical role of early bronchoscopic evaluation and targeted antifungal therapy in preventing irreversible airway damage.

Discussion

TBA was first recognized as a distinct clinical entity by Kramer et al. in 1991 [6], who described “invasive Aspergillus tracheobronchitis” in lung transplant recipients. Subsequent reports have identified cases in patients without overt immunosuppression, often associated with chronic airway obstruction, post‐tuberculosis tracheal stenosis [7], or bronchial anastomotic sites following pulmonary resection [8]. TBA develops when Aspergillus species invade the tracheobronchial mucosa in the presence of impaired local or systemic defenses. Although most cases occur in immunocompromised hosts, it has also been reported in immunocompetent individuals, where the diagnosis may be challenging due to low clinical suspicion [8, 9, 10]. The infection predominantly affects immunocompromised hosts, including those with hematologic malignancy, organ transplantation, or prolonged corticosteroid exposure [11, 12]. Rapid recognition and early antifungal therapy are essential for favorable outcomes.

Although most cases occur in immunocompromised patients, TBA has also been described in apparently immunocompetent individuals. Similar pseudotumoral presentations of TBA in this population have been reported, although they remain rare, with fewer than 20 well‐documented cases described in the literature. In such patients, the disease may follow an aggressive course due to delayed recognition and low clinical suspicion of fungal infection outside traditional risk groups [13, 14]. In the present case, malignancy was initially suspected given the patient's age and radiologic findings, underscoring the importance of considering fungal etiologies in the differential diagnosis of endobronchial lesions.

The clinical manifestations of TBA are nonspecific and include chronic cough, dyspnea, and wheezing. Fever and systemic symptoms are more typical of immunocompromised patients, whereas severe presentations may feature hemoptysis, airway obstruction, or respiratory failure. Given the broad spectrum of clinical presentations, bronchoscopy is essential for diagnosis. It provides direct visualization of the airway and permits targeted sampling for microbiologic and histopathologic confirmation [7].

Microbiologic confirmation of tissue invasion can be difficult [2]; therefore, noninvasive biomarkers such as galactomannan and β‐D‐glucan have gained diagnostic relevance [3]. Galactomannan detection in serum or BAL can facilitate early diagnosis, whereas β‐D‐glucan, although less specific, provides complementary evidence. Histopathologic evidence of tissue invasion remains the diagnostic gold standard for invasive aspergillosis. In our case, serum galactomannan was negative, consistent with prior reports describing lower sensitivity in non‐neutropenic hosts (< 50%) compared with BAL testing (sensitivity 85%–95%, specificity 80%–90%) [1]. The identification of septate hyaline hyphae in bronchial biopsy specimens confirmed the diagnosis in the absence of malignancy. Chronic fungal inflammation may promote epithelial metaplasia, emphasizing the need for long‐term surveillance.

Bronchoscopic evaluation remains indispensable for assessing the extent and morphology of airway involvement [7]. Based on macroscopic appearance, TBA can be classified into four morphologic types—pseudomembranous, ulcerative, obstructive (granular), and mixed—each correlating with disease severity and prognosis [15]. Our patient exhibited an obstructive pattern with near‐complete airway occlusion, a presentation associated with high morbidity and mortality.

According to current Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [16] and European Society for Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) [17] guidelines, voriconazole remains the first‐line therapy for invasive aspergillosis, including tracheobronchial forms, regardless of immune status or morphologic subtype. In advanced or obstructive cases, adjunctive bronchoscopic debridement or topical antifungal therapy may be required. Wu et al. [7] reported that full‐thickness bronchial invasion predicts poor prognosis, whereas superficial mucosal involvement—as seen in our patient—tends to respond favorably to combined medical and endoscopic management.

TBA is an uncommon but serious form of invasive aspergillosis that can mimic neoplastic disease, even in patients without evident immunosuppression. This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of recognizing TBA in immunocompetent hosts and highlights the importance of early bronchoscopy for direct visualization, tissue sampling, and confirmation of Aspergillus infection. Timely diagnosis and antifungal therapy are essential to prevent disease progression and achieve favorable outcomes.

Aspergillus infection should be considered in any patient presenting with unexplained endobronchial lesions, regardless of immune status. Voriconazole remains the mainstay of treatment, while bronchoscopic intervention may relieve obstruction and support recovery. Optimal management requires close collaboration among pulmonologists, infectious disease specialists, radiologists, and pathologists to ensure diagnostic accuracy and improved clinical results.
