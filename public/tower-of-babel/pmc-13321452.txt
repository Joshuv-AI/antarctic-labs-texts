Coffee, caffeine, and cardiovascular health: navigating risks and benefits—an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Eman E Shaban, Hussam Elmelliti, Ahmed Shaban, Amira Shaban, Ali Elkandow, Mohamed Gafar Abdelrahim, Hany A Zaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between coffee or caffeine intake and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and their risk factors has been extensively researched. However, there has been conflicting evidence. Therefore, the current updated meta-analysis assessed the relationship between coffee or caffeine with CVDs, such as coronary heart diseases (CHDs), myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure (HF), stroke, cardiac arrhythmias, and CVD mortality.MethodsFive electronic databases, namely PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus, were extensively searched for all records published between January 2000 and December 2025. Studies were included if they examined the effects of coffee on any CVD and reported the associations in terms of the hazard ratio (HR), relative risk (RR), or odds ratio (OR). Moreover, quality appraisal was conducted using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale for cohort studies and the Joanna Briggs Institute tool for case-control studies.ResultsAfter exclusions, 38 studies involving 2,856,002 participants were reviewed and analyzed. The pooled analysis showed no significant associations between coffee consumption and total CHDs or HF, when comparing the highest and lowest coffee consumption categories (HR: 0.98; p = 0.80 and HR: 1.03; p = 0.62, respectively). In contrast, the pooled results showed a significant positive association between higher coffee consumption and the risk of developing MI (OR: 1.48; p < 0.0001). The pooled analysis also showed an inverse relationship between coffee consumption and stroke or cardiac arrhythmias (HR: 0.89; p = 0.01 and HR: 0.94; p = 0.04, respectively). Furthermore, we observed a non-linear relationship between caffeine intake and CVD mortality among hypertensive patients (HR: 0.68; p = 0.001).ConclusionHigher coffee intake might increase the risk of MI, but can also offer protective effects against stroke and cardiac arrhythmias. Moreover, higher caffeine intake can reduce the risk of CVD mortality in hypertensive patients.Clinical trial registrationPROSPERO: CRD420251073620.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05693-0.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Coronary heart disease, Myocardial infarction, Heart failure, Stroke, Caffeine, Cardiac arrhythmias

Introduction

Coffee is one of the most popular drinks in the world, with research suggesting that over one billion people consume it worldwide [1, 2]. Up to 73% of coffee consumers drink it every day, while only 3.8% drink it once every week [2]. Generally, coffee contains several substances, including lipids, carbohydrates, minerals, vitamins, phenolic compounds, and alkaloids. Caffeine is one of the alkaloids that is naturally present in coffee beans. It is also present in other dietary sources, including tea, cocoa, chocolate bars, and soft drinks. The caffeine content in these dietary sources varies widely, with research showing that it ranges from 4 to 180 mg per 150 ml of coffee, 24 to 50 mg per 150 ml of tea, 15 to 29 mg per 180 ml of cola, 2 to 7 mg per 150 ml of cocoa, and 1 to 36 mg per 28 mg of chocolate [3–5].

At low doses, caffeine stimulates the central nervous system, resulting in diuresis, smooth muscle relaxation, cardiac/heart muscle stimulation, and increased gastric secretion in adults [6]. Moreover, caffeine consumption has been recognized to have several positive effects on human health, including the prevention of type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and Parkinson’s disease [7, 8]. However, the mechanism behind these benefits is not clearly understood and is not believed to be related to caffeine since decaffeinated coffee also shows similar effects. On the other hand, high doses of caffeine have been shown to contribute to anxiety, nausea, and nervousness [9, 10].

Over the last decade, several meta-analyses have extensively evaluated the association between coffee or caffeine consumption and cardiovascular health. A recently published umbrella review of eleven meta-analyses reported that heavy coffee consumption was associated with an increased risk for cardiovascular diseases. However, further analysis showed that coffee consumption can reduce the risk of dementia and stroke, specifically ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, by preserving the endothelium and antioxidants [11]. Another comprehensive review examining the relationship between coffee consumption and cardiometabolic health reported that coffee consumption has the potential to prevent stroke but has an ambiguous impact on coronary heart disease. Furthermore, the review noted that moderate coffee consumption (1–5 cups per day) is associated with a low risk of heart failure, but the effects on atrial fibrillation remain inconclusive [12]. Collectively, these reviews suggest that coffee consumption has several cardiovascular health benefits and risks. However, important limitations remain. First, coffee consumption and caffeine intake have been used as interchangeable exposures, despite coffee containing other non-caffeine components. Second, these reviews do not incorporate the most recent cohort studies published in the past few years. Finally, most statistical analyses have focused on composite cardiovascular outcomes, which limits insights condition-specific risks, such as arrhythmias. Therefore, we conducted an updated meta-analysis to comprehensively examine the relationship between coffee or caffeine with a wide range of cardiovascular outcomes. Compared to previous reviews, the current meta-analysis uniquely evaluates coffee and caffeine as separate exposures, includes recently published cohort studies, and assesses various cardiovascular diseases.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted according to the guidelines of PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). A completed PRISMA checklist is available in Supplementary File 2. The protocol for this systematic review was pre-registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number [CRD420251073620]). The registration includes the review objectives, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data sources, and planned analysis methods. The full protocol can be accessed at:

https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251073620.

Search strategy and information sources

We extensively searched for potential articles published from 2000 to December 2025 and indexed in four major databases, namely PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus. This online search was performed by an expert reviewer using keywords such as coffee, caffeine, cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, stroke, arrhythmia, and cardiac arrest. The reviewer also searched the bibliographies of previously published meta-analyses and literature reviews to identify additional studies. During the search, grey literature such as ongoing clinical trials, studies not yet published, theses and dissertations were excluded, as they would have undermined the statistical power of our meta-analysis. Nevertheless, to minimize publication bias, we also systematically searched grey literature sources. Clinical trial registries, such as ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, were searched for ongoing and completed trials relevant to coffee, caffeine, and cardiovascular diseases. We also searched preprint servers, such as Research Square, medRxiv, and bioRxiv, to identify studies that are yet to be published in peer-reviewed journal. Theses and dissertations were searched through ProQuest. Complete details on the search strategy used in each database are shown in Appendix A in Supplementary File 1.

Study selection

All the records identified from the electronic databases and grey literature sources were imported into the Zotero citation software, and the duplicates were removed. Two independent review authors screened titles and abstracts for potential eligibility. Studies considered potentially relevant underwent full-text screening according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inter-reviewer agreement during the title/abstract screening was moderate (Cohen’s kappa (k) statistic: 0.59) and near-perfect for full-text screening (k = 0.82). Disagreements during the study selection process were solved through constructive discussions between the two authors.

Eligibility criteria

To be included in the current meta-analysis, studies had to meet the following criteria:

i.Designed as randomized controlled trials or observational studies, such as case-controlled studies, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, etc.

ii.Report the association between coffee or caffeine and any cardiovascular disease in terms of relative risk (RR), hazard ratios (HR), or odds ratio (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Report the association between coffee or caffeine and any cardiovascular disease in terms of relative risk (RR), hazard ratios (HR), or odds ratio (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

iii.Provide the amount of coffee or caffeine consumption.

iv.Written in English.

Studies that did not fulfil the above criteria and those designed as experimental studies, case reports, case series, narrative reviews, meta-analyses, and conference/meeting abstracts were excluded. Studies reporting mixed healthy diets without specifying the effect of coffee or caffeine were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two review authors independently abstracted the data needed for review and analysis using a pre-designed data extraction Excel sheet. The information abstracted from each eligible study included primary author names, publication year, study design, the country in which the study was conducted, sample sizes, gender distribution, mean/median age at baseline, amount of coffee or caffeine intake, follow-up period, adjusted confounders, and the reported outcomes. The discrepancies in extracted data were resolved by discussions between two reviewers or by soliciting the opinion of a third reviewer, who re-examined the studies with conflicting data.

Quality appraisal

Two tools, the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), were used for quality appraisal. The Newcastle Ottawa scale (NOS) was used to assess the methodological quality of cohort studies, while JBI was used to assess case-control studies. Using the NOS tool, studies were assigned scores across three different domains, specified as selection, comparability, and exposure. Afterward, each study was graded as having poor, fair, or high methodological quality based on the total NOS score (NOS score between 0 and 3, 4–6, and 7–9, respectively). On the other hand, the JBI assessed the quality of case-control studies using a 10-question checklist.

Data synthesis

We used the Review Manager software (RevMan version 5.4.1) for all statistical analyses. The associations between coffee or caffeine and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) or CVD mortality were evaluated in terms of adjusted hazard ratio (HR), of which an HR less than 1 indicated low risk and an HR greater than 1 indicated increased risk. In addition, a two-tailed test was used to determine whether the reported associations were significant, with a p-value less than 5% (p < 0.05) indicating a statistically significant difference. Interstudy heterogeneity was also examined using the I2 statistics, of which index values greater than 50% demonstrated significant heterogeneity. A random-effects model was used to pool outcomes displaying heterogeneity, while a fixed-effects model was used to pool outcomes without heterogeneity (I2 = 0).

Results

Search results

The systematic search across the previously mentioned databases yielded a total of 2266 potential records. 1512 of these records were excluded after being deemed to be exact duplicates. Titles and abstracts of the remaining 754 records were thoroughly screened, of which 699 were considered irrelevant to our study objective and were excluded. Thirty-eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The remaining 17 studies were excluded at the full-text stage for the following reasons: 1 was non-English, 8 were animal studies, and 8 were designed as review articles (Fig. 1). The study selection PRISMA flowgram is illustrated in Fig. 1, and the full list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion is provided in Table 3 in Appendix C of Supplementary File 1.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 38 studies involving 2,856,002 participants were reviewed and analyzed. 33 of these studies were cohort studies (29 prospective, 1 retrospective, and 3 undefined), and 5 were case-control studies. Moreover, 7 studies reported coronary heart disease (CHD), 10 reported myocardial infarction (MI), 6 reported stroke, 5 reported heart failure (HF), 9 reported atrial fibrillation, 3 reported any arrhythmia, and 5 reported CVD mortality (Table 1).

Coffee consumption and risk of CHD

Seven prospective studies reported the association between coffee consumption and the risk of CHD. When the results from these studies were combined, we found no significant association between coffee consumption and the development of CHD (p = 0.80) (Fig. 2). However, there was a high heterogeneity across the studies (I2 = 60%). In addition, a subgroup analysis according to gender showed no significant relationship between coffee consumption and CHD risk among men (p = 0.06; I2 = 0%) and women (p = 0.10; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3).

Association between coffee consumption and total coronary heart diseases. Forest plot showing the HR of CHD comparing the highest and lowest categories of coffee consumption, stratified according to gender. The overall pooled effect was statistically insignificant for both men and women

On the other hand, six studies evaluated the relationship between coffee consumption and the risk of developing myocardial infarction (MI). Data pooled from these studies demonstrated a significant association between coffee consumption and the risk of MI when comparing the highest and lowest coffee consumption categories (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the pooled results did not show heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), suggesting that this finding was consistent across the pooled studies. When the outcomes were categorized according to gender, we found a nonsignificant positive association between coffee consumption and MI risk in men (p = 0.05) and in women (p = 0.08) (Fig. 5).

Coffee consumption and risk of stroke

Compared to the lowest coffee consumption category, high coffee consumption was associated with a significantly lower risk of stroke (p = 0.01) (Fig. 6). This finding indicates the protective effect of high coffee intake on stroke incidence. However, the pooled results showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39%). Furthermore, a subgroup analysis according to the different stroke subtypes demonstrated that high coffee consumption provided protection against ischemic stroke (p < 0.0001), but not intracerebral hemorrhage (p = 0.35) or subarachnoid hemorrhage (p = 0.15) (Fig. 7).

The pooled effect indicated that higher coffee consumption is associated with a statistically significant reduced risk of ischemic stroke, but not intracerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage

Coffee consumption and risk of heart failure

Five studies reported the association between coffee consumption and the risk of HF. When the results from these studies were combined in a random-effects model, we found no significant association between high coffee consumption and the development of HF (p = 0.62) (Fig. 8). A subgroup analysis according to gender also showed no significant relationship between high coffee intake and the risk of HF among men (p = 0.77) and women (p = 0.25) (Fig. 9).

Coffee consumption and risk of arrhythmias

Combined results from three studies revealed that the highest coffee consumption category was significantly associated with reduced risk of any arrhythmia compared to the lowest coffee intake category (p = 0.04) (Fig. 10). However, this finding demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 72%), suggesting that the results varied across the pooled studies. In addition, a subgroup analysis according to the different arrhythmia subtypes showed no significant association between high coffee intake and AF (p = 0.07), supraventricular tachycardia (p = 0.29), or ventricular tachycardia (p = 0.13) (Fig. 11).

Caffeine intake and CVD mortality risk

The risk of CVD mortality was evaluated in different subgroups, including the general population, diabetes patients, and hypertension patients. In the general population, evidence suggests that there is no association between high caffeine intake and CVD mortality (p = 0.54). Similarly, in diabetic and prediabetic patients, the highest caffeine intake category showed no significant association with CVD mortality compared to the lowest caffeine intake category (p = 0.31). In contrast, high caffeine intake among hypertension patients was correlated with a 32% lower risk of CVD deaths compared to low caffeine intake (p = 0.001) (Fig. 12).

The pooled effect demonstrated that higher caffeine intake was associated with a reduced risk of CVD mortality in hypertensive patients, but not in the general population and diabetic/prediabetic patients

Caffeine intake and risk of CVDs

The association between caffeine and the risk of CVDs is often reported via caffeinated beverages, such as coffee, tea, and energy drinks. However, few studies have investigated the effects of the amount of caffeine intake per day on cardiovascular health. In our analysis, we found that we found no significant association between caffeine intake and the risk of AF when comparing the highest and lowest caffeine intake categories (p = 0.08) (Fig. 13). Furthermore, the pooled results did not show heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), implying that the results across the pooled studies were consistent.

Association between caffeine consumption and atrial fibrillation. Forest plot showing HR of atrial fibrillation comparing the highest and lowest caffeine intake. The pooled effect was statistically insignificant

On the other hand, a prospective cohort study that adjusted for age, smoking, and other lifestyle and cardiovascular risk factors found that high caffeine intake (> 450 mg/day) was not significantly associated with total CVD events (HR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.70–1.47), CHD (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.67–1.59), and stroke (HR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.46–1.95) when compared with low caffeine intake (< 110 mg/day) [26]. Similarly, after controlling for age, smoking, diet, and exercise, Bodar and colleagues found that high (298–1007 mg) and moderate (120–235 mg) dietary caffeine consumption was not associated with HF (HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 0.99–1.53 and 0.95; 95% CI: 0.77–1.18, respectively) [31].

Discussion

The current study is an updated meta-analysis investigating the benefits and risks of coffee or caffeine on cardiovascular health. In this regard, the aggregated evidence showed that higher coffee consumption had significant protective effects on stroke and arrhythmia incidences. Similarly, higher caffeine intake was correlated with a reduced risk of CVD mortality among hypertensive patients. However, the aggregated data also showed that higher coffee consumption can significantly increase the risk of MI.

The positive association between high coffee consumption and MI risk reported in the current study is consistent with a previous meta-analysis, which found that heavy (3–4 cups/day) or very heavy (> 4 cups/day) coffee consumption significantly increased the risk of MI [51]. This positive association is biologically plausible, since high coffee consumption can activate the sympathetic nervous system, causing physiological changes such as increased heart rate, myocardial contractility, and oxygen demand, that can precipitate ischemic events in susceptible populations [52, 53]. In addition, high coffee consumption has been associated with elevated levels of plasma homocysteine, which may further increase the risk of CVDs, such as MI [54–56]. Nonetheless, inconsistencies across individual studies, including those reporting no significant associations even at high coffee consumption levels [16, 22], highlights that the association between coffee consumption and MI is context-dependent, and might be influenced by baseline cardiovascular risk, lifestyle factors, genetic factors, and coffee preparation method.

In contrast, the present meta-analysis found an inverse relationship between high coffee intake and the risk of stroke, particularly ischemic stroke, which is consistent with previous meta-analytical evidence [57–59]. Compared with MI, the risk of stroke appears to be strongly impacted by vascular and inflammatory pathways, where the non-caffeine bioactive components of coffee might play a protective role. Coffee contains antioxidant phenolic compounds that enhance endothelial function and mitigate atherosclerosis progression [60, 61]. Furthermore, regular coffee intake has been linked to reduced levels of inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein and leukocyte adhesion molecules [62]. Considering the pivotal role of inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke, these processes provide a logical theoretical framework connecting increased coffee consumption to a decreased risk of stroke.

Interestingly, the aggregated data demonstrated an inverse relationship between coffee consumption and arrhythmia risk, further supporting the hypothesis that high coffee consumption may exert protective cardiovascular effects. A probable explanation is the development of tolerance among regular users, which diminishes the initial pro-arrhythmic properties of caffeine with time [63, 64]. Moreover, coffee drinking has been associated with enhanced insulin sensitivity and a decreased incidence of type 2 diabetes [65, 66], both of which are significant risk factors for atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant characteristics of coffee-derived polyphenols, including chlorogenic acid, may also aid in stabilizing cardiac electrophysiology. Nonetheless, the significant heterogeneity shown in this study (I² = 72%) underscores persistent ambiguity and indicates that arrhythmia risk may differ based on demographic characteristics, arrhythmia subtype, and consumption habits. Therefore, further research is needed to support our findings.

In addition, our results have shown a non-linear relationship between caffeine intake and the risk of CVD mortality among hypertensive patients, implying that higher caffeine intake might be beneficial for hypertensive patients. This conclusion is corroborated by scientific findings indicating the antioxidant and anti-atherogenic effects of caffeine, which include the suppression of LDL cholesterol oxidation and the mitigation of inflammatory pathways [67, 68]. Moreover, emerging evidence shows that caffeine metabolites such as 7-methyluric acid may have blood pressure-lowering properties, offering a plausible explanation for the observed decrease in CVD mortality [69]. These findings contradict previous guidelines supporting global caffeine restriction in hypertension patients and imply that tailored guidance may be more suitable.

Clinical implications and future research

Our findings indicate that the cardiovascular effects of coffee and caffeine are complex and outcome-specific, rather than consistently harmful or beneficial. From a clinical standpoint, the findings of this meta-analysis suggest a nuanced approach to dietary counselling, especially for people with known cardiovascular risk factors. The reported negative relationships between coffee consumption and stroke or arrhythmia risk imply that regular limitation of coffee intake in the general public might not be necessary, particularly among frequent drinkers. On the other hand, the possible elevated MI risk linked with high amounts of coffee consumption highlights the significance of moderation, especially in patients with established ischemic heart disease or high sympathetic reactivity. Notably, the nonlinear relationship seen between caffeine consumption and CVD mortality among patients with hypertension shows that tailored advise that includes overall cardiovascular risk, tolerance to caffeine, and regular intake habits may be more suitable.

Future research directions in the context of cardiovascular effects of coffee and caffeine should address several crucial issues. For cardiovascular outcomes, such as MI and arrhythmias, where the results remain heterogeneous and, in some cases, inconsistent, further large-scale prospective studies with longer follow-up durations are needed to establish the direction and magnitude of these relationships. Considerable variation also exists in how amounts of coffee and caffeine consumption are characterized, with definitions of moderate or high intake ranging substantially among studies. Future research should emphasize enhanced dose-response analysis to establish optimum consumption ranges and to investigate if threshold or nonlinear correlations exist for certain cardiovascular outcomes. While the evidence provided in this meta-analysis suggests that the relationship between coffee consumption and cardiovascular outcomes, specifically CHD, MI, and heart failure, does not differ substantially by sex, data remain limited. Therefore, future prospectives studies should systematically evaluate these associations in depth. Finally, the method of coffee preparation represents another major gap in literature. Evidence suggests that unfiltered or boiled coffee contains high diterpenes [70], which have been correlated with serum lipid levels and may impact cardiovascular risk [71]. However, due to limited data we were unable to investigate the association between the different methods of coffee preparation and cardiovascular. Therefore, additional research is warranted to clarify the cardiovascular effects of different coffee preparation methods.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, significant heterogeneity was observed in some of the pooled analyses, specifically those analyzing the association between coffee and total CHD or any arrhythmias. This heterogeneity might have resulted from the variations in sample size, baseline CVD risk factors, type of coffee, method of coffee preparation, and adjusted confounders. However, the impact of heterogeneity on the overall effect sizes was minimized by employing the random-effects model. Second, the amount of coffee or caffeine intake was self-reported across the included studies. This might have caused a misclassification of coffee or caffeine intake; thereby limiting the detection of statistically significant associations in some of the pooled results. Third, in all the included studies, coffee/caffeine intake was measured at a single time point, meaning that the variations in coffee consumption over time were not accounted for. Fourth, only English-written records were eligible for inclusion. Therefore, our statistical analyses were subject to selection bias, since data from non-English records that would have been used to improve the statistical power of our meta-analyses was intentionally omitted. To minimize the bias associated with this restriction, we conducted a comprehensive database and grey literature search to capture all relevant English-published studies. Finally, each study provided different categories of coffee/caffeine amounts. For instance, in some studies, the highest coffee consumption category was either 2 or more cups/day, 4 or more cups/day, 6 or more cups/day, etc. This variation might have limited us from detecting statistically significant associations in some pooled analysis because were compared the highest and lowest coffee/caffeine category in each study irrespective of the amount.

Conclusion

The present meta-analysis suggests that higher coffee consumption is significantly related to the development of MI, with further evaluation indicating that the positive association is slightly heightened in men than in women. However, further research is needed to effectively understand the effect of gender on the association between coffee intake and MI risk. Additionally, the aggregated data revealed a non-linear relationship between coffee consumption and stroke or cardiac arrhythmias, meaning that higher coffee intake might offer protective effects against stroke, specifically ischemic stroke and cardiac arrhythmias. However, the protective effect of high coffee consumption on arrhythmia incidences needs to be evaluated in further studies. The current study has also demonstrated an inverse relationship between caffeine intake and the risk of CVD mortality among hypertensive patients, but not in the general population and diabetic patients. Therefore, higher caffeine intake might be beneficial for hypertensive patients.
