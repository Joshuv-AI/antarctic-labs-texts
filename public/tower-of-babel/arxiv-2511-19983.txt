Error-structure-tailored early fault-tolerant quantum computing
Pei Zeng, Guo Zheng, Qian Xu, Liang Jiang

Abstract
Fault tolerance is widely regarded as indispensable for achieving scalable and reliable quantum information processing. However, the spacetime overhead required for fault-tolerant quantum computation remains prohibitively large. A critical challenge arises in many quantum algorithms with Clifford + φ \varphi compiling, where logical rotation gates R Z L ​ ( φ ) R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi) serve as essential components. The Eastin-Knill theorem fundamentally limits these operations, preventing their transversal implementation in quantum error correction codes and necessitating resource-intensive workarounds through T T -gate compilation combined with magic state distillation and injection. In this work, we consider error-structure-tailored fault tolerance, where fault-tolerance conditions are analyzed by combining perturbative analysis of realistic dissipative noise processes with the structural properties of stabilizer codes. Based on this framework, we design a 1-fault-tolerant continuous-angle rotation gate R Z L ​ ( φ ) R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi) in stabilizer codes, implemented via dispersive-coupling Hamiltonians. Our approach could circumvent the need for T T -gate compilation and distillation, offering a hardware-efficient solution that maintains simplicity, minimizes physical footprint, and requires only nearest-neighbor interactions. Integrating with recent small-angle-state preparation techniques, we can suppress the gate error to 91 ​ | φ | ⋅ p 2 91|\varphi|\cdot p^{2} for small rotation angles | φ | < 0.2 |\varphi|<0.2 rad (where p p denotes the physical error rate). For current achievable hardware parameters ( p = 1 ⋅ 10 − 3 p=1\cdot 10^{-3} ), this enables reliable execution of over 10 7 10^{7} small-angle rotations when | φ | ≈ 10 − 3 |\varphi|\approx 10^{-3} , meeting the requirements of many near-term quantum applications. We estimate that, compared to the 15-to-1 magic state distillation and magic state cultivation approaches, our method reduces spacetime resource costs by factors of 1337.5 × 1337.5\times and 43.6 × 43.6\times , respectively, for a Heisenberg Hamiltonian simulation task under realistic hardware assumptions.

Introduction

The goal of building quantum computer is to provide unique and useful applications compared to classical counterparts.
While recent experimental advances
Huggins et al. 2022
;
Morvan et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025

In contrast, fully fault-tolerant (FT) quantum computers, which are expected to provide unique advantages in various applications such as Hamiltonian simulation and factoring, require the gate resources which are prohibitively high
Lee et al. 2021
;
Regev 2025
, making them unlikely to be available in the near future.
As a transition stage from NISQ to fully FT quantum computers, early FT devices with limited error suppression are attracting increasing attention

;
Lin and Tong 2022
.
Well-designed error correction techniques and quantum algorithms may enable such early fault-tolerant devices to surpass classical simulators and become both practical and impactful in the near future.

In many quantum algorithms designed for the early-stage FT quantum computers

;
Yu et al. 2025

When considering the fully fault-tolerant implementation of

Here, we consider error-structure-tailored fault tolerance, where the FT is defined by carefully examining the noisy dissipative process during the gate implementation of the stabilizer codes. Similar ideas were formerly explored in the gate design of bosonic quantum error correction codes

;

II
Error-structure-tailored fault tolerance

We first review the canonical definition of the FT gadgets
Aliferis et al. 2005
;
Gottesman 2009
.
The FT gadgets are associated to a QEC code, which is usually a
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code with
n
physical qubits and
k
logical qubits. In this work, we focus on the case with
k=1
. A stabilizer code is defined by a set of stabilizer generators
\{S_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-k-1}
which span an abliean subgroup
\mathcal{S}
of the Pauli group
\mathbb{P}^{n}
without
-I
. The distance
d
is defined to be the smallest weight of a non-identity logical operator, i.e.,
d=\min\{|l|\,|\,l\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S})\backslash\mathcal{S}\}
where
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S})
is the centralizer of the stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
.

To begin with, we first introduce the concept of error filter and ideal decoder. Denote the ideal code space projector as

\Pi_{0}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-k-1}\frac{1}{2}(I+S_{i}).

(1)

Now, we extend the projector definition by considering the set of Pauli operators with weight less or equal to
r
,
\mathbb{P}_{r}:=\{P\in\mathbb{P}^{n}||P|\leq r\}
. Here,
r
satisfy
r\leq t:=\lceil(d-1)/2\rceil
.
The
r
-filter with
r\leq t:=\lceil(d-1)/2\rceil
is an extended code space projector with at most
r
errors,

\Pi_{(r)}=\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}_{r,\mathcal{S}}}P\,\Pi_{0}P.

(2)

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{r,\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{P}_{r}/\sim
is a quotient set of
\mathbb{P}_{r}
by the equivalence relationship of
P_{1}\sim P_{2}:P_{1}P_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S})
. We choose
P
in
\mathbb{P}_{r,\mathcal{S}}
to avoid unwanted double counting since
P_{1}\Pi_{0}P_{1}
and
P_{2}\Pi_{0}P_{2}
will correspond to the same space if
P_{1}P_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S})
.

The ideal decoder is a noiseless gadget where we first perform a syndrome measurement by the projectors,

\Pi_{\vec{s}}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-k-1}\frac{1}{2}\left(I+(-1)^{s_{i}}S_{i}\right),

(3)

where
\vec{s}=[s_{0},...,s_{n-k-1}]
is the
(n-k)
-bit syndrome measurement outcome. Based on
\vec{s}
, we then perform an error recovery Pauli operator
P(\vec{s})\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
on the system. The Knill-Laflemme condition ensure that, if a weight-
r

r\leq t
, then we can recovery the code state after the ideal decoding.

Without loss of generality, we focus on the definiton of the FT single-code-block gate gadget. The FT state preparation, measurement, QEC and multi-code-block gate can be defined in a similar manner
Gottesman 2009
.

Definition 1

(
t
-FT gate
Gottesman 2009
)
.

The single-code-block gate is
t
-FT if it satisfies,

1.

If the input state passes the
r
-filter and
s
faults occur during the gate implementation, then the output state can pass the
s+r
filter when
s+r\leq t
.

2.

If the input state passes the
r
-filter and
s
faults occur during the gate implementation, then the output state after the ideal decoder is equivalent to first implement an ideal decoder after the
r
-filter then apply an ideal gate.

Here, the first condition ensure that the error propagation is limited and the second condition guarantee that the function of the noisy gate is close to the idea gate.

The canonical FT definition in Def.
1
counts the discrete faults during the gate implementation. A common practice to perform the FT analysis is to consider a local stochastic Pauli error model
Gottesman 2014
, where a random Pauli error may occur after the implementation of each physical gate or idling operation. While convenient for the theoretical analysis, this model and the FT definition in Def.
1
fail to capture the practical noise structure and the properties of the dynamics.

In practice, the gate is performed by a Hamiltonian control sequence. The (Markovian) noise can be modeled by the dissipative interaction to the environment. The overall process is described by a Lindblad master equation,

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\mathcal{L}(t)\rho=-i[H(t),\rho]+\left(\sum_{j}D[\sqrt{\gamma_{j}}J_{j}]\right)\rho,

(4)

where
H(t)
is the system Hamiltonian and
D[O]=O\bullet O^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{O^{\dagger}O,\bullet\}
is a Lindblad superoperator associated with a jump operator
O
, and
\gamma_{j}
is the dissipation rate for the normalized error
J_{j}
.

Suppose the spectral norm of the system Hamiltonian
\|H\|
is much larger than the dissipation rates
\{\gamma_{j}\}
. In this case, we can expand the Lindblad propagator
\mathcal{G}(t,0)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
by the Dyson series with respect to
\mathcal{D}:=\sum_{j}D[\sqrt{\gamma_{j}}J_{j}]
,

\rho(t)=\mathcal{G}(t,0)\rho(0)=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{q}(t,0)\rho(0),

(5)

where
\mathcal{G}_{0}(t,0)=\mathcal{U}(t,0)=U(t,0)\bullet U^{\dagger}(t,0)
with
U(t,0):=\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\int_{0}^{t}H(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}]
is the noiseless evolution and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{q}(t)=\left[\int_{t_{h}=0}^{t}dt_{h}\right]\mathcal{T}\Big(

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t,t_{q})\mathcal{D}...

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mathcal{U}(t_{2},t_{1})\mathcal{D}\mathcal{U}(t_{1},0)\Big),

is the
q
-th order noise expansion.
q\geq 1
indicates the expansion order related to the dissipator
\mathcal{D}
. Here,
\mathcal{G}_{q}(t)
describes the
q
-th order noisy dynamics of the whole procedure. We can define the error-structure-tailored
t
-FT gate based on the noise expansion.

Definition 2

(Error-structure-tailored
t
-FT gate)
.

Consider a noisy gate described by a Lindbladian master equation in
Eq. 4

\mathcal{G}(t,0)

\mathcal{D}
in
Eq. 5

The single-code-block gate is
t

1.

If the input state passes the
r
-filter, then the output state with the truncated dynamics
\mathcal{G}^{[s]}
can pass the
s+r
filter when
s+r\leq t
.

2.

If the input state passes the
r
-filter, then the output state after the ideal decoder with the truncated dynamics

is equivalent to first implement an ideal decoder after the
r
-filter then apply an ideal gate.

We can also define FT gadgets for the quantum error detection (QED) code in Def.

V.1
Bayesian logical error suppression: review

We review the protocol from Ref.
Choi et al. 2023
, taking the preparation of
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
in the
d=3
rotated surface code as an example. In the protocol, we first prepare logical
\ket{+}_{L}
state fault-tolerantly on the rotated surface code; then, for a given minimal set of qubits supporting logical-
Z
operator, we implement single-qubit
Z
-rotation on these qubits. In the noiseless case, the resulting state becomes,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id}}}

\displaystyle=[(\cos^{3}\theta III+i^{3}\sin^{3}\theta ZZZ)

(10)

\displaystyle+i\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta(ZII+IZI+IIZ)

\displaystyle+i^{2}\cos\theta\sin\theta^{2}(IZZ+ZIZ+ZZI)]\ket{+}_{L}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id,pass}}}+\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{fail|id}}}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id,fail}}}.

Here,
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id,pass}}}\propto(\cos^{3}\theta III+i^{3}\sin^{3}\theta ZZZ)\ket{+}_{L}
represents the post-selected state when projected to the code space, occurring with probability
P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}:=\cos^{6}\theta+\sin^{6}\theta
. The failed component
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id,fail}}}
occurs with probability
P_{\mathrm{fail|id}}:=1-P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}
. The post-selected state simplifies to:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{id,pass}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}}}\left(\cos^{3}\theta I_{L}+i^{3}\sin^{3}\theta Z_{L}\right)\ket{+}_{L}

(11)

\displaystyle=(\cos\varphi I_{L}+i\sin\varphi Z_{L})\ket{+}_{L}.

where
\varphi:=-\sin^{-1}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}}}\sin^{3}\theta)\approx-\theta^{3}+O(\theta^{5})
. This allows determining the physical rotation angle
\theta
needed to implement a target logical rotation
\varphi
, projecting to the desired state
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
.

In the above discussion, we have assumed an ideal noiseless implementation. In practice, when additional noise occurs, it may lead to erroneous implementations of rotation gates. Without loss of generality, we consider the effect of Pauli errors. While many low-weight Pauli errors (e.g., Pauli
XII
errors) occurring before or after the single-qubit rotations are detectable through subsequent surface code syndrome measurements, some specific Pauli errors may remain undetected by the syndrome measurement.
For example, if the Pauli
ZII
noise occurs, the state we prepare becomes,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}}

(12)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(1)}}}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}}+\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{fail|ud(1)}}}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1),fail}}}.

We use the subscript “
\mathrm{ud(1)}
” to denote the weight-
1
undetectable errors, including the Pauli
ZII
,
IZI
, and
IIZ
errors.
Applying QED on
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}}
, the post-selected state in the code space is
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}}\propto(\cos\theta I_{L}+i\sin\theta Z_{L})\ket{+}_{L}
, with the probability
P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(1)}}:=\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta\approx\theta^{2}
. Note that
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}}=\ket{r_{\varphi_{1}}}
with
\varphi_{1}=\theta
. If weight-
2
undetectable errors like the Pauli
ZZI
error occurs, the resulting post-selected state will be different. However, these weight-
2
will occur with a higher-order probability
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}=O(p^{2})
and pass post-selection with probability
P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(2)}}\approx\theta^{4}
, which is negligible in the leading-order error analysis.

In practice, conditioning on passing the quantum error detection, the overall post-selected resulting state is

\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}=P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}+P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}+O(|\varphi|^{2}p^{2}),

(13)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}=\ket{r_{\varphi}}\bra{r_{\varphi}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}=\ket{r_{\varphi_{1}}}\bra{r_{\varphi_{1}}}
.
P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}
is the Bayesian probability of undetectable erorr given by

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{ud(1),pass}}}{P_{\mathrm{pass}}}\approx\frac{P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(1)}}}{P_{\mathrm{id}}P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}}.

(14)

In the approximation, we only keep the leading order term of the denominator with respect to
p
. Consider a small angle limit of
|\theta|\to 0
, we have
P_{\mathrm{id}}P_{\mathrm{pass|id}}\approx 1
in
Eq. 10
and
P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(1)}}\approx\theta^{2}
in
Eq. 12
, hence
P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}\approx P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}\cdot\theta^{2}
.

We can calculate the trace distance
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
between the post-selected state
\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}
and the ideal state
\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{\mathrm{pass}},\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}})=\frac{1}{2}\left|\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}-\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}\right|

(15)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}\left|\,\ket{r_{\varphi}}\bra{r_{\varphi}}-\ket{r_{\varphi_{1}}}\bra{r_{\varphi_{1}}}\,\right|

\displaystyle\approx P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}\frac{P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(1)}}}{P_{\mathrm{id}}}\sin\Delta_{\varphi}.

In the approximation of the second and third line, we only keep the leading order terms with respect to
p
. Here,
\Delta_{\varphi}:=|\varphi-\varphi_{1}|\approx\theta^{3}+\theta
. In the samll angle limit of
|\theta|\to 0
, we have
D_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{\mathrm{pass}},\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}})\approx P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}\theta^{2}\cdot\theta\approx P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}|\varphi|
based on
Eq. 14
.

In the above protocol, the undetectable noise occurs with the probability of
P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}=O(p)
. Therefore, the error of the rotation state preparation is
O(|\varphi|p)
, suppressed by a factor of
|\varphi|
due to the Bayesian property of the undetectable error in
Eq. 14
. Note that this protocol is still non-fault-tolerant, since it does not improve the final error scaling related to the physical error
p
. In
subsection V.2
, we improve the error scaling to
O(|\varphi|p^{2})
.

We remark that the choice of trace distance as the error identifier is important. The infidelity
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
between
\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}
scales as
O(p\cdot|\varphi|^{4/3})

Toshio et al. 2025

\ket{r_{\varphi_{1}}}
to the target state
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}

, we will discuss how to convert the coherent error to the Pauli error.

To understand why the trace distance characterize the error of the rotation state preparation, we consider the usage of the state
\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}
in
Eq. 13
to implement the logical rotation gate
R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi)
based on the circuit in

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(\cdot)=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot)+P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}\mathcal{R}_{\varphi_{1}}(\cdot)+O(|\varphi|^{2}p^{2}).

Here,
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
is the coherent error channel after the ideal rotation unitary channel

\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{\theta})=P_{\mathrm{ud(1)|pass}}\,|\Delta_{\varphi}|\,\sqrt{1+\Delta_{\varphi}^{2}}\approx|\varphi|P_{\mathrm{ud(1)}}.

This provides a worst-case upper bound for the trace distance between the ideal state

In the above discussion, we have assumed ideal syndrome detection. In practice, we can suppress measurement errors by performing multiple syndrome measurement rounds and post-selecting those rounds where all syndromes are consistent. This approach suppresses syndrome measurement errors to higher order, ensuring that the leading-order error scaling in preparing
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}

increases slower than a linear function of

VI
Error mitigation and noise estimation

In
Sec. V
we have shown that the projection scheme with the error-structure-tailored FT design and the Bayesian error suppression
Choi et al. 2023
;
Toshio et al. 2025
can be used to prepare small-angle rotation state
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
with a low error rate of
O(|\varphi|\cdot p^{2})
, which is below

1\cdot 10^{-8}
under the current hardware condition. In this section, we estimate the number of quantum rotation gates we can implement with the prepared rotation state ancillae under proper quantum error mitigation techniques.

Suppose we have prepared the small-angle ancilla state
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
following the projection scheme in Sec.
V
. If we inject the small-rotation state into a target quantum state by the circuits in
Fig. 7
and succeed in the first trial (recall that the rotation state injection always succeeds with a probability of
1/2
), we will implement the following noisy rotation channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(\cdot)=(1-P_{L})\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot)+P_{L}\mathcal{R}_{\varphi_{1}}(\cdot)+O(|\varphi|^{2}p^{4}),

(27)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
indicates the ideal rotation channel with the target small angle
\varphi
while
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi_{1}}(\cdot)
is the wrong rotation with the angle
\varphi_{1}
.
When
\varphi
is small, we have
\varphi\approx\theta^{k}
and
\varphi_{1}\approx-\theta^{k-2}
. The wrong rotation probability
P_{L}
is the conditional probability when the undetected error passes the syndrome detection

\displaystyle P_{L}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}P_{\mathrm{pass|ud(2)}}}{P_{\mathrm{pass}}}

(28)

\displaystyle\approx P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}\theta^{2}=O(p^{2}\theta^{2}).

The derivation of
Eq. 27
is in the proof of Proposition
3
.

We can decompose the rotation channel in
Eq. 27
as
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(\cdot)=\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
where

\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\cdot)=(1-P_{L})\mathcal{I}(\cdot)+P_{L}\mathcal{R}_{\Delta\varphi}(\cdot)+O(|\varphi|^{2}p^{4}),

(29)

the noise induced by the rotation channel, which is a coherent stochastic rotation channel. Here,
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
is the identity channel and
\Delta\varphi:=\varphi_{1}-\varphi=O(\theta^{k-2})
.
The size of the noise
\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
is characterized by the diamond-norm distance
\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}-\mathcal{I}\|_{\diamond}
. Similar to
Eq. 17
, we have

\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{\varphi})=P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}|\Delta_{\varphi}|\sqrt{1+\Delta_{\varphi}^{2}}\approx|\varphi|P_{L}.

(30)

Eq. 29
characterizes the noise introduced by the imperfect resource state. To provide a complete noise estimation, the following two issues must be addressed:

1.

Eq. 27
assumes the injection succeeds on the first attempt. In practice, the RUS injection protocol from
Sec. IV
and
Fig. 8
must be employed. A full noise analysis of this iterative procedure is required.

2.

The noise channel in
Eq. 29
is a coherent stochastic rotation channel. For practical error mitigation and streamlined resource estimation, this should be converted to a standard Pauli noise channel compatible with Pauli error cancellation techniques.

We now analyze these two problems.

VI.1
Noise channel after the repeat-until-success procedure

Consider the RUS protocol for rotation-gate injection as described in
Sec. IV
and illustrated in
Fig. 8
. For trial numbers
K=1,2,3,\dots
, we implement the injection with rotation angles that scale exponentially with
K
:
\varphi^{(K)}=2^{K-1}\varphi
, where
\varphi^{(1)}=\varphi
,
\varphi^{(2)}=2\varphi
,
\varphi^{(3)}=4\varphi
, and so on, until the operation succeeds.
When the injection succeeds on the
K
th trial, the resulting rotation channel is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{K}_{\varphi}(\cdot):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{2^{K-1}\varphi}\circ\mathcal{N}_{-2^{K-2}\varphi}\circ...\circ\mathcal{N}_{-2\varphi}\circ\mathcal{N}_{-\varphi}(\cdot)

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot),

where
\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
is an effective error channel that describes the accumulated error,

\mathcal{E}^{K}_{\varphi}(\cdot)=\mathcal{E}_{2^{K-1}\varphi}\circ\mathcal{E}_{-2^{K-2}\varphi}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{-2\varphi}\circ\mathcal{E}_{-\varphi}(\cdot).

(32)

We remark that, when the rotation angle
\varphi^{j}
where
j=1,2,...K
goes beyond the region of
[-\frac{\pi}{8},\frac{\pi}{8})
, we can always reduce the value of this angle into this region by applying logical
S
gate. We define the wrapping function
\Lambda(x):=(x+\frac{\pi}{8})\text{ mod }\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{8}
which ensures that
|\Lambda(x)|\leq\frac{\pi}{8}
.
Taking the wrapping into account, we can define the average channel
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}
over any possible
K
,

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varphi}(\cdot):=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}^{K}(\cdot)=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot),

(33)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}
denotes the effective error channel introduced by the whole RUS process,

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}(\cdot):=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{K}(\cdot).

(34)

Following the analysis in Appendix E of Ref.
Toshio et al. 2025
, we have

\epsilon_{\diamond}(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi})=O(|\varphi|P_{L}).

(35)

In what follows, we discuss how to mitigate the error
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}
.

VI.2
Probabilistic coherent error cancellation

To mitigate the error with the form of
Eq. 29
, we consider to introduce the probabilistic coherent error cancellation techniques introduced by Toshio et. al.
Toshio et al. 2025
. Consider the noise rotation channel of
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
in
Eq. 27
. After the implementation of
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(\cdot)
, we append the following compensation channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=(1-P_{L})\,\mathcal{I}(\cdot)+P_{L}\,\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{-\Delta\varphi}(\cdot)

(36)

\displaystyle\approx(1-P_{L})\,\mathcal{I}(\cdot)+P_{L}\,\mathcal{R}_{-\Delta\varphi}(\cdot)+O(|\varphi|p^{4}).

Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{-\Delta\varphi}(\cdot)
is the averaged noisy small rotation channel in
Eq. 33
with the target angle
-\Delta\varphi=\varphi-\varphi_{1}
. In the second line, we have used the fact that
P_{L}=P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}=O(p^{2}\theta^{2})
from
Eq. 28
and
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{-\Delta\varphi}=O(|\Delta\varphi|P_{L})=O(|\theta^{k-2}|P_{L})
from
Eq. 35
.

Appending
C_{\varphi}
after the noise channel
E_{\varphi}
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{c}_{\varphi}(\rho)=\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}(\rho)

(37)

\displaystyle\approx(1-P_{L})^{2}\rho+P_{L}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{-\Delta\varphi}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\Delta\varphi}(\rho)

\displaystyle+P_{L}(1-P_{L})(\mathcal{R}_{-\Delta\varphi}+\mathcal{R}_{\Delta\varphi})+O(|\varphi|p^{4})

\displaystyle\approx(1-2P_{L}\sin^{2}\Delta\varphi)\rho+2P_{L}\sin^{2}\Delta\varphi\,Z\rho Z+O(|\varphi|p^{4}),

which becomes an incoherent dephasing error and hence easy to be mitigated by probabilistic error cancellation methods
Temme et al. 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
. Then, the worst-case error of the noise channel
\mathcal{E}^{c}_{\varphi}(\rho)
given by the diamond distance is

\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}^{c}_{\varphi})=2P_{L}\sin^{2}(\Delta\varphi)\approx 2P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}\theta^{2k-2},

(38)

Now, we consider to embed the above coherent error cancellation techniques to the RUS process. Suppose the rotation-state injection succeed in the
K
th trial, we append the compensation channel after each rotation gate,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}^{c,K}(\cdot):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{2^{K-1}\varphi}^{c}\circ\mathcal{N}_{-2^{K-2}\varphi}^{c}\circ...\circ\mathcal{N}_{-2\varphi}^{c}\circ\mathcal{N}_{-\varphi}^{c}

(39)

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{c,K}\circ\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}(\cdot),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{c,K}(\cdot):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{2^{K-1}\varphi}^{c}\circ\mathcal{E}_{-2^{K-2}\varphi}^{c}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{-2\varphi}^{c}\circ\mathcal{E}_{-\varphi}^{c}.

The averaged rotation and noise channel of the RUS process is then given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varphi}^{c}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}^{c,K}(\cdot),

(40)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}^{c}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}^{c,K}(\cdot).

Ignoring the higher-order terms, we can write out the explicit form of the averaged noise channel,

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varphi}^{c}(\rho)=(1-\tilde{P}_{L})\rho+\tilde{P}_{L}Z\rho Z.

(41)

With the averaged logical error rate

\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{K}\sum_{M=1}^{K}\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{2^{M-1}\varphi}^{c}).

(42)

From
Eq. 38
we can see that,
\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}^{c}_{2^{n-1}\varphi})
scales as
O(|\theta|^{2k-2}P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}})=O(|\varphi|^{2-2/k}P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}})
when the parameter
n
is small. However, when the trial number
K
satisfies
2^{K-1}\varphi\geq 1
, the corresponding accumulated error
\sum_{n=1}^{K}\epsilon_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}^{c}_{2^{n-1}\varphi})
cannot be suppressed by the value of
\varphi
and reaches
O(P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}})
. Since the above case appears in the RUS process with the probability of
2^{-K}\approx O(|\varphi|)
, the accumulated error scales with
O(|\varphi|P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}})
.

We can then express the averaged error of the RUS process with coherent error mitigation as

\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}|\varphi|p^{2}.

(43)

The prefactor
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
indicates the cost overhead introduced by the RUS and coherent error mitigation procedure, which is determined by
Eq. 38
and
Eq. 42
. When the value of
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}
is given, we can numerically estimate
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
. Recall from
Eq. 26
that
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)}}=c(k)\cdot p^{2}
. Here, we choose to set the overhead
c(k)=14.7
based on the value of
k=6
in
Fig. 11
. This moderate
k
value is suitable for the projection scheme with a reasonable successful probability. From
Fig. 12
we can see that, with the extended protocol of
m=3
and
k=6
, we can run a surface code of
d=18
with a successful probability of
5.4\%
.

Fig. 13
indicates the dependence of the value
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
with respect to different target rotation angle
\varphi
. The complicated curve shape is due to the angle-dependent wrapping procedure introduced in
subsection VI.1

Toshio et al. 2025
. We can see that the maximum value of the error prefactor
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
is close to
91
. We will use this value for the later resource estimation.

:

\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
of the avaraged gate error
\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)
in

with respect to the target rotation angle

VI.3
Controll error cancellation

In the above discussion, we implicitly assume that the error originates from the incoherent noise procedure of the implemented gates, especially the
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
gates. However, in the actual devices, the control errors due to calibration errors, crosstalks and so on may leads to unwanted effects on the prepared
\ket{r_{\varphi}}
states. Usually, the control errors can be suppressed using dynamical decoupling techniques
Vandersypen and Chuang 2005
.
Here, we discuss the control error cancellation methods in Sec. V of Toshio et al.
Toshio et al. 2025
to cancel the remaining control error by a randomized transversal rotation.

Suppose the actual implemented rotation gates in the projection scheme in
Fig. 9
become

\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)\leftarrow\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta+\phi_{i}),

(44)

where
\phi_{i}
indicates the control error which satisfies
\phi_{i}\ll\theta
.
We now discuss two different types of control errors.

1.

The angle value of
\phi_{i}
is random, centered around 0. This type of control error usually occurs in the experiment when the calibration is done with limited accuracy or the strength of the driving pulses are unstable with fluctuations.
In this case, the effect of
\phi_{i}
to the target rotaion will be suppressed to the second order
\phi_{i}^{2}
.

To see that, we consider a worst-case scenario where
\phi_{i}
will randomly filp from two values
\pm\tilde{\phi}_{i}
where
\tilde{\phi}_{i}
denotes the largest value in the distribution of
\phi_{i}
. We have

\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)\leftarrow\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta+(-1)^{m_{i}}\tilde{\phi}_{i}).

(45)

Based on the calculation in Sec. V of Ref.
Toshio et al. 2025
, we have that the target rotation value
\tilde{\phi}
with be close to the target value of
\varphi
with a relative error of

\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi\cdot(1+\sum_{i}\tilde{\phi}_{i}^{2}/2).

(46)

2.

The angle value of
\phi_{i}
is fixed and independent of the choice of
\theta
. This type of control error usually occurs on the gates before and after the real rotation gate. For example, the control errors on the dispersize coupled
CZ
gates in the ancilla-based
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
gate in
Fig. 4
.
In this case, we can introduce an active randomized multirotation design
Toshio et al. 2025
to suppress the effect of
\phi
to the second order. We actively randomize the implementation of the rotation gates by

\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)\leftarrow\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}((-1)^{n_{i}}\theta+\phi_{i}),

(47)

where
n_{i}(=0,1)
is a parameter indicating whether to flip the direction of the rotation gate or not. After this randomization operation, the target rotation angle
\tilde{\varphi}
will be close to the target value of
\varphi
with a relative error of

\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi\cdot(1+\sum_{i}\phi_{i}^{2}/2).

(48)

Recall from
Eq. 43
that the incoherent error is
\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}|\varphi|p^{2}
. Therefore, as long as the value of
\phi_{i}^{2}
is much smaller than
p^{2}
, the physical noises
\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)
will be the dominant error source and we will ignore the effect of the small control error in the later resource estimation.

VI.4
Resource estimation

In
subsection VI.2
we have shown that, by appling the coherent error cancellation during the whole RUS procedure, we can implement the rotation gate
R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi)
with a Pauli noise channel whose diamond norm distance to the identity channel is given by
Eq. 43
,

\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi)=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}|\varphi|p^{2},

where the prefactor
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
is numerically shown to be smaller than
91
. Since now the noise channel is a Pauli channel, we can then implement the canonical probabilistic error cancellation techniques
Temme et al. 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
to mitigate these residue errors.

Recall from
Eq. 41
that the Pauli noise channel after the coherent error cancellation is a dephasing channel
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}_{\varphi}
with the dephasing rate
\tilde{P}_{L}
. Pauli error cancellation is to implement the inverse map,

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c,-1}_{\varphi}(\rho)=\gamma((1-\tilde{P}_{L})\rho-\tilde{P}_{L}Z\rho Z).

(49)

Here
\gamma=1/(1-2\tilde{P}_{L})
. We have
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c,-1}_{\varphi}\circ\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}_{\varphi}(\rho)=\rho
.
The inverse map
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c,-1}_{\varphi}(\rho)
itself is not a CPTP map since it contains a term with negative probability, hence cannot be directly implemented by quantum circuits. However, if we consider the estimation of the expectation value
\braket{O}=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C}(\rho)O)
of any observable
O
at the end of the quantum circuit
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{N}}
that contains the rotation gate, which is the usual scenario for many early FT algorithms, we can implement the inverse map effectively by

\braket{O}\approx\gamma\left((1-\tilde{P}_{L})\braket{O}_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}_{\varphi}}+\tilde{P}_{L}(-\braket{O}_{\mathcal{Z}\circ\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}_{\varphi}})\right),

(50)

where
\mathcal{Z}(\rho)=Z\rho Z
is the Pauli
Z
channel. As a result, we can achieve the unbiased estimation of
\braket{O}
by applying Pauli
Z
operator with a probability of
\tilde{P}_{L}
. This comes with an extra
\gamma^{2}
sampling cost, as the variance of the estimator in
Eq. 50
is amplified by
\gamma^{2}
.

Now, suppose we are going to perform
N_{g}
rotation gates
\{R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi_{1}),R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi_{2}),...,R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi_{N_{g}})\}
in the circuit
\mathcal{C}
, each attached with an independent error mitigation channel with the form in
Eq. 50
. The variance of the final unbiased estimator is

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{g}}\gamma_{i}^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{g}}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi_{i})}\right)^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle\approx e^{4\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}p^{2}}=:e^{4P_{\mathrm{tot}}},

where we assume the RUS and coherent error cancellation cost
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
is approximately independent of
\varphi
. The total error rate
P_{\mathrm{tot}}
and total rotation angle are defined by

P_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{g}}\tilde{P}_{L}(\varphi_{i})=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}\cdot p^{2},\quad\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{g}}|\varphi_{i}|.

(52)

From
Eq. 51
we can see that the sampling cost increases exponentially with the total error rate
P_{\mathrm{tot}}
. To keep the overall algorithm efficient, we need to limit
P_{\mathrm{tot}}
to be a small constant,

P_{\mathrm{tot}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}\cdot p^{2}\lesssim 1.

(53)

In this case, the error mitigation will introduce an extra sampling cost of
e^{4}\approx 55
comparing to the ideal quantum circuit implementation. We then set a limitation to the overall rotation angle,

\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}}\cdot\frac{1}{p^{2}}.

(54)

Now, consider the current hardware condition with the physical error rate
p=1\cdot 10^{-3}
and
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\leq 91
. We have

\varphi_{\mathrm{tot}}\leq 1.10\cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{rad}.

(55)

In many early FT algorithms, especially the ones related to quantum simulations and quantum phase estimation (QPE), the rotation angles
\{\varphi_{i}\}
are usually small. For example, in the QPE for the Hubbard model, to encure the energy accuracy of

VII
Summary and outlook

In this work, we have shown how to generalize the framework of FT gadget analysis to leverage the specific error structures during the gate implementation.
We provide a rigorous framework to define error-structure-tailored fault tolerance in the canonical stabilizer code circuits.
As concrete example, we have shown how to implement the 1-FT arbitrary rotation gates in [[4,1,1,2]]-code and surface codes without digital compiling and magic state distillation, which is formerly impossible due to the Eastin-Knill’s theorem.
Furthermore, we combine our 1-FT error-structure-tailored gate design with the recently proposed small-angle projection scheme
Choi et al. 2023
;
Toshio et al. 2025
and show that we can implement the small angle rotation gate efficiently with an error of
\tilde{P}_{L}=\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\cdot|\varphi|p^{2}
where
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}
is almost a constant. By carefully taking the cost of probabilistic coherent error cancellation and repeat-until-success injection implementation into account, we have shown that the error overhead
\alpha_{\mathrm{RUS}}\lesssim 91
, which allow us to perform about
1.10\cdot 10^{7}
small rotation gates
R_{Z_{L}}(\varphi)
with
\varphi\approx 10^{-3}
when the error mitigation techniques are introduced, enabling many promising early FT algorithms.

Recently, there are advances showning that
T
-ancillary state preparation can be performed in a high accuracy by the cultivation techniques without the usage of magic state distillation
Gidney et al. 2024
. It is interesting to explore the possibility of similar techniques in our rotation state preparation
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
procedure to further reduce the cost by the
T
gate compiling. Meanwhile, we can also explore the usage of our error-structure-tailored FT design to further improve the
T
-state cultivation performance.

In our FT gate design, we leverage the error structure of the 2-level bare qubits or 3-level qutrits. To further enhance the performance, it will be beneficial to consider the hardware-level encoding to suppress physical errors to higher order
Gottesman et al. 2001
;
Mirrahimi et al. 2014
;
Brock et al. 2025
, introduce error bias
Aliferis et al. 2008
;
Réglade et al. 2024
;
Putterman et al. 2025
or detect leakage errors
Teoh et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2022
. We anticipate this will further suppress the error rate and boost the successful probability as well.

\displaystyle U(t,t_{1})\ket{g}\bra{e}U(t,t_{1})^{\dagger}

,

, and

, respectively. For

F.3
Projection scheme simulation

In
Fig. 10
,
Fig. 10
and
Fig. 12
in
Sec. V
, we perform the circuit-level simulation of the projection scheme on a

surface code where

using the Stim package
Gidney 2021

is the number of physical rotation gates, while

is the number of qubits involved during the implementation of the rotation gates. We have

for the ordinary

for the

We focus on the successful probability and error estimation of the preparation of
\ket{r_{\varphi}}_{L}
in the projection scheme. During the scheme, we need to perform many rotation gates
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
with the angle
\theta\approx\varphi^{1/k}
. Since the rotation gates are non-Clifford gates, we cannot simulate the whole circuit efficiently. However, following the approach in Ref.
Toshio et al. 2025
, we can still estimate the successful probability and the trace distance efficiently based on the Monte-Carlo sampling of a set of Clifford circuits.

Recall from Appendix
C.3
that the post-selected noisy state in the projection scheme can be written as

\rho_{\mathrm{pass}}\approx P_{\mathrm{id|pass}},\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}+P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}},\rho_{\mathrm{ud(2),pass}}+O(|\varphi|^{2}p^{4}),

(96)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{id,pass}}=\ket{r_{\varphi}}\bra{r_{\varphi}}
is the ideal rotation state and
\rho_{\mathrm{ud(2),pass}}=\ket{r_{\varphi_{1}}}\bra{r_{\varphi_{1}}}
is the leading-order wrong rotation state.
P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}
and
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}
are the Bayesian probability of no error happens and undetectable error happens conditioning on the passing of the QED stage. Since the value of
\varphi
and
\varphi_{1}
are know in advance, the major idea of our numerical estimation is to estimate the Bayesian probabilities
P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}
and
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}
and then calculate the successful probability and the trace distance based on the analytical formulas.

Recall that we can decompose the effect of multi-rotation gate as

\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{k}}u_{|b|}(Z_{(2)})^{b}

(97)

where
b
is a
k
-bit string,
|b|
denotes the weight of
b
,
u_{w}:=i^{w}\sin^{w}\theta\cos^{k-w}\theta
and

(Z_{(2)})^{b}:=\prod_{i:b_{i}=1}Z_{2i}Z_{2i+1}.

(98)

Applying the multi-rotation gate on
\ket{+}_{L}
, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)\ket{+}_{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{k}}u_{|b|}(Z_{(2)})^{b}\ket{+}_{L}

(99)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{k-1}}\left(u_{|b|}(Z_{(2)})^{b}+u_{|\bar{b}|}(Z_{(2)})^{\bar{b}}\right)\ket{+}_{L}

\displaystyle=:\sum_{b=0}^{2^{k-1}}\ket{\psi_{b}}.

Here,
\bar{b}
is the bitwise complement of
b
. In the second line of
Eq. 99
, we pair the terms with complement
b
values. We remark that, since
(Z_{(2)})^{\bar{b}}(Z_{(2)})^{b}=Z_{L}
, the summand in
Eq. 99
specified by
b
is in the same syndrome subspace with the one specified by
\bar{b}
. Specifically,
\ket{\psi_{00...0}}\propto\ket{r_{\varphi}}
is the ideal rotation state. In the noiseless quantum circuit, when performing the syndrome projection, we will obtain the state
\ket{\psi_{b}}
with the probability,

\Pr(b)=|u_{|\mathrm{b}|}|^{2}+|u_{|\bar{\mathrm{b}}|}|^{2}.

(100)

Now, suppose a Pauli error
E
occurs during the state preparation,
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
gate implementation or the syndrome measurement procedure. The erroneous state becomes

E\prod_{i=1}^{k}R_{ZZ,i}(\theta)\ket{+}_{L}=\sum_{b=0}^{2^{k-1}}E\ket{\psi_{b}}.

(101)

The new state
E\ket{\psi_{b}}
will then belongs to a different syndrome subspace of the surface code as long as the weight of
E
is less than
d
. We remark that, the set of quantum state
\{E\ket{\psi_{b}}\}
preserves the orthogonality of
\{\ket{\psi_{b}}\}
and the sampling probability
\Pr(b)=|u_{|\mathrm{b}|}|^{2}+|u_{|\bar{\mathrm{b}}|}|^{2}
. The only effect is that the syndrome value have changed from the ones related to the string of
(Z_{(2)})^{b}
to the ones related to
E(Z_{(2)})^{b}
. As a result, the error sampling of
E
and the sampling of bit-string
b
by the syndrome measurement can be regarded as independent procedure.

Thanks to the independence of the sampling of
E
and the bit-string
b
, we can estimate the Bayesian probabilities of
P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}
and
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}
by the following procedures,

1.

Prepare the
\ket{+}_{L}
state by a single round of surface code syndrome measurement on the state
\ket{+}^{n}
;

2.

Instead of implementing
k
rotation gates
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
, sample a single bit-string
b\in\{0,1\}^{2^{d-1}}
with probability
\Pr(b)
in
Eq. 100
then apply
(Z_{(2)})^{b}
on the qubits;

3.

Apply the error channels introduced by the
k
rotation gates
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
, which is obtained from the QuTip simulation of the lindbladian procedure;

4.

Perform three rounds of surface code syndrome measurement. Determine whether the circuit passes the post-selection or not.

Here, instead of implementing
k
rotation gates
R_{ZZ}(\theta)
directly, we sample the bit string
b
induced by these gates based on
Eq. 99
. This is reasonable because the Pauli errors
E
generated during the whole circuit and the string
(Z_{(2)})^{b}
have independent effect onto the syndrome measurement results.

Suppose we have run
N
samples and
N_{\mathrm{pass}}
rounds pass the post-selection. We can then estimate

,
P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}
and
P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}
by

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{pass}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N_{\mathrm{pass}}}{N},

(102)

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{id|pass}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N_{\mathrm{wt(b)=0,pass}}}{N_{\mathrm{pass}}},

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{ud(2)|pass}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N_{\mathrm{wt(b)=1,pass}}}{N_{\mathrm{pass}}},

where
N_{\mathrm{wt(b)=0,pass}}
and
N_{\mathrm{wt(b)=1,pass}}
indicates the number of samples with
\mathrm{wt}(b)=0
and
\mathrm{wt}(b)=1

N_{\mathrm{pass}}
rounds which passes the post-selection.

is the weight of the
k
-bit string

with the Bayesian probabilities.
