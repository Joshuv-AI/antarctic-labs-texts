Reinforcement learning in linear embedding space unlocks generalizable control across soft robot configurations
Authors: Xinglong Zhang, Cong Li, Hangjie Mo, Yue Jiang, Wenyu Cao, Xin Xu, Wei Jiang, Zhenshan Bing, Yihe Yang, Xiaojian Li, Yueneng Yang, Huimin Lu, Ling-li Zeng, Alois Knoll, Dewen Hu, Li Wen, Wei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft-bodied organisms exhibit prominent morphological adaptability, dynamically reconfiguring shape and stiffness to achieve versatile behaviors. Inspired by these systems, soft robots with diverse morphologies have emerged, yet a unified control framework that rapidly adapts across configurations remains elusive. Here, we introduce a generalizable control system that enables rapid cross-configuration adaptation via reinforcement learning in a shared linear Koopman embedding space. By encoding robot dynamics into this embedding space, our method decouples control policies from specific morphologies, allowing real-time, model-free policy adaptation without retraining from scratch. We validate our system across 33 distinct robot configurations. Our system achieves a 75 × reduction in transfer samples across configurations, while sustaining robust performance under high-speed motion, heavy payloads, and multiactuator faults, and achieving real-world skills previously unattainable in soft robotics. This work establishes an adaptable control framework for diverse soft robot configurations and may offer insights for generalizable control in complex physical systems.

Introduction

Natural soft-bodied organisms demonstrate prominent morphological adaptability through dynamic reconfiguration and adaptive control1–4. For instance, octopuses reshape their body structures and elephant trunks modulate stiffness to enable versatile functionality that ranges from agile locomotion to flexible manipulation. This adaptability stems from embedded sensorimotor loops that continuously adjust to structural changes, allowing seamless multitask execution. Inspired by biological systems, soft robots leverage compliant materials and structures to achieve inherent adaptability and safe interaction5–9. These features enable applications in delicate manipulation5, human-robot cooperation10, and confined-space navigation11. Despite advances in material design and structural reconfiguration of soft robots to meet diverse task demands12–14, developing a generalizable control framework capable of fast adaptation across diverse configurations remains a long-standing problem12,15. Existing controllers are typically configuration-specific, requiring extensive remodeling or controller redesign for each configuration16–19, limiting the scalable deployment of soft robots.

One challenge for multi-configuration adaptation is the domain expertise and intensive labor required for dynamical modeling of soft robots20. Analytical modeling methods, such as the Cosserat rod theory, achieve high modeling accuracy under quasi-static conditions for specific configurations but come with prohibitive computational costs21,22. Data-driven modeling approaches, such as neural network and Gaussian process regression, reduce the need for prior dynamic knowledge, but require extensive data collection and training23–26. The Koopman operator27–29 offers a promising alternative by capturing nonlinear dynamics through linear evolutions in an infinite-dimensional embedding space. Finite-dimensional approximations of Koopman models are commonly integrated with the linear quadratic regulator (LQR)17 and model predictive control (MPC)30, enabling efficient controller synthesis. Among them, Koopman-based MPC has demonstrated its effectiveness in the control of soft robots, but mainly in quasi-static space31–34. A recent configuration-specific Koopman-based control approach advanced a soft robotic arm operating in an inertial dynamic regime through time-delay embeddings17. Despite these advances, the aforementioned approaches require repetitive modeling and controller design to adapt to new configurations, limiting their practical applicability in reconfigurable robotic systems.

The second challenge is the rapid generation of policies to accommodate dynamic variations in multiple configurations. Although reinforcement learning (RL) offers a promising model-free policy design approach35–38, prevalent RL algorithms face high sample complexity, particularly in continuous control tasks39,40. This high sample demand is costly for real-world training, due to the delicate nature and susceptibility to wear and tear of soft robots41. A common approach is to design physical simulators as an alternative to real-world training, including the use of Koopman operators to learn surrogate models for data generation42,43. However, RL algorithms such as deep deterministic policy gradient44 and proximal policy optimization45 still require millions of data to converge39. Moreover, building high-fidelity simulators of soft robots is challenging due to their nonlinear, configuration-dependent dynamics, exacerbating the sim-to-real gap.

In this work, we introduce a generalizable control system that enables fast adaptation across diverse configurations in soft robots (Fig. 1). Our system consists of two key modules: (a) a linear embedding RL (LERL) framework that learns control policies within a linear Koopman embedding space for fast multi-configuration adaptation (Fig. 1a), and (b) three types of soft robot platforms with diverse configurations for experimental validation, including an elephant-trunk-shaped robot, a soft-muscle robot, and a worm-like robot, as well as cross-combinations of the three segment types and hybrid parallel-series configurations (Fig. 1b–d). These robot configurations feature diverse materials, stiffnesses, structural geometries, and assembly patterns. The presented LERL framework is built upon the hypothesis that different soft robot configurations share fundamental but also exhibit configuration-dependent dynamic characteristics. We validated our system experimentally across 33 robot configurations, including 23 variations of elephant-trunk robots, three soft-muscle robot variants, three worm-like robots, and four hybrid-assembled robots combining honeycomb and soft-muscle segments (Fig. 1a and Fig. 6). The experimental results demonstrate that LERL achieves a 6.8× reduction in training samples of a fixed configuration and a 75× reduction in transfer samples across robot configurations while maintaining robust performance in challenging conditions, including high-payload manipulation (1 kg), high-speed motion (Worm: 1.89 m s−1; Elephant-trunk: 0.78 m s−1), and multi-actuator fault scenarios (Table 1). Critically, LERL enables rapid policy transfer to execute diverse tasks (Fig. 1d), allowing a base policy with online updates to empower robots performing previously unattainable real-world skills, including carpenter-inspired nail hammering into wooden boards, bartender-like drinks serving with safe human-robot interaction, fluid calligraphy brush handwriting, and quick hand-eye reaction games mimicking athletic and child training. The presented work represents a step toward a generalizable control framework that enables rapid multi-configuration adaptation in soft robots. Through online data-efficient RL within a pretrained Koopman embedding space, our proposed system decouples control policies from specific configurations, bridging mechanical reconfigurability and control flexibility. Linear embedding RL framework

LERL formulates control policy learning in a pretrained linear embedding space, allowing computationally and data efficient online policy learning across diverse configurations (Fig. 2). The initial step involves pretraining a multilayer perceptron (MLP)-based Koopman embedding Ψ(x) using input-state data collected from a single honeycomb segment (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{S}^{1}$$\end{document}ES1, the subscript “S” denotes “Short” and the superscript “1” indicates the lowest stiffness level determined by a honeycomb hole of 45 mm, as shown in Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Table 1), wherein \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{x}}}}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{12}$$\end{document}x∈R12 the pose (position and orientation) and velocity (linear and angular velocities) of the robot tip (Methods and Supplementary Section 1). In Koopman embedding learning, we introduce control-oriented regularization techniques along with regression objectives to ensure key open-loop control properties (stability and controllability) in the embedding space, which are crucial for closed-loop performance.

With pretrained Koopman embedding, model-free RL is employed to update policies online in the linear embedding space (Fig. 2b). The Koopman embedding and control policy are then transferred across configurations, facilitating real-time policy adaptation for diverse configurations in the unified linear embedding space. To address different adaptation scenarios, we introduce two variants of our method: LERL and LERL-transfer. LERL trains the policy from scratch using a pretrained configuration-specific Koopman embedding. LERL-transfer extends LERL by incorporating policy transfer, beginning with a pretrained policy and Koopman embedding from one configuration, and subsequently updating the policy online to adapt to new configurations.

Results

In this section, we conducted comprehensive experiments to validate the generalizability and adaptability of our control system. The first experiment was to validate the pretrained Koopman embedding and online policy learning performance on one configuration. In subsequent experiments, the policy was transferred to control 33 distinct configurations, with comprehensive validations across all cases. Throughout the experimental study, we compared our approach with the Koopman control method with inertial dynamics (KCID)17, a state-of-the-art (SOTA) solution for dynamic control of soft robots with fixed configurations. KCID is a model-based control method with Koopman operators, which is limited by its reliance on configuration-specific modeling of soft robots. In contrast, our method is configuration-agnostic, achieving a 75× sample reduction in policy transfer across robot configurations and eliminating the bottlenecks of repetitive modeling when adapting to new configurations. Finally, the control policy was transferred across diverse configurations to accomplish real-word challenging tasks previously unattainable for soft robots, including carpenter-inspired nail hammering, bartender-like drinks serving, fluid calligraphy brush handwriting, and quick hand-eye reaction games.

With the pretrained Koopman embedding, model-free RL is employed to update policies online in the linear embedding space. The control policies within the pretrained embedding space were generated using 2000 (2k) samples: 500 samples for offline feedforward policy generation (Fig. 2b; “Methods” and Supplementary Section 2) and 1.5k samples for online feedback policy training (“Methods” and Supplementary Section 4). The data size of 2k and its composition were determined through extensive experiments using different data collections (Fig. 8a). As demonstrated in Fig. 3, LERL using 22k training samples, 20k for offline Koopman embedding training and 2k for policy generation (rightmost bar, Fig. 3d), achieves an 11.37% reduction in tracking error compared to KCID with 6.8× more data (150k samples; leftmost bar, Fig. 3e); and results in a steady-state position error of 0.32 mm, nearly 4× smaller than KCID’s 1.26 mm. The comparison under multiple sample conditions in Fig. 3f demonstrates a substantial sample reduction of LERL over KCID even when the configuration is unchanged. Specifically, LERL with 20k pretraining samples performs on par with KCID using 100k pretraining samples, reducing training data by 5× in a fixed configuration. For new tasks, LERL-transfer can further refine the transferred policy online with just 2k samples, surpassing KCID in both control performance and sample efficiency (Fig. 3g, h).

Policy adaptation to soft-muscle and hybrid-assembled robots

We further validate LERL’s versatility by transferring the control policy to heterogeneous soft-muscle robots and hybrid-assembled robots (Fig. 4c). Although the number of actuators of soft-muscle segments differs from those of honeycomb segments, the learned policies are directly transferable via an action re-allocation strategy (Supplementary Section 5). The hybrid-assembled robots, composed of honeycomb and soft-muscle segments, exhibit substantial differences in materials, structures, and actuations (Fig. 1b, c). Nevertheless, LERL-transfer with only 2k samples enables hybrid-assembled robots to complete tracking tasks, achieving an 8.10% reduction in tracking error compared to KCID with 150k samples (Fig. 4c). These results highlight LERL’s potential for rapid policy adaptation across heterogeneous robots with distinct dynamic characteristics.

Policy adaptation to worm and hybrid-assembled robots

During policy adaptation to the worm segment OM, the Koopman embedding pretrained on the honeycomb segment is reused. An offline policy initialization procedure (Supplementary Section 7) was conducted using 10k samples, as direct policy transfer from the elephant-trunk robot is infeasible due to different actuation modes (positive vs. negative pressure; Fig. 1b, d). As shown in Fig. 4d, LERL-transfer with 12k samples (10k were used for offline policy initialization) outperforms KCID with 20k and 150k samples. In policy adaptation to worm robots with two and three segments, the online learned policy using 2k samples outperforms KCID with 150k samples, reducing the sample size by 75× in policy transfer across robot configurations (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Fig. 6). Moreover, with only 2k samples, LERL enables rapid policy transfer to heterogeneous hybrid-assembled robots, including a parallel-serial hybrid soft robot (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, in the worm robot with configuration OM-OL, LERL achieves position tracking with a steady-state position error of 3.22 mm while operating at the extreme condition (Fig. 4f) with velocities and accelerations up to 1.89 m s−1 and 22.34 m s−2 (Table 1), which are 24% and 92% higher than the previously recorded17 velocity of 1.52 m s−1 and acceleration of 11.6 m s−2.

Serving drinks with safe interactions

With the rise of embodied intelligence, there has been growing interest in enhancing the intelligent manipulation capabilities of robotic arms, focusing primarily on rigid body designs46. However, soft robots provide safer human-robot interactions compared to rigid robots, as rigid bodies with intelligent algorithms do not ensure absolute safety. Our transferred LERL policy enables the elephant-trunk robot to perform the drinks-serving task with precise and smooth operation (Fig. 5d), emulating the actions of a skilled human bartender. As demonstrated in Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Movie 2, the soft robot interacts with the human safely through a fist bump and handshake, adaptively adjusts its position and orientation to pour drinks smoothly into the cup, throws the bottle into the trash can, and hands the cup to the guest gently and safely. The average end-effector positional velocity is 0.018 m s−1 with a variance of 0.000695 m2 s−2, demonstrating smooth motions throughout the serving process. In addition, the robot can maintain stable operation under external human interventions (Supplementary Movie 2) through online policy updates. This task demonstrates our approach’s capability in adaptive control of soft robots under dynamic loads, highlighting the potential in daily delicate task operations that require safe human-robot interactions.

Calligraphy brush handwriting

Calligraphy handwriting with soft arms and brushes is a challenging task. It requires the soft arm and brush to precisely adjust their movements to adapt to the dynamic pressure, speed, and angle required for fluid and clear strokes, while ensuring stability in each brushstroke. Our transferred LERL policy enables the hybrid-assembled soft robot to achieve smooth handwriting (Fig. 5g), overcoming the challenges of high-precision control posed by dynamic variations across different segment types. It ensures that the brush tip, mounted at the robot’s end, accurately and stably hovers at a specific height above the paper while precisely following the character strokes. This avoids distortion or blurring of characters due to excessive force (caused by low height) or uneven speed, as demonstrated in Fig. 5h, i and Supplementary Movie 3. This result highlights the capability of our framework to enable hybrid soft robots with soft brushes to achieve handwriting capabilities previously demonstrated only by rigid robots with rigid pens.

Quick hand-eye reaction game

The control capabilities under high-speed conditions were evaluated on the worm robot for a challenging hand-eye reaction game (Fig. 5j). This task is commonly used by athletes and children to train hand-eye reaction and coordination speeds. An autonomous machine equipped with sticks is hanging at a height of 0.48 meters above the robot, and the sticks are free to fall in an unknown and random order. A camera detects the movements of sticks to determine which stick falls. The soft robot is equipped with a net pocket at its tip, which moves quickly to catch the free-fall stick within 100 ms. The robot with the transferred LERL policy reached speeds of up to 1 m s−1, and intercepted all free-fall sticks (Fig. 5k, l, Supplementary Movie 4). This result demonstrates the potential of our LERL framework for challenging tasks that require high-performing control in high-speed conditions.

Discussion

This work presents a generalizable control system that achieves rapid policy adaptation across 33 distinct soft robot configurations, effectively bridging the longstanding gap between configuration versatility and scalable real-world deployment. By overcoming key limitations in configuration-specific modeling and control, our system addresses one of the primary barriers to the practical application of soft robotics6.

Conventional model-based approaches demand extensive dynamical remodeling for each new configuration, while model-free RL methods typically require either data-intensive real-world interactions or high-fidelity simulators, which are especially challenging to construct for soft robots due to their nonlinear and configuration-dependent dynamics. In contrast, our method achieves rapid adaptation without configuration-specific modeling or controller redesign, enabled by three critical factors. First, the fundamental dynamic characteristics of soft robots are encoded into a unified, pretrained linear latent space, transforming configuration-dependent nonlinear dynamics into structured, approximately linear variations within this shared space. Second, these variations are efficiently addressed through an online, data-efficient RL algorithm, eliminating the need for configuration-specific remodeling and retraining. This approach achieves better performance with substantial sample reduction in policy generation and transfer than prior methods, and operates with real-time efficiency (Supplementary Table 2). Third, our control policy is directly learned and updated within the linear embedding space, mitigating the modeling-control gap that often undermines indirect optimal control strategies. As detailed in our theoretical analysis (Supplementary Section 8), this direct formulation substantially improves adaptation speed and control performance.

The presented framework enables fast multi-configuration adaptation across 33 configurations, demonstrating capabilities ranging from high-payload manipulation (1 kg) to rapid locomotion (1.89 m ⋅ s−1) and human-like skills such as bartending, carpentry, brush calligraphy, and quick hand-eye reaction. Unlike existing configuration-specific controllers, our method dynamically updates policies online in response to configuration changes, emulating the adaptability observed in natural organisms. We believe this online RL method within a pretrained Koopman embedding space has the potential to establish future “foundation” control strategies of heterogeneous robotic systems. Future work will extend the LERL method to robotic systems with more diverse and complex architectures. This includes unified and adaptable control of both rigid and soft robots (Supplementary Section 6 for an elementary validation of rigid robots).

The demonstrated effectiveness on multiple platforms suggests a broad impact potential in the medical, industrial, and exploratory domains, laying the foundation for the development of agile, embodied intelligent systems10 capable of configuration adaptability. Furthermore, the real-time efficiency of policy training facilitates rapid deployment in time-sensitive applications such as surgical robotics, where rapid adaptation is essential for operational efficiency.

Each segment of the Type II robot consists of three parallel pneumatic muscles created by the rubber material. Type III soft robotic platform

Type III robots use a worm-like segment structure 3D-printed from the polyvinyl chloride material, including three configurations ranging from one to three segments and two hybrid soft robots (Fig. 6). The Type III soft robotic platform (Supplementary Fig. 4b) utilizes the same motion capture module but implements the controller in C++ on a CPU (Intel Core i9-13900HX). The calculated actions (50 Hz) are sent to an Arduino Mega 2560 microcontroller, generating analog signals to control vacuum proportional valves (HETIAN ITV2090-212CL5) connected to a vacuum pump. The valve control modulates the air chamber pressure, enabling dynamic arm deflection.

Overview of the LERL methodology

Our three soft robotic platforms encompass 33 distinct configurations (Fig. 6), including diverse assemblies of honeycomb, muscle, and worm segments with varying segment numbers, assembly orders, cross-type combinations, and hybrid parallel-series structures. Collectively, these configurations constitute a representative robot configuration library that spans a wide range of heterogeneous and nonlinear dynamics and supports diverse complex tasks. For example, high-stiffness honeycomb segments enable stable, high-payload tasks such as hammering nails and serving drinks, whereas flexible worm-like segments facilitate quick hand-eye reaction tasks requiring instantaneous high-speed motions (Fig. 5).

Although the fundamental control objective remains consistent across configurations–precise trajectory tracking through robotic tip manipulation–developing a generalizable controller with multi-configuration adaptability presents substantial challenges. Prior approaches, including model-based controllers17,31–34 and data-driven RL methods39,44,45, demonstrate limitations in policy adaptation due to labor-intensive remodeling requirements and persistent sim-to-real transfer gaps.

Here, we present a data efficient LERL framework (Fig. 2), which captures intricate dynamic characteristics through Koopman operators and employs online model-free RL to adapt to dynamic variations across multiple configurations. This framework offers two key features: First, it enables online model-free policy learning in the linear Koopman embedding space, facilitating rapid and data-efficient adaptation to new robot configurations without the computational burden of dynamics remodeling or parameter retraining. Second, conventional modeling and control approaches often neglect the modeling errors that are intrinsic parts of robot dynamics or treat them merely as disturbances to be rejected, degrading control performance. In contrast, our method implicitly incorporates these modeling errors through direct policy optimization (as shown later in equation (9)), improving rather than degrading control performance.

Our methodology begins with data-driven training of the Koopman embedding from a single honeycomb segment, which maps the tip’s pose and velocity into a linear embedding space (Fig. 2a). Building upon this, a data efficient RL algorithm is deployed to update control policies in real-time to address configuration-specific dynamic variations, enabling rapid adaptation across diverse configurations via a base policy with online updates (Fig. 2b).

Loss functions

The standard loss functions in Koopman embedding training include linear regression loss (Le), reconstruction loss (Lr), and regularization on the matrix T (LT), detailed in Supplementary Section 1. To bridge the gap between regression accuracy and control performance, we introduce two control-oriented regularization terms: (a) eigenvalue regularization for open-loop stability guarantees and (b) singular value regularization for enhanced controllability. First, the sparse structure of A is explicitly designed, combining q parameterized real eigenvalues and (nψ − q)/2 conjugate eigenvalue pairs. Specifically, the regularization loss for A is formulated as 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{{{{\bf{A}}}}}={\sum }_{j\in {{{{\mathcal{E}}}}}_{{{{\bf{A}}}}}}\parallel \lambda {({{{\bf{A}}}})}_{j}-{\eta }_{1}{\parallel }^{2},$$\end{document}LA=∑j∈EA∥λ(A)j−η1∥2, where λ(A)j represents the j-th eigenvalue of A, η1 < 1; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{E}}}}}_{A}$$\end{document}EA is the collection of indices associated with eigenvalues that satisfy λ(A)j > η1.

With regularization term LA, the eigenvalues of A could be restricted within the unit circle, achieving balance between regression accuracy and system stability. However, the controllability matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{C}}}}:=[\begin{array}{ccccc}{{{\bf{B}}}} & {{{\bf{A}}}}{{{\bf{B}}}} & {{{{\bf{A}}}}}^{2}{{{\bf{B}}}} & \cdots & {{{{\bf{A}}}}}^{{n}_{\psi }-1}{{{\bf{B}}}}\end{array}]$$\end{document}C:=[BABA2B⋯Anψ−1B] may exhibit undesirably small singular values corresponding to large eigenvalues of A, particularly those that lie within but approach the unit circle. This phenomenon can degrade closed-loop control performance and lead to suboptimal dynamic control behavior. Hence, we introduce an additional regularization loss term to shape the singular value distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{C}}}}$$\end{document}C, formulated as 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{{{{\mathcal{C}}}}}={\sum }_{j\in {{{{\mathcal{E}}}}}_{{{{\mathcal{C}}}}}}\parallel \sigma {({{{\mathcal{C}}}})}_{j}-{\eta }_{2}{\parallel }^{2},$$\end{document}LC=∑j∈EC∥σ(C)j−η2∥2, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma {({{{\mathcal{C}}}})}_{j}$$\end{document}σ(C)j is the j-th singular value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{C}}}}$$\end{document}C, η2 > 0; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{E}}}}}_{{{{\mathcal{C}}}}}$$\end{document}EC is the collection of indices associated with singular values smaller than η2.

The overall loss function used for training the Koopman embedding is as follows: 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L={\alpha }_{1}{L}_{e}+{\alpha }_{2}{L}_{r}+{\alpha }_{3}{L}_{T}+{\alpha }_{4}{L}_{A}+{\alpha }_{5}{L}_{{{{\mathcal{C}}}}},$$\end{document}L=α1Le+α2Lr+α3LT+α4LA+α5LC, where αi, i = 1, ⋯ , 5 are the tunable parameters to balance each loss function. Optimizing the loss in equation (5) generates the configuration-agnostic Koopman embedding Ψ(x) along with the configuration-specific model parameters A and B in equation (1). Only Ψ(x) is shared among diverse configurations for online policy adaptation.

Online model-free policy learning

This section presents the online, model-free policy learning approach with the shared configuration-agnostic Koopman embedding, enabling real-time and rapid adaptation to diverse new configurations. We first describe the policy design within the embedding space, followed by the learning process using a model-free \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{Q}}}}$$\end{document}Q-learning algorithm.

Policy design in the embedding space

Prior studies used an approximated nominal Koopman model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{{{{\bf{s}}}}}}^{+}={{{\bf{A}}}}{{{\bf{s}}}}+{{{\bf{B}}}}{{{\bf{u}}}},$$\end{document}s^+=As+Bu, to formulate LQR17 and MPC31 approaches for soft robots, which often neglect the effect of modeling uncertainty w(s, u) existing in the real Koopman model (1). Also, the Koopman model that best fits model training data could be suboptimal for controller design51. To address this issue, we directly learn the control policy in the linear embedding space through input-state data. The modeling uncertainty is hidden in the input-state data used during policy learning, which contributes to the policy optimization (proven in Section S8).

Policy adaptation across multiple configurations

This section describes how the control policy learned on the single honeycomb segment, with online updates, can be directly adapted to elephant-trunk robots, soft-muscle robots, hybrid-assembled robots, and worm robots, featured with distinct configurations and stiffnesses.

Policy adaptation to elephant-trunk robots

Transferring the policy to new configurations of the elephant-trunk robot does not require extensive remodeling. Only 500 motion data samples are collected under quasi-static conditions to train the feedforward policy ur = Φ(xr) in equation (6). Here, Φ(⋅) is a neural network with a 12-32-64-32-m architecture and Rectified ReLU activation functions (Supplementary Section 2). The feedback policy ue in equation (9) is then refined online to adapt to new configurations in real-time.

Policy adaptation to soft-muscle and hybrid-assembled robots

Each soft-muscle segment has three control inputs, unlike the four in the honeycomb segment. To enable policy transfer from honeycomb to soft-muscle segments, an action re-allocation strategy is designed to map the four-dimensional control space to three through geometric transformations (Supplementary Section 5). This approach allows for the direct deployment of learned policies to heterogeneous soft-muscle and hybrid-assembled robots without requiring prior modeling.

Policy adaptation to worm robots and hybrid-assembled robots

The control policy of the elephant-trunk robot cannot be directly transferred to the worm robot, as they utilize different actuation modes (positive vs. negative pressure actuation). For policy adaptation to the worm robot, a policy initialization for ue is employed, utilizing 10k samples to calculate the associated gain matrix K through KCID (Supplementary Section 7). Subsequently, the learned policy for the configuration OM is transferred and updated online in the linear embedding space to accommodate other configurations of the worm robots. The effectiveness of rapid policy adaptation to heterogeneous worm robots is demonstrated in Fig. 4d. However, a key question arises: Is a newly trained Koopman embedding essential for policy transfer to heterogeneous robots? As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, policy transfer with a newly trained Koopman embedding in a worm segment (LERL-new-transfer) only slightly outperforms that using the pretrained Koopman embedding from the honeycomb segment (LERL-transfer). These results highlight that our framework enables rapid policy adaptation in heterogeneous robots without notable performance degradation. Empirical validation

We conducted comparative studies to verify the effectiveness of our framework, including: (i) online policy learning with different embeddings; (ii) online multi-episode learning performance; (iii) policy transfer performance under varied dataset compositions; (iv) workspace generalization evaluation; (v) performance under different dataset sizes and accelerations; (vi) robustness validation under actuator faults and high payloads; (vii) fast-moving traction stick tracking; and (viii) ablation study on the integral action.

Learning with different embeddings

We evaluated the effectiveness of our approach from two perspectives: the choice of embeddings and the ability to enhance control performance through online learning. As the embedding structure is not unique, we validate the effectiveness of LERL with embedding choices such as MLP, Gaussian kernel (GK), and time-delay (TD) embeddings. As shown in Fig. 7a, online learning reduces tracking error across all adopted embeddings as the learning episodes increase, verifying the effectiveness of online policy learning in performance improvement. The MLP-based embedding achieves the best performance, reducing the tracking error by 43.2% compared to GK and 19.84% compared to TD embedding. Furthermore, increasing the kernel numbers of GK, time delay steps of TD embedding, and the hidden layers of MLP contribute to the reduction of the tracking error (Fig. 7b).

Dataset compositions in policy transfer

We investigated the dataset composition required to enable effective policy transfer and learning across different robot configurations. Specifically, we first conducted experiments using 50, 250, and 500 offline samples to train and evaluate the feedforward policy. We then evaluated transfer performance with 1k, 2k, 3k, and 5k total samples, where 500 samples were allocated to feedforward policy training, and the remainder were used for online policy learning. The results in Fig. 8a show that the feedforward policy offline trained with 500 samples substantially outperforms those with 50 and 250 samples. With 500 offline samples, adding 1.5k online training interaction data could lead to substantial performance improvements. In contrast, further increasing the dataset size beyond 2k samples yields only marginal gains. This suggests that 2k samples (500 offline and 1.5k online) constitute an appropriate dataset size for policy transfer across robot configurations. We also investigated how different sampling distributions influence control performance. Since the 1.5k online interaction data of 2k may not follow a predefined distribution, we utilize two excitation schemes, i.e., sinusoidal and random control inputs, to collect the 500 quasi-static data samples for feedforward policy training (Supplementary Section 2). The comparative results in Fig. 8b show that the feedforward policy trained with sinusoidal input excitation outperforms that trained with random input excitation, and this advantage persists even in online learning scenarios. The reason is that random input excitation could induce a twisted motion status of the soft robotic arm, leading to multiple steady-state control inputs corresponding to the same robot tip state. This non-uniqueness introduces ambiguity during feedforward policy training. In contrast, sinusoidal input excitation mitigates this effect by promoting smoother and more consistent arm motions.

Performance under varied data sizes and accelerations

We compared LERL with KCID under varying data sizes. The LERL was implemented using the transferred policy with MLP embeddings, while KCID was implemented using both TD embeddings and MLP embeddings. LERL and KCID with TD embeddings were evaluated using datasets of 1k, 1.5k, 2k, 3k, and 5k samples, where both methods used 500 samples to train the feedforward policy. The remaining samples were used for online learning in LERL and for updating model parameters in KCID with TD embeddings, respectively. In KCID with MLP embeddings, we used 20k and 150k samples to derive the model-based control policy for comparison. As shown in Fig. 8d, LERL achieves substantially lower tracking error than KCID with TD embeddings under the same sample condition, and also outperforms KCID with MLP embeddings even when the latter uses substantially larger datasets (20k and 150k samples). To further evaluate the practical utility of our method, we adopt a success criterion requiring the position tracking error to be within 1% of the robot length45, i.e., 3 mm of 300 mm. Under this criterion, LERL reaches a 100% of success rate when the number of samples used for policy transfer exceeds 2k (Fig. 8d). In contrast, KCID fails in reaching a precision of 3 mm under all the adopted sample scenarios. With the same robot configuration, we further conducted a comparative study between LERL (transferred with 2k samples) and KCID (trained with 2k samples) to complete hammering experiments with a positional tolerance of 3 mm. The results demonstrate that LERL achieves a success rate of 9 out of 10 trials, whereas KCID succeeds in only 4 out of 10 trials (Fig. 8e, Supplementary Movie 1). We then compared LERL with KCID in a circular tracking task under varied accelerations, ranging from 3.62 mm s−2 to 82.14 mm s−2 across six levels (Fig. 9b, Supplementary Movie 6). The results demonstrate that LERL experiences slight performance degradations when acceleration grows and outperforms KCID at all acceleration conditions.

Ablation study on integral action

The impact of integral action (IA) on policy learning was evaluated using feedforward policies trained with 0.5 and 1.5k quasi-static motion samples. As shown in Fig. 7g, incorporating IA improves performance by 49.5% and 53.54% in the 0.5k and 1.5k sample scenarios, respectively. However, without IA, increasing feedforward policy training data from 0.5 to 1.5k yields only a marginal reduction in tracking error. These results demonstrate that IA effectively compensates for inaccurate feedforward policies and accelerates policy transfer to new configurations with limited quasi-static motion data.
