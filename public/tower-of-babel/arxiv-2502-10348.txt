On Incremental Approximate Shortest Paths in Directed Graphs
Adam Górkiewicz, Adam Karczmarz

Abstract
In this paper, we show new data structures maintaining approximate shortest paths in sparse directed graphs with polynomially bounded non-negative edge weights under edge insertions. We give more efficient incremental ( 1 + ϵ ) (1+\epsilon) -approximate APSP data structures that work against an adaptive adversary: a deterministic one with O ~ ​ ( m 3 / 2 ​ n 3 / 4 ) \widetilde{O}(m^{3/2}n^{3/4}) 1 1 1 Throughout, for convenience we use the standard notation O ~ ​ ( Y ) \widetilde{O}(Y) as a shorthand for the expression O ⁡ ( Y ​ polylog ⁡ n ) O(Y\operatorname{polylog}{n}) . total update time and a randomized one with O ~ ​ ( m 4 / 3 ​ n 5 / 6 ) \widetilde{O}(m^{4/3}n^{5/6}) total update time. For sparse graphs, these both improve polynomially upon the best-known bound against an adaptive adversary [ KŁ19 ] . To achieve that, building on the ideas of [ CZ21 , KMG22 ] , we show a near-optimal ( 1 + ϵ ) (1+\epsilon) -approximate incremental SSSP data structure for a special case when all edge updates are adjacent to the source, that might be of independent interest. We also describe a very simple and near-optimal offline incremental ( 1 + ϵ ) (1+\epsilon) -approximate SSSP data structure. While online near-linear partially dynamic SSSP data structures have been elusive so far (except for dense instances), our result excludes using certain types of impossibility arguments to rule them out. Additionally, our offline solution leads to near-optimal and deterministic all-pairs bounded-leg shortest paths data structure for sparse graphs.

Introduction

Computing shortest paths is one of the most classical algorithmic problems on graphs, with numerous applications in both theoretical and practical scenarios.
Dynamic variants of the shortest paths problem are undoubtedly among the most extensively studied dynamic graph problems.

In
dynamic shortest paths
problems, the goal is to design a data structure that maintains (or provides efficient query access to) the desired information about the shortest paths in a graph
G=(V,E)
subject to edge set updates.
If both edge insertions and deletions on
G
are supported, the data structure is called
fully dynamic
.
If only edge insertions or only edge deletions are allowed, it is called
incremental
or
decremental
, respectively.
Incremental and decremental settings are together referred to as
partially dynamic
.
In the fully dynamic setting, one seeks data structures with low (amortized) update time, whereas in partially dynamic settings, the typical goal is to optimize total update time, i.e., the time needed to process an entire sequence of updates.

While we would ideally like to have efficient fully dynamic data structures, their existence
2
2

2

Or their feasibility without resorting to complex and impractical tools such as fast matrix multiplication
[
AV14
]
.
is often ruled out by conditional lower bounds (e.g.,
[
AV14
,
AHR+19
,
JX22
]
).
Partially dynamic scenarios are often more approachable algorithmically, while still being very useful in applications (such as max-flow, e.g.,
[
BGS21
,
Mad10
]
), including designing the said fully dynamic data structures.

When analyzing randomized dynamic algorithms, one needs to specify the assumptions about how an adversary may adapt to the data structure‘s outputs for the data structure to work correctly and efficiently.
An
adaptive
adversary can adjust the sequence of updates and queries it issues freely based on the preceding data structure‘s outputs.
An
oblivious
adversary has to fix the entire update sequence before the process starts (but without revealing it upfront).
Adaptive data structures are more desirable; e.g., they can be applied in a black-box manner when designing static algorithms.

1.1
Incremental shortest paths

In this paper, we focus on
incremental
shortest paths data structures for weighted
directed
graphs.

Exact data structures.

Even in incremental weighted digraphs, maintaining shortest paths
exactly
is computationally challenging.
As proved recently by
[
SVXY25
]
, one cannot even exactly maintain the distance between a
single
fixed source-target pair in an incremental graph within
O(m^{2-\epsilon})
total update time (for any density
m
), i.e., significantly faster than recomputing from scratch.
The lower bound is conditional on the Min-Weight 4-Clique Hypothesis (eg.
[
AVW14
,
BT17
,
LVW18
]
) and holds even in the
offline
setting, that is, when the update sequence is revealed upfront.
A folklore observation is that the all-pairs shortest paths (APSP) matrix can be updated after an edge insertion in
O(n^{2})
time, and hence this yields an incremental APSP data structure with
O(mn^{2})
total update time.
This is optimal if the
n^{2}
distances are to be maintained explicitly.

Non-trivial exact data structures are known for the special case of unweighted digraphs. Single-source shortest paths (SSSP) in unweighted digraphs can be maintained in
O(nm)
total time
[
ES81
,
HK95
]
, whereas APSP can be maintained in
\widetilde{O}(n^{3})
total time
[
AIMN92
]
.
Both these bounds are near-optimal if the distances are to be maintained explicitly.
There is also an
n^{2-o(1)}
lower bound for maintaining a single source-target distance in an incremental sparse digraph
[
GVW20
]
.

Approximate SSSP.

The lack of non-trivial approaches for exactly maintaining shortest paths in incremental weighted digraphs motivates studying approximate data structures.
For SSSP, the classical ES-tree
[
ES81
,
HK95
]
can be generalized to partially dynamic weighted digraphs with real weights in
[1,W]
at the cost of
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation (where
\epsilon=O(1)
) and has
\widetilde{O}(mn\log(W))
total update time (see, e.g.,
[
Ber09
,
Ber16
]
).
While quadratic in the general case, the approximate ES-tree is rather flexible and might be set up to run much faster – in
\widetilde{O}(mh\log(W))
total time – if guaranteed that shortest (or short enough) paths from
s
use at most
h
hops.

Henzinger, Krinninger and Nanongkai
[
HKN14
,
HKN15
]
were the first to break through the quadratic bound of the ES-tree polynomially, also in the decremental setting, albeit only against an oblivious adversary.
Probst Gutenberg, Vassilevska Williams, and Wein
[
GVW20
]
showed the first SSSP data structure designed specifically for the incremental setting against an adaptive adversary, with
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}\log(W))
total update time.
Their data structure is deterministic and near-optimal for dense graphs.
Chechik and Zhang
[
CZ21
]
showed two data structures for sparse graphs: one deterministic with
\widetilde{O}(m^{5/3}\log(W))
total update time and another Monte Carlo randomized with
\widetilde{O}((mn^{1/2}+m^{7/5})\log(W))
total update time, correct w.h.p.
3
3

3

With high probability, that is, with probability
1-n^{-c}
for any desired constant
c\geq 1
.
against an adaptive adversary.
These two bounds were subsequently improved to
\widetilde{O}(m^{3/2}\log(W))
(deterministic) and
\widetilde{O}(m^{4/3}\log(W))
(randomized) respectively by Kyng, Meierhans and Probst Gutenberg
[
KMG22
]
.

Very recently,
[
CKL+24
]
gave a deterministic
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate incremental min-cost flow algorithm implying that a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate shortest path for a
single
source-target pair in polynomially weighted digraphs can be maintained in almost optimal
m^{1+o(1)}
time.

No non-trivial conditional lower bounds for incremental approximate SSSP have been described and thus the existence of an SSSP data structure with near-linear total update time remains open.

Approximate APSP.

Bernstein
[
Ber16
]
showed a Monte Carlo randomized data structure maintaining
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate APSP in either incremental or decremental setting with
\widetilde{O}(mn\log(W))
total update time.
This bound is near-optimal (even for incremental transitive closure
[
HKN15
]
), but the data structure gives correct answers w.h.p. only against an oblivious adversary.
A deterministic
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate data structure with
\widetilde{O}(n^{3}\log(W))

[
KŁ20

This is near-optimal for dense graphs

HKN15

Interestingly, the incremental APSP data structures for sparse digraphs

Instead, they work by running approximate ES-trees on carefully selected instances where approximately shortest paths from the source have sublinear numbers of hops.
Note that a deterministic bound
\widetilde{O}(nm^{3/2}\log(W))

\widetilde{O}(nm^{4/3}\log(W))

[

This black-box application does not improve upon
[

It is thus interesting to ask whether the data structures of
[

KMG22

1.2
Our contribution

In this paper, we give new incremental
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate shortest paths data structures for sparse directed graphs.
With the goal of obtaining improved all-pairs data structures, we first identify a useful special case of the incremental SSSP problem for which a data structure with near-linear total update time is achievable.
Building on the ideas from
[
CZ21
,
KMG22
]
, we prove:

Theorem 1.1

(Simplified version of Theorem
4.1
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a digraph with edge weights in
{\{0\}\cup[1,W]}
and a source
s\in V
. There exists a deterministic data structure explicitly maintaining distance estimates
d:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying

\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)\leq d(v)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)

for all
v\in V
and only supporting insertions (or weight decreases) of
source edges

e=sv
,
v\in V
.

The total update time of the data structure
is
O(m\log{(nW)}\log^{2}(n)/\epsilon+\Delta)
, where
m
is the final number of edges in
G

and
\Delta
is the total number of updates issued.

Even though the repertoire of possible updates in
Theorem

1.1
seems very limited, we remark that in the exact setting, explicitly maintaining distances under source updates like this requires
\Omega(mn)
total time since one could reduce static APSP to a sequence of
O(n)
updates of this kind.
4
4

4

This is precisely the argument used in
[
RZ11
]
to reduce static APSP to partially dynamic SSSP.

We also show (
Lemma

4.9
) that without compromising the total update time,
Theorem

1.1
can be extended – at the cost of slight accuracy decline – to also accept arbitrary edge insertions, as long as they do not improve the distances from the source too much.
With this observation, we can combine
Theorem

1.1
with the near-optimal APSP data structure for dense digraphs
[
KŁ20
]
and obtain improved incremental APSP data structure for sparse graphs against an adaptive adversary:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a digraph with edge weights in
\{0\}\cup[1,W]
and let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. There exist data structures explicitly
maintaining distance estimates
d:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq d(u,v)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)

for all
u,v\in V
,
subject to edge insertions or weight decreases issued to
G
:

•

A deterministic one with
O(m^{3/2}n^{3/4}\log^{2}(nW)\log(n)/\epsilon^{3/2}+\Delta)
total update time.

•

A Monte Carlo randomized one that produces correct answers against an adaptive adversary (whp.)
with
O(m^{4/3}n^{5/6}\log^{3}(nW)\log(n)/\epsilon^{7/3}+\Delta)
total update time.

Here,
m
denotes the final number of edges in
G
and
\Delta
the total number of updates issued.

For sparse graphs, the deterministic variant improves upon the total update time of the data structure of
[
KŁ19
]
by a factor of
n^{1/12}

By employing the randomized technique of preventing error buildup due to
[
CZ21
]

[

]
is increased to a factor of

.

We believe that
Theorem

1.1
and its extensions may find other applications in the future.

In this paper, we only deal with non-negatively weighted digraphs

We write

An SSSP data structure for source insertions

KMG22
]

Running time analysis.

First of all, observe that all edge updates that do not immediately
decrease some estimate by a factor of at least

The total cost of processing such updates is hence

.

It is easy to see that, apart from the above, the running time is dominated by the total time of the
\PD

In fact, the total update time can be (coarsely) bounded by summing the degrees of the vertices that are pushed to the queue
Q
in
\PD

Similarly as in
Section

3

O(m\ell+n\ell\log{n})=O((m+n\log{n})\log(nW)/\xi).

Note that the only other way a vertex

can be pushed to the queue in

(without a drop in the estimate

) is if

is in the input set of the
\PD
call inside

Thus, consider some call
\PD(C_{k}\cup C_{k+1}\dots\cup C_{r})
inside

for

such that

\deg(C_{k})\leq\deg(C_{k+1})+\dots+\deg(C_{r})
.
Observe that in such a case we can bound the cost of processing the vertices initially pushed to the queue

by
O(\log{n})

5.2
Running time analysis

First of all, by
Theorem

4.1
and
Lemma

4.9
,
the total cost of setting up and maintaining the single-source data structures
(\mathcal{D}_{s})_{s\in V}

and
(\mathcal{D}^{R}_{t})_{t\in V}
through all updates, excluding the resets and the
\Delta
terms denoting the numbers of updates issued to these data structures is
O(n\cdot m\log(nW)\log(n)/\xi)
.

For each of the
O(m\log(W)/(\epsilon b))
phases, we reinitialize and run a fresh data structure of
Theorem

5.1

on a graph with
O(b)
vertices and
O(b^{2})
edges.
The total update time of that data structure, excluding the
\Delta
term counting the issued updates, is
O(b^{3}\log(nW)\log(n)/\xi)
per phase.
Hence, the total cost incurred through all phases is
O(mb^{2}\log^{2}(nW)\log(n)/(\epsilon\xi))
.

Now, let us consider the number of updates issued to the maintained components.
Recall our assumption that the total number of updates issued to
G
is
O(m\log(nW)/\epsilon)
.
Consequently, the total number of ’’non-shortcut‘‘ updates issued to the single-source data structures is
\widetilde{O}(nm\log(nW)/\epsilon)
.
Similarly, the number of updates issued to the data structures of
Theorem

5.1
that are not caused by some estimate change in the single-source data structures is
O(m\log(nW)/\epsilon)
.

Every update of a ’’shortcut‘‘ edge in the maintained data structures is caused by an estimate change in another component.
Hence, the total number of shortcut edge updates can be bounded by the total number of estimate changes in all the maintained components.
That quantity can be bounded in turn by the total update time of all the maintained components ignoring the
\Delta
terms denoting the numbers of
updates issued.
We conclude that this additional cost is asymptotically no more than what we have taken into account so far.

Recall that in the deterministic variant, a reset costs
O(mn\log{n})
time.
In the randomized variant, by
Theorem

4.11
applied with
{\lambda=\rho^{*}}
, a reset costs
O\left(\frac{nm\log^{2}(nW)}{p^{3}\xi^{2}\log{n}}\right)
time.
Recall that the number of resets is
O(m\log(W)/(\epsilon bp))
,
so the total cost of all resets is
O\left(m^{2}n\log(W)\log(n)/(\epsilon bp)\right)

in the deterministic variant and
O\left(\frac{m^{2}n\log^{3}(nW)}{\epsilon bp^{4}\xi^{2}\log{n}}\right)

in the randomized variant.
By using
{\xi:=\epsilon/10p(\log{m}+1)}
, the final running time in the deterministic case becomes:

O\left(nm\log(nW)/\epsilon+nmp\log(nW)\log^{2}(n)/\epsilon+mb^{2}p\log^{2}(nW)\log^{2}(n)/\epsilon^{2}+\frac{m^{2}n\log(n)\log(W)}{\epsilon\cdot b\cdot p}\right).

By setting
b=\sqrt{n}
and
p=\frac{m^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}{n^{1/4}\log{n}}
, the running
time becomes
O(m^{3/2}n^{3/4}\log^{2}(nW)\log(n)/\epsilon^{3/2})
.

In the randomized case, the last term is replaced with

m^{2}n\log^{3}{(nW)}\log{n}/(bp^{2}\epsilon^{3})
.
By setting
b=\sqrt{n}
and
p=\frac{m^{1/3}}{n^{1/6}\epsilon^{1/3}}
, the running time becomes

O(m^{4/3}n^{5/6}\log^{3}(nW)\log(n)/\epsilon^{7/3})
.

Main idea.

We first give a quick overview of the approach.

-approximation of
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,u)
.

Now let
t\in X
and take any simple shortest path
P_{st}
from
s
to
t
in
G^{\gamma}
.
Let
e=uv
be the last edge on
P_{st}
, such that
u\not\in X
and
v\in X
, the existence of which follows from
s\notin X
.
By definition,
d_{\mathrm{init}}(v)\leq d_{\leq\alpha}(u)+w(e)
.
Let
P_{vt}
denote the suffix of the path
P_{st}
going from vertex
v
to
t
.
The length of
P_{vt}
is equal to
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)
as it must itself be a shortest path.
Observe that since
P_{vt}
consists of vertices from the set
X
, it exists in the graph
H
, implying
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(v,t)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)
.
Putting everything together we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,v)+\mathrm{dist}_{H}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{init}}(v)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\leq\alpha}(u)+w(e)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi)^{h+1}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,u)+w(e)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi)^{h+1}\left(\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}\left(s,u\right)+w\left(e\right)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}\left(v,t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1+\xi)^{h+1}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,t).

The lower bound
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,t)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,t)
can be proven similarly.
Let
P_{ft}
be any simple shortest path from
f
to
t
in
H
and let
v
be vertex following
f
on
P_{ft}
.
By definition we have
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,v)=d_{\mathrm{init}}(v)=d_{\leq\alpha}(u)+w(e)
for some
e=uv\in G^{\gamma}
.
We know that the remainder of the path
P_{ft}
(from
v
onwards) is contained in
G^{\gamma}
, thus
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{H}(v,t)
.
We thus get

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}_{H}(f,v)+\mathrm{dist}_{H}(v,t)

\displaystyle\geq d_{\leq\alpha}(u)+w(e)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,u)+w(e)+\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\gamma}}(s,t),

which completes the proof.
∎

Now observe that the depth of every (not immediately ending) recursive call is at most
\log_{2}(\Delta)
since the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
is halved in children calls.
By putting
\xi:=\epsilon/(2\log_{2}(\Delta)+2)
, the approximation factor for every estimate stored in
\mathcal{D}
is
1+\epsilon
by reasoning similar to
Remark

4.2
.

Corollary 6.2
.

For any query
(u,j)
,
d_{\leq j}(u)
is a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate estimate of
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
.

Proof.

Let
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\beta)
be the call for which
\gamma=\left\lfloor\left(\alpha+\beta\right)/2\right\rfloor
is equal to
j
– observe that there must be exactly one such call in the recursion tree.
If
u\in X
for that call, we know that
d_{\gamma}(u)
is stored in
\mathcal{D}
and by
Lemma

6.1
we get

d_{\leq j}(u)\leq(1+\xi)^{h+1}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
, where
h
denotes the depth of the call.
If
u\not\in X
, we had
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)\leq(1+\xi)d_{\geq\beta}(u)
at the beginning of the call, implying that the answer is also at most

(1+\xi)^{h+1}\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
analogously as in (
4
) from the proof of
Lemma

6.1
.
We get the lower bound
d_{\leq j}(u)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
simply because
d_{\leq j}(u)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{i}}(s,u)
for some
i\leq j
and of course
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{i}}(s,u)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
.
Since
(1+\xi)^{h+1}\leq 1+\epsilon
, the proof is complete.
∎

6.3
Running time analysis

We first describe some lower-level details of the implementation.
Starting off, the set
\mathcal{D}
can be implemented as a collection of balanced binary search trees.
For each vertex
u
, we have a separate balanced BST that stores the set of approximations of
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
for different timestamps
j
, sorted in order of increasing
j
.
We can thus perform insertions and queries for the largest/smallest approximation stored for a given range of timestamps in time logarithmic to the number of stored elements.
Later on, we prove that the size of each stored BST is
O(\log(nW)\log^{2}(\Delta)/\epsilon)
.
Therefore, all high-level operations involving
\mathcal{D}
take
O(\log(\log(nW)\log(\Delta)/\epsilon))
time.

Secondly, observe that the bottleneck of the cost of a call
Search
(
Algorithm

4
) is running Dijkstra‘s algorithm in line
8
.
First, we need to construct the set
X
efficiently in line
2
.
It is too costly to do that naively by iterating through all vertices.
Thankfully, observe that any vertex
v
may belong to the set
X
only if it belonged to the set
X
of the parent procedure call, if such a call exists.
Therefore, we could use a slightly modified algorithm, where we pass the set
X
to the children calls as
X_{0}
, so that when constructing respective sets
X
in children calls, we only iterate through
X_{0}
.
Then, the total cost of computing all the sets
X
can be easily seen to be proportional to their total size times the cost of checking the condition
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)>(1+\xi)d_{\geq\beta}(u)
.

The key claim that we prove to argue about the efficiency of the proposed preprocessing is that each vertex may belong to the set
X
of only
O(\log(nW)\log(\Delta)/\xi)
recursive calls of
Search
.

Fix some vertex
u\in V
.
If
u
belongs to the set
X
inside some
Search
call, we say that the call is
costly
for
u
.
Our strategy is to bound the number of costly calls at any fixed depth of the recursion.
Formally, we say that the two recursive
Search
calls are the
1
-descendants
of the call that invoked them.
Inductively, for any integer
h>1
, the
h
-descendants
of some
Search
call are the
(h-1)
-descendants of its
1
-descendants.
For convenience, we also say that any call is its own
0
-descendant.
We can now finally state our main lemma in this section.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
h\geq 0
and any call
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\beta)
that was invoked at some point of the algorithm, the number of its costly
h
-descendants is at most

{1}^{\circ})

0
if
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)=0
,

{2}^{\circ})

1+\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\alpha}(u))
if
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)>0
and
d_{\geq\beta}(u)=0
,

{3}^{\circ})

\log_{1+\xi}\left(\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\beta}(u)}\right)
if
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)>0
and
d_{\geq\beta}(u)>0
.

Proof.

We say that the call
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\beta)
is the
root
call.
Case

1
∘

is obvious: if
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)=0
, then the root call is not costly and hence none of its descendants are.

We prove cases

2
∘

and

3
∘

by induction on
h
.
First, consider
h=0
.
If the root falls in case

2
∘

, then the number of costly
0
-descendants is at most
1
and the claimed bound is
1+\log_{1+\xi}d_{\leq\alpha}(u)\geq 1
, since
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)\geq 1
— we use the fact that the edge weights are in
\left\{0\right\}\cup[1,W]
.
If the root falls in case

3
∘

and is costly, then
d_{\leq\alpha}(u)>(1+\xi)d_{\geq\beta}(u)
and the bound is
\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\alpha}(u)/d_{\geq\beta}(u))>1
, which completes the induction base.

Now assume
h\geq 1
and that the desired bounds hold for
(h-1)
-descendants of every
Search
root call.
If the root is a leaf call, there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, let
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
and
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
be the
1
-descendants of the root call.
Also assume that the root is costly, since in the other case, we also have no costly descendants.
Before the recursive calls, the root computes the estimate
d_{\gamma}(u)
and stores it in
\mathcal{D}
.
Let
d_{\geq\gamma}(u)
denote the largest estimate of
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
stored in
\mathcal{D}
for any
j\geq\gamma
when
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
is called.
Similarly,
let
d_{\leq\gamma}(u)
denote the smallest estimate of
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{j}}(s,u)
stored in
\mathcal{D}
for any
j\leq\gamma
when
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
is called.
Observe that we have
d_{\leq\gamma}(u)\leq d_{\gamma}(u)\leq d_{\geq\gamma}(u)
.
We now have to consider some cases.
If the root falls into case

2
∘

, we have two sub-cases:

2.{1}^{\circ})

d_{\gamma}(u)=0
.
In that case, the call
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
must fall into case

2
∘

and
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
into case

1
∘

.
The total number of their costly
(h-1)
-descendants is by induction
1+\log_{1+\xi}d_{\leq\alpha}(u)
, which bounds the number of costly
h
-descendants of the root.

2.{2}^{\circ})

d_{\gamma}(u)>0
.
In that case,
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
falls into case

3
∘

and
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
into either case

1
∘

or

2
∘

.
Similarly, by
d_{\leq\gamma}(u)\leq d_{\geq\gamma}(u)
, we get the bound

\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\gamma}(u)}+1+\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\gamma}(u))\leq 1+\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\alpha}(u)).

If the root falls into case

3
∘

, we also have two sub-cases:

3.{1}^{\circ})

d_{\gamma}(u)=0
.
Then, the call
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
falls into case

3
∘

, since
d_{\geq\gamma}(u)\geq d_{\geq\beta}(u)>0
, and
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
into case

1
∘

.
We get the bound

\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\gamma}(u)}\leq\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\beta}(u)}
, as desired.

3.{2}^{\circ})

d_{\gamma}(u)>0
.
In that case, the call
\textsc{Search}(\alpha,\gamma-1)
falls into case

3
∘

, and
\textsc{Search}(\gamma+1,\beta)
into either case

1
∘

– where we again get

\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\alpha}(u)/d_{\geq\gamma}(u))\leq\log_{1+\xi}(d_{\leq\alpha}(u)/d_{\geq\beta}(u))
–
or case

3
∘

, where the number of costly
h
-descendants is bounded by:

\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\gamma}(u)}+\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\gamma}(u)}{d_{\geq\beta}(u)}\leq\log_{1+\xi}\frac{d_{\leq\alpha}(u)}{d_{\geq\beta}(u)}.

∎

Applying the above theorem to the call
\textsc{Search}(0,\Delta)
, we can bound the total number of costly calls for a given vertex
u
by

\left(1+\log_{1+\xi}\left(nW\right)\right)\cdot\log_{2}\Delta
, which after substituting

\xi=\Theta(\epsilon/\log\Delta)
is

O(\log(nW)\log^{2}(\Delta)/\epsilon)
.
The total cost of the
Dijkstra
invocations (line
8
of
Algorithm

4
), combined with the total number of
Search
calls, is

O(\Delta\log(\Delta)+m\log(n)\log(nW)\log^{2}(\Delta)/\epsilon).

Recall from
Section

5
that for large values of
\Delta=\Omega(m\log(W)/\epsilon)
, we can reduce our problem to the case where we have at most
O(m\log(W)/\epsilon)
weight decreases in total by filtering the updates in
O(\Delta)
time at the start.
The time complexity thus becomes

O(m\log(n)\log(nW)\log^{2}(\Delta)/\epsilon+\Delta)
.

7
Randomized reset

This section is devoted to proving
Theorem

4.11
.
Below we state the procedure
Randomized-Reset
that achieves the goal if called with parameter
\lambda
.
After the procedure completes, we simply reset the rank offset
\rho
to
\lambda
.
Provided that the procedure makes every vertex
\lambda
-certified, any particular vertex
v
is clearly

(\rho+\rank(v))
-certified afterwards.

Algorithm 5

Randomized-Reset
(
\lambda
)

1:

Z:=\emptyset

2:

for

i=1,\ldots,\lceil c\cdot(25\ell/\lambda)\log{n}\rceil

do

3:

sample an integer
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,\lfloor\lambda/8\rfloor\}
uniformly at random

4:

Y:=\{v:(1+\xi)^{j\cdot 8\ell/\lambda}\leq d(v)\leq(1+\xi)^{(j+2)\cdot 8\ell/\lambda}\}

\triangleright
compute
Y
only if
\deg(Y)=O(m\ell/\lambda^{2})

5:

if

\deg(Y)\leq 400m\ell/\lambda^{2}

then

6:

Z:=Z\cup Y

7:

Propagate
(
Z
)

Note that in
Randomized-Reset
,
the sum of degrees of the vertices in the sampled set
Z
is
O(m\ell^{2}\log{n}/\lambda^{3})
.
Hence the total cost of
\PD(Z)
that cannot be charged to estimate drops
is
O(m\ell^{2}\log^{2}(n)/\lambda^{3})=O(m\log^{2}(nW)\log^{2}(n)/(\lambda^{3}\xi^{2}))
.

The following lemma proves the correctness of
Randomized-Reset
.

Lemma 7.1
.

After
\textsc{Randomized-Reset}(\lambda)
completes, every
v\in V
is
\lambda
-certified
w.h.p.

Proof.

If
P
is a simple path, we will write

to denote the subpath of

that is an
x\to y
path.
Suppose the lemma does not hold and let
u\in V
be a vertex that is not
\lambda
-certified.
There thus exists a shortest path
P=u\to t
in
G[V\setminus\{s\}]
such that

d(t)>(1+\xi)^{\lambda}(d(u)+w(P))
and
|P|
is minimal.

For any
v
, let
d^{\prime}(v)
be the estimate
d(v)
before running
Randomized-Reset
.
By
3.1
, we have
d^{\prime}(v)\leq(1+\xi)(d^{\prime}(u)+w(e))
for any edge
uv=e\in E
.
Since the estimates only decrease, we also have
d^{\prime}(t)>(1+\xi)^{\lambda}(d(u)+w(P))

Define vertices

on the path

as follows. Let
u_{0},\ldots,u_{k}

all lie on

in this order and for all

u_{i}
is the
last

such that the subpath

The vertex

.
Note that one can chain the above inequalities for

Hence, by our assumption, we conclude

.
Observe that, by the definition of

and

, for any

d^{\prime}(u_{i+1})>(1+\xi)(d^{\prime}(u_{i-1})+w(P_{i}P_{i+1}))\geq(1+\xi)d^{\prime}(u_{i-1})

(5)

and

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(u_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi)(d^{\prime}(u_{i})+w(P_{i+1}))

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi)((1+\xi)(d^{\prime}(u_{i-1})+w(P_{i}))+w(P_{i+1}))

Note that there exists at most

Let thus
