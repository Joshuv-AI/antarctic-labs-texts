Accuracy and risk factors for poor cup orientation in total hip arthroplasty: a comparison between supine and lateral registration using a portable navigation system
Authors: Shin Matsumoto, Tomonori Tetsunaga, Kazuki Yamada, Ryuichiro Okuda, Yasutaka Masada, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Yuki Okazaki, Toshifumi Ozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccurate acetabular cup orientation is essential in total hip arthroplasty (THA). Portable inertial navigation may improve reproducibility, but accuracy in lateral decubitus THA may depend on the pelvic registration workflow. We compared cup placement accuracy between supine and lateral registration workflows using NAVBIT (Smith & Nephew PLC) and explored factors associated with large navigation errors.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively reviewed 110 consecutive primary cementless THAs performed in the lateral decubitus position using NAVBIT. The supine-registration workflow included 52 hips (supine pelvic registration followed by repositioning), and the lateral-registration workflow included 58 hips (registration and cup implantation in the lateral decubitus position). NAVBIT recorded radiographic inclination (RI) and radiographic anteversion (RA) intraoperatively; postoperative RI and RA were measured via computed tomography. Accuracy was defined as the absolute difference between navigation records and postoperative measurements. We compared navigation accuracy outcomes between propensity score–matched groups. Outliers were defined as an absolute error > 5° in RI or RA; factors associated with outliers were evaluated in the overall cohort.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 46 matched pairs (n = 92) were analyzed. Postoperative cup orientation was similar between workflows (RI: 40.8°±3.4° vs. 40.3°±4.1°, p = 0.51; RA: 15.2°±3.3° vs. 14.6°±5.9°, p = 0.56). Mean absolute errors were smaller with supine registration for both RI and RA, with a significant reduction for RA (RI: 2.7°±2.3° vs. 3.4°±2.7°, p = 0.24; RA: 2.2°±1.5° vs. 4.1°±3.4°, p < 0.001). Bland–Altman analysis showed narrower limits of agreement with supine registration, particularly for RA. In the overall cohort, lateral registration increased the odds of outlier status (OR 3.11; 95% CI, 1.36–7.09; p = 0.007).ConclusionsFor NAVBIT-assisted THA performed in the lateral decubitus position, standardized supine pelvic registration followed by repositioning reduced absolute navigation errors, particularly for anteversion, compared with lateral registration.

Introduction

Accurate acetabular cup orientation is essential in total hip arthroplasty (THA) because malposition can contribute to instability, impingement, and accelerated wear [1, 2]. Computer-assisted techniques have been used to improve the reproducibility of cup alignment compared with conventional freehand techniques [3, 4]. However, the accuracy of such navigation systems can be compromised by factors such as the registration method and intraoperative pelvic motion. For example, imageless navigation systems that rely on anterior pelvic landmarks may be susceptible to digitization errors due to variable soft tissue thickness [5]. Furthermore, even when rigid positioning devices are used in the lateral decubitus position, component alignment may still be affected by intraoperative pelvic motion and variability in pelvic orientation [6–8].

NAVBIT (Smith & Nephew PLC, Hertfordshire, UK) is a portable inertial navigation system that provides intraoperative measurements of cup orientation [9]. In clinical practice, NAVBIT can be used in both the supine and lateral decubitus positions. For supine THA, pelvic registration can be performed in the supine position; for lateral decubitus THA, registration can be performed either in the lateral decubitus position or in the supine position, followed by repositioning to the lateral decubitus position (flip technique).

In a prior study using lateral decubitus registration, the navigation system was more accurate than a goniometer for anteversion, but not for abduction, suggesting limited measurement accuracy in the lateral decubitus position [10]. A subsequent study comparing supine and lateral decubitus registration suggested that supine registration followed by the flip technique may reduce measurement errors [11]. However, workflow alone does not ensure accurate measurements if pelvic orientation is inconsistent during registration. Pelvic rotation during registration can bias navigation-based cup angles, and pelvic motion can also occur during THA in the lateral decubitus position [12–14].

Therefore, we compared cup placement accuracy between the supine registration and lateral registration workflows using NAVBIT in primary cementless THAs performed in the lateral decubitus position, with standardized supine registration to minimize the influence of pelvic rotation. We quantified absolute navigation errors in radiographic inclination (RI) and radiographic anteversion (RA) and assessed the frequency of outliers. In addition, we explored patient- and surgery-related factors associated with large absolute navigation errors.

Materials and methods

Patient background characteristics

This study was approved by the University’s Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (approval number: 2310-010). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

This was a retrospective review of consecutive patients who underwent primary cementless THA at our institution between May 2023 and April 2025 using a portable navigation system (NAVBIT; Smith & Nephew PLC). Patients were eligible if they underwent primary cementless THA in the lateral decubitus position using NAVBIT during the study period and had both intraoperative navigation records and postoperative CT measurements available. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision THA, had missing intraoperative navigation data, had unavailable postoperative CT data, or if postoperative CT-based cup orientation could not be reliably measured. During the study period, 146 hips were assessed for eligibility, and 110 hips in 110 consecutive patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included in the analysis.

Between May 2023 and March 2024, 52 patients were registered while in the supine position, and then underwent THA in the lateral decubitus position (supine registration group). Between March 2024 and April 2025, 58 patients were registered in the lateral position, and then underwent THA in the lateral decubitus position (lateral registration group).

Surgical procedure and navigation

THA was performed by experienced orthopedic surgeons at our institution. The core surgical team did not change between the two workflow periods. The surgical approach was the modified Watson–Jones approach in 82 hips, the Hardinge approach in 24 hips, and the posterior approach in four hips. A portable navigation system (NAVBIT; Smith & Nephew PLC) was used in all cases. NAVBIT is an inertial navigation system that uses integrated gyroscopes and accelerometers to provide intraoperative measurements of cup orientation.

Registration was performed with the patient in the supine position in the supine registration group. First, we confirmed that the pelvis was parallel to the operating table using a steel square ruler and a mechanical level guide; alignment parallel to the table was confirmed when the ball was centered. Then, a pair of 3.2-mm pins were inserted in parallel into the iliac crest on the operative side using a dedicated guide. The base component of the navigation system was attached to these pins. The table tilt registration method was used for coordinate acquisition: the operating table was tilted 10° to the left and right on each side, and the resulting gravitational vectors were recorded. The axis perpendicular to these vectors was defined as the functional longitudinal axis of the body. This process established a functional coordinate system to determine RI and RA. After registration, the patient was repositioned into the lateral decubitus position, with the navigation base covered using a sterile cup to maintain sterility (flip technique) (Fig. 1).

In the lateral decubitus position, patients were secured using a pelvic positioner with anterior support at the pubic symphysis and posterior support at the sacrum. Pelvic orientation after positioning was verified by palpation of pelvic landmarks. In the lateral registration group, registration and cup placement were performed with the patient maintained in the lateral decubitus position throughout the procedure. After positioning, a pair of 3.2-mm pins were inserted in parallel into the iliac crest on the operative side using a dedicated guide, and the navigation base was fixed to the pins. Coordinate acquisition was then performed using the same table tilt registration method as in the supine registration group: the operating table was tilted 10° to the left and right on each side, and the gravitational vectors were recorded to define the functional longitudinal axis. Based on this functional coordinate system, the system displayed RI and RA during cup placement (Fig. 2).

After acetabular reaming, a single cementless hemispherical cup system (R3 cup, Smith & Nephew PLC, Tokyo, Japan) was implanted in all cases. The standard intraoperative target cup orientation was RI 40° and RA 15°; however, minor adjustments of a few degrees were made at the surgeon’s discretion when spinopelvic alignment or functional cup orientation was considered clinically relevant. Postoperative RI and RA were measured via computed tomography (CT) using three-dimensional templating software (ZedHip; LEXI, Tokyo, Japan), with reference to the functional pelvic plane. The functional pelvic plane was defined as the horizontal plane passing through both anterior superior iliac spines, as described in previous navigation-based THA studies [15]. Radiographic inclination and anteversion were calculated according to the radiographic definitions of cup orientation. Postoperative CT scans were obtained 1 week after surgery. Postoperative CT measurements were performed by two experienced orthopedic surgeons (Observer 1 and Observer 2) who were blinded to the intraoperative navigation values and the registration workflow (supine vs. lateral) at the time of measurement. Observer 1 measured all cases for the primary analysis. To assess measurement reliability, a random sample of 30 hips was re-evaluated: Observer 2 independently measured the same cases, and Observer 1 repeated the measurements after an interval of at least 2 weeks while blinded to the previous results.

The intraoperative cup alignment values recorded by the navigation system were compared with the postoperative measurements to assess accuracy. Navigation accuracy was defined as the absolute difference between the intraoperative navigation values and postoperative CT measurements for both RI and RA.

Sample size and statistical analysis

As this was a retrospective study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. We included all consecutive eligible patients during the study period (110 hips). The primary outcome was the mean absolute error in RA between intraoperative navigation records and postoperative CT measurements in the propensity score–matched cohort. Secondary outcomes included the mean absolute error in RI, signed differences between CT and navigation, Bland–Altman agreement, the proportion of hips within a 5° error margin for both RI and RA, and outlier status. A 5° threshold was selected because this margin has been commonly used in navigation accuracy studies and represents a clinically meaningful tolerance for cup orientation error [11, 16, 17]. Because this was an exploratory retrospective study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed; therefore, secondary analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Propensity score matching was performed to reduce baseline imbalances between the registration workflows. The propensity score, defined as the probability of undergoing the supine registration workflow, was estimated for each hip using a multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diagnosis, and surgical approach as covariates. Greedy 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was conducted on the logit-transformed propensity score within the region of common support, using a caliper width of 0.25 standard deviations of the logit propensity score. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) (rather than hypothesis testing).

In the propensity score–matched cohort, continuous outcomes were compared using paired t-tests, and paired binary outcomes were compared using McNemar tests. Intra- and inter-observer reliability for postoperative CT measurements of RI and RA were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Interobserver ICCs were calculated using a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement (single-measure ICC [1, 2]), and intraobserver ICCs were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (single-measure ICC [1, 3]).

Agreement between intraoperative navigation records and postoperative CT measurements was additionally assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Differences were defined as postoperative CT minus navigation, and 95% limits of agreement were calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations of the differences. Bland–Altman plots were created separately for RI and RA in each registration workflow. Patients were classified as outliers if the absolute error in RI or RA was > 5°, and as non-outliers otherwise. Non-outliers were defined as absolute errors ≤ 5° in both RI and RA.

In the overall cohort, baseline and surgical variables were compared between outliers and non-outliers. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with outliers, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Risk factor analyses for outlier status (> 5° error) were conducted in the overall cohort (n = 110). Because only 40 outlier events were observed and several candidate predictors contained sparse categories, multivariable logistic regression was not performed to avoid model instability and overfitting. Therefore, risk factor analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient demographics and study flow

The patient selection and propensity score matching process is summarized in Fig. 3. During the study period, 146 hips were assessed for eligibility. Thirty-six hips were excluded according to the eligibility criteria, including revision THA (n = 32), missing navigation data (n = 2), unavailable postoperative CT data (n = 1), and unreliable CT-based cup orientation measurement (n = 1). Consequently, 110 hips in 110 patients who underwent primary cementless THA in the lateral decubitus position using NAVBIT were included in the overall cohort. The preoperative diagnoses included osteoarthritis (92 hips), osteonecrosis of the femoral head (eight hips), rheumatoid arthritis (one hip), subchondral insufficiency fracture (two hips), rapidly destructive coxarthrosis (three hips), and post-traumatic osteoarthritis (four hips). The mean age at the time of surgery was 68.3 years (range, 29–91 years); mean height, 1.56 m (range, 1.39–1.79 m); and mean BMI, 24.7 kg/m² (range, 16.0–39.2 kg/m²).

Accuracy of cup orientation

The navigation system functioned normally in all cases. Postoperative RI measured on CT was 40.8°±3.4° in the supine and 40.3°±4.1° in the lateral registration groups, with no difference between groups (p = 0.51). Postoperative RA was 15.2°±3.3° in the supine and 14.6°±5.9° in the lateral registration groups (p = 0.56). Postoperative CT measurements showed high reliability; in a random subset of 30 hips, intraobserver ICCs were 0.98 (95% CI, 0.94–0.99) for RI and 0.98 (95% CI, 0.96–0.99) for RA, and interobserver ICCs were 0.96 (95% CI, 0.91–0.98) for RI and 0.97 (95% CI, 0.93–0.99) for RA. These ICCs indicated excellent reliability of postoperative CT measurements.

Absolute errors between the intraoperative navigation records and postoperative CT measurements were smaller in the supine registration group for both RI and RA than those in the lateral registration group. The mean absolute error for RI was 2.7°±2.3° (range, 0°–9°) in the supine and 3.4°±2.7° (range, 0°–11.4°) in the lateral registration groups (p = 0.24). For RA, the mean absolute error was 2.2°±1.5° (range, 0.3°–7°) in the supine and 4.1°±3.4° (range, 0.2°–16.2°) in the lateral registration groups (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

As a sensitivity analysis, cases with rapidly destructive coxarthrosis and post-traumatic osteoarthritis or post-traumatic hip conditions were excluded, and propensity score matching was repeated using the same matching strategy. Seven hips were excluded, and repeated matching yielded 39 matched pairs (n = 78). In this sensitivity cohort, the mean absolute RA error remained smaller in the supine registration group than in the lateral registration group (2.2° ± 1.6° vs. 3.4° ± 2.7°, p = 0.020), whereas the difference in mean absolute RI error was not significant (2.7° ± 2.3° vs. 3.4° ± 2.5°, p = 0.266).

Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated narrower 95% limits of agreement in the supine registration group than those in the lateral registration group, particularly for RA (Fig. 4).

For RI, the mean difference (CT − navigation) was 1.7° with 95% limits of agreement from − 4.5° to 7.9° in the supine registration group, and 2.0° with limits from − 5.5° to 9.5° in the lateral registration group. For RA, the mean difference was − 0.1° (− 5.2° to 5.3°) in the supine and − 0.4° (− 10.9° to 10.1°) in the lateral registration groups.

As shown in Fig. 5, the proportion of hips within the target alignment was numerically higher in the supine group than in the lateral group. Using a 5° error margin for both RI and RA (i.e., absolute errors in both RI and RA ≤ 5°), 78.3% of hips in the supine group and 58.7% in the lateral group were within the target alignment (p = 0.053). Conversely, 21.7% (10/46 hips) in the supine group and 41.3% (19/46 hips) in the lateral group were classified as outliers (absolute error > 5° in either RI or RA). No intraoperative complications, such as fracture, loosening of the navigation base, or system malfunction, occurred. No postoperative dislocation or revision surgery was required during follow-up (mean, 25.1 months; median, 25.5 months; range, 12–35 months).

Factors associated with poor accuracy

Risk factor analyses for outlier status were performed in the overall cohort of 110 hips. Overall, 40 hips were classified as outliers (absolute error > 5° in either RI or RA), whereas 70 hips were classified as non-outliers (absolute errors ≤ 5° in both RI and RA). In the univariable comparison, registration position (supine vs. lateral) was the only factor associated with an absolute error > 5° (p = 0.007), whereas no significant associations were observed for age, sex, diagnosis, Crowe classification, height, weight, BMI, or surgical approach (Table 3). In the univariable logistic regression analysis, registration in the lateral position was significantly associated with being an outlier, with the odds of an absolute error > 5° being approximately threefold higher in the lateral group than in the supine group (OR, 3.11; 95% CI, 1.36–7.09; p = 0.007) (Table 4). No other demographic or surgical factors demonstrated a statistically significant relationship with outliers in this model.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated the accuracy of acetabular cup orientation using an inertial portable navigation system (NAVBIT, Smith & Nephew PLC) in primary cementless THA performed in the lateral decubitus position. Supine registration followed by lateral decubitus surgery (flip technique) resulted in smaller absolute errors than those in registration performed entirely in the lateral decubitus position. In univariable analysis, registration workflow was the only factor that showed a statistically significant association with outlier status, defined as an absolute error > 5° in RI or RA, with lateral registration showing approximately threefold higher odds of being an outlier than supine registration (OR, 3.11; 95% CI, 1.36–7.09).

Cup malposition can contribute to instability, impingement, and wear; therefore, improving alignment reproducibility and reducing outliers remain practical goals in THA [18]. However, alignment accuracy should not be judged solely by whether the cup falls within a single “safe zone.” The Lewinnek safe zone, originally proposed to reduce dislocation risk, has long served as a benchmark for cup orientation [19]. However, accumulating evidence shows many dislocations still occur even when cup orientation falls inside the Lewinnek safe zone [20–22]. Thus, the notion of a universal safe zone applicable to every patient has been challenged [23]. One reason is that pelvic orientation differs among patients and can change with spinopelvic motion, which alters functional cup orientation [24–26]. In light of these issues, we considered “outliers,” cases with large alignment deviations, as a more pragmatic endpoint than simply “hitting the safe zone.” We therefore examined patient- and surgical factors associated with large navigation errors.

Previous studies have identified BMI and surgical approach as potential factors associated with acetabular cup malpositioning [27], and higher BMI has been associated with larger cup angle errors when accelerometer-based portable navigation systems are used [16]. However, susceptibility to body habitus may be system dependent, as BMI-associated accuracy loss has been reported with accelerometer-based navigation but not with three-dimensional mini-optical navigation [17]. In landmark-based imageless navigation, anterior pelvic soft-tissue thickness and the reliability of pelvic reference planes may also affect cup positioning accuracy [28–30]. In the present NAVBIT cohort, BMI and surgical approach were not significantly associated with outlier status; however, because the sample size was limited and soft-tissue thickness and navigation base stability were not directly assessed, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Unlike landmark-based imageless navigation systems, which rely on digitization of anatomical pelvic landmarks, NAVBIT establishes a functional coordinate system using inertial sensors and table-tilt registration based on gravitational vectors. Variability in pelvic orientation during lateral decubitus THA may explain the workflow effect. Previous studies have shown that pelvic tilt and movement can vary during the procedure and that external alignment guides or rigid positioning do not fully prevent variability in lateral pelvic orientation [7, 8, 31]. With NAVBIT, lateral registration may be affected by subtle pelvic roll, tilt, or rotation during coordinate acquisition, whereas supine registration permits acquisition of the reference coordinate system before lateral positioning. This mechanism is consistent with previous reports showing that accelerometer-based portable navigation can provide accurate cup placement in both supine and lateral decubitus positions and that imageless accelerometer-based navigation can improve cup placement accuracy during lateral decubitus THA [32, 33]. At the same time, these reports support the concept that registration and coordinate acquisition remain important determinants of accuracy in portable inertial or accelerometer-based workflows. Although prior studies have reported mixed effects of the flip technique depending on the reference-plane definition, registration method, and system-specific error sources, our findings support the relevance of registration workflow in inertial portable navigation [4, 11].

Recent advances in digital THA have also emphasized the role of AI-assisted 3D preoperative planning for implant sizing, acetabular cup positioning, and reconstruction of complex hip deformities [34–36]. These technologies are complementary to intraoperative navigation: preoperative planning defines the intended reconstruction and component position, whereas intraoperative navigation may help reproduce the intended orientation during surgery. The present study specifically focused on the intraoperative registration workflow of a portable inertial navigation system.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study, and the two registration workflows were used in different time periods. Therefore, chronological bias related to learning curve effects, increasing familiarity with the navigation system, workflow refinement, and other temporal factors could not be excluded. Although such bias would likely have favored the later lateral-registration group, the non-contemporaneous design remains an important limitation. Second, pelvic kinematics and intraoperative pelvic motion were not directly measured; therefore, the proposed mechanism linking lateral registration to increased error should be interpreted as an association rather than proof of causality. Third, preoperative spinopelvic parameters, including pelvic incidence, pelvic tilt, sacral slope, and lumbar lordosis, were not collected; therefore, patient-specific functional cup targets could not be evaluated. Fourth, THA was performed by multiple experienced surgeons, and surgeon-level variation in registration technique or cup implantation may have influenced the results. Fifth, the outlier definition was based on a 5° threshold; other thresholds may yield different associations. In addition, multiple secondary outcomes were analyzed without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons; therefore, secondary findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the study focused on radiographic navigation accuracy and was not designed or powered to assess rare clinical outcomes such as dislocation or revision.

In conclusion, NAVBIT provided acceptable intraoperative guidance for acetabular cup orientation in primary cementless THA performed in the lateral decubitus position. Supine registration followed by lateral decubitus surgery achieved smaller absolute errors than lateral registration performed entirely in the lateral position. Registration workflow was the only factor that showed a statistically significant association with outlier status in univariable analysis, whereas BMI and surgical approach were not significantly associated with outlier status in this cohort. These findings suggest that selecting an appropriate registration workflow is important to optimize navigation accuracy.
