Access Probability Optimization in RACH: A Multi-Armed Bandits Approach
Ahmed O. Elmeligy, Ioannis Psaromiligkos, and Au Minh

Abstract
The use of cellular networks for massive machine-type communications (mMTC) is an appealing solution due to the availability of the existing infrastructure. However, the massive number of user equipments (UEs) poses a significant challenge to the cellular network’s random access channel (RACH) regarding congestion and overloading. To mitigate this problem, we first present a novel approach to model a two-priority RACH, which allows us to define access patterns that describe the random access behavior of UEs as observed by the base station (BS). A non-uniform preamble selection scheme is proposed, offering increased flexibility in resource allocation for different UE priority classes. Then, we formulate an allocation model that finds the optimal access probabilities to maximize the success rate of high-priority UEs while constraining low-priority UEs. Finally, we develop a reinforcement learning approach to solving the optimization problem using multi-armed bandits, which provides a near-optimal but scalable solution and does not require the BS to know the number of UEs in the network.



I
Introduction

The
random access channel (RACH)
is a crucial component in cellular networks, enabling
user equipments (UEs)
to request access for communication and establish a connection to the
base station (BS)

[
1
]

The RACH is created through a periodic allocation of time and frequency resources, known as
RACHs
slot
[
2
]
.
The periodicity of the
RACH
slot is determined by the physical
RACH
configuration index, which is broadcasted by the
BS
.
During a
RACH
slot,
UEs
can access the network by transmitting a randomly selected preamble to the
BS
from a finite set of preambles.
A preamble is a sequence of symbols uniquely identifying a
UE
to the
BS
; it can be thought of as a
UE
’s digital signature.

If a single
UE
chooses a unique preamble, the
BS
can successfully decode it.
However, challenges arise in the context of
massive machine-type communication (mMTC)
applications, where a large number of
UEs
accessing the network through the
RACH
can lead to congestion and overloading issues
[
3
,
4
]
.
Futhermore, congestion can be exacerbated by recurring
UEs
that periodically synchronize to communicate simultaneously with application servers
[
5
]
.
The
RACH
congestion problem arises when multiple
UEs
select the same preamble during a slot, resulting in collisions
[
6
]
.
In essence, this situation can be likened to a scenario where a finite set of
resource blocks (RBs)
, represented by the preambles, are randomly accessed by a pool of
UEs
.
The collisions pose a significant challenge in effectively managing the access requests from a massive number of
UEs

[
7
,
8
]
.

The challenge intensifies due to the implementation of
priority access (PA)
, which consolidates multiple
mMTC
applications to be served by a single cellular network.
This strategic approach aims for cost efficiency by leveraging existing infrastructure and eliminating the necessity for deploying multiple networks.
However, the amalgamation of diverse
mMTC
applications seeking
PA
introduces complexities in the
RACH
mechanism, necessitating innovative approaches to mitigate congestion and enhance the overall efficiency of the access procedure.

I-A

Related Work

There have been several studies on the
PA
problem in the literature.

Statistical based multiple access (SPMA)
is a
PA
scheme first utilized by the U.S. Military
Tactical Targeting Network Technology (TTNT)

[
9
]
.
In
SPMA
, each
UE
first estimates how busy the channel is by calculating the
channel occupancy statistic (COS)
.
Several methods have been developed to calculate the
COS

[
9
,
10
]
; for example, to find the
COS
, each
UE
counts the number of transmissions heard from other
UEs
in the network using a weighted sum over a predefined statistical sliding window
[
11
]
.
Then, the
COS
is compared to a threshold that depends on the
UE
’s priority.
If the
COS
is below the threshold, the
UE
can access the channel.
Otherwise, the
UE
is denied access.
Once channel access is granted,
SPMA
uses a combination of frequency and time hopping in conjunction with turbo codes to split the packet into multiple bursts and transmit them over the channel
[
12
]
.

On the other hand, the work in
[
13
]
dynamically adjusts the number of available
RBs
for each priority class based on the network load before each
RACH
slot to reduce access delay.
Another
PA
scheme is
Access class barring (ACB)
proposed by the
3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)
to mitigate access congestion in
long-term evolution (LTE)
random access
[
14
]
.
In
ACB
, each
UE
is assigned a backoff time or barring rate depending on the predetermined priority level of the
UE
.
A challenge with
ACB
is that the barring rate for each
UE
is fixed; as a result, if a
low-priority user equipment (L-UE)
is denied access multiple times to the channel, its buffer might overflow, resulting in dropped packets.
A solution to the above problem is the
queue-aware priority access classification (QPAC)
-based
ACB
, which dynamically adjusts the barring rate based on the packet queue size of each
UE

[
15
]
.
In
QPAC
, a higher priority is assigned to
UEs
with longer packet queues in their respective buffers.

The authors in
[
16
]
dynamically allocate non-overlapping
RBs
to each priority class by first estimating the number of
UEs
in each class using a Bayesian approach.
This estimation is based on the number of empty
RBs
, leaving out potentially useful information that could improve the estimator’s accuracy.
Based on the estimation, the
RBs
are then distributed to achieve a success rate that aligns with the corresponding priority class.

Moving beyond conventional approaches, several studies have explored the application of
reinforcement learning (RL)
techniques to optimize resource allocation in cellular networks
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

RL
, a subfield of machine learning, involves training agents via trial and error to make decisions in an environment to achieve specific goals.
Through continuous interaction and adaptation,
RL
enables autonomous decision-making, making it well-suited for addressing the complex and evolving nature of resource allocation challenges in cellular networks, where the network is subject to constant changes in
UE
behavior, traffic patterns, network conditions, and overall demand for resources.
For instance, in the context of cellular networks, Q-learning has been employed to maximize spectrum utilization efficiency
[
21
]
, while in
[
22
]
, learning automata in combination with Q-learning is used to minimize
UE
collisions.
Furthermore, the authors in
[
23
]
leverage a proximal policy optimization
RL
algorithm to dynamically adjust the number of available
RBs
and find
ACB
parameters that maximize the number of successful
UEs
.
Deep
RL
approaches, as seen in
[
24
]
, aim to reduce collisions and improve the success probability of
UEs
by dynamically distributing
RBs
across different priority classes instead of using a fixed allocation such as in
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
; this is possible due to the ability of deep
RL
to leverage deep neural networks and learn complex and non-linear hierarchical relationships between the network’s state and the optimal action to take.
Additionally, distributed
RL
is applied in
[
28
]
to each
UE
’s
RB
selection policy to minimize the access delay.

The authors in
[
29
]
mitigate congestion by aggregating data from multiple UEs before transmission. Q-learning is employed to determine the optimal amount of data to aggregate before initiating the random access process, effectively reducing the number of UEs contending for access.

These
RL
techniques showcase promising avenues for enhancing the efficiency and performance of cellular networks in dynamic and complex environments.

I-B

Novelty and Contributions

All the above
PA
schemes assume that the
UE
randomly selects a
RB
from a uniform distribution.
To our knowledge, there is a gap in the existing literature regarding a model that considers non-uniform preamble selection, which offers increased flexibility in resource allocation across different
UE
priority classes.
The flexibility of non-uniform preamble selection allows each priority class to achieve the desired
quality of service (QoS)
metrics by adjusting the access probabilities.
It is noteworthy that our investigation, as detailed in
Section

V
, demonstrates that the optimal solution, i.e., the access probabilities that maximize the success rate of high-priority
UEs
while constraining low-priority
UEs
, involves a non-uniform preamble selection approach.
This highlights the practical importance of considering other access probability distributions beyond the conventional uniform distribution, as it can significantly enhance the performance of cellular networks.

Specifically, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We introduce a novel dimension to the field by accommodating non-uniform preamble selection within the same
RACH
slot. This approach generalizes the existing literature that assumes uniform access probabilities and allows us to find the optimal access probabilities for each priority class.

•

We present a two-priority
RACH
model, which allows us to define access patterns that describe the random access behavior of
UEs
as observed by the BS in terms of the locations of
high-priority user equipment (H-UE)
successes,
L-UE
successes, collisions, and empty
RBs
in
RACH
slots.

•

We propose an algorithm that finds the optimal access probabilities to maximize the success rate of
H-UEs
while constraining
L-UEs
.

•

We develop a
multi-armed bandit (MAB)

RL
approach to solving the access probabilities optimization problem, which provides a near-optimal but scalable solution and does not require the
BS
to know the number of
UEs
in the network. Our
RL
method employs a systematic approach that initially discretizes the
action space (AS)
, then reduces its size. Subsequently, the method incorporates reward scaling to refine the learning process further
[
30
]
. We utilize cross-entropy for the
MAB
updates, which is particularly advantageous for large-scale scenarios, as it does not necessitate trying out every single action, thereby significantly improving computational efficiency.

•

We present an alternative
compact action space (CAS)
that incorporates a lookup table with the optimal access probabilities of varying network, allowing us to compress the
AS
further and as a byproduct estimate the network load. However, the tradeoff involves introducing additional hyperparameters, which would need to be tuned based on a rough estimate of the network load.

This paper is structured as follows:

Section

II
presents the two-priority
RACH
model.
The optimal access pattern allocation problem is formulated in
Section

III
.

Section

IV
presents the
MAB
formulation to solve the optimization problem.
Finally,
Sections

V
and
VI
present the numerical results and conclude the paper, respectively.

II
System Model and Problem Statement

We consider a system model with
n^{h}

H-UEs
and
n^{l}

L-UEs
assuming that
n^{h}
and
n^{l}
are fixed but unknown to the

.
Time is discretized into slots, indexed by

, and each slot is divided into
M

.
At each slot

have a packet to transmit and that each

randomly chooses an

By denoting

and

the respective probabilities that a

i
and an
L-UE
chooses
RB

i
, let
\bm{p}^{h}=[p^{h}_{1},\dots,p^{h}_{M}]
and
\bm{p}^{l}=[p^{l}_{1},\dots,p^{l}_{M}]
be vectors collecting the
RB

, and an
L-UE
, respectively.
Clearly, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p^{h}_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p^{l}_{i}=1
.
We assume the access probability vectors
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p}^{l}
are known at the
BS
, which is responsible for generating and broadcasting these probabilities to the
UEs
.

At each of the
M

RBs
, one of the following events may occur: 1)Event
H_{i}
: A single
H-UE
selects
RB

i
. 2) Event

: A single

selects
RB

i
. 3) Event
\Phi_{i}
: No
UE
selects
RB

i
, i.e., the
RB
is unoccupied or empty. 4) Event

: More than one
UE
selects
RB

i
, i.e., a collision occurs.

Each event can be detected by the

, however, in the case of a collision, the

does not know how many
UEs
are involved
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
23
]
.
Thus, at time slot

, the
BS
observes an access pattern that is the sequence of events over the
M

RBs
.
An example of such patterns observed over
T
time slots is shown in
Fig.

1
.
It is assumed that
\bm{\pi}_{1},\bm{\pi}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\pi}_{T}
are
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
.

The problem we consider in this paper is to find the optimal access probability vectors
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p}^{l}
that maximize the
H-UE

RACH
throughput while guaranteeing that the
L-UE

RACH
throughput is above a certain threshold.
In the next section, we first define the
H-UE

RACH
and
L-UE

RACH
throughput metrics, and then formally describe the optimization problem.

III
Access Probability Optimization Formulation

We will start by developing the performance metrics, using which we will then formulate the optimization problem.
For this section, we assume that the
BS
knows the network load,
n^{h}
and
n^{l}
.
This assumption is relaxed in the next section when we develop a
MAB
approach to solve the optimization problem.

III-A

Throughput Metrics

The
RACH
throughput is defined as the average number of UEs per RACH slot that successfully avoid transmission collisions
[
13
]
.
Since two priority classes was defined in this paper, we consider two throughputs
\mu_{h}
and
\mu_{l}
as the average number of successful
H-UEs
and
L-UEs
per
RACH
slot respectively
In such a condition, by denoting
H_{\bm{\pi}}
and
L_{\bm{\pi}}
the number of successful
H-UEs
and
L-UEs
for a given access pattern
\bm{\pi}
, the expected number of successful
H-UEs
and
L-UEs
per
RACH
slot, are given by

\mu_{h}=\sum_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}H_{\bm{\pi}}P(\bm{\pi})

(1)

\mu_{l}=\sum_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}L_{\bm{\pi}}P(\bm{\pi})

(2)

where
\Pi
is the set of all possible access patterns.
However, finding
\Pi
is non-trivial, especially for large networks.
Hence, we develop a recursive backtracking algorithm, the details of which are omitted for brevity.

The calculation of
P(\bm{\pi})
represents the probability of observing the access pattern
\bm{\pi}
.
Let sets
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathbf{\mathit{\Phi}}
, and
\mathcal{X}
represent the indices of
RBs
with events
H_{i}
,
L_{i}
,
\Phi_{i}
, and
X_{i}
, respectively.
Thus,
\bm{\pi}
is the intersection of events
H_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{H}
,
L_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{L}
,
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in\mathbf{\mathit{\Phi}}
, and
X_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{X}
.
The probability
P(\bm{\pi})
is then found from this intersection.

P(\bm{\pi})=P\left(\overline{H}\right)P\left(\overline{L}\middle|\overline{H}\right)P\left(\overline{\Phi}\middle|\overline{H},\overline{L}\right)P\left(\overline{X}\middle|\overline{H},\overline{L},\overline{\Phi}\right)

(3)

where
\overline{H}=\bigcap_{i\in{\mathcal{H}}}H_{i}
,
\overline{L}=\bigcap_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}}L_{i}
,
\overline{\Phi}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbf{\mathit{\Phi}}}\Phi_{i}
, and
\overline{X}=\bigcap_{i\in{\mathcal{X}}}X_{i}
.
More detailed derivation of the factors in
3
can be found in our previous work
[
34
]
.

Problem Formulation for Optimal Access Pattern Allocation

This section formulates the optimization problem based on the previously developed two-priority
RACH
model.
We focus on maximizing the expected
H-UE

RACH
throughput,
\mu_{h}
,
under the constraint that the
L-UE

RACH
throughput,
\mu_{l}
, is greater than or equal to a specified threshold,
\gamma
.

1

1

Otherwise, the
L-UE

RACH
throughput will be zero, and the network will be reduced to a single-priority network.

The optimization problem is formulated as a constrained non-linear continuous optimization problem, as shown in
4a
to
4f
.
Constraints
4c
,
4d
,
4e
and
4f
ensure that
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p^{l}}
are probability vectors.

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{p}^{h},\bm{p}^{l}}\quad

\displaystyle\mu_{h}

(4a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mu_{l}\geq\gamma

(4b)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}p^{h}_{i}=1

(4c)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}p^{l}_{i}=1

(4d)

\displaystyle p^{h}_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}

\displaystyle p^{l}_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}

(4f)

The optimization problem can be solved using a gradient-based optimization solver implemented such as the
sequential quadratic programming (SQP)
algorithm, which has been proven to be a robust algorithm that efficiently solves non-linear optimization problems; Schittkowski
[

]
showed that

surpasses other optimization methods in efficiency, precision, and robustness.
As we will see in
Section

, the above optimization formulation does not scale well in terms of time complexity.
Thus, we propose a novel approach to solve the optimization problem using

.

IV
Multi-Armed Bandits Problem Formulation

The
MAB
problem is a sequential decision-making problem where an agent repeatedly chooses from a set of actions, known as arms, in a stochastic environment.
Each arm has an unknown reward distribution, and the agent’s goal is to maximize the reward over time.
This is done by utilizing a Q-value for each action, which serves as an estimate of the expected reward associated with choosing that action.
Q-values enable the agent to make informed decisions based on past experiences.

MAB
is a simplified version of the
RL
problem since the agent does not have to consider the state of the environment when selecting an action.

The agent must balance exploration and exploitation to obtain a good estimate of the Q-value associated with each action. We denote an action by
A
, and the set of all possible actions as
AS
.
Each time the agent performs
A
it observes a reward
r
.
Then, the Q-value for
A
,
Q(A)
, is updated as follows:

Q(A)=Q(A)+\frac{1}{v[A]}\left(r-Q(A)\right)

(5)

where
v[A]
is the number of times
A
has been performed.

IV-A

Action Space and Reward

For the problem at hand, we define
A
as a pair of access probability vectors
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p}^{l}
.
The elements in
\mathbf{p}^{h}
and
\mathbf{p}^{l}
are continuous, which means that
A
is uncountable.
Therefore, the
MAB
problem formulation is not directly applicable to our problem as it assumes that the
AS
is discrete and finite.
This assumption is made for several reasons.
First, many classic
MAB
algorithms, such as
epsilon-greedy (
\epsilon
-greedy)
,
decaying epsilon-greedy (DEG)
,
upper confidence bound (UCB)
, and softmax, are designed for discrete
ASs
.
Second, discrete
ASs
lower the computational complexity and improve the memory efficiency of algorithms since look-up tables can store and manage information about the values or rewards associated with each action.
This is particularly important when dealing with large-scale problems.
In
Section

IV-B
we will present two discrete and finite
ASs
tailored to our problem.

Our goal is to find the optimal access probability vectors
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p}^{l}
that maximize the
H-UE

RACH
throughput while maintaining a minimum
L-UE

RACH
throughput.
Thus, we define the reward,
r
, as follows:

r=\begin{cases}\mu_{h,T}&\text{if }\mu_{l,T}\geq\gamma\\
\rho\mu_{h,T}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(6)

where
\rho\geq 0
is a penalty factor,
T
is the number of
RACH
slots, and
\mu_{h,T}
and
\mu_{l,T}
, are the numerical expectations of the
RACH
throughputs, respectively, given by

\displaystyle\mu_{h,T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(RB_{i}=h)

(7)

\displaystyle\mu_{l,T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(RB_{i}=l)

(8)

In the above,
RB_{i}
represents the
i
-th
RB
in the
RACH
slot while,
\mathbbm{1}(RB_{i}=h)
and
\mathbbm{1}(RB_{i}=l)
are indicator functions that return one if the
RB

i
is occupied by a single
H-UE
or
L-UE
, respectively, and zero otherwise.
In
6
, the reward is
\mu_{h,T}
if the
L-UE

RACH
throughput is above the threshold
\gamma
; otherwise, the reward is
\mu_{l,T}
multiplied by the penalty factor
\rho
to penalize actions that result in
\mu_{l,T}<\gamma
.

In our definition of the reward, we did not use the throughputs
\mu_{h}
and
\mu_{l}
, defined by
1
and
2
, respectively, for two reasons: First, unlike
\mu_{h}
and
\mu_{l}
,
\mu_{h,T}
and
\mu_{l,T}
do not require the knowledge of
n^{h}
and
n^{l}
.
Second, numerical expectations are more suitable for
MAB
as they significantly reduce the time and computational resources required to simulate the
RACH
procedure.

IV-B

Action Space Formulations

We will consider two distinct

IV-B

IV-B
2
Compact Action Space

We now propose an alternative approach to construct a reduced-size
AS
.
In this approach, the
AS
includes the optimal access probabilities for each pair
(n^{h},n^{l})
where
n^{h}
ranges from
0
to
N^{h}_{s}
and
n^{l}
from
0
to

, with
N^{h}_{s}
and
N^{l}_{s}
denoting the maximum number of
H-UE
and
L-UE
to consider, respectively.
The optimal access probabilities are calculated offline inadvance by solving the optimization problem in
Section

III-B
, and saved in a look-up table for future use by the
MAB
optimization process.

An interesting byproduct of this approach is an estimate of the network load, which can be easily obtained as the values of
n^{h}
and
n^{l}
that correspond to
\bm{p}^{h}
and
\bm{p^{l}}
determined by the MAB method.
The accuracy of this network load estimate is investigated in

The size of the

is
N^{h}_{s}\times N^{l}_{s}
, which is significantly smaller than the

and
N^{l}_{s}
are chosen carefully: Large enough to capture the true values of

and
n^{l}
but small enough to ensure that the

However, selecting appropriate values for
N^{h}_{s}
and
N^{l}_{s}
is challenging and requires a rough prior estimation of the network load. Although the approach ultimately provides an accurate network load estimate, this prerequisite remains a critical consideration.

Selecting between the discretized
AS
, and a
CAS
requires careful consideration of the specific application, network size and characteristics.
For the discretized
AS
, the level of granularity (i.e. the discretization step) is a critical factor, determining how close the actions align with the constraints threshold,
\gamma
.
Applications requiring high precision or strict adherence to constraints will benefit from finer granularity, although this increases the computational overhead.

On the other hand, adopting a
CAS
relies on a rough prior estimate of the network load, as these influence the choice of hyperparameters like
N^{h}_{s}
and
N^{l}_{s}
.
While this prior estimate is a key consideration, it is not directly addressed in this work.
The
CAS
approach is particularly well-suited for scenarios where precomputing optimal access probabilities for manageable subsets of
H-UE
and
L-UE
is feasible, as it balances computational efficiency and network performance.
Ultimately, the choice of the
AS
hinges on finding the right balance between computational complexity, precision, and practical application requirements.
Results for both the discrete action space and
CAS
approaches are presented in
Section

V
, highlighting their respective tradeoffs and applicability.

IV-C

Reward Scaling

Most
RL
algorithms show a performance increase when adopting a bounded reward
[
38
,
39
]
.
This approach simplifies the hyperparameter tuning process as the reward scale is fixed
[
40
]
.
Moreover, limiting the reward signal facilitates learning across different tasks using a single algorithm and hyperparameter configuration, as in the original
deep Q-network (DQN)
paper, where the reward signal is clipped to the range
[-1,1]

[
41
]
.
Additionally, scaling the reward can improve the stability of the learning process, as the reward signal is less likely to explode or vanish
[
42
,
43
]
.
Constraining the range of rewards prevents overly large or small rewards from dominating the learning process and disproportionately influencing the policy.

In this paper,
n^{h}
and
n^{l}
can be very large and may experience frequent and unpredictable fluctuations depending on the specific system or environment.
Scaling the reward can help ensure the system’s scalability and adaptability under diverse operating conditions while maintaining similar hyperparameter configurations.
The most common approach for scaling the reward is to normalize it to the range
[0,1]
.
However, this approach requires knowledge of the maximum reward which can be challenging to obtain.
For example, in the two-priority
RACH
model, as will be shown in
Section

V-C
, the maximum value of
\mu_{h,T}
can be easily determined only when
n^{h}\leq M-1
by setting
\mathbf{p}^{h}
and
\mathbf{p}^{l}
to
9
and
10
, respectively.

p^{h}_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{M-1}&\text{if }1\leq i\leq M-1\\
0&\text{if }i=M\end{cases}

(9)

p^{l}_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }1\leq i\leq M-1\\
1&\text{if }i=M\end{cases}

(10)

When
n^{h}>M-1
, the maximum reward is not straightforward to determine.
So, instead of normalizing
\mu_{h,T}
, we propose to scale the reward by dividing
\mu_{h,T}
by the value of
\mu_{h}
obtained when
\mathbf{p}^{h}
and
\mathbf{p}^{l}
are set to
9
and
10
, respectively.

To maximize
\mu_{h}
, when there is no constraint on
\mu_{l}
, we need to set
\mathbf{p}^{h}
to a uniform distribution across
M-1

RBs
and allocate all
L-UEs
to the remaining
RB
.
This strategy achieves the maximum reward when
n^{h}\leq M-1
.
In this case, the scaling factor aligns with the maximum achievable reward, resulting in a maximum scaled value of 1.
However, for
n^{h}>M-1
, using the same strategy for
\mathbf{p}^{h}
and
\mathbf{p}^{l}
causes
\mu_{h}
to drop due to increased contention among
UEs
across the
M-1
RBs.
To mitigate this drop, a non-uniform allocation strategy is required, which adjusts
\mathbf{p}^{h}
and
\mathbf{p}^{l}
to better handle the higher load, as will be demonstrated in
Section

V-C
.
Nevertheless, since the scaling factor remains based on a less efficient strategy, i.e. uniform allocation, it does not fully reflect the improvements made by the new non-uniform allocation strategy.
Therefore, the
\mu_{h}
appears slightly greater than 1 when scaled.

The advantages of scaling the reward as described above are similar to those of normalizing the reward to the
[0,1]
range, with the added benefit of avoiding the task of determining the maximum reward when
n^{h}>M-1
.
This contrasts the priority-based
RACH
model, developed in
Section

II
, where
\mu_{h}
and
\mu_{l}
, are bounded to the range
[0,n^{h}]
and
[0,n^{l}]
, respectively.
We are now ready to utilize the priority-based
RACH
model to formulate the
MAB
problem used in optimizing the access probability vectors.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel two-priority
RACH
scheme that utilizes non-uniform access probabilities to improve the
RACH
throughput of
H-UEs
while maintaining a minimum one for
L-UEs
.
We formulated the problem as an optimization problem to be solved so that the optimal access probabilities using
SQP
can be found.
Furthermore, a
MAB
formulation that discretizes the
AS
is proposed to solve the optimization problem in a more time-efficient and scalable manner.
A
CE
based algorithm along with a size reduction technique are proposed to tackle the large
AS
size.
Moreover, a
CAS

MAB
formulation is proposed to reduce the search space and solve the optimization problem more efficiently, with a byproduct of estimating the network load.

The results show that our formulation surpases the traditional uniform access probability scheme in terms of
RACH
throughput for the
H-UEs
, while maintaining a minimum
RACH
throughput for the
L-UEs
.
Moreover, the
MAB
formulations are within 5% of the optimal solution for the constrained problem while being significantly faster to compute than solving the optimal access probability problem.

An interesting direction for future research is to consider the impact of the estimation process, where the
BS
does not initially know the number of
H-UEs
and
L-UEs
in the network. The
BS
first needs to estimate these numbers before optimizing the access probabilities. Future studies could explore the effects of estimation errors on the optimization problem, conduct a sensitivity analysis to understand how robust the solutions are to these errors, and investigate how different
RBs
access probabilities influence the accuracy of the estimations.

Finally, we could explore varying
UE
activation distributions, such as using uniform or beta distributions for activation times
[
46
]
. This approach would allow for a deeper understanding of their effects on network performance and resource allocation. By considering different distributions, future studies could simulate more realistic activation scenarios, offering insights into how such variations influence system efficiency, reliability, and overall robustness.
