Double versus single fluoroscopy for screw fixation of slipped capital femoral epiphysis: does double fluoroscopy improve outcomes? A retrospective comparative study
Authors: Tayfun Bacaksız, Melikşah Uzakgider, Mehmet Maden, Özgür Doğan Aydın, Ihsan Akan, Fatih Sürenkök, Cemal Kazımoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of using simultaneous double fluoroscopy, which provides anteroposterior (AP) and lateral images concurrently, on screw placement accuracy, surgical duration, and radiation exposure in the treatment of slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE).MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 92 patients (73 males, 19 females; mean age 12.78 ± 1.54 years) who underwent in situ screw fixation for SCFE between 2017 and 2024. Patients were divided into two groups according to intraoperative imaging technique: single fluoroscopy (Group 1, n = 44) and double fluoroscopy (Group 2, n = 48). Demographic, operative, and radiographic parameters were compared between groups. Screw entry point and physeal fixation were graded in both AP and lateral planes.ResultsThere were no significant differences in demographic or preoperative radiological parameters between groups (p > 0.05). The mean preparation time was less in Group 1 (9.68 ± 1.83 min) compared to Group 2 (17.38 ± 1.93 min, p < 0.001). However, the surgical time was significantly longer in Group 1 (37.64 ± 10.68 min) compared to Group 2 (23.06 ± 6.45 min, p < 0.001). The total operation time was extended in Group 1 (47.32 ± 10.92 min) than in Group 2 (40.44 ± 6.68 min, p < 0.001). The mean fluoroscopy time was also longer in Group 1 (44.46 ± 9.15 s) than in Group 2 (39.73 ± 8.03 s, p = 0.01). Radiographic analysis revealed superior screw positioning in the double fluoroscopy group, with significantly higher rates of optimal entry points in the lateral plane (p = 0.004) and improved physeal fixation in both AP (p = 0.036) and lateral (p = 0.019) planes. Complication rates were lower in Group 2 (2%) compared to Group 1 (9%).ConclusionsSimultaneous double fluoroscopy during screw fixation of SCFE was associated with improved screw placement accuracy, reduced radiation exposure, and shorter total operative time, despite requiring slightly longer preparation. This method appears to be a reliable and effective technique that can enhance surgical precision and safety in hip surgery.

Introduction

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) is one of the most common causes of hip pain during adolescence and is more common with increasing obesity rates [1, 2]. The proximal femoral epiphysis is kept in the acetabulum by the ligamentum teres, while the femoral metaphysis displaces over the femoral head, causing coxa vara [3]. As a result, patients suffer from hip pain and gait disturbance, and this causes degenerative joint disease, which is the most common reason for total hip arthroplasty [4].

Although SCFE is a frequently missed pathology, most patients are diagnosed with mild or moderate degrees of slippage and can be successfully treated with in situ fixation [5]. The current gold standard treatment for these patients is fixation with a single cannulated screw [6]. The screw must be placed as close as possible to the centre of the epiphysis, perpendicular to the physes in both the coronal and sagittal planes, to ensure optimal biomechanical fixation and avoid joint penetration [5, 7]. This may be possible with an entry point more anterior and proximal on the femur as the severity of the epiphyseal slippage increases. Complications such as delayed physeal closure, progression of deformity, chondral damage and chondrolysis due to joint penetration, and avascular necrosis due to vascular damage are observed when an appropriate entry point and screw position providing adequate physeal fixation cannot be achieved [6, 8, 9].

The optimal metaphysical entry point in the proximal femur and the ideal physeal fixation location have been described in the literature to ensure proper screw placement [10–12]. Several techniques have been developed to determine these points and the appropriate screw position, ranging from obtaining repeated anteroposterior (AP) and lateral images with fluoroscopy, better visualisation of the joint with arthrography, to using computed tomography (CT)-guided navigation systems or guide systems obtained from three-dimentional printers [12–15]. However, each method also has disadvantages such as technical difficulties, accessibility issues, or additional costs. Therefore, techniques performed with conventional fluoroscopy images remain current [14, 16].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of double fluoroscopy, which provides simultaneous AP and lateral images, on achieving the optimal screw position in SCFE treatment, preventing potential complications, and reducing surgical and radiation times.

Materials and methods

A retrospective study was performed on patients diagnosed with SCFE and operated on by the same surgeons together at the same hospital between 2017 and 2024. The surgical team included two senior authors with at least five years of pediatric orthopedic experience. After ethics committee approval, data were obtained from patient files and the hospital imaging archive.

Patients who had complete medical records and radiographic data, along with unilateral fixation using a single cannulated screw, were included in the study. Patients with bilateral lesions, unilateral lesions with prophylactic screws on the contralateral side, safe dislocation, arthrography, multiple screws for fixation, or other implants instead of screws; patients whose data could not be regularly recorded; or patients who were lost to follow-up were excluded from the study. The patients’ demographic data and body mass index (BMI) were recorded at the time of surgery. Patients were divided into two groups according to whether single fluoroscopy (Group 1) or simultaneous double fluoroscopy (Group 2) was used for imaging during surgery (Fig. 1). The patients’ group selection was determined based on the availability of the second fluoroscopy device. During this period, when both fluoroscopy devices were available, patients were operated on by the same surgical team under double fluoroscopy. When the second device was unavailable due to high trauma volume or malfunction, patients were operated on under single fluoroscopy. Both techniques were used simultaneously during the study period, rather than switching from single fluoroscopy to double fluoroscopy. Thus, simple randomisation was achieved, preventing bias between groups due to differences in surgical experience and technological shifts.

Surgical technique

A radiolucent table was used for all surgical procedures. Patients were placed on the table in the supine position. The preparation and imaging technique for patients in the single fluoroscopy group was performed as described by Blasier et al. [10]. The patient’s legs were placed in a neutral position and kept free. The C-arm of the fluoroscopy device was set in the AP position and stabilised. The leg was manually rotated slightly internally to make the ipsilateral femoral neck orthogonal to the anteroposterior X-ray. Lateral fluoroscopic images were obtained manually by placing the leg in abduction and flexion, and an image was taken in the “frog-leg position”. Patients using simultaneous double fluoroscopy, different from the other group, were fixed with the leg-holder while their ipsilateral extremities were kept free in a neutral position and their contralateral extremities were placed in abduction and flexion. The fluoroscopy units were positioned similarly to that described by Westberry et al. [14] (Fig. 2). The leg holder of the unaffected limb was abducted approximately 45 degrees from the midline. A thigh support was placed under the unaffected limb to provide space for positioning the C-arm in the lateral view. The C-arm was directed from the lateral side and over the abdomen for the AP view. Following the AP and lateral views of control, the fluoroscopy devices were stabilised.

The skin incision and placement of the guide wire were performed as described by Lindaman et al. to position the screw centrally in the epiphysis and perpendicular to the physis in both planes [11]. The hip joint was slowly moved through a full range of motion under continuous fluoroscopy to confirm that there was no joint penetration after the screw was inserted [17, 18]. Additionally, in patients using simultaneous double fluoroscopy, the first fluoroscope was positioned to obtain an image with the screw tip directly at the centre of the epiphysis, and the second fluoroscope was positioned perpendicular to the first fluoroscope to obtain a control image (Fig. 3). Thus, the blind spot area was minimalised.

Evaluation criteria

Patient data

The age, gender, BMI and affected sides of the patients were documented. The patients were classified according to whether the slippage was acute, chronic or acute on chronic. The angle of slippage of the SCFE was calculated on lateral radiographs preoperatively and categorised as mild (0–29), moderate (30–60) and severe (> 60) [19].

Surgical procedure data

The preparation time, surgical time, and fluoroscopy time were measured using operating room records. Preparation time was defined as the time from the induction of anaesthesia to the moment of surgical incision. Surgical time was defined as the time from the moment of surgical incision to the skin closure. The sum of these two times was given as the total operation time.

The fluoroscopy time was used to indicate the radiation dose in our study, similar to previous studies [20–22]. Both fluoroscopy devices used in the study were the same brand and model (Philips BV Pulsera, 32.3 mGy/min), and the standard fluoroscopy protocol was applied to all patients. The data were obtained separately from the patient-specific records of each device. The total radiation time for patients in the double fluoroscopy group was obtained by summing the times obtained from both devices.

Patients who underwent reduction manoeuvres after positioning and repeated manoeuvres during the procedure were noted.

Radiological evaluation

The radiographic measurement of screw placement was performed on AP and lateral radiographs taken postoperatively. Optimal screw placement was defined as the position that secured fixation at the exact centre of the physis and was placed perpendicular to the physis, as described by Blasier et al. [10] (Fig. 4a, b). The optimal fixation location of the screw tip in the epiphysis was graded according to the method used by Aronson and recently described by Jamil et al. [23, 24] (Fig. 4c, d). Accordingly, the screw was classified as position 1 when it was on the centre line of the femoral head or at a distance of half the screw diameter, position 2 when the distance was between half and one screw diameter, and position 3 when the distance was greater than one screw diameter. The optimal insertion site of the screw in the lateral cortex was determined as the point where the axis intersects the lateral cortex, allowing for perpendicular positioning to the physis, as described by Nguyen and Morrissy [5]. The screw insertion site was classified as position 1 when the screw insertion point was at the optimal axis or at a distance of half the screw diameter, position 2 when the distance was between half the screw diameter and one screw diameter, and position 3 when the distance exceeded one screw diameter (Fig. 4e, f). Classifications were performed using both AP and lateral radiographs. Radiological assessment was performed by two senior authors and one radiologist. Radiographic measurements were performed at the same time by the raters, and a consensus was reached to obtain final data in disagreement. The presence of joint penetration was confirmed with AP, lateral and oblique radiographs at the postoperative period. A cartilage gap measuring three millimetres or less was considered chondrolysis, and the presence of sclerosis or collapse at the femoral head in the imaging obtained at the final follow-ups was assessed as avascular necrosis [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 22 software (Released 2013 NY: IBM Corp.). Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were presented as number (n), percentage (%), mean, and standard deviation (±). The Pearson Chi-square test was used when comparing categorical data between groups. For the comparison of numerical values, the independent t-test (Student’s t-test) was used if the data were normally distributed, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used if the data were not normally distributed. In all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As the study was a retrospective analysis, data availability determined the sample size. A power analysis was performed to determine the capacity of the sample size. Considering the radiation durations in similar studies in the literature (Group 1 41.19 ± 9.43, Group 2 47.36 ± 8.86), it was calculated that a sample size of 92 patients (Group 1/Group 2 distribution ratio 44/48) at an alpha level of 0.05 with an effect size of 0.7 would provide 95% power to detect a significant difference using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 programme [22].

Results

The study population was 92 patients who met the inclusion criteria. The mean age of the patients was 12.78 ± 1.54 (9–17), and 73 (79.3%) of the patients were male and 19 (20.7%) were female. The mean BMI was 27.59 ± 3.93 (19–41). The affected side was the right hip in 42 patients (45.7%) and the left hip in 50 patients (54.3%). During the surgical procedure, 44 (47.8%) patients who underwent single fluoroscopy were in Group 1, while 48 (52.2%) patients who underwent simultaneous double fluoroscopy were in Group 2. Discussion

This study demonstrated that the use of simultaneous double fluoroscopy during screw fixation of SCFE is a highly effective technique for optimal screw placement. It also significantly reduces radiation dose, which is particularly important in pediatric cases. Although the preparation period is relatively longer, the advantages it provides in surgical time significantly reduce the total operative time.

Screw placement in the optimal position is critical to reduce the risk of joint penetration and potential joint damage [14]. It has also been reported that the eccentricity of screw placement is directly related to the increase in time required for physis fusion [27]. Therefore, the search for the most suitable screw placement becomes even more important. Oetgen et al. reported that they placed screws in a more appropriate position using the navigation system they employed, and they associated this with proximity to the joint [13]. However, they have not clearly stated their views on the learning curve and accessibility. Bono et al. stated that they were able to achieve optimal epiphyseal placement at the 91% rate they mentioned using a computer-assisted navigation system [28]. Westberry et al. reported that they achieved optimal epiphyseal placement in 88.2% of cases when using double fluoroscopy and 69.1% when using single fluoroscopy; however, they did not provide information on determining the screw entry point [14]. Similarly, we also found that the screws epiphyseal placement was better in both the AP and lateral planes in the group using double fluoroscopy.

Determining the appropriate screw entry point in the lateral cortex is the most important factor in ensuring the optimal position of the screw in the epiphysis. Therefore, the search for different techniques to determine the most appropriate entry point continues, and alternative, easier-to-apply entry points are also being investigated [12, 24]. However, both the difficulty in imaging and the bending or displacement of the guide wire while attempting to identify the appropriate entry point in both planes increase the likelihood of complications and prolong the operation time. In their study, Feijoo and Conklin stated that they prevented the bending of the guide wire by using biplanar fluoroscopy [16]. Westberry et al. also noted that when using a single scope, the optimal position and visualization of the guide wire can easily be lost, particularly in the lateral plane [14]. Consistent with this, our study has shown that the double fluoroscopy technique is significantly effective, especially in providing the optimal entry point in the lateral plane. Furthermore, a guide wire fracture occurred during surgery in one patient in the single-scope group, further supporting the reduced risk of complications with double fluoroscopy use.

The stabilizing effect of the screw on the physis is related to the number of threads it uses to secure the epiphysis, as well as its placement. For this reason, it is recommended that the epiphysis be held in place with at least five threads [23, 29]. However, the risk of penetration increases as the addition grows closer to ensure a sufficient number of threads. To prevent this, some routinely use the approach-withdraw technique, even at the expense of stability after the final position [14]. In our study, we did not examine the number of threads in the epiphysis. However, following the final position utilizing double fluoroscopy alongside blind spot visualization, we observed no joint penetration in the double fluoroscopy group. Consequently, there was no necessity to implement the approach-withdraw technique.

It’s expected that using a double fluoroscopy will require more preoperative preparation time than using a single scope. However, as we found in our study, the advantage it provides during surgery is significant enough to shorten the overall time. This benefit is significant not only for SCFE surgeries but also for trauma patients. Günaydın ve ark [22]. reached similar results in their study on supracondylar humerus fractures using double fluoroscopy, as did Çelik et al. [21] in their study on intertrochanteric femur fractures using double fluoroscopy. In both studies, the preparation time was longer compared to single fluoroscopy use, while the surgical time and total time were shorter in the groups using double fluoroscopy. This demonstrates that the adage “throw out a sprat to catch a mackerel” also applies to double fluoroscopy use.

Minimizing radiation exposure for both the patient and the surgical team is particularly important, especially in orthopedic procedures performed during childhood. For this reason, various protection methods and surgical techniques have been developed to reduce the radiation dose received [30, 31]. The use of double-scope is controversial in this regard. While Schmucker et al. [32] stated that double fluoroscopy had no effect on radiation dose in the treatment of supracondylar fractures, there are also studies stating that double fluoroscopy significantly reduced radiation times compared to the use of single fluoroscopy for similar procedures [21, 22]. Although Westberry et al. [14] who used double fluoroscopy in the treatment of SCFE, did not investigate the effect of double fluoroscopy on radiation dose, in our current study we found that the use of double fluoroscopy significantly reduced the total fluoroscopy time.

Joint penetration with wires or screws in SCFE treatment is one of the most avoided complications due to the risk of both chondrolysis and joint damage as well as avascular necrosis [6, 8, 9, 25]. Another risk for avascular necrosis is the high number of reduction maneuvers performed [33]. In our study, we believe that the use of double fluoroscopy is also effective in preventing complications, as there was no joint penetration by wire or screw in the double fluoroscopy group and fewer repeated reduction maneuvers were performed in the double fluoroscopy group.

This study has certain limitations. The main limitations are that the study is retrospective and the patient population is relatively small. However the radiological assessment performed by two senior authors and a radiologist simultaneously by consensus increases consistency, the inability to evaluate intra- and inter-observer reliability can be considered a separate limitation. Although robust data were obtained regarding screw position, operation duration, radiation exposure times, and complications, another limitation is that the effect of double fluoroscopy use on the number of threads engaging the epiphysis, the duration of physical closure, and the determination of the optimal screw length was not examined. There is a continuing need for studies with larger case series that also collect this information and enhance postoperative joint visualization with additional methods, such as computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging.

Conclusion

The use of double fluoroscopy is attracting attention as an effective method for ensuring optimal screw position and appropriate entry point in the treatment of SCFE. It is also associated with benefits, such as reducing fluoroscopy dose and total operating time. In additional to SCFE, it also holds promise as a method that could become widely used, particularly in the treatment of femoral neck and trochanteric region fractures, to prevent fixation failure and joint penetration.
