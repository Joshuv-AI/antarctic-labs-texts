Systolic blood pressure strata and acute kidney injury risk in heart failure: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qing Zeng, Yiheng Pang, Yongrong Jiang, Naiqian Sa, Ge Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveIn patients with acute heart failure (HF), both hypotension and hypertension may compromise renal perfusion, yet the association between systolic blood pressure (SBP) and acute kidney injury (AKI) risk is controversial and poorly quantified. This study aimed to define this relationship.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort analysis of 19,604 HF patients from the MIMIC-IV database, individuals were categorized into three SBP strata based on their mean SBP during the first 24 hours of intensive care unit admission: strict (<110 mmHg), standard (110–139 mmHg), and lenient (≥140 mmHg). We employed multivariable logistic regression, propensity score weighting, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to evaluate associations. To further address confounding, we incorporated standardized ICU severity scores (OASIS, SAPS II, APACHE III) into the models. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative SBP cutoffs and in a subcohort with available BNP measurements. A predictive model was developed and internally validated via bootstrapping.ResultsThe overall incidence of AKI was 36.6%. After multivariable adjustment, compared with the lenient SBP stratum (≥140 mmHg), both strict (<110 mmHg; aOR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67-0.82) and standard (110-139 mmHg; aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69-0.86) SBP strata were associated with significantly lower odds of AKI. These associations remained robust after adjustment for OASIS (strict: aOR 0.71, 0.64–0.79; standard: aOR 0.75, 0.68–0.84), SAPS II (strict: aOR 0.67, 0.60–0.75; standard: aOR 0.75, 0.67–0.83), and APACHE III (strict: aOR 0.69, 0.62–0.77; standard: aOR 0.76, 0.68–0.85). RCS analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship, with the lowest predicted AKI risk at an SBP of approximately 120 mmHg. Key independent risk factors included chronic kidney disease (aOR 2.64) and elevated 48-hour creatinine ratio (aOR 2.07 per unit). The full prediction model demonstrated moderate discrimination (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.726, 95% CI: 0.718–0.733) but significant calibration issues (Hosmer-Lemeshow P < 0.001). Internal validation yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.726. Notably, in sensitivity analyses focusing on severe AKI, both standard and lenient strategies were associated with significantly lower risk compared to strict control. The simplified model (6 variables) showed poor calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P < 0.001, Brier 0.205) but provided positive net benefit in decision curve analysis across thresholds 0–0.7; however, its poor calibration limits clinical application, and the net benefit estimates should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionCompared with the lenient SBP stratum, both strict and standard SBP strata were associated with significantly lower odds of overall acute kidney injury in heart failure patients. However, the relationship is non-linear, with the lowest predicted risk observed at approximately 120 mmHg. These findings suggest that an observed SBP of approximately 120 mmHg was associated with the lowest risk of AKI in this population, warranting prospective validation.Keywords: Systolic blood pressure, Acute kidney injury, Heart failure, Retrospective study, Risk prediction

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome resulting from impaired ventricular systolic and/or diastolic function and is frequently accompanied by significant hemodynamic abnormalities [1, 2]. As a major global public health and economic burden, HF currently affects approximately 64 million people worldwide [3]. The disease substantially impairs health-related quality of life, with pronounced detrimental effects on physical function and vitality [4, 5]. Worsening renal function is a common and prognostically critical complication in HF patients and is strongly associated with increased risks of hospitalization and mortality [6, 7]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is highly prevalent in this population, particularly among those requiring admission to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) [8].

Blood pressure (BP) management constitutes a cornerstone of comprehensive HF treatment [9]. BP not only reflects cardiovascular load status but is also a key determinant of renal perfusion. Both venous congestion and hypoperfusion are recognized as significant risk factors for AKI, suggesting that BP management strategies may profoundly influence renal outcomes [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the impact of different BP control levels on AKI risk in HF patients remains controversial. Some studies suggest that strict BP lowering may increase the risk of AKI [12, 13], whereas others indicate that stricter BP control reduces all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events without significantly increasing serious renal adverse events [14].

Current evidence regarding the optimal BP management strategy in HF patients remains limited. Previous studies have often failed to adequately adjust for potential confounding factors, and major clinical guidelines have not reached a unified consensus [15, 16]. Consequently, substantial variability exists in real-world BP management practices for HF patients, highlighting the urgent need to address this evidence gap.

This study aimed to conduct a retrospective cohort study utilizing the large-scale Medical Information Mart for strict Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database to systematically explore the associations between different levels of systolic blood pressure (as achieved in the first 24 h) and the risk of AKI. We employ multivariable adjustment, propensity score weighting, and sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our findings. Furthermore, we plan to develop a clinical prediction model to explore the potential for individualized BP management and to inform future interventional studies.

Methods

Study design and data source and ethics

This retrospective cohort study was conducted with data extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 2.2) database, covering the period from 2008 to 2019. Our research team was granted access to the database after completing the required Protecting Human Research Participants web-based course.

The MIMIC-IV database has received approval from the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) (Protocol Number: 2001P000129). Because all patient data in the database are fully de-identified and anonymized prior to public release, the requirement for separate ethics approval for this specific study was waived under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4). The requirement for individual informed consent was also waived by the same IRBs. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study population

The study cohort was identified from the MIMIC-IV database (2008–2019) on the basis of the following criteria: adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a primary diagnosis of heart failure (based on relevant ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes for all subtypes) and an ICU length of stay ≥ 24 h were included. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) end-stage renal disease requiring maintenance dialysis; (2) history of kidney transplantation; (3) pregnancy; or (4) absence of key clinical data (e.g., blood pressure and serum creatinine). After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final cohort for analysis comprised 19,604 heart failure patients.

Data extraction and variables

The following data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database for all included patients:

Demographics

age, sex, admission type, admission source, discharge disposition.

Comorbidities

defined by ICD codes, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

ICU vital signs

heart rate; peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂); body temperature; and blood pressure (see Exposure Definition below).

Laboratory parameters

serum creatinine (baseline, peak, and mean values), blood urea nitrogen, albumin, hemoglobin, hematocrit, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, etc.), and liver function tests.

Medications

use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics within the first 7 days.

Treatments and interventions

use of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, and 24-hour net fluid balance.

Clinical outcomes

Acute kidney injury (AKI, see definition below), severe AKI (KDIGO stage 2 or 3), in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay.

Exposure: systolic blood pressure strata

The primary exposure was the mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) during the first 24 h of ICU admission, calculated from extracted vital signs data. Patients were categorized into three SBP strata: strict (mean SBP < 110 mmHg), standard (mean SBP 110–139 mmHg), and lenient (mean SBP ≥ 140 mmHg). It is critical to note that these categories reflect achieved blood pressure, not an intentionally targeted treatment strategy. Mean BP values were derived from at least three valid measurements. We implemented a standardized data cleaning procedure that excluded physiologically implausible values.

BP Measurement Source: The BP data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database, which contains recordings from both invasive arterial catheter and non-invasive cuff measurements, identified by specific item identifiers (e.g., for systolic pressure: 220050, 220179). For this analysis, all valid SBP readings within the first 24 h were included without distinguishing the measurement method, as this reflects the pragmatic, composite nature of “achieved” blood pressure in a real-world ICU setting.

The chosen cutoffs are consistent with clinical guidelines (e.g., ACC/AHA and ESC hypertension guidelines) and prior studies in HF populations. To test the robustness of our findings to the choice of cutoffs, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds: strict < 100 mmHg, standard 100–129 mmHg, lenient ≥ 130 mmHg; strict < 100, standard 100–139, lenient ≥ 140; strict < 110, standard 110–129, lenient ≥ 130; and strict < 120, standard 120–139, lenient ≥ 140.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was the occurrence of AKI during the ICU stay. AKI was diagnosed and staged according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) serum creatinine criteria. Baseline serum creatinine was defined as the first available measurement obtained within 24 h of ICU admission. This definition was chosen for its high clinical relevance, as it best reflects renal function at the onset of the critical care episode. Using this baseline, AKI was defined as either: (1) an absolute increase in serum creatinine of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (≥ 26.5 µmol/L) within 48 h, or (2) an increase in serum creatinine to ≥ 1.5 times the baseline within 7 days.

We acknowledge that this definition may overestimate AKI incidence compared to a stable outpatient baseline. Sensitivity analyses using alternative baseline definitions confirmed that the direction of the primary association remained unchanged.

Severe AKI was defined as KDIGO Stage 2 (serum creatinine 2.0–2.9 times the baseline) or Stage 3 (serum creatinine ≥ 3.0 times baseline, or initiation of renal replacement therapy during the ICU stay).

To minimize the risk of reverse causality, SBP was defined using measurements from the first 24 h, and AKI was diagnosed based on creatinine changes occurring after 24 h.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Continuous and categorical variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (or median with IQR) and frequencies (percentages), respectively. Group comparisons used ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, or chi-square tests.

To examine associations between SBP strata and AKI, we developed multivariable logistic regression models. Candidate covariates were selected from the extracted variables (Sect. 2.3) based on clinical relevance and an initial univariate screening (P < 0.1). The final adjusted model included: age, sex, chronic kidney disease, sepsis diagnosis, 48-hour creatinine ratio, 24-hour urine output, blood urea nitrogen, potassium, hematocrit, 24-hour net fluid balance, SpO₂, diuretic use, and mechanical ventilation use. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). To further account for baseline illness severity, we extended the models by sequentially adding standardized ICU severity scores (OASIS, SAPS II, and APACHE III). Due to the unavailability of SOFA scores in the dataset, we selected these well‑validated alternatives.

We performed a dose-response analysis by modeling the first 24-hour mean SBP as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 3 knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the SBP distribution.

A prediction model was constructed based on the multivariable analysis. Model discrimination and calibration were evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curves, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Internal validation was performed via 1000 bootstrap repetitions. A simplified model was derived by retaining the six most important variables based on the magnitude of standardized coefficients in the full model and clinical relevance, prioritizing factors known to influence AKI risk. For this simplified model, we additionally assessed calibration using the Brier score and evaluated clinical utility via decision curve analysis (DCA) with 50 bootstrap replicates, plotting net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted based on different SBP cutoffs, in a BNP‑available subcohort, and after excluding the 48‑hour creatinine ratio. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The final cohort comprised 19,604 HF patients. Baseline characteristics stratified by SBP stratum are presented in Table 1. The study population had a mean age of 70.0 ± 13.9 years and included 10,743 (54.8%) males. The lenient stratum patients were slightly younger, had a lower proportion of males, but had significantly higher prevalences of CKD (55.6% vs. 40.0%) and diabetes (54.2% vs. 38.8%), and higher first creatinine levels (2.53 mg/dL vs. 1.91 mg/dL) compared to the strict stratum. Markers of initial illness severity (e.g., sepsis diagnosis, vasopressor use) were generally lower in the lenient group, while net fluid balance was higher.

Incidence and distribution of AKI

The overall incidence of AKI was 36.6% (7,181/19,604). AKI incidence across SBP strata was: strict stratum 37.0% (4,564/12,343), standard stratum 34.3% (1,764/5,150), and lenient stratum 40.4% (853/2,111) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). This pattern suggests that lenient blood pressure SBP strata may be associated with increased AKI risk in heart failure patients.

Bar chart showing the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) across the three systolic blood pressure (SBP) strata: Strict (< 110 mmHg), Standard (110–139 mmHg), and Lenient (≥ 140 mmHg). The overall AKI incidence was 36.6%

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

We present three sets of multivariable models: the primary model (without severity scores) and models additionally adjusted for OASIS, SAPS II, and APACHE III (Table 2). After multivariable adjustment, compared with the lenient SBP stratum (≥ 140 mmHg), both strict (< 110 mmHg) and standard (110–139 mmHg) strata were independently associated with significantly lower odds of AKI (Fig. 2). The persistence of these associations after adjustment for OASIS, SAPS II, and APACHE III suggests that the observed relationship is not substantially confounded by baseline illness severity. Other significant independent risk factors included pre‑existing chronic kidney disease, higher 48‑hour creatinine ratio, diuretic use, and lower 24‑hour urine output (Table 2). The variance inflation factors for all included variables were below 2.0, indicating no substantial multicollinearity.

Predictive model performance

The full multivariable model comprising 14 predictors (Table 2) demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for AKI, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.726 (95% CI: 0.718–0.733) (Fig. 3). The model’s performance characteristics are summarized in Table 3. The optimal probability cutoff, determined by maximizing the Youden’s index, was 0.365, yielding a sensitivity of 66.4% and a specificity of 67.8%.

The model’s explanatory power was limited, with a Nagelkerke’s R² of 0.181. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was 23006.0. Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.726, indicating minimal evidence of overfitting.

To enhance clinical utility, a simplified model was derived by retaining the six most important variables (chronic kidney disease, 48-hour creatinine ratio, diuretic use, mechanical ventilation use, 24-hour potassium, and blood pressure strategy). This simplified model (presented as a nomogram in Fig. 5) maintained 95% of the full model’s discriminative ability (AUC = 0.709 vs. 0.726) while significantly reducing complexity. However, its calibration was also poor (Hosmer‑Lemeshow χ² = 108.3, df = 8, P < 0.001; Brier score = 0.205).

Decision curve analysis for the simplified model

Decision curve analysis (Fig. 6) demonstrated that the simplified model provided a higher standardized net benefit compared to both the treat all and treat none strategies across a wide range of clinically plausible high‑risk thresholds (0–0.7). The net benefit was particularly pronounced at thresholds below 0.4 (corresponding to a cost‑benefit ratio of approximately 1:1.5), where the simplified model substantially outperformed the treat all strategy. At thresholds above 0.7, the net benefit of the model approached zero, similar to the treat none strategy. However, given the model’s poor calibration, the net benefit estimates should be interpreted with caution, and the model is currently more suitable for risk ranking than for providing accurate absolute risk estimates.

Dose-response relationship between SBP and AKI Risk

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between the first 24-hour mean SBP and AKI risk (P for nonlinearity < 0.001) (Fig. 7). The predicted probability of AKI showed a U-shaped relationship, decreasing from lower SBP levels to reach a nadir at approximately 120 mmHg, and then increasing again at higher SBP values (> 140 mmHg).

Robustness analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the association between blood pressure strata and AKI risk, we performed propensity score weighting and multiple sensitivity analyses. Propensity score weighting using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline characteristics across the three blood pressure groups (Table 4). After weighting, 9 out of 12 covariates achieved adequate balance (SMD < 0.1), with residual imbalance for three covariates (age, potassium, SpO₂) (Fig. 8; Table 5). The effective sample sizes after weighting were 343 (Lenient), 4,221 (Standard), and 11,597 (Strict).

BNP‑available subcohort analysis

Among the 3,006 patients with available BNP measurements, the strict stratum remained significantly associated with lower AKI risk (aOR 0.60, 95% CI 0.45–0.79), while the standard stratum showed a non‑significant trend in the same direction (aOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.58–1.05). Higher log‑transformed BNP was independently associated with increased AKI risk (aOR 1.36 per log‑unit, 95% CI 1.27–1.45).

Exclusion of 48‑hour creatinine ratio

Removing this variable from the primary model did not materially change the association for SBP strata (strict: aOR 0.72, 0.65–0.80; standard: aOR 0.75, 0.68–0.83). We acknowledge that inclusion of the 48‑hour creatinine ratio may introduce overadjustment, as this variable is closely related to the AKI definition; however, this sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of our primary findings.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort of hospitalized heart failure patients, we systematically evaluated the association between early systolic blood pressure levels and the risk of acute kidney injury. Our primary finding indicates that, compared with the lenient SBP stratum (≥ 140 mmHg), both strict (< 110 mmHg) and standard (110–139 mmHg) SBP strata observed in the first 24 h of ICU care were independently associated with significantly lower odds of developing AKI. This suggests that avoiding hypertension may be renoprotective in this acute setting [12, 17, 18], consistent with the growing body of evidence linking sustained hypertension to progressive kidney damage [19, 20] and highlighting the importance of renal perfusion pressure regulation [21]. However, a more nuanced picture emerges from the continuous dose-response analysis, which revealed a significant U-shaped, non-linear relationship between SBP and AKI risk, with the nadir of risk occurring at approximately 120 mmHg. This indicates that while a higher SBP is detrimental, an excessively low SBP may not confer additional benefit and could potentially be harmful, defining an optimal range for renal protection [14, 22]. This therapeutic window concept is further supported by recent studies emphasizing the role of blood pressure variability and circadian rhythms in target organ damage [23, 24].

Our results present an apparent complexity: the strict SBP stratum was associated with lower overall AKI risk, yet sensitivity analyses showed that compared with the strict stratum, both standard and lenient strata were associated with a significantly lower risk of severe AKI (KDIGO Stage 2–3). This apparent contradiction can be reconciled by considering the distinct pathophysiological pathways underlying AKI onset versus progression. Patients in the strict stratum had substantially higher baseline illness severity (Table 1), including higher rates of sepsis and vasopressor use. In this subgroup, while a lower SBP may reduce the risk of AKI from venous congestion and afterload‑related injury, the underlying hemodynamic instability likely predisposes them to rapid progression to severe AKI once kidney injury occurs. This distinction underscores that AKI prevention and AKI progression may be governed by different mechanisms, and that the optimal SBP target may differ depending on the patient’s baseline risk profile, a concept increasingly recognized in precision medicine approaches to hypertension management [25]. Importantly, after adjusting for standardized severity scores (OASIS, SAPS II, APACHE III), the protective associations of the strict and standard strata persisted, indicating that our findings are not merely artifacts of residual confounding by baseline severity. Recent advances in risk prediction models incorporating renal and inflammatory markers [26] further emphasize the need for comprehensive risk stratification in this population.

The most important limitation of our study is its observational and retrospective design. The SBP strata are defined by achieved blood pressure in the first 24 h, which is a composite result of underlying hemodynamic status, disease severity, and therapeutic interventions (e.g., vasopressor use). Therefore, the observed associations cannot be interpreted as causal effects of an intentional BP management strategy. Patients in the strict SBP stratum likely had higher baseline illness severity (as evidenced by higher rates of sepsis, vasopressor use, and fluid balance), which is a potent confounder for AKI risk. Despite multivariable and propensity score adjustments, residual confounding by indication remains a plausible alternative explanation for our findings.

The U-shaped relationship we observed helps reconcile conflicting evidence. While intensive BP lowering in trials like SPRINT was associated with increased AKI events [27, 28], chronic hypertension is a well-established risk factor for progressive kidney damage [19]. Our finding supports the concept of a therapeutic window for renal perfusion pressure. Several intertwined mechanisms may explain this. First, patients with long-standing hypertension and CKD have impaired renal autoregulation, making their glomeruli vulnerable to both pressure-mediated injury at high SBP and hypoperfusion at low SBP [29, 30]. This autoregulatory dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key driver of hypertensive nephropathy and AKI susceptibility [31, 32]. Second, in acute HF, a higher SBP may reflect elevated systemic vascular resistance and afterload, which can compromise cardiac output and thus renal blood flow [23]. Emerging evidence also implicates neurohormonal activation and immune-mediated inflammation in this process [33, 34]. Third, venous congestion, a major contributor to renal dysfunction in HF [11, 35], may coexist with elevated SBP. The significantly higher net fluid balance in our strict group, coupled with their higher severe AKI risk, suggests that congestion may be a predominant mechanism of injury in that subgroup.

The sensitivity analyses using alternative SBP cutoffs confirmed that our primary findings are robust to the choice of thresholds, strengthening confidence in the observed associations. In the BNP‑available subcohort, the protective effect of the strict stratum remained significant, and higher BNP was independently associated with AKI, consistent with the known role of heart failure severity in renal outcomes. Although LVEF and NYHA class were unavailable, this sensitivity analysis partially mitigates concerns regarding unmeasured heart failure severity. Our findings align with recent studies highlighting the utility of BNP and other biomarkers in refining risk stratification in hypertensive and heart failure populations [36, 37].

The clinical prediction model we developed offers a tool for risk stratification. Its moderate discriminative ability indicates it can reasonably identify patients at high versus low risk for AKI. However, the significant miscalibration underscores a crucial limitation: the model is currently more reliable for ranking risk than for providing precise individual probability estimates [20]. We acknowledge that inclusion of the 48‑hour creatinine ratio may introduce overadjustment, as this variable is closely related to the AKI definition. However, sensitivity analyses excluding this variable did not materially alter the association between SBP strata and AKI, supporting the robustness of our primary findings. The simplified nomogram enhances bedside usability but, like the full model, requires external validation and calibration in independent cohorts before clinical implementation can be recommended [21]. The poor calibration of the simplified model suggests that its clinical utility is currently limited; it should be used with caution and primarily for risk ranking rather than for guiding individual clinical decisions. The integration of machine learning approaches and novel biomarkers, as explored in recent hypertension research [38], may offer opportunities to enhance predictive performance in future studies.

Our study benefits from a large, detailed clinical database, rigorous multivariable and propensity score adjustments, and the use of RCS to model non-linear effects. However, several limitations warrant caution. First, the observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding (e.g., by unmeasured echocardiographic data or HF phenotype) remains possible [15]. Second, defining AKI based on the first inpatient creatinine, while pragmatic, may overestimate incidence compared to a stable outpatient baseline and may misclassify some cases of acute kidney disease [25]. Recent consensus statements emphasize the importance of standardized AKI definitions and the role of novel biomarkers in improving diagnostic accuracy. Third, despite incorporating standardized severity scores, we could not adjust for LVEF or NYHA class due to data unavailability. However, the sensitivity analysis in the BNP‑available subcohort (n = 3,006) yielded consistent results, partially mitigating concerns regarding unmeasured heart failure severity. Fourth, the high rate of missing BNP precluded a definitive assessment of confounding by heart failure severity. Fifth, the SBP strata reflect “achieved” pressures influenced by disease severity and treatment, not intentionally assigned management targets. Sixth, the prediction model’s poor calibration limits its clinical applicability, and its net benefit should be interpreted in light of this limitation. Seventh, the time window for SBP (first 24 h) and AKI (≥ 24 h) was defined to minimize reverse causality, but without precise AKI timing, some degree of temporal overlap may persist. Eighth, due to the unavailability of SOFA scores in the dataset, we used OASIS, SAPS II, and APACHE III as alternative severity measures; while these are well‑validated, the absence of SOFA may limit comparability with studies that used SOFA as the primary severity adjustment. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as generating hypotheses about potential optimal SBP ranges for renal outcomes in acute HF, rather than providing definitive evidence for clinical targets. Future prospective studies incorporating dynamic hemodynamic monitoring, renal biomarkers, and standardized protocols for BP management are urgently needed to validate these findings [39–43].

Conclusion

In this observational study, an achieved SBP of approximately 120 mmHg in the first 24 h was associated with the lowest risk of AKI in critically ill HF patients. Both lower (< 110 mmHg) and higher (≥ 140 mmHg) SBP strata were associated with increased risk, depicting a U‑shaped relationship. These hypothesis‑generating findings highlight the need for prospective studies to determine whether intentionally targeting a moderate SBP range can improve renal outcomes in this population.
