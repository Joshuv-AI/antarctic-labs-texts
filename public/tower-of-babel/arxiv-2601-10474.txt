Optimal error estimates for a discontinuous Galerkin method on curved boundaries with polygonal meshes


Abstract
We consider a discontinuous Galerkin method for the numerical solution of boundary value problems in two-dimensional domains with curved boundaries. A key challenge in this setting is the potential loss of convergence order due to approximating the physical domain by a polygonal mesh. Unless boundary conditions can be accurately transferred from the true boundary to the computational one, such geometric approximation errors generally lead to suboptimal convergence. To overcome this limitation, a higher-order strategy based on polynomial reconstruction of boundary data was introduced for classical finite element methods in [ 28 , 30 ] and in the finite volume context in [ 7 , 11 ] . More recently, this approach was extended to discontinuous Galerkin methods in [ 32 ] , leading to the DG–ROD method, which restores optimal convergence rates on polygonal approximations of domains with curved boundaries. In this work, we provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the DG–ROD method, establishing existence and uniqueness of the discrete solution and deriving error estimates for a two-dimensional linear advection-diffusion-reaction problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on both convex and non-convex domains. Following and extending techniques from classical finite element methods [ 30 ] , we prove that, under suitable regularity assumptions on the exact solution, the DG–ROD method achieves optimal convergence despite polygonal approximations. Finally, we illustrate and confirm the theoretical results with a numerical benchmark.

1.  Introduction

In this work, we study an approach for solving boundary value problems posed in a curved boundary domain of arbitrary shape in the context of discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods. The study of boundary value problems in curved boundary domains is a subject of growing interest in the numerical analysis community. One of the major problems is the reduction in the order of convergence of numerical methods when considering the approximation of the domain by a polygonal mesh. In particular, the DG solutions are highly sensitive to the accuracy of approximations of the curved boundaries
[
4
]
. It has been shown that, given homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a curved boundary domain
\Omega
, if these conditions are imposed on the polygonal domain
\Omega_{h}
, any finite element method will be at most second-order accurate
[
33
]
. This highlights the importance of the boundary condition treatment since the errors in the boundary may pollute the solution inside the domain.

Over the past few decades, several techniques have been developed to remedy this loss of accuracy. There are two main strategies to address this issue. The isoparametric finite element method
[
4
]
and the isogeometric analysis
[
18
]
aim to reduce the geometric error without modifying the variational form. Therefore, this technique requires the construction of a mesh with curved elements on the boundary, which is a challenging geometric problem where ineligible cells can be produced. Moreover, this approach also raises some numerical challenges since it considers non-constant Jacobian transformations from the reference element.

Another strategy considers a polygonal approximation domain
\Omega_{h}
and focuses on a modified variational formulation. There has been a growing body of research focused on correcting the error that results from the approximation of the physical boundary
\partial\Omega
by a polygonal boundary
\partial\Omega_{h}
, by modifying the boundary condition. In
[
25
]
, the authors consider a computational polygonal domain in place of the physical domain and modify the normal vector involved in the wall boundary condition. However, this method can only be formulated for slip-wall boundary conditions, and the work is limited to 2D geometries. In
[
35
]
, the author proposes a modified DG scheme defined on polygonal meshes that avoids integrals inside curved elements. However, integrations along boundary curve segments are still necessary. This approach was extended to solving three-dimensional Euler equations and it was simplified by considering the relation between the normal vector of the computational domain and the surface Jacobian
[
34
]
. In the Shifted Boundary Method (SBM), the location where the boundary conditions are applied is shifted from the true boundary to an approximate (surrogate) boundary. The value of boundary conditions is modified by means of Taylor expansion, in order to reflect this displacement (see
[
3
]
and the references therein). Within the framework of Hybrid High-Order methods, in
[
17
]
, the authors introduced a formulation that combines Nitsche’s technique to weakly impose boundary conditions on curved and complicated Lipschitz domains. Another method proposed in the literature is the so-called Extensions from Subdomains
[
10
]
. The main idea of this approach consists of determining a new Dirichlet boundary condition on the polygonal computational boundary from the one evaluated on the physical boundary.

The development of the method considered in this work began in the finite element (FE) context with
[
28
]
, which introduced polygonal meshes for curved domains while preserving high-order accuracy. Subsequent works
[
6
,
30
,
29
]
refined the approach and provided a rigorous theoretical analysis in the FE setting. Independently, a similar strategy was proposed for finite volume (FV) methods, first in
[
7
,
11
]
and later extended in
[
13
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
, where it became known as the ROD (Reconstruction for Off-site Data) method. The approach was also adapted to finite difference (FD) schemes on Cartesian grids
[
9
]
. Building on these developments, we extended this technique for DG schemes in
[
32
]
, the so-called DG–ROD method.

Although numerical evidence demonstrates the effectiveness of the ROD approach in FV, FD, and DG settings, a rigorous theoretical analysis has so far been limited to FE. In this work, we extend the theoretical framework to DG by establishing error estimates for a two-dimensional linear advection–diffusion–reaction problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, in both the DG norm and the
L^{2}
norm.

The DG–ROD method can be implemented either iteratively – alternating between the DG solver and the polynomial reconstruction – or via a global formulation solved in a single step. Here, we adopt the latter approach, providing an efficient and robust strategy for accurately handling curved boundaries in DG simulation

This document is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces the basic concepts, including mesh notations, the space of discontinuous functions, and the formulation of the problem under consideration. In Section
3
, we analyse fundamental properties of the method, establishing the boundedness of the bilinear form and proving an inf-sup condition. We also study the existence and uniqueness of the solution for the advection–diffusion–reaction problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The core of this work is presented in Section
4
, where we derive error estimates for the DG–ROD method introduced in Section
2
, considering both convex and non-convex domains. This analysis follows the theoretical framework developed for the classical finite element method
[
30
]
. For convex domains, we prove that the DG–ROD solution achieves the optimal
\mathcal{O}(h^{N+1})
convergence rate in the
L^{2}
norm when
N
-degree piecewise polynomials are used, under appropriate regularity assumptions on the solution.

Finally, Section
5
reports numerical experiments and results, and Section
6
summarises the main findings, providing final comments and perspectives for future work.

2.  The DG Variational Formulation

This section addresses a DG formulation for a two-dimensional linear boundary value problem on a curved boundary domain, which is discretised with piecewise linear elements. This method has the advantage of overcoming the difficulties inherent to curved mesh approaches by discretising the physical domain with polygonal meshes constructed from the conventional meshing algorithms, where piecewise linear elements approximate the arbitrary curved boundary. The main idea of the DG method is based on the use of discontinuous functions to obtain an approximate solution. Additionally, the DG–ROD method employs specific polynomial reconstructions for the prescribed boundary conditions on the physical boundary. From a methodological point of view, this approach constitutes a DG extension of the method proposed in
[
28
,
30
]
. Since the method studied in this paper is formulated within a similar variational setting as in
[
30
]
, the description provided in this section should be understood as an adaptation of the corresponding methodology to the DG context. The first step is the definition of the mesh and the broken polynomial spaces. After providing an overview of some basic ideas related to computational meshes, we present the primal formulation of the method, which incorporates the modification derived from the polynomial reconstruction of the boundary conditions.

k\in I^{B}
,
I_{h}(w)
interpolates
w
at the
m_{N}+2
nodes of
T^{k}
outside
e^{kB}
and the
N-1
nodes on
\partial\Omega
corresponding to the interior of
e^{kB}
(right panel, Figure

4.1
Convex case

Let
\Omega
be a convex domain,
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
the exact solution of (
1
)–(
2
), and
v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
. Since
\Omega_{h}\subset\Omega
and

\llbracket u\rrbracket=0,

\llbracket\nabla u\rrbracket=0,

(see
[
16
]
, Lemmas 1.23–1.24), using the jump/average formula (
35
), we have

\displaystyle B^{DG}_{h}(u,v)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{h}u,\nabla_{h}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}-(\bm{b}u,\nabla_{h}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}+(cu,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\llbracket u\rrbracket\cdot\{\!\{\nabla_{h}v\}\!\}\dif s-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\llbracket v\rrbracket\cdot\{\!\{\nabla_{h}u\}\!\}\dif s+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\eta}{h_{e}}\llbracket v\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket u\rrbracket\dif s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\bm{b}\cdot\llbracket v\rrbracket\{\!\{u\}\!\}\dif s+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}|}{2}\llbracket v\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket u\rrbracket\dif s

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{h}u,\nabla_{h}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}-(\bm{b}u,\nabla_{h}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}+(cu,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\llbracket v\rrbracket\cdot\{\!\{\nabla_{h}u\}\!\}\dif s+\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\bm{b}\cdot\llbracket v\rrbracket\{\!\{u\}\!\}\dif s.

Applying elementwise integration by parts and using the fact that
\llbracket u\rrbracket=\llbracket\nabla u\rrbracket=0
, we get

\displaystyle B^{DG}_{h}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(-(\Delta u,v)_{L^{2}(T^{k})}+(\nabla\cdot(\bm{b}u),v)_{L^{2}(T^{k})}+(cu,v)_{L^{2}(T^{k})}\big)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta u+\nabla\cdot(\bm{b}u)+cu,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}=(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}.

Hence, the DG–ROD method is consistent, and the Galerkin orthogonality holds

B^{DG}_{h}(u-u_{h},v)=0,\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}.

(63)

We now derive rigorous error estimates for the DG–ROD method in the case of convex domains. We first establish an estimate in the DG norm (also called energy norm), which captures both the elementwise
H^{1}
-seminorm and the contributions from jumps across element interfaces. Then, leveraging duality arguments, we derive an
L^{2}
-norm estimate exhibiting optimal convergence rates.

Theorem 4.1

(DG-norm estimate)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex domain and
u\in H^{N+1}(\Omega)
the solution of the boundary value problem (
1
)–(
2
). Then, for
h
sufficiently small, there exists a constant
\mathcal{C}>0
, independent of
h
and
u
, such that the DG–ROD solution
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
satisfies

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\leq\mathcal{C}h^{N}|u|_{H^{N+1}(\Omega)},

(64)

where
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}
denotes the DG norm defined in (
2.3
).

Proof.

The proof proceeds by adapting the arguments presented in Theorem 4.3 of
[
30
]
. Let
I_{h}(u)\in\mathcal{W}_{h}
denote the
\mathcal{P}_{N}
-interpolant of
u
at the nodes of the DG space. By the triangle inequality:

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}+\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u_{h}-I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}.

Using the inf-sup condition (Theorem
3.1
) for the DG bilinear form
B_{h}^{DG}
, we have

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u_{h}-I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B^{DG}_{h}(u_{h}-I_{h}(u),v)}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}v\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}}.

(65)

Adding and subtracting
u
in the numerator, using Galerkin orthogonality (
63
), and boundedness of
B_{h}^{DG}
(see (
20
)), we obtain

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\leq\left(1+\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha}\right)\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}.

Unlike standard continuous finite elements, the DG interpolant
I_{h}(u)
is discontinuous across element boundaries. Therefore, the DG norm of the interpolation error includes jump terms:

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}

\displaystyle u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}(T^{k})}\!+\!\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\right\rvert}^{2}_{H^{1}(T^{k})}\!+\!h_{k}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\right\rvert}^{2}_{H^{2}(T^{k})}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}h_{e}^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\llbracket u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\rrbracket\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}(e)}\!+\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert|\bm{b}\!\cdot\!\bm{n}_{e}|^{1/2}\llbracket u\!-\!I_{h}(u)\rrbracket\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}(e)}.

(66)

Using the trace inequality (
23
) and classical polynomial approximation results (Lemma 4.1 in
[
30
]
), we conclude

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-I_{h}(u)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\leq\mathcal{C}_{a}h^{N}|u|_{H^{N+1}(\Omega)}.

Finally, combining the previous steps gives the desired estimate (
64
) with

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{a}\left(1+\frac{C_{b}}{\alpha}\right).

∎

Theorem 4.2

(
L^{2}
-norm estimate)
.

Let
\Omega
be convex and
u
the solution of (
1
)–(
2
) with
u\in H^{N+1+r}(\Omega)
,
r=1/2+\epsilon
,
\epsilon>0
. Then, for
h
sufficiently small and
N>1
, the DG–ROD solution
u_{h}
satisfies

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}h^{N+1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}_{H^{N+1+r}(\Omega)},

(67)

where
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is independent of
h
and
u
.

Proof.

The proof follows the duality argument introduced in
[
30
]
. Recall that every function in
\mathcal{W}_{h}
is defined in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega_{h}
. Let
z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
denote the solution of the adjoint problem

\displaystyle-\Delta z\left({\bm{x}}\right)-\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla z(\bm{x})+c\left({\bm{x}}\right)z\left({\bm{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=u\left({\bm{x}}\right)-u_{h}\left({\bm{x}}\right),\quad{\bm{x}}\in\Omega,

\displaystyle z\left({\bm{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,\quad{\bm{x}}\in\partial\Omega.

Standard elliptic regularity
[
20
]
implies
z\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and there exists a
constant
C(\Omega)>0
such that

\|z\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega)\|u-u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(68)

Consequently,

\|u-u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega)\,\frac{(u-u_{h},-\Delta z-\bm{b}\cdot\nabla z+cz)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|z\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}}.

(69)

Considering
\Delta h=\Omega\setminus\Omega_{h}
and using
z=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle(u\!-\!u_{h},-\Delta z\!-\!\bm{b}\!\cdot\!\nabla z\!+\!cz)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\!=\!(u\!-\!u_{h},-\Delta z-\!\bm{b}\!\cdot\!\nabla z\!+\!cz)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle\quad+(u-u_{h},-\Delta z-\bm{b}\cdot\nabla z+cz)_{L^{2}(\Delta h)}

\displaystyle=-\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!\int_{\partial T^{k}}\!(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!)\frac{\partial z}{\partial n_{h}}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\!+\!(\nabla_{h}(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!),\nabla z)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}\!-\!(\bm{b}(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!),\nabla z)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}

\displaystyle\quad+\!(u\!-\!u_{h},cz)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}\!-\!\int_{\partial\Omega_{h}}(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!)\frac{\partial z}{\partial n_{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\!+\!b_{1h}(\!u\!-\!u_{h},z\!)\!+\!B^{DG}_{\Delta h}(u\!-\!u_{h},z)

with

\displaystyle B^{DG}_{\Delta h}(u\!-\!u_{h},z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta h}\nabla_{h}(u\!-\!u_{h})\cdot\nabla z\!-\!\bm{b}(u\!-\!u_{h})\cdot\nabla z\!+\!(u\!-\!u_{h})cz\dif\bm{x},

(70)

\displaystyle b_{1h}(u-u_{h},z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega}(u-u_{h})\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}\dif s.

(71)

Since
\llbracket z\rrbracket=0
and
\llbracket a\nabla z\rrbracket=0
, we can rewrite

\displaystyle(u-u_{h},-\Delta z-\bm{b}\cdot\nabla z+cz)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\!B^{DG}_{h}(u\!-\!u_{h},z)+B^{DG}_{\Delta h}(u\!-\!u_{h},z)\!+\!b_{1h}(u\!-\!u_{h},z)\!-\!2\int_{\partial\Omega_{h}}(u\!-\!u_{h})\frac{\partial z}{\partial n_{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega_{h}}\frac{\eta}{h_{e}}\llbracket u-u_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket z\rrbracket\dif s-\int_{\partial\Omega_{h}}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\bm{b}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}\right\rvert}}{2}\llbracket u-u_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket z\rrbracket\dif s.

(72)

Let
\Pi_{h}(z)
denote the continuous piecewise linear interpolant of
z
over the mesh vertices and set
z_{h}=\Pi_{h}(z)\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
in
\Omega_{h}
. Then, by definition,

B^{DG}_{h}(u,z_{h})=(f,z_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}=B^{DG}_{h}(u_{h},z_{h}).

(73)

Following
[
30
]

e_{h}(z)=z-\Pi_{h}(z)
and consider

\displaystyle b_{2h}(\!u\!-\!u_{h},\Pi_{h}(z)\!)

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{k\in I^{B}}\!\int_{\Delta_{k}}-\Delta(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!)\!)\Pi_{h}(z)\!-\!\bm{b}(\!u\!-\!u_{h}\!)\!\cdot\!\nabla\Pi_{h}(z)\dif\bm{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\in I^{B}}\int_{\Delta_{k}}c(u-u_{h})\Pi_{h}(z)\dif\bm{x},

(74)

\displaystyle b_{3h}(u-u_{h},\Pi_{h}(z))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in I^{B}}\int_{(T^{k}\cup\Delta_{k})\cap\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial(u-u_{h})}{\partial n}\Pi_{h}(z)\dif s,

Thus,

By Galerkin orthogonality (

Defining the remaining boundary terms

\displaystyle b_{7h}(u-u_{h},z)

and combining (

Using the boundedness inequality (

\displaystyle\leq\!C_{b}\!\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u\!-\!u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e_{h}(z)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\!\leq\!C_{b}\mathcal{C}h^{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert u\right\rvert}_{H^{N+1}(\Omega)}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e_{h}(z)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}.

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\tilde{u}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}+\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u_{h}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}.

By standard DG interpolation theory for
\tilde{u}\in H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})
, there exists a constant
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{a}
independent of
h
such that

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\tilde{u}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{a}h^{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{u}\right\rvert}_{H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})}.

Applying the discrete inf-sup condition for
B^{DG}_{h}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u_{h}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B^{DG}_{h}(u_{h}-I_{h}(\tilde{u}),v)}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}v\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B^{DG}_{h}(\tilde{u}-I_{h}(\tilde{u}),v)+B^{DG}_{h}(u_{h}-\tilde{u},v)}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}v\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}}.

The first term is bounded by the continuity of
B^{DG}_{h}
:

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert B^{DG}_{h}(\tilde{u}-I_{h}(\tilde{u}),v)\right\rvert}\leq C_{b}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\tilde{u}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}v\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}.

For the second term, using the DG formulation and the definition of the residual
R(\tilde{u})=-\Delta\tilde{u}+\nabla\cdot(\bm{b}\tilde{u})+c\tilde{u}
, we have

B^{DG}_{h}(u_{h}-\tilde{u},v)=(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}-B^{DG}_{h}(\tilde{u},v)=-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{Q}^{B}}(R(\tilde{u}),v)_{L^{2}(\Delta_{k})},

where
\Delta_{k}=T^{k}\cap(\Omega_{h}\setminus\Omega)
. Using Cauchy-Schwarz and trace inequalities on
\Delta_{k}
(see Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 3.1 in
[
30
]
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{Q}^{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert(R(\tilde{u}),v)_{L^{2}(\Delta_{k})}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{Q}^{B}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R(\tilde{u})\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Delta_{k})}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Delta_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq C^{3/2}_{\partial\Omega}C_{J}h^{5/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R(\tilde{u})\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}v\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}.

Combining the above bounds yields

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u_{h}-I_{h}(\tilde{u})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\leq\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{a}C_{b}}{\alpha}h^{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{u}\right\rvert}_{H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})}+\frac{C^{3/2}_{\partial\Omega}C_{J}}{\alpha}h^{5/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R(\tilde{u})\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})}.

Finally, we conclude

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}h^{N}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{u}\right\rvert}_{H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})}+\widehat{C}_{0}h^{5/2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R(\tilde{u})\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})},

with
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}=\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{a}\left(1+C_{b}/\alpha\right)
and
\widehat{C}_{0}=C^{3/2}_{\partial\Omega}C_{J}/\alpha
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.  Numerical Results

Let denote by
u_{h}
an approximation of the solution
u
for a given mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\left\|u-u_{h}\right\|
the norm of the error. The method is of convergence order

\left\|u-u_{h}\right\|\leq Ch^{p},

with
C
a real constant independent of
h
. The errors are assessed at the node points of the elements,
T^{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
. We compute the
L^{2}
-errors

\displaystyle E_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{h}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}(T^{k})}}.

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}(T^{k})}\!=\!\left(u^{k}_{h},u^{k}_{h}\right)_{L^{2}(T^{k})}=\int_{T^{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{p}}u^{k}_{i}u_{j}^{k}\ell_{i}^{k}\left(\bm{x}\right)\ell_{j}^{k}\left(\!\bm{x}\!\right)\dif\bm{x}\!=\!(\bm{u}^{k})^{\textrm{T}}M^{k}\bm{u}^{k},

where
M_{ij}^{k}=\left(\ell_{i}^{k},\ell_{j}^{k}\right)_{L^{2}\left(T^{k}\right)}
.

Consider two different meshes, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}
, whose the corresponding numerical solutions are denoted as
u_{h_{1}}
and
u_{h_{2}}
, respectively. Then, the convergence order between two successively finer meshes is determined as

\mathcal{O}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}},\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\right)=\frac{\log\Big(E_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}\right)/E_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\right)\Big)}{\log\left(h_{1}/h_{2}\right)}.

We now present a benchmark for the advection-diffusion-reaction problem on several curved boundary domains, namely a disk, an annulus, and a rose-shaped domain. For additional numerical results concerning diffusion-reaction problems, we refer to
[
32
]

-errors. They also explored different strategies to define the nodes of

on
\partial\Omega

(4)

Annulus domain

Now, we consider an annulus domain with inner radius
R_{I}=0.5
and outer radius
R_{E}=1
meshed with triangular elements (see Figure
5
). The analytic solution corresponds to the manufactured solution
u(x,y)=\log(x^{2}+y^{2})
and the boundaries are prescribed with constant Dirichlet boundary conditions. The numerical simulations are carried out with successively finer meshes generated by Gmsh. As for the previous test case, simulations are performed for the classical DG method and for the DG–ROD method. The results are reported in Tables
4
,
5
and
6
considering
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(1,1)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=1
,
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(\exp{(x)},0)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\exp{(x)}
, and
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(2-y^{2},2-x)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=1+(1+x)(1+y)^{2}
, respectively. The optimal convergence orders are recovered due to the polynomial reconstruction of the boundary conditions.

Remark
.

Note that if there exists a function
\tilde{u}
defined in
\tilde{\Omega}
such that
\tilde{u}\in H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})
,
\tilde{u}
coincide with
u
in
\Omega
,
\tilde{u}
satisfies (
1
) in
\tilde{\Omega}
and
\tilde{u}
vanishes on
\partial\Omega
in the sense of trace, then, for
h
sufficiently small, we may prove that there exists a mesh-independent constant
\mathcal{\tilde{C}}
such that

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}u-u_{h}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{\tilde{C}}h^{N}|\tilde{u}|_{H^{N+1}(\tilde{\Omega})},

(110)

where
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{\prime}
denotes the norm defined in (
4.2
). Thus, if the assumptions described above hold, the optimal convergence can be achieved in the
L^{2}-
norm. However, given a regular
f
in
\tilde{\Omega}
, the existence of an associated
\tilde{u}
satisfying the above assumptions is not ensured. In this test case, the solution satisfies the conditions described above. Thus, the method recovers the optimal convergence orders for
N>2
.

Rose–shaped domain

Consider a geometry obtained by applying a diffeomorphic transformation to an annular domain
\Omega\textsc{\char 13\relax}
, whose physical interior and exterior boundaries have radius
r_{I}
and
r_{E}
, respectively. This diffeomorphic transformation defines a mapping from
\Omega\textsc{\char 13\relax}
to
\Omega
, where
\Omega
denotes the resulting rose-shaped domain, described in polar coordinates

\Omega\textsc{\char 13\relax}\to\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\begin{bmatrix}r\textsc{\char 13\relax}\\
\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}r\\
\theta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R(r\textsc{\char 13\relax},\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax};\alpha,\beta)\\
\theta\end{bmatrix},

(111)

where
\alpha
is the number of petals and function
R(r\textsc{\char 13\relax},\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=R(r\textsc{\char 13\relax},\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax};\alpha,\beta)
corresponds to a periodic radius perturbation of magnitude in
[-\beta,\beta]
, with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, given as

R(r\textsc{\char 13\relax},\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax};\alpha,\beta)=r\textsc{\char 13\relax}\left(1-\beta+\beta\cos(\alpha\theta\textsc{\char 13\relax})\right).

(112)

Thus, the parametrisations of the interior and exterior physical boundaries are defined as
R_{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=R(r_{I},\theta)
and
R_{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=R(r_{E},\theta)
, respectively. The analytical solution is chosen as the manufactured solution
u\left(x,y\right)=\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)
. In this test case, non-constant Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed on both the interior and exterior boundaries.

We set
r_{I}=0.5
,
r_{E}=1
, the number of petals is
\alpha=8
, and the perturbation magnitude is
\beta=0.1
. The resulting rose-shaped domain is discretised using triangular elements (see Figure
6
). The advection-diffusion-reaction equation is solved, and the approximate solution is compared with the exact solution. The numerical simulations are carried out with successively finer meshes generated by Gmsh. Tables
7
,
8
and
9
report the errors and convergence rates for the classical DG method and for the DG–ROD method considering
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(1,1)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=1
,
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(\exp{(x)},0)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\exp{(x)}
, and
\bm{b}(\bm{x})=(2-y^{2},2-x)^{\textrm{T}}
and
c(\bm{x})=1+(1+x)(1+y)^{2}
, respectively. The results for the classical DG method confirm the accuracy deterioration due to the lack of specific treatment for curved boundaries, where the error convergence is limited to the second order. On the other hand, the results for the DG–ROD method show that the optimal convergence orders are recovered. The method behaves similarly to the previous test case, where the optimal convergence orders can be achieved for
N>2
.

6.  Conclusions

In this work, we discussed a discontinuous Galerkin scheme on a polygonal computational domain
\Omega_{h}
for boundary value problems posed on two-dimensional domains
\Omega
with curved boundaries. The DG–ROD method compensates for the geometric inconsistency resulting from approximating the curved boundary
\partial\Omega
by a piecewise linear
\partial\Omega_{h}
via a polynomial reconstruction of the boundary data, incorporated directly into the variational formulation. In practice, DG–ROD can be interpreted as an iterative combination of the standard DG method with boundary data reconstruction, enabling the use of simple polygonal meshes while restoring optimal convergence rates.

We presented a study on the existence and uniqueness of the solution for the advection-diffusion-reaction equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Following and extending the analysis in
[
30
]
, we provided a complete mathematical study of convergence in the natural DG norm, as well as
L^{2}
-error estimates, for both convex and non-convex domains. For convex domains, we proved that the DG–ROD solution achieves an optimal
\mathcal{O}(h^{N+1})
convergence rate in the
L^{2}
-norm when
N
-degree piecewise polynomials are used, under suitable regularity assumptions on the solution. For non-convex domains, optimality is not attained for
N>2
unless the solution satisfies stronger regularity conditions and the computational domain
\Omega_{h}
approximates the physical domain
\Omega
with higher accuracy, i.e., when
\Omega_{h}\setminus\Omega
is of order
h^{q}
, with
q>2
. In other words, the error is affected by the geometric mismatch of order
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
between the curved physical boundary and its piecewise linear representation. In this case, we proved that the DG–ROD solution exhibits an optimal
\mathcal{O}(h^{3})
convergence rate in the
L^{2}
-norm when piecewise polynomials of degree
N=2
are used, under appropriate regularity assumptions.

The sharpness of the theoretical results is confirmed by a series of numerical experiments on both convex and non-convex domains. It is important to highlight that the mesh size assumption is only a sufficient condition for the formal analysis, and accurate results can be obtained even on relatively coarse meshes. For instance, in Section
5.1
, the polynomial reconstruction of the boundary data successfully corrects the error induced by the polygonal approximation of the curved boundary even for meshes with as few as
K=64
elements, for which the mesh size
h
is of the same order of magnitude as the radius of the disk. We also note that, in
[
32
]
, the authors considered a diffusion–reaction problem and constructed the DG–ROD method through an iterative combination of the DG method and polynomial reconstruction, exploring different strategies for placing the nodes of
\mathcal{W}_{h}
on
\partial\Omega
; in their numerical results, they quantified accuracy via the
L^{\infty}
- and
L^{1}
-errors.

Extensions of this work to nonlinear equations and time-dependent problems remain challenging and will be pursued in future research. We also plan to extend the DG–ROD approach to other types of boundary conditions (Neumann, Robin) and derive corresponding error estimates.

Finally, it is noteworthy that the DG–ROD method can be extended to three-dimensional problems. Indeed, the three-dimensional setting has been investigated for classical Lagrange finite elements in
[
31
]
, where a closely related technique was analysed. Following the same principles as in the two-dimensional case, both the numerical strategy and the associated error analysis can be adapted to the DG framework.
