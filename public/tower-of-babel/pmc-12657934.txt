Pre-storage leucoreduction of platelet concentrates reduced biological response modifier concentrations and TRALI incidence in an in vivo sheep model
Authors: John-Paul Tung, Gabriela Simonova, Elise Hewlett, Sanne Engkilde Pedersen, Arlanna Esguerra Lallen, Fahad Alkhureeb, Rebecca Wellburn, Sara Chiaretti, Fergal T Temple, Thy Duong, Michelle Cinel, Corey Giles, Peter J Meikle, John F Fraser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is a potentially fatal transfusion reaction. It can be caused by the transfusion of proteins, lipids, and extracellular vesicles that accumulate in blood components during routine storage and are collectively referred to as biological response modifiers (BRMs). It has been suggested that leucoreduction might reduce TRALI risk by limiting the accumulation of leucocyte-derived BRMs; however, there are conflicting data from haemovigilance reports and animal models regarding this. We harvested supernatant (SN) from leucoreduced buffy coat pooled platelet concentrates (PCs) at days 2, 5, or 7 (n = 25 each). The SNs from each time-point were pooled, aliquoted, and frozen. Similar SN pools collected from non-leucoreduced PCs in a previous study were also used. The PC SN pools underwent analyses for protein, extracellular vesicle and lipid BRMs. The leucoreduced PC SN pools were also transfused to lipopolysaccharide-treated sheep for TRALI assessment (n = 7 per group), with a further four sheep receiving saline infusion as a control. Leucoreduction limited storage-related accumulation of several potential BRMs. This was associated with a lower incidence of TRALI compared to our previous study using non-leucoreduced PC SN. Sheep who developed TRALI had lower plasma levels of interleukin-10, providing further evidence that it may play a protective role against TRALI development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26159-x.Keywords: Transfusion-related acute lung injury, TRALI, Leucoreduction, Sheep modelSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biomarkers, Diseases, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) is a potentially fatal adverse transfusion reaction. The term “TRALI” was first used in 19831, and consensus definitions for TRALI diagnosis were published in 2004 and 20052,3. More recently, an update of the consensus definition for TRALI has been proposed4. This replaces the terms “TRALI” and “possible TRALI” with “TRALI Type I” and “TRALI Type II” where the latter accounts for the presence of risk factors for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and the worsening of pre-existing stable hypoxaemia4. It also allowed for better alignment with the Berlin redefinition of ARDS and acute lung injury5.

TRALI cases are often associated with antibodies against human neutrophil antigens (HNA) or either class I or class II human leucocyte antigens (HLA)6–10. These antibodies are more prevalent in multiparous women and people who have received multiple transfusions8. Therefore, to reduce the risk of TRALI caused by these antibodies, many blood services limit the use of blood from female donors6,11. For example, in Australia, only male donors are used for clinical plasma and aphaeresis platelets10,11. While these TRALI risk reduction strategies have successfully reduced the incidence of TRALI, cases continue to occur10,12–14. For example, in the US in the 2021 Fiscal Year there were seven fatalities attributed to either TRALI or possible TRALI13, while in the UK in 2023 there were five reports of TRALI Type II and one report of TRALI – transfusion associated circulatory overload (TACO)14, and in the Australian state of Queensland in 2019 there were two cases of possible TRALI10.

While many TRALI cases are associated with leucocyte antibodies, in at least 20–30% of cases an antibody is not identified9,10,15. As the current risk reduction strategies primarily focus on antibody-mediated TRALI6,11, these non-antibody mediated TRALI cases are likely to continue to occur. A wealth of experimental evidence has implicated biological response modifiers (BRMs) as the possible causative agents in these cases of non-antibody mediated TRALI7. Potential BRMs include some of the lipids, proteins and extracellular vesicles (EVs) that accumulate during the routine storage of blood components7,16–21.

Many blood services introduced pre-storage leucoreduction of blood components, primarily to reduce the risk of graft versus host disease (GVHD), although there are additional putative benefits. Leucoreduction also limits the accumulation of potential BRMs, such as lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC)20 and cytokines such as interleukin (IL) -1, IL-6, IL-8, and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)22. Therefore, its introduction may simultaneously have reduced the risk of non-antibody mediated TRALI. However, it is difficult to ascertain the impact of leucoreduction in haemovigilance reports due to the introduction of other risk-reduction strategies across similar time-periods, and also because of improvements in awareness and reporting. For example, after leucoreduction was introduced in the UK in late 1999, fewer TRALI reports and deaths were reported in 200015. However, from then until the introduction of TRALI risk reduction strategies in 2003, there was an increase in both TRALI reports and deaths, possibly due to increased awareness of TRALI and reporting of cases15. Furthermore, data from a rat in vivo model showed that leucoreduction of red cell units did not prevent the development of TRALI23.

We previously reported the development of TRALI in an in vivo model where LPS-treated sheep were transfused with supernatant from units of day 5 (date-of-expiry at the time) non-leucoreduced human pooled buffy coat platelets concentrates (PCs)24. However, in Australia, pooled buffy coat PCs are now leucoreduced and have a 7 day shelf-life17,25. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether TRALI developed less frequently in our sheep model with the use of supernatant (SN) from leucoreduced day 5 or day 7 human pooled buffy coat PCs.

Materials and methods

Ethics and animal preparation

The use of human blood products was approved by the Ethics Committee of Australian Red Cross Lifeblood. Animal experiments were approved by the University Animal Ethics Committee of the Queensland University of Technology (ethics approval 1600000623) and were conducted in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes and the ARRIVE guidelines.

Twenty-five female sheep (Ovis aries, Merino breed) were sourced from the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation. They were housed at Queensland University of Technology facilities with free access to shade and water and fed with proprietary sheep feed and lucerne. Prior to experiments, sheep were fasted overnight.

Platelet concentrate supernatant pool preparation

Leucoreduced buffy coat pooled PCs prepared according to standard Australian Red Cross Lifeblood procedures (n = 75) were stored at 20 – 25 °C with continuous agitation. At either day (d) 2, 5 or 7 (n = 25 each), PCs were centrifuged (1,500 × g, 15 min, room temperature) and supernatant collected into sterile tubes (BD Biosciences, North Ryde, NSW, Australia) and centrifuged again (3300 × g, 10 min, room temperature). The final PC supernatant was then decanted and stored (−80 °C) until pooling. This pooling approach was chosen to average out any impact of individual PC units with lower or higher BRM concentrations, and so that the sheep within each experimental group could be transfused with the same product, thus allowing us to reduce the number of sheep required for the study. Pools were prepared by thawing and pooling the PC supernatant from each time point. This was followed by heat-inactivation treatment at 56 °C for 30 min to inactivate complement and fibrinogen and to prevent widespread thrombus formation23,24,26. Pools were then aliquoted into sterile 50 mL tubes (BD Biosciences) and re-frozen (−80 °C) until required. The three supernatant pools were designated day 2 leucoreduced PC supernatant (d2-LR-PC-SN), day 5 leucoreduced PC supernatant (d5-LR-PC-SN), and day 7 leucoreduced PC supernatant (d7-LR-PC-SN). Additionally, aliquots of non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools prepared previously24, underwent potential BRM assessment. These non-leucoreduced PC supernatant24 were designated day 1 non-leucoreduced PC supernatant (d1-NLR-PC-SN) and day 5 non-leucoreduced PC supernatant (d5-NLR-PC-SN).

Assessment of potential biological response modifiers in platelet concentrate supernatant pools

Potential BRMs were assessed in aliquots of the three leucoreduced and two old non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools. Proteins were quantified using commercial enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) and multiplex cytometric bead array (CBA) as follows: Quantikine ELISA kits for epithelial neutrophil-activating peptide 78 (ENA-78), growth-regulated alpha protein (GRO-α), and platelet factor 4 (PF4) (all from R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA); Flex Sets for CBA for intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-10, IL-6, IL-8, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), regulated upon activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), soluble CD40 ligand (sCD40L), soluble E-selectin (sCD62E), soluble P-selectin (sCD62P), soluble L-selectin (sCD62L), TNF-alpha, vascular cell adhesion protein 1 (VCAM-1), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (all BD Biosciences). Assays were completed as per manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications to CBA procedures whereby sample and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were used in half volumes. Corresponding control sets were used in conjunction with ELISAs. Where appropriate, samples were diluted to achieve a concentration corresponding to the standard curve centre. All samples were measured in duplicate. Absorbance readings for the ELISA plates were obtained using a Synergy plate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA) and data were analysed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1; GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, California, USA). Fluorescence readings for the CBA plates were obtained on a FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) and analysed using FCAP array (BD Biosciences). In both cases, analyses were completed and logistic regression (either 4 or 5 parameter) was selected for standard curve interpolation as per manufacturer’s instructions. Results below the lowest standard were reported as not detectable.

As an indication of EV content, the concentration and size of extracellular particles (EPs) < 1000 nm was determined in aliquots of the three new and two old PC supernatant pools using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight NS300; Malvern Panalytical; Malvern, UK). All samples were diluted in 0.2 µm filtered (Sartorius; Goettingen, Germany) phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) to achieve a concentration between 108 and 1010 particles/mL. Ten experimental replicates of one minute were analysed per aliquot. The EP concentration and size distribution were analysed using NTA 3.2 software (Malvern Panalytical).

Lipids were isolated from three aliquots of each of the three new and two old PC supernatant pools using a single phase butanol:methanol extraction as previously described27. Briefly, randomised samples (10 µL) were extracted in a single-phase extraction with 100 µL of BuOH:MeOH (1:1) containing an internal standard mix of between 10 and 10,000 pmol each of 30 non-physiological or stable isotope-labelled lipid standards (Supplementary Table 1). Lipid analysis was performed by liquid chromatography electrospray ionisation-tandem mass spectrometry using an Agilent 1290 HPLC coupled to an Agilent 6495C triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Liquid chromatography was performed on a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column, 100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm (Agilent Technologies) with the column compartment set to 45 °C. The solvent system consisted of solvent A) 50% H2O / 30% acetonitrile / 20% isopropanol (v/v/v) containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 5 µM medronic acid and solvent B) 1% H2O / 9% acetonitrile / 90% isopropanol (v/v/v) containing 10 mM ammonium formate. We utilised a stepped linear gradient with a 15 min run time per sample and a 1 µL sample injection27. The first 0.8 min of each analytical run was diverted to waste27. Peak areas for each lipid species were integrated using Agilent Masshunter Quantitative software v10.027.

The oxylipin lipid extraction was performed via solid phase extraction (SPE) as described previously28. In brief, 100 µL of PC supernatant was mixed with 10 µL of internal standards (Supplementary Table 1), 200 µL of 10% glycerol in water, and 2.7 mL of 25% acetonitrile in water. The samples were vortexed thoroughly and loaded onto a pre-conditioned Oasis MAX SPE cartridge (3 cc, 60 mg sorbent; Waters, USA). The SPE cartridge was washed with 3 mL of 25% acetonitrile in water, followed by 3 mL of acetonitrile. Oxylipins were eluted using two additions of 650 µL of 1% formic acid in acetonitrile, with both eluents collected in tubes containing 200 µL of 10% glycerol in methanol. Eluents were dried under nitrogen at 40 °C then reconstituted in 100 µL of 50/50 acetonitrile/methanol and transferred to a glass vial with insert. The samples were stored at −80 °C until mass spec analysis.

Analysis of oxylipin SPE extracts was performed on an Agilent 6495C triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an Agilent 1290 series HPLC system and a ZORBAX eclipse plus C18 column, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm (Agilent Technologies) with the column compartment set to 45 °C. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed in negative ion mode with the solvent system consisted of solvent A) 100% H2O containing 0.1% acetic acid and solvent B) 80% acetonitrile / 20% isopropanol (v/v/v) containing 0.1% acetic acid. We utilised a stepped linear gradient with a 20 min run time per sample and a 5 µL sample injection28. The first 0.6 min of each analytical run was diverted to waste28. Peak areas for each lipid species were integrated using Agilent Masshunter Quantitative software v10.028.

Animal study design and experimental protocol

Sheep (n = 25) were randomly allocated to be infused with saline (control; n = 4) or transfused with either d2-LR-PC-SN, d5-LR-PC-SN, or d7-LR-PC-SN (n = 7 per group). While the cross-species design of this and similar xenogeneic transfusion models20,23,24,26,29,30 might impact the reactivity of some BRMs, they offer the advantage that the BRMs are mostly the same as those implicated in clinical cases of TRALI. In contrast, the storage lesion and BRM profiles of animal PCs are poorly understood and likely to differ significantly to that of human PCs.

The experimental protocol (Fig. 1) was based upon that described previously24,26 with minor modifications as described below. Sheep had a multilumen central venous catheter and 8 Fr sheath placed in the left internal jugular vein under local anaesthesia. Initial intravenous analgesia and anaesthesia induction was via buprenorphine (300 µg), midazolam (~ 0.5 mg/kg), and alfaxalone (~ 6 mg/kg). On-going intravenous anaesthesia was via ketamine (~ 8 mg/kg/hour), midazolam (~ 0.7 mg/kg/hour), fentanyl (~ 50 µg/kg/hour), and alfaxalone (~ 6 mg/kg/hour) titrated as required to maintain adequate anaesthesia. Sheep were mechanically ventilated on a Galileo ventilator (Hamilton Medical, Bonaduz, Switzerland) set to volume control mode, initially via an endotracheal tube (Portex; Smith’s Medical Australia, Brisbane, Queensland, Australia), and then, after tracheostomy, via a tracheostomy tube (Portex). To account for the possible effects of dehydration, sheep were given 11.25 mL/kg Hartmann’s solution over a 45 min period. Sheep were instrumented as previously described24,26; however, the arterial line was situated in the facial artery rather than in the carotid artery. At the completion of instrumentation, a ventilatory lung recruitment procedure was performed, and 1 h of haemodynamic monitoring was commenced.

Overview of study design and experimental protocol. Female Merino sheep (n = 25) were allocated into four study groups (n = 3 for saline group and n = 7 per transfusion group). Animals were anaesthetised, ventilated, underwent instrumentation, and were infused with 11.25 mL/kg Hartmann’s solution. After a 1 h baseline period, animals were infused with 11.25 µg/kg lipopolysaccharide (LPS; O55:B55 serotype) over a 3 h period. After a 1 h post-LPS monitoring period, animals were transfused over a 1 h period with either saline, day (D) 2 leucoreduced (LR) platelet concentrate (PC) supernatant (SN), D5-LR-PC-SN, or D7-LR-PC-SN based upon the study allocation. Transfusion volume was 10% v/v based upon an estimated 65 mL/kg circulating blood volume. Animals were monitored for 2 h following the completion of the transfusion, after which they were euthanised (pentobarbitone sodium) and organs were retrieved for analyses. Venous blood samples were collected at specific time-points as indicated by the red asterisks (pre-anaesthesia, baseline (post-instrumentation), post-LPS infusion, post-transfusion, and pre-mortem). Arterial blood gas samples were collected and analysed every 15 min during the experiment. d2-LR-PC-SN: day 2 leucoreduced platelet concentrate supernatant; d5-LR-PC-SN: day 5 leucoreduced platelet concentrate supernatant; d7-LR-PC-SN: day 7 leucoreduced platelet concentrate supernatant; LPS: lipopolysaccharide; PC: platelet concentrate; SN: supernatant.

After 1 h, sheep were infused over a 3 h period with 10 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; from Escherichia coli serotype O55:B5; Sigma-Aldrich, Castle Hill, NSW, Australia) at a dose of 11.25 µg/kg. Following the completion of the LPS-infusion, ventilation was changed to pressure control mode. One hour following the end of the LPS infusion, sheep were infused with saline or transfused with aliquots of either d2-LR-PC-SN, d5-LR-PC-SN, or d7-LR-PC-SN over a 1 h period. Saline infusion / PC-SN transfusion volume was 10% v/v based upon an estimated blood volume of 65 mL/kg24,26, with a mean volume of 303.2 mL transfused (standard deviation: 32.8 mL). Since the majority of clinical cases of TRALI manifest within this time-frame31, sheep were monitored for 2 h following the completion of the infusion/transfusion and were then euthanised with 12 mL pentobarbitone sodium (325 mg/mL; Virbac Animal Health, Milpera, NSW, Australia).

Sample collection and processing

Samples of venous blood were collected at pre-anaesthesia, baseline (post-instrumentation), post-LPS infusion, post-transfusion, and pre-mortem. Aliquots of serum and plasma were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. Samples of arterial blood were collected throughout the experiment at 30-min intervals for arterial blood gas (ABG) measurements on the ABL System 625 (Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). Samples of EDTA blood were tested using the veterinary mode of an AcT diff™haematology analyser (Beckman Coulter Australia Pty Ltd, Lane Cove West, Australia) for full blood evaluation.

As described previously24,26, post-mortem tissue samples were collected from the lower lobe of the left lung, for histology and wet/dry weight analyses. Samples for histology were immediately fixed in 10% formalin and then processed and embedded in paraffin using routine methods. Histological examination of lung sections was as previously described24,26. In brief, 4 µm sections of lung underwent haemotoxylin and eosin staining before being semi-quantitatively assessed for pulmonary oedema by two researchers blinded to the experimental groupings. Twenty fields of lung histology for each section were graded for pulmonary oedema, alveolar structure, inflammatory cell infiltration, vascular pathology, and bronchus/bronchiole pathology via a scoring system of 0- normal, 1- mild oedema, 2- moderate oedema and 3- severe oedema. Samples for wet/dry weight analysis were immediately weighed (wet weight) and then dried in an oven at 50 °C until a stable weight was achieved (dry weight).

Definition of transfusion-related acute lung injury

Similar to the clinical definition4 and as previously described24,26, TRALI in the sheep model was defined by the development of hypoxaemia (PaO2/FiO2 < 300 on arterial blood gas) during or within 2 h of transfusion accompanied by post-mortem histological evidence of pulmonary oedema (average score ≥ 1). Where hypoxaemia was present prior to the start of the transfusion, TRALI was defined by a worsening of the hypoxaemia either during or within 2 h of transfusion accompanied by post-mortem histological evidence of pulmonary oedema (average score ≥ 1). As all the sheep received LPS, this model could be considered to represent TRALI Type II4.

Assays

Changes in pulmonary and systemic coagulation have previously been reported in a rat models of BRM-mediated TRALI32,33. Therefore, we used ROTEM® and MuItiplate® (both Haemoview Diagnostics, Brisbane, Australia) to investigate whether coagulation and platelet function respectively were altered in the sheep model. These were used as per manufacturer’s instructions and as described previously34,35; however, as sheep were not heparinised, FIBTEM rather than HEPTEM was performed. In-house ELISAs for sheep inflammatory mediators, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and IL-10 were performed on serially collected sheep plasma samples as previously described36.

Statistics

Aliquots of the three leucoreduced and two non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools underwent BRM testing, and the results of these technical replicates are presented. Storage-related or leucoreduction-related changes of 50% or higher (i.e. ≤ 50% or ≥ 150% vs. the comparator) were defined as being biologically relevant.

The incidence of TRALI was compared between groups using two-way contingency tables and Fisher’s exact test (http://statpages.org/ctab2x2.html). GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1) was used for subsequent statistical testing with P < 0.05 considered significant. Physiological data measured throughout the experiments were compared between groups using mixed effects models with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction applied and Sidak’s multiple comparisons post-test. Comparisons between groups for plasma cytokines, ROTEM® and MuItiplate® were performed using either repeated measures two-way ANOVA or by mixed effects models with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction applied in the case of missing data-points. In both cases, Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-test was also used. End-point measurements (e.g. lung wet/dry weights, lung histology scores, BAL total protein) were compared between groups using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-test.

For the comparisons between sheep that did and did not develop TRALI, physiological data collected either from the start of LPS administration to immediately prior to transfusion or from the start of the transfusion to the end of the experiment were compared using mixed effects models with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction applied and Sidak’s multiple comparisons post-test. ROTEM® and MuItiplate® responses either from start of LPS administration to immediately prior to transfusion or from the start of the transfusion to the end of the experiment were compared using either repeated measures two-way ANOVA or by mixed effects models with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction applied in the case of missing data-points. In both cases, Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-test was also used. Plasma cytokine concentrations at either the post-LPS treatment or post-transfusion time-points were compared using Mann Whitney tests. End-point measurements (e.g. lung wet/dry weights, lung histology scores, BAL total protein) were compared using unpaired two-tailed t-tests.

Results

Concentrations of potential biological response modifiers were lower in leucoreduced compared to non-leucoreduced platelet concentrate supernatant pools

Various BRMs have been implicated in TRALI development7, and concentrations of some BRMs are impacted by leucoreduction20,22. Therefore, we investigated a panel of potential BRMs in the three leucoreduced PC supernatant pools prepared for this study, as well as the two previously prepared non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools used in the previous sheep TRALI models24,26 (Tables 1, 2, 3). Several potential protein BRMs were undetectable in all of the PC supernatant pools throughout the storage period (Table 1). For the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, storage-related increases in IL-8, RANTES, sCD62P, VEGF, GRO-α, PF4, and ENA-78 were observed at day 5. Notably, moderate increases (~ 30%) were observed in sCD62L and VCAM-1. Through to day 7, a further storage-related increase was seen in ENA-78, with small increases (~ 30%) in RANTES and PF4. For the two non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, storage-related increases in RANTES, sCD62P, sCD40L, VEGF, GRO-α, PF4, and ENA-78 were observed at day 5. Comparison of the d5-LR-PC-SN and the d5-NLR-PC-SN pools demonstrated leucoreduction-associated decreases in IL-8, RANTES, sCD40L, GRO-α, PF4, and ENA-78 in the former.

Accumulation of EVs, a type of EP, has been reported during the routine storage of both red cell units37 and PCs16,17, and both have been shown to induce TRALI in mouse models16,18,19. Therefore, as a proxy of EV content, we also assessed the EP content of the five PC supernatant pools (Table 2); however, none of the changes met our threshold of 50% for a biologically relevant difference. In the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, there was a modest 29% increase in EP concentration during storage from day 2 to day 5, with no further increase to day 7. In comparison, the non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools produced only a marginal 13% increase in EP concentration from day 1 to day 5 of storage. Comparisons of non-leucoreduced and leucoreduced PC supernatant pools demonstrated no difference in EP concentration for fresh pools (d1-NLR-PC-SN vs. d2-LR-PC-SN), and only a modest 9% higher EP concentration in stored pools (d5-NLR-PC-SN vs. d5-LR-PRC-SN). We also assessed the size of the EPs in the five PC supernatant pools, and this ranged from 173.9 to 204.0 nm (Table 1). In the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, there was only a 7% increase in mean EP size during storage from day 2 to day 5, with a further 4% increase at day 7 (i.e. a 12% increase vs. day 2). In the non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, there was an 11% increase in mean EP size during storage from day 1 to day 5. Comparisons of non-leucoreduced vs. leucoreduced PC supernatant pools demonstrated no difference in mean EP size for day 5 PC supernatant pools, but that the mean EP size was 5% larger in the d2-LR-PC-SN pool compared to the d1-NLR-PC-SN pool.

Lipid BRMs implicated in TRALI include platelet activating factor (PAF) / lyso-PAF38,39, LPC20,38,39, arachidonic acid20, hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETE)20, and ceramides16, while sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) has been shown to have a protective effect16,40. Therefore, we also investigated the lipid composition of the five PC supernatant pools (Tables 3, 4). In the three leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, at day 5 we observed increased 12-HETE, 15-HETE, and 15-HETrE, as well as decreased sphingosine (Sph) compared to day 2. We also observed a moderate decrease (43%) in S1P. Then at day 7, we observed increased PAF, lyso-PAF, 12-HETE, 15-HETE, and 15-HETrE, as well as decreased Sph compared to day 2. At day 7, we also observed a moderate increase in LPC (46%) and a moderate decrease in S1P (41%) compared to day 2. For the non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, there were storage-related increases in PAF (a), PAF (b), lyso-PAF, 11-HETE, 12-HETE, 15-HETE, and 15-HETrE, as well as storage-related decreases in Sph and S1P. Notably, in the leucoreduced and non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, a storage-related increase in ceramides was not observed at either a class level (Table 1), or at an individual species level (Supplementary Table 2). Other lipid classes not previously associated with TRALI where we observed storage-related changes in the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools included increased phosphatidic acid, lysoalkenylphosphatidylcholine, lysoalkenylphosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol monophosphate, and phosphatidylserine. Similarly, for the non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, there were storage-related increases in phosphatidic acid, phosphatidylserine, and alkyldiacylglycerol, as well as a storage-related decrease in diacylglycerol. Comparison of d5-LR-PC-SN and d5-NLR-PC-SN demonstrated leucoreduction-related decreases in phosphatidic acid, free fatty acid, diacylglycerol, 5-HETE, 11-HETE, 12-HETE, 15-HETrE, and lyso-PAF in the former.

Transfusion-related acute lung injury development was not widespread

LPS infusion itself can cause lung injury in sheep41, and while unlikely based upon repeating the transfusion volume used in our previous sheep TRALI models24,26, circulatory overload was another consideration. Therefore, we included a group of LPS-treated sheep who were infused with saline. Only one of these sheep had worsening hypoxaemia following the saline infusion but did not display evidence of pulmonary oedema. Only one of these four sheep displayed evidence of pulmonary oedema, but this was not associated with new or worsening hypoxaemia during or following the saline infusion. Hence, none of these four sheep primed with LPS and infused with saline were considered to have developed lung injury (0% incidence).

According to the threshold hypothesis42, the lower concentrations of potential BRMs present in the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools might reduce the incidence of TRALI compared to that previously observed with non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools24. Therefore, we investigated TRALI development resulting from the transfusion of the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools into LPS pre-treated sheep (Table 5). While either new or worsening hypoxaemia was observed in six of the seven sheep transfused with d2-LR-PC-SN, only one of these sheep also showed evidence of pulmonary oedema. Hence, only a single case of TRALI was diagnosed in this group, corresponding to an incidence of 14.3%.

In laboratory and animal models, the development of BRM-mediated TRALI has been associated with increased blood component storage duration7,16,18,19,24,29,33,43. Many countries have a five day shelf-life for PCs17, which was investigated in the previous sheep TRALI model24. Therefore, we assessed 5-day stored leucoreduced PCs in this sheep model (Table 5). Five of the seven sheep transfused with d5-LR-PC-SN developed hypoxaemia (either new or worsening) with three of these sheep also showing evidence of pulmonary oedema. Therefore, three cases of TRALI were diagnosed in this group, corresponding to an incidence of 42.9%, statistically no different to that observed with d2-LR-PC-SN (P = 0.24).

Some countries, including Australia25, have extended PC shelf-life from five to seven days17, therefore we also investigated whether this posed any additional risk of TRALI in the sheep model. As with the d5-LR-PC-SN, five of the seven sheep transfused with d7-LR-PC-SN developed hypoxaemia (either new or worsening; Table 5). However, only two of these sheep also developed pulmonary oedema, hence only two cases of TRALI were observed in this group (28.6% incidence). Again, this incidence was statistically no different to that observed with d2-LR-PC-SN (P = 0.51).

Comparison of the incidence of TRALI between the three groups of sheep transfused with PC-SN failed to demonstrate an association between increased PC storage duration and TRALI incidence. Similarly, comparison of respiratory/pulmonary parameters (P/F ratios, histological pulmonary oedema scores, histological total lung injury scores, lung wet/dry weights, BAL total protein, static pulmonary compliance, and peak airway pressures) were not different between these three groups of sheep (data not shown). Furthermore, no differences between these three groups were observed for haemodynamic parameters (heart rate, MAP, CVP, cardiac output, and PAP), arterial lactate, platelet responses (velocity, aggregation, and AUC in response to either collagen or ADP on Multiplate), coagulation (CT, CFT, A30, alpha, and MCF in INTEM, EXTEM, and FIBTEM), or inflammation (plasma IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10) (data not shown). Differences that may have predisposed lipopolysaccharide-treated sheep to transfusion-related acute lung injury development

The threshold hypothesis suggests that both recipient and blood component factors interact to precipitate TRALI development42. Therefore, we compared the post-LPS/pre-transfusion characteristics of sheep that developed TRALI with those that did not (Fig. 3). No differences were observed in respiratory parameters (P/F ratios, static pulmonary compliance, and peak airway pressures), haemodynamic parameters (heart rate, MAP, CVP, and cardiac output), arterial lactate, platelet aggregation responses (velocity, aggregation, and AUC in response to either collagen or ADP on Multiplate), coagulation (CT, CFT, A30, alpha, and MCF in INTEM, EXTEM, and FIBTEM), or plasma IL-6 and IL-8. We had previously demonstrated that treatment of sheep with LPS resulted in a neutropenia that corresponded with pulmonary sequestration of neutrophils24. While we were unable to measure circulating neutrophil levels in the present study, we did observe that WBC counts following LPS treatment were significantly lower in sheep that developed TRALI compared to those that did not (3.8 ± 3.1 × 109/L vs. 1.5 ± 0.3 × 109/L; P = 0.0289; Fig. 3B).

Administration of IL-10 was protective against anti-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I monoclonal antibody (mAb) mediated TRALI development in mice44, and low IL-10 levels were reported in patients with TRALI45,46. Therefore, we hypothesised that sheep that developed TRALI might have had lower levels of IL-10 in response to LPS compared to sheep that did not develop TRALI. Our testing supported this hypothesis, as sheep that developed TRALI had a lower mean plasma IL-10 concentration compared to sheep that did not develop TRALI (7,781 ± 2,309 pg/mL vs. 10,490 ± 3,563 pg/mL; P = 0.0274; Fig. 3C).

Changes associated with the development of transfusion-related acute lung injury

To understand the pathophysiology associated with TRALI development, we also compared the changes that occurred post-transfusion between sheep that did and did not develop TRALI (Fig. 4). The development of TRALI was associated with more severe pulmonary oedema (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3A) and total lung injury score (P = 0.0027; Fig. 4B). However, no differences were observed in P/F ratios, lung wet/dry weights, BAL total protein, static pulmonary compliance, and peak airway pressures. Furthermore, no differences between groups were observed for several haemodynamic parameters (heart rate, CVP, and PAP), arterial biochemistry (pH, lactate, and base-excess), platelet responses (velocity, aggregation, and AUC in response to either collagen or ADP on Multiplate), coagulation (CT, CFT, A30, alpha, and MCF in INTEM, EXTEM, and FIBTEM), or inflammation (plasma IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10). While differences between outcomes over time were observed in MAP (P = 0.0003 by mixed-effects analysis), post hoc tests failed to demonstrate any differences between groups at specific time-points. Sheep that developed TRALI had a lower cardiac output than those that did not (P = 0.0062 by mixed-effects analysis and P = 0.0389 at the 6-h time-point by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test).

Discussion

TRALI cases and fatalities continue to be reported despite the introduction of risk-reduction strategies. It is possible that many of these residual cases are attributable to BRMs as current risk-reduction strategies target antibody-mediated TRALI. Pre-storage leucoreduction of blood components was introduced primarily to reduce the risk of GVHD; however, as many BRMs are leucocyte-derived, it may also have reduced the risk of BRM-mediated TRALI. Specific BRMs associated with TRALI development that have been shown to accumulate in PCs during routine storage include LPC39, sCD40L21, VEGF47, and EVs19, particularly EVs high in ceramide and low in S1P16. Overall, in the present study, we noted that the storage related accumulation of potential BRMs and of other mediators that stimulate neutrophils (e.g. PF448,49, and ENA-7849) were reduced in leucoreduced compared to non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools.

Within our leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, sCD40L concentrations were undetectable throughout the 7 day storage period. While a small storage-related increase was seen in the non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, the 120 pg/mL concentration seen at day 5 was far lower than the 11,800 pg/mL previously reported for day 5 aphaeresis PCs21. VEGF concentrations were undetectable in the fresh non-leucoreduced and leucoreduced PC supernatant pools (day 2 and day 1 respectively); however, were detectable at similar concentrations by day 5 of storage in both, with no further increase by day 7 of storage for the former. The VEGF concentrations observed at day 5 (~ 60 pg/mL) were less than half of those previously described in leucoreduced aphaeresis PCs (~ 150 pg/mL)47. Notably, the concentrations of VEGF observed here were far less than those shown to induce lung leak in an ex vivo rat lung model of TRALI (5750 pg/mL)47. The differences observed in both sCD40L and VEGF concentrations here compared to other studies21,47 may simply reflect differences between buffy coat pooled and aphaeresis PCs or other manufacturing differences between Australia and the US. It is also possible that the heat-inactivation step may also have reduced concentrations of protein BRMs such as sCD40L and VEGF. Regardless, this heat-inactivation step is unlikely to impact lipid BRM concentrations and our previous studies with heat-inactivated supernatant from non-leucodreduced PCs24 or red cells26 resulted in a TRALI incidence of 80% in LPS-treated sheep.

We observed only a small storage-related increase in EPs in the leucoreduced and non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools that was less than the 50% we defined as biologically relevant. This is comparable to some previous reports of unchanged EV concentrations during storage50,51; however, other studies have reported a storage-related increase in EV concentration16,52. Thus, it remains unclear whether there is an association between increased storage duration of PCs and EV release. This likely relates to variation between donors50 as well as differences in manufacturing processes and in EV detection methods. For example, differences in EV concentrations were observed in PCs prepared using different aphaeresis instruments50, and differences in EV concentrations in red cell units were observed between tunable resistive pulse sensing, flow cytometry, and dynamic light scattering methods53. Furthermore, we note that our use of NTA did not allow us to determine the proportion of EPs that were EVs, as compared to other EPs such as lipoproteins54. Based upon expert panel recommendations54, further research is needed using orthologous techniques to determine the proportion of EPs that are EVs in the various PC supernatant pools. Further research is also needed to characterise these EVs, including their cell of origin and cargo. An important aspect for TRALI development of the EV cargo in PCs is the balance between the expression of the lipids, S1P and ceramide, with the former being protective and the latter being injurious of endothelial barrier function16,40. Initially, EVs are high in S1P and low in ceramide; however, during storage, imbalance of the sphingolipid rheostat results in a change to low S1P and high ceramide EV expression16. We measured the overall S1P and ceramide concentrations in the PC supernatant pools rather than what was specifically associated with EVs. The storage-related decrease in S1P that we observed in our leucoreduced PC supernatant pools (43 and 41% at day 5 and day 7 respectively) was smaller than that seen in our non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools (77%). However, we saw no evidence of storage-related increases in ceramide concentrations in either leucoreduced or non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, even when we looked at individual ceramide species. Further experiments characterising the concentrations of both EV-associated ceramides and non-EV-associated ceramides would be required to better understand the role of ceramides in BRM-mediated TRALI.

In addition to S1P and ceramide, several other lipids have been identified as potential BRMs in TRALI. In the leucoreduced and non-leucoreduced PC supernatant pools, we observed only a modest increase in LPC concentration at day 5 (25 and 18% respectively); however, in the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools a further small increase was seen at day 7 (45.7% vs. day 2). Typically, much of the research on LPC and TRALI focused on its accumulation in non-leucoreduced red cell units30,38; however, leucoreduction has been shown to limit its accumulation during storage20,55. Notably, an earlier study reported a 248% increase in palmitoyl LPC in whole blood PCs by day 5 of storage39. This difference is likely due to differences in manufacturing processes and analysis methods across the two studies. Early focus on the potential of LPC to mediate BRM-mediated TRALI was partly due to their close resemblance to PAF38,39,56, a known neutrophil priming agent57. Here, we observed that levels of lyso-PAF and PAFs increased markedly in non-leucoreduced PCs during the 5 days of storage. It appeared that this increase occurred very early during PC storage as levels in the d2-LR-PC-SN were also markedly higher compared to the d1-NLR-PC-SN. We note that it is a limitation of our study that it is difficult to directly compare these two PC supernatant pools due to the additional day of storage for the former; however, in Australia, due to the introduction of bacterial contamination screening, PCs are now typically only released to hospitals on day 2 of storage. Interestingly, by day 5 and even through to day 7 of storage for the leucoreduced PC supernatant pools levels of lyso-PAF and PAFs were no higher than in the d5-NLR-PC-SN pool, suggesting that while these lipids can prime neutrophils, they were not a key factor in the different incidences of TRALI observed here (42.9% for LPS + d5-LR-PC-SN and 28.6% for LPS + d7-LR-PC-SN) compared to previously (75% for LPS + d5-NLR-PC-SN)24. Another class of lipids implicated in TRALI are the HETEs20. Similar to lyso-PAF and PAF, we also observed an increase in HETE levels in non-leucoreduced PCs markedly during the 5 days of storage and that some of these increases occurred very early during PC storage as 12-HETE, 15-HETE and 15-HETrE levels in the d2-LR-PC-SN were also markedly higher compared to the d1-NLR-PC-SN. Notably, levels of 11-HETE and 2-HETE were markedly higher in d5-NLR-PC-SN compared to both the d5-LR-PC-SN and d7-LR-PC-SN. These findings mirror the differences in TRALI development seen in the sheep model here compared to previously24, suggesting that these lipids might contribute to TRALI development in the sheep model. Further experiments could test purified 11-HETE and 12-HETE in either the sheep model, or other experimental or animal models of TRALI.

Experimental and animal models have implicated PC storage duration as a factor in the development of BRM-mediated TRALI29,33,43 including our previous study using sheep and d5-NLR-PC-SN24. This includes other xenogeneic models transfusing human PC supernatants into rat lungs29, rats43 or sheep24. However, in our sheep model, we no longer observed any increase in TRALI incidence with either d5-LR-PS-SN or even d7-LR-PC-SN compared to d2-LR-PC-SN. This is perhaps unsurprising considering our evidence that leucoreduction reduced levels of many potential BRMs in the PC supernatant pools, and the threshold hypothesis for TRALI development42. This hypothesis proposed that neutrophil activation resulting from interactions between patient and blood component factors must surpass a threshold for TRALI to develop42. In our study, if we assume that, despite the slight change in LPS-infusion protocol, the effect of LPS as a first hit was similar to the previous model24, then the reduced potential BRM content resulting from leucoreduction, would mean that it was less likely that this threshold would be surpassed by the transfusion of pooled leucoreduced PC supernatant, even in the increased day 7 storage group. Our finding that leucoreduced PC supernatant posed a low TRALI risk is supported by a study that found that autologous leucoreduced aphaeresis PC transfusion, regardless of storage duration, did not induce TRALI in LPS-treated human volunteers58. Nonetheless, variations in BRM levels between donors and/or individual PC units and how this might impact the risk of BRM-mediated TRALI is an additional consideration worthy of further research.

Despite the lack of association between leucoreduced PC storage duration and TRALI development observed in our model, several sheep did develop TRALI. In the context of the threshold hypothesis for TRALI development42, we therefore sought to understand what recipient factors might have predisposed these sheep to TRALI development. We investigated the responses following LPS-infusion and observed increases in PAP, and WBC count, as well as a smaller increase in plasma IL-10 in sheep that developed TRALI compared to sheep that did not. Pulmonary arterial hypertension and pulmonary neutrophil sequestration are observed with LPS-infusion26, so the higher PAP and WBC counts associated with TRALI development might represent an increased susceptibility of these sheep to LPS. Pulmonary arterial hypertension was also associated with anti-MHC class I mAb-mediated TRALI in mice, and its prevention via inhibition of TRPC6 calcium channels present on periarteriolar smooth muscle cells protected mice from TRALI59. Tregs release IL-10 and their depletion was shown to reduce IL-10 concentrations and make mice susceptible to anti-MHC class I mAb-mediated TRALI development44. Lower plasma IL-10 concentrations have also been reported in patients that developed TRALI45,46, while administration of recombinant IL-10 to mice was protective against anti-MHC class I mAb-mediated TRALI development44. Accordingly, we observed decreased plasma IL-10 concentrations in response to LPS in sheep that developed TRALI compared to sheep that did not. This is an interesting finding, as it suggests that IL-10’s protective mechanism applies to both antibody and BRM-mediated TRALI. Future studies could administer IL-10 to sheep, either prior to the development of TRALI or following the onset of symptoms, to further investigate its potential as a treatment for TRALI. However, to do so, the sheep model would need to be re-optimised to increase the incidence of TRALI compared to what was observed in the present study. The threshold hypothesis42 suggests that this might be achieved by increasing the severity of the response to LPS and/or increasing the dose of BRMs transfused such (e.g. by increasing the PC supernatant transfusion volume from 10 to 20%).

We also sought to understand the changes associated with TRALI development. As would be expected given that pulmonary oedema was part of the TRALI definition and a component of the total lung injury score, we observed increases in both measures in sheep that developed TRALI compared to those that did not. Similarly, as would be expected since several of the sheep that developed TRALI had worsening of pre-existing hypoxaemia rather than new hypoxaemia, we did not observe any difference in P/F ratios between sheep that developed TRALI compared to those that did not. Interestingly, the only other differences observed were in MAP and cardiac output. Sheep that developed TRALI appeared to be more volume-dependent than sheep that did not develop TRALI as their MAP both increased during the transfusion and decreased after the transfusion to a greater extent. We had previously reported hypotension that appeared to not be associated with the development of TRALI in sheep after treatment with LPS and transfusion with d5-NLR-PC-SN24. In contrast, sheep treated with LPS and transfused with pooled supernatant from d42 human non-leucoreduced red cells had a higher MAP than those treated with LPS and transfused with pooled SN from d1 human NLR red cells. Thus, the observed hypotension and possible volume-dependence may be specific only to this model. In the present study, we observed that sheep that developed TRALI had a lower cardiac output than sheep that did not. While we did not previously observe any differences in cardiac output in our NLR-PC-SN study24, in our non-leucoreduced red cell supernatant study we also noted a decreased cardiac output in LPS-treated sheep transfused with d42 pooled supernatant compared to d1 pooled SN. Further studies, including with leucoreduced human red cells, would help better understand this observation.

In conclusion, we report evidence of reduced concentrations of several potential BRMs in leucoreduced PC SN compared to non-leucoreduced PC supernatant. This was associated with a lower incidence of BRM-mediated TRALI compared to our previous study using non-leucoreduced PC supernatant24. We also provide further evidence that IL-10 may play a protective role against TRALI development.
