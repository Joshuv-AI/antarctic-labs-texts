Challenging norms: the history and future of physicians with disabilities in Canada
Authors: Kendra Hebert
Published: 2026

A century ago, the prospect of a person with a disability becoming a physician in Canada would have been dismissed outright. Medical schools emphasized physical endurance, dexterity, and the ability to perform demanding tasks without error.1 These expectations were rooted in a deep-seated understanding of what a physician should be: a body that appeared strong, efficient, and unencumbered. Applicants whose bodies did not meet the ideal of the perfect physician — such as those with missing limbs, impaired vision, or hearing loss — were commonly excluded under the guise of professional unfitness.1,2 These assumptions were reinforced by laws in provinces such as Alberta and British Columbia and that explicitly linked social worth to physical fitness, legitimizing discrimination under the pretext of scientific and moral progress.3,4 Such policies were not fringe expressions of prejudice but instead were embedded within mainstream social, medical, and political views.4 Physicians were central actors in the implementation of sterilization laws against those with disabilities, such as the Alberta Sexual Sterilization Act (1927 to 1972), positioning eugenics as a rational and preventive health strategy.3,4 The message delivered to people with disabilities was clear: if your body could not conform, you did not belong in medicine in Canada.

Public attitudes began to shift during and after World War II, when thousands of veterans returned from combat with new physical disabilities. In response, rehabilitation programs and rehabilitation medicine as a formal discipline expanded rapidly, emphasizing restored function, independence, and reintegration into society.5 Still, as public programs sought to reframe disability and embrace rehabilitation, and resilience and adaptability were celebrated, medicine remained largely inaccessible to people with disabilities. The profession interpreted disability primarily as a problem to be solved rather than as a natural form of diversity.

During the postwar era, stories of physicians with disabilities in Canada were framed as extraordinary triumphs. When a person with a disability did manage to enter or succeed in medicine, the narrative focused on their ability to overcome their disability, reinforcing the belief that disability was inherently incompatible with medical practice.5 The systemic barriers that kept most people with disabilities out of medicine were rarely questioned. Instead, these physicians were held up as inspirational exceptions, rather than exemplifying a profession capable of accommodating diverse bodies. An example of such an exceptional physician is Dr. Albin T. Jousse, who was born with syringomyelia and served as medical director at Lyndhurst Lodge, Canada’s first facility for people with spinal cord injuries, from 1945 to 1975.6

Social movements in the 1960s and 1970s — including civil rights activism, feminist advocacy, and disability rights campaigns — began to challenge the assumption that people with disabilities had to either hide or make up for their differences to achieve equality.7,8 Activists increasingly rejected charitable and medicalized understandings of disability, instead demanding community living, access to education, equity of employment, and political self-representation.8 They argued against institutionalization and segregation, and reframed disability not as personal tragedy but as a social and political identity that is shaped by exclusionary structures.8 This shift toward a social model of disability exposed discrimination embedded within professional institutions, including health care and education.8 Legislative reforms followed, including the Canadian Human Rights Act (1977), which provided formal protections against discrimination in employment and education. These developments set the stage for greater participation of people with disabilities, yet medicine lagged.

During this period, many Canadian medical schools established detailed technical standards specifying the physical, sensory, and communication abilities that medical students were expected to possess. 9 These standards embedded ableism into admissions criteria and sent a clear message that the ideal medical student remained normatively able-bodied.

Research conducted in the 1990s and early 2000s documented the tangible barriers faced by medical students and residents with disabilities across Canada. They encountered inaccessible classrooms, hospital environments, and laboratories; inconsistent and burdensome accommodation processes; and a pervasive culture that discouraged disclosure. 10,11 Clinical rotations, call schedules, and assessments were typically designed under the assumption that all learners could perform tasks without modification. Students with invisible disabilities often refrained from seeking accommodations in fear of stigma, negative evaluations, and damage to their future career prospects.10 The hidden curriculum, the unwritten cultural norms about who belongs in medicine and what a “competent” physician looks like, reinforced these pressures.11 Success for trainees with disabilities required persistence and resilience, as well as repeated demonstrations of competence within a system not built for them.

Despite these barriers, students and physicians with disabilities persisted. Advocacy from within and outside the medical profession began to reshape culture and policy. Physicians with disabilities bring unique forms of expertise to patient care, particularly for patients with disabilities.9 Having lived experience as both health care users and providers, many physicians with disabilities are aware of accessibility needs, barriers to care, and the realities of navigating the health care system. These insights enhance patient-centred care and improve outcomes.10 The presence of physicians with disabilities challenges previous assumptions about competence and shows that ability is not a single trait but a combination of skills, knowledge, adaptability, and judgment.9

The historical exclusion of physicians with disabilities in Canada has intersected with other forms of marginalization. Prospective medical students who identify as women, Indigenous, or racialized, and those with socioeconomic disadvantages face compounded barriers.12 Gender, disability, race, and social class cannot be disentangled when considering advantages or disadvantages in medical admissions and training; these identities often shape each other, reinforcing patterns of exclusion. The exclusion of learners with disabilities is part of a broader history in which medicine has privileged certain bodies and perspectives, while marginalizing others.2,7

Recently, many Canadian medical schools have taken meaningful steps toward inclusion. Several institutions have revised previous rigid technical standards, shifting from lists of unaided physical abilities to broad assessments of total competency, and explicitly acknowledging the legitimacy of accommodations and assistive technologies.9 Medical curricula have been changed and now frequently include content on disability awareness, accessibility, and anti-ableism. Accommodation processes have become more transparent and less adversarial, reducing the burden on students with disabilities.9 Accommodations can be as diverse as the disabilities they are designed to support, with examples being extra time on examinations, private testing environments, assistive technology, service animals, flexible schedules, specialized clinical placements, peer support, and faculty support. Furthermore, mentorship programs and social groups for medical trainees with disabilities have grown to create a community in a profession where these trainees have often felt isolated.6

Yet cultural change continues to lag. Many medical students with disabilities describe persistent pressure to “perform normalcy,” conceal accommodations, or avoid appearing different from their able-bodied peers during their training.10 This shows that the most persistent barriers are often based on attitudes. Inclusion remains an aspiration, rather than a fully realized practice, and embedded norms continue to influence how competence is defined and tested.

Nevertheless, the benefits of inclusion extend beyond trainees with disabilities themselves. When the medical profession embraces accessibility and diversity, all patients benefit. Physicians trained in inclusive environments develop a more nuanced understanding of patient experience, more empathetic communication skills, and increased awareness of structural barriers to care.9 Accessible environments benefit peers, staff, and patients alike. A profession that welcomes diverse minds and bodies becomes more reflective of, and responsive to, the population it serves.

The future of inclusion of people with disabilities in Canadian medicine requires more than retrofitted buildings and revised technical standards. It requires redefining what it takes to be a physician. This involves questioning long-standing assumptions and ensuring that disabled voices are included in the development of educational policies and environments.

Physicians with disabilities have long contributed to medicine, often in systems that were not designed for them. Their persistence and innovation demonstrate that the profession can, and must, adapt to reflect the diversity of the society it serves. By recognizing its history of exclusion, Canadian medicine can transform a legacy of ableism into one of equity and progress.
