Intercellular mechanical communication drives the directional migration of Jurkat T immune cells
Authors: Hongjie Liu, Qingyu Zhang, Jia Guo, Linyan Chen, Bo Li, Mingxing Ouyang, Linhong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although the mechanisms by which chemokines regulate immune responses have been extensively elucidated, the contributions of biophysical mechanical cues to the modulation of immune cell migratory behaviors remain largely unexplored. In this study, we employed a modular co-culture system in which suspension Jurkat T lymphocytes (with RAW264.7 macrophages serving as a positive migratory control) were paired with two force-generating cell types—human airway smooth muscle cells (hASMCs) and human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells—cultured on type I collagen hydrogels to investigate the effects of intercellular mechanical signals on immune cell migration. Jurkat T cells exhibited directional migration toward force-generating cells on a 2D hydrogel surface, with migratory trajectories consistently oriented toward these cells. Glutaraldehyde-mediated crosslinking of collagen or collagen supplemented with Matrigel, which disrupted cell–cell mechanical interactions, significantly impaired the directional migration of Jurkat T cells. Similar impairment was observed following inhibition of hASMC contractility or culture of force-generating cells on a polystyrene surface, further confirming the role of mechanotaxis in this migratory phenotype. Notably, Jurkat T cells exhibited greater migratory efficiency toward hASMCs than toward A549 cells, a phenomenon likely attributable to cell type-specific intrinsic mechanical properties. Furthermore, 3D culture, in which cells were sandwiched between collagen layers, significantly attenuated the directional migration of Jurkat T cells toward both force-generating cell types. Collectively, these results demonstrate that T cell mechanotaxis is modulated by the intrinsic mechanical phenotype of force-generating cells and the dimensionality of the extracellular matrix microenvironment. These findings indicate that biophysical mechanical cues, independent of yet complementary to chemokine gradients, are potent regulators of directional T cell migration, providing new insights into the emerging field of immunomechanics.

Introduction

Cellular mechanical communication has emerged as a key research frontier, encompassing cell–cell mechanical interactions, cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) mechanical crosstalk, and mechanosensitive channel signaling1, 2, 3. These processes regulate fundamental immune functions and disease progression. At the immune synapse, T cells bind to intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) on target cells through lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), thereby transmitting mechanical signals that enhance immune responses.4 Increased ECM stiffness inhibits the Hippo pathway through mechanical tension, thereby promoting the nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ and the expression of fibrotic or tumorigenic genes.5 In addition, the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 converts mechanical forces into biochemical signals that promote tumor angiogenesis.6 Collectively, these mechanotransduction pathways play critical roles in mediating cellular mechanical communication.

Chemokines are key regulators of immune responses. According to the prevailing paradigm, the directional accumulation of immune cells primarily depends on chemical factors. C-C motif chemokine ligands 19 and 21 (CCL19 and CCL21) contribute to immune surveillance by directing T cell migration to lymph nodes through their shared receptor CCR7.7 At sites of inflammation, LFA-1 mediates T cell adhesion to endothelial cells and subsequent crawling along the vascular endothelium, thereby facilitating transendothelial migration.8 In addition, CX3CR1, a receptor for the chemokine C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CX3CL1), plays an important role in regulating inflammatory responses, including monocyte homeostasis and macrophage phenotype and function.9

Mounting evidence indicates that immune cell functions are also regulated by mechanical cues. Fluid shear stress enhances the cytotoxic activity of NK cells against circulating tumor cells through NKG2D-dependent mechanosensation.10 CD8+ T cells sense substrate stiffness via Piezo1, and excessive ECM stiffness promotes CD8+ T cell exhaustion.11 Memory B cells exhibit high sensitivity to mechanical forces, whereas naive B cells display a threshold-dependent mechanosensory response that relies on myosin IIA and integrins.12 Collectively, these findings highlight that immune cell behavior is regulated by both chemical and mechanical signals.

Despite advances in cancer immunotherapy, the mechanistic basis underlying force-modulated immune responses remains poorly understood. Piezo1 deficiency alleviates lung inflammation in bacterial infection models.13 Immune cells exhibit varying degrees of activation in response to distinct mechanical stimuli.14 Certain immune cell populations are particularly sensitive to mechanical stimuli and ECM stiffness, thereby influencing immune responses.15 Furthermore, mechanical cancer therapies, such as focused ultrasound, induce immune activation through both thermal and mechanical effects, enhancing vascular permeability, promoting T cell infiltration, and suppressing tumor growth.16 Engineered ultrasound-responsive T cells further highlight the translational potential of mechanoimmunotherapy.17

Macrophages play a central role in immune responses by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) that recruit immune cells to sites of inflammation.18 Integrin-mediated adhesion to the ECM initiates signaling pathways that regulate macrophage activation.19 Increased ECM stiffness leads to upregulation of Piezo1 expression and activation of the mechanotransduction effector YAP, thereby regulating macrophage polarization.20 In addition, macrophages can track cancer cells through collagen-transmitted mechanical signals, likely because cancer cells generate these cues by remodeling the ECM.21 However, whether mechanical signals can direct the migration of circulating immune cells remains unclear.

In this study, we employed a collagen hydrogel-based model to investigate the effects of mechanical cues generated by force-producing cells on the directional migration of suspension immune cells. Human airway smooth muscle cells (hASMCs) and human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells were used as force-generating cell types, with Jurkat T cells serving as the primary experimental model and muRAW264.7 murine macrophages serving as a positive migratory control. We demonstrate that mechanical cues, in addition to the canonical chemotactic factors that drive immune cell migration, effectively guide the directional migration of T cells. These findings provide new insights beyond the chemokine-centric model of immune cell migration and offer a theoretical framework for integrating mechanical regulation into immune responses.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

hASMCs, Jurkat T cells, A549 cells, and RAW264.7 cells were purchased from BeiNa Biology. All cell types were cultured in media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. hASMCs were maintained in low-glucose DMEM, Jurkat T cells in RPMI 1640, A549 cells in F-12, and RAW264.7 cells in high-glucose DMEM. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Jurkat T cells were maintained on a mini-shaker (approximately 20 rpm) inside the incubator to support suspension growth.

All cell culture media, FBS, Lipofectamine 3000, and Opti-MEM were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Rat-tail type I collagen was procured from Advanced Biomatrix, Matrigel™ from BD Biosciences (CA, USA), and cytochalasin D (Cyto D) from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA).

Construction of a hydrogel model for two-cell-type mechanical interactions

To establish the experimental model, hASMCs were used as the force-generating cells, and Jurkat T cells served as the experimental immune cells. hASMCs are larger than Jurkat T cells, and the latter were further stained with the live-cell dye CellTracker Red to facilitate distinguishment of cell types during microscopic imaging. Unless otherwise specified, all procedures were performed on ice. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molds were prepared by thoroughly mixing the two liquid components of the Sylgard 184 kit (Dow Corning) at a 10:1 mass ratio in a Petri dish, followed by curing at 70°C. A PDMS mold (inner diameter ∼1 cm; outer diameter ∼1.5 cm; thickness ∼2 mm) was affixed to the glass bottom of a confocal dish. Rat-tail type I collagen (3.9 mg/mL) was mixed with a neutralizing buffer at a 9:1 ratio and subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 1.0 mg/mL with phosphate buffeted saline (PBS). The prepared collagen solution was dispensed into the PDMS mold using a 10-μL pipette and spread evenly until the entire inner surface of the mold was uniformly coated. The hydrogel was then incubated at 37°C for 15 min to allow complete gelation.

Jurkat T cells were stained with CellTracker Red as follows. Briefly, 3 mL of the suspension culture was centrifuged, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of serum-free RPMI 1640 medium containing 5 μL of CellTracker Red. The cells were then incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Following centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium at a final concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL. hASMCs were detached by trypsinization and diluted to 1 × 105 cells/mL. Subsequently, 20 μL of the hASMC suspension was evenly seeded onto the collagen gel within the PDMS ring (∼2500 cells/cm2), after which the dish was placed in a six-well holder (Carl Zeiss) and incubated for 1 h to allow cell attachment. Next, 20 μL of the stained Jurkat T cell suspension (∼12,500 cells/cm2) was added and incubated for an additional 1 h. A 1:1 mixture of RPMI 1640 medium and low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was prepared for the co-culture of hASMCs and Jurkat T cells. Subsequently, 2 mL of the mixed medium was gently added along the edge of the dish to avoid disturbing the cells, after which the samples were subjected to time-lapse imaging using a live-cell imaging workstation. The cell seeding density was optimized so that only a few force-generating cells were present within each imaging field under a 20× objective.

Four cell interaction pairs were evaluated: Jurkat T cells + hASMCs, Jurkat T cells + A549, RAW264.7 macrophages + hASMCs, and RAW264.7 macrophages + A549 cells, following the same preparation procedure described above. As inert negative controls, magnetic beads (∼5 μm in diameter) were added at a similar density in place of the immune cells. For the 3D collagen embedding experiments, an additional layer of collagen hydrogel was overlaid onto the 2D culture model to create a sandwich-like configuration. Specifically, collagen solution (1.0 mg/mL) was applied over the collagen hydrogel containing the pre-seeded force-generating cells (hASMCs or A549 cells) and immune cells, followed by incubation at 37°C for 15 min to allow gelation before the addition of culture medium. This configuration helped maintain both cell types at the same interface during live-cell imaging, thereby facilitating accurate tracking of cell migration.

Glutaraldehyde crosslinking of the hydrogel

Glutaraldehyde crosslinking of the collagen hydrogel reduces the transmission of traction forces through the matrix and serves as a negative control condition in this study. Briefly, the 1.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogel was treated with 0.05% glutaraldehyde solution for 15 min and subsequently neutralized with Tris-HCl solution for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBS (5 min per wash). Subsequent procedures for seeding the two cell types were identical to those described above.

Alternatively, to mimic in vivo basement membrane matrices, experiments were performed using a hydrogel consisting of 50% Matrigel (∼5 mg/mL) supplemented with 1.0 mg/mL type I collagen. Compared with the untreated control condition, the Matrigel + COL hydrogel was either crosslinked using 0.05% glutaraldehyde, or the force-generating cells (hASMCs) were pretreated with Cyto D (1.0 μg/mL) for 1 h to inhibit cellular contractility. hASMCs and Jurkat T cells were also co-cultured directly on a polystyrene tissue culture surface as a control condition lacking traction force transmission.

Live-cell imaging

Time-lapse imaging was performed using a Zeiss Observer.Z1 live-cell imaging workstation equipped with a temperature- and CO2-controlled chamber (37°C, 5% CO2). Cell culture dishes were placed in a six-well holder within the chamber. Images were acquired at 30-min intervals over a period of 36 h. Red-stained Jurkat T cells were visualized using the rhodamine fluorescence channel.

Cell trajectory tracking and analysis

Time-lapse image sequences were imported into ImageJ for cell trajectory analysis. Image calibration was performed using Analyze > Set Scale after defining a known distance with the Straight Line tool and then entering the corresponding distance in the Analyze column to convert pixel measurements to physical dimensions. Cell migration was tracked using the Manual Tracking plugin. Individual cell positions were recorded frame by frame using the Add Track function to generate coordinate data and reconstruct migration trajectories. The resulting data files were imported into MATLAB 2015b software to calculate migration rate, velocity, trajectory, and cumulative distance traveled, as previously described.22 Specifically, a circular region with a diameter of 300 μm (ROI) was centered on each force-generating cell. When a force-generating cell migrated during the imaging period, the center of the circular region was updated accordingly to maintain alignment with the moving cell. Immune cells entering the circle were identified based on their migration trajectories. To calculate the percentage of attracted cells, all immune cells that entered the circle within the 36-h observation period were manually counted as the denominator. The number of cells that migrated toward the force source cells was counted as the numerator. The percentage of attracted cells was then calculated by dividing the numerator by the denominator.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism and Origin 2020. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results

Inert magnetic beads were included as a negative control to distinguish passive displacement resulting from collagen deformation from active cell migration. Cell migration was analyzed within a 300-μm circular region centered on each force-generating cell. Quantitative analysis showed that Jurkat T cells exhibited directional migration toward force-generating cells on 2D collagen hydrogels, similar to RAW264.7 macrophages (Figs. 1D and 2D). Time-lapse recordings (Movies 1 and 2) further visualized the directional migration trajectories of Jurkat T cells toward force-generating hASMCs or A549 cells on the 2D collagen hydrogels. Because the force-generating cells also migrated during the imaging period, the calculated trajectories of Jurkat T cells reflected both individual Jurkat cell movement and the coordinated movement resulting from displacement of the force-generating cells.

Quantitative analysis further showed that more than 60% of Jurkat T cells and RAW264.7 macrophages migrated toward force-generating cells after entering the 300-μm ROI, whereas fewer than 20% of magnetic beads showed directional movement (Figs. 1E and 2E). Time-lapse recordings (Movies 3 and 4) further showed that magnetic beads displayed minimal movement toward either hASMCs or A549 cells on the 2D collagen hydrogels. Higher-resolution recordings and images (Movie 5 and Fig. 1F) further revealed collagen matrix deformation and the trajectory of Jurkat T cells toward hASMCs. Taken together, these results demonstrate that Jurkat T cells can migrate toward force-generating cells, suggesting that, in addition to chemical signals, mechanical cues may contribute to the directional migration of circulating immune cells during immune responses.

3D collagen matrices attenuate the directional migration of jurkat T cells

To mimic a 3D ECM microenvironment, immune cells were embedded within collagen hydrogels for migration analysis. The experimental procedure was similar to that described for 2D hydrogels, except that 1.0 mg/mL ungelled collagen solution was overlaid onto a 1.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogel pre-seeded with CellTracker Red-stained immune cells and hASMCs or A549 cells. Following gelation at 37°C for 15 min, culture medium was added for subsequent live-cell imaging.

As shown in Fig. 3, Fig. 4, the directional migration of Jurkat T cells toward force-generating cells was significantly reduced in the 3D collagen matrix, as evidenced by the reduced proportion of migrating cells, migration velocity, and distance traveled. Compared with Jurkat T cells, RAW264.7 macrophages exhibited greater migration efficiency and a higher proportion of migrating cells (Figs. 3D and E and 4D and E). Glutaraldehyde crosslinking of collagen hydrogels attenuates the directional migration of jurkat T cells toward force-generating cells

To determine whether mechanical cues contribute to the directional migration of Jurkat T cells, collagen hydrogels were crosslinked with glutaraldehyde to inhibit matrix-mediated traction force transmission. Briefly, 1.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogels were treated with 0.05% glutaraldehyde for 15 min and subsequently neutralized with Tris-HCl to restore physiological pH. All subsequent procedures were performed as described above. As shown in Fig. 5A–D, glutaraldehyde crosslinking significantly reduced both the directional migration of Jurkat T cells toward force-generating hASMCs and the proportion of cells exhibiting directional migration. Furthermore, Jurkat T cells exhibited shorter migration distances toward hASMCs after glutaraldehyde crosslinking, indicating reduced directional migration efficiency (Fig. 5C). In addition, magnetic bead trajectory analysis showed reduced collagen matrix deformation, indicating inhibited matrix-mediated traction force transmission with glutaraldehyde crosslinking (Fig. 5E). Taken together, these findings indicate that mechanical cues contribute to the directional migration of Jurkat T cells. In vivo, T cells must migrate through basement membranes, which are extracellular matrices underlying vascular and epithelial tissues.25,26 To further evaluate the role of mechanical cues in Jurkat T cell migration, hydrogel matrices containing 50% Matrigel (∼5 mg/mL), which mimic basement membrane components, supplemented with 1.0 mg/mL type I collagen (COL), were used as described in our previous work.22 The Matrigel + COL hydrogels were crosslinked with glutaraldehyde to increase matrix stiffness, whereas hASMC contractility was inhibited by pretreatment with Cyto D. As a negative control in which matrix-mediated traction force transmission was absent, hASMCs and Jurkat T cells were co-cultured directly on polystyrene surfaces. Compared with the untreated control condition, the directional migration of Jurkat T cells was almost completely abolished following glutaraldehyde crosslinking of the hydrogel, Cyto D pretreatment of hASMCs, or co-culture on polystyrene surfaces (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these findings further demonstrate that dynamic matrix mechanics are essential for Jurkat T cell mechanotaxis in this experimental model.

Jurkat T cell migration is influenced by the type of force-generating cells

Different cellular microenvironments may also influence the migratory ability of Jurkat T cells. In the two co-culture models using hASMCs or A549 cells as force-generating cells (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), Jurkat T cells exhibited higher migration efficiency and a greater proportion of migrating cells toward hASMCs than toward A549 cells, as demonstrated by the pairwise comparisons in Fig. 6A–C. This difference may be attributable to differences in the intrinsic mechanical properties of the two cell types. hASMCs express both α-smooth muscle actin and β-actin and therefore produce greater contractile forces than A549 lung epithelial adenocarcinoma cells, which predominantly express β-actin 27,28. To further evaluate this possibility, collagen hydrogel deformation induced by hASMCs and A549 cells was compared. Magnetic beads exhibited greater spatial displacement in response to hASMCs than to A549 cells over an 18-h period (Fig. 6D), indicating stronger contractile forces generated by hASMCs. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that Jurkat T cells undergo directional migration toward force-generating cells on type I collagen hydrogels, similar to macrophages, whereas no such migration was observed in inert beads (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Glutaraldehyde-mediated collagen crosslinking attenuated this mechanotactic response, indicating that dynamic matrix mechanics are required for T cell mechanosensation (Fig. 5). Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the regulation of immune cell migration beyond the conventional chemokine gradient model.

Cell–cell mechanical communication has been shown to direct the migration of adherent macrophages29; however, whether circulating T cells exhibit a similar response to mechanical cues has remained unclear. Our experiments demonstrated that Jurkat T cells migrating on the surface of type I collagen hydrogels exhibited directional migration, with their trajectories consistently oriented toward mechanically active target cells. In contrast, inert magnetic beads did not display directional movement, supporting the conclusion that the observed migration of Jurkat T cells is mediated by cell–cell mechanical interactions. Furthermore, when the collagen network or the mixed Matrigel + COL hydrogel was crosslinked with glutaraldehyde to inhibit long-range matrix-mediated traction force transmission, the directional migration of Jurkat T cells was significantly reduced. Similar reductions were observed following Cyto D pretreatment of hASMCs to inhibit cell contractility or when cells were co-cultured directly on rigid polystyrene surfaces (Fig. 5A–D). Taken together, these results indicate that the mechanical properties of collagen fibers and their ability to transmit traction forces are key determinants of T cell mechanosensing within their microenvironment.

The differential migratory responses of Jurkat T cells toward hASMCs and A549 cells likely reflect distinct mechanical microenvironments, as differences in the contractile activity of these force-generating cell types may differentially influence the mechanical properties of the surrounding collagen matrix. The greater bead displacement observed around hASMCs than around A549 cells (Fig. 6D) indicates stronger cell-generated contraction by hASMCs, providing support for the higher migration rates of Jurkat T cells toward hASMCs than toward A549 cells (Fig. 6A–C). Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed differences in Jurkat T cell migration likely arise from cell-type-specific mechanical signaling patterns.

When both the force-generating cells and immune cells were embedded within the collagen hydrogels, the directional migration of Jurkat T cells toward hASMCs or A549 cells was nearly abolished, whereas RAW264.7 macrophages retained efficient directional migration (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). These findings indicate that the traction forces generated by hASMCs or A549 cells were still transmitted through the collagen hydrogel, as evidenced by the preserved directional migration of RAW264.7 macrophages. In contrast, Jurkat T cells were unable to penetrate through the dense collagen network. These results are consistent with previous observations that chimeric antigen receptor T cells exhibited limited penetration through collagen barriers to reach solid tumors during immunotherapy.30

Our study may advance the field of immunomechanics by highlighting the role of mechanical force sensing in the migration of Jurkat T cells and providing new insights into the mechanisms by which immune cells migrate in response to matrix mechanics in addition to chemotactic cues. However, a dense extracellular microenvironment may impair T cell migration, potentially leading to dysfunctional immune infiltration. A limitation of this study is that Jurkat cells are an immortalized T cell leukemia line; therefore, the findings should be further validated using primary T cells to establish the broader applicability of the proposed mechanisms.

Conclusion

Conclusion Using live-cell imaging and quantitative migration analysis, this study demonstrates that mechanical cues, in addition to canonical chemotactic gradients, can direct Jurkat T cell migration at the single-cell level. Furthermore, Jurkat T cell mechanotaxis is influenced by the type of force-generating cells and the dimensionality of the matrix. These findings provide new insights beyond the chemokine-dominated model of immune cell migration and may help explain impaired immune infiltration in stiff fibrotic and tumor stroma.
