Therapy-induced modulation of cellular response in Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C) - insights into selected immune cells’ markers expression
Authors: Karolina Bukowska-Strakova, Izabela Siemińska, Karolina Leś, Małgorzata Stec, Katarzyna Ptak, Izabela Szymońska, Anna Olchawa-Czech, Nina Mól, Przemysław Błyszczuk, Jarek Baran, Przemko Kwinta, Maciej Siedlar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C) is a rare but serious condition that develops weeks after SARS-CoV-2 exposure and involves systemic inflammation affecting multiple organs. Its exact pathophysiology remains unclear, with proposed mechanisms including cytokine storms, antibody-dependent enhancement, and genetic predispositions. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of immunomodulatory therapy-intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) and glucocorticosteroids (GCS) on peripheral immune cell composition in 24 children with MIS-C. Innate and adaptive immune cells populations were analyzed by flow cytometry before and after treatment (IVIG 2 g/kg; GCS 1–2 mg/kg/day). Prior to therapy, patients showed elevated counts and altered phenotypes of neutrophils, monocytes, and T cells. Following treatment, a reduction in CD64 expression on neutrophils and monocytes was observed, along with decreased numbers of inflammatory cells, indicating an immunomodulatory effect of IVIG and GCS. An increase in CD56 expression on monocytes suggested additional phenotypic changes within this compartment. These findings highlight CD64 as a marker of inflammatory activation in MIS-C and demonstrate that therapy reduces its expression, potentially reflecting diminished inflammation. Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has triggered one of the most significant pandemics of our time, presenting a wide range of clinical manifestations, including Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C). This condition typically occurs in children, 3 to 6 weeks after a SARS-CoV-2 infection or following contact with an infected individual1. The precise pathophysiology of MIS-C remains elusive, with several theories proposed but none definitively proven. One prominent hypothesis posits a delayed cytokine storm, in which SARS-CoV-2 suppresses type I and type III interferons, thereby delaying the surge of previously inhibited cytokines and triggering an exaggerated immune response2. This cytokine-driven hypothesis is supported by studies highlighting elevated cytokine levels in MIS-C3. Another theory suggests antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), where antibodies generated from the initial infection might strengthen the immune response upon re-exposure, with MIS-C possibly representing a post-infectious, immune-mediated reaction, driven by non-neutralizing IgG antibodies2. A third theory proposes a genetic predisposition, noting that children of Asian and African descent in Europe and South America may be more susceptible to severe disease due to a higher density of ACE2 receptors, which facilitate viral entry into lung cells. Additionally, defects in IFN genes or interferon-stimulated genes have been associated with a more severe course of infection, highlighting the importance of functional IFN signaling in mitigating the severity of COVID-19 (coronavirus SARS-CoV-2)2,4.

Emerging evidence also points to disruptions in both the innate immune response and cellular immunity in MIS-C5. This has spurred investigations into how these immune pathways are affected by treatment, particularly the use of intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG). Initially developed for treating immunodeficient patients, IVIG are now widely used in the treatment of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, e.g. primary immune thrombocytopenia and Kawasaki disease. IVIG-derived antibodies affect immune responses by blocking Fc receptors on phagocytic cells, neutralizing autoantibodies, inhibiting their production by B lymphocytes, altering cytokine production by monocytes and macrophages, and influencing T lymphocyte activity. IVIG have also been shown to reduce the non-classical population of monocytes6 and the ratio of CD4 to CD8 lymphocytes, suggesting their potential immunomodulatory effects7.

Despite its widespread use, the effectiveness of IVIG in treating children with MIS-C is still being studied in ongoing clinical trials8. While numerous studies have explored cellular immune responses in COVID-19 patients, many have not considered treatment as a key variable affecting the outcomes. This underscores the need for further research into the impact of IVIG and other therapies on immune regulation in MIS-C. Also, GCS are potent immunomodulatory drugs, with strong anti-inflammatory effects through suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-1β and TNF), reducing activation and migration of immune cells, and thereby limiting the cytokine storm and multi-organ inflammation9.

In this context, our objective was to better understand the immunomodulatory effects of IVIG and GCS therapies, focusing on changes in both the number and phenotype of various immune cells, indirectly reflecting the functional status of the immune system. By examining how IVIG and GCS interfere with the immune response in MIS-C, we aim to provide insights that could guide more effective and targeted treatment strategies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for affected children.

Materials and methods

Patients

In this study, 24 children diagnosed with MIS-C were enrolled, and a healthy control group of 20 children matched by age (median: (interquartile range- IQR): 6.729 (2.75–10) versus 6.5 (2.8–10.3) years; p = 0.545) and sex; female/male: 14/10 versus 11/9; p = 1.0 was recruited at the Krakow University Children’s Hospital. All patients had a confirmed history of prior SARS-CoV-2 infection or exposure, based on PCR and/or serological testing and clinical history.

The majority of children with MIS-C (83.3%, 20 out of 24) had a mild clinical course (fever, polymorphous rash, non-exudative conjunctivitis, and mucosal changes such as strawberry tongue or lip fissures, hand or foot swelling and gastrointestinal symptoms) while 16.7% (4 out of 24) experienced a severe course with cardiovascular complications. Whole blood samples (2 mL) were collected in EDTA anticoagulant from the patients at two time points: upon hospital admission (before treatment) and 7–10 days after administration of IVIG (2 g/kg body weigh) and GCS administration (methyleprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day). Patients with severe cardiac manifestations were initially managed with intravenous fluid resuscitation as first-line treatment for systemic hypotension. In cases of inadequate response, intravenous milrinone infusion was initiated. Only one patient required transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) and treatment with vasoactive agents. All patients received antiplatelet therapy with low-dose aspirin (3–5 mg/kg/day). In cases of severe cardiac dysfunction or elevated D-dimer levels, additional anticoagulation therapy was administered (n = 6). All patients showed clear clinical improvement, including resolution of fever and systemic symptoms, and were discharged without signs of active inflammation at the time of post-treatment sampling. Given that IVIG and glucocorticosteroids were administered concurrently in all patients, the observed immunological changes should be interpreted as a combined effect of immunomodulatory therapy rather than attributed to a specific agent.

The patients’ and participants parents or legal guardians gave their informed consent for children participation in the study, and the study protocol was approved by the local Bioethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University (nr 1072.6120.360.2020). All research methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations, and consents were obtained from all patients’ and participants’ legal guardians.

As part of routine assessments, patients underwent measurements of CRP concentration (Cobas b 101 system) and complete blood count (CBC) (Sysmex VN1000), allowing for the calculation of the total cell count per microliter of blood. The discrepancy in the number of patients in some determinations (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes and monocytes count; n = 8 for healthy controls) is due to the lack of CBC, which is the basis for obtaining absolute values for some parameters.

Flow cytometry

Whole blood (100 µl) was stained with the set of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) listed in Table 1 (Supplementary data) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. After 20 min incubation in 4 °C incubation, lysing buffer (BD Biosciences) was used for red blood cell lysis and cells were washed with PBS and suspended in 0.2 ml PBS. To determine the level of non-specific antibody staining and cell autofluorescence, the fluorescence minus one (FMO) control samples were incubated in parallel. The samples were analyzed on the FACS CantoX flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Immunocytometry Systems, San Jose, CA) using FACSDiva 8.1 (BD Biosciences). All values are presented as percentage of nucleated cells (NC), cells positive for staining with SYTOTM41 (Invitrogen, Eugene, OR). The expression of the examined markers was evaluated based on the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Two panels of antibodies described in Table S1 were prepared to analyze individual cell populations; antibodies were used according to the manufacturer’s recommendation.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). As all variables demonstrated non-parametric distribution, only non-parametric tests were applied. For comparisons between two independent groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. For multiple group comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric equivalent of ANOVA) was applied. For paired data (e.g., before and after treatment), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. All results are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Changes in basic inflammatory markers

Initially, the total white blood cell count (WBC) was assessed. The results indicate that the total number of WBC in the patients was slightly higher than in the control group and further increased after therapy with IVIG/GCS (Fig. 1a).

White blood cell count (WBC) (a) and CRP level (b) before and after IVIG/GCS administration in MIS-C patients compared to healthy control (CTR); * p<0.05, ** p<0.01, *** p<0.001, **** p<0.00001.

The C-reactive protein levels reached elevated concentrations in patients before treatment (approximately 151 mg/L), whereas after IVIG/GCS administration, CRP levels declined to approximately 10 mg/L, indicating a resolution of inflammation (Fig. 1b).

Granulocyte profile assessment

Before therapy the total number of neutrophils significantly exceeded those of the control group. IVIG/GCS administration led to a slight, nonsignificant decrease in cell number (Fig. 2a). Moreover, immature neutrophils (CD64+ CD16−) absolute number, showed an increase in the patients prior to treatment, in contrast to healthy controls (Fig. 2b). After IVIG/GCS treatment, an even greater increase in the absolute number of immature neutrophils was observed (Fig. 2b).

Differences in neutrophils absolute numbers and phenotype expression before and after IVIG/GCS administration among the studied groups. p<0.05, ** p<0.01, *** p<0.001. a)number of neutrophils before and after IVIG/GCS administration in patients with MIS-C compared to healthy control (CTR). b)number of immature neutrophils before and IVIG/GCS administration in patients with MIS-C compared to healthy control (CTR). c)CD16 expression presented as mean fluorescence intensively (MFI) in neutrophils before and after IVIG/GCS administration in patients with MIS-C compared to healthy control (CTR). d)CD64 expression presented as MFI on neutrophils before and after IVIG/GCS administration in patients with MIS-C compared to healthy control (CTR).

In the subsequent analysis, the expression levels of CD16 and CD64 on neutrophils were assessed. CD16 serves as a maturation marker, as its low expression is characteristic for immature neutrophils. In contrast, CD64 expression on neutrophils is related to their activation and widely recognized as a marker of systemic infection10. The expression of CD16 on the neutrophil surface in patients before treatment closely resembled that of the control group, whereas IVIG and GCS administration resulted in a slight decrease in CD16 expression (Fig. 2c). Conversely, the expression of CD64 (a high-affinity receptor for Fc fragment of IgG), was notably elevated in the patients before therapy compared to the control group. However, following IVIG/GCS treatment, a significant reduction in activation status (CD64 expression level evaluated as MFI) was observed (Fig. 2d).

The number of eosinophils remained consistent across both the study and control group, with no noticeable impact from IVIG and GCS administration on their levels (Fig. S2a). In contrast, patients treated with IVIG and GCS exhibited a significantly higher number of basophils (Fig. S2b).

Monocyte populations and their phenotypical changes

Monocytes were subdivided into three subpopulations: classical monocytes (CD64++ CD16−), intermediate monocytes (CD64++CD16+), and non-classical monocytes (CD64+ CD16++) each with distinct roles in immune responses, such as inflammation, phagocytosis, tissue surveillance, and cytokine production. We observed a significant increase in the absolute number of monocytes after IVIG/GCS treatment, which was also seen with respect to the classical monocytes subset. However, in the case of non-classical monocytes, a decrease in their absolute number was noted after therapy. (Fig. 3a).

Differences in monocyte absolute number and phenotype between the studied groups and their changes after IVIG/GCS administration. p<0.05, ** p<0.01, *** p<0.001. a)Absolute number of monocytes and their subpopulations (classical, intermediate and non-classical) before and after IVIG/GCS administration in MIS-C patients compared to healthy control (CTR). b)CD4 expression (MFI) on monocytes and their subpopulations (classical, intermediate and non-classical) before and after IVIG/GCS administration in MIS-C patients compared to healthy control (CTR). c)CD56 expression (MFI) on monocytes and their subpopulations (classical, intermediate and non-classical) before and after IVIG/GCS administration in MIS-C patients compared to healthy control (CTR). d)CD64 expression (MFI) on monocytes and their subpopulations (classical, intermediate and non-classical) before and after IVIG/GCS administration in MIS-C patients compared to healthy control (CTR).

Subsequently, three individual subpopulations of monocytes were analyzed by flow cytometry to assess the expression of CD4, CD56, and CD64 molecules on their surface (measured by MFI), selected as activation markers. The expression of the CD4 molecule on the surface of all monocyte subpopulations, except the non-classical one, was lower in patients before therapy compared to both controls and MIS-C after therapy. IVIG/GCS treatment caused alignment to control levels (Fig. 3b). CD56 expression increased after IVIG/GCS administration across all monocyte subpopulations with no difference between healthy controls and patients before therapy (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, CD64 expression were notably higher on patients’ monocytes across all subpopulations before therapy. However, IVIG/GCS administration induced a decline in CD64 expression in all examined subpopulations (Fig. 3d).

Changes in lymphocyte and NK cell subpopulations

T lymphocytes were identified as CD3+, the various stages of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ lymphocyte differentiation were analyzed, focusing on naive, central memory (CM), effector memory (EM), and terminally differentiated effector memory (TEMRA) populations, which were identified based on the differential expression of CD45RA, CD45RO, and CCR7 markers: naive (CD45RA⁺CCR7⁺), CM (CD45RO⁺CCR7⁺), EM (CD45RO⁺CCR7⁻), and TEMRA (CD45RA⁺CCR7⁻). Among CD4⁺ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs) were identified as CD3⁺ CD4⁺ CD25⁺ CD127⁻. Natural killer (NK) cells were categorized into two subsets: CD56++CD16- and CD16+CD56+.

The absolute number of lymphocytes in patients before treatment was slightly lower compared to the control group, with a notable increase observed after IVIG/GSC administration (Fig. S3a). Similar results were obtained for the percent values of lymphocytes, with a significant decrease in patients before therapy (Fig. S3b). Analogous observations were made for the absolute number of Tregs characterized as CD3+CD4+ CD25+ and CD127-/low, reflecting the quantification of CD4+ cells. The absolute number of Tregs was significantly lower in the group of patients before therapy than in the healthy control group, whereas their proportion (a percent value) was significantly higher. However, in terms of both percentages and absolute numbers, IVIG/GCS administration resulted in a significant increase in Tregs, exceeding even the levels observed in the control group (Fig. S3c, d).

In the context of CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocyte differentiation stages, no differences were found in the level and absolute number of CD4+ T cells between the patients and the control. Similarly, no significant changes were observed after IVIG/GCS administration, except for a decrease in the level of TEMRA CD4+ cells (Fig. S3e-h).

For CD8+ naive T cells, patients exhibited a higher percentage of these cells before and after IVIG/GCS administration, compared to the control group (Fig. S3j). In the case of central memory cells, their percent value was lower in patients, especially in the group before IVIG/GCS administration, (Fig. S3j). For effector memory T cells, no differences or changes in their level after IVIG/GCS were observed (Fig. S3k). Similarly to CD4+, for CD8+ TEMRA T cells we noticed a decrease in the percentage of these cells following IVIG/GCS administration (Fig. S3l).

Regarding NK cells, known as large granular lymphocytes (LGL), their absolute number initially decreased in patients, but returned to normal levels after therapy (Fig. S4a). However, the patients, particularly after therapy, showed a decreased percentage of NK cells (Fig. S4b). Additionally, we distinguished two main subpopulations of NK cells: CD56++CD16− NK cells and CD56+CD16++ NK cells. The percentage of CD56++CD16− NK cells was slightly decreased in patients in comparison to the control group, but after therapy, their level increased (Fig. S4c). Similar, but slightly more pronounced, changes were observed for CD56+ CD16++ NK cells (Fig. S4d).

Subsequently, the expression levels of CD16 and CD56 were compared on NK cell subpopulations. The expression of the CD16 molecule on CD56+ CD16++ NK cells was slightly higher in patients however, under the influence of IVIG/GCS, CD16 expression decreased, reaching values lower than those observed in the control group (Fig. S4e). In contrast, the expression of CD56 on CD56+ CD16++ cells was initially higher than in the control group, and after IVIG treatment, it slightly decreased to levels statistically similar to those of the control group (Fig. S4f). The frequency of CD19⁺ B cells was increased in MIS-C patients compared to healthy controls, but no significant changes were observed following IVIG/GCS treatment Fig. S6.

Discussion

One dominant theory regarding MIS-C suggests a disruption in both innate and adaptive immune responses5. This study aimed to investigate this phenomenon, with a particular focus on the immune cells, especially monocytes and neutrophils, with a focus on selected molecules, such as CD4, CD16, CD64, and CD56, and assessed the impact of IVIG/GCS on these parameters. The selection of these markers was based on their established roles in delineating distinct immune cell subsets and their functional states. While CD4 is primarily known as a co-receptor on helper T lymphocytes, its expression has also been described on monocytes and macrophages. Increased CD4 expression on monocytes has been observed in inflammatory conditions and could reflect an activated state or altered immunoregulatory function11. CD16 (FcγRIII) and CD64 (FcγRI) are receptors for Fc fragments of IgG, implicated in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis, particularly on monocytes and neutrophils. Whereas CD56 is a marker used to identify NK cell subsets. These markers were selected because previous studies have suggested abnormalities in the function of NK cells and T lymphocytes12.

Previous studies in children with MIS-C have rarely reported significant changes in total blood count, however, when present, these changes are most often associated with leukocytosis accompanied by lymphopenia13. In our patients, we also observed a slight increase in WBC count, which became more pronounced after therapy. However, leukocyte levels may vary depending on the stage of inflammation and the effects of anti-inflammatory treatment. In this context, GCS have a well-established stimulatory effect on leukocytosis14 whereas IVIG in proinflammatory diseases, e.g. Kawasaki syndrome, causes a decrease in WBC count15. Therefore, the increase in WBC count observed after therapy should be interpreted with caution and may reflect treatment effects rather than disease-related mechanisms.

Neutrophil count appears to be a reliable marker of COVID-19 severity in both adults and children, with elevated levels particularly notable in intensive care unit patients16. In our cohort, neutrophil numbers were elevated before therapy and declined after IVIG, resembling the pattern observed in Kawasaki disease30 and contrasting with the known neutrophil increases induced by GCS17. Regarding the qualitative analysis of granulocyte populations, we observed a pronounced upregulation of CD64 expression (measured by MFI) on neutrophils, indicating their activated status. This pattern reflects observations in Kawasaki syndrome. In contrast, the increase in neutrophil count, which is indicative of cellular activation18 decreased following IVIG/GCS therapy. In sepsis, CD64 level decreases within 48 h after the stimulation is withdrawn19. This suggests that in MIS-C, despite the absence of persistent antigen stimulation, the immune system remains activated. However, the observed decrease in surface CD64 expression may not solely reflect a reduction in receptor synthesis. CD64 is known to undergo ligand-induced internalization after IgG binding, which can transiently lower its detectable surface levels despite preserved or even increased total cellular CD64. Therefore, both regulatory down-modulation of inflammatory signaling and receptor internalization could contribute to the observed decrease in CD64. Eosinophil levels remained stable in our cohort consistent with the limited data available on MIS-C20.

Regarding monocytes, we observed a slight decrease in monocyte counts in MIS-C patients, consistent with previous studies18. Following IVIG and GCS therapy, the absolute number of monocytes increased, mainly due to the expansion of the classical monocyte subset, while the number of intermediate and non-classical subsets declined. This shift may reflect suppression of cytokine-driven monocyte activation in MIS-C. Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF and GM-CSF, which characterize the acute phase of MIS-C, promote activation and differentiation of monocytes toward inflammatory phenotypes3. Rapid decline in the concentration after treatment may therefore shift the circulating monocyte compartment toward classical monocytes and reduce pro-inflammatory subsets.

The observed changes in monocyte subsets may reflect both disease-related immune activation and the effects of immunomodulatory therapy. Non-classical monocytes are recognized as a major source of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF, and their expansion has been associated with systemic inflammation. In this context, their increased activity in MIS-C may contribute to the hyperinflammatory phenotype of the disease21,25.

The reduction in non-classical monocytes following IVIG/GCS therapy may be explained by several mechanisms. Glucocorticosteroids have been shown to selectively reduce circulating non-classical and intermediate monocyte subsets, likely through redistribution, apoptosis, and inhibition of differentiation toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes22. In parallel, IVIG exerts immunomodulatory effects on monocytes, including downregulation of Fc receptor–mediated activation and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production, which may further limit the expansion of these subsets6,26.

Consequently, the relative increase in classical monocytes observed after treatment likely reflects a shift toward a less differentiated, less inflammatory phenotype rather than true expansion, consistent with resolution of systemic inflammation and restoration of immune homeostasis.

Given reports suggesting that SARS-CoV-2 may bind to CD4, potentially promoting its interaction with ACE2 and subsequent cell infection, it may also contribute to reduced monocyte levels through cell destruction27. Thus, we assessed the expression of CD4 on monocytes and observed an intriguing pattern - the MIS-C patients showed reduced CD4 expression, similar to adult COVID-19 cases28,29.

Another qualitative feature assessed in this study within the monocyte population was the expression of CD56. CD56 expression is known to increase in monocytes as a consequence of immune system activation30,31. In our study, we observed a significant increase in CD56 expression across all monocyte subpopulations after therapy, surpassing levels observed in monocytes from healthy children. Previous studies have identified a distinct population of CD14⁺CD56⁺ peripheral blood monocytes capable of producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and stimulating T-cell proliferation, suggesting that these cells may play a role in shaping adaptive immune responses during inflammatory conditions32.

Although the functional significance of CD56⁺ monocytes in MIS-C remains unclear, studies in other conditions suggest that CD56⁺ monocytes may represent a more activated subset with enhanced antigen-presenting capacity and the potential to differentiate toward dendritic-like cells32,33. Therefore, the increase in CD56 expression observed after therapy may reflect a shift toward a functionally altered monocyte phenotype during the resolution phase of MIS-C.

Previous reports examining changes in gene or protein expression following IVIG administration did not include analyses of CD56 expression on monocytes34, or focused only on CD56 expression on NK cells35, making our findings the first to describe an increased expression of CD56 monocyte population following IVIG and GCS treatment.

Regarding the role of CD14⁺+CD56⁺ monocytes, it has been proposed that they may serve as precursors to dendritic cells36. Notably, treating immunodeficient patients with immunoglobulins has been shown to stimulate dendritic cell maturation. However, high-dose immunoglobulin administration (2 g/kg body weight), as used in inflammatory diseases, e.g. MIS-C, has been associated with the inhibition of dendritic cell maturation and function37. Therefore, the significant changes in CD56 expression on monocytes following IVIG/GCS treatment warrant further investigations.

Lastly, we confirmed elevated CD64 expression on monocytes in MIS-C, which decreased significantly after therapy. Increased CD64 expression on monocytes is a typical characteristic of monocyte activation in inflammatory conditions38. A recent study also reported similar changes in monocytes among children with MIS-C39. The observed decrease after IVIG/GCS administration is a well-documented phenomenon in the literature, resulting from the capture and internalization of immune complexes by CD64 on the monocyte surface40.

Although NK cells are reduced in both conditions, they exhibit signs of phenotypic differentiation. In MIS-C specifically, patients show a decreased number of CD56low NK cells12. In our study, NK cell counts declined before therapy, while CD56 and CD16 expression were increased within the CD56⁺CD16⁺/⁺⁺ NK subset at baseline. Following IVIG/GCS treatment, CD16 expression decreased, consistent with prior reports of IVIG-mediated reduction of NK cell cytotoxic activity, particularly in CD56dim NK cells35,41. ADCC is a primary mechanism through which NK cells exert their cytotoxic effects, relying heavily on the interaction between the CD16 receptor on NK cells and the Fc fragment of antibodies41.

Unlike COVID-19, MIS-C patients in our cohort did not show a decrease in CD4⁺ T-cell numbers14, suggesting that global CD4⁺ lymphopenia is not a key feature of MIS-C-associated immune dysregulation. Regulatory T cells, which were reduced before treatment, increased following IVIG/GCS therapy, consistent with IVIG and IVIG/GCS-associated Treg expansion42. Pro-inflammatory cytokines promote T-cell activation and differentiation toward effector phenotypes3. Treatment also reduced the number of CD8⁺ TEMRA cells, supporting previous findings on IVIG-mediated inhibition of CD8⁺ T-cell responses. In contrast, the proportion of effector memory cells remained relatively stable, possibly reflecting a balance between differentiation of central memory cells and migration of activated effector cells to inflamed tissues. These alterations in T-cell differentiation may be related to the pro-inflammatory cytokine milieu typical of MIS-C, including cytokines such as IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ and IL-18, as well as chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10, which promote T-cell activation and effector differentiation. The decline of these mediators following IVIG/GCS therapy may therefore contribute to the reduction of highly differentiated effector populations such as TEMRA cells and support restoration of immune homeostasis.

Taken together, our findings indicate that MIS-C is characterized by activation of innate immune cells, particularly neutrophils and classical monocytes, whereas CD8⁺ T cells predominantly displayed a memory phenotype rather than overt activation. Elevated CD64 expression on neutrophils and monocytes was observed in patients and significantly decreased after IVIG/GCS therapy, which likely reflects attenuation of inflammatory activation. This observation is consistent with previous reports in other inflammatory conditions, including Kawasaki disease, where IVIG treatment has been associated with CD64 downregulation and suppression of excessive inflammation40,41. Given that neutrophil CD64 has been identified as a sensitive marker of systemic inflammation and infection43 our findings further support its potential utility as an indicator of disease activity and treatment response in MIS-C. However, as IVIG and glucocorticosteroids were administered concurrently in our cohort, the individual contribution of each therapy cannot be distinguished. Notably, we also observed a therapy-associated increase in CD56⁺ monocytes, suggesting additional phenotypic changes within the monocyte compartment that may reflect immune reprogramming during the resolution phase potentially associated with enhanced antigen-presenting capacity or differentiation toward dendritic-like phenotypes, as suggested in other inflammatory settings33. Taken together, these findings support the potential utility of CD64 as a marker of systemic inflammation, and its modulation may reflect changes in inflammatory activity and treatment response in MIS-C; however, further studies are required to determine its predictive or prognostic value and clinical applicability.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small cohort size, the exploratory nature of the analyses without correction for multiple comparisons, and the predominance of mild clinical cases may limit the strength and generalizability of our findings. This limitation increases the risk of type I error and should be taken into account when interpreting statistically significant findings. In addition, the overlapping use of IVIG and glucocorticosteroids may confound the interpretation of treatment-related immune changes. Therefore, further studies in larger cohorts, including functional analyses of immune cells, are required to better define the immunological mechanisms underlying MIS-C and the effects of immunomodulatory therapy.
