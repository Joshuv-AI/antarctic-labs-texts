Production systems and breeding practices of Begaria cattle breed as input for a community-based breeding program
Authors: Tesfalem Aseged, Tesfaye Getachew, Sandip Banerjee, Teklewold Belayhun, Awoke Melak, Aweke Engdawork, Esho Kefyalew, Abraham Assefa, Abebe Hailu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to describe the production systems and breeding practices of Begaria cattle breeds as a resource for community-based conservation and breeding program. A semi-structured questionnaire and focus group discussions were employed to identify and describe the breeding practice of the farmer. Three kebeles: Almahal, Fanguso, and Mankush were considered for the study. The data were analyzed using R software. Cattle were kept for a variety of reasons, including milk, breeding, meat, cash, and saving. It was typical throughout the study area to see herd mixing and keeping together (4.7 ± 0.33 herds on average), herd movement during the dry season, and breeding bull sharing (1.9 ± 1.55 shared bulls). During the summer season, cattle rearing in the areas was hindered by feed and water shortages. The breed's major reproduction constraints were abortion (24 %), repeat breeding (19), and calf mortality (14). The primary reason for keeping Begaria cattle was for milk (29 %), cash (26), meat (22), and breeding (0.22). The average age at first calving (AFC), calving interval (CI) and milk yield per day of the breed were 41.5 ± 1.63, 16.0 ± 0.43 months, and 3.2 ± 0.12 L respectively. The average age of breeding bull selection was 3 ± 0.1 years, and it was based on several criteria, including their phenotype, growth, and white coat colour. Based on the current findings, it is recommended that a multi-trait selection program be designed with full stakeholder participation.Keywords: Begaria cattle, Bull sharing, Herd mixing and mobility, Production constraints, Reproduction performance

Introduction

It has been reported in a study by the United Nations that the human population at the global scale is expected to escalate to more than nine billion heads by 2050 [1]. The population growth will enviably lead to stress in agronomic and livestock output. This is because there will be a shrinkage of agricultural land and also other allied natural resources [2,3]. Studies by Thornton [4] have also indicated that anthropogenic and climatic factors lead to newly emerging diseases. This is all the more important in developing countries, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa [4]. Consequently, the widening gap between the demand and supply of livestock products can be narrowed by increasing productivity. Therefore, sustainable productivity improvement of farm animal genetic resources needs an understanding of the prevailing husbandry practice and the genetic potential of the livestock at hand [5,6].

The livestock in the tropics are not specialized and has to survive under the vagaries of nature besides the diseases and parasites which prevail in the area [2,7,8]. Likewise, cattle in Ethiopia play multifarious roles viz. meat, milk, hide (the skins/pelt of large animals), dung cakes (made from the dung, used as a heat source and as a building material), draught power, manure, social prestige, source of savings, and risk buffering for the resource-poor people [[9], [10], [11]].

Genetic diversity is expected in countries where specific adaptive attributes are vital and where several constraints are making natural selection more important than the selection made by the breeders [12]. Traditionally, livestock breeders only improve upon that livestock surviving the natural selection process [13,14]. Therefore, the adaptation of the native livestock is appropriate to the environment where they are raised [15]. The rich diversity in indigenous cattle's adaptive and productive traits is crucially important for the sustainable development of cattle production and the unknown future [16]. However, currently, there is a known loss of diversity in animal genetic resources [10,11]. Many factors contribute to this, including a failure to value local breeds, unsuitable breed substitution, a change to profit-oriented agriculture, the development of inappropriate policies and negligence of the breed found in the lowlands, poor organization, a lack of good description and complete consideration of production systems when designing a breeding program [[17], [18], [19]].

As a result, indigenous cattle breeds, particularly those from lowland areas, are currently underutilized, due to limited infrastructure and the prejudice of others against the breed (mainly regarded as slow-growing and low-yielding) [[20], [21], [22]]. However, as mentioned above, lowland cattle are known to be adapted to harsh environments and these characteristics need to be maintained to ensure sustainable production, respond to climate change, and for future policy and regulatory actions, particularly in lowland areas.

Begaria cattle breed (Fig. 1) belongs to the Bos indicus species and is one among 28 registered indigenous cattle breeds of Ethiopia, adapted to climate stressful conditions of the warmer area of the Benishangul Gumuz region [23]. The breed has special preference by the community for its thick milk, fast growth of birth, short calving interval, and adaptation to feed and water shortage [23]. However, the expensiveness of the breed and low cost of neighbouring cattle, illegal live animal trade, inbreeding, and changes in the production system are the most important threats to the genetic diversity of the breed [23,24]. Therefore, sustainable use of this breed is unlikely unless non-genetic factors influencing cattle production, such as farmer breeding practices and production challenges are addressed. As a result, this study aims to describe the production systems and breeding practices of Begaria cattle breed to contribute to the community-based conservation and breeding program with the following hypothesis: a) traditional husbandry practices can make a significant contribution to the breed's long-term improvement and conservation, and b) there are critical production and reproduction constraints.

Materials and methods

The study area

The study was conducted in Guba district of Metekel zone of the Benishangul Gumuz Regional State of Western Ethiopia. The district was selected purposively as this was situated in the breeding tract of the Begaria cattle. Based on the representativeness of the breed and suitability for data collection and recording, three kebeles (lowest political administrative units) namely; Mankush, Almahal, and Fanguso were selected.

The climate in the Guba district can be classified as arid and semi-arid. Agro-ecologically the study area is found in the lowlands within an altitude ranging from 531 m.a.s.l. at Fanguso to 860 m.a.s.l. at Mankush having flat to rolling topography. The district receives an average annual precipitation of 965 mm. The highest temperature recorded at Mankush reaches 31 °C in April with a monthly mean of 26 °C calculated based on 20 years of data (1986–2006). The rainy season in the area is from June to September, which forms an uni-modal character [25].

The breed

Begaria cattle breed has been raised in the area since time immemorial [23]. The total population of Begaria cattle breed can be estimated at around 17, 450 heads which were distributed across nine adjacent kebeles of the district (source: District agricultural office).

The majority of the breed was characterized by a large body length of an average of 111.5 cm, long legs, narrow horn spacing (<30 cm), flat facial profile, and straight-edged ears with lateral orientation plus erect service-thoracically positioned humps predominantly consisting of small humps. The breed has a uniform coat colour pattern, with white and grey body colour. Pigmentations are common on the muzzle, eyelid, and hoof of cattle.

Methods of data collection

Production system data

A total of 75 households (25 for each kebele) who keep Begaria cattle breed were selected randomly and interviewed by trained interviewers who speak the local language and had experience in similar studies [5]. As Amharic was widely spoken in the area, the semi-structured questionnaire was translated from English to Amharic. Sometimes for the need for translation, the translator was used to avoid the chance of any misunderstanding and confusion.

During the household interview data pertaining to household demography, the education status of the respondent's animal feeding and allied practices was also included. The study also encompassed the traditional selection practices as had been practiced by the farmers in the area and the farmer trait of interest was also studied. The survey also included the owner recall method for reproductive performance data.

The Focus Group Discussion (FGD) was a key component of the study and was designed to provide a platform for local stakeholders to openly discuss and provide in-depth information about the cattle production system of the region, such as the types of animals raised, general animal husbandry practices, and different constraints and opportunities within the prevailing production system. It was held in each kebele and comprised of local leaders, extension agents, traders, and cattle owners. The information collected from the FGD was then used to develop a comprehensive understanding of the prevailing production system and the challenges and opportunities within it and ultimately, to identify potential areas for improvement and sustainable utilization of the resource.

Herd mobility and herding

Distance, the herd traveled (km) and duration of stay (months) in difficult times were asked. The way of herding before, after and during movement was also inquired, i.e., herded separately, or mixed with another herd. Were all mixed herds in permanent place moved and herded together or part of mixed herds in permanent place moved and herded together or mixed with other new herds or herds kept separately during movement were interviewed. The mean number of mixed herds was also incurred and calculated. The source of the breeding bull, selection criteria, ownership and sharing practice were also cross-examined, were the same breeding bulls as in the permanent place or new bulls from the new mixed herds?

Data preparation and statistical analysis

Qualitative data

Qualitative data like the challenge of cattle production in the study area, reproduction problems, the purpose of keeping Begaria cattle, ways of herding and cattle keeping during movement, mating practice during movement and source of breeding bull during movement collected through questionnaires were analyzed by using Rcmdr package version 2.6–0 [26] in R software version 3.6.1 [27]. A contingency table was used to produce cross-tabulations. Fisher's exact test was employed to test independence between kebeles and different qualitative variables like trait preferences, bull-sharing activity and herd mixing.

Quantitative data

Quantitative data like Begaria cattle reproduction performance, the breed size and the number of herd mixing and age of selection for breeding collected through questionnaires were analyzed using analysis of variance in R software [27] fitting kebele (Almahal, Mankush and Fanguso) as an independent fixed factor. Results and discussions

General household information

The household characteristics of the respondents are presented in Table 1. Most of the respondents in the study areas were males (68 %). This may be ascribed to the social hierarchy in the tropics where the female members of the family are not encouraged to divulge family information to strangers [29,30]. However, this gives rise to gender inequality and hence needs to be corrected to obtain holistic information regarding the production system of the cattle breed. Most of the respondents belonged to the working age category where they are physically strong to carry out the suggested interventions alongside experiences in the field of livestock rearing. However, the study indicates that most of half of the respondents are illiterate (45.3 %), which is an impediment to modern livestock husbandry [31]. In such cases, the authorities must devise methods for the effective flow of information as well as the recording of observations. Thus, the development agents need to be innovative enough to devise methodologies for imparting training to less educated livestock keepers. The study further indicates that most of the respondents were farmers (88 %) which indicates that they are well-versed in the husbandry practices associated with the cattle in question. The average family size (7 ± 0.41 individuals) as reported in the study is also in close accordance with the findings of [29]. The family size is however higher than those of the national average of 4.80 individuals [32]. The higher the family size means more are working hands for agrarian activities, however, the already family resources are further compromised and hence the per unit availability of already stretched resources is at stake. Therefore, the family size and the conservation of farm animal genetic resources go in tandem [21,33].

Herd structure and cattle holding per household

The average number of cattle holding per household is presented in Table 2. There were differences (p < 0.05) in herd size across the study areas and a higher value was observed in Almahal than in others. The findings also show that the chances of crossings were higher in Almahal when compared to the other locations, as the numbers of highland zebu and Felata cows were higher out there. Therefore, to reduce further genetic dilution, restrictions need to be imposed on the importation of highland zebu and exotic cattle breeds. The other means of arresting genetic dilution is through niche marketing of the products obtained from Begaria cattle [19]. In doing so, the keepers will be lured to keep the Begaria cattle and get better returns from the sale of milk and beef [34]. In the study areas, the number of cows (irrespective of the genotypes) is higher than those of the bulls, which is in accordance with the findings of [6]. However, in doing so there are chances of inbreeding within the herd and it requires its own strategies to deal with it. The optimal utilization of the bulls followed by rotation (among the districts) followed by culling can be one such strategy that can help to keep inbreeding under check [31]. However, all this is possible only if the performance recordings are maintained followed by regular evaluation of the same. Based on the findings, strategies have to be devised and actions and follow-up carried out.

Challenges of cattle production in the study area

Cattle production constraints across various seasons are presented in Table 3. The findings indicate that feed and fodder shortages alongside the shortage of water were predominant in the study areas, especially during the summer months (Tables S1 and S2). In developing countries (and Ethiopia being no exception) livestock keepers face several challenges viz. diseases and parasites and in the absence of good and adequate quality and quantity of feed, the problems manifest further [36]. Hence, the rearers are usually advised to cultivate feed and fodder at their farms, however, in Ethiopia, the average farmland is quite less and is expected to diminish further due to population explosion, under such conditions, farmers usually prioritize the cultivation of food crops; therefore, the development agents should impart training related to conservation of animal feed which is usually abundant in the wet season. The training must focus conversion of crop residues so as to feed them to the cattle during times of feed shortages [36].

Water is the most neglected nutrient especially when it comes to livestock feeding [4]. Good quality water has to be provided in order to ensure proper digestion and assimilation of the consumed feed [37]. Hence, the respondents need to be trained on methods associated with rainwater harvesting and its proper utilization [37]. As indicated in the focus group discussion (FGD), the water in Fanguso area was stagnant and mainly contaminated with thins, poisoned plants, and chemicals for the purpose of fish catching, which is unsafe for the animals to drink. The river found in Almahal area was seasonal and only stayed from June to October and the remaining period would be covered by movement in search of water. During movement, costs related to the establishment of new barns, water holes, and payments for permission were not uncommon. The good opportunity is, in the areas, there is a possibility to get water with 4–5-m depth digging. The study also indicates that during the wet season, lack of market was considered a challenge in Almahal and Mankush with a close index value, while fodder was still a major constraint at Fanguso. This is not due to inadequate availability of feed in the wet season but rather the presence of predators which was reported as the second most ranked constraint with an index value of 0.22 in the area. As a result, farmers never allow sending animals to the denser areas where both the animal feed and predator are likely found. Therefore, feed conservation and using a cut and carrying system would be advisable. As indicated in FGD, the market was a common problem across the study areas. Marketing of livestock has always been a challenge among smallholder farmers as distress selling has been reported from different parts of the country [22,38,39].

During the wet season, most of the agricultural operations take place and farmers are in need of ready cash to procure the agricultural inputs. Therefore, in order to mitigate the problem, there is a need to set up cooperatives in the region alongside microfinance institutions, these institutions can provide soft loans to the livestock rearers alongside ensure that the distressed selling can be stopped and the middlemen cannot exploit the needy farmers. As explained in FGD, most of the livestock are sold based on the eye estimates for their weight and hence weighing scales need to be put into place and should be maintained and calibrated on a regular basis.

The constraints in the wet season too, are related to marketing infrastructure and feed shortages as indicated in the dry season, with marketing problems being considered as the major constraint as discussed for the dry season. Lack of marketing intelligence has been considered one of the major constraints which can also be overcome through the establishment of cooperatives in the area. The cooperatives through the collective participation of its members can purchase feed and fodder, besides establish units for veterinary care and also provide marketing infrastructure for the livestock products [22]. The cooperatives can also be an effective medium for the channelization of government funds and other benefits extended by other allied agencies.

The presence of wildfires and instability were also mentioned as challenges. It contributes to the shortage of animal feed in the area. Fire from October to November is mainly acceptable for the purpose of regeneration of the grass, otherwise unacceptable and unavoidable. Some farmers may initiate the manageable fire by themselves for the purpose of fire break for the upcoming unstoppable fire. Otherwise, others do it illegally.

Reproduction constraints

Reproduction constraints of Begaria cattle breed are presented in Table 4 and Table S3. The study shows that abortion followed by repeat breeding and calf mortality as the major impediments with their respective index value of 0.24, 0.19, and 0.14. The incidences of abortion can be associated with some disorders, due to disease prevalence, poor quality and inadequate quantity of feed and fodder [40]. Poor nutrition often leads to negative energy balance resulting in several constraints like abortion which can be followed by repeat breeding and vice versa [41]. Poor quality of feed and fodder can also lead to high calf mortality as this is also the fallout of inadequate nutrition [36]. The findings from Fanguso show that abortion (0.28) and dystocia (0.21) were the major problems in the area, these observations are in close accordance with the observations of [24]. All the problems as indicated by the respondents are in some way or another associated with a lack of quality feed and fodder shortage which leads to immune compromise and weakness [40]. Therefore, conservation of feed and fodder besides techniques to improve the quality of feed and fodder has to be established so that the general health of the dam is improved and consequently the calves too are strong enough and calf mortality is minimized [36].

Purpose of keeping Begaria cattle breed

The main purpose of rearing Begaria cattle breed is presented in Table 5 and Tables S4 and S5. The results show that most of the bulls are raised for meat, cash, and breeding purposes with close index values followed by draft (0.14), saving (0.11), and wealth (0.08). The finding shows the multipurpose role of the breed. These observations are in close accordance with the findings of Endalew [8], Ftiwi & Tamir [41] for tropical cattle of the low input system. However, Haile [42] has indicated that most of the farmers in Ethiopia rear bovines for cash sales, and the importance of cattle raised for beef are quite low in preference, this may be ascribed to a lack of storage facilities for the carcass [43]. The bulls are also raised for breeding in the study areas which too indicates that there is importance of cattle in the study areas. Therefore community-based breeding should be aimed toward the selection of bulls for meat quality traits as it is expected that these traits have been traditionally selected over the years [23,24].

The cows are primarily raised for milk purposes as is traditionally carried out in most of the developing countries, this may also be because the cows are raised as bull mothers and hence milk is an important component for the development of strong calves at weaning [44,45]. This is alongside the usages of butter (kibe) and cottage cheese (ayeb) which are traditionally part of the Ethiopian cuisine making dairy traits an important component in the selection process of cows [6,15,46]. The other aspect of the selection of the cows is for cash and breeding purpose which is also a prelude to the economy of the cattle and also the milk obtained from them [8].

The purpose of keeping Begaria cattle varied considerably (p < 0.05) among locations for cash and wealth status for both males and females, draft for males, and milk, meat, and saving for female cattle (Table 5). Male Begaria cattle were mainly kept for cash generation in Almahal and Mankush which had higher index values (p < 0.05) compared to Fanguso. This might be due to the relative presence of nearby towns in Almahal and Mankush areas which would encourage the farmer to do cash. Relatively, the larger values given for meat were observed in Mankush and for cash in Almahal for female cattle. These indicate those cash generation by selling live animals directly or through slaughtering is more common in these areas than in others. Having such experience might be a good opportunity for the alternative breeding program that might be designed in the area. Hence surplus and culled animals can be sold easily. Comparatively higher index values for milk were observed in Almahal and Mankush. This indicates milk production is more important for these areas, due to the demand in the small town. However, milk produced in Fanguso is solely used for home consumption in the matter of market.

Rearing males for draft purposes across the study area was not also uncommon. Since traction is a common activity in all areas. However, a relatively higher value was indicated for Almahal and Fanguso, since in Mankush farmers usually use other Highland Zebu cattle for their relatively cheap costs. However, such activities might be resulted in genetic erosion of the breed, unless animals would not use for breeding purposes.

A Relatively higher value for wealth status was observed in Almahal for males and both in Almahal and Fanguso for females. This might be one reason for the large herd size observed in Almahal. Therefore, this showed the higher cultural attachment of the farmer to the breed in these areas. A higher index value for saving was also observed in Fanguso for female cattle. The absence of a bank in the area might be the possible reason. Keeping animal for multiple purposes were also reported in the studies done in pastoral areas of Ethiopia [8].

Begaria cattle (re) production performance

Table 6 shows the productive and reproductive performance of Begaria cattle breed. The average age at sexual maturity (ASM) of the bulls (34.6 ± 0.32 months) and cows (35.2 ± 0.24 months) did not vary across the study areas (p > 0.05). Age at sexual maturity is correlated with the growth of the calves and earlier the ASM is expected to have more numbers of lifetime calves and hence are economically advantageous since bulls can be sold at an early age and also if they are used for a draft purpose, they will be an asset for their rearers [47]. This trait is also correlated with the nutrition received by the cattle and cattle receiving optimum nutrition usually mature early [44]. ASM is more influenced by an animal's body weight than its age [45].

The study further indicates that there were no significant differences (p > 0.05) in age a first calving (AFC) and calving interval (CI) of the cows reared across the study areas with the respective mean values of 41.5 ± 1.63 and 16.0 ± 0.43 months. The AFC and CI of Begaria cattle breed reported here are shorter than from some other dairy-type breeds of the country like Begait [8], Horro [45] and Bonga [47]. AFC is correlated with AFM as animals that mature early and are provided with appropriate management and nutrition are expected to have an early AFC [48]. Similarly, CI is also associated with the nutrition received by the cattle [41] has been reported that CI can be optimized through proper feeding. In cattle, CI is correlated with the lifetime production and productivity of the cattle.

The marketable age (MA) of the bulls varied (P < 0.05) across the study areas which can be related to the types of feed and fodder available in the study areas besides the management of the cattle as a whole. The MA is also correlated with the age at sexual maturity [44].

The estimated average milk yield per day (3.2 ± 0.12 L), did not vary across the study areas and is much higher than the previous study's 1.9 L [24]. The study indicates that Begaria breed has a higher daily milk yield than Begait [8], Horro [45], and Bonga cattle breeds [47] with a value of 2.25, 1.98, and 1.65 L respectively, this may be ascribed to both the genetic makeup of the cattle and also the management received.

The average lactation lengths (LL) of the breed were 2.8 ± 0.09 months which was shorter than many other zebu cattle breeds [24]. Suboptimal LL can lead to a poor weaning weight of the calves and therefore increase the chances of calf mortality as has been reported in the earlier tables (Table 5). The lactation length of the calves can also be improved through optimizing the management of the cows.

The milking frequencies of the cows (1.1 ± 0.04) are lower than those reported by Fasil [24], 1.5 per day. This may be on one hand allowing the adlib suckling of the calves but on the other hand, the overall per-unit productivity will be low [45]. The milking frequency may also be low because of poor milk marketing infrastructure in the area. However, it is always advisable that the calves are provided with milk replacers and provided with milk based on their body weight; such practice will ensure proper development of the calves and also minimize mortality.

The average weaning ages of the animals were 4.4 ± 0.12 months. which is higher than the previous report of 3.8 months [24]. The weaning age, however, is quite early as has been reported in the zebu cattle and early weaning can lead to high mortality of the calves [49,50]. Early weaning can also be correlated with short LL and hence the farmers need to be trained on the use of calf replacers [51]. The number of calves born in the lifetime (10.8 ± 0.20) is quite high and is also higher than the findings of Taye [52]. The higher lifetime numbers of the calves indicate that the reproductive potential of the cows is optimum, which can be ascribed to the genotype by environmental interaction and adaptability of the cows [52]. However, the study further indicates that the numbers of calves weaned are lesser which is also correlated with calf mortality. High calf mortality influences the overall productivity of the cows and lifetime economics [51]. Therefore, use of milk replacers can be used to minimize calf mortality as suggested ahead.

In the study area, the average number of births was 9.6 ± 0.98 calves the proportion of calves surviving to wean was 84.5 ± 2.47 %, the proportion of calves surviving to yearling was 83.4 ± 2.61 %, the number of abortions was 10.2 ± 1.39 %, the number of bull calves, and cow calves’ mortality occurred for the last year were 0.8 ± 0.13 and 0.6 ± 0.11, respectively. The differences in parturition and the number of calves born per cow lifetime might be attributed to the management of the cows and the nutrition obtained throughout the year. The mortality among the bull calves is higher than those of the cow calves, which may be because of sexual dimorphism and nutritional requirements of the bull calves, which are higher than those of cow calves of the same age.

The findings also show that the bulls are selected at a later age (p < 0.05) at Fanguso (3.2 ± 0.10 years) when compared to the other studied areas. This may be ascribed to the fact that the bulls are used for agrarian activities and the draft ability is considered as a selection criterion hence the ages at which the bulls are selected are higher than the other two areas.

Ownership of breeding bull

Table 7 shows the bulls sharing practices of the respondents. Most of the respondents (85.5 %) own breeding bulls which may ensure the cows are bred regularly. This is besides the fact that the bulls are reared for social prestige and also as a means of savings to take care of any unforeseen eventualities. Except for Fanguso most of the respondents rear only one bull which gives rise to inbreeding within the herd. At Fanguso the number of bulls reared is more in numbers because they are also used for agrarian activities and two bulls are required for the ploughing operations [52,53].

Bull sharing practice

The results as indicated in Table 7 show that bull-sharing practices are highly prevalent in Fanguso when compared to the other study areas. These observations are in close accordance with the findings of Dereje [38]. While sharing of bulls among the farmers can help in lowering the instances of inbreeding, however in the absence of records such sharing of bulls can lead to an increase in inbreeding within the herd [54]. However, over time the number of farmers who are willing to share the bulls is expected to increase which too is in close accordance with the findings of Dereje [38]. Findings from Table 7 further indicate that the farmers are desisting the rearing of bulls in most of the places in the tropics due to shortages of manpower and resources. While this is favourable in case there are some community-based breeding programs in place as it will avoid unplanned breeding by the farmers [55]. However, in absence of the such a program, this is expected to increase the rate of inbreeding among the cattle and also spread any undesired genes among the population traders [56].

Number of bulls used for sharing

There was no significant (p > 0.05) difference across the location in the average number of shared bulls and the overall average number (± standard error of the mean) was 1.9 ± 1.55 bulls (Table 7) which is the same as with the overall number of breeding bull owning. This clearly indicated that nearly all breeding bulls owned were also used for sharing purposes within the herd members. This is a good indication of the farmer's interest to improve the breed in cooperation.

There was no significant (p > 0.05) association between locations and the bull to whom shared. Large proportions of respondents (98 %) were sharing bulls with the herd members. This is an important finding in the understanding of the current sire use practices. Therefore, designing a breeding program that might work for herd members would be advisable as many activities basically operate within herd members.

Breeding bull selection criteria

The breeding bull selection criteria of the respondents are presented in Table 8. Breeding bulls are selected based on several criteria viz. their phenotype alongside their growth and white coat colour. As indicated in FGD there is no negotiation for black coat color across the study areas. The preferred colours were mainly white, white, and grey or brown shed and they mainly call this “Woyina Kelem”. However, the reasons for such colour preference were not similar across the study areas. In Almahal the reason was mainly attached to the purity of the breed, while in Fanguso for cultural interest, and in Mankush with the respondent perception as a milk type and for easy observation/to trace out the breed from the deep forest. Physically the bulls have to have long bodies, strong legs, and also strong necks similar observations have also been reported by Fasil [24]. White coat colour is preferred as an adaptive trait among tropical breeds [2]. Studies by Burrow [48] have indicated that white-coated cattle are able to survive better under the tropical summer and also attract fewer ectoparasites [15]. The bulls’ calves which are fast growers are selected because they are genetically superior over the slow growers [57]. Fast growth also indicates that the dam of the calf had a high milk yield which ensured the trait it manifested [58].

The average number of herds mixed together is presented in Table 9. In most of the cases, the cattle were herded together at Fanguso (84 %), which may one-time help in minimizing inbreeding within the herd [38], plus the payment for the hired keeper becomes shared and owners who had not has own breeding bull can get free bull service with the mixed herd members. However, this also has a negative side because the rate of inbreeding may be spread across the herds because a superior bull is expected to have many offspring. In case the bulls have some lethal genes then the gene can be multiplied rapidly within the herd. There was no significant (p > 0.05) difference in the average number of herds mixing across locations and the overall mean (± standard error) was 4.7 ± 0.33 herds.

Herd mobility

Herd mobility, duration, and distance are presented in Table 10. Herd mobility is prevalent across all the study areas. which may be ascribed to the traditional transhumance activities in the areas. This may be because of differences in feed availability across the study areas. The distance traveled is correlated with the feed and water availability and the average distance was 27.8 ± 1.81 km for the duration of 5.7 ± 0.20 months. The study also indicates that all classes of cattle are taken together which is unlike many transhumance systems where the female animals, calves, and old and debilitating animals are not taken away during times of feed and fodder shortages which correlates with dry season [59]. The duration of movement also correlates with the rainfall pattern of the area as has been reported in studies by Zergaw [59]. The study also indicates that there is variation in the duration of transhumance activities with lesser movement (p < 0.05) at Mankush (4.6 ± 1.87 months) while there were no differences in the other two locations. The shortest movements were observed for Fanguso about 20.1 ± 7.41 km. As indicated in FGD, at the time of moving, the owners might be exposed to extra costs, such as the cost for grazing, watering, and new barn establishment.

Cattle keeping during movement

Cattle-keeping activities during movement are presented in Table 11. Many of the respondents move their herds across the place and keep them together (46.3 %) in a place on an annual basis. While the rest of the respondents kept their herd separately, even in a permanent place (38.5 %). The respondents who do not allow their animals to mingle with other cattle may be doing so to keep their animals free from any communicable diseases which they may encounter from other cattle. These findings are in close accordance with the observations of Sun [60] for disease spreading of cattle movement in China. While studies by Dereje [38] have indicated that most of the respondents allow their cattle to move with other herds which they think minimizes the chances of inbreeding in the herd. The study also indicates that the respondents from Mankush are strict when it comes to allowing their herd to mix with newer herds which are however not such strict true in the other two locations. The observations are in close accordance with the findings of Sun [60]. The mixing of the herds in Fanguso and Almahal areas takes place at the watering points. Also moving some divisions of the herds was reported by some respondents of Fanguso and Mankush. As revealed by FGD, the owners differentiate their animals from others mainly by the coat colour, ear notch, and brands on the cattle thighs.

Conclusion

In the study area, cattle were kept for a multipurpose role. The production environment was marked by severe feed and water shortages, which frequently resulted in mobility as a mitigation strategy. The study has also shown that the farmer had a range of traditional systems in animal identification, breeding bull selection, and sharing. This would be an excellent opportunity to hire exceptional bulls in exchange for rapid improvement. The presence of herd mixing and keeping together, mobility, and disease prevalence, all require careful consideration. To lighten the problem of animal feed, farmers should practice feed conservation activities, as plenty of feeds are available during the wet season. With the owner's interest and the breed's ability to contribute more to livelihood income, local and export markets put forward the need for a cattle improvement program to be designed in the area. Based on the current findings, it can be advised that a multi-trait selection program be designed and that full stakeholder participation be taken into account. This is done to sustainably maintain the unique productive and adaptive traits of the breed.

Limitations of the study

Limited access to include a greater number of study sites.
