Reliability and agreement in measurement of cervical spine joint repositioning error in the retraction-protrusion plane
Authors: Peter G Osmotherly, Declan T Spencer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractJoint position error (JPE) occurring with retraction and protrusion movements of the cervical spine remains uninvestigated. This study examined reliability and agreement of JPE measurement in the retraction-protrusion plane and the impact of sagittal displacement on rotation JPE estimates. In a single session repeated measurement study, JPE was assessed in 20 asymptomatic individuals following retraction, protrusion and axial rotation in retracted and protruded positions. Movements were measured three times using an electromagnetic tracking system. Constant, absolute and variable JPE was recorded for each movement relocation. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC2,3), standard error of measurement and minimal detectable change was calculated for the repositioning error of each movement. For constant error, ICC for JPE for retraction was 0.80 and protrusion 0.89. ICCs for relocation in the transverse plane to protrusion or retraction ranged from 0.75 to 0.88. For absolute error, ICC for JPE following retraction was 0.34 and protrusion 0.79. ICCs for relocation in the transverse plane to protrusion or retraction ranged from 0.69 to 0.89. Relocation following protrusion can be measured with moderate reliability. Whilst constant repositioning error for measurement following retraction displays substantial agreement, repositioning absolute error cannot be measured with adequate agreement. No effect on axial rotation JPE due to protrusion/retraction plane positioning was evident.Keywords: Proprioception, Cervical spine, Kinaesthesia, AgreementSubject terms: Medical research, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Cervical spine joint position error (JPE) refers to the error in accurately repositioning the head in relation to the trunk to a starting position after performance of a cervical spine active movement and is considered indicative of proprioceptive ability of the head and neck1. Position sense in the upper cervical spine arises from a highly dense and vast network of proprioceptors found in the cervical spine joints and in muscle spindles of the intrinsic and superficial muscles of the neck2. Joint position error in the cervical spine has been utilised in studies of cervicocephalic kinaesthesia involving the movements of axial rotation1,3–6, lateral flexion4,6 and whole cervical spine flexion and extension1,6. Head translation through protrusion and retraction has been associated with a number of clinical presentations including neck pain, dizziness and balance issues and headache7–10 and has been the subject of clinical and biomechanical research11–13. This movement has been of primary interest to clinicians and researchers exploring upper cervical spine dysfunction.

The concept of agreement addresses the question whether measurements are identical or similar, or the degree to which they differ14 and assesses how close the repeated measurements are by estimating the measurement error in repeated measurements15. Examination of the level of agreement of these measurements has produced varying results, potentially due to variations in methodology and number of trials assessed. Louden et al. reported ICC values of 0.975 to 0.985 for the agreement of JPE measurements of axial rotation and lateral flexion in a combined sample of asymptomatic and neck pain individuals4. Both Swait et al.6 and Jorgensen et al.16 examined cervical flexion, extension, lateral flexion and axial rotation yielding ICCs of 0.73 to 0.84 in asymptomatic adults and 0.05 to 0.66 in a combined pain and asymptomatic sample respectively. In contrast, Kristjansson et al.3 examined the agreement of cervical spine rotation measurement in an asymptomatic population producing ICCs of 0.44. Purckhauer and colleagues17 examined JPE in asymptomatic individuals noting that JPEs may differ between the upper and lower cervical areas after retraction-protrusion movements. No examination of the agreement of the measurement of protrusion and retraction movements have been undertaken and published to date.

In order to accurately ascertain JPE in upper cervical conditions, the ability to determine the agreement of measured JPE in the upper cervical spine needs to be established to permit an understanding of the consistency of repeated measurement and the influence of measurement error14. Further, it has not been investigated whether joint position in the retraction-protrusion plane influences measured joint position error in other planes when the head and neck is removed from a natural neutral posture. The aims of this study were to assess in healthy individuals with asymptomatic necks: (1) estimates of the agreement of measurements when relocating to cervical spine neutral from both active cervicocephalic protrusion and retraction and (2) to determine whether protruded or retracted positions of the upper cervical spine alter the ability to relocate the head to the mid position in the axial plane.

Materials and methods

This observational study was conducted in the School of Health Sciences, The University of Newcastle, Australia. The study received approval from the University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided informed written consent prior to participation, and the study was undertaken in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Potential participants responded to advertisements at The University of Newcastle. All participants provided written informed consent. This convenience sample of participants were consecutively recruited and included in the study if they were over the age of 18 with no history of neck pain in the preceding 12 months. Individuals were excluded if they reported a history of trauma, previous surgery or disease affecting the cervical spine, previously diagnosed inflammatory joint disease, diagnosed congenital abnormalities of the cervical spine or persistent neurological signs (numbness, pins and needles). Using published guidelines18 with 80% power, alpha = 0.05 and three trials per participant with a lower confidence level limit ICC of 0.6, 26 participants were deemed necessary to complete the study.

Procedure

The study design was based upon GRRAS recommendations for reliability and agreement studies19. Participants were assessed on one occasion. Measurements were recorded following a predetermined process. Demographic information collected included age and sex.

For the assessment of cervical JPE, measurements were obtained using a Polhemus Liberty electromagnetic motion tracking system (Polhemus, Colchester, USA). The Polhemus Liberty motion tracking system captures six degrees of freedom data. The setup consisted of a transmitter and three sensors attached to each subject, all hardwired to the systems electronic unit. The transmitter generates a low frequency electromagnetic field, which is detected by the sensors. The systems electronic unit determines the relative orientation and position of the sensors. Static accuracy reported for the Polhemus unit for RMS in X, Y, Z position is 0.76 mm and 0.15° RMS for rotation. Data was collected using a set sampling rate of 240 Hz. No additional filter parameters were used beyond the default mode settings. The transmitter was located at a set distance of 60 cm from the participants’ chair.

Participants were seated in a high-backed chair with arms by their side and feet positioned on the floor. A lightweight cap containing three sensors for the tracking system was fitted on the participant’s head. Sensors were mounted on the crown of the head, the centre of the forehead and over the tragus of the left ear. A blindfold was placed over the subject’s eyes and remained in place during all relocation tests (Fig. 1). Participants were initially asked to perform movements actively through the full range of cervical spine movement to ensure unrestricted pain-free movement, returning each time to their natural head posture. The following relocation tests were then performed (Fig. 2), each participant performing movements in a randomised order predetermined by a random number sequence generator:

All movements were active, i.e. under participant control. All relocation tests were repeated at a self-selected speed and recorded three times consecutively using the Polhemus Liberty electromagnetic tracking system. Prior to each movement, sensors were electronically aligned in orientation and position with the user system coordinates to establish reference angles. This was achieved using the “boresight” function to designate the starting position as the sensor zero orientation. A brief relaxation period was provided between repetitions and movements to minimise any learning effect. No feedback of performance was given during the assessment.

Outcome measures

Joint reposition error was measured and recorded as three variables. Constant error was determined where the amount that the starting position has been over or underestimated during repositioning, i.e. the direction of error is retained2. Absolute error was determined as the difference between the starting position of the head and its location after repositioning by the participant irrespective of direction of error. Variable error, a measure of inconsistency in movements by estimating a person’s variability about the mean value was calculated as the standard deviation of the constant error2,20. The reposition error outcomes assessed included: sagittal plane joint reposition error relative to the NHP, measured in centimetres; axial rotation joint reposition error whilst being in either craniocervical protrusion or retraction measured in degrees.

Data analysis

Output data for each participant was produced in ASCII format and was exported from the tracking system as a CSV file. ICC(2,3) of constant and absolute joint position error from the three trials were estimated using a two-way random effect model with measures of absolute agreement. ICCs were interpreted using the classification system proposed by Shrout; 0.81–1.0 substantial, 0.61–0.8 moderate, 0.41–0.60 fair, 0.11–0.40 slight, 0.00–0.10 virtually none21. Standard error of measurement was calculated for each of the six reposition tests based on the standard formula described by Harvill22. Minimal detectable difference was calculated as 1.96 × SEM x . SPSS Statistics software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NT, USA) was used to analyze the data.

Reliability of JPE measurements of constant error ranged from moderate to substantial. In the sagittal plane, the ICC for relocation from protrusion was estimated to be 0.89 whilst relocation from retraction calculated to be 0.80. The SEM for both relocation manoeuvres was similar being 0.25 cm from protrusion and 0.30 cm from retraction. The MDCs for constant error were 0.69 cm for protrusion and 0.83 cm for retraction. Joint position constant error in the transverse plane ranged from 0.75 to 0.88 degrees with SEM ranging from 0.46 to 0.59 cm.

Within the sagittal plane, reliability of absolute error of cervical spine relocation from retraction was considerably lower than cervical spine protrusion, with an ICC of 0.34 for retraction and 0.79 for protrusion relocation. The SEM for protrusion was 0.26 cm. and 0.22 cm for retraction. The MDC for absolute error repositioning from protrusion was 0.61 cm and from retraction 0.72 cm. In comparison, JPE in the transverse plane was represented by consistent ICC values between 0.69 and 0.89. The SEM for axial rotation error ranges between 0.23 and 0.46 cm.

ICCs for relocation from axial rotation in both protruded and retracted positions was substantial for both constant and absolute error ranging between 0.79 and 0.93 for constant error and 0.83 and 0.97 for absolute error. SEMs ranged between 0.81degrees and 1.32 degrees. Estimates of MDC were from 2.72 to 3.66 degrees for constant error and 2.25 and 2.88 degrees for absolute error.

Discussion

The results obtained from this exploration of joint position error focussed on the upper cervical spine indicate that constant error in repositioning can be measured with moderate to substantial agreement. However, similar levels of agreement are not attained measuring the absolute error of repositioning from retraction in the sagittal plane.

Whilst the SEM values for of absolute agreement for relocation from protrusion and retraction were low and consistent indicating agreement between measures and suggesting the observed measurements are close to the true measurements, the agreement of relocation measurements from a retracted position was considerably less than from protrusion. Moderate agreement was attained in relocating from protrusion whilst the agreement of measures when relocating from retraction could only be considered slight. This contrasts with the corresponding estimate of constant error when relocating from retraction. The substantial magnitude of agreement in constant error indicates consistency of relocation error whether overshooting or undershooting the starting position by individual participants. The direction of error is not taken into account when calculating absolute error resulting in a greater variability of measurements as indicated by the width of the 95% confidence interval and is reflected in the greater MDC derived for relocation from retraction compared to protrusion to NHP which suggests that more variability is present in this measurement, hence reducing agreement for absolute error.

Whilst the constant error for relocation from retraction suggested some overshooting by participants, the magnitude of this observation appears clinically insignificant. Purckhauer et al.17 reported small but consistent upper cervical spine overshooting of the starting position after retraction which they suggested may be attributable to additional stretching of the suboccipital muscles leading to an increased forward movement via the gamma loop. Our findings are consistent with this pattern of error measurement but would have little practical impact on any measurement used clinically.

ICCs for both test–retest axial rotation relocation constant and absolute error demonstrated moderate to substantial reliability. The axial rotation relocation error ICCs provided in similar studies on asymptomatic participants has varied16,23. Our results indicate that axial rotation repositioning error when the cervical spine and head are displaced in the sagittal plane can be measured with moderate agreement. The range between 1.33 and 1.74° for absolute repositioning error and 1.61 to 4.66° for constant repositioning error SEM scores is consistent with prior research4,24–26 and indicates accuracy in this plane of movement. Due to the narrow range of ICC scores obtained when measuring relocation from axial rotation in protrusion and relocation from axial rotation in retraction, there appears to be no effect of sagittal plane positioning on the reliability of measuring joint position error when relocating to the mid position in the axial plane.

Consistent with many previous studies of this region, the current study measured three repetitions of each movement in each measured direction3,4,23,27. Swait et al.6 have suggested that three trials may be insufficient to ensure optimal examination of relocation ability, with five or six trials improving outcome measurement. This has been restated in a recent systematic review suggesting that up to six trials may reduce the impact of outlier results28. Estimates of protrusion and retraction agreement may have thus been improved with a greater number of trials although such a requirement to achieve agreement of measurement may reduce the utility of joint position error testing in the clinical situation. In interpreting the findings of this study, it should be noted that these results pertain to the agreement of measures in an asymptomatic population. Measures in a clinical population of individuals with neck pain may differ and this is yet to be investigated. Indeed, it is possible that either greater or less consistency in measured joint position error could be indicative of pathology in a neck pain population. Furthermore, the clinical applicability of the findings may be limited by the fact that this exploration was performed using a 3D tracking system, an option not available in standard clinics. However, given the accuracy of this system caution in interpretation of retraction joint position error is warranted when measured using less sophisticated methods such as laser tracking. The estimation of MDC values for relocation from retraction and protrusion is novel. Given that MDC is expressed in the same units as the test, this provides better opportunity for interpretation of JPE values in this plane and their translation into clinical practice.

This was the first study to assess the agreement of JPE measurement in the directions of protrusion and retraction. These results indicate that constant relocation error from retraction and protrusion, and absolute relocation error from protrusion can be measured with moderate agreement. However, absolute relocation error from retraction cannot be measured with the same result limiting its utility to meaningfully evaluate proprioceptive deficit or performance. The results of this study may provide a first step to inform future studies which look to utilise the measurement of JPE in protrusion and retraction in clinical conditions such as cervical degeneration, cervicogenic headache, inflammatory disease, pain and dysfunction.

Conclusion

The results obtained in this study demonstrate that relocating to the mid position in the axial plane, whilst in both protrusion and retraction, can be measured with moderate agreement. Relocation error, either constant or absolute, from protrusion to the natural head posture can also be measured with moderate agreement. However, the results indicate that agreement in measuring absolute JPE when relocating from retraction is poor.
