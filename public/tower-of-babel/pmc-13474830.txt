Matters arising: effect modification by chronic kidney disease on renal outcomes in septic shock patients undergoing high-MAP targeting: a secondary analysis of the OPTPRESS trial
Authors: Maajid Mohi Ud Din Malik, Rajesh Prasad Jayaswal
Published: 2026

Okuzawa et al. [1] put a useful question to the OPTPRESS dataset [2]: does pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD) shift the renal response to a high mean arterial pressure (MAP) target in older patients with septic shock? The signal they report, fewer renal replacement therapy-free days (RRT-FD) under high-MAP targeting among patients without CKD (adjusted β − 3.5 days; 95% CI − 5.94 to − 1.11), and no detectable signal in those with CKD, is offered as a hypothesis-generating interaction (p = 0.040). The question is worth asking. The data, in our reading, ask more than they can answer.

First, the CKD subgroup is too small for the inference being drawn. The stratum holds 70 patients (32 control, 38 high-target); the adjusted within-stratum estimate spans nearly 13 days (95% CI − 4.34 to + 8.35). A confidence interval that wide is compatible with substantial benefit, substantial harm, or no effect. The authors describe this as "no significant association" [1]. A more accurate reading is that the analysis was unable to rule anything out. The interaction itself is then carried by a tight, clearly negative estimate in the non-CKD stratum (n = 361) set against a noisy, near-null estimate in the CKD stratum. This configuration is what one would expect from an underpowered subgroup, but imprecision in the CKD stratum does not exclude a true differential effect; it limits the confidence that can be placed in the estimate. The data are therefore compatible with both a genuine biological interaction and a chance finding, and the wide interval does not adjudicate between them. Applied to the credibility criteria of Sun et al. [3], this analysis would not pass on prespecification, on the magnitude of the interaction, or on independent replication.

Second, CKD ascertainment is too soft to carry a stratification. The authors define CKD as documented clinical history at enrolment, and they explicitly note that the OPTPRESS dataset held no standardised diagnostic criteria, no staging, and no uniformly available pre-admission eGFR. The resulting CKD prevalence of roughly 16% in patients aged ≥ 65 years sits well below KDIGO-defined late-stage CKD prevalence in older populations [4], which generally exceeds 25–30%. Noteworthily, median admission serum creatinine in the so-called non-CKD group runs between 1.49 and 1.65 mg/dL, values that, in many elderly patients, correspond to an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. If a meaningful share of "non-CKD" patients in fact had stage 3 CKD, the contrast on which the interaction depends is being driven partly by misclassification rather than biology, and the direction of bias is non-trivial to predict.

Third, the main result is not as robust to the authors’ own sensitivity analyses as implied in the abstract. There are two specifications of importance here. The two-part hurdle model is statistically the better choice for a bounded, mortality-inflated outcome such as RRT-FD and gives interaction odds ratios with confidence intervals of 0.11–1.18 and 0.43–17.07 which are not significant. The interaction was attenuated by multiple imputation, so that it was no longer statistically significant, as the authors themselves report. A finding that exists only in the complete-case linear regression and disappears under two more conservative specifications should not be called directionally consistent. Sterne et al. provide clear guidance on this [5]: if an effect changes meaningfully with multiple imputation, it is the complete-case estimate that should be treated with suspicion, not the imputed one. The Discussion mentions this in passing. The abstract does not.

Fourth and most consequentially for the interpretation of the paper, MAP target and vasopressor exposure are entangled and cannot be disentangled in the analysis. The authors note that high MAP targets required more norepinephrine and vasopressin in both strata, and then suggest in Discussion that catecholamine toxicity rather than MAP elevation itself may be the problem for the kidneys of non-CKD patients. That is plausible. It is also a different causal claim than that of the abstract. The vasopressor dose delivered during the intervention lies on the causal pathway between the assigned MAP target and the renal outcome: it is a consequence of the higher target and a plausible mediator of its effect, not a classical confounder, and the regression model adjusts only for pre-randomisation norepinephrine. The data do not differentiate between “high MAP harms the kidney” and “the vasopressor load required to achieve a high MAP harms the kidney” without a formal mediation analysis. The latter would be in keeping with the parent OPTPRESS trial which was stopped early for harm overall [2] and the 65 Trial in which permissive vasopressor exposure did not worsen renal outcomes in older patients [6]. The framing the authors then choose, autoregulatory physiology in CKD versus non-CKD, is several steps removed from what was measured.

Here are a few smaller points. The MAKE secondary outcome in non-CKD patients (adjusted OR 1.53; 95% CI 0.94–2.49; p = 0.084) is described as directionally consistent with the primary result. That is fair; what is missing is the corresponding observation, in the abstract, that the secondary outcome did not reach significance. Baseline MAP differs visibly between treatment arms within the CKD stratum (median 55 vs 62 mmHg) despite randomisation, not unexpected at n = 70, but a reason to read within-stratum estimates with extra care. The Background invokes SEPSISPAM [7] as supporting evidence. We would point out that the renal benefit in SEPSISPAM was confined to the chronic-hypertension subgroup of a separate trial; in the present analysis it reads less as a supporting result and more as a contrast that the authors should reconcile with their negative finding in non-CKD patients.

The interaction Okuzawa et al. report may yet prove real. It is also fully compatible with chance, misclassification of the stratifier, the entanglement of MAP target and on-treatment vasopressor exposure, or some combination of the three. Treating it as a hypothesis worth testing in a prospective trial that stratifies on baseline renal function is appropriate. Treating it as evidence that CKD status meaningfully alters the renal response to MAP targeting, which the Conclusion comes close to doing, is more than these data can support.
