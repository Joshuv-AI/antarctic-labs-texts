On Discrete Curvatures of Trees
Sawyer Jack Robertson

Abstract
There are several interweaving notions of discrete curvature on graphs. Many approaches utilize the optimal transportation metric on its probability simplex or the distance matrix of the graph. Since each of these ingredients is well understood when the underlying graph is a tree, in this note, we review existing and obtain new formulas for three different types of curvature on graphs. Along the way we obtain: a comparison result for the curvatures under consideration, a degree-diameter theorem for trees, and a combinatorial identity for certain sums of distances on trees.



Keywords:
curvature on graphs, tree graphs, optimal transportation, graph distance matrix

Introduction

Discrete notions of curvature on graphs have been subject to active research for many years.
A priori
there are deep questions which suggest such a concept would be challenging to construct in general; e.g., whether curvature is a property of each node in a graph, each edge, or both; or, how far from a given node or edge one should study the structure of the ambient graph so as to obtain a suitably local formulation of curvature at each particular place. Despite these challenges, there exist many such formulations which satisfy powerful properties (e.g., eigenvalue estimates, Bonnet-Myers-type theorems). In this article we focus on two families of discrete curvature which are based, respectively, on the optimal transportation metric and graph distance matrices.

In the former case, we consider Ollivier’s Ricci curvature, defined originally for Markov chains on metric spaces
[
11
]
(denoted

Definition

Meanwhile, the last three years have seen the emergence of several new approaches to discrete curvature on graphs which utilize the shortest-path metric (and other metrics, see
[
5

Here, a tree is a finite, simple graph which is connected and acyclic.

Background on graphs and matrices

Let us consider a graph

We denote by

\displaystyle A_{ij}

We denote by

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }e_{j}=(i,\cdot)\\
-1&\text{ if }e_{j}=(\cdot,i)\\

2.3
Distance-based curvatures

In this subsection we will introduce the curvature formulation of Steinerberger, defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Steinerberger curvature
[
15
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be any graph and
i\in V
. Let
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined by the equation

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}=\mathcal{D}^{+}(n\mathbf{1}).

For
i\in V
, we define the
Steinerberger curvature of
G
at
i
, denoted
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}_{i}
, to be given by the
i
-th entry of
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}
.

Ordinarily one must exercise a bit of care in defining
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}
as the solution to the equation
\mathcal{D}\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}=n\mathbf{1}
since this may not in general admit any solution, and when it does, the solution will in general be nonunique. Therefore in this paper we adopt the usage of
\mathcal{D}^{+}
for concreteness. However for trees, the distance matrix
\mathcal{D}
is nonsingular
[
7
]
and thus in fact
\mathcal{D}^{+}=\mathcal{D}^{-1}
, so this does not present any difficulties.

Remark 2.4
.

We remark at this junction that two other variants of curvature on graphs, namely that of Devriendt and Lambiotte
[
5
]
, as well as that of Devriendt, Ottolini, and Steinerberger
[
6
]
, are highly related to the Steinerberger curvature which comes into focus on this article. These notions, up to a scaling factor, are constructed in the same manner as
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}_{i}
except with the usage of the effective resistance matrix
\mathcal{R}
in lieu of the distance matrix
\mathcal{D}
. As it happens, when the underlying graph is a tree, all three of these formulations are identical up to various (positive) scaling factors which depend possibly on the graph and its weights. Therefore, the formulas and comparative results obtained for
\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}_{i}
may be readily extended to these cases as well.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T=(V,E,w)
be a tree and let
i\in V
be fixed. Then we have the following identity:

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j\in V}d_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{vol}}(G)+\sum_{j\in V}d_{ij}|d|_{j}.

To establish this lemma we use a path-counting argument which requires some setup and discussion before we write the main proof. Letting

if

appears in

(we identify only

as the “loop”

is a partially ordered set with maximal element

. Identify each edge
\{j,k\}\in E(T)
with the ordered tuple
(j,k)
where

and write

to denote the set of all such tuples. If we think of

as being embedded in the plane with the root situated at the top and the remainder of the nodes and edges arranged in a downward-oriented fashion away from the root, the relation
j\prec_{i}k
holds if

appears on the branch connecting

to

Corollary 4.2
.

Let

be a weighted tree and

is given by

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mathsf{s}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n(2-|d|_{i})}{{\operatorname{vol}}(G)}=\frac{n(2-|d|_{i})}{\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}}.

Let
i\in V
be fixed. Observe by

that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in V}d_{ij}(2-|d|_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{vol}}(G).

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\kappa=n\mathbf{1}

(15)
