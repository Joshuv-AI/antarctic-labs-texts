Abstract
Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has achieved strong performance in cooperative adversarial tasks. However, most existing methods typically train agents against fixed opponent strategies and rely on such meta-static difficulty conditions, which limits their adaptability to changing environments and often leads to suboptimal policies. Inspired by the success of curriculum learning (CL) in supervised tasks, we propose a dynamic CL framework for MARL that employs an self-adaptive difficulty adjustment mechanism. This mechanism continuously modulates opponent strength based on real-time agent training performance, allowing agents to progressively learn from easier to more challenging scenarios. However, the dynamic nature of CL introduces instability due to nonstationary environments and sparse global rewards. To address this challenge, we develop a Counterfactual Group Relative Policy Advantage ( CGRPA ), which is tightly coupled with the curriculum by providing intrinsic credit signals that reflect each agent’s impact under evolving task demands. CGRPA constructs a counterfactual advantage function that isolates individual contributions within group behavior, facilitating more reliable policy updates throughout the curriculum. CGRPA evaluates each agent’s contribution through constructing counterfactual action advantage function, providing intrinsic rewards that enhance credit assignment and stabilize learning under non-stationary conditions. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method improves both training stability and final performance, achieving competitive results against state-of-the-art methods. The code is available at https://github.com/NICE-HKU/CL2MARL-SMAC .

III-A

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

MARL is essentially a sequential decision-making task under multi-agent cooperative context, which can be formally modeled as a
decentralized partially observable markov decision process
(
Dec-POMDP
)
[
47
]
.
Formally, a Dec-POMDP is defined by the tuple
G=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{U},P,r,\Omega,O,n,\gamma\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{U}
is the action space for each of the
n
agents,
P(s^{\prime}|s,\bm{u}):\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{U}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
is the state transition function,
r(s,\bm{u}):\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the shared reward function,
\Omega
is the observation space with emission probabilities
O(s,a):\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Omega
, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor.
At each timestep
t
, each agent
a\in\{1,...,n\}
selects an action
u^{a}\in\mathcal{U}
, forming a joint action
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
. The environment transitions according to
P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},\bm{u}_{t})
, generating a scalar reward
r_{t}=r(s_{t},\bm{u}_{t})
. Agents receive individual observations
z^{a}_{t}\sim O(s_{t},a)
that may represent partial state information.

Each agent maintains an action-observation history
\tau^{a}_{t}\in\mathcal{T}=(\Omega\times\mathcal{U})^{*}
, which maps to actions through a stochastic policy
\pi^{a}(u^{a}|\tau^{a}):\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{U}\to[0,1]
. The joint policy
\bm{\pi}
induces a centralized action-value function:

Q^{\bm{\pi}}(s_{t},\bm{u}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{s_{t+1:\infty},\bm{u}_{t+1:\infty}}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}r_{t+i}\Big|s_{t},\bm{u}_{t}\right].

(1)

While training can leverage centralized information (full state
s
and all agents’ histories
\bm{\tau}
), execution must be decentralized - each agent’s policy
\pi^{a}
depends only on its local history
\tau^{a}
. This framework subsumes both the fully observable MMDP case (when
O(s,a)=s
) and standard POMDPs (when
n=1
). The key challenge emerges from the exponential growth of joint action space
\mathcal{U}^{n}
and the partial observability constraints during execution.

MARL algorithms are typically categorized into three training paradigms:
centralized training and execution
(
CTCE
)
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
,
decentralized training and execution
(
DTDE
)
[
51
]
, and
centralized training with decentralized execution
(
CTDE
)
[
52
,
53
]
. Among these, CTDE has become predominant due to its superior learning efficiency while maintaining the flexibility of decentralized execution.
The algorithms under CTDE paradigm combine decentralized execution where each agent
a
acts via its own policy
\pi^{a}(u^{a}|\tau^{a})
conditioned on local action-observation histories
\tau^{a}=(z_{0}^{a},u_{0}^{a},...,z_{t}^{a})
, with centralized training that optimizes a joint action-value function
Q_{tot}(\tau,u,s)=g(\{Q^{a}(\tau^{a},u^{a})\}_{a=1}^{n},h(s))
where: (1) individual utilities
Q^{a}
depend only on local
\tau^{a}
, (2) the state encoder
h(s)
integrates global information, and (3) the mixing network
g(\cdot)
enforces consistency between decentralized policies and centralized value estimation through the monotonicity constraint
\partial Q_{tot}/\partial Q^{a}\geq 0

Notable algorithms including VDN

]

III-B

Curriculum Learning (CL)

CL

[
16
]
is a novel continuous learning paradigm originally proposed by Bengio et al.
[
56
]
for supervised learning tasks in CV and NLP. This paradigm progressively exposes a machine learning model to data samples of increasing complexity, mimicking the structured learning process in human education. Formally, in supervised training, given a training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\lVert\mathcal{D}\rVert}
where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is an input (e.g., an image or text sequence),
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is its corresponding label, and
\lVert\mathcal{D}\rVert
denotes the sample size, CL introduces a
difficulty measure

d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
that quantifies the complexity of each training example.

The learning process is governed by a
curriculum scheduler
,or
training scheduler
,
\mathcal{C}(t)
, which defines a sequence of training distributions
\{Q_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
over
\mathcal{D}
, let
T
denote the number of curriculum stages. At each training stage
t
, the model optimizes a reweighted objective:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim Q_{t}}\left[\mathcal{L}(f_{\theta}(x),y)\right],
where
f_{\theta}
is the model with parameters
\theta
,
\mathcal{L}
is the loss function, and
Q_{t}
is a distribution that prioritizes easier examples early in training before gradually incorporating harder ones. The scheduler ensures that the effective training data transitions smoothly from a simpler subset
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subset\mathcal{D}
to the full dataset
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\mathcal{D}
, satisfying:

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t}}[d(x,y)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{t+1}}[d(x,y)],\quad\forall t\in\{1,\dots,T-1\}.

(2)

Formally, the implementation of
curriculum scheduler
can be defined
Q_{t}
as a reweighting of the original data distribution
P(x,y)
:

Q_{t}(x,y)=w_{t}(x,y)\cdot P(x,y),

where
w_{t}(x,y)
is a time-dependent weight function that increases for harder examples as
t
progresses.
For instance, in
self-paced learning

[
57
]
, a CL variant, its weights are binary (
w_{t}\in\{0,1\}
) and determined by a threshold
\tau_{t}
on difficulty:
w_{t}(x,y)=\mathbb{I}[d(x,y)\leq\tau_{t}],\quad\text{with}\quad\tau_{t}\leq\tau_{t+1}.

Noteworthy, the final effectiveness of curriculum is determined by the alignment between
d(x,y)
and the model’s current capability—examples deemed ‘
easy
’ should be those the model can learn reliably at stage
t
, while ‘
hard
’ examples require more mature representations. In NLP,
d(x,y)
might measure sentence length or syntactic complexity. In CV, it could reflect image clutter, label noise, or distributional mismatch. The scheduler
\mathcal{C}(t)
may be predefined (e.g., linear annealing of
\tau_{t}
) or adaptively tuned based on model performance.

synergistic dual-metric evaluation, momentum-driven dynamic adjustment buffer

As shown in

1
,
FlexDiff
establishes a dynamic mapping relationship between agent performance and environmental difficulty by monitoring dual-channel metrics (i.e.,
Win Rate
and
Episode Reward
) in real-time during MARL training. This ensures the multi-dimensional model learning feedback during MARL policy optimization.
We formalize its core mechanisms as follows:

Synergistic dual-metric performance evaluation:

During the training phase, we conduct periodic evaluations at fixed intervals to assess the current model’s decision-making capabilities.
For each evaluation, we run multiple, e.g., 32 test episodes at the default difficulty level and calculate two key metrics, i.e., the average win rate and the mean cumulative reward.
All historical tested performances are maintained in an experience buffer-like history list.

To monitor the agents’ recent training progress, we employ two fixed-length sliding windows to track the performance metrics of win rates and averaged rewards. Formally, we define the performance evaluation window
\mathcal{W}_{t}
at training step
t
as:

\mathcal{W}_{t}=\{(w_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=t-N+1}^{t},

(3)

where
w_{i}\in[0,1]
represents the normalized win rate (victories divided by total testing episodes) at the
i_{\rm th}
evaluation cycle, and
r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes the correspondingly episode reward.

The overall stability of agents’ performance is formalized through the following constraints:

\displaystyle\sigma_{w}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(w_{i}-\mu_{w})^{2},\quad\mu_{w}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i},

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{i}-\mu_{r})^{2},\quad\mu_{r}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(\sigma_{w}<0.08)\otimes\mathbb{I}(\sigma_{r}<0.1\mu_{r}),

(6)

where
\mathbb{I}
acts as a binary classifier for stability conditions,
A\otimes B
denotes the simultaneous satisfaction of both constraints
A
and
B
. The stability indicator
\mathcal{S}\in\{0,1\}
combines low value in both success rates and rewards, ensuring reliable policy evaluation before difficulty adjustments.

For the windowed win rates
{w_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, the temporal trend
\beta_{w}
is quantified through linear regression:

\beta_{w}=\frac{N\sum x_{i}w_{i}-\sum x_{i}\sum w_{i}}{N\sum x_{i}^{2}-(\sum x_{i})^{2}},\quad x_{i}=i-t+N-1,

(7)

In Eq. (
7
), the slope parameter
\beta_{w}
reveals three distinct operational regimes: 1). significant improvement (
\beta_{w}>\zeta
), 2). stabilized performance (
|\beta_{w}|\leq\zeta
), and 3). performance degradation (
\beta_{w}<-\zeta
), where we define a threshold hyperparameter
\zeta
to separate the upper and lower bounds of performance fluctuations. The term
\sum
denotes the summation across data points in the sliding window
1,\ldots,N
. This enables early detection of learning convergences and breakthrough moments.
Then, we compute second-order differences for reward to detect phase transitions:

\nabla^{2}r_{t}=r_{t}-2r_{t-1}+r_{t-2}.

(8)

In Eq. (
8
), positive values (
\nabla^{2}r_{t}>0
) indicate convex acceleration where marginal improvements grow exponentially, characteristic of effective policy refinement.
Near-zero values (
\nabla^{2}r_{t}\approx 0
) indicate linear progress with a constant learning rate, while negative values (
\nabla^{2}r_{t}<0
) signal a slowdown in convergence, where returns decrease, often preceding a performance collapse.

Momentum-driven dynamic adjustment buffer:

To avoid frequent adjustments, we need to consider adding a cooling mechanism. Specifically, we introduce a self-adaptive momentum term
m_{t}\in[-1,1]
that integrates historical gradient information, which can be formalized as follows:

m_{t}=\gamma m_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)\tanh(\beta_{w}+0.5\nabla^{2}r_{t}),

(9)

with
\gamma=0.8
controlling memory decay. The hyperbolic tangent non-linearity bounds momentum growth while preserving directional sensitivity to performance trends.

Dynamic boundary constraints:

To prevent the difficulty from being switched due to unreasonable win rate performance, which could lead to training instability, we employ a flexible threshold mechanism that adapts to the current difficulty level
d_{t}
, further constraining the difference scheduler:

\displaystyle\tau_{h}(d_{t})

\displaystyle=\min(\gamma,0.5+0.03d_{t}),

(10)

\displaystyle\tau_{l}(d_{t})

\displaystyle=\max(\eta,0.4-0.02d_{t}).

(11)

This implements adaptive boundary self-regulation via the reason of higher difficulty levels require stronger evidence for progression (
\tau_{h}
increases slower than
\tau_{l}
decreases), enforcing cautious performance increasing and quick intervention to prevent performance decreasing.

Finally, as depicted in Eq. (
12
), the difficulty adjustment policy integrates multiple performance indicators through a three-branch decision structure:

d_{t+1}=\begin{cases}d_{t}+1&\text{if }\mu_{w}>\tau_{h}\land\mathcal{S}=1\land m_{t}>0.2,\\
d_{t}-1&\text{if }(\mu_{w}<\tau_{l}\lor\nabla^{2}r_{t}<-0.05)\land m_{t}<-0.2,\\
d_{t}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(12)

First, the step-by-step difficulty changes (
\pm 1
) ensure gradual progression matching standard difficulty levels. Second, the strict condition of task difficulty upgrade (
\mu_{w}\land\mathcal{S}\land m_{t}
) requires concurrent high performance, stability, and positive momentum. Third, the degradation trigger (
\mu_{w}\lor\nabla^{2}r_{t}
) responds quickly to either direct failure or accelerating decline. On the above basis, this flexible mechanism prevents unstable training while maintaining learning progress.

Consider a cooperative multi-agent system with

\quad\partial Q_{\text{tot}}/\partial Q_{i}\geq 0

Traditional value-based MARL algorithms

In light of these challenges, we introduce a novel algorithm,

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we identified and addressed such a critical challenge of “
environmental meta-stationarity
” in MARL, where fixed-difficulty training conditions limited policy generalization and traped agents in suboptimal solutions.
Specifically, we introduced a novel framework that integrates dynamic CL with MARL to overcome the limitations of environmental meta-stationarity.
Specifically, our method combined two key innovations: (1)
FlexDiff
, an self-adaptive difficulty scheduler that dynamically modulates task complexity based on real-time agent performance, and (2)
CGRPA
, a novel credit assignment mechanism that merges
group relative policy optimization
with
counterfactual action advantage estimation
to maintain learning stability during curriculum transitions.
Extensive experiments on the
SMAC

benchmark demonstrated significant improvements in both final performance and training efficiency compared to static-difficulty MARL baselines. Remarkably, our proposed approach demonstrated straightforward
plug-and-play compatibility
with baseline MARL algorithms, requiring only minimal architectural modifications while achieving superior task performance at the same training steps without compromising convergence speed.

In future work, we will focus on the following directions: First, we will explore more intelligent environment difficulty scheduling methods, including novel environment difficulty setting and grading approaches, to enhance the application of CL in MARL. Second, while our method has demonstrated strong performance in cooperative adversarial scenarios, we will extend its application to other MARL scenarios to thoroughly evaluate our method’s generalizability. Third, we will investigate heterogeneous multi-agent systems with diverse agent roles and capabilities to advance adaptive MARL frameworks.
Meanwhile, we will also attempt to scale to large-scale systems and enhancing sample efficiency for real-world deployment, thereby further validating the effectiveness of CL in MARL applications.
