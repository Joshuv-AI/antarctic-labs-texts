Effectiveness of a comprehensive therapeutic milieu on quality of life and level of stress among patients with heart failure - An experimental study
Authors: Shoba Govindasamy, Kayalvizhi Elumalai, Seethalakshmi Avudaiappan, Rosaline Rachel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Heart failure (HF) is a chronic and progressive condition associated with significant physical limitations, psychological distress, and impaired quality of life (QoL). Despite advances in medical management, many patients continue to experience persistent symptoms and elevated stress levels. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of a comprehensive therapeutic milieu on QoL and stress level among patients with HF.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A true experimental pretest–posttest control group design was used. The sample size (n = 240) was calculated based on 80% power, a 5% level of significance, and a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5). Participants were allocated to study and control groups using stratified randomization based on age and gender. The intervention group received a 90-day comprehensive therapeutic milieu, while the control group received routine care.RESULTS:At pretest, most participants in both groups reported poor QoL and moderate stress levels. By posttest, the study group demonstrated significant improvement in the physical dimension, emotional dimension, and overall QoL compared to the control group (P < 0.001). The mean stress score in the study group decreased from 23.28 ± 3.85 at baseline to 16.57 ± 4.22 at posttest III, whereas the control group showed minimal change. A moderate positive correlation was observed between stress and QoL scores (r = 0.571 in the study group; r = 0.658 in the control group, P < 0.001), indicating that increased stress is associated with poorer QoL as higher scores on both scales represent worse outcomes.CONCLUSION:The study finding concluded that integrating structured psychosocial support and low-intensity physical activity into routine cardiac care may enhance holistic management and improve long-term patient outcomes.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a major global health burden, affecting more than 64 million individuals worldwide and accounting for substantial rates of hospitalization, morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.[1] HF is a complex clinical syndrome resulting from structural or functional impairment of ventricular filling or ejection of blood, leading to insufficient cardiac output to meet the body’s metabolic demands.[2]

Despite advances in pharmacological and device-based therapies, HF remains a lifelong condition requiring continuous medical management and lifestyle adjustments. Patients with HF continue to experience persistent symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, fear of disease prognosis, and dependency on caregivers. [34] In addition, uncertainty regarding disease prognosis contributes to diminished life satisfaction and emotional distress, ultimately impairing overall QoL. [56]

Regular physical activity is considered a cornerstone of nonpharmacological management in HF. Low-intensity physical exercises, such as walking, cycling, and chair-based activities, improve cardiovascular efficiency, muscle strength, and oxygen utilization. However, adherence to exercise programs remains suboptimal due to symptom burden, frailty, and fear of overexertion.[7]

Guidelines from European Society of Cardiology, 2023 and American Heart Association, 2022 recommend tailored exercise programs as safe and beneficial for most HF patients[89] statements recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity weekly for stable HF patients, emphasizing gradual progression. Adherence to moderate- or high-intensity exercise programs remains suboptimal among patients with HF due to symptom burden, frailty, or fear of overexertion.[10]

Beyond the physiological limitations, HF has profound psychosocial implications, including, anxiety, depression, and social isolation.[11] Chronic stress activates neurohormonal pathways, including the sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, which may further exacerbate cardiac dysfunction[12] and it can interfere with medication adherence, dietary regulation, and increased rehospitalization rates due to poor follow-up compliance, thereby worsening clinical outcomes.[1314]

The multidimensional impact of HF emphasizes the need to adapt an innovative approach such as a comprehensive therapeutic milieu that integrates a structured support, stress reduction techniques, and therapeutic education.[15] Such interventions may help reduce stress, depression, anxiety, and improved health-related quality of life (QoL) and sleep quality in HF patients. [1617]

However, evidence suggests that there is a paucity of research evaluating the impact of companied effect of this intervention on QoL and stress among patients with HF highlighting the need for further research.

Novelty of the study

This study evaluates a comprehensive therapeutic milieu package combining dyadic education, low-intensity chair-based exercise, and mindful moist mouth technique, an integrative intervention that has not been widely tested in HF populations as a combined structured protocol. Unlike previous studies focusing on single-component interventions, this study provides evidence on a multimodal, nurse-led, non-pharmacological intervention delivered over 90 days in a true experimental design. As a result, it adds fresh data to patient-centered long-term HF care.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A true experimental pretest–posttest design was employed. The study was conducted in the cardiac wards of a multispecialty hospital at South India, from April 2022 to May 2023.

Study participants and sampling

Study participants were screened using the New York Heart Failure (NYHA) functional classification. Patients aged ≥30 years with HF, classified as NYHA Class I–III, and admitted in the cardiac wards, were included. NYHA Class IV patients, patients admitted to cardiac ICU, those with arrhythmias, and those who had undergone cardiac surgeries, were excluded due to their clinical instability, severe functional limitation, and inability to participate in physical activity-based interventions.

Stratified random sampling was employed, with stratification based on age and gender. Power analysis determined the required sample size based on 5% level of significance and 80% statistical power and a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), as supported by previous studies. [1819] Total sample size was 240 (Study = 120, Control = 120). Data were analyzed using a per-protocol approach, including only participants who completed the intervention and follow-up assessments. Participants who were lost to follow-up or withdrew during the study period were excluded from the final analysis. The final analysis included was 231 participants after attrition.

Data collection tools and techniques

The study tool consists of a structured questionnaire to collect demographic and clinical variables. The demographic variables included were age in years, gender, educational status, marital status, occupation, religion, monthly family income, place of residence, and mode of relaxation. The clinical variables included comorbidities, duration of illness, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification of HF, medications received, history of smoking, history of alcohol consumption, and exercise pattern.

Health-related QoL was assessed using the Minnesota living with heart failure questionnaire.[20] The perceived stress scale was used to assess stress level.[21] Tools got validated by the experts from the field of Nursing, nutrition, physiotherapy, and cardiology. The reliability of both tools was assessed using the test-retest method. The reliability scores identified were 0.85 and 0.84, respectively.

Intervention tool

Following the pretest assessment, the study group received a comprehensive therapeutic milieu, while the control group continued with routine hospital care.

The intervention comprised dyadic care education, low-intensity physical activity, and a mindful moist mouth technique. The dyadic care intervention included a structured 30–45-minute teaching session for patients and their primary caregivers, focusing on the heart action plan, HF management, healthy diet, and self-care behaviors. Low-intensity chair-based aerobic and stretching exercises were demonstrated, with participants instructed to perform 10 repetitions of each exercise twice daily. The mindful moist mouth technique was used as a nonpharmacological stress management strategy to promote relaxation and reduce psychological distress among patients with HF. The mindful moist mouth technique was demonstrated and redemonstrated, with participants advised to practice it three times per cycle whenever fluids were consumed. The intervention was continued for 90 days. Posttest assessments were conducted on Day 7, Day 30, and Day 90.

The participants were encouraged to maintain a self-monitoring diary on everyday basis by the patient or with the help of caregivers on symptom monitoring, fluid intake and output chart, and activities performed every day.

Outcome assessment was not blinded, as the same investigator was involved in both intervention delivery and data collection.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IECVBMHR/LTR/2022/021). The study was conducted as part of a Ph.D. thesis under reg no: 4419015. Written informed consent was obtained, and participants were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. Ethical principles were strictly adhered and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Jamovi statistical software. Descriptive statistics, frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used to assess the demographic, clinical, and study variables. Chi-square tests assessed group homogeneity. Paired t-tests compared within-group differences; Z-tests compared between-group differences. Pearson correlation assessed relationships between study variables. One-way ANOVA examined associations with demographic variables. The multiple regression analysis identified predictors of posttest outcomes. [1819]

Results

Participants were equally distributed between the study and control groups using matching criteria for age and gender. The majority of participants in both groups were aged between 61 and 70 years 60% and male 70%. Most participants, 50% in both groups, were graduates or had higher education. Additionally, 20.83% of the study group and 25.83% of control group were retired from employment; furthermore, 69.17% of study and 64.17% of control group sample lived in joint families, more than half of the participants resided in urban areas (55% in the study group and 65% in the control group), and 38.33% in the study group and 40.83% of control group samples reported watching television as their primary mode of relaxation.

Regarding clinical characteristics, hypertension and coronary artery disease were reported as comorbidities in 25% of the study group and 35% of the control group. An illness duration of 1–2 years was observed in 43.33% of the study group and 38.33% of the control group. The majority of participants were classified as NYHA Class II (55% in the study group vs. 49.16% in the control group). A history of one hospitalization in the past year was noted in 58.33% of participants in both groups. The use of beta-blockers and digoxin was reported in 23.33% and 12.5% of participants, respectively. Nonsmoking status (56.67% in the study group vs. 61.66% in the control group) and nonalcohol use (60.8% in the study group vs. 58.33% in the control group) were predominant. Rare engagement in exercise was reported by 55% of the study group and 40% of the control group.

At pretest, the majority of participants in the study group (81.74%) had poor QoL, with a mean score of 65.2 ± 13, while in the control group, 70.69% of participants had poor QoL, with a mean score of 64.7 ± 14.5. By posttest, 53.04% of participants in the study group reported a moderate QoL, with a reduced mean score of 40.8 ± 13.2. In contrast, most participants in the control group (95.69%) continued to report poor QoL, with an increased mean score of 67.5 ± 12.4.

Th between-group analysis demonstrated that there was no statistically significant difference in the pretest. A marked improvement in QoL in the study group posttest III was noted, whereas the control group showed a decline in QoL at post-test III. A significant difference was observed between the groups at posttest (P < 0.001) [Table 1].

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effect of a comprehensive therapeutic milieu on QoL and the level of stress among patients with HF. The findings demonstrate that the intervention significantly improved QoL and reduced stress levels in the study group compared to the control group over 90 days, highlighting the effectiveness of structured psychosocial and behavioral interventions in HF management.[1]

The intervention differed from routine care by addressing physical, psychological, and behavioral domains simultaneously. The inclusion of dyadic education enhanced patient–caregiver collaboration, which may have contributed to improved adherence and clinical outcomes. The intervention components were evidence-based and previously validated for effectiveness independently.[222324]

The participants with a caregiver (dyadic) were involved in a structured training session, focusing on daily monitoring, symptom management, eating healthy with HF, and self-care behaviors. This aligns with recent evidence emphasizing HF management as a dyadic process, where collaborative patient–caregiver interventions improve adherence, symptom control, and QoL outcomes.[25]

Followed by the education, a demonstration session was given about low-intensity physical activities, mindful moist mouth techniques were demonstrated, and participants were instructed to perform. Similar interventions were found in the literature that were used to effectively bring improvement in HF outcomes.[2627]

At baseline, both groups were comparable in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Similar demographic patterns have been reported in HF populations, where older adults and males constitute the majority. For instance, it is reported that most HF patients were aged above 60 years and predominantly male, with comparable clinical profiles, supporting the baseline homogeneity observed in the present study.[28]

At baseline, the majority of participants in both groups had poor QoL. A previous literature also reported with the same finding of diminished physical and psychological well-being, emotional stability, and social participation among HF patients.[29] This is also in line with finding reported that HF patients commonly experience significantly impaired QoL due to persistent symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, and psychological distress.[30]

Following the intervention, a significant improvement in QoL across physical, emotional, and overall domain was observed in the study group at posttest. This improvement may be attributed to enhanced functional capacity and reduced symptom burden, as supported by studies demonstrating the benefits of low-intensity exercise and behavioral interventions such as psychoeducation and stress management in HF patients.[313233]

The significant improvement observed in both the physical and emotional dimensions of QoL may be attributed to the integrated components of the therapeutic milieu, which address both physiological and psychosocial aspects of HF. Dyadic care education likely enhanced self-care behaviors and strengthened caregiver involvement, thereby promoting better disease management and emotional support. This finding is consistent with recent evidence, highlighting the critical role of caregivers in influencing patient outcomes in HF. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that supportive educational interventions for caregivers significantly improved QoL and reduced caregiver burden, both immediately after the intervention and at follow-up assessments.[34]

The observed improvements may also be explained by the synergistic effect of combining educational, behavioral, and supportive strategies like a comprehensive approach, which enables patients and caregivers to actively participate in care, leading to improved symptom recognition and QoL.

The present study found a progressive decline in QoL among patients with HF in the control group over time, as the study group experienced symptom reduction and the control group experienced worsening of the symptoms. This finding is consistent with evidence, the chronic, progressive nature of HF. Studies demonstrated that, in the absence of structured interventions, patients may experience deterioration in health-related QoL, primarily due to increasing symptom burden, functional limitations, and inadequate coping mechanisms.[35]

In contrast, a highly significant difference between the groups at posttest (P < 0.001) indicates the sustained effectiveness of the comprehensive therapeutic milieu intervention in improving QoL beyond routine care. This suggests that structured and multidimensional interventions play a crucial role in altering the disease trajectory and enhancing patient outcomes. Similar findings have been reported in studies evaluating structured Heart Failure Nursing Protocols, which include patient education, lifestyle modification, and regular follow-up. Such interventions have been associated with significant improvements in QoL, along with enhanced self-care behaviors, better medication adherence, and improved symptom management.[36]

However, some studies have reported mixed findings regarding the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions in patients with HF. Improvements in QoL are not consistently significant, and in some cases, the benefits are limited or short term. Additionally, while such interventions may improve psychological well-being, their impact on physical functioning may be less pronounced. These variations may be attributed to differences in intervention duration, intensity, and patient-related factors.[3738]

The observed improvement in QoL identified in the study may be attributed to the multidimensional nature of the therapeutic milieu, which promotes emotional support, enhances patient engagement, and strengthens coping abilities, thereby positively influencing both physical and psychological well-being.

Stress levels were moderate in both groups at baseline, indicating a comparable psychological status before the intervention. This finding is consistent with existing literature that recognizes HF as a psychologically distressing chronic condition, often associated with elevated stress levels.[39] Furthermore, a recent study conducted using the perceived stress scale reported that patients with HF commonly experience mild-to-moderate stress, with a substantial proportion categorized under moderate stress at baseline.[40]

The study group demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in stress levels at posttest, whereas the control group showed minimal change. This highlights the effectiveness of the intervention in alleviating psychological distress among patients with HF. The observed improvement may be attributed to the incorporation of structured relaxation techniques, which facilitate emotional regulation and enhance coping capacity. These findings are consistent with previous studies, reporting that mindfulness-based stress reduction significantly decreases psychological distress and improves emotional well-being among patients with HF when compared to usual care.[41]

Further evidence suggests that enhancing positivity skills can effectively reduce perceived stress among patients with chronic HF, highlighting the importance of psychological resilience in chronic disease management. Similarly, the study emphasizes the role of stress modulation in improving patient outcomes. These findings suggest that integrating psychosocial interventions into routine cardiac care may enhance overall QoL and treatment adherence.[42] Similarly, psychosocial interventions targeting stress and depression have been shown to enhance emotional well-being and reduce symptom burden in individuals with HF, reinforcing the importance of integrating such approaches into routine care.[43]

However, some studies have reported that stress management interventions in patients with HF demonstrate only short-term improvements in anxiety, depressive symptoms, QoL, and exercise capacity, with benefits not sustained at follow-up. Additionally, variations in outcomes have been linked to participant characteristics such as gender distribution and disease severity, rather than the type of intervention itself. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of such interventions may be influenced by patient-related factors and the duration of follow-up.[44]

In contrast, the sustained improvements observed in the present study may be due to the comprehensive and structured intervention combined with continuous follow-up. This emphasizes the value of integrated and ongoing care in improving psychological outcomes and QoL in patients with HF.

The study findings also demonstrated a moderate positive correlation between stress levels and QoL in both groups, indicating that higher stress is associated with poorer QoL.

This finding is consistent with prior research, showing that perceived stress negatively influences mental QoL among patients with HF, with higher stress levels contributing to diminished QoL.[45] Similarly, elevated stress is associated with worsening clinical outcomes and reduced QoL, underscoring the importance of effective stress management strategies.[46] The minimal change observed in the control group further suggests that routine care alone may be insufficient to address the psychological needs of patients with HF. Additionally, reduced physical functioning and increased emotional burden have been identified key contributors to poor QoL in these patients.[47]

These findings have important clinical implications, as reducing stress levels may indirectly improve QoL and functional outcomes in patients with HF. The observed relationship between stress and QoL highlights the interdependence of psychological and physical health, where increased stress may worsen symptom perception and reduce functional capacity.

This underscores the need to incorporate integrated care approaches addressing both psychological and physiological aspects into routine nursing practice. Such interventions are cost-effective, nonpharmacological, and feasible in clinical settings to enhance overall patient outcomes. A key strength of this study is the implementation of a structured intervention with repeated follow-up assessments, enabling the evaluation of both immediate and sustained effects on QoL and stress.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study highlight the importance of incorporating an integrated care approach addressing both psychological and physiological aspects into routine cardiac rehabilitation programs. Such nonpharmacological interventions are cost-effective and feasible in clinical settings to improve overall patient outcomes. Nurses and healthcare professionals play a crucial role in facilitating such interventions and promoting holistic care.

Strength of the study

One of the major strengths of this study is its true experimental design with a control group, ensuring strong internal validity. In addition, the substantial sample size and repeated follow-up assessments contribute to the robustness and reliability of the findings.

Limitations and recommendation

Despite these strengths, the study has certain limitations. The single-center design may limit generalizability. Additionally, the use of self-reported measures may introduce response bias, as responses can be influenced by social desirability, recall errors, and subjective perceptions, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of outcomes. Outcome assessment was not blinded, as the same investigator was involved in both intervention delivery and data collection.

Future studies with multicenter samples and longer follow-up periods are recommended to validate these findings and assess long-term sustainability. Comparative studies of psychosocial interventions, inclusion of objective measures, and evaluation of caregiver involvement and cost-effectiveness are also suggested.

Conclusion

The study finding reveals that a comprehensive therapeutic milieu may improve QoL and reduce stress among patients with HF. These outcomes are consistent with substantial health policy criteria that prioritize patient-centered, holistic, and nonpharmacological care to chronic disease management as supported by global and national health authorities like the World Health Organization and National health policies. The findings support the integration of such interventions into standard cardiac care processes to enhance patient-reported outcomes, foster psychological well-being, and assist in the more cost-effective and long-term management of chronic conditions.
