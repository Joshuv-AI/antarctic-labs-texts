Gradient Is Not Enough: Stroke Volume Index as the True Arbiter of Risk in Advanced Aortic Stenosis
Authors: Ashish H Shah
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

I read the report by Hemelrijk et al.1 with great interest. In the worldwide The Cerebrovascular Events in Patients Undergoing Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation 2 (CENTER2) patient-level cohort of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) recipients, 15,233 patients were classified into 4 aortic stenosis (AS) phenotypes using baseline aortic valve area (AVA), mean pressure gradient, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Nearly 1 in 5 (18.8%) had “discordant” AS, including low-gradient AS with impaired LVEF (7.3%), low-gradient AS with preserved LVEF (10.6%), and discordant high-gradient AS (0.9%). While 1-year mortality did not differ between the aggregate discordant group and concordant high-gradient AS (13.1 vs. 11.9%; adjusted hazard ratio: 1.19; p = 0.08), outcomes were not uniform: low-gradient AS with impaired LVEF had higher 1-year mortality than concordant high-gradient AS (15.9 vs. 11.9%; adjusted hazard ratio: 1.43; p = 0.01). This signal warrants emphasis.

These differences are most parsimoniously explained by low-flow physiology rather than the “discordance” label itself.2 Transvalvular gradient is intrinsically flow-dependent; for a given valve area, the pressure gradient varies approximately with the square of flow. Accordingly, patients with comparable anatomical stenosis can present with different gradients because effective forward stroke volume differs. Low flow is generally operationalized as a stroke volume index (SVi) ≤35 mL/m2, a threshold embedded in guideline-based definitions of severe low-gradient AS (e.g., AVA ≤1.0 cm2 with mean pressure gradient <40 mmHg when SVi is < 35 mL/m2).3 Thus, the coexistence of a small AVA and low gradient often signals reduced forward flow due to impaired LVEF, restrictive remodeling with preserved LVEF, atrial fibrillation, or significant mitral/tricuspid regurgitation; each is linked to adverse outcomes after TAVR.

In this context, CENTER2’s limitations are mechanistically central. The authors note that SVi, computed tomography calcium scoring, and stress echocardiography were not used for subtype classification and that SVi was not routinely collected as recommended by guidelines.1 This creates the potential for inclusion of normal-flow, low-gradient AS and even nonsevere AS within low-gradient groups, yielding heterogeneous phenotypes in which the highest-risk subset is likely the truly low-flow population rather than discordance per se.

Convergent evidence also comes from AS staging by cardiac structural damage. Others have reported excess mortality among patients with advanced structural damage (stage 3/4) in severe AS, irrespective of high- or low-gradient phenotype.4 While staging reflects cumulative anatomical injury, the unifying abnormality across higher-risk phenotypes is plausibly reduced effective forward flow, again best captured by SVi. Advanced stages are often characterized by atrial fibrillation and moderate-or-greater mitral/tricuspid regurgitation, each lowering effective forward output and thereby diminishing systemic SVi even when LVEF appears preserved.

We therefore propose that future registries and longitudinal surveillance should (1) mandate routine reporting of SVi (and flow rate where feasible), using SVi ≤35 mL/m2 to define low flow; (2) integrate cardiac structural damage staging with flow characterization to better align “phenotype” with underlying pathophysiology and prognosis; and (3) systematically determine which patients derive hemodynamic benefit, as reflected by improvement in SVi after TAVR. Prospective studies should evaluate whether earlier intervention, triggered by falling SVi and/or progression in structural stage, before irreversible remodeling improves outcomes. A flow-centered paradigm, grounded in SVi as the fundamental hemodynamic unit, may better align anatomical staging with physiological risk and ultimately refine timing of intervention in AS (Figure 1).
