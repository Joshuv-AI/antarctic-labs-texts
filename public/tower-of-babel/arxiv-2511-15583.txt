Abstract
The Nyström method is a widely used technique for improving the scalability of kernel-based algorithms, including kernel ridge regression, spectral clustering, and Gaussian processes. Despite its popularity, the numerical stability of the method has remained largely an unresolved problem. In particular, the pseudo-inversion of the submatrix involved in the Nyström method may pose stability issues as the submatrix is likely to be ill-conditioned, resulting in numerically poor approximation. In this work, we establish conditions under which the Nyström method is numerically stable. We show that stability can be achieved through an appropriate choice of column subsets and a careful implementation of the pseudoinverse. Our results and experiments provide theoretical justification and practical guidance for the stable application of the Nyström method in large-scale kernel computations.

To illustrate the issue, consider the matrix

\displaystyle\left\|{A-A_{N}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\

where

\displaystyle\left\|{A-\widehat{A}_{N}}\right\|=\left\|{A-\hat{B}\hat{B}^{T}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{4}}{(\gamma+\tilde{\gamma})^{2}}\right|.

1.1
Existing methods

Prior works have investigated the potential numerical instability of the Nyström method when computing the pseudoinverse of the core matrix
S^{T}\!AS
.
In particular, Tropp et al.
[
44
]
proposed introducing a small shift to the core matrix before computing its pseudoinverse; see Algorithm 3 in
[
44
]
. The small shift ensures that the singular values of
S^{T}\!AS
remain sufficiently larger than the unit roundoff, thereby improving numerical stability while preserving the spectral decay of
A
.
Carson and Dauz̆ickaitė
[
5
]
analyzed the numerical stability of this approach. While this strategy appears to be numerically stable in practice, their analysis is limited: the
bound suggests that
roundoff error is amplified approximately by
\sigma_{1}(A)/\sigma_{r}(A)
, which can be extremely large for matrices with fast decaying singular values; this is often the case when the matrix is well-approximated by a low-rank matrix.
Moreover, the method requires computing the QR factorization of an
n\times r
matrix, and the resulting low-rank factors cannot be kept as subsets of the original matrix, which can be considered a drawback. To our knowledge, shifting has been the only technique proposed to address the potential instability of the Nyström method. We note that the shifted formulation does not yield the exact Nyström approximation in exact arithmetic, as the introduced shift slightly perturbs the eigenvalues of the core matrix.

Related approaches have been used in the broader context of low-rank approximations. One common strategy is the use of the
\epsilon
-pseudoinverse, which truncates singular values below a threshold
\epsilon
before (pseudo)inversion. For example, Chiu and Demanet
[
8
]
applied this idea to the CUR decomposition to improve numerical stability, and subsequent analyses
[
39
]
derived stability bounds for the CUR decomposition using an
\epsilon
-pseudoinverse. This idea has also been applied to generalized Nyström method
[
36
]
.

Contributions

In this work, we study the numerical stability of the Nyström method with
locally maximum-volume indices
(see

1
) by employing the

-pseudoinverse. We propose a numerically stable approach for computing the Nyström approximation that truncates small singular values of the core matrix before taking its pseudoinverse as outlined in

Overview of the Nyström method and Kernel matrices

The Nyström method is widely used in data science to efficiently approximate kernel matrices
[
18
,
21
,
47
]
, especially in scenarios where computing and storing the full kernel matrix is computationally prohibitive. By leveraging low-rank approximations, the Nyström method improves the scalability of kernel-based machine learning algorithms in both memory and computational costs. This section provides an overview of the Nyström method and kernel matrices.

Given
n

d
-dimensional data points
z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a kernel function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the kernel matrix of
\kappa
with respect to
z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n}
is defined by

A_{ij}=\kappa(z_{i},z_{j}).

Appropriate choices of
\kappa
ensure that
A
is SPSD and we interpret the kernel function as a measure of similarity between a pair of data points.
In this case, the kernel function determines a feature map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}
such that
A_{ij}=\langle\Phi(z_{i}),\Phi(z_{j})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. Here,
\mathbb{H}
is a Hilbert space and dim
(\mathbb{H})
can be much larger than
n
or even infinite. Therefore, kernel matrices implicitly compute inner products in a high-dimensional feature space without explicitly transforming the data.
When data are mapped to a high-dimensional feature space, it is often easier to extract nonlinear structure in the data. Machine learning algorithms where this is exploited through kernel matrices include kernel SVM
[
13
]
, kernel ridge regression
[
41
]
and kernel k-means clustering
[
47
]
. Popular kernels include polynomial kernels, Gaussian kernels, and Sigmoid kernels. See, for example,
[
41
,
28
]
for details.

For a given dataset, constructing the kernel matrix involves evaluating the kernel entries, a process that becomes increasingly expensive as
n
and

become large. This issue is particularly problematic when the kernel function is computationally expensive to compute. To address this, choosing
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
as a column subsampling matrix in the Nyström method has proven to be an effective strategy for improving the scalability of kernel methods. When
S
is a column subsampling matrix,
AS
is an
r

A
, and
S^{T}\!AS
is an
r\times r
principal submatrix of
A
. Consequently, the Nyström approximation requires only
nr
kernel evaluations instead of the full

. Therefore, we focus on column subsampling matrices in this work.

Other popular choices for

include random embeddings such as Gaussian embeddings and sparse maps; see
[
33
,
21
]
.

There are several ways of choosing the important columns for the Nyström approximation. The methods used to choose columns significantly impact the accuracy of the Nyström method. There are numerous practical algorithms in the literature for constructing a subsampling matrix, including uniform sampling
[
48
,
18
]
, leverage score sampling
[

,

,

]
, determinantal point process (DPP) sampling

\left\|{A-A_{N}}\right\|_{*}\leq(1+r)\left\|{A-\llbracket{A}\rrbracket_{r}}\right\|_{*}.

Although many effective strategies exist for identifying the important columns of an SPSD matrix, this work focuses on
locally maximum-volume indices
(locally max-vol indices)
[

,

]
. The formal definition is given below.

Definition 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an SPSD matrix. A subset
J\subseteq[n]
with
|J|=r
is called a set of locally max-vol indices if

\mathrm{det}(A(\hat{J},\hat{J}))\leq\mathrm{det}(A(J,J))

for every
\hat{J}\subseteq[n]
with
|J\cap\hat{J}|=r-1
, and
|\hat{J}|=r
.

Since
A
is SPSD,
\mathrm{det}(A(J,J))
equals the volume of the submatrix
A(J,J)
. A locally max-vol index set is therefore locally optimal in the sense that its volume cannot be increased by replacing a single index.

Locally max-vol indices enjoy several useful approximation guarantees. In particular, they satisfy the following quasi-optimal bound in the max-norm
[
34
, Lemma 1]
:

\left\|{A-A_{N}}\right\|_{\max}\leq(1+r)\sigma_{r+1}(A),

where
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\max}
denotes the maximum absolute entry of the argument.

Compared with globally maximum-volume (max-vol) indices, i.e.,

J_{*}\in\underset{J\in[n],|J|=r}{\mathrm{argmax}}\mathrm{det}(A(J,J)),

locally max-vol indices offer important practical advantages. Constructing a globally max-vol set (or approximate max-vol set) is NP-hard
[
9
]
and therefore computational infeasible in general, whereas verifying and improving a set of indices to a locally max-vol index can be done in
\text{O}(nr^{2}\log r)
time
[
34
]
. Locally max-vol indices also yield high-quality low-rank approximations of SPSD matrices. Since locally max-vol indices are highly effective, the analysis in this work serves as a good proxy for understanding the behaviour of any high-quality set of indices.

In this paper we focus on a locally max-vol index set
J
and its associated subsampling matrix
S
, defined so that
A(:,J)=AS
. We establish stability of the resulting Nyström’s method, when implemented as in
Algorithm

2
. Although the (locally) max-vol choice has historically been the gold standard, leading to a number of desirable properties, it does not necessarily minimize the error
\|A-A_{N}\|_{*}
, and is not guaranteed to satisfy (
1

S
have been introduced to satisfy such bounds
[
12
,
38
,
15
]
. Nevertheless, we believe that, just as with (locally) max-vol
S
, Nyström method remains stable under any ‘good’ choice of
S
when implemented according to
Algorithm

2
.

In this section, we provide a bound for the accuracy of the stabilized version of the Nyström method for SPSD matrices under the assumption that

The stabilized version of the Nyström method is given by

\|A-AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{-1}S^{T}\!A\|_{\max}\leq(r+1)\,\sigma_{r+1}(A),

where
\|\cdot\|_{\max}
denotes the maximum magnitude among the entries
of the matrix argument.

Now consider the following optimization problem

\min_{C}\|{A-ASC}\|_{F}.

(2)

Since the optimization problem (
2
) has the solution
(AS)^{\dagger}A=\arg\min_{C}\|{A-ASC}\|_{F}
, we have

\|{A-AS(AS)^{\dagger}A}\|_{F}=\min_{C}\|{A-ASC}\|_{F}\leq\|{A-AS(S^{T}AS)^{-1}S^{T}\!A}\|_{F}.

Therefore, putting these together we obtain

\displaystyle\|{(I-(AS)(AS)^{\dagger})A}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{A-AS(AS)^{\dagger}A}\|_{F}\leq\|{A-AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{-1}S^{T}\!A}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq n\|{A-AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{-1}S^{T}\!A}\|_{\max}\leq n(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A),

where in the penultimate inequality, we used
\|{B}\|_{F}\leq n\|{B}\|_{\max}
for
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

\square

A recent refinement by
[
2
]
slightly sharpens the
dependence on the singular values, and also establishes guarantees
for approximate max-vol subsampling. For the purposes of this work, we retain the classical bound in order to keep the presentation simple.

Lemma 4
.

Given a SPSD matrix

.
Then

\|P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon}\|_{2}=\|AS(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{nr}.

Proof.

Let
AS=QR
be the thin QR decomposition of

\displaystyle\|AS(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|QR(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\|R(S^{T}AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!Q)R(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}\|_{2}\|(S^{T}\!AS)(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}\|_{2},\leq\sqrt{nr}

where
Lemma

2
was used for the last inequality.

\square

Lemma 5
.

With
P^{\epsilon}_{AS,S}=AS(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}S^{T}
as in

4

P^{\epsilon}_{AS,S}AS=AS+E_{\epsilon},

where
\|{E_{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\leq 2\epsilon\sqrt{nr}
.

We first notice that there exists
A_{\epsilon}=A+E

(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}=S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S.

Indeed if

is the SVD, with
\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon

0&0\end{bmatrix}[U_{1},U_{2}]^{T}
as the SVD of

.
Hence we have
P^{\epsilon}_{AS,S}AS=AS(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!AS)=AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!AS)

\displaystyle P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon}AS

\displaystyle=AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!AS)=AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!(A_{\epsilon}-E)S)

\displaystyle=AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)-AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}S^{T}\!ES,

where the second term in the final equality satisfies

\displaystyle\|{AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}S^{T}\!ES}\|_{2}\leq\|{AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}}\|_{2}\|{S^{T}\!ES}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{nr}

. Then

\displaystyle AS(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S)

\displaystyle=ASU_{1}U_{1}^{T}=AS-ASU_{2}U_{2}^{T}

and

\displaystyle\|{ASU_{2}U_{2}^{T}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|QRU_{2}U_{2}^{T}\|_{2}=\|RU_{2}U_{2}^{T}\|_{2}=\|{(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}S^{T}\!QRU_{2}U_{2}^{T}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|{(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}S^{T}\!ASU_{2}U_{2}^{T}}\|_{2}\leq\|{(S^{T}\!Q)^{\dagger}}\|_{2}\|{S^{T}\!(A_{\epsilon}-E)SU_{2}U_{2}^{T}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{nr}\|{-S^{T}\!ESU_{2}U_{2}^{T}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{nr},

where
Lemma

2

S^{T}\!A_{\epsilon}S\tilde{U}_{2}=0
was used in the penultimate inequality. The result follows by setting

and noting that

where

We now combine the lemmas to analyze the accuracy of the stabilized Nyström method in exact arithmetic.

Theorem 6
(Accuracy of Stabilized Nyström).

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a SPSD matrix and
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
be a locally max-vol subsampling matrix for
A
.
Then

\|A-A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}\|_{2}\leq n^{2}r(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+2nr\epsilon.

Proof.

We first note that, by
Lemma

5
,

\displaystyle A-A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}=(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A=(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A(I-SM_{S})+E_{\epsilon}M_{S}

for any matrix
M_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and
E_{\epsilon}
is as in
Lemma

5
, satisfying
\|{E_{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\leq 2\epsilon\sqrt{nr}
. Now choose
M_{S}=(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T}
where
Q
is the orthonormal matrix in the thin QR decomposition of
AS
. We get

\displaystyle(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A(I-SM_{S})

\displaystyle=(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A(I-S(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T})

\displaystyle=(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A(I-QQ^{T})(I-S(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T})

since
Q^{T}\!S
is non-singular by
Lemma

2
and

\|{E_{\epsilon}M_{S}}\|_{2}\leq\|{E_{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|{(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}}\|_{2}\leq 2\epsilon\sqrt{nr}\sqrt{nr}=2\epsilon nr

by
Lemma

2
. Finally, we get

\displaystyle\|A-A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{(I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon})A(I-QQ^{T})(I-S(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T})}\|_{2}+\|{E_{\epsilon}M_{S}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{I-P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|{A(I-QQ^{T})}\|_{2}\|{I-S(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T}}\|_{2}+2nr\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\|{P_{AS,S}^{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|{A(I-QQ^{T})}\|_{2}\|{S(Q^{T}\!S)^{\dagger}Q^{T}}\|_{2}+2nr\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{nr}\|{A(I-QQ^{T})}\|_{2}\sqrt{nr}+2nr\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}r(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+2nr\epsilon,

where from the second to the third line we used the fact that any oblique projector
P
satisfies
\|I-P\|_{2}=\|P\|_{2}
and in the last two inequalities we used
Lemmas

2
,
4
, and
3
.

\square

4
Numerical Stability of SN

So far, we have analyzed SN in exact arithmetic, showing that it closely preserves the accuracy of the Nyström algorithm. Here we address the problem of the stability of the algorithm; that is, we provide an actual implementation of the algorithm and show that even taking into account round-off errors, the computed approximant
fl(AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}S^{T}\!A)
has error
\|A-fl(A_{SN})\|_{F}
comparable to
A-A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}
. We consider the following implementation: we first compute
AS
and
S^{T}\!AS
. Next, we compute a truncated Cholesky decomposition
R_{\epsilon}^{T}R_{\epsilon}=(S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}
. Finally, we obtain the low-rank factor
ASR_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}
.

Throughout the analysis, we let
u
denote the unit roundoff, and use the symbol
u_{*}

to represent either a scalar or a matrix whose norm is bounded
by at most a low-degree polynomial in
n
and
r
, multiplied
by
u
. In particular,
\|u_{*}\|_{F}
does not grow
exponentially with
n
, nor does it scale inversely with small
quantities such as
\epsilon
or
\sigma_{r}(A)
.
Similarly, we distinguish between the notation

\text{O}(1)
to denote a quantity bounded in magnitude by a constant

c
, where
c
is independent of the problem size and
\O(1)
which suppresses low-degree polynomial in
n
and
r
.
While this may appear to be a simplification, it is a standard
convention in stability analyses (see, e.g.,

[
35
]
). The precise value of
u_{*}
may vary from one occurrence
to another, but we always assume that
\epsilon\gg u_{*}
.
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we normalize so that
\|A\|_{2}=1
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\ (n>r)
be any orthonormal matrix.
There exist matrices
\widehat{A}=A+\Delta\widehat{A}
and
\widetilde{A}=A+\Delta\widetilde{A}
, with
\|\Delta\widehat{A}\|_{2},\|\Delta\widetilde{A}\|_{2}\leq u_{*}
such that

•

fl(AS)=\widehat{A}S
;

•

fl(S^{T}\!AS)=S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S
.

These statements are perhaps trivially true when
S
is a subsampling matrix, often with
\widehat{A}=A
and
\widetilde{A}=A
. We state a more general version in case there are errors incurred in computing with
S
.

Proof.

For any matrix
A
and
B
,
\|fl(AB)-AB\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}u_{*}

[
25
, Section 3.5]
and since
\|S\|_{2}=1
and
\|A\|_{2}=1

fl(AS)=AS+u_{*}=(A+u_{*}S^{T})S=:\widehat{A}S

and

fl(S^{T}\!AS)=S^{T}\!AS+u_{*}=S^{T}\!(A+Su_{*}S^{T})S=:S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S.

\square

We will also assume that the computed truncated Cholesky factor
\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}=fl(\text{chol}((S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)_{\epsilon}))
satisfies

\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{T}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}=(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)_{\epsilon}+u_{*},

(3)

where
\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{r}\times r}
, with
\hat{r}\leq r
, is numerically full-rank. In fact, by applying the Cholesky algorithm with complete pivoting, one can establish the pessimistic bound
[
25
, Theorem 10.14]

\|(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)_{\epsilon}-\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{T}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\|_{2}\leq 4^{\hat{r}}u_{*},

even though the dimensions of the matrix are relatively small, there is still an exponential dependence in
\hat{r}
, which we could not ignore in general. However, as explained in
[
25
, Theorem 10.14]
, this bound tends to be overly pessimistic in practice, and for our purposes, we can safely disregard it.

We now prove the floating-point version of the
Lemmas

4
and
5
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\widehat{A}
and
\widetilde{A}
be as in

7
, and suppose

\|\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}\|_{2}\leq 1+2\sqrt{nr}.

(4)

Proof.

Let
AS=QR
be the thin QR decomposition of

. Then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|(A+u_{*})S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\|AS(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+\|u_{*}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|QR(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+1=\|R(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+1

\displaystyle=\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{-1}(S^{T}\!Q)R(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+1\leq\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{-1}\|_{2}\|(S^{T}\!Q)R(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+1

\displaystyle\leq\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{-1}\|_{2}\left(\|(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}+\|(S^{T}\!u_{*}S)(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}\right)+1

Lemma 9
.

Let
\widehat{A}
and
\widetilde{A}
be as in

7

\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{A}S)^{T}\!S=AS+E_{\epsilon},

(5)

\|E_{\epsilon}\|_{2}\leq 4\epsilon\sqrt{nr}

Proof.

As in

5

\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}=\widetilde{A}+E

(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)_{\epsilon}=S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S.

Hence we have

\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}S)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{A}S)^{T}S=\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!\widehat{A}S)=\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!(\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}-u_{*})S).

The term

\|\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}S^{T}\!u_{*}S\|_{2}\leq\|\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}\|_{2}\|S^{T}\!u_{*}S\|_{2}\leq u_{*}(1+2\sqrt{nr}).

S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S=[\tilde{U}_{1},\tilde{U}_{2}]\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}[\tilde{U}_{1},\tilde{U}_{2}]^{T}

\displaystyle\widehat{A}S(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)^{\dagger}(S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S)

where
Lemma

2
and
S^{T}\!\widetilde{A}_{\epsilon}S\tilde{U}_{2}=0

\square

We now analyze the quantity

Lemma 10
.

Let
fl([AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}S^{T}\!A]_{ij})
be obtained by first computing an
\epsilon

\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}

and then solving linear systems
of the form
fl([AS]_{k}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{\dagger})

k=i,j
, using a backward stable underdetermined linear solver.
Then

fl\left([AS(S^{T}\!AS)^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}S^{T}\!A]_{ij}\right)=[AS+u_{*}]_{i}(S^{T}\!AS+u_{*})^{\dagger}_{\widetilde{\epsilon}}[AS+u_{*}]_{j}^{T},

(6)

where
\widetilde{\epsilon}\geq\epsilon-u_{*}

Proof.

Recall that
fl(AS)=AS+u_{*}
. Thus, the overdetermined least-squares linear systems that we need to solve are of the form,

\min_{x}\left\|x\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}-([AS]_{k}+u_{*})\right\|_{2}

(7)

for
k=i,j
. Since the matrix
\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}
is numerically full-rank, by
[
25

the computed solution satisfies

fl\left(\left([AS]_{k}+u_{*}\right)\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\right)=\left(\left([AS]_{k}+u_{*}\right)+u_{*}\right)\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}=\left([AS]_{k}+u_{*}\right)\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}.

It remains to multiply
([AS]_{i}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}
and
\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger T}([AS]_{j}+u_{*})^{T}

\displaystyle fl\left(([AS]_{i}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger T}([AS]_{j}+u_{*})^{T}\right)=

\displaystyle\qquad=([AS]_{i}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger T}([AS]_{j}+u_{*})^{T}

\displaystyle\qquad+u_{*}\left\|([AS]_{i}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}\left\|([AS]_{j}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}.

We now prove that

\left\|([AS]_{k}+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}=\text{\O}(1)

\displaystyle\left\|([AS]+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(S^{T}\!Q)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|(S^{T}\!AS+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\text{\O}(1)\left\|\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{T}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+\epsilon+u_{*}\right)\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\text{\O}(1)\left(\left\|\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{T}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\right)\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}+\left\|(\epsilon+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}\right).

\sigma_{k}((S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon})-u_{*}\leq\sigma_{k}((S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon}+u_{*})\leq\sigma_{k}((S^{T}\!AS)_{\epsilon})+u_{*}.

\displaystyle\|(\epsilon+u_{*})\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\text{O}(1),

completing the proof of (

At the same time, being product of two rank

\sigma_{k}\left(\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{T}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)\right)=0.

Hence

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{T}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)\right)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)^{T}\left(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}+u_{*}\right)\right)_{\widetilde{\epsilon}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left(S^{T}\!AS+u_{*}\right)^{\dagger}_{\widetilde{\epsilon}}.

Combining all the steps, we arrive at the desired result.

\displaystyle+u_{*}+{\widetilde{\epsilon}}\sqrt{nr}

\displaystyle\|{A-A_{N,\epsilon}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{nr}(n(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A))\sqrt{nr}+{\widetilde{\epsilon}}\sqrt{nr}+u_{*}

\displaystyle=(4n\sqrt{nr}(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+\widetilde{\epsilon})\sqrt{nr}+u_{*}

In particular

\left|A_{ij}-\left[fl\left(A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}\right)\right]_{ij}\right|<(4n\sqrt{nr}(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+\widetilde{\epsilon})\sqrt{nr}+u_{*}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|{A-fl\left(A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}\right)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|A_{ij}-\left[fl\left(A_{N}^{(\epsilon)}\right)\right]_{ij}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(4n\sqrt{nr}(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+\widetilde{\epsilon})\sqrt{nr}+u_{*}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(4n\sqrt{nr}(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+\widetilde{\epsilon})\sqrt{nr})^{2}+u_{*}}

\displaystyle=n\sqrt{nr}\left(4n\sqrt{nr}(r+1)\sigma_{r+1}(A)+\epsilon+u_{*}\right)+u_{*},

where in the third line we use the assumption that
\sigma_{r+1}(A)\leq\|{A}\|_{2}=1
.

\square

The inequality in
Theorem

11
shows that, when using a locally max-vol indices, the error incurred in computing the Nyström approximation in finite-precision arithmetic is bounded—up to a small polynomial factor—by the error obtained in exact arithmetic. This establishes the numerical stability of the method when implemented as described in
Lemma

10
.
