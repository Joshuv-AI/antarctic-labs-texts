Antithrombotic therapy and risk of intracranial hemorrhage in patients with cerebral cavernous malformations: a multicenter propensity-matched cohort study
Authors: Hamza Adel Salim, Dhairya A Lakhani, Orabi Hajjeh, Muhammed Amir Essibayi, Huanwen Chen, Aneri Balar, Nimer Adeeb, Ahmed Msherghi, Melissa Jung, Adam A Dmytriw, Marco Colasurdo, Ajay Malhotra, Dheeraj Gandhi, Vivek Yedavalli, FEymen Ucisik, Rami W Eldaya, Max Wintermark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe safety of antithrombotic therapy in patients with cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) without prior intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) remains uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicenter, propensity score-matched cohort study using the TriNetX Analytics Platform. Adult patients with CCMs and no prior ICH were included. Patients receiving antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelet or anticoagulant) were compared with those receiving no antithrombotic therapy. The primary outcome was ICH; secondary outcomes included seizures, inpatient readmission, and emergency department (ED) visits. Subgroup analyses evaluated outcomes by antithrombotic class. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.ResultsAmong 8989 eligible patients, 1540 in each group were matched. ICH occurred more frequently in the antithrombotic group (6.3%) than in the no-therapy group (3.9%) (HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.28–2.44; log-rank p < 0.001). Seizures (18.6% vs 10.9%; HR 1.84, 95% CI 1.52–2.23), inpatient readmission (57.3% vs 31.8%; HR 2.38, 95% CI 2.13–2.65), and ED visits (39.3% vs 28.4%; HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.50–1.92) were more common. Introduction

Cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs) are complex vascular lesions that occur in the central nervous system; they are the second most common incidental finding on brain magnetic resonance imaging [1, 2] and account for nearly 5–10% of all cerebral vascular pathologies [3]. Despite their benign nature, CCMs can lead to serious complications such as seizures and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), with an estimated annual risk of ICH ranging from 2 to 6% [4, 5].

Complications of CCMs have been linked to multiple factors, including lesion location, genetic mutations, structural fragility, and disruption of the blood–brain barrier [6, 7]. More recently, the role of vascular occlusion within the lesion has gained attention in the literature as a potential contributor to both hemorrhagic and non-hemorrhagic complications [8, 9]. CCM can be treated with a variety of modalities to prevent or minimize complications. Microsurgery is the first-line treatment for accessible and symptomatic lesions; stereotactic radiosurgery may be considered though it carries postsurgical bleeding risk [10].

Antithrombotic agents are commonly prescribed for comorbid vascular conditions that affect approximately one quarter of patients with CCMs [8]. However, evidence regarding their safety in this population is inconsistent. Some studies suggest a protective effect, while others report an increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage [8, 11–13]. Existing studies are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, and incomplete adjustment for confounders [8, 11, 12].

In light of that, we aim, in this retrospective, multicenter, propensity-matched study, to evaluate whether antithrombotic therapy increases the risk of adverse outcomes in patients with CCMs who have never bled.

Methods

This study was conducted using the TriNetX Analytics Platform (https://trinetx.com/) and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [14].

Study population and inclusion criteria

We included adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with CCMs without any prior history of intracranial hemorrhage. Two non-overlapping cohorts were created: one comprising patients who received antithrombotic therapy (either antiplatelet or anticoagulant) and another comprising those who received no antithrombotic therapy. Antithrombotic exposure was defined as initiation of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy at any time after the diagnosis of cerebral cavernous malformation. The index event was defined as the first qualifying encounter, and clinical outcomes were assessed over a 5-year period following the index date.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was ICH. Secondary outcomes included seizures, inpatient readmission, and emergency department (ED) visits. All outcomes were assessed using ICD-10 diagnostic codes over a 5-year follow-up period, beginning one day after the index event, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Data source

TriNetX is a federated network of anonymized electronic health records from over 120 healthcare organizations across multiple countries, representing more than 275 million patients. Data includes inpatient and outpatient encounters, medications, diagnoses, and procedures, standardized via ICD-10 and RXNORM codes. TriNetX applies natural language processing to extract structured and unstructured clinical information, and only aggregate-level data is accessed to ensure patient privacy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using TriNetX’s built-in analytics tools. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 1:1 ratio using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Matching covariates included age, sex, race, hypertension, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, prior cerebral infarction, venous thromboembolism, and substance use. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized differences (ASD), with values < 0.1 considered acceptable.

Time-to-event analyses were performed for all outcomes, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using log-rank testing. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To explore the differential effect of specific antithrombotic agents, subgroup analyses were performed separately for patients treated exclusively with anticoagulants and those treated exclusively with antiplatelet agents. Each subgroup was independently propensity-matched 1:1 to a cohort of patients who received no antithrombotic therapy using the same matching covariates, and outcomes were evaluated using the same time-to-event analytical approach.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 8989 patients met eligibility criteria, including 1,833 who received antithrombotic therapy and 7,156 who received no such therapy. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 1540 patients remained in each group. Prior to matching, patients in the antithrombotic group were older and had a higher prevalence of vascular risk factors, including atrial fibrillation and ischemic heart disease. After matching, the two groups were well balanced on all measured covariates (all ASDs < 0.1). Complete baseline characteristics before and after matching are presented in Table 1.

Outcomes: Antithrombotic vs. No Therapy

In the matched cohort, intracranial hemorrhage occurred more frequently in the antithrombotic group than in the no-therapy group (6.3% vs 3.9%; HR 1.77; 95% CI 1.28–2.44; log-rank p < 0.001). Seizures were also more frequent in the antithrombotic group (18.6% vs 10.9%; HR 1.84; 95% CI 1.52–2.23; p < 0.001). In addition, inpatient readmission (57.3% vs 31.8%; HR 2.38; 95% CI 2.13–2.65; p < 0.001) and emergency department visits (39.3% vs 28.4%; HR 1.70; 95% CI 1.50–1.92; p < 0.001) were significantly more common. Subgroup analysis: anticoagulant vs. no therapy

Among patients treated with anticoagulant therapy alone, the rate of intracranial hemorrhage was significantly higher compared with matched patients receiving no antithrombotic therapy (6.8% vs 3.6%; HR 2.14; 95% CI 1.31–3.51; p = 0.002). Discussion

In this large, matched study of patients with CCMs without a history of intracranial hemorrhage, intracranial hemorrhage occurred more frequently in the antithrombotic group than in the no-therapy group (6.3% vs 3.9%; HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.28–2.44; log-rank p < 0.001). Among the secondary outcomes, we found that the antithrombotic group had an increased incidence of seizures (18.6% vs 10.9%), inpatient readmission (57.3% vs 31.8%), and ED visits (39.3% vs 28.4%).

CCMs are low-flow, low-pressure malformations composed of thin-walled capillaries devoid of neural tissue [2, 15]. The fragile structure of these malformations predisposes them to rupture and hemorrhage. However, studies have shown that thrombosis within these capillaries might be the cause of hemorrhage and even non-hemorrhagic complications manifesting as focal neurological deficits and seizures [9]. This theory has raised the question about whether using antithrombotic therapy might offer protective effects by mitigating intralesional thrombosis.

Schneble et al.’s prospective cohort study on 87 patients showed a neutral effect in both groups [11]. This finding was also reported in a systematic review of four cohorts [16]. On the other hand, Zubbier et al.’s cohort study on 306 patients, of whom 61 used antithrombotics, found a lower incidence of intracranial hemorrhage [8]. A similar effect was also reported by a multicenter case–control study on 257 patients [17]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of nine cohort studies showed a protective effect of antithrombotic use; however, the authors attributed their findings to possible ascertainment and assignment bias, as well as the lack of control for other medications, such as statins [13].

Although our findings differ from prior studies, most of those studies were limited by smaller sample sizes, the inclusion of patients with prior hemorrhage, and a lack of control for confounding variables. Many of these limitations were addressed, and the validity of our findings is strengthened by our study’s substantial sample size, matched cohorts, and long-term follow-up. However, despite achieving statistical significance, the observed increase in ICH risk was modest and may not translate into a clinically meaningful difference in most cases.

On further subgroup analysis, we found that only anticoagulants increased the incidence of ICH significantly (6.8% vs 3.6%; HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.31–3.51; p = 0.002). By contrast, antiplatelet therapy alone was not significantly associated with intracranial hemorrhage (5.2% vs 4.3%; HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.73–2.08; p = 0.424). These differing effects likely reflect their pharmacological mechanisms. Anticoagulants exert their effect by systemically inhibiting the coagulation pathway, resulting in a lower hemorrhage threshold, [18] especially in vulnerable areas such as CCM. On the other hand, antiplatelets act primarily on primary hemostasis, exerting a more localized effect, which may explain the absence of significant association [19].

Interestingly, we found a significant increased incidence of seizures in the antithrombotic group (18.6% vs 10.9%; HR 1.84, 95% CI 1.52–2.23). This evidence further reinforces the need to deepen our understanding of the disease, particularly in light of literature linking CCM complications to thrombus formation, which may contribute to focal neurological deficits and other outcomes.

Our findings carry important clinical implications. Although traditional recommendations advised against antithrombotic use in CCMs, recent literature has challenged this position. Our results reassert the need for cautious, individualized risk–benefit assessment, particularly when anticoagulation is considered. There is a critical need for further research into the natural history of CCMs and the differential impact of various antithrombotic agents to inform evidence-based guidelines and optimize patient outcomes.

Our study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the largest real-world analyses of antithrombotic therapy in patients with CCMs without prior hemorrhage. The large sample size, long-term follow-up, and large geographic and institutional representation enhance the generalizability of our results. Incorporating subgroup analyses for anticoagulant and antiplatelet treatments offers a more comprehensive insight into risk stratification in this at-risk population. Ultimately, the use of propensity score matching across several clinically relevant covariates improves internal validity and reduces the likelihood of selection bias.

However, our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis of real-world electronic health record data, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite propensity score matching, and identification of cerebral cavernous malformations relied on ICD-10-CM coding, as no dedicated ICD-10 code exists for this condition; although competing intracranial vascular malformations and neoplastic diagnoses were excluded to improve cohort specificity, some misclassification may persist. Second, outcomes were identified using diagnosis codes without access to diagnostic position, imaging confirmation, or clinician attribution and therefore represent all-cause intracranial hemorrhage rather than definitively cavernoma-related events. Third, granular lesion-level data, including cavernoma location, size, multiplicity, and genetic factors, were unavailable, precluding adjustment for established hemorrhagic risk modifiers. Finally, detailed information on antithrombotic indication, duration, adherence, and treatment continuity during follow-up could not be fully ascertained, introducing potential confounding by indication.

Conclusion

In this large, multicenter, propensity-matched cohort study of patients with CCMs without prior ICH, antithrombotic therapy was associated with an increased risk of ICH, seizures, inpatient readmission, and emergency department visits. Importantly, this observed risk was primarily driven by anticoagulant use, whereas antiplatelet therapy was not associated with a significantly increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage. These findings highlight the need for individualized risk–benefit assessment when considering antithrombotic therapy in this patient population and highlight the importance of future prospective studies to further inform clinical decision-making.
