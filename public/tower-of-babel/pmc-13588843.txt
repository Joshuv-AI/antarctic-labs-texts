ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewTherapeutic goals in BCR::ABL1-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are evolving to include biologically anchored measures of disease modification with control of blood counts, splenomegaly, and symptoms. Disease modification endpoints and their linkage to survival in MPNs were central to the framework of MPN Asia 2026, which took place in Seoul. Introduction

The 9th Annual International Symposium on Myeloproliferative Neoplasms, MPN Asia 2026, provided a timely forum to examine how therapeutic objectives in BCR::ABL1-negative MPNs are being redefined. Across sessions on polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and myelofibrosis (MF), spanning disease biology, clonal evolution, and clinical implementation, a recurring question emerged: can contemporary therapies achieve more than hematologic and symptomatic control, and can they meaningfully alter the natural history of the disease?

This question is particularly important because the major complications of MPNs occur over different time scales. PV and ET are chronic diseases with a natural history spanning decades, and patients remain at risk for thrombosis, hemorrhage, symptom burden, treatment intolerance, progression to secondary MF, and blast-phase transformation. In contrast, primary or secondary MF is associated with higher near-term risks of disease-related morbidity and mortality, driven by constitutional symptoms, splenomegaly, progressive bone marrow fibrosis with marrow-failure cytopenias, clonal evolution linked to leukemic transformation, and a frequent need for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Consequently, no single clinical or laboratory parameter is likely to capture disease modification across the MPN spectrum [1–4].

Molecular response has emerged as an important investigational and clinical endpoint in chronic phase MPNs. In PV, serial assessment of JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF), or allele burden, provides a practical and meaningful measure of disease biology. JAK2V617F molecular response was associated with improved event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) [5, 6].

In ET, interpretation of molecular response is complicated by driver heterogeneity, particularly between JAK2-mutated and CALR-mutated disease. In MF, molecular profiling is already incorporated into prognostic models and therapeutic decision-making, but more so to identify concurrent high molecular risk mutations than to monitor driver mutation VAF response. A recent phase II study including patients with prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis (prePMF) and low to intermediate-1 risk MF demonstrated that ropeginterferon alfa-2b, a monopegylated interferon-based therapy, is effective in reducing both JAK2V617F and CALR mutant VAFs [7]. However, these reductions in driver mutation burden alone have not been validated as surrogate markers of improved survival, disease modification, or treatment-free remission (TFR) for MF [1–4].

The growing interest in survival endpoints, including EFS, progression-free survival (PFS) and OS, as well as TFR, reflects a shift toward long-term therapeutic goals for MPNs beyond conventional clinico-hematologic responses. However, the biological and clinical proxies for survival outcomes and the prerequisites for successful TFR need to be defined. This review summarizes key themes from MPN Asia 2026 and relevant recent literature, examining the evolving roles of molecular response and long-term clinical benefits within a broader disease-modification framework across PV, ET, and MF.

Defining Disease Modification in MPNs

Historically, treatment strategies in PV and ET were directed primarily toward prevention of vascular complications. In PV, management centered on thrombosis prophylaxis with low-dose aspirin, and control of hematocrit, neutrophilia, and thrombocytosis with cytoreductive therapy to decrease thrombotic risk. In ET, treatment focused on antiplatelet therapy and cytoreduction in selected patients at increased risk of thrombosis. In MF, management focused on symptom palliation, spleen size reduction, and in some patients, cytoreduction of extreme leukocytosis and thrombocytosis, alongside supportive care for cytopenias, or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation for select patients.

Although the discovery of JAK2, CALR, and MPL mutations reframed our diagnostic approach, risk stratification, and treatment of MPNs, it did not automaticallydefine a measurable biomarker for disease modification and therapeutic monitoring [8]. A clinically meaningful definition of disease modification encompasses multiple domains, not just symptomatic benefit and thrombotic prevention. At the clinical level, disease-modifying therapy is expected to reduce disease-related events (symptoms, thrombosis, hemorrhage), progression to MF, leukemic transformation, and mortality. Although these outcomes differ in mechanism, acuity, and clinical impact, their cumulative incidence reflects the overall burden of disease and provides a practical measure of whether therapy is altering the clinical course. At the hematologic level, disease modification should provide sustained control of erythrocytosis, thrombocytosis, and leukocytosis with an acceptable safety profile. From a biological standpoint, disease-modifying treatment needs to suppress the malignant clone and deplete the disease-originating mutant stem cells and reactivate normal dormant stem cells, attenuate disease-sustaining inflammation, and improve bone marrow architecture and fibrosis. Among currently available therapies, interferon-based therapies have a strong track record of disease modification. For example, the PROUD-PV and CONTINUATION-PV trials provide strong evidence supporting disease-modifying potential of ropeginterferon alfa-2b, demonstrating durable hematologic responses together with deep and durable reductions in JAK2V617F VAF [9, 10]. In clinical studies with ropeginterferon alfa-2b, molecular response as defined by the European Leukemia Net (ELN) criteria (≥ 50% reduction if baseline VAF is < 50%; or ≥ 25% reduction if baseline VAF is > 50%; applies only to patients with a baseline VAF value > 10%) [11] were found to be associated with improved EFS in patients with PV [6, 12, 13]. Ruxolitinib also shows disease-modifying potential. The MAJIC-PV trial demonstrated that a ≥ 50% reduction in JAK2V617F VAF with ruxolitinib treatment correlated with improved EFS (P = 0.001), PFS (P = 0.001), and OS (P = 0.01) [5].

Despite these advances, molecular response endpoints require additional validation to serve as surrogates for survival outcomes. A meta-analysis of JAK2V617F VAF in PV confirmed associations between higher VAF and adverse hematologic and clinical characteristics, as well as increased risk of disease progression, while also highlighting substantial heterogeneity among studies and the need for standardized assays, response thresholds, and longitudinal outcome data before VAF can be considered a universal biomarker of disease modification [14]. Emerging data on hematopoietic stem-cell fitness further suggest that stem cell clonal dominance, lineage bias, and impaired lymphoid differentiation may represent more proximal indicators of disease biology and long-term disease trajectory [15].

Interpretation of molecular response is further complicated by differences in assay methodology, sample source and cellular compartment used to measure. Since JAK2V617F or CALR VAF values are typically being measured from whole blood (WB), which contains a variable mixture of clonal myeloid cells and non-clonal lymphoid cells, it may underestimate the true burden of the malignant myeloid clone and may vary according to blood-cell composition at the time of testing. Additionally, since WB VAF has little to no significant stem and progenitor cell contribution, it does not capture the stem cell clonal burden nor the therapeutic effects of stem cell directed therapy. More rigorous assessment of clonal burden requires standardized assays and, where feasible, lineage-specific measurement in purified cellular compartments as performed in many translational studies. These methodological considerations are particularly important when comparing molecular-response rates across trials, defining deep molecular response, or designing treatment-discontinuation strategies.

Inflammation represents a second major biological axis of disease modification. Mechanistic studies have linked disease progression and post-MPN acute myeloid leukemia to inflammatory and stress-response pathways, including DUSP6 and RSK1 signaling [16, 17]. These observations broaden the concept of disease modification beyond reductions in driver-mutation VAF and support therapeutic strategies aimed at suppressing cytokine production, modulating monocyte activation, reversing marrow stromal remodeling, and eliminating persistent malignant stem and progenitor cell populations.

Taken together, these findings suggest that disease modification needs to be viewed as a multidimensional construct rather than a single endpoint. Molecular response is an important component of this framework, but its ultimate significance will depend on demonstrating durable improvements in clinically meaningful outcomes and potentially enabling TFR in selected patients.

Polycythemia Vera: The Model MPN for Molecular Response Assessment and Disease Modification

Of the MPNs, PV has the most robust evidence from clinical trials and real-world studies for the feasibility of assessing molecular response, EFS, and disease modification, as well as identifying conditions for TFR [5, 6, 18]. The near-universal presence of JAK2V617F provides a consistent molecular marker that can be quantified from peripheral blood specimens for monitoring treatment response in PV. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b has been distinguished from the non-specific cytoreductive hydroxyurea by its capacity to progressively reduce JAK2V617F VAF and induce molecular responses, which was found to correlate with improved EFS [6, 9]. The JAK2V617F VAF reduction was associated with ropeginterferon alfa-2b exposure (average concentration) in vivo and its treatment at a higher initial dose and accelerated titration (HIDAT) regimen induced high levels of molecular response, as defined by the ELN criteria [11], in 37 of 43 patients (86.0%) at 3 years of treatment [13, 19]. JAK2 inhibition by ruxolitinib has also been shown to induce molecular response-associated survival advantage in PV [5]. A central scientific question is whether the depth and durability of molecular response translate into outcomes that matter to patients, including reduced vascular risk, delayed disease progression, improved quality of life, side effects of therapy and their tolerability, and the possibility of safely discontinuing therapy.

Interferon Biology and Clonal Suppression

Although JAK2V617F and JAK2 exon 12 mutations drive constitutive JAK-STAT signaling, disease phenotype is further shaped by germline predisposition [20], cooperating somatic mutations and mutation order [21, 22], inflammatory milieu [23], iron homeostasis [24], vascular risk factors [2, 25], and, in erythrocytosis biology more broadly, hypoxia-sensing pathways [26, 27]. In PV and ET, augmented hypoxia-inducible factor signaling has been linked to a thrombo-inflammatory transcriptional program, including upregulation of thrombotic, inflammatory, and HIF-regulated genes in granulocytes and platelets, with higher expression of selected genes among patients with thrombosis [28, 29]. Interferon may also modify this biology: pegylated interferon-α, unlike hydroxyurea, has been associated with reductions in inflammatory signaling and induction of KLF2, an antithrombotic transcriptional regulator whose downregulation in PV and ET is linked to prothrombotic gene expression [30, 31]. Interferon may exert anti-clonal activity through multiple mechanisms, consisting of direct effects on mutant stem and progenitor cells, JAK2-mutated peripheral blood cells including neutrophils, immune modulation, suppression of cytokine-driven signaling, and partial restoration of polyclonal hematopoiesis [31, 32].

In a JAK2V617F-induced murine MPN model, TYK2 was shown to be essential for the therapeutic effect of IFN-α [33], while earlier murine and translational studies demonstrated depletion or targeting of JAK2V617F-mutant stem and progenitor compartments after interferon exposure [31, 34]. Type 1 interferons preferentially inhibit the cell cycle progression of human transformed or malignant cells and induce malignant cell senescence associated with a loss of tumorigenicity [35–37]. These findings support direct effects of interferon signaling on JAK2-mutant clones.

Ropeginterferon alfa-2b, an interferon-based therapy, reduces JAK2V617F VAF potentially through preferential targeting of JAK2-mutated, transformed cells, via interferon-mediated growth-regulatory pathways [38]. Its mechanism of action is different from ruxolitinib which progressively reduces JAK2V617F VAF by suppressing the JAK1 and JAK2 kinase activities, leading to inhibition of the hyperactive signaling that promotes abnormal cell growth, cytokine production, and inflammation [39].

Clinical Evidence Supporting Earlier Intervention

The MAJIC-PV and PROUD-PV/CONTINUATION-PV studies established well-characterized therapeutic models linking molecular response with survival outcomes [5, 6, 9]. Importantly, an analysis of patients with PV reported fewer clinical events among those achieving molecular response and demonstrated an association between lower JAK2V617F burden and lower modeled event risk [6]. These findings strengthen the biological and clinical rationale for incorporating molecular response into therapeutic goals in PV.

The low-risk PV setting has become particularly relevant to discussions of early intervention. The Low-PV trial demonstrated that ropeginterferon alfa-2b combined with phlebotomy and aspirin improved maintenance of hematocrit targets compared with conventional phlebotomy-based management [40]. The extension study similarly reported hematocrit control, together with reductions in moderate-to-severe symptom burden and palpable splenomegaly among responders [41]. Reduction of JAK2V617F VAF was also reported among the participants who received ropeginterferon alfa-2b.

Emerging Evidence from Asia

Studies from Asia provide important complementary evidence. In a Japanese low-risk PV cohort of 20 patients, ropeginterferon alfa-2b achieved hematocrit control below 45% in 60% and 85% of patients at weeks 24 and 52, respectively; and complete hematologic response (CHR) was achieved in 30% and 60% at the same time points. Mean JAK2V617F decreased from 75.8% at baseline to 53.7% at week 52, and no thrombotic or bleeding events were reported [42]. A long-term, 36-month extension study of 27 Japanese patients with PV showed increasing CHR rates without phlebotomy at 12, 24, and 36 months, from 29.6% to 66.7% and 81.5%, respectively. At 36 months, the median JAK2V617F allele burden change from baseline was − 74.8%, with no thrombotic or hemorrhagic events during follow-up [43].

Chinese phase 2 data using the HIDAT strategy reported a high CHR rate of 61.2% at 24 weeks of treatment, accompanied by reductions in JAK2V617F VAF [44]. Similarly, a Japanese study using the HIDAT regimen showed a CHR rate of 57.1% with most patients (17/21; 81.0%) having a decrease in JAK2V617F at 24 weeks [45]. Follow up over 2 years in the Chinese phase 2 study showed that JAK2V617F VAF continued to decrease despite stable CHR rates. At two years of treatment, the median JAK2V617F VAF declined from 61.2% at baseline to 7.8% and the rate of molecular response as defined by the ELN criteria [11] was observed in 36/44 patients (81.8%) [46]. Together with the exposure-response analysis [19], the data suggest that high molecular response rate is dependent upon adequate drug exposure. A small Taiwanese consecutive case series employing HIDAT regimen in younger patients with PV or ET likewise reported favorable hematologic responses and complete molecular response in 2 of 5 cases assessed for JAK2V617F VAF [47]. Currently there are no side-by-side comparisons regarding molecular response between ropeginterferon alfa-2b and ruxolitinib. Exploratory analysis of the international RESPONSE trial showed that ruxolitinib treatment also led to notable molecular responses, with a molecular response rate of 34.4% observed approximately at 3 years [39].

Korean prospective data further strengthen this regional evidence base. In a multicenter phase 2 study of 99 patients with PV requiring cytoreductive therapy, ropeginterferon alfa-2b was administered using the HIDAT regimen. CHR increased from 27% at week 12 to 63% at week 48, while molecular response as defined by the ELN criteria increased from 32% to 57% over the same period [48].

The Korean data are also informative for patients with inadequate response to hydroxyurea. In the hydroxyurea-resistant or -intolerant subgroup of the same clinical study, one-year results showed CHR in 52.8% and molecular response in 39.4%, with a mean JAK2V617F VAF reduction of 16.95%; no patient discontinued therapy because of adverse events [49]. A subsequent propensity-matched real-world comparison reported a higher 48-week CHR rate with ropeginterferon alfa-2b than with available salvage therapies (52% versus 15%; p < 0.0001), supporting its role as a clinically active option [50].

Predictive biomarkers remain at an early stage of development. A Taiwanese cohort of ropeginterferon-treated MPN patients reported CHR within one year in 70% of patients and major molecular response (VAF decrease by more than 90%) in 45%, while suggesting that ITPA polymorphisms may be associated with inferior hematologic or molecular responses and increased susceptibility to hepatotoxicity [51]. Although these observations are not yet ready for routine clinical implementation, they illustrate the type of pharmacogenetic research that may eventually enable individualized interferon strategies aimed at long-term clonal suppression. Consistent with this broader applicability, sensitivity analyses of ropeginterferon alfa-2b have not demonstrated major pharmacokinetic, efficacy, or safety differences among different ethnic groups [52].

However, some evidence suggests that tolerability may vary across patient populations. In an early phase 1/2 study in PV, treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was reported in 20% of Caucasian participants receiving ropeginterferon alfa-2b [53]. More recently, adjunctive metformin therapy was reported to improve ropeginterferon-related fatigue and/or myalgias in 10 of 11 patients with PV or ET [54]. These observations warrant further investigation in randomized clinical trials, as well as mechanistic studies to better understand interferon-related adverse events and strategies to improve long-term treatment persistence.

Biomarker Monitoring for Clinical Benefits Including TFR and EFS in PV

TFR in PV remains an area of considerable clinical and scientific interest. Achieving a CHR and durable suppression of the JAK2V617F VAF to < 10% with interferon therapy potentially allows for successful treatment cessation without subsequent hematologic relapse [55, 56]. Studies with ropeginterferon alfa-2b further indicated the importance of a robust molecular response in potentially predicting sustainable remission after treatment discontinuation [57]. However, published evidence for discontinuation after long-term interferon therapy remains limited. A credible discontinuation framework would likely require sustained phlebotomy-free CHR, deep and durable molecular response, an adequate consolidation period after achievement of response, and protocol-defined monitoring after treatment cessation. Criteria for relapse should encompass loss of hematocrit control, renewed phlebotomy requirements, clinically meaningful platelet or leukocyte increase, symptom recurrence, vascular events, disease progression, and molecular progression beyond predefined thresholds.

Early JAK2V617F kinetics and inflammatory biomarkers may provide complementary information for monitoring treatment response. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) half reduction, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline, has been associated with both hematologic and molecular responses in patients with PV receiving ropeginterferon alfa-2b. In a Korean secondary analysis of 95 patients with PV treated for 48 weeks, NLR half reduction predicted hematologic response at week 24 (odds ratio [OR], 6.42; p = 0.001) and molecular response at week 24 (OR, 27.94; p < 0.001), with the molecular response correlation persisting at week 48 (OR, 20.58; p < 0.001) [58]. These observations are concordant with analyses in PV: a prospective ECLAP cohort linked NLR ≥ 5 with worse overall survival and more than twofold increase in mortality compared to NLR < 5, whereas analyses of ECLAP, Low-PV, and PROUD-PV/CONTINUATION-PV found that ropeginterferon alfa-2b reduced NLR, with NLR reduction correlating with JAK2V617F VAF reduction and EFS [59, 60]. A subsequent viewpoint proposed JAK2V617F VAF and NLR as complementary biology-guided endpoints for future PV studies [61]. Such markers should not replace molecular testing, but they may help integrate inflammatory activity and clonal dynamics into routine monitoring strategies using this inexpensive and routinely obtained laboratory parameter.

An additional consideration is that JAK2V617F VAF may not fully quantify the entire malignant clone in PV. In a study of patients with JAK2V617F-positive MPN who subsequently transformed to acute myeloid leukemia, leukemic blasts were frequently JAK2V617F-negative, and clonal analyses supported a shared JAK2V617F-negative ancestral clone in selected cases [62]. These observations imply that a primordial pre-JAK2V617F clone may persist beneath the clinically dominant JAK2-mutant population and may contribute to disease evolution or leukemic transformation.

Non-interferon-based Approaches in PV

Ruxolitinib remains an important treatment option for patients with PV who have had an inadequate response to or who are resistant or intolerant to hydroxyurea. Next generation JAK inhibitors such as type 2 JAK2 inhibitors or JAK2 mutation selective inhibitors are in development for MF and may potentially be useful for the management of PV [63–65]. Combination of ruxolitinib with pegylated interferon alfa-2a also showed notable efficacy, with peripheral blood cell count remission observed in 92% of patients and a molecular response rate of 68% in a phase 2 setting [66]. These approaches may offer disease-modifying benefits with their known mechanism of action, yet data including survival outcomes such as EFS remains to be seen. Iron/hepcidin-axis strategies with rusfertide, sapablursen, divesiran and others address important unmet needs by reducing phlebotomy burden and improving hematocrit control. Rusfertide has demonstrated control of hematocrit levels, phlebotomy need and symptoms in PV through hepcidin mimetic activity [67, 68]. At present, these approaches are viewed primarily as hematocrit-control and symptom-management strategies rather than established approaches for achieving molecular response, disease modification, or TFR.

Essential Thrombocythemia: Extending Molecular Endpoints Beyond PV

ET has traditionally been managed through thrombotic-risk stratification, an approach that remains clinically important but is increasingly recognized as incomplete. In addition to thrombosis, patients may experience substantial symptom burden, impaired quality of life, treatment intolerance, hemorrhagic complications, pregnancy-related concerns, and long-term risks of myelofibrotic or leukemic transformation. ET is also biologically heterogeneous. JAK2-mutated disease is more strongly associated with thrombosis, whereas CALR-mutated disease often exhibits a distinct clinical phenotype, including lower thrombotic risk but a greater propensity for myelofibrotic progression; MPL-mutated ET is less common but has also been associated with several fold increased risk of progression compared to JAK2-mutated ET. Unlike PV, the genetic heterogeneity calls for driver-specific approaches to molecular monitoring and response assessment [3, 69–72].

Molecular Response in Clinical Development

The SURPASS-ET trial was a major focus of discussion at MPN Asia 2026. In patients with high-risk, hydroxyurea-resistant or hydroxyurea-intolerant ET accompanied by leukocytosis, ropeginterferon alfa-2b demonstrated clinical benefits over anagrelide in achieving durable modified ELN responses measured at months 9 and 12 [73]. The modified ELN response was defined as simultaneous achievement of: (i) peripheral blood count remission (platelets ≤ 400 × 10⁹/L and WBC < 9.5 × 10⁹/L); (ii) improvement or non-progression of splenomegaly, assessed clinically and by ultrasound if palpable at baseline; (iii) symptom improvement or non-progression by the MPN-Symptoms Assessment Form (MPN-SAF) Total Symptom Score (TSS), with prespecified thresholds by baseline score; (iv) absence of hemorrhagic or thrombotic events; and (v) durable response at both months 9 and 12 [73]. The study is particularly relevant to the Asian MPN community because it enrolled the majority of patients from multiple sites in Asia, as well as other international sites. Durable modified ELN criteria responses at both months 9 and 12, were seen in 42.9% of patients (39/91) in the ropeginterferon alfa-2b arm compared with 6.0% (5/83) in the anagrelide arm (p = 0.0001). Major thrombotic events occurred in 1 (1.1%) of 91 in the ropeginterferon alfa-2b arm and in 8 (10.0%) participants in the anagrelide arm (p = 0.0131) [73]. Reported reductions in both JAK2V617F and CALR VAF with ELN-defined molecular response rates (including both partial and complete molecular response rates) being 30.6% for JAK2V617F and 30.0% for CALR. The data provide further support for evaluating ropeginterferon alfa-2b as a potential disease-modifying therapy in ET. Whether these molecular responses correlate with reductions in thrombosis or survival-related outcomes such as EFS remains to be assessed in the future. Complementing SURPASS-ET, the EXCEED-ET clinical trial provides a North American perspective on ropeginterferon alfa-2b in a broader ET population that included both treatment-naive and hydroxyurea-pretreated patients [74]. This single-arm, multicenter study enrolled 91 patients across 28 centers in the United States and Canada and used a similar HIDAT dosing schedule. Durable modified ELN response at both months 10 and 13 was achieved by 60.2% of patients, with a median time to hematologic response of 8.4 weeks. Molecular responses were observed across all 3 MPN driver mutations. At 13 months, ELN-defined molecular response rates were 35.0% for JAK2V617F, 16.0% for CALR, and 25.0% for MPL [74]. These findings, alongside future studies correlating molecular response to EFS, would support the utilization of molecular response as a surrogate endpoint in the management of ET.

Real-World Perspective from Asia

Population-based studies from Taiwan provide relevant context regarding the clinical burden of ET in Asia. A cross-sectional analysis demonstrated a substantial ET burden, frequent use of hydroxyurea and anagrelide, and a high recurrence rate among patients with a history of thrombosis [75]. A separate five-year population-based study documented increasing prevalence, evolving treatment patterns, and a lower risk of thrombosis among patients treated with hydroxyurea compared with antiplatelet therapy alone [76].

Although these studies did not incorporate molecular endpoints, the data highlight the clinical outcomes that future disease-modifying strategies in ET need to improve. Given ET is associated with an increasing prevalence and a generally chronic clinical course that can be complicated with events, whether a biomarker such as molecular response that can serve as a surrogate endpoint to determine risk and response to potentially disease-modifying therapy is of utmost importance to address.

Emerging Molecularly Directed Therapies

Several emerging therapies may further reshape the ET treatment landscape by bringing molecular endpoints closer to clinical development. Bomedemstat, an LSD1 inhibitor, has demonstrated hematologic activity together with reductions in driver-mutant VAF in previously treated ET, and phase 3 evaluation is ongoing [77]. In parallel, mutant CALR-directed therapy with INCA033989 represents one of the first mutation-specific therapeutic strategies in ET. Early phase 1 data in patients with mutant CALR-expressing ET or MF showed rapid and durable platelet count normalization in ET, hematologic responses, reductions in mutant CALR VAF, and exploratory marrow findings consistent with reduced mutant CALR-positive megakaryopoiesis, supporting continued evaluation while longer follow-up is awaited [78]. Furthermore, other mutant CALR-targeted therapies and JAK2V617F mutant-selective inhibitors are also being developed for the treatment of ET [79, 80].

If these findings are confirmed in larger and longer-term studies, molecular response assessment in ET may become increasingly precise and biologically tailored according to driver mutation. Such advances would represent an important step toward a more personalized framework for disease modification, particularly if molecular responses can be linked to reductions in thrombosis, disease progression, symptom burden, or treatment dependency.

EFS and TFR in ET

The EFS and TFR data of potentially disease-modifying therapies in ET remain to be established since there is less studied a lack of results from randomized, prospective clinical trials. The ongoing SURPASS-ET extension study with ropeginterferon alfa-2b could provide long-term clinical outcomes including EFS in the future. For TFR, the feasibility of interferon therapy discontinuation was also assessed in ET [55, 56]. However, disease heterogeneity, the absence of validated molecular-response thresholds, and limited long-term data linking molecular responses to clinical outcomes currently preclude routine consideration of TFR as a therapeutic goal. Future studies will need to provide long-term clinical benefits and define the molecular prerequisites for treatment discontinuation, establish clinically meaningful relapse criteria, and determine the long-term safety of treatment-free strategies in carefully selected patients.

Myelofibrosis: Molecular Risk, Early Intervention, and Composite Endpoints

MF differs fundamentally from PV and ET in its clinical urgency, disease biology, and therapeutic priorities. Patients may present with constitutional symptoms, splenomegaly, anemia, thrombocytopenia, transfusion dependence, cachexia, advanced bone marrow fibrosis, and a substantially increased risk of leukemic transformation. Consequently, molecular testing has become indispensable in MF; however, its principal role remains prognostic, and for therapeutic decision-making regarding transplant [81], rather than serving as a validated measure of molecular response [4]. Nonetheless, molecular responses have been observed in several studies in prefibrotic and overt MF [5, 82].

Molecular Profiling as a Foundation for Risk-Adapted Care

The MPN Asia meeting reviewed the evolution of prognostic assessment from clinically based systems such as the Dynamic International Prognostic Scoring System (DIPSS) and DIPSS-plus to molecularly integrated models, including the Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Scoring System for transplant-age patients with primary myelofibrosis (MIPSS70), MIPSS70 + version 2.0, and the Myelofibrosis Secondary to PV and ET-Prognostic Model (MYSEC-PM) [83, 84]. High-molecular-risk mutations, TP53 alterations, cytogenetic abnormalities, driver-mutation status, and VAF patterns increasingly inform transplantation timing, clinical trial prioritization, and post-transplant monitoring. These advances have improved risk stratification and personalized treatment planning. Unlike in PV, where reductions in JAK2V617F burden are increasingly explored as markers of disease modification, MF lacks validated molecular-response thresholds linked to long-term clinical benefit. MF is associated with clonal and mutational complexity underlying the risk of leukemic transformation [85]. Lower JAK2V617F VAFs could be found in patients who eventually have leukemia [86]. Therefore, JAK2VAF is less of a predictor of leukemia in MF, depending on whether the disease progression is driven by competing clones or a JAK2 mutation-carrying subclone.

Early Intervention and Disease Modification

The concept of early intervention in MF emerged as an important theme at MPN Asia 2026. Biologically, earlier treatment may offer a greater opportunity to influence clonal evolution, inflammatory signaling, and fibrotic progression before potentially irreversible disease features become established.

Supporting this concept, a phase 2 study of ropeginterferon alfa-2b in prefibrotic PMF and lower-risk fibrotic primary or secondary MF requiring cytoreduction reported clinicohematologic complete response in 63.5% of patients at 48 weeks of treatment. Reductions in JAK2V617F in 70% of patients and CALR mutant VAF were seen in 70% and 42.9% of patients at 48 weeks, respectively. Furthermore, improvements in bone marrow reticulin fibrosis were observed among evaluable patients at week 104 [7]. These findings are relevant to the disease-modification paradigm, particularly in earlier-stage MF, but require prospective validation and longer follow-up for progression, leukemic transformation, and survival outcomes. In this regard, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial to assess the efficacy and safety of ropeginterferon alfa-2b in patients with prefibrotic or lower risk PMF (HOPE-PMF) is ongoing with patient recruitment completed [87]. The early intervention to inhibit fibrotic progression could also apply to the JAK inhibitors or their combination with interferon therapy. In addition, targeting fibrosis-promoting neoplastic cells such as fibrocytes could be another effective antifibrotic strategy [88].

Real-world evidence further highlights the importance of biological timing. A retrospective cohort of ropeginterferon alfa-2b-treated Philadelphia chromosome-negative MPNs demonstrated hematologic and molecular responses across PV, ET, and prefibrotic MF, but more limited efficacy in overt MF, particularly among patients harboring high-molecular-risk mutations [89]. This observation suggests that therapeutic opportunities may diminish as fibrosis, cytopenias, inflammatory burden, and clonal complexity accumulate.

Composite Endpoints for Future MF Studies

Modern MF drug development increasingly recognizes that no single endpoint adequately captures disease modification. As a result, clinical trials are moving toward multidimensional assessment strategies that integrate clinical, biological, and patient-centered outcomes. Combination approaches such as pelabresib plus ruxolitinib have generated data across multiple disease-relevant domains, including spleen response, symptom improvement, anemia outcomes, bone marrow fibrosis, molecular responses, and progression events [90]. Other emerging strategies, including selinexor plus ruxolitinib [91], MDM2 inhibition with navtemadlin [92], telomerase inhibition with imetelstat [93], and anemia-directed therapy with luspatercept [94], target distinct components of MF pathobiology and clinical disease burden.

In MF, meaningful disease modification may ultimately be assessed through composite endpoints that include overall survival, progression-free survival, leukemic transformation, marrow fibrosis and morphology, spleen and symptom responses, anemia outcomes, molecular evolution, and treatment tolerability. Within this framework, molecular response represents one important component of disease assessment rather than the dominant therapeutic objective [95].

TFR currently occupies a limited role in MF, where transplantation offers the only curative option. The biological complexity of advanced disease, the absence of validated molecular-remission criteria, and the continued need for disease-directed therapy in most patients make routine treatment-discontinuation strategies difficult to justify. Maintaining a durable TFR post-transplant is achievable and closely tied to monitoring for molecular relapse [96]. Molecular tracking of JAK2 mutations by using Droplet Digital PCR is a valid approach for monitoring Measurable Residual Disease (MRD) post-transplant. It can successfully guide treatment decisions between relapse intervention and TFR [97–99].

Potential Framework for Molecular Response and TFR Research

The maturity of molecular-response endpoints differs substantially across MPN subtypes. Among the classical MPNs, PV is currently closest to clinical implementation because JAK2V617F is nearly universal, and ropeginterferon alfa-2b and ruxolitinib have both demonstrated JAK2V617F VAF reductions. Prospective treatment-discontinuation strategies are increasingly feasible with existing data [55–57]. ET is entering a similar phase in which molecular response assessment will likely require driver-specific interpretation. In contrast, MF requires a broader composite framework in which molecular findings of driver VAF and co-occurrent mutations are interpreted alongside symptom burden, spleen response, cytopenias, bone marrow fibrosis, disease trajectory, and survival outcomes [2–4, 95].

Future studies of TFR may need to clearly define disease subtype, prior treatment exposure, minimum duration of hematologic response, molecular-response thresholds, assay methodology and sensitivity, duration of sustained molecular response, consolidation requirements before treatment discontinuation, surveillance schedules after treatment cessation, molecular and clinical relapse criteria, and indications for treatment reinitiation. The studies may also need to evaluate whether disease control can be regained after relapse by restarting the same therapeutic agent, and whether treatment discontinuation influences thrombosis, disease progression, quality of life, healthcare utilization, and survival.

Accordingly, future disease-modification research needs to consider emerging measures, including stem-cell fitness, lineage-specific VAF, single-cell genomic approaches, cytokine profiling, bone marrow morphology, and practical inflammatory biomarkers, may provide complementary insights into disease biology and treatment response. The value of molecular response will depend on the depth and durability of VAF reduction, and on its ability to provide meaningful predictions on thrombosis risk, disease progression, and long-term clinical benefits including survival outcomes and durable TFR (Tables 1 and 2).

Future Directions

Several priorities emerged from the discussions at MPN Asia 2026 and may help guide the next phase of research on molecular response, disease modification, EFS, and TFR in MPNs.

First, the field would benefit from greater harmonization of molecular-response terminology. Standardized definitions of deep molecular response, molecular relapse, and clinical relapse are needed to facilitate comparisons across studies and support future regulatory and clinical implementation. Second, prospective TFR studies in PV need to consider incorporating standardized JAK2V617F assays, predefined molecular-response thresholds, structured discontinuation criteria, and prespecified treatment-reinitiation algorithms. Long-term follow-up will be essential to determine whether TFR can be achieved safely without compromising vascular outcomes, disease control, or quality of life [57]. Third, ET research should focus on establishing the clinical relevance of molecular response. Future studies should determine whether reductions in JAK2 or CALR VAF predict thrombosis risk, disease progression, symptom improvement, or durable treatment-free intervals. Driver-specific response frameworks may be particularly important given the biological heterogeneity of ET [3, 69]. Fourth, MF studies should continue to advance composite disease-modification endpoints that integrate survival, disease progression, anemia outcomes, spleen and symptom responses, bone marrow fibrosis, molecular evolution, and patient-reported outcomes. Such multidimensional approaches are likely to provide a more meaningful assessment of disease modification than any individual biomarker [95]. Finally, Asia has played an important role in advancing interferon-based therapy, ET clinical research, and real-world evidence generation. Because treatment access, dosing strategies, monitoring practices, and patient characteristics vary across regions, continued multinational collaboration will be essential to ensure that disease-modification strategies are applicable across diverse healthcare settings. Collectively, these priorities reflect a broader shift in MPN research: from controlling blood counts and symptoms toward understanding how molecular response can be translated into durable clinical benefit, disease modification, and, ultimately, TFR in appropriately selected patients.
