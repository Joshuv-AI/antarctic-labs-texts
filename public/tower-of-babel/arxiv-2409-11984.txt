Multi-set spectral clustering of time-evolving networks using the supra-Laplacian
Gary Froyland, Manu Kalia, Péter Koltai

Abstract
Complex time-varying networks are prominent models for a wide variety of spatiotemporal phenomena. The functioning of networks depends crucially on their connectivity, yet reliable techniques for learning communities in time-evolving networks remain elusive. We adapt successful spectral techniques from continuous-time dynamics on manifolds to the graph setting to fill this gap. We consider the supra-Laplacian for graphs and develop a spectral theory to underpin the corresponding algorithmic realisations. We develop spectral clustering approaches for both multiplex and non-multiplex networks, based on the eigenvectors of the supra-Laplacian and specialised Sparse EigenBasis Approximation (SEBA) post-processing of these eigenvectors. We demonstrate that our approach can outperform the Leiden algorithm applied both in spacetime and layer-by-layer, and we analyse voting data from the US senate (where senators come and go as congresses evolve) to quantify increasing polarisation in time.

Introduction

Complex interconnected systems from diverse applications such as biology, economy, physical, political and social sciences can be modelled and analysed by networks (
[
New10
,
BP16
]
).
Network function is governed by its connectivity structure.
The learning of communities–the problem of finding groups of nodes in networks
[
New10
]
–is thus a central issue when analysing networks, or their mathematical manifestations, graphs (
[
NG04
]
).
In many situations, this connectivity structure and thus the underlying communities vary over time.
For example, one may consider an online social network composed of vertices represented by users, and edges that represent the degree of connection between users (likes, comments, post shares, tags etc.). Communities in such a network may correspond to mutual interests or those formed by friends and family. A temporal evolution of these communities is described by changes in user interaction, essentially strengthening or weakening the edges between users as time passes, see
[
GDC10
,
BA15
]
for examples.

Time-evolving networks have been introduced before in the literature as
multilayer
networks (
[
New10
,
KAB+14
]
),
where the vertex set and edge weights may change over time.
A special case of a multilayer network is a
multiplex
network,
where vertices are copied across layers and each vertex maintains temporal connections only with its counterparts one layer forward and backward in time.
Multilayer and multiplex networks are used to model time-varying networks where each vertex behaves as a state varying in time, and are thus ubiquitous in the literature; see
[
MHI+21
,
RC18
]
for reviews on this topic.
This study focuses on detecting communities in both multiplex networks and multilayer networks (where the set of vertices themselves may change over time; see Sec.
6
).

Community detection methods of multiplex networks include modularity maximisation adapted to spacetime graphs (
[
MRM+10
,
GDC10
]
), random walk-based approaches (
[
KM15
,
KDC22
]
), hierarchical clustering (
[
MH19
]
), detect-and-track methods (
[
BA15
]
) and ensemble methods that generalise to multilayer networks (
[
TAG17
]
).
In this work we prioritise the development of novel techniques for Laplacians on undirected spacetime graphs to detect time-evolving spacetime communities, particularly in challenging situations where existing non-spacetime and spacetime methods fail.
We do so for several reasons. First, the eigendata from graph Laplacians contain crucial information about the optimal partitioning of a graph, allowing one to relate their spectra and eigenvectors to balanced graph cuts and the resulting Cheeger constants from isoperimetric theory (
[
Chu96a
,
JM85
]
).
Second, graph Laplacians on multiplex networks, called
supra-Laplacians
(
[
SRDDK+13
,
RA13
,
DP17
]
), have structure that we exploit to develop theoretical justifications for the quality of spacetime communities extracted from their eigendata.
Third, spectral clustering is a well-studied and robust algorithm for community detection.
Spectral partitioning is computationally efficient as the resulting supra-Laplacians are sparse and only require the computation of the first few eigenpairs.

Previous works have glossed over establishing results about the spectrum of supra-Laplacians (
[
SRDDK+13
,
RA13
,
DP17
]
).
We develop such spectral theory, which also formally quantifies the performance of our spatiotemporal spectral partitioning through Cheeger constants.
From this new theory we formulate an algorithm to detect multiple spatiotemporal clusters; our theory provides a quality guarantee of the partitions across time.
Finally, we adapt our spectral partitioning approach to non-multiplex type networks, where vertices may appear and disappear in time.
Our spectral clustering constructions determine
several spatiotemporal clusters
, including in
multilayer graphs
where vertices may come and go.
Each of these aspects have to our knowledge not been formulated before.

Our constructions are inspired by the
dynamic
and
inflated dynamic
Laplacians formulated for continuous-space dynamics on manifolds (
[
Fro15
]
) and spacetime manifolds (
[
FK23
,
AFK24
]
), respectively.
The dynamic Laplacian for graphs was formulated in
[
FK15
]
.
The supra-Laplacian is the graph analogue of the inflated dynamic Laplacian, where inflation refers to expanding the graph by copying vertices across time.
We will use the terminology “supra-Laplacian” in the sequel, to maintain consistency with established literature (
[
SRDDK+13
,
RA13
,
DP17
]
).

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We construct the unnormalised and normalised supra-Laplacian on graphs and state the corresponding spacetime Cheeger inequalities, which provide worst-case guarantees on clustering quality. In Prop.
3
, we also prove an intuitive result concerning the monotonicity of spacetime Cheeger constants with respect to the number of spacetime partition elements.

2.

Vertices in individual network layers are typically connected across time with certain strengths, which represent diffusion across time in the associated supra-Laplacian. We analyse the limit of increasing temporal connectivity strength, and in Thm.
5
(resp. Thm.
6
) we prove that the spectra of the unnormalised (resp. normalised) supra-Laplacians approach that of the dynamic Laplacian (resp. normalised temporal Laplacian) in the hyperdiffusion limit. As a result, we gain a better understanding of how to select the appropriate diffusion constant for the supra-Laplacian for graphs, which is important for the spectral partitioning algorithm.

3.

We formulate a novel spectral partitioning algorithm (Algorithm
1
) in conjunction with the Sparse Eigenbasis Algorithm (SEBA) (
[
FRS19
]
) for multiplex networks to find
several
spatiotemporal clusters from
several
spacetime eigenvectors. We illustrate the efficacy of our approach in the challenging setting of small, slowly varying networks, and demonstrate superior time-varying cluster detection when compared to (i) a spacetime application of the Leiden algorithm (
[
TWvE19
]
) and (ii) Leiden applied on individual time slices.

4.

In Algorithm
3
we formulate a spacetime clustering procedure for non-multiplex networks, which are characterised by time-varying edge weights, and vertices that appear and disappear in time. We analyse a real-world network of voting similarities between US senators in the years 1987–2025, previously studied by e.g. (
[
MRM+10
,
WPF+09
]
), and show increasing political polarisation over recent decades.

https://github.com/mkalia94/TemporalNetworks.jl
.

2.1
Spacetime graphs and clusters

Let
G=(V,E,\mathcal{W})
be a general undirected graph with finite sets of vertices
V\subset\mathbb{N}
and edges
(x,y)\in E
for vertices
x,y\in V
. The function
\mathcal{W}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}

\mathcal{W}(x,y)>0

of a vertex

We now extend these definitions to spacetime graphs. Let
V=\{1,\dots,N\}
denote the spatial vertex set and
V^{\prime}=\{1,\dots,T\}
be the temporal vertex set for finite

. We define the spacetime vertex set
\bm{V}=V^{\prime}\times V
which gives rise to the
spacetime, multiplex graph

\bm{G}
defined by the tuple
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E},\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)})
where
\bm{E}
is the edge set connecting vertices
\bm{V}
defined as follows,

\displaystyle\bm{E}

\displaystyle=\{((t,x),(s,y))\ |\ (t,x)\sim(s,y)\ \textnormal{in }\bm{G}\}.

The notation
(t,x)\sim(s,y)
denotes a connection between vertices
(t,x)
and
(s,y)
in
\bm{G}
.
For every graph
\bm{G}
we associate a weight function
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}
parameterised by
a>0
such that
\smash{\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}}
is the weight of the edge joining the vertices
(t,x)
and
(s,y)
. We decompose
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}
into
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}
and
a^{2}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}
, the spatial and temporal components of
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}
, so that

\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}=\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}+a^{2}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp},

(3)

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(s,y)}

\displaystyle=0,\ t\neq s,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,y)}

\displaystyle=0,\ x\neq y.

(4)

We further restrict
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}
so that it is space-independent, i.e.,

\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}=\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,y),(s,y)}=:\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{t,s}\textnormal{ for all $t,s,x,y$}.

Clustering spacetime graphs.

Individual clusters are of spacetime type and can thus appear or disappear, or eject or absorb vertices as time passes. For clarity, we formally define these transitions below. When discussing clustering we will use the notion of
packing
which is more general than
partitioning
.
A
K

\bm{\mathfrak{X}}=\{\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{K}\}

\bm{V}
is defined by a collection of disjoint subsets
\bm{X}_{i}\subset\bm{V}

or (ii)

, where
\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\bm{X}_{k}\subsetneq\bm{V}

\bm{\Omega}=\bm{V}\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\bm{X}_{k}

2.2
Laplacians of spacetime graphs

From
[
Chu96b
]
, the unnormalised and normalised graph Laplacians for general graphs are defined as follows. The unnormalised graph Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
acts on functions
f:V\to\mathbb{R}
and is given by

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\sum_{y}\mathcal{W}(x,y)\left(f(x)-f(y)\right).

(8)

The corresponding normalised Laplacian
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
is given by

\overline{\mathcal{L}}f(x)=\sum_{y}\mathcal{W}(x,y)\left(\frac{f(x)}{d(x)}-\frac{f(y)}{d(x)^{1/2}d(y)^{1/2}}\right).

(9)

We extend these definitions to the spacetime case. First, we define by
\bm{d}^{(a)}
,
\bm{d}^{\rm spat}
and
\bm{d}^{\rm temp}
the degree functions over vertices
(t,x)\in\bm{V}
and subsets of vertices
\bm{X}\subset\bm{V}
.
The expressions are as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s,y)\in\bm{V}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)},

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{(a)}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t,x)\in\bm{X}}\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x),

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{\rm spat}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\bm{V}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(t,y)},

\displaystyle{\bm{d}^{\rm spat}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t,x)\in\bm{X}}\bm{d}^{\rm spat}(t,x),

\displaystyle\bm{d}^{\rm temp}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in V^{\prime}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,y)},

\displaystyle{\bm{d}^{\rm temp}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t,x)\in\bm{X}}\bm{d}^{\rm temp}(t,x).

(10)

Note that by (
5
),
\bm{d}^{\rm temp}(t,x)=\sum_{s\in V^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{t,s}
and is therefore independent of
x
. Thus we use
d^{\rm temp}(t)
in the rest of the paper.
On the graph
\bm{G}
equipped with weights
\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}
,
we define the unnormalised graph supra-Laplacian
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}
as an operator acting on functions
\bm{f}:\bm{V}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}\bm{f}(t,x):=\sum_{(s,y)\in\bm{V}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\left(\bm{f}(t,x)-\bm{f}(s,y)\right).

(11)

We define the normalised graph supra-Laplacian
\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}
acting on functions
\bm{f}:\bm{V}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}\bm{f}(t,x):=\sum_{(s,y)\in\bm{V}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\left(\frac{\bm{f}(t,x)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)}-\frac{\bm{f}(s,y)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)^{1/2}\bm{d}^{(a)}(s,y)^{1/2}}\right).

(12)

We note that in the case of the normalised supra-Laplacian, the corresponding graph
\bm{G}
should not contain isolated vertices in order to make sure
\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)>0

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\bm{V}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(t,y)}\left(\bm{f}(t,x)-\bm{f}(t,y)\right),

(14)

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm temp}\bm{f}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in V^{\prime}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}\left(\bm{f}(t,x)-\bm{f}(s,x)\right).

For now it is sufficient to note that by construction all graph Laplacians considered here are self-adjoint (with respect to different inner products), positive semidefinite, and hence have purely real nonnegative spectrum. Definition 2

2.3
Graph cuts in spacetime

We define the (edge) cut value
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X})
between a subset
\bm{X}\subset\bm{V}
and its complement
\bm{X}^{\complement}:=\bm{V}\setminus\bm{X}
by

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{(t,x)\in\bm{X}}\sum_{(s,y)\in\bm{X}^{\complement}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\,.

(18)

We think of clusters being “good” if their cut values are low.
For the graph
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E},\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)})
, we are interested in the
balanced graph cut problem
. We consider a
K
-packing
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}=\{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{K}\}
of the spacetime vertex set
\bm{V}
. Recall that

\bm{X}_{k}\cap\bm{X}_{l}=\emptyset
,
\forall k\neq l
. The goal is to minimise
\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{k})
produced by such a packing while making sure that the node count or degree of each individual
\bm{X}_{k}
remains large. The standard quantity to minimise over the
K
-packing
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}
are the unnormalised and normalised Cheeger ratios
\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})
and
\overline{\bm{H}}(\bm{X}_{k})
defined by

\bm{H}(\bm{X}):=\frac{\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X})}{|{\bm{X}}|},\quad\overline{\bm{H}}(\bm{X}):=\frac{\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X})}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(\bm{X})}.

(19)

Denotethe maximal Cheeger ratio of
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}
by
\bm{H}_{K}(\bm{\mathfrak{X}}):=\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})
.
The unnormalised Cheeger constant
\bm{h}_{K}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{K}:=\min_{\bm{\mathfrak{X}}\text{ is a $K$-packing of }\bm{V}}\bm{H}_{K}(\bm{\mathfrak{X}})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{K}}\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})

(20)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{K}}\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\frac{\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{k})}{|{\bm{X}_{k}}|}.

Similarly, define
\overline{\bm{H}}_{K}(\bm{\mathfrak{X}}):=\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\overline{\bm{H}}(\bm{X}_{k})
. The normalised Cheeger constant
\overline{\bm{h}}_{K}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{h}}_{K}:=\min_{\bm{\mathfrak{X}}\text{ is a $K$-packing of }\bm{V}}\overline{\bm{H}}_{K}(\bm{\mathfrak{X}})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{K}}\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\overline{\bm{H}}(\bm{X}_{k})

(21)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{K}}\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\frac{\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{k})}{\bm{d}^{(a)}({\bm{X}_{k}})}.

In the case
K=2
in (

) we have the celebrated Cheeger inequality (
[

]
) relating the normalised Cheeger constant and the second eigenvalue of the normalised supra-Laplacian:

\overline{\bm{h}}_{2}\leq\sqrt{2\overline{\bm{\lambda}}_{2,a}}\,.

(22)

in

.

For
K\in\mathbb{N}
we have,

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{h}}_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\bm{h}}_{K+1},

(23)

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{K}

\displaystyle\leq{\bm{h}}_{K+1}.

Proof.

See Appendix

.

For general

-th smallest eigenvalue

of
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}}
, which follows directly from

. For a graph

and given

, there exists a

\forall i\neq j
for some

For reasons to be discussed in Sec.

we will focus on the unnormalised supra-Laplacian
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}
in the following and in particular in the numerical investigations below. However, Cheeger inequalities involving the unnormalised Cheeger constant
\bm{h}_{K}
and the unnormalised supra-Laplacian

are less developed than their normalised counterparts, which we have just discussed. From
[
KM16
, Thm. 3.1]
we know that for a graph
G=(V,E)
equipped with adjacency

\bm{h}_{2}\leq\sqrt{2\bm{\lambda}_{2,a}\max_{(t,x)}\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)}.

(25)

To compare the relative tightness of (

) and (

\bm{d}^{(a)}(x,t)\equiv d

3
Eigenvalues of supra-Laplacians and the hyperdiffusion limit

The graph cuts defined in the previous section critically depend on the eigenvalues of the associated supra-Laplacians.
The eigenvectors of the (un)-normalised supra-Laplacian are used ahead to perform spectral partitioning of the spacetime graph to detect persistent communities. The supra-Laplacian depends on the parameter

3.2
The normalised Laplacian
\overline{\mathbfcal L^{(a)}}

Now we turn to the normalised supra-Laplacian

and its hyperdiffusion limit. We will show that in this case only temporal information prevails, essentially due to the normalisation by the degrees which are for

dominated by the temporal contributions. This greatly reduces the usefulness of

Consider the spacetime graph

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}f(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{t,s}\left(\frac{f(t)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)}-\frac{f(s)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)^{1/2}d^{\rm temp}(s)^{1/2}}\right),

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}}\bm{f}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}\left(\frac{\bm{f}(t,x)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)}-\frac{\bm{f}(s,x)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\rm temp}(s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

4

We formulate the spectral partitioning Algorithm

by defining a matrix representation of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}

Consider a general graph

from (

where

\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}N_{2}\times M_{1}M_{2}}\text{\reflectbox{$\in$}}\mathrm{A}\otimes\mathrm{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{A}_{1,1}\mathrm{B}&\dots&\mathrm{A}_{1,M_{1}}\mathrm{B}\\

(39)

Recalling the definitions in (

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{(a)}_{\bm{i}(t,x),\bm{i}(t,x)}

\displaystyle:=\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{\rm spat}_{\bm{i}(t,x),\bm{i}(t,x)}

\displaystyle:=\bm{d}^{\rm spat}(t,x),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{\rm temp}_{\bm{i}(t,x),\bm{i}(t,x)}

\displaystyle:=d^{\rm temp}(t).

Note that vertices
(t,x)\in\bm{V}
along the respective diagonals are ordered as per (

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{(a)}-\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{\rm spat}-\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{\rm spat})+a^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{\rm temp}-\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{\rm temp})

\displaystyle=:\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{\rm spat}+a^{2}\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{\rm temp}.

4.2
A spectral partitioning algorithm

To identify clusters in a given spacetime multiplex graph, including their appearance and disappearance through time, we begin by constructing the supra-Laplacian matrix
\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{(a)}
from a collection of adjacency matrices
\{\mathrm{W}^{\rm spat}_{t}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
.
The algorithm that proceeds from the construction of the supra-Laplacian to obtaining spacetime packings is summarised below.
The individual steps in the algorithm are elaborated in the paragraphs that follow.

Algorithm 1

Spectral partitioning using the supra-Laplacian.

Input :
Number of vertices
N
, number of time slices
T
, graph
\bm{G}
represented by a collection of spatial weights

\{\mathrm{W}^{\rm spat}_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,T}
,
\mathrm{W}^{\rm spat}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and temporal weights
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
.

Output :

K
-packing
\{\bm{X}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n_{K}}\}
.

1

Let
\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{(a)}
be constructed using
\{\mathrm{W}^{\rm spat}_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,T}
as per (
35
), (
36
) and (
38
);

2

Construct
\bm{\mathrm{D}}^{(a)}
and
\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{(a)}
using (
40
) and (
41
);

3

Identify leading nontrivial spatial and temporal eigenvalues using Lemma
4
and compute
a=a_{c}
such that
\smash{\bm{\lambda}^{\rm spat}_{2,a_{c}}\lesssim\ \bm{\lambda}^{\rm temp}_{2,a_{c}}}
(for instance via bisection);

4

Compute
\smash{(\bm{\lambda}^{\rm spat}_{k,a},\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{k,a})}
, the
k
-th spatial eigenpair of
\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{(a)}
, for
k=2,\ldots,R+1
. Select a suitable value for
R
as described in step 4 in the main text;

5

Construct companion eigenvectors
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{k,a}
: Define
\smash{{\mathrm{f}}_{k,a}:=[\|{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}^{\rm spat}_{k,a}(1,\cdot)\|,\ldots,\|\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{k,a}(T,\cdot)\|]\in\mathbb{R}^{T}}
,
k=2,\dots,R+1
. Define the spacetime extension
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{k,a}:={\mathrm{f}}_{k,a}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{N}
;

6

Isolate spacetime packing elements: Apply the SEBA algorithm (
[
FRS19
]
) to the collection
\{\smash{\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{2,a},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{2,a},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{R+1,a},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{R+1,a}}\}
. The output SEBA vectors are denoted
\{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{R}_{j,a}\}_{j=1,\dots,2R}
;

7

Identify spurious SEBA vectors: If the
j^{th}
SEBA vector is approximately constant on each index block corresponding to a single time fibre, i.e. if
[\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{R}_{j,a}]_{z}\approx C_{j,t}
for
z=\bm{i}(t,x)
,
1\leq x\leq N
,
1\leq t\leq T
;

8

Define packing elements:
Let
\{{n_{1}},\ldots,{n_{K}}\}
,
K\leq 2R
denote indices of the non-spurious SEBA vectors. Define
\bm{X}_{n_{j}}=\{(t,x):[\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{R}_{n_{j},a}]_{z}>0,z=\bm{i}(t,x)\}
for each
j=1,\ldots,K
. If a single spacetime vertex
(t,x)
belongs to two or more
\bm{X}_{n_{j}}
, assign it only to the
\bm{X}_{n_{j}}
with the largest
[\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{R}_{n_{j},a}]_{z}
value. One may augment the packing with the unclustered set
\smash{\bm{\Omega}=\{(t,x)\,:\,[\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{R}_{n_{j},a}]_{z}=0,z=\bm{i}(t,x)\}}
to obtain a
K+1
partition, where
\bm{i}
is the ordering from (
33
).

Identify spatial and temporal eigenvalues and choose the temporal diffusion strength parameter
a
(step 3).

We compute eigenpairs of
\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{(a)}
denoted by
(\bm{\lambda}_{k,a},\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{k,a})
for
k=1,\dots,TN
. Spatial eigenvalues are identified by locating the corresponding eigenvectors that have constant spatial means as per Lemma
4
. That is, eigenvectors
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{k,a}
such that
\sum_{x\in V}\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{k,a}(t,x)=C_{k}
, for all
1\leq t\leq T
. This is verified numerically as in
[
FK23
, Sec. 4.2]
by computing the variance of the means of the time fibres
\bm{\mathrm{f}}_{k,a}(t,\cdot)
; the eigenvector is spatial if this value is close to zero.
We now fix a value of
a
. We do so by finding the smallest value of
a
for which
\smash{\bm{\lambda}^{\rm spat}_{2,a}\lesssim\bm{\lambda}_{2,a}^{\rm temp}}
. In the case of the unnormalised Laplacian
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{(a)}
, this choice of
a
is unique as the eigenvalues are monotonic in
a
, spatial eigenvalues
\smash{\bm{\lambda}_{k,a}^{\rm spat}}
saturate as
a\to\infty
by Thm.
5
, and the temporal eigenvalues scale with
\mathcal{O}(a^{2})
. A similar heuristic was used in the continuous-time supra-Laplacian in
[
FK23
, Sec. 4.1]
. The non-multiplex case in Sec.
6.1
will be treated in a similar spirit.

Choosing the number of spatial eigenvectors
R
to input to SEBA (step 4).

To spectrally partition using SEBA, we require the parameter
R
, which is the number of nontrivial spatial eigenvectors of the supra-Laplacian to be used. The first
R
spatial eigenvectors and corresponding
R
companion vectors (step 5, described below) yield
2R
SEBA vectors.
For a selected value of
R
we then follow the remaining steps 5–8 of Algorithm
1
to construct a
K
-packing of the network, where
K\leq 2R
. We determine a final value for
R
using a balance of two criteria:

1.

Spectral gap
: After fixing
a
as per step 3 of Algorithm
1
, we compute eigenvalues
{\bm{\lambda}}_{k,a}^{\rm spat}
and look for the first significant gap between these values. If the first significant gap arises between eigenvalues
{\bm{\lambda}}_{k,a}^{\rm spat}
and
{\bm{\lambda}}_{k+1,a}^{\rm spat}
, then
R
is chosen to be
k-1
. This is based on the spectral gap theorem, see
[
LGT14
, Thm. 4.10]
.

2.

Mean Cheeger ratio
\tilde{\bm{H}}(R)
:
For each value of
R\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain
2R
SEBA vectors, each with support
\bm{X}_{k}
. We compute the mean Cheeger ratio
\tilde{\bm{H}}(R)=\frac{1}{2R}\sum_{k=1}^{2R}\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})
where
\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})
is defined as per (
19
). The value of
R
is then chosen as
R=\arg\min_{R}\tilde{\bm{H}}(R)
.

In the examples presented in sections
5
and
6
, we use each of these criteria to fix a choice of
R
. We prioritise the criterion which provides the most clear choice for
R
.

Constructing companion eigenvectors for input to SEBA (step 5).

In the previous paragraph we discussed the choice of number of spatial eigenvectors
R
.
Because of the orthogonality relationships between the different eigenvectors, it is possible that the leading nontrivial
R
spatial eigenvectors encode more than
R
spatiotemporal clusters.
To fully extract all relevant cluster information from
R
eigenvectors, for each eigenvector
k=2,\ldots,R+1
we create a companion vector
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{k,a}
with
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{k,a}(t,x):=\|\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{k,a}(t,\cdot)\|_{2}
, which is a spacetime vector that is constant on each time slice, with constant value given by the
\ell_{2}
norm of the eigenvector on each time slice.
This procedure regenerates the additional degrees of freedom that are limited by orthogonality of the spatiotemporal eigenvectors, and is explained in greater depth in
[
AFK24
, Secs. 6.3.2 and 7.1.1]
in the context of supra-Laplace operators on Riemannian manifolds with Neumann boundary conditions.

Isolating packing elements using SEBA (step 6).

The Sparse Eigenbasis algorithm (SEBA) was introduced in
[
FRS19
]
to disentangle cluster elements from eigenfunctions of Laplacians that represent packing/partitioning of a set. We summarise the algorithm here. Given a set of vectors
\{\mathrm{v}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{v}_{r}\}
,
\mathrm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the algorithm computes a basis
S
(whose elements are sparse vectors) of a subspace that is approximately spanned by the
\mathrm{v}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
. This is done by computing the ideal rotation matrix
\mathrm{Q}
, which when applied to
\mathrm{V}:=[\mathrm{v}_{1}|\mathrm{v}_{2}|\cdots|\mathrm{v}_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
produces a matrix
\mathrm{S}=[\mathrm{s}_{1}|\mathrm{s}_{2}|\cdots|\mathrm{s}_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
that is sparse and has approximately orthogonal columns which define the SEBA vectors
S=\{\mathrm{s}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{s}_{r}\}
. More precisely, one solves the following nonconvex minimisation problem,

\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{Q}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{S}\mathrm{Q}\|_{F}^{2}+\mu\|\mathrm{S}\|_{1},

(44)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the matrix Frobenius norm,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the matrix 1-norm and
\mu
is an appropriately chosen regularisation parameter (the standard choice in
[
FRS19
]
is
\mu=0.99/\sqrt{m}
). More details can be found in
[
FRS19
]
and code is available at
github.com/gfroyland/SEBA
.
We apply the SEBA algorithm to the
R
leading nontrivial spatial eigenvectors and their corresponding
R
companion vectors
\{\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{2,a},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{2,a},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{f}}^{\rm spat}_{R+1,a},\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{R+1,a}\}
constructed in Step 5.
SEBA returns a collection of vectors
\smash{\{\bm{\mathrm{s}}_{j,a}^{R}\}_{j=1,\dots,2R}}
for a fixed choice of
R
and
a
. The supports of these vectors are individual clusters that form a packing of
\bm{V}
.

Identifying spurious packing elements (step 7).

We observe in our experiments that SEBA can produce spurious vectors
{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}_{j,a}^{R}
that do not provide meaningful clustering information and correspond to undesirably high cut values
\bm{H}(\bm{X}_{k})
. This occurs when the true number of clusters
K
lies somewhere strictly between
R
and
2R
. SEBA outputs
2R
vectors in total,
K
of which are useful vectors representing spacetime clusters, and
2R-K
of which are spurious.
These spurious vectors are easily identified because they are constant on time fibres; this is illustrated in
[
AFK24
, Figure 25]
in the manifold setting. In the graph setting, these vectors can be identified by being constant on time fibres or having a much larger cut value. The latter is illustrated in Sec.
5.2
.

5
Networks with clusters that appear and disappear in time

The spacetime graphs shown in this subsection are generated artificially, see Appendix
E
for details. We perform a spectral partitioning on the corresponding supra-Laplacian
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{(a)}
using Algorithm
1

We intentionally chose small graphs having
N=20
vertices and a smooth, non-abrupt transitioning of their cluster structure to provide non-trivial and challenging benchmark cases.
We compare our approach with one of the most established clustering strategies, the Leiden algorithm presented in
[

(an improvement of the so-called Louvain algorithm in
[

Using both visual comparison and Cheeger ratios as quality measures, we show that both instances of the Leiden algorithm do not capture the spatiotemporal nature and evolution of the clusters.
In contrast, our approach accurately detects all of the transitions that the clusters undergo, and identifies times when no clusters exist.

7
Conclusion

In this work we made several important contributions towards a more rigorous theory for spectral clustering of time-varying complex networks and formulated a versatile algorithm for both multiplex and non-multiplex cases.
Our key construction is the supra-Laplacian
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}
, which balances spatial and temporal diffusion through a scalar diffusion constant
a
.
We constructed both unnormalised and normalised supra-Laplacians on graphs and stated corresponding spacetime Cheeger inequalities.
We fully explored the hyperdiffusivity limit
a\to\infty
in both the unnormalised and normalised cases, proving convergence of of the spectrum and eigenvectors of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}
and
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}}
to the dynamic Laplacian
\mathcal{L}^{D}
and normalised temporal Laplacians
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}
, respectively.

This theory informed the construction of novel spectral clustering algorithms, which automatically identified the eigenvectors that carry cluster-related information, and applied sparse eigenbasis approximation (SEBA) to isolate individual spacetime clusters.
Our spectral clustering algorithms are designed for time-varying networks with (i) constant-in-time vertex sets on the network’s layers (multiplex), and (ii) vertex sets that may vary across the network’s layers (non-multiplex).
We demonstrated that our novel spectral approach is effective in detecting and tracking several communities at once, as well as their gradual appearance and disappearance, in both multiplex and non-multiplex situations.
The algorithms are conveniently implemented in
https://github.com/mkalia94/TemporalNetworks.jl
.

\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E},\linebreak\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp})
.
Let us denote the spatial and temporal components of
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}}
by
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm spat}}
and
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm temp}}
, respectively. Thus,

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}=\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm spat}+a^{2}\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm temp}.

(80)

Using (
10
), we note that

\lim_{a\to\infty}\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)=\lim_{a\to\infty}\sum_{y}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(t,y)}+a^{2}\sum_{s}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}=\infty.

(81)

Consider the expression
\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm spat}\bm{f}
. Expanding using (
11
), we get

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm spat}\bm{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,y}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\left(\frac{\bm{f}(t,x)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)}-\frac{\bm{f}(s,y)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{d}^{(a)}(s,y)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\to 0\quad(\textnormal{as }a\to\infty),

(82)

which is obtained as
\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)\to\infty
for
a\to\infty
from (
81
). Further we note that the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{a\to\infty}\frac{a^{2}}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{a\to\infty}\frac{a^{2}}{\sum_{s,y}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{(a)}_{(t,x),(s,y)}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{a\to\infty}\frac{a^{2}}{\sum_{y}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(t,y)}+a^{2}\sum_{s}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{a\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sum_{y}\frac{1}{a^{2}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm spat}_{(t,x),(t,y)}+\sum_{s}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{s}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,x)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{s}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{s,t}}=\frac{1}{d^{\rm temp}(t)}.

(83)

Now consider the expression
a^{2}\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm temp}\bm{f}.
Expanding using (
11
), we get

\displaystyle a^{2}\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{\rm temp}\bm{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,y}a^{2}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\left(\frac{\bm{f}(t,x)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)}-\frac{\bm{f}(s,y)}{\bm{d}^{(a)}(t,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{d}^{(a)}(s,y)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\to\sum_{s,y}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(s,y)}\left(\frac{\bm{f}(t,x)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)}-\frac{\bm{f}(s,y)}{d^{\rm temp}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\rm temp}(s)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle(\textnormal{as }a\to\infty)

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}}\bm{f},

(84)

where the limit is obtained from (
83
).
Now, the claim (
79
) follows from combining (
80
) with (
82
) and (
84
). As
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, from Dini’s theorem we have that

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(a)}}\to\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}}\textnormal{ uniformly as }a\to\infty.

(85)

Finally from
[
Kat95
, Thm. II.5.1]
we know that an arbitrary continuous perturbation of
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}}}
(in the uniform topology) yields eigenvalues and eigenspaces that are arbitrarily close to those of
\smash{\overline{\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm temp}}}
.
Thus, we have that the eigenvalues
\overline{\bm{\lambda}}_{k,a}
can only have accumulation points
\mu_{j}
for some
j=1,\ldots,T
(and their corresponding eigenspaces accordingly).
∎

Appendix E
Graph generation

We lay out an empirical graph generation method that incorporates graph transitions explicitly. We begin with a spacetime graph
\bm{G}
with
N
spatial nodes,
T
time fibers. We define by
\alpha=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\}
and
s=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots\}
,
s_{j},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
a sequence of states
\alpha_{i}
that represents the number of communities/partitions we would like to have at times
s_{i}\in[0,T]
respectively. For instance,
\alpha=\{1,0,1\}
and
s=\{0,s_{1},T\}
represents a network with a single cluster at initial time
t=0
that 2-disappears to a network state with no clear partitions at time
t=s_{1}
, and transitions back to a network state with a single appearing cluster (2-appearance) at time
t=T
. We also require the scalar parameters
\eta,\beta>0
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
. Their role will be made clear after the procedure is explained.

Given a sequence
\alpha
and the corresponding times
s
, the network
\bm{G}
represented by a sequence of adjacencies
\{\mathrm{W}^{(t)}\}_{t=1,\dots,T}
– which will constitute the spatial weight matrices
\mathrm{W}^{\rm spat}_{t}
on the individual time slices, having only entries 0 and 1 – is generated as follows:

1.

For every
\alpha_{i}\in\alpha
, construct a time slice/network layer as follows:

(a)

Generate
\alpha_{i}
fully connected networks of size
\lfloor N/(\alpha_{i}+\beta)\rfloor
each, for some
\beta
. These represent clusters. This still leaves nodes in
\{1,\dots,N\}
that are unclustered.

(b)

Take the remaining unclustered nodes and construct a
d
-regular graph, where
d
is the smaller of the cluster size or the number of remaining nodes.

(c)

In case
\alpha_{i}=0
, generate a
d
-regular graph over all nodes where
d=N/\gamma
, for some
\gamma
.

(d)

Construct
(1-\eta)\lfloor N/(\alpha_{i}+\beta)\rfloor
edges between the clusters and the remaining nodes each.

2.

Consider two network states
\mathrm{W}^{(s_{i})}
and
\mathrm{W}^{(s_{i+1})}
with respect to
(\alpha_{i},s_{i})
and
(\alpha_{i+1},s_{i+1})
respectively and edge sets
E_{i}
and
E_{i+1}
respectively constructed using step 1. The transient states
\mathrm{W}^{(s_{i}+1)},\dots,\mathrm{W}^{(s_{i+1}-1)}
are constructed by progressively adding/deleting relevant edges
(E_{i}\cup E_{i+1})\setminus(E_{i}\cap E_{i+1})
.

3.

Repeat step 1 and 2 over all pairs of states
(\alpha_{i},s_{i})
and
(\alpha_{i+1},s_{i+1})
to generate the network
\{\mathrm{W}^{(t)}\}_{t=1,\dots,T}
.

The role of the parameters
\eta,\gamma
and
\beta
are as follows:

•

The quality parameter
\eta>0
is related to an individual cluster and represents the maximal ratio of number of intracluster edges to the total number of edges of cluster.

•

The density parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
represents the inverse fraction of edges, compared to a complete network, that a
d
-regular network representing the unclustered vertices will have.
For higher values of
\gamma
, the regular network has fewer edges between vertices.

•

The parameter
\beta>0
represents the ratio of number of vertices assigned as clustered to those assigned as unclustered. From step 1-a, note also that higher values of
\beta
result in smaller sized clusters.

Higher values of
\beta
and
\gamma
result in fewer constructed edges per regular graph, which results in a good balance between fast and smooth transitions in time. In our experiments in Sec.
5.1
and Sec.
5.2
we chose
\alpha=\{0,1\},s=\{1,21\}
and
\alpha=\{0,1,2\},s=\{1,40,60\}
respectively. For both experiments we fix
\beta=1.5
,
\gamma=3
and
\eta=0.8
.

Appendix F
Matching time-slice clusters from Leiden

The application of the Leiden algorithm on each time slice yields a sequence of partitions
\{\mathfrak{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathfrak{X}^{(T)}\}
, where the partition on time slice
t
is
\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}=\{X^{(t)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(t)}_{K_{t}}\}
and
X^{(t)}_{k}\subset V
, for
t=1,\ldots,T
and
k=1,\ldots,K_{t}
.
We wish to combine these clusters across time to form a single spacetime partition
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}=\{\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{K}\}
, where
K=\max_{1\leq t\leq T}K_{t}
.
A spacetime partition element
\bm{X}_{k}\subset\bm{V}
,
1\leq k\leq K
will be of the form
\smash{\bm{X}_{k}=\cup_{t=1}^{T}{\{t\}\times}X^{(t)}_{k}}
, but it is nontrivial to sort the indices of the elements of
\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}
for each
t=1,\ldots,T
in such a way that this union creates sensible spacetime clusters
\bm{X}_{k}
with small spacetime cut values
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{k})
, defined in (
18
).
This section describes how to correctly perform this reindexing on each time slice in order to link the partitions
\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}
across time to create a spacetime partition
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}
with a low total spacetime cut value
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathfrak{X}})=\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{X}_{k})
.

F.1
Matching clusters between adjacent time slices

Fixing
t
, we consider the partitions
\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}
and
\mathfrak{X}^{(t+1)}
and wish to match partition elements at time
t
to those at
t+1
.
Each partition has a given indexing of its elements.
Without loss, we suppose that
K_{t}\geq K_{t+1}
(if not, it will be clear how to match from
t+1
to
t
).
We compute cut values between all pairs of clusters, indexed by
1\leq i\leq K_{t}
and
1\leq j\leq K_{t+1}
, leading to a
K_{t}\times K_{t+1}
matrix
\mathrm{C}
containing these values.

\mathrm{C}_{ij}:=-a^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X^{(t)}_{i}\\
y\in X^{(t+1)}_{j}\end{subarray}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\rm temp}_{(t,x),(t+1,y)}.

(86)

We consider an auxiliary bipartite graph with two sets of vertices:
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{K_{t}}\}
and
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{K_{t+1}}\}
.
The first set of vertices represent the
K_{t}
clusters at time
t
and the second set of vertices represent the
K_{t+1}
clusters at time
t+1
.
Linking cluster
i
at time
t
with cluster
j
at time
t+1
will be indicated by including an edge (in an edge cover, see below) between
u_{i}
and
w_{j}
.
For all pairs
(i,j)
the edge from
u_{i}
to
w_{j}
is given a weight equal to the cut value
\mathrm{C}_{ij}
given by (
86
).
We wish to find a restricted maximum weighted edge cover for the bipartite graph
G
with vertices
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{K_{t}},w_{1},\ldots,w_{K_{t+1}}\}
and weights
\mathrm{C}_{ij}
,
i=1,\ldots,K_{t}
,
j=1,\ldots,K_{t+1}
.
Requiring an edge cover means that all nodes have at least one incident edge.
We restrict the cover by insisting that each vertex in

\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{K_{T}}\}
has exactly one incident edge.
If the cover includes an edge linking
u_{i}
and
w_{j}
then we will connect the cluster
\smash{X_{i}^{(t)}}
with the cluster
\smash{X_{j}^{(t+1)}}
; this is elaborated below.

By the above properties we see that each cluster
\smash{X_{i}^{(t)}}
is linked to one or more clusters
\smash{X_{j}^{(t+1)}}
; if more than one cluster, we merge two or more clusters from time slice
t
into a single cluster at time slice
t+1
. Note that in the case of appearance/disappearance of clusters we assume that the set of unclustered vertices at time
t
is contained in
\smash{X_{j}^{(t)}}
for some
j
.

The maximality of the restricted maximum weight edge covering in (
86
) means that the total intercluster weight is minimised.
We solve a restricted maximum weight edge cover problem (RMWECP) as a binary integer program, with binary variables
\mathrm{A}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
,
i=1,\ldots,K_{t};j=1,\ldots,K_{t+1}
, and where
\mathrm{A}_{ij}=1
if an edge in the cover is incident on
u_{i}
and
w_{j}
(
\mathrm{A}_{ij}=0
otherwise):

\displaystyle\max_{\mathrm{A}_{ij}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{t+1}}\mathrm{C}_{ij}\mathrm{A}_{ij}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K_{t}}\mathrm{A}_{ij}=1\quad\mbox{for }j=1,\ldots,K_{t+1}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K_{t+1}}\mathrm{A}_{ij}\geq 1\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\ldots,K_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\ldots,K_{t};j=1,\ldots,K_{t+1}

Matching clusters across all time slices

Suppose that we have combined the partitions

into a partial spacetime partition
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}
(i.e. up to time

).
For simplicity of exposition, we assume that the indexing for
\bm{\mathfrak{X}}

\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}

To extend this to time

\mathfrak{X}^{(t)}

\mathfrak{X}^{(t+1)}

\mathfrak{X}^{(t+1)}

.
