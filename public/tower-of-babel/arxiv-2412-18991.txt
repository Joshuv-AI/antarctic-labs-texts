Abstract
. Answering an open question raised by Cooper, we show that there exist Δ 2 0 \Delta^{0}_{2} sets D D and E E such that the singleton degree of E E is a minimal cover of the singleton degree of D D . This shows that the Σ 2 0 \Sigma^{0}_{2} singleton degrees, and the Δ 2 0 \Delta^{0}_{2} singleton degrees, are not dense (and consequently the Π 2 0 \Pi^{0}_{2} Q Q -degrees, and the Δ 2 0 \Delta^{0}_{2} Q Q -degrees, are not dense). Moreover D D and E E can be built to lie in the same enumeration degree.

1.
Introduction

The importance of enumeration reducibility and its degree structure has
considerably grown in recent years, in the wake of several striking results,
such as the first order definability of the Turing degrees inside the
enumeration degrees
[
4
]
. Enumeration reducibility is a
formalization of the notion of relative enumerability between sets of natural
numbers, the informal idea being that a set
A
is enumerable relatively to a
set
B
if there exists an algorithm which enumerates
A
given any
enumeration of
B
. Formally, given sets
A,B\subseteq\omega
(where

\omega
denotes the set of natural numbers) we say that
A
is

enumeration reducible
(or, simply,
e
-reducible
) to
B
(in
symbol:
A\leq_{e}B
) if there exists a computably enumerable (c.e.) set

\Phi
such that

A=\{{x:(\exists u)[\langle x,u\rangle\in\Phi\,\&\,D_{u}\subseteq B]}\},

where
D_{u}
is the finite set with canonical index
u
. (In the following,
we will often identify finite sets with their canonical indices, thus writing
for instance
\langle x,F\rangle\in\Phi
meaning that
F
is finite and
\langle x,u\rangle\in\Phi
where
F=D_{u}
.) We also write in this case
A=\Phi(B)
, thus
viewing any c.e. set as an operator (called an
e
-operator
) from
sets to sets. The structure of the
e
-degrees
is the set of
equivalence classes of the equivalence relation
\equiv_{e}
on sets, in which

A\equiv_{e}B
if and only if
A\leq_{e}B
and
B\leq_{e}A
. The
e
-degree of
a set
A
will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}=\deg_{e}(A)
.

An important restricted version of
e
-reducibility is the so-called

singleton enumeration reducibility
, or simply
s
-reducibility
,
which only uses
e
-operators
\Phi
subject to the restriction

\langle x,F\rangle\in\Phi\Rightarrow[\textrm{$F=\emptyset$ or $F$
is a singleton}].

Any
e
-operator satisfying this restriction is called an
s
-operator. We
say that a set
A
is
s
-reducible
to a set
B
(denoted by

A\leq_{s}B
) if there is an
s
-operator
\Phi
such that
A=\Phi(B)
. The
equivalence relation originated by
\leq_{s}
is denoted by
\equiv_{s}

(where
A\equiv_{s}B
if
A\leq_{s}B
and
B\leq_{s}A
), and its
equivalence classes are called
s
-degrees
: the
s
-degree of a set

A
will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}=\deg_{s}(A)
.

In general, for
r\in\{s,e\}
we use the same symbol
\leq_{r}
to denote
both the reducibility (i.e. the pre-ordering relation on sets) and the
partial ordering relation induced by the reducibility on the
r
-degrees.

Although
s
-reducibility is strictly included in
e
-reducibility, in most
concrete examples where
e
-reducibility shows up in mathematics or logic, it
takes the form of
s
-reducibility. This is probably due to the fact that

\leq_{e}
nicely embeds into
\leq_{s}
by
A\leq_{e}B
if and only if

A^{*}\leq_{s}B^{*}
, where, given a set
X
, we denote by
X^{*}
the set
of all finite subsets of
X
, identified by their canonical indices. Interest
in
s
-reducibility (often through its isomorphic presentation as

Q
-reducibility, of which we recall later the precise definition), relies
also in its many applications to other sections of computability theory, to
logic, and general mathematics. For instance it plays a key role in
Marchenkov’s solution of Post’s Problem using Post’s methods
(
[
15
]
); and has applications to word problems (see
for instance,
[
2
,
9
]
) and to abstract computational
complexity (see for instance,
[
3
]
,

[
12
]
). For a survey on
Q
-reducibility and its
applications see
[
17
]
.

Throughout the rest of the paper we assume to have fixed some effective
listing
\{{\Phi_{e}:e\in\omega}\}
of the
s

2.1.
The theorem

The following theorem answers Cooper’s question
whether the
\Sigma^{0}_{2}

s
-degrees are dense.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exist
\Delta^{0}_{2}
, in fact co-2-c.e., sets
D
and
E
such that
D<_{s}E
and there is no set
Z
with
D<_{s}Z<_{s}E
.

Proof.

We wish to construct sets
A
and
D
satisfying the following requirements,
for all numbers
k,e\in\omega
,

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle:A\neq\Phi_{k}(D),

\displaystyle S_{e}

\displaystyle:Z_{e}=\Phi_{e}(A\oplus D)\Rightarrow\left[Z_{e}=\Gamma_{e}(D)~\vee~A=\Delta_{e}(Z_{e})\right],

where
\Gamma_{e}
and
\Delta_{e}
are two
s
-operators built by us.
Satisfaction of all
R
-requirements gives that
A\nleq_{s}D
, thus
D<_{s}E
where
E=A\oplus D
. On the other hand, satisfaction of all

S
-requirements guarantees that there is no
Z
such that
D<_{s}Z<_{s}E
. This follows from the fact that, by the
S
-requirements, if
D\leq_{s}Z\leq_{s}A\oplus D
then either we have
Z\leq_{s}D
and thus

Z\equiv_{s}D
, or
A\leq_{s}Z
and thus
A\oplus D\leq_{s}Z
giving
A\oplus D\equiv_{s}Z
.

2.2.
The strategies

The construction is similar to the construction of an Ahmad pair in the

\Sigma^{0}_{2}
e-degrees, see
[
1
]
. We briefly outline
the strategies.

2.2.1.
The
R_{k}
-strategy

The strategy to meet the requirement
R_{k}
picks a
follower
(or, a

witness
)
x\in A
; waits for
x
to show up in
\Phi_{k}(D)
via some
axiom
\langle x,F\rangle\in\Phi_{k}
with
F\subseteq D
(we say in this case that

x
becomes
realized
); at this points extracts
x
from
A
, and
restrains
x\in\Phi_{k}(D)
, by restraining
F\subseteq D
.

2.2.2.
The
\Gamma
-strategy of the
S_{e}
-strategy

The strategy to meet the requirement
S_{e}
tries at first to build an

s
-operator
\Gamma_{e}
, such that
Z_{e}=\Gamma_{e}(D)
, as follows. For every

z\in Z_{e}\smallsetminus\Gamma_{e}(D)
the strategy appoints an axiom
\langle z,F\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}
, and tries to keep
\Gamma_{e}
correct, making sure
that
z
gets extracted from
\Gamma_{e}(D)
(by making
F\nsubseteq D
, and
thus invalidating the axiom
\langle z,F\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}
) if
z
leaves

Z_{e}
. In more detail, if we see
z\in\Phi_{e}(\emptyset\oplus\emptyset)\smallsetminus\Gamma_{e}(D)
then we enumerate the axiom
\langle z,\emptyset\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}
: notice that in this case
z
will never leave

\Phi_{e}(A\oplus D)
and, consistently, the axiom which has been enumerated
into
\Gamma_{e}
permanently keeps
z\in\Gamma_{e}(D)
; if we see
z\in\Phi_{e}(\emptyset\oplus\{{d}\})\smallsetminus\Gamma_{e}(D)
with
d\in D

(we say in this case that
d
is a
D
-use of
z
in
\Phi_{e}
)
then we
enumerate the axiom
\langle z,\{{d}\}\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}
: notice that such an
axiom does not require any correcting action because if
z
leaves
Z_{e}

due to
d
leaving
D
then the axiom
\langle z,\{{d}\}\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}

automatically becomes invalid; if we see
z\in\Phi_{e}(\{{x}\}\oplus\emptyset)\smallsetminus\Gamma_{e}(D)
and
x\in A
(we say in this case
that
x
is an
A
-use
of
z
in
\Phi_{e}
) then we appoint a

\gamma_{e}
-marker
\gamma_{e}(z,x)=d\in D
for the pair
(z,x)
,
and enumerate the axiom
\langle z,\{{d}\}\rangle\in\Gamma_{e}
; subsequently, the

S_{e}
-strategy may want to correct the value of
\Gamma_{e}(D)(z)
by
extracting the
\gamma_{e}
-marker
\gamma_{e}(z,x)
from
D
if it sees that

z
leaves
Z_{e}
due to
x
leaving
A
. The
\gamma_{e}
-markers are
picked in an injective way: distinct pairs

get distinct markers. The
attempt of

at satisfying itself, by building and maintaining a suitable

s
-operator
\Gamma_{e}
, will be called
the
\Gamma
-strategy of

S_{e}
.

2.2.3.
R_{k}
below
S_{e}

Let us describe how the individual actions of the strategies for
S_{e}
and

R_{k}
interact with each other when
S_{e}
has higher priority than

R_{k}
. As already explained, the
R_{k}
-strategy picks a witness

and waits for this witness to become realized. Waiting forever corresponds to
a winning

(since in this case

\Gamma
-strategy of

S_{e}

\Gamma_{e}

If at some stage we see

R_{k}
-strategy would
like to extract

from

and restrain
x\in\Phi_{k}(D)
. At this point,
we have
x\in\Phi_{k}(D)
thanks to some axiom
\langle x,F\rangle\in\Phi_{k}
with

F\subseteq D
. Since there might be more than one such set

the one such that
\langle x,F\rangle

at any given stage, this set is uniquely determined by

However, the extraction of

-strategy
aiming at maintaining a correct

with

\Phi_{e}
, for which we have already appointed

-cleared

.

” are required to hold
only if

takes the infinitary outcome corresponding to the

\Delta
-strategy of

. If this outcome is the true outcome, then in fact
all markers

will be extracted from

. In fact, if

keeps getting new axioms in the

, then all of
these axioms will be automatically killed every time

takes the
infinitary outcome. Hence at the end of time, the only possibly applicable

-axiom for

2.3.
The tree of strategies

The strategies used in the construction have outcomes that can be
conveniently described by the elements of the set
\Lambda=\{{\mathrm{stop},\mathrm{wait},\mathrm{inf}}\}\cup\{{\infty_{i}:i\in\omega}\}
ordered by

\mathrm{stop}<_{\Lambda}\infty_{0}<_{\Lambda}\infty_{1}<_{\Lambda}\cdots<_{\Lambda}\infty_{n}<_{\Lambda}\cdots<_{\Lambda}\mathrm{wait}<_{\Lambda}\mathrm{inf}.

By
\Lambda^{<\omega}
we denote the set of finite strings of elements of

\Lambda
. We use standard notations and terminology about strings. In
particular, if
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda^{<\omega}
, then the symbol
\left|\alpha\right|
denotes the length of
\alpha
; the symbol
\alpha<_{L}\beta
denotes that
\alpha
is
to the left
of
\beta
, i.e. there
exists
i
such that the
i
th component
\alpha(i)
of
\alpha
is different
from the
i
th component
\beta(i)
of
\beta
, and for the least such
i
we
have
\alpha(i)<_{\Lambda}\beta(i)
. The symbol

denotes

, or
\alpha
is an
initial segment of
\beta

(denoted by

\alpha\subseteq\beta

then we write

); the symbol

denotes

but
\alpha\neq\beta
. If

then
we say that

has
higher priority
than
\beta
(or
\beta
has

lower priority
than
\alpha
). If

is an infinite path through

\Lambda^{<\omega}
and

is a number, then

denotes the initial segment of

having length

.

We now define the
tree of strategies

as follows. We effectively order the

- and
S
-requirements with the same
order type as
\omega
, and effectively list them as
\{{U_{e}:e\in\omega}\}
: we say that

has
higher priority
than
U_{e}
(or
U_{e}

has
lower priority
than

) if

. By induction on the length of
the strings, we define which elements of
\Lambda^{<\omega}
belong to

,
the way they are assigned requirements, and whether or not they are active or
satisfied along their extensions. If

is a requirement, we will say that
a string

is a

-string
if it has been assigned the
requirement

. In particular, an

-string
is a string which has
been assigned an
R
-requirement, and an
S
-string
is a string which
has been assigned an
S
-requirement. The elements of

will be simply

(as we do not care about strings in

), or also

, or

.

Let the empty string

lie in

, and let

be assigned to

. Assume that we have already defined that
\alpha\in T
, and

\alpha
has been assigned a requirement. We now define by cases the
successors of
\alpha
, i.e., the elements

with

:

Case 1
:
\alpha
is an
S
-strategy. Then
\alpha^{\frown}\langle\mathrm{inf}\rangle

is the immediate successor of
\alpha
. We will say that

is

active along

. Furthermore, for every

, if

is active or satisfied along

then

is
active
or
satisfied along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle\mathrm{inf}\rangle
. The idea here is that if
\alpha
is active, it is actively
trying to satisfy its requirement by building and correcting a suitable

s
-operator
\Gamma
(at which it may fail only due to the action of a lower

-strategy killing

and building a different
s
-operator

\Delta
). Assign to

the highest priority
U
such
that

is not assigned to any
\beta
which is either active or satisfied
along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle\mathrm{inf}\rangle
.

Case 2:

\alpha
is an
R
-strategy. Then let
\{{\beta_{i}:i<n}\}
,
with

-strategies above

which are active along

(allowing

). We let the immediate successors of

be

\alpha^{\frown}\langle o\rangle
for

. For

, we say that
\alpha
is

satisfied along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle o\rangle
, and for every
\beta\subset\alpha
, if

is active or satisfied along

then we say that

\beta
is
active
or
satisfied along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle o\rangle
. Fix

: we say that
\alpha
is
neither active nor satisfied along

\alpha^{\frown}\langle\infty_{i}\rangle
; for all

we say that
\beta_{j}
is

active along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle\infty_{i}\rangle
; we say that
\beta_{i}
is

satisfied along
\alpha^{\frown}\langle\infty_{i}\rangle
; for all

that
\beta_{j}
is
neither active nor satisfied along

or

if they are so along

\alpha
. For every outcome

of
\alpha

such that

is not assigned to any

\beta

. The

can infinitely injure the

-strategy of some

as described above, and in the process

isolates the strategy

of highest priority for which this happens,

-strategy to the

-strategy for

(as described in Section

.(a)). Moreover, the

,

, and

If
\alpha
is an

-strategy then we may also write

for

; likewise, if

is an

-node then we may write

for
\Phi_{e}
, and we may write

to represent

. If

is an

.

2.4.
The construction

The construction is in stages. At the end of each stage
s
we will have
defined a cofinite approximation
A_{s}
to the final desired set

, which
will be eventually defined as

A=\{{x:\left(\exists t\right)\left(\forall s\geq t\right)[x\in A_{s}]}\},

and a cofinite approximation
D_{s}
to the final desired set
D
, which will
be eventually defined as

D=\{{x:\left(\exists t\right)\left(\forall s\geq t\right)[x\in D_{s}]}\}.

Each stage
s>0
consists of substages
t
, with
t\leq u
for some
u\leq s
. At the end of each substage
t
of stage
s
, we appoint some string

\alpha_{s,t}\in T
, with
|\alpha_{s,t}|=t
, which extends the strings
appointed at the previous substages of stage
s
. If
t=s
or
\alpha_{t-1}

has stopped the stage
s
after appointing
\alpha_{t}
, then we go to stage

s+1
. At the end of stage
s
we define
f_{s}
to be the string appointed at
the end of the last substage of stage
s
. We say that a stage
s
is

\alpha
-true
if

, i.e.
\alpha
is appointed
at some substage of stage
s
.

The parameters and their approximations.
Each node
\alpha
has its
own parameters, which may include: a
current witness

x_{\alpha}
;

-operators
\Delta_{\alpha}
,
\Gamma_{\alpha}
; a set of numbers, called the

stream of
\alpha
and denoted by
\operatorname{St}(\alpha)
; and

\gamma_{\alpha}
-markers. Within any given stage
s
, the parameters are
updated substage by substage, so that they get a corresponding approximation
at the end of each substage
t
. If
s>0
is any stage then at the beginning
of substage
t=0
of stage
s
the various sets, parameters, or operators,
together with the current values of
A
and
D
, are understood to be
evaluated as at the end of stage
s-1
(i.e., by taking the approximations
defined at the end of the last substage of stage
s-1
); at the beginning of
a substage
t>0
of stage
s
, they are understood to be evaluated as at the
end of substage
t-1
. We
warn
the reader that throughout the
construction we will be very lazy in specifying stages or substages at which
the various parameters are approximated, confident that this will be clear
from the context. Thus for instance, at the beginning of a substage

, by

we denote the current approximation to this set under
the conventions above specified, meaning in fact the set

\Phi_{e,s}(A_{s,t-1}\oplus D_{s,t-1})

and

at the end of substage

of stage

(with

, and similarly,

, and

•

-true stage

(1)

(2)
