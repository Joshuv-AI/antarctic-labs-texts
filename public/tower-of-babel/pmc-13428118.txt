It’s More than Warm Communication: A Pre-Registered Analysis of Patient’s Compassion Priorities
Authors: Briar G. P. Dunn, Nathan S. Consedine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompassion is central to healthcare and linked to better outcomes for patients and providers. Past studies have employed small, clinically-specific samples and qualitative designs, producing a characterisation of compassion emphasising communicative and affective elements. What compassion “looks like” in primary care remains unclear.ObjectiveQuantify how doctor behaviours are seen as signalling compassion to primary care patients, consider questions of structure and the possibility that different behaviours might be more or less central to the experience of provider compassion in different groups.MethodsA pre-registered, anonymous, online, survey design recruited 513 patients from the general population. Participants provided demographic and health data before rating behaviours in terms of their importance to compassion.ResultsStructural analyses revealed three elements to compassionate behaviour: ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’, ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’, and ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’. Exploratory analyses suggested Māori and male patients saw affective and health deliverables as less important, older and/or preoccupied patients prioritised reassuring communication, and Asian and male patients saw inclusive care and tailored treatment as more important.ConclusionConsistent with theory, these data suggest that compassion is more than warm or supportive communication but it also involves concrete actions to alleviate suffering; different patient groups may vary in their preferences.

Introduction

The ability to practice medicine with compassion — recognising suffering and acting to alleviate it 1 — is a key professional competency and central to clinical care.2,3 Practicing with compassion predicts greater job satisfaction 2 and lower burnout 4 among providers and is mandated by ethical guidelines and professional bodies. 5 Equally, compassion is important to patients. Patients and families rate compassion among the most important elements of healthcare.6,7 Greater compassion is associated with patient satisfaction 8 and can enhance patient-physician communication, 9 potentially improving diagnostic accuracy. 2

However, while compassion is central to the provision of sustainable patient care, it is not invariably present. 10 Empirically, studies have focused on providers rather than patients,11-13 investigating methods to increase compassion [see 14 ], strategies to sustain compassion over time, 15 and the barriers to compassion,16-18 as well as seeking to identify processes that may facilitate compassion [see 2,19-21]. These are important studies, but the patient perspective has historically received less attention in comparison to an increasingly coherent provider-focused literature.

Early studies have shown that patients and their families view compassion as important,7,11 with experiences of compassion (or its lack) being among the elements of healthcare encounters that are best remembered. 22 However, few studies have examined what compassion actually looks like to patients, with early characterisations reflecting the demands of particular contexts — nursing, 3 oncology,22,23 long-term care, 24 mental health, in-patient, 3 and palliative care settings 25 and primarily among adult, European, and Western patient populations. For example, research among advanced cancer patients (n=100) has suggested that communicating in terms the patient understands, attempting to understand their situation, and building connections are perceived as compassionate by patients. 23 Similarly, focus group data, from academic staff, students, healthcare providers, and patients (n=45), suggests that good communication and interpersonal skills and investing time are important. 26

More recently, a topic modelling study (n=767) 11 revealed seven groupings of provider “behaviours” experienced as indicating compassion — listening and paying attention, delivering solutions, service continuity and holistic care, respecting preferences, genuine understanding, body language and empathy, and counselling and advocacy. Importantly, while listening and paying attention appeared important to compassion (reported by 70.7% of the sample), at least some patients also viewed actions that alleviate suffering as central to compassion in providers. Other text-based studies suggest that affiliation words increase perceptions of compassion while “insight” language diminishes it. 27 However, while these studies offer a useful beginning, they are limited in some important ways.

First, studies have tended to be small-scale, qualitative, and concentrated in niche settings; different clinical settings may have their own needs and preferences regarding compassion 17 and research in a broader range of clinical, professional, and cultural contexts is needed. 28 A recent review 10 showed that over 80% of compassion studies were conducted in Western settings; settings that have distinct patient and clinical profiles that may influence how compassion is expressed by providers14,18 or perceived by patients. 29 Methodologically, patient-focused studies need to move beyond small, qualitative designs to larger, psychometrically rigorous measurement in which the core components of compassion for patients can be identified. In this light, the current report offers a pre-registered psychometric examination of what compassion “looks like” to a diverse group of primary care patients.

Second, prior studies have concentrated on the “affective” elements of provider behaviour.2,3 This focus creates the possibility that key elements of compassion (notably regarding the provision of practical solutions to suffering) have been omitted (or at least downplayed) from our characterisation of the patient experience. Although compassion is usefully thought of as a feeling, it is also a feeling that is characterised by the motivation and actions that alleviate suffering. Basic research suggests compassion might be signalled in body language and facial characteristics,30,31 vocalisations 32 and touch.33,34 One study identified communication and understanding as essential 3 and another suggested body language and an empathic demeanour were commonly referenced by patients. 11 However, there is more than “good” communication or empathy to compassion. Such elements do not capture the entirety of what researchers mean by the term and at least some patient accounts prioritise the delivery of services that alleviate suffering. 11 Preliminarily characterising the structure of compassionate behaviour in primary care in a way that identifies both affective and non-affective elements of compassion is a second aim of the current report.

Finally, most studies are predicated on the assumption that increasing compassion among providers will result in patients experiencing greater compassion. This assumption tends to imply that there are ‘standard’ compassion signals and that such signals are experienced comparably across persons, cultures, and contexts. In our view, however, multiple processes likely intervene between a provider’s compassion and the patient’s experience of the provider as compassionate. Most obviously, if we want to increase compassion in a way that impacts the patient’s experience, we must start identifying how patients prefer to feel cared for. There are likely multiple ways for providers to communicate compassion just as there are multiple ways for patients to interpret provider behaviour. Although there are few data, it is possible that different elements of provider behaviour might be identified as more or less central to the experience of compassion in different patient groups. Preliminarily testing this possibility is the final aim of the current report.

Method

Study Design

Following consultation regarding recruitment and measurement among underrepresented groups, an online, anonymous survey design was employed. For eligibility, patients needed to be 18+ years old, English-speaking, and report having a primary care physician they had seen at least once in the previous twelve months.

Procedure

The study was advertised via social media, email lists, flyers, and word-of-mouth. A link directed prospective participants to a detailed description of the study. Given the potential sensitivity of ratings about providers, participation was anonymous and data submission was taken as consent. Participants provided demographic and health data before completing qualitative items regarding compassion in healthcare (not analysed here). Participants then completed a measure of patient compassion 35 (not presented here) and the single-item Relationship Questionnaire, 36 before completing a specifically developed measure regarding the relative importance of different physician behaviours to their experience of compassion. Finally, participants were thanked for their time and provided with options to enter prize draws and/or receive a summary of findings.

Measures

Demographics

Participants provided information on gender, age, ethnicity, and duration of relationship with primary care provider (GP). In line with local best practice, ethnicity was categorised into NZ European, Māori, Asian, and Other.

The Relationship Questionnaire (RSQ)

Because preferences regarding how we relate to others are likely related to preferences regarding compassion in healthcare, participants completed the Relationship Questionnaire (RSQ). 36 The RSQ is a single-item measure consisting of four short paragraphs, each describing a prototypical way of relating to others in close relationships; participants select the paragraph that best describes how they generally are in close relationships.

Priorities and Preferences Regarding Compassion in Healthcare (PPCH)

Given the absence of measures indexing the relative importance of different elements of provider behaviour to compassion, a measure was specifically developed. To maximise content validity, an initial list of approximately 45 items was developed. Construct validity in terms of the two-part definition of compassion — the ability to recognise a patient’s suffering and the desire to relieve it 1 — was kept prominent. Second, items were taken/adapted from established measures such as The Compassion Scale, 4 the Sinclair Compassion Questionnaire, 37 and the Compassion Competence Scale. 38 Third, theories and prior data were considered. Specifically, elements of compassion within the healthcare empirical model 2 and topics reported as examples of compassionate care among patients 11 were incorporated. The initial list of 45 items was then refined and streamlined with input from psychometric and content experts, with a final list of 34 items ultimately seen as maximising content and construct validity while minimising item redundancy. Although the measure was reviewed by an ad hoc group, it was not formally piloted in a separate sample prior to the primary study.

Analyses

Data analyses were conducted as per the pre-registered analytic plan using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS®) version 29.0.1.0. Initially, we tested for differences between completers and non-completers using ANOVA or chi-square analyses; variables that differed were used as covariates in the primary analyses. Second, a principal components analysis (PCA) using varimax rotation was used to identify the structure of physician behaviours indicating compassion to patients. Three, using the components thus identified, chi-square analyses tested for links between compassionate behaviours and background variables.

Research questions were tested in two ways. First, in assessing the relative importance of different aspects of provider behaviour to the experience of compassion, a repeated-measures ANOVA tested for mean-level differences across the components. Second, multiple regressions and a series of mixed model ANOVAs tested whether gender, age, ethnicity, and attachment style were associated with systematic variation in the importance of the different components.

Results

Participants

Ultimately, 513 participants were retained. Participant age ranged from 18 to 83 years (M = 39.36, SD = 13.50), with 89.7% identifying as female. Two participants identified as ‘other’ (0.35%) and one elected not to report gender (0.17%). Most identified as ‘New Zealand European’ (N= 291, 56.7%), followed by ‘Māori’ (N=89, 17.3%). Participants reported an average provider relationship of 8.15 years (SD= 9.04). Retention analyses showed that older participants were less likely to drop out, F(1, 648) = 5.12, p=.024, but there were no other predictors of retention. Three participants did not complete the attachment question hence their data were excluded from analyses involving this variable. The Structure of Physician Compassionate Behaviour

Our pre-registered analytic plan used a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to preliminarily assess the structure of the 34 behaviours that might indicate compassion. After checking assumptions of normality, linearity, and a lack of conflation, an exploratory unconstrained PCA analysis identified five components with eigenvalues greater than one, explaining 63.6% of the total variance in the importance ratings. Scree plot examination led us to run two-component and three-component confirmatory analyses using varimax rotation. The combination of item loadings (0.500 or greater), theory, 11 and face validity meant a three-component solution was ultimately adopted.

Based on theory, prior work 11 and face/content validity, the first component was named ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’, the second ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’, and the third ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’. The first component included items such as ‘pays close attention to you (2)’, ‘appears genuinely concerned for your health and wellbeing (15)’, and ‘follows up on testing and communicates results (32)’. The second component was more overtly affective and included items such as ‘reassures or comforts you (21)’, ‘relays information to you in a sensitive manner (23),’ and shows warmth and kindness in responses (29)’. The third component included items such as ‘respects your background/culture (11)’, ‘involves you in the decision making regarding your treatment (9)’, and ‘is open to considering different treatments (8)’. Internal reliabilities for the ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ component was α =.93, for ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ was α=.92, and for ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’ was α =.86. As expected then, structural analyses revealed multiple reliable and interpretable groupings of items indicative of the range of priorities and preferences patients hold regarding compassion in healthcare.

Individual Differences in the Importance of Different Physician Compassion Behaviours

We expected that the more affective elements of physician behaviour (‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ in this study) would be rated as more important than other elements. However, while a repeated-measures ANOVA showed that mean scores for the different components varied, F(1.85, 197.54) = 111.29, p<.001, η p 2 =.179, post-hoc comparisons revealed that scores on the ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ component (M=4.15, SD=.60) were higher than those for the ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ component (M=3.81, SD=.76), p<.001, but did not differ from scores for the ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’ component (M=4.10, SD=.71), p=.060. Mean scores for the ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ component were lower than those for the ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’ components (p = <.001).

As stipulated in the pre-registration, exploratory analyses of possible group differences in compassion priorities began by testing whether the variables of interest (gender, age, ethnicity, attachment) were conflated with other variables. As per the pre-registered plan, demographic confounds on the focal predictor variables were force-entered into subsequent regression analyses. It is important to reinforce the point that because these analyses are exploratory and the differences that emerge are small in magnitude, effects should be interpreted as early indications of the likelihood that different groups may have different compassion priorities rather than definitive group effects.

Compassionate Behaviour Component 1: Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers

In combination, the entered variables accounted for 4% variance of scores on the ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ component, Adjusted R 2 =.03, F(8, 498) =2.87, p=.004. Scores on this component were lower among men (β = -.10, p=.026) and Māori (β = -.14, p=.003). Age and attachment did not predict importance ratings for this component.

Compassionate Behaviour Component 2: Positive and Reassuring Communication

In combination, the entered variables accounted for 5% of the variance in scores on the ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ component, Adjusted R 2 =.03, F(8, 498) =3.04, p=.002. Scores on this component were greater with age (β= .167, p=<.001) and preoccupied attachment (β= .107, p=.021). Gender and ethnicity did not predict importance ratings for this component.

Compassionate Behaviour Component 3: Attends to Concerns and Preferences

In combination, the entered variables accounted for 4% of the variance in scores on the ‘Attending to Concerns and Preferences’ component, Adjusted R 2 =.03, F(8, 498) =2.93, p=.003. Scores on this component were higher among male (β = -.155, p=<.001) and Asian patients (β = -.155, p=<.001). Age and attachment style did not predict importance ratings for this component.

Are Different Aspects of Compassionate Behaviour Differentially Important to Different People?

Although it represents a useful start, analysing whether individual difference variables concurrently predict a single compassionate behaviour component is only one way to assess potential differences in preferences regarding compassion in healthcare. Given age, 39 gender, 40 and ethnic 41 differences in scale use, it is possible that differences in compassion importance ratings are “driven” by differences in scale use rather than by substantive differences. Thus, to further evaluate the relative importance of different aspects of provider behaviour to compassion, mixed-model ANOVAs on preregistered demographic/background variables were conducted.

A 2 (gender) x 3 (physician compassion component) mixed model ANOVA showed that the relative importance of the three components was different, F(1.86, 945.24) =32.07, p=<.001, η p 2 =.059 and that ratings differed between males and females, F(1, 508) =8.87, p=.003, η p 2 =.017. Scores were higher for females (M=4.05) than males (M=3.78), with the non-significant interaction between gender and component, F(1.86, 945.24) =3.05, p=.051, η p 2 =.006 indicated that all three components were rated as more important among women.

To test for possible age moderation, participants were median split into younger (18-36 years) and older (37-83 years) groups. A 2 (dichotomised age) x 3 (compassion component) mixed model ANOVA showed the same pattern of differences, F(1.86, 953.03) =116.47, p=<.001, η p 2 =.186, but no differences as a function of age, F(1, 511) =.718, p=.397, η p 2 =.001. However, the interaction between age and component was also significant, F(1.86, 953.00) =16.98, p=<.001, η p 2 =.032. Plot inspection together with follow up t-tests showed that while scores for ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ (p = .02) and, marginally, ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ (p = .11) tended to be lower in the younger group, ‘Attending to Concerns and Preferences’ (p = .08) was rated as marginally more important.

A 4 (ethnic group) x 3 (compassion component) mixed model ANOVA tested whether ethnic group membership predicted differences. As previously, the three components varied in importance, F(1.85, 941.92) =91.69, p=<.001, η p 2 =.153. Again, however, while ethnicity per se was not linked to variations in importance, F(1, 509) = 2.24, p=.083, η p 2 = .013, the degree of difference across components varied across groups, F(5.55, 941.92) = 3.74, p=.002, η p 2 = .022. Post-hoc comparisons indicated ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ scores were lower among Māori versus NZ European (p=<.001), Asian (p=.017), and ‘Other’ groups (p=.031). A visual inspection tended to suggest the interaction was driven by a pattern in which Māori reported a lower valuation of ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ than NZ European but did not differ from NZ European (or the other groups) on the other two components (i.e., ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ or ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’).

Finally, a 4 (attachment categorisation) x 3 (component) mixed model ANOVA showed differences across the components, F(1.86, 941.68) =66.32, p=<.001, η p 2 =.116, but no systematic variation as a function of attachment style F(1, 3) =1.63, p=.182, η p 2 =.010 and no interaction, F(1.64, 941.68) =1.79, p=.103, η p 2 =.011; the previously indicated differences patients ascribed to the three compassion components were consistent across attachment styles.

Discussion

The current report makes several contributions to the study of compassion in healthcare. First, as expected, pre-registered structural analyses identified multiple elements to the behaviours that signal physician compassion — ‘attentively diagnosing and treating’, ‘positive and reassuring communication’, and ‘addressing patient concerns and preferences’. Second, while prior qualitative studies have emphasised the affective (emotional) elements of compassion, analyses consistently showed that patients rated the practical aspects of healthcare (e.g., effective diagnosis, treatment, and involvement in decision-making) as important (or more important). Finally, exploratory findings challenge the implicit assumption that compassion is necessarily seen in the same way across people, indicating that gender, age, and ethnicity were associated with systematic variation in the relative importance placed on different physician behaviours.

The Structure of Compassionate Physician Behaviour

One initial contribution of this work lies in providing a psychometrically robust, quantitative assessment of compassion in a large sample of primary care patients. Small sample sizes, particular clinical settings, and qualitative methods have tended to characterise provider compassion in terms of affective/empathic communication. However, while such characteristics are important, other elements that are theoretically important (i.e., behaviours that alleviate suffering) have been less obvious, creating a particular “view” of compassion.

Empirically identifying three types of provider behaviour that signal compassion to patients is important because it demonstrates that there are multiple ways to signal compassion. The first component included actions such as paying close attention, providing immediate treatment or symptom relief, and showing genuine concern is somewhat evident in prior work [e.g., 11, 23, 26], although this element also included action in the current analyses (discussed below). The second component appears more similar to prior affective characterisations and included offering reassurance, showing warmth, and maintaining eye contact. The third component included behaviours like respecting patient reservations, considering cultural background, and involving patients in decisions. This component captured a value being placed on being included in the treatment process and tailoring treatments to individual needs.

Importantly then, these data provide early evidence that there is more to compassion than affective communication — actions that actively alleviate suffering are important to patients. 11 To the extent that prior studies have characterised compassion in terms of active listening or good communication [e.g. 23, 26], the emergence of a positive and reassuring communication component is expected. However, our analysis shows that these two elements of compassion are not subjectively distinct; this more action oriented aspect of compassion incorporated both listening to the patient’s suffering and providing practical solutions.

More broadly, our data suggest that patients value provider behaviours involving (a) listening, paying attention, and the alleviation of suffering and (b) personalised care more than overtly affective behaviours. Patients value rapid, competent diagnoses and effective treatment as well as appropriate referral while also seeking active participation; these more concrete elements are not just important to the healthcare services they want but are important to their experience of compassion. Contrasts indicated that body language and comforting communication were generally less important than focused attention and health deliverables or actively including patient preferences and tailoring treatment. Thus, while positivity and comforting communication remain important, patients actually place a greater importance on actions that alleviate suffering.

Individual Differences in Patient Priorities Regarding Compassion

A final question regarded whether different patients might have different priorities regarding compassion in primary care. Prior work has tended to implicitly assume that if providers feel greater compassion, the patient will experience them as compassionate. This is clearly problematic and common sense suggests different patients may value different aspects of physician conduct. Prior work suggests that men value genuine understanding while women value continuity and holistic care. 11 In the current report, male patients placed a greater value on ‘Attends to Concerns and Preferences’ and less on the ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ component. Such a pattern is broadly consistent with evidence suggesting a premium on emotional support among women, 42 but a need for both emotional and instrumental support among men. 43 In healthcare, men may prefer active involvement in their treatment such that they experience consideration of their preferences as compassionate.

Early data regarding possible age and ethnic differences in preferences regarding compassion were also in evidence. Older adults tend to prioritise affective outcomes,44,45 with older participants rating ‘Positive and Reassuring Communication’ as more important than younger patients. Similarly, and although the specific ethnic composition of the current sample limits generalisability, Māori were more likely to report body language and individual preferences as indicators of compassionate care while New Zealand European and Asian participants were more likely to report genuine understanding, counselling, and advocacy. 6 In the current report, Māori rated items within the ‘Attentively Diagnoses, Treats, and Refers’ component as less important to their experience of compassion than New Zealand Europeans, perhaps implying a preference for holistic care that extends beyond listening and providing treatment.

Cultural context is likely to play a key role both in shaping how compassion is expressed by providers as well as in how it is interpreted by patients. 46 In the present study, differences observed across ethnic/cultural groups — particularly in relation to attentively diagnosing and treating — should be interpreted as initial evidence that the behaviours that signal compassion to primary care patients likely vary across groups. However, given issues with sampling, the particular recruitment and healthcare context, and the limited and uneven representation of cultural groups in the current report, the specific findings should be seen as preliminary. Future research could derive more specific predictions by using culturally-grounded methodologies to identify likely sources of variation as well as more representative sampling to better understand how compassion is enacted and experienced across cultural groups.

A final potential predictor of patient preferences was attachment. Attachment dynamics shape how individuals perceive and respond to care, with insecurely attached individuals having pronounced fears regarding receiving compassion from others 47 but nonetheless relying on external sources of comfort.48,49 Preoccupied individuals are less confident, seek closeness for support, and express distress with strong emotions. 50 Clinically, interactions with insecure patients are enhanced by engagement, 51 a pattern that is consistent with our finding that preoccupied (anxious) patients rated the positive and reassuring communication as more important than the other elements of physician behaviour.

Again, these subgroup differences should be interpreted cautiously as effect sizes were small and the analyses were exploratory. They provide preliminary evidence of variation in patient preferences, rather than definitive or generalisable demonstrations of group differences.

Clinical Implications, Limitations, and Future Directions

The current report has clear implications for research, clinical practice, and training. Most obviously, patients clearly have ideas regarding how providers should behave to be experienced as compassionate. Importantly, however, our analyses suggest there is no single way to communicate compassion to patients but, rather, that multiple aspects of provider conduct may be experienced as indexing compassion. Indeed, in contrast to the emphases in prior qualitative work, our data suggest that any characterisation of provider compassion that exclusively foregrounds affectively-sensitive communication will fail to capture key elements of what is experienced as “compassionate” in healthcare.

In terms of clinical practice, these findings suggest that providers may benefit from remembering that while empathic communication is important to patients [e.g., 2], behaviours that contribute to the alleviation of suffering are also critical to general practice patients’ experience of compassion. In concrete terms, this focus on “action” includes clearly explaining diagnostic reasoning, initiating timely investigations or treatment, and explicitly involving patients in decision-making, actions that are linked via a focus on the direct and practical alleviation of patient presenting issues (and suffering).

In addition to broadening the ways we think about compassion beyond empathic communication, identifying behaviours that convey compassion offers potential targets for training. Items describing concrete behaviours (e.g., ‘frequent eye contact’ (20), ‘provides immediate treatment’ (5), ‘arranges diagnostic testing and screening’ (6)) offers a promising foundation for future research and training. Developing models of what compassion “looks like” to patients can help researchers and providers remember that compassion is more than a feeling — it is an inherently practical response including concrete actions that alleviate suffering. Of course, in making this suggestion we are in no way advocating for a situation in which “performative” compassion should be trained. Rather, we are suggesting that understanding what compassion looks like is a necessary precursor to being able to manifest this virtuous response in ways that patients recognise.

Training programmes may benefit from using behavioural modelling, role-play, and feedback to enhance the ways in which sensitive communication and concrete clinical action can be combined. For example, training could explicitly teach providers how to integrate compassionate communication with clear care planning, shared decision-making, and concrete responsiveness to patient concerns. Incorporating patient-informed examples of compassionate care (both the more affective and the more concrete elements) into training curricula may also enhance clinicians’ ability to recognise how their behaviour is interpreted in practice.

Second, the current data are clear in suggesting that research and clinical practice must move away from implicitly assuming that compassion “looks the same” to all patients. Rather, patients’ unique histories, cultural backgrounds, and values likely influence how they interpret provider conduct and the specific aspects of behaviour that they experience as most compassionate. For example, a physician could learn that when attending to an older patient, that positive and reassuring communication may be particularly important to the patient’s perception of physician compassion.

At a service level, these findings highlight the importance of iterative learning and flexibility in patient experience initiatives. Standardised approaches to compassionate care may fail to meet the needs of diverse patient group. Instead, healthcare systems nay benefit from embedding opportunities for patient preference elicitation, such as normatively asking patients what aspects of care are most important for them, particularly in culturally diverse settings where expectations of compassion may vary.

Despite these contributions, the current report is not without weaknesses. Obviously, self-report measures can be influenced by desirability, self-presentational, and methodological biases. 14 Second, a study of this kind might appeal to particular types of people, meaning generalisability remains an ongoing issue. Three, the index of preferences regarding compassion was deliberately designed to prioritise breadth of content and, because of its intended use in a general population sample, employed a 1-5 scale. High mean scores suggest changes to the metric or the scale anchors might generate greater variance in subsequent studies.

Given these limitations, the analyses presented here are clearly exploratory in nature. The relative absence of data regarding the structure of patient’s compassion preferences mean that principal component analysis (PCA) was used as a data reduction technique rather than to confirm a theoretically specified structure. Although PCA is appropriate for early-stage scale development and identifying structure in new datasets, it does not provide a test of latent constructs in the same way as confirmatory factory analysis (CFA). Accordingly, the components seen here should be interpreted as preliminary and descriptive, pending replication and confirmatory validation in independent samples. More broadly, although the current measure was informed by theory and existing instruments, more formal psychometric testing is needed, including among clinically and culturally diverse patient groups.

As with most compassion research in healthcare settings, most participants identified as female. Although we tested for gender differences, the generalisability of the findings (both structural and mean-level) remains unknown. More broadly, the sample were self-selected and English-speaking, with access to primary care; again, the extent to which findings will generalise to other populations including those with limited healthcare access or from non-Western cultural contexts is unknown. Studies that recruit more balanced and representative samples to better capture the diversity of patient experiences are an obvious next step.

Such limitations noted, the current report nonetheless extends prior studies in several important ways. While compassion is mandated in most healthcare settings, expected by patients, and associated with better outcomes for both patients and practitioners, what compassion looks like to patients remains unclear. Prior research has offered a heavily affective characterisation and has been conducted in specialty groups that may have particular needs vis-à-vis compassion. More consistent with notions of compassion as an active response that includes actions that alleviate suffering, this study of primary care patients identified three clusters of provider behaviours that patients see as indicative of compassion. Ironically, items related to body language and positive communication were subjectively less important than items related to attention/health deliverables or including the patient’s perspective in diagnoses and tailored treatment, perhaps implying a need to broaden our ideas about what compassion in healthcare looks like. Finally, these early data suggest that different people view different aspects of physician behaviour as differentially important to compassion. Given the intuitive likelihood that compassion does not look the same to all people, ongoing work into how different patients interpret compassionate care is clearly needed.
