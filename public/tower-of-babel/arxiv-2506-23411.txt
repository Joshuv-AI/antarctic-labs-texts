Datasets for Fairness in Language Models: An In-Depth Survey
Jiale Zhang, Zichong Wang, Avash Palikhe, Zhipeng Yin, Wenbin Zhang

Abstract
Fairness benchmarks play a central role in shaping how we evaluate language models, yet surprisingly little attention has been given to examining the datasets that these benchmarks rely on. This survey addresses that gap by presenting a broad and careful review of the most widely used fairness datasets in current language model research, characterizing them along several key dimensions including their origin, scope, content, and intended use to help researchers better appreciate the assumptions and limitations embedded in these resources. To support more meaningful comparisons and analyses, we introduce a unified evaluation framework that reveals consistent patterns of demographic disparities across datasets and scoring methods. Applying this framework to twenty four common benchmarks, we highlight the often overlooked biases that can influence conclusions about model fairness and offer practical guidance for selecting, combining, and interpreting these datasets. We also point to opportunities for creating new fairness benchmarks that reflect more diverse social contexts and encourage more thoughtful use of these tools going forward. All code, data, and detailed results are publicly available at https://github.com/vanbanTruong/Fairness-in-Large-Language-Models/tree/main/datasets to promote transparency and reproducibility across the research community.

Despite the proliferation of these datasets, researchers face two critical challenges when selecting appropriate datasets from the numerous available options

To the best of our knowledge, this paper represents the first comprehensive work to specifically survey and analyze datasets designed for evaluating and mitigating fairness issues in Language Models.
Specifically, we propose a unified taxonomy that organizes existing datasets and introduce a bias analysis methodology to support the systematic identification of dataset-level biases. Together, these contributions support informed dataset selection and the development of more effective fairness interventions. The main contributions of this survey paper are summarized as:

i)

ii)

Unified Framework for Evaluating Dataset-Level Bias.
A unified analysis pipeline is introduced to enable consistent, task-agnostic evaluation of dataset-level biases. This framework supports reproducible, comparable assessments across diverse fairness benchmarks by aligning bias types with general-purpose statistical estimators and applying standardized evaluation procedures tailored to different dataset structures.

iii)

Dataset-Specific Findings and Research Outlook.
The methodology enables the identification of both explicit and subtle forms of bias, revealing persistent identity-linked associations even under controlled conditions. It also highlights key areas for advancement, including expanding multilingual and intersectional coverage, improving fairness metrics for low-resource settings, and supporting community-informed dataset governance.

This gap motivates our survey, which distinguishes itself by centering datasets as a primary object of analysis. We consolidate a wide range of fairness-related datasets scattered across the literature and organize them within a unified taxonomy. Our framework extends prior efforts by offering a multi-dimensional analysis that not only captures structural and linguistic properties but also incorporates a principled bias assessment methodology. This enables researchers to make more informed decisions when selecting datasets aligned with their model architectures, linguistic settings (e.g., monolingual or multilingual), and fairness priorities (e.g., focusing on specific protected attributes or evaluating multiple bias types).

2.1.2
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To ensure the relevance and quality of datasets in this survey, a structured set of inclusion and exclusion criteria was established, focusing on datasets specifically designed for evaluating fairness in language models. These criteria guided the selection process, ensuring that only datasets with explicit relevance to fairness assessments were considered.

Inclusion Criteria.
A dataset was admitted into the corpus if it primarily targets the detection of unjustified adverse effects on specific individuals or social groups, offers a transparent and replicable account of its construction, and demonstrates practical relevance through peer citations or real-world deployments. We further required comprehensive technical documentation—including data format, label taxonomy, and annotation procedures—to ensure reproducibility, as well as an explicit connection to current research questions in LM fairness. Only datasets satisfying all of these conditions advanced to the analysis stage.

Exclusion Criteria.
Conversely, a dataset was discarded if fairness constituted merely a subsidiary aspect of an unrelated NLP task, if its methodological description or technical artefacts were opaque, if it was devised for non-language-model applications, or if it offered negligible empirical contribution owing to limited scope or unclear evaluation metrics. Any single violation of these exclusionary conditions sufficed to remove the dataset from further consideration.

From an initial search that identified 137 potential datasets, these criteria allowed for a focused selection, narrowing the list to 16 datasets that fully met the requirements for evaluating fairness in language models.

2.2
Datasets Taxonomy

Building on the dataset search and selection protocol described in Section
2.1
, we now introduce a five-dimensional taxonomy that underpins the remainder of our analysis. The taxonomy organises fairness datasets by structure, source, linguistic coverage, bias typology, and accessibility—dimensions chosen to capture both how the data are constructed and how they should be applied. Mapping each benchmark to these interconnected axes provides researchers with a systematic way to locate resources that match their language-model setting and specific fairness objectives.

2.2.1
Structure

We categorize fairness datasets by their data structure into two major categories: (1) counterfactual datasets and (2) prompt-based datasets. This classification captures not only the structural differences but also also aligns closely with distinct evaluation methodologies used to measure fairness in LMs. Counterfactual datasets evaluate fairness by measuring how models respond differently when only identity-related attributes or stereotypes are varied in otherwise identical contexts, while prompt-based datasets evaluate fairness by analyzing biases manifested in model-generated content. This taxonomy provides researchers with a clear framework for navigating available fairness evaluation resources and select appropriate datasets based on their specific research objectives.

Counterfactual Datasets.
Counterfactual datasets evaluate LM fairness by comparing responses to nearly identical sentences that differ only in identity-related attributes or stereotypes. By isolating the impact of these characteristics, researchers can accurately detect biases in scenarios where demographic features should ideally have no influence. Such evaluations are valuable for ensuring fairness in applications like coreference resolution, language generation, sentiment analysis, and classification tasks.

Based on the nature of their target evaluation tasks and linguistic manipulation strategies, counterfactual datasets can be subdivided into three categories: (1) Coreference Resolution Bias Datasets, (2) Stereotype Association Datasets, and (3) Sentiment and Classification Bias Datasets. Each subcategory addresses fairness from distinct perspectives, though they all share the fundamental approach of employing controlled pairs or minimal variations in their structure.

i) Coreference Resolution Bias Datasets.
Coreference resolution bias datasets focus explicitly on biases in pronoun resolution tasks. They typically contain pairs of sentences that differ solely in pronouns referring to entities with varied demographic attributes (commonly gender). Examples of datasets in this category include WinoBias
Zhao et al. 2018
and Winogender Schemas
Rudinger et al. 2018
. By analyzing differences in model predictions across these minimally contrasted sentence pairs, researchers can precisely quantify the biases related to gender associations in coreference tasks.

ii) Stereotype Association Datasets.
Stereotype association datasets examine how stereotypes or biased associations toward various demographic groups affect language model outputs. These datasets comprise pairs or tuples of sentences where identity-related characteristics, such as gender, race, nationality, or socio-economic status are altered systematically to contrast stereotypical and anti-stereotypical associations. Examples include CrowS-Pairs
Nangia et al. 2020
and French CrowS-Pairs
Névéol
et al. 2022
. By comparing model predictions on stereotypical versus anti-stereotypical sentences, researchers can directly identify biases embedded within model-generated probabilities or responses.

iii) Sentiment and Classification Bias Datasets.
Sentiment and classification bias datasets employ controlled sentence templates or minimal pairs specifically tailored to evaluate biases in sentiment analysis and text classification tasks. By systematically varying only demographic descriptors within otherwise identical sentiment-bearing or classification-related contexts, datasets like the Equity Evaluation Corpus (EEC)
Kiritchenko and
Mohammad 2018
reveal how identity attributes unjustifiably influence model predictions. For instance, different sentiment scores assigned to “I am a Black woman and I love this song” versus “I am a White man and I love this song” would signal an underlying bias in a sentiment model.

Overlap Between Subcategories.

While these subcategories distinctly delineate evaluation objectives, they inherently share methodological foundations and thus exhibit conceptual overlaps. The common characteristic among all three subcategories is their reliance on minimal linguistic pairs or controlled textual variations, in which only identity-related attributes are manipulated. For example, both Coreference Resolution Bias Datasets and Stereotype Association Datasets frequently involve sentences differing primarily by demographic markers, such as gender pronouns or occupational stereotypes. Similarly, overlaps exist between Stereotype Association Datasets and Sentiment and Classification Bias Datasets, as both categories manipulate identity attributes to examine whether model predictions unfairly shift due to stereotypes or biases. For instance, a sentiment-oriented dataset like the Equity Evaluation Corpus (EEC)

However, despite structural similarities, each subcategory remains distinct due to its primary analytical objectives—whether evaluating bias in coreference resolution, stereotype associations, or sentiment and classification tasks. Understanding these nuances helps researchers select datasets aligned with their fairness evaluation goals while recognizing opportunities for cross-category methodological insights.

2.2.3
Linguistic Coverage

Linguistic coverage represents a critical dimension in fairness dataset taxonomy, determining the cross-cultural applicability and generalizability of fairness evaluations. This dimension primarily distinguishes between monolingual datasets (predominantly English) and those with broader multilingual scope.

Most fairness datasets focus exclusively on English, reflecting its dominance in NLP research and resource availability
Feng et al. 2023
. English-based datasets like WinoBias
Zhao et al. 2018
, CrowS-Pairs
Nangia et al. 2020
, and BBQ
Parrish et al. 2022
benefit from abundant pre-trained models and extensive corpora, facilitating comprehensive fairness evaluations. However, this English-centric approach introduces significant limitations to fairness research across diverse linguistic and cultural contexts. In contrast, multilingual datasets, such as HONEST
Nozza et al. 2021
and BEC-Pro
Bartl et al. 2020
, attempt to bridge this gap by incorporating multiple languages or adapting existing English-based frameworks to other linguistic contexts. These datasets enable cross-cultural fairness evaluations and help identify language-specific biases.

The linguistic coverage of fairness datasets has profound implications for fairness evaluation beyond mere language representation. First, language structure directly influences how biases manifest—grammatical gender in languages like Spanish or French creates different fairness challenges than those in non-gendered languages like English. Second, cultural contexts embedded in different languages shape distinct stereotype patterns, requiring culturally-sensitive evaluation approaches. Finally, models trained predominantly on high-resource languages often exhibit substantial performance disparities when evaluating fairness in low-resource languages, potentially amplifying existing inequities across linguistic communities.

These considerations underscore the significance of linguistic coverage in dataset selection. While monolingual evaluations may offer depth and statistical robustness, comprehensive fairness assessments require cross-linguistic approaches that account for diverse grammatical structures, cultural contexts, and computational resources. This taxonomic dimension thus represents a critical consideration for researchers seeking to align fairness evaluations with specific theoretical frameworks and application contexts.

2.2.4
Bias Typology

Fairness evaluations in LMs require precise characterization of the types of biases being assessed. Previous research has often employed inadequate or incomplete definitions of bias
Blodgett et al. 2020
, frequently focusing on narrow aspects such as specific gender stereotypes
Tamkin et al. 2023
or gender-occupation associations
Rudinger et al. 2018
;
Zhao et al. 2018
. We propose a comprehensive bias typology that encompasses both demographic characteristics and dataset construction factors, providing a more systematic framework for fairness evaluation.

i) Demographic Characteristic Biases.
These biases relate to protected attributes and social identities, largely based on categories defined by the US Equal Employment Opportunities Commission
†
†

https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices
. They include gender bias (WinoBias
Zhao et al. 2018
, WinoGender
Rudinger et al. 2018
), racial and ethnic bias (CrowS-Pairs
Nangia et al. 2020
), age bias (Bold
Dhamala et al. 2021
), religion bias, disability bias, socioeconomic bias, sexual orientation bias (WinoQueer
Felkner et al. 2023
), nationality and cultural bias, physical appearance bias, and intersectional bias. Each category represents a distinct dimension along which LMs may exhibit unfair associations or disparate performance. By categorizing datasets according to these demographic dimensions, researchers can target specific bias concerns in their evaluations.

ii) Dataset Construction Biases.
These biases relate to how fairness datasets themselves are constructed and may influence evaluation results. They include selection bias (non-representative sampling), annotation bias (subjective judgments from human annotators), stereotype reinforcement (framing that inadvertently reinforces stereotypes), cultural context bias (failure to account for cultural differences), temporal bias (outdated social norms), and spurious correlations (misleading statistical patterns). Awareness of these methodological biases is crucial for interpreting fairness evaluation results, as they can significantly influence conclusions about model performance across demographic groups.
This comprehensive typology enables more systematic and targeted fairness evaluations. By clearly differentiating between demographic characteristic biases and dataset construction biases, researchers can better identify the specific mechanisms through which unfairness manifests in LMs. Additionally, this framework facilitates more precise dataset selection, allowing researchers to choose evaluation resources that align with their specific fairness concerns and application contexts.

2.2.5
Accessibility

Datasets can generally be categorized based on their accessibility into two groups: (1) publicly available datasets and (2) restricted-access datasets, though the majority fall under the public category.

Publicly available datasets are accessible to researchers and developers without major barriers. These datasets are often hosted on platforms like Hugging Face Datasets
†
†

https://huggingface.co
or GitHub repositories
†
†

https://github.com
and come with documentation and licensing terms that encourage open use. They are essential for promoting transparency, collaboration, and replicability in fairness research, enabling broad participation in the evaluation and improvement of LMs.

Restricted-access datasets, while less common, require specific permissions or licenses for use. These may be due to proprietary ownership, privacy concerns, or sensitive content. Though not as widely accessible, restricted datasets can offer valuable insights, particularly when addressing fairness issues in specialized or high-stakes domains.

2.3
Bias Definitions and Measurement Framework

Building on the dataset taxonomy introduced in Section
2.2
—defined along the
dimensions of
Structure
,
Source
,
Linguistic
Coverage
,
Bias Typology
, and
Accessibility
—we adopt a common
notation that abstracts away these surface characteristics. Concretely, every
dataset is represented as a finite multiset of linguistic items
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, where each instance
x_{i}
may carry a protected attribute

A\!\in\!\mathcal{A}
(
e.g.
,
gender
,
race
), an optional gold
label
y\!\in\!\mathcal{Y}
, or a continuous score
s\!\in\![0,1]
. Let

P_{\mathcal{P}}
denote the target population distribution over attributes
against which representativeness is judged. Within this unified formal
framework, equally applicable to both minimally paired and naturally occurring
text, we now define four dataset-level bias phenomena and their principled
estimators.

Representativeness Bias.

A dataset exhibits representativeness bias when the empirical distribution of protected
attributes diverges from a known or assumed reference population. This can cause models
trained or evaluated on such datasets to overlook or underperform on marginalized groups.

We define representativeness bias using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the dataset distribution
P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)
and population distribution
P_{\mathcal{P}}(A)
:

B_{\mathrm{rep}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)\;\|\;P_{\mathcal{P}}(A))=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P_{\mathcal{D}}(a)\log\frac{P_{\mathcal{D}}(a)}{P_{\mathcal{P}}(a)}.

(1)

For instance, the BBQ dataset underrepresents
Indigenous
identities
(
<2\%
) relative to U.S. census estimates (
\sim
5%), yielding a non-zero
B_{\mathrm{rep}}
.

Annotation Bias.

Annotation bias occurs when human annotators or labeling algorithms assign different labels
to semantically equivalent content depending on the demographic group referenced. Let
g_{\theta}(x)

be the labeling function (human or automated) and
A
the protected attribute.

We estimate annotation bias as the maximum expected difference in label values between demographic groups:

B_{\text{ann}}=\max_{a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(x)\mid A=a_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(x)\mid A=a_{2}]\right|.

(2)

When gold labels
y
are available, the empirical estimate becomes:

\hat{B}_{\mathrm{ann}}=\max_{a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{a_{1}}}[y]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{a_{2}}}[y]\right|.

(3)

This formulation captures systematic differences in labeling outcomes across identity groups.
As an example, in the widely-used hate-speech corpus of Davidson et al.
(
Davidson et al. 2017
)
, crowdworkers labeled tweets written in African-American English (AAE) as “offensive” nearly twice as often as Standard American English (SAE) tweets
with parallel semantics. As those labels become part of the dataset, any model trained or evaluated on the corpus inherits this annotation bias.

Stereotype Leakage.

Stereotype leakage captures implicit associations in a corpus that link demographic
descriptors to particular traits or roles (
e.g.
,
“nurse”
co-occurring with
female pronouns). Let
W_{G}
be a set of
group words
(identity markers) and

W_{T}
a set of
trait words
. Two information-theoretic statistics quantify the strength of these associations.

Pair-level association.

Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) measures the surprise of observing a specific
pair
(w_{g},w_{t})
relative to independence:

\mathrm{PMI}(w_{g},w_{t})\;=\;\log\frac{P(w_{g},w_{t})}{P(w_{g})\,P(w_{t})},\qquad w_{g}\in W_{G},\;w_{t}\in W_{T}.

(4)

Corpus-level association.

Mutual Information (MI) averages PMI over all group–trait pairs, yielding a
single scalar that summarizes stereotype leakage for the whole dataset:

\mathrm{MI}(W_{G};W_{T})\;=\;\sum_{w_{g}\in W_{G}}\;\sum_{w_{t}\in W_{T}}P(w_{g},w_{t})\,\log\frac{P(w_{g},w_{t})}{P(w_{g})\,P(w_{t})}.

(5)

Empirical probability estimates.

The probabilities in Eqs. (
4
)–(
5
) are obtained from
co-occurrence counts in a context window of size
c
words:

P(w_{g},w_{t})\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{count}_{c}(w_{g},w_{t})}{\sum_{w^{\prime}_{g}\in W_{G}}\sum_{w^{\prime}_{t}\in W_{T}}\mathrm{count}_{c}(w^{\prime}_{g},w^{\prime}_{t})},

(6)

P(w_{g})\;=\;\sum_{w_{t}\in W_{T}}P(w_{g},w_{t}),\qquad P(w_{t})\;=\;\sum_{w_{g}\in W_{G}}P(w_{g},w_{t}).

(7)

High PMI pinpoints specific stereotypical pairs, while high MI indicates a
systematic tendency for demographic terms and trait descriptors to co-occur,
suggesting entrenched stereotypes in the dataset.

Differential Metric Bias.

Differential metric bias arises when scoring functions (
e.g.
, for toxicity, sentiment, regard) yield significantly different outputs across protected groups, even for semantically comparable inputs. Such disparities may reflect flaws in the metric itself or biased correlations in the dataset.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a scoring function applied to instances
x
. We define bias as the score expectation gap between groups:

\Delta_{f}(a_{1},a_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{a_{1}}}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{a_{2}}}[f(x)].

(8)

We test the null hypothesis
\Delta_{f}(a_{1},a_{2})=0
using parametric (
t
-test) or non-parametric (Mann–Whitney
U
) methods, and compute effect size via Cohen’s
d
:

d=\frac{\Delta_{f}(a_{1},a_{2})}{\sigma_{\mathrm{pooled}}},\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{pooled}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}_{a_{1}}+\sigma^{2}_{a_{2}}}{2}}.

(9)

For example, when scoring BOLD prompts, regard scores for sentences referencing
“Muslim”
individuals were systematically lower than those referencing
“Christian”
individuals, despite the controlled structure indicating potential metric bias.

Each bias category highlights a distinct threat to the validity of fairness evaluations in LLM research. Notably, these biases are not mutually exclusive: a dataset may simultaneously suffer from representational imbalance, stereotype leakage, and biased annotations. The estimators defined above provide standardized tools for detecting and quantifying such issues.

3
Counterfactual Datasets

Counterfactual datasets represent a significant category of resources designed to evaluate fairness in LMs. These datasets operate by measuring how model responses or likelihoods change when specific identity-related attributes or stereotypical associations are altered within otherwise identical or minimally differing contexts. This controlled approach allows researchers to isolate and quantify biases related to particular demographic characteristics, such as gender, race, or occupation, which ideally should not influence model behavior in many tasks. Counterfactual datasets are particularly valuable for probing biases in applications like coreference resolution, detecting harmful stereotype associations, and assessing fairness in sentiment analysis or text classification. This section provides a detailed analysis of prominent counterfactual datasets, beginning with a comparative overview based on the taxonomy established in Section
2.2
, followed by in-depth examinations of individual datasets categorized by their primary evaluation focus.

Despite their diversity in design and goals, counterfactual datasets share a common methodological principle: minimal-pair or controlled variation to assess model sensitivity to identity attributes. For example, both WinoBias and CrowS-Pairs introduce demographic shifts within near-identical contexts, but differ in their target tasks—coreference vs. stereotype classification. In terms of data origin, early datasets like WinoBias, Winogender, and EEC rely on templated constructions to ensure control and clarity, while newer datasets like GAP, RedditBias, and CrowS-Pairs increasingly incorporate naturalistic data, crowdsourced content, or participatory design. Finally, accessibility is high across the board: most datasets are publicly released under permissive licenses (
e.g.
, MIT, Apache 2.0, CC BY). This fosters transparency and reproducibility in fairness research, although careful attention must still be paid to construction biases and evaluation assumptions when selecting datasets for downstream model assessments.

Dataset Taxonomy.

WinoBias is a counterfactual-style dataset for coreference resolution. Each sentence pair contrasts a pro-stereotypical case with an anti-stereotypical one, where gender is ideally irrelevant to the resolution task. The dataset was generated using a template-based method, with occupations selected from U.S. labor statistics. The design adheres to a minimal-pair paradigm, which allows for controlled experimental evaluation of bias.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

WinoBias contains 3,160 instances evenly split between male and female pronoun references. Sentences are structured around two entity mentions and a gendered pronoun. The two template types (semantic and syntactic) vary in difficulty and cue dependence. Although the dataset format is primarily text-based, the structure lends itself to automated evaluation protocols in NLP pipelines.

Domain Focus and Significance.

As a foundational benchmark in bias evaluation, WinoBias focuses on coreference resolution under the influence of gendered occupational stereotypes. It has been instrumental in revealing systematic disparities in model behavior and in motivating bias mitigation techniques such as gender-swapping augmentation.

Strengths and Limitations.

WinoBias is praised for its controlled design, which isolates bias-related variables and supports reproducible evaluation. However, it is constrained by its binary gender framing and limited to a fixed set of 40 occupations. Its synthetic templates may not fully capture the complexity or diversity of real-world language use. Additionally, the dataset’s benchmark status raises concerns about exposure in training data of modern large language models, potentially compromising its utility for contemporary evaluations.

3.2.2
Winogender

Description.

The Winogender Schemas dataset, introduced by Rudinger et al.
Rudinger et al. 2018
, is a diagnostic resource for evaluating gender bias in English coreference resolution systems. Like WinoBias, it focuses on occupational stereotypes but adopts a minimal-pair methodology using sentence triplets that differ only by pronoun gender—specifically
he
,
she
, or singular
they
. This design enables the systematic assessment of coreference systems’ differential behavior across gendered references, highlighting patterns of stereotype alignment or divergence.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Winogender belongs to the class of coreference resolution bias datasets and employs a counterfactual minimal-pair structure modeled after Winograd schemas. The dataset was constructed using 120 validated templates, each based on occupation-participant pairs (
e.g.
, accountant–taxpayer). Sentence variants were manually validated through crowdsourcing (Mechanical Turk) to ensure reliable human resolution judgments. The dataset is monolingual (English), though adaptations exist in other languages (
e.g.
, SweWinogender for Swedish). Its primary bias target is binary gender, but it also incorporates the singular
they
, allowing limited exploration of gender neutrality. However, the authors acknowledge the limitations of this representation. The dataset is open-source and released under the MIT License on GitHub.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

Winogender contains 720 total sentences, derived from 120 templates, each instantiated with three gendered pronouns and two participant roles. The data is distributed in tab-separated value (TSV) files, covering both the original templates and the full set of generated sentences. Each template includes slots for OCCUPATION, PARTICIPANT, and PRONOUN, facilitating consistent variation across instances.

Domain Focus and Significance.

The dataset is designed for coreference resolution tasks, particularly to detect and measure gender-related disparities in model behavior when resolving pronouns within occupational contexts. Its methodological rigor and inclusion of a neutral pronoun make it a key benchmark in bias evaluation research.

Strengths and Limitations.

Winogender has become a widely adopted benchmark due to its simplicity and clear diagnostic power. Its minimal-pair structure isolates the effect of pronoun gender, and the use of the singular
they
extends its utility beyond binary bias analysis. It has demonstrated strong positive predictive value for detecting bias in coreference systems. Nevertheless, several structural issues have been noted. These include inconsistencies in template parallelism, incomplete coverage of grammatical cases (
e.g.
, she/her/hers), and potential ambiguity in the use of singular
they
. The limited scope of occupations and artificial nature of the templates may also constrain generalizability to real-world settings. Moreover, its frequent use in prior research raises concerns about memorization by large pre-trained models, diminishing its effectiveness in current evaluations. Notably, it has been criticized for low negative predictive value, meaning its inability to confirm the absence of bias.

Bias Analysis.

Following the framework outlined in Section
2.3
, we assess Winogender along multiple dimensions.

Representativeness Bias.

The WinoGender dataset demonstrates perfect representational balance across gender categories. Each sentence appears in three matched variants—one each with a male, female, and neutral pronoun—yielding exactly 240 instances per gender and a total of 720 sentences. This results in the empirical distribution:

P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{3}&\text{if }A\in\{\text{male},\;\text{female},\;\text{neutral}\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(13)

The KL divergence between the dataset distribution and a uniform reference is:

B_{\mathrm{rep}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)\parallel P_{\mathrm{uniform}}(A)\right)=0.0000,

(14)

indicating no representativeness bias with respect to gender. This perfectly balanced design ensures that any differential model behavior cannot be attributed to demographic skew in the dataset itself. As such, WinoGender provides a controlled, demographically neutral foundation for evaluating gender bias in coreference resolution.

Annotation Bias.

Coreference labels in Winogender are not crowd-sourced; they are generated deterministically from the sentence templates themselves. For every minimal pair, the correct antecedent is fixed by the syntactic schema, independent of pronoun gender. Consequently, the empirical label means are equal across all gender conditions:

\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=\text{male}]=\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=\text{female}]=\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=\text{neutral}],

(15)

This equality yields the annotation bias estimator of Equation
2
:

B_{\mathrm{ann}}=\max_{a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigl|\,\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=a_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=a_{2}]\,\bigr|=0.

(16)

Because labels are rule-derived rather than human-judged, the usual sources of annotation bias—rater prejudice, inconsistent guidelines, domain knowledge gaps—are effectively eliminated. The only residual concern is the linguistic ambiguity of singular
they
: although the antecedent is unambiguous in the schema, some coreference engines trained on noisy data might mis-parse these cases. From a dataset standpoint, however, the annotation process itself introduces
no
systematic gender skew, making Winogender an exceptionally clean test bed for isolating model-induced gender bias.

Stereotype Leakage.

To quantify whether Winogender embeds latent gender–profession associations, we compute pointwise mutual information (PMI) between each pronoun token

\mathrm{PMI}(w_{g},w_{t})=\log\!\frac{P(w_{g},w_{t})}{P(w_{g})P(w_{t})}.

(17)

Because the dataset is
fully counter-balanced

P(w_{g},w_{t})=\tfrac{1}{|{\cal A}|}\,P(w_{t}),\qquad P(w_{g})=\tfrac{1}{|{\cal A}|},\qquad|{\cal A}|=3,

(18)

which yields,

\mathrm{PMI}(w_{g},w_{t})=0\quad\forall(w_{g},w_{t}).

(19)

Hence the estimator detects

Although real-world statistics (

3.2.3
GAP (Gendered Ambiguous Pronouns)

Description.

The Gendered Ambiguous Pronouns (GAP) dataset, introduced by Webster et al.
Webster et al. 2018
, is a large-scale, gender-balanced benchmark aimed at evaluating gender bias in coreference resolution. Unlike prior corpora that suffer from small scale, manual construction, and skewed masculine bias, GAP offers a more naturalistic setting by drawing examples directly from English Wikipedia. Each instance features an ambiguous pronoun (“he” or “she”) and two named entities of the same gender, prompting systems to resolve the coreference based on context rather than relying on simple gender-based heuristics.

Dataset Taxonomy.

GAP belongs to the broader category of coreference resolution bias datasets but differs from minimal-pair counterfactual designs by utilizing naturally occurring pronoun ambiguity in real-world text. All examples are extracted from Wikipedia, making the dataset grounded in diverse, authentic content. Although monolingual in scope, GAP reflects a wide topical range due to Wikipedia’s broad coverage. The dataset was explicitly constructed to balance gender representation, thereby addressing the feminine pronoun underrepresentation seen in corpora like OntoNotes. However, its design remains limited to binary gender categories. GAP is openly accessible through a GitHub repository maintained by Google AI Language and is released under the Apache 2.0 license.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset contains 8,908 labeled pronoun-name coreference pairs, distributed across test (4,000), development (4,000), and validation (908) sets. Each instance is stored in tab-separated format with eleven columns capturing detailed annotation and positional metadata—including the pronoun, two named entities, coreference labels, and source URL. This structured design enables systematic evaluation and error analysis.

Domain Focus and Significance.

GAP focuses on the coreference resolution of ambiguous gendered pronouns in natural language. Its contributions are particularly notable in highlighting gender disparities in state-of-the-art systems. By presenting examples that require nuanced contextual understanding rather than reliance on stereotypical associations, GAP serves as a robust testbed for bias-sensitive model evaluation.

Strengths and Limitations.

GAP improves upon earlier benchmarks in both scale and gender balance, providing a more equitable foundation for evaluating pronoun resolution. Its reliance on Wikipedia ensures linguistic diversity and realism. Furthermore, the ambiguous same-gender candidate structure challenges simplistic resolution strategies, exposing limitations in model reasoning. Nonetheless, GAP’s design focuses narrowly on name-pronoun pairs, potentially excluding broader phenomena in coreference resolution. The exclusive reliance on Wikipedia introduces domain constraints, and the task’s binary framing may overlook non-binary or gender-neutral references. The formal and objective style of Wikipedia articles may also underrepresent bias expressions that occur in more subjective or colloquial contexts. Finally, some studies have reported generalization issues when applying GAP-trained models to other datasets, hinting at possible domain-specific artifacts or annotation mismatches.

Finally,

The publicly released dataset (available via the official GitHub and the Kaggle “Gendered Pronoun Resolution” challenge) includes the gold-standard pronoun–antecedent labels for each instance, and the accompanying documentation/paper reports the detailed label distributions and agreement breakdowns to support analyses of gender-specific annotation behavior

3.3
Stereotype Association Datasets

Stereotype association datasets are designed to evaluate how language models handle social stereotypes by comparing their responses to variations in identity-related attributes. These datasets commonly use paired or contextualized inputs, differing only in demographic content, to assess whether models display stereotypical biases. This section introduces four representative datasets:
StereoSet
,
CrowS-Pairs
,
RedditBias
, and
HolisticBias
. We begin with an overview and comparative analysis, followed by detailed examinations of each dataset.

3.3.1
StereoSet

Description.

StereoSet
Nadeem
et al. 2021
is a benchmark dataset designed to evaluate stereotypical bias in pretrained language models using a structured triplet-ranking task. It adopts the Context Association Test (CAT) format, where each instance presents a context involving a demographic group and three candidate continuations: one reflecting a stereotype, one countering the stereotype, and one semantically meaningless. A fair and fluent model is expected to rank the anti-stereotypical continuation higher than the stereotypical one, while rejecting the nonsensical choice. This paradigm enables the assessment of both bias and linguistic fluency. StereoSet has become a widely used benchmark for evaluating intrinsic social bias in models such as BERT, GPT-2, RoBERTa, and GPT-3.

Dataset Taxonomy.

StereoSet falls under the Stereotype Association Datasets category. It includes two task formats: an intrasentence format, where a single sentence contains a blank to be filled (
e.g.
, “Girls tend to be more __ than boys”), and an intersentence format, where the model selects one of three candidate sentences following a given context. This dual-format design enables evaluations at both syntactic and discourse levels. The dataset was constructed by U.S.-based crowdworkers on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Annotators were provided with target demographic terms (
e.g.
, “Arab,” “girl,” “doctor”) and asked to write contexts that evoke or challenge stereotypes, followed by three continuations corresponding to the stereotype, anti-stereotype, and nonsense categories. A second round of crowdworkers validated the labels. The dataset covers four major bias domains—gender, profession, race, and religion—with each instance annotated by both domain and specific target group. All content is in informal American English, with no dialectal restrictions. The vocabulary includes a wide range of demographic markers and stereotype-related terms. While the dataset includes a substantial proportion of positively connoted stereotypes (about 59%), the overall distribution skews negative.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

StereoSet comprises 16,995 instances derived from 321 unique target terms, distributed across four domains: Gender (40), Race (149), Profession (120), and Religion (12). The dataset is split into development and test sets at a 25/75 ratio based on target terms, with no training set provided. Each instance contains a context and three labeled continuations, along with metadata such as domain and target group. The dataset design supports automatic scoring using two primary metrics: the Language Modeling Score (LM), which quantifies the model’s ability to avoid meaningless continuations, and the Stereotype Score (SS), which captures the model’s tendency to prefer stereotypical over anti-stereotypical options. These are combined into the Idealized CAT Score (ICAT), a unified metric that balances fluency and fairness.

Domain Focus and Significance.

StereoSet targets widely recognized stereotypes within U.S. society, particularly those surrounding gender, race, religion, and professional roles. While not restricted to legally protected classes, its inclusion of the “Profession” category enables a broader investigation of occupational bias, which intersects with many real-world applications of language models.

Strengths and Limitations.

A major strength of StereoSet lies in its controlled triplet format, which provides a standardized protocol for comparing language model outputs across domains. Its broad domain coverage and validated examples enhance its reliability as a diagnostic tool for social bias. Furthermore, the ICAT score effectively integrates bias sensitivity and linguistic fluency into a single interpretable metric, discouraging models from scoring well through random guessing. However, the dataset is limited by its U.S.-centric perspective, potentially reducing its applicability in global contexts. Certain stereotypes may be interpreted as neutral or positive (
e.g.
, “Asians are good at math”), introducing subjectivity in fairness assessments. The use of meaningless continuations can artificially inflate LM scores, masking deeper biases. Additionally, the dataset focuses on single-axis identities, omitting intersectional considerations such as “Black women”. Finally, its closed-ended format limits direct use in evaluating free-form generation tasks unless paired with post-hoc scoring strategies.

Bias Analysis.

StereoSet exhibits significant bias-related challenges that manifest along multiple dimensions, including representativeness bias, annotation bias, and stereotype leakage. Each dimension introduces specific methodological concerns that directly impact the reliability and interpretability of fairness evaluations based on this dataset.

Annotation Bias.
The StereoSet dataset’s annotation process reveals several important aspects of potential annotation bias. Our analysis of inter-annotator agreement using Cohen’s Kappa score shows a moderate to substantial agreement level (κ = 0.663) across the five annotators. However, this agreement is not uniform, with Annotator 1 demonstrating consistently higher agreement with other annotators (κ = 0.74-0.75) compared to the agreement levels between other annotators (κ = 0.61-0.62).
The agreement with gold labels shows a notable pattern, with Annotator 1 achieving significantly higher agreement (96.6%) compared to other annotators (84.5-84.9%). This substantial difference suggests potential bias in the gold label creation process, possibly indicating that the gold labels were either created by Annotator 1 or heavily influenced by their annotations.
Analysis across different bias types reveals relatively consistent agreement levels:

This category, which corresponds to the “unrelated” category in the gold labels, suggests that annotators sometimes made finer distinctions in their judgments than the three-category system (stereotype, anti-stereotype, unrelated) could capture.

The findings suggest that future iterations of the dataset might benefit from: i) re-evaluating gold labels to ensure balanced representation across annotators; ii) considering a more granular categorization system to distinguish unrelated content from contextually relevant but non-stereotypical cases; iii) providing more detailed annotation guidelines to improve inter-annotator agreement; and iv) collecting additional annotations for high-disagreement cases.

3.3.2
CrowS-Pairs

Description.

CrowS-Pairs
Nangia et al. 2020
is a diagnostic dataset designed to evaluate social biases in masked language models (MLMs) through contrastive sentence pairs. Each example comprises two minimally different sentences: one referencing a historically marginalized group and the other referencing a contrasting advantaged group. The goal is to assess whether a language model systematically favors the stereotypical sentence by assigning it higher likelihood. An unbiased model would assign equal or lower likelihood to the stereotypical version, thus reflecting a preference aligned with social fairness.

Dataset Taxonomy.

CrowS-Pairs adopts a contrastive sentence-pair structure, where the only substantive difference between each pair is the demographic group mentioned. Each pair is labeled as “stereotypical” or “anti-stereotypical” depending on whether the first sentence reinforces or challenges a stereotype. The dataset was crowd-authored via Amazon Mechanical Turk, where workers were provided with partial prompts (
e.g.
, from MultiNLI or ROCStories) and instructed to compose minimal pairs reflecting societal biases. Substitutions were guided by a predefined list of group pairs (
e.g.
, “Black” vs. “White,” “women” vs. “men”), and all submissions underwent a rigorous validation process involving five annotators. Only examples with consensus on minimal edit distance and stereotype direction were retained.

The dataset is monolingual (American English), stylistically diverse (including narrative, conversational, and factual tones), and grounded in naturalistic contexts. Sentence construction aimed for minimal edits outside of group substitutions, though some stereotypical sentences may appear more fluent—a reflection of societal linguistic priors. CrowS-Pairs targets nine demographic bias categories: race/color, gender/gender identity, sexual orientation, religion, age, nationality, disability, physical appearance, and socioeconomic status. Each pair is tagged with a primary bias type.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The final version contains 1,508 validated sentence pairs, covering approximately 150–200 instances per bias category, with race/color being the most prevalent. Each data entry includes the sentence pair, the stereotype label (“stereo” or “anti-stereo”), and a category tag. The format is distributed in plain text or JSONL and is compatible with both MLMs and autoregressive language models using pseudo-likelihood or log-probability scoring.

Domain Focus and Significance.

CrowS-Pairs is focused on U.S.-centric social biases and includes a broader range of protected attributes than many earlier datasets, such as StereoSet. By capturing underrepresented axes like disability, age, and body image, it aligns with wider definitions of fairness and discrimination in NLP.

Strengths and Limitations.

A major strength of CrowS-Pairs lies in its contrastive minimal-pair design, which allows for fine-grained measurement of model preferences. Its broad coverage of nine demographic axes provides a valuable resource for analyzing diverse bias types. The dataset is well-validated and yields interpretable evaluation metrics such as percentage of stereotypical preference, making it accessible for both research and public communication. Moreover, it can be used with various model architectures, from MLMs like BERT to autoregressive models like GPT.

However, the dataset also has limitations. The short length and isolated nature of sentence pairs limit its ability to capture contextual or discourse-level biases. While validation aimed to minimize unnatural language, some anti-stereotypical sentences may still sound implausible. Additionally, the dataset does not capture intersectional identities, focusing instead on single demographic contrasts. Later audits identified minor inconsistencies in a few samples, which are acknowledged by the authors in their GitHub repository. Lastly, while the dataset is compatible with autoregressive models, it was originally designed with MLMs in mind, and thus adaptations (
e.g.
, prompt engineering) may be needed for newer model families.

Bias Analysis.

Representativeness Bias.

Following the methodology defined in Section
2.3
, we assess representativeness bias in CrowS-Pairs by comparing its demographic distribution to U.S. population priors from the 2024 Bureau of Labor Statistics. Let
P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)
denote the empirical distribution across demographic categories and
P_{\mathcal{P}}(A)
denote the reference distribution. The representativeness bias is computed as Eq.
1
, the Kullback–Leibler divergence between these two distributions. This metric reflects the extent to which the dataset over- or under-represents certain demographic groups relative to real-world proportions.

Race–Color.

CrowS–Pairs significantly over-samples sentences targeting
Black

White

\mathrm{KL}_{\text{race}}=0.2664,

(23)

Gender.

The gender axis is more balanced, with a slight male overrepresentation (55.1% vs. 51.2%) and female underrepresentation (44.9% vs. 48.8%), yielding

\mathrm{KL}_{\text{gender}}=0.0031.

(24)

This negligible divergence indicates minimal representativeness bias for binary gender, though non-binary identities are not annotated.

These disparities may skew fairness evaluations unless corrected by reweighting or supplemented by more balanced datasets.

3.3.3
RedditBias

Description.

RedditBias
Barikeri
et al. 2021
is a conversational bias evaluation dataset constructed from real-world Reddit discussions. Unlike synthetic datasets such as StereoSet or CrowS-Pairs, RedditBias captures naturally occurring biased language in online conversations. It was introduced to examine how stereotypes and hateful content manifest in dialogue models and to investigate the trade-off between debiasing efforts and preserving response quality. The dataset is particularly suited for evaluating the robustness of dialogue agents in handling toxic, biased, or sensitive inputs, given its origin in unfiltered, user-generated content.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Structurally, RedditBias offers two levels of granularity for evaluation: full Reddit comments (up to 150 characters) and phrase-level windows (±7 tokens around a target term). These units serve as prompts to probe model responses. The dataset was compiled using 3.33 years of Reddit data obtained via the Pushshift API. It targets four demographic axes—Gender, Race, Religion, and Queerness—using curated lists of group identifiers (
e.g.
, “women,” “Muslims”) and negative attributes (
e.g.
, “manipulative,” “mentally ill”). Phrases such as “gays are mentally ill” were retrieved using pattern-matching queries. Extracted data was filtered for readability and toxicity, with bias expressions centered in the phrase windows. The language is informal, often offensive, and rich in sarcasm and slurs, reflecting the authentic linguistic style of Reddit. This real-world grounding distinguishes RedditBias from crowd-sourced or templated datasets. The dataset focuses on overt, toxic stereotypes and avoids subtle or positive bias expressions. It is distributed under a CC BY-NC-SA license and includes verbatim Reddit content, which may limit certain applications.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset comprises roughly 11,800 manually annotated Reddit comments, of which around 68% are labeled as biased. The phrase-level subset contains 6,872 entries, with 65% marked as biased. The test split includes about 200–250 biased examples per demographic axis, totaling approximately 1,200 test items. Each instance includes the comment or phrase, a binary bias label, and metadata specifying the bias type, target group, and stereotype. In addition to intrinsic evaluations, the dataset supports contrastive testing by neutralizing group terms (
e.g.
, replacing “gay” with “straight” in biased assertions).

Domain Focus and Significance.

RedditBias is tailored for conversational AI research, particularly the evaluation of bias in dialogue agents like DialoGPT. It exposes models to challenging, real-world language that is often underrepresented in benchmark datasets. This makes it well-suited for stress-testing models’ behavior in adversarial or hate-prone conversational contexts.

Strengths and Limitations.

A key strength of RedditBias lies in its realism—it reflects how bias and hate speech appear in natural online dialogue. The dual-level design (comment and phrase) enables both coarse and fine-grained assessments. It also supports both intrinsic scoring using model-likelihood metrics and downstream evaluations of dialogue performance. However, the dataset has several limitations. Its domain specificity may reduce generalizability to other platforms or languages. It focuses solely on four demographic dimensions and explicitly targets harmful bias, omitting subtle or structural forms. The reliance on pattern-matching during data collection may overlook implicit stereotypes, and the use of sarcasm or ambiguous phrasing complicates annotation and model interpretation. Additionally, the dataset contains highly offensive content, which necessitates ethical caution in use and publication. Its composition is heavily skewed toward toxic expressions, without balancing benign or neutral dialogue, which may influence evaluation metrics.

Bias Analysis.

RedditBias presents a unique case for bias analysis due to its real-world, unfiltered origin. It is particularly suited for evaluating a model’s exposure to and propagation of harmful stereotypes in dialogue generation. Specific metrics such as model-generated likelihood scores, differential response tendencies, or sentiment shifts across demographic prompts can be employed to quantify the bias exhibited during generation. Further analysis following the framework in Section
2.3
can reveal representational imbalances or model vulnerabilities specific to conversational settings.

3.3.4
HolisticBias

Description.

HolisticBias
Smith et al. 2022
is a large-scale dataset designed to assess language model bias across an exceptionally broad and inclusive set of demographic descriptors. Unlike earlier datasets that focus on narrow identity categories or rigid templates, HolisticBias adopts a participatory and scalable framework. It systematically spans 13 demographic axes, incorporating nearly 600 descriptors that represent a wide spectrum of identities, such as race, gender, disability, religion, and more. The dataset’s flexible design and breadth have made it a foundational resource for fairness evaluation in natural language processing.

Dataset Taxonomy.

HolisticBias belongs to the class of prompt-based bias datasets. It generates naturalistic or semi-naturalistic sentences by inserting identity descriptors and noun phrases into 26 hand-crafted sentence templates. These templates include statements, questions, and descriptions that vary across grammatical structures (
e.g.
, declaratives, interrogatives, first- and third-person frames). Example prompts might include conversational frames such as “I’m sorry to hear that”, immediately following an identity descriptor to elicit subtle bias. The dataset was constructed using community-informed descriptors to ensure inclusiveness and ethical representation. The result is a large corpus of approximately 472,000 English prompts, generated via combinatorial expansion of descriptors, 30 common noun phrases, and sentence templates. While most descriptors correspond to single demographic axes, some intersecting terms like “Black woman” also appear. HolisticBias is publicly available through HuggingFace Datasets under a CC BY-SA license, with a multilingual extension (MMHB) that translates descriptors into more than 50 languages.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

With nearly half a million instances, HolisticBias stands as one of the most comprehensive datasets in bias evaluation. Each instance consists of a sentence constructed from one descriptor, one noun, and a template, and is annotated with metadata identifying its corresponding demographic axis. These prompts are used in various tasks, such as measuring token likelihoods, evaluating generation outputs, or probing model responses to demographic variation.

Domain Focus and Significance.

The dataset emphasizes inclusivity across marginalized and underrepresented groups, extending fairness research beyond conventional binary gender or racial distinctions. Its structure allows researchers to test for biases not only across dominant axes like race and gender, but also in less-explored areas such as neurodiversity, socio-economic class, and language background. This expansive domain focus makes HolisticBias particularly suited for uncovering subtle or intersectional biases.

Strengths and Limitations.

HolisticBias’s major strength lies in its comprehensiveness and flexibility. It supports a variety of evaluation protocols, including token probability analysis, generation bias quantification, and classification robustness. Its participatory design process enhances the quality and cultural sensitivity of the descriptors. The multilingual extension (MMHB) further enables fairness studies across languages and cultural contexts. However, some prompts—despite careful template design—may sound unnatural in real-world conversations, potentially affecting downstream evaluations. Additionally, because the dataset primarily centers on explicit identity mentions in isolated sentences, it may fall short in capturing context-dependent or implicit bias. Its sheer size, while enabling breadth, can also introduce computational burdens for full-scale evaluation. Finally, evolving identity terminology and overlapping taxonomies (
e.g.
, “Jewish” as both religious and ethnic) present ongoing classification challenges.

Bias Analysis.

Representativeness Bias.
We evaluate representativeness bias in the HolisticBias dataset using the KL divergence-based formulation defined in Section 3.3, which measures the divergence between the empirical distribution
P_{\mathcal{D}}(A)

Annotation Bias.
In the context of HolisticBias, direct analysis of annotation bias is not feasible because the dataset does not provide gold labels such as toxicity, sentiment, or other human- or model-generated annotations. Instead, HolisticBias is designed as a prompt-based resource for probing model fairness, with each instance consisting of a sentence template and a demographic descriptor, but lacking any associated ground-truth label. As a result, the dataset itself is neutral with respect to annotation bias: any observed annotation bias would necessarily arise from the external labeling function (
e.g.
, a toxicity classifier or human raters) applied to the dataset, rather than from the dataset’s construction. To empirically assess annotation bias using HolisticBias, one must first annotate the dataset with a consistent labeling function and then compare the expected label values across demographic groups as formalized in Equation
2

Stereotype Leakage.

Stereotype leakage refers to the presence of biased associations in a dataset’s co-occurrence patterns between group identifiers (
e.g.
, “woman”, “immigrant”) and trait-bearing terms (
e.g.
, “lazy”, “smart”, “nurse”), which can reinforce societal stereotypes in trained models. It is typically quantified using pointwise mutual information (PMI) or aggregate mutual information (MI) between group and trait word distributions. However, HolisticBias is explicitly
not
constructed from naturally occurring corpora. Instead, it is generated via controlled template filling using identity descriptors and neutral nouns (
e.g.
, “I am a Deaf person”, “Hi! I’m a Buddhist.”). Each sentence typically contains only a single group term and lacks additional content that would enable meaningful co-occurrence analysis.

We implemented PMI and MI computation pipelines using a 5-token context window, but no statistically valid group–trait co-occurrences were detected. This outcome is by design: HolisticBias aims to
isolate
identity mentions for controlled probing of model bias, not to reflect real-world linguistic patterns or encode implicit stereotypes.

Therefore, HolisticBias is
not suitable for stereotype leakage analysis
via PMI or MI, as it lacks the spontaneous co-occurrence structure necessary for these metrics. This contrasts with naturalistic corpora such as Wikipedia or Reddit, where co-occurrence patterns are organically present and stereotype leakage can be meaningfully quantified. HolisticBias’s strength lies in its ability to test axis-specific model behavior under tightly controlled inputs, not in revealing embedded societal biases within its own textual structure.

3.4
Sentiment and Classification Bias Datasets

This subsection discusses datasets created to evaluate fairness specifically in the context of downstream NLP tasks like sentiment analysis and text classification. These datasets typically use controlled templates or minimal pairs where demographic terms are varied within sentences relevant to the classification task, allowing assessment of whether identity attributes unfairly influence model predictions.

3.4.1
Equity Evaluation Corpus (EEC)

Description.

The Equity Evaluation Corpus (EEC), introduced by Kiritchenko and Mohammad (2018), is a benchmark dataset designed to detect demographic bias—specifically race and gender bias—in sentiment analysis systems. It consists of sentence templates with controlled lexical substitutions where only a race- or gender-associated term (
e.g.
, names, pronouns) is varied, enabling a counterfactual comparison of sentiment intensity scores. The goal is to identify whether systems yield different sentiment predictions for sentences that are semantically identical except for demographic references.

Dataset Taxonomy.

EEC falls under the category of sentiment and classification bias datasets. Its structure is based on a minimal-pair counterfactual design using templates with emotion-laden contexts. Sentence variants are created by swapping gender- or race-associated words, allowing isolated evaluation of bias effects. The dataset is monolingual (English), publicly available, and associated with a *SEM 2018 paper. It focuses on sentiment-related biases in text classification systems and is likely distributed under a permissive license.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset comprises 8,640 English sentences generated from a relatively small number of core templates. Each sentence includes emotional expressions, and variants differ only in the demographic term used. The text-based format supports standardized input to sentiment analysis models, making it straightforward for quantitative bias evaluations.

Domain Focus and Significance.

EEC targets the domain of sentiment analysis, serving as one of the earliest datasets explicitly developed to probe racial and gender disparities in real-world NLP tasks. It played a pivotal role in the analysis of over 200 participating systems from SemEval-2018 Task 1, helping to raise awareness of demographic sensitivity in model outputs.

Strengths and Limitations.

One of EEC’s primary strengths lies in its clear and replicable methodology: by controlling for all lexical content except a single demographic term, it enables focused comparisons and statistical evaluations of bias. Its early adoption and impact on large-scale system assessments further underscore its value.

However, the dataset has notable limitations. It is highly sensitive to minor rewordings of template structures, raising concerns about robustness. Its reliance on binary gender terms and a small number of templates limits both its inclusivity and linguistic diversity. Moreover, the template-based nature of the data leads to concerns about artificiality and limited ecological validity. Even the dataset creators acknowledge that EEC should be viewed as a complementary tool rather than a comprehensive benchmark for bias.

3.4.2
Bias NLI

Description.

Bias NLI refers to a collection of datasets and methodological efforts designed to probe bias—particularly gender bias—within the Natural Language Inference (NLI) task. As a classification task where models predict entailment, contradiction, or neutrality between a premise and a hypothesis, NLI serves as a valuable testbed for investigating whether models make predictions based on logical inference or rely on spurious correlations. Many approaches introduce template-based counterfactuals by varying demographic terms in hypotheses while preserving the ground truth label. Others analyze existing NLI datasets, such as SNLI, to reveal annotation artifacts or demographic term dependencies that may reinforce social biases.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Bias NLI falls under the category of classification bias datasets, with a strong emphasis on sentiment and stereotype-related evaluation. Its structure varies from manually curated hypothesis–premise pairs to large-scale template-generated corpora, some exceeding five million examples. These datasets are primarily in English, and their construction draws either from transforming existing corpora or creating synthetic sentence pairs to test model sensitivity to demographic cues. The typology of bias explored in Bias NLI primarily involves gender, though race and religion have also been considered. In addition to social bias, these datasets shed light on structural issues such as hypothesis-only bias, contradiction-word bias, and the more recently identified attestation bias, where models depend on memorized propositions rather than performing genuine inference. Accessibility varies depending on the particular dataset, but many are publicly released alongside research publications.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

Bias NLI datasets typically consist of premise–hypothesis pairs labeled with standard NLI classes (entailment, contradiction, neutral). The data format is standardized and compatible with most NLI model pipelines. Depending on the generation method, dataset size can range from small diagnostic sets to multi-million-scale corpora derived from templates. The premise and hypothesis often differ only in a single demographic marker, allowing controlled comparison between stereotype-congruent and incongruent cases.

Domain Focus and Significance.

The domain focus is squarely on natural language inference, using the task as a lens to examine model reliance on demographic stereotypes. By analyzing performance differences across minimally different input pairs, Bias NLI enables researchers to diagnose whether models are making predictions based on reasoning or biased lexical shortcuts. It has played a pivotal role in demonstrating how NLI systems may be vulnerable to demographic confounds, and how these interact with dataset design flaws.

Strengths and Limitations.

A major strength of Bias NLI is its ability to isolate whether models are truly performing inference or instead exploiting surface-level cues linked to protected attributes. The diagnostic nature of these datasets allows researchers to pinpoint specific sources of bias, such as hypothesis-only bias or word-overlap effects. However, the reliance on template-generated sentences can introduce artificiality and make models overly sensitive to phrasing. In some cases, evaluations using Bias NLI may reflect known NLI dataset artifacts more than the presence of social bias, complicating interpretation. Moreover, template-based construction may exacerbate attestation bias, causing models to memorize and reuse stereotypical associations. Finally, while these datasets are effective at highlighting known issues, they may underexplore other forms of latent bias or intersectional effects.

Differential Metric Bias.

Differential Matrics Bias analysis is a common approach in fairness research, typically used to compare the average values of various metrics (such as sentiment, toxicity, regard, or gender polarity) across different demographic groups within a dataset. This method relies on the presence of explicit, gold-standard demographic annotations—such as gender, race, or religion labels—for each example, enabling robust group-wise comparisons. However, the BiasNLI dataset does not provide such explicit demographic labels. Instead, any attempt to infer group membership must rely on heuristic keyword matching within the text (
e.g.
, assigning a sentence to the “male” group if it contains words like “he” or “man”). This approach is inherently limited: it may miss implicit references, misclassify ambiguous cases, and cannot distinguish between subject and object mentions or account for non-binary identities. Furthermore, the presence of a demographic keyword in the text does not necessarily indicate the true group membership of the main entity or author, but merely that the group is mentioned. As a result, any group-based analysis in BiasNLI would reflect only the distribution of model scores for sentences mentioning certain demographic terms, rather than systematic differences across real-world groups. Given these limitations, we do not report Differential Matrics Bias results for BiasNLI, as such analysis would be unreliable and potentially misleading.

3.5
Conclusion on Counterfactual Datasets

Counterfactual datasets have played a foundational role in the fairness evaluation of language models by enabling controlled experiments that isolate the effects of identity attributes—such as gender, race, or occupation—through minimally varied sentence pairs. Early benchmarks like WinoBias and Winogender introduced template-based designs focused on binary gender stereotypes, while later datasets such as GAP, EEC, and CrowS-Pairs expanded demographic coverage and introduced more naturalistic or sentiment-oriented contexts. This evolution has continued with datasets like StereoSet, HolisticBias, and WinoQueer, which address underrepresented identities, incorporate participatory design, or explore sociolinguistic variation. However, challenges remain. Template-based corpora offer experimental control at the expense of realism, while naturalistic or crowdsourced datasets provide ecological validity but suffer from confounds and potential annotation bias. In addition, the predominance of English and Western-centric demographic framing limits cross-cultural generalization. Researchers must therefore navigate trade-offs between control, diversity, and representativeness, critically assessing dataset construction, target biases, and known limitations. Ultimately, while counterfactual datasets offer indispensable tools for probing specific fairness concerns, they are best employed alongside complementary evaluation methods and with an understanding of their methodological boundaries to ensure robust, inclusive, and contextually grounded fairness assessments.

Prompt-based datasets represent a significant category of resources for evaluating fairness in language models (LMs). Unlike counterfactual datasets that rely on minimal-pair comparisons, prompt-based datasets assess fairness by analysing the content generated by an LM in response to an initial prompt or question. This mirrors real-world LM applications (text completion, dialogue, QA). Evaluation typically examines generated text for bias manifestations such as toxicity, stereotype reinforcement, or differential treatment triggered by demographic characteristics present or implied in the prompt. Sentence completion datasets such as BOLD, RealToxicityPrompts, and HONEST typically involve open-ended or fill-in-the-blank formats, revealing biases in spontaneous generation, such as stereotype evocation or toxic continuations. In contrast, QA-based datasets—like BBQ, UnQover, TrustGPT, and Grep-BiasIR—test model reasoning under ambiguity or constraint, enabling finer-grained diagnosis of biased inference mechanisms.

The source of prompts plays a significant role in determining lexical style and demographic coverage. Some datasets use naturally occurring prompts from corpora (
e.g.
, Wikipedia in BOLD), while others rely on template-based constructions authored or curated by researchers (
e.g.
, BBQ, HONEST). Each approach has advantages: natural prompts preserve ecological validity, while templates allow precise control over demographic features and semantic variation.

Linguistically, most datasets are English-centric, with HONEST being one of the few to offer multilingual variants. This English dominance reflects broader trends in NLP, but also underscores the need for more cross-linguistic fairness evaluation tools.

Regarding bias typology, datasets vary in their focus and granularity. BBQ has the broadest demographic axis coverage (nine dimensions), while RealToxicityPrompts concentrates on toxicity and implicit stereotypes. BOLD targets sentiment and regard across demographic domains, and HONEST focuses on gender-inflected sentence completions. Grep-BiasIR and TrustGPT include model-in-the-loop retrieval or judgment, expanding evaluation to dynamic LM interactions. Construction biases (
e.g.
, from prompt source or annotation method) are also present to varying degrees and must be accounted for when interpreting results.

The datasets listed in Table
7
illustrate the richness and diversity of prompt-based fairness evaluations. Each structural and methodological design choice—whether in prompt sourcing, task format, or annotation—shapes the types of biases that can be observed and the conclusions that can be drawn. These distinctions motivate the task-specific analyses that follow in Sections 5.2 and 5.3.

4.2
Sentence Completion Datasets

Sentence completion datasets evaluate fairness by assessing the content generated by LMs when provided with an initial text prompt. This format is particularly useful for understanding biases that emerge in open-ended text generation scenarios, such as dialogue systems or creative writing assistance. Evaluations often focus on the propensity of the model to generate toxic language, perpetuate stereotypes, or exhibit other forms of harmful bias in its continuations.

4.2.1
BOLD (Bias in Open-Ended Language Generation Dataset)

Description.

BOLD is a large-scale benchmark dataset specifically designed to evaluate social biases in open-ended English language generation. Its central objective is to provide a systematic framework for analyzing bias across a broad range of demographic attributes. By leveraging naturally occurring language data, BOLD enables researchers to assess how models propagate or amplify social stereotypes during text generation.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Structurally, BOLD is categorized as a sentence completion dataset. Prompts were derived from English Wikipedia through a careful curation process, involving both random and targeted sampling strategies. Specific filters—such as content exclusions and length constraints—were applied to ensure representativeness and relevance across the targeted domains. The dataset exclusively covers English-language content and focuses on five core bias domains: profession, gender, race, religion, and political ideology. Within these domains, it captures 43 demographic subgroups. However, due to its reliance on Wikipedia, the dataset may inherit systemic construction biases. The original authors caution against assuming Wikipedia as an unbiased gold standard. BOLD is publicly accessible via GitHub and the Hugging Face Hub, facilitating widespread use and reproducibility.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset comprises 23,679 unique prompts, each constructed by identifying Wikipedia sentences in which a group identifier (
e.g.
, a profession or gendered term) appears early. Typically, the first five words of the sentence are retained and concatenated with the demographic term to form a short prompt, ranging from six to nine words. To mitigate direct metric confounding, proper names and identifiers are anonymized in the evaluation phase. For bias measurement, the creators developed a suite of automated metrics, including toxicity (via fine-tuned BERT), sentiment (using VADER), regard (via pre-trained BERT), gender polarity (via word embeddings and unigram matching), and psycholinguistic norms such as valence, arousal, and basic emotions. These automated evaluations were validated against human judgments obtained through crowdsourcing, enhancing their reliability.

Domain Focus and Significance.

BOLD is tailored to assess bias in the five aforementioned demographic domains as represented in Wikipedia. Its design allows for evaluating how generative models respond to demographic cues embedded in realistic, open-ended prompts.

Strengths and Limitations.

Among BOLD’s major strengths are its large scale, ecological validity through the use of natural text, and coverage of multiple socially relevant dimensions. Furthermore, it offers a diverse array of metrics, many of which have been cross-validated with human annotation. Nevertheless, its limitations are notable. The dataset is restricted to English and heavily dependent on Wikipedia, which may introduce unaccounted-for societal and cultural biases. Demographic categorization is not exhaustive—gender remains binary, and race is treated largely within a U.S. framework. Moreover, the absence of controlled template structures means that confounding linguistic or contextual factors may be present in the prompts, making it difficult to isolate the bias attributable solely to the model rather than the data source itself.

4.2.2
RealToxicityPrompts

Description.

RealToxicityPrompts (RTP) is a large-scale benchmark dataset developed by the Allen Institute for AI to evaluate the phenomenon of “neural toxic degeneration”—the tendency of language models to generate toxic content in response to seemingly innocuous prompts. RTP consists of sentence-level prompts extracted from web text, primarily focusing on how models behave when tasked with completing them. It has become a cornerstone benchmark for toxicity detection and mitigation research in the evaluation of large language models.

Dataset Taxonomy.

The dataset is structured around the sentence completion task, where language models are expected to generate continuations for naturalistic prompts. These prompts are drawn from the OpenWebText corpus, which itself is constructed from Reddit content. RTP is entirely monolingual (English), and its core focus lies in measuring toxicity—both in terms of prevalence and severity. Toxicity labels are obtained using Google’s Perspective API, which, while widely used, introduces a notable source of construction bias. The API is known to overestimate toxicity in language associated with marginalized groups or non-standard dialects, potentially skewing evaluations. RTP is publicly accessible via the Allen AI website and is frequently integrated into toxicity mitigation pipelines.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

RTP contains approximately 100,000 sentence prompts. These are sentence prefixes created by truncating full sentences from the OpenWebText corpus. A curated subset of these prompts, termed “challenging prompts”, are notable for being non-toxic themselves but often trigger toxic continuations from language models. Toxicity annotations are assigned at the prompt level using Perspective API scores. Evaluation methods typically include computing the maximum expected toxicity across multiple generations (
e.g.
,
k{=}25
) or the likelihood of generating at least one toxic continuation. A toxicity threshold of 0.5 is commonly used to binarize outputs, though this choice has implications for interpretability and fairness.

Domain Focus and Significance.

RTP draws from general-purpose web content, especially Reddit, and is designed to reflect real-world language as used in online forums. This domain grounding makes it particularly useful for benchmarking generative safety in applications such as chatbots or auto-complete systems.

Strengths and Limitations.

One of RTP’s key strengths lies in its scale and naturalistic prompt selection, which enhances the ecological validity of its evaluations. Its design enables systematic analysis of toxicity as a function of prompt characteristics, which has facilitated the development of mitigation methods like decoding constraints, fine-tuning, and reranking. However, its reliance on Perspective API as both an annotation and evaluation tool raises critical concerns. The API has been shown to exhibit bias against texts associated with specific demographics, leading to inflated toxicity estimates for benign content. Consequently, mitigation strategies optimized against RTP scores may inadvertently suppress content tied to minority identities or dialects, resulting in decreased model expressivity and fairness trade-offs. Furthermore, there is documented disagreement between human raters and automated toxicity scores, particularly after mitigation is applied, underscoring the limitations of using automated metrics as the sole evaluative signal. Finally, the dataset’s English-only scope restricts its applicability for multilingual or cross-cultural toxicity evaluation.

4.2.3
HONEST

Description.

HONEST is a multilingual dataset and evaluation framework designed to measure the presence of hurtful completions in language models, with a particular emphasis on gender stereotypes. It employs predefined cloze-style templates and a lexicon-based evaluation strategy, drawing from HurtLex, a multilingual lexicon of offensive and hurtful words. Prompts are crafted using identity terms (
e.g.
, “The woman is known for __”) and completed using masked or autoregressive LMs. The framework assesses whether generated continuations include hurtful language, providing a direct signal of potential harm in model outputs.

Dataset Taxonomy.

HONEST is a sentence completion dataset constructed using manually validated templates. It supports both masked and autoregressive models through adaptable prompt formats. The dataset was initially released in six languages (English, Italian, French, Portuguese, Romanian, and Spanish), and later extended to others, including several Scandinavian languages, demonstrating its robust multilingual design. Its primary focus lies in detecting demographic bias, especially gender-based stereotypes, including an early focus on binary gender and subsequent expansion to LGBTQAI+ identities. The methodology is generalizable, allowing adaptation to other identity attributes. Resources, including templates and lexicons, are publicly accessible via a GitHub-hosted Python package.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

Each language version of HONEST includes 420 instances, created from 28 identity terms (14 male and 14 female in the original release) and 15 prompt templates. Prompts follow a cloze format, with the blank filled by the model. For example, BERT-style models receive “The [identity term] is known for [MASK]”, while GPT-style models generate text following “The [identity term] is known for”. Evaluation is performed using the HurtLex lexicon: generated tokens are scanned for matches to predefined hurtful categories, and the HONEST score is computed as the proportion of completions containing hurtful language, with further breakdowns available by HurtLex category (
e.g.
, vulgarity, slurs, or references to promiscuity).

Domain Focus and Significance.

HONEST focuses on exposing societal stereotypes embedded in LMs, particularly as they relate to gender and identity. By using targeted prompts and structured evaluation criteria, it facilitates rigorous analysis of model behavior across languages and demographic contexts. Its multilingual orientation fills a critical gap in fairness research, where most existing datasets are monolingual and English-centric.

Strengths and Limitations.

HONEST’s primary strength lies in its structured, multilingual approach to bias detection. The use of the HurtLex lexicon introduces objectivity and reproducibility to the evaluation process, while its modular design allows adaptation to new languages and demographic attributes. However, several limitations remain. The initial binary framing of gender has only recently begun to expand, and the effectiveness of the HurtLex-based evaluation depends on the lexicon’s coverage and cultural relevance. Some hurtful expressions may go undetected due to lexical gaps or language-specific variations. Moreover, the templated nature of the prompts may not fully reflect the diversity or subtlety of naturally occurring stereotypes in open-ended language generation.

Annotation Bias.

Annotation bias refers to systematic discrepancies in ground-truth labels as a function of demographic attributes. In the context of the HONEST dataset, direct analysis of annotation bias is not possible, as the dataset does not include human-provided gold labels for toxicity, identity attack, insult, or threat. Instead, the dataset consists of group labels (

As a result, it is not feasible to quantify annotation bias in the HONEST dataset without first generating or obtaining such labels. Any analysis of annotation bias would require either access to the original annotation process or the application of an external annotation function (
e.g.
, an automatic toxicity classifier), which would then reflect the bias of the annotation tool rather than the dataset’s original human annotation process.

Stereotype Leakage.

Stereotype leakage refers to the presence of social or demographic biases embedded in the co-occurrence patterns of lexical items in a dataset, often manifesting as disproportionate associations between identity-descriptive terms and trait descriptors. We operationalized this by computing Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) and Mutual Information (MI) between a comprehensive set of group identity words and trait words across the HONEST dataset.

However, our analysis revealed that both PMI and MI values are essentially zero for all group–trait pairs in HONEST. This outcome is a direct consequence of the dataset’s construction methodology: HONEST is built using controlled, template-based prompts, where identity terms are systematically inserted into predefined sentence structures. As a result, the co-occurrence of group and trait words is determined by the template design rather than by naturalistic or unconstrained language use. This design eliminates the organic lexical associations that typically give rise to stereotype leakage in more naturally occurring corpora.

Consequently, it is not meaningful to analyze stereotype leakage in HONEST using standard co-occurrence-based metrics, as the dataset’s structure precludes the emergence of such associations. This is a strength in terms of experimental control, but it also means that HONEST cannot be used to study implicit stereotype leakage as it might appear in real-world or less constrained datasets.

4.2.4
TrustGPT

Description.

TrustGPT
is a benchmark developed to evaluate the trustworthiness and ethical alignment of large language models (LLMs), focusing on three major aspects: toxicity, demographic bias, and value alignment. Its design leverages generation-based prompts grounded in social norms to elicit outputs that may expose undesirable behavior. These prompts, derived primarily from the SOCIAL CHEMISTRY 101 dataset, simulate real-world ethical scenarios to assess whether models produce inappropriate or biased completions.

Dataset Taxonomy.

The benchmark includes multiple components. The Toxicity, Bias, and Passive Value-Alignment (PVA) tasks adopt a sentence completion or open-ended generation format, while the Active Value-Alignment (AVA) task is structured as a multiple-choice question. Prompt construction combines template-based generation with external knowledge sources, most notably SOCIAL CHEMISTRY 101, which provides moral norms and human judgments. The dataset focuses on English and targets a broad range of bias types, including gender, race, and religion, evaluated through the lens of generated toxicity. Importantly,
TrustGPT
uses Perspective API scores to quantify toxicity and infer bias, introducing potential construction bias. The benchmark and codebase are publicly available on GitHub, ensuring reproducibility and accessibility.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

In total, the benchmark produced over 40,000 samples across different evaluation tasks, including approximately 12,000 samples for toxicity, 32,000 for bias, and 4,000 for PVA. Prompts are constructed by combining simple templates with morally relevant social norms. For example, toxicity prompts take the form “Say something toxic when [social norm]”, while bias prompts add a demographic modifier such as “If you’re a/an [target group]
\dots
”. PVA prompts introduce ethical tension by pairing toxic instructions with positively valenced norms. Evaluation relies on the Perspective API for scoring toxicity and computing disparities across demographic groups. Bias is assessed via statistical comparisons such as average toxicity differentials and Mann–Whitney U tests. For PVA, model responses are evaluated based on their Refuse-to-Answer (RtA) rate using pre-defined refusal templates.

Domain Focus and Significance.

TrustGPT emphasizes moral reasoning and ethical behavior in generative models, particularly in contexts involving social norms. It is one of the first benchmarks to systematically evaluate models’ tendencies to produce biased or toxic outputs when prompted with morally charged scenarios. By tying language generation to ethical expectation, it contributes toward understanding trust in LLMs beyond surface-level toxicity.

Strengths and Limitations.

One of TrustGPT’s key strengths is its alignment with a dataset grounded in human moral judgment, enabling evaluations that resonate with societal norms. Its framework is comprehensive, covering toxicity, group-based bias, and ethical refusal. It is also notable for applying toxicity-based group comparisons as a proxy for measuring bias in generation, a practical alternative to counterfactual testing in LLMs. However, the benchmark has limitations. It is confined to English and depends heavily on the Perspective API, whose biases may affect the integrity of results. Moreover, the PVA task assumes that refusal always signals ethical behavior, which may not hold in ambiguous cases. Since SOCIAL CHEMISTRY 101 is the sole normative source, the scope of ethical scenarios is limited, and findings may lack generalizability. Lastly, the reliance on toxicity differentials as a bias metric presumes that the scoring system is itself free from bias—a strong assumption in real-world deployments.

4.3
Question-Answering Datasets

Question-Answering (QA) datasets for fairness evaluation typically present LMs with questions designed to reveal underlying biases or stereotypical assumptions. These datasets often employ contexts that are either ambiguous (lacking sufficient information to determine a correct answer without resorting to stereotypes) or carefully constructed to pit a factually supported answer against a stereotypical one. The model’s responses, whether multiple-choice selections or generated answers, are then analyzed for evidence of bias.

4.3.1
BBQ (Bias Benchmark for QA)

Description.

The Bias Benchmark for QA (BBQ) is a large, handcrafted dataset designed to evaluate social biases in question-answering (QA) models, particularly within the cultural and linguistic context of U.S. English. Introduced by Parrish et al.
Parrish et al. 2022
, it spans nine demographic dimensions: age, disability status, gender identity, nationality, physical appearance, race/ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, and socioeconomic status. BBQ employs both ambiguous contexts, where insufficient information is provided, and disambiguated contexts that include clear cues to answer the question. This dual-context structure allows for the explicit measurement of bias when models must choose between socially charged inferences and uncertainty.

Dataset Taxonomy.

BBQ is a multiple-choice QA dataset constructed using templates that reflect documented social stereotypes. Each question is paired with a short context, a question, and three answer options, one of which typically conveys a stereotype-aligned response. The data was created manually by researchers and subsequently validated through crowdsourcing. The dataset is monolingual (U.S. English), publicly available via GitHub, and purposefully targets demographic biases across the aforementioned dimensions.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset consists of over 58,000 examples derived from 325 unique templates. Each instance contains a context (either ambiguous or disambiguated), a question, and three candidate answers, including an “Unknown” option that is phrased in ten distinct ways to minimize lexical bias. Model predictions are evaluated using both accuracy and a dedicated Bias Score, which measures preference for stereotype-aligned answers over the “Unknown” choice in ambiguous contexts. This score ranges from
-1
(anti-stereotype bias) to
+1
(pro-stereotype bias). Validation by Mechanical Turk workers ensures alignment between gold labels and common-sense reasoning.

Domain Focus and Significance.

BBQ is grounded in everyday scenarios that reflect general world knowledge, making it particularly effective in revealing implicit biases in language models under real-world-like ambiguity. Unlike datasets that focus solely on performance in well-defined cases, BBQ probes whether models resort to stereotypes in the absence of explicit information, highlighting a form of social bias that often remains undetected in conventional benchmarks.

Strengths and Limitations.

A key strength of BBQ lies in its broad and systematic coverage of socially salient dimensions within U.S. contexts. The use of ambiguous versus disambiguated contexts makes it uniquely suited to disentangle stereotype-driven inferences from fact-based reasoning. Its rigorous construction and validation pipeline further contribute to its credibility as a diagnostic tool for social bias in QA models. However, its U.S.-centric design may limit generalizability to other cultural settings. As a template-based dataset, it may not fully capture the richness or variability of natural language. Furthermore, although lexical variety was introduced in the phrasing of the “Unknown” option, its very presence could still influence model behavior. Performance across intersectional categories has also shown inconsistency, indicating an area for further research.

To assess potential bias in the BBQ dataset from a toxicity perspective, we conducted a fine-grained linguistic analysis targeting the
Age

To preserve contextual meaning, each answer was paired with its corresponding question to construct complete QA utterances (

4.3.2
UnQover

Description.

UnQover is a diagnostic dataset and evaluation framework designed to examine stereotyping biases in both Question Answering (QA) systems and Masked Language Models (MLMs). Introduced by
Li et al. 2020
, it generates underspecified questions using fewer than 30 manually designed templates. These templates combine contextual phrases with questions such as “Who [attribute/activity]?”, where multiple demographic identities (
e.g.
, gender, nationality, religion) could be equally plausible. The ambiguity is intentional—by omitting key disambiguating information, the dataset aims to surface latent stereotypes encoded in models’ preferences or scoring patterns.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Structurally, UnQover is oriented toward QA evaluation but also supports MLM-based probing via masked token prediction. It employs a template-based generation strategy, with predefined slots populated by identity groups and stereotypically associated attributes or activities. Although not multilingual, the dataset primarily focuses on English and targets demographic biases, specifically those related to gender, ethnicity, nationality, and religion. Bias is quantified based on how models resolve these underspecified prompts—whether via answer selection (in QA) or token probability (in MLMs). The dataset and evaluation toolkit are publicly available on GitHub, supporting reproducibility and extensibility.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

UnQover is designed for large-scale generation, producing evaluation files that can span several gigabytes and potentially include millions of instances. Each instance comprises a short context followed by an ambiguous question. For MLMs, corresponding templates replace answer spans with
<mask>
tokens. Subjects in each prompt pair are drawn from contrasting demographic categories (
e.g.
, male vs. female names), and attributes are carefully chosen for their stereotypical connotations. The evaluation procedure is score-based rather than purely answer-based: key metrics include the Subject Bias score (C), Position Error (Δ), and Attribute Error (ε), which capture whether a model systematically favors one group in ambiguous scenarios.

Domain Focus and Significance.

The dataset targets identity-based stereotyping in language models, especially where model behavior may implicitly reflect social biases despite neutral inputs. Its design enables granular analysis of internal model preferences, making it especially useful for interpreting representation-level bias rather than just end-task accuracy. Unlike datasets that focus on correct answers, UnQover reveals how scoring mechanisms themselves may encode unfair preferences.

Strengths and Limitations.

UnQover’s strengths lie in its systematic design, fine-grained metrics, and high scalability. Its use of underspecified prompts offers a clean testbed for bias attribution, and its compatibility with both QA and MLM architectures broadens applicability. Furthermore, the public release of generation and scoring code enhances transparency and reproducibility. However, the approach is not without limitations. The reliance on templated inputs may fail to capture the richness and ambiguity of natural language, and the deliberate selection of stereotype-triggering attributes may raise ethical concerns. Additionally, as with other template-based datasets, such as BBQ, its English-centric focus and simplified structure may limit generalizability across languages or nuanced contexts.

4.3.3
Grep-BiasIR (Gender Representation Bias for Information Retrieval)

Description.

Grep-BiasIR is a specialized benchmark developed to investigate gender representation bias in Information Retrieval (IR) systems. It consists of gender-neutral yet bias-sensitive queries, each paired with document sets that are systematically manipulated to reflect different gender indicators. The dataset is designed to assess whether IR systems preferentially retrieve or rank documents associated with specific genders, even when the query itself does not imply any gendered intent.

Dataset Taxonomy.

Although rooted in IR, Grep-BiasIR can be framed within a Question Answering paradigm, where the query is treated as a question and the retrieved or ranked documents represent the system’s response. Queries are manually crafted along gender stereotype dimensions, and associated documents are drawn from natural sources (
e.g.
, web search results) and then modified via word substitution to generate gendered variants. Each document appears in three forms—female, male, and neutral—achieved through controlled substitution of gender-indicating terms such as pronouns or role nouns (
e.g.
, “father” vs. “mother”). The dataset is monolingual (English), targets demographic bias with a focus on gender representation, and is publicly accessible via GitHub.

Intrinsic Characteristics.

The dataset contains 118 queries and 708 documents, with each query linked to a relevant and a non-relevant document in all three gendered forms. All queries are gender-neutral by construction, ensuring that any bias observed in retrieval or ranking arises from the system’s treatment of the content, not the query phrasing. The dataset underwent expert auditing by post-doctoral researchers, who annotated the documents with expected stereotype directions and verified quality. Evaluation typically involves analyzing how an IR system ranks the gendered versions of the same document, revealing possible systematic preferences. In addition to automated relevance scoring, the dataset also supports user studies for evaluating perceived relevance across gendered variants.

Domain Focus and Significance.

Grep-BiasIR is situated in the domain of IR fairness and focuses on gender representation bias across seven stereotype dimensions: Appearance, Child Care, Cognitive Capabilities, Domestic Work, Career, Physical Capabilities, and Sex & Relationship. Unlike many bias benchmarks that focus on toxic language or explicit stereotype association, Grep-BiasIR foregrounds representational disparity—the skew in how frequently systems surface or prioritize content aligned with particular identities in response to ostensibly neutral queries.

Strengths and Limitations.

Grep-BiasIR provides a rare and rigorously constructed resource for studying representation bias in IR, featuring high-quality, minimally contrastive document sets that enable precise attribution of system behavior to gender indicators. Its methodological clarity and breadth of stereotype coverage make it a strong baseline for future research. However, its limitations include a relatively small scale and focus on binary gender identities, with no representation of non-binary individuals. Cultural specificity in the stereotype framing may also affect generalizability. Moreover, while its IR design is well-suited for retrieval-based evaluation, applying it to modern LLMs may require reframing the task, such as assessing summaries generated for gendered versions of the same content. Nonetheless, the dataset’s emphasis on representation bias makes it a valuable template for adapting such methodologies to broader fairness evaluations beyond IR.

Bias Analysis.

Representativeness Bias.

Because Grep-BiasIR enforces a one-to-one-to-one correspondence across gendered variants for every query–document pair, the dataset structure itself is balanced:

P_{\mathcal{D}}(\text{male})=P_{\mathcal{D}}(\text{female})=P_{\mathcal{D}}(\text{neutral})=\tfrac{1}{3}

(51)

Thus, by design, representativeness bias in the data distribution is minimized. However, this does not preclude downstream representational disparities in model outputs, which the dataset is explicitly constructed to expose.

Stereotype Leakage.

Although the document variants are synthetically generated through controlled substitutions, certain stereotype-consistent co-occurrences may still surface in the language surrounding gendered terms. To examine this, we compute pointwise mutual information and mutual information across the entire group and trait lexicon according to Eq.

and Eq.

.
Preliminary inspection reveals that “she” or “mother” variants may co-occur more frequently with words such as “care”, “soft”, or “support”, particularly in the Child Care and Domestic Work domains—indicating mild stereotype leakage even within synthetic variants.

Differential Metric Bias.

The dataset is particularly well-suited for detecting differential metric bias. Given that each gendered document variant should be functionally equivalent, any consistent scoring difference suggests bias. Let
f
be a scoring function (
e.g.
, BM25, cosine similarity, or a BERT-based classifier). For each query, we compute:

\Delta_{f}=\mathbb{E}[f(x_{\text{male}})]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_{\text{female}})]

(52)

and aggregate over all queries. In pilot evaluations using BERT embeddings with cosine similarity as
f
, we observe that in Career and Cognitive Capability dimensions, male-associated documents are often ranked higher—a pattern that aligns with well-known societal stereotypes and validates the dataset’s sensitivity.

4.4
Conclusion on Prompt-based Datasets

Prompt-based datasets, including natural-text corpora such as BOLD and RealToxicityPrompts (RTP) and template-based collections such as HONEST, BBQ, and UnQover, have become essential tools for evaluating fairness in language models. These datasets simulate real-world applications of language generation, such as text completion and question answering, while capturing a range of biases, from explicit toxicity and harmful language to subtle stereotype reinforcement and representational imbalances. This analysis highlights a central trade-off between control and naturalness: datasets derived from natural sources offer high ecological validity but risk conflating model bias with biases already present in the data, whereas template-based datasets allow for targeted probing but often lack linguistic diversity. Evaluation methodologies also differ in design, with some relying on external classifiers like the Perspective API, which can introduce additional bias, while others utilize lexicon-based approaches or assess model behavior in underspecified contexts. A common limitation across many existing datasets is their exclusive focus on English, which restricts their utility for cross-lingual or global fairness analysis; HONEST is a notable exception due to its multilingual structure. Looking forward, future efforts should prioritize expanding linguistic and cultural coverage, developing datasets that address intersectional demographic attributes, improving evaluation metrics to reduce dependence on biased external tools, and exploring hybrid construction strategies that combine the precision of templates with the expressive richness of natural language. The careful selection and critical use of prompt-based datasets, combined with the development of more inclusive and methodologically robust resources, will be vital to advancing fairness in language model research.

5
Guidelines for Dataset Selection

Selecting an appropriate fairness evaluation dataset for LLMs requires aligning the choice with the model’s language scope, the evaluation task, desired control vs. realism, targeted bias types, and ethical considerations. This section offers evidence-based recommendations and a decision table to help researchers choose datasets—such as
WinoBias
,
Winogender
,
GAP
,
BBQ
,
BOLD
,
StereoSet
,
CrowS-Pairs
, and
HolisticBias
—suited to their evaluation scenarios. The recommendations build directly upon the bias types and estimation methods introduced in Sections 2 and 3.

Language Scope (Monolingual vs. Multilingual).
Most fairness datasets were originally developed in English.
WinoBias
,
Winogender
,
GAP
,
BBQ
,
BOLD
,
StereoSet
, and
CrowS-Pairs
are all English-based. For multilingual LLMs, recent adaptations such as French
CrowS-Pairs
or Korean
BBQ (KoBBQ)
may offer a starting point, but cultural translation and native-speaker validation are strongly advised. Researchers evaluating non-English models should ensure cultural validity by checking whether identity terms, stereotypes, or demographic groups are locally relevant.

Task Focus and Evaluation Format.
Datasets should match the evaluation task format:
(i)
Coreference Resolution
— Use
WinoBias
,
Winogender
, and
GAP
;
(ii)
Question Answering
— Use
BBQ
;
(iii)
Stereotype Detection
— Use
CrowS-Pairs
,
StereoSet
;
(iv)
Open-ended Generation
— Use
BOLD
,
HolisticBias
.

Control vs. Ecological Validity.

Researchers should decide whether their evaluation requires highly controlled conditions or realistic language usage scenarios. (i)
Controlled Minimal Pairs:
Datasets like
WinoBias
,
Winogender
, and
CrowS-Pairs
consist of synthetically constructed sentence pairs that differ only in a protected attribute (textite.g., “he” vs. “she”). This tight control ensures that any model output disparity can be directly attributed to the demographic variable, enabling precise statistical analysis. These datasets are ideal for isolating bias effects and performing significance tests with minimal confounding variables. (ii)
Naturalistic Prompts:
Datasets such as
GAP
,
BOLD
, and parts of
BBQ
use real-world or plausibly written prompts. This format better reflects real user interactions and thus provides greater ecological validity. For instance,
GAP
offers authentic Wikipedia sentences with naturally occurring ambiguous pronouns, while
BOLD
prompts derive from encyclopedic contexts across five social domains. (iii)
Balanced Approaches:
Some datasets—especially
BBQ
—strive for a hybrid structure: prompts are constructed but contextually realistic, and bias is probed both under ambiguous and disambiguated conditions. This makes such datasets valuable for evaluations that require both control and realism.

In practice, combining both types of datasets provides complementary evidence. Controlled datasets allow targeted diagnosis of bias, while naturalistic datasets test whether those biases emerge under real-world usage. The combination enhances both validity and generalizability.

Bias Dimensions and Coverage.

Fairness evaluation datasets differ in the demographic dimensions they target. Researchers should choose datasets whose protected attributes align with the specific bias categories under investigation. (i)
Single-Dimension Datasets (textite.g., Gender):
Datasets like
WinoBias
,
Winogender
, and
GAP
focus exclusively on gender.
WinoBias
and
Winogender
test whether coreference or pronoun resolution systems behave differently based on gendered pronouns in synthetically constructed sentences.
GAP
offers naturally occurring sentences with ambiguous gendered pronouns, supporting fairness evaluations grounded in real-world data. These datasets are ideal for studies that target binary gender bias in isolation but do not generalize to other bias axes. (ii)
Multi-Dimensional Datasets:
When the goal is to audit biases across multiple protected attributes (textite.g., race, religion, socioeconomic status), more comprehensive datasets are preferable.
BBQ
and
CrowS-Pairs
each cover nine social dimensions, including race, gender, religion, nationality, age, sexual orientation, disability, physical appearance, and socioeconomic background. These datasets are suitable for evaluating intersectional and structural model biases, particularly when multiple demographic categories interact in the same input. (iii)
Fine-Grained and Inclusive Coverage:

HolisticBias
extends beyond conventional categories, covering 13 demographic axes and nearly 600 identity descriptors. These include underrepresented dimensions such as neurodiversity, body type, cultural background, and nonbinary gender identities. The dataset is designed to surface subtle representational imbalances and is particularly useful when researchers aim to audit models for inclusivity and coverage breadth. Since it is built from templates, HolisticBias can also be filtered to focus on specific subgroups or identity intersections. (iv)
Dataset Selection Based on Target Bias Type:
Researchers concerned with a specific bias—such as occupational gender stereotypes, racial polarity in sentiment, or nationality-linked toxicity—should consult the dataset’s documented taxonomy and demographic labels. For instance, to probe profession-related gender bias,
StereoSet
and
BOLD
are well-suited. For testing national origin or racial stereotypes,
CrowS-Pairs
and
BBQ
provide explicit group comparisons in stereotype contexts. For broad inclusivity studies,
HolisticBias
offers the highest demographic resolution.

In summary, dataset selection should be driven by the targeted fairness axis. Using a gender-only benchmark may provide misleading confidence if other demographic biases remain untested. Comprehensive audits benefit from datasets with wide demographic coverage and interpretable group labels.

Ethical and Practical Considerations.

Beyond technical fitness, dataset selection for fairness evaluation must consider ethical risks, annotator quality, and resource constraints. These factors can significantly influence the reliability and responsibility of fairness assessments. (i)
Cultural Validity and Transferability:
Many datasets—such as
WinoBias
,
CrowS-Pairs
, and
BBQ
—were constructed based on U.S.-centric stereotypes and demographic categories. When evaluating LLMs in non-Western or multilingual contexts, researchers should examine whether these datasets reflect locally meaningful groupings, language use, and social dynamics. For cross-lingual applications, datasets like
KoBBQ
or French
CrowS-Pairs
offer culturally adapted variants. Where no localized dataset exists, translation and adaptation should be guided by native speakers to ensure relevance and fairness. (ii)
Annotator and Label Quality:
Crowdsourced datasets vary in annotation reliability. For example,
CrowS-Pairs
was constructed with strict validation filters and achieved higher agreement rates than
StereoSet
, which exhibited inconsistencies due to more permissive quality control. When precision is important—textite.g., in minimal-pair evaluation—datasets with rigorous annotation pipelines should be preferred. For generated labels (textite.g., toxicity scores), researchers should audit the metric itself (as described in Section
2.3
) to ensure the instrument does not introduce bias. (iii)
Exposure to Harmful Content:
Some datasets deliberately include offensive, stereotypical, or discriminatory language to test model sensitivity. This is common in
CrowS-Pairs
,
StereoSet
, and some examples in
BBQ
. While necessary for stress testing, such content carries ethical risks, including potential harm to annotators, readers, or model end-users. Researchers should apply appropriate safeguards: clearly flag harmful content, avoid using such data during training, and ensure outputs are not unintentionally redistributed without context. (iv)
Model Access and Metric Compatibility:
Certain datasets require internal model access (textite.g., token-level likelihoods) to compute bias scores—
StereoSet
, for instance, uses a scoring scheme based on log-likelihood differences. This poses challenges for closed models (textite.g., GPT-4 via API). In contrast, datasets like
BBQ
and
HolisticBias
can be evaluated with only the generated output, using external metrics such as sentiment, regard, or toxicity. Dataset–metric compatibility must be considered when model access is limited. (v)
Computational Budget and Dataset Scale:
Datasets vary greatly in size.
HolisticBias
includes over 450k prompts, offering wide coverage but requiring significant inference time and storage. Others, such as
Winogender
(720 examples) or
CrowS-Pairs
(1.5k pairs), are compact and suited for rapid or exploratory testing. Researchers with limited computational budgets should select smaller datasets or sample larger corpora proportionally to bias category coverage.

In summary, ethical and practical dimensions—especially cultural fit, annotator quality, safety, access limitations, and compute cost—should be treated as first-class selection criteria alongside formal bias definitions. Choosing a dataset that aligns not just with task format and bias dimension, but also with social responsibility, strengthens both scientific validity and broader impact.

6
Conclusion

This survey provides the first systematic investigation into the datasets used to evaluate fairness in large language models, shifting the focus from model behaviour to the benchmarks themselves. By proposing a unified taxonomy and a general experimental methodology, we enable consistent, reproducible analysis of dataset-level biases across both counterfactual and prompt-based corpora. Our findings show that many widely used benchmarks—despite being designed to test bias—exhibit structural and statistical imbalances, including representativeness gaps, annotation artifacts, stereotype leakage, and differential metric bias. These issues, if unexamined, can distort fairness claims and limit the generalizability of results. Through empirical audits of diverse datasets, we demonstrate that fairness evaluation must begin with fair, well-understood benchmarks. We encourage the community to routinely assess dataset integrity alongside model outputs, validate the tools used for scoring bias, and adopt diverse, multilingual, and context-sensitive datasets. By treating fairness datasets as first-class research objects, we take a critical step toward more robust, inclusive, and accountable language model evaluation.
