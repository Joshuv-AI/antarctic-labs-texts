A Classic and Unusual Presentation of Atheroembolic Renal Disease: Report of Two Cases
Authors: Kai Zong Teo, Angela Bayly, Surjit Tarafdar, Lisa Phipps, Jian Cheng, Seethalakshmi Viswanathan, Poorani Gurumallesh Prabu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtheroembolic renal disease (AERD) is an underdiagnosed condition with diverse clinical presentations that can mimic various renal pathologies. Most cases follow intravascular procedures or anticoagulation, while only rare case reports of spontaneous AERD have been described. We present two contrasting cases of AERD with markedly different presentations. The first patient was a 74‐year‐old male who presented with worsening dyspnoea, acute renal failure and skin changes following coronary bypass surgery, typical of AERD. The second patient was a 70‐year‐old male who presented with symptomatic hypertension and acute‐on‐chronic kidney injury without identifiable precipitating factors, initially suspected to be rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Renal biopsies revealed cholesterol emboli within arteries, characterised by needle‐shaped clefts surrounded by foreign body giant cells, with background interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy confirming the diagnosis. These contrasting cases demonstrate the diagnostic challenges of AERD and highlight the importance of renal biopsy for accurate diagnosis in both classic and atypical presentations.Keywords: atheroembolic renal disease, pathology, renal biopsy, unusual clinical presentation

1. Introduction

Atheroembolic renal disease (AERD), the renal manifestation of cholesterol embolisation syndrome occurs when fragments of atheromatous plaques from large vessels break off and shower downstream smaller arteries with cholesterol crystal emboli [1, 2]. It is an underdiagnosed condition and has a wide range of clinical presentations, mimicking various conditions [1–4]. The condition typically affects elderly patients with widespread atherosclerosis and is most frequently encountered following endovascular procedures, cardiothoracic or vascular surgery, anticoagulation or thrombolytic therapy. However, spontaneous cases without an identifiable precipitating event have also been described in the literature, and these atypical presentations pose particular diagnostic challenges as they may clinically mimic rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, vasculitis or other parenchymal renal diseases.

We present a classic and a rare spontaneous case of AERD with markedly different clinical presentations, with the aim of highlighting the diagnostic difficulties encountered in clinical practice, illustrating the central role of renal biopsy in establishing the diagnosis and discussing the management implications of recognising this underdiagnosed condition.

2. Case 1

A 74‐year‐old male presented to emergency department with a 2‐day history of acute worsening dyspnoea. He denied any infective symptoms, chest pain, dizziness, palpitations or visual disturbances. He had recently undergone coronary artery bypass graft 3 months prior and had a history significant for ischaemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and atrial fibrillation for which he was on warfarin. An examination revealed a blood pressure of 226/122 mmHg, bibasal crackles on auscultation, elevated jugular venous pressure and peripheral oedema. Laboratory investigations showed an elevated serum creatinine to 350 μmol/L from a baseline of 100 μmol/L. Proteinuria and haematuria were minimal, and no dysmorphic red blood cells or urine casts were identified. Vasculitis and glomerulonephritis screens were negative. There was no eosinophiluria, eosinophilia or hypocomplementemia. Imaging was negative for obstructive uropathy. Livedo reticularis over the lower limbs was identified on further examination following renal consultation, raising clinical suspicion for cholesterol emboli. Renal biopsy was subsequently performed to confirm clinical suspicion and rule out other causes of renal failure.

3. Case 2

A 70‐year‐old male presented to emergency department following several days of headache, dizziness, visual disturbances and high home blood pressure readings. He denied any chest pain, palpitations, dyspnoea or neurological symptoms and was passing normal amounts of urine. He had a background of chronic kidney disease secondary to renovascular disease, hypertension, gastro‐oesophageal reflux disease and hyperthyroidism. There were no significant events other than laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair 2 weeks prior. Observations showed a blood pressure of 194/89 mmHg with other parameters within normal limits, and the physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory investigations showed an elevated serum creatinine to 534 μmol/L from a baseline of 150 μmol/L and subnephrotic range proteinuria with haematuria. No dysmorphic red blood cells were identified, and glomerulonephritis, vasculitis and myeloma screens were negative. Imaging was negative for obstructive uropathy. Kidney biopsy was performed following clinical concerns for rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.

3.2. Light Microscopy

Cholesterol emboli were seen within a single artery in both cases with marked background chronic changes in the form of interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy and mild chronic tubulointerstitial inflammation (Figures 1A,B and 2A,B). At least 50% of glomeruli showed global sclerosis for both patients. The viable glomeruli showed ischaemic changes and were otherwise unremarkable. There was no increase in mesangial matrix or cellularity; no endocapillary proliferation, capillary basement membrane thickening, double contours or spikes seen on special stains. There were no areas of segmental sclerosis, necrotising lesions or crescents. A Congo red stain was negative for amyloid, and there was no evidence of vasculitis. Acute tubular injury was noted in one of the cases.

3.3. Immunofluorescence (IF)

IF was negative for IgG, IgA, C3, kappa and lambda and showed trace, patchy mesangial and glomerular tuft deposition of IgM. Moderate patchy tubulointerstitial deposition of fibrinogen was seen in one case.

3.4. Electron Microscopy (EM)

EM assessment was hindered in one of the cases due to significant fixation artefact. The other case showed chronic ischaemic changes with no deposits seen (Figure 1C).

4. Discussion

The exact incidence of AERD is unknown. Reported incidence of AERD ranges between 10% and 27% in postmortem studies and 1%–4.5% in biopsies [1, 2]. Most cases occur following intravascular procedures or major surgery with manipulation of large vessels or in the context of anticoagulation or thrombolysis [1–10]. Spontaneous cases of AERD have been reported in the literature but are rare [5–7, 11].

Atherosclerotic plaque disruption is a key pathogenic event, which may occur through direct mechanical trauma when passing guidewires and catheters in endovascular procedures or intraoperative manual manipulation and clamping of arteries. Anticoagulation and thrombolytic therapy are recognised as potential contributing factors, possibly by promoting intraplaque haemorrhage, destabilising vulnerable plaques and impairing the protective thrombus that overlies ulcerated atheromatous lesions, thereby facilitating continued embolisation of cholesterol crystals into the distal circulation [1–3].

Severe hypertension may also play a role in plaque disruption. Hypertensive crisis can precipitate mechanical shear stress on vulnerable atheromatous plaques, contributing to plaque rupture, and cholesterol crystal embolisation. Importantly, severe hypertension may be both a precipitant of and a consequence of AERD since cholesterol embolisation produces renal ischaemia and activation of the renin–angiotensin system, further exacerbating blood pressure control [3, 4].

Once in the bloodstream, the cholesterol emboli lodge in small arteries 100–200 μm in diameter triggering a foreign body response initially with transient neutrophils and eosinophils followed by macrophages, giant cell formation, intimal proliferation and intimal fibrosis leading to arterial obstruction and ultimately ischaemia of renal parenchyma [3]. Additionally, activation of both classical and alternative complement pathways is also involved in the inflammatory response. Because these cholesterol crystals cannot be removed by phagocytosis and are insoluble in body fluids, they persist indefinitely [1–3].

Clinically, three forms of AERD have been described: acute, subacute and chronic. In the acute form, renal failure develops within 7 days of the inciting event due to massive embolisation. Subacute AERD is the most frequently observed and is characterised by a stepwise decline in renal function over weeks or months due to recurrent embolic showers [1, 3]. The chronic form of AERD may be clinically silent or presents as slowly progressive renal impairment. This form of AERD is often overlooked as it mimics several other chronic kidney diseases occurring in the same patient population of several other chronic kidney diseases, such as hypertensive nephrosclerosis, diabetes mellitus and ischaemic nephropathy [1, 3, 10, 11].

When other organs are affected, patients may present with a variety of musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, neurological and visual findings. The triad of acute/subacute renal failure, skin changes such as livedo reticularis (as in one of our cases) or blue toe syndrome and recent vascular intervention strongly suggest the diagnosis of AERD [1–4, 10].

Proteinuria and haematuria are usually minimal in AERD, although nephrotic range proteinuria can be seen. Serology commonly shows transient eosinophilia, C3 hypocomplementemia and elevated inflammatory markers. Glomerulonephritis and vasculitis screens for immunocomplexes and paraproteins are usually negative [3]. However, rare cases of ANA‐ and ANCA‐positive AERD have also been reported [5].

Histological examination is the definitive test for diagnosis of AERD and characteristically shows occlusion of muscular arteries, arterioles or even glomerular capillary loops by cholesterol emboli [1–3], defined by empty biconvex needle‐shaped clefts surrounded by foreign body giant cells due to dissolution of the crystals during routine processing. Early lesions may show a perivascular neutrophilic and eosinophilic infiltrate, while later stage disease shows perivascular fibrosis. Background chronic changes including interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy are frequently seen, and glomeruli may show focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. IF is generally negative for IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, C1q, fibrin, kappa and lambda light chains and noncontributory. EM may show podocyte foot process effacement and rare cholesterol crystals in capillaries or arterioles. The sensitivity of renal biopsy is about 75%, as involvement of renal vasculature is patchy and cholesterol clefts may be seen in limited samples [3, 12].

AERD is commonly associated with a poor prognosis and irreversible organ damage. There are currently no specific therapies, and management remains largely supportive and individualised, focused on prevention of further embolic events through optimisation of statin therapy for plaque stabilisation, control of cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and hyperglycaemia) and rationalisation of anticoagulation [1–3, 10, 13].

Corticosteroids have been employed in some cases of AERD with the aim of reducing the inflammatory foreign body response and complement activation triggered by cholesterol crystals. Several case reports and small case series have described clinical and biochemical improvement following corticosteroid therapy [14]. Although robust randomised data are not yet available, a trial of corticosteroids may be considered on an individualised basis, particularly in patients with evidence of ongoing inflammation or progressive renal decline despite optimal conservative management.

Patient 1 was initially conservatively managed with optimisation of blood pressure control and maximisation of statin therapy; however, his renal function remained poor. A trial of corticosteroids was commenced. Warfarin was converted to antiplatelet therapy following a multidisciplinary discussion weighing the ongoing thromboembolic risk of atrial fibrillation against the contribution of anticoagulation to continued embolisation.

Patient 2 did not have any obvious history or triggering event for AERD, and the initial clinical presentation was suspicious for rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, and pulse methylprednisolone was commenced empirically pending biopsy. Once histology demonstrated cholesterol crystal emboli without evidence of glomerulonephritis or vasculitis, corticosteroids were discontinued and statin therapy and blood pressure control was optimised. The patient subsequently required dialysis.

5. Conclusion

Renal biopsies provide invaluable information as it allows exclusion of other causes of renal failure (for example, immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis) and allows confirmation of the diagnosis. In our cases, the diagnosis of AERD resulted in significant changes in the clinical management for both patients. These contrasting cases highlight that AERD should be considered not only after vascular procedures but also in patients with atherosclerosis and severe hypertension in whom no obvious precipitant is identified. Increased clinician awareness, combined with timely renal biopsy, is essential to establish the diagnosis, avoid unnecessary immunosuppression and direct management towards secondary prevention through statin optimisation, rationalisation of anticoagulation, blood pressure control and supportive renal care.

General Statement

This case report presents two contrasting manifestations of AERD, including a rare spontaneous case without identifiable precipitating factors. Our report demonstrates the diagnostic challenges posed by AERD’s variable clinical presentations, from classic postcardiac surgery presentation to an atypical presentation that clinically mimicked rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.

The educational value lies in the unexpected histological diagnosis of AERD in a patient initially treated for presumed glomerulonephritis, illustrating how clinical presentation can be misleading even for experienced clinicians. This emphasises the critical role of renal biopsy in establishing definitive diagnosis and guiding clinical management, particularly when clinical suspicion is low. The contrasting presentations highlight the need for increased awareness of AERD, potentially improving diagnostic rates of this underrecognised condition.
