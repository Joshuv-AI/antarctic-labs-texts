Reversal of Glass Transition Temperature at Buried Interfaces in Thermosetting Epoxy Reveals Competition Between Network Maturity and Solid-Induced Confinement
Authors: Tatsuki Abe, Honori Tajima, Yuji Yasukochi, Keiji Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glass transition at buried interfaces critically influences the reliability of thermosetting adhesives and composites, yet direct determination of interfacial glass transition behavior remains challenging. Here, we report a label-free fluorescence lifetime approach that directly excites aromatic backbones of an epoxy resin using third-harmonic generation pulses, eliminating probe-induced artifacts and enabling thermally robust and noninvasive measurements. By combining this approach with evanescent wave illumination, we selectively probed the buried adhesion interface and found that the interfacial glass transition temperature was lower than the bulk value under conventional curing conditions. Upon additional thermal annealing, however, the interfacial glass transition temperature exceeded the bulk value. These results resolve the interplay between chemical network maturity and substrate-induced confinement, establishing a mechanistic framework for interfacial glass transition in thermosetting polymers.

Polymer adhesives based on epoxy resins are indispensable in structural bonding and composite technologies owing to their high mechanical strength, thermal resistance, and environmental durability. − In both adhesive joints and particle- or fiber-reinforced composites, mechanical failure and long-term degradation frequently initiate at buried interfaces rather than in the bulk. − The interfacial region thus constitutes a critical zone in which network structure and molecular motion collectively govern stress distribution and, consequently, macroscopic reliability. A molecular-level understanding of thermal motion at such interfaces is therefore essential for the rational design of high-performance epoxy systems.

For thermoplastic polymers, extensive experimental studies have established that chain mobility is suppressed near solid substrates due to adsorption-induced structural relaxation and entropic confinement. − This suppression frequently results in an elevated interfacial glass transition temperature (T g i) compared with the bulk value (T g b), and mobility gradients extending tens of nanometers from the interface have been reported. ,,− These findings provide a well-defined framework in which substrate-induced confinement governs interfacial dynamics. However, whether this framework can be directly extended to thermosetting systems remains an open question.

In thermosetting polymers such as epoxy resins, irreversible curing reactions continuously modify network structure and thus molecular motion during interfacial formation. − Interfacial segregation of reactive species − and diffusion-limited curing near solid surfaces − can generate spatial heterogeneity in cross-linking density and chemical maturity. As a result, T g i may reflect not only substrate-induced confinement but also incomplete network development. Direct experimental determination of T g i at buried adhesive interfaces is intrinsically challenging. Conventional thermal analyses predominantly probe bulk-averaged responses and lack interfacial selectivity, while fluorescence-based approaches require elevated temperatures in high-T g epoxy networks, where external probes may undergo thermal degradation or interfacial segregation, thereby perturbing local chemistry. Achieving spatial selectivity while maintaining thermal robustness and avoiding chemical perturbation remains a central experimental difficulty.

To address these challenges, we employed a label-free fluorescence lifetime approach based on direct excitation of aromatic backbones using the third-harmonic generation (THG) of a Ti:Sapphire laser. By eliminating external probes, this method enabled stable measurements even at elevated temperatures without compositional perturbation. Furthermore, by combining backbone excitation with evanescent-wave illumination under total internal reflection, we selectively probed the buried adhesion interface with nanometric depth resolution. Using this approach, we demonstrated that the T g i of an epoxy resin (ER), composed of bisphenol A diglycidyl ether of (DGEBA) and 4,4′-diaminodiphenylmethane (DDM), − was lower than T g b under conventional curing conditions but exceeded T g b after additional thermal annealing. These findings revealed that the interfacial glass transition in thermosetting polymers is governed by competition between chemical network maturity and substrate-induced confinement, providing a mechanistic framework applicable to both adhesive joints and epoxy-based composite materials.

DGEBA and DDM were used as the epoxy base and amine hardener, respectively. They were mixed stoichiometrically at a molar ratio of 2:1 and cast onto quartz substrates or trapezoidal sapphire prisms, depending on the measurement. The mixture was pre-cured at 363 K for 3 h under nitrogen and subsequently post-cured at 453 K for 12 h under the same nitrogen atmosphere. The apparent reaction conversion, determined by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy from the consumption of epoxy groups, reached ∼100% within the experimental resolution under these curing conditions, based on the disappearance of the epoxy absorption peak after postcuring. Thus, “∼100% conversion” refers to the detection limit rather than complete conversion.

Thermal properties were evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC 6200, Hitachi High-Tech Science Co., Tokyo, Japan) at a heating rate of 10 K min–1. The T g b of the fully cured resin was determined to be 442 K. Steady-state ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (U-3100, Hitachi High-Technologies Co., Tokyo, Japan). Fluorescence spectra were examined with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4500, Hitachi High-Technologies Co., Tokyo, Japan). Quartz substrates were used to support the ER films to ensure optical transparency in both excitation and emission wavelength ranges. The excitation wavelength (λ ex) was set to 265 nm, corresponding to the absorption of aromatic moieties − and matching the THG pulse used in the fluorescence lifetime measurements.

For temperature-controlled fluorescence lifetime measurements, ER films with a thickness of approximately 1 μm were prepared on trapezoidal sapphire prisms by drop-casting. The films were covered with a quartz plate immediately after casting and cured under the same conditions described above. Sapphire prisms were employed as model oxide surfaces of aluminum adherends and to enable total internal reflection due to their high refractive index (n) at 267 nm. Figure (a) shows a schematic representation of the optical configuration used for the measurements. An ER film was placed in a homemade cell equipped with ceramic heaters incorporating thermocouples (Sakaguchi E.H Voc Co., Tokyo, Japan) under vacuum conditions. An amplified Ti:Sapphire laser system (Solstice, Spectra-Physics, CA, USA) operating at 1 kHz with a pulse width of 100 fs (fwhm) was used as the excitation source. The 800 nm fundamental pulse was converted to 267 nm THG pulses for excitation. Time-resolved fluorescence was detected using a streak camera system (C4334–01, Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan). Lifetime analysis was performed as described previously.

(a) Optical configuration for fluorescence lifetime measurement. (b) Schematic representation of the measurement geometry at θ i = 70 and 75°, set below and above the critical angle θ c, respectively.

Excitation pulses were introduced through the prism at controlled incident angles. When the incident angle (θ i) exceeded the critical angle (θ c), total internal reflection occurred at the ER/prism interface, generating an evanescent field that decays exponentially along the direction normal to the interface. This configuration enabled selective excitation of the buried adhesion interface. The θ c value for the ER/sapphire system was calculated to be 72° using Snell’s law. Figure (b) and (c) show schematic representations of the measurement geometry at θ i = 70 and 75°, set below and above the θc, respectively.

Figure shows the UV–vis absorption and steady-state fluorescence spectra of the cured ER film. In the absorption spectrum, a maximum was observed at 268 nm, which could be assigned to the 0–0 transition of aromatic rings in DGEBA. A weaker band observed at 310 nm corresponds to the 0–0 transition of DDM units. The intense absorption below 250 nm is attributed to π–π* transitions of the aromatic moieties in both DGEBA and DDM. The fluorescence spectrum obtained upon excitation at 265 nm exhibited a broad emission band centered at 355 nm. In aromatic polymers such as polystyrene, monomer emission from isolated benzene rings typically appears at 280∼300 nm, whereas longer wavelength emission around 320∼350 nm has been attributed to excited state dimers, excimers, formed through intermolecular π–π interactions. ,− The emission at 355 nm observed in the ER films is thus most plausibly associated with an excimer-like species arising from interacting aromatic units within the cross-linked network. Although the precise nature of the emitting state (e.g., excimer vs exciplex) could not be strictly distinguished in the current system, its formation is known to be sensitive to intermolecular proximity and segmental mobility. Consistent with this interpretation, concentration dependent fluorescent measurement of the DGEBA/DDM mixture in tetrahydrofuran (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information (SI)) showed enhanced long-wavelength emission at higher concentrations, supporting the assignment of the 355 nm band to an interaction-induced excited state. Importantly, since this emission is governed by aromatic interactions that depend on local molecular motion, its fluorescence lifetime provides a direct probe of thermally activated segmental dynamics in the network.

Steady-state UV–vis absorption and fluorescence spectra of the cured ER film. The fluorescence emission band centered at 355 nm is attributed to an excimer-like species arising from interacting aromatic moieties.

Figure shows the temperature dependence of the fluorescence lifetime for the ER, which was pre-cured at 363 K for 3 h and post-cured at 453 K for 12 h, examined at θ i = 70°, where the excitation pulse at λ ex of 267 nm propagated through the film and predominantly probed the bulk region. The fluorescence lifetime was normalized using the value at the lowest temperature in the data set. Since our primary focus is on identifying the inflection point in the temperature dependence of the fluorescence lifetime, namely T g, the relative change is more important than the absolute value. We confirmed that the determination of T g was not affected by the choice of normalization reference, indicating that the analysis is robust with respect to this procedure. The lifetime of the 355 nm excimer-like emission decreased linearly with increasing temperature, reflecting increased probability of nonradiative decay driven by thermally activated molecular motion. A distinct change in slope was observed at approximately 445 K. T g was determined as the inflection point in the temperature dependence of the fluorescence lifetime using a bilinear fitting procedure, in which linear fits to the low- and high-temperature regions were intersected. The extracted T g values varied only within a narrow range upon reasonable changes in the fitting range, indicating that the determination is robust. The dotted lines in Figure represent the corresponding bilinear fits. This change reflects the activation of segmental motion associated with the glass transition, where large-scale molecular motion markedly increases the probability of nonradiative deactivation. The T g b of the cured ER determined independently by DSC was 442 K, in good agreement with the transition identified from the lifetime analysis. These results demonstrate that direct excitation of aromatic moieties in the ER network provides a sensitive probe of segmental motion and thus enables determination of T g without the use of external fluorescent probes.

To selectively access the buried adhesion interface, the direct excitation of aromatic backbones was achieved using evanescent waves. Under conditions of total internal reflection, the excitation field decays exponentially normal to the interface, enabling preferential excitation of the interfacial region. The intensity profile I(z), where z denotes the distance from the interface, of the evanescent field is described as 1 I(z)=I0⁡exp(−2z/dp) Here, I 0 is the intensity at the outermost interface and d p is the penetration depth given by 2 dp=λ2πnprism{sin2⁡θi−(npolymernprism)2}−1/2 Here, λ and n denote the wavelength of excitation pulses and the refractive index, respectively. When absorption in the polymer is considered, the effective analytical depth (d a) becomes 3 da=(2dp+4πkλ)−1

Using optical constants determined by spectroscopic ellipsometry (Figure S2 in the SI), the d a was estimated to be 48 nm at θ i = 75°. This depth inherently includes depth-convoluted information. Although it extends beyond a molecularly sharp interface, the exponential decay of the excitation field ensures that the detected signal is strongly weighted toward the interface. Since the measurement predominantly reflects molecular motion in the vicinity of the buried adhesion interface, the analyzed region is referred to as an interface-dominant region.

Figure shows the temperature dependence of the fluorescence lifetime for the ER, which was pre-cured at 363 K for 3 h and post-cured at 453 K for 12 h, examined at θ i = 75°. Under this condition, the excitation pulse was totally reflected at the ER/substrate interface, and the network near the adhered interface was selectively excited by the evanescent field. Similar to the bulk case, the fluorescence lifetime decreased linearly with increasing temperature, reflecting enhanced nonradiative decay. However, a distinct change in slope was observed at 398 K, indicating the T g i. This value is markedly lower than the corresponding T g b. These results demonstrate that, under the current curing condition (pre-cured at 363 K for 3 h and post-cured at 453 K for 12 h), molecular motion associated with the glass transition was activated at a lower temperature at the interface than in the bulk.

In thermoplastic polymers, T g i is often higher than the corresponding T g b because of substrate-induced dynamical constraints. In contrast, the current ER exhibited a lower T g i than the T g b. This opposite trend implies that in this case, chemical factors dominated over the confinement effect imposed by the solid substrate. We therefore hypothesized that the lower T g i arose from incomplete network formation at the interface. This interpretation is supported by the observed increase in T g with increasing reaction conversion in the bulk DGEBA/DDM system, as determined by temperature-modulated differential scanning calorimetry (Figure S3 in the SI). However, because the reaction conversion at the buried interface could not be directly quantified in the present study, this interpretation remains semiquantitative. Free volume gradients near solid interfaces may also influence segmental dynamics. Previous studies suggest that the free volume near solid interfaces is generally smaller than that in the bulk, which would tend to increase rather than decrease T g. Thus, although this contribution cannot be completely ruled out, it is less likely to account for the observed reduction in interfacial T g.

This incomplete curing may originate from suppressed molecular and/or fragmental diffusion in the interfacial region, which lowers the local reaction conversion and cross-linking density. Another possible contributing factor is a deviation from stoichiometry resulting from interfacial segregation of the amine hardener. To examine the latter possibility, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy combined with a gas cluster ion beam (GCIB-XPS) depth profiling was carried out. The interfacial molar ratio of DGEBA to DDM deviated from the bulk feed ratio, indicating enrichment of amine species near the interface, as shown in Figure S4 in the SI. If the conversion versus T g relationship is taken as a first approximation, the observed T g i of 398 K corresponds to a reaction conversion lower than that of the fully post-cured bulk. Although interfacial stoichiometric deviations and dynamic constraints, as later discussed, may modify this quantitative relationship, the analysis strongly suggests that the interfacial network is chemically less mature than the bulk under the current curing conditions. This interpretation is consistent with earlier studies. To further test this hypothesis, additional thermal annealing experiments were performed.

Figure shows the temperature dependence of fluorescence lifetime for the ER film that was additionally annealed at 453 K for 24 h after postcuring. The θ i was again set to 75°, ensuring that the analytical depth was identical to that used in Figure . The fluorescence lifetime decreased linearly with increasing temperature, however, no change in slope was observed near 398 K. Instead, a distinct inflection point appeared at approximately 460 K. This result indicates that the interfacial glass transition temperature shifted to a higher value after additional thermal annealing and became more than 10 K higher than the corresponding T g b.

Temperature dependence of the fluorescence lifetime of the ER film after additional annealing at 453 K for 24 h, examined at θ i = 75° in the interface-dominant region. Lifetime values were normalized to the value at 303 K.

The elevated T g i after additional annealing requires careful consideration. DSC confirmed that the T g b remained unchanged after the additional annealing, indicating that the curing reaction in the bulk had already reached completion within experimental resolution during postcuring. Thermogravimetry-differential thermal analysis further showed that the annealing temperature was well below the onset of thermal degradation for DGEBA, DDM, and the cured epoxy network, excluding the possibility that the increase in T g i originated from bulk overcuring or thermal degradation. Instead, additional annealing promotes maturation of the interfacial network, allowing the confinement effect imposed by the solid substrate to dominate over chemical deficiency and resulting in T g i exceeding T g b, as commonly observed in thermoplastic systems. These findings indicate that under the initial curing conditions the interfacial network was chemically less developed than the bulk, whereas further annealing reduced this disparity and revealed the intrinsic interfacial dynamical constraints. Although interfacial amine segregation may contribute to the reduced T g i under the initial curing conditions, the present results show that substrate-induced confinement becomes the dominant factor once the interfacial network is sufficiently matured.

In addition to the above interpretation, other factors may also contribute to interfacial dynamics. Structural relaxation, such as enhanced adsorption of interfacial chains, may reduce segmental mobility and can be regarded as part of the substrate-induced confinement effect. However, in thermosetting systems, detailed characterization of such adsorbed layers is challenging due to network percolation, which hinders isolation of interfacial chains. Furthermore, possible covalent bond formation between epoxy groups and hydroxyl groups present on sapphire surfaces during curing has been suggested in previous studies. While such interfacial reactions cannot be ruled out, their contribution in the present system is expected to be limited. Taken together, multiple factors may coexist at solid interfaces and their individual contributions are difficult to fully decouple. Nevertheless, the strong correlation between T g and reaction conversion (Figure S3), combined with previous reports of suppressed molecular mobility near solid interfaces, supports the conclusion that network maturity is the dominant factor governing the interfacial T g.

In conclusion, we demonstrated a label-free approach for determining the T g of a thermosetting epoxy resin by directly exciting aromatic backbones and monitoring fluorescence lifetime. The T g b identified from lifetime analysis agreed with DSC measurements, validating the method. Application of evanescent wave excitation enabled selective probing of the buried adhesion interface, revealing that the T g i was initially lower than the corresponding T g b under conventional curing conditions. This reduction in T g was attributed to incomplete interfacial network formation. Upon additional thermal annealing, however, the T g i exceeded the T g b, indicating that once chemical maturity is achieved, substrate-induced confinement dominates the interfacial glass transition behavior. These findings demonstrate that the interfacial thermal and thus mechanical properties in thermosetting polymers is governed by competition between chemical network development and solid-induced dynamical constraints, providing a framework for the rational control of curing protocols in adhesive systems.
