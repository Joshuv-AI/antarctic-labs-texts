EUS‐Guided Choledochoduodenostomy Versus Hepaticogastrostomy in Case of Concomitant Gastric Outlet Obstruction Treated by EUS‐Guided Gastroenterostomy: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Marco Spadaccini, Yen‐I Chen, Roberto Leone, Antonella Durante, Maria Margherita Massaro, Giuseppe Vanella, Maria Terrin, Matteo Colombo, Carlo Fabbri, Shannon Melissa Chan, Alessandro Fugazza, Roberto De Sire, Jeremie Albouys, Silvia Carrara, Jeremie Jacques, Daryl Ramai, Andrea Anderloni, Cesare Hassan, Paolo Giorgio Arcidiacono, Anthony Yuen Bun Teoh, Alessandro Repici, the BRIDGE study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBiliary (BO) and gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) frequently complicate pancreatic cancer, often delaying treatment. While EUS‐guided gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE) is preferred over duodenal stenting for GOO, the optimal approach for biliary drainage (BD) between EUS‐guided choledochoduodenostomy (EUS‐CDS) and hepaticogastrostomy (EUS‐HGS) remains undefined. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to identify the preferred BD strategy in patients undergoing EUS‐GE for concurrent GOO and BO.MethodsWe searched Medline, Scopus, and EMBASE up to February 2026. Studies were eligible if they reported outcomes of EUS‐CDS or EUS‐HGS in patients with malignant BO and concomitant GOO treated by EUS‐GE. Outcomes included technical and clinical success, stent dysfunction, and adverse events (AEs) analyzed using a random‐effects model. Results were reported as relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsFive studies were included (128 patients; 45.5% female; mean age range 63.0–73.0 years), with 61 undergoing EUS‐CDS and 67 undergoing EUS‐HGS. Four studies were European, one American, and one prospective. Technical and clinical success rates were similar between groups (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.83–1.11; I 2 = 0%; EUS‐CDS 94.5% vs. EUS‐HGS 96.4%), (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.92–1.19; I 2 = 0%; EUS‐CDS 96.5% vs. EUS‐HGS 89.1%). Stent dysfunction was significantly higher in EUS‐CDS (RR 4.42, 95% CI 1.71–11.43; I 2 = 0%; EUS‐CDS 45.1% vs. EUS‐HGS 10.9%), while AEs were comparable (RR 1.14, 95% CI 0.24–5.50; I 2 = 40.8%; EUS‐CDS 10.7% vs. EUS‐HGS 16.0%).ConclusionsEUS‐CDS and EUS‐HGS appear similarly safe and effective for managing malignant BO with concomitant GOO. However, EUS‐CDS carries a significantly higher risk of stent dysfunction, potentially delaying critical cancer treatment.Keywords: EUS‐BD, EUS‐CDS, EUS‐GE, EUS‐HGS, GOO, malignant double obstruction

Introduction

Biliary obstruction (BO) and gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) are the most frequent complications associated with pancreatobiliary tumors [1], both of which can significantly impair quality of life. Although endoscopic ultrasound‐guided gastroenterostomy (EUS‐GE) has demonstrated superiority over duodenal self‐expandable metal stents (dSEMS) for the management of GOO [2], the optimal strategy for biliary drainage (BD) following successful EUS‐GE remains uncertain.

Randomized trials comparing EUS‐guided BD (EUS‐BD), including both EUS‐guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS‐HGS) and choledochoduodenostomy (EUS‐CDS), have been shown to be superior to percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) while being at least comparable to endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In the concomitant presence of GOO, EUS has emerged as the optimal approach given that the major ampulla is often inaccessible for ERCP. The optimal EUS transmural technique (HGS or CDS), however, remains unclear in this context. Several studies have suggested that duodenal invasion may increase the risk of EUS‐CDS dysfunction due to stasis of food leading to food impaction or sump syndrome [10, 11], whereas EUS‐HGS, while potentially overcoming this limitation, remains a technically demanding procedure with a potentially higher risk of serious adverse events (AEs) [12]. The introduction of single‐step cautery‐enhanced lumen‐apposing metal stents (CE‐LAMS) has indeed significantly reduced the technical complexity of EUS‐CDS, contributing to its increased adoption in clinical practice. Although the primary aim of these interventions is to achieve effective BD, it is also critical to ensure a stable resolution of obstructive jaundice to facilitate timely initiation or continuation of chemotherapy when indicated. Thus, a key objective of EUS‐BD should be to provide durable stent patency through a safe and effective approach.

Our aim was to identify the optimal BD strategy in patients undergoing EUS‐GE for concomitant GOO and BO. We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis summarizing the outcomes of the first reported individual experiences. We hypothesized that EUS‐HGS is associated with lower rates of stent dysfunction and is comparable in safety and technical success when compared to EUS‐CDS in the context of concomitant GOO treated with EUS‐GE.

Methods

Overview

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero; registration number ID: CRD420251127999).

Search Strategy and Study Selection

We performed a comprehensive literature search of the MEDLINE, Scopus, and Embase databases from the inception to February 28, 2026, to identify studies evaluating outcomes of endoscopic management in patients with malignant jaundice and GOO. In addition, we searched the PROSPERO database to identify ongoing or recently completed systematic reviews. The electronic search was supplemented by manual screening of reference lists from the included studies and relevant review articles. Detailed information regarding the search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, study selection process, data extraction, and quality assessment is provided in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1.

Outcomes

For the purposes of this analysis, we assessed the following outcomes:

Technical success, defined as successful completion of EUS‐guided BD through the intended route (EUS‐CDS or EUS‐HGS);

Technical success, defined as successful completion of EUS‐guided BD through the intended route (EUS‐CDS or EUS‐HGS);

Clinical success, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in bilirubin by day 15, or normalization of bilirubin by day 30, or normalization of liver function tests allowing continuation of oncologic treatment, as reported in the individual studies;

Clinical success, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in bilirubin by day 15, or normalization of bilirubin by day 30, or normalization of liver function tests allowing continuation of oncologic treatment, as reported in the individual studies;

Stent dysfunction, defined as any occurrence of biliary stent migration, obstruction, cholangitis, or recurrent jaundice after initial clinical success;

Stent dysfunction, defined as any occurrence of biliary stent migration, obstruction, cholangitis, or recurrent jaundice after initial clinical success;

AEs, reported and classified according to the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) lexicon [14] or the AGREE severity grading system [15], depending on the criteria adopted by individual studies.

Outcome definitions of the individual studies are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Pooled proportions and event rates were calculated using a random‐effects model in the presence of substantial heterogeneity; otherwise, a fixed‐effects model was applied [16]. Dependent variables were modeled on the logit (log‐odds) scale and transformed into percentages with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic (with > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity) and the Q test, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.10 for heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots with trim‐and‐fill analysis and Begg and Mazumdar's rank correlation test [17]. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18. All tests were two‐sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant, except for heterogeneity testing where p < 0.10 was used.

Results

Study Characteristics and Quality

The literature search yielded 124 articles (Figure 2). Following title and abstract screening, 8 articles were selected for full‐text review, of which six met the inclusion criteria. Given the substantial overlap between the patient populations reported in two of the eligible studies, and to avoid double counting while preserving data independence, the study by Bronswijk et al. [18] was excluded from the quantitative synthesis: this study did not provide extractable comparative data for patients undergoing combined EUS‐BD and EUS‐GE according to the biliary drainage strategy (CDS vs. HGS). Consequently, five studies were ultimately included in the analysis. These studies encompassed a total of 128 patients (45.5% female; mean age range: 63.0–73.0 years), with 61 undergoing EUS‐CDS and 67 undergoing EUS‐HGS. Four studies were conducted in Europe [19, 20, 21, 22], and one in the United States [23]; all were multicenter studies with 3 retrospective and one prospective design [20].

The methodological quality of the included studies, evaluated using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale, yielded an average score of 5.2 (range: 4–6; Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Table S2). Study characteristics are summarized in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Table S3.

Outcomes

Effectiveness

The technical features of the included studies are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Table S4. Technical success was achieved in 94.5% (95% CI: 89.0%–100.0%) of patients treated with EUS‐CDS and 96.4% (95% CI: 92.0%–100.0%) of those treated with EUS‐HGS (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Figures S1 and S2). The pooled technical success rates were comparable between the two groups (RR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.83–1.11; Figure 3), as were clinical success rates (RR 1.04, 95% CI: 0.92–1.19; Figure 4). In detail, clinical success was achieved in 96.5% (95% CI: 90.7%–100.0%) of patients undergoing EUS‐CDS and in 89.1% (95% CI: 78.8%–99.4%) of those treated with EUS‐HGS (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Figures S3 and S4). No heterogeneity was observed across studies for either technical or clinical success (I 2 = 0%).

The risk of stent dysfunction was significantly higher in the EUS‐CDS group compared with EUS‐HGS (RR 4.42, 95% CI: 1.71–11.43; I 2 = 0%; Figure 5). Loss of stent patency occurred in 45.1% (95% CI: 22.3%–67.9%) of cases treated with transduodenal LAMS (EUS‐CDS) versus 10.9% (95% CI: 3.3%–18.5%) with transgastric self‐expandable metal stents (SEMS; EUS‐HGS), over a mean follow‐up period ranging from 3 to 6 months (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Figures S5 and S6). Safety

The incidence of procedure‐related AEs was comparable between the two approaches (RR 1.14, 95% CI: 0.24–5.50), with a moderate level of heterogeneity across studies (I 2 = 40.8%; Figure 6). Specifically, the pooled AEs rate was 10.7% (95% CI: 2.8%–18.7%) for EUS‐CDS and 16.0% (95% CI: 7.3%–24.7%) for EUS‐HGS (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Figures S8 and S9).

Severe AEs occurred in 13.7% (95% CI: 0.4%–27.0%) of EUS‐CDS cases, and 3.8% (95% CI:0.9%–8.6%) of EUS‐HGS cases with no significant difference between the groups (RR 1.68, 95% CI: 0.24–12.02; I 2 = 16.44%). A detailed breakdown of AEs is provided in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Table S5. See Supporting Information S1: Appendix 2, Figure S10 to find publication bias for safety outcomes.

Discussion

Our meta‐analysis shows that both EUS‐CDS and EUS‐HGS are associated with similarly high technical and clinical success rates and comparable safety profiles. Although EUS‐CDS showed a non‐significant trend toward higher clinical success rates compared with EUS‐HGS, it was however clearly associated with a significantly higher risk of stent dysfunction.

These findings are clinically relevant in this complex yet not so rare encountered patient population. As a matter of fact, although EUS‐CDS has gained widespread adoption in recent years, often becoming the preferred approach in this setting due to the improved technical simplicity introduced by EC‐LAMS, our pooled analysis suggests that the altered gastrointestinal anatomy following EUS‐ BD may predispose transduodenal LAMS to a higher risk of dysfunction, particularly due to ascending cholangitis. This is likely attributable to both stent placement in the duodenal bulb, an area prone to food stasis, and the design of LAMS, which may increase the risk of either persistent or transient stent occlusion and cholangitis [24, 25]. In contrast, EUS‐HGS, which typically employs tubular covered metal stents with a longer intraluminal segment, may mitigate this risk by providing a longer drainage tract, a more stable anatomical configuration in the presence of a bypassed duodenum, and no pooling of food at the location of the gastric end of the stent, potentially explaining the observed durability of biliary drainage.

Malfunctioning biliary stents can lead to cholangitis, delays in chemotherapy, and the need for reintervention, each of which may significantly compromise oncologic outcomes and patient quality of life. Therefore, although the initial technical ease of EUS‐CDS is appealing, its use in the setting of concomitant GOO managed with EUS‐GE should be carefully weighed against the long‐term risk of dysfunction. Furthermore, although the adoption of EUS‐HGS remains largely confined to tertiary referral centers and expert interventional endoscopists because of its technical complexity, several dedicated stents [26, 27, 28, 29], guidewires [30, 31], dilation devices [32, 33], and delivery systems [34, 35, 36] are currently under investigation with the aim of improving procedural feasibility. One of the most challenging aspects of EUS‐HGS is the need to dilate the fistulous tract between the gastric wall and the intrahepatic bile duct through the liver parenchyma, often requiring device exchange. These steps may increase the risk of bile leakage and subsequent biliary peritonitis [37]. Preliminary studies have suggested that dilation‐free delivery platforms [29, 31, 34, 36] may simplify the procedure and potentially reduce the need for accessory exchanges; however, the available evidence is limited to early feasibility studies and small case series. Therefore, the clinical impact, safety profile, and generalizability of these novel devices remain to be established through larger prospective and comparative studies before their routine adoption can be recommended.

Notably, both the technical success rate and the incidence of procedure‐related AEs did not differ significantly between the two approaches, reinforcing the overall feasibility and safety of both techniques when performed by experienced operators, even in this complex clinical setting. While EUS‐CDS with LAMS is well known for being an endoscopist‐friendly procedure, the slower adoption of EUS‐HGS has raised concerns about its technical feasibility. However, recent analyses have emphasized that the risk of technical failure and AEs is closely linked to the endoscopist's experience with the procedure [38]. Our findings, which synthesize data from high‐volume centers, support the notion that when performed by experts, EUS‐HGS does not expose patients to excessive risk, while potentially offering more durable outcomes. However, we need to disclose that because the choice between EUS‐CDS and EUS‐HGS across the included studies was determined by treating endoscopists rather than by random allocation, the observed differences in stent dysfunction may be influenced by confounding by indication, as anatomical and disease‐related factors that guided the selection of the drainage approach (e.g., duodenal invasion, altered anatomy, ascites, or biliary duct characteristics) may themselves affect stent patency.

This meta‐analysis is the first to directly compare two EUS‐BD techniques in the context of malignant double obstruction. Notably, our findings in this specific setting gain further relevance as they align with previous data comparing the two techniques in the setting of isolated biliary drainage [39, 40]. A key methodological strength of our analysis lies in the strict inclusion criteria, which were narrowly defined to focus on a highly specific and clinically meaningful patient population, thereby enhancing the real‐world applicability of our findings.

Despite its strengths, this study faces limitations inherent to the current body of literature. First, the small number of included studies and overall sample size limit statistical power and preclude detailed subgroup analyses, constraining our ability to account for unmeasured confounders such as patient performance status, tumor burden, and specific technical preferences of endoscopists. However, considering that all but one of the included studies enrolled fewer than 30 patients—rendering reliable comparisons difficult—we believe that a cumulative sample size approaching 150 patients offers a meaningful overview of the strengths and limitations of the techniques. Second, variability in procedural techniques (including differences in stent types, dilation strategies, and operator experience) may have influenced outcomes, but could not be systematically assessed. Conversely, the detailed description of technical features in the included studies provides valuable insight, enabling readers to appreciate the practical nuances of this complex approach. However, the definition of outcomes also varied substantially across the included studies. In particular stent dysfunction definition ranges from recurrence of jaundice after initial clinical success to the need for stent revision or replacement, and in some cases relying on device‐specific classifications; therefore, the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution, as this source of clinical heterogeneity is not adequately captured by statistical measures. Furthermore, the interval between EUS‐GE and BD was inconsistently reported, which may affect both technical success and stent patency. Nevertheless, the strict inclusion criteria applied to treatment strategies for both GOO and BO—although they limited the number of eligible studies—were critical to reducing heterogeneity, and supporting robust conclusions within a well‐defined clinical context. Finally, follow‐up duration was variable and often insufficient to capture long‐term outcomes such as sustained stent patency, quality of life, or overall survival. In particular, differences in follow‐up duration among the included studies represent an important limitation for stent dysfunction, which is inherently time‐dependent. As incidence rates, person‐time data, and survival‐based measures were not consistently reported, our analysis relied on aggregate event proportions, which may not fully account for variations in observation time and could have affected the pooled estimates. Considering the limitations of our analysis, it is important to acknowledge that our results should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating and warrant confirmation in larger prospective trials comparing EUS‐CDS and EUS‐HGS in the setting of concomitant GOO treated with EUS‐GE, with standardized techniques, unified definitions, and datasets including long‐term oncologic follow‐up.

In conclusion, both EUS‐CDS and EUS‐HGS represent viable options for BD in patients undergoing EUS‐GE for malignant GOO. However, the significantly lower rate of stent dysfunction observed with EUS‐HGS suggests that it may be the preferred approach in this complex clinical setting. Given the potential technical challenges of this procedure, centralization of care to high‐volume referral centers may be warranted.
