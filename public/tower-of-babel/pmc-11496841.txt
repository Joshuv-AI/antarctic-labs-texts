Optimal design of deep stormwater drainage tunnels to address urban flooding in Korea
Authors: Yong Jun Cho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep stormwater drainage tunnels play a crucial role in mitigating severe urban flooding in Korea, particularly as climate change leads to more extreme weather events. These tunnels typically consist of an entrance reservoir, a circular reinforced concrete culvert with a flat slope, a downstream reservoir, and an outlet to a nearby river. In this system, the flow within the circular culvert is driven solely by the momentum of free-falling stormwater runoff. However, concerns have emerged within the Korean water resources engineering community about the drainage capacity of these tunnels, even with a substantial fall height of approximately 30 m. Intensified localized rainfall, the abrupt transition from flood waves to pressurized flow within the circular culvert, and trapped air pockets can also pose significant challenges to the tunnel’s drainage efficiency. Conventional design methods often fail to optimize the culvert’s diameter and spatial configuration, leading to suboptimal performance. In response, this study utilizes 3D numerical simulations with the interFoam solver from the OpenFOAM toolbox to address these issues. The results indicate that smaller culvert diameters—provided they do not exceed the maximum permissible flow velocity—enhance drainage capacity by reducing the impact of shock waves and stabilizing flow. These findings challenge the prevailing design practice in Korea, which holds that larger culverts inherently offer superior drainage capacity. Moreover, the simulations suggest that as culvert diameters increase, the size of trapped air pockets grows, further reducing efficiency. Although air chambers can help mitigate the retarding effects of trapped air pockets, their effectiveness diminishes if positioned near the origin of shock waves, where they risk being filled with stormwater. Based on these insights, several key recommendations are proposed: First, the design of deep stormwater tunnels should prioritize minimizing the extent of trapped air pockets, even when air chambers are used. Second, the current focus on detention capacity in design practices should be reevaluated, as excessive detention capacity may exacerbate air pocket formation. Finally, modifying the inlet channel to induce spiral flow within the entrance reservoir could reduce impulsive forces, lower maintenance costs related to armoring rocks at the reservoir bottom, and stabilize flow more quickly, thereby enhancing overall drainage capacity.Keywords: Stormwater drainage tunnel, Optimal design, Spiral flow, Inter-foam, Trapped air pocket, Air chamberSubject terms: Natural hazards, Engineering

Introduction

Korea has recently witnessed a surge in localized heavy rainfall and unprecedented super typhoons, driven largely by rapid climate change. This trend is expected to persist, highlighting the urgent need for global initiatives to transition to renewable energy and reduce carbon emissions. However, the significant costs associated with these efforts pose challenges to their immediate implementation. Consequently, central districts in Seoul, such as Gangnam and Gwanghwamun, have suffered severe flooding (see Fig. 1). The Seoul municipal government attributes these events to intensified rainfall, climate change-driven super typhoons, and rapid urbanization, all of which contribute to higher peak flood waves and faster arrival times. In response to these challenges, Seoul is constructing deep stormwater drainage tunnels to enhance its flood management capabilities. These tunnels feature a circular concrete culvert with a diameter of 8 m and extend over 3 km at depths of around 30 m. They include an entrance reservoir, a downstream reservoir, and an outlet to the Han River (see Figs. 1 and 2)1–7. A comparable large-scale project is the Dubai Deep Tunnel Storm Water System (DTSWS)8, which has a 10.5-meter internal diameter, making it one of the largest stormwater tunnels globally. The DTSWS spans over 10 km, with some sections reaching depths of more than 45 m. Due to its massive scale, the DTSWS relies on gravity to move water through the tunnel, minimizing the need for mechanical pumping and employing Vortex Drop Shafts as energy dissipators to stabilize flow. This aspect distinguishes the DTSWS from Seoul’s deep stormwater drainage tunnels, where the flow within the circular culvert is solely driven by the momentum of free-falling stormwater. As a result, extensive energy dissipation methods, such as those enforced by Vortex Drop Shafts, would reduce the conveyance capacity in Seoul’s context. This comparison underscores the importance of optimizing stormwater drainage tunnels according to their unique geomorphological, economic, and hydraulic constraints. For instance, the deep stormwater drainage tunnels in Seoul, constrained by local geomorphology and budgetary considerations, are designed to manage 30-year flood events. This results in a smaller diameter with a flat slope compared to the DTSWS. However, with the expected increase in stormwater management needs due to ongoing climate change, it is crucial that Seoul’s stormwater tunnels maximize their drainage capacity under given conditions. Challenges such as trapped air pockets within the culverts can significantly hinder drainage efficiency9–12. To mitigate this, the tunnels are equipped with air chambers designed to extract these pockets. The design of deep stormwater drainage tunnels is further complicated by uncertainties stemming from climate change, which intensifies localized rainfall and affects flood wave dynamics. The rapid transition from flood waves to pressurized flow, coupled with the potential for shock waves and trapped air pockets, can compromise the tunnels’ conveyance capacity. Despite these complexities, most deep stormwater tunnels in Korea have been designed using deterministic methods, with limited reliance on reliability-based design optimization approaches, leading to suboptimal performance. A common design practice involves placing armoring rock at the entrance reservoir bottom to reduce the impulsive force accompanying a falling flood wave. However, this method introduces additional turbulence into the drainage system when the flood wave impacts the armoring rock, reducing its overall drainage capacity. Consequently, there are significant opportunities for improvement in the current design platform. These deficiencies are partly due to the relatively recent introduction of such tunnels in Korea and the limited experience within the Korean water resources engineering community with reliability-based design13. Since these tunnels—typically designed with an 8-meter diameter for 30-year return period events—have yet to undergo optimization for drainage capacities, exploring reliability-based design methods could significantly enhance their effectiveness. Optimizing these tunnels would not only improve their performance but also ensure they remain resilient in the face of evolving climate challenges.

As part of basic research aimed at establishing the necessary database for the reliability-based design of deep stormwater drainage tunnels, 3D numerical simulations are conducted to analyze stormwater runoff with a 30-year return period, classified under the Type 4 flow regime according to the United States Geological Survey (USGS)10,11. The hydraulic characteristics of pressurized flow within the circular concrete culvert, which constitutes the deep stormwater tunnel, are influenced by several factors. These include the momentum flux of the stormwater entering the tunnel, the water surface elevation difference between the entrance and downstream reservoirs, energy losses due to contraction near the culvert entrance, friction along the culvert walls, shock waves originating from the downstream end, the presence of trapped air pockets4–7,9, and the significant impulsive forces acting on the reservoir bottom due to the substantial fall height. These simulations are essential for identifying the critical design factors that determine the drainage capacities of such tunnels. A key innovation explored in this study to enhance the drainage capacity of deep stormwater tunnels is the introduction of spiral flow adjustments in the inlet channel. This method offers a significant improvement by eliminating the need for armoring rock, which is traditionally used to reduce the impulsive force of incoming floodwaters but often results in added turbulence that can decrease drainage efficiency. The spiral flow adjustment conceptually aligns with some principles found in Vortex Drop Shafts—a system widely used in spillways for the safe release of floodwaters. However, while Vortex Drop Shafts are specifically designed to dissipate a substantial amount of energy, the spiral flow adjustment introduced here focuses on inducing a more orderly flow pattern within the entrance reservoir. This method is designed to minimize energy loss, helping to stabilize the flow more effectively as it enters the tunnel system. By reducing turbulence and enhancing flow stability, the spiral flow adjustment significantly improves the overall drainage capacity of the stormwater tunnel. This innovative approach not only boosts efficiency but also potentially lowers maintenance costs, as it mitigates the need for extensive protective measures like armoring rock. The simulations employ interFoam, a multiphase solver within the OpenFOAM-based Toolboxes15–18. InterFoam is particularly adept at modeling the formation and behavior of air pockets and their interaction with the flow induced by the entry of flood waves into the stormwater drainage tunnels. This modeling continues until the pressurized flow fully saturates the circular culvert15. Notably, the interaction between trapped air pockets and the flow induced by excess stormwater runoff significantly influences the conveyance capacity of deep stormwater tunnels. However, this interaction has not been fully integrated into the design platform for deep stormwater tunnels in Korea, primarily due to the inherent complexities in analyzing such phenomena. InterFoam utilizes a modified Volume of Fluid (VOF) approach19–30, which incorporates an interfacial compression flux term to counteract numerical smearing effects at the interface. This method is widely recognized for its effectiveness in handling two-phase flow problems across various applications31–36. The accuracy of this approach in describing the dynamics of air pockets and their impact on tunnel conveyance is crucial for the development of more reliable and efficient stormwater drainage systems in Korea. Through these simulations, the study aims to refine the design parameters and improve the overall performance of deep stormwater tunnels, ensuring they can meet the increasing demands posed by climate change.

On enhancing the drainage capacity of the deep stormwater tunnel through spiral flow within the entrance reservoir

To address the issue of mitigating the impulsive force acting at the bottom of the entrance reservoir of the deep stormwater tunnel, a numerical simulation was conducted. As discussed in Section Air pocket formation in the circular concrete culvert, this impulsive force could potentially threaten the structural integrity of the tunnel. Traditionally, the impulsive force from a free-falling flood wave is mitigated by placing armoring rock at the bottom of the entrance reservoir. However, this method introduces additional turbulence when the flood wave impacts the armoring rock, thereby reducing the tunnel’s drainage capacity. In this study, the methodology involved adjusting the inlet channel to induce a spiral flow within the reservoir (see Fig. 16). This adjustment aims not only to reduce the impulsive force at the bottom of the entrance reservoir but also to stabilize the flow within the circular culvert more rapidly, thereby enhancing drainage capacity. Figure 17 compares the time series data of the vertical component of the impulsive force at the bottom of the entrance reservoir, demonstrating how the spiral flow mitigates this force. Figure 18 summarizes the time series data of flow velocity observed at the gauge mounted at X = 25.0 m. The data indicate that the impulsive force during the initial downfall of the flood wave is reduced by up to threefold, and the initial flow velocity appears notably swift due to the more orderly flow. This suggests that the anticipated enhancement in flow is indeed being achieved. Figure 19 presents the streamlines observed in the numerically simulated flow during the initial entry of the flood wave, compared with the streamlines observed when the flood wave falls directly. The spiral flow, traveling along the entrance reservoir wall, completes approximately two turns before entering the circular culvert and transitioning into a straight, orderly motion. After stabilization, the flow velocity in the circular culvert decreases slightly, which is attributed to the extended motion trajectory and energy dissipation (see Fig. 18). In summary, the spiral flow adjustment in the inlet channel offers an advantage over conventional methods by eliminating the need for armoring rock and can be easily implemented during the design stage.

Suggestion on the design of deep stormwater drainage tunnel

The simulations demonstrated that deep stormwater tunnels with circular reinforced concrete culverts on a flat slope can effectively manage runoff from 30-year rainfall events. The results challenge the traditional belief that larger culvert diameters offer superior drainage, revealing that smaller diameters enhance drainage capacity by reducing shock wave impacts and stabilizing flow more quickly. Larger culvert diameters, conversely, increase the size of trapped air pockets, decreasing drainage efficiency. While air chambers can mitigate these effects, their effectiveness diminishes when placed near shock wave origins, where they are more likely to fill with stormwater. Additionally, modifying the inlet channel to create a spiral flow within the entrance reservoir was shown to reduce impulsive forces, lower maintenance costs by eliminating the need for armoring rock, and stabilize flow within the culvert more rapidly, thus improving drainage capacity.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed:

A.Culvert Diameter.Design the culvert diameter to achieve the maximum permissible flow velocity for the flow rate associated with a 30-year return period flood.

Conclusion

The design of deep stormwater drainage tunnels, which are increasingly vital in mitigating severe urban flooding in Korea due to climate change, is influenced by a multitude of factors. These tunnels are designed to accommodate a flood with a 30-year return period and typically consist of an entrance reservoir, a circular reinforced concrete culvert with a flat slope, a downstream reservoir, and an outlet to a nearby river. The flow within the culvert is intended to be driven by the momentum of free-falling stormwater runoff, shaped by local geomorphology and budgetary constraints. Given the anticipated increase in stormwater management needs due to ongoing climate change, it is crucial that Seoul’s stormwater tunnels maximize their drainage capacity under existing conditions. Despite a significant fall height of around 30 m, concerns have arisen within the Korean water resources engineering community regarding the drainage capacity of these tunnels. Factors such as intensified rainfall, the abrupt transition from flood waves to pressurized flow within the culvert, and the formation of trapped air pockets during this transition pose significant challenges to the tunnels’ drainage efficiency. Despite these complexities, most deep stormwater drainage tunnels in Korea have been designed using deterministic methods, with limited use of reliability-based design optimization approaches, often resulting in suboptimal performance. The rationale behind this argument can be found in the preferred design strategies within the current design platform, such as placing armoring rock at the bottom of the entrance reservoir to reduce the impulsive force of a falling flood wave. However, this method introduces additional turbulence into the drainage system when the flood wave impacts the armoring rock, ultimately reducing the tunnel’s overall drainage capacity. Consequently, there are significant opportunities for improvement in the current design platform.

In light of this rationale, this study aims to address the intrinsic limitations of the current design platform by establishing a database for the reliability-based design optimization of deep stormwater drainage tunnels. To achieve this, 3D numerical simulations were conducted using interFoam, a multiphase solver from the OpenFOAM toolbox. This solver can accurately describe the formation of air pockets and their interaction with the flow until pressurized flow fully develops within the circular culvert. This interaction significantly influences the tunnels’ drainage capacity but has not been fully integrated into the current design platform due to the complexity involved in its analysis. The results of these simulations indicate that deep stormwater tunnels with circular reinforced concrete culverts of a flat slope are capable of providing adequate drainage capacity to manage runoff from 30-year rainfall events, addressing the concerns regarding the drainage capacity of these tunnels that have arisen within the Korean water resources engineering community. Furthermore, the findings suggest that drainage capacity is maximized when the culvert diameter is minimized, as long as this does not exceed the maximum permissible flow velocity, reduces the impact of shock waves, and facilitates the rapid stabilization of pressurized flow within the circular culvert. These results challenge the prevailing design practice in Korea, which holds that larger culverts inherently offer superior drainage capacity. The study also reveals that larger culvert diameters are associated with an increased spatial scope of trapped air pockets, further reducing drainage efficiency. While air chambers can mitigate the retarding effects of these trapped pockets, their effectiveness diminishes if positioned near the downstream end, where high-pressure shock waves may fill the chamber with stormwater. Based on these insights, the study offers several recommendations: First, designs should focus on minimizing the spatial extent of trapped air pockets, even when deploying air chambers. Second, the emphasis on detention capacity in current designs should be reconsidered; if the culvert can efficiently convey stormwater runoff without exceeding maximum permissible flow velocities, additional detention capacity may not be necessary. Finally, modifying the inlet channel to induce spiral flow within the entrance reservoir could reduce impulsive forces at the reservoir’s bottom, potentially lowering maintenance costs related to armoring rocks at the reservoir bottom and improving the rapid stabilization of flow within the culvert, thereby enhancing overall drainage performance.
