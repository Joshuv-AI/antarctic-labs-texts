Opening the Scope of Openness in AI
Tamara Paris, AJung Moon, Jin L.C. Guo

Abstract
. The concept of openness in AI has so far been heavily inspired by the definition and community practice of open source software. This positions openness in AI as having positive connotations; it introduces assumptions of certain advantages, such as collaborative innovation and transparency. However, the practices and benefits of open source software are not fully transferable to AI, which has its own challenges. Framing a notion of openness tailored to AI is crucial to addressing its growing societal implications, risks, and capabilities. We argue that considering the fundamental scope of openness in different disciplines will broaden discussions, introduce important perspectives, and reflect on what openness in AI should mean. Toward this goal, we qualitatively analyze 98 concepts of openness discovered from topic modeling, through which we develop a taxonomy of openness. Using this taxonomy as an instrument, we situate the current discussion on AI openness, identify gaps and highlight links with other disciplines. Our work contributes to the recent efforts in framing openness in AI by reflecting principles and practices of openness beyond open source software and calls for a more holistic view of openness in terms of actions, system properties, and ethical objectives.

1.
Introduction

Today, open source practices, such as making source code and components of an AI system publicly available, are often highly encouraged in the
AI
(
AI
) community
(
Shrestha et al., 2023
;
Zuckerberg, 2024
)
. This is in part because of the crucial role open source practices play in advancing transparency, interoperability, and progress in
AI

(
Palmer et al., 2024
;
Shrestha et al., 2023
;
White et al., 2024
;
Zuckerberg, 2024
)
, similar to the benefits they offer for traditional software. However, existing
OSS
(
OSS
) licenses and the open source definition proposed by
OSI
(
OSI
)
(
Perens et al., 1999
)
do not readily apply to
AI
systems and their components — AI systems are not only composed of source code but also of model weights, which are themselves generated by code and data, making them different from the usual structure of software
(
Maffulli, 2022
;
Sijbrandij, 2023
;
White et al., 2024
)
. Moreover, the access to the code and weights without the documentation and training data is not sufficient to allow the freedoms that are supposed to be enabled by the open source practice (i.e., to study, modify, share and use the system). In 2022, the
OSI
launched Deep Dive AI, a global multi-stakeholder co-design process to define open source AI
(
Initiative, 2024
)
, highlighting the need for a specific notion of openness to be tailored for the domain. The first version of the definition was then released in October 2024 and largely adapts the definition of
OSS
to
AI
; it requires code, parameters and data information to be made available in a manner that enables freedom to use for any purpose, study, modify, and share.

However, framing openness in
AI
involves addressing multiple challenges beyond those of
OSS
. First, because
AI
systems and their development process are highly complex, the binary conception of openness (open vs. closed) as in the
OSS
definition is too limiting to express the different types of components and parts of architectural elements that may be open
(
Solaiman, 2023
)
. Hence, some suggest the adoption of a gradient and composite notion of openness for AI
(
Solaiman, 2023
;
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
;
Basdevant et al., 2024
)
. Secondly, the benefits usually associated with
OSS
, such as security, redistribution of influence, and transparency, are not completely transferable to
AI
. For example, developing highly capable models requires significant resources that are only accessible to a limited number of actors; unlike
OSS
, the availability of AI related code and knowledge does not mean that the capabilities of AI have been freely distributed or that the model can be reproduced
(
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Widder et al., 2023
)
. Finally, given the documented risks associated with
AI
models and their applications, the decision to make certain
AI
open can involve a heightened degree of ethics considerations than those involved in traditional software
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
;
Chan et al., 2023
;
Ope, [n. d.]
)
.
For example, misuse of open foundation models is one of the central risks of openness
(
Widder et al., 2022
)
, whereas this concern is not as prevalent for traditional software.
Therefore, while the open source AI definition provided by the OSI is already a significant step forward for the
AI
community, it should be complemented by considering other aspects of openness that extend both the practices and ethical objectives of
OSS
to address these challenges specific to AI.

Looking beyond
OSS
, many concepts related to openness in other domains can enrich the discussion of openness in
AI
. For example, the Model Openness Framework introduced by
White et al. (2024)
draws inspiration from concepts such as
open science
,
open access
, and
open data
to suggest a more responsive openness framework for AI. Moreover, openness is a multifaceted concept that is not confined to computer science
(
Chuang et al., 2022
)
. Its usage is grounded in a shared set of ideas, despite its specificity in each field. Previous work on openness in other disciplines has described some core aspects of openness, such as inclusivity
(
Peters, 2014
)
. Those aspects are also considered important for
AI

(
Zowghi and Bano, 2024
)
, but are not emphasized in the existing discussion concerning openness. Such an observation suggests that the current discourse of openness in AI is narrowly limited to certain aspects and misses significant interdisciplinary inputs to ensure its relevancy.

In this study, we zoom out to take a broader and more interdisciplinary understanding of
openness
to reflect on the current practice concerning openness in AI. We seek to inspire a discussion of what a truly open AI technology and community could and should mean. We first analyze openness concepts expressed across a wide range of academic literature, identified through topic modeling of a large multidisciplinary corpus of scientific articles. We then qualitatively analyzed 98 concepts that surfaced from this process and present it as a taxonomy of openness. The concepts include both abstract and concrete ones, allowing us to highlight properties that are both physical and metaphorical. Finally, we situate
AI
Openness within this broader taxonomy of openness, using which we highlight unexplored perspectives.
Our work contributes to the responsible
AI
community by providing a comprehensive taxonomy of openness that can be used by future work to examine practices and principles of openness in
AI
. By highlighting the multiplicity of the nuances associated with this term, we call for a broader vision of openness beyond its current use in
AI
that can promote discussions on tools and practices related to openness and their relation with ethical objectives.

2.
Background

2.1.
From OSS to Open Source AI

At the dawn of computer commercialization in the 1950s, the source code of software was directly accessible to users. Developers collaborated with each other to create this code, marking the beginnings of open source development practices without being named as such
(
Wolff and Schlagwein, 2021
)
.
In the 1980s, in response to the rise of proprietary software, the free software movement emerged to promote these practices and advocated for everyone’s freedom to run, modify, study, and share software code
(
Stallman, 1985
)
. However, the philosophical and political stance of the free software movement made openness practices less appealing to the software industry. Thus, the term “open source” was created to distance itself from the connotations of free software while adopting its practices
(
Initiative, 2006
)
. Although the two movements share a common foundation (i.e., freedom of use, study, redistribute, and modify the source code), their goals diverge. Notably, free software focuses much more on protecting users’ fundamental freedoms against corporate interests, whereas open source promotes a set of practices that can also be beneficial in pursuit of profit
(
Stallman, 2009
;
Schlagwein et al., 2017
)
.

The
OSI
was created in 1998 to promote the open source philosophy, maintain a definition and approve associated licenses
(
Perens et al., 1999
)
.
Although open source as defined by the
OSI
is a binary concept – either a piece of software is open source or it is not – there are still nuances of openness beyond this definition. For example, some open source licenses are copyleft, requiring that any software reusing the code, whether modified or not, can only be distributed if it preserves the same rights
(
Foundation, [n. d.]
)
. Among proprietary software, some are referred to as “source available” when their code is visible to everyone, even though they do not provide the same freedoms guaranteed by open source
(
FOSSID, [n. d.]
)
.

OSS
has been particularly beneficial in AI. For example, many open source machine learning development frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow helped accelerate technological advancement
(
Langenkamp, 2022
)
. Numerous powerful
AI
models have been introduced to the public as being “open” or “open source”, often using these terms interchangeably
(
Widder et al., 2023
)
. But, similar to how these terms have sometimes been misused to represent some software and data as “open”
(
Heimstädt, 2017
;
Pomerantz and Peek, 2016
)
, some organizations use them as a marketing ploy without meeting the expectation associated with these terms. This raised debates and criticisms about “open-washing” practices
(
Widder et al., 2024
;
Osborne, 2024
;
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
;
Widder et al., 2023
;
Liesenfeld et al., 2023
)
.
For example, many AI models released as “open” restrict certain uses, do not release training source code, and/or do not make the necessary dataset publicly accessible
(
Liesenfeld et al., 2023
;
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
)
(as is the case with LLaMa
(
AI, 2023
;
Maffulli, 2023
)
).
At the same time, some “open” models do not strictly adhere to the usual practices associated with
OSS
for different purposes. For example, Open Responsible AI Licenses (OpenRAIL) restrict certain uses of AI to prevent misuse while preserving many desirable properties of openness
(
Ferrandis and Contractor, [n. d.]
;
McDuff et al., 2024
;
Contractor et al., 2022
)
.

Moreover, there is no clear consensus on what openness means in AI, making it more difficult to identify actual instances of open-washing. While the
OSI
has contributed significantly to defining open source AI
(
Initiative, 2024
)
, it has faced numerous criticisms, particularly regarding the lack of requirements for data openness
(
Brockmeier, 2024
;
Barker, 2024
;
Schneier, 2024
)
. The OSI’s definition is only one perspective among many others, exemplified by the multiple other frameworks that provide a way to assess openness with a less binary approach, such as the Model Openness Framework by
White et al. (2024)
, the Community-Driven Assessment Framework by
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse (2024)
, and the classification scale proposed by
Eiras et al. (2024a)
.

Beyond open-washing, ethical and security risks also poses critical concerns related to openness in AI
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Eiras et al., 2024a
;
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Chan et al., 2023
;
Eiras et al., 2024b
)
.
For example, the emergence of harmful misuses of technology, such as non-consensual pornographic deepfakes, challenges the support for unbridled openness
(
Widder et al., 2022
)
. Balancing the risks and benefits of openness in AI is a crucial challenge for the responsible AI community; researchers are calling for greater caution in open publication of models with significant risks of misuse
(
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Whittlestone and Ovadya, 2020
;
Shevlane and Dafoe, 2020
;
Ope, [n. d.]
)
. Many of these ethical concerns present challenges distinct from traditional software, such as greater capabilities or a higher concentration of resources required for development
(
Widder et al., 2023
)
. Consequently, broadening the definition of openness beyond
OSS
is not only necessary to critically consider
AI
-specific technical realities, but also indispensable to mitigate the societal risks associated with these systems.

2.2.
Openness Across Disciplines

From
open education
,
open science
, to
open innovation
, the concept of openness can be found across disciplines. Even if openness is primarily contextual, prior studies have highlighted common principles among these concepts. For example,
transparency
,
access
,
participation
, and
democratization
are described in the openness framework presented by
Schlagwein et al. (2017)
, and
inclusivity
and
freedom
are highlighted by
Peters (2014)
. Moreover, even if these common principles and practices connect in various forms, the realization of these concepts does not always meet the ideals
(
Chuang et al., 2022
)
. For example, although open source software development allows anyone to participate, making these practices theoretically “inclusive,” in practice women are often left out due to the barrier of sexism present in these communities
(
Chuang et al., 2022
;
Lee and Carver, 2019
)
.

Openness, being extensively used throughout the literature, presents a real challenge in capturing all its nuances. Previous efforts have provided detailed understandings of many openness concepts and how they interact with one another
(
Peters and Roberts, 2015
;
Chuang et al., 2022
)
, while other have offered frameworks for this notion
(
Schlagwein et al., 2017
)
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing work offers a thematic framework of openness that is both extensive and structured: previous work either focuses on a few aspects already known by the
AI
community
(
Schlagwein et al., 2017
)
, or it describes a wide range of concepts but lacks a precise structure that can be easily applied to the
AI
domain
(
Peters and Roberts, 2015
)
.

3.
Method

Figure 2.
Summary of the method used to derive the taxonomy of openness.
Summary of the method section. The goal of this figure is to illustrate the 5 main steps of the method to derive the taxonomy of openness: collection of article abstracts, topic modeling, openness concepts extraction, concept analysis and taxonomy. The step of the concept analysis is sub-divided into 3 sub-steps: abstracts analysis, formal definition retrieval and reflexive thematic analysis on definition.

In this study, we define
openness
as encompassing all uses of the adjective “open” or the noun “openness”.
Consequently, an
openness concept
refers to any unique construct in the academic literature containing these words. This includes both abstract concepts (e.g.,
open access
and
trade openness
) and concrete concepts referring to tangible phenomena (e.g.,
open water
and
open surgery
).
The volume of literature related to openness is too extensive for a manual review (our final corpus contains 224,123 articles, Sect.
3.1
). Hence, we employed topic modeling (Sect.
3.2
) to discover a diverse set of
openness concepts
, to distinguish between homonyms (e.g.,
open field
in agriculture versus in neurology), and to group closely related concepts (e.g.,
open circuit
and
open phase fault
).
Once we established a list of
openness concepts
and retrieved their definitions, we conducted a reflexive thematic analysis on their definitions (Sect.
3.3
).
Figure
2
provides an overview of our method.

3.1.
Collection of Article Abstracts

We queried abstracts from three bibliographic databases: the Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and The Lens. These databases include open-access and subscription-based publications, representing a broad disciplinary coverage such as science, engineering, medicine, and the humanities
(
Gusenbauer, 2022
;
Gusenbauer, 2019
;
Chadegani et al., 2013
;
Zhu and Liu, 2020
;
Tarazi, 2024
)
.
Each database was queried with the term “open|openness” in the title. Our search was limited to journal, conference, or review articles written in English and published between 1980 and 2024. Bibliographic data of all matched entries were collected on September 5 and 6, 2024. Entries without abstracts or with abstracts but shorter than 200 characters were removed. Following these steps, we obtained a total of
555,216
article abstracts (
202,786
from Scopus,
137,755
from Web of Science, and
214,675
from The Lens).
We then removed
267,734
duplicates using unique identifiers, along with titles, author names, sources, and years of publication when no identifier was available.
We also excluded
63,359
articles that do not contain “open|openness” in the abstract, use “open” only as a verb in the title (e.g., “Opening a door”), or appear as erroneous entries.
Our resulting corpus contains
224,123
bibliographic records.

3.2.
Extraction of the Openness Concepts

We applied standard preprocessing steps on each record, including removing links, emails, HTML tags, punctuation, numbers, one- or two-character words, stopwords, credits, and disclosure sections. We also excluded keywords commonly found in abstracts that would introduce noise into our model, such as “methodology” and “results”. We then performed lemmatization on the remaining text using Spacy
(
Honnibal et al., 2020
)
, and included 2-, 3- and 4-grams. Finally, any words and n-grams appearing less than 30 times were removed from the corpus.

We applied Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) using Tomotopy
(
Lee, 2022
)
to uncover the topics.
Through an iterative evaluation, we determined that 80 topics provided the best trade-off between separating concepts into distinct topics and reducing redundancy between topics.
For each topic, we classified abstracts based on the document-topic distance and n-grams containing the terms “open” and “openness” using the word-topic distance. Among these n-grams, we identified at most two cohesive openness concepts per topic by reading abstracts associated with it. In total, 98 concepts were identified from the 80 topics (58 for the first round of analysis and 40 for the second). The complete list of topics and openness concepts is presented in Appendix
A
, along with other information related to the topic models.

3.3.
Qualitative Analysis of the Openness Concepts

Article abstracts rarely contain formal definitions. Therefore,
we used Google Scholar to retrieve formal definitions of each openness concept identified in our abstract-centric corpus. In particular, we queried each concept as a keyword alongside words that typically signal the occurrence of a definition, such as “is defined,” “refers to,” or preceded by “the concept of” or “the term.” This process yielded a collection of at least five formal definitions for each openness concept as expressed by the scholars of the relevant disciplines. We manually examined whether the definitions were consistent with the context of the abstracts previously read.
We then analyzed the resulting definitions through a reflexive thematic analysis process
(
Braun and Clarke, 2006
;
Braun and Clarke, 2019
)
. We built a final taxonomy through two rounds of analysis and validation among all authors.

4.
Taxonomy of Openness

We organize our result as a taxonomy of openness along two dimensions: (1) the
themes
covered by the openness concepts and (2) the
approaches
used to define the openness concepts. The first dimension is composed of three main themes:
Interactivity
(Section
4.1
),
Freedom
(Section
4.2
), and
Inclusiveness
(Section
4.3
), each themes encompassing several sub-themes.
The second dimension outlines the three approaches employed in the literature to define the openness concepts: through (1)
properties
intrinsic to what is open (e.g.,“permeability” and “modular structure”), (2)
afforded action
that openness enables or prevents (e.g.,“inspect” and “interfere”), and (3)
desired effects
of the openness concepts (e.g., “transparency” and “democratization”). Some concepts are only define through
properties
,
afforded actions
, or
desired effects
, while others relate to multiple or all of these approaches. An overview of the two dimensions is provided in Fig.
1
.

Our taxonomy provides a detailed framework of how openness is defined in the literature, highlighting the thematic links between different aspects of openness and the cause-and-effect relationships. For example, the
property
of “modularity” and the
afforded action
of “building add-on products” found in the concept of
open architecture

(
Vogel et al., 2014
;
Murphy, 2003
)
are not only examples of the theme “reuse”; one is also an enabler for the other. When introducing our taxonomy, we also discuss how each theme and sub-themes are related to the existing discussion of openness in AI.

interactions between inside and outside entities
, where “inside” refers to
what
is made open. This includes interactions initiated from inside (e.g., distribution), outside (e.g., inspection), and both (e.g., collaboration). See Table

for examples of
properties
,
afforded actions
and
desired effects
for each sub-theme.

4.1.1.
Access

Many openness concepts feature the ability or right of an external entity to access an internal element. The notion of access and availability are sometimes used interchangeably in the definitions of those concepts. In some contexts, access represents a stronger notion than availability: access can be afforded by a practicable entry (e.g., an incision to access the organs in
open surgery

(
Beretta et al., 2019
;
Panayiotou Charalambous, 2022
)
) or an identifiable access-path (e.g., a link to access the full text in
open access

(
Chan et al., 2002
;
Koutras, 2020
)
), which is not guaranteed by availability. For example,
open source intelligence
(OSINT) is defined as intelligence “produced from publicly available sources”
(
Böhm and Lolagar, 2021
)
. This definition encompasses sources that cannot be easily found through major search engines
(
Balaji and Karmel, 2022
)
. OSINT is therefore a concept that emphasizes availability rather than accessibility, unlike
OSS
, where the means to access the source code must be well publicized
(
Perens et al., 1999
)
. Accessibility can also describe the
desired effect
of the openness concept (e.g.,
open science
) rather than an
afforded action
, then referring to the ease of access rather than access itself (e.g.,
open science

(
Bertram et al., 2023
)
).

Access
in
AI
Openness:
Which components of an AI system are accessible and how they are accessible are key considerations in AI governance
(
Kembery et al., 2024
)
. Prior studies that primarily focused on assessing the risks of openness typically include in their scope all models with accessible model weights and architecture
(
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Ope, [n. d.]
)
, while efforts to define AI openness such as the
OSI
’s Open Source AI Definition
(
Initiative, 2024
)
and the Model Openness Framework
(
White et al., 2024
)
include access to additional components in their scope, such as training code, dataset descriptions, evaluation results, etc. Moreover, the release options for
AI
models are varied
(
Solaiman, 2023
)
, so access itself can refer to different practices, such as API call or downloading components. However, not all types of access provide the same guarantees. For example, being able to download the model avoids relying on third-party tools, provides autonomy and privacy, which is not guaranteed when using APIs
(
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Zuckerberg, 2024
)
. Other types of access, such as through indexed and version controlled public code repositories are possible, but many models claimed “open” do not implement these types of access
(
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
;
Liesenfeld et al., 2023
)
.

4.1.2.
Inspectability

Openness sometimes refers to the possibility or the right of an external entity to inspect what is inside. This can be achieved by several
properties
such as non-concealment (e.g.,
open label

(
Chakraborty, 2011
;
Wolzt and Aschauer, 2016
)
), unobstructed access (e.g.,
open heart surgery

(
Lee, 2014
)
), honesty (e.g.,
open dialogue

(
Riley and Kaneakua, 2024
)
), non-secrecy (e.g.,
open strategy

(
Sunner and Ates, 2019
)
), and can be facilitated by detailed documentation (e.g.,
open source hardware

(
Ezoji et al., 2021
)
). Several openness concepts are defined by the
desired effects
enabled by inspection, such as transparency (e.g.,
open science

(
Bertram et al., 2023
)
,
open government

(
Matei-Sarandi, 2024
)
,
open strategy

(
Sunner and Ates, 2019
)
), reproducibility and reliability (e.g.,
open science

(
Bertram et al., 2023
)
), or accountability (e.g.,
open government

(
Kudo, 2019
)
).

Inspectability
in
AI
Openness:
Inspection is often phrased as one benefit of allowing access to AI systems and a fundamental step for AI auditability, reproducibility, trust, and verifiability of the system
(
Widder et al., 2024
;
Palmer et al., 2024
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Eiras et al., 2024a
)
, along with reducing the risk of major security, safety, or privacy flaws
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
;
Bommasani et al., 2024
;
Chan et al., 2023
)
. While inspection can be facilitated by making model cards
(
Mitchell et al., 2019
)
, datasheets
(
Gebru et al., 2021
)
, and data cards
(
Pushkarna et al., 2022
)
available to the public – which are sometimes included as requirements in the definitions of open models
(
White et al., 2024
;
Initiative, 2024
)
– this documentation does not always translate to auditability and reproducibility of the model, that can be limited by resources
(
Widder et al., 2023
)
. Moreover, the quality of the documentation and the trend of disseminating information via blog posts or preprints rather than peer-reviewed articles is a big concern for transparency
(
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
)
.

4.1.3.
Distribution

Openness can be characterized by the cross-boundary exchange of resources, services (e.g.,
open market

(
Nuhu, 2020
;
Pilinkiene, 2016
)
), and knowledge (e.g.,
open innovation

(
Lichtenthaler, 2011
;
Liu et al., 2018
)
). It includes the right and the possibility to share, redistribute (e.g.,
open educational resources

(
Wiley et al., 2014
;
Condruz-Bacescu, 2019
)
), or sell even without modifications (e.g.,
open source hardware

(
Ezoji et al., 2021
;
Wenzel, 2023
)
). These exchanges can be commercial (e.g.,
open innovation

(
Lichtenthaler, 2011
;
Liu et al., 2018
)
) or purely altruistic (e.g., sharing ideas in
open education

(
Cronin and MacLaren, 2018
;
Blyth, 2013
)
). Some forms of redistribution require acknowledgment of the source (e.g.,
open access

(
van Leeuwen et al., 2015
;
Koutras, 2020
)
) or the right to maintain the same license (e.g.,
OSS

(
Perens et al., 1999
)
). These exchanges are often factors that then enable reuse and collaboration.

Distribution
in
AI
Openness:
The possibility and right to share is an integral part of the definition of
open source
AI
as defined by the
OSI

(
Initiative, 2024
)
, and the right to redistribution is also mentioned in the Model Openness Framework
(
White et al., 2024
)
. However, these two frameworks do not specify whether the model provenance must be acknowledged.

4.1.4.
Reuse

Reuse includes the external use of internal resources or the internal use of external resources, with or without modification (e.g.,
open source hardware

(
Ezoji et al., 2021
;
Wenzel, 2023
)
and
open educational resources

(
Wiley et al., 2014
;
Condruz-Bacescu, 2019
)
). Reuse can be facilitated by
properties
such as modularity, standardization, and consensual practices (e.g.,
open architecture

(
Vogel et al., 2014
;
Murphy, 2003
)
). For example, some services are designed to be integrated into another project (e.g.,
open APIs

(
Wassholm and Lindblom, 2024
;
Mondres, 2018
)
) or to serve as portable solutions (e.g.,
open standards

(
Shah et al., 2008
)
). Through interoperability, these practices permit to build new works on top of existing works (e.g.,
open data

(
Jati et al., 2022
)
).

Reuse
in
AI
Openness:
Reuse and customization of open models is a widely covered topic in the literature on
AI
openness, and presented as an advantage for accelerating innovation, research, and the adoption of state-of-the-art models
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
;
White et al., 2024
;
Eiras et al., 2024a
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Osborne, 2024
;
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Bommasani et al., 2024
;
Shrestha et al., 2023
)
.
Widespread use of a model would also make it possible to test the model limits
(
Widder et al., 2024
)
, and improve its quality
(
Osborne, 2024
)
. At the same time, the reuse of AI models – particularly foundation models – carries serious risks such as harmful misuse, environmental impacts, and fairness issues
(
Bommasani et al., 2021
;
Shelby et al., 2023
)
. Openness can facilitate these misuse, for example by allowing ethical guidelines bypass
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
)
or by fine-tuning and combining open models with other tools to introduce new vulnerabilities or uses not intended by the developers
(
Ope, [n. d.]
)
, questioning its responsibility in case of harms
(
Bommasani et al., 2024
)
. Beyond the use of an open model’s weights for downstream tasks, reusing other components becomes more challenging with limited resources
(
Widder et al., 2023
)
.

4.1.5.
Collaboration

Many openness concepts encompass participatory and collaborative practices. For example, external stakeholders can contribute to decisions and tasks that are usually reserved to people inside the organization (e.g.
open strategy

(
Sunner and Ates, 2019
)
). It can also refer to collaboration, cooperation, and partnerships between entities, as opposed to them operating independently (e.g.
open business model

(
Weiblen, 2014
)
). This enables entities to form networks and communities whith peer-recognition (e.g.
open source software development

(
Singh et al., 2018
;
Khanjani and Sulaiman, 2011
)
).

Collaboration
in
AI
Openness:
Collaboration is often presented as a benefit of
AI
Openness rather than as a part of its definition.
For example, the
OSI
definition
(
Initiative, 2024
)
mentions the benefit of “collaborative improvement” enabled by Open Source
AI
, but does not require an
AI
system to be built or maintained collaboratively to be labeled as “open source.” Similarly, the reuse of external machine learning models allows developers and researchers to collaborate by making practical advancement and scientific findings on top of existing ones
(
Ope, [n. d.]
;
White et al., 2024
)
. However, these forms of collaboration remain limited: they rely on a closed entity that retains control over any modifications made to the original model. Additionally, even if Hugging Face is often used to share
AI
models and collaborate, the most popular models on this platform are often developed by large companies with little external collaboration
(
Osborne et al., 2024
)
.

In parallel, some works emphasize the principle of open collaboration in
AI
Openness, which allows a wider and more diverse audience to freely collaborate to create and maintain
AI
systems by lowering the barriers to participate in project developments
(
Ding et al., 2023
;
Akiki et al., 2022
)
. For example, the BigScience Workshop, an open research collaboration initiative, demonstrated the effectiveness of such practices in building large datasets
(
Laurençon et al., 2023
)
and models
(
Scao et al., 2023
)
.

4.2.1.
No obstacle

Openness can refer to the removal of obstacles (e.g., obstacles blocking access to scientific information in
open access

(
Pisoschi and Pisoschi, 2016
)
), a low density of obstacles (e.g.,
open habitat

(
Melaschenko, 2010
;
Hay et al., 2024
)
), or even emptiness (e.g.,
open field test

(
Mottaghi et al., 2019
;
Ahmad and Richardson, 2013
)
). For instance,
open university
remove formal entry requirements and other technical barriers such as time, pace, and place constraints
(
OKUR, 2019
;
Agbu et al., 2016
)
.
The obstacles removed can also include abstract barriers (e.g., judgment in
open dialogue

(
Mazibuko, 2024
)
) or tangible obstacles (e.g., ice in an
open ocean

(
Dietel et al., 2024
;
Womack, 2021
)
).

No obstacle
in
AI
Openness:
The main obstacle removed in
AI
openness is the cost; most models considered “open” allow users to download their weights for free
(
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
)
. However, price is not the only limitation in practice: financial and computational resources, talent, and high-quality data resources are concentrated in few academics or industry AI research labs
(
Ope, [n. d.]
)
, and the barriers to joining these labs is a major obstacle to participate in innovation
(
Ding et al., 2023
)
. Training a large language model requires considerable hardware resources, which can be difficult to access outside those labs
(
Wolfe et al., 2024
;
Widder et al., 2024
)
; even with the emergence of decentralized methods for training models, such as cloud sourcing, might lower this barrier
(
Chan et al., 2023
)
.

4.2.2.
Organic

Freedom in openness can be exemplified by the removal of external interferences and forms of governance to allow an organic evolution. For example, individuals are free to move within the natural space without artificial blockage or prohibition (e.g.,
urban open spaces

(
Jing-xiao et al., 2010
;
Nasution and Zahrah, 2015
)
). Unlike
no obstacle
(Section
4.2.1
), this sub-theme does not necessarily imply the absence of obstacles. For instance, the removal of tariffs, subsidies, and quotas in an
open market
allows the market to evolve organically by enabling natural competition to take place
(
Nour, 2022
)
. However, obstacles might still form organically, potentially hindering the participation of certain actors.
Organic freedom can also exist within regulations that are neutral and less interventionist (e.g.,
open source licenses

(
Perens et al., 1999
)
do not restrict types of use).

Organic
in
AI
Openness:

OSS
, which greatly inspires openness in
AI
, contains several organic elements: it requires software to be neutral regarding the reuse; it places no constraints on individuals, technologies, or domains that will reuse its source code
(
Perens et al., 1999
)
. Open source developers assume that it is not their role to dictate how their code is used. However, many
AI
models labeled as “open,” such as LLaMa 2, restrict specific types of use in their license
(
Maffulli, 2023
;
AI, 2023
)
. Many other open models opt for Responsible AI Licenses (RAIL), which is also restrictive in its allowed use cases
(
Contractor et al., 2022
;
McDuff et al., 2024
)
. This lack of neutrality, combined with the lack of accessible components and information in some open models, has sparked debates about the loose use of “open” and “open source,” qualifying them as “open-washing”
(
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
)
.

At the same time, the misuse of certain open foundation models is a genuine concern
(
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
)
, which drives some developers to restrict their usage. These restrictions are often implemented through licenses. The emergent efforts, such as self-destructing models
(
Henderson et al., 2023
)
, demonstrate an attempt to establish technical barriers as well. Moreover, some researchers advocate for greater caution in scientific publications on AI
(
Whittlestone and Ovadya, 2020
;
Shevlane and Dafoe, 2020
)
. The irreversibility of openness makes it impossible to moderate harmful uses of models once released openly
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
;
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
)
, and can discourage some developers from making their models publicly accessible. The risk of misuses is not unique to AI: similar debates have already taken place about offensive cybersecurity tools that are open source
(
Haleliuk, 2023
)
. However, some AI models can be used in a wider range of situations than software can, making this risk more prominent in AI.

4.2.3.
Non-isolation

Openness sometimes refers to a system that is not entirely isolated, characterized by the absence of a solid boundary (e.g.,
open boundary

(
Shankar et al., 2001
;
Shi and Zhang, 2016
)
), permeability (e.g.
open framework

(
Yaghi et al., 2003
)
), porosity (e.g.
open cell foam

(
Chen et al., 2021
;
Huisseune et al., 2015
)
), or disconnections (e.g.,
open circuit

(
Reese et al., 2011
;
Samoylichenko, 2024
)
). The state of non-isolation can provide passage between the outside and the inside, including uncontrolled passage. Such systems are prone to leakage (e.g.,
open resonator

(
Qian et al., 2024
;
Ruiz-Riquer, 1996
)
), exchange with the external environment (e.g.,
open fracture

(
Evans, 2016
;
Halawi and Morwood, 2015
)
), or discharge of internal resources outside the system (e.g.,
open cycle

(
Digwijaya et al., 2023
)
). Non-isolation can also result in the interruption of internal flows, as exemplified by the disruption of electrical current in an
open circuit

(
Reese et al., 2011
;
Samoylichenko, 2024
)
. This concept of openness sometimes connotes vulnerability and risky exposure, as seen in cases such as
open fractures

(
Evans, 2016
;
Halawi and Morwood, 2015
)
.

The sub-themes under
interactivity
(Section
4.1
), such as
access
(Section
4.1.1
) or
inspect
(Section
4.1.2
), can lead to non-isolation. What differentiates non-isolation from interactivity is its involuntary nature. At the same time, voluntary interactions can cause similar effects of non-isolation, such as unintended leakages. For example, publicly accessible data, such as open data, are vulnerable to personal information leaks by cross joining different datasets
(
Kao et al., 2017
)
.
In general, allowing external entities to inspect an internal system exposes it to security threats and potential leaks.

Non-isolation
in
AI
Openness:
Open models present risks of component leakage and security flaws due to their permeability and exposure
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
)
. For example, white-box attacks are attacks on
AI
models that are only possible if the inner works of the system, such as its architecture, are known to the attackers
(
Vassilev et al., 2024
;
Ebrahimi et al., 2018
)
. Even if only the weights of a system are open, data and model components might still be recovered through the use of the model
(
Tramèr et al., 2016
;
Carlini et al., 2024
;
Zanella-Beguelin et al., 2021
)
. Open collaboration in
AI
can also allow some permeability and facilitate poisoning attacks
(
Hintersdorf et al., 2025
)
. On the other hand, non-isolation could act as a cause of openness: it is not uncommon to see proprietary models leak to the public
(
Vincent, 2023
;
Roberts-Islam, 2024
)
, and the large number of people involved in the creation of AI models makes these leaks more difficult to prevent.

4.2.4.
Broader boundary

The broadening of edges, limits, or boundaries is a recurring theme in openness. This refers to spatial expansion (e.g.,
open cluster

(
Lamers et al., 2005
;
Mathew et al., 2008
)
) or the broadening of possibilities by reducing constraints (e.g.,
open-ended problem
have no constraints on their solution approach
(
University et al., 2021
;
Moskal, 1997
)
). In this sub-theme, openness can be defined as the opposite of a narrower, closed state (e.g.,
open-angle glaucoma

(
Buttan et al., 2022
)
). This broadening also refers to abstract notions, such as the enhancement of creativity and imagination (e.g.
openness to experience

(
King et al., 1996
;
Williamson, 2018
)
).

Broader boundary
in
AI
Openness:
In prior sections, we include within AI openness previous AI articles that use the terms “open source,” “open,” or “openness.” However, the term “open-ended” is also found in the literature, describing a machine learning model capable of generating sequences that are both novel and learnable by an observer
(
Hughes et al., 2024
;
Ecoffet et al., 2020
)
. We have chosen to separate this concept from the rest of AI openness because its definition is distinct from other openness-related terms in AI. However, open-ended models encapsulate the idea of pushing boundaries beyond what is already known and learned, making it a relevant concept for the sub-theme of
broader boundaries
. The intersection between open-ended models and AI openness could play a big role in future research on risk of open foundation models, especially when considering concerns about the harmful misuse enabled by access.

4.2.5.
Undetermined

Openness can refer to a currently undetermined event that could be determined in the future (e.g.,
open problem
) or a space with undefined, indeterminate, or indeterminable boundaries (e.g.,
open set
in machine learning
(
Baktashmotlagh et al., 2018
;
Sisti and Bennette, 2022
)
). It includes areas outside the boundaries that can not be precisely delineate, such as
urban open space
, which includes all urban areas outside of buildings
(
Jing-xiao et al., 2010
;
Nasution and Zahrah, 2015
)
. Undetermined also refers to the absence of expectations regarding a future event (e.g.,
open-ended games
in education
(
Cahyani et al., 2020
)
). This leads to adaptability to future events that are impossible to predict, exemplified by
open set
in machine learning, which refers to models encountering examples they were not trained to classify before
(
Baktashmotlagh et al., 2018
;
Sisti and Bennette, 2022
)
.

Undetermined
in
AI
Openness:
Similar to what was previously described in the sub-theme
broader boundaries
(Section
4.2.4
), the concept of open-ended models in AI closely aligns with
undetermined
. Open-ended models produce novel outcomes, which therefore could not have been determined by the observer beforehand
(
Hughes et al., 2024
)
. However, the inability to predict or determine the open-ended models’ outcome, as well as the irreversibility of this process, also greatly amplifies the risks associated with those models.

4.2.6.
Autonomy

In openness, autonomy refers to the freedom to act according to its own rules, without the need of permission. Student’s autonomy is one of the core principles of
open education

(
Cronin and MacLaren, 2018
;
Blyth, 2013
)
. Students are able to make their own choices, without being constrained to a particular pace of work or a prescribed curriculum. Students are responsible for their own education without a higher authority dictating decisions. Similarly, autonomy presents in many other education-related concepts, such as
open university

(
OKUR, 2019
)
and
open-ended games

(
Cahyani et al., 2020
)
.
Autonomy is also influenced by other principles of openness, such as inspectability (Section
4.1.2
), which allows individuals to be well-informed to make their own decisions. Autonomy also promotes individual empowerment: for example,
open banking
gives customers the power to decide whether to allow third parties to access their data
(
Chan, 2020
;
Xie and Hu, 2024
)
.

Autonomy
in
AI
Openness:

OSI
’s Open Source AI Definitions explicitly state that components can be reused without asking for permission
(
Initiative, 2024
)
. The ability for developers to download AI components and make their own copies increases developers’ autonomy. For example, developers can choose not to update these components
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
)
.

. This includes representativeness, eliminating discrimination, seeking diversity, recognizing individual needs, and democratization. Inclusiveness can also apply to non-individual entity such as countries, companies or scientific fields. See Table

4.3.1.
Fairness

Equality, equity, and justice are common principles across several concepts of openness. For example, access to
open source software

(
Perens et al., 1999
)
,
open educational resources
, and
(
Wiley et al., 2014
)

open standards
should be non-discriminatory towards groups of individuals, ensuring equality for all. However, equality does not always imply equity or justice. For example,
open market
promotes equal opportunities but does not involve equity
(
Singh, 2019
)
. In contrast,
open education
promotes equity and justice among students, acknowledging that some individuals have greater needs than others
(
Burgos and Saïda, 2022
)
. Fairness also encompasses impartiality and objectivity, exemplified by
open-mindedness
, which refers to an individual’s willingness to consider opposing opinions without biases
(
van Brussel et al., 2023
;
Gallimore, 2021
)
.

Fairness
in
AI
Openness:
To some extend, openness can help mitigating inequality between social groups; for example, inspectability enabled by openness of
AI
models allows detecting biases or harms toward marginalized groups, thereby improving the fairness of the models
(
White et al., 2024
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
)
. Openness in
AI
also facilitates the redistribution of access to state-of-the-art technologies, helping to close the inequality gap in technological capabilities
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
)
. However, as discussed above, equal access to
AI
does not imply equity; there remains a gap in capabilities, knowledge and human resources between different entities, social groups, and geographical areas that opening access does not address
(
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Widder et al., 2023
)
.

4.3.2.
Diversity

While removing discriminatory barriers generally improves diversity, it does not guarantee it. For some concepts of openness, diversity must be actively sought and encouraged. For example,
openness to experience
describes individuals who are not only tolerant of differences but also actively seek out novelty and diversity
(
Shi et al., 2016
)
. Similarly, the variety of colloquiums offered by
open universities
permits to attract diverse profiles of students
(
Agbu et al., 2016
)
. One of the benefits of diversity is the multiplicity of perspectives, which is particularly valued in
open strategy
by involving a wide range of stakeholders in the formulation and implementation of corporate strategy
(
Sunner and Ates, 2019
;
Retva, 2024
)
. Diversity also includes representativeness and adaptation to minorities.

Diversity
in
AI
Openness:
The adaptability of open models enables to address a broader diversity of needs, thereby making
AI
accessible to marginalized populations. For instance, large language models do not support or perform well with low-resource languages
(
Blasi et al., 2021
)
, but the customization potential of open models is promising for bridging this gap
(
Pipatanakul et al., 2023
;
Jayakody and Dias, 2024
;
Chan et al., 2023
)
. However, like fairness, diversity in
AI
openness is considered more as a consequence of openness than a foundational principle.

4.3.3.
Democratization

Inclusion not only includes being fair and ensuring diversity, it also refers to democratizing and redistributing power, knowledge, or resources across the population. For example,
open access

(
van Leeuwen et al., 2015
;
Koutras, 2020
)
,
massive open online course

(
Sukirman et al., 2022
)
, and
urban open space

(
Li et al., 2011
;
Erkang et al., 2020
)
all refer to access that is public, available to all individuals without distinctions, unlike
open innovation
, which can be limited to only a few companies
(
Dąbrowska et al., 2013
;
Liu et al., 2018
)
. But public access is often not enough to democratize or redistribute power, the empowerment and democracy may also be necessary. For example,
open education
refers not only to public education but also to democratic education, emphasizing the importance of redistributing power to individuals in addition to public access
(
Demo et al., 2021
)
. Another example is
open science
, which aims to democratize knowledge, empower end users by allowing them to be producers of ideas, and make science truly accessible to everyone
(
Leonelli, 2023
;
Bertram et al., 2023
)
.

Democratization
in
AI
Openness:
While some previous studies refer to models as “open model” when their weights are “widely available,” with some potential exceptions (e.g. age limit)
(
Kapoor et al., 2024
)
, others define an open model as publicly available, with no restrictions on who can access to the components
(
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Initiative, 2024
)
. However, public access does not guarantee democratization or the redistribution of power. The group of stakeholders most empowered by openness in AI are developers, particularly those creating downstream applications with powerful models
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
)
.
But, while some argue that openness reduces monopolies by enabling small companies or groups of developers to reuse models they would not have the resources to develop on their own
(
Kapoor et al., 2024
;
Ope, [n. d.]
;
Bommasani et al., 2024
)
, others counter that openness empowers large companies by making downstream applications dependent on their models and increase their competitive advantage
(
Widder et al., 2024
;
Osborne, 2024
)
.

5.
Discussion

Our taxonomy provides a systematic lens through which openness could be more broadly understood for the AI community.
We find that openness in AI emphasizes
access
,
inspectability
,
reuse
,
organic
and
democratization
but does not represent some other possible notions of openness, such as
non-isolation
,
autonomy
,
fairness
, and
diversity
, as other concepts do.
Many of these openness sub-themes are interconnected, just like how
open strategy
– the involvement of external individuals in strategy development (i.e., collaboration) – enables the diversification of perspectives (i.e., diversity), the redistribution of power (i.e., democratization), and relies on the disclosure of information that is usually private (i.e., inspection)
(
Sunner and Ates, 2019
)
.
Similar connections between the themes can be made in AI: grouping together a wide range of practices and principles under the concept of AI openness can enable a more holistic discussion that facilitates the discovery of links and relationships between the themes.

However, some of the themes addressed in the taxonomy may be undesirable when applied to AI. For example, the sub-theme “organic” in our taxonomy, when applied to AI, would refer to a reduction in governance and the removal of potential regulations, including those on the downstream uses of AI models. However, the absence of regulation can lead to irreversible risks, and it is reasonable to seek to prevent these risks through restrictions on possible uses, as exemplified by Responsible AI Licenses
(
McDuff et al., 2024
;
Contractor et al., 2022
)
. Given the positive connotation of openness supported by many governments and industry stakeholders, it is crucial to shape this notion so that it aligns with the ethical standards. The definition of AI openness can shape regulation, shift legal liability, encourage some AI development practices at the expense of others. The framework used to define and assess AI openness therefore plays a crucial role in preventing irreversible risks, and attenuate or demand certain notions of openness in AI over others.
It can also be used to tangibly shift the term “openness” in AI from a marketing ploy to ethical guarantees.

5.1.
Properties and Desired Effect

In the
AI
community today, “open” and “open source” primarily refer to the actions afforded by the
AI
system (e.g. being able to access, use, share, modify, study).
Both the definition of Open Source AI from
OSI

(
Initiative, 2024
)
and the Model Openness Framework
(
White et al., 2024
)
are examples of this; they define AI openness in terms of who can perform these actions (e.g. anyone), how they can be performed (e.g. without any fees or the need for permission), and on what part of the AI system (e.g. model weights, inference code, training code). Defining openness solely through
afforded actions
presents two limitations. First, these actions can be unattainable ideals. For example,
even with detailed documentation and access to all system components, a large portion of the population would still be unable to properly study an AI system, so this
afforded action
is difficult to achieve for everyone. In contrast, clear properties such as “being accompanied by a model card” are objectively verifiable and implementable. Therefore, defining openness through the
properties
of the AI system could be valuable in establishing efforts toward common practices, such as implementing standardized interfaces or providing modular components for AI development.

The second limitation of defining openness through
afforded actions
is that their ethical impacts might not be achieved. Impacts such as democratization of AI development
(
Eiras et al., 2024a
;
Osborne, 2024
)
and transparency of AI model
(
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, 2024
;
Widder et al., 2024
;
Kapoor et al., 2024
)
are presented as consequences of openness in previous work but might be at stake depending on the context of how openness is realized. For instance, being able to inspect a model does not always guarantee auditability
(
Widder et al., 2023
)
. On the contrary, if openness in AI is defined by the
desired effect
, e.g., being audited, it could create direct incentives for these audits. We therefore recommend using all three approaches when defining openness in AI (
properties
,
afforded actions
and
desired effect
) to clearly specify the actions, the goal of the actions, and the properties that enable those actions.

5.2.
Openness at Different Scales

Openness
as an overarching notion can be applied to different scales within a single field. For example, in education,
open educational resources

(
Wiley et al., 2014
;
Condruz-Bacescu, 2019
)
,
open university

(
OKUR, 2019
;
Agbu et al., 2016
)
, and
open education

(
Cronin and MacLaren, 2018
;
Blyth, 2013
)
respectively refer to the openness of resources, universities, and the field of education as a whole. Moreover, concepts at different level can interact and contribute to each other, helping to construct a comprehensive understanding of openness. For example,
open education
includes the use of
open educational resources
along with other principles applicable at the scale of the education field. This hierarchy within the concepts allows for compatibility between them, which can then simplify corresponding regulations and policies. It also highlights the missing parts needed to generalize an effect from one scale to another: for example, the accessibility of educational resources cannot be generalized to the accessibility of education, which faces additional challenges such as providing education to students that lack of free time to study, independently of access to educational resources
(
Cronin and MacLaren, 2018
)
.

While discussions in
AI
largely focus on the openness of
AI
models or systems, distinct openness concepts could be used to address the openness at the level of
AI
organizations,
AI
development processes,
AI
governance models, or the
AI
field itself. Topics like democratization
(
Seger et al., 2023
)
and participation
(
Maas and Inglés, 2024
)
have already been discussed in different contexts. Connecting them with AI openness at the levels beyond the model could facilitate a holistic view of their impact. Similarly to how accessibility of open educational resources does not extend to accessibility of education, it would also be relevant to measure to what extent the openness of AI systems allows the field itself to open up, or whether other properties need to be enforced at the field level to meet such an objective.

5.3.
Limitations

The methodology we applied to identify openness concepts in the literature through topic modeling does not produce an exhaustive list of concepts related to openness; therefore, some notions of openness may have been overlooked in our taxonomy. This may be the case for openness concepts that are under-represented in the academic literature, concepts with a highly heterogeneous associated vocabulary, or those that are primarily discussed in languages other than English. Despite these limitations, the concepts collected in this study represent a wide range of openness notions – sufficient for the results of our analysis to provide meaningful insights on openness in
AI
.

6.
Conclusion

This study contributes to the ongoing discourse on AI openness by broadening its scope through interdisciplinary perspectives. Our taxonomy, developed from a qualitative analysis of diverse openness concepts, highlights the relevance of the transdisciplinary notion of openness to AI beyond traditional open source paradigms. By emphasizing the themes of
interactivity
,
freedom
, and
inclusiveness
and describing different approaches to define openness, our taxonomy provides a framework to re-evaluate principles and practices of openness in AI. We encourage future work to address underexplored themes of AI openness such as
collaboration
,
non-isolation
and
diversity
, and enrich AI openness at all scales, ranging from defining concrete
properties
of open models to discussing
desired effects
of openness at the scale of the AI field.

Appendix A
Appendix: Details of the Topic Model

Here we provide further details on the evaluation methods, hyperparameters and results of the topic model used to extract the openness concepts. A complete list of topics and associated openness concepts is provided in Section
A.3
.

A.1.
Evaluation

We computed the stability of each topic to identify topics that failed to appear in other seeds, thereby improving the reproducibility and robustness of our results. The stability score computed is based on the cosine similarity of the terms-topics distances with the topics of 5 other seeds and matching topics using a recurrent approach (one-to-many)
(
Hosseiny Marani and Baumer, 2024
)
. We chose this method to reduce impact on the stability score of topics splitting or merging across seeds and to only detect topics that failed to appear in other seeds.

We also computed several coherence measures based on the work of
Röder et al. (2015)
. The coherence and stability measures allowed us to narrow down the range of hyperparameter values, and the quality of the topics was then manually evaluated at each iteration to select a number of topics relevant to our analysis.
