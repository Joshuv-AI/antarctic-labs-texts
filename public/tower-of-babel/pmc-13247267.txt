Low-cost Kirigami-inspired deployable dual-polarized MIMO antenna with angular pattern diversity
Authors: Sayed Sabir Shah, Slawomir Koziel, Shahid Bashir, Raza Ullah, Sadiq Ullah, Aadel Mohammed Alatwi, Syed Imran Hussain Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents a low-cost, easy-to-fabricate, kirigami-inspired deployable MIMO antenna operating at 2 GHz, featuring dual polarization and angular pattern diversity. Inspired by the Yagi antenna design, the proposed MIMO antenna comprises two orthogonally oriented antennas, each consisting of a rectangular radiating element (monopole) and one parasitic rectangular strip (director). These are integrated onto a foldable, staircase-shaped Kirigami structure made of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) sheet. Each antenna includes a reflector attached to the Kirigami structure’s back. Operating at 2 GHz, the antenna achieves a measured peak gain of 9 dBi with a -10 dB impedance bandwidth of 630 MHz (1.82 GHz to 2.45 GHz). Mutual coupling between the radiating elements is maintained below -15 dB, and the total efficiency exceeds 80% across the operating band. The antenna system meets MIMO specifications, demonstrating an Envelope Correlation Coefficient (ECC) below 0.0025 and a Channel Capacity Loss (CCL) below 0.35 bps/Hz. This combination of high performance, low cost, and ease of fabrication makes the proposed design a promising candidate for diverse microwave applications.Keywords: Kirigami antennas, Deployable antenna, Yagi antenna, Foldable antennaSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Deployability in satellite communication systems refers to the capability of an antenna structure to expand from a compact, stowed configuration to its full operational form. This attribute is particularly critical for applications requiring rapid mobility, space efficiency, and ease of deployment. In satellite missions, antennas must be sufficiently compact to fit within the constrained volume of launch vehicles, yet capable of expanding to their full dimensions once in orbit1. Moreover, many modern missions demand antennas that can operate over multiple frequency bands to support diverse communication functions2. These dual requirements such as compact stowage and broadband operation have motivated extensive research into innovative deployable antenna systems. Among the commonly explored configurations, planar, reflector, and wire antennas remain popular choices3–5. Planar antennas, including patch and slot types, have attracted attention in Pico and nanosatellite systems due to their low profile and ease of fabrication; however, their inherently narrow bandwidth remains a limitation6,7. Reflector antennas offer higher gain but often involve complex mechanical deployment mechanisms and limited bandwidth8. These challenges become even more pronounced at lower frequencies such as HF and UHF, where antennas grow physically larger, complicating integration and transportation3. Consequently, deployable antenna concepts have emerged as viable solutions, offering compact stowage during launch and expansion to full size post-deployment, thereby minimizing cost and volume constraints9.

Origami-inspired mechanisms have introduced a promising paradigm for realizing deployable antennas, owing to their lightweight, foldable, and easily reconfigurable structures10. In particular, for military and remote-area communication systems, origami-based antennas are highly advantageous due to their portability, rapid deployability, and minimal logistical requirements11. Conventional field antennas often require specialized assembly tools or rigid support structures, limiting their usability in harsh or time-sensitive environments10. In contrast, origami antennas can be fabricated from flexible materials using simple folding patterns, enabling rapid setup in remote or emergency scenarios without the need for complex infrastructure10,12. Extending the origami concept, kirigami introduces deliberate cuts into the structure, enhancing flexibility and reconfigurability12–14. Kirigami, by integrating paper-cutting techniques into origami methodologies, retains the benefits of origami while introducing greater adaptability and the capacity to realize more diverse designs13–15. To create intricate three-dimensional (3D) structures that use both compression and tension, kirigami deliberately makes cuts in planar sheets prior to folding, in contrast to traditional origami, which mostly depends on folding. Through the careful design of positional-dependent incisions in certain patterns on flexible substrates, engineers can create precisely customized structures and greatly improve reconfigurability through bending, twisting, and stretching. Recent studies have demonstrated the versatility of kirigami for foldable, stretchable, and reconfigurable electronic systems, expanding their potential for field-deployable communication platforms10,12,13,16.

Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (MIMO) technology introduces an essential advancement in antenna design by utilizing multiple transmitting and receiving elements to achieve higher data rates, improved channel capacity, and enhanced spectral efficiency compared to conventional Single-Input Single-Output (SISO) configurations15–17. Over the past decade, MIMO techniques have been extensively implemented across various wireless communication standards, including 5G, LTE, UWB, and WLAN, owing to their ability to support massive connectivity and mitigate multipath fading effects18–25. Despite these significant developments, the integration of MIMO principles within structurally reconfigurable and deployable antenna platforms, particularly those based on origami and kirigami mechanisms has received limited attention in the literature. To date, only a few studies have reported kirigami-inspired MIMO antenna prototypes26,27. While most origami/kirigami-based antennas remain limited to SISO designs10. Such configurations inherently restrict data throughput and adaptability in complex field environments, underscoring the need for MIMO-enabled deployable architectures.

To address the mentioned limitations, the present work proposes a foldable and deployable MIMO antenna that combines the structural adaptability of kirigami with the high spectral efficiency of MIMO. The design aims to support robust and high-capacity communication links in remote, mobile, and military field environments, where compactness, rapid deployment, and reliable performance are of paramount importance. The proposed configuration employs two rectangular monopole radiators with directors, integrated on a PET substrate with PCB reflectors. It resonates at 2.0 GHz, offering a measured gain of 9 dBi and a − 10 dB impedance bandwidth of 630 MHz. Parametric analysis confirms optimal monopole spacing with mutual coupling maintained below − 15 dB, ensuring stable and efficient operation under field-deployable conditions. Furthermore, comparative studies demonstrate superiority of the proposed design to a range of state-of-the-art antennas reported in the recent literature. The novelty of this work lies in the integration of a kirigami-inspired deployable architecture with a dual-polarized, two-element MIMO configuration exhibiting angular pattern diversity, implemented on a low-cost flexible PET substrate.

Kirigami geometry design and antenna fabrication

A.Kirigami Geometry:

Kirigami Geometry:

The kirigami geometry depicted in Fig. 1 was designed to enable a compact stowed configuration and a stable three-dimensional deployed state, while maintaining good electromagnetic performance. The staircase-shaped kirigami pattern was selected because it provides discrete elevation levels, allowing precise control of the spacing between the radiating elements and the reflector.

Proposed Kirigami antenna with key dimensions such as Lr = 145 mm, Wr = 145 mm, Ld = 30 mm, Wd = 10 mm, Lm = 30 mm, Wm = 10 mm.

Strategic vertical cuts enable out-of-plane deformation, while the horizontal fold lines define the stair heights, providing precise control over the elevation of each step. This staircase configuration also allows controlled placement of the two monopole radiators at specific levels of the kirigami structure. In the optimized design, the two monopoles are positioned symmetrically on the third staircase level, providing appropriate electrical spacing from the reflector and ensuring stable mechanical deployment. Consequently, the kirigami geometry not only enables deployability but also supports the spatial arrangement of the monopoles and directors, contributing to improved radiation characteristics, polarization diversity, and reduced mutual coupling between the antenna elements.

The monopole radiators were positioned to achieve an optimal trade-off among isolation, polarization diversity, and deployability. As the proposed structure is symmetric, the director associated with antenna-2 (monopole-2) is fixed on the fourth staircase level on the right side, while antenna-2 is located on the third staircase. In contrast, the position of antenna-1 (monopole-1), starting from the first step on the left side together with its director, was varied along the horizontal direction to determine the optimal configuration. The resulting three configurations, denoted as cases a, b, and c, along with their corresponding S-parameters and radiation patterns, are illustrated in Fig. 2.

S-parameters and 3D radiation pattern for Ant-1 and Ant-2 for the three cases: (a), (b) and (c).

For case a, the resonant frequency of antenna-1 shifts upward to 2.2 GHz and is accompanied by degradation in S21 and the radiation pattern, as shown in Fig. 2(a). In case b, the resonant frequencies and radiation pattern of antenna-1 become distorted, although S21 improves within the desired band, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b).

In contrast, case c exhibits improved S-parameter performance along with stable gain and radiation characteristics, as shown in Fig. 2(c). However, in this configuration, the isolation between the two antennas S21 is degraded. So, for optimal trade-off between S-parameters, gain along with stable radiation we choose the optimized design shown in Fig. 1. In the optimized designed the two monopole radiators were placed in a way that provided the best trade-off regarding isolation, polarization diversity and deployability. The two monopoles are positioned symmetrically on the third level of the staircase of the kirigami structure to maintain the mechanical stability and uniform deformation throughout the deployment. Their orthogonal format allows dual polarization and low correlation of patterns, and their opposite layout reduces the interaction of surface currents, which enhances isolation without further decoupling networks. The 36 mm spacing between each monopole and the corresponding reflector was optimized to maximize forward radiation and gain and ensure that mutual coupling is less than − 15 dB which was verified by parametric analysis.

B.Antenna Fabrication:

Antenna Fabrication:

The Kirigami geometry is implemented on a PET sheet with a dielectric constant of 3 and a loss tangent (of 0.02. The Kirigami-based pop-up geometry shown in Fig. 1 ensures that the antenna is radially deployable. The complete and step-by-step procedure of the proposed design is shown in Fig. 3. The proposed Kirigami-based MIMO antenna consists of two radiating elements (monopoles), with a parasitic element (director), in front of each monopole. Two reflectors made of copper sheets are attached at backside of Kirigami geometry. The metallic reflectors positioned behind each monopole serve to suppress backward radiation, enhance forward gain, and improve front-to-back ratio. In addition, the reflectors contribute to reduced mutual coupling by limiting surface wave propagation between the radiating elements. The orientation of the two antennas is orthogonal; one is horizontal while the other one is vertical.

The design process begins with the preparation of a sketch containing the required dimensions, was drawn on paper as shown in Fig. 3(a). A PET sheet of 145 mm × 145 mm × 0.25 mm was then placed over the paper sketch. The PET sheet was carefully aligned with the central reference lines as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). The red vertical solid lines which are shown in Fig. 3(c) with yellow dotted circles BBˊ were cut using a standard cutter. While the green horizontal solid line highlighted by black dotted circles CCˊ and red horizontal solid line which is encircled by blacked dotted circles DDˊ which are indented with a screwdriver and marker shown in Fig. 3(d) are bends inward (red lines) and outward (green lines), after which the paper was removed, yielding the staircase-shaped PET sheet which is shown in Fig. 3(e). After this a copper strip with required dimensions which as shown in Fig. 3(f) are pasted on PET sheet. The copper strips which comprise of two monopoles and two directors with required dimensions, shown in Fig. 1, are pasted at two different locations on PET sheet. The two monopoles along with directors on PET sheet can be seen in Fig. 3(g) and Fig. 3(h) respectively. Each monopole was fed using a 50-Ω SMA connector, with the inner conductor soldered to the monopole and the outer conductor connected to the ground/reflector. The inner conductor (pin) of SMA connector soldered with monopole can be seen in Fig. 3(i) while the outward structure of SMA connected with ground/reflector can be seen in Fig. 3(j). The final assembled design, comprising two reflectors, two monopoles, and directors, is shown in Fig. 3(k). The antenna system was modeled and simulated using CST Microwave Studio, a commercial full-wave electromagnetic solver.

Parametric analysis

A parametric study was performed at a resonance frequency of 2.0 GHz to optimize the radiation performance of the antenna, specifically gain, beamwidth, and mutual coupling. The number of parasitic directors, the monopole length Lm, and the distance SL between the monopole and reflector were the main parameters investigated. The proposed antenna incorporates two rectangular monopole resonators placed symmetrically on the third staircase level, positioned opposite each other and located 36 mm away from the reflector, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This configuration yields an optimal bandwidth of 630 MHz with mutual coupling below − 15 dB within the resonance band. A systematic investigation of the design parameters revealed that increasing the distance between the resonating monopoles further reduces mutual coupling. However, this adjustment also shifts the resonance frequency and reduces gain. Conversely, decreasing the spacing by moving the monopoles closer to the reflectors further reduced coupling but also diminished gain and altered the resonance frequency. The optimal monopole length of 30 mm was determined to provide a balance of high gain, wide bandwidth, and minimized mutual coupling. Deviations from this length shift the resonance frequency, with longer monopoles exhibiting lower coupling than shorter ones. Even though the suggested design already exhibits a large beamwidth compared to conventional Yagi antennas, the gain was not appreciably increased by adding a second director. Therefore, a single director was adopted to achieve a peak azimuthal measured gain exceeding 9 dBi. The width of the monopole (set at 10 mm) was found to have negligible impact on antenna performance.

Figure 4(a) shows the parametric analysis of monopole length Lm in the range from 25 mm to 45 mm. We choose a value of Lm = 30 mm due to good matching and an optimal bandwidth. Figure 4(b) illustrates the relationship between the antenna gain and the number of directors. As the number of directors increases to one, the gain improves due to enhanced directivity and constructive field combination, and it remains relatively stable up to two directors. However, when the number of directors reaches three, mutual coupling effects become dominant, leading to a degradation in gain performance. Figure 4(c) depicts the variation of SL and its effect on the S-parameters while the monopole length is kept constant. Figure 4(d) depicts gain and S21 variation against the stair’s height with discrete values such as 12 mm, 17 mm, 22 mm and 27 mm. When the stairs height increases from 12 mm to 17 mm the gain increases above 9 dB while S21 decreases further touching the value of − 22.5 dB. When the stairs height changes to 22 mm the gain drops and the S21 decreases further till the value reverses and increases while the gain value is lowest. This optimized design offers an optimal beamwidth compared to traditional Yagi antennas.

Additionally, to provide angular pattern diversity, low ECC (< 0.0025), and isolation below − 15 dB, the proposed strategy uses a two-element architecture with optimum inter-element spacing. In contrast to Ref14., the proposed antenna is fabricated on a low-cost PET substrate instead of a flexible PCB, reducing material costs and simplifying manufacturing. Furthermore, the kirigami pop-up structure enables a simpler deployment mechanism without additional structural support, further easing fabrication and deployment. The multiband 3D MIMO antenna in18 demonstrated excellent isolation (–30 dB) and included comprehensive MIMO metrics such as ECC (< 0.18) and CCL (< 0.35 bps/Hz). However, its structural complexity, additional FSS layers, and rigid construction limited its suitability for portable or foldable applications. The reconfigurable helix antenna using shape memory alloys in31 offered mechanical reconfigurability with moderate bandwidths (0.45/1.60 GHz), but only reported pattern correlation coefficients rather than ECC/CCL. In addition, its low gain (2.78 dBi), mechanical actuation requirements, and paper substrate reduced robustness. An origami-inspired, foldable MIMO antenna in32 covers a wideband range from 8.7 to 12 GHz. It features a 2-element MIMO setup that is physically reconfigurable. The antenna can transition between three distinct physical states and achieves a stable gain of 4 to 6.2 dBi and high radiation efficiency (> 90%). However, it uses 3D-printed dielectric resonators, which adds significant profile height (thickness) and weight to the antenna. Also, 3D printing dielectric resonators with specific permittivity can be expensive or difficult to mass-produce compared to standard PCB/substract processes. In33 a flexible 2-element MIMO patch antenna for 2.45 GHz applications was presented. While their work demonstrates excellent ECC and flexibility on Kapton substrates, however it is a standard flexible antenna. It can bend, but it does not change its fundamental physical shape or “unfold” to improve gain or change beam direction. Similarly, in34 an origami-inspired pattern reconfigurable antennas based on magic spiral cubes is presented but it is single element and lack polarization diversity.

In contrast, the proposed PET-based two-element Kirigami MIMO antenna delivers high measured gain (9.05 dBi) with optimal impedance bandwidth (630 MHz) at 2.0 GHz using a lightweight, low-cost, and foldable PET substrate. Although its bandwidth is narrower than14, it provides a more effective balance of gain, bandwidth, and simplicity, while avoiding the complexity of18 and31. Importantly, it offers excellent MIMO-specific performance, with ECC consistently below 0.0025, total efficiency above 80% (peaking near 95%), and CCL under 0.35 bps/Hz, values comparable to or better than those in14] and [18. The orthogonal orientation and spatial separation of the two monopole elements result in angular pattern diversity, providing distinct radiation patterns for Ant-1 and Ant-2. This arrangement facilitates dual linear polarization, with each element emitting an orthogonal polarization state. The measured radiation parameters confirm that such polarization and pattern variety lead to a lower envelope correlation coefficient (ECC) and improved MIMO performance. These results demonstrate not only robust diversity performance but also the advantage of achieving it with just a two-element configuration instead of more costly four-element array. Overall, the proposed design distinguishes itself by combining foldable simplicity, low cost, and reliable MIMO diversity, making it a strong candidate for modern deployable communication systems.

Conclusion

This work presented a novel, low-cost, deployable dual-polarization MIMO antenna based on a Kirigami pop-up geometry. The proposed design, realized on a PET substrate with monopoles, directors, and a reflector, demonstrated an optimal impedance bandwidth of 630 MHz and a peak gain of 9.05 dBi at 2.0 GHz. In addition to its structural simplicity and reconfigurability, the antenna exhibits excellent MIMO performance, with an ECC below 0.0025 and a CCL below 0.35 bps/Hz across the operating band. The achieved combination of high gain, compact deployable form, and robust diversity characteristics, surpasses the capabilities of the state-of-the-art Kirigami-based antenna systems reported in the literature. Although this study primarily focuses on electromagnetic performance, the antenna architecture is also well suited for satellite and field-deployable missions owing to its lightweight PET substrate, compact stowed configuration, and passive deployment mechanism. These attributes ensure compatibility with launch volume constraints and rapid deployment requirements. Consequently, the proposed antenna is a strong candidate for field-deployable military communication systems, rapidly deployable emergency and disaster-relief networks, and portable microwave communication platforms that demand compact stowage, high gain, and reliable MIMO performance.
