A comparison of dual vs. single diagnostic code approaches for neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome surveillance in neonates ≥ 35 weeks gestational age
Authors: Janine Y. Khan, Hannah L. Neuman, James M. Groh, Marina G. Feffer, Phoebe E. Troeller, Chariya Christmon, Keith A. Dookeran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUsing dual ICD-10-CM codes for exposure/withdrawal (P04.49 or P96.1, respectively) may be more sensitive for identifying Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) than P96.1 alone. There are currently no studies that report on national prevalence using this dual code approach.ObjectivesWe examined U.S. national NOWS prevalence trends comparing the dual vs. single code approach for surveillance.Methods2016–2022 cycles of cross-sectional data from the U.S. Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, Kids’ Inpatient Database were used to estimate weighted NOWS prevalence in newborns ≥ 35 weeks excluding iatrogenic cases. Generalized linear models with predictive margins were used to estimate prevalence differences for select sociodemographic factors stratified by race/ethnicity. Select measures of morbidity and mortality were evaluated as secondary outcome measures for NOWS.ResultsThe survey weighted population was 1,084,633 pediatric discharges and crude NOWS prevalence decreased across 2016–2022 cycles. Using dual codes, crude NOWS prevalence doubled overall at 19.05/1000 (95% CI: 18.33, 19.8) (vs. single: 9.48; 95% CI: 9.05, 9.93) and across most factors and was highest in White infants (dual: 25.15; 95% CI: 24.09, 26.26, single: 13.98; 95% CI: 13.27, 14.71). Crude prevalence for Black infants almost tripled (dual: 19.67; 95% CI: 18.46, 20.95, single: 6.19; 95% CI: 5.65, 6.78). Medicaid payer-type (dual: 35.93; 95% CI: 34.6, 37.3, single: 18.12; 95% CI: 17.29, 18.98), lowest ecologic income-quartile (dual: 29.43; 95% CI: 28.12, 30.79, single: 13.72; 95% CI: 12.92, 14.58), and rural hospital-location (dual: 33.22; 95% CI: 30.75, 35.88, single: 13.88; 95% CI: 12.31, 15.66) were factors with the highest crude prevalence. Trends for relative measures for within category comparison of crude prevalence were largely similar but showed greater magnitude of change for survey year and income quartile. In fully adjusted models, effect modification was evident by year, payer type, and income level, for all race/ethnicity comparisons with dual codes, and short-term morbidity risk appeared higher with dual codes (15.2% vs. single 6.2%) whereas length of stay was greater with single code (7.0 vs. 11.7 days).ConclusionsThe dual code approach provides an alternative strategy for NOWS surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07015-x.

Background

Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS), previously referred to as Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS), is a drug withdrawal syndrome that occurs after birth in neonates exposed to opioids in-utero [1, 2]. It is an increasing public health problem throughout the U.S. [3, 4]. Coincident with a reported substantial increase in opioid use disorder (OUD) in women of childbearing age, the prevalence of NOWS has grown over the past two decades [5–7]. The increase in OUD, arising from prescribed opioids for short- and long-term pain management, and recreational opioid and polysubstance use among women of reproductive age and by extension during pregnancy [8, 9], is highlighted by the 2019 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) survey findings that 6.6% of pregnant women have used opioids prescribed during pregnancy, and of these, 21% reported subsequent drug misuse [10]. This exacerbates the healthcare burden due to adverse health outcomes, excessive health care costs, and sociodemographic disparities nationwide [11–15].

Adequately addressing the NOWS crisis is contingent on accurate identification of its prevalence. Our prior study, utilizing 2016 and 2019 data cycles from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), Kids’ Inpatient Database (KID) [11], reported weighted sample prevalence of U.S. NAS using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code P96.1 (specifically defined as neonatal withdrawal symptoms from maternal use of drugs of addiction) only for newborns ≥ 35 weeks gestational age (GA), with overall rates per 1000 population of 9.84 for combined cycles, and 14.72 in non-Hispanic White (White) compared to 6.02 in non-Hispanic Black (Black) and 4.3 in Hispanic infants [16]. This study highlighted the importance of sociodemographic disparities and reported that although Black and Hispanic infants were more likely to be in the lowest income quartile and have Medicaid (public) insurance, White infants on Medicaid, and in the lowest income quartile, were found to have the highest NAS prevalence. Similarly, Goldberg et al. recently reported on NOWS (identified with P96.1 only) using the 2018 New York State Inpatient Database, and their findings align with our prior study with higher rates among White infants, residents of rural areas, lower income ZIP codes, and those with public insurance [17].

A report from the CDC, however, demonstrated that using dual codes for exposure or withdrawal [i.e., exposure code P04.49 (specifically defined as newborn affected by maternal use of other drugs of addiction) or withdrawal code P96.1] might be more sensitive for identifying NOWS than using P96.1 alone, and in an exposure scenario for opioids only, sensitivity and specificity of P04.49 or P96.1 vs. P96.1 only for clinically confirmed NAS diagnosis was 92.1%/99.2% vs. 80.2%/99.9%, respectively [18]. This dual code approach may be applied as an alternate NOWS surveillance strategy, but currently, there are no studies that report on national prevalence using this approach. Higher positive predictive value (PPV) for P96.1 alone also suggests greater burden of short-term neonatal morbidity compared with the dual code approach; however, this also remains to be determined.

The opioid crisis worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic as prescription opioid death rates grew about 10% from 2019 through 2021, with the largest increase being in adults aged 25–34 [19], which coincides with the age range at which most women give birth in the U.S. [20]. A study from Wisconsin using code P96.1 to measure NAS found that statewide NAS rates did not increase from 2019–2022 [21]. However, the impact of COVID-19 on nationwide NOWS prevalence remains unclear.

Differences in NAS and maternal opioid use have also been identified for rural versus urban communities. Villapiano et al. analyzed 2004–2013 HCUP, Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) data and found that compared with their urban peers, rural infants and mothers with opioid-related diagnoses were more likely to be from lower-income families, have public insurance, be transferred to another hospital following delivery, and that the proportion of infants diagnosed with NAS who were from rural counties increased from 12.9% in 2003–2004 to 21.2% in 2012–2013 [22]. Few studies, however, have examined recent trends in rural communities.

Our study uses recently available 2016–2022 HCUP KID and NIS data to examine several current issues in public health related to NOWS/NAS surveillance. We examine temporal trends in U.S. NOWS prevalence, and associated race/ethnicity (R/E) and sociodemographic disparities (including rural versus urban settings), comparing estimates based on single (P96.1) vs. dual (P96.1 or P04.49) diagnostic code approaches. We further examine whether sociodemographic factors modify NOWS risk, and how temporal trends vary in relation to the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, we describe morbidity and mortality patterns for both code approaches and interpret how these might support surveillance activity.

Methods

Study design

We utilized cross-sectional ICD-10-CM coded data from the HCUP KID database 2016, 2019, and 2022 cycles for primary analyses. The KID is the largest publicly available all-payer pediatric inpatient care database in the U.S. and weighted, it estimates roughly 6 million hospitalizations each year, and sample size is fixed. The target universe for KID is U.S. community, non-rehabilitation hospitals with pediatric discharges [11]. The KID database has been previously used to evaluate newborn hospitalizations among maternity care hospitalizations [23, 24].

Variable definitions

NOWS prevalence in newborns ≥ 35 weeks GA was examined using single (P96.1 only) vs. dual code diagnostic criteria (P96.1 or P04.49) as primary outcomes [18]. Iatrogenic cases (P96.2) were excluded. In addition, we chose to use the ≥ 35 weeks GA restriction criteria as infants less that this GA are routinely admitted to the NICU and commonly exposed to opioid analgesia/sedation as part of therapeutic intervention but may not be fully captured by code P96.2. The variable I10_HOSPBIRTH was used to limit to in-hospital births [25]. Additional covariate selection related to R/E (i.e., Black, White, and Hispanic infants), and hospital and sociodemographic factors was as previously described [16]. The variable HOSP_LOCTEACH (Location/teaching status of hospital) was used to create a binary rural vs. urban hospital location variable. The All Patient Refined Diagnosis Related Groups (APR-DRGs) is an enhanced version of the standard DRG classification system, designed to better capture clinical severity [25]. Select measures of morbidity and mortality were evaluated as secondary outcome measures for NOWS status comparing code approaches and included APR-DRG based severity of illness and risk of mortality subclasses (respectively, loss of function and likelihood of dying: > moderate vs. ≤ moderate); death during hospitalization (yes/no), and median length of stay as a measure of hospitalizations (days).

Statistical analysis

Covariates were found to have < 1% missing data and complete case analysis was used. Multivariable generalized linear models with marginal standardization (predictive margins) were used to estimate associations with NOWS outcomes reported as risk differences (RDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Weighted NOWS prevalence is reported as cases per 1000 newborn hospitalizations for code comparison. Interaction terms between R/E and all other covariates were included in fully specified models to facilitate production of complex stratified estimates. Final models for overall predicted mean risk of NOWS were adjusted for cycle/year, sex, insurance payer type, ecologic quartile of the estimated median household income, and hospital size and type by teaching status and geographic region. All analyses were conducted using Stata statistical software, version 19 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX), accounted for complex survey design, and utilized a common primary sampling unit survey weight variable across cycles.

Sensitivity analysis

Annual HCUP NIS data from 2016–2022 was used for sensitivity analysis to show more detailed yearly trends across the pandemic period. The NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer U.S. inpatient healthcare database and weighted, it estimates around 35 million hospitalizations nationally each year. The NIS approximates a 20% stratified sample of all discharges from U.S. community hospitals, and sample size is fixed [26]. Sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding was performed using the methodology proposed by VanderWeele and Ding [27].

Ethics approval

The University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed our study protocol and determined that it did not meet the definition of human subject research, and as such, IRB review and approval was not required.

Results

Crude prevalence trends

The survey weighted population for the KID was 1,084,633 pediatric discharges for 2016–2022. Table 1 shows the weighted national prevalence of NOWS comparing single vs. dual codes. With dual codes, NOWS prevalence more than doubled overall (dual RD 19.05; 95% CI: 18.33, 19.80 vs. single RD 9.48; 95% CI: 9.05, 9.93). NOWS prevalence rates were found to decrease significantly over time for both approaches [respectively: 2016, 2019, 2022 – dual: 25.55, 17.67, 14.30 (p-for-trend < 0.0001); single: 10.11, 9.47, 8.91 (p-for-trend = 0.026)]. Overall, the dual code approach consistently amplified absolute differences across groups, indicating greater discrimination of disparities in absolute terms. However, relative measures for within category comparison were largely similar and only showed a greater magnitude of change using the dual code approach with trends for survey year and ecologic median household income quartiles.

Stratified estimates

NOWS prevalence was almost doubled for dual vs. single codes across most sociodemographic factors and continued to be higher among White infants (dual RD 25.15; 95% CI: 24.09, 26.26 vs. single RD 13.98; 95% CI: 13.27, 14.71). NOWS prevalence for Black infants almost tripled (dual RD 19.67; 95% CI: 18.46, 20.95 vs. single RD 6.19; 95% CI: 5.65, 6.78). NOWS prevalence also increased for Hispanic infants (dual RD 10.27; 95% CI: 9.44, 11.16 vs. single RD 4.47; 95% CI: 3.95, 5.06). Overall, Medicaid payer type (RD 35.93; 95% CI: 34.60, 37.30), lowest income-quartile (RD 29.43; 95% CI: 28.12, 30.79), and rural hospital location (RD 33.22; 95% CI: 30.75, 35.88) had the highest observed NOWS prevalence with dual codes.

Figure 1 shows stratified estimates for select factors by R/E and comparing code approaches. Using dual codes demonstrated greater NOWS prevalence for White infants across all factors. Effect modification was evident by year, payer type, and income level, for all R/E comparisons with dual codes; White infants on Medicaid, and in the lowest income quartile, had the highest NOWS rates (RD 73.4, 95% CI: 69.45, 77.27). Using the single code approach, comparison of Black and Hispanic infants did not suggest any difference across sociodemographic factors (Fig. 1). In contrast, with dual codes NOWS prevalence appeared higher for Black compared to Hispanic infants across all factors except for rural and northeast hospital location, and year 2022. NOWS prevalence among White infants demonstrated a monotonic and dose–response pattern across ecologic median household income quartiles for both dual and single code approaches and was highest in the lowest income quartile. A similar pattern was observed for Black infants using dual codes, but not for Black infants using the single code, nor for Hispanic infants using either code, although NOWS risk was elevated for Hispanic infants in the lowest income quartile using dual codes.Fig. 1Stratified estimates for national prevalence of NOWS per 1000 birth hospitalizations in the 2016–22 Kids’ Inpatient Database by select sociodemographic factors related to race/ethnicity disparities

Pandemic trends

NOWS risk appeared to decrease over the pandemic period using the dual code approach for all R/E groups and for White infants only with the single code approach (Fig. 1). Sensitivity analysis using annual NIS data also demonstrates that crude NOWS frequency decreased across this period overall but with slight elevation occurring in 2021 for both code approaches (eTable 1; NIS survey weighted population was 2,028,434 discharges). In addition, R/E stratified estimates show that this pattern was observed for White infants only using either code approach (eFigure 1), while trends for Black and Hispanic infants across the pandemic period were either flat or diminishing.

Rural vs. urban trends

The survey weighted rural population for the KID was 72,246 pediatric discharges or 6.7%. Compared with urban, the rural population was more likely to be White (respectively, urban vs. rural: 56% vs. 78%), on Medicaid (47% vs. 60%), poorer (lowest income quartile: 24% vs. 48%), and admitted to large (57% vs. 65%) and Midwest (18% vs. 30%) hospitals (eTable 2). Crude NOWS risk was observed to be higher for the rural population with both code approaches [urban vs. rural: (single: 0.9% vs. 1.4%); (dual: 1.8% vs. 3.3%)]. NOWS trends for rural vs. urban status over time and by code approach are shown in eFigure 2. For the single code approach, the trends are largely flat and near indistinguishable with overlapping CIs. For the dual code approach, however, NOWS risk is higher overall, with rural rates exceeding urban at each time point, and diminishing trends evident for both over time. In 2022, NOWS risk was 22.2 per 1000 birth hospitalizations (95% CI: 18.5, 25.9) for dual, vs. 14.4 (95% CI: 13.4, 15.5) for the single code approach.

Morbidity and mortality related to NOWS

A comparison of select morbidity and mortality outcomes by code group in KID data is shown in Table 2. Mortality risk appeared largely similar for both single and dual code approaches. However, short-term morbidity risk appeared substantially higher for the dual code approach. NOWS risk for > moderate severity of illness subclass was 15.2% (95% CI: 13.7, 16.8) for dual vs. 6.2% (95% CI: 4.2, 8.3) for the single code approach. Median length of stay, however, was less for the dual code approach at 6.9 days (95% CI: 6.49, 7.28) vs. 11.5 days (95% CI: 10.94, 12.16) for the single code approach.

Comment

Principal findings

In both KID and NIS data, crude NOWS prevalence rates overall decreased from 2016 through 2022 for dual and single code approaches, but there was a steeper gradient evident for dual codes. Annual NIS data, however, also shows that for both code approaches, NOWS risk appeared bimodal, peaking in 2017, with a second elevation in 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic. Crude NOWS prevalence was highest for White infants, Medicaid payer type, rural location, and the lowest income quartile, and these estimates approximately doubled when using dual codes.

Regarding R/E stratified estimates using the dual code approach, diminishing trends were observed for all subgroups over the study period, and NOWS prevalence continued to be higher among White compared to Black and Hispanic infants across all sociodemographic domains, but it was also observed that NOWS prevalence among Black infants was almost three-fold higher compared to Hispanic infants. This is a substantive change from prior results using P96.1 only, which showed no difference between the latter groups [16]. Effect modification was evident by payer type and income level for all R/E comparisons with dual codes. NOWS risk among White infants using dual codes demonstrated a monotonic and dose–response pattern across ecologic median household income quartiles (i.e., increasing prevalence with decreasing household income quartile), which is similar to prior results using P96.1 only [16]. This monotonic dose–response pattern with dual codes was also evident for Black compared with Hispanic infants, and this finding has not been previously described.

Compared with urban, rural populations were more likely to be White, on Medicaid, poorer, and admitted to large and Midwest hospitals. Both code approaches show decreasing NOWS risk over time for rural and urban populations, but trends were higher for the rural group with better separation using dual codes. Lastly, analysis using the APR-DRGs morbidity and mortality classifications suggest that mortality risk appeared similar for NOWS identified with both single and dual code approaches, but short-term morbidity risk appeared higher for the dual code approach. However, analysis using LOS as a surrogate for morbidity suggests the opposite, as this was greater with the single code approach.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the dual code approach may provide greater separability of crude prevalence disparities across several R/E and sociodemographic domains in absolute but not relative measure comparisons, although for the latter, trends for survey year and income quartile were exceptions. However, effect modification patterns are clearly more evident with the dual code approach.

Strengths of the study

Study strengths include use of the largest national population-based database representative of pediatric discharges throughout a diverse range of neonatal units in the U.S. (KID). In addition, the study years examined included only ICD-10-CM codes, which reduces diagnosis speculation that may have affected earlier studies, and comprehensively included all diagnostic fields in the KID database. We also specifically excluded iatrogenic cases through use of a dedicated code. Finally, sensitivity analysis was performed in another large national population-based database with annual samples (NIS).

Limitations of the data

Limitations include the following: NOWS is considered a subset of NAS, and use of dual withdrawal or exposure codes may overestimate NOWS. However, it has been shown that polysubstance use is an increasing problem contributing to opioid toxicity and death, since the effects of stimulants, benzodiazepines and/or alcohol that prolong the high from opioid use have become a common phenomenon [28], therefore the overlap of NOWS and NAS may be closely approximated.

Furthermore, restriction to infants ≥ 35 weeks GA may result in an overestimate of NOWS prevalence [for example, the national rate of NAS per 1000 newborn hospitalizations in 2022 using code P96.1 only in NIS data from HCUP Fast Stats [29] is 5.4 vs. 11.6 for NIS and 8.9 for KID in our study], and it is important to note that this study is not designed to determine the best measures for NOWS surveillance, rather it demonstrates the real-world application of alternate surveillance strategies. Observed variation in NOWS prevalence estimates reported in this manuscript compared with national NAS estimates reported by HCUP, are likely due, in part, to differences in application of sample weights and our restriction to the subset of newborns ≥ 35 weeks GA. The definition of rural vs. urban location used in this study is limited as it is based on hospital location rather than true patient residence location.

Additional methodological limitations are similar to those outlined in a prior report [16]. The unit of analysis was hospital discharges rather than individual newborns, and although a newborn may have more than one admission for the same illness episode thus introducing a potential source of bias [30], we consider that the likelihood of duplicate admissions is infrequent in our data analysis and we specifically found no duplicates within each KID cycle. We also restricted our dataset to discharges with an indicator of birth in a hospital, further reducing the likelihood of duplicate admissions. It should be noted, however, that the latter is not available in the NIS dataset, and together with difference in weighting universes are likely causes of minimal variation observed between the NIS and KID estimates. We also acknowledge that prevalence may be underestimated when restricting to birth hospitalizations and prior research suggests that select NIS and KID estimates may differ when annual discharge counts are low.

KID and NIS discharge data are obtained from hospital discharge abstracts, and these data are subject to misclassification errors and rely on accurate coding. We acknowledge that inconsistencies in NAS definitions have been previously reported, and that we were unable to investigate the extent or effect of this issue in the KID database [31]. In addition, the KID and NIS data cycles are cross-sectional in design, and cycle combination assumes that secular data trends are minimal. Per HCUP documentation, not all HCUP partner organizations provide information on race and ethnicity, and the availability of this information may differ by year. Additionally, the reporting of information on race and ethnicity can vary by hospital which again raises concerns of misclassification errors [25]. The explanation for the higher short-term morbidity risk with the dual code approach is uncertain, particularly as LOS measure appears to suggest the opposite effect, and warrants further investigation with regards to possible occurrence of false positives. Lastly, sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding [27] indicates that for the dual code approach, the observed risk ratio of 13.76 (for effect of Public/Medicaid vs. Private insurance payer on NOWS prevalence in the fully adjusted model on the multiplicative scale) could be explained away by an unmeasured confounder that was associated with both the exposure and outcome by a risk ratio of 27-fold each, above and beyond the measured confounders, but weaker confounding could not do so.

Interpretation and implications for practice

Chiang et al. summarized surveillance definitions used across jurisdictions in the U.S. and described discordance in diagnosis codes used in surveillance definitions, with 25 states using P96.1, four using P04.49, and only two (Massachusetts and Missouri) using P96.1 or P04.49 for passive surveillance [32].

Goyal et al. reported the application of dual codes for identifying neonates with NOWS/NAS following enactment of a Massachusetts law mandating birth hospitals report the number of newborns exposed to controlled substances [18]. The dual codes were used for identifying substance-exposed neonates across three exposure scenarios: opioids, opioids or benzodiazepines, and any controlled substances [18]. They showed that although the withdrawal code (P96.1) alone had a higher PPV (i.e., ≥ 92% vs. ≥ 64% for dual) and was good at identifying infants with NAS, use of dual withdrawal or exposure codes (i.e., P96.1 or P04.49), was more sensitive for identifying NAS than P96.1 alone (i.e., ≥ 79% vs. ≥ 92% for dual) because it captures infants displaying signs consistent with NAS who have not received a diagnosis of withdrawal by healthcare providers [18]. As such, using P96.1 only for surveillance means that cases identified are likely truly NAS but there may also be many unidentified cases which could be a disadvantage for surveillance.

Several other studies with diverse methodology have attempted to investigate the accuracy of multiple codes for NOWS surveillance with varying results. Elmore et al. studied P96.1 and P04.49 in a sample of 203 infants in Florida and concluded that only P96.1 (95.3%, vs. 13.2% for P04.49 only) displayed high PPV, but this study only included 7 cases where dual codes applied [33]. Lind et al. in a study involving New Mexico, Vermont and Illinois reported highest PPV across states for P96.1 only (59% to 80%, vs. 10% to 30% for P04.49 only) [34]. Kuzniewicz et al. performed a population-based cross-sectional study at 15 Kaiser-Permanente Northern California hospitals from 2010–2019 and found that infant codes for drug exposure were not sensitive (31.6%) and had low PPV, while codes for newborn withdrawal had low sensitivity (31.6%) but high PPV (85%) [35]. Taken together, these studies provide conflicting results regarding identification of infants with NOWS using multiple codes.

Clinically, identification is important because it has been reported that as many as 50% to 80% of infants with prenatal opioid exposure will develop NOWS [36]. Diagnosis of affected mother-infant dyads is desired in real time to facilitate early enrollment in appropriate programs that optimize neurocognitive outcomes in infants. Emerging evidence suggests that infants diagnosed with NOWS would also benefit from access to formal developmental-focused follow-up care, which is currently not uniformly available throughout the U.S. It has been shown that neurodevelopmental outcomes in infancy and childhood have demonstrated variable deficiencies in cognitive, speech and language, and psychomotor functioning at 2 years of age, and these deficits appear to persist through adolescence [37]. Children with a history of NOWS tend to have higher referral rates for disability evaluations, resulting in the need for additional classroom therapies and services compared to their peers [38], therefore close follow-up of affected infants and children is warranted. Although diagnosis and management of NOWS are well-documented, downstream unintended consequences of in-utero opioid exposure, including structural birth defects and possibly autism, have not yet been fully investigated and/or appreciated by pediatric healthcare providers [39, 40].

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends early universal screening to identify and treat women with substance use disorders using validated screening tools [41]. We endorse this ACOG recommendation and universal screening for opioids during pregnancy is one of the public health surveillance tools that could inform implementation of critical programs aimed at short- and long-term health management of the mother-infant dyad since NOWS is a foreseeable and treatable condition seen in 30–80% of neonates born to persons taking prescription or recreational opioids, or opioid agonist therapies (e.g., methadone, buprenorphine) [41]. More importantly, screening may be used to leverage funding for longitudinal studies that will ultimately raise awareness of the future impact of NOWS during childhood and beyond, resulting in mitigation of adverse effects with early intervention measures which have been shown to be successful in other populations at risk for neurocognitive impairments.

Conclusions

The dual code approach offers an alternative strategy for NOWS surveillance. Further, modelling strategies demonstrate that effect modification patterns are more clearly evident with the dual code approach, and may be useful in crafting targeted surveillance strategies. Use of dual exposure or withdrawal codes could be considered in future surveillance research as it may optimize ascertainment of NOWS, particularly for policymakers and perinatal quality improvement efforts. Under-representing NOWS prevalence may lead to insufficient resource allocation to local programs for substance use disorder and its sequelae, resulting in suboptimal management of affected newborns that may ultimately lead to progressive complications impacting childhood and adolescent neurocognitive health.
