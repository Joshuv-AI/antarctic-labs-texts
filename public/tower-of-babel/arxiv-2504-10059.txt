Central limit theorem for ϵ \epsilon -independent products and higher-order tensors
Guillaume Cébron and Patrick Oliveira Santos and Pierre Youssef

Abstract
. We establish a central limit theorem (CLT) for families of products of ϵ \epsilon -independent random variables. Building on the approach in [ 4 ] , we utilize graphon limits to encode the evolution of independence and characterize the limiting distribution. Our framework subsumes a wide class of dependency structures and includes, as a special case, a CLT for higher-order tensor products of free random variables. Our results generalize those in [ 4 ] , extend the findings of [ 8 ] , which were limited to order-2 tensors, and recover as a special case the recent tensor-free CLT of [ 10 ] , which was obtained through the development of a tensor analogue of free probability. In contrast, our approach is more direct and provides a unified and concise derivation of a more general CLT via graphon convergence.

Introduction

The Free Central Limit Theorem (CLT) serves as a cornerstone of free probability theory
[
13
]
,
[
11
, Lecture 8]
. It parallels the classical CLT, replacing classical independence with free independence and the Gaussian limit with the semi-circle law.
Recently, the authors
[
4
]
unified these two by establishing a central limit theorem for
\epsilon
-independent variables.
Let
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
be a noncommutative probability space. Based on the graph product of groups
[
6
]
, the notion of
\epsilon
-independence was introduced in
[
9
]
and elaborated in several subsequent works
[
1
,
5
,
12
]
(see also
[
2
,
3
]
for matrix models). Given a loopless graph
g_{n}=([n],E)
over
n
vertices, we say that subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n}\subset\mathcal{A}
are
g_{n}
-independent if

(1)

For every
(i,j)\in E
, the subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{i},\mathcal{A}_{j}
are classical independent (in particular, they commute);

(2)

For every
k\geq 1
,
i\in[n]^{k}
such that for all
j_{1}<j_{2}
with
i_{j_{1}}=i_{j_{2}}
, there exists
j_{1}<j_{3}<j_{2}
with
(i_{j_{1}},i_{j_{3}})\notin E
, then for any centered random variables
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
,
j\in[k]
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})=0.

Note that when the graph
g_{n}
is complete (resp. edgeless), the above corresponds to classical independence (resp. freeness).
The authors in
[
4
]
used the theory of graphons to track the evolution of the graphs of independence. Recall that a graphon consists of a measurable bounded symmetric function
w:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
. Given a graphon
w
and graphs
f=(V(f),E(f)),g=(V(g),E(g))
, we define

\displaystyle\rho(f,w):=\int_{[0,1]^{V(f)}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}w(x_{u},x_{v})\,\text{d}x;

\displaystyle\rho(f,g)=\frac{1}{|V(g)|^{|V(f)|}}\sum_{\phi:V(f)\to V(g)}\prod_{(u,v)\in E(f)}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),\phi(v))\in E(g)}.

A sequence of simple graphs
g_{n}
in
n
nodes converges to a graphon
w
if, for every simple finite graph
f=(V(f),E(f))
, we have

\displaystyle\rho(f,g_{n})\to\rho(f,w).

We say that
a sequence of (self-adjoint) variables
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{A}
converges in distribution to
a\in\mathcal{A}
if, for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau(a_{n}^{p})\to\tau(a^{p})
as
n\to\infty
. More generally, we define the convergence in
*
-moments of a sequence of not necessarily self-adjoint variables
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
towards some variable
z
if for any
k\geq 1
,
t\in\{1,*\}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(b_{n}^{t})\to\tau(z^{t}),

where

\displaystyle b_{n}^{t}:=b_{n}^{t_{1}}\cdots b_{n}^{t_{k}}.

The authors in
[
4
]
established the following CLT for
\epsilon
-independent variables encompassing the classical and free central limit theorems.
The combinatorial proof of those limit theorems latter deals with pair partitions. We denote by
P_{2}(p)
the set of pair partitions of
[p]
and for a pair partition
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
, we define its intersection graph
f_{\pi}
whose vertex set consists of the blocks of
\pi
, and there exists an edge between two blocks if they cross.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
g_{n}
be a graph over
[n]
, and
a\in\mathcal{A}
be a self-adjoint random variable with mean
\lambda
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Assume that
g_{n}\to w
in the graphon sense. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
be
g_{n}
-independent and identically distributed random variables with common law
a
. Let

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in[n]}(a_{k}-\lambda).

Then,
S_{n}
converges in distribution to a measure
\mu_{w}
that depends only on
w
, whose odd moments vanish and even moments are equal to

\displaystyle\int x^{2p}\,\text{d}\mu_{w}=\sum_{\pi\in P_{2}(2p)}\rho(f_{\pi},w).

The class of measures
\mu_{w}
is quite vast, encompassing well-known distributions like
q
-Gaussians as well as many exotic laws (see
[
4
]
for details).

In another direction motivated by the study of the limiting behavior of random quantum channels,
[
8
]
establishes a central limit theorem for tensor product random variables of the form
c_{k}=a_{k}\otimes a_{k}
, where
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a free family of variables.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be a self-adjoint random variable with mean
\lambda
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and denote

\delta^{2}:=\sigma^{2}(\sigma^{2}+2\lambda^{2}),\quad\alpha:=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+2\lambda^{2}}\in[0,1].

Given
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of free copies of
a
, the distribution
\mu_{S_{n}}
of the normalized sum

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in[n]}(a_{k}\otimes a_{k}-\lambda^{2})

converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
to

\sqrt{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{2}\right)+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\,s_{3},

where
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
are semi-circle random variables,
s_{1},s_{2}
are classical independent, both free independent from
s_{3}
.

The limiting law appearing in the above theorem is partially reminiscent of the laws uncovered in Theorem
1.1
. For instance, the law of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{2}
coincides with that of
\mu_{w}
when the graphon
w
is given by
\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/2)\times[1/2,1]}+\mathbf{1}_{[1/2,1]\times[0,1/2)}
(see
[
4
, Example 7.4]
).
In view of this, it is natural to seek a connection between both results.
Moreover, the analysis of higher-order tensors naturally generalizes the results for order-two tensors, models more intricate structures, and introduces a rich interplay between free and classical independence. Notably, tensors of random variables correspond to products of independent variables. Indeed, tensors of the form
a_{k}^{(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes a_{k}^{(L)}
coincide with the product of
L
independent variables
b_{j}=1\otimes\ldots\otimes a_{k}^{(j)}\otimes\ldots\otimes 1
,
1\leq j\leq L
. As such, a CLT for fixed-order tensors can be interpreted as a study of the free sum of products of independent variables. This realization provides a new perspective, allowing us to model these products and sums through mixtures of free and classical independence.

To formalize this idea, we employ the concept of
\epsilon
-independence mentioned above, enabling us to consider a general setting beyond the fixed-order tensors, considering mixtures of free and independent variables, as well as mixtures of their sums and products.
In
[
4
]
, we demonstrated the utility of graphon theory in tracking the evolution of these mixtures and establishing the corresponding limit theorems. Here, we also make use of these techniques to analyze the sum of mixtures of products modeled by
\epsilon
-independence. More precisely, let
g_{L}
be a graph over
[L]
, and
(\mathcal{A}_{l})_{l\in[L]}\subset\mathcal{A}
be
g_{L}
-independent subalgebras. For every
1\leq l\leq L
and every
n\geq 1
, let
g^{\prime}_{n}
be a graph over
[n]
and consider variables
(a_{k}^{(l)})_{k\in[n]}\subset\mathcal{A}_{l}
which are
g^{\prime}_{n}
-independent. We aim to capture the limiting behavior, as
n\to\infty
, of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(\prod_{l=1}^{L}a_{k}^{(l)}-\lambda^{L}\big).

Theorem
1.1
corresponds to the case
L=1
, while Theorem
1.2
corresponds to
L=2
with
g_{2}
being the complete graph and
g_{n}^{\prime}
being the edgeless graph.
Our main theorem generalizes both results to cover any depth
L
and any graphs of independence
g_{L}
and
g_{n}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
g_{L}
be a graph over
[L]
,
g^{\prime}_{n}
be a graph over
[n]
, and
a\in\mathcal{A}
be a self-adjoint random variable with mean
\lambda
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Assume that
g^{\prime}_{n}\to w
in the graphon sense. Let
(\mathcal{A}_{l})_{l\in[L]}
be
g_{L}
-independent subalgebras and
(a_{k}^{(l)})_{k\in[n]}\subset\mathcal{A}_{l}
be
g^{\prime}_{n}
-independent and identically distributed random variables with common law
a
. Let

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in[n]}\big(\prod_{l=1}^{L}a_{k}^{(l)}-\lambda^{L}\big).

Then,
S_{n}
converges in
*
-distribution to an explicit measure
\mu_{g_{L},w,\lambda,\sigma}
that depends on
g_{L},w,\lambda,\sigma
.

To improve readability and avoid technicalities, we do not explicitly state the limiting law
\mu_{g_{L},w,\lambda,\sigma}
here, referring instead to Section
3
for a precise formulation (see Theorem
3.3
). When
L=1
, this law simplifies to
\mu_{w}
and Theorem
1.3
recovers Theorem
1.1
. Likewise, in the setting of Theorem
1.2
, the law
\mu_{g_{L},w,\lambda,\sigma}
properly normalized aligns with the one in Theorem
1.2
. Further details are provided in Section
3
.

To illustrate the broad applicability of Theorem
1.3
, we consider the special case where
g_{n}^{\prime}
is the edgeless graph, leading to the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
L\geq 1
be fixed, and
g_{L}
be a simple graph over
L
vertices. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{L}\subset\mathcal{A}
be
g_{L}
-independent subalgebras and
a\in\mathcal{A}
be a self-adjoint random variable with mean
\lambda
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. For each
l\in[L]
, let
(a_{n}^{(l)})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{A}_{l}
be free i.i.d copies of
a
, and define

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in[n]}\big(\prod_{l\in[L]}a_{k}^{(l)}-\lambda^{L}\big).

Then,
S_{n}
converges in
*
-moments to

\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)}\lambda^{|J^{c}|}\sigma^{|J|}s_{J},

where
\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)
denotes the collection of nonempty subsets of
[L]
.
Each variable
s_{J}
is

•

a semi-circle random variable if the subgraph of
g_{L}
generated by
J
is the complete graph;

•

a circular random variable otherwise.

Finally, the collection
(s_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)}
is
h_{L}
-independent, where the set of vertices and edges of
h_{L}
are given by

\displaystyle V(h_{L})=\mathcal{P}^{*}(L);

\displaystyle E(h_{L})=\{(J_{1},J_{2}):J_{1}\times J_{2}\subseteq E(g_{L})\}.

Recall that a circular random variable
c
has a law given by

c=\frac{s+\sqrt{-1}s^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
where
s,s^{\prime}
are free identically distributed semi-circle laws. Further specified to the setting of tensor products, i.e., taking
g_{L}
to be the complete graph, we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
L\geq 1
be fixed,
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{L}
classical independent subalgebras, and
a_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,a_{n}^{(l)}\in\mathcal{A}_{l}
be self-adjoint free identically distributed random variables, with mean
\lambda
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, for all
l\in[L]
. Then,

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in[n]}\big(\bigotimes_{l\in[L]}a_{k}^{(l)}-\lambda^{L}\big)

converges in distribution towards
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)}\lambda^{|J^{c}|}\sigma^{|J|}s_{J}
,
where the
s_{J}
’s are
h_{L}
-independent semi-circle laws with the edge set of
h_{L}
given by

\displaystyle E(h_{L}):=\{(J_{1},J_{2})\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L):J_{1}\cap J_{2}=\varnothing\}.

It is easy to check that when
L=2
, the above recovers Theorem
1.2
, unveiling a law consisting of classical and free convolutions of semi-circle random variables. Moreover, note that if
\lambda=0
, then for any
L
, the above limiting law reduces to the semi-circle, extending a phenomenon observed in the case of order-two tensors in
[
8
]
.
More generally, Corollary
1.5
recovers the recent tensor-free central limit theorem established in
[
10
, Theorem 1.2, Remark 9.3]
. The approach in
[
10
]
consists of introducing the notion of tensor freeness, defining tensor-free cumulants, and establishing a corresponding moment-cumulant formula. In contrast, we deduce Corollary
1.5
as a special case of a more general framework for sums of products of
\epsilon
-independent variables, leveraging the theory of decorated graphons to formulate and prove a corresponding general limiting theorem (see below). Once the appropriate landscape is set up, the proof is reduced to a direct application of the moment method, which is carefully coupled with graphon convergence.

Theorem
1.3
and its Corollaries are inscribed in a yet more general setting, which we elaborate in Section
3
(see Theorem

). The general setting considers an independence structure captured by a grid graph over

, while Theorem

deals with the special case of a grid graph given by the lexicographical product of a graph

over
[L]
and a graph

over

. We use the so-called decorated graphon theory from
[
7
]
to study the convergence of grid graphs, following a route similar to
[
4

. We refer to Section
2
for the precise definitions.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall key notions from free probability and graphon theory. We denote by
P(k)
the set of all partitions of
[k]
, by
P_{2}(k)
the set of all pair partitions of
[k]
, by
\operatorname{NC}(k)
of all noncrossing partition of
[k]
, and by
\operatorname{NC}_{2}(k)
the set of all noncrossing pair partitions of
[k]
. More generally, for

, we denote

\displaystyle T_{\leq 2}=\{\pi\in T:|v|\leq 2,\forall v\in\pi\};

\displaystyle T_{\geq 2}=\{\pi\in T:|v|\geq 2,\forall v\in\pi\};

\displaystyle T_{2}=T_{\leq 2}\cap T_{\geq 2}.

For a given partition
\pi\in P(p)
and

, we write
r\sim_{\pi}s
if
r,s
belong to the same block of
\pi
.

For
n,k\geq 1
, and

, we denote
\ker(i)\in P(k)
the partition such that
i_{k}=i_{l}
if and only if
k
and
l
belong to the same block of
\ker(i)
.
A central tool in our analysis is the moment-cumulant formula for
\epsilon
-independence
[
12
]
. Given a simple graph
g_{n}=([n],E)
in
n
nodes, we denote
\operatorname{NC}(g_{n},i)
the set of
(g_{n},i)
-noncrossing partitions, which are the partitions
\pi\in P(k)
satisfying the following two properties

•

\pi\leq\ker(i)
, meaning that
\pi
only groups elements that are already connected in
\ker(i)
;

•

if there exist indices
p_{1}<p_{2}<q_{1}<q_{2}
such that
i_{p_{1}}\nsim_{\pi}i_{p_{2}}
,
i_{p_{1}}\sim_{\pi}i_{q_{1}}
,
i_{p_{2}}\sim_{\pi}i_{q_{2}}
, then the edge
(i_{p_{1}},i_{p_{2}})
must be present in
E
.

Finally, we denote the free cumulants of
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
by
\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
n,k\geq 1
,
g_{n}=([n],E)
be a simple graph in
n

i\in[n]^{k}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
be
g_{n}
-independent subalgebras, and
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{j}}
, for

. Then

\displaystyle\tau(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})=\sum_{\pi\in\operatorname{NC}(g_{n},i)}\prod_{v=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\}\in\pi}\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{m}(a_{r_{1}},\ldots,a_{r_{m}}).

As mentioned at the end of the introduction, we will make use of the notion of decorated graphon
[
7
]
, which we will now formally introduce.

Let
L\geq 1
,
\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)
be the collection of nonempty subsets of
[L]
and
\mathcal{P}(L)
be the collection of subsets of
[L]
. We denote
d_{L}=|\mathcal{P}(L)|=2^{L}
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{L}
be the space of
[0,1]
-valued
d_{L}\times d_{L}
matrices. We equip this space with the inner product and Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\displaystyle\langle S,T\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(ST^{*})=\sum_{I,J\in\mathcal{P}(L)}S_{IJ}T_{IJ}\,;\quad\|S\|_{\operatorname{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{I,J\in\mathcal{P}(L)}S_{IJ}^{2}.

An
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graphon
w
is a map
w:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{M}_{L}
satisfying the symmetry condition
\|w(x,y)\|_{\operatorname{HS}}=\|w(y,x)\|_{\operatorname{HS}}
for every
x,y\in[0,1]
. An
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graph is a pair
G=(g,\beta)
, where:

•

g=(V(g),E(g))
is a graph;

•

\beta:E(g)\to\mathcal{M}_{L}
is a map that assigns a matrix to each edge.

Given such a decorated graph, we can define the associated decorated graphon by

\displaystyle w_{G}(x,y)=\beta(i,j),\quad\text{for }(x,y)\in\left[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}\right)\times\left[\frac{j-1}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right).

Given an
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graph
F=(f,\beta)
and an
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graphon
w
, we define the homomorphism density

\displaystyle\rho(F,w)=\int_{[0,1]^{V(f)}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E(f)}\langle\beta(u,v),w(x_{u},x_{v})\rangle\,\text{d}x.

When the graphon
w
is induced by a decorated graph
G
i.e.
w=w_{G}
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\rho(F,G):=\rho(F,w_{G})=\frac{\hom(F,G)}{|V(g)|^{|V(f)|}};

\displaystyle\hom(F,G)=\sum_{\phi:V(f)\to V(g)}\prod_{e\in E(f)}\langle\beta(e),\gamma(\phi(e))\rangle,

where
\phi(e)=(\phi(u),\phi(v))
, for
e=(u,v)
.
Similarly, the injective homomorphism density is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{hom}_{\operatorname{inj}}(F,G)=\sum_{\phi:V(f)\hookrightarrow V(g)}\prod_{e\in E(f)}\langle\beta(e),\gamma(\phi(e))\rangle;

\displaystyle\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F,G)=\frac{\operatorname{hom}_{\operatorname{inj}}(F,G)}{|V(g)|^{|V(f)|}},

where
\phi:V(f)\hookrightarrow V(g)
denotes an injective map.

A sequence of
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graphons
(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is said to converge to an
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graphon
w
, denoted by
w_{n}\to w
, if
\rho(F,w_{n})\to\rho(F,w)
for every
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graph
F
(see
[
7
, Theorem 3.7]
for more details). This convergence can be expressed using the cut distance
\delta_{\Box}
defined as:

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\Box}:=\sup_{f,g:[0,1]\to[0,1]}\left\|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}w(x,y)f(x)g(y)\text{d}x\text{d}y\right\|_{\operatorname{HS}};

\displaystyle\delta_{\Box}(w_{1},w_{2}):=\inf_{\phi}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\circ\phi\|_{\Box},

where the infimum is over measure-preserving transformations
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
satisfying
w\circ\phi(x,y):=w(\phi(x),\phi(y))
.
This cut distance provides a criterion for the convergence
w_{n}\to w
. The proof of continuity for the mapping
w\mapsto\rho(F,w)
under
\delta_{\Box}
is detailed in
[
7
, Section 2]
.

In our setting, we utilize decorated graphons in a specific way. Given a graph
f=(V(f),E(f))
and
J\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)^{V(f)}
, we define its
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graph
F_{f}^{J}=(f,\beta_{J})
where the decoration map is given by

\displaystyle\beta_{J}(u,v)=\mathbf{P}_{J_{u},J_{v}},\;\text{for }(u,v)\in E(f),

where
\mathbf{P}_{I,J}
denotes the canonical basis element in
\mathcal{M}_{L}
.
For an intersection graph
f_{\pi}
of a pair partition
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
and a word
\alpha\in\{1,*\}^{p}
, we define
F_{f_{\pi}}^{J,\alpha}=(\tilde{f}_{\pi},\beta_{J})

\tilde{f}_{\pi}

by adding an extra vertex

(1a)

\displaystyle V(\tilde{f}_{\pi})=V(f_{\pi})\sqcup\{\xi\}

(1b)

\displaystyle E(\tilde{f}_{\pi})=E(f_{\pi})\sqcup\{(\{r,s\},\xi):\{r,s\}\in\pi,\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}\}

(1c)

\displaystyle\beta_{J}(u,\xi)=\mathbf{P}_{J_{u}\varnothing};\quad\beta_{J}(\xi,u)=\mathbf{P}_{\varnothing J_{u}}.

For a graph
g_{n}=([n]\times[L],E(g_{n}))
, we define an
\mathcal{M}_{L}
-decorated graph
G_{g_{n}}=(V(G_{g_{n}}),E(G_{g_{n}}),\gamma_{n})
where
(V(G_{g_{n}}),E(G_{g_{n}}))
corresponds to the complete graph
K_{n}
over
n

\gamma_{n}
is given for

by

(2a)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{n}(u,v))_{J_{1}J_{2}}=\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{1}\times J_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{((u,l_{1}),(v,l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}

(2b)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{n}(u,v))_{J\varnothing}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},l_{2})\in J\times J\\
l_{1}\neq l_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{((u,l_{1}),(u,l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}

(2c)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{n}(u,v))_{\varnothing J}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},l_{2})\in J\times J\\
l_{1}\neq l_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{((v,l_{1}),(v,l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}

(2d)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{n}(u,v))_{\varnothing\varnothing}=1,

for
J_{1},J_{2},J\in\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)
.
Finally note that
\|\gamma_{n}(u,v)\|_{\operatorname{HS}}=\|\gamma_{n}(v,u)\|_{\operatorname{HS}}
. The following lemma allow us to compute
\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F^{J,\alpha}_{f_{\pi}},G_{g_{n}})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
,
\alpha\in\{1,*\}^{p}
,
J\in[\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)]^{V(f_{\pi})}

g_{n}=([n]\times[L],E(g_{n}))

\displaystyle\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F^{J,\alpha}_{f_{\pi}},G_{g_{n}})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n-p/2}{n}\right)\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\phi:V(f_{\pi})\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=\{r,s\}\in V(f_{\pi})\\
\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{u}^{2}\\
l_{1}\neq l_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),l_{1}),(\phi(u),l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{(u,v)\in E(f_{\pi})}\left(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{u}\times J_{v}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),l_{1}),\phi(v),l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}\right).

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F^{J,\alpha}_{f_{\pi}},G_{g_{n}})=\frac{1}{n^{1+p/2}}\sum_{\phi:V(f_{\pi})\sqcup\{\xi\}\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{(u,v)\in E(\tilde{f}_{\pi})}\langle\beta_{J}(u,v),\gamma_{n}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\rangle.

u=\{r,s\}

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{J}(u,\xi),\gamma(\phi(u),\phi(\xi))\rangle

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{n}(\phi(u),\phi(\xi)))_{J\varnothing}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{u}^{2}\\
l_{1}\neq l_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{((\phi(u),l_{1}),(\phi(u),l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}.

In particular, the inner product does not depend on
\phi(\xi)

\displaystyle\rho(F^{J,\alpha}_{f_{\pi}},\gamma_{n})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n-p/2}{n}\right)\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\phi:V(f_{\pi})\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=\{r,s\}\in V(f_{\pi})\\
\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{u}^{2}\\
l_{1}\neq l_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),l_{1}),(\phi(u),l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{(u,v)\in E(f_{\pi})}\langle\beta_{J}(u,v),\gamma_{n}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\rangle.

For an edge

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{J}(u,v),\gamma(\phi(u),\phi(v))\rangle

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{n}(\phi(u),\phi(v)))_{J_{u}J_{v}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{u}\times J_{v}}\mathbf{1}_{((\phi(u),l_{1}),(\phi(v),l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}.

The result follows.

,
\alpha=(*,1)
and
\ker(i)=\pi
, then we obtain

\displaystyle\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(b_{i_{2}}^{(J_{2})}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}=a_{i_{1}}^{(2)}a_{i_{1}}^{(1)}a_{i_{1}}^{(2)}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3};

\displaystyle\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})=((i_{1},2),(i_{1},1),(i_{1},2)).

Now, suppose
\pi
contains a block of size one, say
v=\{v\}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
v=1
. Define
\theta_{1}=(i_{1},l_{1})
so that
x_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{1}}
. By Speicher’s lemma, we have

\displaystyle\tau(x_{1}\cdots x_{m})=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{NC}(g_{n},\theta)}\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\sigma}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}).

Since
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{k}
for all
j\geq 2
(as
i_{1}\neq i_{j}
for all
j\neq 1
), and every
\sigma\in\operatorname{NC}(g_{n},\theta)

\sigma\leq\ker(\theta)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\sigma}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m})=\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{1}(x_{1})\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\sigma\setminus\{\{1\}\}}(x_{2},\cdots,x_{m})=0,

since
x_{1}
is centered. Therefore, partitions with single blocks do not contribute, leading to

\displaystyle\tau\left((S_{n}^{(J_{1})})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(S_{n}^{(J_{p})})^{\alpha_{p}}\right)=\sum_{\pi\in P_{\geq 2}(p)}\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}.

Suppose
v\in\pi
satisfies
|v|\geq 3
. Then
|\pi|<p/2
, and we estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}\leq C_{a,J,\alpha}n^{|\pi|-p/2},

where

\displaystyle C_{a,J,\alpha}=\max_{i\in[p]^{p}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}<\infty,

Since
|\pi|-p/2<0
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}\to 0.

Thus, in the large-
n
limit, only pair partitions contribute, leading to

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau\left((S_{n}^{(J_{1})})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(S_{n}^{(J_{p})})^{\alpha_{p}}\right)=\sum_{\pi\in P_{2}(p)}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Define

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J):=\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\tau\left\{\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}\right\}.

Following the previous proof, let
m=|J_{1}|+\cdots+|J_{p}|
, so that

\displaystyle\left(b_{i_{1}}^{(J_{1})}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\left(b_{i_{p}}^{(J_{p})}\right)^{\alpha_{p}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{m},

where each
x_{k}=a_{i_{j}}^{(l)}
depends only on
\pi,\alpha
and
J
. Let
\theta_{k}
be the algebra associated with

, i.e.,
x_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{k}}
. Figure

\displaystyle J_{1}=J_{3}=\{1,2\};\;J_{2}=J_{4}=\{3,4,5\};\;J_{5}=J_{6}=\{1,2\}.

As before, for any partition
\sigma\in P(m)
containing a singleton block,

\displaystyle\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\sigma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=0.

Thus

\displaystyle\tau(x_{1}\cdots x_{m})=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{NC}_{\geq 2}(g_{n},\theta)}\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\sigma}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}).

Since
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
, any
v\in\ker(\theta)
satisfies
|v|\leq 2
, meaning
\ker(\theta)\in P_{\leq 2}(m)
. In particular, if
\sigma\in\operatorname{NC}_{\geq 2}(g_{n},\theta)
, then
\sigma\leq\ker(\theta)
implies
\sigma\in P_{\leq 2}(m)
. Consequently, we must have
\sigma=\ker(\theta)
, leading to
\operatorname{NC}_{\geq 2}(g_{n},\theta)=\{\ker(\theta)\}
whenever it is nonempty.
If
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\kappa^{\operatorname{free}}_{\ker(\theta)}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m})=1,

since the variables are normalized.
Thus,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J)=\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)}.

Next, we give a simple condition under which
\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J)=0
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\{1,*\}^{p}
,
J\in[\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)]^{p}
and
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
. If there exists
\{r,s\}\in\pi
such that
J_{r}\neq J_{s}
, then
\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J)=0
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists an element
l\in J_{r}\setminus J_{s}
. Without loss of generality, by cyclicity, we take
r=1
and set
x_{1}=a_{i_{1}}^{(l)}
. Since
\pi\in P_{2}(p)
is a pair partition, the index
i_{1}
appears exactly once more, namely as
i_{s}
. Given
l\notin J_{s}
, we observe that
\theta_{1}=(i_{1},l)
differs from
\theta_{k}
for all
k\geq 2
. Consequently,
\{1\}\in\ker(\theta)
, implying that
\ker(\theta)\notin\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
. Thus,
\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J)=0
, completing the proof.
∎

Recalling the definition

\displaystyle P_{2}^{J}(p)=\{\pi\in P_{2}(p):\forall\{r,s\}\in\pi,J_{r}=J_{s}\}.

By Lemma
4.2
, we conclude that
\rho_{n}(\pi,\alpha,J)=0
whenever
\pi\notin P_{2}^{J}(p)
. Moreover,
\ker(\theta)\in P_{2}(m)
for every
\pi\in P_{2}^{J}(p)
.
To complete the argument, we now characterize the condition
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\{1,*\}^{p}
,
J\in[\mathcal{P}^{*}(L)]^{p}
,
\pi\in P_{2}^{J}(p)
and
\ker(i)=\pi
. Then
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

If a pair
\{r,s\}\in\pi
satisfies
\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}
, the subgraph
\{(i_{r},l):l\in J_{r}\}\subseteq g_{n}
is complete.

(2)

If two pairs
\{r_{1},s_{1}\},\{r_{2},s_{2}\}\in\pi
form a crossing, then
((i_{r_{1}},l_{1}),(i_{r_{2}},l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})
for all
(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{r_{1}}\times J_{r_{2}}
.

Proof.

Let us first prove that if the two conditions are satisfied, then
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in\ker(\theta)
be two crossing blocks. We denote
v_{k}=\{\theta_{1}^{(k)},\theta_{2}^{(k)}\}
, where
\theta_{1}^{(k)}=(i_{r_{k}},l_{k})
and
\theta_{2}^{(k)}=(i_{s_{k}},l_{k})
, for
k=1,2
(
i_{r_{k}}=i_{s_{k}}
). We either have i)
i_{r_{1}}=i_{r_{2}}
; or ii)
i_{r_{1}}\neq i_{r_{2}}
.

(1)

Case (i) ensures that
\{r_{1},r_{2}\}\in\pi
, and by definition of
\ker(\theta)
, we have
\alpha_{r_{1}}=\alpha_{r_{2}}
.

(2)

Case (ii) ensures that
\{r_{1},s_{1}\},\{r_{2},s_{2}\}\in\pi
form a crossing, by definition of
\ker(\theta)
.

In both cases, Conditions
1
and
2
imply that
((u_{r_{1}},l_{1}),(u_{r_{2}},l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})
and it follows that
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
.

Now suppose that
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
. Let us prove that the two conditions are satisfied.

(1)

Let
\{r,s\}\in\pi
be such that
\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}
. By definition of
\ker(\theta)
, the blocks
v_{1}=\{(i_{r},l_{1}),(i_{s},l_{1})\}
and
v_{2}=\{(i_{r},l_{2}),(i_{s},l_{2})\}
will cross for any distinct
l_{1},l_{2}\in J_{r}
. Since
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
, it follows that
((i_{r},l_{1}),(i_{r},l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})
for any distinct
l_{1},l_{2}\in J_{r}
. The first condition follows.

(2)

Let
\{r_{1},s_{1}\},\{r_{2},s_{2}\}\in\pi
be such that they form a crossing. By definition of
\ker(\theta)
, the blocks
v_{1}=\{(i_{r_{1}},l_{1}),(i_{s_{1}},l_{1})\}
and
v_{2}=\{(i_{r_{2}},l_{2}),(i_{s_{2}},l_{2})\}
will cross for any
(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{r_{1}}\times J_{r_{2}}
. Since
\ker(\theta)\in\operatorname{NC}_{2}(g_{n},\theta)
, it follows that
((i_{r_{1}},l_{1}),(i_{r_{2}},l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})
for any
(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{r_{1}}\times J_{r_{2}}
. The second condition follows, and the lemma is proved.

∎

We are now ready to finish the proof of Theorem
3.1
. In view of Lemma
4.3
, we deduce that for any
\pi\in P_{2}^{J}(p)
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\pi,J,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]^{p}\\
\ker(i)=\pi\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{r,s\}\in V(f_{\pi})\\
\alpha_{r}=\alpha_{s}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{(i_{r},l):l\in J_{r}\}\subseteq g_{n}\text{ is complete}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{(\{r_{1},s_{1}\},\{r_{2},s_{2}\})\in E(f_{\pi})}\left(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in J_{r_{1}}\times J_{r_{2}}}\mathbf{1}_{(i_{r_{1}},l_{1}),(i_{r_{2}},l_{2}))\in E(g_{n})}\right).

Recalling the definitions of
F_{f_{\pi}}^{J,\alpha}=(\tilde{f}_{\pi},\beta)
and
G_{g_{n}}=(K_{n},\gamma_{n})
, and in view of the one-to-one map from injective maps

such that
\ker(i)=\pi

\phi(u)=i_{r}
for a block

, Lemma
2.2
implies that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\pi,J,\alpha)=\left(\frac{n}{n-p/2}\right)\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F^{J,\alpha}_{f_{\pi}},G_{g_{n}}).

Since
\rho_{\operatorname{inj}}(F_{f_{\pi}}^{J,\alpha},G_{g_{n}})

are asymptotically equivalent as

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau\left((S_{n}^{(J_{1})})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(S_{n}^{(J_{p})})^{\alpha_{p}}\right)=\sum_{\pi\in P_{2}^{J}(p)}\rho(F_{f_{\pi}}^{J,\alpha},w).
