Exercise-Induced Activation of IGF-1/mTOR Signaling and Hippocampal BDNF/PGC-1α Expression Reverses Age-Related Motor Decline
Authors: Taewan Kim, Jinkyung Cho, Yoonhwan Kim, Sang Koo Woo, Donghyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAging is associated with a progressive decline in skeletal muscle function and motor coordination, contributing to reduced mobility and quality of life. While exercise is a well-established intervention to counteract age-related deterioration, the underlying molecular mechanisms in both muscle and the brain remain incompletely understood.MethodsYoung (4 months) and aged (20 months) male C57BL/6N mice were assigned to control or treadmill exercise groups. After 8 weeks of training, the grip strength, muscle histology, rotarod performance, and molecular markers related to muscle hypertrophy and brain plasticity were analyzed.ResultsTreadmill exercise significantly increased grip strength in both young and aged mice without changes in muscle fiber size, indicating improved muscle quality. Molecular analysis revealed upregulation of insulin-like growth factor 1/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and mTOR/p70 ribosomal protein S6 kinase signaling in both young and aged mice. Exercise also modulated myogenic markers in an age-dependent manner, increasing paired box 3 (Pax3) and myogenic differentiation 1 in aged mice and Pax7 in young mice. Rotarod performance significantly improved over time, suggesting enhanced motor coordination and learning. These functional gains were accompanied by elevated hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha expression.ConclusionsEight weeks of treadmill exercise enhances muscle strength and motor performance in both young and aged mice. These benefits occur independently of muscle hypertrophy and are associated with favorable molecular adaptations in muscle and the brain. These findings underscore the potential of exercise to preserve neuromuscular function during aging through peripheral and central mechanisms.Keywords: Aging, Exercise, Muscle quality, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, Insulin-like growth factor I

• HIGHLIGHTS

- Treadmill exercise improved grip strength and motor performance in both young and aged mice without muscle fiber hypertrophy.

- Exercise activated insulin-like growth factor 1/AKT and p70S6K signaling and differentially regulated myogenic markers in an age-dependent manner.

- Hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha expression increased following treadmill exercise, suggesting coordinated peripheral and central adaptations that may contribute to preserved neuromuscular function during aging.

INTRODUCTION

Aging is associated with a progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and motor coordination, leading to reduced mobility, an increased risk of falls, and diminished quality of life in older adults [1]. Sarcopenia, characterized by the age-related loss of muscle mass and function, is accompanied by reductions in motor neurons and satellite cell activity [2, 3]. These age-related physiological changes contribute to frailty, loss of independence, and increased mortality in the elderly human population [4].

Numerous epidemiological studies report strong associations between reduced muscle strength and increased mortality in older adults—collectively underscoring the importance of preserving muscle function as humans age [5]. Exercise training is one of the most effective nonpharmacological strategies to maintain muscle health, resulting in improved strength, cardiovascular function, and nervous system adaptability [6, 7].

Exercise-based adaptations may be related to multiple intracellular signaling pathways that are involved in muscle regeneration and growth, which can improve age-related muscle atrophy [8]. In particular, exercise activates anabolic and regenerative pathways such as the insulin-like growth factor 1/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (IGF-1/AKT/mTOR) axis, which promotes muscle growth, suppresses proteolysis, and reduces inflammation [9]. Additionally, IGF-1 enhances satellite cell activity, and contributes to muscle regeneration and quality [10].

The benefits of exercise extend to the central nervous system. Treadmill running increases the hippocampal expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α), which are key regulators of neuroplasticity, mitochondrial biogenesis, and cognitive function [11, 12]. These molecular adaptations may underlie improvements in motor learning and coordination [13]. Increasing evidence also suggests that exerciseinduced adaptations in skeletal muscle may influence brain function through muscle-brain cross-talk mechanisms [14].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether treadmill exercise improves muscle strength and motor performance in young and aged mice, and to determine whether these effects are associated with molecular adaptations in both skeletal muscle and the hippocampus.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Four-month-old (young) and 20-month-old (aged) male C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, Korea). These mice were classified by age into young (weight: 20.7±1.8 g) and old (weight: 34.2±2.9 g) groups. All mice were housed in sterile cages under a day-and-night cycle, with ad libitum access to solid food (sterilized using high-pressure steam) and drinking water during the experimental period. The temperature was maintained at 22°C–24°C and the humidity was 50%±10%.

Experimental Protocol

The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sungkyunkwan University, and is illustrated in Fig. 1. The experimental animals were randomly divided into 4 groups: (1) young control group (Y-Con, n=10); (2) young exercise group (Y-Exe, n=10); (3) aged control group (A-Con, n=10); and (4) aged exercise group (A-Exe, n=10). The body weight of each mouse was recorded twice a week for the duration of the study. The exercise groups were trained on a motor-driven treadmill (Columbus Instruments, Inc., USA) for 40 minutes per session, 5 days per week, for 8 weeks. The mice ran on the treadmill at a 0° incline. The running speed was set at 15 m/min during weeks 1–2 and increased to 20 m/min during weeks 3–8, corresponding to moderate-intensity aerobic exercise in mice [15]. During this 8-week exercise period, grip strength and rotarod tests were performed to assess exercise performance. The mice were then euthanized using a combination of alfaxan (alfaxalone, 80 mg/kg) and xylazine (Rompun, 10 mg/kg) as anaesthesia.

Experimental design. Y-Con, young control group; Y-Exe, young exercise group; A-Con, aged control group; A-Exe, aged exercise group.

Grip Strength Test

All mice underwent weekly muscle strength measurements using a muscle strength tester (BIOSSEB’s Grip strength test, France), commencing on the first week of the experiment. The procedure began with an experimenter carefully holding the mouse torso and tail, directing it to grasp the bar of the muscle strength meter located 10 cm from the ground. Subsequently, the tail was steadily pulled back, causing the paws to release from the bar. The peak muscle strength (g) was recorded, and the procedure was repeated twice to obtain an average tension.

Rotarod Test

The motor performance of all mice was assessed using a Rota-Rod test apparatus (mouse Rota-Rod 47700, UGO BASILE, Italy) once every 2 weeks for 8 weeks. In this procedure, the mouse was placed on a rotating cylindrical bar, initially rotating at a rate of 4 rpm and gradually accelerating. The rotation speed increased to a maximum of 40 rpm within 5 minutes. The time (seconds) until the mouse fell was measured. Measurements were conducted for up to 300 seconds, and average values were calculated from 2 consecutive trials separated by a 20-minute interval.

Hematoxylin & Eosin Staining

The muscle tissues extracted from the gastrocnemius were placed in 4% neutral buffered formalin solution and fixed at 4°C for 24 hours. The fixed muscle tissues were processed in 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol solutions for more than 16 hours, and then fixed in a paraffin block. Using a paraffin section (Leica, Germany), 4-μm thick sections were placed on glass slides. The glass slides were dehydrated by incubation in xylene solution for 3 minutes and then soaked in 100%, 90%, 70%, and 50% ethanol for 5 minutes each. Subsequently, the slides were washed with distilled water (DW), stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes, washed several times with DW, stained with eosin for 2 minutes, rinsed with DW, and dehydrated for 5 minutes each in 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol solutions. The slides were immersed in xylene thrice, for 3 minutes each, and covered with a cover slide and mounting solution. The images were captured by a Leica DM759 light microscope and analyzed using Image J software.

Western Blot

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate protein expression in the gastrocnemius muscle. The extracted gastrocnemius tissues were lysed in lysis buffer containing 50mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, 1.5mM magnesium chloride hexahydrate, 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 2-mg/mL leupeptin, 1-mg/mL pepstatin, 1mM sodium orthovanadate, and 100mM sodium fluoride. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 minutes, and the protein concentration was quantified using the Bradford assay. Twenty micrograms of total protein from each sample was electrophoresed on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfatepolyacrylamide gel, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour with 4% skim milk, followed by incubation with a primary antibody for 24 hours at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-IGF1 (1:1,000; Abcam, UK; Cat. ab9572), rabbit anti-phospho-PI3K (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat. #4228), rabbit anti-PI3K (1:1,000; Abcam; Cat. ab302958), rabbit anti-phospho-AKT (1:1,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. 44-621G), rabbit anti-AKT (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. #4060), rabbit anti-β-actin (1:1,000; Bethyl Laboratories, USA; Cat. A300-491A), rabbit anti-phospho-mTOR (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat. #5536), rabbit anti-mTOR (1:1,000; Abcam; Cat. ab134903), rabbit anti-phospho-p70S6K (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. #97596), rabbit anti-p70S6K (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers; Cat. #34475). Subsequently, the membrane was washed thrice, for 10 minutes each, with 1× Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature. After 3 washes with 0.05% Tween 20 in 1X TBST for 10 minutes, the membrane was developed using chemiluminescent Western blotting detection reagents (Amersham, Pharmacia Biotech GmbH, Germany). Protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Band intensities of target proteins were normalized to the corresponding housekeeping protein (β-actin) to control for loading variability. Relative protein expression levels were calculated as the ratio of the target protein band intensity to the β-actin band intensity from the same sample.

Immunofluorescence

Brain tissues were blocked with blocking solution (B10710, Invitrogen, USA) and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-PGC-1α (1:200; Bioss, USA; Cat. BS-1832R), anti-BDNF (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. #75-470). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline, sections were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. A11012) and anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. A11029). Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA; Cat. SC-62248). Slides were mounted with DAKO Fluorescence Mounting Medium and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Fluorescence images were acquired under identical microscope settings for all samples. Quantification of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The mean positive area was measured in the cornu ammonis (CA)1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus. The average value from multiple sections per animal was used for statistical analysis. Image acquisition and analysis were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental group allocation.

Statistical Analyses

Data are presented as mean±standard deviation. A 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the main effects of time (grip strength test: weeks 1–8; rotarod test: 2 week, 4 week, 6 week, 8 week) and group (Y-Con, Y-Exe, A-Con, A-Exe), as well as the group by time interaction on grip strength in the grip strength test and latency to fall in the rotarod test. One-way ANOVAs were used to compare differences in the outcome variables between the groups of mice. The Tukey post hoc multiple comparison test was used to test main effects (2-way ANOVA) and group differences (1-way ANOVA) in the measured parameters, as necessary. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05, and all analyses were carried out with IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 23.0 (IBM Co., USA).

RESULTS

Eight Weeks of Treadmill Running Improved Grip Strength in Young and Aged Mice

To examine changes in muscle strength following 8 weeks of treadmill running, grip strength was measured weekly (Fig. 2A). A significant increase in muscle strength was observed in the exercise-trained mice, with a significant group×time interaction (F [21, 252]=92.988, P<0.001). Post hoc analysis revealed that both the Y-Exe and A-Exe groups demonstrated significantly greater grip strength at week 8 (Fig. 2B) compared with that of their respective controls (Y-Exe vs. Y-Con, P<0.001; A-Exe vs. A-Con, P<0.001) (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that treadmill exercise progressively improves grip strength in both young and aged mice.

(a) P<0.05 vs. Y-Con, (b) P<0.05 vs. Y-Exe, (c) P<0.05 vs. A-Con, and (d) P<0.05 vs. A-Exe. Values are presented as mean±standard deviation. n=10 per group.

Eight Weeks of Treadmill Running Improved Muscle Quality in Young and Aged Mice

The changes in muscle fiber size were assessed by hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 3). No significant changes in muscle fiber size were observed following exercise in young and aged mice. However, young mice had significantly larger muscle fibers than aged mice (Y-Con vs. A-Con and A-Exe, P<0.001 for both; Y-Exe vs. A-Con and A-Exe, P<0.001 for both). These findings suggest that muscle fiber size is primarily influenced by age, whereas treadmill exercise enhances muscle quality and functional strength in both young and aged mice, even in the absence of muscle fiber hypertrophy.

Eight Weeks of Treadmill Running Enhanced Hypertrophy and Myogenesis Pathways in Both Young and Aged Mice

To assess the exercise-induced modifications in muscle hypertrophy, IGF-1 pathway markers were analyzed. The expression levels of IGF-1, PI3K, and AKT in the gastrocnemius muscle after 8 weeks of treadmill running are shown in Fig 4A. The expression level of IGF-1 was significantly lower in A-Con mice than in Y-Con mice (P<0.001), whereas the exercise-trained groups had a significant increase in IGF-1 expression. Specifi-cally, IGF-1 levels were significantly higher in Y-Exe mice than that in Y-Con mice (P=0.023), and in A-Exe mice compared with A-Con mice (P=0.001). Regarding the phosphorylated phosphoinositide 3-kinase/total phosphoinositide 3-kinase (p-PI3K/t-PI3K) ratio, a significant increase was observed in AExe mice compared with the other groups (vs. Y-Con, P=0.040; vs. Y-Exe, P=0.042; vs. A-Con, P=0.037). In addition, exercise intervention significantly increased the p-AKT/t-AKT ratio in both young and aged mice (Y-Con vs. Y-Exe, P<0.001; A-Con vs. A-Exe, P<0.001), with no significant differences observed between the other groups.

With respect to exercise-induced muscle cell differentiation in the gastrocnemius muscle, markers of mTOR/p70S6K pathway were assessed (Fig. 4B). The p-mTOR/t-mTOR ratio was significantly lower in young mice compared with aged mice (P=0.018), with the highest levels observed in the A-Exe group. The p-p70S6K/t-p70S6K ratio was significantly elevated in both Y-Exe and A-Exe mice compared with their respective control groups (both P<0.001), while Y-Con mice exhibited significantly lower levels than A-Con mice (P<0.001).

Regarding myogenic markers (Fig. 4C), A-Con mice showed significantly lower levels of Pax3 and MyoD compared with Y-Con mice (both P<0.001). Exercise significantly increased the expression of Pax3 (P=0.037) and MyoD (P=0.001) in aged mice, as well as Pax7 (P=0.046) in young mice. However, exercise led to a reduction in Pax3 expression in young mice and had no significant effect on Pax7 in aged mice or MyoD in young mice. These findings suggest that treadmill exercise activates key signaling pathways involved in muscle hypertrophy and differentiation, and differentially regulates myogenic markers in an age-dependent manner.

Eight Weeks of Treadmill Running Improved Motor Performance and Learning in Both Young and Aged Mice

The rotarod test was conducted 4 times at biweekly intervals to evaluate locomotor coordination and motor memory. The latency to fall, or the time each mouse remained on the rotating rod, was measured (Fig. 5A). Repeated-measures 2-way ANOVA revealed a significant group×time interaction (F [9, 108]=15.717, P<0.001). A significant improvement in latency to fall was observed over time in both the Y‑Exe (P=0.002) and A‑Exe (P=0.036) groups. Post hoc analysis revealed that Y-Exe mice had a significantly longer latency than Y-Con mice (P<0.001), and A-Exe mice had significantly longer latency than A-Con mice (P=0.001) at week 8 (Fig. 5B). Treadmill exercise appears to enhance motor coordination and motor memory in both young and aged mice, with greater improvements observed in young mice.

(a) P<0.05 vs. Y-Con, (b) P<0.05 vs. Y-Exe, (c) P<0.05 vs. A-Con, and (d) P<0.05 vs. A-Exe. Values are presented as mean±standard deviation. n=10 per group.

Eight Weeks of Treadmill Running Elevated PGC-1α and BDNF Expression in Both Young and Aged Mice

As shown in Fig 6, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that 8 weeks of treadmill running significantly increased BDNF expression in young mice in both the CA1 (P=0.005) and CA3 regions (P=0.039), and in aged mice in the CA3 region (P=0.048). Similarly, the expression of PGC-1α was significantly elevated by treadmill running in both young (P=0.032) and aged mice (P=0.009) in the CA3 region, with no significant changes observed in the CA1 region. These findings suggest that treadmill exercise promotes region-specific neuroplasticity in the hippocampus, enhancing BDNF and PGC-1α, which may contribute to improved motor learning and memory in both young and aged mice.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that an 8-week treadmill exercise intervention leads to significant improvements in both muscular and neural parameters in young and aged mice. Exercise enhanced grip strength and motor coordination in both age groups without muscle fiber hypertrophy, suggesting improvements in muscle quality. These functional gains were accompanied by upregulation of key molecular markers involved in muscle hypertrophy, myogenic differentiation, and hippocampal neuroplasticity.

Aging contributes to progressive declines in muscle mass and strength, as observed in longitudinal studies involving elderly humans [16-18]. Similarly, animal models have shown age-related reductions in muscle fiber size and increased oxidative stress [19, 20]. Consistent with this, our results showed diminished grip strength and smaller gastrocnemius muscle fibers in aged mice.

In this study, 8 weeks of treadmill running significantly improved grip strength and muscle fiber quality, particularly in aged mice. Although no substantial hypertrophy was observed, the improvements in strength suggest enhanced muscle quality, potentially mediated by mitochondrial efficiency and neuromuscular remodeling [21]. These findings are supported by previous work demonstrating that endurance or resistance exercise mitigates muscle atrophy and improves recovery following muscle damage in aged populations [22, 23]. Human-based studies have similarly reported that resistance training in the elderly improves muscle size and strength [24], while Zhao et al. [25] found that moderate-intensity treadmill training preserved muscle fiber cross-sectional area and myonuclear number in aged mice. However, our findings showed no changes in muscle fiber size in aged mice following treadmill running. Consistent with this, Delmonico et al. [26] reported that muscle strength does not always correlate with muscle size in older adults, emphasizing muscle quality.

On a molecular level, we observed activation of the IGF-1/PI3K/AKT and mTOR/p70S6K signaling pathways, both crucial regulators of muscle protein synthesis and hypertrophy. The aged exercise group showed significant upregulation of p-PI3K and p-AKT, while p-mTOR expression was not significantly increased following exercise. This suggests that aging muscle remains responsive to treadmill exercise, despite blunted hypertrophic responses. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that decreased expression of AKT, mTOR, and p70S6K occurs with aging in skeletal muscle [27]. Moreover, aging has been associated with a downregulation of IGF-1 expression both systemically and locally, further exacerbating muscle atrophy [28]. Additionally, IGF-1 isoforms such as IGF-1Ea and IGF-1Eb have demonstrated protective effects against age-related muscle loss and declines in strength [9].

Furthermore, the aged group exhibited greater Pax3 and MyoD expression and young mice displayed elevated Pax7 induced by treadmill running, meaning enhanced satellite cell activity [29]. These findings are in line with previous studies that reported resistance training significantly increases MyoD and Pax7 expression in the soleus and extensor digitorum longus muscles of aged rats [30], and that maternal exercise enhances embryonic myogenesis through thyroid hormone signaling, particularly via thyroid hormone receptor α-mediated upregulation of Pax3/7 and MyoD [31].

In addition to improvements in grip strength, the enhanced performance across 4 rotarod trials suggests increased motor coordination, motor learning, and memory. This finding indicates that treadmill exercise may promote both neuromuscular and cognitive enhancements, particularly relevant in aging where motor learning capacity often declines. These findings align with previous research; for example, McMurdo and Rennie [32] showed that exercise interventions can improve muscle strength and functional capacity even in very old adults, including individuals up to 91 years of age. Similarly, Faber et al. [33] and Lustosa et al. [34] reported that exercise training enhances strength and functional performance, as assessed by tools such as the Performance Oriented Mobility Assessment, Physical Performance Score, Groningen Activity Restriction Scale, timed up and go test, and the 10-Meter Walk Test.

Neural adaptations may partly explain the observed motor improvements. Treadmill exercise enhanced hippocampal expression of BDNF and PGC-1α, neurotropic factors critical for synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and mitochondrial function [12, 13]. The upregulation of BDNF and PGC-1α suggests that peripheral muscle adaptations may be linked to central nervous system plasticity through myokine release, which has been implicated in enhanced hippocampal function [35]. Interestingly, CA3 appeared more responsive to treadmill exercise in aged mice. This may relate to the CA3’s extensive recurrent excitatory circuitry and associative network properties that distinguish it from CA1, potentially conferring greater sensitivity to interventions that modulate synaptic plasticity [36].

This study has several limitations. First, aged mice may experience a relatively higher physiological load at the same absolute running speed compared with young mice, as exercise intensity was not standardized based on maximal running capacity. Second, the present findings should be interpreted as associative rather than mechanistic. Third, circulating factors, potentially mediating muscle-brain communication, were not measured. Fourth, mitochondrial function was not directly assessed, and changes in mitochondrial-related protein expression may not reflect functional capacity. Fifth, only male mice were included, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future studies employing pathway-specific approaches, systemic biomarker analyses, direct assessments of mitochondrial function, and inclusion of both sexes are needed to clarify causal mechanisms, confirm functional significance, and determine whether the observed adaptations are generalizable.

In conclusion, treadmill exercise appears to improve muscle strength and motor performance in both young and aged mice through mechanisms that involve molecular adaptations in skeletal muscle and hippocampal plasticity. These benefits were achieved in the absence of hypertrophy, emphasizing the value of exercise for preserving neuromuscular health and cognitive resilience during aging. This study supports a growing body of evidence that exercise training serves as a comprehensive strategy for combating age-related physical and cognitive decline.
