Genome resequencing reveals an XX/XY sex determination system and male-specific markers in Glyptosternum maculatum
Authors: Zhuangzhuang Wang, Jianshe Zhou, Haoming He, Mengyu Wu, Wanliang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlyptosternum maculatum is an endangered cold-water fish endemic to Xizang, with pronounced sexual dimorphism in growth. Here, we combined whole-genome resequencing, pool-seq, population genomic analyses, and targeted marker validation to characterize its genetic sex determination system. We identified linkage group LG01 as the candidate sex chromosome and localized a sex-determining region spanning 2.7–2.8 Mb that showed strong differentiation between males and females. Within this region, 195 male-specific SNPs and 20 male-specific indels were identified, and 53 sex-linked markers were developed. Targeted sequencing of 29 males and 26 females showed that 93.1% of males were heterozygous and 96.2% of females were homozygous across the marker set. The locus LG01:27,671,222 showed complete genotype-phenotype concordance in the validation set. These findings support an XX/XY sex determination system and provide molecular tools for sex identification, conservation, and future breeding applications.

Introduction

Glyptosternum maculatum is a cold-water fish species endemic to the high-altitude regions of Xizang and represents an important aquatic genetic resource with both ecological and economic value. This species has evolved distinct physiological adaptations to survive in extreme hypoxic environments, as evidenced by genomic studies demonstrating enhanced hypoxia tolerance and metabolic regulation mediated by specific genetic variants.1 Additionally, the gut microbiota of G. maculatum, including the genus Cupriavidus, plays a pivotal role in maintaining physiological homeostasis under high-altitude conditions.2 Renowned for its favorable sensory qualities and high nutritional value, G. maculatum is recognized as one of China’s top ten elite aquatic genetic resources.3 However, its wild populations face severe threats from habitat degradation, primarily driven by climate change and increasing anthropogenic disturbances. Predictive ecological modeling using MaxEnt has proven effective in identifying potentially suitable habitats, thereby providing scientific support for conservation planning and sustainable management.3 Moreover, investigations into its digestive physiology, including enzyme activity responses under different temperature and pH regimes, provide valuable references for optimizing artificial breeding and culture protocols.4 Despite being a nationally protected species in China, overexploitation and ongoing environmental pressures continue to pose substantial risks to its long-term persistence.3 Genetic studies have revealed low genetic diversity across its populations, particularly among populations originating from the Lasa and Niyang rivers.5 Despite its biological significance and pronounced sexual dimorphism in growth traits, the genetic basis and mechanism of sex determination in this species remain largely unknown. This knowledge gap not only limits our understanding of its reproductive biology but also constrains the development of efficient breeding strategies in aquaculture.

Understanding sex determination mechanisms in fish is essential for improving breeding efficiency and optimizing genetic resource management. Fish exhibit a wide range of sex determination systems, which are primarily classified into XX/XY and ZZ/ZW chromosomal mechanisms. The order Siluriformes, commonly known as catfishes, represents one of the most species-rich groups of freshwater teleosts and exhibits remarkable diversity in sex determination mechanisms. Within Siluriformes, the family Sisoridae constitutes one of the largest and most ecologically specialized groups, comprising over 100 species predominantly distributed across riverine systems of the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and the eastern Himalayas. Despite their high species diversity and ecological importance, systematic studies on sex determination mechanisms within Sisoridae remain extremely scarce. In closely related catfish families, accumulating evidence supports the prevalence of male-heterogametic (XX/XY) systems.6 For instance, in channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus, family Ictaluridae), male-specific SNPs have been identified and shown to be associated with the zbtb38 gene.7,8 In the longsnout catfish (Leiocassis longirostris, family Bagridae) and yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco, family Bagridae), XX/XY systems have been confirmed through genome-wide marker analyses, and males exhibit significantly faster growth rates than females, making mono-sex aquaculture highly desirable. Similarly, in the southern catfish (Silurus meridionalis, family Siluridae), sex-linked DNA markers have been identified and applied in marker-assisted selection programs.9,10 These findings collectively suggest that male heterogamety may be a conserved feature across multiple catfish lineages. However, whether this pattern extends to Sisoridae remains to be determined. G. maculatum exhibits pronounced sexual dimorphism (Figure 1), with mature males being significantly larger than females in body length, weight, and width. Notably, during the breeding season, males and females exhibit distinct growth trajectories, with males continuing to grow in body length while females maintain a more consistent growth rate across body parameters. In many sisorid species, sex-based differences in body size are widespread and are generally considered to reflect divergent reproductive strategies and sexual selection pressures.11,12 Consequently, the identification of genetic markers associated with sexual dimorphism represents a critical step toward the development of mono-sex breeding strategies. Numerous catfish species have been demonstrated to possess an XX/XY genetic sex determination system. For example, the longsnout catfish Leiocassis longirostris has been confirmed to exhibit a male-heterogametic XX/XY system, in which males grow significantly faster than females, making all-male culture economically advantageous in aquaculture.15,16 Similarly, the channel catfish Ictalurus punctatus has been shown to possess an XX/XY sex determination system, although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely resolved.17,18,19 Genomic analyses of the sex chromosome region in I. punctatus revealed that the X and Y chromosomes share largely identical gene content; however, allele-specific hypermethylation on the X chromosome leads to silencing of the hydin-1 gene, whereas the Y-linked allele remains transcriptionally active. This monoallelic expression pattern, resembling genomic imprinting, has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying sex determination in this species.20 XX/XY systems have also been reported in other catfish species. In certain Hungarian strains of the African catfish Clarias gariepinus, male heterogamety was confirmed using male-specific DNA markers.21 Chromosome-level genome assembly of Clarias fuscus revealed the presence of differentiated sex chromosome regions,22 while genome-wide SNP analyses in Clarias macrocephalus likewise supported a male-heterogametic system.23 Moreover, Pelteobagrus vachelli exhibits an XX/XY system in which males display faster growth rates than females, highlighting its considerable potential for mono-sex aquaculture.24 The family Sisoridae represents one of the largest and most diverse groups of Asian catfishes, with the majority of species distributed across river systems of the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and the eastern Himalayas.25,26 Despite their high species diversity, studies on sex determination mechanisms in sisorid catfishes remain remarkably scarce compared with those in other catfish families. Advancing research in this area is essential not only for understanding reproductive biology and evolutionary processes but also for conservation and aquaculture applications.27 G. maculatum, an ecologically and economically important sisorid species endemic to Xizang, exhibits pronounced sexual dimorphism.26 Elucidating its sex determination mechanism is therefore particularly important for endangered species conservation, as such knowledge can facilitate the development of targeted breeding and management strategies. From an aquaculture perspective, many catfish species display strong sexual dimorphism in economically important traits such as growth rate and body size, making mono-sex culture highly desirable.28,29,30 For instance, females grow faster than males in Silurus meridionalis,30 whereas males exhibit superior growth performance in Leiocassis longirostris and Pelteobagrus fulvidraco.15,28,31 Consequently, the development of rapid and accurate sex-specific molecular markers is critical for implementing sex control in breeding programs.32,33 Although SNP-based PCR methods for sex identification have been established in Ictalurus punctatus18,32 and high-density genetic maps have been constructed in yellow catfish to localize sex-related loci,34 such studies remain limited within Sisoridae. Although sex determination systems have been extensively studied in several model and commercially important fish species, studies focusing on Sisoridae remain extremely limited, and the underlying genetic mechanisms are still poorly understood. This lack of knowledge is particularly evident for G. maculatum, for which no sex-linked genomic regions or molecular markers have been reported to date.

Given the ecological importance and aquaculture potential of G. maculatum, as well as the virtual absence of sex determination studies within the family Sisoridae and the current lack of any sex-linked genomic information for this species, this study aims to identify sex-linked SNPs and indels using whole-genome resequencing combined with pool-seq analysis, and to determine its genetic sex determination system. Furthermore, we develop sex-specific molecular markers based on amplicon sequencing for early sex identification in juveniles, offering a molecular foundation for artificial mono-sex breeding and future comparative studies on sex determination mechanisms across Sisoridae fishes.

Population genetic structure

Principal component analysis (PCA) using genome-wide SNPs and indels revealed no clear genetic separation between male and female individuals (Figures 3A and 3B), indicating minimal genome-wide differentiation between sexes and supporting the hypothesis that the sex chromosomes of G. maculatum remain at an early stage of differentiation.Figure 3Population genetic structure of male and female G. maculatum individuals(A) PCA scatterplot based on the filtered SNPs.(B) PCA scatterplot based on the filtered indels.

To assess whether the subtle sex-based differentiation was driven by variants in the sex-determining region (SDR), we re-ran PCA after excluding SDR-associated variants. The PCA clustering patterns remained largely unchanged, suggesting that genome-wide variation, rather than SDR variants alone, contributed minimally to the observed structure. Furthermore, we quantified the proportion of variance explained by SDR versus non-SDR autosomal regions: SDR variants accounted for 0.67% of the total variance, while non-SDR autosomal regions explained the remaining 99.33%, confirming the limited contribution of SDR variants to overall population structure. To mitigate the influence of missing or uncertain genotypes, we also conducted PCA using ANGSD, which incorporates genotype likelihoods. The results were consistent with the filtered SNP-based PCA, and outlier points were substantially reduced, supporting the robustness of our population structure analyses.

Localization of the sex-determining region

Collectively, these results indicate that ChrLG01 is the candidate sex chromosome in G. maculatum, with the putative SDR located at ChrLG01:2,700,000–2,800,000 base pair (bp). Sex differentiation analysis based on Fst revealed a significant peak (> 0.25) on LG01, suggesting that this chromosome harbors sex-linked loci (Figure 4A). Using PSASS and pooled BAM files, we further confirmed that the region between 2,700,000 and 2,800,000 bp on LG01 exhibited a strong sex-specific signal in Fst, SNP frequency, and coverage patterns (Figures 4B–4D). From this region, we identified 195 male-specific SNPs and 20 male-specific indels, in contrast to only 2 female-specific SNPs and 1 indel (Figure 5). Validation of sex-linked markers

To validate the SDR, we performed targeted multiplex PCR sequencing for 53 candidate sex-linked variants (48 SNPs and 5 indels) within LG01:27,399,813–28,190,114. The average sequencing depth exceeded 5,000×, with each marker covered by > 100× in all samples (Table 3), indicating high sequencing accuracy and data robustness. Among 29 male individuals, 27 (93.1%) exhibited heterozygous genotypes (XY) across all 53 markers. The remaining 2 males showed homozygosity in 18 upstream markers (27,399,813–27,649,471) and heterozygosity in 35 downstream markers (27,671,222–28,190,114). Conversely, 25 out of 26 female individuals (96.2%) showed homozygosity (XX) at all 53 markers, while 1 female had heterozygosity in the downstream region (27,743,066–28,190,114) and homozygosity upstream (27,399,813–27,671,222) (Figure 6). Together, these results strongly confirm that the 53 markers are tightly linked to phenotypic sex and support the existence of a distinct SDR on LG01. The high concordance between genotype and phenotypic sex observed in the validation experiments is consistent with the stringent selection criteria applied during marker identification. By restricting candidate loci to those exhibiting strong sex-specific allele frequency differences within the SDR, the likelihood of false-positive markers was substantially reduced, thereby resulting in a high validation success rate. Discussion

Establishment of the sex-determining system in G. maculatum

Fish display remarkably diverse sex-determining systems, ranging from strictly genetic mechanisms (e.g., XX/XY or ZZ/ZW) to environmentally influenced and epigenetically regulated pathways.35,36,37 Among these, SNPs and indels represent high-frequency and evolutionarily stable genomic variants, making them particularly suitable for mapping sex-linked loci and elucidating underlying sex-determination mechanisms.38,39,40 In male-heterogametic systems (XX/XY), sex-linked SNPs and indels typically exhibit heterozygosity in males and homozygosity in females, whereas the opposite pattern is observed in female-heterogametic systems (ZZ/ZW).38,41,42,43 In this study, we identified a discrete and well-defined SDR on linkage group LG01 in G. maculatum, spanning from 2,700,000 to 2,800,000 bp. Within this SDR, a pronounced excess of male-specific variants was detected, including 195 male-specific SNPs and 20 male-specific indels, compared with only 2 SNPs and 1 indel specific to females, a pattern fully consistent with a male-heterogametic XX/XY system.44 Furthermore, validation through targeted amplicon sequencing demonstrated that 93.1% of male individuals were heterozygous at the 53 sex-linked loci, whereas 96.2% of females were homozygous, confirming tight linkage between genotype and phenotypic sex. The fact that these markers are consistently heterozygous in males and homozygous in females suggests that the male-determining Y chromosome has diverged from its homologous X counterpart in this SDR, while the rest of the genome remains largely undifferentiated. Together, these findings provide compelling genomic evidence for an XX/XY sex-determining system in G. maculatum, representing the first such report in a sisorid catfish. These findings contribute to a better understanding of sex-determination diversity in teleosts and offer crucial molecular tools for sex identification in this species.

Development and application of sex-specific markers

The identification and validation of sex-specific molecular markers have significant implications for aquaculture and conservation biology. Such markers facilitate early sex identification, allow for the implementation of sex-controlled breeding programs, and enable the study of sex-biased growth and physiological traits. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of this strategy across diverse fish taxa, including the identification of six male-specific markers in Spinibarbus hollandi13 or a highly significant sex-linked SNP on LG20 in Trachinotus blochii.45 In our study, 53 tightly sex-linked markers (48 SNPs and 5 indels) were validated in 29 male and 26 female G. maculatum individuals. The strong concordance between genotypic patterns and phenotypic sex (over 93% in both sexes) highlights the utility of these markers. The near-complete validation success rate of the selected markers is not incidental but rather reflects the rigorous marker selection strategy employed in this study. Specifically, candidate loci were filtered based on their localization within the SDR, high genetic differentiation (Fst), and strict allele frequency thresholds consistent with an XX/XY system. This multi-step screening approach effectively enriched for markers tightly linked to the sex-determining locus, thereby maximizing validation accuracy. Importantly, the locus at LG01:27,671,222 exhibited complete sex specificity, being heterozygous in all male individuals and homozygous in all females. Notably, the SNP at LG01:27,671,222 exhibited complete genotype-phenotype concordance in the validation set, with all males showing heterozygous genotypes and all females showing homozygous genotypes, suggesting that this locus represents an optimal candidate marker for practical sex identification. This site may serve as an optimal molecular marker for practical applications such as genetic screening and mono-sex fry production in aquaculture operations. Moreover, the existence of a small proportion of mismatched genotypes—6.9% of males and 3.8% of females—may reflect localized recombination events or transitional regions within the SDR, rather than simple false positives. This interpretation is supported by the consistency of genotype patterns across multiple adjacent markers and individuals, as well as the high overall validation accuracy achieved in this study. This pattern indicates that recombination suppression within the SDR is substantial but not yet complete, a feature that is a hallmark of early-stage sex chromosome evolution. Such incomplete recombination suppression is widely recognized as a hallmark of early-stage sex chromosome evolution in teleost fishes. In many species, X and Y chromosomes remain partially recombining, and only a limited genomic region surrounding the sex-determining locus exhibits differentiation. Therefore, the observed genotype inconsistencies are more plausibly explained by ongoing recombination processes rather than technical artifacts. However, it should be noted that the present study primarily focused on the identification and validation of sex-linked markers, and did not aim to functionally characterize the underlying genes within the SDR. Although these markers show strong association with phenotypic sex, their direct functional relevance to sex determination remains unclear. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that all individuals used in this study were sampled from a single breeding population at the Yarlung Zangbo River Fishery Resources Breeding Base. Although the high concordance between genotype and phenotypic sex (93.1% in males and 96.2% in females) is encouraging, the universal diagnostic value of these markers across geographically distinct populations of G. maculatum remains to be confirmed. Population-level variation in allele frequencies at sex-linked loci has been reported in other fish species, and cross-population validation will therefore be an important step before these markers can be recommended for broad aquaculture and conservation applications.

Evidence for early-stage sex chromosome differentiation

The sex chromosomes of many teleosts, including catfish, remain poorly differentiated and often morphologically indistinct—classified as homomorphic sex chromosomes. This is in stark contrast to the highly heteromorphic sex chromosomes observed in mammals and birds. In fish, recombination suppression between X and Y (or Z and W) chromosomes typically evolves gradually, starting at a sex-determining locus and spreading outward over evolutionary time.46,47,48,49,50,51 In our study, the presence of sex-reversed genotypes in a small subset of individuals provides compelling evidence for incomplete suppression of recombination. Specifically, two male individuals showed loss of heterozygosity in the upstream part of the SDR, and one female individual exhibited unexpected heterozygosity downstream, suggesting that recombination had occurred between the sex chromosomes in these regions. These atypical genotype patterns are unlikely to represent false positives, but instead provide molecular evidence of recombination events within the SDR. We acknowledge alternative explanations for these atypical genotype patterns, including potential phenotypic sex misclassification, sample mix-up or contamination, local mapping artifacts, or sex reversal. However, several lines of evidence suggest that incomplete recombination suppression is the most likely explanation. First, the phenotypic sex of all individuals was carefully determined through gonadal dissection, minimizing the likelihood of misclassification. Second, strict laboratory procedures were followed during DNA extraction and sequencing to prevent sample mix-up and contamination. Third, potential mapping artifacts were mitigated by stringent variant filtering criteria, and the high concordance between whole-genome resequencing and high-depth amplicon sequencing results further reduces the likelihood of systematic errors. Although sex reversal is known to occur in fish, the observed genotype-phenotype discordance in only a few individuals and within specific regions of the SDR is more consistent with incomplete recombination suppression during early sex chromosome differentiation, rather than widespread sex reversal. Similar patterns have been reported in other teleosts, such as Oncorhynchus mykiss, supporting this interpretation.

The fact that these patterns occur in contiguous genomic regions, rather than as isolated random loci, further supports the presence of recombination breakpoints or transitional zones. Based on these recombination patterns, we infer a putative pseudoautosomal boundary located between 27,649,471 and 27,743,066 bp on LG01. Structural variants such as chromosomal inversions may also contribute to recombination suppression within the SDR; however, reliable detection of such variants was beyond the resolution of the present short-read dataset and should be addressed in future studies using long-read sequencing. This boundary likely separates the differentiated SDR from the pseudoautosomal region (PAR), where X-Y recombination persists. These observations are consistent with findings in other fish species, such as Oncorhynchus mykiss, where most of the Y chromosome still recombines with its X counterpart, and only a small non-recombining region harbors the sex-determining gene.52,53 Similar patterns of low differentiation and active recombination have also been reported in Pelteobagrus fulvidraco54,55 and Hypostomus albopunctatus.56 In future work, the application of long-read sequencing technologies to resolve X- and Y-linked haplotypes within the SDR, coupled with divergence time estimation, will provide direct molecular evolutionary evidence for the early-stage differentiation of sex chromosomes in this species. Together with the PCA results showing no clear genome-wide separation between sexes, our data strongly suggest that the sex chromosomes of G. maculatum are at an early stage of differentiation. We acknowledge that the relatively moderate sequencing depth (3–5×) of individual whole-genome resequencing data in this study may introduce some uncertainty in genotype calling at the individual level. To minimize this effect, several strategies were employed. First, our analyses primarily focused on population-level genetic patterns, such as Fst and sex-specific allele frequency differences, which are generally robust to random errors in low-depth sequencing data. Second, stringent variant filtering criteria were applied (minimum read depth ≥ 10, maximum missing rate ≤ 10%, and minimum quality score ≥ 30) to ensure high-confidence SNP and indel datasets. Third, our key findings were independently validated using amplicon-based targeted sequencing, with an average sequencing depth exceeding 5000× per sample (Table 3), providing strong support for the association between the 53 sex-linked markers and phenotypic sex. Together, these multiple lines of evidence support the robustness of our conclusions. Future studies will benefit from higher sequencing depth and genotype likelihood-based approaches to further refine recombination boundaries and candidate sex-determining loci. Future research should prioritize comprehensive gene annotation within the SDR, functional validation of candidate sex-determining genes, and comparative synteny analyses with closely related species to elucidate the evolutionary history and molecular mechanisms underlying sex determination in sisorid fishes. Although a comprehensive annotation of the SDR is beyond the scope of the present study, preliminary inspection of this genomic region suggests that it may harbor genes or regulatory elements homologous to those involved in known vertebrate sex-determination pathways, such as DMRT1 and SOX9, or other factors associated with gonadal differentiation. These genes have been widely implicated in sex determination across teleosts and other vertebrates, indicating that the SDR identified here may represent a functionally relevant genomic region. However, detailed gene annotation, transcriptomic integration, and functional validation will be required to confirm their roles. In addition, resolving the functional genomic landscape of the SDR, including gene content and regulatory elements associated with the identified markers, will be essential for linking genetic variation to phenotypic sex determination.

Limitations of the study

Although our genomic analyses and marker validation support an XX/XY sex determination system in Glyptosternum maculatum, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study focused on identifying and validating sex-linked genomic markers and did not functionally characterize candidate genes within the SDR; therefore, the causal gene(s) underlying sex determination remain to be determined. Second, all individuals were sampled from a single breeding population, and the diagnostic performance of the identified markers across geographically distinct populations remains to be evaluated. Third, the moderate sequencing depth of individual whole-genome resequencing data may introduce some uncertainty in individual genotype calling, although stringent filtering and independent high-depth amplicon sequencing were used to minimize this limitation. Future studies integrating comprehensive SDR annotation, transcriptomic and functional analyses, long-read sequencing, and cross-population validation will help resolve the molecular basis and evolutionary history of sex determination in this species.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive evidence for an XX/XY genetic sex-determination system in G. maculatum. Using genome-wide resequencing and population-level variant analyses, we identified linkage group LG01 as the candidate sex chromosome. We localized a well-defined SDR spanning 2,700,000–2,800,000 bp. Within this SDR, 53 tightly sex-linked molecular markers (48 SNPs and 5 indels) were identified and validated, exhibiting strong genotype-phenotype associations across independent individuals. The SNP locus at LG01:27,671,222 emerged as a highly reliable sex marker, showing complete discrimination between male and female genotypes, and represents a promising tool for early sex identification and the development of mono-sex breeding strategies. Furthermore, the presence of genotype recombination in a small number of individuals indicates incomplete recombination suppression, suggesting that sex chromosome differentiation in G. maculatum is still in its nascent evolutionary stage. Overall, the results presented here not only provide practical molecular tools for aquaculture and conservation of this economically and ecologically important species but also contribute to our understanding of the diversity and evolution of sex-determination mechanisms in teleosts. Future research should focus on expanding population sampling and employing functional genomics approaches to unravel the key genes and regulatory elements involved in sex determination and chromosome differentiation in G. maculatum. Particular emphasis should be placed on investigating the functional context of the identified sex-linked markers within the SDR. In particular, future efforts should include systematic gene annotation within the SDR, identification of candidate sex-determining genes, and comparative synteny analyses with related siluriform genomes. These approaches will help to clarify whether conserved or lineage-specific genetic mechanisms underlie sex determination in G. maculatum and will provide deeper evolutionary insights into sex chromosome differentiation in Sisoridae. Future studies integrating functional genomics, long-read sequencing, and comparative evolutionary analyses will be essential for identifying the master sex-determining gene(s) and reconstructing the molecular timeline of sex chromosome evolution in G. maculatum. These approaches will also help detect potential structural variants within the SDR, such as inversions, that may underlie recombination suppression. In addition, cross-population validation of the identified sex-linked markers across geographically distinct populations—including those from the upper and middle reaches of the Yarlung Zangbo River and its major tributaries—will be critical for assessing the universal diagnostic applicability of these markers across the natural range of the species.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals and sample collection

A total of 95 Glyptosternon maculatum individuals were sampled from the Yarlung Zangbo River Fishery Resources Breeding Base, including 46 phenotypic females and 49 phenotypic males. The animals were sexually mature fish, as confirmed by gonadal anatomical examination following anesthesia with MS-222. No genetically modified or experimentally engineered animals were used in this study. Individual genomic genotypes were determined through whole-genome resequencing and downstream variant-calling procedures. Muscle tissue samples were collected from each individual and preserved in anhydrous ethanol at −80°C for subsequent genomic DNA extraction. Sex was the primary biological variable in this study because the genomic analyses were specifically designed to identify genetic variation associated with phenotypic sex and to characterize the genetic sex determination system. Comparisons between male and female individuals were therefore central to the identification of sex-linked genomic regions and the development and validation of sex-linked molecular markers. The sex composition of the samples was maintained throughout the whole-genome resequencing, Pool-seq, and marker-validation analyses.

Ethical approval

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Fisheries Science, Xizang Autonomous Region Academy of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Sciences (Ethics Approval No. 2024002), and were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and regulatory requirements.

Method details

DNA extraction and experimental design

Approximately 100 mg of muscle tissue from each individual was finely ground in liquid nitrogen using a sterilized mortar and pestle. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol. DNA quality and integrity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, while DNA purity and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For whole-genome resequencing, DNA samples from 20 female individuals (C1–C20) and 20 male individuals (X1–X20) were randomly selected for individual sequencing. In parallel, pooled DNA samples were prepared for the female group (CH; C1–C20) and male group (XH; X1–X20), with 100 ng DNA contributed by each individual, to facilitate high-depth Pool-seq analysis of sex-associated genomic variation. The remaining 26 female and 29 male individuals were used for subsequent validation of sex-linked markers by multiplex PCR-based targeted sequencing.

Whole-genome resequencing and sequence quality control

Genomic DNA libraries were prepared using standard protocols for the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform. DNA was fragmented to 300–350 bp using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode), and libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit (ND607), including end repair, dA-tailing, adapter ligation, and two rounds of magnetic bead purification. Following PCR enrichment and quantification, DNA nanoballs were generated by rolling-circle amplification and sequenced using the combinatorial probe-anchor synthesis (cPAS) technique.

Raw sequencing reads were processed using Fastp v0.23.4. Reads containing >40% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 15), >5 ambiguous bases (N), or shorter than 15 bp were removed. Adapter sequences were trimmed from both read ends. The resulting high-quality clean reads were aligned to the publicly available G. maculatum reference genome using BWA v0.7.17. Duplicate reads were identified and removed using GATK MarkDuplicates. SNP and indel variants were subsequently called using GATK HaplotypeCaller under default parameter settings.

Population genetic structure analysis

Filtered SNP and indel datasets were used to evaluate genome-wide population genetic structure between male and female individuals. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using VCF2PCACluster v1.38. A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.25, and the resulting tree was visualized using Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) v6.8.

To further evaluate the robustness of the population structure analysis in the presence of low-depth and uncertain genotypes, an additional PCA based on genotype likelihoods was performed using ANGSD v0.940. The results were compared with the PCA obtained from the filtered SNP dataset. The current Results indicate that ANGSD-based PCA produced patterns consistent with the filtered SNP-based PCA and reduced the number of outlier points.

Identification of sex-linked genomic regions and candidate variants

High-confidence SNPs and indels were obtained from the whole-genome resequencing data of 20 female and 20 male individuals using VCFtools v0.1.16. Variants were retained using a minimum read depth of ≥10, a maximum missing rate of ≤10%, and a minimum quality score (QUAL) of ≥30. Fst values were calculated to assess genetic differentiation between the male and female groups.

Sex-linked genomic regions were further identified using PSASS (Population-Specific Allele Structure Scanner) v1.0 based on pooled BAM files from the female (CH) and male (XH) groups. Under a canonical XX/XY system, allele frequencies were expected to be approximately 1.0 in the homogametic sex (XX females) and 0.5 in the heterogametic sex (XY males). Accordingly, candidate male-linked SNPs were defined using an allele frequency of ≤0.2 in females and 0.5 in males. Candidate female-linked SNPs were defined using an allele frequency of ≥0.8 in females and ≤0.2 in males. SNPs occurring at frequencies >80% in both sexes were considered common variants.

Candidate loci were not selected randomly. Only variants satisfying the predefined sex-specific allele-frequency thresholds and located within the identified sex-determining region (SDR) were retained for downstream marker development and validation.

Multiplex PCR-based targeted sequencing for marker validation

A total of 53 sex-linked variants, comprising 48 SNPs and 5 indels, were selected within the SDR for multiplex PCR amplification. The corresponding marker information and primer sequences are provided in Table 1. The marker region spanned LG01:27,399,813–28,190,114. Candidate loci were selected based on strong sex-specific allele-frequency differences and high Fst values within the SDR.

Primers were designed for each candidate variant as listed in Table 1. Universal Illumina adapter sequences were added to the 5′ ends of the forward primer (5′-ACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-3′) and reverse primer (5′-CTTGGCACCCGAGAATTCCA-3′). Amplicon libraries were constructed from DNA obtained from 29 male and 26 female individuals and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Sequencing reads were demultiplexed, aligned to the reference genome, and used to determine genotypes at the target loci for evaluation of marker–sex associations and validation of the proposed XX/XY system.

To further validate the most sex-specific candidate locus, an independent PCR assay targeting LG01:27,671,222 was performed using five male and five female individuals. The forward primer was 5′-TCCCCCATCTTTATCGGTAGTC-3′ and the reverse primer was 5′-ACCATCACTGTTTCAGCTTGC-3′. The expected amplicon length was 178 bp, with an annealing temperature of 58°C. PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. Sequence chromatograms were visually inspected to determine whether the target locus was homozygous or heterozygous.

All genomic coordinates of the SDR and sex-linked markers reported in this study are based on the publicly available G. maculatum reference genome assembly.

Quantification and statistical analysis

High-quality variants were filtered using a minimum read depth of ≥10, a maximum missing rate of ≤10%, and a minimum QUAL score of ≥30. Genetic differentiation between male and female groups was assessed using Fst. Genome-wide population structure was evaluated using PCA based on filtered SNP and indel datasets, and robustness was additionally assessed using genotype-likelihood-based PCA implemented in ANGSD v940.

Sex-linked candidate variants were identified using predefined sex-specific allele-frequency thresholds and their localization within the SDR. Candidate markers were subsequently validated using targeted amplicon sequencing in 29 males and 26 females. The genotype–phenotype association of the most sex-specific locus, LG01:27,671,222, was further assessed by PCR and Sanger sequencing in five males and five females.

The key genomic analyses in this study were based on population-level genetic differentiation, allele-frequency patterns, variant filtering, and genotype–phenotype concordance rather than conventional group-comparison tests represented by significance asterisks. No pooled-data mean ± SD/SEM error-bar statistics were used in the current figures. Where applicable, the definitions of graphical summaries and analytical measures are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
