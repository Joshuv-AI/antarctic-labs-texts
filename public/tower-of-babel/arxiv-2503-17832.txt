Multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of derivative type
Thomas Wolfs

Abstract
We characterize the biorthogonal ensembles that are both a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble and a polynomial ensemble of derivative type (also called a Pólya ensemble). We focus on the notions of multiplicative and additive derivative type that typically appear in connection with products and sums of random matrices respectively. Essential in the characterization is the use of the Mellin and Laplace transform: we show that the derivative type structure, which is a priori analytic in nature, becomes algebraic after applying the appropriate transform. Afterwards, we use the characterization to show that the eigenvalue densities of products of JUE and LUE matrices are essentially the only multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type. We also show that the eigenvalue densities of sums of dilated LUE and GUE matrices are examples of multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble of additive derivative type, but provide other examples as well. Finally, we explain how these notions of derivative type can be used to provide a partial solution to an open problem related to orthogonality of the finite finite free multiplicative and additive convolution of polynomials from finite free probability. In particular, we obtain families of multiple orthogonal polynomials that (de)compose naturally using these convolutions. Keywords: random matrices, polynomial ensembles, Mellin transform, Laplace transform, finite free probability, multiple orthogonal polynomials.

Introduction

Over the last decade, a program was set out to obtain explicit expressions for the density of the eigenvalues of products and sums of random matrices. One of the first steps was taken in
[
1
]
as Akemann and Burda obtained an expression for the density of the eigenvalues of products of square Ginibre matrices. A (complex) Ginibre matrix is a random matrix whose entries are independent standard complex Gaussian random variables, see
[
16
]
. Afterwards, in
[
3
]
, Akemann, Kieburg and Wei also managed to describe the squared singular values of products of square, and later in
[
2
]
, rectangular, Ginibre matrices. Since this problem turned out to be more natural, see also
[
32
]
, at least for products of random matrices focus shifted more towards their squared singular values rather than their eigenvalues. In this direction, an important milestone was achieved in
[
31
]
as Kuijlaars and Stivigny showed that it is possible to describe the squared singular values of products of any random matrix for which the squared singular values follow a so-called polynomial ensemble, with Ginibre matrices. Later in
[
24
]
, it was shown by Kieburg, Kuijlaars and Stivigny that one can also handle products with truncated Haar distributed random unitary matrices instead of Ginibre matrices. We refer to
[
27
]
for an introduction to polynomial ensembles. The definition of a polynomial ensemble is as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
x\mapsto x^{k-1}v_{j}(x)
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for all
j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. The polynomial ensemble associated to
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is the probability density
\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(x_{k}-x_{j})\det[v_{j}(x_{k})]_{j,k=1}^{n}\geq 0,\quad\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1)

where
Z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
is the normalization constant. We will write
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]
to indicate that an
n\times n
random matrix
X
has joint eigenvalue density (
1
).

Polynomial ensembles arise as a special class of biorthogonal ensembles, see
[
9
,
31
]
, and are therefore determinantal point processes, see
[
18
]
for an introduction. A large class of polynomial ensembles are the so-called multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles. They were introduced in
[
26
]
(see also
[
8
]
) and generalize the well-studied class of orthogonal polynomial ensembles (
r=1
), also known as Coulomb gasses with
\beta=2
, see
[
14
]
for an introduction. The correlation kernel for a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble is completely determined by the multiple orthogonal polynomials associated to the underlying system of weights, see
[
13
]
. Often, it is convenient to identify a given ensemble as a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble, because then a Riemann-Hilbert problem becomes available, which can be used to study the asymptotics of the kernel, see, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
4
,
7
]
,
[
28
,
29
]
and
[
33
,
34
]
.

We will use the following definition of a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble. Note that, unlike in
[
26
]
, we assumed that the associated multi-index is on the step-line

\mathcal{S}^{r}=\{\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\mid n_{1}\geq\dots\geq n_{r}\geq n_{1}-1\}.

This is standard practice when working with multiple orthogonal polynomials. Doing so, there is no ambiguity in the condition
n=\left|\vec{n}\right|
as there is a unique multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
for which this equality holds.

Definition 1.2
.

We call the polynomial ensemble associated with
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble with respect to
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, if

\displaystyle\text{span}\{v_{j}(x)\mid j=1,\dots,n\}=\text{span}\{x^{k-1}w_{j}(x)\mid k=1,\dots,n_{j},j=1,\dots,r\},

for (the unique)
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
n=\left|\vec{n}\right|
. In that case, we write

\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]=\text{MOPE}[n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r}].

If
r=1
, we opt to write
\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]=\text{OPE}[n;w_{1}]
.

For our convenience, given
r
functions
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}:X\to\mathbb{R}
with
X\subset\mathbb{R}
, we will use the notation

\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})=\text{span}\{X\to\mathbb{R}:x\mapsto x^{k-1}w_{j}(x)\mid k=1,\dots,n_{j},j=1,\dots,r\},

where
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
is such that
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
. Observe that there is some freedom in how the underlying functions are chosen as
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{0,1},\dots,w_{0,r})=\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
whenever
(w_{0,j})_{j=1}^{n}=(w_{j})_{j=1}^{n}U
and
U
is an invertible upper-triangular
r\times r
matrix. The functions
(w_{0,j})_{j=1}^{n}
and
(w_{j})_{j=1}^{n}U
therefore give rise to the same multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble.

The joint eigenvalue densities of Jacobi Unitary Ensemble (JUE), Laguerre Unitary Ensemble (LUE) and Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) are well-known examples of orthogonal polynomial ensembles and their correlation kernels involve the classical Jacobi, Laguerre and Hermite orthogonal polynomials respectively. Below, we recall the structure of these densities (which we can also see as the definition of the corresponding matrix ensembles).

Example 1.3
.

Let
b>a>-1
and let
X
be an
n\times n
JUE matrix with parameters
(a,b)
. We will also denote this as
X\sim\text{JUE}[n;a,b]
. Then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{B}^{a,b}]
in terms of the beta density
\mathcal{B}^{a,b}(x)=x^{a}(1-x)^{b-a-1}
on
(0,1)
.

It is well-known that the squared singular values of a truncated Haar distributed random unitary matrix follow a JUE, see, e.g.,
[
31
, §3.1]
.

Example 1.4
.

Let
a>-1
and let
X
be an
n\times n
LUE matrix with parameter
a
. We will also denote this as
X\sim\text{LUE}[n;a]
. Then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{G}^{a}]
in terms of the gamma density
\mathcal{G}^{a}(x)=x^{a}e^{-x}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

It is well-known that the squared singular values of a Ginibre matrix follow a LUE, see, e.g.,
[
31
, §3.3]
.

Example 1.5
.

Let
X
be an
n\times n
GUE matrix. We will also denote this as
X\sim\text{GUE}[n]
. Then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{G}]
in terms of the Gaussian
\mathcal{G}(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
on
\mathbb{R}
.

The next milestone was achieved in
[
22
]
as Kieburg and Kösters showed that instead of considering products of a random matrix for which the squared singular values follow a polynomial ensemble, with Ginibre and truncated random unitary matrices, one can actually handle products with any random matrices for which the squared singular values follow a so-called polynomial ensemble of derivative type. The JUE and LUE are examples of such ensembles, as explained in
[
15
, Ex. 2.4
d)
&
c)
]
. In this multiplicative setting, we will define the notion of polynomial ensembles of derivative type through sets of functions of multiplicative derivative type, which we will introduce first. In the literature, typically only the associated polynomial ensemble is considered, but for our central result, the underlying functions don’t necessarily have to give rise to a
positive
polynomial ensemble.

Recall that a function
f:X\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is locally absolutely continuous on
X\subset\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
f\in AC_{\text{loc}}(X)
, if and only if there exists
g\in L^{1}(X)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that
f(x)=\alpha+\int_{1}^{x}g(t)dt
for all
x\in X
, and that in that case,
f
is differentiable a.e. on
X
with a.e.
f^{\prime}=g
.

Definition 1.6
.

We call
n
functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
of multiplicative derivative type with respect to a function
\omega\in C^{n-2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, with
\omega^{(n-2)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, if

\displaystyle\text{span}\{v_{j}(x)\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{(x\frac{d}{dx})^{j-1}[\omega(x)]\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type now arise from functions of multiplicative derivative type, whenever they give rise to a
positive
polynomial ensemble.

Definition 1.7
.

We call the polynomial ensemble associated with
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
of multiplicative derivative type with respect to
\omega:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
, if the underlying functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
have this property. In that case, we write

\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]=\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega].

Unfortunately, in the literature on polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type, there is some ambiguity about which space
\omega^{(n-2)}
belongs to. Originally, in
[
22
]
, Kieburg and Kösters demanded that
\omega_{n}^{(n-2)}\in AC(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. This is equivalent to the above because implicitly through Definition
1.1
, we assume that
\omega_{n}^{(n-1)}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. Later in
[
15
]
, Förster, Kieburg and Kösters demanded the stronger condition that
\omega_{n}^{(n-2)}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
. For our purposes, it is important that weights of multiplicative derivative type are defined in the weaker way above as we want to focus solely on their structural properties and don’t want to involve any additional properties coming from the associated polynomial ensemble.

The next milestone was achieved in
[
30
]
as Kuijlaars and Román provided the analogue for the eigenvalues of sums of random matrices. This work was preceded by
[
11
]
, where Claeys, Kuijlaars and Wang described the eigenvalues of the sum of a random matrix for which the eigenvalues follow a polynomial ensemble and a random matrix for which the eigenvalues follow a GUE. Generally, in the additive setting, it is possible to describe the eigenvalues of sums of a random matrix for which the eigenvalues follow a polynomial ensemble, with random matrices for which the eigenvalues follow a polynomial ensemble of another kind of derivative type. As explained in
[
15
, Ex. 2.4
c)
&
b)
]
, the LUE and GUE are examples such ensembles. The precise definition of this additive derivative type is stated below. Similarly as before, our approach will be to define this notion through functions of additive derivative type, which we will introduce first.

Definition 1.8
.

We call
n
functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of additive derivative type with respect to a function
\omega\in C^{n-2}(\mathbb{R})
, with
\omega^{(n-2)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, if

\displaystyle\text{span}\{v_{j}(x)\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{\omega^{(j-1)}(x)\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Polynomial ensembles of additive derivative type now arise from functions of additive derivative type, whenever they give rise to a
positive
polynomial ensemble.

Definition 1.9
.

We call the polynomial ensemble associated with
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
of additive derivative type with respect to
\omega:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, if the underlying functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
have this property. In that case, we write

\text{PE}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]=\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega].

Again, in the literature, there is some minor ambiguity about which space
\omega^{(n-2)}
belongs to. Originally, in
[
30
]
, no explicit conditions on
\omega
are mentioned. Later in
[
15
]
, Förster, Kieburg and Kösters demanded that
\omega_{n}^{(n-2)}
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
. For our purposes and because of the similarities to the multiplicative setting, we prefer the conditions stated above, which are less strong.

By now, polynomial ensembles of multiplicative and additive derivative type are relatively well-understood: in
[
15
]
, the functions
\omega
in Definition
1.6
&
1.8
that give rise to a
positive
polynomial ensemble of derivative type were characterized in terms of Pólya frequency functions. We refer to
[
19
, Ch. 7]
for an introduction to Pólya frequency functions. As a consequence, such polynomial ensembles of derivative type are sometimes called Pólya ensembles.

Recently, several other notions of derivative type, compatible with other symmetry classes of random matrices, have been introduced as well. For example in
[
15
]
, notions for random complex rectangular, Hermitian anti-symmetric and Hermitian anti-self-dual matrices were defined. In
[
25
]
, a notion of derivative type compatible with random unitary matrices was considered.

2
Main results

Initially, the main goal of this work was to provide many examples of multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of derivative type. This was motivated by the fact that the associated multiple orthogonal polynomials would have desirable (de)composition properties with respect to the finite free multiplicative and additive convolution from finite free probability, see
[
39
]
. Such (de)composition properties have recently received increased attention from the orthogonal polynomial community, because they are helpful in studying the properties of their zeros as explained in
[
41
,
42
]
. A deeper connection between orthogonality and (de)compositions using the finite free multiplicative and additive convolution is suggested by an open problem posed by Martínez-Finkelshtein at the Hypergeometric and Orthogonal Polynomials Event in Nijmegen (May, 2024), see
[
40
]
. Remarkably, after making the appropriate identifications, the open problem turns out to be equivalent to the one that drove the previously mentioned research around products and sums of random matrices. We will make this connection precise in Section
5
. Doing so, we can formulate a partial solution to the open problem.

Ultimately, our study of multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of derivative type did not only lead to many new examples, but to a characterization of them. It turns out that the eigenvalue densities of products of JUE and LUE matrices are essentially the only multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type and that the eigenvalue densities of sums of dilated LUE and GUE matrices are examples of multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble of additive derivative type, but not the only ones. By identifying such ensembles as multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles, a Riemann-Hilbert problem becomes available to perform an asymptotic analysis of the associated correlation kernel. Previously, such a connection was only known for the squared singular value densities of products of Ginibre matrices, see
[
32
]
. Such densities arise as particular cases of the eigenvalue densities of products of LUE matrices.

Essential in the characterization, is the use of an appropriate integral transform. In the multiplicative setting, this role is played by the Mellin transform. In the additive setting, the Laplace transform is used. These transforms have several useful properties, which we will discuss in Section
3
. The idea of using such integral transforms is that the derivative type structure, which is a priori analytic in nature, becomes algebraic after applying the appropriate transform. The latter will be more prone to analysis and will reveal some implicit structure on the functions associated to a polynomial ensemble of derivative type. Results of this kind will be proven in Section
6.1
. In the particular case of a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble of derivative type, the algebraic structure will enable a characterization of the underlying weights.

A class of weights that give rise to multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type is described in the result below. We will prove this in Section
6.2.1
. The appropriate space
L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
to which these weights should belong will be defined in Section
3.1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n

w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
are given by

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s)=c^{s}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\Gamma(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

(2)

for all
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, and the Mellin transform of
\omega_{1,n}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
is given by

\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(s)=c^{s}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\Gamma(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\Gamma(s+b_{i}+n_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

(3)

for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
b_{i},c\in\mathbb{R}
,
d_{1},d_{2}:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1\}
with all
d_{1}(i)=1
or all
d_{2}(i)=1
. Then
\text{MOPE}[n;w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r}]=\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega_{1,n}]
.

Remark 2.2
.

An alternative way to write down (
2

\vec{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}

\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}

\sigma:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{1,\dots,q\}

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s)=c^{s}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma(s+a_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}\Gamma(s+b_{i})}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\sigma(j)}(s+b_{i})},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Although this seems more insightful, it would be more involved to work with in the proofs.

with

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s)=c^{s}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma(s+a_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}\Gamma(s+b_{i})}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\max\{j,q\}}(s+b_{i})},\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\sigma(j)}(s+b_{i})},

in terms of an increasing and surjective function

).

Several examples of the ensembles described in Theorem

ii)
and

arise as the joint eigenvalue densities of sums of shifted LUE and GUE matrices, but generically, there are other examples as well. We will explain this in detail in Section

.

Observe that in Theorem
2.3
&

, we didn’t impose any explicit conditions on the parameters that can appear in (

), (

), (

) & (

). Results of this kind will be discussed in Section

In the multiplicative setting, the only any point orthogonal polynomial ensembles of derivative type are the eigenvalue densities of JUE and LUE matrices. We will show this in Section

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we will review some properties of the Mellin and Laplace transform. These transforms will play an essential role in the study of functions of multiplicative and additive derivative type. Our main reference for the results involving the Mellin transform is
[
10
]
, which serves as an excellent introduction to the topic. Under the appropriate conditions, many of the results discussed there also carry over to the Laplace transform.

3.1
Mellin transform

The Mellin transform of a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

(\mathcal{M}f)(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{s-1}dx,

whenever this integral exists. For convenience, we will work with functions in the space

L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})=\{f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(x)x^{s-1}|dx<\infty,\ s\in\Sigma\},

where
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an interval (or, if explicitly mentioned, a singleton). Clearly, instead of considering the interval
\Sigma
, we may also consider the strip
\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
in the right half of the complex plane. We may rephrase our results for intervals
-\Sigma
in the left half of the complex plane by making use of the fact that
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
if and only if
\tilde{f}\in L_{\mathcal{M},-\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
with
\tilde{f}(x)=f(1/x)
.

The elementary examples below will play a role in the characterization of sets of multiplicative derivative type.

Example 3.1

(Eq. 5.2.1,
[
44
]
)
.

Let
b>a>-1
. The beta density
\mathcal{B}^{a,b}(x)=x^{a}(1-x)^{b-a-1}
on
(0,1)
has Mellin transform

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{B}^{a,b}(s)=\Gamma(b-a)\frac{\Gamma(s+a)}{\Gamma(s+b)},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

Example 3.2

(Eq. 5.12.1,
[
44
]
)
.

Let
a>-1
. The gamma density
\mathcal{G}^{a}(x)=x^{a}e^{-x}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
has Mellin transform

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{G}^{a}(s)=\Gamma(s+a),\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

The following properties of the Mellin transform are well-known and will be used extensively throughout this work (see
[
10
, Thm. 1, 3 & 7]
). First, we recall the fact that the Mellin transform of a function
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
is always analytic on the associated strip
\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
. Second, whenever
f,g\in L_{\mathcal{M},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, there exists a Mellin convolution

(f\ast_{\mathcal{M}}g)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)g\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)\frac{dt}{t},\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},

with the property that

(\mathcal{M}[f\ast_{\mathcal{M}}g])(s)=(\mathcal{M}f)(s)\cdot(\mathcal{M}g)(s),\quad s\in c+i\mathbb{R}.

Lastly, whenever
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
\mathcal{M}f\in L^{1}(c+i\mathbb{R})
, there is an inverse transform

f(x)=\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\mathcal{M}f(s)x^{-s}\frac{ds}{2\pi i},\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

In particular, this implies that the Mellin transform is injective on
L_{\mathcal{M},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
in an a.e.-sense.

The results below are less standard, but will play an important role later. The first result, known as the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma for the Mellin transform, will be used to establish whether certain functions can appear as a Mellin transform.

Proposition 3.3

(Thm. 2,
[
10
]
)
.

Suppose that
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. Then, for every
s\in\Sigma
, we have

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\mathcal{M}f(s+it)=0.

The second result describes the behavior of the Mellin transform with respect to the differential operator that appears in Definition
1.6
. Typically the conditions on the underlying function are stronger and one can use integration by parts, but for us it is crucial that the conditions are minimal and that we have the stated equivalence.

Proposition 3.4

(Cor. 7,
[
10
]
)
.

Suppose that
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the differential operator
(Dg)(x)=-xg^{\prime}(x)
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

there exists
F\in C^{r-1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, with
F^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
D^{r}F\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, such that
f=F
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

ii)

there exists
g\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
such that
\mathcal{M}g(s)=s^{r}\mathcal{M}f(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
.

In that case,
g=D^{r}F
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

The last result handles uniqueness of solutions of first order difference equations that arise as Mellin transforms of positive functions. It essentially follows from the generalization of the Bohr-Mollerup theorem in
[
43
]
(see also
[
50
, Thm. 3.1]
for another formulation with slightly stronger conditions).

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose that
g:\mathbb{R}_{>s_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(n+1)/g(n)=1
. Then, up to a scalar multiplication, the difference equation
F(s+1)=g(s)F(s)
for
s>s_{0}
has at most one solution of the form
\mathcal{M}f
with
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\mathbb{R}_{>s_{0}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
f>0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proof.

We will first show the result in the special case that
s_{0}=0
. In fact, we will show something stronger, namely that, up to a scalar multiplication, there is at most one strictly positive log-convex solution. The fact that Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}f
of a function
f\in L_{\mathcal{M},\mathbb{R}_{>0}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
with
f>0
a.e. is log-convex follows in a straightforward way from Hölder’s inequality.
Note that by taking logarithms, any strictly positive log-convex solution of
F(s+1)=g(s)F(s)
for
s>0
, corresponds to a real-valued convex solution of
(\Delta F)(s)=\ln g(s)
for
s>0
(here
\Delta
denotes the forward difference operator
(\Delta F)(s)=F(s+1)-F(s)
). However, as
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Delta\ln g)(n)=0
, the latter are uniquely determined by
\ln g
up to an additive constant due to
[
43
, Thm. 1.4]
.

In order to extend to general
s_{0}\geq 0
, we note that any solution of
F(s+1)=g(s)F(s)
for
s>s_{0}
of the form
\mathcal{M}f
gives rise to a solution of
F(s+1)=g(s)F(s)
for
s>0
of the form
\mathcal{M}f_{0}
through the operator
f_{0}(x)=x^{s_{0}}f(x)
. Clearly still
f_{0}>0
a.e. and since
\mathcal{M}f_{0}(s)=\mathcal{M}f(s+s_{0})
for
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we also have
f_{0}\in L_{\mathcal{M},\mathbb{R}_{>0}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
.
∎

3.2
Laplace transform

The Laplace transform can be seen as the additive analogue of the Mellin transform. The (bilateral) Laplace transform of a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

(\mathcal{L}f)(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-sx}dx,

whenever this integral exists. For convenience, we will work with functions in the space

L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})=\{f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)e^{-sx}|dx<\infty,\ s\in\Sigma\},

where
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an interval (or, if explicitly mentioned, a singleton). Note that instead of considering the interval
\Sigma
, we could also have considered the strip
\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
in the right half of the complex plane. It is straightforward to see that, given
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, by defining
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}:x\mapsto f(-\ln x)
, we have
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
\tilde{f}\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. In that case,
\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{M}\tilde{f}
on
\Sigma
. As a consequence, many results for the Mellin transform can be rephrased in terms of the Laplace transform.

The following elementary examples will play a role in the characterization of sets of additive derivative type.

Example 3.6

(Eq. 5.9.1,
[
44
]
)
.

Let
a>-1
. The gamma density
\mathcal{G}^{a}(x)=x^{a}e^{-x}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
has Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}^{a}(s)=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{(s+1)^{a+1}},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

Example 3.7

(Table 1.14.1,
[
44
]
)
.

The Gaussian density
\mathcal{G}(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
on
\mathbb{R}
has Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}(s)=\sqrt{\pi}e^{s^{2}/4},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

In what follows, we will state some well-known properties of the Laplace transform. These properties essentially carry over from the corresponding results for the Mellin transform. For example, the Laplace transform of a function
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is also analytic on the associated strip
\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
. The convolution law and inversion theorem take the following form. Whenever
f,g\in L_{\mathcal{L},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, there exists a Laplace convolution

(f\ast_{\mathcal{L}}g)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)g(x-t)dt,\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R},

with the property that

(\mathcal{L}[f\ast_{\mathcal{L}}g])(s)=(\mathcal{L}f)(s)\cdot(\mathcal{L}g)(s),\quad s\in c+i\mathbb{R}.

Whenever
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
\mathcal{L}f\in L^{1}(c+i\mathbb{R})
, there is an inverse transform

f(x)=\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\mathcal{L}f(s)e^{sx}\frac{ds}{2\pi i},\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular, this implies that the Laplace transform is injective on
L_{\mathcal{L},\{c\}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
in an a.e.-sense.

In the setting of the Laplace (or rather Fourier) transform, the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma is also standard. Later, we will use this result to establish whether certain functions can appear as a Laplace transform.

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose that
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for every
s\in\Sigma
, we have

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\mathcal{L}f(s+it)=0.

Less standard is the following analogue of Proposition
3.4
. Since, to our knowledge such a result for the Laplace transform (or Fourier transform) with minimal conditions on the underlying functions hasn’t appeared before in the literature, we provide an explicit self-contained proof (based on some ideas that appear in
[
10
, §8]
). Alternatively, one could use make use of the corresponding result for the Mellin transform.

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

there exists
F\in C^{r-1}(\mathbb{R})
, with
F^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
F^{(r)}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
f=F
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
,

ii)

there exists
g\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}g(s)=s^{r}\mathcal{L}f(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
.

In that case,
g=F^{(r)}
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose that
f\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

there exists
F\in C^{r-1}(\mathbb{R})
, with
F^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
F^{(r)}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
f=F
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
,

ii)

there exists
g\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{L}g(s)=s^{r}\mathcal{L}f(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
.

In that case,
g=F^{(r)}
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We will first prove the implication
i)\Rightarrow ii)
. We will show that
\mathcal{L}F^{(r)}(s)=s^{r}\mathcal{L}f(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma
. The challenge is to circumvent the fact that the Laplace transform of the intermediate derivatives doesn’t need to exist, otherwise we could use an argument based on integration by parts. We will compute the Laplace transform of

F_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}F^{(r)}(x+x_{1}+\dots+x_{r})dx_{r}\dots dx_{1},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

in two ways. The function
F_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is well-defined because
F\in C^{r-1}(\mathbb{R})
and
F^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, hence
F^{(j)}\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,r\}
. In fact, in that case, we can use the identity
(\Delta h)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}(x+t)dt
to show that
F_{0}=\Delta^{r}F
on
\mathbb{R}
and thus (see
[
44
, Eq. 3.9.4]
)

F_{0}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}(-1)^{r-k}F(x+k),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Its Laplace transform is then given by

\mathcal{L}F_{0}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}(-1)^{r-k}e^{sk}\mathcal{L}F(s)=(e^{s}-1)^{r}\mathcal{L}F(s),\quad s\in\Sigma.

On the other hand, since
F^{(r)}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we can also compute the Laplace transform of
F_{0}
directly by applying Fubini’s theorem and performing a change of variables

\mathcal{L}F_{0}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}e^{s(x_{1}+\dots+x_{r})}\mathcal{L}F^{(r)}(s)dx_{r}\dots dx_{1}=\frac{(e^{s}-1)^{r}}{s^{r}}\mathcal{L}F^{(r)}(s),

for
s\in\Sigma
. Combining both results, we obtain the desired identity.

We will now prove the other implication
ii)\Rightarrow i)
. Since
g,1_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we can consider the Laplace convolutions
g_{j}=g\ast_{\mathcal{L}}(\ast_{\mathcal{L}}1_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})^{r-j}
for
j\in\{0,\dots,r\}
, which are a priori defined a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we have
g_{j}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and by the assumption

\mathcal{L}g_{j}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}g(s)}{s^{r-j}}=s^{j}\mathcal{L}f(s),\quad s\in\Sigma.

As a consequence, by taking appropriate linear combinations, for every
c\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a function
g_{c}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\mathcal{L}g_{c}(s)=(s-c)^{r}\mathcal{L}f(s),\quad s\in\Sigma.

Now we fix
c\in\Sigma
and consider

F_{c}(x)=e^{cx}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{x_{r-1}}g_{c}(x_{r})e^{-cx_{r}}dx_{r}\dots dx_{1},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that
F_{c}=g_{c}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}(\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\exp_{c})^{r}
, with
\exp_{c}(x)=\exp(cx)1_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}(x)
on
\mathbb{R}
, and that
g_{c},\exp_{c}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma_{c}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, with
\Sigma_{c}=\{s\in\Sigma\mid s>c\}
. The latter is non-empty, because
\Sigma
is an open interval and
c\in\Sigma
. As a consequence,
F_{c}
is well-defined a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we have
F_{c}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma_{c}}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and

\mathcal{L}F_{c}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}g_{c}(s)}{(s-c)^{r}}=\mathcal{L}f(s),\quad s\in\Sigma_{c}.

Injectivity of the Laplace transform then implies that
F_{c}=f
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
. We will show that
F_{c}
satisfies the other conditions in
i)
as well. Denote, for every
j\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}
,

F_{j,c}(x)=e^{cx}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{j+1}}\dots\int_{-\infty}^{x_{r-1}}g_{c}(x_{r})e^{-cx_{r}}dx_{r}\dots dx_{j+1},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then we can show by induction that, for every
n\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}
,

F_{c}^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}c^{n-k}F_{k,c}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

and that
F_{c}^{(n)}(x)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, for
n\in\{0,\dots,r-2\}
, and
F_{c}^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. To this end, we can use the fact that functions of the form
x\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{x}h(t)dt
are in
AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and are in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for
h\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
F_{c}^{(r-1)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, its derivative exists a.e. and is given by

F_{c}^{(r)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}c^{r-k}F_{k,c}(x),\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}.

It then remains to show that
F_{c}^{(r)}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Observe that

\mathcal{L}F_{k,c}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}g_{c}(s)}{(s-c)^{r-k}}=(s-c)^{k}\mathcal{L}f(s),\quad s\in\Sigma_{c},

and therefore

\mathcal{L}F_{c}^{(r)}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{r}{k}c^{r-k}(s-c)^{k}\mathcal{L}F_{c}(s)=s^{r}\mathcal{L}f(s),\quad s\in\Sigma_{c}.

Therefore, by the assumption,
\mathcal{L}F_{c}^{(r)}(s)=\mathcal{L}g(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma_{c}
. Since the Laplace transform is injective, we can then conclude that
F_{c}^{(r)}=g
a.e. and hence
F_{c}^{(r)}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Note that in several steps of the above proof, we used implicitly that
0\not\in\Sigma
, which is why we restricted to intervals
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
at the start of this section.

4
Examples

In this section, we will provide many examples of the ensembles described in Theorem
2.3
&
2.6
. In the multiplicative setting, we will show that the eigenvalue densities of products of JUE and LUE matrices fit into this framework. In fact, they turn out to be the generic examples of ensembles covered by Theorem
2.3
. In the additive setting, we will show that the eigenvalue densities of sums of (dilated) LUE and GUE matrices fit into the framework of Theorem
2.6
. However, here it turns out that we can give many other examples as well. In both settings, we will analyze the values of the parameters under which the functions in (
3
) and (
5
) give rise to a
positive
ensemble of derivative type, in which case Theorem
2.3
&
2.6
is applicable. This will allow us to show Proposition
2.7
&
2.8
.

4.1
Multiplicative setting

The eigenvalue densities of JUE and LUE matrices are elementary examples of the (multiple) orthogonal polynomial ensembles covered by Theorem
2.3
. This follows immediately from Example
3.1
&
3.2
. The fact that they are of multiplicative derivative type was first observed in
[
15
, Ex. 2.4]
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Suppose that
b>a>-1
.

i)

If
X\sim\text{GUE}[n;a]
, i.e.
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{G}^{a}]
, then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\mathcal{G}^{a}]
.

ii)

If
X\sim\text{JUE}[n;a,b]
, i.e.
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{B}^{a,b}]
, then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\mathcal{B}^{a,b+n-1}]
.

Since the multiplicative derivative type structure of the eigenvalue densities of independent random matrices is preserved under taking their products, i.e. if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent random matrices with
\text{EV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega_{1}]
and
\text{EV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega_{2}]
, then
\text{EV}(X_{1}X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega_{1}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\omega_{2}]
, see
[
15
]
, the above leads to many other examples of polynomial ensembles of derivative type. Theorem
2.3
then shows that they are also multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
. Suppose that all
a_{i}>-1
and that all
b_{i}+n_{i}-n+1>a_{i}
. Let
X_{i}\sim\text{JUE}[n;a_{i},b_{i}+n_{i}-n+1]
, for
j\in\{1,\dots,q\}
, and
X_{i}\sim\text{GUE}[n;a_{i}]
, for
i\in\{q+1,\dots,r\}
, be independent random matrices. Then
\text{EV}(X_{1}\dots X_{r})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega_{n,1}]

with

\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(s)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma(s+a_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}\Gamma(s+b_{i}+n_{i})},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0,

and
\text{EV}(X_{1}\dots X_{r})\sim\text{MOPE}[n;w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r}]
with

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma(s+a_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}\Gamma(s+b_{i})}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\max\{j,q\}}(s+b_{i})},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

It turns out that we can slightly weaken the conditions on the parameters and still have that
\omega_{1,n}
gives rise to a
positive
ensemble of derivative type, but then we lose the connection to JUE and LUE matrices. The case where the parameters are integers is essentially covered by the techniques from
[
24
, Cor. 2.4]
and
[
31
, Thm. 2.1]
. In
[
11
, Thm. 2.19]
, special care was given to the setting with all
d_{1}(i)=d_{2}(i)=1
and an earlier question of optimally of the conditions in this setting was answered.

Even though Corollary
4.2
doesn’t cover all of the ensembles described by Theorem
2.3
, the result below, in particular
i)
, shows that they can be seen as the generic examples. As discussed in the introduction, it would be interesting to obtain an optimal set of conditions for which we the weights
\omega_{1,n}
in (
2
) give rise to a
positive
polynomial ensemble of multiplicative derivative type. A first step into this direction is the following in which we exploit the fact that the underlying space
L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
induces certain conditions on the parameters.

Proposition 4.3
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.3
, we have

i)

all
d_{1}(i)=1
,

ii)

all
\text{Re}\,a_{i}>-1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\text{Im}\,a_{i}=0
,

iii)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(b_{i}-a_{i})>-1
if all
d_{2}(i)=1
.

Proof.

We will first show a weaker version of
ii)
, namely that all
d_{1}(i)\text{Re}\,a_{i}>-1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{1}(i)\text{Im}\,a_{i}=0
. After showing
i)
, this then gives
ii)
. For the first part, we note that
\Sigma
contains
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
(by integrability of the moments), hence it must be of the form
\Sigma=(s_{0},\infty)
for some
s_{0}\in[0,1)
. However,
\Gamma(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}
needs to be defined for all
s\in\Sigma
, so we must have that
d_{1}(i)\text{Re}(a_{i})\geq-s_{0}>-1
. The second part follows from the fact that
\mathcal{M}v_{1}
is real-valued, hence
d_{1}(i)a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}
must appear in conjugate pairs (consider, e.g.,
\mathcal{M}v_{1}(2)/\mathcal{M}v_{1}(1)
).

We will now use this, in combination with Proposition
3.3
, to prove
i)
and
iii)
. Since
w_{1,1}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, for every
c\in\Sigma
, we must have

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(c+it)=0.

Denote
p=|d_{1}^{-1}(\{1\})|
and
q=|d_{2}^{-1}(\{1\})|
. Stirling’s asymptotic formula (see, e.g.,
[
44
, Eq. 5.11.7]
) implies that

\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(c+it)\asymp(it)^{(p-q)(c+it-\frac{1}{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(d_{1}(i)a_{i}-d_{2}(i)b_{i})-d_{2}(1)}e^{-(p-q)(c+it)},

as
|t|\to\infty
. Recall that the notation
f(t)\asymp g(t)
as
t\to\infty
denotes that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)/g(t)=C
for some
C\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
. If we write
t=|t|e^{i\arg t}
and
i=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}
, the above becomes

|\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(c+it)|\,\asymp|t|^{(p-q)(c-\frac{1}{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(d_{1}(i)\text{Re}(a_{i})-d_{2}(i)b_{i})-d_{2}(1)}e^{-(p-q)(c+t(\frac{\pi}{2}+\arg t))},

as
|t|\to\infty
. Note that here we used that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{1}(i)\text{Im}\,a_{i}=0
. Assume now that some
d_{1}(i)=0
, i.e.
p<q
, then the power of the exponential factor is strictly positive as
t\to\infty
and thus
\lim_{t\to\infty}|\mathcal{M}v_{1}(c+it)|\,=\infty
. Hence we must have
i)
.

Assume now that all
d_{1}(i)=1
and all
d_{2}(i)=1
, i.e.
p=q
, then the power of the exponential factor is
0
and we must have
\sum_{i=1}^{r}(d_{1}(i)\text{Re}\,a_{i}-b_{i})-1<0
. Together with
i)
and
ii)
, this implies
iii)
.
∎

A consequence of the above is that one of the classes of weights that appears in Theorem
2.3
necessarily have a sign change in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and therefore don’t give rise to a
positive
ensemble.

Corollary 4.4
.

A function
v:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
with a Mellin transform as in (
2
) or (
3
), with some
d_{1}(i)=0
, possesses a sign change in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proof.

If
v
doesn’t have a sign change, we have
\mathcal{M}|v|=\pm\mathcal{M}v
and therefore
v\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. In that case, we can apply Proposition
4.3
to get a contradiction.
∎

By considering the simplest setting covered by Theorem
2.3
and Proposition
4.3
, i.e.
r=1
, we can show that the eigenvalue densities of JUE and LUE matrices are essentially the only orthogonal polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type. This is the content of Proposition
2.7
, which we will prove now.

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

The fact that the eigenvalue densities of JUE and LUE matrices are orthogonal polynomial ensembles of multiplicative derivative type was already recorded in Corollary
4.1
. By Theorem
2.3
, the latter are characterized by a weight
w_{1,1}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
with a Mellin transform of the form

\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)=c^{s}\,\frac{\Gamma(s+a)^{d_{1}}}{\Gamma(s+b)^{d_{2}}}\frac{1}{(s+b_{2})^{d_{2}}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

with
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
b,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,1\}
with
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}=1
. Since
c\neq 0
, without loss of generality, we can assume that
c=1
. Otherwise, we can consider the dilated weight
\tilde{w}_{1,1}(x)=w_{1,1}(cx)
instead. By Proposition
4.3
, we must have
d_{1}=1
,
a>-1
and
b-a>-1
(so
b>-2
). Suppose that
d_{2}=0
, then

\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)=\Gamma(s+a),\quad s\in\Sigma,

Hence, by Example
3.2
and the Mellin inversion theorem, we must have that
w_{1,1}=\mathcal{G}^{a}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Suppose that
d_{2}=1
, then

\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)=\frac{\Gamma(s+a)}{\Gamma(s+b+1)},\quad s\in\Sigma,

Therefore, by Example
3.1
and the Mellin inversion theorem, we must have that
w_{1,1}=\mathcal{B}^{a,b+1}
on
(0,1)
.
∎

4.2
Additive setting

In the additive setting, the eigenvalue densities of LUE and GUE matrices are elementary examples of the (multiple) orthogonal polynomial ensembles covered by Theorem
2.6
. This follows immediately from Example
3.6
&
3.7
. The fact that they are of additive derivative type was already observed in
[
15
, Ex. 2.4]
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that
a>-1
.

i)

If
X\sim\text{LUE}[n;a]
, i.e.
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{G}^{a}]
, then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\mathcal{G}^{a+n-1}]
.

ii)

If
X\sim\text{GUE}[n]
, i.e.
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{G}]
, then
\text{EV}(X)\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\mathcal{G}]
.

Since the additive derivative type structure of the eigenvalue densities of independent random matrices is preserved under taking their sums, i.e. if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent random matrices with
\text{EV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega_{1}]
and
\text{EV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega_{2}]
, then
\text{EV}(X_{1}+X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega_{1}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\omega_{2}]
, see
[
15
]
, the above can be used to obtain other examples of polynomial ensembles of derivative type as well. It turns out that adding LUE matrices to an LUE matrix only changes the value of the parameter in the underlying weight and adding GUE matrices to a GUE matrix only dilates the underlying weight. The specific structure of the Laplace transform of the underlying weight therefore remains unchanged. In order to go beyond this class, we will introduce a dilated version of the LUE, denoted by
\text{LUE}[n;a,c]
, with eigenvalue density
\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{D}_{c}\mathcal{G}^{a}]
, in terms of the shift operator
(\mathcal{D}_{c}f)=f(x/c)/c
and
c>0
. Note that by Example
3.6
, we have

\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{D}_{c}\mathcal{G}^{a}](s)=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{(1+cs)^{a+1}}.

The influence of a similarly dilated version of the GUE, denoted by
\text{GUE}[n;c]
, with eigenvalue density
\text{OPE}[n;\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2c}}\mathcal{G}]
, is less substantial. Note that by Example
3.7
, we have

\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{2c}}\mathcal{G}](s)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(cs^{2}\right),\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

Theorem
2.6
shows that the eigenvalue densities of sums of these dilated LUE and GUE matrices are multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
. Suppose that all
a_{i}+n_{i}-n>-1
and that all
c_{i}>0
are distinct. Let

X_{i}\sim\text{GUE}[n;c_{i}]
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,q_{0}\}
,

X_{i}\sim\text{LUE}[n;a_{i}+n_{i}-n,c_{i}]
, for
i\in\{q_{0}+1,\dots,q_{1}\}
,
be independent random matrices. Then
\text{EV}(X_{1}+\dots+X_{q_{1}})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega_{2,n}]

with

\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(s)=\frac{\exp\left((\sum_{i=1}^{q_{0}}c_{i})s^{2}\right)}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{q_{1}}(1+c_{i}s)^{a_{i}+n_{i}}},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0,

and
\text{EV}(X_{1}+\dots+X_{q_{1}})\sim\text{MOPE}[n;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,q_{1}-q_{0}+1}]
with

\mathcal{L}w_{2,j}(s)=\frac{\exp\left((\sum_{i=1}^{q_{0}}c_{i})s^{2}\right)}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{q_{1}}(1+c_{i}s)^{a_{i}}}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{\max\{q_{0}+j,q_{1}\}}(1+c_{i}s)},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

Recall that in the multiplicative setting, most of the ensembles covered by Theorem
2.3
arose as products of certain elementary ensembles (related to JUE and LUE matrices). This could be explained by the fact that the underlying functions
\omega_{1,n}
in (
3
) could be decomposed into the functions associated to the elementary ensembles using the Mellin convolution. Remarkably, for the functions
\omega_{2,n}
in (
5
), a similar property in terms of the Laplace convolution doesn’t hold. Hence, in the additive setting, the ensembles described in Theorem
2.6
have a much richer structure.

It turns out that in order to construct most of the functions
\omega_{2,n}
in (
5
), we require the following generalizations of the elementary weights.

Definition 4.7
.

i)

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
c>0
. We define

\text{Ai}_{d}^{c}(x)=\int_{1+i\mathbb{R}}\exp\left((-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}cs^{d+2}+sx\right)\frac{ds}{2\pi i},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

ii)

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a>-1
,
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
and
c>0
. We define

\text{Be}_{d}^{a,b,c}(x)=c^{-1}e^{-x/c}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{b^{k}(x/c)^{dk+a}}{k!\Gamma(dk+a+1)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

The functions in
i)
generalize the normal density
\mathcal{G}
and Airy function
Ai
since

\text{Ai}_{0}^{c}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi c}}\mathcal{G}(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2c}}),\quad\text{Ai}_{1}^{c}(x)=\frac{1}{(3c)^{\frac{1}{3}}}\text{Ai}(-\frac{x}{(3c)^{\frac{1}{3}}}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

The functions
x\mapsto\text{Ai}_{2n+1}^{1}(-x)
already appeared in
[
17
]
, where they are called higher-order Airy functions. The functions in
ii)
generalize the gamma density
\mathcal{G}^{a}
and variants of the
I
-Bessel function
I_{a}
since

\text{Be}_{0}^{a,b,c}(x)=\frac{e^{b}}{\Gamma(a+1)}\mathcal{G}^{a}(x/c),\quad\text{Be}_{1}^{a,b,c}(x)=\left(\frac{x}{bc}\right)^{\frac{a}{2}}e^{-x/c}I_{a}(2\sqrt{bx/c}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

The functions
x\mapsto e^{x}\text{Be}_{d}^{a-1,1,1}(x)
already appeared in
[
44
, Eq. 10.46.1]
, where they are called generalized Bessel functions (or Wright functions).

Using the Laplace inversion theorem and the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, it is straightforward that show that the functions
\text{Ai}_{d}^{c}
and
\text{Be}_{d}^{a,b,c}
are in
L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\mathbb{R}_{>0}}(\mathbb{R})
and that their Laplace transforms are given by

\mathcal{L}\text{Ai}_{d}(s)=\exp\left((-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}cs^{d+2}\right),\quad\mathcal{L}\text{Be}_{d}^{a,b,c}(s)=\dfrac{\exp\left(\frac{b}{(1+cs)^{d}}\right)}{(1+cs)^{a+1}}\quad,\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

The result below shows that at least some of these functions give rise a
positive
polynomial ensemble of additive derivative type. To our knowledge, the associated ensembles have not been studied before.

Proposition 4.8
.

The function
\text{Be}_{1}^{a,b,c}
defines an
n
-point polynomial ensemble of additive derivative type for all
n<a+2
.

Proof.

It suffices to show this for
c=1
as
\text{Be}_{1}^{a,b,c}=\mathcal{D}_{c}\text{Be}_{1}^{a,b,1}
. To do so, we will employ
[
19
, §3.2: Thm. 2.1]
, where it was shown that functions of the form

f_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\frac{x^{k+a}}{\Gamma(k+a+1)},\quad x>0,\\
0,\quad x\leq 0,\end{cases}

are (additive) Pólya frequency functions of any order
n<a+2
, whenever
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
0<\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}<\infty
and
\det[c_{i-j}]_{i,j=1}^{n}\geq 0
(
c_{k}:=0
for
k<0
). In a subsequent remark, it was further noted that these conditions hold for
c_{k}=1/k!
. The desired result then follows from
[
15
, Thm. 2.9 (1)]
.
∎

We can now slightly extend Corollary
4.6
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
. Suppose that all
a_{i}+n_{i}-n>-1
, all
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}
and that all
c_{i}>0
are distinct. Let

X_{i}\sim\text{GUE}[n;c_{i}]
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,q_{0}\}
,

X_{i}\sim\text{LUE}[n;a_{i}+n_{i}-n,c_{i}]
, for
i\in\{q_{0}+1,\dots,q_{1}\}
, and

X_{i}
with
\text{EV}(X_{j})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}(n;\text{Be}_{1}^{a_{i}+n_{i}-1,b_{i},c_{i}})
, for
i\in\{q_{1}+1,\dots,q_{2}\}
,
be independent random matrices. Then
\text{EV}(X_{1}+\dots+X_{q_{2}})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega_{2,n}]

with

\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(s)=\frac{\exp\left((\sum_{i=1}^{q_{0}}c_{i})s^{2}+\sum_{i=q_{1}+1}^{q_{2}}\frac{b_{i}}{1+c_{i}s}\right)}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{q_{2}}(1+c_{i}s)^{a_{i}+n_{i}}},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0,

and
\text{EV}(X_{1}+\dots+X_{q_{2}})\sim\text{MOPE}[n;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,q_{2}-q_{0}+1}]
with

\mathcal{L}w_{2,j}(s)=\frac{\exp\left((\sum_{i=1}^{q_{0}}c_{i})s^{2}+\sum_{i=q_{1}+1}^{q_{2}}\frac{b_{i}}{1+c_{i}s}\right)}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{q_{2}}(1+c_{i}s)^{a_{i}}}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=q_{0}+1}^{\max\{q_{0}+j,q_{2}\}}(1+c_{i}s)},\quad\text{Re}(s)>0.

Similarly as in the multiplicative setting, it turns out that many of the functions
\omega_{2,n}
in (
5
) are not admissible, i.e. they don’t belong to
L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R})
or don’t lead to a
positive
ensemble. A first step into this direction is the following in which we examine some constraints induced by the underlying space
L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 4.10
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.6
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{1}(i)\text{Im}\,a_{i}=0
. If we write

\exp\left(c\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}dt\right)=\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}s^{k+1}+O(s)\right)}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)\beta_{i}}},\quad s\to\infty,

in terms of
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

i)

(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{d}<0
if
d\geq 1
and
d\equiv_{2}1
,

ii)

(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor}(s(d+1)\alpha_{d}+\alpha_{d-1})<0
for all
s\in\Sigma
if
d\geq 1
and
d\equiv_{2}0
,

iii)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}>-1
if
d=0
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{L}v_{1}
is real-valued, the
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}
must appear in conjugate pairs (consider, e.g.,
(\mathcal{L}v_{1})^{\prime}(s_{0})
), hence after a partial fraction decomposition all
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. We will use Proposition
3.8
, i.e.

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\mathcal{L}v_{1}(s+it)=0,

for every
s\in\Sigma
. Suppose that
d\geq 1
, then the asymptotics of
|\mathcal{L}v_{1}(s+it)|
are determined by the exponential part in the above representation. The stated conditions then follow from the fact that

\text{Re}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\alpha_{k}(s+it)^{k+1}\right)=\begin{cases}(s(d+1)\alpha_{d}+\alpha_{d-1})t^{d}(1+o(1)),\quad d\equiv_{4}0,\\
-\alpha_{d}t^{d+1}(1+o(1)),\quad d\equiv_{4}1,\\
-(s(d+1)\alpha_{d}+\alpha_{d-1})t^{d}(1+o(1)),\quad d\equiv_{4}2,\\
\alpha_{d}t^{d+1}(1+o(1)),\quad d\equiv_{4}3.\\
\end{cases}

Suppose that
d=0
, then the asymptotics of
|\mathcal{L}v_{1}(s+it)|
are determined by the rational part in the above representation. Since
d=0
, we must have all
d_{2}(i)=1
, and hence
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}+1>0
.
∎

Positivity
of the ensemble also seems to induce certain conditions on the parameters of the functions
\omega_{2,n}
in (
5
). For example, several functions of the form
\text{Ai}_{d}^{c}
are known to oscillate on part of the real line so that
\omega_{2,n}
can not be of this form. In fact, it seems that whenever
\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}
contains of factor of the form
\mathcal{L}\text{Ai}_{d}^{c}
with
d\geq 1
, the function
\omega_{2,n}
will have some oscillations on the real line. Evidence of this may be provided by applying a steepest descent analysis on its inverse Laplace transform. We will not explore this here as it is rather technical to describe the conditions on the parameters under which such an asymptotic analysis can be carried out. As mentioned in the introduction, it would be interesting to have an optimal set of conditions for which
\omega_{2,n}
in (
5
) gives rise to a
positive
ensemble of additive derivative type, because of the connection to (additive) Pólya frequency functions of finite order.

By considering the simplest setting covered by Theorem
2.6
, i.e.
r=1
, we can show that the eigenvalue densities of LUE and GUE matrices are essentially the only orthogonal polynomial ensembles of additive derivative type. This is the content of Proposition
2.8
, which we will prove now.

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

The fact that the eigenvalue densities of LUE and GUE matrix are orthogonal polynomial ensembles of additive derivative type was already observed in Corollary
4.5
. By Theorem
2.6
, the latter are characterized by a weight
w_{2,1}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R})
with a Laplace transform of the form

\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)=\exp\left(c\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{(t+a)^{d_{1}}}{(t+b)^{d_{2}}}dt\right)\frac{1}{(s+b)^{d_{2}}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

where
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
b,c,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,1\}
with
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}=1
. Suppose that
d_{2}=0
, then

\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)=\exp\left(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}s+\alpha_{2}s^{2}\right),\quad s\in\Sigma.

with
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha_{2}>0
according to Proposition
4.10
. We can then assume that
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=0
and
\alpha_{2}=1/4
, otherwise we can apply an appropriate scaling and affine transformation to
w_{2,1}
. In that case,
\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)=\exp\left(s^{2}/4\right)
for
s\in\Sigma
and thus
w_{2,1}=\mathcal{G}/\sqrt{\pi}
on
\mathbb{R}
by Example
3.7
and the Laplace inversion theorem. Suppose that
d_{2}=1
, then

\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)=\frac{\exp\left(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}s\right)}{(s+b)^{\beta+1}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

with
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
-b\not\in\Sigma
and
\beta>-1
according to Proposition
4.10
. We can then assume that
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=0
and
b=1
, otherwise we can apply an appropriate scaling and affine transformation to
w_{2,1}
. In that case,
\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)=1/(s+1)^{\beta+1}
for
s\in\Sigma
(as
s>0
) and thus
w_{2,1}=\mathcal{G}^{\beta}/\Gamma(\beta+1)
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
by Example
3.6
and the Laplace inversion theorem.
∎

5
Finite free probability

The goal of this section is two-fold. First, we will provide a partial answer to an open problem posed by Martínez-Finkelshtein at the Hypergeometric and Orthogonal Polynomials Event in Nijmegen (May, 2024), see
[
40
]
, related to orthogonality of the finite free multiplicative and additive convolution of polynomials from free probability. We will do this by interpreting the problem in terms of random matrices and by making use of the notion of functions (or polynomial ensembles) of multiplicative and additive derivative type. Second, we will explain how in a more restricted setting the associated multiple orthogonal polynomials come into play. We will show that such polynomials (de)compose naturally under the finite free multiplicative and additive convolution.

5.1
Multiplicative setting

We first recall the notion of finite free multiplicative convolution, see
[
39
, Def. 1.4]
.

Definition 5.1
.

The finite free multiplicative convolution of two polynomials
p_{1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{1}[k]x^{k}
and
p_{2}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{2}[k]x^{k}
of degree at most
n
is defined as

(p_{1}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2})(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{p_{1}[k]p_{2}[k]}{(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}}x^{k}.

The first part of the open problem involves orthogonality of the finite free multiplicative convolution of two polynomials that satisfy given orthogonality relations. More precisely, one considers polynomials
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
of degree
n
that satisfy

\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{j,n}(x)q_{j,k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},\quad j\in\{1,2\},

for some functions
(q_{1,k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
and
(q_{2,k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
and then asks whether their finite free multiplicative convolution also satisfies

\int_{0}^{\infty}(p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n})(x)q_{k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},

for some explicit functions
(q_{k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
.

We will interpret this problem in terms of products of random matrices using the result below, which is essentially a reformulation of
[
39
, Thm. 1.5]
in terms of random matrices instead of deterministic ones. Note that it is essentially the finite version of the free convolution law for products of random matrices, i.e. the fact that, if
\mu_{j}
is the limiting eigenvalue distribution of
X_{j}
, the eigenvalue distribution of
X_{1}X_{2}
tends to the free multiplicative convolution
\mu_{1}\boxtimes\mu_{2}
as
n\to\infty
, whenever one of the underlying matrix ensembles is invariant under unitary conjugation.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent
n\times n
normal random matrices and assume that one of the matrix ensembles is invariant under unitary conjugation. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}X_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})]\boxtimes_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{2})].

Consequently, also

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}X_{2})(X_{1}X_{2})^{\ast})]=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}X_{1}^{\ast})]\boxtimes_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{2}X_{2}^{\ast})].

Proof.

Since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent random matrices, the expectation on the left hand side is given by

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}X_{2})]=\iint\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}X_{2})d\nu_{1}(X_{1})d\nu_{2}(X_{2}).

It was shown in
[
39
, Thm. 1.5]
that for normal matrices
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, one has

\int_{U(n)}\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}QX_{2}Q^{\ast})d\nu_{\text{Haar}}(Q)=\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})\boxtimes_{n}\det(xI_{n}-X_{2}).

Since at least one of the underlying matrix ensembles is invariant under unitary conjugation, we thus obtain

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}X_{2})]=\iint\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})\boxtimes_{n}\det(xI_{n}-X_{2})d\nu_{1}(X_{1})d\nu_{2}(X_{2}).

Now we can use bilinearity of the finite free multiplicative convolution to obtain the desired result.
For the second claim, we can assume without loss of generality that the ensemble associated to
X_{1}
is invariant under unitary conjugation, otherwise we can swap the order of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
via the identity

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}X_{2})(X_{1}X_{2})^{\ast})]=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}X_{2})^{\ast}(X_{1}X_{2}))].

In that case, we can use the first result to write the expectation on the left as

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})]\boxtimes_{n}(\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{2}X_{2}^{\ast})]\boxtimes_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}^{\ast})]).

After using the fact that the finite free multiplicative convolution is associative and commutative, this can be written as

(\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})]\boxtimes_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1}^{\ast})])\boxtimes_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{2}X_{2}^{\ast})],

We can then apply the first result again.
∎

It turns out that for certain random matrices, similar decompositions also occur on a deeper level. This is straightforward to see from the results in
[
22
,
23
]
after making the proper identifications. Given an
n\times n
random matrix
X
, we will use the notation
\text{SSV}(X)\sim\text{PE}[n]
, resp.
\text{SSV}(X)\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n]
, to denote that the squared singular values of
X
follow a polynomial ensemble, resp. polynomial ensemble of multiplicative derivative type.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent random matrices with
\text{SSV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}[n]
and
\text{SSV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{MDT}}[n;\omega]
. Let
(p_{k,j},q_{k,j})_{j=0}^{n-1}
be the biorthogonal system for the correlation kernel of
\text{SSV}(X_{k})
. Then the biorthogonal system
(p_{k},q_{k})_{j=0}^{n-1}
for the correlation kernel of
\text{SSV}(X_{1}X_{2})
is given by

p_{j}=p_{1,j}\boxtimes_{j}p_{2,j},\quad q_{j}=q_{1,j}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\omega,\quad j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}.

Proof.

Denote
p_{1,j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}p_{1,j}[k]x^{k}
. It was shown in
[
23
, Cor. 3.7]
that

p_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}p_{1,j}[k]\frac{(\mathcal{M}\omega)(j+1)}{(\mathcal{M}\omega)(k+1)}x^{k},\quad q_{j}=q_{1,j}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\omega_{n}.

On the other hand, according to
[
22
, Lem. 4.2]
, one has

p_{2,j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-k}\binom{j}{k}\frac{(\mathcal{M}\omega)(j+1)}{(\mathcal{M}\omega)(k+1)}x^{k}.

It then remains to use the definition of
\boxtimes_{j}
.
∎

We will now provide a partial answer to the previously mentioned open problem. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent
n\times n
normal random matrices for which

p_{j,n}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{j}X_{j}^{\ast})],\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

In order to encode the orthogonality conditions through the random matrices, it is natural to assume that
\text{SSV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{1,0},\dots,q_{1,n-1}]
and
\text{SSV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{2,0},\dots,q_{2,n-1}]
. Indeed, in that case, we automatically have

\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{j,n}(x)q_{j,k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

With this set-up, Proposition
5.2
allows us to describe the finite free multiplicative convolution of
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
explicitly:

(p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n})(x)=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}X_{2})(X_{1}X_{2})^{\ast})].

Again, if we would like
p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n}
to satisfy

\int_{0}^{\infty}(p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n})(x)q_{k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},

it is natural to demand that
\text{SSV}(X_{1}X_{2})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1}]
. The problem is then to determine when exactly this occurs. This is exactly the problem that drove the research in random matrix theory surrounding products of random matrices that ultimately led to the notion of multiplicative derivative type. We now know that whenever one of the initial polynomial ensembles is of multiplicative derivative, the squared singular values of the product also follow a polynomial ensemble. The associated functions are known explicitly, see
[
23
, Cor. 3.7]
(or Proposition
5.3
above): if
q_{2,0},\dots,q_{2,n-1}
are of multiplicative derivative type w.r.t.
\omega
, then
q_{k}=q_{1,k}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\omega
for
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

In the more restrictive setting of the problem in which the polynomials
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
are assumed to be multiple orthogonal polynomials, one of the initial polynomial ensembles ends up being a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble of multiplicative derivative type. These are exactly the objects that we characterized in Theorem
2.3
. Following the proof of
[
22
, Lem. 4.2]
, we can determine the associated multiple orthogonal polynomials explicitly.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
with
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\subset\Sigma
. If, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the functions in
\mathcal{W}(m;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
are of multiplicative derivative type, then the type II polynomial w.r.t.
(w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
for the multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
is given by

P_{\vec{n}}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left|\vec{n}\right|}(-1)^{\left|\vec{n}\right|-k}\binom{\left|\vec{n}\right|}{k}\frac{(\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n})(\left|\vec{n}\right|+1)}{(\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n})(k+1)}x^{k},

(6)

where
\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}
is as in (
3
).

Proof.

Note that
P_{\vec{n}}
is a monic polynomial of degree
\left|\vec{n}\right|
. We have to show that it satisfies the orthogonality conditions

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{k}w_{j}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n_{j}-1\},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,r\}.

By Theorem
6.2
, this is equivalent to showing that

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\vec{n}}(x)(D^{k}\omega_{1,n})(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},

where
(Df)(x)=-xf^{\prime}(x)
and
n=\left|\vec{n}\right|
. To do so, we will write

P_{\left|\vec{n}\right|}(x)=(-1)^{n}n!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(n+1)}{\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(t+1)}\frac{x^{t}}{(-t)_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{2\pi i},

in terms of a counterclockwise contour
\mathcal{C}
in
\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
enclosing
[0,n]
once. We can do this because
\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(t)\neq 0
for
\text{Re}(t)\in\Sigma
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\subset\Sigma
. After interchanging the integrals, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{n}(x)(D^{k}\omega_{1,n})(x)dx=(-1)^{n}n!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(n+1)}{\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(t+1)}\frac{(\mathcal{M}D^{k}\omega_{1,n})(t+1)}{(-t)_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}.

By Proposition
3.4
, we have
(\mathcal{M}D^{k}\omega_{1,n})(t)=t^{k}\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}(t)
for
t\in\Sigma+i\mathbb{R}
and thus

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\vec{n}}(x)(D^{k}\omega_{1,n})(x)dx=(-1)^{n}n!(\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n})(n+1)\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{(t+1)^{k}}{(-t)_{n+1}}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}.

Since
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, the integrand is
O(|t|^{-2})
as
|t|\,\to\infty
, and thus by blowing up the contour, we can show that the integral vanishes.
∎

The polynomials in (
6
) have some nice (de)composition properties in terms of the finite free multiplicative convolution: if we denote them by
P_{\vec{n}}(x;\omega_{1,n})
, then we have
P_{\vec{n}}(x;\omega_{1,n})=P_{\vec{n}}(x;\omega_{1,n}^{1})\boxtimes_{\left|\vec{n}\right|}P_{\vec{n}}(x;\omega_{1,n}^{2})
whenever
\omega_{1,n}=\omega_{1,n}^{1}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\omega_{1,n}^{2}
. The systems of weights to which Proposition
5.4
applies are described in (
2
).

5.2
Additive setting

We start by recalling the notion of finite free additive convolution, see
[
39
, Def. 1.1]
.

Definition 5.5
.

The finite free additive convolution of two polynomials
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
of degree at most
n
is defined as

(p_{1}\boxplus_{n}p_{2})(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{1}^{(k)}(x)p_{2}^{(n-k)}(0).

The second part of the open problem involves orthogonality of the finite free additive convolution of two polynomials that satisfy given orthogonality relations. More precisely, one consider polynomials
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
of degree
n
that satisfy

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{j,n}(x)q_{j,k}(x)dx=0,\quad k=0,\dots,n-1,

for some functions
(q_{1,k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
and
(q_{2,k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
and then asks whether their finite free additive convolution also satisfies

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(p_{1,n}\boxplus_{n}p_{2,n})(x)q_{k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},

for some explicit functions
(q_{k})_{k=0}^{n-1}
.

In this setting, we will interpret the problem in terms of sum of random matrices instead of products. To this end, we will make use of the result below, which is essentially a reformulation of
[
39
, Thm. 1.2]
in terms of random matrices instead of deterministic ones. Observe that it is essentially the finite version of the free convolution law for sums of random matrices, i.e. the fact that, if
\mu_{j}
is the limiting eigenvalue distribution of
X_{j}
, the eigenvalue distribution of
X_{1}+X_{2}
tends to the free additive convolution
\mu_{1}\boxplus\mu_{2}
as
n\to\infty
, whenever one of the underlying matrix ensembles is invariant under unitary conjugation.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent
n\times n
normal random matrices and assume that one of the matrix ensembles is invariant under unitary conjugation. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}+X_{2}))]=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})]\boxplus_{n}\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{2})].

Proof.

The idea of the proof is the same as in the multiplicative setting (Proposition
5.2
). The only difference is that we have to use the fact that

\int_{U(n)}\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}+QX_{2}Q^{\ast}))d\nu_{\text{Haar}}(Q)=\det(xI_{n}-X_{1})\boxplus_{n}\det(xI_{n}-X_{2}),

for normal
n\times n
matrices
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, see
[
39
, Thm. 1.2]
.
∎

Again, for certain random matrices, similar decompositions occur on a deeper level as well. This essentially follows from the results in
[
21
]
after making the proper identifications (and using the Laplace transform instead of the Fourier transform).

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent random matrices with
\text{EV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}[n]
and
\text{EV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}_{\text{ADT}}[n;\omega]
. Let
(p_{k,j},q_{k,j})_{j=0}^{n-1}
be the biorthogonal system for the correlation kernel of
\text{EV}(X_{k})
. Then the biorthogonal system
(p_{j},q_{j})_{j=0}^{n-1}
for the correlation kernel of
\text{EV}(X_{1}+X_{2})
is given by

p_{j}=p_{1,j}\boxplus_{j}p_{2,j},\quad q_{j}=q_{1,j}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\omega,\qquad j=0,\dots,n-1.

Proof.

According to
[
21
, Thm. III.8]
, one has

p_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r}\left(r-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n-1}\left[\frac{p_{1,j}(x)}{\mathcal{L}\omega(t)}\right]_{t=0}dr,\quad q_{j}=q_{1,j}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\omega.

On the other hand, it was shown in
[
21
, Thm. III.1]
that

p_{2,j}(x)=\frac{1}{j!}\left(x-\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{j}\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}\omega(t)}\right]_{t=0}.

After expanding the
(n-1)
-th power in the integral representation for
p_{j}(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k!(n-1-k)!}\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{k}\left[\frac{p_{1,j}(x)}{\mathcal{L}\omega(t)}\right]_{t=0}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r}r^{n-1-k}dr,

and thus

p_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}\omega}\right)^{(k)}(0)(p_{1,j})^{(k)}(x).

Now use the fact that

p_{2,j}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!(j-k)!}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}\omega}\right)^{(k)}(0)x^{j-k},

and the definition of
\boxplus_{j}
.
∎

Following a similar strategy as in the multiplicative setting, we will now provide a partial answer to the previously mentioned open problem. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent
n\times n
normal random matrices for which

p_{j,n}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-X_{j})],\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

In order to encode the orthogonality conditions through the underlying random matrices, it is again natural to assume that
\text{EV}(X_{1})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{1,0},\dots,q_{1,n-1}]
and
\text{EV}(X_{2})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{2,0},\dots,q_{2,n-1}]
, because then we automatically have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{j,n}(x)q_{j,k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

By Proposition
5.6
, the finite free additive convolution of
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
is given by

(p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n})(x)=\mathbb{E}[\det(xI_{n}-(X_{1}+X_{2})].

Again, if we would like
p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n}
to satisfy

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(p_{1,n}\boxtimes_{n}p_{2,n})(x)q_{k}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\},

it is natural to demand that
\text{EV}(X_{1}+X_{2})\sim\text{PE}[n;q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1}]
. The problem is then to determine when exactly this occurs, which is exactly the problem that drove the research in random matrix theory surrounding sums of random matrices that ultimately led to the notion of additive derivative type. We now know that whenever one of the initial polynomial ensembles is of additive derivative, the eigenvalues of the sum also follow a polynomial ensemble. The associated functions are known explicitly, see
[
21
, Thm. III.8]
(or Proposition
5.7
above): if the functions
q_{2,0},\dots,q_{2,n-1}
are of additive derivative type w.r.t.
\omega
, then
q_{k}=q_{1,k}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\omega
for
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

Whenever the polynomials
p_{1,n}
and
p_{2,n}
in the open problem are assumed to be multiple orthogonal polynomials, one of the initial polynomial ensembles ends up being a multiple orthogonal polynomial ensemble of additive derivative type. These are exactly the objects that we characterized in Proposition
2.6
. In the following result, we will use some ideas in the proof of
[
21
, Thm. III.1]
to determine the associated multiple orthogonal polynomials explicitly. Note that the assumption that all
\mathcal{L}w_{j}
are analytic at the origin is the analogue of the condition
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\subset\Sigma
in the multiplicative setting (see Proposition
5.4
) as it ensures that all the moments of
w_{j}
exist.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R})
and suppose that the
\mathcal{L}w_{j}
are analytic at the origin. If, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the functions in
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
are of additive derivative type, then the type II polynomial w.r.t.
(w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
for the multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with
\left|\vec{n}\right|=n
is given by

P_{\vec{n}}(x)=\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(0)\sum_{k=0}^{\left|\vec{n}\right|}(-1)^{\left|\vec{n}\right|-k}\binom{\left|\vec{n}\right|}{k}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}}\right)^{(\left|\vec{n}\right|-k)}(0)\,x^{k},

(7)

where
\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}
is as in (
5
).

Proof.

Note that
P_{\vec{n}}
is a monic polynomial of degree
\left|\vec{n}\right|
. We have to show that it satisfies the orthogonality conditions

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{k}w_{j}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n_{j}-1\},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,r\},

By Theorem
6.3
, this is equivalent to showing that

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\vec{n}}(x)\omega_{2,n}^{(k)}(x)dx=0,\quad k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}.

To do so, we will write

P_{\vec{n}}(x)=(-1)^{n}n!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(0)}{\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(t)}\frac{e^{-tx}}{t^{n+1}}\frac{dt}{2\pi i},

in terms of a counterclockwise contour
\mathcal{C}
enclosing
0
once. We can do this because
\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}
is analytic at the origin as

\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}\in\text{span}\{(\mathcal{L}w_{j})^{(k-1)}\mid k=1,\dots,n_{j},j=1,\dots,r\}.

After interchanging the integrals, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{n}(x)\omega_{2,n}^{(k)}(x)dx=(-1)^{n}n!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(0)}{\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(t)}\frac{(\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}^{(k)})(t)}{t^{n+1}}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}.

3.10
, we have
(\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}^{(k)})(t)=t^{k}\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}(t)
for

and thus

The integral therefore vanishes for all

.
∎

The polynomials in (

, then we have

whenever
\omega_{2,n}=\omega_{2,n}^{1}\ast_{\mathcal{L}}\omega_{2,n}^{2}
. The systems of weights to which Proposition

applies are described in (

).

6
Proof of Theorem

&

In this section, we will prove Theorem

&

. We will do so in several steps. In order to prove Theorem

, structurally, we have to show the following. Recall that

\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})=\{x\mapsto x^{k-1}w_{j}(x)\mid k=1,\dots,n_{j},j=1,\dots,r\}.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\subset\Sigma
and there exists positive

6.1
Algebraic description of derivative type

Crucial to our approach are the two theorems below, which can be seen as an alternative definition of collections of functions of multiplicative/additive derivative type. They essentially allow us to convert the analytic conditions in Definition
1.6
/
1.8
into algebraic ones by moving to the Mellin/Laplace space. These results also motivate the precise analytic conditions that appear in these definitions.

Theorem 6.4
.

Suppose that
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
are of multiplicative derivative type w.r.t. some function
\omega:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
,

ii)

there exists a function
\hat{\omega}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{M}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{s^{j-1}\hat{\omega}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In that case,
\mathcal{M}\omega=\hat{\omega}
on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Denote
(Df)(x)=-xf^{\prime}(x)
for functions
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
. For the implication from
i)
to
ii)
, we know that
\omega\in C^{n-2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, with
\omega^{(n-2)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, is such that a.e.

\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{D^{j-1}\omega\}_{j=1}^{n}.

If we apply Proposition
3.4
to
\nu_{j}=D^{j-1}\omega\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, we obtain

\mathcal{M}\nu_{j}(s)=s^{j-1}\mathcal{M}\omega(s),\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus

\text{span}\{\mathcal{M}\nu_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{s^{j-1}\hat{\omega}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In particular,
\dim\text{span}\{\mathcal{M}\nu_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n}=n
. Together with the fact that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{M}\nu_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\text{span}\{\mathcal{M}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad s\in\Sigma,

this leads to desired result.

For the implication from
ii)
to
i)
, we take
\nu_{j}\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
such that
\mathcal{M}\nu_{j}(s)=s^{j-1}\hat{\omega}(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma
. Since
\mathcal{M}\nu_{n}(s)=s^{n-1}\mathcal{M}\nu_{1}(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma
, it follows from Proposition
3.4
that there exists
\omega\in C^{n-2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, with
\omega^{(n-2)}\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, such that a.e.
\nu_{1}=\omega
and
\nu_{n}=D^{n-1}\omega
. We will show by induction on
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
that also a.e.
\nu_{j}=D^{j-1}\omega
. In that case, the functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
will be of multiplicative derivative type because

\text{span}\{\nu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n},

and
\dim\text{span}\{\nu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}=n
(if it would be less, we can take the Mellin transform and get a contradiction). For
j=1
, there is nothing to prove. Suppose that
\nu_{j}=D^{j-1}\omega
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, then
\mathcal{M}\nu_{j+1}(s)=s\mathcal{M}[D^{j-1}\omega](s)
for all
s\in\Sigma
. Proposition
3.4
then implies that there exists
F\in AC_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
such that a.e.
F=D^{j-1}\omega
and
\nu_{j+1}=DF
. Since both
D^{j-1}\omega,F\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, we must have that
F=D^{j-1}\omega
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and thus a.e.
\nu_{j+1}=D^{j}\omega
.
∎

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is of additive derivative type w.r.t. some function
\omega:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
,

ii)

there exists a function
\hat{\omega}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{L}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n}=\text{span}\{s^{j-1}\hat{\omega}(s)\}_{j=1}^{n},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In that case,
\mathcal{L}\omega=\hat{\omega}
on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

We can prove this using the same ideas as in the multiplicative setting, but using the Laplace transform instead of the Mellin transform and Proposition
3.10
instead of Proposition
3.4
.
∎

A side product of the previous results is the following connection between functions of additive and multiplicative derivative type.

Proposition 6.6
.

Consider the operator
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}:x\mapsto f(-\ln x)
on
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
. The functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\subset L_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
are of additive derivative type if and only if the functions
\tilde{v}_{1},\dots,\tilde{v}_{n}\subset L_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
are of multiplicative derivative type.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the algebraic conditions in Theorem
6.4
&
6.5
and the fact that
\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{M}\tilde{f}
on
\Sigma
.
∎

In what follows, we will describe some common properties of functions of multiplicative and additive derivative type, based on the algebraic description in Theorem
6.4
&
6.5
. As such, we will state all the results in terms of a linear operator
\mathcal{T}
acting on functions on some set
X\subset\mathbb{R}
. In the next section, we will restrict again to
\mathcal{T}\in\{\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}\}
.

We will first describe a necessary condition for consecutive collections of functions to be of multiplicative/additive derivative type. The previous algebraic description reveals the following structure on the transform of the underlying derivative type functions.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:X\to\mathbb{C}
and consider a linear operator
\mathcal{T}
that acts on
v\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{T}v:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
is defined on some infinite set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
and is non-vanishing. If, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there exists a function
\hat{\omega}_{m}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m}=\text{span}\{s^{j-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m},\quad s\in\Sigma,

then there exists
b_{0},\dots,b_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
d:\{1,\dots,n-1\}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{b_{0}\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

We will show this by induction on
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For
m=1
, this is immediate. Let
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and suppose that the formula holds for all integers
<m
. By the assumption, we know that for every
\nu\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, there exists a polynomial
p_{m-1}(s;\nu)
of degree at most
m-1
such that

\mathcal{T}\nu(s)=p_{m-1}(s;\nu)\hat{\omega}_{m}(s),\quad s\in\Sigma.

Note that both
\mathcal{T}\nu,\hat{\omega}_{m}\in\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
so that they are non-vanishing on
\Sigma
. In that case, the same holds for
\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}:s\mapsto p_{m-1}(s;\nu)
. Hence, for every
\nu\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, we must have that

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{T}\nu(s)}{p_{m-1}(s;\nu)},\quad s\in\Sigma.

By considering appropriate functions
\nu
, we can establish the desired formula. Consider first
\nu_{1}\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m-1}
with
\mathcal{T}\nu_{1}(s)=s^{d_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)
where
d_{0}=\#\{1\leq i\leq n-2:d(i)=0\}
. Since the degree of
s^{d_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}
is at most
m-2
, such
\nu_{1}
exists by the assumption. By the induction hypothesis, we then have

\mathcal{T}\nu_{1}(s)=b_{0}s^{d_{0}}\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s),\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{b_{0}s^{d_{0}}\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{1})},\quad s\in\Sigma,

for infinitely many
s\in\Sigma
. On the other hand, by the assumption, we can also consider
\nu_{2}\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m-1}
with
\mathcal{T}\nu_{2}(s)=\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)
. The induction hypothesis then implies that

\mathcal{T}\nu_{2}(s)=\frac{b_{0}\mathcal{T}w_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{b_{0}\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

By combining the two identities, we obtain

p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{1})=p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{2})s^{d_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In fact, since
\Sigma
contains infinitely many points, we can view this as a formal identity between the polynomials. As the degree of
s^{d_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}
is exactly
m-2
, the degree of
p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{2})
is at most
1
. We can therefore write
p_{m-1}(s;\nu_{2})=(s+b_{n-1})^{d(m-1)}
so that

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

as desired.
∎

The previous result also allows us to uncover the structure of the transforms of the initial functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}:X\to\mathbb{C}
and consider a linear operator
\mathcal{T}
that acts on
v\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{T}v:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
is defined on some infinite set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
and is non-vanishing. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there exists a function
\hat{\omega}_{m}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m}=\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m},\quad s\in\Sigma,

ii)

there exists
p_{0},\dots,p_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}[s]
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
d:\{1,\dots,N-1\}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, such that, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{m}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}=\frac{p_{m-1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and
\max\{\deg p_{m-1}(s),\deg\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}\}=m-1
.

In that case, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\mathcal{T}\omega_{m}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

We will first prove the implication from
i)
to
ii)
. It follows from Proposition
6.7
that there exists
b_{0},\dots,b_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
d:\{1,\dots,n-1\}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{b_{0}\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In that case, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there exists a polynomial
p_{m-1}\in\mathbb{R}[s]
of degree at most
m-1
such that

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{m}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}=\frac{p_{m-1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

We have to show that the degree conditions are satisfied. Consider the smallest value
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
for which

\max\{\deg p_{m-1}(s),\deg\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}\}<m-1.

In that case,
\deg p_{m-1}\leq m-2
and
\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}=\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})
. A partial fraction decomposition then gives

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{m}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{c_{j}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

This implies that
\mathcal{T}v_{m}\in\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m-1}
, which contradicts the fact that

\dim\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m}=\dim\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m}=m.

We will now show that the implication from
ii)
to
i)
holds. We will show
i)
by induction on
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
using the stated expression for
\omega_{m}
. For
m=1
, the desired result is immediate. Let
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and suppose that
i)
holds for all integers
<m
. We have to show that

\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m}=\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m},\quad s\in\Sigma.

We will first prove the inclusion from left to right. Let
v\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
so that
\mathcal{T}v=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\mathcal{T}v_{j}
. Then by the induction hypothesis and
ii)
, we can write

\mathcal{T}v(s)=\mathcal{T}v_{1}(s)\left(\frac{q_{m-2}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-2}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}}+c_{m}\frac{p_{m-1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}}\right),\quad s\in\Sigma,

in terms of some polynomial
q_{m-2}(s)
of degree at most
m-2
. After writing both terms on the common denominator
\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}
, we can conclude that
\mathcal{T}v(s)\in\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m}
.

For the implication from right to left, let
q_{m-1}(s)
be a polynomial of degree at most
m-1
and consider
\mathcal{T}v(s)=q_{m-1}(s)\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)
. We will show that there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s))\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\in\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m-1},\quad s\in\Sigma.

In that case,
\mathcal{T}v\in\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, because

(q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s))\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\in\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}v_{j}(s)\}_{j=1}^{m-1},\quad s\in\Sigma,

by the induction hypothesis, and
p_{m-1}(s)\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\mathcal{T}v_{m}(s)
by
ii)
. Suppose first that
d(m-1)=1
and thus

\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)=\frac{\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)}{s+b_{m-1}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Since
p_{m-1}(-b_{m-1})\neq 0
(otherwise the degree condition would be violated), we can take
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s)
vanishes at
s=-b_{m-1}
. In that case, we have

(q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s))\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\in\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m-1}.

Suppose now that
d(m-1)=0
and thus

\hat{\omega}_{m}=\hat{\omega}_{m-1},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Under this condition we have
\deg p_{m-1}=m-1
(otherwise the degree condition would be violated) and we can take
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that the degree of
q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s)
is at most
m-2
. In that case, we have

(q_{m-1}(s)-cp_{m-1}(s))\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)\in\text{span}\{s^{k-1}\hat{\omega}_{m-1}(s)\}_{k=1}^{m-1},

as desired.
∎

By taking appropriate linear combinations of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, we can slightly simplify the structure of the corresponding ratios
\mathcal{T}v_{n}/\mathcal{T}v_{1}
.

Proposition 6.9
.

In the setting of Proposition
6.8
, there exists an invertible upper-triangular matrix
U
such that the weights defined by
(v_{0,j})_{j=1}^{n}=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{n}U
satisfy, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{0,m}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{0,1}(s)}=\frac{s^{n-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Proposition
6.8
that there exists
v_{0,m}\in\text{span}\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
such that
\mathcal{T}v_{0,m}(s)=s^{m-1}\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)
for all
s\in\Sigma
. Then we can use the stated expression for
\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)
.
∎

With the result below, we aim to provide some more intuition on Proposition
6.8
. It essentially states that the
m
-dependence in
\hat{\omega}_{m}(s)
can be removed if besides multiplying
\mathcal{T}v_{1}
by
s
, we are also allowed to multiply with some
1/(s+b_{j})
.

Proposition 6.10
.

In the setting of Proposition
6.8
, there exists an invertible upper-triangular matrix
U
such that the weights defined by
(v_{0,j})_{j=1}^{n}=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{n}U
satisfy, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{m+1}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{m}(s)}=\begin{cases}s,\quad d(m)=0,\\
\frac{1}{s+b_{m}},\quad d(m)=1,\end{cases}\qquad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.9
, we can assume that

\frac{\mathcal{T}v_{0,m+1}(s)}{\mathcal{T}v_{0,m}(s)}=\frac{s}{(s+b_{n})^{d(n)}}.

If
d(m)=0
, we obtain the first case. If
d(m)=1
, we can do a partial fraction decomposition and subtract
v_{0,m}
from
v_{0,m+1}
to obtain the other case.
∎

6.2
Characterization

6.2.1
Multiplicative setting

Using the results from the previous section, we can obtain the following characterization of functions of multiplicative derivative type.

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{M},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
.
Then the following are equivalent:

i)

for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
are of multiplicative derivative type w.r.t. some function
\omega_{m}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
,

ii)

there exists
p_{0},\dots,p_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}[s]
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
d:\{1,\dots,n-1\}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{\mathcal{M}v_{m}(s)}{\mathcal{M}v_{1}(s)}=\frac{p_{m-1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and
\max\{\deg p_{m-1}(s),\deg\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}\}=m-1
.

In that case, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\mathcal{M}\omega_{m}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{M}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

We have to combine Theorem
6.4
and Proposition
6.8
.
∎

By considering the collections of functions
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
relevant for multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles, the above allows us to identify the moments of the first function
w_{1}
. Using the compatibility relations in Proposition
6.9
, we can then describe the moments of all the functions
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
:
i)
.

Since
r<n
, according to Proposition
6.11
, we can write

\frac{\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s+1)}{\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s)}=c\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
b_{i},c\in\mathbb{R}
and
d_{1},d_{2}:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1\}
with all
d_{1}(i)=1
or all
d_{2}(i)=1
. Consecutive use of this identity for
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, then leads to

\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s)=\mathcal{M}w_{1}(1)c^{s-1}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(a_{i}+1)_{s-1}^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(b_{i}+1)_{s-1}^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

Since
\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s)
is defined for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and doesn’t vanish, we must have
a_{i},b_{i}\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-1}
and
c\neq 0
. Hence, there exists
c_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, namely

c_{0}=\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(b_{j}+1)^{d_{2}(i)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(a_{j}+1)^{d_{1}(i)}},

such that

\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s)=c_{0}c^{s}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+a_{j})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+b_{j})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

Proposition
6.9
then implies that there exists an invertible upper-triangular matrix
U
such that the functions
(w_{1,j})_{j=1}^{r}=(w_{j})_{j=1}^{r}U
satisfy

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s)=c^{s}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+a_{j})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+b_{j})^{d_{2}(i)}}\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

After the reparametrization
\vec{b}\mapsto(b_{2},\dots,b_{r},b_{1}+1)
and modifying
d_{2}
accordingly, we obtain the desired result.
∎

In order to describe the Mellin transform on an interval
\Sigma
and not only at integer values, we have to impose an additional assumption on
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
. This is an analytic obstruction that also appears in connection to the Bohr-Mollerup Theorem, see, e.g.,
[
43
]
. The idea is that the moments can’t detect functions of the form
e^{\sin(\pi s)}
as part of the Mellin transform. In line with that theorem, we decide to demand positivity of on
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
. This is a natural assumption, because later we want to use these functions to create an ensemble.

We are now ready to prove the implication from
i)
to
ii)
in Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
:
i)\Rightarrow ii)
.

Since
r<n
, Proposition
6.11
gives rise to a first order difference equation for

of the form

\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s+1)=c\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s),\quad s\in\Sigma,

for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
b_{i},c\in\mathbb{R}
and
d_{1},d_{2}:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1\}
with all
d_{1}(i)=1
or all
d_{2}(i)=1
. We will now verify that Proposition
3.5
is applicable so that, up to a scalar multiplication, the above difference equation has a unique solution of the form
\mathcal{M}w_{1}
with a.e.

\Sigma=(s_{0},\infty)
for some
s_{0}\in[0,1)

Since

\mathcal{M}w_{1}>0
on

on
\Sigma
. Moreover,
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(n+1)/g(n)=1
. The conditions of Proposition

are therefore satisfied. With Theorem
6.2

i)
in mind, it is then straightforward to show that the unique solution is given by

\mathcal{M}w_{1}(s)=c_{0}c^{s}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+a_{j})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Gamma(s+b_{j})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

We can then proceed similarly as in the proof of Theorem

to describe the Mellin transform of the other weights.
∎

The last step in the proof of Theorem

is to prove the equivalence between

and
iii)
for all

(structurally, this is what we need to prove for Proposition

of Theorem

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that the Mellin transforms of

Proof.

We will prove this by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
via Proposition
6.11
. For
n=1
, we have
\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)/\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)=1
and
ii)
is satisfied. Hence,
\mathcal{W}(1;w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r})
is of multiplicative derivative type w.r.t. some
\omega_{1}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
with

\mathcal{M}\omega_{1}(s)=\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s),\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus
\mathcal{M}\omega_{1}=\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,1}
on
\Sigma
as desired. Suppose that
\mathcal{W}(m;w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r})

\omega_{1,m}

n=r\eta+j\geq 2

i)

m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}

ii)
for
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}

also holds for

\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1,1},\dots,w_{1,r})

\omega_{n}

\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s+\eta)/\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)

\frac{\mathcal{M}w_{1,j}(s+\eta)}{\mathcal{M}w_{1,1}(s)}=c^{\eta}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+a_{i})_{\eta}^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})_{\eta}^{d_{2}(i)}}\frac{s^{j-1}(s+b_{1})^{d_{2}(1)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j}(s+b_{i}+\eta)^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Note that the factor

. Take
\vec{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}
with

\mathcal{M}\omega_{n}=\mathcal{M}\omega_{1,n}
on

6.2.2
Additive setting

There is a similar characterization of functions of additive derivative type.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
are of additive derivative type w.r.t. some function
\omega_{m}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
,

ii)

there exists
p_{0},\dots,p_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}[s]
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
d:\{1,\dots,n-1\}\to\{0,1\}
such that, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{\mathcal{L}v_{m}(s)}{\mathcal{L}v_{1}(s)}=\frac{p_{m-1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and
\max\{\deg p_{m-1}(s),\deg\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}\}=m-1
.

In that case, for all
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\mathcal{L}\omega_{m}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}v_{1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(s+b_{i})^{d(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

Proof.

We have to combine Theorem
6.5
and Proposition
6.8
.
∎

By applying the previous result to collections of functions
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{1},\dots,w_{r})
relevant for multiple orthogonal polynomial ensembles, we will be able to identify the Laplace transform of the first function
w_{1}
. Compared to the multiplicative setting, to do so, we won’t have to impose any additional assumptions on
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
. The Laplace transforms of the remaining functions
w_{2},\dots,w_{r}
will follow from the compatibility conditions in Proposition
6.9
. This is exactly the content of the implication from
i)
to
ii)
in Theorem
6.3
.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
:
i)\Rightarrow ii)
.

We first note that by analyticity of
\mathcal{L}w_{j}
on
\Sigma
, we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{k-1}w_{j}(x)e^{-sx}dx=(\mathcal{L}w_{j})^{(k-1)}(s),\quad s\in\Sigma.

Since
r<n
, Proposition
6.13
then leads to a first order differential equation for
\mathcal{L}w_{1}
of the form

\frac{(\mathcal{L}w_{1})^{\prime}(s)}{\mathcal{L}w_{1}(s)}=c\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
b_{i},c\in\mathbb{R}
and
d_{1},d_{2}:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1\}
with all
d_{1}(i)=1
or all
d_{2}(i)=1
. Its solution is given by

\mathcal{L}w_{1}(s)=\mathcal{L}w_{1}(s_{0})\exp\left(c\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}dt\right),\quad s\in\Sigma,

where
s_{0}\in\Sigma
. Therefore, by Proposition
6.9
, there exists an invertible upper-triangular matrix
U
such that the functions
(w_{2,j})_{j=1}^{r}=(w_{j})_{j=1}^{r}U
satisfy

\mathcal{L}w_{2,j}(s)=\exp\left(c\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}dt\right)\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

After the reparametrization
\vec{b}\mapsto(b_{2},\dots,b_{r},b_{1})
and modifying
d_{2}
accordingly, we may write this as

\mathcal{L}w_{2,j}(s)=\exp\left(c\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}dt\right)\frac{s^{j-1}}{\prod_{i=2}^{j}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma.

It then remains to note that we can write

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i}^{\ast})^{d_{1}^{\ast}(i)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}-\frac{1}{(t+b_{1})^{d_{2}(1)}},

by taking
a_{i}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}
and
d_{1}^{\ast}:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\{0,1\}
such that
\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i}^{\ast})^{d_{1}^{\ast}(i)}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}+\prod_{i=2}^{r}(t+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}
. In that case, all
d_{1}(i)=1
is equivalent to all
d_{1}^{\ast}(i)=1
.
∎

In order to fully prove Theorem
6.3
, it remains to prove the equivalence between

and
iii)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(structurally, this is also what we need to prove Proposition
2.4
).

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that the Laplace transforms of
w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,r}\in L^{1}_{\mathcal{L},\Sigma}(\mathbb{R})
are given by (

Proof.

We will show this by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
via Proposition
6.13
. For
n\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, by definition, we have

\frac{\mathcal{L}w_{2,n}(s)}{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}=\frac{s^{n-1}}{\prod_{i=2}^{n}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and

is satisfied. Hence,
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,r})

\mathcal{L}\omega_{n}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}{\prod_{i=2}^{n}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus
\mathcal{L}\omega_{n}=\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}

\Sigma
as desired.
For

, we take the logarithmic derivative of

\frac{(\mathcal{L}w_{2,1})^{\prime}(s)}{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}=c\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{(s+a_{i})^{d_{1}(i)}}{(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}}-\frac{d_{2}(1)}{s+b_{1}},\quad s\in\Sigma,.

After writing the right hand side on a common denominator, it is straightforward to check that the degree condition in
ii)
is satisfied and that the denominator is
\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}
. Hence,
\mathcal{W}(r+1;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,r})
is of additive derivative type w.r.t. some
\omega_{r+1}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
with

\mathcal{L}\omega_{r+1}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

and thus
\mathcal{L}\omega_{r+1}=\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,r+1}
on
\Sigma
as desired.
Suppose that
\mathcal{W}(m;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,r})
is of additive derivative type w.r.t.
\omega_{2,m}
for all
m<n
and
n=r\eta+j\geq r+2
with
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,r-1\}
. Since
i)
is satisfied for
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we obtain
ii)
for
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. We need to show that
ii)
also holds for
m=n
so that, according to
i)
,
\mathcal{W}(n;w_{2,1},\dots,w_{2,r})
is of additive derivative type w.r.t. some
\omega_{n}
. If
(\mathcal{L}w_{2,j})^{(\eta)}(s)/\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)

\mathcal{L}\omega_{n}=\mathcal{L}\omega_{2,n}
on
\Sigma
. We know that

\frac{(\mathcal{L}w_{2,j})^{(\eta-1)}(s)}{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}=\frac{p_{\eta-1,j}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)(\eta-1+1_{2\leq i\leq j})}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathcal{L}w_{2,j})^{(\eta)}(s)}{\mathcal{L}w_{2,1}(s)}=\frac{p_{\eta,j}(s)}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(s+b_{i})^{d_{2}(i)(\eta-1+1_{2\leq i\leq j})}},\quad s\in\Sigma,

\Sigma
as desired.
