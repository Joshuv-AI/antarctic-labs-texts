Clinical Experience of Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve-In-Ring Interventions With Incomplete Rings: Transjugular and Transfemoral Approaches

Introduction

Despite the high rate of recurrent tricuspid regurgitation (TR) after surgical repair, transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-ring (TVIR) remains infrequently performed.1 Incomplete annuloplasty rings, designed to spare the septal annulus, increase the risk of valve embolization and paravalvular leak (PVL) compared with valve-in-valve procedures.2 This study describes our single-center experience and evaluates the feasibility of transjugular and transfemoral approaches for TVIR.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed all patients who underwent TVIR at Henry Ford Hospital (Michigan, USA) from 2015 to 2025. Baseline demographics; procedural details; and laboratory, imaging, and catheterization data were collected. TR grading and PVL was assessed by transthoracic echocardiogram at discharge. Extended 5-grade system was used in TR grading scheme, and the echocardiography was assessed locally.

The primary endpoints were procedural success, access-site complications, and in-hospital mortality. Procedural success was defined as successful device deployment with retrieval of the delivery system, patient survival to procedure end, and reduction of TR (including PVL) to moderate or less. Optimal device performance was considered as residual total TR graded as mild or less. Endpoint definitions followed Tricuspid Valve Academic Research Consortium criteria. Due to the small sample size, analyses were solely descriptive without statistical testing.

Ethical Statement

The study was approved by the institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed consent for the procedure.

Results

Twelve patients underwent TVIR implantation: 7 via transjugular and 5 via transfemoral approach. Transfemoral cases were performed earlier (2015-2018), whereas transjugular cases occurred later (2019-2025). All rings were incomplete. Ring sizes ranged from 26 to 34 mm (transjugular) and 26 to 32 mm (transfemoral). Baseline characteristics were comparable, with all patients in New York Heart Association class III/IV and predominant severe TR.

Ring types included rigid (Contour 3D, CG Future band, and MC) and semirigid (Carpentier-Edwards [CE]) rings. The transjugular group comprised 2 Contour 3D, 1 CE, and 4 MC rings. The transfemoral group comprised 2 Contour 3D, 2 CE, and 1 MC ring. Contour 3D rings had the highest PVL rate (3/4), followed by CE rings (2/2). MC rings (0/4) and the CG Future band (0/1) showed no PVL. Most valves were 29 mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra (transjugular 86% and transfemoral 80%), with later cases (2024-2025) using the SAPIEN 3 Ultra.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiogram guidance. An e-sheath was used in 100% of transfemoral cases and 57% of transjugular cases. The remainder used a DrySeal sheath. Rapid septal pacing was used in 100% of transfemoral cases but only 14% of transjugular cases. Valve delivery used a Lunderquist wire anchored in the right pulmonary artery (PA) (71% transjugular) or Amplatz Extra-stiff wire anchored in the right ventricle (80% transfemoral).

Procedural success (residual TR ≤ moderate) was 86% (6/7) in the transjugular group versus 60% (3/5) in the transfemoral group. No access-site complications occurred with transjugular access, whereas 1 groin complication (20%) occurred transfemorally. PVL rates were 29% (transjugular) versus 60% (transfemoral). One valve embolization to the superior vena cava in the transjugular group was successfully managed with a second valve. One in-hospital death due to cardiogenic shock occurred in the transfemoral group. No major bleeding, stroke, myocardial infarction, or unplanned surgery was observed. TR reduction ≥2 grades was achieved in 72% (transjugular) and 60% (transfemoral).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first descriptive user experience assessing transjugular and transfemoral approaches for TVIR implantation. Our findings demonstrate that Contour 3D rigid rings were associated with the highest rates of PVL, followed by semirigid CE rings. In contrast, MC3 and CG Future bands showed the lowest PVL rates.

Several anatomical and procedural factors may explain the numerically superior performance of the transjugular approach. Most tricuspid annuloplasty rings are incomplete to preserve the septal conduction tissue, predisposing to septal PVL. During transfemoral access, the anatomical offset between the inferior vena cava and tricuspid annulus creates a septal bias of the stiff wire as it crosses the annulus. This bias is particularly unfavorable for incomplete rings. In contrast, the transjugular route offers more direct, coaxial alignment due to the smaller offset between the superior vena cava and tricuspid annulus, minimizing septal wire bias (Figure 1a, b, and f).

Balloon-expandable valves tend to first contact the septum during deployment. Septal bias in the transfemoral approach can therefore result in suboptimal valve positioning with septal-to-lateral asymmetry relative to the ring, increasing the risk of PVL—especially in rigid rings (Figure 1a and b). Furthermore, transfemoral wire anchoring in the right ventricular apex often produces a sharp 90-degree bend in the right atrium, limiting valve alignment adjustments and frequently leading to an overly atrial deployment on the anterior side with resultant skirt leak (Figure 1d and e). The transjugular wire trajectory from the right internal jugular vein to the right PA creates a favorable U-shaped configuration (Figure 1c), allowing easy adjustment of wire redundancy for optimal coaxial alignment. In addition, the cranially oriented plane of most tricuspid rings aligns better with a superior access approach.

Additional advantages of transjugular access include compressible puncture sites with lower upright hydrostatic pressure, reducing bleeding risk, and shorter device travel distance that minimizes venous tortuosity and Eustachian ridge interference.

Technical Considerations

Room setup is shown in Figure 1h. We found that the Gore-sheath provides superior alignment for balloon mounting due to its rigidity and straight segment, reducing the risk of underinflation. One case of valve embolization requiring a second valve was associated with use of the e-sheath, where the balloon was not mounted on a straight portion.3 Moreover, when used via transjugular access, the expandable segment of the e-sheath remains outside the body, potentially increasing bleeding risk.

We recommend advancing a PA catheter to the right PA followed by a single-curve Lunderquist wire. Balloon sizing with real-time transesophageal echocardiogram helps assess waist and leaks to guide valve sizing, especially when computed tomography measurements are intermediate between 26 and 29 mm. The off-label SAPIEN valve can be deployed slowly without pacing. Postdilatation is advised to optimize sealing. Operators should prepare bailout strategies such as second valve implantation or PVL closure (Figure 1g).

Limitations

The small sample size of our study may result in an increased type II error with respect to adverse events. Notably, the transjugular and transfemoral cases were performed during nonoverlapping time periods, necessitating consideration of temporal bias and learning-curve bias, including operator experience. The newer device Sapien3 Ultra versus Sapien 3, featuring an extended external skirt, may potentially reduce PVL.

Conclusion

TVIR in incomplete tricuspid rings appeared technically feasible via both transjugular and transfemoral routes, with acceptable short-term safety. The transjugular route showed numerically better outcomes and potential technical advantages. Larger registries are warranted.
