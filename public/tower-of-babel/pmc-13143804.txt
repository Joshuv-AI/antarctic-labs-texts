Nutritional Risk Assessed by Royal Free Hospital-Nutritional Prioritizing Tool Predicts Survival in Cirrhosis With or Without Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Hyun Joon Park, Jin Kyu Jung, Jeonghwan Noh, Yeonsu Kim, Sang Uk Lee, Byung Cheol Yun, Jung Wook Lee, Hyung Jun Kim, Kwang Il Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNutritional status is an independent factor that affects clinical outcomes in patients with liver cirrhosis (LC). This study aimed to investigate the prognosis of patients with LC according to their nutritional risk.MethodsFrom January 2020 to April 2021, the medical records of patients with LC with/without hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who underwent abdominal computed tomography and nutritional risk evaluation using the Royal Free Hospital-Nutritional Prioritizing Tool (RFH-NPT) were retrospectively reviewed.ResultsIn total, 243 patients were analyzed, 92: LC without HCC and 151: LC with HCC. Nutritional risk analysis using RFH-NPT revealed that 121 (49.8%) patients were low-risk, 35 (14.4%) moderate-risk, and 87 (35.8%) high-risk. Child-Pugh class (P < 0.001), albumin-bilirubin score (P < 0.001), and psoas muscle index (P < 0.001) significantly correlated with nutritional risk in all patients. Serum sodium levels were significantly different based on the nutritional risk in LC without HCC (P = 0.008) and with HCC (P = 0.001). Total cholesterol levels were significantly correlated with nutritional risk in all patients (P < 0.001) and in patients with LC with HCC (P < 0.001). Survival rates differed significantly according to nutritional risk in all patients (P < 0.001), patients with LC without HCC (P = 0.017), and patients with LC with HCC (P < 0.001). RFH-NPT high risk (hazard ratio [HR], 3.115; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.396–6.950; P = 0.006), Child-Pugh C (HR, 12.802; 95% CI, 5.062–32.372; P < 0.001) and HCC (HR, 9.078; 95% CI, 3.657–22.535; P < 0.001) were significant factors affecting the survival rate of all patients.ConclusionRFH-NPT is a useful method for evaluating nutritional risk and predicting the survival rate of patients with LC with/without HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Liver disease is a progressive condition that can result in irreversible liver cirrhosis (LC). The prognosis of chronic liver disease varies depending on liver function.1 The median survival rate for patients with cirrhosis is more than 12 years, but for patients with decompensated cirrhosis, it is less than 2 years.2 Because of differences in survival rate, the improvement of liver function has been the focus in the hepatology field. As a result, the mortality rate of esophageal varices was over 30% in the 1990s, but has decreased to less than 10% since 2010.3

Malnutrition has been proved related with liver function4 and suggested as a prognostic factor in patients with LC.5 Nutritional status as assessed by handgrip strength has found that the incidence of complications such as hepatic encephalopathy and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis was significantly increased in patients with cirrhosis with a malnourished status.5 In addition, malnutrition has also been found to independently affect the survival rate and the incidence of complications in patients with LC.6 Therefore, nutritional screening, monitoring, and intervention are crucial in managing patients with LC.7 Academic societies have suggested nutritional guidelines to screen nutritional risk, assess appropriate nutritional status, and recommend interventions based on the result of screening and assessment.18

However, it is difficult to perform a sequential process by screening nutritional risk, assessing nutritional status based on the results, and performing interventions in clinical practice. Studies have shown that the prognosis of patients with liver disease can be predicted based on nutritional risk screening results alone. The Royal Free Hospital Nutritional Prioritizing Tool (RFH-NPT) is a well-known nutritional risk screening tool, originally developed in the United Kingdom to screen the nutritional risk of patients with liver disease.9 Although further validation is needed, RFH-NPT is currently regarded to be associated with clinical outcomes and liver function in patients with liver disease. This study aimed to investigate the prognosis of patients with LC with or without hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) according to nutritional risk status and to identify objective parameters that reflect this risk in a Korean cohort. We hypothesized that a higher nutritional risk, as assessed by RFH-NPT, would be associated with poorer survival in patients with LC, with or without HCC. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the relationship between RFH-NPT scores, clinical parameters (including psoas muscle index, sodium level, and cholesterol level), and overall survival.

METHODS

Study design

From January 2020 to April 2021, the medical records of patients with LC with or without HCC who underwent abdominal computed tomography (CT) and nutritional risk screening at Kosin University Gospel Hospital were retrospectively reviewed. No formal sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective design; therefore, all eligible patients were included during the study period. Patients who had no follow-up data for > 30 days or died within 30 days after the examination (CT and nutritional risk screening) were excluded from this study because the loss of medical records or the cause of mortality could not be related to nutritional risk status alone (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study patients. A total of 265 patients with liver disease were screened using RFH-NPT and underwent abdominal computed tomography. Twenty-two patients who were followed up for less than 30 days were excluded (three patients died and 19 patients were not followed up). Finally, 243 patients until the date of death or loss to follow-up were included in this study. Ninety-two patients were diagnosed with LC without HCC and 151 patients were diagnosed with LC and HCC.

LC = liver cirrhosis, HCC = hepatocellular carcinoma, RFH-NPT = Royal Free Hospital Nutritional Prioritizing Tool.

The nutritional risk screening method used in this study was the RFH-NPT, a well-validated questionnaire. Two hepatologists asked questions about RFH-NPT and recorded the patients’ answers.

LC was diagnosed if one of the following was identified: evidence of cirrhosis on imaging studies (CT or sonography), esophageal or gastric varices on endoscopy, laboratory data suggesting LC, or a history of clinical symptoms (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or variceal bleeding). Decompensated LC was diagnosed in patients with jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy. HCC was diagnosed radiologically or histologically using the criteria of the KLCA guideline.10 The stage of HCC was classified according to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification.11

The following methods were used to evaluate liver function. Child-Pugh class was performed using previously published methods.12 Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score was calculated using the following equation: ALBI Score = −0.085 × (Albumin [g/L] + 0.66 × log10 (Bilirubin [μmol/L]).13 Model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score was calculated using the following formula: 9.57 × ln(Creatinine [mg/dL]) + 11.2 × ln(INR) + 3.78 × ln(bBlirubin [mg/dL]) + 6.43.14

Nutritional risk screening tool

The RFH-NPT grading system was used to categorize the patients into three groups according to their nutritional risk status. According to the RFH-NPT system, patients were classified as low-risk (0 points), moderate-risk (1 point), or high-risk (2–7 points). First, patients with acute alcoholic hepatitis or tube feeding were immediately evaluated as the high-risk group. Second, the patients’ physical conditions, including peripheral edema, ascites, body mass index (BMI), unplanned weight loss in the past 3–6 months, and reduced dietary intake by half or more over the last 5 days were determined. Third, the scores were summarized and the patients were assigned to their corresponding groups. The RFH-NPT grading system recommends that physicians encourage diet and consult a dietitian for high-risk patients (scores of 2–7) and monitor nutritional status carefully for moderate-risk patients (score of 1).9

Skeletal muscle estimation

It is well known that psoas muscle mass measured at the L4-5 level or L3 level is highly correlated with total body skeletal muscle volume.15 Thus, we used the psoas muscle index (PMI) as the skeletal muscle mass in this study. The PMI was obtained by averaging the sum of the areas of both psoas muscles and standardized with the patients’ squared heights (cm2/m2). The psoas muscle area was measured using abdominal CT within 1 month of nutritional risk screening. All abdominal CT images were analyzed using a picture archiving and communication system. The cross-sectional areas of the bilateral psoas muscles at the lower level of the third lumbar vertebra were measured using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

According to the normality test results, means and standard deviations were calculated for all continuous variables. To compare the three groups according to nutritional risk, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed for continuous variables and linear-by-linear association analysis was performed for categorical variables. Post-hoc analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney test. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to analyze survival rates according to nutritional risk status using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed to assess the independent risk factors for survival. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. In the post-hoc test, Bonferroni’s method was used to determine statistical significance at P < 0.017. The SPSS software version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Kosin University Gospel Hospital (KUGH 2022-05-011) and the requirement for informed consent was waived. This research was conducted in compliance with publication issues.16

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 265 patients with liver disease were screened using RFH-NPT and underwent abdominal CT. Twenty-two patients who were followed up for less than 30 days were excluded (three patients died and 19 patients were not followed up). Finally, 243 patients until the date of death or loss to follow-up were included in this study. Ninety-two patients were diagnosed with LC without HCC and 151 patients with LC and HCC (Fig. 1).

In all patients, the mean age was 62.2 (± 9.99) and 186 (76.5%) of the patients were male. The mean BMI was 23.6 (± 4.05), PMI was 2.94 (± 0.94) cm2/m2, and follow up duration was 520 (± 261.8) days. Sixty-nine (28.4%) patients had diabetes and 86 (35.4%) had hypertension. Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity was observed in 94 (38.7%) patients, and anti-hepatitis C virus (HCV) positivity was observed in 46 (18.9%) patients. A total of 142 (58.4%) participants reported high-risk alcohol intake. A total of 160 (65.8%) patients were categorized as Child-Pugh class A. The mean ALBI score was −2.27 (± 0.69) and mean MELD score was 4.95 (± 0.86). Nutritional risk status assessed using RFH-NPT revealed that 121 (49.8%) patients were in the low-risk group, 35 (14.4%) in the moderate-risk group, and 87 (35.8%) in the high-risk group (Table 1).

LC without HCC

Ninety-two patients were diagnosed with LC without HCC. Their mean age was 59.0 (± 10.8) years. Fifty-five patients (59.8%) were males. The mean BMI was 23.8 (± 4.42). Fourteen (15.2%) patients were positive for HBsAg, and 10 (10.9%) patients were positive for anti-HCV. The number of patients with high-risk alcohol intake was 57 (62.0%). Forty (43.5%) patients were classified as Child-Pugh class A, 32 (34.8%) as Child-Pugh class B, and 20 (21.7%) as Child-Pugh class C (Supplementary Table 1).

Based on the nutritional risk status evaluated using RFH-NPT, patients with LC without HCC were categorized into three groups: 38 (41.3%) low-risk, 14 (15.2%) moderate-risk, and 40 (43.5%) high-risk. Body weight (P = 0.012) and BMI (P = 0.002) were significantly different according to the nutritional risk status. Liver function methods, such as Child-Pugh class (P < 0.001) and ALBI score (P < 0.001) were found to correlate with nutritional risk status, except for the MELD score (P = 0.132). Total cholesterol levels tended to correlate with nutritional risk status (P = 0.059).

LC with HCC

A total of 151 patients were diagnosed with LC and HCC. Their mean age was 64.2 (± 8.92) years and BMI was 23.5 (± 3.482). One hundred thirty-one (86.8%) patients were males. HBsAg was positive in 80 (53.0%) patients, and anti-HCV was positive in 36 (23.8%) patients. The number of patients with high-risk alcohol intake was 85 (56.3%). Regarding BCLC stage, 17 (11.3%) patients had a very early stage, 54 (35.8%) had stage A, 52 (34.4%) had stage B, 19 (12.6%) had stage C, and 9 (6.0%) had stage D (Supplementary Table 2).

The nutritional risk status evaluated using the RFH-NPT was low in 83 (55.0%), moderate in 21 (13.9%), and high in 47 (31.1%) patients. BMI (P < 0.001), PMI (P < 0.001), and total cholesterol levels (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with nutritional risk status. The Child-Pugh class (P < 0.001) and ALBI score (P < 0.001) also significantly worsened as nutritional risk deteriorated (Supplementary Table 2). In the low-risk group, 49 (59.1%) patients were diagnosed with early stage HCC (very early or stage A), and six (7.2%) were diagnosed with advanced-stage HCC (BCLC stage C or D). However, 12 (25.5%) patients in the high-risk group had early stage HCC (very early or stage A) and 18 (38.3%) had advanced HCC (BCLC stage C or D). Advanced-stage HCC was significantly more frequent in the high-risk group with a nutritional risk status (P < 0.001).

Liver function with nutritional risk screening results

Child-Pugh class

The Child-Pugh class correlated well with nutritional risk status in all patients (P < 0.001). It was confirmed that the number of patients with high nutritional risk increased as the Child-Pugh class moved from A to C (Table 1, Fig. 2). Moreover, Child-Pugh class was significantly associated with nutritional risk status assessed by RFH-NPT, regardless of the presence of HCC or not (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

ALBI score

The ALBI score correlated well with nutritional risk status in all patients (P < 0.001), LC without HCC (P < 0.001), and LC with HCC (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Post-hoc analysis of all patients revealed that ALBI score of the low-risk group was significantly lower than that of the moderate-risk group (P < 0.001) and the high-risk group (P < 0.001). In LC without HCC, post-hoc analysis revealed that the ALBI score of the low-risk group was significantly lower than that of the moderate-risk (P < 0.001) and high-risk group (P = 0.008). In LC with HCC, ALBI score of the low-risk group was significantly lower than that of the high-risk group (P < 0.001).

Laboratory findings with nutritional risk screening results

Serum sodium

Serum sodium levels were not associated with the nutritional risk status in all patients (P = 0.498) (Fig. 4A). However, serum sodium levels were significantly different based on the nutritional risk status in patients with cirrhosis without HCC (P = 0.008) and with HCC (P = 0.001) (Fig. 4B and C). In patients with LC without HCC, the serum sodium level of the high-risk group was lower than that of the low-risk group (P = 0.002). The serum sodium levels in the high-risk group of patients with LC and HCC were also lower than those in the low-risk group (P < 0.001).

Survival rate with nutritional risk screening results

In all patients, survival was significantly associated with nutritional risk as stratified by RFH-NPT (P < 0.001). One-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates were 96.5%, 89.2%, and 87.3%, respectively, in the low-risk group; 80.1%, 76.4%, and 50.1%, respectively, in the moderate-risk group; and 80.1%, 69.3%, and 46.7%, respectively, in the high-risk group (Fig. 7A). Among patients with cirrhosis without HCC, survival was significantly longer in the low-risk group than in the moderate- and high-risk groups (P = 0.017). Notably, the one-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates in the low-risk group were all 100.0% (Fig. 7B). In the subgroup of patients with cirrhosis with HCC, survival also differed significantly according to nutritional risk (P < 0.001), with 3-year survival rates of 81.5%, 71.9%, and 33.7% in the low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups, respectively (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Table 4).

In all patients, multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed Child-Pugh C (hazard ratio [HR], 12.802; 95% confidence interval [CI], 5.062–32.372; P < 0.001), high nutritional risk by RFH-NPT (HR, 3.115; 95% CI, 1.396–6.950; P = 0.006), and presence of HCC (HR, 9.078; 95% CI, 3.657–22.535; P < 0.001) were independent predictors of poor survival (Table 2). In the subgroup of patients with LC and HCC (n = 151), multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that BCLC stage D (HR, 6.657; 95% CI, 1.254–35.337; P = 0.026) and higher ALBI scores (HR, 2.142; 95% CI, 1.010–4.542; P = 0.047) were independently associated with poor survival. Although high-risk RFH-NPT was not statistically significant, the HR remained elevated (HR, 1.689), suggesting a potential prognostic association (Supplementary Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that higher nutritional risk, as assessed using the RFH-NPT, predicted poorer survival in patients with LC with or without HCC in Korea. These findings support our initial hypothesis that the nutritional risk status is a clinically meaningful prognostic factor in patients with LC. In addition, nutritional risk closely correlated with liver function indices, including Child–Pugh class and ALBI score. Patients with advanced HCC tended to have a more severe nutritional risk. Clinical variables such as the PMI, serum sodium level, and total cholesterol level were also significantly associated with nutritional risk, regardless of the presence of HCC.

Malnutrition is one of the most important prognostic factors in chronic liver disease, and all patients with cirrhosis regardless of decompensation, are advised to undergo nutritional risk screen.1 Both the RFH-NPT tool and Liver Disease Undernutrition Screening Tool are endorsed by European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guidelines.17 Especially, RFH-NPT score was reported to correlate with clinical deterioration, severity of disease, and complications. In our study, RFH-NPT results were also found to be correlated with Child-Pugh class and ALBI score. These data demonstrate that Korean patients with cirrhosis have a similar relationship between nutritional risk status and liver function. In addition, this study suggests that nutritional risk screening is clinically relevant for predicting the prognosis of patients with LC. While tumor stage is a major prognostic factor in patients with cirrhosis and HCC,18 nutritional risk assessed using RFH-NPT was also significantly associated with outcomes in the overall cohort, irrespective of the presence of HCC.

Although many laboratory findings are commonly used to evaluate nutritional status, it is difficult to accurately evaluate the nutritional risk status in patients with LC.8 For example, serum albumin and coagulation factors in patients with LC cannot represent nutritional risk status well because they are synthesized by the liver itself. However, this study found that some laboratory findings may have a potential role in screening for nutritional risk in patients with LC. Serum sodium and total cholesterol levels changed significantly according to the nutritional risk status screened using RFH-NPT. As LC worsens, fluid retention occurs, resulting in dilutional hyponatremia.19 As a result, it suggests that serum sodium levels may indirectly reflect the nutritional risk status of patients with cirrhosis. Cholesterol is mainly synthesized in the liver.20 In patients with liver disease, liver function is closely related to nutritional status. Therefore, as the liver function deteriorates, the ability to synthesize cholesterol decreases. Consequently, cholesterol levels can reveal the nutritional risk status of patients with LC.

Changes in body weight do not objectively represent the nutritional status of patients with LC.7 Therefore, several academic societies have suggested specific guidelines for assessing the nutritional status of patients with cirrhosis. The most important factors commonly used in each guideline are muscle mass and BMI.1817 Therefore, muscle mass is an objective and reproducible parameter to evaluate nutritional status in patients with cirrhosis.21 The relationship between muscle mass and nutritional status is well known, and muscle mass can affect the survival rate of patients with LC.222324 In this study, we found that PMI was significantly correlated with nutritional risk status in patients with cirrhosis in Korea. In clinical practice, RFH-NPT may be helpful in screening not only the nutritional risk status but also the risk of sarcopenia.

This study had some limitations. First, because this study had a retrospective design, the analysis was conducted based on medical records. Therefore, there may be limitations in accuracy and reliability. Second, although two well-trained hepatologists directly queried patients using the RFH-NPT questionnaire, the patients may have provided incorrect answers or did not answer honestly. Third, although this study assessed RFH-NPT at a single time point, nutritional risk is likely to be dynamic in patients with cirrhosis. Future studies evaluating serial RFH-NPT measurements may provide insights into how changes in nutritional risk over time relate to clinical outcomes and whether nutritional interventions can modulate this risk trajectory. Fourth, this study used the original MELD score rather than the most recent MELD system. Fifth, the PMI was measured directly using ImageJ software. However, technical measurement errors may occur. Sixth, the relatively small number of patients in the moderate-risk group and large number of patients in the high-risk group might have resulted in distortion of the nutritional risk status. Therefore, it is difficult to generalize the results of this study. In addition, nutritional interventions such as dietary counseling or BCAA supplementation were not consistently documented in the medical records; therefore, their potential influence on survival could not be evaluated in this study. Nevertheless, it is meaningful to confirm that nutritional risk assessed using RFH-NPT can predict survival in patients with cirrhosis, regardless of the presence of HCC.

Screening for nutritional risk using RFH-NPT in patients with LC with or without HCC is very useful for evaluating nutritional risk status and predicting patient prognosis. Clinically, objective markers, such as PMI, serum sodium level, and total cholesterol level, are also expected to be helpful in nutritional risk screening.
