Fair pricing, fair access; a systematic review of cost-effectiveness of new hyperlipidemia injectable medication in developing countries
Authors: Samad Azari, Hamid Pourasghari, Mohammad Ali Rezaei, Masoud Behzadifar, Shahrzad Salehbeigi, Soheila Rajaei, Dariush Jafarzadeh, Samira Soleimanpour, Masih Tajdini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLipid-lowering therapy is central to cardiovascular disease (CVD) management. Proprotein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin Type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors (alirocumab, evolocumab) and inclisiran offer greater low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction and improved outcomes beyond statins and ezetimibe, but high costs raise affordability concerns in low- and middle-income countries. This study reviews their cost-effectiveness in developing settings.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched up to March 1, 2024. Studies were assessed for Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs), Life Years Gained (LYGs), costs, and “Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratios” (ICERs). Quality and bias were evaluated using the “Integrated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards” (CHEERS) and ECOBIAS checklists, following “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses” (PRISMA) guidelines.ResultsOf 580 records, 13 studies (2019–2024) from China (n = 8), Thailand, Mexico, Taiwan, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia met inclusion criteria, mostly evaluating evolocumab for primary and secondary prevention using Markov models (Time horizon: 2 years –lifetime, Discount rates: 3–5%). Studies were generally from a healthcare perspective. Evolocumab was not cost-effective in Chinese post-myocardial infarction (MI) patients (ICERs up to $281,762/QALY) but favorable in familial hypercholesterolemia(FH) ($57,018/QALY), and when evolocumab added to statins was cost-effective in patients with acute MI ($7,819 to $61,242 per QALY) and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) (vs. ezetimibe + statins) ($26,110/QALY) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) (vs. placebo + statins)($4,268/QALY). Alirocumab ($490,198/QALY) and inclisiran ($335,404.88/QALY) were not cost-effective, while ezetimibe was ($22,965/QALY). In Thailand, PCSK9 inhibitors ($1,496,139/QALY), and ezetimibe ($33,246/QALY) exceeded thresholds. Evolocumab was cost-effective in Mexico ($37,925/QALY-$44,346/QALY) and Saudi Arabia ($49,502/QALY-$71,968/QALY) but not in Qatar. Sensitivity analyses identified drug prices, event risks, and utilities as key ICER drivers, with probabilistic analyses showing low cost-effectiveness in China (~ 2%) but higher in high-risk groups or at elevated thresholds.ConclusionCost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran varies across developing countries, driven by drug prices, WTP thresholds, and healthcare perspectives. Evolocumab may be cost-effective in high-risk subgroups or in Mexico and Saudi Arabia but remains largely unaffordable elsewhere. Ezetimibe was consistently more favorable. Price reductions, tiered pricing, pooled procurement, and context-specific thresholds are essential to improve access and equity.Keywords: Cost-effectiveness, Cardiovascular diseases, PCSK9, Inclisiran, Developing countries

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of death and disability worldwide. According to the Global Burden of Diseases report, the prevalence of CVD has increased from 271 million cases in 1990 to 523 million in 2019, representing a more than two-fold rise over the past three decades [1].

Lipid-lowering therapies are central to CVD prevention and management. While statins and ezetimibe remain the cornerstone treatments, newer lipid-lowering agents such as Proprotein Convertase Subtilisin/Kexin Type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors (alirocumab and evolocumab) and inclisiran have demonstrated substantial efficacy in reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and improving cardiovascular outcomes [2–4].

Despite their proven clinical benefits, these therapies come at a high cost. Their pricing poses a significant barrier to widespread adoption, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where healthcare budgets are more constrained. The issue of cost-effectiveness is therefore critical, as it balances the reductions in cardiovascular events and improvements in quality of life against the substantial financial burden of these treatments [5, 6].

In the current study, we aimed to review the cost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors and Inclisiran in developing countries. By understanding and addressing these cost-effectiveness concerns, we can work towards making PCSK9 inhibitors and Inclisiran more accessible and beneficial to patients worldwide, especially in middle- and low-income countries.

Methods

The present systematic review aimed to assess the cost-effectiveness analysis of Evolocumab, alirocumab and Inclisiran in Developing Countries.

The present study was conducted and reported based on the principles outlined in the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses” (PRISMA) statement [7] and was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) database (CRD42024586411).

Study identification

Databases search

To conduct this study, the following electronic scientific databases from inception to March 1, 2024, were searched:

PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL).

The above databases were searched without any language, time, study design, or publication status restrictions. A unique search strategy is used for each database that includes a combination of relevant keywords and medical thematic headings (medical subject headings, or MeSH in the case of PubMed/MEDLINE) were searched for relevant literature. Search for other resources

The list of included reference studies was further examined to identify relevant studies. To make the search as complete as possible, the Google Scholar search engine was also used. All of the above studies were entered into Endnote software (version X7; Thomson Reuters).

Screening and selection of studies

After the articles were collected and duplicates were removed, the titles and abstracts were reviewed and irrelevant articles were removed. The full texts of the remaining articles were reviewed in detail using the pre-determined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the reasons for exclusion were recorded. All steps of article review and selection were performed independently by two researchers. Any disagreements were discussed to make a final decision.

Assessing the reporting quality of studies

The “Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards” (CHEERS) tool was used to evaluate the quality of included studies [8]. The CHEERS tool consists of twenty-eight questions in seven sections.

Assessing risk of bias

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias in included economic evaluations using the ECOBIAS checklist, a structured tool specifically developed for economic modeling studies [9]. The checklist comprises 22 items, divided into two parts:

Part A: Overall biases in economic evaluation (11 items), which assess general issues such as perspective, comparator choice, cost and utility measurement, discounting, sensitivity analyses, sponsorship, and reporting transparency.

Part A: Overall biases in economic evaluation (11 items), which assess general issues such as perspective, comparator choice, cost and utility measurement, discounting, sensitivity analyses, sponsorship, and reporting transparency.

Part B: Model-specific biases (11 items), which focus on structural assumptions, model type, time horizon, data identification, baseline data quality, treatment effects, utility weights, incorporation of data, scope, and internal consistency.

Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by discussion.

Data extraction and analysis (synthesis)

A data collection form was used to extract the entered data. This form included the major characteristics of studies and outcome information, such as author, country and year of study, patient population, comparator, study perspective, time horizon, sensitivity analysis performed, discount rate, type of modelling, health outcomes, funding, study question and intervention performed, LYGs, QALYs, annual and total cost, ICER and cost-effectiveness. Data extraction related to each of the entered studies was performed independently by two researchers and was controlled by a third one. The cost-effectiveness information of the compared technologies was tabulated and qualitatively synthesized. Results were reported by categorizing studies according to World Bank country income level (2024 designation) [10]. We then standardized the ICER values to 2024 US dollars using the CCEMG-EPPI-Centre cost converter, which first inflates costs to the reference year using the GDP deflator index and subsequently converts them to US dollars via purchasing power parity (PPP) [11].

General characteristics

The results of the study show that the studies from 2019 to 2024 have been published. Also, from the developing countries included in the study, eight studies in China [12–19], one study in Thailand [20], one study in Taiwan [21], one study in Mexico [22], one study in Qatar [23], and one study in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) [24] were published.

Studies generally focused on primary and secondary prevention. Also, most of the economic evaluation studies have been on evolocumab inhibitors. Studies were generally from a healthcare perspective. Across the included studies, most reported direct medical costs, covering expenditures such as hospital stays, medications, diagnostic tests, and physician services, within a healthcare perspective. One study adopted a societal perspective, including both direct medical and non-medical costs (e.g., patient transportation, caregiving) [20], and another incorporated indirect costs such as productivity loss alongside direct medical costs [17]. In two studies, the cost type was not reported [14, 23]. The results of the study show that the time horizon of the entered articles was very different and fluctuated from 2 to lifetime. All economic evaluation studies included in the present study were based on Markov models. The discount rate for economic evaluation studies in this study is in the range of 3–5%. The general characteristics of included studies are presented in Table 2.

Quality assessment results

The CHEERS 2022 quality assessment showed that the included studies generally demonstrated good reporting in key areas such as title, abstract, background, comparators, perspective, time horizon, outcome selection, cost measurement, modeling, and presentation of main results. However, several gaps were identified, including insufficient reporting of study population characteristics, discount rates, outcome valuation methods, and cost year/conversion details. Transparency in analytic assumptions [12–14, 19, 21–23] and input parameters [14, 21, 23] was also limited in some studies, and only a few addressed heterogeneity [12, 15, 17, 20]. Importantly, none of the studies reported patient or stakeholder involvement. Furthermore, disclosures on funding sources and conflicts of interest were missing in a subset of studies [14, 21, 23]. The quality assessment results are presented in Table 3.

Risk of bias assessment results

Across the 22 ECOBIAS items, most domains were rated as low risk of bias, particularly in areas related to comparator choice (Item 2), discounting (Item 6), sensitivity analysis (Item 7), model structure (Item 12), and internal consistency (Item 22), where nearly all studies scored favorably. However, several domains showed partial risk of bias, including cost measurement omission (Item 3), double counting (Item 9), baseline data (Item 17), and data identification (Item 20), indicating limitations in data sources or incomplete methodological justifications. A small number of items reflected high risk of bias, most notably for quality-of-life weights/utilities (Item 19), where 6 studies failed to adequately address potential bias. Some domains, such as discounting (Item 10) and ICER measurement (Item 11), also demonstrated mixed ratings with both partial and high risk. Very few items were rated as unclear, and no items were classified as not applicable.

Overall, the majority of evaluations demonstrated methodological rigor. A summary of risk of bias assessment results is presented in Fig. 2.

Sensitivity analysis

Across studies, deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) identified health utility values [12–14], cardiovascular mortality or event risks [15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 24], and treatment-related costs, especially PCSK9 inhibitors or inclisiran [16, 19], as key ICER drivers. Uncertainty in cardiovascular risk parameters could shift ICERs between cost-effective and non–cost-effective ranges [16, 17], though most base-case results were robust.

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) showed low cost-effectiveness probabilities for evolocumab in China (~ 2%) [12, 14], but high values in higher-risk groups or at higher willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds [15, 18, 22, 24]. Alirocumab was rarely cost-effective in China (≤ 1.7%) [13], though one study suggested 74% at three times GDP per capita [21]. Inclisiran showed 0% at current price but 55.7% if reduced [16]. Ezetimibe plus statin was more consistently favored (53.1%) compared with PCSK9-based strategies [19].

Discussion

The present study aimed to systematically review the cost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran in patients with CVDs in developing countries. The results showed that 13 studies evaluated the cost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran in developing countries.

Alirocumab is a monoclonal antibody administered via subcutaneous injection, typically every two weeks or once a month, depending on the dose [25]. It is approved for adults with primary hyperlipidemia, patients with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia and individuals at high cardiovascular risk who need additional LDL-lowering beyond statins [25]. Its common side effects include injection site reactions (pain, redness), flu-like symptoms and potential for allergic reactions which affect quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) [26]. Two studies evaluated cost-effectiveness of alirocumab [13, 21]. In one study, alirocumab was not cost-effective versus placebo in patients with myocardial infarction (MI) in China but as a subgroup analysis, it was cost-effective in patients with polyvascular disease in 3 vascular beds in China [13]. The ICERs were 1,613,997 CNY per QALY gained ($US 490,198 per QALY) and 111,750 CNY per QALY ($US 33,940 per QALY), respectively [13]. In the other study, alirocumab with high-intensity statin as background therapy was not cost-effective compared to high-intensity statin plus ezetimibe in Taiwan [21]. The ICER was USD53,938.7 per QALY [21]. The cost-effectiveness of alirocumab in developing countries is complex and multifaceted. The high cost per injection of alirocumab can be a major barrier in developing countries where healthcare budgets are limited. While statins are cheaper, they may not achieve adequate LDL reduction in all patients. Alirocumab may be more suitable for high-risk individuals who do not respond to traditional treatments statin-intolerant patients, potentially justifying the higher cost. China and Taiwan are two developing countries with geographical similarities. Chinese study was done from a healthcare perspective [13] but Taiwanese study was done from National Health Insurance [21]. This difference in study perspective causes a noticeable difference in calculated costs of treatment with alirocumab in these countries.

Evolocumab is a monoclonal antibody administered via subcutaneous injection, typically every two to four weeks [27]. It is indicated for patients with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia or established CVDs who require additional LDL-lowering [27]. It can be used in conjunction with other lipid-lowering therapies including statins, ezetimibe and more. Clinical trials, such as the DESCARTES and FOURIER studies, have demonstrated that evolocumab significantly reduces LDL cholesterol levels compared to placebo [28, 29]. It is generally well-tolerated but common side effects include injection site reactions, muscle aches, and in some cases, allergic reactions [26]. Eight studies included in the current study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of evolocumab in developing countries including China, Mexico, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) and Qatar [12, 14, 15, 17, 18, 22–24]. Three studies compared the cost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors with ezetimibe and their results were contrasting [15, 19, 20]. Two studies were done in China [15, 19] and one study was done in Thailand [20]. In the first study, the combination of evolocumab + statins was found to be cost-effective at a threshold of 217,341 CNY in patients with recent acute coronary syndrome (ACS) events compared with ezetimibe + statins in China [15]. In the second study, in Chinese adults receiving high-dose statins for the secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), adding ezetimibe is cost-effective compared to adding PCSK9 inhibitors [19]. In this study, PCSK9 inhibitors plus high-dose statin offered 7.21 QALYs and ezetimibe plus high-dose statin offered 7.04 QALYs but PCSK9 inhibitors plus high-dose statin offered US$39,590 and ezetimibe plus high-dose statin offered US$27,825 [19]. The ICER of PCSK9 inhibitors plus high-dose statin was USD68,910 ($US 78,180/QALY) which exceeded from WTP threshold of USD37,655 in China [19]. In the third study, despite the proven effectiveness of PCSK9i + statin and ezetimibe + statin because of the costs of these agents, they were not cost-effective versus statin alone in Thailand [20]. More studies are needed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of PCSK9 inhibitors versus ezetimibe in China and Thailand. WTP threshold in Thailand was noticeably less than in China and the discount rate in Thailand was 3% versus 5% in China which can describe the differences observed in the cost-effectiveness of these agents between these two countries. Two studies evaluated the cost-effectiveness of evolocumab plus statins versus statins alone and combination of placebo and statins in China [15, 17]. Evolocumab plus statins was cost-effective versus statins alone and combination of placebo and statins in China [15, 17]. Overall, accordance to results of these two studies, it seems adding evolocumab to statins can be a suitable option in China. It is needed to do more research on the cost-effectiveness of adding alirocumab to statins versus statins in different developing countries. There were three studies which one of them was done in Mexico [22] and two studies were done in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) [24] and Qatar [23]. In Mexico study, it was shown evolocumab added to standard of care (SoC, high-intensity statin with/without ezetimibe) may provide a cost-effective intervention in primary hypercholesterolemia and mixed dyslipidemia (PHMD) with a history of either myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke and heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HeFH) [22]. The ICER was 348,629 MXN/QALY ($US 44,346/QALY) for PHMD plus history of myocardial infarction (MI) or ischemic stroke (IS) and 298,148 MXN ($US 37,925/QALY) for HeFH which did not exceed WTP threshold of 521,435 MXN [22]. In contrast, in Qatar, the addition of evolocumab to standard therapy in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease was not cost-effective compared to standard therapy [23]. The time horizon in this study was 50 years which affects the results [23]. It is recommended to do studies with the time horizon of less than 50 years in Qatar to determine the cost-effectiveness of these combinations [23]. In line with the results of the study in Mexico and contrast with the results of the study in Qatar, in KSA, evolocumab can be considered a cost-effective treatment option for patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia [24].

Inclisiran is a novel small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy designed to lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [30]. It works by targeting the PCSK9 gene, which plays a significant role in cholesterol metabolism [30]. Inclisiran is administered via subcutaneous injection and is notable for its long-lasting effects, allowing for dosing every six months [30]. This extended duration of action differentiates it from other LDL-lowering therapies, which may require more frequent dosing. Its extended dosing interval may improve QALY. While inclisiran is generally well-tolerated, the most common side effect reported includes injection site reactions [30]. One study included in the current study, evaluated the cost-effectiveness of inclisiran in China [16]. In this study, it was shown the combined use of inclisiran and statins is not cost-effective compared with statin alone [16]. The ICER was USD 316,343.32/QALY ($US 335,404.88/QALY) and the WTP threshold was USD 38,223.33/QALY [16]. It was shown inclisiran will be cost-effective only if the price of inclisiran is reduced by more than 88% [16]. This study showed the high cost of inclisiran may be an important challenge in developing countries. Some studies have shown that inclisiran can significantly lower LDL-C levels, often achieving reductions of more than 50% from baseline [31, 32]. Inclisiran may have a high upfront cost compared to traditional statins. However, when considering its longer dosing intervals and reduced overall medication requirements, could lead to cost savings in the long term [16]. Also, few studies have been done in developing countries. It is also recommended to research the cost-effectiveness of inclisiran in those countries.

In one study in Russia, it was shown ezetimibe, alirocumab, evolocumab and inclisiran used in combination with statins were cost-effective compared with current preferential drug provision (PDP) practice (use of atorvastatin, simvastatin and minimal use of other drugs) according to LYG criterion, but not according to the QALY criterion [33]. The ICER for LYG (1,949,393 rubles per LYG) did not exceed the WTP threshold in all scenarios, while the ICER for QALY (3,598,156 rubles per QALY) exceeded the WTP threshold by 29–44% [33]. The WTP threshold was 2.8 million rubles per effect unit in this study [33].

Across studies, all used Markov models (mostly 1-year cycles) but differed in health state detail, cost, and utility sources, which drove large ICER variations. Chinese studies relied on local hospital/insurance data and drug procurement prices, with utilities often borrowed from UK/European surveys, leading to ICERs ranging from very high (≥ 1 M CNY/QALY) to favorable (< 20k CNY/QALY) depending on whether older or post-price-cut PCSK9 inhibitor costs were used. Other countries (Thailand, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Taiwan, Qatar) similarly applied national price lists or hospital tariffs, with utilities from published sources, producing ICERs that were generally high (tens of thousands to millions per QALY).

A narrative synthesis of the included studies revealed that pricing strategies, healthcare perspective, and funding source substantially influenced ICER outcomes. Studies adopting a societal perspective tended to capture broader costs such as non-medical expenses, generally resulting in more favorable cost-effectiveness ratios compared to those limited to direct medical costs only [20]. Similarly, time horizons varied widely (from 2 years to lifetime), with longer horizons generally making PCSK9 inhibitors appear more cost-effective due to accrued benefits. Funding source also appeared influential. Several studies funded by Amgen reported more favorable outcomes for evolocumab [15, 18, 22, 24], while unfunded or publicly funded studies more often found PCSK9 inhibitors not to be cost-effective [12, 13, 17, 19, 20]. Finally, variation in discount rates (3% vs. 5%) and reporting of costs (some omitting indirect costs altogether) further contributed to heterogeneity in ICER results across countries.

In our review, willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds were often based on the conventional one to three times GDP per capita. While widely used, this approach has limitations as it may not reflect true opportunity costs or local budget constraints, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. We found that some studies applied GDP-based thresholds without clear justification or alignment with national guidelines. To improve interpretation, our review emphasizes the need for context-specific, empirically informed thresholds that better reflect local health system priorities and resources.

While this review focused on cost-effectiveness, it is important to note potential equity implications. High prices of PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran may restrict access to wealthier patients or urban populations, worsening disparities. At the same time, these drugs could be particularly valuable for high-risk groups such as those with familial hypercholesterolemia, polyvascular disease, or statin intolerance. Equity-sensitive policies such as tiered pricing, subsidies, or local production, are needed to ensure fair access in resource-limited settings.

As the current study shows, the high cost of PCSK9 inhibitors poses major challenges in developing countries. To improve affordability and access, strategies such as local production, where feasible, could reduce reliance on imports and lower distribution costs. However, feasibility varies by country and depends on existing pharmaceutical infrastructure, technology transfer agreements, and regulatory capacity. In parallel, innovative pricing models like tiered pricing, in which pharmaceutical companies set different prices based on a country’s income level or ability to pay, voluntary licensing agreements, in which patent holders authorize generic manufacturers to produce and distribute these agents at lower cost, public subsidies, and pooled procurement mechanisms at the national or regional level, may also be considered to mitigate affordability barriers and ensure equitable access.

Like any other study, the current study faced some limitations

(1) No study was done in developing countries in Africa (2) Some studies did not declare funding sources which is so important in determining the conflict of interest (3) Some studies did not determine the perspective (4) Just studies in the English language were included in current study (5) Some studies did not report discount rates which makes it challenging to analyze the results.

Future research should focus on country-specific cost-effectiveness analyses, particularly in low-income and African settings, and adopt longer time horizons to reflect the durable effects of agents like inclisiran. Studies should also assess equity impacts, especially for high-risk groups such as patients with familial hypercholesterolemia or statin intolerance.

Conclusion

Across countries, cost-effectiveness results for PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran showed wide variation, largely driven by drug prices, willingness-to-pay thresholds, and healthcare system perspectives. In China, evolocumab was generally not cost-effective in the general MI population but became favorable in high-risk subgroups or when added to statins in recent ACS or ASCVD patients, whereas alirocumab and inclisiran were not cost-effective at current prices. Thailand and Qatar reported PCSK9 inhibitors as not cost-effective, with substantial price reductions required. In contrast, Mexico and Saudi Arabia demonstrated potential or consistent cost-effectiveness for evolocumab in patients with ASCVD or FH, reflecting differences in thresholds and healthcare cost structures. Ezetimibe was more consistently cost-effective across settings, often dominating PCSK9-based regimens. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that cardiovascular risk parameters, health utilities, and treatment costs were key ICER drivers, with probabilistic results showing higher cost-effectiveness probabilities in high-risk subgroups or at elevated thresholds.

Taken together, while PCSK9 inhibitors may be justified for selected high-risk populations in some upper-middle- and high-income countries, their affordability remains a critical barrier in most developing contexts. Strategies such as price reductions, tiered pricing, pooled procurement, and context-specific willingness-to-pay thresholds should be adopted by policymakers to improve access and ensure equitable use.
