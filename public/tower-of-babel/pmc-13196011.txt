When safety competes with nutrition: maternal dietary diversity and coping strategies in conflict-affected Sokoto State, Nigeria
Authors: Lauriya Yusuf, Jimoh Amzat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal dietary diversity is a critical indicator of micronutrient adequacy, yet it remains severely compromised in conflict-affected settings. In north-western Nigeria, persistent insecurity disrupts food systems, livelihoods, and market access, exacerbating maternal nutritional vulnerability. This study examines the determinants of maternal dietary diversity among pregnant and lactating women in conflict-affected areas of Sokoto State.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods design was employed. Quantitative data were collected from 768 women using structured questionnaires incorporating a 24-hour recall to compute the Maternal Dietary Diversity Score (MDDS). Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of adequate dietary diversity (≥ 5 food groups). Qualitative data from in-depth interviews and focus group discussions were analysed thematically to contextualise statistical findings.ResultsDietary diversity was markedly low, with 70.0% consuming fewer than five food groups. Bivariate analysis revealed significant associations between dietary diversity and location (χ²=59.81, p < 0.05), education (χ²=182.37, p < 0.05), employment (χ²=68.11, p < 0.05), and household role (χ²=187.82, p < 0.05). Multivariable regression showed that duration of displacement significantly increased the likelihood of adequate dietary diversity: 6–12 months (AOR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.01–3.28), 1–2 years (AOR = 2.35; 95% CI: 1.37–4.03), and ≥ 2 years (AOR = 3.10; 95% CI: 1.85–5.20). Women in Sabon-Birni were more likely to achieve adequate dietary diversity than those in Illela (AOR = 1.85; 95% CI: 1.02–3.35). Similarly, IDPs (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.12–3.29) and refugees (AOR = 3.10; 95% CI: 1.58–6.08) had higher odds compared to host communities. Conversely, households with changing income were significantly less likely to achieve adequate dietary diversity (AOR = 0.10; 95% CI: 0.04–0.24). Qualitative findings revealed coping strategies including maternal self-deprivation, reliance on monotonous diets, and constrained market access due to insecurity.ConclusionsMaternal dietary diversity in conflict settings is shaped by structural determinants including displacement, location, and economic instability. Interventions must integrate nutrition support with livelihood recovery, market stabilization, and security-sensitive programming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27293-5.Keywords: Maternal Health, Food Security, Food Insecurity, Conflict, Nutrition, Dietary Diversity

Introduction

Global food security remains one of the most pressing challenges confronting public health systems, particularly in regions affected by armed conflict and chronic insecurity. Maternal dietary diversity (MDD)—defined as the number of different food groups consumed within a specified reference period—is a widely recognised indicator of micronutrient adequacy and overall diet quality [1, 2]. Adequate dietary diversity during pregnancy and lactation is critical for maternal wellbeing, foetal development, and infant survival. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), structural inequalities, poverty, and limited access to healthcare services continue to exacerbate nutritional vulnerabilities among women of reproductive age.

Pregnancy and lactation are characterised by increased nutritional demands, and insufficient dietary diversity during these stages is associated with heightened risks of maternal morbidity, low birth weight, impaired child growth, and long-term developmental deficits [3–5]. While overt hunger remains visible in global health discourse, micronutrient deficiencies—often described as “hidden hunger”—affect far larger populations and produce enduring health consequences across the life-course [6, 7]. Diets dominated by starchy staples but lacking in fruits, vegetables, and animal-source foods contribute significantly to these deficiencies, particularly among women in resource-constrained settings.

Food insecurity is further exacerbated in contexts of armed conflict. Conflict directly undermines food security by dismantling agricultural systems, disrupting supply chains, destroying infrastructure, and displacing populations from their livelihoods [8, 9]. Across Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), conflict-driven food insecurity has intensified in recent decades, leading to inflated food prices, reduced market access, and diminished availability of diverse foods [10, 11]. Violence forces farmers to abandon farmlands, restricts pastoral movements, and collapses local markets, thereby eroding both food availability and economic access. Large-scale displacement further overwhelms already fragile food and sanitation systems, pushing millions into camps and informal settlements characterised by poor access to nutritious foods [12, 13].

Pregnant and lactating women bear a disproportionate share of these burdens due to the intersection of biological vulnerability and entrenched gender inequalities. Gender norms, caregiving responsibilities, restricted mobility, and limited control over household resources heighten women’s exposure to food insecurity during conflict [14–16]. The destruction of productive assets such as livestock, seeds, and farming tools across conflict-affected countries including Nigeria, Mali, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Ethiopia has resulted in prolonged humanitarian crises with severe nutritional consequences [17]. Undernutrition rates in conflict zones consistently exceed those in non-conflict areas, underscoring the profound impact of organised violence on food systems and health outcomes [18].

Nigeria exemplifies these overlapping crises of conflict, food insecurity, and maternal vulnerability. While the country faces multiple forms of insecurity—ranging from militancy in the Niger Delta to farmer–herder clashes and widespread banditry—the Boko Haram insurgency in the North-East has been particularly devastating, leading to massive displacement, infrastructure destruction, and the collapse of social services [19, 20]. In Sokoto State—identified as a malnutrition hotspot [17]—persistent insecurity driven by banditry, kidnapping, and communal violence has intensified food system disruptions. These dynamics have led to farmland abandonment, fractured trade routes, constrained pastoral livelihoods, and entrenched food insecurity, compounding already poor maternal health indicators and overall health system vulnerabilities. This convergence underscores the suitability of the state as a critical context for this study [19].

Conflict-related gender-based violence (GBV) further compounds women’s nutritional vulnerability by limiting their safety, mobility, and access to markets and humanitarian assistance [16, 21]. As a result, access to nutrient-dense foods—such as fruits, vegetables, and animal-source proteins—is severely constrained. Economic hardship driven by livelihood loss, market inflation, and poverty renders nutritious foods unaffordable for many households [22, 23]. At the same time, healthcare services including antenatal care, nutrition counselling, and emergency obstetric services become inaccessible due to insecurity, damaged infrastructure, and shortages of trained personnel [24]. Psychological stress associated with violence, trauma, and displacement further disrupts appetite and eating patterns, independently affecting maternal nutrition [25, 26]. Maternal undernutrition in conflict-affected settings is therefore a major driver of maternal mortality, adverse birth outcomes, and intergenerational health inequalities [4, 5, 27]. Nigeria already bears one of the highest global burdens of maternal and child mortality, with conflict-affected regions recording the poorest indicators [20, 28]. Despite growing global evidence on conflict and nutrition, there remains a critical knowledge gap regarding the local-level mechanisms through which conflict constrains maternal dietary diversity in under-studied contexts such as Sokoto.

This study addresses this gap by examining maternal dietary diversity among pregnant and lactating women in conflict-affected communities in Sokoto State. The study is anchored in the Food Capability Approach and Social Capital Theory, both of which are linked to the empirical variables measured. Food capability dimensions—access, utilization, and agency—are operationalized through indicators such as household food access, maternal dietary diversity scores, nutrition-related knowledge, healthcare utilization, and women’s decision-making autonomy, all of which are shaped by insecurity [29, 30]. Social capital is captured through measures of social support, reliance on kinship and community networks, and participation in informal coping arrangements, which influence food access and adaptive strategies. Insecurity and displacement are further examined as factors disrupting these networks and constraining both capability and support systems [31–34]. By aligning theory with measurable constructs, the study provides empirically grounded insights into how structural and relational factors interact to shape maternal nutrition in insecure settings.

Methods

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative data to enhance analytical robustness. A cross-sectional survey of 768 pregnant and lactating women was complemented by in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, enabling triangulation of findings and a comprehensive understanding of maternal dietary diversity in conflict-affected settings.

Study design

This study adopted a convergent mixed-methods design to examine maternal dietary diversity among pregnant and lactating women in conflict-affected areas of Sokoto State, Nigeria. The design enabled the simultaneous collection and analysis of quantitative and qualitative data [35], allowing for triangulation and a more nuanced understanding of how conflict shapes dietary practices and coping strategies. The quantitative component employed a cross-sectional survey to generate population-level estimates of dietary diversity and food insecurity, while the qualitative component provided contextualised insights into women’s lived experiences, perceptions, and adaptive behaviours under conditions of insecurity. The integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence strengthened the analytical depth of the study by linking statistical patterns with explanatory narratives, thereby enhancing the credibility and interpretive validity of the findings [36].

Study area and population

The study was conducted in Illela and Sabon-Birni Local Government Areas (LGAs) of Sokoto State, located in north-western Nigeria. These LGAs were purposively selected due to their prolonged exposure to insecurity arising from banditry, farmer–herder conflicts, and attacks by non-state armed groups. Both LGAs are predominantly rural, agrarian communities whose populations rely largely on subsistence farming and livestock rearing for livelihoods. According to projections from the National Population Commission, Sabon-Birni has an estimated population of 479,013, while Illela has approximately 422,405 residents, yielding a combined population of 901,418. Recurrent displacement, livelihood disruption, and restricted mobility in these areas have significantly undermined household food systems and access to maternal health and nutrition services.

The study population comprised pregnant and lactating women aged 18–49 years residing in host communities, internally displaced households, returnee settlements, and refugee contexts within the selected LGAs. In this study, pregnant women and lactating mothers were combined into a single analytical category of “maternal respondents” based on both conceptual and empirical considerations. Conceptually, both groups share similar physiological nutritional vulnerabilities and are targeted jointly within the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W) framework, which is designed for all women of reproductive age (15–49 years), irrespective of pregnancy or lactation status.

Sampling size and sampling techniques

The sampling strategy combined purposive, multi-stage, and systematic sampling techniques. Illela and Sabon-Birni LGAs were purposively selected based on their high exposure to conflict-induced displacement and food insecurity. Within each LGA, two conflict-affected communities were selected following consultations with local authorities and community leaders. This sample size aligns with standard population proportion estimates for cross-sectional studies, using the Raosoft online sample size calculator, assuming an interval of 95%, confidence interval, 5% margin of error and 50% response distribution, based on projected population of women aged 18–49: 161,730 (Illela) and 267,588 (Sabon-Birni). A total sample size of 768 pregnant and lactating women was targeted and achieved, with 384 respondents selected from each LGA. The sample size was considered sufficient to capture variability across displacement status, socioeconomic background, and dietary outcomes.

In households with more than one eligible woman, one respondent was selected using simple random selection (balloting) to avoid selection bias. Some hard-to-reach areas existed due to security concerns, only accessible and safe communities were included in the sampling frame to ensure the safety of both participants and field staff. Within each selected community, households were mapped and identified through systematic house-to-house visits, where every third house was visited, conducted with the support of trained female research assistants and community contact persons. Eligibility criteria included being pregnant or lactating at the time of the study, aged between 18 and 49 years, and residing in the community for at least three months prior to data collection. Participant selection accounted for diversity in age, education level, marital status, employment status, and displacement category.

To complement the quantitative survey, a qualitative sub-sample was purposively drawn from the study communities. This included: Eight In-Depth Interviews (IDIs) with pregnant and lactating women (two per community); and Four Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) (one per community), each comprising 6–10 women (both pregnant and lactating) with shared experiences of conflict-related food insecurity. The qualitative participants included women from host communities, internally displaced persons (IDPs), refugees, and returnees to ensure diverse perspectives. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted in strict adherence to ethical research protocols, including informed consent, confidentiality, and culturally appropriate community entry. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sokoto Ministry of Health with Ref Number SKHREC/102/2024. Prior to data collection, community leaders were engaged to facilitate access and ensure participant safety. The research instruments (see Supplementary File) were developed for this study. Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered through Computer-Assisted Personal Interviews (CAPI) with Open Data Kit (ODK) on electronic devices. The questionnaire captured information on: Maternal dietary diversity; Household food access and insecurity; Income and livelihood status, Market access and food availability; and Coping strategies in response to food shortages.

The Maternal Dietary Diversity Score (MDDS) is defined as the number of food groups consumed by a woman over a 24-hour recall period, based on the following 10 standard MDD-W food groups: (1) grains, white roots and tubers, and plantains; (2) pulses (beans, peas, and lentils); (3) nuts and seeds; (4) dairy products; (5) meat, poultry, and fish; (6) eggs; (7) dark green leafy vegetables; (8) other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables; (9) other vegetables; and (10) other fruits. A dichotomous indicator is further derived, where consumption of at least five out of the ten food groups constitutes minimum dietary diversity, consistent with global MDD-W guidelines [37, 38]. MDDS was incorporated by counting the number of food groups consumed by each respondent within the reference period. Each food group consumed scored one point, and the total score for each respondent was used to classify dietary diversity. On the other hand, to make the measure less tighter, the study also used “broader maternal diversity” meaning the consuming a wide variety of food groups i.e., moving beyond monotonous staples to incorporate diverse vegetables, fruits, proteins, and grains, irrespective of the number or combination.

Qualitative data were generated through FGDs and IDIs (conducted in Hausa, the dominant local language). Sessions were held in safe, neutral locations within the communities to encourage open discussion. All interviews and discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Eighteen female data collectors fluent in Hausa and familiar with the cultural context were trained in ethical research conduct, qualitative interviewing techniques, and the use of digital data collection tools. The use of female interviewers was particularly important given the sensitive nature of maternal nutrition and household food practices. This ensures gender concordance in data collection has been linked to trust improved data quality, and higher participant engagement, particularly in sensitive research (and religious) contexts [39].

The research instruments were developed but the section on MDDS was adapted from previously validated tools and aligned with the study objectives and theoretical framework. Content validity was ensured through expert review by specialists in nutrition, public health, and social research. The instruments were pretested in a comparable setting to assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and flow, leading to necessary revisions. Reliability was evaluated using internal consistency measures, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients indicating acceptable levels of instrument stability and consistency.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were exported from Open Data Kit (ODK) and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Prior to analysis, data were systematically cleaned to address missing values, logical inconsistencies, and outliers. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, were computed to summarise respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics, food access conditions, maternal dietary diversity, and coping strategies. Maternal Dietary Diversity Score (MDDS) was operationalised based on the Minimum Dietary Diversity for Women (MDD-W) indicator, with a dichotomous outcome defined as adequate (≥ 5 food groups) or inadequate (< 5 food groups) dietary diversity.

Bivariate analyses were conducted using chi-square (χ²) tests to examine associations between maternal dietary diversity and key explanatory variables such as displacement status, education, employment, household role, and location. Variables were selected for multivariable modelling based on both theoretical relevance and empirical criteria. Specifically, variables identified a priori from the study’s conceptual framework were retained, while those with demonstrating significant association at the bivariate level were considered for inclusion. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of the outcome variables while controlling for potential confounders. Variables that were statistically significant at the bivariate level (p < 0.05) were entered into the multivariable model. The strength and direction of associations were interpreted using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). An AOR greater than 1 indicated increased likelihood of the outcome, while an AOR less than 1 indicated reduced likelihood. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, and variables with confidence intervals that did not cross unity were considered significant predictors.

Qualitative data were transcribed verbatim and, where necessary, translated into English with accuracy checks to preserve meaning. The analysis followed a thematic analysis approach, combining both inductive and deductive coding strategies. An initial coding framework was developed based on the study objectives and theoretical constructs (e.g., food capability and social capital), while allowing for the emergence of new themes from the data. Coding was conducted using qualitative data analysis software, with transcripts read iteratively to ensure familiarity and depth of interpretation. Codes were systematically applied and refined through constant comparison, leading to the development of categories and overarching themes. To enhance reliability, the authors independently coded a subset of transcripts and subsequently compared coding outputs. Inter-coder reliability was assessed through discussion and consensus-building, with discrepancies resolved through iterative review and refinement of the codebook [40]. This process ensured consistency in code application and strengthened the credibility and dependability of the findings. Quantitative results provided empirical patterns and predictors of dietary diversity, while qualitative narratives explained the mechanisms through which conflict, displacement, and gendered responsibilities shape maternal nutrition. This integrative approach enhanced the explanatory power of the study and ensured that statistical findings were grounded in social context.

Findings

This section presents the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents and addresses the specific objectives of the study.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

The study analysed data from 768 pregnant and lactating women residing in conflict-affected areas of Sokoto State. Respondents were evenly distributed across Illela and Sabon-Birni LGAs, with each LGA accounting for 50% of the total sample. The four selected wards contributed equally, reflecting balanced geographical representation. The age distribution shows that the majority of respondents were within their prime reproductive years. Most respondents were aged 18–29 years (43.5%), closely followed by those aged 30–39 years (42.7%), while a smaller proportion were aged 40–49 years (13.8%). This indicates a predominantly young to middle-aged study population with limited representation of older participants. This age profile reflects the heightened nutritional vulnerability of younger women in reproductive phases.

Educational attainment was generally low. Most respondents (77.2%) had only primary education, while 9.5% had Quranic education only. Secondary and post-secondary education levels were notably limited, at 3.9% and 4.4% respectively. A further 4.9% reported having no formal education. These patterns suggest restricted access to formal education, which may influence nutritional knowledge and decision-making capacity. Marital status revealed complex household dynamics. While 30.1% of respondents were in polygamous marriages, substantial proportions were divorced (28.1%), widowed (11.8%), or separated (11.3%). Monogamous marriages accounted for only 9.5%, while 9.1% of respondents had never married. These patterns reflect the social disruptions associated with conflict and their implications for women’s economic and nutritional security.

In terms of employment, 58.7% of respondents were unemployed, while 41.3% reported some form of employment. Most women identified as full-time housewives (58.7%) or students (32.6%), underscoring high dependency levels and limited independent income sources. Engagement in formal or skilled occupations such as civil service, professional work, or artisanship was minimal.

Household status further highlights displacement-related vulnerability. One-third of respondents (33.5%) were host community members, while 31.5% were internally displaced persons (IDPs). Returnees accounted for 19.9%, and refugees for 13.8%. The high proportion of displaced women signals elevated exposure to food insecurity and livelihood disruption.

Conflict and insecurity affecting maternal food access

This section examines how conflict and insecurity disrupt maternal food access and the overall food system, limiting the availability and affordability of diverse foods. It highlights the impact of displacement, market instability, and restricted humanitarian aid on food security for mothers. Additionally, it explores coping mechanisms adopted by women to navigate these challenges and maintain dietary diversity.

Table 1 demonstrates the pervasive and multidimensional impact of conflict and insecurity on maternal food access among pregnant and lactating women (N = 768). In terms of food availability, an overwhelming majority (94.1%) reported experiencing difficulty accessing food at least sometimes, with 71.9% indicating this occurred “sometimes” and a further 22.2% reporting it “often” or “always.” This highlights how insecurity has normalized food access challenges rather than presenting as isolated shocks. Consistently, 84.3% of respondents reported that the frequency of going without food had worsened since the onset of conflict, reflecting deepening household food deprivation.

Access to nutritious food has been particularly affected: 82.2% reported that access had “significantly worsened,” indicating not just reduced quantity but a serious decline in diet quality. This is reinforced by changes in local food availability, where nearly three-quarters (74.6%) reported reduced food variety, undermining dietary diversity essential for maternal health. Food affordability further compounds these challenges. More than half of respondents (58.2%) reported that food prices increased sometimes or always due to conflict, while reliance on market purchase as the primary food source (90.8%) exposes households to price volatility and insecurity-related market disruptions.

Finally, food utilization outcomes reveal health issues: one-third reported regular health issues linked to diet changes, and over 90% indicated that food quality had worsened. Collectively, the table underscores how conflict systematically erodes availability, affordability, and utilization of food, placing pregnant and lactating women at extreme nutritional risk. Respondents consistently emphasized the dire food insecurity they experience due to conflict and displacement. Many families have lost their primary sources of income and are unable to afford or access sufficient food. This has forced them to rely on unsustainable and often degrading means to survive. Many respondents in the qualitative interviews reported a significant reduction in the variety and quality of their diets compared to pre-conflict times. Families that once had access to diverse and nutritious meals now struggle to secure even the most basic food items. Participants expressed their struggles thus:

I have to harvest firewood from the forest or woodland and also sometimes we beg for what to eat or send our children for either scavenging activity or street begging because they are not going to school since the conflict started from our village Sarma. – FGD with Women in Sabon Birni.

We are finding it difficult to eat with our children; we are seriously suffering, always struggling for what to take into our stomach.” - FGD with Women in Sabon Birni.

The respondents reported a sharp decline in their ability to access sufficient and nutritious food. Many described a significant reduction in the variety and quality of their diets compared to pre-conflict times. The inability to farm due to insecurity has exacerbated food scarcity. Many respondents highlighted the dangers of accessing farmlands, which has forced them to abandon agricultural activities. Similarly, a participant observed that:

We are banned from going to farms because of the insecurity. Anything can happen on your way to the farm.” – IDI with a woman in Illela.

Table 2 highlights the extensive disruption of the broader food system caused by conflict and insecurity among the study population (N = 768), spanning food production, distribution, governance, and health services. With respect to food production, the vast majority of respondents (76.0%) reported that local food production was moderately to severely affected, including 39.5% who described the impact as severe and 3.4% who reported that production had completely stopped. This underscores how insecurity directly undermines agricultural activities, particularly in predominantly agrarian communities.

Respondents’ suggestions for improving food access reveal a strong demand for both short-term and structural interventions. Increased food aid and better security measures were equally prioritised (28.0% each), followed by improved market access (24.6%) and support for local food production (19.4%), indicating recognition of the need to stabilise both supply and access. Food distribution systems were also compromised. Although local markets continued to function, 68.8% did so with difficulties, while nearly one-quarter (24.0%) reported that markets barely functioned or had ceased entirely. Food aid coverage was limited, with 63.5% reporting no access, and perceptions of effectiveness were largely negative, as 63.6% rated food aid as ineffective or not very effective.

Food governance indicators reveal widespread exposure to insecurity, including fear (58.6%), violence, abduction, and displacement. These conditions intersect with health services, where 84.6% reported irregular or no access to maternal health services, and 64.6% indicated that conflict had severely or completely restricted access. Overall, Table 2 illustrates how conflict systematically destabilizes the entire food system, with direct consequences for maternal health and nutrition.

The deterioration in local food production, compounded by disruptions in market functionality and ineffective food aid distribution, suggests an urgent need for targeted interventions to improve food security. The qualitative interviews reveal deeply embedded coping strategies shaped by chronic food insecurity, insecurity of movement, and displacement. Women consistently described prioritizing their children’s survival over their own nutritional needs, often enduring prolonged hunger as a routine sacrifice. Restricted mobility due to insecurity further constrains access to food, transforming market participation into a high-risk activity rather than a livelihood option. These qualitative insights complement the quantitative findings by illustrating how structural factors such as displacement and income instability translate into lived experiences of constrained dietary choices and adaptive coping behaviours. The food might be there, but getting to it is like walking into danger… Sometimes it’s better to go hungry than to take that risk. - (FGD with Pregnant women in Illela.

Maternal self-deprivation emerged as a dominant theme, reflecting entrenched gendered norms of care and responsibility. Women repeatedly articulated silent endurance as both necessity and moral obligation: “There are times when I go the whole day without eating so that my children can share the little porridge we cook” (FGD with Internally Displaced Lactating Women in Sabon-Birni). Another noted, “Even when I’m sick or weak, I make sure they eat first” (FGD with Pregnant in Illela). These narratives illustrate how coping is internalized, normalizing maternal nutritional neglect in crisis settings.

Quantitative patterns reinforce these accounts. When resources are scarce, women overwhelmingly prioritize staple foods such as rice and wheat (65.5%), while far fewer prioritize protein-rich foods (15.5%), fruits and vegetables (9.6%), or dairy products (9.4%). This coping logic favors caloric sufficiency over dietary diversity, increasing the risk of micronutrient deficiencies. Adaptive strategies such as new cooking or food preservation methods are uncommon, with only 13.5% reporting adoption, suggesting limited access to knowledge, inputs, or enabling conditions for innovation.

Community-based support remains fragmented and unreliable. More than 60% report never receiving community support for dietary diversity. Where support exists, it is largely informal and episodic. Women emphasized mutual aid as a survival buffer, though one constrained by shared poverty: “We help each other when we can, but everyone struggles” (IDI_with a Lactating mother Illela). Acts of solidarity, such as sharing food, firewood, or cooking duties, provided temporary relief but lacked sustainability: “It’s not much, but we pool what we have. If not for this, many of us would have perished” (FGD with pregnant and Lactating woman in Illela). Participation in formal community food security programs is low, and perceived effectiveness is weak. As one respondent noted, “There’s a community group that shares food, but only when donors come” (IDI with Female Household Head Sabon-Birni). Similarly, food aid is inconsistently accessed and often viewed as nutritionally inadequate. Nearly half believe it reduces dietary diversity, reinforcing reliance on monotonous staples. The findings underscore the resilience of women’s coping strategies while highlighting their nutritional cost and the urgent need for predictable, diversity-sensitive, and context-responsive food security interventions.

Discussion of findings

This study provides empirical evidence on how armed conflict and persistent insecurity undermine maternal dietary diversity in Sokoto State, Nigeria. The findings show low dietary diversity which reflects structural constraints associated with conflict, including disrupted food systems, reduced agricultural productivity, and limited market access. These findings align with evidence from conflict-affected regions where insecurity constrains both food availability and household purchasing power [13, 41]. The dominance of starchy staples and the limited consumption of protein-rich and micronutrient-dense foods observed in this study reflect structural constraints rather than individual dietary preferences.

Conflict-related disruptions to livelihoods and markets have significantly reduced household purchasing power while simultaneously inflating food prices, thereby restricting women’s ability to diversify their diets. These findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating how economic shocks in fragile settings translate directly into compromised maternal nutrition [23, 42]. Even where food was physically available, insecurity limited women’s mobility and market participation, transforming food access into a high-risk activity. Similarly, studies have submitted that conflicts affect maternal health through multiple mechanisms including deleterious effect shelter, sanitary facilities, healthcare personnel and reduced access to quality health services [42, 43].

Displacement emerged as a critical determinant of dietary outcomes. The positive association between duration of displacement and dietary diversity suggests adaptive mechanisms over time. Prolonged displacement may facilitate integration into host communities, improved access to humanitarian assistance, and gradual livelihood stabilization, thereby enhancing dietary options. However, refugees and internally displaced women were significantly more likely to adopt severe coping strategies, underscoring how forced migration compounds nutritional vulnerability. Displacement disrupts access to farmland, erodes livelihood assets, and weakens social networks that traditionally function as informal safety nets. These findings resonate with previous research linking displacement to heightened food insecurity and poor maternal outcomes in conflict settings [27, 44].

Furthermore, the differential effects observed between Illela and Sabon-Birni LGAs suggest that localised conflict dynamics and market structures play a decisive role in shaping dietary outcomes. The widespread practice of maternal self-sacrifice identified in this study represents a deeply gendered coping mechanism. Many women reported routinely reducing or skipping meals to ensure that their children could eat, even during pregnancy or lactation. While such practices reflect maternal care and resilience, they also expose women to heightened risks of undernutrition, anaemia, and adverse birth outcomes. Similar patterns have been documented in other low-resource and conflict-affected settings, where women prioritise household survival over their own nutritional needs [4, 5]. This underscores the intergenerational consequences of food insecurity, as compromised maternal nutrition directly affects foetal development and child health.

Although nutrition education was relatively widespread among respondents, its impact on dietary diversity was limited. Comparatively, a study found that supplementary nutrition education and counselling was associated with significant improvements in nutrition knowledge, dietary diversity and nutrition behaviour [45]. However, this finding challenges assumptions that knowledge alone is sufficient to improve nutritional practices in fragile contexts. Structural constraints such as poverty, insecurity, and limited food availability effectively neutralise the potential benefits of nutrition counselling. This aligns with the Food Capability Approach, which emphasises that individuals’ ability to achieve adequate nutrition depends not only on knowledge but on the real opportunities available to them [29, 30]. In conflict settings, women’s food capabilities are systematically constrained by factors beyond their control.

The erosion of community support systems observed in this study further illustrates the relevance of Social Capital Theory. Traditionally, rural communities in northern Nigeria rely on strong social networks, food sharing, and reciprocal support to buffer periods of scarcity. However, conflict and displacement have weakened these ties, leaving many women isolated and unable to rely on communal assistance. Another study also found limited reach and inconsistency of community-based support mechanisms reported in this study reflect the breakdown of trust and reciprocity under prolonged insecurity [24]. Where social capital is depleted, women’s coping options become increasingly constrained.

Food aid, while intended as a protective intervention, was found to be both inconsistent and nutritionally limited. Nearly half of respondents reported that food aid reduced dietary diversity, largely due to its focus on monotonous staple foods. This finding aligns with critiques of humanitarian food assistance that prioritises caloric sufficiency over dietary quality [46]. Moreover, insecurity and irregular distribution further limited access to aid, reinforcing dependence without adequately addressing nutritional needs. These shortcomings highlight the need for more context-sensitive, nutritionally diverse, and predictable food assistance strategies. Economic shocks intensified vulnerability and pushed some women toward harmful coping strategies, including selling assets, borrowing money, and engaging children in labour. Such strategies may offer short-term survival but often perpetuate long-term poverty, social exclusion, and gender inequality. These findings mirror patterns documented in other conflict-affected settings, where women’s economic desperation increases exposure to exploitation and undermines household resilience [4, 19].

By applying the Food Capability Approach and Social Capital Theory, this study highlights how conflict erodes women’s nutritional agency and dismantles informal support networks that traditionally buffer food shocks. The limited effectiveness of food aid and nutrition education in this context underscores the need for integrated, gender-sensitive responses that address both immediate nutritional needs and underlying structural vulnerabilities. Taken together, these findings illustrate that maternal food insecurity in conflict settings is not simply a nutritional problem but a multidimensional social crisis. Conflict undermines women’s economic agency, weakens social support systems, restricts mobility, and exacerbates gendered inequalities, all of which converge to constrain maternal dietary diversity.

As previously mentioned, this study provides empirical evidence on how persistent insecurity undermine maternal dietary diversity in Sokoto State, Nigeria. Despite the study’s contributions, the study has limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, while the 24-hour recall method may be subject to recall bias. Additionally, security constraints have restricted access to certain areas, potentially affecting representativeness.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that maternal dietary diversity in conflict-affected areas of Sokoto State is critically compromised by the intersecting effects of insecurity, displacement, and weakened social systems. The findings reveal that maternal food insecurity is not primarily a consequence of food scarcity but rather the result of structural and gendered constraints that limit women’s capacity to access, utilise, and benefit from adequate nutrition. In more specific terms, the findings suggest that interventions considering nutrition-sensitive food assistance that incorporates protein-rich and micronutrient-dense foods, alongside targeted inclusion of vulnerable host communities. Strengthening local market functionality through security corridors, price stabilization mechanisms, and support for small-scale food vendors may offer more sustainable gains than parallel aid systems. Additionally, women-centered livelihood support—such as safe home-based food production and petty trading—represents a realistic entry point in insecure environments where mobility is restricted. Importantly, integrating maternal nutrition goals into broader security, livelihoods, and social protection frameworks is critical to ensuring policy effectiveness in protracted conflict settings.
