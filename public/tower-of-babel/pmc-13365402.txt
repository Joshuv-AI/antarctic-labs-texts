Negative-space 3D printed reconstruction of the pterygopalatine fossa: anatomical design and educational evaluation
Authors: Ayşe Ömerli, Fatma Çınar, Fatma Nur Ergöz, Feyza Tamer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pterygopalatine fossa (PPF) is a deep, anatomically complex midfacial space that serves as a major neurovascular crossroads. Owing to its confined boundaries and multidirectional communications, it is difficult to visualize using conventional teaching materials. This study aimed to develop a physically enlarged negative-space three-dimensional (3D) printed model of the PPF and to evaluate its impact on anatomical comprehension. A three-dimensional (3D) digital model of the pterygopalatine fossa was developed using pre-existing digital bone models obtained from a commercial repository and refined using ZBrush software, ensuring reproducibility of the modeling workflow. A negative-space representation of the fossa was created and scaled to twice its anatomical size. The model was exported in STL format and printed using acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) filament. Internal neurovascular structures were represented using color-coded materials. Fifty-two third-year dental students were divided into a theoretical instruction group (n = 27) and a 3D model group (n = 25). Pre- and post-instruction assessments were performed, and within- and between-group comparisons were analyzed using paired and independent t-tests. Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in post-test scores (p < 0.001). However, the mean score increase was significantly greater in the 3D model group (3.96 ± 2.47) compared with the theoretical group (2.22 ± 1.60) (p = 0.004). The effect size for the difference in improvement between groups was large (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.84). The enlarged negative-space 3D printed model was associated with improved short-term anatomical understanding of the pterygopalatine fossa compared with conventional theoretical instruction. This reproducible and low-cost model may represent a useful adjunct in clinically oriented teaching of complex deep facial spaces.Keywords: Pterygopalatine fossa, 3D printing, Anatomy education, Maxillofacial anatomy, Negative-space modelSubject terms: Anatomy, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The pterygopalatine fossa (PPF) is a small but highly complex anatomical space located deep within the midface, bounded by the maxilla, palatine, and sphenoid bones. Despite its limited volume, it functions as a major neurovascular crossroads, transmitting the maxillary nerve (V2), terminal branches of the maxillary artery, the pterygopalatine ganglion, and multiple communicating branches to the orbit, nasal cavity, palate, and nasopharynx1,2. Owing to these intricate anatomical relationships, precise understanding of the PPF is essential in both surgical and radiologic practice.

In maxillofacial surgery, particularly during Le Fort I osteotomy, careful separation of the pterygomaxillary junction is critical to avoid injury to the vascular and neural elements within the PPF3,4. The proximity of this region to the pterygomaxillary fissure, skull base foramina, and orbital structures increases the risk of significant hemorrhagic and neurologic complications5. Consequently, accurate three-dimensional knowledge of the PPF is indispensable for safe surgical intervention.

From an educational perspective, the PPF represents one of the most challenging anatomical spaces for students to conceptualize. Its deep localization, “negative space” configuration, and multidirectional communications limit clear visualization during cadaveric dissection and are inadequately represented in two-dimensional atlases. Advanced imaging-based morphometric analyses have emphasized the structural complexity of the PPF6, yet translating this complexity into effective educational tools remains difficult. Additive manufacturing technologies, commonly referred to as three-dimensional (3D) printing, encompass a range of fabrication methods including fused deposition modeling (FDM), stereolithography (SLA), and selective laser sintering (SLS). In dentistry and oral surgery, these technologies have been increasingly applied for the production of surgical guides, prosthetic components, and anatomical teaching models, owing to their ability to reproduce complex three-dimensional geometries with high precision and at relatively low cost. Several studies have demonstrated the educational value of 3D printing in complex anatomical regions7,8. Specifically, Bannon et al. (2018), in a study published in Surgical and Radiologic Anatomy, introduced a negative-space 3D model of the PPF and highlighted its anatomical clarity. Similarly, Javan et al. (2020) proposed CT-based visualization frameworks for complex craniofacial spaces9,10.

However, limited studies have evaluated physically enlarged, color-coded negative-space models specifically designed to enhance the understanding of the internal neurovascular architecture of the PPF while simultaneously assessing their educational impact.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was twofold:

(1) to develop a reproducible 3D-printed model of the pterygopalatine fossa enlarged to twice its anatomical size, and (2) to evaluate its effect on short-term anatomical comprehension compared with conventional theoretical instruction.

By integrating technical model development with quantitative educational assessment, this study seeks to contribute to clinically oriented anatomical teaching in complex deep facial regions.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval: This prospective comparative study was conducted following approval from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Nuh Naci Yazgan University (Approval No: 2025/04/002). All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study. The project was supported by the TÜBİTAK–2209-A University Students Research Projects Support Program.

The study consisted of two phases: Development of a three-dimensional (3D) printed anatomical model of the pterygopalatine fossa (PPF), and evaluation of its impact on short-term anatomical comprehension compared with conventional theoretical instruction.

Development of the 3D anatomical model

Digital three-dimensional models of the bones forming the boundaries of the pterygopalatine fossa — namely the maxilla, palatine bone, and sphenoid bone — were obtained from CGTrader (CGTrader UAB, Vilnius, Lithuania), a professional 3D model repository. As no patient-derived imaging data were used in the development of the anatomical model, ethical approval and informed consent for model construction were not required. No DICOM-based segmentation was performed, as the model was constructed using pre-existing digital bone models obtained from a commercial 3D repository.

The acquired models were subsequently imported into ZBrush (Pixologic Inc., USA) for anatomical adaptation and refinement. Within this software, the boundaries of the pterygopalatine fossa were digitally reconstructed and edited to delineate its spatial relationships and communications. A negative-space cavity representing the pterygopalatine fossa was created by digitally assembling the three osseous components within ZBrush and applying Boolean subtraction to define the boundaries of the fossa. Additional digital refinement and smoothing were performed prior to printing. The final model was scaled to twice its original anatomical size to enhance visualization of this deep and narrow space, particularly to improve discrimination of narrow foramina such as the foramen rotundum and pterygoid canal. The three bones were subsequently separated and each exported as an individual STL file for printing. Printing each osseous component separately was preferred to ensure that the fine anatomical details of each bone were fully preserved and clearly visible, as a single-piece print may have obscured critical surface features. The three printed components were subsequently assembled using a cyanoacrylate-based adhesive to reconstruct the boundaries of the pterygopalatine fossa.

Three-dimensional printing was performed using a Creality Ender series FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) printer. Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) filament was selected due to its mechanical durability and suitability for post-processing. Printing was performed using standard FDM parameters appropriate for ABS filament, including a layer height of 0.2 mm; detailed printing parameters were determined and applied by the external printing service. Surface refinement was achieved using acetone vapor smoothing, taking advantage of the solubility properties of ABS11.

Following printing of the osseous boundaries, the internal neurovascular structures of the pterygopalatine fossa were manually placed within the cavity. Prior to placement, each neurovascular component — arterial, venous, and neural structures — was individually examined using Complete Anatomy (3D4Medical, Elsevier), a three-dimensional interactive anatomy platform, to ensure accurate morphology and positional reference. Following individual examination, all structures were collectively reviewed within the same platform to confirm their spatial relationships and neighborhood anatomy. Structures were positioned based on established anatomical references, including Sobotta Atlas of Human Anatomy, and were color-coded to enhance visual discrimination: nerves were represented in yellow, arteries in red, and veins in blue. Color coding was achieved using flexible colored wires of corresponding colors, which were shaped to replicate the morphology and course of each neurovascular structure. The flexible colored wires were secured within the osseous cavity using a cyanoacrylate-based adhesive to ensure structural stability and positional accuracy. Each structure was labeled and numerically identified on the model to facilitate structured anatomical instruction.

Anatomical validation of the 3D model

Following completion of the assembled model, anatomical validation was performed through a multi-modal verification process conducted at the anatomy laboratory of Nuh Naci Yazgan University. The osseous boundaries and foraminal openings of the pterygopalatine fossa were verified against dry skeletal specimens available in the departmental collection. Commercially available anatomical bone replicas present in the laboratory were additionally consulted for structural comparison. The overall anatomical accuracy of the model, including the spatial relationships and neurovascular contents of the fossa, was confirmed using Complete Anatomy (3D4Medical, Elsevier). This validation process ensured that all relevant anatomical landmarks, communications, and neurovascular structures of the pterygopalatine fossa were accurately represented in the final printed model.

The following structures were represented in the model: Maxillary artery, Posterior superior alveolar artery and vein, Infraorbital artery and vein, Pterygoid venous plexus, Descending palatine artery, Artery of the pterygoid canal, Sphenopalatine artery, Greater palatine artery, Infraorbital nerve, Zygomatic nerve, Orbital branches, Maxillary nerve (V2), Ganglionic branches, Posterior superior alveolar nerve, Pharyngeal nerve, Nerve of the pterygoid canal, Lesser palatine nerves, Greater palatine nerve, Posterior superior nasal nerves, Pterygopalatine ganglion.

Each structure was labeled and numerically identified on the model to facilitate structured anatomical instruction.

Participants and educational intervention: A total of 52 third-year dental students voluntarily participated in the study. Students were allocated into two groups based on existing classroom organization rather than formal randomization: a theoretical instruction group (n = 27) and a 3D model instruction group (n = 25). Baseline anatomical knowledge of the pterygopalatine fossa was assessed using a pre-test consisting of structured multiple-choice questions. Following the pre-test, The theoretical group received conventional slide-based instruction. The model group received instruction using the 3D printed anatomical model, allowing direct visualization of boundaries and neurovascular relationships. Both instructional sessions were delivered by the same instructor to minimize inter-observer variability and ensure consistency of teaching quality. The duration of the instructional sessions was equivalent across both groups to control for potential time-related bias. After completion of the instructional sessions, the same test was administered as a post-test to assess short-term knowledge acquisition.

The enlargement factor was specifically selected to improve discrimination of narrow foramina such as the foramen rotundum and pterygoid canal, which are frequently underrepresented in standard-scale models.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the TURCOSA cloud (Turcosa Analytics Ltd. Co., Turkey) statistical software (https://turcosa.com.tr/). Normality of score distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Within-group comparisons between pre-test and post-test scores were analyzed using paired-samples t-tests, and between-group comparisons of score improvements (post-test minus pre-test) were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for the between-group mean difference. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 52 third-year dental students completed both the pre-test and post-test assessments. No participants were excluded from the analysis. Prior to the intervention, no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups in baseline anatomical knowledge, as assessed by pre-test scores (theoretical group: 3.33 ± 1.27; 3D model group: 3.64 ± 1.85; independent-samples t-test, p = 0.488), indicating that the groups were comparable at baseline (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Within-group comparisons

In the theoretical instruction group (n = 27), the mean pre-test score was 3.33 ± 1.27 and the mean post-test score was 5.56 ± 1.45. The mean improvement was 2.22 ± 1.60 (paired-sample t-test, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

In the 3D model group (n = 25), the mean pre-test score was 3.64 ± 1.85 and the mean post-test score was 7.60 ± 1.96. The mean improvement was 3.96 ± 2.47 (paired-samples t-test, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Between-group comparison

The mean difference in improvement between groups was 1.74 (95% CI: 0.59–2.89; independent-samples t-test, p = 0.004), indicating greater improvement in the 3D model group. The effect size for the between-group difference was large (Cohen’s d = 0.84) (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study introduces an enlarged negative-space reconstruction of the pterygopalatine fossa combined with color-coded neurovascular structures, providing a novel approach for visualizing the internal spatial organization of this complex anatomical region.

From a surgical anatomy perspective, accurate understanding of the pterygopalatine fossa is important due to the concentration of critical neurovascular structures within a confined space2,6. In procedures such as Le Fort I osteotomy, inadequate spatial awareness may increase the risk of vascular or neural injury3,5. Therefore, educational tools that enhance three-dimensional comprehension of this region may contribute to safer surgical training12.

Previous studies investigating 3D-printed models of the PPF have primarily focused on anatomical description and spatial visualization, without providing quantitative evidence of their educational impact. Furthermore, no prior study has evaluated a physically enlarged, internally reconstructed, and color-coded negative-space model specifically designed for structured educational use. The present study was therefore designed to address these gaps by combining technical model development with objective assessment of short-term learning outcomes.

Negative-space modeling represents a distinctive approach for visualizing complex anatomical cavities13,14. By emphasizing internal boundaries and spatial relationships, this technique may enhance understanding of deep craniofacial regions that are difficult to conceptualize using conventional models7,8.

Bannon et al. (2018), in Surgical and Radiologic Anatomy, introduced a negative-space model of the pterygopalatine fossa and emphasized its potential for clarifying spatial relationships. Building on this concept, the present study incorporates three key methodological refinements: enlargement to twice the anatomical scale, systematic color-coded reconstruction of neurovascular structures, and quantitative evaluation of its educational impact, providing objective support for its use in anatomy education.

In addition, three-dimensional (3D) visualization tools, including digital and CT-based reconstructions, have been proposed for the PPF10. However, physical models may offer advantages in tactile perception and depth interpretation, particularly in narrow and irregular spaces. Horowitz and Schultz (2014) suggested that tangible 3D representations enhance spatial cognition, especially when complex cavities are involved15.

Traditional anatomical atlases and two-dimensional illustrations have long been used to teach complex craniofacial regions such as the pterygopalatine fossa. Classical anatomical references, including the Sobotta Atlas of Human Anatomy, provide detailed descriptions of the neurovascular structures located within this space; however, they remain limited in conveying the spatial depth and multidirectional communications of the fossa16. Similar challenges in anatomy education have been noted by Van Engelshoven and Wilmink, who emphasized that conventional teaching approaches often fail to fully illustrate complex anatomical relationships17. Recent educational research has therefore explored alternative visualization strategies. Systematic reviews of the literature have confirmed that 3D printed anatomical models represent a valuable pedagogical tool across multiple domains of human anatomy education18. For example, Preece et al. demonstrated that physical anatomical models may provide advantages over purely digital or textbook-based learning in understanding complex imaging anatomy19. Likewise, studies on educational applications of three-dimensional technologies suggest that tactile and visually interactive learning tools can enhance spatial cognition and student engagement in anatomy education. Experimental studies using stereoscopic visualization and three-dimensional learning environments have also reported improved comprehension of complex anatomical structures when compared with traditional two-dimensional instruction20–22.

It is noteworthy that both instructional modalities improved post-test performance, indicating that structured theoretical teaching remains effective. Nevertheless, the significantly greater improvement observed in the model group suggests that spatial reinforcement through physical representation enhances comprehension of deep craniofacial spaces. Although the present study evaluated only short-term learning outcomes, future studies should examine long-term retention and potential applications in surgical training.

Although the present study utilized commercial software for digital model development, several free and open-source alternatives are available that may facilitate the adoption of similar workflows in resource-limited settings. Tools such as 3D Slicer, MeshLab, and Meshmixer offer comparable capabilities for mesh editing and STL file preparation, potentially lowering the financial barriers associated with anatomical model production in medical and dental education.

In this context, the use of enlarged negative-space models (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8) may therefore represent a practical educational bridge between traditional anatomical atlases and advanced digital visualization tools.

Taken together, the findings of the present study suggest that negative-space reconstruction of the pterygopalatine fossa may serve as a useful adjunct in clinically oriented anatomy teaching. This approach provides a reproducible and accessible framework that may be adaptable to other complex deep craniofacial spaces.

Conclusion

The present study suggests that a physically enlarged, color-coded negative-space 3D printed model of the pterygopalatine fossa may be associated with improved short-term anatomical understanding compared with conventional theoretical instruction. Given the surgical relevance of this region, such models may be considered as potential adjuncts in clinically oriented anatomy teaching. Further studies are required to evaluate long-term educational outcomes and possible applications in surgical training. As the model is reproducible and relatively low-cost, it may offer a practical resource for anatomy laboratories and educational settings.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small cohort, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study evaluated only short-term knowledge acquisition, and long-term retention or clinical performance were not assessed. Third, participants were allocated based on existing classroom organization rather than formal randomization, which represents a methodological limitation. Additionally, comparisons with other teaching modalities, such as cadaveric dissection or virtual reality tools, were not included. Future multi-center studies incorporating larger cohorts, multiple teaching modalities, and long-term follow-up assessments are needed to further validate the educational effectiveness of 3D-printed negative-space anatomical models.
