Prevalence of Pneumonia and Its Associated Factors Among Children Under Five Years in Eastern Uganda: A Cross-Sectional Hospital-Based Study
Authors: John Michael Okusa, Safina Akello, James Kateregga, Abudu Rahuman Ssekabira, Jacob Eperu, Clement Munguiko, Josephine Namujju, Julius Okiror Okiria, Alice C Alum, Joseph Atukwatse, Clare Nankinga, Samuel Okello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumonia remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children under five, accounting for 740,180 deaths globally and 14% of all under-five deaths. In Uganda, it is the second leading cause of mortality in this age group. Despite several studies on pneumonia risk factors, findings have been inconsistent. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of pneumonia in children under five in Eastern Uganda.MethodsA hospital-based descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 385 children aged 2 to 59 months admitted to the pediatric ward. Pneumonia diagnosis followed the 2014 WHO guidelines and the Integrated Management of Newborn and Childhood Illness (IMNCI) classification. Data on socio-demographic, socioeconomic, child-related, and facility-related factors were collected using semi-structured questionnaires. Descriptive statistics determined prevalence, while logistic regression analysis using SPSS version 27 assessed factors associated with pneumonia.ResultsThe study found that the prevalence of pneumonia among children under five was 25.2% (95% CI: 19.1–31.2%). The factors which were found to be associated with pneumonia include; use of grass as roofing material for the house (AOR: 4.658, P-value=0.016), child being malnourished (AOR: 15.749, P-value<0.001), child not exclusively breastfed (AOR: 3.588, P-value= 0.044), child not having received vitamin A supplementation (AOR: 12.624, P-value=0.021), and staying beyond 5 km from the nearest health facility (AOR: 6.160, P-value= 0.011).ConclusionThis study found a high prevalence of pneumonia among children under five in Eastern Uganda, significantly exceeding the global target of 2.5%. The factors which were found to be associated with pneumonia included, being from household using grass as roofing material, malnutrition, nonexclusively breastfed, lack of vitamin A supplementation, staying a distance of more than 5 km from nearest health facility. These findings underpin a need for integrated community and policy-level interventions that improve child nutrition, promote optimal infant feeding practices, enhance preventive care, and strengthen access to healthcare services, while addressing underlying socioeconomic and environmental determinants.

Background

Pneumonia continues to be the foremost cause of morbidity and mortality among children under the age of five, resulting in the demise of 740,180 children globally and contributing to 14% of all deaths in this age group, with 22% of deaths occurring among children aged 1 to 5 years.1,2

Globally, about 120 million cases of pneumonia are reported per year, leading to around 1.3 million deaths, whose impact is particularly pronounced among children below two years in developing countries, constituting nearly 80% of child deaths associated with pneumonia.3 Pneumonia caused death of over 700,000 children below 5 years, including more than 153,000 newborns.4 This shows the vulnerability of this age group with at least one child succumbing to pneumonia every 45 seconds.

Despite a global reduction in cases of pneumonia from “178 to 138 million” annually,1 progress in Sub-Saharan Africa is generally slow and by 2017, the pneumonia prevalence among children below 5 years reached 1,870 per 100,000 children.5 “A systematic review and meta-analysis in East Africa” found a pneumonia prevalence of 34% among under-fives.1

Pneumonia remains the second principal source of hospital admissions among children below 5 years in Uganda, accounting for 753,978 admissions and 13,632 deaths during the period2013−2021.6 Mortality rates due to pneumonia in children have fluctuated, with rates of 9.4% in 2011 (UDHS, 2011), 9% in 2016 (UDHS, 2016), and a notable increase to 17% in 2022 (UDHS, 2022). Research conducted at “Mulago National Referral Hospital” in Uganda reported a (53.7%) pneumonia prevalence among children.7

Most studies have found the leading factors associated with pneumonia to be “lack of exclusive breastfeeding, undernutrition, indoor air pollution, low birth weight, overcrowding, lack of immunization, comorbid conditions, parental smoking, maternal education, daycare attendance, humidity, and mothers’ caregiving experience”, however, findings on these factors are inconsistent across various studies.1,8,9

To combat the burden of pneumonia, WHO and UNICEF devised a global initiative as part of the “Global Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Pneumonia and Diarrhea (GAPPD)”. This wits steered low-cost interventions, emphasizing universal immunization coverage, HIV prevention, and a healthy environment.10 The government of Uganda has also instigated measures since 2012, including promotion of breastfeeding, immunization, vitamin A supplementation, appropriate antibiotic treatment for pneumonia, and the “Integrated Community Case Management of childhood diseases” (diarrhea, pneumonia, and malaria) in all districts.11

Despite these initiatives, pneumonia still accounts for 10% of under-five deaths in Uganda, with at least 25 children succumbing to pneumonia every 45 seconds nationwide.11 Therefore, this research aimed to find out “the prevalence and associated factors of pneumonia in children under 5 years to guide healthcare providers, policymakers, and researchers in designing evidence-based interventions that can effectively prevent, diagnose pneumonia in this vulnerable population ultimately leading to improved child health outcomes and reduced child mortality rates”.

Methods

Study Design

The study employed both a descriptive and analytical cross-sectional study design. A descriptive design was used to determine “the prevalence of pneumonia among children under five”. Analytical cross-sectional design was used to determine “the association between the independent and dependent variables”. The cross-sectional study design was used because it collects data in a snapshot without the follow-up of participants.

Study Setting

This study was conducted in the pediatric ward at Soroti Regional Referral Hospital (SRRH) in Soroti City. Pediatric ward operates from Monday to Sunday 24 hours a day with two (2) nurses per shift and two (2) doctors. This ward is always busy admitting an average population of 473 children per month. Pediatric ward has subsections like laboratory, cannulation room, pharmacy, pediatric OPD, emergency section for emergency cases, acute section, recovery section, nutrition section, nurses room, and doctor’s room. SRRH is one of the 17 public RRHs in the country, located 320 km northeast of Kampala. It was opened as a syphilis treatment Centre in the mid-1920s, it became a district hospital in 1978 and was elevated to an RRH in 1996. According to the 2014 census data, SRRH serves a population of 296,833 located in Soroti District with a capacity of 251 beds offering both general and specialized services to people in Eastern Uganda.

Study Population

The study targeted children under 5 years admitted to the pediatric ward at SRRH who were admitted between March and April, 2024 in the pediatric ward.

Inclusion Criteria

The study included children aged above 2 months and under five years who were admitted to the pediatric ward at Soroti Regional Referral Hospital (SRRH) between March and April 2024, whose caretakers provided informed consent. Additionally, only children who had resided in Eastern Uganda for at least three months prior to the study were included to ensure appropriate exposure to the regional risk factors.

Exclusion Criteria

Children under five years whose caretakers did not consent, those who were critically ill and required emergency care, and those whose caretakers were mentally impaired were excluded from participating in the study.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was calculated using the “Kish and Leslie formula (1965)” for the single proportion. Therefore, data was collected from 385 participants.

Sampling Procedure

Simple random sampling was used where a list of all caretakers with children under five admitted in the pediatric ward at SRRH was made and coded with numbers from 1 to 20 on pieces of paper and placed in a basket and shaken.

Each mother was then be allowed to select a piece of paper from the basket and their number recorded and the paper placed back in the basket to maintain an equal number of chances. Those mothers who picked a number corresponding to those generated by the Random Integer Generator participated in the study. Simple random sampling was used because it avoids bias as participants were selected on an equal basis.

Study Variables

Dependent Variables

Prevalence of pneumonia in children under five years. This was measured by assessing the signs and symptoms of pneumonia in these children through asking the caretakers questions and also confirmed from their files. The classification of pneumonia was then made using the IMNCI classification and the prevalence was then determined using descriptive analysis using SPSS.

Independent Variables

Socio-Economic Factors

Exposure to cigarette smoke: this was assessed by asking caretakers whether there was someone smoking indoors.

Housing conditions: caretakers were asked for the type of the roofing material and floor material they used for the house where this child sleeps.

Maternal education level: this was assessed by asking the caretakers whether the mother to the child did not go to school, stopped at primary, secondary, or tertiary level of education.

Crowding: Caretakers were asked questions on the number of rooms they have at home, the number of people living in those rooms, and how many people share a room with the child.

The crowding index was then calculated from:

(Number of people living in the house + Number of people sharing a room with the child)

Number of rooms available at home

High crowding index of >1.5 indicated overcrowding and a low crowding index of 1.5 and below indicated no crowding.

Use of wood as a fuel source: Participants were asked whether they were using wood for cooking or not and were required to answer Yes or No.

Child-Related Factors

Age: this was assessed by asking the caretaker for the age of the child in months which was later categorized from 2–5, 6–11, 12–23, and 24–59.

Immunization status and vitamin A deficiency: child immunization cards were used to assess the immunization status of children. Appropriate history about immunization using questions on different vaccines as per schedule were asked for caretakers who did not have immunization cards at the time of data collection. Children who had not received all vaccinations expected for their age or had never been immunized at all were labelled not up to-date for age and those that had received were labelled up to date.

Nutrition status: nutritional status was assessed according to WHO classification using BMI. Weight was measured using a weighing scale, and the body length/height was measured using a Stadiometer and length measuring board and recorded in centimeters, Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was measured using a MUAC tape and recorded in centimeters for children who were 6 months and above.

Exclusive breastfeeding: this was taken as feeding the child only breast milk with no other foods or drinks for the first six months of life, where participants were asked for the age at which they introduced other foods or drinks.

History of ARTI: this was assessed by asking the participants whether the child had a history of an ARTI or not.

Facility-Related Factors

Hospital Stay: This was assessed by asking the participants whether the child was hospitalized recently within the past three months, and how long did they stay in the hospital, either for <2 weeks or ≥2 weeks.

Accessibility to healthcare services: participants were asked how far they are from the nearest health facility as less than 1 km, 2–5 km, or beyond 5 km and whether they found it difficult reaching the health facility.

Data Collection Methods

Data was collected through conducting questionnaire-guided interviews.

Tools of Data Collection

Data was got from participants through a semi-structured interviewer-guided questionnaire which was written in both simple English and translated into Ateso to facilitate a better understanding of the information and to overcome potential communication barriers. The questionnaire predominantly consisted of closed-ended questions, that enabled quick recording of responses and efficient use of time, as recommended by (Polit & Beck, 2006). The choice of a structured interviewer-guided questionnaire was based on its advantages, including applicability to both literate and illiterate individuals, the ability to observe non-verbal behaviors and mannerisms, the opportunity to clarify questions in case of any misunderstandings and its ability to collect standardized data among participants.

Data Collection Procedure

This study utilized face-to-face interviewer-guided semi-structured questionnaires during data collection. Research assistants aided in consistent questioning of respondents, recording their responses in designated spaces. Interviews were conducted between 09:00 and 17:00 hours at the pediatric ward. Criteria for selecting research assistants included language proficiency, critical thinking, and subject understanding. The target population in this study was mothers of children above 2 months and below 5 years admitted in the pediatric ward at SRRH. The researchers approached the mothers where they were briefed about the study, and these briefings always took 7 minutes. Mothers were then taken through the consenting process, and the following considerations about the study were clearly stated: voluntary participation, autonomy, non-maleficence, justice and respect for privacy, data security, and their right to withdraw without impacting healthcare access. Each participant was then taken to a private room where they were taken through a deeper understanding of the study and study procedures and consented before structured questionnaire-based interviews. Participants were thanked sincerely at the end of the interview.

Data Quality Control

Validity

To enhance validity, a thorough literature review was undertaken, focusing on recent studies about different aspects of “the prevalence of pneumonia and its associated factors”. The questions were subjected to scrutiny by experts in the pediatric ward and research supervisors to ensure that they effectively capture the desired responses related to the variables being measured. This process aimed to certify that the inferences drawn from the study accurately reflected the characteristics of the study population. Additionally, the questions were formulated in a straightforward, clear, and precise manner to provide respondents with the opportunity to give unambiguous and accurate answers.

Reliability

Reliability is the extent to which research tools consistently yield similar results across multiple trials. To assess this, a pilot study was conducted at the pediatric ward at Lira Regional Referral Hospital, examining the correlation of participant responses for consistency. During the pilot study, respondents had the opportunity to express any difficulties understanding the questions, allowing for necessary adjustments. No missing items were identified. The pilot study aimed to assess the reliability of the questionnaire concerning the language, structure, and arrangement of the questions.

To maintain reliability in the interviewer-guided questionnaire, research assistants underwent training in its use. The questions were designed to be simple, concise, and brief.

Data Management

All data were anonymized and confidentiality was strictly maintained between the researcher and the respondents. All information derived from the questionnaires was securely stored under lock and key to prevent access by unauthorized individuals and to minimize the risk of data loss. A thorough process of data editing, coding, and item-by-item entry was undertaken to ensure completeness, accuracy, and consistency in the dataset.

Data Analysis

Data collected was coded, tabulated, and entered into an Excel statistical computer program. It was then transferred and analyzed using SPSS V27. Prevalence of pneumonia was determined using descriptive analysis, and the association between factors and pneumonia was determined through a simple binary logistic regression model which was fitted for each of the independent variables at 95% CI. Variables with P-value less than or equal to 0.20 in the simple binary logistic regression were selected for the multiple binary logistic regression. An Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) was reported as a measure of association and all variables with a P-value <0.05 were considered as statistically significant factors associated with pneumonia. Pie charts, and tables obtained were used to discuss results.

Discussion

This study revealed a high prevalence of pneumonia (25.2%) among children under five in Eastern Uganda and identified several significant associated factors, including poor housing conditions (grass-thatched roofing), malnutrition, lack of exclusive breastfeeding, absence of vitamin A supplementation, and long distance (>5 km) to the nearest health facility, as discussed below;

Prevalence of Pneumonia

In this study, the prevalence of pneumonia is high (25.2%) as compared to the global target of 2.5%.12 The high prevalence of pneumonia in this study might be as a result of deficiencies of the community-based pneumonia care and prevention programs and difficulties in accessing healthcare facilities. The results from this study are consistent with those reported by a cross-sectional study conducted at the Pediatric Department of Kampala International University – Teaching Hospital by8 that reported a pneumonia prevalence of 25.6%. However, the prevalence finding in this study is not in line with the findings reported by two cross-sectional studies conducted in Ethiopia, from rural Kebeles, Northwest Ethiopia,13 and the University of Gondar Referral Hospital,14 which reported a pneumonia prevalence of 16.1% and 18.5%, respectively. These discrepancies could stem from differences in study settings, case definitions, diagnostic measures used, seasonal fluctuations, data collector skills, environmental factors, data collection period, and sample size used.

On the other hand, prevalence (25.2%) of pneumonia in children in this study is lower than findings from a cross-sectional survey in Uganda by,7 which reported pneumonia prevalence of (53.7%). The prevalence of (25.2%) in this study is also lower compared to the prevalence finding of (34%) reported by a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by1 in under five children in East Africa. This could be due to the difference in the sample size, the case definition and diagnostics used, and study settings where Mulago Hospital is in a more urban area where children are too much exposed to pollution within the city, overcrowding, and use of biomass for cooking.

Factors Associated with Pneumonia Among Children Under Five years

The study found that children who stayed in households made of grass as the roofing material were 4.7 times more likely to have pneumonia compared to those from households made of iron sheets. This finding aligns with a study from Indonesia,15 which reported a strong link between housing conditions and pneumonia (p=0.000). The use of poor roofing materials (eg, grass) was found to contribute to indoor air pollution, predisposing children to pneumonia.

The study also found that children who were malnourished were 15.7 times more likely to have pneumonia compared to those who were well-nourished. This finding was in agreement with “a cross-sectional study conducted on the prevalence and associated factors of pneumonia among under-five children” in Ethiopia9 that revealed undernutrition as a great risk factor for developing pneumonia (OR=4). This was attributed to the fact that “undernutrition weakens a child’s overall immune system as an adequate amount of protein and energy is needed for proper immune system functioning and undernourished children have weakened respiratory muscles, which inhibits them from adequately clearing secretions found in their respiratory tract”.

From this study, children who had not received vitamin A supplementation were 12.6 times more likely to have pneumonia compare to those who had received. This finding is consistent with the findings reported by16 in their study of “the relationship between vitamin A deficiency and pneumonia incidence of children under five years of age in Western Java” which reported that inadequate vitamin A intake increased the risk of pneumonia (OR=1.011,95% CI=0.690–1.481). This can be attributed to the fact that Vitamin A deficiency compromises the structural integrity, differentiation, and maintenance of epithelial tissues, which are the first line of non-specific defense against infections and it also plays a crucial role in the production, growth and differentiation of red cells, lymph cells and antibodies, and its deficiency weakens the immune system, making children more susceptible to pneumonia.

Furthermore, this study found that children who were not exclusively breastfed were 3.6 times more likely to have pneumonia compared to those who were exclusively breastfed. The findings were comparable to what was found in Indonesia that revealed children who had not been exclusively breastfed to have a 6.7 increased risk of getting pneumonia compared to those exclusively breastfed and the report made by a pediatric respiratory review on risk factors for child pneumonia that revealed a 14-fold increase in mortality for non-breastfed children as matched to those exclusively breastfed for 6 months in developing countries with “(RR) 14.4; 95% (CI) 6.1–33.9)”.17 This was attributed to the fact that breastmilk has some antibodies that can fight against microbes.18

The study also found that children from homestead 5 km away from the nearest health facility were 6.2 times more likely to have pneumonia, than those who stay near the health facilities. This was similar to the findings of a prospective cohort study on economic barriers and delayed treatment in childhood pneumonia which reported that poor access to healthcare services contributes highly to pneumonia prevalence (p=0.003).19 This can be due to the fact that inadequate healthcare access can lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment of pneumonia, resulting in increased severity and complications.20

Limitations of the Study

This study did not use chest radiography and blood culture and/or culture of bronchi alveolar lavage to confirm pneumonia, therefore, this study may not have diagnosed pneumonia as strong as confirmation using laboratory diagnostic tools. However; to overcome such limitation, we diagnosed pneumonia based on the 2014 standard clinical WHO and integrated management of new born and childhood illness classification of cases. We also operationalized variables, used appropriate protocols, and trained professional data collectors to ensure data quality.

This study also faced surrogate bias because we had caretakers responding on behalf of the children. The respondents may have provided inaccurate or distorted information due to various factors which may include social desirability bias (respondents providing answers they think are socially acceptable). However, this was controlled by double checking with the clinical notes in the child’s file and employing proxy data collectors because proxy subjects may yield reliable information about behavior of target persons.

Conclusion and Recommendation

This study found a high prevalence of pneumonia (25.2%) among children under five in Eastern Uganda, significantly exceeding the global target of 2.5%. The factors which were found to be associated with pneumonia included, being from household using grass as roofing material, malnutrition, nonexclusively breastfed, lack of vitamin A supplementation, staying a distance of more than 5 km from nearest health facility.

The study recommends strengthening community and primary healthcare interventions targeting identified risk factors by promoting exclusive breastfeeding, improving child nutrition, and scaling up vitamin A supplementation programs. Policies should prioritize improving access to healthcare services, especially for populations living more than 5 km from health facilities, through outreach services and community-based care.
