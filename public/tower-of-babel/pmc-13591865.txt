Alterations of oral microbiome in oral potentially malignant disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Sajad Torki, Mostafa Qorbani, Hanieh-Sadat Ejtahed, Arghavan Etebarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesOral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), including conditions such as Oral lichen planus, Oral leukoplakia, Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia and Oral submucous fibrosis are associated with an increased risk of oral squamous cell carcinoma. Emerging evidence suggests that alterations in the oral microbiome may contribute to the pathogenesis of these disorders and their malignant transformation. This systematic review aimed to synthesize current evidence on oral microbiome composition and diversity in patients with OPMDs.MethodsA comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar was conducted to identify eligible human studies published through August 2025. Studies evaluating oral microbiome profiles in patients with clinically and/or histopathologically diagnosed oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) were included. Data on study characteristics, sampling and analytical methods, microbial diversity, and differentially abundant taxa were extracted independently by two reviewers. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Owing to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, findings were synthesized qualitatively.ResultsA total of 56 studies were included, comprising 30 studies of Oral lichen planus, 9 of Oral leukoplakia, and 17 examining other OPMDs. 16srRNA sequencing was mostly used and whole unstimulated saliva was the most frequently collected specimen. The most prevalent microbial phyla identified in OPMD patients included Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Fusobacteria, but the direction and magnitude of changes varied across OPMD subtypes and sampling methods. Several studies reported an increase in pathogenic species such as Porphyromonas and Prevotella, while commensal bacteria like Streptococcus were less abundant. Findings concerning microbial richness and diversity were inconsistent.ConclusionsOral microbial dysbiosis is a common feature of oral potentially malignant disorders; however, a reproducible microbial signature has not yet been established. Substantial heterogeneity in study design, sampling strategies, and analytical approaches limits causal inference and clinical applicability. Future standardized longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the role of the oral microbiome in malignant transformation and its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker.Trial registrationProspero registration number: CRD42024516735.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09734-0.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health burden, accounting for an estimated 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. Oral cancer is among the most common malignancies globally [2] and represents a subset of head and neck cancers arising within the oral cavity, including the lips, tongue, gingiva, floor of the mouth, and palate [3]. More than 90% of oral malignancies are oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs), which originate from the oral mucosal epithelium [4]. The etiology of OSCC is multifactorial, with the relative contribution of risk factors varying across geographic regions. Tobacco use and alcohol consumption are well-established major risk factors, while increasing age, male sex, ultraviolet radiation exposure, immunosuppression, and infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, have also been implicated in disease development [5–9]. The global burden of oral cancer is unevenly distributed, with the highest incidence rates reported in developing countries, especially in South Asia, including India and Pakistan [10, 11]. In 2022, oral cancer accounted for more than 389,000 new cases and over 188,000 deaths worldwide [1]. Despite substantial advances in treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis of OSCC remains poor, largely due to delayed diagnosis and presentation at advanced stages. Consequently, the overall 5-year survival rate has remained close to 50% over recent decades [12, 13].

A growing body of evidence suggests an association between specific microorganisms, including the oral microbiome, and the development of various malignancies. A well-established example is the role of Helicobacter pylori in gastric adenocarcinoma and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, supporting the broader concept of microbial involvement in carcinogenesis [14, 15]. In the oral cavity, the close interaction between microorganisms and mucosal surfaces—often described as a “lock-and-key” mechanism of selective adhesion and colonization—highlights the potential relevance of microbial communities in oral cancer development [16, 17].

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of oral mucosal lesions, including oral lichen planus (OLP), oral leukoplakia (OLK), proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL), and oral submucous fibrosis (OSF), associated with an increased risk of malignant transformation compared with clinically and histologically normal mucosa [18, 19]. At the molecular level, these lesions are characterized by genetic instability and dysregulated signaling pathways, frequently manifesting as epithelial dysplasia. Although such changes may not always be readily apparent clinically or histopathologically, they reflect disruption of normal cellular homeostasis and increased malignant potential [20, 21].

Oral dysbiosis, defined as a reduction in microbial diversity and an imbalance between commensal and pathogenic microorganisms [22, 23], has been linked to chronic inflammation and altered immune responses, contributing to both local and systemic disease processes [24]. In oral diseases, interactions between microbial communities and host immune mechanisms can drive inflammatory injury and tissue damage [25, 26]. Emerging evidence increasingly implicates the oral microbiome as a key factor in oral carcinogenesis, with studies suggesting that specific bacterial taxa may promote tumorigenesis through modulation of apoptosis, cellular proliferation, invasion, and chronic inflammation [22, 27–29]. However, much of this evidence is derived from experimental models, and translational relevance remains under investigation. In addition, polymicrobial communities are now recognized as potential contributors to cancer development and have been proposed as emerging hallmarks of cancer [30].

Despite increasing interest in the role of the oral microbiome in oral cancer, its specific alterations in oral potentially malignant disorders and their relationship to malignant transformation remain insufficiently characterized.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to synthesize current evidence on oral microbiome dysbiosis in OPMDs, with a focus on its association with dysplastic changes and its potential implications for early detection and prevention of malignant transformation.

Materials and methods

Design

This systematic review was conducted following the updated PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [31] and the extended PRISMA-S (PRISMA - literature search extension) model [32], registered under CRD42024516735 in Prospero on 4 March 2024 (Supplementary data-1).

Search strategy

This systematic review examined research published up to August 2025 in several key databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The review focused on oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), with an emphasis on the prevalence of diseases such as oral lichen planus, leukoplakia, erythroplakia, erythroleukoplakia, oral submucous fibrosis, proliferative verrucous leukoplakia, oral verrucous carcinoma, and actinic cheilosis. The detailed search strategy is mentioned in the supplementary data-2.

A citational tracking method was also employed to identify additional relevant studies not captured by the initial search in databases.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Case–control studies investigating the oral microbiome in any oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD) and human studies with a definitive clinical and/or histopathological diagnosis of OPMDs were included, regardless of age, sex, or ethnicity. Studies incorporating healthy controls and/or intra-individual healthy control sites were eligible. To ensure consistency in reporting, only English-language publications were considered.

Exclusion criteria

Conference abstracts, theses, dissertations, case reports, case series, grey literature, and studies focusing exclusively on individual microorganisms rather than the broader oral microbial community were excluded, as this review aimed to evaluate community-level microbiome composition and dysbiosis. Such studies are not suitable for assessing overall microbial diversity or detecting uncultivated taxa and are not directly comparable with community-profiling studies. Non-English-language publications and non-human studies were also excluded to minimize language and population bias.

The remaining eligible studies underwent full-text screening. Data extraction focused on study characteristics, participant demographics, exposure assessment methods, and outcome measures. A calibration exercise among reviewers was performed to ensure consistency and reduce extraction bias.

Identification, screening, and inclusion process was reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [31]. In addition, citation searching and manual screening of reference lists were conducted to identify further relevant studies not captured in the initial database search.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Data extraction was performed according to predefined categories: (1) OPMD subtype (OLP, OLK, OLK, OSF, oral verrucous hyperplasia (OVH), and mixed/unspecified OPMDs); (2) specimen type (saliva, mucosal or lesion swab, tissue/biopsy, plaque, oral rinse, or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue); (3) microbiome analysis method (16 S rRNA sequencing, shotgun metagenomics, culture-based methods, hybridization techniques, immunohistochemistry, and other molecular approaches); and (4) outcomes, including alpha diversity, beta diversity, differentially abundant taxa, and comparisons with oral squamous cell carcinoma where reported.

In addition, the following information was extracted from each study and summarized in the systematic review: first author, publication year, sample size, participant demographics (mean age and sex distribution), study focus as indicated in the title, microbiome analysis and sequencing techniques, sampling methods, and key qualitative and quantitative findings related to oral microbiome composition.

Qualitative synthesis

The methodological quality and risk of bias of cohort and case–control studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Two reviewers independently evaluated each study across three domains: (1) selection, assessing the adequacy of case and control definition, selection procedures, and representativeness; (2) comparability, examining adjustment for key confounders such as age, sex, and relevant comorbidities; and (3) exposure (or outcome assessment, as applicable), evaluating the method of exposure ascertainment and adequacy of follow-up where relevant. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third reviewer.

In addition, two artificial intelligence tools (ChatGPT Pro and GROK, September 2025) were used as supplementary aids for quality assessment. Each study was independently re-evaluated by the AIs after human reviewer assessment by providing the search strategy, NOS criteria, and individual study details; however, final judgments were based solely on human reviewer consensus.

Given substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity—including variations in lesion types, specimen sources, sequencing regions, bioinformatic pipelines, comparator groups, and outcome reporting—a meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate. Accordingly, findings were synthesized qualitatively.

Results

Due to the methodological and clinical heterogeneity of the included studies, the results were synthesized narratively rather than quantitatively. Findings are presented according to OPMD subtype and major microbiome outcomes, including microbial composition, diversity metrics, and shifts in pathogenic and commensal taxa. Study quality was considered during interpretation. Findings consistently reported across multiple good-quality studies were emphasized, while findings from isolated studies, small sample sizes, or studies with lower methodological quality were interpreted with caution.

Study selection

In this systematic review, 56 records were included based on the inclusion criteria. As shown in the PRISMA 2020 flow chart (Fig. 1), 153 out of 337 records identified initially were removed as duplicates. Then, one hundred twenty-three records were excluded after the title and abstract check. The remaining fifty-nine articles were assessed for eligibility, and after evaluation, eighteen records were excluded. In addition, out of twenty-three records added after the citational search, eight articles were excluded, and full texts were retrieved for the remaining articles.

Study characteristics

As reported in Table 1, this systematic review is based on 56 studies, examining the oral microbiome in patients with OPMDs, including OLP, OLK, PVL, OSF, erythroplakia, erythroleukoplakia, oral verrucous carcinoma (OVC), and actinic cheilosis. These studies were selected based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Human studies in English with a definitive diagnosis of OPMDs were included, and studies focusing on specific microorganisms of the oral microbiome or involving non-human subjects were excluded. The minimum sample size was 4 participants in each group; the largest sample size included 127 patients, and 12 studies included less than 10 participants in all groups. Case-control designed studies comparing the oral microbiome in OPMD patients with healthy controls were included primarily, with mostly balanced case and control group sizes. Most studies enrolled adult patients and included both male and female participants, with near-equal mean age and gender distribution, with different microbiome sample types.

Out of 56 studies, 3 studies used an adjacent or contralateral normal site as a control group, 8 used both healthy patient samples and a normal site, and 45 used healthy control patients. Notably, as mentioned by Carvalho et al., non-specific inflammatory lesions were used as a healthy control group for OLP cases in their article [54].

The majority of studies employed the 16 S rRNA technique (n = 38) (various regions) for analyzing and sequencing, and 5 of them combined other techniques too. Other studies used 16 S rDNA (n = 7), total DNA (n = 3), total RNA (n = 3), immunohistochemistry (n = 2), metagenomic sequencing (n = 2), next generation sequencing (n = 2), culturing (n = 2), and DNA-DNA hybridization (n = 1). In addition, in situ hybridization (n = 3) was used in studies with formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples. Few studies (n = 6) combine 2 different sequencing methods.

Whole unstimulated saliva samples were the most frequently used sampling technique, collected in 34.5% (n = 19) of the studies. The other most common sampling techniques included buccal swabs, saliva samples, mucosal biopsies, subgingival/supragingival plaque samples, and oral rinses. Other sampling techniques, such as FFPE, oral swab samples, or mucosal swab samples, might be used together in some studies. FFPE samples were used in 12 studies, with 8 of them combining FFPE with other sampling techniques.

In addition, 39.2% of studies were conducted in China, followed by India with 7 studies and USA with 5 studies. Other studies from Republic of Korea, Japan, Spain, Taiwan and the other countries with only one study.

Since microbiome findings varied substantially across OPMD subtypes, sampling methods, and sequencing techniques, the results are presented primarily according to each OPMD subtype. Within each subtype, available findings on microbial composition, diversity metrics, and pathogenic versus commensal taxa are summarized. Because the aim of this review was to synthesize evidence on oral microbiome alterations in OPMDs, findings are presented primarily according to OPMD subtype. OSCC-related findings were not analyzed as a separate outcome and are mentioned only when included studies used OSCC groups for contextual comparison.

Most common microbiome in each group

As shown in Table 2, the oral microbiome profile in OPMD patients was significantly different in comparison to healthy controls. The most prevalent phyla in the included studies were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Fusobacteria, with different prevalence across different lesion types and sampling techniques. The elevation in microbial diversity is mostly related to the increase in pathogenic microbiome, such as Porphyromonas and Prevotella, while the decrease in microbial diversity is related to the reduction of commensal and beneficial bacteria, such as Streptococcus.

The most prevalent phyla in all groups:

Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Fusobacteria, Saccharibacteria

-According to Shannon and Chao1 indices, alpha diversity was not significantly different among OLP groups and HCs

-At the phylum level, Bacillota and Actinomycetota were more prevalent in NE-OLP group while Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota remained relatively stable among groups

-Saccharibacteria was highest in the HCs and progressively decreased in NE-OLP and E-OLP

-Capnocytophaga exhibited a decreasing trend from the HCs to NE-OLP and E-OLP

The most prevalent phyla in all groups:

Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Patescibacteria, Fusobacteriota, Campilobacterota

-According to the Simpson diversity index, the community diversity was significantly lower in OLPs compared to HCs

-At the phylum level, OLPs were enriched of Proteobacteria and depletion of Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Patescibacteria, Fusobacteriota, and Campilobacterota relative to the levels in HC was observed

-At the genus level, community analysis showed the significant abundance of Pseudomonas in OLPs compared to HCs

-OLPs were significantly enriched of Proteobacteria and Pseudomonas, while Bacteroidota, Actinobacteria and Porphyromonas were enriched in the HCs

-At the family level of supragingival samples, Corynebacteriaceae and Porphyromonadaceae and at the genus level, Corynebacterium, Porphyromonas, and Megasphaera were less abundant in the OLP group compared to HCs

-At the family level of subgingival samples Flavobacteriaceae and Rhodocyclaceae were more abundant and Actinomycetaceae was less abundant in the OLP group compared to HCs

-At the genus level of subgingival samples, Capnocytophaga and an unnamed genus from the Rhodocyclaceae family were more abundant in the OLP group

- In subgingival samples, no significant difference was observed in alpha diversity of OLP group and HCs

Oral Lichen Planus (OLP)

A total of 53.5% of the included studies (n = 30) evaluated the oral microbiome in patients with oral lichen planus, of which 12 studies compared different OLP subtypes (erosive versus reticular/non-erosive). One study reported that the majority of cultured bacterial isolates in OLP lesions were Gram-positive and that Candida colonization was higher in OLP lesion sites than in healthy controls [60]. Compared with healthy controls, OLP samples demonstrated increased relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria, and Spirochaetes, whereas commensal taxa such as Rothia, Capnocytophaga, and Saccharibacteria were less abundant [33, 48]. Furthermore, Tick-borne encephalitis virus, Bacillus virus SPO1, and Brochothrix bacteriophage were identified as the most prevalent viral species in OLP patients [48]. Among fungal communities, the predominant phyla detected in OLP samples were Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Zygomycota [43, 55].

Several studies reported significant differences in microbial diversity and richness between OLP patients and healthy controls, with increased microbial diversity observed particularly in erosive OLP lesions. In contrast, Ok et al. found no significant differences in microbial diversity among healthy controls and OLP subtypes [33, 51, 56]. Additionally, salivary microbial diversity and oral mucosal microbial communities were reported to be more diverse than those identified in oral tissue samples [49].

Oral Leukoplakia (OLK)

Nine studies evaluated the oral microbiome in OLK. Among these, one study investigated both OLK and PVL, one assessed OLK and OSF, and another simultaneously compared OLP and OLK as case groups. Compared with healthy controls, OLK samples exhibited higher relative abundances of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria and lower abundances of Firmicutes [75, 87]. At the genus level, Porphyromonas gingivalis was detected in OLK lesions but not in healthy controls [74]. Network analysis further demonstrated positive correlations between the OLK group and bacterial genera such as Prevotella and Salmonella [73]. Additionally, greater microbial richness was reported in OLK patients than in healthy controls [76].

Ten studies investigated the oral microbiome in betel quid chewers, smokeless tobacco users, and patients with PVL, OSF, OVH, or epithelial dysplasia. Comparisons of the oral microbiome with other oral conditions, including recurrent aphthous stomatitis (n = 2) and oral cancer (n = 19; 17 studies specifically evaluated OSCC), were also reported.

Proliferative Verrucous Leukoplakia (PVL)

Two studies evaluated the oral microbiome in PVL. Compared with healthy controls, PVL lesions demonstrated greater microbiome homogeneity but lower microbial diversity [81]. The predominant bacterial genera identified in PVL lesions included Fusobacterium, Campylobacter, Tannerella, Haemophilus, and Rothia [64, 80, 81].

Oral Verrucous Hyperplasia (OVH)

Only one study investigated the oral microbiome in OVH. Although no significant differences were observed in microbial diversity indices, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) revealed significantly higher abundances of Veillonella parvula, Rothia dentocariosa, and members of the Leptotrichiaceae family in OVH lesions [86].

OPMD patients with smokeless tobacco use

Marked alterations in the oral microbiome were reported among smokeless tobacco users with OPMDs, including reduced abundance of Candida and increased abundances of Aspergillus, Staphylococcaceae, and Corynebacterium [83]. Sajid et al. reported significantly greater bacterial species richness in smokeless tobacco users with OPMDs than in users without OPMDs based on the Chao1 index. Furthermore, Shannon and Simpson indices indicated higher bacterial diversity in users with OPMDs than in users without OPMDs. Increased relative abundances of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria were also observed in users with OPMDs compared with both users without OPMDs and healthy control [82].

OPMDs without subtype specification

Eight of the 56 included studies evaluated OPMDs as the case group; however, seven of these studies did not specify the OPMD subtype. Studies assessing OPMDs without subtype classification reported greater species richness than that observed in healthy controls and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) groups [70]. Metagenomic sequencing demonstrated higher bacterial diversity in OPMD punch biopsy samples than in healthy control tissues, with increased relative abundance of Fusobacterium nucleatum and decreased relative abundances of Gemella haemolysans and Streptococcus mitis in OPMD tissues [67]. In oral swab samples, the relative abundances of Neisseria, Gemella, and Granulicatella were higher in OPMD patients than in controls [70]. In contrast, Shitozawa et al. reported lower microbial diversity in lesion swabs than in normal-site swabs and oral rinse samples from OPMD patients based on Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices [66]. Analysis of oral wash samples further demonstrated significantly higher abundances of periodontal-associated genera, including Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Alloprevotella, and Veillonella, in OPMD patients compared with healthy controls, whereas the commensal genus Streptococcus was less abundant [68].

Discussion

This systematic review assessed the evidence on oral microbiome composition in patients with OPMDs, to understand the relationship between microbial dysbiosis and the development of oral cancer. As OPMDs are considered precursor lesions for OSCC, the findings of this review may provide preliminary insights into microbiome alterations associated with malignant transformation risk, while requiring confirmation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.

OPMDs and the role of the oral microbiome

OPMDs are characteristically marked by genetic instability and dysplastic changes within the oral epithelium, which results in a departure from normal cellular homeostasis and an elevated risk of malignant transformation. The potential for malignant progression rate varies significantly among them, with PVL, OSF, and OLK generally exhibiting higher transformation rates compared to OLP [89].

The oral microbiome creates a complex environment in different areas of the mouth, such as the buccal mucosa, gingiva, and dorsum of the tongue. It plays an important role in supporting both oral and systemic health [90]. The delicate balance of these bacterial ecosystems can be disturbed by several factors, including tobacco smoking, psychological stress, poor eating habits, and chronic periodontitis [91]. The imbalances of the oral microbiome, oral dysbiosis, have been increasingly related to the pathogenesis of a wide variety of diseases, including dental caries, periodontal diseases, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer [92]. The ability to identify specific microbial shifts, whether within the lesion itself or in easily accessible samples like saliva, represents a significant step towards developing microbiome-based interventions for oral cancer prevention and management [76, 93].

Furthermore, the description of OPMDs showing “genetic instability and dysplastic changes” along with bacterial actions such as “subversion of apoptotic pathways, stimulation of cellular proliferation, facilitation of cellular invasion, and induction of chronic inflammation” suggests a strong synergistic relationship. The bacterial actions mentioned either reflect or exacerbate the hallmark features of cancer, including uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and chronic inflammation, which suggests a plausible association between dysbiotic microbial communities and cellular processes relevant to malignant transformation.

In fact, the differences in microbial profiles among OPMD subtypes may reflect distinct lesion microenvironments rather than a single microbiome pattern shared by all OPMDs. Erosive or ulcerated lesions, keratinized plaques, verrucous surfaces, fibrosis, inflammatory activity, tobacco or areca-nut exposure, and sampling site can each influence microbial colonization. Differences in sequencing targets may also contribute to apparently disease-specific findings. Therefore, the observed taxa should be interpreted as subtype- and method-dependent associations rather than established causal organisms.

Key messages from the qualitative synthesis

The findings of this systematic review suggest that oral microbiome alterations in OPMDs are heterogeneous and appear to vary according to lesion subtype, sampling site, and microbiome assessment method. Across the included studies, several OPMD groups showed shifts toward potentially pathogenic or pro-inflammatory taxa, together with reductions or alterations in commensal bacteria. However, the direction of diversity changes was inconsistent, indicating that alpha diversity alone is not sufficient to characterize OPMD-associated dysbiosis. Instead, the identity and functional potential of the altered taxa may be more clinically relevant than diversity indices alone.

Importantly, the available evidence supports an association between oral microbial dysbiosis and OPMDs, but it does not establish a causal relationship with disease progression or malignant transformation. Therefore, oral microbiome findings should currently be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as validated diagnostic or prognostic markers.

Differences between healthy control and disease group microbiomes: Diversity metrics and pathogenic-commensal imbalance

This systematic review of 56 studies revealed significant alterations in the oral microbiome profiles of OPMD patients in comparison to healthy controls. Among the various types of OPMD, the most prevalent bacterial phyla identified were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Fusobacteria. However, their specific prevalence varied significantly depending on lesions and sampling techniques. The presence of these fundamental phyla shows a core oral microbial community. However, the varying increase of certain genera and species, such as Pasteurellaceae in erosive OLP compared to Porphyromonas gingivalis in OLK, or Campylobacter in PVL, suggests that the dysbiosis depends heavily on the patient, type, and even subtype of OPMD, which indicates the different mechanisms of microbial contribution in pathogenesis. In addition, these subtype-specific patterns may help guide future research into microbial signatures of OPMDs, although their clinical relevance requires further validation.

Across the included studies, OPMD patients showed oral microbiome profiles that differed from healthy controls, but the direction and magnitude of these differences varied according to lesion subtype, sampling site, and microbiome assessment method. Several studies reported shifts in dominant phyla, including Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria; however, these changes were not uniform across all OPMDs. This suggests that OPMD-associated dysbiosis should be interpreted as a group of subtype-specific microbial alterations rather than a single shared microbiome pattern. Generally, OPMD patients exhibited a higher species richness and bacterial diversity in their oral microbiome compared to healthy controls, particularly in punch biopsies [67]. However, as Baek et al. mentioned, this trend was not universal [49]. Less diversity in OPMD lesion swabs was observed compared to normal site swabs and oral rinse samples [66], which highlights the complexity and site-specificity of diversity changes. In addition, some studies (without specifying the subtype) found higher species richness compared to healthy controls and even OSCC groups [67, 70]. This elevation in diversity, however, often accompanies a higher relative abundance of potentially harmful species like Fusobacterium nucleatum, and a decrease in beneficial commensals such as Gemella haemolysans and Streptococcus mitis [67]. The conflicting results about microbial diversity indicate that “diversity” alone does not fully explain the disease progression. The nature of the diversity, meaning which specific species are involved, is more critical than just the total number of species. An increase in diversity caused by the growth of diverse pathogenic or pro-inflammatory species can be harmful. This suggests a shift towards a community that promotes disease, as seen in conditions like periodontitis, where dysbiosis is characterized by an increased abundance of specific pathogenic bacteria that drive the initiation and progression of the disease [94]. On the other hand, as mentioned by Herreros Pomares et al., a decrease in diversity can also be concerning. If it signals a loss of beneficial commensal bacteria, it can reduce the colonization resistance and lead to an opportunistic takeover by pathogens [81]. This indicates that future research should concentrate on phylogenetic diversity and the functional potential of the microbial community. It should move beyond simply measuring alpha diversity to distinguish between beneficial and pathogenic diversity.

Overall, several studies suggested a shift toward taxa commonly associated with inflammation, periodontal disease, or dysbiosis, together with reductions or alterations in commensal taxa. These findings may reflect an altered lesion microenvironment, but they should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that specific microorganisms cause OPMD progression or malignant transformation.

OPMD microbiome in relation to malignant transformation: limited OSCC comparisons

Comparisons between OPMDs and established OSCC may provide useful information for understanding microbiome changes related to malignant. Although assessment of the OSCC microbiome was not a primary aim of this review, a limited number of included studies compared OPMD samples with oral cancer or OSCC groups. These comparisons may provide contextual information about microbial changes that occur along the spectrum from potentially malignant lesions to established carcinoma. Some studies suggested that OPMDs and OSCC may share dysbiotic features, including alterations in dominant oral phyla and enrichment of potentially pro-inflammatory taxa. However, the available evidence was limited and heterogeneous, and these findings should not be interpreted as a systematic assessment of OSCC-associated microbiome profiles.

Therefore, OSCC-related findings in this review should be considered only as contextual evidence for understanding OPMD-associated microbial alterations. Further longitudinal studies following OPMD patients over time are required to determine whether specific microbiome signatures are associated with malignant transformation.

Methodological heterogeneity and interpretation of findings

The observed variability in oral microbiome assessment results across the studies included in this systematic review is complex, coming from differences in study design, methodological approaches, and patient characteristics. To design future research and interpret current literature, understanding these contributing factors is essential. Oral microbiome profiles may differ substantially according to sampling site, including saliva, mucosal swabs, tissue biopsies, plaque samples, oral rinses, and FFPE specimens. In addition, differences in sequencing methods, targeted 16 S rRNA regions, taxonomic resolution, bioinformatic pipelines, and statistical thresholds can influence the taxa identified as enriched or depleted. Clinical heterogeneity also contributed to variability, as different OPMD subtypes, tobacco or betel quid exposure, oral hygiene status, periodontal status, and demographic characteristics may shape the oral microbial community. Therefore, inconsistent findings across studies should not necessarily be interpreted as contradictory, but rather as reflecting the complexity of OPMD-associated dysbiosis and the need for standardized future research.

Clinical implications and future directions

Modulation of the oral microbiome may represent a potential future strategy for reducing dysbiosis-associated inflammation in patients with OPMDs, but evidence for reducing malignant transformation risk remains insufficient. Future clinical translation will require validated microbial profiles, standardized methods, and evidence from longitudinal or interventional studies. However, microbiome-based biomarkers or interventions cannot yet be recommended for routine clinical use because the available evidence is heterogeneous, observational, and not sufficiently validated. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal designs, standardized sampling and sequencing protocols, and careful adjustment for confounding factors such as smoking, alcohol use, betel quid or smokeless tobacco use, periodontal status, oral hygiene, diet, and medication use.

Microbiome-targeted approaches, including probiotics, prebiotics, and oral hygiene-based interventions, may represent promising future strategies, but their role in preventing malignant transformation in OPMD patients remains uncertain. At present, the most clinically applicable implications are careful monitoring of OPMD patients, elimination of established risk factors, improvement of oral hygiene, management of chronic irritation, and further research into validated microbial signatures.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of OPMD subtype, clinical characteristics, sampling site, sequencing method, targeted 16 S rRNA region, taxonomic level, and bioinformatic analysis, which limited direct comparison across studies and precluded meta-analysis. Second, most studies had observational case-control or cross-sectional designs; therefore, the findings support associations but cannot establish causality or determine whether microbial dysbiosis precedes or follows OPMD development. Third, several studies had small sample sizes, and some OPMD subtypes were represented by only a limited number of studies. Fourth, potential confounding factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, betel quid or smokeless tobacco use, oral hygiene, periodontal status, diet, medication use, and demographic differences were not consistently controlled across studies. Finally, because few studies directly evaluated malignant transformation or longitudinal microbiome changes, the clinical utility of microbiome-based biomarkers for predicting progression to OSCC remains uncertain.

Conclusions

This systematic review suggests that OPMDs are associated with heterogeneous alterations in the oral microbiome, with several studies reporting enrichment of potentially pathogenic or pro-inflammatory taxa and reduction of commensal bacteria. These findings indicate that oral microbial dysbiosis may be clinically relevant in OPMDs, particularly for future risk assessment, monitoring, and prevention strategies.

However, the available evidence remains limited by differences in OPMD subtype, sampling method, sequencing technique, taxonomic reporting, and study design. Therefore, microbiome-based biomarkers cannot yet be recommended for routine clinical diagnosis or prediction of malignant transformation. Future longitudinal studies using standardized methods and careful control of confounding factors are required to determine whether specific microbial signatures can support clinical decision-making in patients with OPMDs.

Integrating oral microbiome research into future clinical studies may help improve risk stratification, early monitoring, and preventive strategies for patients with OPMDs, ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes once validated microbiome-based markers become available.
