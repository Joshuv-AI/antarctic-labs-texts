An optical frequency shifter based on continuous-wave pump fields
Anica Hamer, Frank Vewinger, Michael H. Frosz, Simon Stellmer

Abstract
Practical implementations of quantum information networks require frequency conversion of individual photons. Approaches based on a molecular gas as the nonlinear medium cover a wide range of the optical spectrum and promise high efficiency at negligible background. We present polarization-preserving frequency conversion in a hydrogen-loaded hollow core fiber using continuous-wave pump fields. We demonstrate conversion efficiency at the level of a few per mille, discuss various limitations and loss mechanisms, and present a route to increase conversion efficiency to near unity.



I
Introduction

Quantum computing and quantum communication will be based on hybrid architectures, at least partially in the optical domain. To facilitate interaction among different components, the information transfer via single photons needs to preserve the quantum state
Kimble 2008
;
Rütz et al. 2017
;
Zaske et al. 2012
;
Krutyanskiy et al. 2017
;
Fisher et al. 2021
;
Bell et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2014
;
Ikuta et al. 2011
;
De Greve et al. 2012
;
Tyumenev et al. 2022
;
Winzer et al. 2018
;
Deutsch et al. 2023
. The process is called quantum frequency conversion and relies on the nonlinearity of materials. The most well-known experiments are either using the
\chi^{(2)}
nonlinearity of crystals
Rütz et al. 2017
;
Zaske et al. 2012
;
Fisher et al. 2021
;
Bell et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2014
;
Ikuta et al. 2011
;
De Greve et al. 2012
and the
\chi^{(3)}
nonlinearity of atomic and molecular gases
Eramo and Matera 1994
;
Tamaki et al. 1998
;
Babushkin and Herrmann 2008
;
Cassataro et al. 2017
;
Tyumenev et al. 2022
;
Rowland et al. 2024
.

Crystals are known for their compactness combined with high conversion efficiency, but unfortunately also for their incoherent background and narrow spectral bandwidth. The
\chi^{(3)}
nonlinearity of molecular gases, on the other hand, can take advantage of its high acceptance bandwidth and has also demonstrated very high conversion efficiencies when using pulsed lasers
Tyumenev et al. 2022
or when operated close to atomic resonances
Rowland et al. 2024
. In particular, coherent Stokes and anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CSRS/CARS) processes show great potential, as they can utilize virtual energy levels, as demonstrated in Refs.
Boyd 2008
;
Shipp et al. 2017
;
Rigneault and Berto 2018
;
Grössle et al. 2020
;
Tian et al. 2023
;
Gonzalez-Raya et al. 2025
.

Here, we present frequency conversion from the near-infrared (NIR) to the telecom S-band (
1460\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}1530\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
) by using the CSRS process in a hydrogen-filled anti-resonant reflecting hollow-core fiber (HCF) with continuous-wave lasers. The here used signal wavelength (
1474\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
)
Javadi et al. 2015
, along with other wavelengths from previous experiments
Aghababaei et al. 2023
;
Hamer et al. 2024
, is of interest as it serves as a test wavelength for quantum dots, which facilitate interactions between different quantum systems.

In previous works
Hamer et al. 2024
;
Hamer et al. 2025
, we showed polarisation preservation, the importance of optimized incoupling and homogenous pressure as well as the dependence of the intensity of the pump fields. The efficiency of the system reached a peak of
5.2\text{\times}{10}^{-5}\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}\text{\,}{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}
for a
6\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
HCF.
We build on previously gained insights showing now the dependence of the conversion efficiency on the fiber length leading to an efficiency of
0.266\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
. Furthermore, we comment on the HCF-bend radius and discuss the potential challenges related to attenuation and undesired Raman scattering.

Frequency Conversion

We are measuring the efficiency
\eta

\eta\propto{|\chi^{(3)}(\omega)|}^{2}L^{2}{\mathrm{sinc}^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta\beta(p)\cdot L}{2}\right)I_{\text{pump1}}I_{\text{pump2}},

(1)

where
\chi^{(3)}(\omega)
denotes the third-order nonlinear susceptibility and

the interaction length

.
Furthermore, the phase matching condition
\Delta\beta=-\beta_{\text{pump1}}+\beta_{\text{pump2}}+\beta_{\text{probe}}-\beta_{\text{signal}}
relies on the propagation constant
\beta=2\pi\nu/c\cdot n_{\text{eff}}(p,\lambda)
, where the effective refractive index
n_{\text{eff}}
is obtained from the Zeisberger model
Zeisberger et al. 2018
and depends on the pressure
p
and the wavelength
\lambda

III.2
Losses

An increase in fiber length will also increase the total, eventually defining an optimum length. Here, we study three loss mechanisms in detail: leakage loss
Tyumenev et al. 2022
;
Zeisberger et al. 2018
, bend loss
Frosz et al. 2016
;
Carter et al. 2017
;
Fokoua et al. 2023
, and absorption loss due to hydrogen absorption
Bahari et al. 2022
. Imperfection losses and material absorption have been described in Ref.
Yu and Knight 2016
and will be ignored throughout this work.

III.2.1
Transmission loss

Coupling between the core modes and capillary wall modes contributes to the transmission losses
Tyumenev et al. 2022
;
Zeisberger et al. 2018
. They occur as broad resonances in the absorption spectrum, where the position of the resonances depends critically on the wall thickness of the capillaries. For the fiber used here, the wall thickness is about
1.28\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.

Here, we determine the loss parameter
\alpha
through cut-back measurements, where we record the transmission of the Gaussian-like HE
11
fiber mode for various lengths, see Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:
Transmission losses for the three incoming light fields, depending on the length of the fiber. Linear curves are fitted to each data set (dashed lines). The intercept accounts for incoupling losses.

Transmission loss is hardly noticeable for
1550\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
with a loss parameter of
\alpha_{\text{1550\,nm}}=$0.07(\pm 0.04)\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
, which is expected since the fiber is designed for this wavelength (lower as
0.035\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}
given by the manufacturer). However, there is a resonance centered around
1330\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, and the other two wavelengths are located only in the third transmission window, where losses are significant
Zuba et al. 2023
. Here, we measure
\alpha_{\text{942\,nm}}=$0.37(\pm 0.16)\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
for the second pump wavelength and
\alpha_{\text{914\,nm}}=$0.93(\pm 0.37)\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
for the probe photons. The position of the resonances can be shifted through proper choice of the capillary wall thickness, and selection of a different fiber is expected to significantly reduce losses at the relevant wavelengths.

III.2.3
Parasitic Raman processes in hydrogen

Not only is efficiency an important factor for the usability of frequency conversion, but also a low background. While frequency conversion in crystals often struggles with undesired Raman scattering, it is minimized in hollow-core fibers due to the minimal overlap between the cladding and the core mode
Tyumenev et al. 2022
.

Another type of background could originate from parasitic Raman processes in hydrogen gas itself. As the bandpass filter utilized to separate the light converted to the telecom band has a bandwidth of
25\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, we have investigated if parasitic processes are present. For this, we have examined if near-resonant Raman processes are possible, which lead to Stokes or anti-Stokes radiation near
1474\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. We have focused only on strong transitions, which are maximal two orders of magnitude smaller than the normally used
Q_{1}(1)
transition, which are expected to dominate the background signal. An overview of the absorption lines is obtained from Refs.
Gordon et al. 2022
;
Black and van Dishoeck 1987
and shown in Fig.
6
.

IV
Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated the scaling of conversion efficiency with fiber length. We achieved a maximum internal efficiency of

0.0044(6)\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}\text{\,}{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}
. For the 1.85-meter fiber used here and available pump powers of
8\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
(at
942\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
) and
15\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
(at
1550\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
), this would yield a theoretical maximum efficiency of
1.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
. In the experiment, losses from otical components and propagation losses within the fiber reduce the efficiency to
0.27\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.

Increasing the efficiency further could be achieved by an increase in fiber length and increased pump power. In earlier work, we have worked with a fiber of smaller transmission losses at wavelengths away from hydrogen absorption lines
Hamer et al. 2025
. Assuming the quadratic scaling with fiber length, incoupling efficiency of
83\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
and an attenuation of 15.9 dB/km for all wavelengths, the optimum fiber length is about
21\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, and, for pump powers of
8\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
and
15\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
, we arrive at a conversion efficiency of
70\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
. This value is on par with the performance achieved in pulsed operation
Tyumenev et al. 2022
.

Such efficiency could also be achieved by higher pump powers, which would allow for a shorter fiber and thus reduce transmission losses. A substantially higher efficiency could also be achieved through use of a different molecular gas with asymmetric vibrational modes and potentially stronger third-order nonlinearities
Ward and Miller 1979
.

An additional contribution to losses is bending. It not only leads to the breakdown of transmission below a certain critical radius
Fokoua et al. 2023
, but also causes the optimal pressure to be reached at lower values. This is unfavorable for the conversion process, as the susceptibility scales quadratically with the number of molecules and thus negatively affects the efficiency
Rigneault and Berto 2018
. We find that for bending radii above 30 cm, this effect becomes negligible.

The wavelength of the pump fields can be chosen freely, provided that their difference matches the
Q_{1}(1)
resonance. Care should be taken to avoid pump fields interacting with hydrogen and creating photons in the signal region via Raman scattering.
