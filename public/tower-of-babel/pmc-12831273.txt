PIBF1 (p.R405Q) germline variant identified in cancer susceptibility family impairs protein stability and function
Authors: Lili Shao, Liang Wen, Qiao Xu, Jianing Zhao, Rongji Yu, Yang Wang, Xinchun Liu, Jinzheng Wu, Shubing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately 5–10% of all cancer types are hereditary cancer syndromes, which are caused by pathogenic mutations in cancer susceptibility genes. In this investigation, a hereditary cancer pedigree was collected from a province in southern China, and the proband was a 31-year-old woman with breast cancer. Utilizing blood whole exome sequencing technology and bioinformatics analysis, the sole heterozygous missense mutation in PIBF1 that exhibits trait segregation was identified: PIBF1 (p.R405Q). The pedigree also included two other mutations that may be linked to carcinogenesis: RAD51D (p.K91Ifs*13) and BRCA2 (p.G3134Afs*29). This research concentrated on PIBF1 (p.R405Q) and employed breast cancer as a tumor model. In vitro and in vivo experiments showed that PIBF1-WT suppressed breast cancer cell proliferation, colony formation, invasive ability, and tumorigenesis. However, PIBF1 (p.R405Q) attenuated or inhibited the function of PIBF1-WT. Mechanistically, PIBF1-WT resisted cisplatin-induced DNA damage, significantly down-regulated the expression of γ-H2AX, and affected DNA damage repair, thus exerting a cancer inhibitory function. Interestingly, PIBF1 (p.R405Q) affects protein stability, thereby mitigating or eliminating the inhibitory effect of PIBF1. Furthermore, this family’s polygenic risk factors for cancer are analyzed, and there is speculation about potential synergistic effects between PIBF1 and DNA damage repair genes like BRCA2 and RAD51D. In conclusion, PIBF1 regulates the cell cycle and DNA damage repair, PIBF1(p.R405Q) increases susceptibility to cancer, multiple DNA damage repair gene mutations may synergistically promote cancer progression in this cancer family lineage, and PIBF1(p.R405Q) may be one of the polygenic risk factors for familial hereditary cancer syndromes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04132-y.Keywords: Cancer susceptibility gene, Hereditary cancer syndromes, PIBF1, DNA damage repair, Protein stability

Introduction

Defective DNA damage response mechanisms exacerbate genomic instability, whereby the accumulation of aberrant genetic variations confers a growth advantage to malignant cells, therefore culminating in carcinogenesis [1, 2]. At least 350 of 22,000 protein-coding genes (1.6%) in the human genome show recurrent somatic mutations in cancer [3]. Although the exact mechanisms of cancer remain incompletely understood, as a multifactorial disease, various sociodemographic, behavioral, genetic, environmental exposure, and reproductive risk factors contribute to cancer susceptibility [4–7]. To date, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous genetic variants that increase cancer risk. These risk variants exhibit varying levels of penetrance and contribute to diverse malignant phenotypes. Using breast cancer as an example, high-risk genes and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are associated with up to > 50% of lifetime risk and progression, demonstrating substantial individual effects. With advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, genomic risk variants have now been defined and categorized into high, moderate, and low-risk variants based on quantitative assessments [8–10]. Although the classification of variation into “high, moderate, and low-risk” is absolute, the risk is continuous and these categories are arbitrary, usually based on odds ratios. Currently, known high-penetrance cancer susceptibility genes account for only a subset of cancer cases with significant familial clustering. In individuals with familial cancer, the genetic predisposition is primarily driven by the cumulative effect of a large number of low-to-moderate penetrance variants, as quantified by the polygenic risk score (PRS), in combination with shared environmental and lifestyle factors [11, 12]. Hereditary cancer syndromes are typically characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and often present with multiple cancer types within a family [13, 14]. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that cancer is a genomic disorder, and one of the primary objectives of cancer research lies in identifying the driver genes for diverse cancer subtypes, and the pathogenic variants within cancer susceptibility genes that have been hitherto identified account for merely a fraction of the genetic risk [15, 16].

In 1982, Reddy et al. identified the first naturally occurring human oncogenic sequence change, a base G > T in codon 12 of the HRAS gene that results in a substitution from glycine to valine [17], this significant discovery ushered in the era of active human search for aberrant genes behind the development of cancer, proving that mutations arising from DNA damage lead to the development of cancer. The majority of cancer susceptibility genes that have been found so far affect the development of cancer by controlling physiological functions such as immunity, cell cycle regulation, DNA mismatch repair, and metabolism [18, 19]. Among them, BRCA1 and BRCA2 repair DNA damage by homologous recombination (HR). Besides CHEK2, ATM, RAD51C, TP53, and PTEN are also identified as cancer-susceptibility genes, with frequent crosstalk between them [20–26]. However, there are still a significant number of cancer susceptibility genes harboring pathogenic variants that have not yet been documented. Nevertheless, there remain numerous unreported pathogenic variants of cancer susceptibility genes, and the discovery of genetic cases and the exploration of the involved mechanisms are of urgent necessity.

The progesterone-induced blocking factor 1 (PIBF1), also known as PIBF, JBTS33, CEP90, and C13orf24, is located on chromosome 13, specifically at 13q.21.33-q22.1, and comprises 18 exons that encode a full-length protein of 90 kDa without significant homology of its amino acid sequence to any known protein. PIBF1 is a protein located in the nucleus or centrosome, produced by lymphocytes from pregnant women. Its presence is a hallmark of normal pregnancy, whereas a lack of PIBF1 is associated with spontaneous pregnancy loss [27]. Additionally, PIBF1 is produced in malignant tumors with the main function of influencing centrosome replication, participating in primary cilia formation, and maintenance of mitotic spindle integrity [28–30]. Multiple pathogenic variants of PIBF1 have been reported to be intimately associated with an autosomal recessive disorder, Joubert syndrome, despite there have been no reports of these variants in families affected by cancer [31–33]. The chromosome 13q21-q22 region is regarded as a frequent site for somatic deletions in several types of malignancies, such as breast cancer and pancreatic cancer [34, 35].

In this research, a heterozygous germline mutation PIBF1(p.R405Q) that conforms to the characteristic of trait segregation was identified in a family with hereditary cancer syndromes through whole-exome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis of blood. In vitro cellular function and in vivo xenograft experiments confirmed that the germline mutation PIBF1(p.R405Q) is oncogenic and is a potentially pathogenic variant in hereditary cancer syndromes. Analysis of TCGA data revealed that PIBF1 is associated with mutations in multiple cancer susceptibility genes in cancer patients, suggesting that PIBF1 mutations enhance polygenic risk factors in this genetic family line. Meanwhile, PIBF1 may be implicated in cisplatin-induced DNA damage pathways, leading to S-phase arrest; whereas the PIBF1 (p.R405Q) variant reduces protein stability, thereby impairing its function. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrate the potential oncogenic nature of this germline mutation in PIBF1 (p.R405Q).

Methods

DNA extraction and whole-exome sequencing

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, use TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit to isolate whole genome DNA from blood samples. The BerryGenomics Institute of Bioinformatics (Beijing, China) performed exome sequencing using the Agilent SureSelect Human Whole Exon V6 Kit. Sequencing of the library was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and Clean reads were compared with the reference genome hg38 (GRCh38) using BWA software. SNVs, InDel, and CNVs detected were annotated using Berry’s proprietary Enliven annotation system, following the ACMG (American Society for Medical Genetics and Genomics) guidelines for mutation interpretation. All identified mutations were validated through Sanger sequencing.

Plasmid construction and targeted mutagenesis

PIBF1 gene template was purchased from Miaoling Biologicals (Cat# P12901), and the PIBF1-WT sequence was cloned into CD513B-3×Flag vector using the following primers: 3×Flag-PIBF1-WT-F: CGGGATCCATGTCTCGAAAAATTTCAAA,3×Flag-PIBF1-WT-R: TTGCGGCCGCCTAGGTCTTCATCTTTTGTT. And point mutation primers were designed to construct 3×Flag-PIBF1-MUT: mutation-F: GGAAATGTATGAACAAGAAAACAGAAATCTCCGAGAAGC, mutation-R: CTTGTTCATACATTTCCCTAGAGGCATTTCGA.The sequencing data for all constructed plasmids have been provided in Supplementary Data S1.

Cell culture and transfection

MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells were grown in DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with media changes every 48 h. Transfections were performed using 5 µL Hieff TransTM Liposome Reagent and 3 µg plasmid DNA, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Success was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting.

CCK8 and colony formation assays

In the CCK8 assay, cells were trypsinized and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. The medium was refreshed every 24 h with a 1:10 dilution of CCK8 reagent (Cat#C0005, TargetMol, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours before measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (EnSpire 2300, PerkinElmer, USA). For the colony formation assay, cells were plated at 1 × 103 cells per well in 6-well plates with medium changes every 48 h. After 10 days, colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Cat #G1063, Solarbio, China) for 20 min, rinsed with PBS, and counted after photography under a microscope.

Protein stability assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and transfected. After 48 h, 10 µM CHX (Cat#T1225, TargetMol, USA) was added. Protein lysates were collected at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h time points. The collected protein samples were subjected to protein concentration quantification and Western blot analysis.

Flow assay for cell cycle

Following transfection, cells (1 × 105 per well) were seeded into 6-well plates, washed with 70% ethanol, and fixed by freezing. After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the cells were washed twice with PBS, followed by incubation with 1 mL of a mixture containing propidium iodide (100 µg/mL) and RNase (10 µg/µL) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Samples were acquired and analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Cat# BCA02, DingGuo, China). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a 0.45 μm PVDF membrane at 400 mA for 1 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight, washed with TBST for 8 min twice, and then incubated with a secondary antibody (1:5000, Cat#AP307P, sigma, USA) for 2 h. Finally, the membranes were washed again with TBST for 8 min twice and protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescent system (Bio-Rad, USA).A complete list of primary antibodies used is provided in Supplementary S2. The raw, uncropped images for all western blots are shown in Supplementary S2.

Immunofluorescence

After cell transfection, treat with 5 μm cisplatin for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% PBST, blocked with 5% BSA, and stained with γ - H2AX antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, # 2577, 1:500). The nuclei were displayed with DAPI (blue) and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.2-conda_forge software. The protein system was constructed in an explicit solvent environment, with necessary ions added to neutralize the system, utilizing the appropriate force field and water model. Following energy minimization and NVT/NPT equilibration, a production simulation was conducted for a total duration of 500 ps with an integration time step of 2 fs. Temperature and pressure were controlled via suitable coupling methods. Trajectory analyses included: calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein using gmx rmsf to assess conformational changes; determining the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of individual atoms using gmx rmsf to analyze residue flexibility; computing the radius of gyration (Rg) with gmx gyrate to evaluate overall protein folding and compactness; and performing principal component analysis (PCA) using gmx covar to extract the dominant motion modes of the protein and project the trajectory onto the first few principal components. All analyses were based on selected atom groups (e.g., Protein or Cα atoms), and simulation stability and conformational changes were assessed in conjunction with time-window analysis.

In vivo xenografts

A 150 µL cell suspension (2 × 10 7/mL) of MDA-MB-231 cells was mixed with 150 µL of Matrigel medium (Cat#356234, Corning, USA) and injected into nude mice. Tumor volumes were observed and measured weekly, and after 4 weeks, mice were executed by cervical dislocation. Each group was repeated 4 times.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using Prism 9.0 software(GraphPad, San Diego, CA). The results, derived from three separate data sets, are presented as mean ± SD. T-tests or one-way ANOVA were used for analyses comparing two or more groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant compared with the control group (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Result

Pedigree and mutation of the family

The proband in this study was a 31-year-old woman from a province in southern China, along with five patients and eight healthy individuals included in the family pedigree (Fig. 1A). At the time of blood sample collection, three consecutive generations presented with the diagnosis of breast cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. Ultrasonography showed a mixed echogenic nodule measuring approximately 18.8 × 12.8 × 21.5 mm at 9 o’clock in the right breast, 2–2.5 cm from the nipple (Fig. 1B). Combination of MRI examination (Fig. 1C) and pathologic HE staining (Fig. 1D) confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma with poor differentiation in the right breast of the proband. Family members II-1, II-4, III-3, and III-2 provided blood samples, genomic DNA was isolated, and whole exome sequencing using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 next-generation sequencing technology, in conjunction with NGS-based genetic diagnosis, revealed a rare heterozygous missense mutation in the PIBF1 gene: PIBF1 (p.R405Q) (Fig. 1E). This mutation was the only one that met the criteria for segregation of traits. Further validation of the PIBF1(p.R405Q) mutation was achieved by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1F), which confirmed the results obtained from second-generation sequencing. Particularly, full exon sequencing in this family pedigree revealed mutations in driver genes related to cancer formation, such as BRCA2 (p.G3134Afs*29) and RAD51 (p.K91Ifs*13) (Table 1). Since PIBF1 (p.R405Q) has a high gene frequency in the population, we speculated that it might be possible that PIBF1 (p.R405Q) acts in combination with mutations in other cancer susceptibility genes (e.g. BRCA2, RAD51) to promote the development of cancers in this lineage. In order to understand whether PIBF1 mutations often act in conjunction with mutations in other cancer susceptibility genes, we analysed the genetic data of cancer patients in the TCGA database, in which 121 patients with PIBF1 mutations showed highly significant correlations with BRCA2 and RAD51(Fig. 1G).

PIBF1 (p.R405Q) missense mutations are potentially pathogenic variants

The PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation is caused by base G replacing base A at base 1214 on exon 9, which results in the mutation of CGA to CAA (c.1214G > A). This missense mutation causes glutamine (Gln, Q) to replace arginine (Arg, R) (p.R405Q) (Fig. 2A). An analysis of the amino acid conservation of the PIBF1 protein at position 405 revealed a high level of conservation across several species using different sequence comparison techniques (Fig. 2B). Then the pathogenicity of PIBF1 (p.R405Q) was analyzed by bioinformatics methods, and the results of Polyphen-2, Mutation Taster showed that PIBF1 (p.R405Q) was a potentially pathogenic and deleterious protein, and the analysis of I-Mutant2.0 showed that the protein stability of PIBF1 (p.R405Q) was downregulated, and the ExAC analysis of this mutation was “Conflicting interpret” (Fig. 2C). Previous investigations have established that the PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation has been reported as a pathogenic variant in Joubert Syndrome. Furthermore, PIBF1 may also be associated with various diseases such as gout, emphysema, congenital hypopituitarism, and cancer [36–41]. Consequently, there has been speculation that PIBF1 (p.R405Q) may be a causative variant of cancer. However, the specific biological function and pathogenic mechanism of this mutation have not been documented. To evaluate the potential impact of PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation on the protein structure of PIBF1, we predicted its three-dimensional structure using AlphaFold2 and compared it with the wild type (Fig. 2D, E). Structural analysis suggests that this mutation may lead to a change in the isoelectric point of the protein. In light of these findings, additional research would be conducted on the biological function of PIBF1 (p.R405Q).

PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation is a candidate gene for hereditary cancer syndromes

Breast cancer was utilized in this research as a model tumor for hereditary cancer syndromes since, among the five cancer patients in this genetic family pedigree, including the proband, there were actually two occurrences of the disease. First, clinical data from the UALCAN database were obtained for analysis to assess the expression level of PIBF1 in both normal and cancer cells. Experimental findings indicated a notable decrease in PIBF1 expression in breast cancer cells as compared to normal cells (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, it was observed that the expression of PIBF1 was correlated with various stages of breast cancer as it progressed (Fig. 3B). Analysis of survival curves for PIBF1 in breast cancer patients using Kaplan-Meier Plotter showed that decreased expression of PIBF1 was linked to a negative prognosis (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that PIBF1 may have a suppressive effect on the advancement of breast cancer. Subsequently, the sequences 3×Flag-PIBF1-WT and 3×Flag-PIBF1(p.R405Q) were inserted into the CD513B vector. Following the successful assembly and sequencing of the plasmid, the presence of PIBF1-WT and PIBF1(p.R405Q)-MUT was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 3D, E). To investigate the biological functions of PIBF1(p.R405Q), the effects of PIBF1-WT and PIBF1(p.R405Q) on breast cancer cell proliferation and invasion were further examined. The discoveries demonstrated that PIBF1(p.R405Q) exhibited increased cell proliferation (Fig. 3F), colony formation (Fig. 3G, H), and invasive ability (Fig. 3I) compared to PIBF1-WT. As activation of the ERK signaling pathway is closely related to cell proliferation, it was found that the PIBF1(p.R405Q) variant promoted ERK phosphorylation and thus cell proliferation by WB assay (Fig. 3J). These findings suggest that PIBF1(p.R405Q) dramatically enhanced the proliferation of breast cancer cells in comparison to PIBF1-WT.

PIBF1 participates in cell cycle regulation and its (p.R405Q) variant destabilizes the protein

Concerning the strong association between the PIBF1 gene and cell cycle regulation, cell cycle changes were evaluated using flow cytometry. The PIBF1-WT group showed a notable increase in the proportion of cells in the S phase compared to the control group. Conversely, the PIBF1(p.R405Q) group had a diminished or suppressed effect of the S phase block compared to the PIBF1-WT group (Fig. 4A, B). During the S phase of the cell cycle, DNA replication takes place and is crucial for preserving the stability of the cellular genome. Therefore, it has been hypothesized that PIBF1 may have a role in the network responsible for repairing DNA damage. To examine the correlation between PIBF1 and DNA damage repair, we measured the expression of γ-H2AX protein in cells following DNA damage induced by cisplatin treatmen. Furthermore, we observed a significant down-regulation of γ-H2AX expression in the PIBF1-WT group, while the expression of γ-H2AX was higher in the PIBF1(p.R405Q) group compared to the PIBF1-WT group (Fig. 4C, D). Thus, it appears that PIBF1 plays a role in the process of DNA damage repair induced by cisplatin.

To gain a deeper understanding of the precise mechanism of PIBF1(p.R405Q) pathogenicity, various physical and chemical parameters of PIBF1(p.R405Q) were characterized by ProtParam, and it was found that PIBF1(p.R405Q) variants altered the theoretical isoelectric point of the protein. Building upon the aforementioned experimental findings, it was examined that PIBF1(p.R405Q) could result in reduced or complete loss of PIBF1 function via modifying the protein’s stability. Consequently, following the introduction of PIBF1-WT and PIBF1(p.R405Q) plasmids into MDA-MB-231 cells, various time intervals were chosen to introduce CHX to suppress protein synthesis. The findings indicated that PIBF1(p.R405Q) was nearly fully inactive by CHX treatment for 8 h, while PIBF1-WT remained detectable (Fig. 4E, F). Identifying the PIBF1(p.R405Q) variant revealed a reduction in the protein’s half-life and increased protein degradation. To investigate the impact of the mutation on the tertiary structure, 500 ps molecular dynamics simulations were performed on the backbones of both the wild-type PIBF1 and PIBF1 (p.R405Q) proteins. A comparative analysis of their atomic root mean square deviation (RMSD) over time revealed distinct behaviors: the RMSD value of the wild-type system increased modestly during the initial simulation phase and then rapidly entered a stable equilibrium state, maintaining at a relatively low level throughout the entire simulation. In contrast, the RMSD values of the mutant system were significantly higher overall than those of the wild-type. After reaching equilibrium, the mutant exhibited a markedly higher average RMSD value and a larger fluctuation range compared to the wild-type. These results indicate that the mutation compromises the intrinsic structural integrity of the protein, leading to elevated backbone flexibility and reduced stability(Fig. 4G). Subsequently, an analysis of fluctuations in internal interatomic distances within the protein backbone was conducted for both the wild-type PIBF1 (PIBF1-WT) and the mutant PIBF1 (p.R405Q) over the 500 ps simulation. The results similarly demonstrated that the RMSD values of the mutant PIBF1 (p.R405Q) were significantly and consistently higher than those of the wild-type, remaining elevated throughout the entire simulation period. This indicates that the R405Q mutation substantially disrupts the internal interaction network within the protein, leading to a notable increase in backbone flexibility or mobility, and a decrease in the stability of the structural core(Fig S3). To further investigate the local flexibility and dynamic properties of the protein, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was performed. The overall RMSF values of the mutant PIBF1 (p.R405Q) were markedly elevated across most regions compared to the wild-type, indicating that the PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation induces a widespread increase in protein flexibility, resulting in a generally looser and more dynamic structure, particularly in the vicinity of residue 405(Fig. 4H). Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently employed to project the high-dimensional molecular dynamics trajectories onto the two most dominant motion modes, thereby visualizing the conformational space explored by the proteins during the simulation. The conformational distribution of the wild-type protein was predominantly confined to one or a few tightly clustered low-energy basins. This indicates that the wild-type protein samples a limited conformational subspace, primarily fluctuating around one or a few highly stable low-energy states, and exhibits high structural determinism and convergence. In contrast, the mutant exhibited a more dispersed and broader conformational distribution, along with more substantial conformational rearrangements. This suggests that the mutation disrupts the native stable state, enabling the protein to access a wider diversity of conformations that are less populated in the wild-type, thereby increasing conformational entropy and resulting in a more flexible and loosely packed structure(Fig. 4I). Consequently, this reduces the effectiveness of PIBF1 induced S phase cell blockade of breast cancer cells, and may therefore reduce the inhibition of cancer cell proliferation.

PIBF1 (p.R405Q) promotes tumorigenesis in xenografts

Subcutaneous injections of MDA-MB-231 cells transfected with a control vector, PIBF1-WT, or PIBF1(p.R405Q) were administered to nude mice (Fig. 5A). After 4 weeks, the weight and volume of the tumors, together with the body weights of the mice, were assessed using high-precision vernier calipers and electronic balances. The tumor weights and volumes in the PIBF1-WT group were found to be lower than those in the PIBF1(p. R405Q) group. Meanwhile, there was no notable difference in the body weight of the mice (Fig. 5B, C, D, E). The in vivo studies provided confirmation that PIBF1-WT suppressed the development of tumors, but PIBF1(p.R405Q) facilitated carcinogenesis in comparison to the PIBF1-WT group.

Discussion

Genetic elements serve as a principal risk determinant for cancer, especially the pathogenic variants residing within cancer susceptibility genes [42, 43]. In this study, whole-exome sequencing revealed a segregating PIBF1 (p.R405Q) heterozygous missense mutation in a hereditary cancer syndrome family, which also harbored BRCA2 and RAD51 variants. Studies of PIBF1 in cancer are limited, whereas its pathogenic variants are more commonly associated with Joubert syndrome, despite the established cancer link of the 13q21-q22 locus [34, 35].The PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation examined in this investigation has been documented in Joubert syndrome, Congenital hypopituitarism (CH), and other diseases [44, 45]. While the underlying mechanism requires further investigation, our bioinformatic analysis identifies PIBF1 p.R405Q as a likely cancer-predisposing variant in this family, nominating it as a target for genetic counseling and early screening in syndromes such as hereditary cancer and Joubert syndrome. Notably, prior studies on the disease-causing potential of PIBF1 (p.R405Q) variations often include other potential pathogenic variants such as PIBF1 (p.L452fs), PIBF1 (c.1669delC/), PIBF1 (c.673-? 1322+?del; p.?). The pathogenic role of the PIBF1 p.R405Q variant remains largely unexplored. Analysis of public datasets indicated that low PIBF1 expression in breast cancer is associated with poorer prognosis, suggesting a potential tumor-suppressive function. Consistent with this, functional experiments demonstrated that the germline PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation enhanced breast cancer cell proliferation, colony formation, and xenograft tumor growth compared to wild-type PIBF1. These findings identify PIBF1 (p.R405Q) as a likely driver mutation in cancer progression. Ro EJ et al. reported PIBF1 promotes proliferation and motility of triple-negative breast cancer cells. PIBF1 was shown to promote proliferation and motility in triple-negative breast cancer [46]. In contrast, analysis by Shin et al. correlated PIBF1 expression with hormone receptor positivity and favorable prognosis [47]. This discrepancy leads us to hypothesize that differential PIBF1 expression and function are potentially mediated by various hormonal regulators.

Most cancer risk genes are associated with DNA damage repair or cell cycle regulation. As a nuclear and centrosomal protein, PIBF1 is likely involved in these processes. Indeed, in breast cancer cells, PIBF1 induced S-phase arrest and significantly impacted the DNA damage response post-cisplatin treatment, as evidenced by γ-H2AX detection. In contrast, the PIBF1 (p.R405Q) variant abrogates this function.Technically, while the missense mutation did not result in any notable alteration in the secondary structure of the protein, it did lead to a significant modification in the charge in close proximity to the location. Introducing a single-point missense mutation in a protein is highly probable to induce protein instability [48]. The protein instability caused by missense mutations is considered to play an important role in widely existing genetic human diseases. Mermet Meillon, F observed a significant impact of mutations on cell proliferation when studying the ARID1B gene, and ARID1B mutations led to a significant decrease in protein stability [48]. Sim õ es Correia J et al. also explained pathogenicity in hereditary diffuse gastric cancer through unstable E-cadherin missense mutation [49]. Experimental confirmation of the instability of PIBF1 (p.R405Q), which is responsible for tumorigenesis and carcinogenicity in this family of hereditary cancer syndromes, was obtained by the addition of CHX.

Apart from the genetic susceptibility caused by high-risk mutations in cancer susceptibility genes, cancer also has a highly polygenic inheritance pattern. Large-scale genetic screening has already identified some genetic loci related to cancer risks, and these variations usually disrupt specific pathways that maintain the DNA damage repair process [50]. The current body of knowledge about the combined impact of DNA repair genes and tumor suppressor gene variants on cancer risk is limited. Kailas and colleagues documented the presence of heterozygous variant genotypes of XRCC 1 at the Arg 280 position. The presence of his polymorphism, together with heterozygous arginine/proline genotypes of TP53 at codon 72, was strongly linked to an increased risk of breast cancer in the population [51]. Wang et al. discovered the autosomal dominant impact of germline mutations in P53 V157D and PMS2 R20Q in a familial pedigree of hereditary cancer [52].

The studied pedigree also harbored two additional mutations, BRCA2 p.G3134Afs29 and RAD51D p.K91Ifs13, which—despite not segregating with the trait—are linked to carcinogenesis. The BRCA2-RAD51 complex plays a vital role in maintaining genomic stability by facilitating homologous recombination repair of double-strand breaks [53, 54]. A frequent polymorphism of ALDH2 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) known as rs10744777 has been identified by Zamani et al. as a major risk factor for those with BRCA2 (p.K3326*) mutations [55]. Furthermore, we hypothesize that PIBF1 (p.R405Q) plays a synergistic role in promoting carcinogenesis with other oncogenic mutations in this pedigree by influencing the mechanism of DNA damage repair. The primary objective of future investigations will be to determine the presence and mode of interaction between PIBF1 and BRCA2-RAD51.

Conclusion

This mutation, an uncommon missense variant in PIBF1 (p.R405Q), elevates the risk of tumor development in a disorder marked by inherited cancer. The PIBF1 (p.R405Q) mutation has been found to decrease the stability of the protein, leading to a decrease or complete loss of PIBF1 protein activity. Studies employing in vitro cellular models and in vivo xenograft experiments indicate that PIBF1 (p.R405Q) may possess oncogenic traits within this family of hereditary cancer syndromes. In addition, based on the gene sequencing data in this study, it is speculated that PIBF1 may exhibit synergistic interactions with BRCA2 and RAD51D. Specifically, the combination of PIBF1 (p.R405Q), BRCA2 (p.G3134Afs29), and RAD51D (p.K91Ifs13) mutations further elevates the risk of cancer development.
