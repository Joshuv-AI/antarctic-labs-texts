The Free Energy of an Enriched Continuous Random Energy Model in the Weak Correlation Regime
Alexander Alban, Fu-Hsuan Ho, Justin Ko

Abstract
We revisit the proof of the limiting free energy of the continuous random energy model (CREM) using the Hamilton–Jacobi approach for mean-field disordered systems. To achieve this, we introduce an enriched model that interpolates between the CREM and the Ruelle probability cascade. We focus on the weak correlation regime, where the CREM’s covariance function A A is bounded above by the identity function. In the weak correlation regime, we show that the free energy is given by the Hopf formula. The resulting expression is independent of A A , confirming that in this regime the free energy does not depend on the precise form of the covariance function. Outside of the weak correlation regime, the Hamilton–Jacobi framework no longer applies. Moreover, we provide an example where a formal application of the associated variational principle fails to yield the correct free energy.

Introduction

The continuous random energy model (CREM) was introduced in
[
5
]
as an extension of the generalized random energy model (GREM) in
[
16
,

. Thanks to the tractability of these models, the limiting free energy of the GREM was computed in
[

[

Both of these models were introduced as an analytically tractable model of spin glasses
[

,

. In contrast to the other spin glass models, the limits of the free energies in the CREM and GREM have a simple form of the limit.

More recently, a PDE approach to understanding mean-field disordered systems was developed to shed new insights on the free energies of these models. While most of the work in these areas has been focused on spin glasses
[
26
,
28
,
31
]
or statistical inference
[

,
11
,
12
,
18
,
25
,
26

, the tools developed are quite general and can be adapted to study other class of models. In this work, we apply these approaches to a model where the spin configurations do not have independent coordinates. In particular, we revisit the CREM free energy from this point of view and show that the limiting free energy can be expressed as the solution to a particular Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

We study a generalization of the free energy of the CREM with an enrichment and prove a Hopf variational formula for the limiting free energy in the weak correlation regime. We recall that the weak correlation regime corresponds to covariance functions that are bounded by the identity. This formula reduces to the case of the classical CREM without enrichment. The Hopf formula can be solved explicitly for the CREM, and we obtain a one-parameter variational representation of the free energy. We get as an immediate corollary that the free energy is universal for all models in the weak correlation regime.

The case outside the weak correlation regime is trickier, since it does not fall under the existing theory developed for monotonic non-linearities
[
13
]
. This regime is interesting because it is a one-dimensional example of a model non-convex covariance structure which has been a recent topic of great interest in
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
27

. We show that a naive extension of the Hopf formula does not agree with the known formula of the limiting free energy outside of the weak correlation regime. Hopefully, this work provides the groundwork for a simpler toy model, which can be used as an analytically tractable model to understand the non-convex mean field models in future work.

In

, Mourrat found a link between the free energy of mean field spin glasses and the solution of an infinite-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The existence and uniqueness of the solutions to these equations were established in

We emphasize that although the CREM was originally introduced as a toy model for spin glasses, it is not an immediate consequence of the variational formulae derived for the mean-field spin glass models on Hilbert spaces
[

Classically, the REM and its variants were introduced as a simplified model of spin glass to study generic phenomena that one expects to see in such models. However, from the point of view of Hamilton–Jacobi techniques, these models introduced several technical difficulties due to the stronger geometric structure of the configuration spaces, which were not seen previously in inference and spin glass applications. Namely, the spins have a hierarchal structure so a simple application of the cavity method fails for such models, since our Hamiltonian depends on
\tilde{\sigma}|_{i}
which includes its ancestors on the binary tree, so we cannot use independence to easily split the Hamiltonian into its cavity fields like in the mixed
p
-spin models (see
[
33
, Section 1.3]
). We exploited convexity in a crucial way to get around the cavity computations and synchronization
[
19
, Chapter 6]
.

Furthermore, the initial condition, which was a trivial consequence of independence for spin glasses, becomes much more difficult in this setting. So the computation of the initial conditional, although explicit, does not factorize nicely since it couples two types of spins (
\sigma
and
\alpha
) with competing geometry. In Theorem
1.7
, we compute the limiting initial condition by computing the recursive expected values using truncations. The resulting initial condition is explicit, so the variational formula can be explicitly solved. This allows us to draw several concrete conclusions about the behavior of the CREM, providing independent proof of the universality of the CREM in the weak correlation regime and evidence of the fact that it is 1RSB, which was previously proven in
[
5
, Theorem 3.6]

Surprisingly, from the point of view of Hamilton–Jacobi equations, the CREM has some rich structure that was not seen in previous models. Most interestingly, in the one-dimensional case, the covariance function of mean field spin glasses is proper and, in particular, is always convex. On the other hand, in the CREM, there exist covariance functions that violate this condition, which was previously only seen in models with two or more vector spin dimensions, such as the bipartite models. The limiting free energy for the CREM has an explicit formula, and we hope that by studying this model, we have a more tractable tool to verify the validity of
[
27
]
. In this paper, we establish the variational formula for the fully enriched free energies in the convex case. We leave the rigorous extension to non-convex covariance functions to follow-up work. We hope that the formulae here can be used as a toy model to understand the more complex behaviors for non-convex mean field models.

2.2
Derivatives of the Free Energy

Throughout this section, we fix
M,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

0=\zeta_{0}<\zeta_{1}<\zeta_{2}<\dots<\zeta_{M-1}<\zeta_{M}<\zeta_{M+1}=1
. We define the convex cone

\displaystyle C_{\leq}^{(M)}\coloneqq\left\{q=(q_{0},\dots,q_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M+1}\colon 0\leq q_{0}\leq\dots\leq q_{M}\right\}.

(2.18)

Its interior is

\displaystyle C_{<}^{(M)}\coloneqq\left\{q=(q_{0},\dots,q_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M+1}\colon 0<q_{0}<\dots<q_{M}\right\}.

(2.19)

Then we can regard

\displaystyle F_{N}(t,q)=F_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M})\quad\text{where}\quad\mathsf{q}_{M}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}q_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[\zeta_{k},\zeta_{k+1})}

(2.20)

as a function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times C_{\leq}^{(M)}
.
The goal of this section is to record several useful facts about the derivatives of the free energy
F_{N}(t,q)
.

Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{N,M}=\left\{-1,1\right\}^{N}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{M}
the state space of our coupled spins
\sigma
and
\alpha
. For
(t,q)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times C_{<}^{(M)}
, denote by

\displaystyle\mu_{t,q}(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha})=\frac{v_{\hat{\alpha}}\exp\big(H_{N}(t,q,\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha})\big)}{\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}v_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{N}}\exp\big(H_{N}(t,q,\sigma,\alpha)\big)}

(2.21)

the weight of the (random) Gibbs measure
\mu_{t,q}
at
(\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha})\in\mathcal{S}_{N,M}
, where
H_{N}(t,q,\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha})=H_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M},\hat{\sigma},\hat{\alpha})
with
\mathsf{q}_{M}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}q_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[\zeta_{k},\zeta_{k+1})}
.
Note that replacing
\left(v_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}
by the unnormalized weights
\left(w_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}
does not change (
2.22
).
For any
H_{N}
and
v_{\alpha}
, the
1
-fold Gibbs average on the spin space
\mathcal{S}_{N,M}
w.r.t.
H_{N}(t,q,\cdot,\cdot)
and
\left(v_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}
of a bounded and measurable function

is denoted by

\displaystyle\langle g(\sigma,\alpha)\rangle_{t,q}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}\sum_{\sigma\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{N}}g(\sigma,\alpha)\mu_{t,q}(\sigma,\alpha).

(2.22)

For

and

bounded and measurable, the
n
-fold Gibbs average

\displaystyle\langle g(\sigma^{(1)},\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(n)},\alpha^{(n)})\rangle_{t,q,n}

(2.23)

is defined as the average of
g
w.r.t.
\big(\mu_{t,q}\big)^{\otimes n}
. The subscripts
t
,
q
and
n
will be omitted when the dependency is clear in the context.

The first lemma shows that the partial derivatives of
F_{N}(t,q)
with respect to the

’s are non-negative, and they are monotone.

Lemma 2.5

Let
N,M\geq 1
and

0=\zeta_{0}<\zeta_{1}<\zeta_{2}<\dots<\zeta_{M-1}<\zeta_{M}<\zeta_{M+1}=1
.
For all
(t,q)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times C_{<}^{(M)}
and

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as Lemma 2.4 in
[
27
]
. The key difference is that the enriched term has a slightly different form, which makes the integration by parts more complicated. We first define an embedding of

We identify the first

unit vectors

in an arbitrary but fixed order. The map

\displaystyle\iota_{N}\colon\left\{-1,1\right\}^{N}

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{H}}_{N}={\mathbb{R}}^{2^{N+1}},

\displaystyle\sigma\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{\sigma|_{i}},

defines an embedding of
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{N}

\displaystyle\iota_{N}(\sigma)\cdot\iota_{N}(\tilde{\sigma})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}e_{\sigma|_{i}}\cdot e_{\tilde{\sigma}|_{j}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma|_{i}=\tilde{\sigma}|_{j}}(\sigma,\tilde{\sigma})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma|_{i}=\tilde{\sigma}|_{i}}(\sigma,\tilde{\sigma})=\sigma\wedge\tilde{\sigma}.

(2.27)

\displaystyle\partial_{q_{\ell}}F_{N}=\frac{\zeta_{\ell+1}-\zeta_{\ell}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{N+1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[\big\langle\iota_{N,i}(\sigma)\big\rangle D_{\ell+1,M}\,\middle|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}\right]^{2}D_{1,\ell}\right]

Since

Proposition 2.6
.

Let

(2.39)

Proof.

We differentiate

to get that for any

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\ln\left(\textstyle\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}v_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\exp\Big(H_{N}\big(t,\mathsf{q},\sigma,\alpha\big)\Big)\right)\right]

for

2.2

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{q_{k}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\ln\left(\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}v_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\exp\Big(H_{N}\big(t,q,\sigma,\alpha\big)\Big)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{k=M}(k)-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\Big\langle\partial_{q_{k}}Y_{q}(\sigma,\alpha)\Big\rangle_{t,q}

where

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big\langle\partial_{q_{k}}Y_{q}(\sigma,\alpha)\Big\rangle_{t,q}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big\langle\partial_{q_{k}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{M}(q_{j}-q_{j-1})^{1/2}z_{\sigma|_{i},\alpha|_{j}}\Big\rangle_{t,q}

using that

for

and

In the case

Inserting (

) into (

\displaystyle\partial_{q_{k}}F_{N}(t,q)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big\langle\partial_{q_{M}}Y_{q}(\sigma,\alpha)\Big\rangle

Proposition 2.7

Proof.

By Proposition

The goal of this section is to prove Proposition

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix

\mathsf{q}\in Q_{r}

\displaystyle Q^{(M)}_{\text{equidist}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathsf{q}\in Q\colon\text{ there exist }0\leq q_{0}\leq\dots\leq q_{M}\text{ so that }\mathsf{q}=\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{M}q_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\frac{j}{M+1},\frac{j+1}{M+1}\right)}\right\},

this implies that both

are dense in

w.r.t.

Indeed, for any
\mathsf{q}\in Q_{1}
, by choosing a sequence

implies that the unique Lipschitz extension to

exists.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

The proof is standard.
Let
\mathsf{q}\in Q_{r}
. It suffices to show that
\mathsf{q}_{M}
defined in the statement of the lemma converges to
\mathsf{q}
in

because
\mathsf{q}_{M}\in Q^{(M)}_{\text{equidist}}\subseteq\bigcup_{M=0}^{\infty}Q^{(M)}_{\text{equidist}}\subseteq\bigcup_{M=0}^{\infty}Q^{(M)}
. First of all,
\mathsf{q}_{M}

at every continuity point of

, which is all but countably many points of

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}F_{N}\left(t,\mathsf{q}^{(\tilde{\lambda})}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}(q_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k})\partial_{q_{k}}F_{N}\left(t,q^{(\lambda)}\right),

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(\tilde{\lambda}q_{0}+(1-\tilde{\lambda})\tilde{q}_{0},\dots,\tilde{\lambda}q_{M}+(1-\tilde{\lambda})\tilde{q}_{M}\big).

By (

(3.11)

If

for some

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{q_{k}\ln 2}+\int_{\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{q_{k}}}}^{1}\mathsf{q}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{q_{k}\ln 2}+q_{k}\left({\textstyle{\frac{k+1}{M+1}}}-\sqrt{{\textstyle{\frac{\ln 2}{q_{k}}}}}\right)+\sum_{j=k+1}^{M}{\textstyle{\frac{q_{j}}{M+1}}}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{q_{k}\ln 2}+q_{k}{\textstyle{\frac{k+1}{M+1}}}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{M}{\textstyle{\frac{q_{j}}{M+1}}}.

(5.6)

Thus,

{\textstyle{\frac{k+1}{M+1}}}-\sqrt{{\textstyle{\frac{\ln 2}{q_{k}}}}}={\textstyle{\frac{k+1}{M+1}}}-x_{*}(\mathsf{q}),&\text{ if }j=k,\\
{\textstyle{\frac{1}{M+1}}},&\text{ if }j\in\{k+1,\ldots,M\}.\end{cases}

(5.7)

In particular, in each case, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\phi}-\nabla F_{N}\in{C_{\leq}^{(M)}}^{*}.

(5.31)

In particular, by (4.12) of Lemma 4.4 in
[
29

, we have

\displaystyle(\divergence\tilde{\phi}-\divergence F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}})(t_{N},q_{N})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\partial_{q_{m}}\tilde{\phi}(t_{N},q_{N})-\partial_{q_{m}}F_{N}(t_{N},q_{N})\geq 0.

(5.32)

Thus, as
\partial_{t}F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}}(t_{N},q_{N})=\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}(t_{N},q_{N})

is smooth, we conclude that

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\phi-\divergence\phi)(t_{\star},q_{\star})=\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}(\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}-\divergence\tilde{\phi})(t_{N},q_{N})\leq\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}(\partial_{t}F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}}-\divergence F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}})(t_{N},q_{N})=0,

and the proof is completed.

6
Outlook

6.1
Removing the Left Derivative Hypothesis

We claim that to remove the left derivative hypothesis in Theorem
1.3
, it will suffice to study the random energy model (REM). Recall that the REM corresponds to the special case when the covariance function
A(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}
, and it’s
Hamiltonian takes a particularly simpler form:

H_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(\sigma)=\sqrt{N}z_{\sigma}

where the
(z_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Sigma}
are independent Gaussians. Suppose that we can show that

\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(t,q)\geq\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}}(t,q).

(6.1)

Then by Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}\leq A\leq\mathrm{id}
for any convex
A
, we conclude that

F_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(t,q)\leq F_{N}^{A}(t,q)\leq F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}}(t,q).

(6.2)

Combining (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) and applying Theorem
1.3
to
A=\mathrm{id}
will give that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(t,q)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}^{A}(t,q)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}^{\mathrm{id}}(t,q)=\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1)}\left\{\lambda t+\int_{1-\lambda}^{1}\mathsf{q}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\lambda}\right\}.

Unfortunately, computing the limit of
F_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(t,q)
cannot be deduced from Theorem
1.3
by a simple continuity argument. We sketch the main barrier below.

Let
A_{p}(x)=x^{p}
. Notice that as
p\to\infty
,
A_{p}(x)\to\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}
pointwise. We have that the Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{N,p}(x)=\sqrt{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(A_{p}\Big(\frac{i}{N}\Big)-A_{p}\Big(\frac{i-1}{N}\Big)\bigg)^{1/2}z_{\sigma|_{i}}.

If we consider the free energy

\displaystyle F_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M};p)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\textstyle\ln\left(\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{M}}v_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\exp\big(H_{N,p}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M},\sigma,\alpha)\big)\right)\right]

where

\displaystyle H_{N,p}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M},\sigma,\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{2t}\,H_{N,p}(\sigma)-Nt+\sqrt{2}\,Y_{\mathsf{q}_{M}}(\sigma,\alpha)-Nq_{M}.

Then we see that uniformly in
t,\mathsf{q}_{M}
, we have that the Lipschitz constant of
p\mapsto F_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M};p)
is bounded from above by
p
. As usual, let
\langle\cdot\rangle_{p}
denote the Gibbs average with respect to
H_{N,p}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M},\sigma,\alpha)
. This is because of an integration by parts,

\displaystyle\partial_{p}F_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M};p)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}\langle\partial_{p}H_{N,p}(x)\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2N}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\langle\sqrt{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(A_{p}\Big(\frac{i}{N}\Big)-A_{p}\Big(\frac{i-1}{N}\Big)\bigg)^{-1/2}\bigg(A_{p-1}\Big(\frac{i}{N}\Big)-A_{p-1}\Big(\frac{i-1}{N}\Big)\bigg)z_{\sigma|_{i}}\bigg\rangle_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\langle A_{p-1}\Big(\frac{\sigma^{1}\wedge\sigma^{2}}{N}\Big)-A_{p-1}\Big(1\Big)\bigg\rangle_{p}

so

|\partial_{p}F_{N}(t,\mathsf{q}_{M};p)|\leq\frac{p}{2}.

This is unfortunate because by the simple bound we do not get a uniform Lipschitz constant, so taking
p\to\infty
will not work. Instead, one will have to compute the limit of
F_{N}^{\mathbbm{1}_{x=1}}(t,q)
directly, which can potentially be done using the explicit computation of Section
4
.

6.2
Outside the Weak Correlation Regime

We now explore what happens for non-convex covariance functions. In the CREM, recall from (
1.2
) that the limit free energy is given by

f^{A}_{CREM}(t,0)=t\hat{A}(x(t))-(1-x(t))\ln 2-2\sqrt{t\ln 2}\int_{0}^{x(t)}\sqrt{\hat{A}^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(6.3)

where
x(t)=\sup\left(x\in(0,1)\colon\hat{A}^{\prime}(x)\geq\frac{\ln 2}{t}\right)
. This formula holds even for
A
that are not necessarily convex. However, there appear to be some technical barriers that prevent an extension of the simple variational formula to this setting. In fact, we will show in this section that we can explicitly solve the variational formula for a simple non-convex covariance function and prove that the variational formula is a strict lower bound.

To this end, we consider the a simple two speed non-convex function, which is parameterized by
(\theta,c)

A_{\theta,c}(x)=\theta_{1}x\mathbbm{1}(x\leq c)+[\theta_{2}(x-c)+\theta_{1}c]\mathbbm{1}(x>c)

where
\theta_{1}:=\theta>1
is the slope on
[0,c]
and
\theta_{2}:=\frac{1-\theta_{1}c}{1-c}
is the slope on
[c,1]
.
Since
A
must be increasing, we require that
\theta_{1}c<1
. When applied to the piecewise linear covariance function
A_{\theta,c}(x)
the limit in (
6.3
) simplifies to the following:

Lemma 6.1
.

For the non-linearity given by
A_{\theta,c}
we have

f^{A_{\theta,c}}_{CREM}(t,0)=\begin{cases}-\ln 2&t\leq\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{1}}\\
c\theta_{1}t-(1-c)\ln 2-2c\sqrt{\theta_{1}(\ln 2)t}&\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{1}}<t\leq\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{2}}\\
t-2\sqrt{\ln 2t}(c\sqrt{\theta_{1}}+(1-c)\sqrt{\theta_{2}})&\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{2}}<t\end{cases}.

We compare this with the solution for the variational formula. For the piecewise linear non-convex covariance function, we define the naive extension of the variational formula by

\displaystyle f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,\mathsf{q})=\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}}\inf_{\mathsf{y}\in Q_{2}}\left(\Psi(\mathsf{y})+\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{p}(u)\cdot(\mathsf{q}(u)-\mathsf{y}(u))\mathtt{d}u+t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\right)

(6.4)

where

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathsf{q})=-\ln 2+\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathsf{q}(u)-{\textstyle{\frac{\ln 2}{u^{2}}}}\right)_{+}\mathtt{d}u.

(6.5)

This can be explicitly solved, which is the main result of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

For the non-linearity given by
A_{\theta,c}
we have

f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)=\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}\cap\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\bigg).

Proof.

Notice that

f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)=\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}}\inf_{\mathsf{y}\in Q_{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathsf{y}(u)-{\textstyle{\frac{\ln 2}{u^{2}}}}\right)_{+}\mathtt{d}u-\int_{0}^{1}\mathsf{p}(u)\cdot\mathsf{y}(u)\mathtt{d}u+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u-\ln 2\right).

(6.6)

We solve this variational problem explicitly. Let
u_{*}(\mathsf{y})=\sup\{u\in[0,1]\coloneqq\mathsf{y}(u)<\frac{\ln 2}{u^{2}}\}
. We can write (
6.6
) as

\displaystyle f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)=\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}}\inf_{\mathsf{y}\in Q_{2}}\left(\int_{u_{*}}^{1}(1-\mathsf{p}(u))\mathsf{y}(u)\mathtt{d}u-\int_{0}^{u_{*}}\mathsf{p}(u)\mathsf{y}(u)\mathtt{d}u+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u-\frac{\ln(2)}{u_{*}}\right).

First of all, notice that if
\mathsf{p}(u)>1
for any
u
, then the minimum is attained at
-\infty
, so we can consider the case when
\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}<1
. Notice that
(1-\mathsf{p}(u))\geq 0
and
\mathsf{p}(u)\geq 0
on
Q_{2}
, so the function is minimized if
\mathsf{y}(u)
is as big as possible on
[0,x_{*}]
and as small as possible on
[x_{*},1]
. Since
\mathsf{y}
must be increasing, this implies that
\mathsf{y}
must be constant. For constant values of
\mathsf{y}
, we have

u_{*}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{y}}\wedge 1.

Therefore, for any fixed
p\in Q_{2}
such that
\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}
, we have that

\displaystyle f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\geq 0}\left(\int_{x_{*}}^{1}(1-\mathsf{p}(u))y\mathtt{d}u-\int_{0}^{x_{*}}\mathsf{p}(u)y\mathtt{d}u+t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u-\frac{\ln(2)}{u_{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\bigg(\inf_{y\leq\ln 2}\left(-y\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u-\ln 2\right),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\inf_{y\geq\ln 2}y-y\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}-2\sqrt{y\ln 2}+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\bigg)

\displaystyle=\min\left(-\ln 2(1+\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1})+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u-\ln 2,-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+t\int_{0}^{1}A_{\theta,c}(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u,

and the proof is completed.
∎

Using the simplified formula in Lemma
6.2
, we can explicitly solve the variational formula for the two-piece covariance function.

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
c\in[0,1]
and
0<\theta<\frac{1}{c}
, the variational problem (
6.4
) with non-linearity given by
A_{\theta,c}
has a unique solution given by

f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)=\begin{cases}-\ln 2,&t\leq\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{1}},\\
\theta_{1}t-2\sqrt{\ln 2\theta_{1}t},&\frac{\ln 2}{\theta_{1}}<t\leq\frac{\ln 2}{(1-c^{2})\theta_{1}},\\
\theta_{1}ct-\frac{\ln 2}{1-c},&\frac{\ln 2}{(1-c^{2})\theta_{1}}<t\leq\frac{\ln 2}{(1-c)^{2}\theta_{2}},\\
t-2\sqrt{\ln 2\theta_{2}t},&\frac{\ln 2}{(1-c)^{2}\theta_{2}}<t.\end{cases}

(6.7)

Proof.

Our goal is to compute

f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)=\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}\cap\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\bigg).

1. Upper Bound:
By Jensen’s inequality, since
p\in Q_{2}
is increasing, we have

\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}\cap\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u\bigg)\leq\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}\leq 1\}}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}}+tA_{\theta,c}(\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1})\bigg).

We have reduced the problem to a one-dimensional optimization problem

\displaystyle f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)\leq h(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\lambda}+tA_{\theta,c}(\lambda)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\lambda}+tc\theta_{1}\lambda\mathbbm{1}(\lambda<c)+t(c\theta_{1}+(\lambda-c)\theta_{2})\mathbbm{1}(\lambda\geq c)\bigg).

(6.8)

The critical points of this function satisfy

h^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\frac{\ln 2}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}+t(\theta_{1}\mathbbm{1}(\lambda\in[0,c))+\theta_{2}\mathbbm{1}(\lambda\in[c,1])).

Solving this maximization problem by using the critical point condition or substituting the boundary on the
4
cases gives the representation in (
6.7
).

We will see that using Jensen’s does not cost anything, and this inequality is, in fact, an equality. We will show in the next part of the proof that the maximizer is attained when
\mathsf{p}
is supported on a region of
[0,1]
where
A_{\theta,c}
is affine.

2. Lower Bound:
We now argue that the upper bound is sharp. First of all, if we restrict our maximizers to the set of step functions of the form

\mathsf{p}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\lambda}(x)=\lambda\mathbbm{1}(x\in[1-\xi_{2},1-\xi_{1}])+\mathbbm{1}(x\in[1-\xi_{1},1]),

where
\|\mathsf{p}\|_{1}=\xi_{1}+\lambda(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})=\lambda
and
0<\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<1
, then we get an obvious lower bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathsf{p}\in Q_{2}\cap\{\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}}t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}(u))\mathtt{d}u

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{p_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\lambda}}\bigg(t\int_{0}^{1}A(\mathsf{p}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},d}(u))\mathtt{d}u\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi_{1}+c(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})=\lambda\\
0<\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<1\end{subarray}}\bigg((\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})\theta_{1}ct+\xi_{1}t\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\frac{\lambda-c}{1-c}\wedge 0\leq\xi_{1}\leq\lambda}\big((\lambda-\xi_{1})\theta_{1}+\xi_{1}\big)t,

which can be solved explicitly,

\sup_{\frac{\lambda-c}{1-c}\wedge 0\leq\xi_{1}\leq\lambda}\big((\lambda-\xi_{1})\theta_{1}+\xi_{1}\big)t=\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}(\lambda\theta_{1}t\mathbbm{1}(\lambda<c)+t(c\theta_{1}+(\lambda-c)\theta_{2})\mathbbm{1}(\lambda\geq c))=\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}tA_{\theta,c}(\lambda).

Therefore, we have the lower bound

f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)\geq\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}\bigg(-\frac{\ln 2}{1-\lambda}+tA_{\theta,c}(\lambda)\bigg),

which is identical to (
6.8
).
∎

By Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.3
, we see that the free energy
f^{A_{\theta,c}}_{CREM}(t,0)
is bounded strictly from above by
f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)
.

Proposition 6.4
.

For any
c\in[0,1]
and
0<\theta<\frac{1}{c}
, and covariance function
A_{\theta,c}

\lim_{N\uparrow\infty}F_{N}(t,0)=f^{A_{\theta,c}}_{CREM}(t,0)<f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0).

The fact that we have a one-sided bound in Proposition
6.4
is not too surprising. The function
A_{\theta,c}
is concave, so the proof of Proposition
5.6
can be adapted to show that
f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0)
is always a subsolution, that is

\lim_{N\uparrow\infty}F_{N}(t,0)\leq f_{var}^{A_{\theta,c}}(t,0).

However, as seen in Proposition
6.4
, the inequality is not sharp. There are some gaps in the current theory mainly caused by the lack of monotonicity in the non-convex case, so more work to extend the theory to this setting remains open.
