Phenotyping iPSC-derived neurons from Angelman patients and partial rescue via JNK pathway inactivation
Authors: Valentina Alari, Clara Cambria, Chiara De Cesari, Alessandro Vimercati, Milena Crippa, Sara Perego, Lidia Larizza, Ilaria Tonazzini, Flavia Antonucci, Silvia Russo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAngelman Syndrome (AS, MIM #105,830) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by imprinting defects, and it is marked by motor impairment, intellectual disability, speech limitations, and seizures. The condition arises due to the loss of function in neurons of the maternally-inherited UBE3A gene, mapping in the imprinted chromosomal region 15q11.2-q13, through a variety of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms. UBE3A encodes a ubiquitin E3 ligase, which is essential for synaptic plasticity. Most studies of AS have been performed on animal models, while induced pluripotent stem cells (IPSCs), mainly generated from patients with deletion, remain limited in number.MethodsHere we report on the generation and characterization of human cortical neurons derived from IPSCs obtained from children representative of the main AS genotypes: 3 with 15q11–13 deletion, 3 with UBE3A mutations, 3 with uniparental disomy, and 2 with imprinting defects.ResultsDuring early neuronal differentiation, morphological analyses highlighted a reduced dendritic branching and an increased number of thin filopodia, while migration assays revealed reduced neurite mobility in AS human neurons with the most severe genetic defect, namely the 15q11-13 deletion. At a later differentiation time point, electrophysiological analyses revealed impaired neuronal activity in AS patient-derived neurons. To investigate pathways potentially involved in synaptic dysfunction, we examined the JNK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase) signalling pathway, which plays key roles in neuroinflammation and synaptic development. We found increased JNK activation and, importantly, treatment with a JNK-inhibitor, D-JNKI1, partially rescued the neuronal morphological defects, significantly reducing the number of immature protrusions, as well as defects in electrophysiological properties.ConclusionsOur patient-specific models contribute elucidate how loss of UBE3A leads to aberrant neuronal maturation and function in the precocious stages of neuronal development and how treatment with D-JNKI1 may pave the way for new therapeutic possibilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11689-026-09728-7.Keywords: E6AP, i-neurons, Patch-clamp recordings, Thin spines, Neurite mobility, JNK inhibitor

Background

Angelman syndrome (AS; MIM #105,830) is a rare (1/12,000–1/20,000 individuals) neurodevelopmental disorder, characterized by moderate to severe intellectual disability [1], absence of speech, ataxic or broad-based gait, happy demeanor, and distinctive pathognomonic EEG patterns. Additional traits such as facial gestalt, distinguished by prognathism and spaced teeth, seizures, and sleep impairment, are often reported and included in the updated AS consensus criteria [2, 3].

AS molecular basis includes four classes of 15q11.2-q13 alterations disrupting the neuronal monoallelic expression of the UBE3A gene, which encodes an enzyme that catalyzes the ubiquitination of specific substrates and their degradation via the 26S proteasome [4]. In neuronal cells, UBE3A expression is regulated by a tissue-specific imprinting mechanism, leading to monoallelic expression of the maternal UBE3A allele [5]. Progressive silencing of the paternal allele occurs during embryogenesis [6, 7]. Approximately 50–75% of AS children carry a de novo 5-7Mb deletion on the maternal allele, defined by a common distal breakpoint, BP3, and two different centromeric breakpoints, BP1 or BP2. Both BP1-BP3 and BP2-BP3 deletions encompasse the UBE3A gene and the GABRB3 gene, while BP1-BP3 deletion also includes the NIPA1, NIPA2, CYFIP1 and MKRN3 genes. Loss of function of GABRB3 gene is associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 43 (#617,113), while genes within BP1-BP2 interval have been implicated in patients with neurodevelopmental delay and autism spectrum disorder [8]. Less frequent genetic alterations include paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15, UPD(15)pat (3–7% of cases) and imprinting defects (ID) (3%), whereas maternally inherited or de novo UBE3A point mutations are reported in 11% of AS patients. Studies on large cohorts have confirmed a robust genotype–phenotype correlation with better cognitive performance, communication skills, earlier walking age and later onset or absence of seizures for UPD(15)pat and ID cases, as well as for specific UBE3A missense and late truncating variants [9, 10]. A limited number of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from AS patients have been generated, including lines from 12 individuals carrying deletions [11–17], four with UPD(15)pat [18, 19], one with ID [20] and four with UBE3A point mutations [7, 14, 21, 22] as well as CRISPR-CAS9 engineered human embryonic stem cells [23]. These models have been used to dissect the mechanism underlying the syndrome in a human cellular context and to test the potential therapeutic efficacy of candidate drugs. However, the phenotype and functional alterations in neurons or organoids derived from human AS iPSCs have not been extensively studied.

In mouse model of AS, synaptic dysfunctions [24, 25], increased oxidative stress, metabolomic alterations and apoptosis in neural precursors cells [26, 27] had all been reported.

The JNK3 signaling pathway is particularly relevant in this framework, and its activation following oxidative stress has been reported to contribute to neuronal death [28]. Notably, JNK3 hyperactivation has been observed in the Ube3a m −/p + mouse model and a specific JNK3 inhibitor, the D-JNKI1 peptide, is available and has been shown to rescue several AS-related phenotypes in mice [29, 30]. D-JNKI1 (D-retro-inverso form of c-Jun N-terminal kinase-inhibitor) is a chemically-designed form of JNKI1 peptide [31], and it may cross the blood–brain-barrier (BBB) [32, 33]. This peptide combines a 19-amino acid effector domain derived from the scaffold protein IB1/JIP1 with a 10-amino acid active transporter TAT (transactivator of transcription) and two proline spacers positioned between them; it is cell penetrating protease-resistant, specifically inhibits JNK3 binding to its substrates and upon being activated acts almost exclusively in the pathological processes [34, 35].

By interacting with its synaptic targets, D-JNKI1 attenuates the damage triggered by pathway hyperactivation at the neuronal level [34]. Therapeutic effect of D-JNKI1 have been carried out on retinal exotoxicity, neuropathic pain, protection of Purkinje cells, auditory disorders, and Rett syndrome [36–38]. Moreover, a study on AS mice indicated that inhibiting the JNK pathway with D-JNKI1 can prevent synaptic impairment and improve cognitive function [30].

Here, we generated and characterized the morphology and functional properties of a panel of cortical neurons derived from iPSCs of 11 individuals representative of the AS genetic heterogeneity. Based on the evidence in AS mouse models, we set out to explore JNK pathway, a pathway related to oxidative stress and apoptosis, and testing DJNKI1 efficacy, in AS IPSC-derived cortical neurons (cortical i-neurons).

Materials and methods

Selection of AS patients for iPSC reprogramming

A cohort of 11 Italian patients with a genetic diagnosis of Angelman syndrome participated in our project, funded by Angelman Syndrome Alliance (ASA, https://angelmanalliance.org). Seven patients received their molecular diagnosis in our laboratory, while the remaining 5 replicated the test before being included in the study. Samples from healthy controls, 3 females (CTRL 1,4,6: two patients’ sisters in pediatric age and a healthy unrelated adult) and 2 males (CTRL5, CTRL8: a patient’s brother and a young healthy adult) were included in the study. For 7 patients genotypes, the acronyms used in this manuscript and clinical data are reported in Table 1, while for the remaining 4 cases the clinical feature were not available in detail. For the rarest and clinically mildest AS defects, two patients affected with ID, and three with UPD(15)pat, IPSCs were obtained and two clones each were differentiated to cortical neurons. However, in this study, morphological analyses, motility study, and electrophysiological recording were focused on the first subset of samples, representative of most classical AS conditions. The selection was based on both disease severity and the prevalence of the respective genetic variant. All the AS children’s and controls’ parents signed the informed consent and were reprogrammed to iPSCs and differentiated to cortical neurons using the same protocol.

iPSCs generation and characterization

Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) from controls and AS patients were isolated with SEPMATE ™ tubes (STEMCELL), in accordance with the protocol. The PBMCs obtained were cultured in SFEM Medium (STEMCELL) for 10 days to enrich the erythroblast population and transduced with Cytotune 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Life Technologies). After three weeks, iPSC colonies were observed and maintained on the Mouse Embryonic Fibroblast (MEF) layer. Single clones were expanded and characterized for stemness and genomic stability. The expression of specific pluripotency markers was confirmed by Immunofluorescence, using antibodies against OCT3/4 and TRA-1–60, and RT-PCR with primers for OCT3/4, SOX2, and NANOG [39]. (Supplementary Fig. 1 A,B).

Karyotype analysis

Karyotype analysis on at least 20 metaphases allowed the visualisation of chromosomal rearrangement, which occurred during reprogramming. Chromosomal preparations were obtained from each iPSC line. To arrest cells in metaphase, cultures were treated overnight with colcemid (10 μg/mL) at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Subsequently, the iPSCs were subjected to hypotonic shock using 0.56% KCl for 6 min at room temperature, followed by a 3-min wash with 5% acetic acid. The cellular pellet was then fixed in a methanol/acetic acid solution (3:1). Q-banded metaphase spreads were visualized and imaged at 100X magnification using a Leica microscope equipped with a digital camera. Karyotypic analysis was performed on more than 30 mitoses utilizing the CytoVision software (Leica) (Supplementary Fig. 1 C).

Generation of Cortical i-neurons

At least two characterized clones for each patient/control were differentiated into cortical neurons according to the monolayer protocol, as described [39, 40].

In brief, human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were manually passaged and transitioned to N2B27 medium (Neurobasal medium, 2% B27 supplement, 1% Insulin Transferrin selenium (ITS), 1% N2 supplement, 2mM L-glut, 0.5% Pen/Strep) supplemented with 500 ng/mL Noggin to initiate neural induction. Following 10–14 days of culture, emergent neural rosettes were manually isolated and expanded as clusters on poly-ornithine/laminin-coated surfaces. Upon reaching confluence, these neural stem cells were dissociated using TrypLE and replated at high density in N2B27 supplemented with 10 µM Y-27632 to facilitate survival. At week 4, the medium was transitioned to Neural Differentiation Medium (NDM) (Neurobasal medium, 2% B27 supplement, 1% NEAA, 2mM L-Glut) enriched with 10ng/mL BDNF, 10ng/mL GDNF, 1µM ascorbic acid, and 200µM cAMP to promote neuronal specification. Finally, at week 5, neural progenitors were seeded at low density (1.0–1.5 × 105 cells/well) for terminal differentiation. Long-term maturation was supported by bi-weekly half-media changes with NDM + GFs and supplemental laminin (1 µg/mL) to maintain neurite attachment and structural integrity.

Real-Time PCR

RNA was isolated from blood and iPSCs with Quick-RNA™ Mini Prep (Zymo Research). 1μg was reverse transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction manual. The cDNAs were analyzed by Real-time PCR using QuantStudio 12k Flex Applied Biosystems (Life Technologies). We used SYBR® Green (Thermo Fisher) with the UBE3A primer pair reported below. Each sample was run in triplicate, and three independent experiments were performed. The results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method to allow the normalization of each sample to GAPDH. The high reproducibility of the GAPDH expression allowed us to use only one reference gene.

Protein extraction and western blot

Total proteins from neurons were extracted using RIPA Buffer (Tris–HCl 50 mM pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0,5% sodio deossicolato, 0,1% SDS), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). The protein concentration was assessed using the DC Protein Assay Kit (Biorad). 15μg of total extracts were separated on a 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose (iBlot Gel Transfer Stacks Nitrocellulose, ThermoFisher). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk, TS1X Buffer (20mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150mM NaCl), and 0.3% Tween-20 for 1 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Biorad) was used for the detection of the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The list of antibodies is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

I-neurons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min, 37 °C) at 42 culture days for morphological analysis and at 100 days for cortical neuron characterization with specific mature markers and UBE3A. IPSC- derived neurons (i-neurons) from AS patients and controls, cultured without and with DJNKI1, were double-stained with MAP2 and TUJ1 neuron-specific markers. Primary antibodies in gelatin dilution buffer (0.2% gelatin, 0.3% Triton-X-100, 20mM Sodium Phosphate Buffer pH 7.4, 0.45M NaCl, all by Sigma) were incubated overnight at 4°C. The appropriate secondary antibodies were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. 1024 × 1024-pixel images were acquired with a Nikon Ti Eclipse-C2-SH confocal microscope with a 40X magnification for arborization and neuronal length measurement, and with a 60X objective for the analysis of protrusions along neurites. The list of antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Analysis

Morphological analyses, carried out on 42-day i-neurons (young neurons) from AS patients and controls, were performed on two clones for each patient and matched with a set of controls. This analysis was performed at two different magnifications, 40x and 60x, on isolated neurons.

We used ImageJ software (NIH) and its Skeletonize Plugin (https://imagej.net/plugins/analyze-skeleton) for the analysis of about 50 cells for clone and two independent experiments were performed. The images of each neuron were binarized, skeletonized, and analysed by the software to calculate the average length of branches, the number of end points per cell, and the neuronal length (Supplementary Fig. 4). The analyses were performed by three distinct operators, all of whom produced identical outcomes. The number of primary neurites (i.e. derived from the soma) was manually counted to categorize uni/bipolar versus multipolar (> or = 3 primary neurites per cell) neurons; these results are expressed in pie charts and reported in histograms with the percentages of multipolar and unipolar/bipolar cells. For the protrusions’ analysis, at least 50 sections (obj 60x, with 50µm size) were examined for every clone.

Single-cell motility assay

Progenitors at 35 days were seeded on Willco-dish glass-bottom dishes (GWST-3512, WillCo Wells BV, Amsterdam, NL), at a density of 25–50 × 103 cells/cm2 and cultured in a standard neuronal-differentiation medium. At a range of time points, after 3–7–15 days in vitro (D3, D7, D15; ± 1 day), living-cell imaging was performed using an inverted Nikon-TiPSF wide-field microscope (Nikon, Japan), equipped with an incubated chamber coupled to the microscope (Okolab, Italy) and an air 20x (NA 0.45, Plan-Fluor) objective, for 5–6 h, sampling every minute. Movies were analyzed with the ImageJ manual tracking plugin MTrack, similarly to our previous work [41, 42]. We tracked soma (i.e. the center of cell soma) or neurite tip movements every minute. We measured:—the soma speed (the length of the track from the start point to the last point divided by time, in μm/min);—the soma displacement (i.e. the displacement from the start point of the track to the last point, here reported in µm). We analysed all the cells with a neuronal-like phenotype visible in each image (not in clusters); cells moving on an underlying layer of glial cells were discarded. For neurite tips, we measured:—the neurite speed (in μm/min);—the neurite displacement (in μm; the mean displacement with respect to the initial position). We analysed all the visible neurites of cells with a neuronal-like phenotype in each image.

We tested i-neurons derived from 9 different clones: 5 CTRLs versus DEL1 (2 clones), DEL2 and DEL3, always run in couples (one CTRL and one AS, but blindly for their genotype), analysing at least 10 neurons/sample, in a blind. Data are reported as the average value ± the standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM), with n ≥ 3 independent samples for each condition (at least 50 soma or 59 neurites per condition in total); the mean values obtained in each repeated experiment were assumed to be normally distributed about the true mean. Data were statistically analyzed by two-Way ANOVA to compare more than 2 conditions and groups. Statistical significance refers to results where P < 0.05 was obtained.

Electrophysiological in vitro recordings

Patch-clamp recordings were performed on i-neurons obtained from control subjects and AS patients DEL1, DEL2, DEL3, and UBE3 N-ter (Table 1). Recordings were carried out serially at differentiation weeks 14, 15, and 16. Recordings were acquired using an Axopatch 200B amplifier and pClamp-10 software (Axon Instruments). Recording pipettes were fabricated from borosilicate glass capillary (World Precision Instrument) using a two-stage puller (Narishige) and had a tip resistance of 3–5 MΩ. Recordings were conducted in Krebs’-Ringer’s-HEPES (KRH) external solution (NaCl 125 mM, KCl 5 mM, MgSO4 1.2 mM, KH2PO4 1.2 mM, CaCl2 2 mM, glucose 6 mM, HEPES–NaOH pH 7.4 25 mM); pipettes were filled with the potassium gluconate-based intracellular solution (KGluc 130 mM, KCl 10 mM, EGTA 1 mM, HEPES 10 mM, MgCl2 2 mM, MgATP 4 mM, GTP 0.3 mM).

In order to identify electrophysiological events (spontaneous excitatory synaptic events and neuronal spiking activity), cells were held at −70 mV and patch-clamp recordings were performed in whole-cell or cell-attached configuration (injected current = 0), respectively (as in Pizzamiglio et al., 2021) [43].

Membrane properties, including input resistance and membrane capacitance (for whole-cell recordings), were monitored throughout the experiments. Series resistance was regularly assessed, and only recordings with stable access were included. Cells were excluded from analysis if series resistance exceeded 20 MΩ or varied by more than 20% during the recording. Cells displaying unstable holding current, large changes in input resistance, or excessive leak currents were also excluded from analysis.

The recorded traces were analyzed offline using Clapfit-pClamp 10 software, applying a manually-defined threshold for event detection.

For pooled analyses, frequency, amplitude, and charge data from each patient were normalized to the mean value obtained from the control patient pool, which was set to 1. For developmental-stage analyses (weeks 14, 15, and 16), all measurements were normalized to the mean value of the control patient at week 14, which was used as the common reference condition. Raw data are reported in the Supplementary Table 3.

DJNKI1 treatment

DJNKI (AM-111 MedChemExpress) was diluted in distilled water at 5μM concentration and added to cortical neurons at each media change for 1 week. For morphological analysis and the study of early biomarkers, it was administered from 35 to 42 differentiation days, while to carry out electrophysiological recordings and Western blot experiments, the drug was administered at a greater degree of maturation, from day 93 to day 100 of the same neuronal cultures.

Statistical analysis

Datasets were used for the statistical analysis, and data were expressed as average value ± the standard error (mean ± SD), except where differently stated. Statistical analysis was performed using Graph Pad Prism 7 program. For non-parametric data (after Shapiro–Wilk normality test), One-way ANOVA, followed by Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s post hoc test, was used, if not otherwise stated. Sidak correction test was used for the analysis of the effect of DJNKI on the JNK pathway (Fig. 6 E). Conversely, for multipolarity studies (comparison between uni/bipolar versus multipolar cells), we used the Mann–Whitney test (Fig. 3 G). The specific P values (considered significant for P < 0.05) are shown on the respective graphs.

Results

iPSC-derived cortical neurons model the four pathological mechanisms of AS

To represent AS genetic heterogeneity, we reprogrammed erythroblasts into iPSCs from a cohort of 11 patients, carrying the different genetic alterations known to cause AS (Table 1 and specific session in methods). iPSC were obtained from all the patients and characterized for stemness by immunostaining of the pluripotency markers OCT3/4 and Tra-1–60 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1 A) and by assessing the expression of OCT3/4, SOX2, and NANOG by RT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To exclude cytogenetic and submicroscopic rearrangements, which might occur during in vitro culture, genomic stability was verified by karyotyping and SNP-array analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1 C, D). The whole panel of karyotypes and SNP arrays of each clone involved have been uploaded in the raw data. No chromosomal anomalies were identified, while some CNVs were detected by SNP Array, but the clones carrying anomalies were discarded. The presence of the expected genetic alteration in each clone was confirmed by DNA sequencing for UBE3A pathogenic variants, while deletion, uniparental disomy, and imprinting defects were assessed by MS-MLPA and SNP array as described in methods.

Cohort of AS patients and derived in vitro models. iPSCs were then differentiated into cortical neurons, in accordance with the monolayer protocol well-established in our laboratory [44, 45]. The i-neurons obtained properly expressed neuronal markers (Fig. 1B).

Clinical features of AS patients

We selected a restricted cohort of AS subjects, whose i-neurons are the main focus of this study. The cohort includes two boys harboring a BP1-BP3 deletion (DEL1 and DEL3), a girl with BP2-BP3 deletion (DEL2), a boy carrier of a heterozygous variant leading to an N-terminal stop codon (UBE3A N-ter), a boy with an in-frame deletion (UBE3A in frame DEL) and a girl with a late truncating variant (UBE3A C-ter). Only one individual carrying UPD(15)pat was selected. The clinical features of the patients and their specific pathogenic variants in UBE3A cases are described in detail in Table 1, and in Supplementary Table 2 the additional signs are reported. The three patients with deletion and the boy carrier of the UBE3A N-ter stop codon displayed a severe intellectual disability; as expected, patients with the in-frame deletion, UBE3A C-ter variant, and UPD(15)pat achieved better cognitive and motor performance.

Multi-level characterization of iPSC-derived cortical neurons

As first, the amount of UBE3A mRNA and protein were measured in i-neurons. mRNA expression levels were analyzed in mature cortical neurons from one clone of DEL1, DEL2, DEL3, UBE3A N-ter, UBE3A C-ter, in-frame and UPD(15)pat by qRT-PCR experiments (Supplementary Fig. 5 A). A highly significant reduction in mRNA expression of UBE3A gene was confirmed for all AS samples, except for UBE3A C-ter case, which showed expression levels comparable to those of healthy controls.

Immunofluorescence and Western blot results revealed a significant decrease in UBE3A protein levels in AS patients-derived i-neurons compared with healthy control i-neurons (Supplementary Fig. 5B, 5 C and Supplementary Fig. 6 A, 6B).

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5D-5F, mature i-neurons (100 days) showed the presence of neuronal markers as CUX1 (specific of cortical neurons), VGLUT1 (specific of excitatory neurons), and synaptophysin (specific for a synaptic protein), together with an almost null presence of UBE3A. These results demonstrate that we successfully generated cortical, excitatory, and mature neurons, representative of AS human neuronal models.

Morphological analyses of human cortical neurons

As a deficit in dendritic arborization and synaptic deficit have been reported in adult mice [46], we investigated the neuronal morphology at a precocious timepoint (42 days of differentiation, one week after the neural progenitor stage), when the i-neurons were still cultured at low density and could be easily analyzed as isolated cells. Morphological analyses were carried out on young i-neurons from DEL1, DEL2, DEL3, and UBE3A N-ter.

In Fig. 2A, representative images of i-neurons is shown for each genotype. Data from i-neurons of DEL1, DEL2 and DEL3 clones pooled together (pool DEL) and from i-neurons of two clones of UBE3A N-Ter (pool UBE3A N-ter) are represented in Fig. 2B-C-D. At this early developmental stage, dendritic arborization exhibited a markedly reduced complexity in AS patient-derived i-neurons compared to controls. By comparing the frequency distribution of different neuronal morphologies (i.e. unipolar, bipolar, multipolar) across i-neurons, we found that i-neurons from patients displayed an higher proportion of uni-and bipolar cells (66,01% ± 1,9 SEM pool DEL and 69,19% ± 4,7 SEM pool UBE3A N-terT respectively). In contrast, control cells demonstrated the opposite distribution, with a predominance of multipolar cells (70.35%), defined by the presence of three or more neurites projecting from the soma (Fig. 2E).

These findings suggest a significant delay in the early stages of neuronal maturation and branching complexity associated with the pathological condition, when compared to the control group (Fig. 2F). While this phenotype likely reflects a slower rate of neurite morphogenesis rather than a complete failure in establishing polarity, it highlights a precocious developmental defect that aligns with the long-term arborization deficits observed in animal models.

Morphological analysis of filopodia-like protrusions

At the same timepoint, morphological analyses were replicated at higher magnification to identify details and quantify the number of neurite protrusions with a filopodia-like shape, which may represent an indicator of neuronal maturation.

Two clones for each sample (DEL1, DEL2, DEL3 and UBE3A N-ter) were analyzed and matched with a set of healthy controls (Fig. 3A). Only cells positive for both MAP2 and TUJ1 pan-neuronal markers were included in the analysis to exclude glial cells. We confirmed a significantly increased density of immature protrusions in neurons from deleted and UBE3A N-ter patients compared with control cells (P < 0,0001, Fig. 3 B). The quantifications were performed on i-neurons stained by MAP2 and TUJ1. As the cytoskeleton of dendritic spines is primarily composed of filamentous (F)-actin, the same neurons were stained with two additional markers expressed in dendritic protrusions: DREBRIN, an actin-binding protein, highly enriched in dendritic postsynaptic spines and involved in early synaptogenesis (Fig. 3 C) and Phalloidin (Fig. 3 D). An increased number of protrusions in AS patients-derived neurons was confirmed with all the three markers.

Neuronal motility

We investigated the migration behavior of our i-neurons, cultured on standard glass dishes and imaged by time-lapse bright-field microscopy for several hours. Their movements were tracked at different stages of differentiation in vitro (D3, D7, D15) (Fig. 4A). Neurons were more mobile at the early stage, and their mobility decreased with advancing differentiation. The speed of neuronal soma migration decreased after D3, then remaining stable at D7 and D15, for both CTRL and DEL1, DEL2 and DEL3 samples (P = 0.0176 for time in vitro, 2-way Anova; Fig. 4B). The mean net soma displacement with respect to the initial position (i.e. the distance reached from the t = 0 position) was not influenced by either time or genotype, and was equal between the two populations over time (Fig. 4C).

We then quantified the mobility of neurites, by tracking the protrusion/retraction of tips (Fig. 4A). Neuritic tips were more mobile than the soma displaying a migration speed that was nearly twice as high, although they exhibited a similar temporal pattern. Neurites from CTRLs and the three cases explored their surroundings at higher speed during early time in vitro, then slowed down their velocity with the differentiation time (P = 0.0114 for time in vitro, 2-way Anova; Fig. 4D). Neurite displacement relative to the initial position remained similar in CTRL neurons at all times, while neurons from DEL1, DEL2 and DEL3 showed a progressive reduction in displacement with the increase of the differentiation time in vitro (Fig. 4E). In this analysis, neurite displacement was tuned by genotype (P = 0.0289 for genotype, 2-way Anova; Fig. 4E).

Overall, the mobility of i-neurons decreases with the differentiation time in vitro. At the soma level, the migration abilities were similar between CTRL and Angelman samples, at all time points. Neurites were more mobile than soma but still showed a decrease in neurite migration speed with the increase of time in culture. Specifically, DEL1, DEL2 and DEL3 exhibited migration speed comparable to those of CTRL neurites but showed a lower final displacement, thus suggesting more frequent back and forth movements and less efficient directional exploration.

Analysis of synaptic activity indicates electrophysiological changes in AS i-neurons

Investigation of morphological features revealed severe defects in i-neurons, as indicated by the higher number of filopodia-like (immature) protrusions and reduced length of neuronal branches. Thus, we tested their ability to generate functional synaptic activity as hallmarks of cellular maturation and proper developmental progression. To this end, patch-clamp recordings were conducted on eight iPSC lines derived from four patients and three control subjects: DEL1 (two clones), DEL2 and DEL3 patients, two clones from one patient with UBE3A-N-ter mutation and individual clones from three control subjects. Based on visual inspection, all recorded cells were identified as sparse bipolar neurons. Voltage clamp were then performed to monitor spontaneous excitatory post-synaptic currents (sEPSCs; synaptic events, Fig. 5A) and cellular spiking activity (recordings in loose cell-attached configuration, Fig. 5H). Indeed, the limited mechanical stability of less differentiated cells and the low abundance of more mature multipolar neurons in these cultures were restricted to the subset of neurons that could be reliably maintained during patch-clamp experiments. Results in Fig. 5B, C, D indicate reduced frequency, amplitude and charge of sEPSCs recorded in pools of 14–16 weeks DEL i-neurons compared with controls. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in the context of UBE3A gene truncation (UBE3A N-ter), indicating that the immature structural phenotype correlates with functional defects in the context of UBE3A deletion but not in the case of UBE3A N-ter truncation, where near-normal synaptic activity was unexpectedly observed. Also, analyzing sEPSCs frequency, amplitude and charge against time in CTRL and AS-derived neurons, we found specific differences. In CTRL and UBE3A N-Ter, sEPSCS frequency remained constant and similar between the two groups over time, suggesting that the maximal efficiency of synaptic vesicles recycling has already been reached by week 14 or that an appropriate abundance of presynaptic terminals is mainly established at this stage (Fig. 5E). Interestingly, sEPSCs frequency in the UBE3A N-ter i-neurons was higher compared to the CTRL during development (div 107). Furthermore, regarding sEPSCs amplitude (Fig. 5F), in CTRL i-neurons we measured increasing values over time, consistent with network maturation and developmental insertion of AMPA receptors. This phenomenon is still evident in UBE3A N-ter i-neurons but is totally prevented in those established from DEL patients, where sEPSCs amplitude remains similar across 14, 15 and 16 week-old cultures (Fig. 5F). The data on charge further support the notion of a significant block in maturation in this genetic context (Fig. 5G). Finally, to assess the ability of AS i-neurons to establish spontaneous spiking activity, we measured single-neuron spiking activity in voltage-clamp recordings performed in the cell-attached configuration (current injected = 0; Fig. 5I). The analysis revealed a significant reduction in terms of spiking frequency (Fig. 5I), suggesting that neurons with an UBE3A gene deletion fire less frequently within the network compared to CTRL cells, possibly due to a lower integration of excitatory currents at the synapse. Once again, no changes occurred in the case of UBE3A gene truncation (UBE3A N-ter). When plotting spiking frequency over time (Fig. 5J), we observed that CTRL and UBE3A N-ter i-neurons followed a comparable developmental trajectory, whereas UBE3A DEL cultures exhibited a distinct pattern, with consistently lower values and only a slight increase at later time points, further supporting the presence of impaired functional maturation in this genetic context. Overall, electrophysiological analysis of glutamatergic transmission displays marked defects in i-neurons with BP1-BP3 and BP2-BP3 deletion and a fairly normal behaviour in UBE3A N-ter mutations, suggesting the possibility of compensatory mechanisms in the glutamatergic system, specifically in UBE3A N-ter truncation.

c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK) pathway activation in Angelman Syndrome i-neurons

The analyses performed in this study highlighted significant synaptic dysfunction in AS i-neurons, suggesting that these abnormalities might be amenable to pharmacological modulation through JNK inhibition. Moreover, a previous study [29] demonstrated JNK activation and the therapeutic efficacy of DJNKI1 in AS mouse models. Before exploring the therapeutic potential of JNK inhibition in ameliorating Angelman syndrome phenotype, we first investigated the JNK pathway dysfunction in the patient-specific i-neurons, focusing on cultures derived from patients with the most severe clinical features.

JNK3 activation can be measured using the p-JNK/JNK ratio, which increases in the presence of stress due to JNK3 autophosphorylation [47]. Thus, the p-JNK/JNK ratio was studied by WB in 100-day-old neurons derived from DEL1 (two clones), DEL2, and DEL3 patients together with four healthy controls (Fig. 6A). Data from the ratio in individual clones are shown in Fig. 6B, whereas the comparison between p-JNK/JNK ratio in the pool of deleted neurons versus that of controls is reported in Fig. 6C. The results highlight the activation of the JNK pathway in AS neurons, as proved by a significantly increased ratio between pJNK/JNK (Supplementary Fig. 7).

DJNKI1 delivery rescues morphological and functional changes of AS i-neurons

D-JNKI1 (AM111- MedChem Express, distributed by DBA Italy) was diluted to final concentrations of 2µM and 5µM and administered to i-neurons from DEL1 and DEL2 and one healthy control at 93–100 days of differentiation. The most effective concentration was 5µM D-JNKI1. Western blot experiments were performed on mature cortical neurons (100 differentiation days). Analysis of individual clones revealed a significant rescue of JNK pathway activation levels in deleted neurons following D-JNKI1 treatment, whereas no significant effect was observed in control cultures (Fig. 6D, 6E and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Recovery of morphological biomarkers

To examine whether or not D-JNKI1 could ameliorate the cellular abnormalities previously observed in AS i-neurons, 5μM D-JNKI1 was added to cultures derived from DEL1, DEL2, DEL3, and UBE3AN-ter patients at each medium change.

As shown in Fig. 7A, the acute DJNKI1treatment significantly rescued of the morphological phenotype in all analysed clones, with an impressive reduction of immature protrusions. The comprehensive analysis of morphological parameters demonstrated that DJNKI1 significantly reduced the number of neuritic terminal points (protrusions) (Fig. 7C), and increased average branch length (Fig. 7D), whereas neuronal length was not significantly improved (Fig. 7E).

Impact of DJNKI1 in the recovery of electrophysiological parameters

Furthermore, electrophysiological recordings of DJNKI1-treated i-neurons revealed a significant rescue of sEPSCs frequency (Fig. 7F-G) with a slight increment in the sEPSCs amplitude (Fig. 7H), suggesting that JNK pathway dysregulation contributes, at least in part, to AS neuronal dysfunction. Following treatment, sEPSC charge remained impaired in AS neurons, reflecting AMPA receptor kinetics and indicating no improvement in AMPA receptor gating (Fig. 7I).

Discussion

Several studies on AS and its underpinning mechanisms have been conducted using animal models carrying Ube3a-maternal deletion in neurons [48–50], which recapitulate the clinical phenotype and the monoallelic gene expression. However, given the differences and the complexity of human neurons, iPSCs and IPCS-derived-neurons have recently emerged as valuable tools for studying the disorder [51, 52]. Patient-specific models provide a unique opportunity to explore the basis of genotype–phenotype correlations. Indeed, children affected by deletion of the 15q11.2-q13 region or by specific truncating UBE3A variants suffer from more severe epilepsy, cognitive and communication deficits than children with UPD(15)pat or imprinting disorder. Several studies have reported iPSCs derived from patients carrying the most common AS genetic defects (i.e. BP1-BP2 and BP2-BP3 deletion), but only a limited number of reports describe iPSCs from patients with UPD(15)pat and ID. To fill this gap, and based on the availability of a large cohort of nearly 200 AS probands diagnosed in our lab, we generated a a collection representative of the major pathogenic mechanisms of AS, including three UBE3A pathogenic variants, differently affecting the protein, three UPD(15)pat and 2 ID. These human neuronal cell models enable the exploration and comparison of the consenquences of AS genetic heterogeneity. Our data confirmed the loss of UBE3A protein in mature i-neurons. However, consistent with previous reports [5, 7], we observed a gradual decrease in UBE3A expression over time, further supporting the progressive silencing of the paternal allele during neuronal maturation [6]. Comparative analysis of the UBE3A protein expression revealed the absence of UBE3A transcript not only in neurons carrying maternal deletion or UPD(15)pat neurons, but also in UBE3A N-ter samples, likely as a consequence of RNA-mediated decay. In contrast, UBE3A transcript levels in the UBE3A C-ter sample were comparable to those observed in controls.

For the first time, we demonstrate significantly altered neuronal morphology in AS i-neurons at early stage of in vitro development. At this developmental stage, connections among cells are at low density, facilitating accurate morphological measurements, as previously suggested [53]. i-neurons from patients with the most severe phenotype (Deletion and UBE3A early truncating variants) revealed a markedly immature phenotype compared with control neurons, showing reduced neuritic arborization and shorter neurites. Interestingly, we observed an increased number of terminal end-points in all the clones derived from the three biological replicates of the deleted patients and also N-ter truncation of UBE3A. Upon high-magnification analysis and immunostaining with βIII -tubulin for neuronal cytoskeleton, we were able to visualize in AS i-neurons an abnormal abundance of thin, needle-like protrusions, resembling dendritic filopodia. The staining with phalloidin, marker specific for filopodia, and drebrin, specific for synaptic spines also at immature stage, supported the nature of dendritic filopodia [54]. Future studies will be required to determine whether an overexpression of drebrin contributes to the formation of these aberrant filopodia protrusions. These data are consistent with previous studies on AS animal models. For example, Furusawa [25] demonstrated in Drosophila class IV dendrite arborization sensory (C4da) neurons that Ube3a actively promoted presynaptic elimination, whereas Ube3a inhibition results in defective dendrite pruning. Similarly, in a study on the primary visual cortex of Ube3a m−/p+ mice, a longitudinal in vivo two-photon microscopy analysis revealed an increased proportion of thin spines versus other more stable types when compared with WT mice [46]. Moreover, several studies have reported reduced dendritic arborization in murine AS models [46, 55, 56]. Taken together, our results suggest that the loss of UBE3A promotes an early and aberrant accumulation of thin filopodia, which may subsequently interfere with proper dendritic branching and synapse formation. These findings support a role for UBE3A gene in dendritic growth and synapse remodeling processes, and may partially explain the neurodevelopment impairment.

Neuronal cells are generally characterized by limited mobility cells in vitro, and our i-neurons confirmed this tendency. We found that a progressive decrease in both soma and neurite motility with increasing differentiation time in both CTRL and AS populations. Our results confirm a general neuronal behaviour for i-neurons in vitro, and in fact, as neurons adhere to the substrate and establish connections, their migration tends to slow down. This reduction can be attributed to several factors (substrate properties, adhesion coating, and intrinsic programs).

At the level of soma, the migration abilities of i-neurons are comparable between control and AS neurons. In contrast, AS neurites exhibited reduced net displacement from their initial position, thus suggesting that they, although moving, may change direction more frequently, move less efficiently, or oscillate without making effective forward progress. These data suggest that AS neurites are less mobile with respect to the CTRL ones, and so may be less capable of exploring their environment, reaching appropriate targets and establishing connections. These results are consistent with previous findings in UBE3A-silenced SH-SY5Y cells, which displayed altered focal adhesion dynamics, delayed adhesion, and defective contact guidance in response to directional topographical stimuli, despite an overall preserved migration activity [42].

A previous study reported severe impairments in intrinsic electrical properties, synaptic transmission, and synaptic plasticity in iPSC-derived neurons established from AS patients with complete UBE3A deletion [21].

Here, we confirm these findings in neurons derived from patients carrying the same genetic deletion and extend the functional characterization to neurons harboring a truncating UBE3A variant. In this distinct genetic background, neurons exhibit only mild functional defects, highlighting the possibility of a genotype–phenotype variability in electrophysiological phenotype. Our data indicate that in neurons, while the UBE3A deletion cause both structural and functional deficits, probably due to a brake in the neuronal development and maturation, the UBE3A N-ter truncation primarily affect neuronal morphology while largely preserving glutamatergic transmission. Of note, in the present study we focused on voltage-clamp analyses and did not include investigation on passive properties already described in previous studies. Based on synaptic dysfunction observed, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of D-JNKI1, a molecule previously shown to be effective in animal models [29].

In this study, we observed that in cortical i-neurons derived from deletion patients exhibited the activation of the JNK3 pathway, as demonstrated by increased pJNK/JNK ratios. Importantly, treatment with the JNK3 inhibitor D-JNKI1 restored JNK pathway activity to near-control levels. Moreover, the aberrant morphology, enriched with thin filopodia-like protusions, reverted with D-JNKI1 supplementation. Furthermore, functional experiments conducted in the presence of the inhibitor revealed the selective ability of the drug to rescue molecular alterations responsible for the reduced sEPSCs frequency detected. This effect may result from increased release of glutamatergic vesicles, a greater number of newly-formed excitatory presynaptic terminals, or enhanced survival of the existing excitatory presynaptic terminals. However, the lack of recovery in sEPSC amplitude indicates D-JNKI1 has limited effects on AMPA receptor trafficking or synaptic insertion. Together, these observations indicate that distinct pre- and post-synaptic compartments contribute to specific morphological and functional alterations in AS neurons.

Using patient-derived neuronal models, our study contributes to the elucidation of how the lack of UBE3A leads to aberrant neuronal maturation at a precocious time of neuron development, and highlights migration and functional deficits. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that a limitation of the present work is the lack of isogenic, CRISPR-corrected control lines to complement our patient-derived cellular panel. Although our study successfully models the distinct molecular classes of AS using a broad patient repository, subtle variations in individual genetic backgrounds could potentially influence or mask specific phenotypic traits. Future efforts will benefit from utilizing these established lines to engineer precise isogenic pairs via CRISPR/Cas9 technologies. This will include the correction of patient clones carrying UBE3A missense and terminating mutations, as well as the introduction of targeted chromosomal microdeletions into healthy control iPSC backgrounds to faithfully reproduce the classical structural BP1–BP3 and BP2–BP3 variants. While engineering such large deletions remains technically challenging, similar ambitious genome editing strategies have been successfully applied to create isogenic controls for Dup15q patient lines [57], significantly maximizing the experimental utility, specificity, and robustness of our human AS modeling platform. In parallel to addressing these genetic background variables, we investigated the deregulation of the JNK pathway in AS and the efficacy of D-JNKI1, one of the small molecules functioning as JNK3 inhibitors capable of preventing synaptic degeneration or dysfunction.

These data may pave the way for new therapeutic possibilities [58].

Supplementary Material 2. Supplementary Figure 3. Confirmation of the genetic defect in iPSCs from patients with UPD, ID and UBE3A deletions. A) SNP array analysis of peripheral blood and iPSC clone 1 from a patient with uniparental disomy (UPD), using a healthy control as reference. The UPD is confirmed by a Log R Ratio centered around zero and a complete loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in the chromosomal region 15q11.2, demonstrating the maintenance of the genetic defect in the reprogrammed cells. B) Representative MS-MLPA analysis comparing peripheral blood and an iPSC clone from a patient with UPD, confirming the methylation defect. The black circle highlights the typical hypomethylation pattern in presence of normal copy number observed in UPD patients. C) SNP array analysis of peripheral blood and a corresponding iPSC clone from an Imprinting Defect (ID) patient showing a pattern similar to that of a healthy control, indicating no detectable copy number or allelic imbalance. D) MS-MLPA analysis performed on both peripheral blood and the same iPSC clone to verify the presence of SNRPN hypomethylation. The black circle highlights the hypomethylation pattern, confirming the preservation of the epigenetic defect in the iPSCs. The absence of CNVs as well as UPD confirmed the diagnosis of ID. E) Representative methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS-MLPA) analysis comparing peripheral blood and iPSCs from a patient with a BP1-BP3 deletion (DEL1), revealed by CNVs and hypomethylation in each sample.

Supplementary Material 5. Supplementary Figure 6. Evaluation of Ube3A protein expression across different Angelman Syndrome genotypes. (A) Representative western blot analysis of Ube3A protein levels in neurons differentiated for 100 days. Protein extracts were obtained from a healthy control line (CTRL4) and Angelman Syndrome (AS) patient-derived lines: C-terminal mutation (UBE3A C-TER) associated with a mild phenotype, imprinting defect (ID), N-terminal mutation (UBE3A N-TER) associated with a severe phenotype, and paternal uniparental disomy (UPD). GAPDH was used as an internal loading control to ensure equal protein loading. (B) Densitometric quantification of Ube3A protein levels. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH. Data are expressed as relative protein expression (fold change) compared to the control group, which was set to 1.0. Bar graphs represent the mean ± SEM (or SD) of three independent biological replicates (n = 3). All AS-derived neurons show a marked reduction in Ube3A levels, with the lowest residual expression observed in the N-TER mutant line. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post-hoc test; *p < 0.05,*p < 0.01.

Supplementary Material 7. Supplementary Figure 8. Morphological impairment and rescue in the single clones. Box Plot on the left illustrate the number of neurite protrusions quantified in three independent control samples (A), three patients with UBE3A gene deletions (B)—including two distinct clones from patient DEL1—and two clones from a patient carrying an N-terminal UBE3A mutation (C). Graphs on the right show protrusion counts before and after a 7-day treatment with DJNKI1 (5 µM), aimed at evaluating the potential rescue of the morphological defect. All analyses were independently conducted by three operators. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism; the statistical tests and corresponding p-values are indicated in the graphs.
