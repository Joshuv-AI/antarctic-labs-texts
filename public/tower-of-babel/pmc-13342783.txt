Divergent Paths: A Comprehensive Review of Mechanisms and Clinical Implications in Atopic Dermatitis and Psoriasis
Authors: Tali Czarnowicki, Kazunari Sugita, Zenas Yiu, Sandra Díez‐Ribas, Irene Garcia‐Jimenez, Emma Guttman‐Yassky, Luis F. Santamaria‐Babí
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAtopic dermatitis and psoriasis are two common cutaneous chronic inflammatory diseases. Although they share similar immunological mechanisms, their clinical implications in the patient are different. This review compares both diseases from different points of view such as genetics, natural history, triggers of disease, and skin barrier defects among other disease aspects. The lessons learned by translational research using targeted therapies have also contributed to clarifying differences.

Introduction

Understanding the mechanisms underlying atopic dermatitis (AD) and plaque psoriasis, as well as their clinical implications, has been greatly advanced by translational research. Patient‐derived data, primarily from studies using targeted biological therapies, show that memory T cells play a crucial role in both diseases, albeit along divergent immunological pathways. Although AD and psoriasis share some basic immunological mechanisms, it is rare for patients to develop both conditions simultaneously. The most effective therapies developed thus far have focused on blocking cytokine‐driven inflammation, either by neutralizing cytokines directly, inhibiting their receptors, or targeting intracellular pathways using small molecules.

From a clinical standpoint, both AD and plaque psoriasis are chronic inflammatory skin disorders that can present erythematous and scaly lesions. This clinical overlap may lead to diagnostic challenges, especially in certain populations such as children, individuals with skin of color, or when lesions occur in less typical locations including the hands, face, or genitals. However, the two diseases differ in important ways. In adults, AD typically presents with flexural distribution, intense pruritus, and a history of atopy, while psoriasis often involves extensor surfaces, well‐demarcated plaques, and a thicker scale. Highlighting both similarities and differences between AD and psoriasis is valuable not only for clinical practice but also for understanding the distinct immune pathways that underlie these conditions and inform the development of targeted therapies.

The similarities and differences between AD and psoriasis can be best studied in patients with moderate‐to‐severe disease, as they are treated with targeted therapies that allow for a precise understanding of the key clinically validated immunological mechanisms in each condition.

The goal of this review is to provide a translational comparison, based on insights derived from patient outcomes following targeted treatment approaches.

Genetics

AD and psoriasis are characterized by distinct genetic predispositions. In AD, genes such as SPINK5, FcepsilonRI‐beta, and PHF11 are implicated in epithelial immunity and skin barrier function [1]. Polymorphisms in the filaggrin gene (FLG) are key determinants of skin barrier integrity and susceptibility to AD [2]. Variants in the IL12B gene may also influence the Th1/Th2 immune balance [3]. Additionally, chromatin interaction studies have identified additional candidate genes, including AFG1L, CLINT1, ADO, LINC00302, and RP1‐140 J1.1, as potential contributors to AD pathogenesis [4].

Genetic predisposition to psoriasis is associated with several loci, notably HLA‐C, SLC9A3R1, NAT9, RAPTOR, and SLC12A8, with HLA‐C showing the strongest link to disease susceptibility [1]. In addition, genes such as CXCL8, STAT1, and MMP9 are involved in the inflammatory and immune response pathways contributing to psoriasis pathogenesis [5].

Certain loci, such as PRKRA and ANXA6/TNIP1, harbor pleiotropic alleles that exert opposing effects on psoriasis and AD risk [6]. Similar to AD, polymorphism in the IL12B gene have also been associated with psoriasis, highlighting its role in immune response modulation [3]. Additionally, both diseases share susceptibility loci on chromosomes 1q21, 3q21, 17q25, and 20p12, suggesting common genetic mechanisms influencing skin inflammation and immunity [1, 7].

Natural History

AD typically manifests in early childhood and may persist into adulthood [8]. The progression of AD to other atopic disorders, such as asthma, allergic rhinitis, and food allergy is referred to as the “atopic march” [9, 10]. About 76% of children outgrow the disease, whereas persistence is associated with heritability, allergic sensitization, and comorbid asthma [11].

Guttate psoriasis is a distinct clinical variant of psoriasis characterized by small, droplet‐like lesions, often triggered by streptococcal infections [12]. However, the evidence regarding the effects of antistreptococcal interventions on guttate and chronic plaque psoriasis remains uncertain [13]. While guttate psoriasis frequently resolves spontaneously, approximately 25% to 39% of cases progress to chronic plaque psoriasis [14]. Genetic predisposition, such as the presence of the HLA‐Cw*0602 allele, significantly increases the likelihood of disease progression [15, 16]. Environmental factors, including infections and stress, also contribute to disease flares and chronicity [17]. Longitudinal studies emphasize the importance of early intervention to prevent chronic disease and address the molecular and cellular changes underlying the inflammatory disease memory in psoriasis [18]. Advances in immunomodulatory therapies have provided effective options for managing both guttate and chronic plaque psoriasis [19].

Skin Barrier, Epithelial Barrier Hypothesis

The pathogenesis of AD and psoriasis has long been a subject of debate, centered on whether immune dysregulation or epidermal barrier impairment serves as the primary driver of these diseases, reflecting the ongoing question of whether they are fundamentally immune‐mediated or keratinocyte‐driven conditions [20].

The epidermal barrier, particularly the stratum corneum (SC), is critical for maintaining skin integrity. It is composed of corneocytes within a lipid‐rich matrix, with corneodesmosomes providing mechanical cohesion. Proteins such as corneodesmosin, kallikrein‐related peptidases, and LEKTI regulate desquamation, and their disruption has been linked to AD. Lipids in the SC are essential for hydration, antimicrobial defense, and barrier cohesion [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Alterations in lipid composition play a key role in the pathogenic barrier observed in AD [26].

Tight junctions (TJs), another crucial barrier component, are located beneath the SC and maintain keratinocyte adhesion. AD patients exhibit TJ deficiencies, which are downregulated by Th2 cytokines and associated with FLG mutations [27]. Filaggrin, a key stratum corneum protein encoded by the epidermal differentiation complex, maintains hydration, pH balance, and microbial defense. Loss‐of‐function FLG mutations disrupt these functions, promoting allergen penetration, Th2 activation, and infection risk [24, 28, 29].

While psoriasis was traditionally considered a T‐cell‐driven disease, recent findings reveal that barrier dysfunction such as alterations in lipid and ceramide synthesis plays a substantial role in its pathogenesis [30, 31]. Evidence suggests an association between psoriasis and mutations in the epidermal differentiation complex (EDC), critical for epidermal development, maturation, and cornification, identified as psoriasis susceptibility locus 4 [30]. Studies showed that epidermal alterations are sufficient to drive the onset of both cutaneous lesions and psoriatic arthritis in psoriasis [32]. Epidermal barrier disruption initiates compensatory epidermal hyperplasia, potentially triggering psoriasis.

Psoriasis disrupts intercellular connections, keratinocyte differentiation, and lipid homeostasis, all of which impair the skin barrier. TJ proteins are mislocalized and reduced in psoriatic lesions, leading to water loss and poor hydration. Adherent junctions also weaken, while corneodesmosin remains intact in the SC instead of degrading normally. Additionally, connexin 26 is upregulated, driving inflammation and linking to genetic susceptibility [33, 34, 35].

Aberrant keratinocyte differentiation is a hallmark of psoriasis, with overexpression of keratins 6, 16, and 17 in plaques. Therapies targeting keratin 17 signaling have shown promising results in psoriasis [36, 37]. Lipid abnormalities in the SC correlate with psoriasis severity. These changes promote water loss, inflammation, and IL‐17‐driven psoriasis‐like features in mouse models [38, 39].

Psoriasis is associated with deletions in LCE3B/3C genes within the EDC, impairing barrier repair and terminal differentiation. Reduced loricrin, filaggrin, and keratin 1 expression leads to barrier defects and elevated cytokine production in psoriatic plaques [40, 41]. PSORS4, a susceptibility locus within the EDC, highlights the genetic contribution to barrier dysfunction [42, 43].

A few methods were used to measure the competency of the epidermal barrier, including transepidermal water loss (TEWL), stratum corneum hydration (SCH), temperature, pH, erythema, and elasticity, among others [33, 44]. In a study evaluating TEWL in psoriasis, it was shown that controls had lower TEWL and higher SCH than psoriatic patients, supporting epidermal barrier dysfunction in psoriasis. Following phototherapy, TEWL and SCH both improved in psoriasis patients [45]. When comparing TEWL and SCH values in non‐lesional and lesional skin of psoriasis and AD, both diseases exhibit increased TEWL, erythema, and temperature, as well as decreased SCH in lesional skin relative to uninvolved skin and controls, correlating with disease severity. Notably, TEWL and temperature are higher in AD compared to psoriasis across both lesional and non‐lesional skin [33].

In AD, therapies aimed at restoring lipid balance, such as ceramide‐dominant moisturizers and LXR agonists, have shown promise in improving barrier integrity and reducing inflammation [46]. It has been recently shown that treatment with tapinarof, an AhR agonist, led to an increase in SCH and a reduction in TEWL in AD patients [47].

In psoriasis, research on the impact of emollients on epidermal barrier function is limited and shows mixed results. One study showed that, as opposed to a water‐based formula, Vaseline jelly reduced TEWL in psoriatic plaques, likely due to its occlusive properties [48]. Another study found no effect of 4 week moisturization on TEWL values in psoriasis patients [49].

Advances in understanding disease mechanisms have demonstrated that targeted therapies that address immune dysregulation also contribute to the restoration of the epidermal barrier in both AD and psoriasis. Rousel et al. [50] investigated the effects of the anti‐IL23 guselkumab therapy on skin barrier function and ceramide profiles in psoriasis patients in a randomized, placebo‐controlled trial. Guselkumab significantly reduced clinical disease severity and improved barrier function in lesional skin.

In AD, treatment with the IL‐4 receptor alpha (IL4Rα) antagonist dupilumab demonstrated greater improvements in reducing TEWL and temperature, while increasing SCH in both lesional and non‐lesional skin, compared to topical corticosteroids and cyclosporine [51]. Further studies have corroborated these findings, demonstrating that dupilumab reduces TEWL and enhances lipid composition, as well as FLG content in AD lesions [52, 53]. Similar data is now accumulating on blockade of IL‐13 [54, 55].

The skin microbiome plays an important role in maintaining barrier integrity and regulating immune responses. In healthy skin, microbial metabolites support homeostasis, but this balance is often disrupted in inflammatory skin diseases such as AD and psoriasis. In AD, microbial diversity is reduced, with frequent overgrowth of Staphylococcus aureus ( S. aureus ), which aggravates inflammation and barrier dysfunction. Psoriasis is associated with shifts in microbial composition, potentially influencing IL‐17‐driven pathways [56, 57, 58, 59].

Both diseases illustrate how genetic predispositions, environmental exposures, and immune dysregulation compromise epithelial barriers. Additionally, the observation that targeting immune dysregulation in these diseases also enhances epidermal barrier features underscores the interconnected nature of immune dysfunction and barrier compromise, highlighting their mutual interplay and crosstalk in the pathogenesis of AD and psoriasis.

Role of the Innate Immune Response Based on Translational Knowledge Generated by Biologics

The innate immune response plays a different role in the pathogenesis of both psoriasis and AD, with recent insights from biological therapies enhancing our understanding [60]. In psoriasis, innate immune cells such as dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, and keratinocytes initiate inflammation by releasing cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐17, and IL‐23 [61]. These cytokines drive Th17 cell responses, leading to keratinocyte hyperproliferation and the formation of chronic plaques. Biological therapies targeting these cytokines, such as guselkumab, secukinumab, and bimekizumab, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing disease severity by disrupting this inflammatory cascade [62]. In addition, biologics reduce the recruitment of neutrophils and DCs and downregulate pro‐inflammatory cytokines, leading to clinical improvement [63].

In AD, epithelial cells release cytokines such as IL‐25, IL‐33, and TSLP, but the lack of activity of targeted therapies against them has shown that their relevance in AD is still poorly known [64], although dupilumab has been shown to affect circulating ILC2 phenotype [65]. These findings underscore the divergent critical role of innate immunity in driving chronic inflammation in psoriasis and AD.

Adaptive Immune Response

Psoriasis and AD are driven by distinct adaptive immune profiles, which underlie their clinical manifestations. In psoriasis, the adaptive immune response is dominated by Th1 and Th17 cells. Key cytokines such as IL‐17, IL‐23, and IFN‐γ stimulate keratinocyte proliferation, resulting in the formation of scaly plaques [66]. IL‐17 producing T cells and clonally expanded memory T cells within psoriatic lesions contribute to disease chronicity and recurrence, even after remission, highlighting the role of inflammatory memory in psoriasis [66, 67]. As it has been shown by the efficacy of therapeutic blocking antibodies, the IL‐23/Th17 axis is central in psoriasis pathogenesis [68].

In contrast, AD is characterized by Th2 and Th22 predominance, with IL‐13, IL‐4, IL‐31 and IL‐22 being the primary drivers of inflammation [69, 70]. Th2 cytokines are involved in pruritus, alteration of skin barrier, facilitation of S. aureus colonization and recruitment of inflammatory cells [71]. Single‐cell analyses reveal that T‐cell clusters in AD are predominantly Th2‐oriented, in contrast to the Th17‐rich environment of psoriasis [67].

Targeted therapeutic approaches confirm the relevance of these immunological differences. In psoriasis, treatments such as IL‐17 and IL‐23 inhibitors provide significant relief for patients [72]. In AD, therapies like dupilumab, an IL‐4Rα blocking antibody that inhibits IL‐4 and IL‐13 signaling, restore skin barrier integrity and reduce systemic Th2 inflammation, improving disease outcomes [52]. Lebrikizumab and tralokinumab, both directed against IL‐13 distinct epitopes, also improve key pathological mechanisms of AD [73]. In addition, neutralizing IL‐31RA has also demonstrated the relevance of IL‐31 in AD [74]. While both diseases involve T‐cell infiltration, their specificities differ. In AD, T cells are predominantly skin‐homing and partially allergen‐specific, whereas in psoriasis T‐cell clones include non‐skin‐homing cells, reflecting systemic antigen specificity [75]. Interestingly, cutaneous lymphocyte‐associated antigen (CLA)+ T cells present in the tonsils of patients with streptococcal‐induced psoriasis showed identical clonal TCRVβ rearrangements to those found in lesional skin. This finding supports the presence of shared T cell clones in both skin and tonsillar tissue, providing a mechanistic link between psoriatic inflammation and streptococcal throat infection [76].

Skin Microbiota

The skin microbiota differs between AD and psoriasis. In AD, S. aureus predominates defining an endotype with increased TEWL and elevated type 2 biomarkers, including eosinophil count, total IgE, CCL17, and periostin. Additionally, AD S. aureus + patients had significantly greater allergen sensitization [77]. The type 2 inflammatory environment promotes S. aureus adherence, and its superantigens are key drivers of Th2 cytokine production, particularly IL‐13 secretion by CLA+ T cells [78], a subset of memory T cells that preferentially express TCRVβ chains recognized by S. aureus superantigens.

In psoriasis, there is a more diverse microbial community with weaker associations to specific disease‐related gene expression patterns. This diversity includes various microorganisms that interact with the host's immune system [79]. The role of the skin microbiome in psoriasis is still being explored; however, streptococcal throat infections are known to trigger and exacerbate both guttate and plaque psoriasis. Tonsillectomy has been considered a potential treatment for psoriasis, particularly for patients who frequently experience sore throat‐associated exacerbations [80]. The underlying mechanism involves T cells that recognize shared determinants between streptococcal M‐protein and keratin in the skin. This molecular mimicry may lead to an autoimmune response that exacerbates psoriasis. CLA+ T cells are activated by Streptococcus pyogenes ( S. pyogenes ), leading to the production of key mediators such as IL‐17, which are involved in the formation of psoriatic lesions [12, 81, 82]. The presence of CLA+ T cells in the blood and tonsils of psoriasis patients suggests a dysregulated immune response, potentially promoting the migration of effector T cells from the tonsils to the skin [76]. Additionally, the presence of specific immunoglobulins, such as IgA, against S. pyogenes and Candida albicans , is associated with IL‐17 responses, suggesting a role for these antibodies in psoriasis pathogenesis [83, 84].

Finally, chronic plaque psoriasis is associated with elevated and heterogeneous levels of IgA and IgA1 antibodies against S. pyogenes in patients with normal anti‐streptolysin O titers, suggesting that persistent tonsillar exposure to S. pyogenes may promote the migration of CLA+ IL‐17A‐producing T cells to the skin [83, 85].

Neuroimmunology

Relevant neuroimmunology mechanisms are involved in both psoriasis and AD. Nociceptive neurons influence skin inflammation through their interactions with immune cells and the release of neuropeptides [86, 87]. In psoriasis, nociceptive neurons expressing TRPV1 and Nav1.8 ion channels interact with dermal dendritic cells, triggering IL‐23 production and activating IL‐17‐producing cells, ultimately driving psoriasis‐like inflammation [88]. Activation of nociceptive neurons triggers the release of neuropeptides like calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) and substance P (SP), promoting vasodilation, edema, and inflammation—a process known as neurogenic inflammation, central to the skin's immune response.

In AD, nociceptive neurons play a crucial role in mediating itch and driving disease pathogenesis. Memory T cells release Th2 cytokines that directly activate sensory neurons, highlighting neuroimmune interactions that exacerbate chronic itch and drive the itch‐scratch cycle in AD [89]. A common feature of targeted therapies against those cytokines is the fast improvement of itch [90]. Sensory neurons also modulate immune responses through neuropeptides that activate mast cells, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates inflammation and itch. Importantly, the skin microbiome might influence these neuroimmune interactions in both AD and psoriasis. Microbial products can activate sensory neurons directly or indirectly via immune cells [91].

Endotypes

Research shows that inflammatory skin diseases exist on a spectrum of diverse phenotypes and endotypes [92, 93]. Plaque psoriasis is characterized by chronic Th17‐driven inflammation with key roles for IL‐17 and IL‐23 [94], while AD is largely driven by Th2‐dominated cytokine activity [29]. Both conditions exhibit activation of Th22 and Th1 pathways, with IL‐22 and IFN‐γ playing supportive roles in epidermal remodeling and inflammation [95].

AD phenotypes vary based on acute vs. chronic stages, IgE levels (intrinsic vs. extrinsic), and demographic factors such as age and ethnicity. Asian and pediatric AD demonstrate a blended Th17/Th22 immune activation and overlap with psoriasis‐like features and histopathology, including parakeratosis and neutrophilic infiltration. African American AD shows dominant Th22 activation and distinct barrier defects, while elderly AD tends to shift towards Th1/Th17 activation with less pronounced barrier dysfunction [93, 96, 97, 98, 99]. These overlapping phenotypes suggest a spectrum where Th2, Th17, and Th22 pathways contribute variably to disease expression. This complexity contrasts with psoriasis, which is predominantly a Th17‐driven disease responsive to single‐axis cytokine blockade [95]. Nevertheless, while different immune axes activation was described in various AD phenotypes, further targeted research is required to delineate the specific contributions of individual cytokine pathways to the disease's pathogenesis.

Detailed molecular profiles for different psoriasis phenotypes are sparse. In a study comparing skin biopsies from patients with chronic plaque and guttate psoriasis with biopsies of acute contact eczema and healthy controls, results revealed differential protein expression among the groups, with hierarchical cluster analysis revealing that lesional guttate psoriasis clustered more closely with contact eczema than with chronic plaque psoriasis. These results emphasize the heterogeneity within psoriasis and suggest distinct molecular pathways underlying its phenotypes [100].

Studying Asian psoriasis phenotype, Kim et al. [101] reported that Asian small plaque psoriasis showed limited spreading but high expression of IL‐17A and related pro‐inflammatory cytokines. In contrast, Western large plaque psoriasis had lower IL‐17A expression but a marked increase in T cells and dendritic cells across the psoriatic skin.

In a study that looked at the immune mechanisms and genomic features of scalp psoriasis, Ruano et al. [102] found that scalp psoriasis exhibits a Th1/Th17‐dominant cytokine environment and increased keratinocyte hyperproliferation. The transcriptomic profile of scalp psoriasis showed significant overlap with skin psoriasis, including activation of IL‐17, TNF‐α, and IL‐22 pathways, but also unique features such as heightened CD4+ T‐cell and Treg enrichment.

A recent study [103] investigated the immune characteristics and molecular endotypes of patients with AD, plaque psoriasis, and an overlap phenotype exhibiting features of both conditions. The results revealed that the overlap phenotype exhibited molecular features predominantly consistent with psoriasis rather than AD, including elevated Th17/IL‐23 cytokine expression and associated blood biomarkers. These findings suggest that patients with overlap features may benefit more from psoriasis biologics targeting the IL‐23/Th17 axis rather than AD biologics targeting Th2 pathways.

This evolving landscape underscores the need for endotype‐driven precision medicine in AD and psoriasis management, particularly as serum proteomic and lesional RNA‐seq approaches have not consistently provided the expected predictive capacity for Th2‐targeted therapies [104, 105].

CLA + T‐Cell Response in AD and Psoriasis

CLA+ memory T cells, characterized by their skin‐specific tropism and recirculation between blood and skin, reflect immune abnormalities in psoriasis and AD. The recirculation of CLA+ memory T cells between the skin and blood was demonstrated by the effects of efalizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD11a subunit of the LFA‐1 molecule, which blocks lymphocyte extravasation into the skin. In patients with psoriasis and AD, efalizumab showed clinical improvement but also induced lymphocytosis of CLA+ T cells, leading to disease relapses after treatment discontinuation [106, 107].

CLA+ T cells play a critical role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis by driving IL‐17‐mediated inflammation. S. pyogenes , is a key trigger of psoriasis, inducing IL‐17 responses specifically in CLA+ T cells, even in patients with negative anti‐streptolysin O titers [80, 83]. Cytokines such as IL‐15 and IL‐23 synergize with CLA+ T cells to amplify IL‐17A and IL‐17F production, further sustaining inflammation. CLA+ T cells also produce IL‐9 in response to S. pyogenes , enhancing IL‐17A secretion [82]. Therapeutically, reductions in circulating CLA+ T cells have been associated with clinical improvements following anti‐TNF‐alpha therapy and phototherapy [108, 109]. These findings underscore the central role of CLA+ T cells in psoriasis pathogenesis and their potential as biomarkers for disease monitoring.

Blood phenotyping of patients with moderate–severe AD demonstrated marked expansion of Th2/Tc2 and Th22/Tc22 subsets within CLA+ T cells, correlating with elevated IgE levels and disease severity. These findings reflect the Th2/Th22 polarization observed in lesional skin, with CLA+ T cells showing higher production of IL‐13 and IL‐22, while IFN‐γ levels were significantly reduced. No significant increase in CLA+ Th17/Tc17 or Th9/Tc9 frequencies was observed in AD [110]. Another study analyzed immune changes in moderate–to–severe AD across infants (0–5 years), children (6–11 years), adolescents (12–17 years), and adults (≥ 18 years), compared to healthy controls. Key findings included decreased Th1/Th2 ratios across all ages and expanded skin‐homing CLA+ memory T cells, particularly Th2 and Th22 subsets, which correlated with disease severity and IgE levels [111].

In a study comparing T‐cell activation among AD and psoriasis patients, AD showed significantly greater systemic activation of central and effector memory T‐cell subsets compared to psoriasis and controls. Skin‐homing CLA+ T cells were notably more activated than their systemic CLA− counterparts in both AD and psoriasis [112].

A recent study [113] showed that house dust mite (HDM) and staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB), two common AD triggers, induced IL‐9 production in skin‐homing/CLA+ and systemic/CLA− memory T cells. The findings highlight IL‐9 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target, particularly in HDM‐sensitized AD patients.

Dupilumab was shown to significantly decrease the percentage of Th2 and Th22 cytokine‐producing skin‐homing CLA+ CD4+ T cells at week 4 [114], further emphasizing the role of CLA+ T cells in disease pathogenesis and their potential utility as biomarkers for therapeutic response [115].

Epigenetic mechanisms present in CLA+ T cells highlight the distinct immunopathology of AD and psoriasis. In AD, reduced DNA methylation at the IL‐13 gene in CD4+CLA+ T cells correlates with increased IL‐13 mRNA expression [116]. In psoriasis, Th17 polarization of CLA+ skin‐homing T cells is sustained through activation‐dependent, AP‐1‐mediated chromatin remodeling [117].

Tissue‐Resident Memory T Cells

Tissue‐resident memory (TRM) T cells are a subset of memory T cells that reside in peripheral tissues, such as the skin [118]. They are characterized by markers like CD69 and CD103, which anchor them within these tissues [119, 120]. TRM cells and recirculating memory T cells have distinct roles in psoriasis and AD. In psoriasis, CD8+ TRM cells, particularly those that produce IL‐17A, play a pivotal role in disease pathogenesis [121]. Current psoriasis treatments impact TRM, but these cells may resist therapy, potentially explaining the recurrence of disease at the same sites [122]. Even in resolved psoriasis lesions, a population of epidermal CD8+ T cells expressing TRM markers persists, capable of producing IL‐17A and IL‐22 long after treatment cessation [123]. Emerging evidence highlights their metabolic adaptations, such as enhanced lipid metabolism, which support their long‐term survival and functionality in the skin [124].

In contrast, AD is characterized by the infiltration of recirculating memory T cells, particularly those expressing CLA [115]. These cells drive the Th2/Th22‐skewed inflammation that dominates AD [125]. Recirculating T cells migrate between the blood and the skin, perpetuating chronic inflammation by replenishing the local immune cell pool [126]. Recent translational research underscores the importance of circulating CLA+ T cells in AD, linking their skin‐homing capabilities to Th2 axis dysregulation and identifying CCL17 as a key biomarker that binds to CCR4, expressed by CD4+CLA+ memory T cells [115, 127].

While TRM cells establish localized immune memory in psoriasis, sustaining site‐specific inflammation and recurrence, recirculating memory T cells in AD facilitate systemic communication and reactivation at distant skin sites. These distinct mechanisms emphasize the significant differences in the contributions of memory T cells to psoriasis and AD. Circulating reactivated TRM cells capable of migrating to inflamed tissues [128, 129, 130, 131, 132] suggest that skin TRM may not be static, but rather part of a dynamic, recirculating process that remains to be fully elucidated.

Successes and Failures of Selected Biologic Therapies in AD and Psoriasis

The existence of distinct immune endotypes in AD and psoriasis highlights the need for precision treatment approaches that match therapies to underlying disease mechanisms. There are currently several categories of biologics available for psoriasis treatment, including anti‐TNFα agents, IL‐12/IL‐23 inhibitors, IL‐17 inhibitors, and IL‐23‐specific inhibitors. Over 90% of plaque psoriasis patients achieve disease control with IL‐17 or IL‐23 inhibitors, demonstrating the efficacy of targeted approaches in polar immune diseases [133, 134].

Studies have shown that early intervention in guttate psoriasis with anti‐IL‐17 or anti‐IL‐23 therapies can induce disease remission and prevent progression to chronic plaque psoriasis, potentially by affecting the survival of tissue‐resident memory T cells [19, 135, 136].

AD requires a more nuanced therapeutic strategy due to its multi‐axis immune activation. While Th2 skewing is a hallmark of all AD and the common denominator across its different phenotypes, the disease is highly heterogeneous, with distinct subtypes exhibiting varied immune activation profiles that may necessitate broader therapeutic strategies.

With the recent FDA approval of the anti‐IL31 nemolizumab, there are now four FDA approved biologic treatments available for AD, with an extensive pipeline of emerging therapies under development. Dupilumab, an IL‐4Rα antagonist, has shown promise in suppressing Th2‐driven inflammation, achieving significant clinical improvement in ∼60% of patients [137]. Since AD exhibits significant endotype heterogeneity, it may be the case that therapies solely targeting the Th2 axis may be insufficient for certain populations. Consequently, combination treatments addressing multiple immune pathways might be necessary. Innovative bi‐ and tri‐specific antibodies are emerging to meet this therapeutic need. For instance, trispecific biologics targeting IL‐4, IL‐13, and IL‐33, or IL‐4, IL‐13, and TSLP are being developed aiming to provide more comprehensive treatment options for AD patients (NCT05995964).

IL‐17A is not a dominant pathogenic driver in AD, even in endotypes with higher Th17 skewing, but possibly targeting IL‐17 alongside Th2 strategies may be necessary to effectively treat AD in patients with a blended endotype [138]. IL‐22 is present in AD and psoriatic lesions and has emerged as a significant driver of epidermal hyperplasia and barrier dysfunction. Nevertheless, anti‐IL‐22 approaches failed in psoriasis, but in AD, Fezakinumab, an IL‐22 antagonist, has demonstrated some efficacy in patients with high baseline IL‐22 levels [69].

The need for treatments, which simultaneously target both Th2 and Th17 pathways, is further underscored by clinical cases where patients exhibit mixed psoriasis and eczema phenotypes [139], or experience iatrogenic shifts between these phenotypes due to selective inhibition of either the Th2 or Th17 axis [140].

While psoriasis serves as a paradigm for targeting single cytokine pathways, AD exemplifies the challenges of managing diseases with heterogeneous immune drivers. Personalized, multi‐axis therapeutic strategies are essential to achieve optimal outcomes for all patients, with future studies needed to refine these approaches and extend the benefits of targeted therapies across disease spectra.

Mechanistic Evidence From Newer Targeted Therapies Clarifies Divergent Pathways in AD and Psoriasis

The clinical and molecular effects of newer targeted therapies continue to provide strong mechanistic confirmation of the divergent immune circuits sustaining psoriasis AD. In psoriasis, the robust responses observed with IL‐23 and IL‐17 inhibitors demonstrate that this axis is both necessary and sufficient to maintain keratinocyte hyperproliferation, neutrophil recruitment, and the characteristic psoriatic transcriptome. IL‐23p19 blockade suppresses the expansion and survival of Th17 cells, leading to rapid normalization of IL‐17A/F, IL‐22, and downstream epidermal remodeling genes, while IL‐17 inhibition induces early transcriptomic resolution [62, 66, 68]. These findings reinforce psoriasis as a highly polarized, single‐axis immune disease. Furthermore, the ability of IL‐17/23 inhibitors to influence tissue‐resident memory T‐cell survival provides additional mechanistic support for their role in modifying disease persistence and recurrence [19, 66, 122].

In contrast, therapeutic responses observed in AD reveal a multi‐layered and heterogeneous immune architecture in which Th2 cytokines are central but not solely responsible for disease expression. IL‐4Rα blockade with dupilumab and selective IL‐13 inhibition with tralokinumab or lebrikizumab consistently normalize barrier‐associated genes such as filaggrin, loricrin, and tight‐junction proteins, improve lipid composition, and reduce S. aureus colonization, underscoring the key role of Th2 signaling in barrier impairment and microbial susceptibility [51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 141]. However, the fact that a substantial fraction of patients do not achieve complete remission with IL‐4/IL‐13 inhibition highlights the contribution of additional cytokine and neural pathways. IL‐31RA inhibition offers direct mechanistic insight into the neuroimmune component of AD, producing rapid reductions in pruritus that precede structural skin improvement and illustrating a pathway uniquely relevant to AD but not psoriasis [74]. Moreover, IL‐22 blockade, ineffective in psoriasis, has shown benefit in IL‐22‐high AD endotypes [69].

The broad therapeutic efficacy of JAK inhibitors provides additional mechanistic evidence for the multi‐axis nature of AD. These agents suppress signaling downstream of numerous cytokines implicated in AD, including IL‐4, IL‐13, IL‐22, IL‐31, TSLP, and IFN‐γ, highlighting the interdependence of epithelial alarmins, Th2/Th22 pathways, and neuronal circuits [64, 142].

Although JAK inhibitors have a defined therapeutic role in AD, their impact in psoriasis highlights important mechanistic differences between the two diseases. Psoriasis demonstrates measurable but modest improvement with selective JAK inhibition, reflecting the fact that JAK‐dependent cytokines (e.g., IFN‐γ, IL‐22) contribute to psoriatic inflammation but do not represent the core disease‐driving loop [143, 144]. IL‐23 and IL‐17 signals, critical in psoriasis, signal primarily through STAT3 and ACT1 pathways and are largely JAK‐independent, explaining why JAK inhibitors do not produce the high‐level responses characteristic of IL‐23/IL‐17 blockade [66, 68]. This contrast mechanistically reinforces the dominance of the IL‐23/17 circuit in psoriasis and the broader multi‐cytokine network sustaining AD.

Emerging data on the OX40/OX40L axis provides additional mechanistic differentiation between the diseases. OX40 is a co‐stimulatory receptor expressed on activated T cells, with OX40L predominantly expressed by antigen‐presenting cells and keratinocytes. OX40/OX40L signaling preferentially expands Th2 and Th22 memory responses, pathways central to AD pathogenesis [145], promoting chronicity by enhancing survival, proliferation, and tissue homing of pathogenic T cells [64, 142]. Early clinical studies with OX40 or OX40L‐blocking antibodies demonstrate significant reductions in circulating Th2/Th22 memory T cells and improvement in AD skin inflammation, supporting OX40 as a key amplifier of chronic type‐2 inflammation [146, 147]. In psoriasis, OX40 signaling is detectable but appears to play an accessory rather than central role because IL‐23‐driven Th17 differentiation proceeds independently of this co‐stimulatory axis [95]. Accordingly, early OX40‐targeting studies have shown limited efficacy in psoriasis compared with AD, aligning with its better fit within the Th2‐dominant pathophysiology of AD [94, 148].

Together, the mechanistic insights derived from these newer therapies provide strong translational support for the divergent immunopathology of AD and psoriasis. Psoriasis behaves as a highly polarized IL‐23/IL‐17‐driven disorder in which interrupting a single cytokine family is sufficient to collapse the inflammatory cascade (Figure 1). AD, in contrast, reflects the interaction of Th2, Th22, alarmin‐driven, neuroimmune, and costimulatory pathways, including IL‐31 and OX40 (Figure 2), that together shape disease expression and therapeutic response [93, 95].

Although psoriasis and atopic dermatitis share immunological mechanisms, translational research demonstrates that these diseases have divergent paths. In psoriasis, the IL‐23‐IL‐17 axis plays a central role since blockade of IL‐23, IL23 signaling, IL‐17A, IL17F, and IL‐17R improves disease.

On the other hand, atopic dermatitis presents a multi‐axis immune response where IL‐13, IL‐31, and IL‐22 pathways are clinically relevant as it is shown by targeted therapies, together with JAK1 signaling inhibition.

Transcriptomics

Clinically relevant transcriptomic pathways differ substantially between cutaneous lesions of AD and psoriasis. Transcriptomic analyses in clinical trials of AD and psoriasis have provided crucial insights into disease‐specific immune pathways, facilitating the development of more targeted biologic therapies [141, 149]. Transcriptomic analyses highlight a dominant IL‐13 pathway in AD, alongside greater molecular heterogeneity compared to psoriasis [150]. Immune and barrier profiles tape strip profiling have identified distinct immune and barrier signatures in lesional and non‐lesional skin of AD and psoriasis. AD is characterized by a Th2 skewing, while psoriasis shows higher levels of Th1/Th17‐related markers [151]. These findings suggest that despite some shared inflammatory components, the two diseases have unique immune signatures. Single‐Cell RNA sequencing has provided insights into the cellular composition of AD lesions, revealing a unique fibroblast subpopulation and an enrichment of inflammatory dendritic cells and tissue‐resident memory T‐cells [152].

Biomarkers

Psoriasis and AD are usually distinguished from each other clinically. However, there are patients with clinical features that may overlap with either condition, and biomarkers that can separate psoriasis and atopic AD may be useful to guide diagnosis and treatment.

For psoriasis, the most up‐to‐date GWAS and TWAS meta‐analysis, published on a pre‐print website, highlights several biomarker pathways [153]. This includes targets for therapies, such as IL23A, IL12B, TNFAIP3, IL‐17RA, AHR, and TYK2, transcriptional regulation of interferon responses such as IRF1 and IRF5, and epidermal proliferation such as ELL. A recent systematic scoping review focused on progression of psoriasis found 22 candidate biomarkers [154]. This includes genetic markers, such as the well reported association with HLA‐C*06:02 and ERAP1, polymorphisms affecting genes involved in the IL‐23 or NF‐ƘB signaling pathway such as IL23A, IL23R, other loci reporting in previous GWAS such as LCE3D. They also reported proteomic markers of cardiovascular inflammation such as anti‐high‐density lipoprotein IgG antibodies and GlycA, as well as other blood markers, such as kallikrein (KLK) 8, tyramine, and mucic acid. Outside of psoriasis severity, other reported biomarkers for psoriasis include KLKs [155], especially overexpression of KLK6 in lesional skin [156].

Similarly, the AD GWAS, most recently published in 2023, has also highlighted several biomarker pathways [157]. This includes well‐known alleles in the skin, such as FLG, immune response‐related alleles such as CAMK4 and TRAF3, previously attempted or current treatment targets such as TNF, IL22, and MAP3K11 as well as new potential targets such as CSF1, CTSS, IL15, and MMP12. As AD is a more heterogeneous condition than psoriasis, there were some specific loci to people of different ethnicities, such as NLRP10 in Japanese and AHI1 in African/Europeans.

A cohort study from Germany [158] looked at the differences in the skin transcriptome between AD patients and healthy controls and found differences in genes involved in skin homeostasis and lipid metabolism. They also found signals that are in keeping with therapeutic targets for AD, including increase in signaling for IL4RA, IL‐31RA, IL13, IL22, and JAK1. The authors describe a dynamic progressive upregulation of immune responses with type 2, Th17, and NK cell signaling which distinguishes between healthy control, AD non‐lesional, and AD lesional skin, showing commonality with psoriasis for an increase in IL‐17 signaling.

Several studies have outlined approaches to differentiating between the two conditions [150, 151]. One particularly non‐invasive method is the use of tape strips which capture the stratum corneum and the upper granular layer [151]. A study group identified common upregulation of markers of epidermal hyperplasia, inflammation, T‐cell proliferation and migration, and JAK/STAT signaling, and downregulation of terminal differentiation, tight junction proteins, and lipid biosynthesis between psoriasis and AD compared with healthy controls [151]. There was common upregulation of Th22‐related proteins, including IL‐22, in both conditions. Distinct immune profiles that separated the two conditions included Th1, particularly in lesional skin, and Th17 skewing for psoriasis, including markers of innate immunity, while AD showed more increase in levels of Th2 related markers, with IL‐31 preferentially expressed only in lesional AD skin.

A recent study has taken the classification of inflammatory skin diseases one step further, and instead of defining conditions by their clinical phenotype, Seremet et al. [159] describe classifying conditions by their gene immune expression module, outlining seven key modules representing the pathways for Th17, Th2, Th1, Type 1 IFN, neutrophilic, macrophagic, and eosinophilic pathways. They included skin biopsies from both psoriasis and AD patients along with other conditions, with some included with more clinically and histologically well‐defined diseases and others less so. Like the published studies, psoriasis showed a skew towards Th17 inflammation and AD to Th2 inflammation. The study team found that when these sets of module genes were used as diagnostic markers, they performed better in clustering for the different inflammatory conditions with strong association between the dominant immune module and the disease diagnosis with high sensitivity and specificity for both psoriasis and AD. Interestingly, using immune modules, they were able to classify 30 erythroderma cases where the clinical classification of disease was unclear, with 10 clustering towards psoriasis and 16 towards AD, with the rest drug eruptions, and confirmed these findings with longitudinal follow‐up of the clinical disease course and other laboratory results.

Why Some Individuals Develop Psoriasis but Not AD and Vice Versa and Disease Prevention

The distinct clinical presentations of AD and psoriasis, despite their shared features of immune dysregulation and epidermal barrier impairment, may arise from a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, immune pathway dominance, and environmental factors. Genetic susceptibility plays a pivotal role in determining disease phenotype. For instance, psoriasis is strongly associated with HLA‐C*06:02, along with polymorphisms in genes such as IL23R, IL12B, and CARD14, which promote Th17/IL‐23‐driven inflammation and keratinocyte hyperproliferation [160, 161]. In contrast, AD is closely linked to loss‐of‐function mutations in FLG, leading to enhanced Th2 immune responses. Polymorphisms in genes regulating Th2 cytokines, such as IL4R and IL13, further amplify the Th2 axis, driving IgE‐mediated sensitization and eosinophil recruitment, hallmarks of AD [162, 163]. The presence of these disease‐specific genetic variants may skew immune responses towards either psoriasis or AD, even in the context of a shared epithelial barrier defect.

Beyond genetic predisposition, the predominance of specific immune pathways likely determines disease outcomes. In psoriasis, the activation of Th17 and Th1 immune responses triggers keratinocyte hyperproliferation, neutrophilic infiltration, and chronic skin inflammation [95]. Conversely, AD is marked by Th2 polarization, with IL‐4, IL‐13, and IL‐31 driving skin barrier disruption, pruritus, and IgE production, while Th22 cytokines like IL‐22 contribute to epidermal hyperplasia [142]. Environmental factors, including microbial colonization, may further interact with these immune pathways to shape disease phenotype. Taken together, the interplay of genetic susceptibility, immune pathway activation, and environmental triggers likely dictates whether an individual develops AD or psoriasis.

Although both AD and psoriasis have strong genetic components, effective strategies for primary prevention remain elusive. In AD, early efforts focused on skin barrier enhancement using emollients in high‐risk infants showed initial promise. However, subsequent large randomized trials failed to demonstrate a consistent preventive effect [164, 165], and there is currently no consensus on evidence‐based primary prevention [166]. More recently, preliminary studies suggest that early intervention with biologics such as dupilumab may modulate the trajectory of the atopic march, although this remains investigational and is not yet an accepted clinical strategy [167]. In contrast, prevention strategies in psoriasis are even less developed. While modifiable risk factors such as streptococcal infection, obesity, and smoking are recognized contributors, no validated preventive interventions exist [168, 169]. Future research may enable immune‐modifying approaches aimed at delaying or preventing disease onset in genetically predisposed individuals.

While precision medicine has already revolutionized psoriasis management through targeted IL‐17 and IL‐23 blockades (Figure 1), emerging precision approaches in AD emphasize the need for more refined, endotype‐driven strategies. Given the greater heterogeneity of immune activation in AD, future therapies will likely require tailored interventions beyond Th2 targeting (Figure 2), addressing specific molecular and clinical endotypes to achieve optimal disease control.

Further integration of single‐cell technologies, spatial transcriptomics, microbiome profiling, and epigenetic studies will refine disease classification beyond traditional clinical phenotypes, enabling a more personalized, mechanism‐based treatment paradigm. Understanding how tissue‐resident and recirculating memory T cells interact with evolving environmental signals, and how neuroimmune pathways modulate chronic inflammation will open new therapeutic opportunities.

Ultimately, continued efforts to unravel the distinct, yet occasionally overlapping, immunobiology of AD and psoriasis will not only improve patient outcomes but may also provide broader insights into the mechanisms that regulate chronic inflammatory diseases across tissues.
