Absorption times for discrete Whittaker processes and non-intersecting Brownian bridges
Neil O’Connell

Abstract
. We present evidence for a conjectural relationship between absorption times for discrete Whittaker processes and maximal heights of non-intersecting Brownian bridges.



1.
Introduction

It is well known (see, for example,
[
1
]
for a survey)
that twice the square of the maximum of a reflected Brownian bridge, starting and ending at zero,
has the same law as the random variable

(1)

S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e_{n}}{n^{2}},

where
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of independent standard exponential random variables, and that twice the square of the
maximum of a standard Brownian excursion has the same law as
S+S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
S
.
In this paper we present evidence for a conjectural generalisation of these identities in law, which relates maximal heights of non-intersecting
reflected Brownian bridges and non-intersecting Brownian excursions to absorption times for the discrete Whittaker processes introduced in
[
14
]
.
The present study of the latter is motivated by an attempt to understand the large scale behaviour of these processes,
in particular the question of whether they belong to the KPZ universality class
[
4
]
, which we now conjecture to be the case
based on this apparent connection. There is an extensive literature on maximal heights of non-intersecting Brownian bridges,
their asymptotics, and connections to 2D Yang-Mills theory on the sphere
[
5
,
6
,
11
,
12
,
16
]
.

Let
\Pi
be the set of nonnegative integer arrays

(\pi_{ij},\ (i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2})

satisfying

\pi_{ij}\geq\pi_{i,j-1}\vee\pi_{i-1,j}

for all
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
with the convention
\pi_{i0}=\pi_{0j}=0
.
We consider a continuous time Markov chain on
\Pi
which evolves
as follows: for each
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
, subtract one from
\pi_{ij}
at rate

(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i,j-1})(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i-1,j}).

A simulation of this process, started at
\pi_{ij}=50
for all
(i,j)
and
run until the first time that
\pi_{50,1}=0
, is shown in its final state in Figure 1.

Figure 1.
Simulation of the Markov chain on
\Pi
, started with
\pi_{ij}=50
for all
(i,j)

and run until the first time that
\pi_{50,1}=0
. The height of this surface over the box with
coordinates
(i,j)
is the value of
\pi_{ij}
at this stopping time, shown here for
1\leq i,j\leq 50
.

We would like to understand the evolution of the zero set

\lambda^{0}=\{(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}:\ \pi_{ij}=0\}.

This corresponds to the flat region on the left of
Figure 1.
To this end, let
T_{r}
be the first time that
\max_{i=1,\ldots,r-1}\pi_{i,r-i}=0
.
Note that the projection of the chain onto the values of
\pi_{ij}
for
1\leq i+j\leq r+1

is autonomously Markov and that
T_{r}
is the absorption time for this projection.

In
[
14
]
it was shown that the above Markov chain on
\Pi
is related to the quantum Toda chain
and has a unique entrance law starting from the array with
\pi_{ij}=+\infty
for all
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
.
In the following we assume the Markov chain is started at
+\infty
and denote its law by
{\mathbb{P}}
.
The Hamiltonian of the
(r+1)
-particle quantum Toda chain is given by

{\mathcal{H}}^{r}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}e^{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}.

The corresponding eigenvalue equation has series solutions known as
fundamental Whittaker functions
[
9
,
10
]
.
In particular, if we consider the series

\phi_{r}(x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}}a_{r}(n)\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{n_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i+1})},

then
{\mathcal{H}}^{r}\Phi_{r}=0
provided the coefficients
a_{r}(n)
satisfy the recursion

(2)

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}\right)a_{r}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{r}(n-e_{i}),

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
are the standard basis vectors in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
and
with the convention
a_{r}(n)=0
for
n\notin{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
.
If we set
a_{r}(0)=1
, then this recursion has a unique solution.
In
[
14
]
it is shown that, for the Markov chain on
\Pi
started from
+\infty
,
the values of
(\pi_{1,r},\pi_{2,r-1},\ldots,\pi_{r,1})
evolve as a Markov chain on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,
which jumps from
n
to
n-e_{i}
at rate
a_{r}(n-e_{i})/a_{r}(n)
, for each
i=1,\ldots,r
.
As
T_{r}
is also the absorption time for this projection, we can therefore study its distribution
using the theory of the quantum Toda chain and its eigenfunctions.

The eigenfunctions we need to consider are for a particular discrete quantisation of the Toda chain
(corresponding to the recursion (
2
)).
We consider families of left eigenfunctions, defined in terms of Mellin transforms of class one
Whittaker functions, and right eigenfunctions, defined in terms of coefficients
of fundamental Whittaker functions. These are discussed in detail in §2.
With these in hand, our approach is to formulate Karlin-McGregor
type expansions for the transition kernel of the chain and hence also the distribution function
{\mathbb{P}}(T_{r}\leq t)
.
This is technically quite difficult however, as the series
in question are highly singular and difficult to justify rigorously. On the other hand,
the eigenfunctions can be factorised for special values of their arguments, leading to explicit
formulas which can be used to provide further evidence for the correctness of the expansions.
For the left eigenfunctions, these factorisations follow from a result of Stade
[
17
]
on Mellin
transforms of class one Whittaker functions and for the right eigenfunctions they follow from
an analogous result for certain coefficients of fundamental Whittaker functions
obtained in the present paper (Theorem
2.3
).

Assuming the expansions are correct, they reveal a remarkable connection with
maximal heights of non-intersecting reflected Brownian bridges and non-intersecting
Brownian excursions, as alluded to above.
From the definition, the corner value
\pi_{11}
evolves as a pure death process
on the nonnegative integers with quadratic rates: it jumps from
n
to
n-1
at rate
n^{2}
.
It follows that
T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}S
where
S
defined by (
1
) and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality
in distribution.
In the case
r=2
, it was shown in
[
14
, Proposition 4.4]
that

T_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}S+S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
S
.
From these representations one can infer, for example, the distribution functions

(3)

{\mathbb{P}}(T_{1}\leq t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{k}e^{-k^{2}t},\qquad{\mathbb{P}}(T_{2}\leq t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(1-2k^{2}t)e^{-k^{2}t}.

The random variables
S
and
S+S^{\prime}
have many interesting properties and interpretations
[
1
]
.
In particular,
S
has the same law as twice the square of the maximum
of a reflected Brownian bridge, starting and ending at zero, and
S+S^{\prime}
has the same law as twice
the square of the maximum of a standard Brownian excursion.

Using our conjectured formulas, we carry out explicit computations for small values of
r
.
For
r=1,2
we recover the formulas (
3
).
For
r=3
, we obtain

{\mathbb{P}}(T_{3}\leq t)=\sum_{k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}P_{k,l}(t)e^{-(k^{2}+l^{2})t},

where

P_{k,l}(t)=(-1)^{k+l}[1-2(k^{2}+l^{2})t+(k^{2}-l^{2})^{2}t^{2}].

Using a result from
[
12
]
we find that this agrees with the distribution function of
twice the square of the maximum height of a pair of non-intersecting reflected Brownian bridges,
each starting and ending at zero.

For
r=4
, we obtain (partially numerically)

{\mathbb{P}}(T_{4}\leq t)=\sum_{k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}Q_{k,l}(t)e^{-(k^{2}+l^{2})t},

where

\displaystyle Q_{k,l}(t)=1-

\displaystyle 4(k^{2}+l^{2})t+3(k^{2}+l^{2})^{2}t^{2}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{2}{3}(k^{2}+l^{2})^{3}t^{3}+\tfrac{4}{3}k^{2}l^{2}(k^{2}-l^{2})^{2}t^{4}.

This agrees with the the distribution function of twice
the square of the maximum height of a pair of non-intersecting Brownian excursions,
as computed in
[
5
,
11
,
16
]
.

This leads us to make the following conjecture. For
N\geq 1
, let
M_{N}
(resp.
H_{N}
)
be the maximum height of
N
non-intersecting reflected Brownian bridges starting and ending
at zero (respectively non-intersecting Brownian excursions).

Conjecture 1
.

For each
N\geq 1
,
T_{2N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}2(M_{N})^{2}

and
T_{2N+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}2(H_{N})^{2}
.

By the asymptotic results obtained in
[
12
]
for the distributions of
M_{N}

and
H_{N}
, this conjecture, if true, would imply the following:

Conjecture 2
.

As
r\to\infty
,

{\mathbb{P}}(T_{r}-2r\leq xr^{1/3})\to F_{1}(x)

where
F_{1}(x)
denotes the Tracy-Widom GOE distribution.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In the next section we recall some
of the relevant background on the quantum Toda chain and its eigenfunctions,
which are known as Whittaker functions. We discuss in particular the discrete
quantisation of interest, and its eigenfunctions. The main new results in this
section are a joint eigenfunction property of the right eigenfunctions (Theorem
2.2
)
and a factorisation for special values of their arguments (Theorem
2.3
).
As an aside, in §
2.6
we explain how this factorisation
yields a series of binomial sum identities which generalise the classical identities of
Vandermonde and Chu. In Section
3
, we summarise
the relevant facts about maximal heights of non-intersecting Brownian bridges.
In Section
4
, we formulate and discuss our proposed
expansions for the discrete Whittaker process transition probabilities and absorption time distribution
functions, with some explicit computations for
r\leq 4
in support of the above conjectures.

2.
Eigenfunctions and their factorisations

2.1.
The Toda chain

Let

(4)

L=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
q_{1}&p_{2}&-1&\cdots&0\\
0&q_{2}&\smash{\ddots}&\smash{\ddots}&\smash{\vdots}\\
\smash{\vdots}&&&p_{r}&-1\\
0&\dots&0&q_{r}&p_{r+1}\end{bmatrix},

and define polynomials
{\eta}_{r,l}(p,q),\ 0\leq l\leq r+1
by

\det(\lambda-L)=\sum_{l=0}^{r+1}(-1)^{l}\lambda^{r-l+1}{\eta}_{r,l}(p,q).

The determinant
\delta_{r}=\det(\lambda-L)
may be computed using the recursion

\delta_{l}=(\lambda-p_{l+1})\delta_{l-1}+q_{l}\delta_{l-2},\qquad l=0,\ldots,r

with
\delta_{-1}=1
and
\delta_{-2}=0
. The first three polynomials are given by

{\eta}_{r,0}=1,\qquad{\eta}_{r,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}p_{i},\qquad{\eta}_{r,2}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r+1}p_{i}p_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}q_{i}.

More generally,

(5)

{\eta}_{r,l}=\sum p_{i_{1}}\ldots p_{i_{s}}q_{j_{1}}\ldots q_{j_{d}},\qquad 1\leq l\leq r+1,

where the sum is over
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq r+1

and
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{d}\leq r
such that
s+2d=l
,

j_{k+1}>j_{k}+1
for all
k
and

i_{a}\notin\{j_{b},j_{b}+1\}
for all
a,b
.

For example,

{\eta}_{2,3}=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}+p_{1}q_{2}+q_{1}p_{3},

\displaystyle{\eta}_{3,3}=p_{1}

\displaystyle p_{2}p_{3}+p_{1}p_{2}p_{4}+p_{1}p_{3}p_{4}+p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}

\displaystyle+p_{1}q_{2}+p_{1}q_{3}+p_{2}q_{3}+q_{1}p_{3}+q_{1}p_{4}+q_{2}p_{4},

{\eta}_{3,4}=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}p_{4}+p_{1}p_{2}q_{3}+p_{1}q_{2}p_{4}+q_{1}p_{3}p_{4}+q_{1}q_{3}.

The Hamiltonians of the quantum Toda chain are the (commuting) differential operators
defined by
{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l}={\eta}_{r,l}(p,q)
with

(6)

p_{i}=\partial/\partial x_{i},\ 1\leq i\leq r+1,\qquad q_{i}=e^{x_{i}-x_{i+1}},\ 1\leq i\leq r.

This choice of
p
and
q
satisfy the commutation relations

(7)

[p_{i},p_{j}]=[q_{i},q_{j}]=0,\qquad[p_{i},q_{j}]=q_{i}\delta_{ij}-q_{j}\delta_{i,j+1}.

The operators
{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l}
are unambiguously defined
by the polynomials (
5
), as each term in the sum
only contains variables which commute with each other.

Denote by
L_{k}
the (partial) backward shift operator defined by

L_{k}f(\ldots,k,\ldots)=f(\ldots,k-1,\ldots).

If we set
q_{i}=L_{n_{i}}
and
p_{i}=n_{i}-n_{i-1}
, with the conventions

n_{0}=n_{r+1}=0
, then these also satisfy the commutation relations (
7
)
and thus give rise to a family of commuting difference operators

h^{r,l}={\eta}_{r,l}(p,q)
,
0\leq l\leq r+1
.
We note that
h^{r,0}=1
,
h^{r,1}=0
and
h^{r,2}=h^{r}
, where

h^{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{n_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}.

Note that the recursion (
2
) may be written as
h^{r}a_{r}=0
.

Denote by
~{}^{*}\!{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l}
and
~{}^{*}\!h^{r,l}

the formal adjoints of these operators. More precisely, we define

~{}^{*}\!{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l}={\eta}_{r,l}(-p,q)
with
p,q
given by (
6
) and

~{}^{*}\!h^{r,l}={\eta}_{r,l}(p,q^{*})
with
p_{i}=n_{i}-n_{i-1}

(with the conventions
n_{0}=n_{r+1}=0
) and
q_{i}^{*}=R_{n_{i}}
,
where
R_{k}
denotes the partial forward shift operator defined by

R_{k}f(\ldots,k,\ldots)=f(\ldots,k+1,\ldots).

These two different quantisations are related via the duality relation

(8)

h_{s}^{r,l}F(s,x)=~^{*}\!{\mathcal{H}}_{x}^{r,l}F(s,x),

where

F(s,x)=y^{-s}\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}^{-s_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}e^{s_{i}(x_{i+1}-x_{i})}.

This follows immediately from the relations

L_{s_{i}}F(s,x)=y_{i}F(s,x),\qquad(s_{i}-s_{i-1})F(s,x)=-\partial_{x_{i}}F(s,x),

with the convention
s_{0}=s_{r+1}=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

If we set
q_{i}=n_{i}^{2}L_{n_{i}}
and
p_{i}=n_{i}-n_{i-1}
, again with the conventions

n_{0}=n_{r+1}=0
, then these also satisfy (
7
)
and thus give rise to a family of commuting difference operators

H^{r,l}={\eta}_{r,l}(p,q)
,
0\leq l\leq r+1
. In this case
H^{r,0}=1
,
H^{r,1}=0
and

H^{r,2}=H^{r}
, where

H^{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}^{2}D_{n_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}

and
D_{k}=L_{k}-I
the partial backward difference operator

D_{k}f(\ldots,k,\ldots)=f(\ldots,k-1,\ldots)-f(\ldots,k,\ldots).

The difference operators
h^{r}
and
H^{r}
are related by

h^{r}=N_{r}(n)^{-1}\circ H^{r}\circ N_{r}(n),\qquad N_{r}(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}!^{2}.

If we define

(9)

A_{r}(n)=N_{r}(n)a_{r}(n),

then
A_{r}
is the unique solution to
H^{r}A_{r}=0

with
A_{r}(0)=1
and the convention
A_{r}(n)=0
for
n\notin{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
.

2.2.
Class one Whittaker functions

For
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r+1}
and
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
define

{\mathcal{Q}}^{(r)}_{\mu}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\left(\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}x^{\prime}_{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e^{x_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i}}+e^{x^{\prime}_{i}-x_{i+1}})\right).

Set
\psi_{\nu}^{(0)}(x)=e^{\nu x}
and, for
r\geq 1
,
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r+1}
,

\psi^{(r)}_{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r+1}}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{r}}{\mathcal{Q}}^{(r)}_{\nu_{r+1}}(x,x^{\prime})\psi^{(r-1)}_{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r}}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Then
\psi_{\nu}=\psi^{(r)}_{\nu}
satisfies the eigenvalue equations

(10)

{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l}\psi_{\nu}=e_{l}(\nu)\psi_{\nu},\qquad 1\leq l\leq r+1,

where
e_{l}(\nu)
denotes the
l^{th}
elementary symmetric polynomial

e_{l}(\nu)=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}}\nu_{i_{1}}\ldots\nu_{i_{l}}.

Equivalently,

\Delta^{(r)}(\lambda)\psi_{\nu}=c_{\lambda}(\nu)\psi_{\nu},

where

\Delta^{(r)}(\lambda)=\sum_{l=0}^{r+1}(-1)^{l}\lambda^{r-l+1}{\mathcal{H}}^{r,l},\qquad c_{\lambda}(\nu)=\prod_{i=1}^{r+1}(\lambda-\nu_{i}).

These eigenfunctions are called class one
GL(r+1,{\mathbb{R}})
Whittaker functions,
for more background see
[
8
]
and references therein.
The joint eigenfunction property (
10
) may be seen as a consequence
of the intertwining relation
[
8
, Lemma 4.1]

\Delta^{(r)}(\lambda)\circ{\mathcal{Q}}^{(r)}_{\mu}=(\lambda-\mu){\mathcal{Q}}^{(r)}_{\mu}\circ\Delta^{(r-1)}(\lambda).

Although it is not obvious from the above definition,
the function
\psi_{\nu}(x)
is symmetric in the parameters
\nu_{i}
.
If
r=1
and
\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}=0
, then

\psi_{\nu}(x)=2K_{\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}}\left(2e^{(x_{1}-x_{2})/2}\right),

where
K_{a}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind

K_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{a-1}\exp\left(-\frac{z}{2}(t+t^{-1})\right)dt.

Let us write

{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}=\{\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}:\ \sum_{i}\nu_{i}=0\}.

If
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
, then
\psi_{\nu}(x)
only depends on
x

through the variables
y_{i}=e^{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
and
so in this case let us denote
\Psi_{\nu}(y)=\psi_{\nu}(x)
.

2.3.
Fundamental Whittaker functions

Let
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
and consider the series

\phi_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{n}a_{r,\nu}(n)e^{(\xi_{n}+\nu,x)}

where the sum is over
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
and

\xi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}(e_{i}-e_{i+1})
.
Then
\phi_{\nu}
satisfies the eigenvalue equation

{\mathcal{H}}^{r,2}\phi_{\nu}=e_{2}(\nu)\phi_{\nu},

at least formally, provided the coefficients
a_{r,\nu}(n)
satisfy the recursion

(11)

\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\nu_{i}-\nu_{i+1})n_{i}\right]a_{r,\nu}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{r,\nu}(n-e_{i}),

with the convention
a_{r,\nu}(n)=0
for
n\notin{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
.
Since
\sum_{i}\nu_{i}=0
, the function
\phi_{\nu}(x)
only depends on
x

through
y_{i}=e^{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
, so let us denote
\Phi_{\nu}(y)=\phi_{\nu}(x)
.
These functions, originally introduced by Hashizume
[
9
]
,
are known as fundamental
GL(r+1,{\mathbb{R}})
Whittaker functions.

Ishii and Stade
[
10
]
obtained the following recursive formula
for the coefficients
a_{r,\nu}(n)
.
For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r-1}
, define

q_{r,\nu}(n,k)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{(n_{i}-k_{i})!(n_{i}-k_{i-1}+\nu_{i}-\nu_{r+1})!},

with the convention
k_{0}=k_{r}=0
.
Define

a_{r,\nu}(n)
,
r\geq 1
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
recursively by

(12)

a_{1,\nu}(n)=\frac{1}{n!(n+\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})!}

and, for
r\geq 2
,

(13)

a_{r,\nu}(n)=\sum_{k}q_{r,\nu}(n,k)a_{r-1,\mu}(k),

where
\mu_{i}=\nu_{i}+\nu_{r+1}/r
,
1\leq i\leq r
, and the sum is over
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r-1}
.
Then, for each
r\geq 1
,

a_{r,\nu}(n)
satisfies the recursion (
11
) with

a_{r,\nu}(0)=\prod_{i<j}\frac{1}{(\nu_{i}-\nu_{j})!}.

It also follows from the definition that, for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,

(14)

a_{r,\nu}(0,k)=\prod_{j=2}^{r+1}\frac{1}{(\nu_{1}-\nu_{j})!}\,a_{r-1,\tau}(k),

where
\tau_{i}=\nu_{i+1}+\nu_{1}/r
,
i=1,\ldots,r
.

If
r=1
, then

\Phi_{\nu}(y)=I_{a}\left(2\sqrt{y}\right),

where
a=\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}
and
I_{a}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind

I_{a}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!(n+a)!}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n+a}.

For
r=2
, we have

(15)

a_{2,\nu}(n,m)=\frac{(n+m+a+b)!}{n!(n+a)!(n+a+b)!m!(m+b)!(m+a+b)!},

where
a=\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}
,
b=\nu_{2}-\nu_{3}
. The formula (
15
) is due to Bump
[
2
]
.

When
\nu=0
, the recursion (
13
) may be conveniently expressed as follows.
Let
\Pi^{r}
be the set of nonnegative arrays
(\pi_{ij},\ 1\leq i+j\leq r+1)

satisfying

\pi_{ij}\geq\pi_{i,j-1}\vee\pi_{i-1,j},

with the convention
\pi_{i0}=\pi_{0j}=0
. For
\pi\in\Pi^{r}
, let
b(\pi)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}

denote it ‘boundary values’
b(\pi)=(\pi_{1,r},\pi_{2,r-1},\ldots,\pi_{r,1})
and
let
\Pi^{r}_{n}
be the set of
\pi\in\Pi^{r}
with
b(\pi)=n
. For
\pi\in\Pi^{r}
, set

w(\pi)=\prod_{1\leq i+j\leq r+1}\frac{1}{(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i,j-1})!(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i-1,j})!}.

Then (
13
) may be written as

a_{r}(n)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{r}_{n}}w(\pi).

Equivalently, the normalised coefficients
A_{r}(n)

2.4.
Mellin transforms of class one Whittaker functions

For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
and

\Re(s_{i})
sufficiently large, define

From the corresponding property of

recently obtained by Stade and Trinh
[

]

of
[

must agree with

Letting

For

[

[

]

Theorem 2.1

(Stade)
.

Remark 2.2

Theorem 2.3

Fix

3.
Non-intersecting Brownian bridges

Let
M
(respectively

) be the maximum height of

It is well known

To be more precise, let

\Delta^{C}_{N}(x)=\prod_{1\leq i\leq N}x_{i}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}),\qquad\Delta^{D}_{N}(x)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}).

Then

P(H\leq h)=A_{N}h^{-2N^{2}-N}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}\Delta^{C}_{N}(x)^{2}e^{-\pi^{2}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}/(2h^{2})},

where

and

are normalisation constants.
In
[

it is shown, using Jacobi’s theta function identity, that

(31)

P(H\leq h)=B_{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}\det\left[H_{2(i+j-1)}\left(k_{i}\sqrt{2}h\right)\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}e^{-2h^{2}\sum_{i}k_{i}^{2}},

where
H_{n}(x)
are the Hermite polynomials and
B_{N}
is a normalisation constant.
The proof given in
[
11
]
is easily modified to give an analogous formula for
M_{N}
:

(32)

P(M\leq h)=B^{\prime}_{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}\det\left[H_{2(i+j-2)}\left(k_{i}\sqrt{2}h\right)\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}e^{-2h^{2}\sum_{i}k_{i}^{2}},

where
B^{\prime}_{N}
is a normalisation constant.
Let us define, for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
and

P_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\tilde{P}_{\sigma k}(t),\qquad Q_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\tilde{Q}_{\sigma k}(t),

where

\tilde{Q}_{k}(t)=B_{N}\det\left[H_{2(i+j-1)}\left(k_{i}\sqrt{t}\right)\right]_{i,j=1}^{N},

and

\tilde{P}_{k}(t)=B^{\prime}_{N}\det\left[H_{2(i+j-2)}\left(k_{i}\sqrt{t}\right)\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}.

Then (
31
) and (
32
) may be written as

P(2M^{2}\leq t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}P_{k}(t)e^{-\sum_{i}k_{i}^{2}t},

P(2H^{2}\leq t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}Q_{k}(t)e^{-\sum_{i}k_{i}^{2}t}.

For
N=1
, these symmetrised coefficients are given by

P_{k}(t)=(-1)^{k},\qquad Q_{k}(t)=1-2k^{2}t,

and for
N=2
,

P_{k,l}(t)=(-1)^{k+l}\left[1-2(k^{2}+l^{2})t+(k^{2}-l^{2})^{2}t^{2}\right],

\displaystyle Q_{k,l}(t)=1-

\displaystyle 4(k^{2}+l^{2})t+3(k^{2}+l^{2})^{2}t^{2}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{2}{3}(k^{2}+l^{2})^{3}t^{3}+\tfrac{4}{3}k^{2}l^{2}(k^{2}-l^{2})^{2}t^{4}.

In general,
P_{k}(t)
has degree at most
N(N-1)
and
Q_{k}(t)

has degree at most
N^{2}
.

4.
Transition probabilities and absorption times

Let
h^{r}=h^{r,2}
and recall that

h^{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{n_{i}}-P(n)

where

(33)

P(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}

and
L_{k}
denotes the partial backward shift operator defined by

L_{k}f(\ldots,k,\ldots)=f(\ldots,k-1,\ldots).

We also recall the notation
D_{k}=L_{k}-I
for the partial backward difference operator

D_{k}f(\ldots,k,\ldots)=f(\ldots,k-1,\ldots)-f(\ldots,k,\ldots).

Since
a_{r}
is strictly positive on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
and satisfies
h^{r}a_{r}=0
, the corresponding Doob transform

{\mathcal{L}}=a_{r}(n)^{-1}\circ h^{r}\circ a_{r}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{a_{r}(n-e_{i})}{a_{r}(n)}D_{n_{i}}

generates a continuous time Markov chain
X_{t},\ t\geq 0
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
.
In
[
14
]
it was shown that this Markov chain has a unique entrance
law starting from
+\infty
.

Denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
the law of the chain starting from

n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
and by
{\mathbb{P}}
the law of chain started from
+\infty
.
Write
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
for the corresponding expectations.
Denote the transition kernel by

p_{t}(n,n^{\prime})={\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X_{t}=n^{\prime}),

and set

F_{n}(t)=p_{t}(n,0)={\mathbb{P}}_{n}(T\leq t),\qquad F(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{t}(n,0)={\mathbb{P}}(T\leq t),

where

T=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ X_{t}=0\}

is the absorption
time for the chain.

For any starting value
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
, the chain remains in the finite set

E_{n}=\{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ m\leq n\}.

Thus, for each
n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,
p_{t}(n,n^{\prime})
is the unique solution to the backward equation

\frac{d}{dt}p_{t}(n,n^{\prime})={\mathcal{L}}_{n}\,p_{t}(n,n^{\prime}),\qquad p_{0}(n,n^{\prime})=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}.

In particular,
F_{n}(t)
is the unique solution to

F_{n}^{\prime}(t)={\mathcal{L}}_{n}\,F_{n}(t),\qquad F_{n}(0)=\delta_{n0}.

Similarly, for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,
p_{t}(n,n^{\prime})
is the unique solution to the forward equation

\frac{d}{dt}p_{t}(n,n^{\prime})={\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{n^{\prime}}\,p_{t}(n,n^{\prime}),\qquad p_{0}(n,n^{\prime})=\delta_{nn^{\prime}},

where
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
denotes the formal adjoint of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

For
s\geq 0
, the generating function

G_{n}(s)={\mathbb{E}}_{n}e^{-sT}=\int_{0}^{\infty}se^{-st}F_{n}(t)\,dt

satisfies the recursion

{\mathcal{L}}_{n}G_{n}(s)=sG_{n}(s)
, that is,

(34)

G_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{s+P(n)}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{a_{r}(n-e_{i})}{a_{r}(n)}G_{n-e_{i}}(s),

with
G_{0}(s)=1
and the convention
G_{n}(s)=0
for
n\notin{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
.
Denote
G(s)={\mathbb{E}}e^{-sT}
.

4.1.
The case
r=1

When
r=1
, we have
{\mathcal{L}}=n^{2}D_{n}
. The absorption time starting from
n
has the same law as the random variable

(35)

S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{e_{k}}{k^{2}},

where
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of independent standard exponential random variables.
This gives the formulas

G_{n}(s)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k^{2}}{s+k^{2}},\qquad\qquad G(s)=\frac{\pi\sqrt{s}}{\sinh\pi\sqrt{s}},

and

(36)

F(t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{k}e^{-k^{2}t}.

Let

\varphi_{k}(n)=\frac{n!^{2}}{(n-k)!(n+k)!},\qquad\varphi^{*}_{k}(m)=(-1)^{k}\frac{(-k)_{m}(k)_{m}}{m!^{2}}.

One can easily check that
{\mathcal{L}}\varphi_{k}=-k^{2}\varphi_{k}
and

(37)

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\varphi_{k}(n)\varphi^{*}_{k}(m)=\delta_{nm}.

It also holds that
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\varphi^{*}_{k}=-k^{2}\tilde{\varphi}_{k}
and, moreover,

(38)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varphi_{k}(n)\varphi^{*}_{l}(n)=\frac{\delta_{kl}+\delta_{k,-l}}{2}.

The transition kernel is therefore given by the Karlin-McGregor type expansion

(39)

p_{t}(n,m)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\varphi_{k}(n)\varphi^{*}_{k}(m)e^{-k^{2}t}.

Note that, since
\varphi_{k}(n)=0
for
|k|>n
and
\varphi^{*}_{k}(m)=0
for
|k|<m
,
the sums in (
37
), (
38
) and (
39
) are all terminating.
Setting
m=0
in (
39
) yields

F_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n}(-1)^{k}\varphi_{k}(n)e^{-k^{2}t}

and, letting
n\to\infty
, we recover (
36
).

4.2.
The general case

To formulate a Karlin-McGregor type formula for the transition probabilities in the general case,
we need to make some assumptions. For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}_{0}
, recall the notation
\nu^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}

defined by
\nu^{\prime}_{i}=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
. For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}_{0}
and
n\in\nu^{\prime}+{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,
let
f_{r,\nu}(n)
be defined by (
21
).
Recall that

(40)

f_{1,\nu}(n)=\frac{1}{(n-\nu_{1})!(n-\nu_{2})!},\qquad f_{2,\nu}(n,m)=\frac{(n+m)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{3}(n-\nu_{i})!(m+\nu_{i})!}.

By Theorem
2.2
, setting
\lambda_{\nu}=e_{2}(\nu)
, these satisfy

(41)

h^{r}f_{r,\nu}=\lambda_{\nu}f_{r,\nu}.

The formulas (
40
) may be used to define meromorphic extensions of
f_{1,\nu}(n)
and
f_{2,\nu}(n,m)

to complex values of
n
and
m
, and it is easily checked that these extensions satisfy the eigenvalue equation (
41
).
By Theorem
2.3
, if

(42)

n_{1},n_{2},\ldots=n_{1},n_{2},n_{1}+n_{2},2n_{2},n_{1}+2n_{2},3n_{2},\ldots

then for
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
, if
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in\nu^{\prime}+{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
and
\Re(n_{r+1})>-1
, we have

(43)

f_{r,\nu}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=n_{r+1}!\prod_{i}\frac{1}{(n_{1}-\nu_{i})!}\prod_{i<j}\frac{1}{(n_{2}-\nu_{i}-\nu_{j})!}.

In general we will assume that
f_{r,\nu}(n)
extends to a meromorphic function of
n\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}

which satisfies both the eigenvalue equation (
41
) and the factorisation property (
43
).
Given this, we also extend the definition of
a_{r}(n)=f_{r,0}(n)
.

For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}_{0}
and
n\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
, let

\beta_{r,\nu}(n)=a_{r}(n)^{-1}f_{r,\nu}(n),\qquad\beta^{*}_{r,\nu}(n)=h(\nu)a_{r}(n)M_{r,-\nu}(n),

where

h(\nu)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r+1}(\nu_{i}-\nu_{j}).

These satisfy
{\mathcal{L}}\beta_{r,\nu}=\lambda_{\nu}\beta_{r,\nu}
and, by (
17
),

{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\beta^{*}_{r,\nu}=\lambda_{\nu}\beta^{*}_{r,\nu}
.

Note that for
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r+1}_{0}
,

\lambda_{\nu}\equiv e_{2}(\nu)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\nu_{i}^{2}=-P(\nu^{\prime})

where
P
is defined by (
33
). For
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}_{0}^{r+1}
,
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
, define

C_{\nu^{\prime}}(x,n,t)=\beta_{r,\nu}(x+n)\,\beta^{*}_{r,\nu}(x)\,e^{-P(\nu^{\prime})t}.

Our proposed formula for the transition kernel is, for
n,n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
,

(44)

p_{t}(n^{\prime}+n,n^{\prime})=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}c_{p}(n^{\prime},n,t)\,e^{-pt},

where

c_{p}(n^{\prime},n,t)=\lim_{x\to n^{\prime}}\lim_{u\to x}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-u_{i})\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ P(\kappa^{\prime})=p}C_{u+\kappa^{\prime}}(x,n,t)\,e^{pt}.

When
r=1
this reduces to (
39
). When
r=2
, it is also correct, as will be outlined in the next section.
In general, by construction, assuming it is well defined, this expression should satisfy the backward
and forward equations. The boundary condition
p_{0}(n^{\prime}+n,n^{\prime})=\delta_{n0}
might be difficult to
establish in general, but can in principle be verified on a case by case basis for
n
and
n^{\prime}
of the form (
42
),
using the factorisations (
43
) and (
19
).

For
n^{\prime}
and
n
of the form (
42
), we can take

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots=x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}+x_{2},2x_{2},\ldots

In this case, using the factorisations (
43
) and (
19
), the formula (
44
) becomes

(45)

p_{t}(n^{\prime}+n,n^{\prime})=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{p}(n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime},n_{1},n_{2},t)\,e^{-pt},

where

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{p}(n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime},n_{1},n_{2},t)=

\displaystyle\lim_{(x_{1},x_{2})\to(n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime})}\lim_{u\to x}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-u_{i})\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ P(\kappa^{\prime})=p}\tilde{C}_{u+\kappa^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2},n_{1},n_{2},t)\,e^{pt},

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2},n_{1},n_{2},t)

\displaystyle=h(\nu)(1+x_{1})_{n_{1}}^{r+1}(1+x_{2})_{n_{2}}^{r(r+1)/2}x_{r+1}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i}\frac{1}{(x_{1}-\nu_{i})_{n_{1}+1}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{1}{(x_{2}-\nu_{i}-\nu_{j})_{n_{2}+1}}\,e^{-P(\nu^{\prime})t}.

In the case
n^{\prime}=0
, taking
x_{1}=x_{2}=\xi
, we can write this as

(46)

F_{n}(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\tilde{c}_{p}(n_{1},n_{2},t)\,e^{-pt},

where, writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})=(\xi,\xi,2\xi,2\xi,\ldots)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{p}(n_{1},n_{2},t)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to 0}\lim_{u\to x}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-u_{i})\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ P(\kappa^{\prime})=p}\tilde{C}_{u+\kappa^{\prime}}(\xi,n_{1},n_{2},t)\,e^{pt},

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{\nu^{\prime}}(\xi,n_{1},n_{2},t)

\displaystyle=h(\nu)n_{1}!^{r+1}n_{2}!^{r(r+1)/2}x_{r+1}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i}\frac{1}{(\xi-\nu_{i})_{n_{1}+1}}\prod_{i<j}\frac{1}{(\xi-\nu_{i}-\nu_{j})_{n_{2}+1}}\,e^{-P(\nu^{\prime})t}.

Since
x+n
also has the form (
42
), it follows from (
43
) that
\beta_{r,\nu}(x+n)\to 1

when
n_{1},n_{2}\to\infty
. Given that there is a unique entrance law from
+\infty
, we can therefore let

n_{1},n_{2}\to\infty
to obtain

(47)

F(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}c_{p}(t)\,e^{-pt},

where, writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})=(\xi,\xi,2\xi,2\xi,\ldots)
,

c_{p}(t)=\lim_{\xi\to 0}\lim_{u\to x}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-u_{i})\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ P(\kappa^{\prime})=p}C_{u+\kappa^{\prime}}(\xi,t)\,e^{pt},

C_{\nu^{\prime}}(\xi,t)=h(\nu)\Gamma(x_{r+1})^{-1}\prod_{i}\Gamma(\xi-\nu_{i})\prod_{i<j}\Gamma(\xi-\nu_{i}-\nu_{j})\,e^{-P(\nu^{\prime})t}.

For computations it will be convenient to ‘split’ the coefficients
c_{p}(t)
as follows.
For
\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
, denote

\kappa=(\kappa_{1}^{\prime},\kappa_{2}^{\prime}-\kappa_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\kappa_{r}^{\prime}-\kappa_{r-1}^{\prime},-\kappa_{r}^{\prime}),\qquad\kappa^{\dagger}=(\kappa_{r+1},\kappa_{r},\ldots,\kappa_{1}).

Define an equivalence relation on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}
by
\rho^{\prime}\sim\kappa^{\prime}
if
\rho=\sigma\kappa
or
\rho=\sigma\kappa^{\dagger}

for some
\sigma\in S_{r+1}
and denote the corresponding equivalence classes by

S_{\kappa^{\prime}}=\left\{\rho^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}:\ \rho^{\prime}\sim\kappa^{\prime}\right\}.

Note that if
\rho^{\prime}\sim\kappa^{\prime}
then
P(\rho^{\prime})=P(\kappa^{\prime})
.
The formula (
47
) may be written as

(48)

F(t)=\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}/\sim}c_{\kappa^{\prime}}(t)\,e^{-P(\kappa^{\prime})t},

where, writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})=(\xi,\xi,2\xi,2\xi,\ldots)
,

c_{\kappa^{\prime}}(t)=\lim_{\xi\to 0}\lim_{u\to x}\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x_{i}-u_{i})\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in S_{\kappa^{\prime}}}C_{u+\rho^{\prime}}(\xi,t)\,e^{P(\rho^{\prime})t}.

Note that the coefficients
c_{p}(t)
are then given as

c_{p}(t)=\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{r}/\sim:\ P(\kappa^{\prime})=p}c_{\kappa^{\prime}}(t).

4.3.
The case
r=2

Writing
(n_{1},n_{2})=(n,m)
, the generator is given by

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{n^{3}}{n+m}D_{n}+\frac{m^{3}}{n+m}D_{m}.

In
[
14
, Proposition 4.4]
it was shown that the absorption time starting from
(n,m)
has the same law as

S_{n}+S^{\prime}_{m}
, where
S_{n}
is defined by (
35
) and
S_{m}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
S_{m}
.
This gives the formulas

G_{n,m}(s)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k^{2}}{s+k^{2}}\,\prod_{l=1}^{m}\frac{l^{2}}{s+l^{2}},\qquad G(s)=\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{s}}{\sinh\pi\sqrt{s}}\right)^{2},

and

(49)

F(t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(1-2k^{2}t)e^{-k^{2}t}.

Writing
(x_{1},x_{2})=(x,y)
, the formula (
45
) may be written as

\displaystyle p_{t}((n^{\prime}+n,m^{\prime}+m),(n^{\prime},m^{\prime}))=

\displaystyle\frac{(n^{\prime}+n)!^{3}(m^{\prime}+m)!^{3}}{n^{\prime}!^{3}m^{\prime}!^{3}}

(50)

\displaystyle\times\lim_{(x,y)\to(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}T^{n,m}_{k,l}(x,y)e^{-P(x+k,y+l)t},

where
P
is defined by (
33
) and

\displaystyle T^{n,m}_{k,l}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{x+y}{x+y+k+l}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=0\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{(a-k)(2x-y+a+k-l)(x+y+a+l)}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{(b-l)(2y-x+b+l-k)(x+y+b+k)},

with the convention that empty products are equal to one.

For small values of
(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})
and
(n,m)
this is easily computed using
Mathematica
.
For example, and writing
q=e^{-t}
, it yields

p_{t}((2,2),(1,1))=\frac{1}{9}\left(16q-48q^{3}+32q^{4}\right),

p_{t}((2,2),(1,0))=\frac{8}{27}\left(-2q+2q^{4}-3(q+q^{4})\ln q\right),

p_{t}((2,2),(0,0))=\frac{1}{27}\left(27-16q-11q^{4}+12(4q+q^{4})\ln q\right).

The formula (
4.3
) is clearly equal to one when
t=0
and
(n,m)=(0,0)
.
One can also show that it vanishes at
t=0
when
(n,m)\neq(0,0)
.
In fact, if
(n,m)\neq(0,0)
,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}T^{n,m}_{k,l}(x,y)=0.

This follows from the more general identity

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=0\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{k}-\mu_{l})(\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l})}

(51)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{(\mu_{b}-\mu_{l})(\mu_{b}+\mu_{l}-\lambda_{k})(\mu_{b}+\lambda_{k})}\,=0,

with the convention that empty products are equal to one and the assumption that
each term in the sum is finite. A proof of (
4.3
) is given in Appendix A.

In the case
n^{\prime}=m^{\prime}=0
, the formula (
4.3
) is easily computed using
Mathematica
.
Only the terms corresponding to
(k,l)
of the form
(k,k)
,
(k,0)
and
(0,k)
contribute,
and the formula yields:

(52)

F_{n,m}(t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left[\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)-2k^{2}H_{k}(n,m)t\right]e^{-k^{2}t},

where

H_{k}(n,m)=\frac{n!^{2}m!^{2}}{(n-k)!(n+k)!(m-k)!(m+k)!},

and
\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)
is defined as follows. If
|k|\leq n\wedge m
, then

\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)=\left[1+k(H_{n-k}-H_{n+k}+H_{m-k}-H_{m+k})\right]H_{k}(n,m),

where
H_{n}
are the harmonic numbers

H_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{j},

with the convention
H_{0}=0
. If
n\geq k>m
, then

\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)=(-1)^{m+k}\,k\,\Gamma(k-m)\,\frac{n!^{2}m!^{2}}{(n-k)!(n+k)!(m+k)!}.

If
|k|>n\vee m
, then
\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)=0
. The remaining cases are defined by

\tilde{H}_{-k}(n,m)=\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)=\tilde{H}_{k}(m,n).

We remark that the formula (
52
) may also be verified directly by showing that

{\mathcal{L}}H_{k}=-k^{2}H_{k},\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{H}_{k}=-k^{2}\tilde{H}_{k}-2k^{2}H_{k},

and

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\tilde{H}_{k}(n,m)=\delta_{n0}\delta_{m0}.

Now let us consider the formula (
47
). In this case it may be written as

(53)

F(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}c_{p}(t)\,e^{-pt},

where

c_{p}(t)=\lim_{x\to 0}\sum_{(k,l)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}:\ P(k,l)=p}T_{k,l}(x)\,e^{pt},

T_{k,l}(x)=\lim_{u\to x}\,(x-u)^{2}C_{u+k,u+l}(x,t),

C_{\nu^{\prime}}(x,t)=h(\nu)\Gamma(2x)^{-1}\prod_{i}\Gamma(x-\nu_{i})\Gamma(x+\nu_{i})\,e^{-P(\nu^{\prime})t}.

Let
q=e^{-t}
. We first note that

T_{0,0}(x)=2x^{3}\Gamma(x)^{2}\Gamma(2x)q^{x^{2}},\qquad c_{0}(t)=\lim_{x\to 0}T_{0,0}(x)=1.

Next consider
(k,l)
of the form
(k,k)
,
(k,0)
and
(0,k)
, where
k>0
.
We compute

T_{k,k}(x)=\frac{2(k+x)^{3}\Gamma(x)^{2}\Gamma(k+2x)^{2}}{(k!)^{2}\Gamma(2x)}q^{(k+x)^{2}},

T_{k,0}(x)=T_{0,k}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{k}(2k+x)\Gamma(2x)\Gamma(-k+x+1)\Gamma(k+x)\Gamma(k+2x+1)}{k!\Gamma(2x)}q^{k^{2}+kx+x^{2}}.

Combining these gives

\displaystyle S_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=T_{k,k}(x)+T_{k,0}(x)+T_{0,k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma(k+2x)}{k!^{2}\Gamma(2x)}q^{k^{2}+kx+x^{2}}\left[(-1)^{k}k!(2k+x)(k+2x)\Gamma(2x)\Gamma(-k+x+1)\Gamma(k+x)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+(k+x)^{3}\Gamma(x)^{2}\Gamma(k+2x)q^{kx}\right]

Letting
x\to 0
, this gives

c_{k^{2}}(t)=\lim_{x\to 0}S_{k}(x)=2(1+2k^{2}\ln q)q^{k^{2}}=2(1-2k^{2}t)e^{-k^{2}t}.

Now suppose
k>l>0
. Then

\displaystyle T_{k,l}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k+l}}{k!l!\Gamma(2x)}(2k-l+x)(-k+2l+x)(k+l+2x)

\displaystyle\times\Gamma(k+2x)\Gamma(l+2x)\Gamma(k-l+x)\Gamma(-k+l+x)q^{-(k+x)(l+x)+(k+x)^{2}+(l+x)^{2}},

and

T_{k,l}:=\lim_{x\to 0}T_{k,l}(x)=-\frac{4(k-2l)(2k-l)(k+l)}{kl(k-l)}q^{k^{2}-kl+l^{2}}.

Now, if
k>l>0
then
k>k-l>0

Appendix A

Here we give a proof of the identity (
4.3
), namely

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=0\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{k}-\mu_{l})(\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l})}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{(\mu_{b}-\mu_{l})(\mu_{b}+\mu_{l}-\lambda_{k})(\mu_{b}+\lambda_{k})}\,=0,

with the convention that empty products are equal to one and the assumption that
each term in the sum is finite.
We first note that we can write the above sum as

(68)

S=\sum_{l=0}^{m}\prod_{a=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{\mu_{b}-\mu_{l}}S_{l},

where

S_{l}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=0\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{k}-\mu_{l})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{(\mu_{b}+\mu_{l}-\lambda_{k})(\mu_{b}+\lambda_{k})}.

We recall the identity, for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
distinct,

(69)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{i}}=0.

See for example
[
13
, Eq. 8.17]
for a proof.
For each
0\leq l\leq m
, taking
N=n+m-1
, we extend the sequence
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots

by defining
\lambda_{n+1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
to be

\mu_{1}+\mu_{l},\ldots,\mu_{l-1}+\mu_{l},\mu_{l+1}+\mu_{l},\ldots,\mu_{m}+\mu_{l}.

Note that in this notation,

S_{l}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=0\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}^{N}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{k}-\mu_{l})}.

Taking
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{l})
, (
69
) implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})(\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{i}-\mu_{l})}=0

or, equivalently,

S_{l}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l^{\prime}=0\\
l^{\prime}\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\,\prod_{a=0}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}-\mu_{l^{\prime}}-\mu_{l})(\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l^{\prime}})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\notin\{l,l^{\prime}\}\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{(\mu_{b}-\mu_{l^{\prime}})(\mu_{b}+\mu_{l}+\mu_{l^{\prime}})}.

Substituting this into (

) gives

S=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}:\ l\neq l^{\prime}}(\mu_{l^{\prime}}-\mu_{l})R_{l,l^{\prime}},

where

\displaystyle R_{l,l^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\prod_{a=0}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l})(\lambda_{a}+\mu_{l^{\prime}})(\lambda_{a}-\mu_{l}-\mu_{l^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{\mu_{b}-\mu_{l}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{\mu_{b}-\mu_{l^{\prime}}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=0\\
b\notin\{l,l^{\prime}\}\end{subarray}}^{m}\frac{1}{\mu_{b}+\mu_{l}+\mu_{l^{\prime}}}.

Since
R_{l,l^{\prime}}=R_{l^{\prime},l}
, it follows that
