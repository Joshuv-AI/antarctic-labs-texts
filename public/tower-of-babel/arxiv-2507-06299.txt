Emma R. Moran, Brett H. Andrews, Jeffrey A. Newman, Biprateep Dey

Abstract
Photometric redshifts (photo- z z ’s) are crucial for the cosmology, galaxy evolution, and transient science drivers of next-generation imaging facilities like the Euclid Mission, the Rubin Observatory, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. Previous work has shown that image-based deep learning photo- z z methods produce smaller scatter than photometry-based classical machine learning (ML) methods on the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) Main Galaxy Sample, a testbed photo- z z dataset. However, global assessments can obscure local trends. To explore this possibility, we used a self-organizing map (SOM) to cluster SDSS galaxies based on their u ​ g ​ r ​ i ​ z ugriz colors. Deep learning methods achieve lower photo- z z scatter than classical ML methods for all SOM cells. The fractional reduction in scatter is roughly constant across most of color space with the exception of the most bulge-dominated and reddest cells where it is smaller in magnitude. Interestingly, classical ML photo- z z ’s suffer from a significant color-dependent attenuation bias, where photo- z z ’s for galaxies within a SOM cell are systematically biased towards the cell’s mean spectroscopic redshift and away from extreme values, which is not readily apparent when all objects are considered. In contrast, deep learning photo- z z ’s suffer from very little color-dependent attenuation bias. The increased attenuation bias for classical ML photo- z z methods is the primary reason why they exhibit larger scatter than deep learning methods. This difference can be explained by the deep learning methods weighting redshift information from the individual pixels of a galaxy image more optimally than integrated photometry.



I
Introduction

Next-generation imaging surveys such as the Euclid mission
(
Laureijs et al. 2011
)
, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(
Ivezić et al. 2019
, LSST; )
, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
(
Spergel et al. 2015
;
Akeson et al. 2019
, Roman; )
will obtain deep multi-band optical and near-infrared imaging over thousands of square degrees.
Nearly all of the extragalactic science drivers of these surveys, such as cosmology, galaxy evolution, and transients, will require redshift estimates
(
Newman & Gruen 2022
)
. Ideally, spectroscopic redshifts (spec-
z
’s) would be used to estimate distances, but obtaining spectra for any significant fraction of the photometrically-detected objects is completely infeasible with current facilities
(
Newman et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. Instead, these imaging surveys must rely on photometric redshifts (photo-
z
’s; e.g.,
The LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration et al. 2018
).

Photometry-based supervised ML algorithms have produced more precise photo-
z
estimates for imaging surveys than physically-motivated techniques, such as template fitting (which involves fitting galaxy spectral templates to a galaxy’s observed integrated photometry), when sufficient training data is available
(
Salvato et al. 2019
)
.
More recently, the advent of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has enabled deep learning photo-
z
models that can leverage pixel-level color information from the images directly (see
Huertas-Company & Lanusse 2023
for a review of deep learning methods applied to galaxy surveys). These modern imaged-based deep neural networks produce the current best photo-
z
predictions if given training samples with complete coverage of color–magnitude–redshift space, such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), even with training sets that are relatively small
(
Pasquet et al. 2019
;
Hayat et al. 2021
;
Henghes et al. 2022
;
Dey et al. 2022a
;
Treyer et al. 2024
;
Ait Ouahmed et al. 2024
, e.g., )
. Despite these success, it remains unclear exactly why they perform better than classical ML methods on global metrics, which motivates this work.

Strong performance on global metrics can mask offsetting systematic errors in local regions of input parameter space.
An extreme case of this effect is the
trainZ
photo-
z
estimator from the LSST Photo-
z
Data Challenge
(
Schmidt et al. 2020
)
, which simply predicted the redshift distribution of the training set as the photo-
z
probability distribution function for all objects, regardless of their photometry.
By construction, the
trainZ
estimator achieved the ideal distribution of the probability integral transform when considered
globally
, beating out the other 11 widely-used photo-
z
methods.
However, it performed the worst in local regions of color space (as characterized by the conditional density estimation loss; see, e.g.,
Izbicki & Lee 2017
).
A full conditional assessment by regressing across color space (e.g., using the Cal-PIT method of
Dey et al. 2022b
) is beyond the scope of this paper.
Rather, we simply partition color space using a self-organizing map (SOM;
Kohonen 1982
;
Kohonen 2001
; see Section
IV
) and assess photo-
z
performance in neighborhoods of color space.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
describes the SDSS dataset used in this analysis, and Section
III
reviews the photo-
z
algorithms used. Section
IV
introduces our method of partitioning the galaxy sample in color space with a SOM. Section
V
presents results on the photo-
z
scatter and bias for individual SOM cells. Finally, Section
VI
discusses the contribution of attenuation bias to photo-
z
scatter for classical ML methods.

Deep Learning Models

Despite their architectural differences, a variety of modern deep learning models—including CNNs

III.3.1
Capsule Networks

Hinton et al. 2011
proposed the idea of capsule networks as an alternative to CNNs, where the artificial neurons of a capsule network are organized into groups (capsules), producing the vector analog to the scalar neurons in CNNs such that they are viewpoint equivariant (as opposed to the viewpoint invariance of CNNs) and therefore could be more efficient to train.
However, actually training capsule networks proved challenging until the introduction of the dynamic routing algorithm by
Sabour et al. 2017
that dramatically improved the viability of their training. Soon after,
Katebi et al. 2019
became the first work to apply capsule networks to galaxy images for galaxy morphology prediction.

Dey et al. 2022a
utilized a deep capsule network for their
encapzulate
model (compared to the shallow network proposed by
Sabour et al. 2017
) developed by
Rajasegaran et al. 2019
. Its architecture consists of three sub-networks: a deep capsule network for encoding the galaxy images to a low dimensional embedding space, a class-independent decoder network for morphological prediction that acts as a regularizer (using disk/spheroid labels from Galaxy Zoo 1;
Lintott et al. 2011
), and a redshift prediction network.

IV
Partitioning Color Space with a Self-Organizing Map

To study the impact of attenuation bias in local neighborhoods of color space, we partition
ugriz
color space using a SOM.
A SOM is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction algorithm that works by grouping together objects with similar properties into discrete cells, with objects in a cell having properties that are most similar to other objects in the same cell and neighboring cells usually containing galaxies that are broadly similar. Since SOMs produce easily interpretable results with clear visualizations, they have been used for photo-
z

Carrasco Kind & Brunner 2014
;
Myles et al. 2021
;
Wright et al. 2025

Masters et al. 2015
;
Masters et al. 2017
;
Stanford et al. 2021
;
Gruen et al. 2023

McCullough et al. 2024

For our implementation, we used the

VI.1
The Impacts of Attenuation Bias Versus Random Scatter

As we have shown, photometry-based classical ML photo-
z

\sim

larger scatter than image-based deep learning photo-

This effect causes output values to be biased towards the mean (i.e., with predictions that are too high when values are lowest and too low when values are highest) due to the impact of measurement errors in the independent variable/feature values (e.g.,
Fuller 1987
;

;

;

;

In this section, we assess the relative contributions of attenuation bias and random scatter to the overall scatter within individual SOM cells.

We expect that the overall scatter (within a SOM cell) can be decomposed into two components whose sum in quadrature should be roughly equal to the overall scatter (if standard deviation were used as the metric rather than

–

=
\mathrm{NMAD}
(

VI.2
Deep Learning Leverages Pixels to Mitigate Attenuation Bias

Ting 2024
investigated strategies to mitigate attenuation bias by leveraging many features that are correlated because they result from the same underlying physical process (see Sections 2.4.2 and 2.5.2 of that work).
They presented a simple scenario where many noisy features (in our case, the individual pixels in a galaxy image) are linearly correlated with a physical property (here, redshift).
Often those features are combined to increase the signal-to-noise ratio (e.g., integrated photometry) before predicting the physical property.
If the relative weightings of features in the combination step is perfect, then using individual features for prediction does not mitigate the attenuation bias.
However, if the relative weightings are imperfect, then predicting from individual features will reduce the attenuation bias.
We have verified these predictions with Monte Carlo models for a toy scenario.

In our case, deep learning photo-
z
models utilize the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of many individual pixels per object that all share the same redshift (ignoring unassociated blends, which are very rare at the depths of the SDSS MGS;
Dawson et al. 2016
) but have uncorrelated noise. The integrated photometry used for the
5
photo-
z
’s is based upon model-fitting photometry which should (if the model is accurate) weight pixels proportionally to an object’s flux. If the photometry model matches the true light distribution of the galaxy perfectly and all pixels’ SEDs are equally sensitive to redshift, then it would be an ideal weighting of the pixels and hence would be expected to yield comparable attenuation bias to using all the pixels as independent features for prediction.

Instead, the attenuation bias in photo-
z
’s is reduced for predictions using the actual pixel values compared to integrated photometry, particularly for bluer or less bulge-dominated galaxies. This suggests that the photometry models for these galaxies are less accurate and/or that some pixels are more informative about redshift than their relative flux implies. Indeed, we would expect that for galaxies with significant spiral structure or irregularity, the simple composite exponential/De Vaucoleurs models used for SDSS photometry would not capture all of the variations in intensity amongst pixels, making the first of these scenarios plausible for later-type galaxies but of little import for early-type galaxies that tend to have much smoother morphologies. Furthermore, late-type galaxies exhibit much stronger gradients in colors and stellar population properties than early-type galaxies do
(
de Vaucouleurs 1961
)
; the colors of the older, redder populations at the centers of these galaxies are more strongly correlated with redshift than the colors of their outer portions
(
Gladders & Yee 2000
)
.

As a result, both of these explanations for our results are very plausible: later-type galaxies have larger deviations of pixel fluxes from the models used for integrated photometry and also have larger variations in pixel sensitivity to redshift than would be expected from their relative brightnesses in the SDSS photometry models alone. This can cause redshift predictions from integrated photometry to suffer from greater attenuation bias than predictions from the raw pixel values, especially for galaxies with smaller bulge-to-total ratios, as we have found. Our results are not sufficient, however, to indicate which of these two possible effects is dominant. One potential approach for disentangling this puzzle would be to make redshift predictions from photometry integrated within multiple circular annuli (which should tend to separate components of different colors; e.g.,
Saxena et al. 2024
) and compare to the attenuation bias and overall
z_{\mathrm{phot}}
–
z_{\mathrm{spec}}
relations when predicting redshift from raw pixel values.

VI.3
Outlook

While a full investigation of
how
deep learning photo-
z
methods are able to mitigate the local attenuation bias that affects classical methods is beyond the scope of this work, a better understanding of how deep learning models are able to utilize the information available in images may provide key insights for designing more efficient photo-
z
strategies, such as new data augmentation procedures for contrastive learning approaches that utilize noise augmentations.
In particular, these efforts will have important implications for developing deep learning photo-
z
estimation algorithms for Euclid
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025
)
, LSST
(
Merz et al. 2025
)
, and Roman (Khederlarian et al. in prep.).
