Carbon Isotopic Characteristics and Evolution of Coal-Measure Gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang Block, Qinshui Basin, China
Authors: Zhixiang Chang, Yang Wang, Yanming Zhu, Tong Zhang, Liu Yang, Mengxi Liu, Gaoyuan Yan, Haoxiong Wang, Jinlong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study experimentally analyzed gas composition and methane carbon isotopes obtained from coal, shale, and silty mudstone. Subsequently, by integrating data on thermal maturity, burial depth, gas content, and geological structural characteristics, the geochemical characteristics and genesis mechanisms of coal-measure gas were systematically investigated. The results show that coal-measure gas in the study area is dominated by CH4, with an average concentration of 90.34% and extremely high drying coefficients. A notable negative correlation between CH4 and N2 concentrations indicates atmospheric intrusion during reservoir formation. δ13C1 values (the carbon isotope ratio of methane) range from −40.412‰ to −16.524‰ (average −32.941‰), generally lower than theoretical thermogenic baselines, suggesting that the early generated thermogenic gas underwent late modifications. Within coal seams, δ13C1 values trend negatively with both gas content and CO2 concentration but decouple from burial depth. In contrast to coalbed methane, shale gas shows greater heterogeneity in its evolution, with δ13C1 values strongly and positively correlated with CO2 concentration. Tectonic faults and folds influence gas preservation and migration, while intense gas diffusion obscures vertical δ13C1 trends. Continuous tectonic uplift and meteoric water infiltration facilitate late-stage microbial colonization, promoting CH4 dissolution and the generation of secondary biogenic gas, which further lowers δ13C1 values. In conclusion, coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block is a composite gas reservoir modified by combined desorption-diffusion–migration effects and secondary biological effects, governed by tectonic movements, atmospheric flushing, and microbial activities.

Introduction

Coal-measure gas, a premium unconventional fuel, is playing an increasingly important role in the global energy landscape. − China, in particular, possesses vast and widely distributed coal-measure gas resources. Developing these resources is key to optimizing the energy mix and improving environmental outcomes. − However, coal-measure gas differs fundamentally from conventional natural gas in terms of reservoir properties, complex hydrocarbon generation histories, and reservoir formation processes.

To understand the geochemistry of coal-measure gas, it is essential to analyze gas composition and methane carbon isotope ratios. These indicators not only help trace the origin of coal-measure gas and elucidate its migration and accumulation, but also guide the efficient development of coal-measure gas resources. − Crucially, these geochemical signatures are controlled by organic-matter type and source-rock thermal maturity, and may be further modified during gas accumulation. In recent years, researchers have developed robust diagnostic diagrams to identify genetic types of gas. These criteria utilize relationships such as those between the carbon isotope ratios of methane and ethane, the carbon isotope ratio of methane and the drying coefficient, and the carbon and hydrogen isotope ratios. −

The central-eastern Qinshui basin is rich in coal-measure gas resources, characterized by low reservoir permeability and favorable gas-bearing property. However, the presence of multiple coal seams that are extensively interbedded with tight sandstones and mudstones results in highly variable geochemical characteristics. , This complicates the vertical migration of coal-measure gas within the domain, affecting both hydrocarbon accumulation and exploitation. A substantial proportion of the extant literature on the central-eastern Qinshui basin has hitherto focused on multiscale reservoir characterization and the prediction of favorable exploration areas. − Consequently, comprehensive research into the factors influencing the methane carbon isotope remains insufficient, resulting in a limited understanding of coal-measure gas evolution in this area. To this end, the study investigated the Permian Shanxi Formation and the Carboniferous Taiyuan Formation of the Yushe-Wuxiang block. Based on analyses of desorbed gas composition and carbon isotope ratio of methane, combined with data on thermal maturity, gas content, burial depth, and geological structures, the influencing factors of methane carbon isotopes and the genesis mechanisms of coal-measure gas were studied. This research is important for establishing a geochemical theoretical framework of coal-measure gas and guiding future exploration and development in this area.

Geological Setting

The Qinshui basin is located in North China (Figure a), with the Yushe-Wuxiang block situated on the eastern side of the compound syncline axis of the Qinshui basin (Figure b). Due to the superimposed influence of multiple tectonic movements, the study area has evolved into the current complex geological system, in which tensile and compressive deformations coexist. The overall structure is a monoclinal system trending from NE to SW, with strata dipping NW at an angle of 3° to 10°. The structural characteristics of this area are the development of small-scale faults and folds. Faults are more extensively developed in the west than in the east, with strike directions predominantly NE-SW and NNE-SSW, and dip directions generally NWW. The orientation of folds is similar to that of faults, mainly NNE-SSW, with folds concentrated in the northwest and southeast of the study area. Synclines and anticlines develop alternately and closely together (Figure c). ,,

The strata in the Yushe-Wuxiang block primarily consist of the following: Quaternary (Q) and Neogene (N) of the Cenozoic; Jurassic (J) and Triassic (T) of the Mesozoic; Permian (P) and Carboniferous (C) of the Upper Palaeozoic; Ordovician (O) and Cambrian of the Lower Palaeozoic; and the Precambrian. Notably, the Silurian (S) of the Lower Palaeozoic, Devonian (D) of the Upper Palaeozoic, Lower Jurassic (J1) and Cretaceous (K) of the Mesozoic, and Paleogene (E) of the Cenozoic are absent. The principal coal-bearing strata include the Benxi, Taiyuan, Shanxi, Lower Shihezi, and Upper Shihezi Formations of the Upper Palaeozoic. The strata under study are predominantly the Taiyuan and Shanxi Formations (Figure d), which are characterized by numerous coal-bearing layers and considerable overall thickness. The No. 3, 8, 12, and 15 coal seams are stable, recoverable, and widely distributed in the area. The Taiyuan Formation is a marine-terrestrial transitional facies, with an average thickness of 120.67 m and a range of 91.80 to 195.10 m. This formation comprises a variety of lithologies, including mud shale, sandy mudstone, siltstone, fine sandstone, limestone, and coal seams. The mud shale layer ranges in thickness from 46.36 to 121.70 m, with an average of 79.36 m. The Shanxi Formation is a shallow-water deltaic sedimentary system that averages 57.39 m in thickness, ranging from 43.55 to 83.50 m. It contains similar lithologies such as mud shale, sandy mudstone, siltstone, fine sandstone, and coal seams. The mud shale layer ranges in thickness from 12.04 to 81.90 m, with an average of 35.95 m. ,

Methodology

During drilling of coal-measure gas wells, 19 samples (Table ) of coal, shale, and silty mudstone were collected from five coal-measure gas wells (ZK03–2, ZK06–1, ZK07–2, ZK08–2, and ZK09–2; Figure c) in the Yushe-Wuxiang block of the Qinshui basin, using the rope coring method. The burial depth of the samples ranges from 1250.87 to 1641.09 m.

Sample Collection Form

According to the Chinese national standard GB/T 19559–2021, “Method of Determining Coalbed Methane Content”, the sample was placed in the desorbed canister within a thermostatic device that simulated reservoir temperature for desorption, and measurements were taken at specified intervals. Measurement of the desorbed gas content was terminated when the average daily desorption volume was consistently less than 10 cm3 in a week. After natural desorption, some of the remaining samples were crushed and then ground in a ball mill for two to 4 h. Subsequently, these samples were placed in a thermostatic device to determine the residual gas content. Additionally, the lost gas content was calculated using the direct method based on natural desorption. The total gas content was calculated as the sum of the lost, desorbed, and residual gas contents. Due to the weaker polarity of 12CH4 compared to 13CH4, lighter isotopes tend to escape preferentially during the initial stages of desorption, resulting in enrichment of the heavier isotope in the residual gas. It should be noted that on the first, third, and fifth days of natural desorption, gases were collected for laboratory analysis of gas composition and carbon isotope measurements; the average test results were used to minimize experimental errors.

According to the Chinese national standard GB/T 13610–2020, “Analysis of natural gas compositionGas chromatography”, gas composition was determined using American Agilent Company’s 8890GC-5977C gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) instrument. The carbon isotopes in the coal-measure gas samples were measured using an Isoprime 100 stable isotope ratio mass spectrometer from Elementar (Germany), in accordance with the international standard Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB). The analytical precision of carbon isotope measurements was better than ± 0.25‰ based on repeated analyses of laboratory standards. International and laboratory reference standards were routinely analyzed to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of Gas Composition

The test results for the composition of desorbed gases reveal that the gas composition of coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block primarily consists of methane (CH4), heavy hydrocarbons (C2+, hydrocarbons containing two to five carbon atoms), nitrogen (N2), and carbon dioxide (CO2). As shown in Figure , CH4 is the dominant component of coal-measure gas in the study area, with its concentration ranging from 18.95% to 98.31% and an average of 90.34%. The CH4 concentration in all coal samples exceeds 96%, whereas in the shale samples it is unstable. The C2+ concentration is very low, with only five samples exceeding 0.001%. Preliminary identification of coal-measure gas based on the drying coefficient (φ­(C1)/φ­(C1–5)) indicated that the values in the study area are more than 0.997, confirming that the coal-measure gas is extra dry. Despite the majority of coal-measure source rocks in the study area being overmature, they have not yet reached their maximum capacity for storing dry gas, which is still conducive to the generation and accumulation of dry gas.

Gas composition.

Nonhydrocarbon gases are primarily N2, with concentrations ranging from 1.48% to 78.9% and an average of 9.12%. CO2 is the second-most abundant nonhydrocarbon gas, with a concentration ranging from 0.06% to 2.1% (average 0.52%). The composition of coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block is dominated by CH4 and N2, with their combined concentration exceeding 97.85% of the total gas. Correlation analysis of CH4 and N2 concentrations revealed a significant negative correlation (Figure ). This correlation indicates that coal-bearing reservoirs in this area have undergone multiple late modifications, with air being a key factor influencing hydrocarbon accumulation. , Following the entry of the atmosphere into the coal-measure source rocks, the N2 concentration increases, while the CH4 concentration decreases comparatively. On-site hydrological observations and pumping test results confirm that groundwater is present in the sandstone fissures of both the Taiyuan and Shanxi formations in the study area. Localized structural development zones exhibit favorable groundwater permeability, with coal-measure gas accumulation influenced by groundwater, potentially leading to gas–water exchange. In addition, the coal-measure source rocks in the study area generally reached the mature stage at the end of the Indosinian Movement, resulting in the generation of a substantial volume of hydrocarbon gases. Subsequently, the basin underwent continuous uplift and multiple sedimentary interruptions, influenced by the Yanshan Orogeny. The subduction of oceanic crust resulted in the development of numerous folds and faults in the region. Subsequent tectonic movements intensified atmospheric replacement processes, including the significant escapes of accumulated CH4, as well as the infiltration of atmosphere and groundwater into the reservoirs. Upon infiltrating coal-bearing strata, N2 and O2 experienced different fates: oxygen, being chemically reactive, readily undergoes oxidation by rocks or minerals and is consumed in this process; nitrogen, by contrast, has comparatively stable chemical properties and is thus easier to preserve. Concurrently, some CH4 dissolved in groundwater to a certain extent, leading to an increase in the N2 concentration of coal-bearing strata.

Carbon Isotopic Characteristics and Evidence of Secondary Modification

Genetic Types of Coal-Measure Gas

Based on indicators such as gas composition, methane carbon isotopes, and geological background, the genesis of coal-measure gas can generally be classified into three categories: biogenic gas, thermogenic gas, and mixed genetic gas. When studying their genetic types, the methane carbon isotope is regarded as one of the most crucial geochemical parameters and is often used in combination with the drying coefficient. According to the methane carbon-isotope test results (Figure ), the measured δ13C1 values (The δ13C1 value is the carbon isotope ratio of methane) of coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block range from −40.412‰ to −16.524‰, with an average of −32.941‰. It is generally believed that when measured δ13C1 values range from −70‰ to −55‰, coal-measure gas is mainly biogenic. The measured δ13C1 values of coal-measure gas in the study area are significantly greater than −50‰, and it is preliminarily inferred that coal-measure gas is predominantly of thermal origin. However, the drying coefficients of coal-measure gas in the study area are extremely high, and it contains almost no heavy hydrocarbons, indicating that the gases exhibit characteristics of both biogenic and thermal origins. Furthermore, the observed negative correlation between CH4 and N2 concentrations suggests that the coal-measure gas reservoirs have undergone multiple late modifications. Combined with the genetic identification diagram of coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block (Figure , values’ upper limit set at 105 for visualization), all coal-measure gas data points in the study area are located in the gas reservoir modification zone under diffusion and oxidation. This finding indicates that coal-measure gas in this area may have undergone complex modifications, including secondary biological effects, groundwater dissolution, or tectonic movements.

Thermal Maturity and Carbon Isotopic Anomalies

The degree of thermal evolution of organic matter is an important factor in determining the genesis type of coal-measure gas. Many studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between the carbon isotope values of natural gas and the thermal maturity of source rocks. − As the degree of thermal evolution of organic matter deepens, the carbon isotope values of natural gas increase accordingly. Specifically, the intensification of thermal evolution has increased the microporosity of coal-measure reservoirs, thereby enhancing their adsorption capacity. These studies revealed a strong positive correlation between δ13C1 values and vitrinite reflectance (R o). However, significant differences exist in the equations established by different scholars for the relationship between δ13C1 values and R o. Therefore, to mitigate the impact of formula-specific errors and accurately identify the genesis types of coal-measure gas, three empirical equations were selected to calculate theoretical δ13C1 values in the study area. As the theoretical values are generally higher than the measured values, it can be concluded that the coal-measure gas in the study area does not originate only from thermogenic processes. Influence of Gas Content on Methane Carbon Isotopes

The total gas content of the coal-measure source rocks in the Yushe-Wuxiang block ranges from 0.3 m3/t to 28.67 m3/t, with an average of 11.87m3/t (Figure ). The gas content of coal seams ranges from 4.39 m3/t to 28.67 m3/t, with an average of 16.71 m3/t; the corresponding δ13C1 values range from −40.412‰ to −26.989‰, with an average of −33.281‰ (Figure ). The gas content of shales ranges from 0.3 m3/t to 2.58 m3/t, with an average of 1.38 m3/t; the corresponding δ13C1 values range from −39.368‰ to −16.524‰, with an average of −32.204‰. As illustrated in Figure , the coalbed methane content in the study area exhibits a weak negative correlation with δ13C1 values, indicating that higher gas content is associated with lower δ13C1 values. Conversely, no clear correlation was observed between the shale gas content and δ13C1 values, with considerable variation in the δ13C1 value. This finding indicates substantial regional variations in the geochemical evolution of shale gas.

The high gas content is often indicative of a reservoir with greater gas potential and more favorable preservation conditions, including well-developed micro porosity, effective sealing by the cap and floor strata, and elevated reservoir pressure. − These conditions are conducive to the accumulation of δ13CH4. Consequently, an elevated gas content is generally accompanied by increased δ13C1 values. However, in the study area, an increase in the coalbed methane content is concomitant with a decrease in δ13C1 values. This finding suggests that the coal seams may have undergone complex geological modification. The ’high gas content, light isotope’ combination characteristic observed in coal seams is likely the result of early formed gas preservation, the incorporation of secondary biogenic gas, and ongoing diffusion fractionation. Therefore, it can be inferred that the controlling factors on the gas content also exhibit a close correlation with the distribution of methane carbon isotopes in coalbed methane. The factors influencing the gas content of coalbed methane primarily include burial depth, CO2 concentration, and tectonic settings. , The subsequent analyses examine the regulatory effect of these factors on δ13C1.

Vertical Distribution Characteristics of δ13C1

Gas content depends on the conditions under which gas is generated and preserved. The maturity of organic matter is a key factor in determining the gas production. Burial depth has been shown to constrain the preservation of coal-measure gas. As illustrated in Figure , a clear negative correlation exists between the burial depth and the measured gas content. This observed anomaly may be attributable to two factors: gas diffusion from deep reservoirs and the mixture of biogenic gas in shallow reservoirs. In previous studies, the carbon isotopic distribution of methane showed vertical zonation. , However, in the study area, this phenomenon was observed to be diminished. No discernible correlation was identified between the burial depth and the δ13C1 value (Figure ). Vertically, deep coal-measure strata exhibit slightly lighter methane carbon isotopes, and samples from ZK07–2 well reveal significant differences in δ13C1 values between adjacent coal seams and shales (Figure ). This phenomenon indicates suboptimal connectivity between local hydrocarbon source reservoirs, thereby impeding the free exchange of fluid. Some shale layers generally function as effective seals with favorable gas-blocking properties. Tectonic disruption modifies their integrity, and δ13C1 is influenced to some extent. Anomalously heavy δ13C1 values in certain shales correlate with high N2 concentration. High concentrations of N2 serve as a tracer for atmospheric intrusion, accompanied by the preferential escape of lighter 12CH4, the accumulation of residual 13CH4, and a decrease in CH4 concentration.

Geochemical Linkage between δ13C1 Values and CO2 Concentration

The adsorption capacity of different gas components on the matrix surface of source rocks varies significantly, with CO2 demonstrating greater adsorption capacity than CH4. This disparity results in the occurrence state and migration mechanisms of CO2 exerting a significant influence on the gas content and gas composition of source rocks. On the one hand, external CO2 injection into reservoirs induces competitive adsorption with CH4, displacing adsorbed CH4 into the free state and thereby increasing the total gas content in the reservoirs. On the other hand, if the source rock originally contained high CO2 concentrations, this indicates greater gas adsorption capacity during its geological evolution, typically correlating with higher gas content.

The CO2 content in the study area is relatively low with minimal variation, and its concentration is well below 20%. This characteristic is consistent with the mechanism of biogenic CO2, characterized by low production and no significant external inputs, suggesting the reservoir may have undergone either biogeochemical or thermochemical processes. As depicted in Figure , there is a substantial correlation between the CO2 concentration and δ13C1 values. An increase in the CO2 concentration leads to elevated δ13C1 values in shale gas but may, to some extent, decrease δ13C1 values in coalbed methane. The pyrolysis process will release a large amount of CO2 and heavier CH4, indicating that the shale gas in the study area may be well preserved and less affected by late modifications. However, an analysis of the drying coefficient (Figure ) reveals that the coal-measure gas in the study area is extra dry and is significantly affected by secondary biological effects. Moreover, there is no clear correlation between the shale gas content and δ13C1 values. The reason for this positive correlation might be the differential effects of complex geological modifications on shale gas at different depths. According to the correlation between the gas content and δ13C1 values (Figure ), it can be inferred that local coal seams may be affected by microbial activities, generating secondary biogenic gas with lighter δ13C1. Ultimately, the outcome of this process is an increase in CO2 concentration, an increase in gas content, and a decrease in δ13C1 values.

Tectonic Controls on Carbon Isotopic Fractionation and Accumulation

The geological structure plays a crucial role in influencing the coalbed methane content and significantly affects the distribution of methane carbon isotopes. As demonstrated in Figure , the gas content of coal seams in Well ZK09–2 is higher, while that in Well ZK07–2 is comparatively low. The overall structure of the study area is monocline, with the gas content being influenced by local high-angle faults and minor fold formations. As illustrated in Figure , the gas content in the turning area between the anticline and syncline far from the fault (Well ZK09–2) is higher, while that in the core of the anticline (Well ZK07–2) is comparatively low. As it is evident, the distribution characteristics of the shale gas content are essentially congruent with those of the coalbed methane content. The δ13C1 in the control area of Well ZK03–2 is comparatively heavy, while in the anticline area of the upper plate of the normal fault (Well ZK08–2), it exhibits a lighter state. It is inferred that the secondary biological effect is the primary cause of the decrease in δ13C1 values in this area, a theory that is supported by the variation characteristics of the gas content. Furthermore, the location of Well ZK08–2 in the core of the anticline, close to the fault, results in suboptimal preservation conditions, which hinder the accumulation of coal-measure gas. Consequently, the total gas content of Well ZK08–2 is lower than that of Well ZK09–2. In the fold transition zone, far from faults, the reservoirs exhibit lower permeability and a more fragmented coal body structure due to the highly concentrated stress that induces intense deformations of coal seams and complex gas diffusion paths. These conditions are conducive to the enrichment of coalbed methane and shale gas, and the zone typically has larger δ13C1 values. In the fault development zone and the core of the anticline, the sealing properties of the roof and floor of reservoirs are poor, resulting in gas escape and relatively low gas content. Concurrently, the infiltration of groundwater comprising bacteria into reservoirs may accelerate the dissolution of some coal-measure gas and processes of secondary biological effects, ultimately leading to an overall reduction in the δ13C1 values in this area.

Formation and Evolution of Coalbed Methane

The coal seams in the study area have been found to contain a high total gas content, with a high CH4 concentration, low concentrations of N2 and CO2, and low concentrations of heavy hydrocarbons. The gas desorbed from coal seams in the study area can be categorized as a typical dry gas. Concurrently, δ13C1 values were found to be lower than the theoretical value, and the overall coalbed methane is lighter and drier. Based on the above correlation analyses, it is known that there is no clear correlation between δ13C1 values and burial depth. However, δ13C1 values show a certain negative correlation with both gas content and CO2 concentration. To a certain extent, the enrichment of coalbed methane is influenced by tectonic movements and secondary biological effects.

As demonstrated in previous studies, the lightening of δ13C1 can be attributed to four primary categories: secondary biological effect, groundwater dissolution, carbon isotope exchange between CH4 and CO2, and desorption-diffusion–migration effect. ,, It is widely accepted that carbon isotope exchange of CH4 and CO2 occurs in the initial phase of coalbed methane formation, when both CH4 and CO2 concentrations are relatively high. In the study area, current reservoir conditions preclude this mechanism: the coal-measure source rocks are predominantly overmature, the coal-measure gas exhibits a lack of heavy hydrocarbons, and the CO2 concentrations are relatively low. Therefore, the carbon isotope exchange of CH4 and CO2 is excluded from the genesis model. Hydrodynamics offer a partial but insufficient explanation. Groundwater functions as a fractionation medium, acting as a weakly polar solvent that preferentially strips the more soluble 13CH4, theoretically driving the residual gas in the reservoirs lighter. The mineralization degree of groundwater in the study area is relatively low, and the local hydrodynamic force is strong. Given that solubility decreases with the carbon number, intense hydraulic washing would therefore enrich the residual phase in heavy hydrocarbons relative to methane. Crucially, however, the groundwater dissolution does not explain why coal-measure gas in the study area is extra dry. It can thus be inferred that groundwater dissolution is not the primary cause of the coalbed methane in the study area becoming drier and lighter.

As demonstrated in Figure , the analysis of burial and thermal evolution histories in the Yushe-Wuxiang block suggests that the initial large-scale hydrocarbon generation in the study area occurred in the late Triassic period. The burial depth increased rapidly, accompanied by a continuous rise in the organic matter maturity of source rocks, which reached the low-maturity stage. At this stage, the further development of microporous structures resulted in the absorption of significant quantities of hydrocarbon gases, thereby leading to a rapid increase in the gas content. It is noteworthy that the δ13C1 values of coalbed methane are generally lower compared to those observed during the high-maturity stage. After the termination of the deep burial process, the strata underwent fluctuations and rises, the diffusion effect intensified, and the gas content decreased to a certain extent. The second peak of gas generation was observed to have occurred during the temporal period spanning from the late Jurassic to the early Cretaceous. Magmatic activities resulted in a substantial increase in reservoir temperature; the source rocks rapidly reached a high-maturity stage; the gas content exhibited a rapid escalation, and the coalbed methane gradually became heavier. However, in the subsequent stage, there was an abnormally high increase in reservoir pressure, and the reservoir experienced the effects of caprock breakthrough and gas loss. The elevated pressure in the reservoir increased porosity, thereby enhancing the system’s interconnectedness. This phenomenon resulted in the migration and dissipation of gas along the pre-existing channels. The sedimentary strata in the study area have undergone continuous uplift and erosion since the Early Cretaceous period, a process that continues to the present day. Temperatures have decreased steadily, and hydrocarbon generation has ceased gradually, while diffusion has persisted. Consequently, the gas content has been decreasing overall. Tectonic movement is prone to inducing the diffusion fractionation of methane carbon isotopes. Since the polarity of 12CH4 is weaker than that of 13CH4, 12CH4 preferentially escapes upward through fissures, leading to a decrease in the gas content in deep coal seams. Conversely, 13CH4 comparatively accumulates more, and coalbed methane becomes heavier. Some 12CH4 is reabsorbed into shallow reservoirs, causing δ13C1 values to decrease and the coalbed methane to become correspondingly lighter. Additionally, bacterial infiltration into reservoirs has been shown to generate secondary biogenic gas, which can dilute and lighten the carbon isotopes. The continuous tectonic uplift and cooling since the Early Cretaceous, coupled with the active infiltration of low-salinity meteoric groundwater, provided an ideal environment for late-stage microbial colonization and secondary methanogenesis. Subsequently, a decrease in δ13C1 values was observed, accompanied by a slight increase in the gas content. In summary, the primary reasons for the coalbed methane becoming lighter and drier in the study area are the desorption-diffusion–migration effect and the secondary biological effect. It should be noted that the proposed genetic interpretation represents the dominant evolutionary processes in the study area rather than a unique or exclusive process.

Differences in the Genesis of Coalbed Methane and Shale Gas

A comparative analysis of natural gases in the study area reveals that shale gas exhibits a lower content, a lower CH4 concentration, a higher N2 concentration with a wider range of variation, and an overall slightly higher CO2 concentration compared to coalbed methane. The primary sources of N2 in natural gas have been identified as atmosphere, organic matter, and deep geothermal flow. − The N2 in the atmosphere primarily enters underground gas reservoirs in the form of dissolved gases via groundwater. Organically derived N2 is further subdivided into biogenic and pyrogenic sources. The former is defined as the biodegradation of organic matter that occurs during the low-maturity stage, and the latter is defined as the pyrolysis of organic matter that occurs during mature to high-maturity stages. Deep geothermal flow may transport deep-seated N2 to coal-measure source rocks or cause stratigraphic heating that induces high-temperature metamorphism of nitrogen-bearing minerals, thereby increasing the N2 concentration. As no helium (He) was detected in the gas composition in association with N2, and given the low mineralization of the groundwater, deep geothermal flow is excluded as the source of N2. Furthermore, the N2 content generated during the thermal evolution of organic matter is relatively low and exhibits a narrow range of variation. It can thus be inferred that the formation of shale gas reservoirs in the study area has undergone more pronounced modifications by biogenic activities and tectonic movements. The relatively confined nature of the groundwater environment is conducive to the biodegradation of organic matter, which results in elevated concentrations of N2. The abnormally high N2 concentration observed in shale gas samples indicates suboptimal preservation conditions, thereby facilitating enhanced atmospheric communication with reservoirs. Furthermore, reservoirs have undergone the persistent desorption-diffusion–migration effects, resulting in a decrease in the gas content and a significant increase in the N2 concentration.

The distribution range of the δ13C1 values for shale gas in the study area is comparable to that of coalbed methane. In the context of open tectonics, desorption-diffusion–migration effects are susceptible to occurrence, and anomalous elevation of δ13C1 values is observed under the influence of isotopic fractionation during the diffusion and migration processes. The notable isotopic discrepancy observed in shale gas across three sedimentary facies is controlled by the types of organic matter, thermal evolution of source rocks, and late modifications. The low thermal maturity of the Yanchang Formation (Ordos Basin) leads to the preferential release of lighter 12C during early stage hydrocarbon generation. In contrast, both the shale gas in this study and the marine shale gas in the Sichuan Basin have reached the overmature stage. At this stage, secondary cracking of retained hydrocarbons causes the enrichment of 13C in methane. It is widely believed that at similar thermal maturity, gases produced by humic organic matter have heavier carbon isotope characteristics than gases produced by sapropelic organic matter. Therefore, the fact that the shale gas from the Longmaxi Formation is slightly heavier in isotopic composition than that in the study area is primarily attributed to the higher upper maturity limit of the Longmaxi Formation and the significant late modifications in the Yushe-Wuxiang block.

In summary, the coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block is a compound-factor-controlled gas reservoir formed by the coupling of two main factors: first, atmospherically modified gas under geological constraints, and second, secondary biogenic gas under bioturbation. It is worth noting that, compared to coalbed methane, shale gas is more sensitive to tectonic movements and more seriously affected by atmospheric influences.

Conclusions

This study analyzed the correlation between δ13C1 values and various parameters, such as the drying coefficient, the degree of thermal evolution, gas content, burial depth, CO2 concentration, and geological structure, based on gas samples collected from a specific structural block of the Qinshui Basin. The findings of this study highlight the complex genesis mechanisms of coal-measure gas in structurally active regions and provide new insights into the identification of gas origin and favorable preservation conditions in the Qinshui Basin and similar coal-bearing basins worldwide. Although the sample size is relatively small, the samples cover multiple lithologies and a continuous burial depth interval, and the observed geochemical trends are internally consistent. Nevertheless, additional samples from a broader spatial range would further improve the understanding of coal-measure gas evolution. The following main conclusions are drawn:(1)Coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block is primarily CH4, and the other gases with higher concentrations are N2 and CO2, with very few heavy hydrocarbons, indicating a high degree of thermal evolution. The N2 concentration varies over a wide range and exhibits a substantial negative correlation with CH4, suggesting that the coal-measure gas is profoundly affected by the atmosphere.(2)The δ13C1 values are distributed between −40.412‰ and −16.524‰, with an average of −32.941‰, which is lower than the theoretical value of thermal genesis, indicating that the coal-measure gas has undergone significant late modifications. The observed combination of the high drying coefficient and the low δ13C1 value is the result of the superimposed influence of desorption-diffusion–migration effects and secondary biological effects.(3)There is a negative correlation in coal seams observed between the δ13C1 values and both the gas content and the CO2 concentration, and no clear correlation between the δ13C1 values and the burial depth. There are notable differences in geochemical characteristics between shale gas and coalbed methane. Shale gas is more sensitive to its own heterogeneity and open tectonics, with δ13C1 values showing only a strong positive correlation with the CO2 concentration. Coalbed methane and shale gas are both significantly influenced by tectonics, with faults and folds affecting gas preservation and migration. Severe gas escapes occur at the core of anticlines and fracture zones, where the δ13C1 values are lower.(4)Coal-measure gas in the Yushe-Wuxiang block is a composite gas reservoir formed by primary thermogenic gas through multiple episodes of tectonic uplift, gas escapes, atmospheric mixing, and secondary biological effects. The combined effect of early hydrocarbon generation and late modification resulted in coal-measure gas in the study area being relatively light and dry.
