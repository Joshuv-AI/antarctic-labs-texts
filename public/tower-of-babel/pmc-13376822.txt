Tracking coherent vibronic and vibrational motions in ultrafast proton transfer
Authors: Somnath Biswas, Jason W Sandwisch, Robert B Weakly, Munira Khalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecades of theoretical and experimental work on excited-state intramolecular proton transfer systems indicate that electronic-vibrational (vibronic) coupling plays a significant role in ultrafast proton transfer reactions. However, the participating atomic motions of this chemical reaction have yet to be directly observed. The challenge in observing these rapid proton transfer processes lies in simultaneously probing both the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom. Addressing these challenges requires advanced spectroscopic techniques that provide multidimensional (electronic and vibrational) structural information with femtosecond time resolution. Here, we use multidimensional electronic-vibrational spectroscopy to directly observe the vibronic motions driving the non-adiabatic excited-state proton transfer process in 10-hydroxybenzo[h]quinoline. We measure the interplay between high and low-frequency vibrations, mapping the proton transfer trajectory on two vibronically coupled electronic states. Our study enables the observation of vibronic coherence transfer, structural rearrangements, and intramolecular vibrational redistribution during and following proton transfer.Subject terms: Excited states, Infrared spectroscopy, Photochemistry

Introduction

Excited state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) is a fundamental chemical reaction that involves the migration of the lightest atom, hydrogen, from a proton donor to an acceptor group within a molecule following photoexcitation. ESIPT is ubiquitous in both chemistry and biology and plays a central role in various processes such as photo-redox chemistry, DNA and RNA mutations, energy transduction in Photosystem II, and efficient luminescence in green fluorescent proteins1–4. ESIPT reactions differ from other hydrogen exchange reactions due to their fast timescale of bond rearrangements, often occurring within a mere few to tens of femtoseconds5–8. The extremely rapid proton transfer suggests a strong coupling between electronic and vibrational motions. Extensive work on ESIPT systems establishes that the coupled electronic and vibrational motions play a crucial role in defining the multidimensional reaction coordinates of the ultrafast proton transfer9–11. Nevertheless, the participating atomic motions coupled to the electronic transitions and proton transfer coordinate have yet to be directly observed, given the experimental challenges in probing both the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom simultaneously on a sub-20 femtosecond timescale. Additionally, ESIPT often involves a large Stokes shift (~9000–11000 cm−1), which is characterized by the red-shifted fluorescence from the product state following proton transfer, making it necessary to understand the anharmonic couplings between different vibrational motions participating in ultrafast energy redistribution and their time-evolution for developing an accurate microscopic description of the proton transfer process5,11.

The intramolecularly hydrogen-bonded, 10-hydroxybenzo[h]quinoline (HBQ) molecule, is an excellent model system to study the coupling of electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom during ESIPT. It undergoes photoinduced enol (E) to keto (K) tautomerization following ESIPT within 13 fs. The ultrafast proton transfer in HBQ has been attributed to the active participation of high-frequency vibrational modes between 1200–1600 cm−1 based on fluorescence up-conversion measurements10,12. Additionally, the involvement of the high-frequency vibrations between 1000–2000 cm−1 during proton transfer in HBQ has also been predicted using atom-specific X-ray spectroscopy and quantum dynamics simulations6,13–16. Several time-resolved studies have also detected vibrational coherences of low-frequency skeletal modes ranging between 230–550 cm−1 and inferred their participation in the proton transfer10,12,17–19. Collectively, these findings indicate that the proton transfer pathway in HBQ involves multiple coupled vibrational degrees of freedom with a range of frequencies. In fact, theoretical studies predict that ring-deformation modes in the 1200–1600 cm⁻¹ frequency range strongly modulate the ring bond lengths and therefore serve as the primary reaction coordinates in ESIPT, rather than the direct transfer of the hydrogen atom itself 20. However, there is a lack of experimental data directly probing the high-frequency vibrations in the mid-infrared regime and their couplings to the photoexcited electronic states (E* and K*) and to other low-frequency vibrational degrees of freedom.

Currently, there exists a debate in the literature about the nature of ESIPT in HBQ. Experimental observations and theoretical simulations have described the ultrafast proton transfer in HBQ as a ballistic, barrierless process that occurs on an adiabatic potential energy surface21–23. However, recent experiments and quantum dynamics simulations provide an alternative perspective, suggesting proton transfer in HBQ may proceed in the non-adiabatic regime8,14,16,24. Femtosecond transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy with sub-12 fs visible pulses on HBQ reveals the presence of coherent oscillations in the spectroscopic signal, which have been interpreted as signatures of non-Born–Oppenheimer reaction dynamics through model calculations8. Quantum dynamics simulations further predict that the non-adiabatic coupling between the excited E* and K* states controls proton-transfer dynamics, where the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom move synchronously as proton transfer occurs14. While this alternative view is intriguing, direct spectroscopic evidence of non-adiabatic proton transfer is challenging and requires advanced spectroscopic techniques that provide multidimensional electronic and vibrational correlations with femtosecond time resolution.

Advances in Fourier-Transform (FT) based non-linear spectroscopies such as two-dimensional (2D) electronic (2D ES), and fifth-order visible and infrared transient 2D IR spectroscopy25–27, have enabled probing of vibronic coupling, albeit indirectly28–33. Degenerate spectroscopic techniques (e.g. 2D ES and transient 2D IR) measure correlations between transition dipole moments associated with the same degrees of freedom (either electronic or vibrational). In contrast, 2D electronic-vibrational (2D EV) spectroscopy is a two-color resonant spectroscopic technique that directly probes the cross-correlation between transition dipole moments arising from both electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom simultaneously34–38. Because the 2D EV signal is intrinsically off-diagonal, it is selective to vibronic coupling, mapping the coherently coupled vibrations (probe axis) across the frequency-resolved electronic pump axis. This explicit mapping reveals a clear picture of how electronic states are coupled with vibrations and the time-evolution of their vibronic coupling during ultrafast photochemical processes37.

In this work, using Fourier Transform 2D and 3D EV spectroscopy, we provide direct observation of vibronic motion that drives the ESIPT process in HBQ. We measure the interplay between the high and low-frequency vibrations, revealing how the proton transfer trajectory maps onto different coupled vibronic and vibrational coordinates, enabling the observation of structural rearrangements and intramolecular vibrational redistribution during and following proton transfer (Fig. 1a). Prior measurements using TA and fluorescence up-conversion experiments lack the suitable resolution and ability to probe the high-frequency vibrational motions in the fingerprint region and their interplay with low-frequency ring skeletal modes12,18,22. Our multidimensional approach of EV spectroscopy, where we pump the sample with a pair of broadband nUV (BBnUV) pulses and the use of a mid-infrared (MIR) probe pulse in the range 1300–1550 cm−1 enables tracking vibronic coherence transfer following from photoexcited enol (E*) state to keto (K*) product state along different high-frequency vibrational coordinates, and their coupling and redistribution to other lower frequency (200–1334 cm−1) vibrational modes (see Fig. 1b, c). During proton transfer, we observe the transfer of vibronic coherence from the excited E* state to the K* product state along a high-frequency (~1375 cm−1) coordinate. This proton transfer event occurs in the non-adiabatic regime, where two diabatic states are vibronically coupled, leading to an abrupt shift in the electronic state along the reaction coordinate. The proton transfer and the dephasing of vibronic coherence occur simultaneously on a timescale of ~15 fs. We additionally demonstrate that two low-frequency vibrational coordinates (550 and 770 cm−1) are coupled to the high-frequency vibronic coordinate driving proton transfer. We find efficient coherent activation of several vibrational modes (200–1334 cm−1) resulting from the multidimensional anharmonic potential energy surface, which can be viewed as coherent vibrational energy flow between vibrational modes following proton transfer. Because proton transfer to the K* product state involves highly excited vibrational modes, we also observe vibrational cooling and intramolecular vibrational relaxation following proton transfer. This report provides a detailed microscopic snapshot of ESIPT captured through the powerful capabilities of EV spectroscopy. We reveal that the ultrafast ESIPT reaction is driven by coupled multidimensional electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom, highlighting the intricate coherent dynamics of proton transfer and vibrational energy flow during and following proton transfer.

Results

Vibronic coherence transfer during ESIPT

Photoexcitation in HBQ leads to the excitation of the enol isomer from the electronic ground state to a Franck-Condon excited enol* state. This enol* state (proton-donor) irreversibly undergoes ESIPT to produce a vibrationally excited product keto* (proton-acceptor) state within 13 fs10,12. Prior experimental and theoretical work suggest that both low (<700 cm−1) frequency skeletal vibrations and high-frequency (1200–1600 cm−1) fingerprint vibrations collectively define the multidimensional reaction coordinate for proton transfer (Fig. 1a)6,10,12,13,18,22. Fig. 1b illustrates the high-frequency vibrations in the fingerprint region for HBQ in the ground enol state obtained through a ground state FTIR measurement. We resolved five vibrations, denoted in Fig. 1b as ν1338, ν1410,ν1422,ν1440,ν1465. These modes represent the combined local stretching and bending motion of the proton donor phenolic group (C-O-H), and delocalized vibrations of the backbone. The assignments of the vibrational modes are supported using the unrestricted B3LYP level of theory and 6–311++G(d,p) basis set with anharmonic corrections (see Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

To directly investigate these high-frequency vibrations and their evolution to the keto* product state during and after proton transfer, we use a mid-infrared (MIR) probe in the range between 1300–1550 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 1b, green shaded region). The principal vibrations contributing to the keto* product state in this fingerprint region are depicted in Fig. 1c. This spectrum is obtained from a transient BBnUV pump and MIR probe experiment (1D EV) at a 10 ps time delay, where the transient positive features are associated with the excited state absorption (ESA) from the keto* product state.

Three distinct ESA features at 1446 cm−1, 1386 cm−1 and 1350 cm−1 (ν1446′,ν1386′, and ν1350′) represent vibrations on the keto* state dominated by delocalized vibrational modes containing the proton-accepting C-N-H moiety (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Our prior work has described the spectral evolution of the ESA features observed in 1D EV experiments and how they reflect vibrational relaxation (vibrational redistribution and vibrational cooling) on the keto* product state39. Here, we focus on the 2D EV and 3D EV measurements to elucidate the evolution of vibronic coherence and multidimensional reaction coordinate of proton transfer. The pulse sequence for a 2D EV experiment is illustrated in the inset of Fig. 2a. Experimental details for 1D EV and 2D EV are provided in Supplementary Notes 3 and 4.

In our 2D EV experiment, two BBnUV pump pulses (Fig. 2a, shaded blue spectrum) with an inter-pulse delay of τ1 excite the red-edge of the HBQ’s electronic absorption (Fig. 2a), generating a coherence or population state, and a third-order non-linear signal is obtained after a waiting time delay of τ2. Fourier transforming the signal along τ1 produces the electronic excitation frequency axis (ω1), while the high-frequency vibrations are detected by spectrally dispersing the MIR probe pulse in a spectrometer (ω3). Therefore, the 2D EV spectrum resolves features across the excitation spectrum (ω1), revealing additional cross peaks that couple the electronic and vibrational (ω3) degrees of freedom. Observing the modulation of the 2D EV signal for a series of 2D EV spectra collected as a function of time (τ2) and a subsequent Fourier transform along τ2 produces a three-dimensional data (3D EV) cube for correlating ω1, ω3, and ω2 frequencies. The 3D EV experiment has the unique ability to resolve low-frequency vibrations (ω2) that are coupled to vibronic transitions and high-frequency vibrational modes37. It is important to note that the term ‘3D EV’ is used here to denote different representations of the same third-order nonlinear 2D EV dataset after Fourier transformation along the waiting time τ₂; and this does not imply higher-order nonlinear experiment40.

Figure 2b shows the absorptive 2D EV spectrum averaged over the first 600 fs of τ2 waiting times. The 2D EV surface shows three ESA features (red, positive signal) along the detection axis (ω3) at ν1446′,ν1386′,ν1350′, consistent with what we observed in 1D EV (Fig. 1c) measurements. However, here we detect these high-frequency vibrational features (ω3) along the entire excitation frequency axis (ω1 = 24000–25800 cm−1), revealing additional spectral signatures associated with vibronic interactions with distinct electronic states. These ESA features dominate the excitation frequency range (ω1) between 25300–25800 cm−1. Additionally, we observe three negative features (blue, negative signal) at the same ω3 frequencies but centered in the excitation frequency (ω1) range between 24200–24700 cm−1. This negative feature is red-shifted compared to the electronic absorption maximum (26200 cm−1) of HBQ (Fig. 2a) and, therefore, is not associated with the ground state bleach of the vibration. These negative features are attributed to stimulated excited state emission (ESE) signals, elucidating a coherence-to-coherence transfer pathway during ultrafast ESIPT as discussed below. It is important to note that both the ESE and ESA features arise from vibrational transitions within the electronically excited state; however, they are clearly resolved here along the electronic excitation frequency axis. Such excitation frequency resolved information cannot be obtained from 1D EV (transient IR) measurements. The ability of 2D EV to resolve excitation frequency dependent vibrational motions enables direct observation of high-frequency vibrational modes coupled to the electronically excited states.

The presence of ESE features that couple to the same ω3 transitions at lower excitation frequency compared to the ESA pathways is a notable result of our study and has not been observed before36,41–43. The negative features could arise from stimulated emission from the E* state or the K* state (Fig. 2c, d). Given the fact that our pump pulse (24000–25800 cm−1) is centered around 25000 cm−1 with FWHM of 1300 cm−1, it primarily excites the red edge of the 0–0’ transition and is unable to excite the 0–1′ transition. Even with higher vibrational excitation, the ESE features are expected to appear blue-shifted in the ω1 dimension (Fig. 2c). We note that during the photoexcitation time window (within τ1 coherence time) a coherence-to-coherence transfer from the E* to an excited vibrational (p’) state of K* state is necessary (∣E,0⟩⟨E*,0′∣→∣E,0⟩⟨K*,p′∣) to observe such negative excited stimulated emission features (Fig. 2d), which is red-shifted along the ω1 axis as observed in our experiments. See Supplementary Note 5 for a detailed discussion on the origin of the ESE features in our 2D EV study. In other words, within the τ1 coherence time, an evolution occurs from the excited E,0⟩⟨E*,0′ state to the E,0⟩⟨K*,p′ state (Fig. 2d), accessing the K* state, and resulting in the appearance of frequency-resolved, and red-shifted ESE features. Therefore, the observed ESE features originate from a distinct electronic state (i.e., not from the E* state) with a lower energy, i.e., the K* product state, implicating ultrafast vibronic coherence transfer from the E* state to a K* state during ESIPT. Possible interaction pathways, including pulse ordering effects and population transfer during τ2, and a detailed discussion on the origin of the ESE features are provided in the Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 834,44–46. We have also considered the possibility of direct excitation of the electronic states within the excitation bandwidth. However, considering the signal amplitude of the ESE and ESA feature, distribution of pump excitation bandwidth, and the expected signal strength of the 2D EV experiments, we negate this possibility as discussed in the Supplementary Note 5 (Supplementary Fig. 7). The excitation-frequency-resolved information enabled by 2D EV is essential for isolating the red-shifted ESE feature observed here and is not accessible in conventional UV pump-MIR probe transient absorption measurements.

To verify the existence of the coherence-to-coherence transfer during τ1, we perform a time-frequency analysis by applying a sliding short-window Fourier transform of the signal along the τ1 delay (See Supplementary Note 6 for details). This approach allows us to track the evolution of the ESE and ESA features as a function of the center of the window function (τ1,center). As a result, we identify the period, frequency, and dephasing of the vibronic coherence during τ1 coherence time. The signal from the resulting analysis between the excitation frequency (ω1) range 24000–24700 cm−1 (ESE region) and 25300–25800 cm−1 (ESA region) as a function of both detection frequency (ω3) and τ1,center, are shown in Fig. 3a, b, respectively. We note that the signal along three different vibrational coordinates (ν1446′,ν1386′, and ν1350′) for both the ESE and ESA region grows within a sub-10 fs time scale and oscillates as a function of τ1,center. Plotting the integrated signal around ν1446′ (Fig. 3c) for both the ESE and ESA regions clearly demonstrates these observations. The sub-10 fs growth and signal modulation during τ1 arise due to a coherence-to-coherence transfer mechanism during ESIPT. Notably, the timescale for the dephasing of the coherent modulation for both the ESE (15 ± 2 fs) and ESA (21 ± 7 fs) regions matches the timescale of proton transfer (~13 fs). Therefore, the coherence dephases as the proton transfer is completed. Our Fourier analysis is robust to the choice of window width, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10. Evidence of such sub-20 fs coherence transfer coupled to the proton transfer on a similar time scale is a signature of the non-Born-Oppenheimer (Non-BO) type dynamics of ESIPT. This is primarily because the intrinsic electronic transition time scale between the enol* and keto* form of HBQ is 3.2 fs (ΔE = 10560 cm−1)11,47. Non-BO dynamics become important when the timescales for vibrational and electronic motions are comparable, and they are strongly coupled. To find what vibrational motions are coupled to the proton transfer coordinate and responsible for the non-BO dynamics, we note that the period of oscillation of the ESE signal along τ1 center is between 22–25 fs. Therefore, the frequencies in the range of 1335–1515 cm−1 corresponding to the vibrational periods of 22–25 fs are expected to strongly participate in the proton transfer. In fact, the periodic modulation of both the ESE and ESA region can be fitted with a common high-frequency vibration of 1375 ± 50 cm−1. Therefore, this high-frequency modulation represents vibronic coherence transfer, where the excitation transition frequencies both at the ESA and ESE region are periodically modulated during proton transfer and dephases as the proton transfer is completed. These findings align with the previous description of the ESIPT in HBQ where the ultrafast nature of the proton transfer hypothesized the participation of modes between 1300–1500 cm−1 12.

Our experimental observations in Fig. 3a, b can be explained using the energy level diagram and a phenomenological model shown in Fig. 3d. Electronic excitation at ω1,ESA to the E* state is non-adiabatically coupled to the vibrationally excited K* state with a time-independent non-adiabatic coupling constant (VNA). The fitted value of VNA = 336 ± 47 cm−1 was determined to reproduce the experimental signatures, allowing excitation of a lower frequency at ω1,ESE, during τ1, consistent with our experimental observations. One can think of this as an intensity borrowing effect between a spectroscopically light (E*) and dark (K*) state as a result of non-adiabatic coupling, as detailed in Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Fig. 11. Briefly, electronic excitation at frequency ω1, ESA creates a coherent state, (E,0⟩⟨E*,0′∣) between the ground (E) and E* state. The excited enol state (E*,0) is vibronically coupled through a high-frequency coherent vibrational (ωp) motion with the vibrationally excited keto (K*,p’) state, facilitating the coherence transfer from ∣E*,0′⟩to∣K*,p′⟩. This high-frequency motion periodically modulates VNA and consequently the proton transfer coordinate, and dephases as proton transfer completes. We describe the experimental signal modulation assuming the presence of two diabatic potential energy surfaces of E* and K*, vibronically coupled through a high-frequency coherent vibrational motion (ωp). Using this framework and performing a similar time-frequency analysis during τ1, we can capture the experimentally observed periodic time evolution with a frequency of 1370 ± 50 cm−1 and dephasing of the states within 13 ± 2 fs (Inset Fig. 3c). This suggests that this high-frequency vibronic coherent motion serves as the primary coordinate during proton transfer. While our phenomenological description captures our experimental findings, an accurate non-adiabatic description of the proton transfer, and involvement of the atomic displacements requires rigorous non-equilibrium quantum dynamic simulations accounting for the combination of all skeletal and ring deformation vibrational modes of HBQ. Such simulations are beyond the scope of the present study; however, our experimental results and approach aim to inspire future method developments for exact non-adiabatic quantum dynamics simulations6.

The observation of τ1 coherence transfer confirms the breakdown of the BO approximation during ultrafast ESIPT on a sub-20 fs time scale. Our 2D EV measurements sensitive to both the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom of HBQ enable a direct experimental demonstration of non-adiabatic ESIPT dynamics beyond the BO approximation. Our results confirm the previous theoretical predictions and indirect spectroscopic evidence of non-adiabatic ESIPT dynamics in HBQ6,8,14,24. A Fourier transform of the experimental signal modulation for the excited state emission reveals that this signal could be modulated with frequencies down to 500 cm−1, with a broad centered high-frequency peak near 1365 cm−1, and low-frequency features between 600–900 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 9). This indicates that the high-frequency proton transfer coordinate is coupled to other low-frequency coordinates.

Vibrational coherence and intramolecular vibrational relaxation following ESIPT

To identify the low-frequency vibrational coordinates (ω2) coupled with the observed electronic transitions (ω1), and high-frequency vibrational coordinates (ω3), 2D EV frequency-frequency (ω1-ω3) correlation maps were experimentally collected at various pump-probe waiting times (τ2), sampled at 12.5 fs intervals over the first 600 fs. The population kinetics were subtracted to isolate the residual coherent oscillations. By performing a Fourier transform on these coherent oscillations along τ2, we generated a 3D frequency correlation map (ω1-ω3-ω2). This approach allows us to track the time evolution and coupling of low-frequency vibrational coordinates (ω2) within the Nyquist limit (200–1334 cm⁻¹) with the electronic and high-frequency vibrational coordinates during and following proton transfer. Therefore, in addition to identifying the high-frequency vibrational coordinates and vibronic coherence transfer with 2D EV, our 3D EV experiments elucidate the ESIPT mechanism along multidimensional coupled vibrational coordinates. However, it is important to emphasize that our representation of the 3DEV data does not refer to a different or higher-order nonlinear experiment. Rather, it reflects an alternative representation of the same third-order dataset, obtained by Fourier transformation along the waiting time (τ₂), which reveals an additional low-frequency dimension coupled to the detected high-frequency dimension as a consequence of anharmonic and vibronic coupling.

Figure 4a presents the low-frequency (ω2) vibrational motions coupled to the high-frequency (ω3) vibrational mode observed at 1446 cm⁻¹ along both the ESE and ESA regions (ω1). We observe several low-frequency vibrational modes between 200 and 1334 cm⁻¹, primarily centered around the excited state absorption region (ω1 = 25,500 cm⁻¹, Fig. 4a bottom panel). In contrast, there are only two (550 and 770 cm−1) low-frequency vibrational modes above the noise level in the excited state emission region (ω1 = 24,500 cm⁻¹ Fig. 4a top panel). A detailed noise analysis of the coherent vibrational amplitude is provided in the Supplementary Note 8. To visualize the presence of low-frequency vibrational modes in both the ESE and ESA regions, we plot slices of the 3D EV cube at ω2 frequencies of 550 cm⁻¹, 770 cm⁻¹, and 290 cm⁻¹ in Fig. 4b–d, respectively. Additional plots for other ω2 frequencies are provided in Supplementary Fig. 12.

We observe an appreciable amplitude of coherent vibrational oscillations above the noise in both the ESE and ESA regions for the modes at ω2 = 550 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 4b) and 770 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 4c). Since the photoexcitation at ESE and ESA is vibronically coupled along the proton transfer coordinate through a high-frequency vibrational motion (1375 ± 50 cm−1), our experiments reveal that the 550 cm−1 and 770 cm−1 low-frequency vibrational modes are coupled with the proton transfer coordinate. To help visualize the vibrational motions, TD-DFT-calculated vibrational modes for high-frequency (1446 cm⁻¹) and low-frequency vibrations (550 and 770 cm⁻¹) on the K* state are shown in Fig. 4e. The 770 cm⁻¹ mode is assigned to the in-plane asymmetric ring-breathing mode. Previous impulsive Raman measurements have also associated this mode with non-adiabatic ESIPT beyond the Born-Oppenheimer approximation8. Additionally, the mode around 550 cm⁻¹ is an in-plane symmetric ring-breathing mode that changes the distance between the proton and oxygen, demonstrating its participation as a potential acceptor mode during proton transfer. It is important to note that these wavepacket motions have maintained coherence in the product state (beating during τ2), suggesting that vibrational coherence is transferred following the very fast (~13 fs) proton transfer. The transfer of vibrational/vibronic coherence in ultrafast photochemical reactions has been previously observed in cases of ultrafast charge separation, bacterial reaction center, intersystem crossing, and curve crossings48–53. Here, we report this phenomenon for the first time in the context of ESIPT. This finding underscores the importance of coherence phenomena to drive ultrafast dynamics and can potentially determine the outcome of photochemical reactions. We observe that, the coherent vibrational amplitude for ω2 = 290 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 4d) and all other ω2 vibrations (Supplementary Fig. 12) is primarily localized in the ESA absorption region (ω1 = 25,500 cm⁻¹), indicating that these vibrational motions are coherently excited on the product K* surface following proton transfer. Below, we describe the primary origin of their generation post-proton transfer.

Non-adiabatic proton transfer impulsively alters the electronic structure along the reaction coordinate. Therefore, our 3D EV measurements can map out the coherent intramolecular vibrational energy flow and vibrational cooling following proton transfer. The amplitude modulation of the ESA features illustrates the generation of coherent low-frequency vibrations coupled to the high-frequency coordinates following proton transfer.

These findings suggest robust multidimensional coherent intramolecular vibrational energy flow following proton transfer. The strong anharmonic nature of the potential energy surface results in momentum transfer across multiple vibrational coordinates, generating coherent vibrational motion along the multidimensional potential energy K* surface50,54,55. Within this framework, we can describe the anharmonic coupling between vibrational modes as the primary mechanism for coherent energy flow and distribution following proton transfer. The impulsive nature of the non-adiabatic proton transfer ensures that quantum coherent effects (phase coherence) remain active in the vibrational relaxation following proton transfer. The generation of lower frequency vibrations at 372 cm⁻¹, 445 cm⁻¹, 551 cm⁻¹, 770 cm⁻¹, and 1003 cm−1 is consistent with previous reports8,10,12,18. Additionally, our 3D EV measurements with a MIR probe report on the generation of vibrations with frequencies of 292 cm−1, 635 cm−1, 854 cm−1, 1050 cm−1 and 1230 cm⁻¹. The assignments of these modes are provided in Table 1 of the Supplementary Note 2, where we note that most of these modes are associated with in-plane ring deformation modes in the Keto* product state. The presence of such multidimensional coherent vibrational modes in the observed product state emphasizes the strong anharmonic character of the vibrations in HBQ; which is uniquely accessed in a 3D EV measurement through coupling with electronic transitions (ω1) and high-frequency vibrational modes (ω3). Akin to the case of 1446 cm−1 mode discussed here, we observe the generation of several lower-frequency vibrational modes along other high-frequency coordinates (1350 and 1386 cm−1) and described in detail in Supplementary Note 9.

Several previous experiments based on optical transient absorption, fluorescence up-conversion measurements, and simulations indicated the presence of low-frequency (230–550 cm−1) vibrational motions in ultrafast ESIPT in HBQ, however, their role in ESIPT reaction is an open question8,10,12,17,18. Here we find that low-frequency (ω2) skeletal vibrational modes with frequencies 550 cm−1 and 770 cm−1 are coupled to the high-frequency proton transfer coordinate, suggesting their participation during proton transfer. Additionally, several other low-frequency vibrational modes are excited on the K* product state following proton transfer reaction, indicating their participation in intramolecular vibrational redistribution and vibrational cooling as described below. The ability to resolve both the ESE and ESA features along the entire electronic excitation frequency axis (ω1) enables us to elucidate the unique role of different low-frequency vibrational motions in ultrafast ESIPT, which was previously challenging to address.

To further investigate the dynamic evolution of the observed frequencies in the K* product state, we performed a time-frequency analysis using the short-time Fourier transform. The details of the analysis and testing of its robustness are provided in Supplementary Note 9. Our findings indicate that the dephasing of the vibrational coherence occurs on a timescale of a few hundred femtoseconds (300–400 fs). This timescale aligns with intramolecular vibrational redistribution (IVR) as reported in previous experimental and non-adiabatic simulation studies6,18. After proton transfer, we observe the redistribution of vibrational energy along low-frequency vibrational coordinates, with coherent motions dephasing as the IVR process completes. Additionally, we observe dynamic blue shifts along several low-frequency vibrational modes that are coupled to all the observed high-frequency vibrational coordinates. Notably, blue shifts are observed in the low-frequency modes coupled to 1446 cm−1 (Fig. 4f), specifically for modes around 290 cm−1, 445 cm−1, 820 cm−1, 1003 cm−1, and 1230 cm−1. The description for the case of modes 1386 cm−1 and 1350 cm−1 has been provided in the Supplementary Note 9. The magnitude of the blue shift ranges from 20 to 100 cm−1, depending on the anharmonicity of the modes, and is consistent with the shifts observed during vibrational cooling37,56. This observation further suggests that the low-frequency vibrational modes are excited as a result of the anharmonic couplings along the non-adiabatically coupled multidimensional enol* and keto* potential energy surfaces, and proton transfer is associated with strong vibrational redistribution and energy dissipation. Dynamic blue shifts and vibrational cooling are also observed along the 1350 cm⁻¹ and 1386 cm⁻¹ modes, as described in Supplementary Note 9 and Supplementary Fig. 15.

Discussion

Resolving the interplay between the complex intramolecular coupling of electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom in photoinduced ultrafast proton transfer has been an outstanding experimental challenge due to the multidimensional nature of the reaction coordinate during and following proton transfer. The powerful capability of 3D EV spectroscopy allows us to elucidate the detailed reaction pathways, as described in Fig. 5.

Mechanism of proton transfer and intramolecular vibrational relaxation along multidimensional potential surfaces following ESIPT.

a Vibronic coherence transfer occurs across the high-frequency vibrational coordinate (1375 cm−1) during proton transfer from the E* state to K* state. The coherence dephases as the proton transfer is complete on a time scale of ~ 15 fs. b Efficient activation of the coherent vibrational modes between 200–1334 cm−1 following proton transfer along the multidimensional reaction coordinates as a result of strong anharmonic coupling. Inset shows the generation of the wavepacket and its subsequent path through the multidimensional anharmonic potential energy surfaces including intramolecular vibrational relaxation and vibrational cooling. The impulsive nature of the non-adiabatic proton transfer ensures that quantum coherent effects remain active i.e. the wavepacket remains phase coherent on the K* state.

During proton transfer, we observe the transfer of vibronic coherence from the excited E* state to the K* product state along the high-frequency vibronic coordinate (~1375 cm−1). This proton transfer event occurs in the non-adiabatic regime (beyond the Born-Oppenheimer approximation), where two diabatic states are coupled vibronically, leading to the transition of the electronic state along the reaction coordinate. The proton transfer and the dephasing of the vibronic coherence occur simultaneously on a timescale of ~15 fs. This picture is consistent with predicted non-adiabatic quantum dynamics simulations where the electronic and vibrational degrees of freedom move synchronously as proton transfer occurs14. Our experimental observations and simulations clearly elucidate the non-adiabatic nature and transfer of vibronic coherence along a high-frequency coordinate during proton transfer. Additionally, we demonstrate that two low-frequency (550 and 770 cm−1) vibrational coordinates are coupled to the high-frequency vibronic coordinate. Following proton transfer, we observe that efficient coherent activation of different low-frequency skeletal modes (200–1334 cm−1) results from the multidimensional anharmonic potential energy surface, which can be viewed as coherent vibrational energy flow between vibrational modes following proton transfer. Since the proton transfer to the K* product state involves highly excited vibrational modes, we observe vibrational cooling following proton transfer.

Previous quantum dynamics simulations, TA, and time-resolved fluorescence experiments demonstrated conflicting interpretations regarding the role of low-frequency (240–684 cm−1) vibrations in proton transfer8,10,12,17,18,57. Several studies have predicted that these low-frequency modes contribute to ultrafast proton transfer reactions, particularly within the framework of the skeletal-mode-assisted, barrierless proton transfer model. However, recently it has been argued that these vibrational motions may instead be generated as a consequence of impulsive proton transfer in HBQ, without directly contributing to proton transfer57. Our 3DEV data and simulations resolve this by directly observing the involvement of the several low-frequency vibrations in proton transfer through analysis of the coupled multidimensional vibrational coordinates. As detailed in our results, we observe the active participation of specific low-frequency vibrational modes (notably at 550 and 770 cm⁻¹), evidenced by the transfer of vibrational coherence following proton transfer. Furthermore, the 3D EV analysis reveals several coherently excited low-frequency skeletal modes (ranging from 200 to 1334 cm⁻¹), indicating that these vibrations are indeed excited by the proton transfer event. Importantly, these low-frequency modes are strongly coupled to the high-frequency vibrations in the K* state, supporting a picture of highly coupled ultrafast vibrational relaxation and structural reorganization that follows proton transfer.

The role of vibrational redistribution in ESIPT complexes remains a subject of interest and has not been fully understood because of the multidimensional nature of the energy dissipation following ESIPT1,58. Intramolecular vibrational redistribution and other vibrationally induced thermal relaxation mechanisms in ESIPT have been thought to contribute to large Stokes shifts, characteristics of ESIPT systems. For example, low-temperature fluorescence measurements of 2-(2-Hydroxyphenyl)benzothiazole (HBT) derivative similar to HBQ revealed a decrease in the Stokes shift at lower temperatures58,59. This observation was found to depend on the degree of intramolecular charge transfer within the system, indicating that vibrational redistribution and vibronic coupling are strongly correlated with the Stokes shift in ESIPT systems, including HBQ. While the magnitude of the Stokes shift is generally determined by differences in electronic structure between the E* and K* states, in the present system, this electronic reorganization is intrinsically coupled to strong vibrational excitation and anharmonic relaxation following ESIPT. The ESIPT process populates multiple vibrational modes and drives substantial structural rearrangement and intramolecular vibrational redistribution, leading to strong vibronic coupling in the excited state. The presence of several different low-frequency skeletal modes (200–1334 cm−1) clearly highlights that the observed high-frequency modes participate in a strongly coupled ultrafast vibrational relaxation and structural reorganization following proton transfer. This extensive anharmonic coupling allows the proton transfer state to access multiple degrees of freedom for energy dissipation, explaining the remarkably large Stokes shift (~9000–11000 cm−1) observed in fluorescence measurements11,47. Therefore, the observed Stokes shift reflects vibrational relaxation on a strongly anharmonic excited-state potential energy surface, rather than a pure electronic structural rearrangements. Notably, such strong anharmonic coupling and coherent vibrational energy flow have also been observed along other high-frequency vibrational coordinates (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). This extensive degree of coupling along multidimensional vibrational and electronic coordinates also ensures the ultrafast impulsive nature of the proton transfer.

While multiple previous experiments and theoretical work on HBQ suggested the involvement of several coupled vibrational degrees of freedom with a range of frequencies (230–2000 cm−1), our results directly observe such a coupled multidimensional reaction coordinate for ultrafast ESIPT for a widely studied model system8,10,12,17,18. Of particular significance, the participation of multidimensional vibronic reaction coordinates is not unique to ESIPT systems but has also been observed in other processes, including singlet fission and symmetry-breaking charge separation, where vibronic/vibrational coherence plays a crucial role in electron dynamics60–63. While challenging, the observation of the reaction coordinates (vibrations/phonons) driving ultrafast chemical reactions in molecules (charge/energy transfer, spin transition, photo isomerization) and photoinduced phenomena in complex materials (polaron formation, phase transitions, etc.) is one of the biggest unresolved problems in physical chemistry. By directly mapping the interplay between high- and low-frequency modes during an ESIPT reaction, our 3D EV experiments provide a detailed picture of how vibronic and vibrational structural reorganization govern ultrafast ESIPT reactions. These insights contribute to a deeper understanding of energy flow in complex molecular systems, which is essential for fields ranging from photochemistry to molecular electronics and charge and energy transfer in complex materials and biological systems.

Methods

Experimental descriptions

The details of the experiments are provided in Supplementary Materials, here we briefly describe the experimental methods. Spectroscopic measurements were conducted on 50 mM 10-hydroxybenzo[h]quinoline (HBQ) dissolved in tetrachloroethylene (PCE). The UV-vis and FTIR linear spectroscopic characterization were performed inside an FTIR stationary cell containing two 4 mm thick CaF2 windows separated by a 50 μm Teflon spacer. The pulses used in the ultrafast experiments are split from a fundamental Ti:Sapphire regenerative amplifier (800 nm, 3.85 W, ~38 fs, 1 kHz). The broadband near ultraviolet (BBnUV) pump pulse was generated from the second harmonic of a spectrally- broadened (five 100 μm thick fused silica plates) fundamental pulse (430 μJ, 38 fs, 800 nm). The BBnUV was linearly chirped using a UV prism compressor before being compressed and shaped by an acoustic optic programmable dispersive filter pulse shaper. The mid-IR probe was generated from the difference frequency generation of the near-IR and idler outputs of a home-built collinear two-stage optical parametric amplifier and is routed to the sample area via a delay stage for τ2 – dependent experiments. The mid-IR signal (1300–1550 cm−1) was routed to a spectrometer and dispersed onto one strip of a 2 × 64 mercury cadmium telluride pixel array. The difference signal was obtained by subtracting the “pump off” spectrum from the “pump on” spectrum using a 500 Hz mechanical chopper, which blocks every alternate laser shot. The phase and amplitude of the shaped BBnUV pulses were optimized by transient grating cross-correction frequency-resolved optical gating (TG XFROG) in a 25 μm thick fused silica plate. The resulting FROG retrieval elucidated a FROG error of 0.0036, temporal FWHM of 23.3 fs, spectral FWHM of 8.0 nm, and a time- bandwidth product FWHM of 0.355. 2D EV measurements were performed in the pump-probe geometry, where the third-order non-linear signal is self-heterodyne detected and provides the absorptive line shape. Our implementation incorporates the waveform streaming option (Fastlite UV-Dazzler) in our pulse shaper and conducts a 2 ×1 x 1 phase scheme (0 and π) to isolate the absorptive, real component of the non-linear polarization. We observe negligible phase distortions and observe good agreement between the under-sampled transformed autocorrelation and that of the pump spectrum. The pump autocorrelation is also in good agreement with the TG XFROG temporal duration for the pump pulses used in the 2D EV experiments. The instrument response (~185 fs) was measured through a well-averaged non-resonant integrated time-resolved infrared (tIR) signal on 250 μm thick silicon wafer.

2D and 3D EV data

2D EV spectrum was obtained from the τ1-dependent change in the absorption data set. This was generated by implementing a phase cycling scheme described in the Supplementary Materials. The resulting time-domain signal resembles the pump autocorrelation. A hyperbolic tangent function was then applied to smooth the data at long τ1 values (apodization). The FT over the spectrally detected, τ1-dependent vibrational differential absorption data yields the electronic excitation spectrum, ω1. The processed time-dependent signal was Fourier transformed, and the real part of the Fourier transform was obtained for each of the 3,000 2D EV spectra collected. The high-frequency vibrational detection dimension, (ω3, 1300–1550 cm−1), is measured using a spectrometer. To identify the low-frequency vibrational coordinates (ω2) that are coupled with the high-frequency vibrational coordinates, 2D EV frequency-frequency (ω1-ω3) correlation maps were experimentally obtained at various pump-probe waiting times (τ2). The population kinetics were subtracted to isolate the remaining coherent oscillations during τ2. By performing a Fourier transform on these coherent oscillations along τ2 for all ω1 and ω3 axes, a 3D frequency correlation map (ω1-ω3-ω2) was created.

Computational details

Vibrational mode assignments of HBQ were performed using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations on the first excited electronic state of HBQ. The ground state structure of HBQ was optimized with a tight convergence criterion using the unrestricted B3LYP level of theory and the 6–311++G(d,p) basis set, which accurately represents the enol ground state. Electronic transitions and vibrational frequencies were calculated with the B3LYP functional and the Aldrich Def2-TZVP basis set. Excited state vibrational modes were obtained through TD-DFT calculations of the first excited state. Vibrational mode calculations for both ground and excited states included anharmonic corrections for all modes, performed on the Hyak supercomputer at the University of Washington. All calculated vibrational modes returned non-negative transition energies with exact frequencies. The optimization of the first excited state structure revealed a notable keto-like geometry. A list and description of relevant vibrational modes are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.
