From profound hypothermia to malignant hyperpyrexia: a case report of biphasic thermoregulatory crisis following acute high cervical spinal cord injury
Authors: Nimasha Ekanayaka, Shamila De Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThermoregulatory dysfunction is a well-recognized yet underreported consequence of high cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). Patients may present with extreme temperature disturbances, ranging from profound hypothermia to malignant hyperthermia, reflecting severe autonomic dysregulation. Early recognition is crucial, as these abnormalities can rapidly become life-threatening. Case presentationA 55-year-old previously healthy man was found unconscious outdoors in the rain. Initial assessment revealed profound hypothermia (31 °C), bradycardia (48 bpm), hypotension (70/50 mmHg), and absent shivering. He was resuscitated with warmed intravenous fluids, external rewarming, hydration and vasopressor support. Neurological evaluation after stabilization demonstrated complete quadriparesis with sensory loss below C5 and bowel and bladder involvement (ASIA Grade A). Imaging revealed C5–C6 retrolisthesis with spinal cord compression.During hospital monitoring, he developed rapid-onset hyperpyrexia (42.2 °C) without sweating, refractory to conventional cooling, ultimately resulting in cardio-respiratory arrest. Extensive evaluation excluded infectious, metabolic, and drug-related causes, suggesting neurogenic/ quad fever secondary to high cervical SCI.ConclusionsThis case illustrates the biphasic and rapidly progressive nature of autonomic thermoregulatory dysfunction in acute high cervical SCI. Neurogenic fever, though uncommon, may occur within hours and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Continuous temperature monitoring, early hemodynamic stabilization, and high clinical suspicion are critical, particularly in resource-limited settings. Background

Autonomic dysfunction is a well-recognized consequence of cervical and upper thoracic spinal cord injury (SCI), where disruption of sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways results in widespread physiological instability [1, 2]. This dysregulation can affect multiple organ systems: cardiovascular dysfunction (neurogenic shock, bradycardia, hypotension, or paroxysmal hypertension), respiratory insufficiency due to diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle paralysis, and severe thermoregulatory disturbances including hypothermia or hyperpyrexia [3].

Thermoregulatory dysfunction is characterized by impaired hypothalamic control of heat balance, resulting in failure to maintain core temperature homeostasis [4]. Although hyperthermia is more frequently described, both hypo- and hyperthermia are recognized complications of SCI. Fever following SCI is reported in approximately 50.6% of patients, with around 8% having no identifiable infectious or inflammatory source, a phenomenon termed neurogenic fever or “quad fever” [5]. This condition, which occurs in approximately 1 of every 20 to 25 patients who sustain acute SCI, [6, 7] is characterized by an abrupt and extreme rise in core temperature (> 40.8 °C/105.4 °F) in the absence of infection, drug reaction, or other systemic cause. Despite its relative frequency and associated high morbidity, limited scientific literature exists to guide preventative or therapeutic measures.

Conversely, high cervical SCI may cause distributive neurogenic shock with profound peripheral vasodilation and impaired thermogenesis, predisposing to severe hypothermia when exposed to cold environments. This may mask the impending onset of hyperthermia and delay recognition of hyperpyrexic crises in SCI [6]. Regular and continuous temperature monitoring is therefore essential during the acute phase of high SCI.

We report an unusual case of a 55-year-old man presenting with profound environmental hypothermia due to overnight exposure, later diagnosed with high cervical SCI complicated by tetraparesis and subsequent fulminant hyperpyrexia reaching 42.2 °C (108 °F) within 12 h of admission.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old person without known medical comorbidities was found unconscious, lying prone in an open area during rainfall, and was brought to the emergency department.

During initial assessment, the GCS was 8/15 (unresponsive to verbal stimuli). Vital signs were heart rate of 48 bpm, blood pressure of 70/50 mmHg, body temperature of 31 °C (87.8 °F) with cold peripheries but absent shivering. Random blood glucose was 176 mg/dL, and arterial blood gas showed a normal pH level without identifiable correctable elements.

He was immediately resuscitated with warmed intravenous fluids, active external rewarming (Bair Hugger blanket), and hemodynamic stabilization, including vasopressor support. ECG revealed marked sinus bradycardia with characteristic Osborn waves (Fig. 1). Non-contrast CT brain excluded intracranial haemorrhage, infarction or traumatic brain injury.

ECG demonstrating marked sinus bradycardia with Osborn (J) waves, consistent with hypothermia which resolved with gradual rewarming

After regaining consciousness, the patient gave a history of falling from a height of approximately six feet the previous night around 11:00 p.m, immediately losing motor function in all four limbs. Unable to move, he had remained outdoors, exposed to rain throughout the night. A plain cervical spine radiograph showed no fracture or dislocation. As MRI was not immediately available, a CT cervical spine was performed, demonstrating retrolisthesis at the C5–C6 level with significant spinal canal narrowing and cord compression (Fig. 2). The orthopedic and neurosurgical teams were urgently consulted and MRI scanning with surgical decompression were planned.

Early hospital course

Continuous temperature monitoring revealed a fever spike (38.0 °C/ 1000F) approximately three hours after normothermia was achieved (i.e., 10 h after presentation). This short interval underscores the rapid transition from one extreme of thermal dysregulation to the other. The patient remained hemodynamically stable (heart rate 96 bpm, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg) and asymptomatic, without chills or rigors. Clinical examination and systemic review revealed no localizing focus of Infection. Laboratory investigations showed WBC 8 × 109/µL, platelets 225 × 109/µL, CRP < 2 mg/L. Creatine kinase was 183 U/L. Serum sodium 136 mmol/l, and potassium was 4.5 mmol/l. Blood and urine cultures were obtained.

He received a single prophylactic dose of IV ceftriaxone and continued intravenous Hartmann’s solution at 100 mL/h while being kept nil orally in preparation for surgery. Pharmacological DVT prophylaxis and high-dose methylprednisolone were withheld pending definitive MRI imaging to assess for intraspinal hematoma and in alignment with current pre-operative protocols for traumatic SCI.

Hyperthermic crisis

Over the next two hours, the patient’s clinical status deteriorated. He became drowsy, appeared flushed and warm to touch, and was noted to have new-onset hyperpyrexia. Remarkably, he exhibited an absence of sweating despite extreme temperature elevation. Core temperature was measured at 42.2 °C (108 °F) in the axilla using a digital thermometer.

Aggressive cooling was initiated immediately, including cold sponging, axillary ice packs, and fan-assisted evaporative cooling. Since he had already received oral paracetamol within the past two hours, no additional antipyretics were administered. Despite these measures, the temperature remained persistently elevated at 42.2 °C.

Hemodynamically, he became tachycardic (heart rate 148 bpm) and hypotensive (blood pressure 88/48 mmHg). Arterial blood gas analysis showed acute type 2 (hypercapnic) respiratory failure with pH 7.28, PO₂ 60 mmHg, PCO₂ 68 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 22.8 mmol/L, and lactate 1.1 mmol/L. No clinical or biochemical evidence of infection was present. He was not on any medication known to induce drug fever.

The anesthesia team was urgently called for airway protection and Intubation. However, despite maximal pre-oxygenation attempts, the patient could not be adequately ventilated and progressed to cardiorespiratory arrest. Resuscitative efforts were unsuccessful.

Discussion

This case illustrates extreme biphasic thermoregulatory instability, initial profound hypothermia followed by extreme hyperpyrexia, within hours of presentation in a patient with high cervical SCI.

Autonomic dysfunction is a well-recognized consequence of cervical and upper thoracic SCI, manifesting as thermal dysregulation that is particularly unpredictable [1, 4]. Fever in high SCI is hypothesized to result from disruption of the neural pathways connecting the spinal cord and hypothalamic temperature-regulating centers [5]. Autonomic dysregulation is most profound in lesions above the major splanchnic sympathetic outflow (T4–T6) where sympathetic disruption is maximal [8]. In addition to disruption of sympathetic pathways, hypothalamic dysfunction mediated by inflammatory cytokines and other neurochemical mediators following SCI may further impair central thermoregulatory control, contributing to the development of neurogenic hyperthermia.

Hypothermia, defined as a core body temperature below 35 °C, occurs when heat loss exceeds heat production, resulting in failure to maintain homeostasis. While common causes include environmental cold exposure and impaired thermogenesis, additional risk factors include extremes of age, hypoglycemia, malnutrition, and endocrine dysfunction. Importantly, excessive peripheral vasodilation secondary to nervous system injury, such as traumatic SCI or cerebrovascular insults, has been well documented as a contributor to hypothermia [9]. Clinical manifestations depend on severity: moderate hypothermia (28–32 °C) is associated with cognitive impairment, lethargy, hyporeflexia, pupillary sluggishness or dilation, hypotension, bradycardia, and bradypnea. Severe hypothermia (< 28 °C) leads to progressive cerebral hypoperfusion, unresponsiveness, and cardiorespiratory failure [9].

Neurogenic shock represents a distributive form of shock arising from loss of sympathetic vasomotor control, particularly in injuries at or above T6 [10]. The resultant combination of loss of sympathetic tone and unopposed parasympathetic activity produces the classic triad of hypotension, bradycardia, and peripheral vasodilation. Patients in neurogenic shock are predisposed to hypothermia, particularly when exposed to cold environments, due to impaired vasoconstriction and heat generation. Persistent bradycardia (HR < 60 bpm) is common, and marked bradycardia (< 45 bpm) is frequently observed in severe cervical spinal cord injury; in one series, it occurred in about 71% of patients with severe cervical lesions, compared with significantly lower rates in milder cervical and thoracolumbar injuries [11].

Acute management of severe high cervical SCI requires a careful balance between immediate supportive measures and mitigating secondary risks. Consistent with contemporary international guidelines, high-dose methylprednisolone was not administered. Furthermore, while the risk of venous thromboembolism is exceptionally high in motor-complete SCI (ASIA A), pharmacological deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis was temporarily deferred. This cautious approach was implemented upon initial assessment and pending definitive MRI imaging to rule out an expanding post-traumatic intraspinal hematoma that could be exacerbated by anticoagulation, and in accordance with pre-operative protocols for spine trauma [12].

Following initial stabilization, the patient rapidly progressed to a fulminant hyperthermic crisis (42.2 °C) without other systemic cause, consistent with a diagnosis of neurogenic fever (quad fever). Quad fever is characterized by an abrupt and extreme rise in core temperature (> 40.8 °C/105.4 °F) in the absence of infection, drug reaction, or other systemic cause. Several case reports and small series have described fatal hyperthermia associated with high spinal cord injury. Ulger et al. described a case series highlighting fatal outcomes due to thermoregulatory dysfunction after acute traumatic SCI, with a reported incidence of quad fever in 28% of patients, while other case reports have similarly documented extreme hyperpyrexia consistent with quad fever [13]. Quad fever may occur at any stage following SCI: during acute care, inpatient rehabilitation, or even post-discharge [3]. A distinctive and clinically important feature of this case is the biphasic thermoregulatory disturbance occurring within 12 h, with rapid transition from profound hypothermia to extreme hyperpyrexia.

Hyperpyrexia in this context induces cerebral hypoperfusion and disrupts central homeostatic mechanisms. At the cellular level, extreme temperatures (40–41 °C) can cause protein denaturation, altered signal transduction, and widespread cellular injury. Other systemic consequences include coagulopathy, hypovolemia, acute tubular necrosis, hepatocellular injury progressing to severe necrosis, cerebral edema, extensive neuronal loss and gliosis, adrenal hemorrhage, bone marrow suppression with thrombocytopenia, and intravascular hemolysis [6, 7]. Achieving normothermia is therefore essential to provide cardioprotection and neuroprotection, interrupting the vicious cycle of thermal dysregulation [5, 14]. Experimental data support the deleterious impact of hyperthermia on SCI outcomes. Yu et al. demonstrated that animals maintained at elevated temperatures (39.5 °C) exhibited greater neurological deficits compared with those kept at normothermia (37 °C) [15]. Even mild hyperthermia post-SCI can exacerbate tissue injury and compromise functional recovery. Physiologically, elevations in core body temperature are associated with substantial increase in metabolic rate; contemporary human data suggest that a 0C rise in deep body temperature can increase whole-body metabolic rate by approximately 11–23%, reflecting enhanced oxygen consumption and energy demand at higher temperatures [16, 17].

The pathophysiological sequence leading to death in this patient was likely multifactorial. In cervical spinal injury at the C5 level, diaphragmatic function may be partially compromised due to disruption of descending respiratory motor pathways, predisposing to ventilatory insufficiency. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated acute hypercapnic respiratory failure with pCO2 68 mmHg, suggesting progressive hypoventilation. Hypercapnia itself may contribute to altered sensorium, autonomic instability, and cardiovascular collapse. The relatively low lactate level observed in this patient probably indicates that profound systemic hypoperfusion had not yet developed at the time of sampling, suggesting that respiratory failure and central autonomic dysregulation are the dominant mechanisms preceding cardiac arrest. Therefore, the fatal outcome likely reflected a combination of severe thermoregulatory dysfunction and ventilatory failure in the context of acute high SCI.

Peak temperature of 42.2 °C was measured using an axillary digital thermometer. Peripheral temperature measurements may underestimate true core body temperature, particularly in critically ill or hemodynamically unstable patients at extreme temperature ranges. In our setting, continuous core temperature monitoring methods were not available during the hyperthermic crisis. Therefore, the recorded value represents the peripheral measurement, and the true core temperature may have differed. For critically ill patients, central measurements from sites like the distal esophagus, pulmonary artery, or rectum are considered the gold standard for reliably guiding therapeutic cooling interventions and ensuring accurate thermal assessment [18].

In our patient, initial laboratory evaluation showed normal white blood cell count and minimal inflammatory marker elevation. Blood and urine cultures were obtained at presentation, and retrospective review after completion of the full incubation period confirmed that both cultures remained negative. Creatine kinase levels were 183 U/L, which did not suggest significant rhabdomyolysis. Although endocrine causes such as thyroid storm or adrenal crisis were considered clinically, there were no supportive clinical or laboratory evidence. He was previously healthy and was not on any medications known to induce drug fever. Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that hyperpyrexia was most consistent with quad fever secondary to high SCI.

Definitive surgical decompression was not performed earlier due to logistical and resource constraints inherent to the treating setting. In our institution, pre-operative MRI is mandatory prior to cervical spine surgery to accurately delineate the extent of spinal cord injury, exclude intraspinal hematoma, and guide the surgical approach. At the time of presentation, immediate MRI access was not available, resulting in unavoidable delays while advanced imaging was arranged. Additionally, MRI brain imaging was not performed, and therefore the possibility of hypothalamic injury or compression contributing to the thermoregulatory dysfunction cannot be completely excluded, as subtle hypothalamic lesions may not be detected on CT imaging alone. During this period, the patient was hemodynamically stabilized, closely monitored, and prepared for surgery in accordance with local protocols. This case highlights the practical challenges of managing time-critical spinal trauma in resource-limited settings, where delays in access to advanced imaging may impact the feasibility of early surgical intervention despite appropriate clinical recognition.

Currently, no universally validated algorithm exists for diagnosing quad fever in SCI. Thompson et al. developed a diagnostic algorithm for quad fever following traumatic brain injury [19]. Savage et al. conducted a qualitative systematic review proposing an algorithm for SCI-associated quad fever, emphasizing the need to exclude all infectious and non-infectious etiologies prior to diagnosis [5]. However, this approach has limitations, particularly in resource-limited settings like Sri Lanka, as it requires extensive testing and invasive procedures, which may delay recognition of rapidly fatal hyperpyrexia. Cervical SCI patients presenting with sudden hyperpyrexia, absent sweating, and rapid thermal fluctuations should be treated with a high index of suspicion for quad fever.

Therapeutic considerations for quad fever remain limited. Incomplete data from previously reported cases impedes development of standardized treatment protocols [3]. In the traumatic brain injury population, partial success has been reported with external cooling and pharmacologic agents including bromocriptine, amantadine, dantrolene, and propranolol [20]. However, their efficacy in SCI-associated neurogenic fever remains unproven. External cooling, environmental temperature reduction, and aggressive supportive care have been reported as potential strategies to control extreme hyperthermia in these patients [21]. In resource-limited settings where advanced cooling devices may not be readily available, alternative methods such as intravenous infusion of cold saline, rectal cooling enemas, and targeted ice-pack placement in high vascular regions including the groin and axilla may provide additional temperature lowering strategies.

Management of extreme hyperthermia in this patient relied primarily on external cooling methods. As discussed earlier, several low-cost strategies may be employed in resource-limited settings. More advanced interventions such as invasive cooling devices, neuromuscular blockade, or pharmacologic therapy with agents such as dantrolene were considered during the hyperthermic crisis but were not immediately available in our unit at the time. These resource constraints limited further escalation of temperature-control strategies.

This case underscores the rapid and potentially fatal course of autonomic thermoregulatory dysfunction following high cervical SCI. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for extreme temperature fluctuations, implement continuous monitoring, and initiate early supportive measures to prevent catastrophic outcomes.

Based on this case, several pragmatic protocol recommendations can be proposed for the acute management of high cervical spinal cord injury. These include early recognition of autonomic instability, continuous or frequent core temperature monitoring from admission, and anticipation of rapid thermal fluctuations even within the first 24 h. Sudden hyperpyrexia accompanied by absent sweating should prompt immediate consideration of quad fever after exclusion of common infectious causes. In resource-limited settings, early implementation of low-cost external cooling measures, strict hemodynamic monitoring, and prompt escalation of airway and ventilatory support are essential. Where immediate surgical intervention is delayed due to imaging or logistical constraints, heightened vigilance for autonomic complications is critical. Adoption of structured, context-appropriate protocols may help mitigate catastrophic outcomes while more definitive therapies are arranged.

Conclusion

This case highlights the extreme biphasic thermoregulatory instability that can occur in patients with high cervical spinal cord injury, culminating in a fulminant hyperpyrexic crisis most consistent with probable quad fever. It underscores the critical importance of continuous temperature monitoring and vigilant hemodynamic support during the acute phase of SCI. Rapid recognition of atypical thermal responses, particularly hyperpyrexia with absent sweating, should raise immediate suspicion for quad fever. In resource-limited settings, early identification and prompt supportive measures are essential to mitigate catastrophic outcomes, as effective pharmacologic interventions remain largely unproven. This report emphasizes the need for heightened awareness and systematic protocols to manage severe autonomic thermoregulatory dysfunction in acute SCI.
