Fast Krasnoselskii–Mann Method with Overrelaxation and Preconditioners Thanks: The research of RIB and EC has been supported by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF), projects W 1260 and P 34922-N, respectively.
Radu I. Boţ ∗, Enis Chenchene ∗, Jalal M. Fadili †

Abstract
. We study accelerated Krasnoselskii–Mann-type methods with preconditioners in both continuous and discrete time. From a continuous-time model, we derive a generalized fast Krasnoselskii–Mann method, providing a new yet simple proof of convergence that leads to unprecedented flexibility in parameter tuning, allowing up to twice larger relaxation parameters. Our analysis unifies inertial and anchoring acceleration mechanisms and offers a broad range of parameter choices, which prove beneficial in practice. Additionally, we extend our analysis to the case where preconditioners are allowed to be degenerate, unifying the treatment of various splitting methods and establishing the weak convergence of shadow sequences.



Keywords: Inertial Accelerations, Nesterov Acceleration, Halpern Method, Fast-Krasnoselskii–Mann Method, Degenerate Proximal Point Algorithm

1.
Introduction

Let
T\colon H\to H
be a nonexpansive operator on the real Hilbert space
H
. Our objective is to solve the fixed-point problem:

(1.1)

\text{Find}\ x\in H\ \text{such that}\ x=T(x)\,,

with fast methods in continuous and discrete time, and apply these results to splitting methods providing convergence of the so-called shadow sequences (to be made precise later).

If
T=I-\nabla f
, where
f\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex and smooth function whose set of minimizers is nonempty, (
1.1
) is equivalent to minimizing
f
. The most popular accelerated method to minimize
f
in discrete time is the celebrated Nesterov’s algorithm
[
52
]
. Su, Boyd, and Candès in
[
57
]
later discovered that Nesterov’s algorithm can be understood as a specific finite differences discretization of the following ODE

(1.2)

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))=0\,,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}{>0}\,

with
\alpha=3
and initial data
(x(t_{0}),\dot{x}(t_{0}))=(x_{0},v_{0})\in H^{2}
. Although the convergence of
x(t)
to a minimizer of
f
remains an open problem for
\alpha=3
(except in one dimension where convergence holds
[
8
]
), the trajectories of (
1.2
) satisfy
f(x(t))-{\min}f=\mathcal{O}(t^{-2})
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, which is an improvement over the
o(t^{-1})
rate of the standard gradient flow. Later, in
[
9
,
30
]
, it has been shown that replacing the critical parameter
\alpha=3
with any
\alpha>3
ensures the weak convergence of
x(t)
to a minimizer of
f
, and improves the rate of convergence for the function values from
\mathcal{O}(t^{-2})
to
o(t^{-2})
.

However, these inertial acceleration methods often suffer from significant oscillations. This issue was addressed by Attouch, Peypouquet, and Redont
[
13
]
, and later by Attouch, Chbani, Fadili, and Riahi
[
7
,
6
]
, who proposed to couple the asymptotically vanishing viscous damping term in (
1.2
) to an additional
geometric Hessian-driven damping term
, previously introduced in
[
3
,
12
]
, resulting in the system

(1.3)

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\beta(t)\frac{d}{dt}\nabla f(x(t))+\nabla f(x(t))=0\,,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,,

which, for
\alpha>3
and appropriate coefficient
\beta(t)>0
, has the important benefit of retaining the
o(t^{-2})
rate on the objective, while ensuring the weak convergence of trajectories, and attenuating oscillations.

Inspired by (
1.3
), we here study the much more general case where
\nabla f
is replaced by any monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator
Q\colon H\to H
:

(1.4)

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\beta(t)\frac{d}{dt}Q(x(t))+b(t)Q(x(t))=0\,,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

For instance, to solve (
1.1
), it is natural to consider
Q:=I-T
, which is indeed monotone and Lipschitz, and the equilibria of (
1.4
) are solutions to (
1.1
). Note that while oscillations attenuation with Hessian-driven damping are quite well-understood and intuitive in optimization contexts (where
Q=\nabla f
), the interpretation of the geometric damping for general monotone operators is not always clear and remains an open problem. In fact, the geometric damping might not always reduce oscillations for general monotone operators as we will see in some case in our numerical results. Nonetheless, we will still stick with this terminology in this paper for consistency reasons.

Although the system in (
1.3
) generates fast converging trajectories for a range of admissible parameters (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
), when one turns to the general operator setting (
1.4
) the situation becomes significantly more delicate. A notable contribution in this direction was made by Boţ, Csetnek and Nguyen in
[
20
]
, who studied (
1.4
) with
b(t):=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{\beta}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\beta(t))
, the so-called fast Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent (Fast-OGDA) system. In particular, they showed that when
\beta
is constant and
\alpha>2
, this dynamical system generates trajectories weakly converging to a zero of
Q
(provided that the set of zeros of
Q
is non-empty) with a convergence rate of
\|Q(x(t))\|=o(t^{-1})
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. Therefore, (
1.4
) can be seen as an accelerated version of the first order system

(1.5)

\dot{x}(t)+Q(x(t))=0\,,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

Indeed, when
Q
is a cocoercive operator, it is known that each solution trajectory generated by (
1.5
) weakly converges to a zero of
Q
with a convergence rate of
\|Q(x(t))\|=o(t^{-\frac{1}{2}})
as
t\rightarrow+\infty

[
5
]
. To further emphasize this crucial difference, we refer to rates of the form
\mathcal{O}(t^{-1})
, or
o(t^{-1})
as
fast rates
.

It turns out that the specific choice
b(t):=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{\beta}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\beta(t))
in (

) considered by
[
20
]
is ultimately due to the impossibility of exploiting function values in the Lyapunov analysis. This immediately leads us to the natural question:

Can Fast-OGDA in
[
20
]
be extended to accommodate more general parameters
b
, enabling new fast and flexible numerical

)?

The Fast-OGDA system is in turn related to a seemingly completely different mechanism: the Tikhonov regularization, also known as anchoring mechanism. Specifically, for

The system (

. A simplified version of (

. The connection between Halpern’s method and momentum-type algorithms have been observed previously in

.

The

, the main focus of our paper. This algorithm solves the fixed-point problem (

) and can be viewed as a discrete-time analogue of the continuous dynamics (

), with

given by (

),
\beta\equiv 1
, and

. It yields an accelerated variant of the classical Krasnoselskii–Mann method

, which for
x^{0}\in H
and

\theta\in(0,1)
, iterates

(1.8)

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\theta\big(T(x^{k})-x^{k}\big)\,,\quad\text{for}\ k\geq 0.

The scheme (

) is known to only exhibit the rate

as

on the fixed-point residual; see
[
46
,
37
]
. We can see that Algorithm

extends (

) by incorporating a

\alpha_{k}:=1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\sigma}
, and features

[

, allowing for more general parameters
\theta\in(1,\alpha-1)
and
\sigma>0
. It results, in particular, in (almost)

relaxation parameters—from
\theta=\frac{\alpha}{2}
to
\theta
arbitrarily close to
\alpha-1
, as well as a general parameter
\sigma>0
. As we will show in Section

1
that matches almost exactly the continuous-time approach, making it easily accessible using the latter as a
guideline
. Additionally, this allows us to cover, in a unifying fashion, the important special case of
\alpha=\sigma=2
, for which the method reduces to the Optimal Halpern Method
[
47
]
, as well as the edge case
\theta=1
, for which Algorithm
1
reduces to a Halpern-type method, much like (
1.7
) in continuous time.

Since Algorithm

allows us to find fixed points of nonexpansive operators, it emerges as a unified algorithmic framework to design accelerated variants of

, such as primal-dual
[

]
, forward–backward
[

1.2.
Notation and Preliminaries

In this section, we fix the notation and introduce some preliminary results on nonexpansive operators in Hilbert spaces and their connection to monotone operators; see
[
14
]
for a comprehensive account. An operator
T\colon H\to H
is said to be
nonexpansive
if

(1.9)

\|T(x)-T(x^{\prime})\|\leq\|x-x^{\prime}\|\,,\quad\text{for all}\ x,x^{\prime}\in H\,.

The set of
fixed points
of
T
is the defined by
\Fix T:=\{x\in H\mid x=T(x)\}
. In this paper, we always assume that
\Fix T\neq\emptyset
. If
T
is nonexpansive, the operator
T_{s}:=sT+(1-s)I
for
s\in(0,1)
is said to be
s
-
averaged
.
T_{1/2}
is called firmly nonexpansive. It follows from
[
14
, Remark 4.34(iv)]
that
I-T_{s}
is
\frac{1}{2s}
-cocoercive, i.e., it satisfies the following inequality:

(1.10)

\left\langle x-x^{\prime},(I-T_{s})(x)-(I-T_{s})(x^{\prime})\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2s}\|(I-T_{s})(x)-(I-T_{s})(x^{\prime})\|^{2}\,,\quad\text{for}\ x,x^{\prime}\in H\,.

In particular,
I-T_{s}
is a
monotone
operator. A set-valued mapping
A\colon H\to 2^{H}
is
monotone
if it obeys

(1.11)

\left\langle a-a^{\prime},u-u^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq 0\,,\quad\text{for all}\ (u,a),(u^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\gra A\,,

where the set
\gra A\subseteq H\times H
is the
graph
of
A
and is defined by
\gra A:=\{(u,a)\mid a\in A(u)\}
. A monotone operator is called
maximal monotone
if its graph is maximal (with respect to the inclusions of sets) among the class of monotone operators. The inverse of a set-valued mapping
A
is denoted by
A^{-1}
and defined through its graph by
\gra A^{-1}:=\{(a,u)\mid(u,a)\in\gra A\}
. The
resolvent
of
A
is the mapping
J_{A}:=(I+A)^{-1}
, where
I
denotes the identity operator on
H
. From
[
14
, Proposition 23.8]
,
A
is maximal monotone if and only if
J_{A}
is a single-valued mapping from
H
to
H
, which is
1
-cocoercive, or simply
cocoercive
(or firmly nonexpansive). Moreover, it is clear that the operator
T:=2J_{A}-I
is nonexpansive. Conversely, from
[
14
, Corollary 23.9]
, if
T
is nonexpansive, there exists a maximal monotone operator
A
such that
T=2J_{A}-I
. Furthermore, fixed points of
T
correspond to
zeros
of
A
, i.e., elements of
\Fix T=\zer A:=\{u\in H\mid 0\in A(u)\}
. We also denote
\rightharpoonup
as weak convergence and
\to
as strong convergence. Eventually, we denote
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket:=\{1,\dots,n\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.1.
Lyapunov analysis

To study the second order dynamical system (
2.1
), we introduce, for
x^{*}\in\zer Q\neq\emptyset
, the energy function
E^{\lambda}\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that will play a fundamental role in our analysis,

(2.3)

\displaystyle E^{\lambda}(t):=\delta(t)\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{\xi(t)}{2}\|Q(x(t))\|^{2}+\frac{c}{2}\|x(t)-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v^{\lambda}(t)\|^{2}\,,

\displaystyle v^{\lambda}(t):=\lambda(x(t)-x^{*})+t\big(\dot{x}(t)+(1-\eta)\beta(t)Q(x(t))\big)\,,

for some constant
\lambda\geq 0
, and parameters given by

(2.4)

\delta(t):=\eta\lambda\beta(t)t\,,\quad\xi(t):=\eta(1-\eta)\beta(t)^{2}t^{2}\,,\quad c:=\lambda(\alpha-1-\lambda)\,,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

This choice of the parameters allows us to establish the following result, whose proof is postponed to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
x^{*}\in\zer Q\neq\emptyset
,
x\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to H
be a solution trajectory to (
2.1
), and
E^{\lambda}
the energy function defined in (
2.3
). Then, for almost all
t\geq t_{0}
, we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E^{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda t\eta\left(\dot{\beta}(t)+\frac{2-\alpha}{t}\beta(t)\right)\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle-\frac{t}{2}(\alpha-1-\lambda)\|\dot{x}(t)+\beta(t)Q(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta(t)t^{2}\left((1-\eta)\eta\left(\dot{\beta}(t)+\frac{2-\alpha}{t}\beta(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-1-\lambda)\frac{\beta(t)}{t}\right)\|Q(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\eta t^{2}\beta(t)\left\langle\dot{x}(t),\frac{d}{dt}Q(x(t))\right\rangle-\frac{t}{2}(\alpha-1-\lambda)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}\,.

Lemma
2.1
naturally leads us to consider the following
growth condition
for
t\mapsto\beta(t)
:

(2.6)

0\leq\frac{\dot{\beta}(t)t}{\beta(t)}\leq\alpha-2-\varepsilon\,,\quad\text{for}\ \varepsilon\in[0,\alpha-2]\,,\ \text{and all}\ t\geq t_{0}\,,

which with a suitable rearrangement, writes equivalently as

(2.7)

\dot{\beta}(t)+\frac{2-\alpha}{t}\beta(t)\leq{-\varepsilon\frac{\beta(t)}{t}}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ t\geq t_{0}\,.

This makes the coefficient in front of the term
\|Q(x(t))\|^{2}
negative and bounded away from zero at least for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\alpha-1
, hence leading to the desired energy decrease. This observation is crucial to establish the following convergence result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
Q\colon H\to H
be a monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator with
\zer Q\neq\emptyset
. Let
x^{*}\in\zer Q
,
x\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to H
be a solution trajectory to (
2.1
) for some
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\geq 2
. Suppose that (
2.6
) holds with
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then, it holds:

(2.8)

\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t\beta(t)}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\|Q(x(t))\|\ =\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t\beta(t)}\right)\quad\mbox{as}\ t\rightarrow+\infty\,.

Additionally, if
\alpha>2
and
\varepsilon>0
, then the following holds:

(i)

We have the following integral bounds:

\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\beta(t)\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle dt<+\infty\,,\quad\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t\beta(t)^{2}\|Q(x(t))\|^{2}dt<+\infty\,,\quad\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}dt<+\infty\,.

(ii)

x(t)
converges weakly to a zero of
Q
as
t\to+\infty
.

(iii)

The following fast rates of convergence hold:

\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle=o\left(\frac{1}{t\beta(t)}\right)\,,\quad\|Q(x(t))\|=o\left(\frac{1}{t\beta(t)}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\dot{x}(t)\|=o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)\quad\mbox{as}\ t\rightarrow+\infty\,.

Remark 2.3

(The gap function rate)
.

The quantity
\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle
is obviously nonnegative by monotonicity of
Q
. To get the significance of the rates related to this quantity, that we dub
gap function
, we first observe that in the simple case where
Q=\nabla f
, where
f
is a smooth convex function, then
f(x(t))-\min_{H}f\leq\left\langle Q(x(t)),x(t)-x^{*}\right\rangle
by convexity, whence we recover the same convergence rate on the objective. This remains true even when minimizing
f+g
where
f
is an
L
-smooth function and
g
is a proper lower semicontinuous convex function with

Remark 2.4

(On the growth of

)
.

The parameter function

[

\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\geq 0
.

3.2.
Energy Decrease

We start with the following preliminary result, which represents the discrete-time counterpart of Lemma
2.1
, and our first main contribution:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T\colon H\to H
be a nonexpansive operator with
\Fix T\neq\emptyset
. Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
,
\sigma>0
, and
\alpha\geq 2
. Then there exists
\underline{\lambda}<\alpha-1
such that for all
\lambda\in(\underline{\lambda},\alpha-1]
and all
k\geq 1
such that
t_{k}\geq\lambda
it holds:

(3.11)

\displaystyle E_{k+1}^{\lambda}-E_{k}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda\eta\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(\left\langle x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1}),T(x^{k-1})-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\bigg[-\eta(1-\eta)(\alpha-2)t_{k}-\frac{\eta}{2}\Big(1-\eta+\eta(\alpha-1)^{2}-\lambda(\alpha-1)\Big)\bigg]\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+(\alpha-1-\lambda)\frac{t_{k}-\left(2\eta\theta+\eta+\theta\right)}{2}\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle-(\alpha-1-\lambda)\frac{t_{k}-\left(\alpha+2+\eta(\alpha-1)\right)}{2}\|T(x^{k-1})-T(x^{k-2})\|^{2}\,.

Observe that the first term on the right-hand side is negative thanks to (
3.10
). The proof of Lemma
3.2
, which is postponed to Appendix
B
, follows exactly the continuous-time approach with
Q:=I-T
. We are now ready to establish the discrete counterpart of Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
T\colon H\to H
be a nonexpansive operator with
\Fix T\neq\emptyset
. Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
,
\sigma>0
, and
\alpha\geq 2
. Then:

(i)

We have
\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|=\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
as
k\to+\infty
.

Suppose now that
\alpha>2
and let
z^{*}\in\Fix T
. Then:

(ii)

We have the summability properties:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\left\langle x^{k}-T(x^{k}),T(x^{k})-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|^{2}\right)<+\infty\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|^{2}<+\infty\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty\,.

(iii)

The sequence
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to a fixed point of
T
as
k\to+\infty
.

(iv)

We have the following fast rates of convergence:

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{k}-T(x^{k}),T(x^{k})-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|^{2}=o(k^{-1})\,,

\displaystyle\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|=o(k^{-1})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|=o(k^{-1})\quad\mbox{as}\ k\rightarrow+\infty\,.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, it is convenient to introduce the additional sequence
(z^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. We recall that the latter is defined by
z^{k+1}:=T(x^{k})
and thus satisfies (
3.5
) with
Q^{k+1}:=Q(x^{k}):=(I-T)(x^{k})=x^{k}-z^{k+1}
for all
k\geq 0
.

(i)
We set
\lambda=\alpha-1
and (
3.11
) becomes, after elementary computations,

(3.12)

\Delta E_{k}^{\alpha-1}\leq-\eta(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)\left(\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)-\eta(1-\eta)(\alpha-2)\Big(t_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\,,

for all
k\geq 1
such that
t_{k}\geq\alpha-1
, which also implies
t_{k}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
as
\alpha\geq 2
. Therefore, using (
3.10
), there is
\bar{k}\geq 1
large enough such that for all
k\geq\bar{k}
we have
\Delta E_{k}^{\alpha-1}\leq 0
. Therefore, for all
k\geq\bar{k}
, we have
\frac{\xi_{k+1}}{2}\|Q^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq E_{k+1}^{\alpha-1}\leq E_{\bar{k}}^{\alpha-1}
whence we get the claim recalling the expressions of
\xi_{k}
and
t_{k}
.

Suppose from now on that
\alpha>2
.

(ii)
Setting again
\lambda=\alpha-1
and embarking from (
3.12
), we have after summing on both sides that

\displaystyle\eta(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)\sum_{i=\bar{k}}^{k}\left(\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\eta(1-\eta)(\alpha-2)\sum_{i=\bar{k}}^{k}(t_{i}-t_{\bar{k}})\|Q^{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq E_{\bar{k}}^{\alpha-1}-E_{k+1}^{\alpha-1}\leq E_{\bar{k}}^{\alpha-1}<+\infty\,.

Passing to the limit as
k\to+\infty
we get the first two summability claims. For the last one, we set
\lambda=\alpha-1-\varepsilon
, for
\varepsilon>0
to be chosen small enough. Then, discarding the first negative term in (
3.11
), we have

(3.13)

\Delta E_{k}^{\alpha-1-\varepsilon}\leq\bigg[-\left(\eta(1-\eta)(\alpha-2)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)t_{k}+\frac{\eta(1-\eta)}{2}\left((\alpha-1)^{2}-1\right)\bigg]\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\\
-\varepsilon\frac{t_{k}-\left(\alpha+2+\eta(\alpha-1)\right)}{2}\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}\,.

Therefore, for
0<\varepsilon<\eta(1-\eta)(\alpha-2)
, there exists
\tilde{k}\geq 1
large enough such that for all
k\geq\tilde{k}
,

\Delta E_{k}^{\alpha-1-\varepsilon}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(t_{k}-t_{\tilde{k}})\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}\,,

where we have discarded the first negative term in (
3.13
). Summing again we get
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty
. Observe now that since
Q^{k+1}=x^{k}-z^{k+1}
by definition, we have

\|\Delta x_{k-1}\|^{2}=\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}=\|\Delta z_{k}+\Delta Q_{k}\|^{2}\leq 2\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}+4\|Q^{k+1}\|^{2}+4\|Q^{k}\|^{2}.

Multiplying by
k
, summing and using the summability claims proved above, we conclude.

(iii)
We argue as in the continuous-time setting and set
\lambda=\alpha-1-\varepsilon
, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough as before. We will emphasize the dependency of
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\lambda
by writing explicitly
(v^{k}_{\lambda})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
k\geq 1
. We have

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\lambda}-E_{k}^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=-\eta(\alpha-1-\lambda)t_{k-1}\left(\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\alpha-1-\lambda)\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\lambda}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\alpha-1}^{k}\|^{2}}_{(\hypertarget{lb:thmc_I}{\text{$I$}})}\,.

Let us now compute (
) separately. By the three point identity, we get

\eqref{lb:thmc_I}:=\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\lambda}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\alpha-1}^{k}\|^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}-(\alpha-1)^{2}}{2}\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}-t_{k-1}(\alpha-1-\lambda)\left\langle\Delta z_{k-1}+(1-\eta)Q^{k},z^{k-1}-z^{*}\right\rangle\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\lambda}-E_{k}^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=-(\alpha-1-\lambda)\Big[t_{k-1}\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}-(1-\eta)\Delta z_{k-1}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta}{2}t_{k-1}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(\alpha-1)}{2}\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}+t_{k-1}\left\langle\Delta z_{k-1},z^{k-1}-z^{*}\right\rangle\Big]\,.

Therefore, this can be written as

(3.14)

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\lambda}-E_{k}^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=-(\alpha-1-\lambda)\Big[(\alpha-1)q_{k}+t_{k-1}\Delta q_{k}-(\alpha-1)I_{k}

\displaystyle-(1-\eta)t_{k-1}\left\langle Q^{k},\Delta z_{k-1}\right\rangle-\frac{t_{k-1}}{2}\left(\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}+(1-\eta)\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)\Big]\,,

where we have denoted

\displaystyle q_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}+I_{k}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad I_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\left\langle Q^{j},z^{j}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{j}\|^{2}\right)\,.

Recall that
I_{k}
is nonnegative thanks to (
3.10
), hence so is
q_{k}
. As argued in the proof of
(i)
,
\Delta E^{\alpha-1}_{k}\leq 0
for all
k
large enough and thus
\frac{c}{2}\|z^{k}-z^{*}\|^{2}\leq E^{\alpha-1}_{k}<+\infty
, i.e.
(z^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a bounded sequence. It then follows from the last two summability properties of
(ii)
that the terms terms in the second line of (
3.14
) converge to
0
.
(ii)
also gives that
I_{k}
admits a limit. Moreover,
E_{k}^{\lambda}
and
E_{k}^{\alpha}
are nonnegative and decreasing for
k
large enough as argued above. Therefore, we deduce that

(3.15)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}q_{k}+\frac{t_{k-1}}{\alpha-1}\Delta q_{k}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}(E_{k}^{\lambda}-E_{k}^{\alpha-1})\in\mathbb{R}\,.

We are then in position to apply
[
23
, Lemma A.4]
(see also
[
6
, Lemma A.2]
for a weaker variant that requires
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
) to (
3.15
) to infer that
(q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a limit. Using again that
I_{k}
has a limit by
(ii)
, we get that
(\|z^{k}-z^{*}\|)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges. Since
\|Q^{k}\|=\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|=\|x^{k-1}-z^{k}\|\to 0
by
(i)
, we have
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|x^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|z^{k}-z^{*}\|^{2}
and thus the limit of
\|x^{k}-z^{*}\|
exists. The weak convergence claim of
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
then follows from the standard Opial’s lemma.

(iv)
Let us first note that since the limit of
(q^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
exists (see the proof of
(iii)
), (
3.15
) yields

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\ni\lim_{k\to+\infty}t_{k-1}\Delta q_{k}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}t_{k-1}\bigg[

\displaystyle\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\Delta z_{k-1},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}\bigg]\,.

Now, from (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\alpha-1)(z^{k-1}-z^{*})+t_{k-1}\left(\Delta z_{k-1}+(1-\eta)Q^{k}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\eta(\alpha-1)t_{k-1}\left(\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)+\frac{(1-\eta)\eta t_{k-1}^{2}}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha-1)^{2}}{2}\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{t_{k-1}^{2}}{2}\left\|\Delta z_{k-1}+(1-\eta)Q^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\eta)\eta t_{k-1}^{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-1)(1-\eta)t_{k-1}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}-(\alpha-1)(1-\eta)t_{k-1}\left\langle Q^{k},\Delta z_{k-1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+(\alpha-1)t_{k-1}\bigg[\left\langle Q^{k},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}+\left\langle\Delta z_{k-1},z^{k}-z^{*}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k-1}\|^{2}\bigg]\,.

Combining (
3.16
) (hence the last line has a limit),
(ii)
(hence the second line converges to
0
), and that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|z^{k-1}-z^{*}\|^{2}
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}E_{k}^{\alpha-1}
exist as proved above, we deduce that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\ t_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Delta z_{k-1}+(1-\eta)Q^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\eta)\eta\|Q^{k}\|^{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Observe now that the summability claims of
(ii)
already give us that

(3.17)

\liminf_{k\to+\infty}k^{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}=0\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{k\to+\infty}k^{2}\|Q^{k}\|^{2}=0\,.

Thus these are actually limits. To transfer the rates from
z^{k}
to
x^{k}
, we use the relation
x^{k}=Q^{k+1}+z^{k+1}
, yielding the last two rates in
(iv)
. Using Cauchy–Schwarz and boundedness of
(z^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we also deduce the first. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4

(The discrete gap function rate)
.

While the convergence rates for the discrete velocity
\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|
and the fixed-point residual
\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|
are relatively natural in this context, the rate

(3.18)

\left\langle x^{k}-T(x^{k}),T(x^{k})-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|^{2}=o(k^{-1})\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,,

may initially appear less intuitive. However, as we will see in Section
4
, it plays a key role in applications to primal-dual-type algorithms, where it serves as a bound for the primal-dual gap, see Example
4.1
. See also Remark
2.3
for its continuous-time counterpart.

Remark 3.5

(Non-asymptotic rates)
.

In this work, we primarily focus on asymptotic convergence rates, i.e., the behavior as
k\to+\infty
. Nevertheless, for each admissible parameter choice and each quantity of interest, we can also derive corresponding non-asymptotic rates with explicit constants. Let us also emphasize here the dependency of
E_{k}^{\lambda}
on
\eta
by writing
E_{k}^{\lambda,\eta}
. Embarking from (
3.12
), we have
E_{k+1}^{\alpha-1,\eta}\leq E_{k}^{\alpha-1,\eta}
for all
k\geq 1
such that
t_{k}\geq\alpha-1
. If we now only consider
\sigma\geq\alpha-1
, we obtain
E_{k+1}^{\alpha-1,\eta}\leq E_{k}^{\alpha-1,\eta}
for all
k\geq 1
, leading to the following explicit bounds:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1}),T(x^{k-1})-z^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{E_{1}^{\alpha-1,\eta}}{\eta(\alpha-1)t_{k-1}}\,,

\displaystyle\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2E_{1}^{\alpha-1,\eta}}{\eta(1-\eta)t_{k-1}^{2}}\,,

where
E_{1}^{\alpha-1,\eta}
is a constant that only depends on the initial conditions, on
z^{*}
, and the algorithms parameters, including
\eta
—see (
3.8
) for the precise definition. A particularly illustrative situation occurs when
\alpha=2
,
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, and
\sigma=1
, which yields
t_{0}=0
and thus

(3.20)

\|x^{k-1}-T(x^{k-1})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4\|z^{0}-z^{*}\|^{2}}{t_{k-1}^{2}}=\frac{4\|T(x^{-1})-x^{*}\|^{2}}{(k-1)^{2}}\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 2\,.

A more in-depth investigation of the two bounds in (
3.19
), including the influence of the parameter
\eta
, is left for future work.

3.3.
Connection to Existing Algorithms

Algorithm
1
offers a flexible and unifying framework that is connected to a broad class of existing methods. In this section, we highlight a few notable special cases and related algorithms to place them more clearly within the literature.

3.3.1.
Connection to
[
21
]

As we mentioned in the introduction, Algorithm
1
is a generalization of the scheme (2.8) in
[
21
]
. The latter can be recovered by setting
\sigma=\alpha+1
and
\theta=\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
, which gives, renaming
x^{k}
as
x^{k+1}
, for all
k\geq 1
:

(3.21)

x^{k+1}=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\right)x^{k}+\frac{\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}T(x^{k})+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\big(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1})\big)\,,

with initial points
x^{0},x^{1}\in H
. This is also a special case of
[
21
, Algorithm 2.1]
, which can be recovered by introducing a step size
s\in(0,1]
and considering
T_{s}:=(1-s)I+sT
, which is now
s
-averaged and has the same fixed points as
T
. Indeed, substituting
T
by
T_{s}
in (
3.21
) we get for all
k\geq 1
:

(3.22)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{s\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}\right)x^{k}+\frac{(1-s)k}{k+\alpha}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})+\frac{s\alpha}{2(k+\alpha)}T(x^{k})+\frac{sk}{k+\alpha}\big(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1})\big)\,.

Conversely, if
T
is
\zeta
-averaged, we shall consider (
3.22
) with
s\in(0,1/\zeta]
, which is equivalent to applying (
3.21
) with
T
replaced by
sT+(1-s)I
.

3.3.2.
Connection to Halpern-type Methods

Similarly to the continuous-time dynamics discussed in Section
2.2.1
, Algorithm
1
reduces to an
anchored
method when we set
\theta=1
. Firstly, when
\alpha=2
,
\sigma=\alpha
, and
x^{0}=T(x^{-1})
, it is clear that Algorithm
1
coincides with the
momentum form
of the Optimal Halpern Method (OHM)
[
47
,
56
]
, and in turn with Kim’s accelerated proximal point algorithm
[
44
]
, since the latter two are equivalent, see
[
62
, Appendix B]
. In the edge case of setting
\theta
equal to
1
, it turns out that Algorithm
1
is indeed equivalent to anchored-type methods for any
\alpha\geq 2
and
\sigma>0
, as we will show in the following result.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
with
\alpha\geq 2
,
\theta=1
and
\sigma>0
. Then,
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies:

(3.23)

x^{k+1}=\varepsilon_{k}v+(1-\varepsilon_{k})T(x^{k})\,,\quad\text{for}\ k\geq 0\,,

where
\varepsilon_{k}:=\frac{\alpha-1}{k+\sigma}
and
v:=\frac{t_{0}}{\alpha-1}(x^{0}-T(x^{-1}))+T(x^{-1})
.

Proof.

Multiplying the update rule of Algorithm
1
by
t_{k+1}:=k+\sigma
, we get

\displaystyle(k+\sigma)(x^{k+1}-x^{k})=T(x^{k})-x^{k}+(k+\sigma-\alpha)(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1}))

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle t_{k+1}(x^{k+1}-x^{k})+x^{k}

\displaystyle-\Big(T(x^{k})+(k+\sigma-1)(T(x^{k+1})-T(x^{k}))\Big)+(\alpha-1)(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1}))=0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\big(t_{k+1}x^{k+1}-t_{k}x^{k}\big)-\big(t_{k+1}T(x^{k})-t_{k}T(x^{k-1})\big)+(\alpha-1)\big(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1})\big)=0\,.

Summing the above equation from
0
to
k
, dividing the result by
t_{k+1}
and rearranging terms we get the claim.
∎

Note that in the edge case
\theta=1
we cannot expect to obtain little-
o
rates for fixed-point residuals, and the convergence of the iterates is in fact in the strong topology, but is established with completely different techniques than those we have applied in Section
3.2
: The former rely on the Grönwall’s lemma (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
), the latter on Opial’s lemma.

3.3.3.
Connection to Inertial KM and Nesterov’s Method

The Fast-KM method is not the first attempt to introduce a Nesterov-type acceleration for iterative methods governed by nonexpansive operators. The earliest such attempt dates back to 2001, with the work of Attouch and Alvarez in
[
4
]
. Starting from an implicit discretization of the celebrated Heavy Ball with Friction (HBF) dynamical system governed by a maximal monotone operator, they arrived at the following numerical algorithm, for a firmly nonexpansive operator
T
:

(3.24)

x^{k+1}=T(x^{k})+\alpha_{k}\big(T(x^{k})-T(x^{k-1})\big)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0\,,

with
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
. Note that if
\alpha_{k}:=1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\sigma}
with
\sigma>0
,
\alpha\geq 3
, and
T:=I-\tau\nabla f
for a
L
-smooth function
f\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tau\in(0,\frac{1}{L}]
, then (
3.24
) coincides with the
Ravine
formulation of the celebrated Nesterov Accelerated Gradient method
[
52
,
11
]
, which is known to yield the optimal convergence rate in convex optimization. While (
3.24
) can still provide weakly converging sequences for general (firmly) nonexpansive operators—specifically if
\alpha_{k}\leq\bar{\alpha}<\frac{1}{3}
, cf.
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
—whether it achieves fast convergence rates in this general setting remains unclear. A first negative answer was provided only recently in
[
50
]
, in a broader context involving iteration-dependent operators and more general parameter choices. Specifically, in the context of (
3.24
),
[
50
, Theorem 4(ii)]
shows that if
\alpha_{k}
satisfies a specific growth condition—implying, in particular, that
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{k}<1
—then one only obtains

(3.25)

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\ \|x^{k}-T(x^{k})\|\leq M\frac{\dist(x^{0},\Fix T)}{\sqrt{n}}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq 1\,,

for some constant
M>0
. This result is
ergodic
and of suboptimal order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
.

Comparing (
3.24
) with Algorithm
1
, we see that (
3.24
) could, in principle, be recovered from Algorithm
1
by replacing the vanishing regularization term
\frac{\theta}{k+\sigma}
with
1
, and by capping
\alpha_{k}
at a value strictly smaller than
1
. In the continuous-time model (
2.1
), this would correspond to taking
b\equiv 1
and a
non
-vanishing damping function, respectively. However, doing so would worsen the rate to (
3.25
). Thus, we learn that letting
\alpha_{k}\to 1
and using a vanishing regularization parameter
\frac{\theta}{k+\sigma}
are both essential for achieving fast convergence rates for general nonexpansive operators.

3.3.4.
Connection to Tran-Dinh’s Method

In
[
61
]
, Tran-Dinh proposed a method that is able to achieve a fast little-
o
rate for solving root-finding problems of the form:

(3.26)

\text{Find}\ x\in H\ \text{such that:}\quad 0=G(x)\,,

where
G\colon H\to H
is
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercive, for
L>0
. The method in
[
61
, Theorem 3]
is formulated as

(3.27)

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\bar{\theta}_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\bar{\eta}_{k}\big(G(x^{k})-\bar{\gamma}_{k}G(x^{k-1})\big)\,,\quad\text{for}\ k\geq 0\,,

with parameter sequences defined, for
\bar{\gamma}\in(0,1)
,
\omega>2
, and all
k\geq 0
, as

(3.28)

\bar{\theta}_{k}:=\frac{k+1}{k+2\omega+2}\,,\quad\bar{\eta}_{k}:=\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{L}\frac{k+\omega+1}{k+2\omega+2}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\gamma}_{k}:=\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{L}\frac{\bar{\theta}_{k}}{\bar{\eta}_{k}}\,.

It turns out that (
3.27
) is a special case of Algorithm
1
. Indeed, considering the operator
T:=I-\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{L}G
, solving (
3.26
) is equivalent to finding a fixed point of
T
,
T
being
\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}
-averaged. If we now apply Algorithm
1
to find a fixed point of
T
setting
\sigma:=2\omega+2
,
\alpha:=2\omega+1
and
\theta:=\omega
, we obtain exactly (
3.27
) with the parameter choices (
3.28
). Interestingly, Theorem
3.3
allows us to further extend the parameter choices proposed in
[
61
]
to
\omega\geq\frac{1}{2}
, instead of
\omega\geq 2
, and
\bar{\gamma}\in(0,2]
instead of
\bar{\gamma}\in(0,1)
, as well as to establish a last-iterate rate on the gap function.

4.
Application to Splitting Methods with Preconditioners

The goal here is apply our results to operator splitting methods for solving monotone inclusion problems, i.e. finding a zero of one or sum of maximally monotone operators. We follow the algorithmic framework introduced in
[
24
]
. For a maximal monotone operator
A\colon H\to 2^{H}
, we introduce a
preconditioner

M
, i.e., a self-adjoint positive semidefinite bounded linear operator
M\colon H\to H
, which induces a Hilbertian (semi-)inner product
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{M}:=\left\langle u,Mv\right\rangle
and associated (semi) norm
\|u\|_{M}:=\sqrt{\left\langle u,u\right\rangle_{M}}
for all
u,v\in H
. Given
A
and
M
, we consider the resolvent:

(4.1)

J_{M^{-1}A}:=(I+M^{-1}A)^{-1}=(M+A)^{-1}M\,.

It is worth noting that without further hypothesis,
J_{M^{-1}A}
is not necessarily single-valued nor defined everywhere on
H
. On the other hand, this representation is helpful in practice, and evaluating
J_{M^{-1}A}
can be facilitated by the right choice of
M
. As first remarked by He and Yuan in
[
43
]
, this is in fact the case for primal-dual-type methods:

Example 4.1

(PDHG)
.

Let
f\colon H\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
g\colon K\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functions on the Hilbert spaces
H
and
K
, and
L\colon H\to K
a linear bounded map. The Primal-Dual-Hybrid-Gradient (PDHG) method by Chambolle and Pock
[
31
]
is a classical scheme to find a solution to the following saddle point problem

(4.2)

\min_{x\in H}\max_{y\in K}\ \mathcal{L}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle Lx,y\rangle-g^{*}(y)\,,

where
g^{*}
is the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate of
g
. This has enormous applications in applied mathematics, see, e.g.,
[
32
]
. The PDHG iteration takes the form

(4.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x^{k+1}=\prox_{\tau_{1}f_{1}}(x^{k}-\tau_{1}L^{T}y^{k})\,,\\
&y^{k+1}=\prox_{\tau_{2}f_{2}^{*}}(y^{k}+\tau_{2}L(2x^{k+1}-x^{k}))\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Under a mild qualification condition, the generated sequence
u^{k}:=(x^{k},y^{k})
converges weakly to a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
as soon as
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\|L\|^{2}<1
, see
[
43
,
24
]
. Following He and Yuan
[
43
]
, the method can be understood a fixed-point iterations with respect to
J_{M^{-1}A}
with

(4.4)

A:=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f&L^{T}\\
-L&\partial g^{*}\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad M:=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}I&-L^{T}\\
-L&\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}I\end{bmatrix}\,.

The operator
J_{M^{-1}A}
can in fact be easily computed due to the block-lower triangular structure of
A+M
(recall the formula (
4.1
) or see
[
24
, Section 3]
for the detailed computations), and the resulting fixed-point iteration coincides with (
4.3
).

The step-size condition
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\|L\|^{2}<1
is equivalent to the positive definiteness of
M

[
24
]
, which makes
M^{-1}A
a maximal monotone operator on the Hilbert space
H
endowed with the norm induced by
M
. Being an instance of the (preconditioned) proximal point algorithm
[
24
]
, the rate on the fixed-point residual is

(4.5)

\|u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\|_{M}=o(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

A second classical convergence measure for (
4.3
) is the
primal-dual gap
4
4

4

More precisely, the primal-dual gap should be defined as
\sup_{u^{*}\in\zer A}\mathcal{G}_{u^{*}}(x,y)
. However, as
\zer A
might be unbounded, this quantity is not easily controllable, leading to notions such as
restricted primal-dual gap

[
31
]
or to further assumptions such as boundedness of
\zer A
. In this example, we keep the presentation simple and avoid taking the
\sup
.
, which we define as

(4.6)

\mathcal{G}_{u^{*}}(x,y):=\mathcal{L}(x,y^{*})-\mathcal{L}(x^{*},y)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ (x,y)\in H\times K\,.

The quantity that in Remark
3.4
we referred to as the gap function (
3.20
) is indeed an upper bound for (
4.6
). Specifically, using convexity and the definition of
A
in (
4.4
), we get

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{u^{*}}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\left\langle u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}),J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})-u^{*}\right\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\langle u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}),J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})-u^{*}\right\rangle_{M}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\|^{2}_{M}\,.

Note that
u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})=u^{k}-u^{k+1}\in M^{-1}A(u^{k+1})
, so the first inner product (
4.7
) is non-negative in this case. Secondly, while the right-hand side of (
4.7
) vanishes at the rate
o(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, it is also summable, leading, using convexity of
\mathcal{G}_{u^{*}}
to the classical
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
ergodic rate for (
4.6
), see
[
32
]
.

By allowing the preconditioner
M
to be
degenerate
, i.e., with
\ker M\neq\emptyset
, Bredies and Sun in
[
26
,
27
]
, and later
[
24
]
recognized that the class of methods representable as iterations with respect to degenerate preconditioners can be greatly broadened to include methods such as the Douglas–Rachford Splitting (DRS)
[
48
]
and its extensions
[
25
]
.

4.1.
Preliminaries on Degenerate Proximal Point

The notion of monotonicity and cocoercivity can be extended to a
preconditioned
setting, even with a degenerate preconditioner
M\colon H\to H
. For a maximal monotone operator
A
and a preconditioner
M
we shall consider the operator
M^{-1}A
as a composition of the multi-valued operators
M^{-1}
(pre-image) and
A
, defined as:
v\in M^{-1}A(u)
if and only if
Mv\in A(u)
. It is easy to check that
M^{-1}A
is monotone with respect to the semi-inner product induced by
M
. We then say that
M^{-1}A
is
M
-
monotone
. Likewise, its resolvent
J_{M^{-1}A}
is
M
-
cocoercive
in the sense that

(4.8)

\left\langle v-v^{\prime},u-u^{\prime}\right\rangle_{M}\geq\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{M}^{2}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ (u,v),\ (u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\gra J_{M^{-1}A}\,.

Due to the degeneracy of the preconditioner, the operator
J_{M^{-1}A}
is not necessarily single-valued and could even have empty domain. To avoid such pathological cases, we suppose
\dom J_{M^{-1}A}=H
. A further hypothesis we shall require is that the operator

(4.9)

(M+A)^{-1}\ \text{is Lipschitz over}\ \Image M\,,

which makes
J_{M^{-1}A}
a well-defined Lipschitz operator on
H
, since indeed
J_{M^{-1}A}=(M+A)^{-1}M
. Additionally, it can be shown to satisfy the fundamental property:

(4.10)

\|J_{M^{-1}A}(u)-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{\prime})\|^{2}\leq L\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{M}^{2}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ u,u^{\prime}\in H\,.

We refer to fixed-point iterations with respect to
J_{M^{-1}A}
as
preconditioned proximal point algorithm
, which for
\theta\in(0,2)
and
u^{0}\in H
, reads as

(4.11)

u^{k+1}=u^{k}+\theta\big(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})-u^{k}\big)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N}\,.

The convergence guarantees of (
4.11
) are given in
[
24
, Theorem 2.9]
. Specifically, if (
4.9
) holds, then
(u^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converge weakly to the same zero of
A
with the rate:

(4.12)

\|u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\|_{M}=o(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

An appealing property of positive semidefinite preconditioners is that evaluating
J_{M^{-1}A}
automatically produces a quotient space operation. Suppose that
M
has closed range and consider a factorization of
M
as
M=CC^{*}
, with
C\colon D\to H
one-to-one from a Hilbert space
D
(
D\cong\Image M
) to
H
. We say that
CC^{*}
is an
onto decomposition
of
M
. In this case, one can prove that
C^{*}
is onto
[
24
, Proposition 2.3]
and the operator
C^{*}\rhd A:=(C^{*}A^{-1}C)^{-1}
is maximal monotone
[
24
, Theorem 2.13]
. The resolvents of
M^{-1}A
and of
C^{*}\rhd A
are tightly connected: If
M
has closed range and
M=CC^{*}
is onto, then

(4.13)

C^{*}J_{M^{-1}A}(u)=J_{C^{*}\rhd A}\left(C^{*}u\right)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ u\in H\,.

This is particularly useful as it reveals that the evaluation of
J_{M^{-1}A}
is somewhat equivalent to evaluating
J_{C^{*}\rhd A}
, which is a resolvent of a maximal monotone operator and is defined on
D
, which is isomorphic to
\Image M
; see
[
33
, Theorem 3.3.3]
for a proof. This implies that the sequence
w^{k}:=C^{*}u^{k}\in D
is a classical proximal point method with respect to the maximal monotone operator
C^{*}\rhd A
:

(4.14)

w^{k+1}=w^{k}+\theta\big(J_{C^{*}\rhd A}(w^{k})-w^{k}\big)\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

However, although
J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})=(M+A)^{-1}(Cw^{k})
from (
4.1
) and
M=CC^{*}
, the weak convergence of
(u^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, in infinite-dimensional spaces, does not follow directly from the that of
(w^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. For clarity, we present a prototypical example:

Example 4.2

(Douglas–Rachford
[
27
,
24
]
)
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\colon H\to 2^{H}
be two maximal monotone operators on
H
and assume that
\zer(A_{1}+A_{2})\neq\emptyset
. Following
[
24
]
, the DRS method for finding a zero of
A_{1}+A_{2}
can be understood as iterating
J_{M^{-1}A}
with the operators:

(4.15)

A:=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}A_{1}&\phantom{-}I&-I\\
-I&\phantom{-}A_{2}&\phantom{-}I\\
\phantom{-}I&-I&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad M:=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}I&-I&\phantom{-}I\\
-I&\phantom{-}I&-I\\
\phantom{-}I&-I&\phantom{-}I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
-I\\
I\end{bmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&-I&I\end{bmatrix}}_{:=C^{*}}\,.

Indeed, since
M+A
is block lower-triangular, evaluating
J_{M^{-1}A}=(M+A)^{-1}M
only requires accessing the resolvents of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, we refer to
[
24
]
for the detailed computation. If
u^{k}:=(x_{1}^{k},x_{2}^{k},v^{k})\in H^{3}
is such that
u^{k+1}:=J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})
the DRS method can be obtained via the substitution
w^{k}:=x_{1}^{k}-x_{2}^{k}+v^{k}
, which yields for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

(4.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{1}^{k+1}=J_{A_{1}}(w^{k})\,,\\
&x_{2}^{k+1}=J_{A_{2}}(2x_{1}^{k+1}-w^{k})\,,\\
&w^{k+1}=w^{k}+x_{2}^{k+1}-x_{1}^{k+1}\,.\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}):=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{k+1}\\
x_{2}^{k+1}\\
w^{k}+2(x^{k+1}_{2}-x^{k+1}_{1})\end{pmatrix}\,.

This corresponds to taking
w^{k}=C^{*}u^{k}
, which defines a fixed-point iteration with respect
J_{C^{*}\rhd A}
, the operator
C^{*}\rhd A
now being maximal monotone. It follows from
[
24
, Theorem 2.9]
that
(w^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to some
w^{*}\in\zer C^{*}\rhd A
, with the rate

(4.17)

\|x_{2}^{k+1}-x_{1}^{k+1}\|=\|w^{k}-J_{C^{*}\rhd A}(w^{k})\|=\|u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\|_{M}=o(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})\,,\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

Additionally,
(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to a zero of
A
, yielding that the
solution estimates

(x_{1}^{k+1})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(x_{2}^{k+1})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(a.k.a. shadow sequences) weakly converge to the same zero of
A_{1}+A_{2}
.

Note that the weak convergence of the shadow sequences remained an open problem for almost
30
years until Svaiter proposed a convergence proof in
[
60
]
. The analysis of iterations associated to (
4.1
) with a degenerate preconditioner allows to recover this result from general principles applicable to a broad range of algorithms; see
[
24
]
.

Example 4.3

(Graph Douglas–Rachford
[
25
,
33
,
2
]
)
.

The graph-Douglas–Rachford Splitting method (Graph-DRS) is an extension of the Douglas–Rachford method to solve instances of:

(4.18)

\text{Find}\ x\in H:\quad 0\in A_{1}(x)+\dots+A_{N}(x)\,,

with
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}\colon H\to 2^{H}
maximal monotone operators. The method only requires picking two real matrices
Z,\hat{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(N-1)}
and a step size
\tau>0
, with
\ker Z^{T}=\Span\{\mathbf{1}\}\subset\ker\hat{Z}^{T}
where
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\dots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then, denoting by
L:=ZZ^{T}
,
\hat{L}:=\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}
and
d:=\diag({L}+\hat{{L}})
, the update rule reads as:

(4.19)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{i}^{k+1}=J_{\frac{\tau}{d_{i}}A_{i}}\bigg(-\frac{2}{d_{i}}\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}\left({L}_{hi}+\hat{{L}}_{hi}\right)x_{h}^{k+1}+\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}Z_{ij}w_{j}^{k}\bigg)\,,\quad\text{for all}\ i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket\,,\\
&w_{j}^{k+1}=w_{j}^{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{ij}x_{i}^{k+1}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ j\in\llbracket 1,N-1\rrbracket\,.\end{aligned}\right.

This method generalizes a number of splitting methods and can in fact be shown to encompass
all
averaged frugal resolvent splitting methods with minimal lifting in the sense of Ryu, see
[
2
]
and the references therein. Similarly to Example
4.2
, it can be understood in the framework of degenerate proximal point methods with respect to
J_{\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\rhd\boldsymbol{A}}
for a maximal monotone operator
\boldsymbol{A}\colon\boldsymbol{H}\to 2^{\boldsymbol{H}}
, and a degenerate preconditioner
\boldsymbol{M}:=\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}
, this time with
\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\colon\boldsymbol{H}\to H^{N-1}
and
\boldsymbol{H}:=H^{2N-1}
, cf.
[
25
]
for their precise (but technical) definition. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{x}^{k+1}:=(x_{1}^{k+1},\dots,x_{N}^{k+1})
are the first
N
components of
(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{w}^{k})
, where
\boldsymbol{w}^{k}:=(w_{1}^{k},\dots,w_{N-1}^{k})
.

While in (
4.17
) the fixed-point residual coincides with the difference of the two solution estimates, for (
4.19
) it controls a natural dispersion functional between the components of
\boldsymbol{x}^{k+1}
, i.e., their
variance
:

(4.20)

\Var(\boldsymbol{x}^{k+1}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x_{i}^{k+1}-\bar{x}^{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}N}\|\boldsymbol{w}^{k}-J_{\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\rhd\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{k})\|^{2}=o(k^{-1})\,,\quad\text{as}\ k\to+\infty\,.

where
\bar{x}^{k+1}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k+1}
, and
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
L
, see
[
25
, Proposition 3.8]
or
[
33
, Lemma 5.2.1]
for a proof of the inequality in (
4.20
). Additionally, thanks to
[
24
, Theorem 2.9]
, all the solution estimates
(x_{i}^{k+1})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
converge weakly to the same solution to (
4.18
).

4.2.
Fast Degenerate Preconditioned Proximal Point Algorithms

Once the preconditioned resolvent
J_{M^{-1}A}
is formed, e.g., as in Examples
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
, we can employ it in conjunction to Algorithm
1
to study accelerated variants of degenerate preconditioned proximal point iterations (
4.11
). Denoting by
\theta_{k}:=\frac{\theta}{k+\sigma}
and
\alpha_{k}:=1-\frac{\alpha}{k+\sigma}
, we can consider, for
s\in(0,2]
, the
M
-averaged operator
T:=I+s(J_{M^{-1}A}-I)
and apply Algorithm
1
, yielding, for all
k\geq 0
,

(4.21)

u^{k+1}=u^{k}+\alpha_{k}(1-s)(u^{k}-u^{k-1})+s\theta_{k}J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})+s\alpha_{k}\Big(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k-1})\Big)\,.

Similarly to the non-accelerated setting, if
M
has closed range, using (
4.13
), the sequence
w^{k}:=C^{*}u^{k}\in D
can be easily seen to define an instance of Algorithm
1
with respect to
\tilde{T}:=I+s(J_{C^{*}\rhd A}-I)
, the latter being
\frac{s}{2}
-averaged in the classical sense since
C^{*}\rhd A
is maximal monotone. Therefore, convergence rates and weak convergence of
(w^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
follow immediately from Theorem
3.3
. However, using this argument, the weak convergence of
(J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
does not follow from that of
(w^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
since the convergence of
(w^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is in the weak topology and
J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})=(M+A)^{-1}(Cw^{k})
is typically nonlinear. The following lemma captures the argument that allows us to close this important gap and extend the analysis to general preconditioners, with non-necessarily closed range. The mechanism underlying this result can be traced back to
[
24
]
, although it was not explicitly established there.

Lemma 4.4

(Opial in the Degenerate Setting)
.

Let
A
be a maximal monotone operator with
\zer A\neq\emptyset
, and
M
a self-adjoint positive semidefinite preconditioner such that
(M+A)^{-1}
is Lipschitz over
\Image M
and
J_{M^{-1}A}
has full domain. Let
(u^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence, and suppose that:

(i)

For all
u^{*}\in\Fix J_{M^{-1}A}
the limit
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\ \|u^{k}-u^{*}\|_{M}
exists;

(ii)

and
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|u^{k}-J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\|_{M}=0
.

Then
J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\rightharpoonup u^{*}\in\zer A
as
k\to+\infty
. If, additionally,
M
has closed range and
M=CC^{*}
is onto, then for
w^{k}:=C^{*}u^{k}
:

w^{k}\rightharpoonup w^{*}\in\zer C^{*}\rhd A\,,\quad\text{and}\quad(A+M)^{-1}(Cw^{k})=J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})\rightharpoonup u^{*}\in\zer A\,,\quad\text{as}\ k\rightarrow+\infty\,.

Proof.

For brevity, let us denote by
v^{k}:=J_{M^{-1}A}(u^{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. From
(i)
, we deduce that the sequence
(Mu^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Therefore, since
(M+A)^{-1}C
is Lipschitz and
J_{M^{-1}A}=(M+A)^{-1}M
, which is then single-valued everywhere on
H
, also
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. Since
M(u^{k}-v^{k})\in A(v^{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|M(u^{k}-v^{k})\|=0
from point
(ii)
, and
A
is maximal monotone (in particular its graph is weak-to-strong sequentially closed
[
14
, Proposition 20.38]
), every weak cluster point of
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
\zer A
. Now we show that, in fact, it admits only one weak cluster point and thus
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to it. Let
(v^{k_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be two weakly converging subsequences and
v^{*},v^{**}\in H
their respective weak cluster points. Note that, from points
(i)
and
(ii)
, for all
u^{*}\in\zer A
,
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|v^{k}-u^{*}\|_{M}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|_{M}
, which we denote by
\ell(u^{*})
. Suppose by contradiction that
\|v^{*}-v^{**}\|_{M}\neq 0
. Thus,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\ell(v^{**})=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\|v^{k_{i}}-v^{**}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\left(\|v^{k_{i}}-v^{*}\|_{M}^{2}+2\langle v^{k_{i}}-v^{*},v^{*}-v^{**}\rangle_{M}+\|v^{*}-v^{**}\|_{M}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\|v^{*}-v^{**}\|_{M}^{2}+\lim_{i\to+\infty}\|v^{k_{i}}-v^{*}\|_{M}^{2}>\ell(v^{*})\,,

where we have used that
v^{k_{i}}\rightharpoonup v^{*}
. Reversing the role of
v^{*}
and
v^{**}
, we also get
\ell(v^{**})<\ell(v^{*})
, hence the desired contradiction, which yields

If

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a generalized version of the Fast-KM method proposed in
[
21
]
, incorporating two additional degrees of flexibility through the parameters
\theta\in(1,\alpha-1)
and
\sigma>0
. We provided a new convergence proof based on a semi-implicit discretization of a continuous-time model. Notably, our approach encompasses the case
\alpha=2
, which corresponds to the Optimal Halpern Method, thereby unifying the convergence analysis of these two acceleration mechanisms. We demonstrated how these results can be applied to operator splitting methods potentially with preconditioners, offering critical convergence guarantees for the so-called shadow sequences. Furthermore, our numerical experiments showcased the benefits afforded by the added flexibility of the method. This paper opens up new intriguing questions, among which: i) what is the tight non-asymptotic rate for Algorithm
1
? ii) Does Algorithm
1
converge when
\theta=\alpha-1
? We leave this for future research.

Discrete Calculus Rules

Let
a:=(a^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
b:=(b^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
c:=(c^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, be three real sequences. The product of two sequences
a
and
b
is denoted by
ab:=(a^{k}b^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. As usual, if
a=b
, we denote
a^{2}=ab
. Recall that
\Delta a_{k}:=a^{k+1}-a^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, Therefore, for all
k\geq 0
it holds

(6.1)

\displaystyle\Delta(ab)_{k}=\Delta a_{k}b^{k}+a^{k+1}\Delta b_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta(abc)_{k}=\Delta a_{k}b^{k}c^{k}+a^{k+1}\Delta b_{k}c^{k}+a^{k+1}b^{k+1}\Delta c_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta a^{2}_{k}=2(a^{k+1}-a^{k})a^{k}+(\Delta a_{k})^{2}=2(a^{k+1}-a^{k})a^{k+1}-(\Delta a_{k})^{2}\,.

This formalism can be readily extended to sequences in a Hilbert space
H
, with products replaced by inner products. For instance, if
(z^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
H
, and
\zeta^{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\|z^{k}\|^{2}
for all
k\geq 0
, can write

(6.2)

\Delta\zeta_{k}=\left\langle\Delta z_{k},z^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}=\left\langle\Delta z_{k},z^{k+1}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta z_{k}\|^{2}\,.

Similar rules as those in (
6.1
) apply for products of sequences in
H
and in
\mathbb{R}
, for instance if
(\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a real sequence, then for all
k\geq 0

(6.3)

\Delta(\varepsilon z)_{k}=\Delta\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}+\varepsilon^{k+1}\Delta z_{k}=\Delta z_{k}\varepsilon^{k}+z^{k+1}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}\,.
