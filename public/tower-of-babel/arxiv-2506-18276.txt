Modulator-Assisted Local Control of Quantum Battery via Zeno Effect
Songbo Xie, Manas Sajjan, Ashok Ajoy, Sabre Kais

Abstract
Quantum batteries are devices designed to store and deliver energy using quantum mechanics. In charger-mediated protocols, energy is transferred from a charger to batteries via their interaction, typically modulated through quenches that require either global or local access to the primary systems. Such control schemes can introduce noise and become impractical for long-range charging scenarios, where the charger and battery are spatially separated. To overcome these limitations, we propose a modulator-assisted scheme that enables indirect control of energy transfer using only local operations on a third system—the modulator—which interacts only with the charger. The charger-battery interaction remains always on and requires no direct control during the charging cycle. Instead, repeated unitary pulses applied to the modulator exploit the quantum Zeno effect to dynamically modulate the effective coupling strength, thereby controlling the battery’s charging speed. Our protocol enables the potential of remote, platform-independent control of energy flow and provides a framework for engineering interactions in spatially distributed quantum technologies.

In CMP setups, the coupling between the charger and the battery is assumed to be directly and manually quenched: the interaction is suddenly turned on to begin the charging phase, allowing energy transfer from the charger to the battery, and then turned off to begin the storing phase, preventing the reverse energy flow. Such time-dependent control of the interaction strength has been demonstrated on various platforms—for example, via tunable couplers in superconducting qubits

To enable long-range quantum battery charging, it is desirable to implement local control—the ability to control either the charger or the battery—without requiring synchronized or joint access to both subsystems. A natural strategy for achieving this is to dynamically modulate their frequency detuning, thereby inducing a tunable effective coupling between them. This can be realized by applying an external field to either the charger or the battery, as the transition frequencies of qubits can be externally controlled
Blais et al. 2004
;
DiCarlo et al. 2009
. However, such approaches still presume local access to the primary system.

In certain scenarios, it may be desirable—or even necessary—to avoid direct access to both the charger and the battery. This may be motivated by practical considerations such as simplifying hardware architecture, or by the need to suppress noise introduced through external control. These constraints motivate the question: Is it possible to dynamically control the effective interaction strength without directly touching the primary system? Achieving this would not only enhance the feasibility of CMP batteries in spatially distributed quantum systems, but also mitigate noise.

In this work, we propose a new framework to address this. Rather than directly modulating the interaction between the charger and battery, we introduce an auxiliary control element—a modulator qubit—which interacts only with the charger. The original charger-battery interaction is kept constantly on, and no direct control is applied to either the charger or the battery during the charging cycle. Instead, by controlling repeated unitary operations to the modulator qubit via the Zeno effect

Xie et al. 2025

Physical model.—
A standard charger-mediated quantum battery involves a battery qubit
b
interacting with a charger qubit
c
. In our model, we include an additional modulator qubit
m
that interacts only with the charger
c
. The modulator and the charger jointly forms an “expanded charger” system, with the Hamiltonian (
\hbar=1
)

H^{mc}=-\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}^{m}+\omega_{0}\sigma_{x}^{m}\sigma_{x}^{c},

(1)

where superscripts
\{m,c,b\}
label the qubits, and subscripts
\{x,y,z\}
label Pauli operators:
\sigma_{x}=|1\rangle\langle 0|+|0\rangle\langle 1|
,
\sigma_{y}=i|1\rangle\langle 0|-i|0\rangle\langle 1|
, and
\sigma_{z}=|0\rangle\langle 0|-|1\rangle\langle 1|
. The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
H^{mc}
are:

|v_{1}\rangle=|\widetilde{+}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
with
\lambda_{1}=-\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
;

|v_{2}\rangle=|\widetilde{0}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}
with
\lambda_{2}=-\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
;

|v_{3}\rangle=|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}
with
\lambda_{3}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
;

|v_{4}\rangle=|\widetilde{-}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
with
\lambda_{4}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
. Here,
|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
are eigenstates of
\sigma_{x}
, and
|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\equiv\exp(i\sigma_{y}\pi/8)|\psi\rangle
, where “
\sim
” indicates a rotation of an arbitrary state
|\psi\rangle
around the
y
-axis.

The expanded charger
mc
is coupled to the battery
b
under the Hamiltonian:

H^{mcb}=H^{mc}+2g(\sigma_{+}^{c}\sigma_{-}^{b}+\sigma_{-}^{c}\sigma_{+}^{b})-\omega_{1}\sigma_{z}^{b}.

(2)

with the raising operator
\sigma_{+}=|1\rangle\langle 0|
and the lowering operator
\sigma_{-}=|0\rangle\langle 1|
, and we choose
\omega_{1}=\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}
. We define the charger’s energy to be
E^{c}=\text{Tr}(\rho H^{mc})
. The battery’s energy is
E^{b}=\text{Tr}[\rho H^{b}]
, where we define the battery’s Hamiltonian to be
H^{b}\equiv\omega_{1}(\mathbb{1}^{b}-\sigma_{z}^{b})
, with the identity operator
\mathbb{1}
ensuring the lowest energy to be 0. Here,
\rho
is the density operator for the three-qubit system.

Assuming
g\ll\omega_{0}
, the rotating wave approximation (RWA) can be applied. By neglecting fast-oscillating terms, the interaction terms in Eq. (
2
) can be approximated as (derivations in
sup 2025
):

\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\left(|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{1}|-|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2}|\right)\otimes\sigma_{-}^{b}+\text{h.c.}\right]

(3)

where
h.c.
denotes the Hermitian conjugate. Under the RWA, the interaction permits transitions only between the pairs
|v_{1}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{3}\rangle
and
|v_{2}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{4}\rangle
for the expanded charger.

We initialize the system in the product state
|v_{3}\rangle^{mc}|0\rangle^{b}
. From the spectrum of
H^{mc}
, the transition
|v_{1}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{3}\rangle
has an energy difference of
2\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
. For the battery, the transition
|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle
has an energy difference of
2\omega_{1}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
. Due to the large detuning between these transitions, energy exchange is suppressed for the interaction Eq. (
3
).

To enable energy exchange, we want to freeze the modulator in its initial state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle
. This can be achieved using the quantum Zeno effect through repeated projective measurements. However, measurements are inherently stochastic, and the cumulative failure rate grows exponentially with time due to the non-unitary nature of repeated measurements. To overcome this limitation, we adopt the unitary control strategy from the super-Zeno method
Dhar et al. 2006
;
Singh et al. 2014
, in which Alice repeatedly applies the unitary operator
P=|\widetilde{0}\rangle\langle\widetilde{0}|-|\widetilde{1}\rangle\langle\widetilde{1}|
to the modulator, thereby suppressing its evolution away from
|\widetilde{1}\rangle
.

In the language of spin-echo techniques used in NMR systems, this operation effectively cancels out the evolution induced by non-
ZZ
interactions—in our case, the term
\omega_{0}\sigma_{x}^{m}\sigma_{x}^{c}
. As derived in
Xie et al. 2025
, freezing the modulator leads to an effective Hamiltonian for the charger given by

H_{\text{eff}}^{c}=\langle\widetilde{1}|^{m}H^{mc}|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{x}^{c}.

(4)

This effective Hamiltonian
H_{\text{eff}}^{c}
governs the charger’s free evolution, now characterized by an energy splitting of
\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
, which is resonant with the battery’s transition energy
2\omega_{1}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
.

Recall that the charger is initialized in
|+\rangle
, the excited state of
H^{c}_{\text{eff}}
, while the battery is initialized in
|0\rangle
, the ground state of
H^{b}
. Since the charger and the battery are resonant, energy flow becomes possible from the charger to the battery. Specifically, assuming
g\ll\omega
and applying the RWA, the interaction terms in Eq. (
2
), when expressed in their respective eigenbasis and with fast-oscillating terms neglected, is approximated as (derivations in
sup 2025
):

g\left(|+\rangle\langle-|^{c}\otimes\sigma_{-}^{b}+\text{h.c.}\right).

(5)

Note that the coupling strength is reduced from
2g
to
g
, with which the battery becomes maximally charged after a time of
\pi/(2g)
.

Tuning the inter-pulse interval.—
In practice, achieving perfectly dense pulsing is challenging. It is therefore important to examine how the system behaves as the inter-pulse interval
\tau
increases. In the limit
\tau\rightarrow 0
(i.e., perfectly dense pulsing), the effective detuning between the charger and battery vanishes. Conversely, when
\tau\rightarrow\infty
(i.e., no pulsing), the detuning reaches
\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
. For finite values of
\tau
satisfying
0<\tau<\infty
, one might naïvely expect the detuning as an increasing function of
\tau
between these two extremes. According to the Jaynes-Cummings model, an increase in detuning leads to an increase in the Rabi frequency, suggesting a larger charging speed
Gerry and Knight 2023
. If the detuning indeed increases with
\tau
, then the charging speed—defined as
1/T
, where
T
is the time when the battery first becomes maximally charged—would be expected to increase with
\tau
as well.

However, this expectation does not hold. In fact, for general finite values of
\tau
with
0<\tau<\infty
, a local effective Hamiltonian for the charger is not well defined, and the notion of detuning is also ill-defined. In Fig.
2
(d), we consider
\tau=\pi/(100g)
, which is 10 times larger than that in Fig.
2
(b). Contrary to the naïve expectation, the charging speed decreases with
\tau
rather than increases, while the amount of energy transferred remains nearly unchanged at
\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
. Specifically, complete energy transfer now occurs at
gt/\pi=\sqrt{2}
, in contrast to
gt/\pi=0.5
in the limit
\tau\rightarrow 0
.

For arbitrary pulse intervals
\tau
, it is observed numerically that the dynamics of the charger’s energy
E^{c}
and the battery’s energy
E^{b}
consistently exhibit sinusoidal behavior under the RWA (examples in
sup 2025

E^{c}
using the function
E^{c}(t)\approx\frac{A}{2}[\cos(\pi t/T)-1]+\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
, where
A
is the charging capacity defined in
Julià-Farré et al. 2020
(i.e., the deposited energy to the battery), and
T
is the time at which the battery first reaches full charge (with
1/T
the charging speed). In literature, the quantity
A/T
is referred to as the average charging power
Binder et al. 2015

\tau
, we extract
A
and
T
from the fitted dynamics and plot them as functions of
\tau
in Fig.
3

At large scale, we observe that
T
increases monotonically with
\tau
, while
A
decreases monotonically. This indicates that both the charging speed and capacity degrade as the pulses become more widely spaced. However, the plots also reveal a set of discrete, singular peaks.

These singularities occur at discrete pulse intervals
\tau_{n}=\sqrt{2}\pi n/\omega_{0}
. For example, when
g=0.01\omega_{0}
, this corresponds to
(1000/\pi)g\tau_{n}=10\sqrt{2}n

3
(more examples in
sup 2025

At these special pulsing intervals
\tau=\tau_{n}
, the effective time evolution operator between two pulses becomes
U(\tau_{n})\equiv(P\otimes\mathbb{1})e^{-iH^{mc}\tau_{n}}
, where
P=|\widetilde{0}\rangle\langle\widetilde{0}|-|\widetilde{1}\rangle\langle\widetilde{1}|
is the pulse acting on the modulator. It can be shown that
U(\tau_{n})(|\widetilde{1}\rangle\otimes|\pm\rangle)=-|\widetilde{1}\rangle\otimes|\pm\rangle
, meaning that these product states are eigenstates of the evolution operator. In contrast to the generic case, where no well-defined effective Hamiltonian exists for the charger, the existence of such eigenstates at
\tau=\tau_{n}
allows the system’s state to factorize effectively. While the modulator remains fixed in the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle
, a local effective Hamiltonian can again be defined for the charger, with
|\pm\rangle
being its eigenstates. However, the energy splitting for this new Hamiltonian does not match that of the battery,
2\omega_{1}
, resulting in a finite detuning. Consequently, energy exchange is suppressed, leading to the large values of
T
and small values of
A
seen at these singular points in Fig.
3
(detailed discussions in
sup 2025

We now comment on the valley points in the upper panel of Fig.
3
, which correspond to pulse intervals
\tau
that enable relatively fast charging. At these locally optimal values of
\tau
, the charging time
T
exhibits a linear dependence on
\tau
. A linear fit through these minima yields
T/\tau\approx 110
, as shown by the green line. Thus, at these optimal points, the charging time scales linearly with the pulse separation.

Comparison with other Zeno-based schemes.—
We note that a prior QB model also leveraged the Zeno effect. They used repeated pulses to freeze the battery’s state during the storing phase to preserve energy
Gherardini et al. 2020
, similar in concept to spin-echo techniques in NMR systems
Hahn 1950
;
Levitt and Freeman 1979
.

In contrast, our protocol applies pulses during the charging phase, not the storing phase. This distinction reduces the number of required pulses, since the charging duration is typically much shorter than the storing time—making our approach more practical for implementation. Furthermore, unlike conventional Zeno-based schemes that rely on ultra-fast pulsing, our method uses the pulse interval
\tau
to effectively tune the interaction strength between the charger and the battery. Larger values of
\tau
slow down energy transfer, resulting in a weaker effective coupling. Therefore, our protocol remains effective even beyond the conventional Zeno regime of small
\tau
. While increasing
\tau
leads to a trade-off in charging speed, the protocol’s robustness to slower pulsing makes it a flexible and experimentally viable tool for engineering interaction dynamics in quantum technologies.

Summary.—
We have proposed a modulator-assisted, charger-mediated quantum battery architecture that harnesses the quantum-Zeno effect to achieve local control of energy transfer. An auxiliary modulator qubit, coupled only to the charger, is driven by a sequence of unitary pulses. The pulse spacing turns an always-on charger-battery interaction into an effective coupling, whose strength—and even on/off character—can be dynamically adjusted in situ. Because neither the charger nor the battery requires direct tuning or synchronized access, the protocol avoids noise typically introduced by direct control pulses, and is inherently platform-independent and well-suited for long-range implementations.

Unlike earlier Zeno-based approaches that freeze battery dynamics during the storing phase, our protocol applies pulses during the charging phase, thereby reducing the number of required operations. Moreover, our method remains effective well beyond the strict Zeno regime, exhibiting a linear trade-off between the number of pulses and both charging speed and capacity.

While the present analysis focuses on a single-cell battery, the underlying control principles can be extended to multi-qubit systems—such as Dicke quantum batteries—where collective effects and other quantum properties are expected to enhance both charging power and capacity
Ferraro et al. 2018
;
Gemme et al. 2023
;
Hogan and Martin 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Seidov and Mukhin 2024
. Extending the protocol to such many-body settings and establishing its experimental feasibility represent compelling directions for future research.

-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}^{m}\otimes|-\rangle\langle+|^{c}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(|\widetilde{0}\rangle\langle\widetilde{0}|^{m}+|\widetilde{1}\rangle\langle\widetilde{1}|^{m}\Big)\otimes|-\rangle\langle+|^{c}=&-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(|\widetilde{0}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}\langle v_{2}|+|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}\langle v_{3}|\Big)\\
=&-\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\Big(|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2}|+|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2}|+|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{3}|-|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{3}|\Big),\\
-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}^{m}\otimes|-\rangle\langle-|^{c}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(|\widetilde{+}\rangle\langle\widetilde{+}|^{m}+|\widetilde{-}\rangle\langle\widetilde{-}|^{m}\Big)\otimes|-\rangle\langle-|^{c}=&-\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{1}|+|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{4}|\Big).\end{split}

(S4)

Assuming
g\ll\omega_{0}
, the Rotating Wave Approximation (RWA) can be applied. Using the Heisenberg equation of motion,
\dot{\mathcal{O}}=i[H^{mcb},\mathcal{O}]\approx i[H^{mc}+H^{b},\mathcal{O}]
, where
\mathcal{O}
is an arbitrary operator, and the interation Hamiltonian is neglected due to the weak coupling, this yields the following time dependence in the Heisenberg picture:

\begin{split}\sigma_{-}^{b}(t)\sim\sigma_{-}^{b}(0)e^{-2i\omega_{1}t}.\end{split}

(S5)

Similarly, for the twelve terms in Eq. (
S4
), one finds:

\begin{split}&|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{1}|\sim|v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{2}|\sim|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{3}|\sim|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{4}|\sim|v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{1}|\sim|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2}|\sim|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{3}|\sim|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{4}|\sim 1,\\
&|v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{4}|\sim|v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{3}|\sim e^{-2\sqrt{2}i\omega_{0}t},\\
&|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2}|\sim|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{1}|\sim e^{2\sqrt{2}i\omega_{0}t}.\end{split}

(S6)

When setting
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
, the frequency
2\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
matches
2\omega_{1}
, so only the terms
|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2}|
and
|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{1}|
remain resonant. All other terms are fast oscillating and are thus averaged out. Therefore, Eq. (
S1
) simplifies to:

\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\left(|v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{1}|-|v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2}|\right)\otimes\sigma_{-}^{b}+\text{h.c.}\right],

(S7)

which is precisely Eq. (3)
, and it explains the transition shown by the solid curves in Fig. 2(a).

In contrast, when setting
\omega_{1}=\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}
, all twelve terms in Eq. (
S4
) are fast oscillating and are thus averaged out. The whole interaction is suppressed and no energy exchange occurs—consistent with the solid flat lines in Fig. 2(b).

II
Derivation of Eq. (5)

When applying repeated pulses to the modulator,
H^{mc}
in Eq. (1) is modified to
H^{c}_{\text{eff}}
in Eq. (4). The operators in the original interaction Hamiltonian
2g(\sigma_{+}^{c}\sigma_{-}^{b}+\sigma_{-}^{c}\sigma_{+}^{b})
can be expressed more explicitly as:

2g(|1\rangle\langle 0|^{c}\otimes\sigma_{-}^{b}+\text{h.c.}),

(S8)

where h.c. is the Hermitian conjugate.

The goal is to decompose the vectors of the Hilbert space
c
in the eigenbasis of
H^{c}_{\text{eff}}
. This leads to:

|1\rangle\langle 0|^{c}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(|+\rangle\langle+|^{c}+|+\rangle\langle-|^{c}-|-\rangle\langle+|^{c}-|-\rangle\langle-|^{c}\Big).

(S9)

Assuming
g\ll\omega_{0}
, the Rotating Wave Approximation (RWA) can be applied. Using the Heisenberg equation of motion,
\dot{\mathcal{O}}=i[H^{mcb},\mathcal{O}]\approx i[H^{c}_{\text{eff}}+H^{b},\mathcal{O}]
, this yields the following time dependence in the Heisenberg picture:

\begin{split}&\sigma_{-}^{b}(t)\sim\sigma_{-}^{b}(0)e^{-2i\omega_{1}t},\\
&|+\rangle\langle+|^{c}\sim|-\rangle\langle-|^{c}\sim 1,\\
&|+\rangle\langle-|^{c}\sim e^{\sqrt{2}i\omega_{0}t},\\
&|-\rangle\langle+|^{c}\sim e^{-\sqrt{2}i\omega_{0}t}.\\
\end{split}

(S10)

When setting
\omega_{1}=\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}
, the frequency
\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
matches
2\omega_{1}
, so only the term
|+\rangle\langle-|
remains resonant. All other terms are fast oscillating and are thus averaged out. Therefore, Eq. (
S8
) simplifies to:

g\left(|+\rangle\langle-|^{c}\otimes\sigma_{-}^{b}+\text{h.c.}\right),

(S11)

which is precisely Eq. (5)
, and it explains the transition shown by the dotted curves in Fig. 2(b).

In contrast, when setting
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
, all terms in Eq. (
S9
) are fast oscillating and are thus averaged out. The whole interaction is suppressed and no energy exchange occurs—consistent with the dotted flat lines in Fig. 2(a).

III
Numerical fitting of the charger’s energy

When the pulses on the modulator are more widely spaced—i.e., when the inter-pulse interval
\tau
increases—both the charging speed and the charging capacity degrade.

Despite this, under the Rotating Wave Approximation (RWA), both the charger’s energy
E^{c}
and the battery’s energy
E^{b}
exhibit sinusoidal dynamics. Furthermore, the two energies are symmetric: their sum remains constant over time. This implies that as one increases, the other decreases.

Hence, it suffices to consider only the charger’s energy, which we approximate using the fit function

E^{c}(t)\approx\frac{A}{2}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)-1\right]+\sqrt{2}\omega_{0},

(S12)

where
A
is the total energy transferred (also referred to as the charging capacity), and
T
denotes the first time when the battery becomes maximally charged. The constant term
\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
ensures that the initial energy at
t=0
is correct.

One may check the following examples for different
\tau
values.

\omega_{0}=1
,
\omega_{1}=1/\sqrt{2}
,
g=0.01
. The inter-pulse interval is
\tau=\pi/(1000g)
. The battery gets maximally charged at
T=\pi/(2g)
. The charging capacity is
A=\sqrt{2}\omega_{0}
.

Setting
\omega_{0}=1
,

,

. The charging capacity is

.

IV
Analysis of the Singular Peaks in Fig. 3

To generalize the analysis, we introduce the additional parameters
\gamma
and
\mu
into the Hamiltonian
H^{mc}
, resulting in a modified eigenbasis given by:

\begin{split}|v_{1}\rangle^{mc}&=|\widetilde{+}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c},\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{1}=-\mu\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\omega_{0},\\
|v_{2}\rangle^{mc}&=|\widetilde{0}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c},\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{2}=-\mu\frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\omega_{0},\\
|v_{3}\rangle^{mc}&=|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c},\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{3}=\mu\frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\omega_{0},\\
|v_{4}\rangle^{mc}&=|\widetilde{-}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c},\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{4}=\mu\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\omega_{0}.\end{split}

(S13)

Here,
\gamma
represents the ratio of eigenvalues, defined by
\gamma\equiv\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}
, and
\mu
denotes an overall energy scaling factor for the Hamiltonian. The resulting Hamiltonian takes the form:

H^{mc}=-\mu\left(\frac{1-\gamma^{2}}{1+\gamma^{2}}\omega_{0}\sigma_{x}^{m}+\frac{2\gamma}{1+\gamma^{2}}\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}^{m}\right)+\mu\omega_{0}\sigma_{x}^{m}\sigma_{x}^{c}.

(S14)

Setting
\gamma=1
and
\mu=1
recovers the original Hamiltonian in Eq. (1).

Main conclusion.
The inter-pulse intervals
\tau
that lead to the singular peaks, as can be observed in Fig. 3, are denoted by
\tau_{n}
. The values of
\tau_{n}
are determined only by the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{4}
, not by
\lambda_{2}
or
\lambda_{3}
. These quantized values occur at

\tau_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{\lambda_{4}},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},

(S15)

where
n
is a positive integer.

Here we analyze the origin of the singular peaks by dividing the values of
\tau
into several regimes:

(1)

Fast-pulsing regime,
\tau\rightarrow 0
:

In the fast-pulsing limit, it has been shown that the local effective Hamiltonian for the charger becomes
\frac{\omega_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{x}^{c}
, with the charger initialized in the excited state
|+\rangle^{c}
. Meanwhile, the battery’s Hamiltonian is
-\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\sigma_{z}^{b}
, with the battery initialized in the ground state
|0\rangle^{b}
. Under the interaction Hamiltonian Eq. (5), energy exchange between the charger and the battery is enabled in this regime.

(2)

No-pulsing regime,
\tau\rightarrow\infty
:

In the absence of pulsing, the possible transitions for the charger, as given in Eq. (
S4
), namely

|v_{1}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{2}\rangle
,

|v_{1}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{3}\rangle
,

|v_{2}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{4}\rangle
, and

|v_{3}\rangle\leftrightarrow|v_{4}\rangle
, all have energy differences that do not match that of the battery transition
|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle
. As a result, energy exchange is suppressed due to detuning.

(3)

Intermediate regime: for general values of
0<\tau<\infty

For a general inter-pulse interval
\tau
, the total evolution operator acting on the modulator-charger system consists of two parts: (a) a free evolution under the Hamiltonian
H^{mc}
for a duration
\tau
, and (b) a control pulse represented by the operator
|\widetilde{0}\rangle\langle\widetilde{0}|^{m}-|\widetilde{1}\rangle\langle\widetilde{1}|^{m}
applied to the modulator. The combined evolution operator is given by:

U(\tau)\equiv\left[(|\widetilde{0}\rangle\langle\widetilde{0}|^{m}-|\widetilde{1}\rangle\langle\widetilde{1}|^{m})\otimes\mathbb{1}^{c}\right]\cdot e^{-iH^{mc}\tau}.

(S16)

Since the modulator is initialized in the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}
, the action of the pulse effectively preserves its state. Consequently, we only need to consider the two states
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}
and
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
in the analysis.

It is straightforward to verify that the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}
is an eigenvector of
U(\tau)
with eigenvalue
-e^{-i\lambda_{3}\tau}
.

On the other hand, the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
is generally
not
an eigenvector of
U(\tau)
, as evidenced by the fact that
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|v_{1}\rangle-|v_{4}\rangle)
and also:

\begin{split}U(\tau)|v_{1}\rangle&=e^{i\lambda_{4}\tau_{n}}|v_{4}\rangle,\\
U(\tau)|v_{4}\rangle&=e^{-i\lambda_{4}\tau_{n}}|v_{1}\rangle.\end{split}

(S17)

Therefore, in this intermediate regime, the local effective Hamiltonian for qubit B is generally ill-defined. The resulting dynamics involve complex coupling between the modulator and the charger, making the energy exchange process complex and lacking a simple physical interpretation.

(4)

Intermediate regime: for
\tau=\tau_{n}\equiv 2\pi n/\lambda_{4}
, with
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

At these discrete values of
\tau
, the total evolution operator
U(\tau_{n})
exhibits a special symmetry:

\begin{split}U(\tau_{n})|v_{1}\rangle&=|v_{4}\rangle,\\
U(\tau_{n})|v_{4}\rangle&=|v_{1}\rangle.\end{split}

(S18)

Since the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
can be written as
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|v_{1}\rangle-|v_{4}\rangle)
,
it follows that

\begin{split}U(\tau_{n})|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}=&-e^{-i\lambda_{3}\tau}|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c},\\
U(\tau_{n})|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}=&-|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}.\end{split}

(S19)

Therefore, at these specific values of
\tau
, both
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|+\rangle^{c}
and
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}|-\rangle^{c}
are eigenstates of the effective evolution operator
U(\tau_{n})
. This implies that, under the assumption that the modulator remains frozen in the state
|\widetilde{1}\rangle^{m}
, a well-defined local effective Hamiltonian exists for the charger, with eigenstates
|\pm\rangle
.

However, the energy splitting of this effective charger Hamiltonian does not match that of the battery, which is
2\omega_{1}
. The resulting detuning prevents resonant energy exchange between the charger and the battery. This mismatch explains the appearance of singularities in Fig. 3: the charging time
T
becomes extremely large and the charging capacity
A
becomes extremely small at those points.

To further validate this analysis, we vary the parameters
\gamma
and
\mu
in the generalized Hamiltonian Eq. (
S14
), and observe that the first singular point consistently appears at
\tau=2\pi/\lambda_{4}
.

It is important to note that when the parameters
\gamma
and
\mu
are varied, the maximum charging capacity
A
also changes. However,
A
depends only on the eigenvalue
\lambda_{3}
, and is given by
A=\lambda_{3}/2=\mu\gamma\omega_{0}/\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}
. Consequently, to achieve resonance between the battery and the charger, the battery frequency should be set to
\omega_{1}=\mu\gamma\omega_{0}/\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}
.

Moreover, the definition of the “
\sim
” symbol should be updated accordingly, in order to make sure that the original expression of
|v_{i}\rangle
are eigenstates of
H^{mc}
. Specifically, for a generic state
|\psi\rangle
, we define the transformed state as
|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\equiv e^{i\sigma_{y}\theta}|\psi\rangle
, where
\theta=\arctan(\gamma)/2
.

With these realignments of the parameters, we obtain the following figures, where it consistently shows that the first singular peak appears at
\tau_{1}=2\pi/\lambda_{4}\equiv\pi\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}/(\mu\omega_{0})
:

Here, we choose
\gamma=0.7
and
\mu=1
, while fixing the system parameters as
\omega_{0}=1
and
g=0.01
. Under these conditions, it can be shown that the first singular peak occurs at
(1000/\pi)g\tau_{1}\approx 12.21
. This matches the analysis of
\tau_{1}=2\pi/\lambda_{4}
.

and

\omega_{0}=1
and

. Under these conditions, it can be shown that the first singular peak occurs at
(1000/\pi)g\tau_{1}\approx 7.07
. This matches the analysis of
\tau_{1}=2\pi/\lambda_{4}
.
