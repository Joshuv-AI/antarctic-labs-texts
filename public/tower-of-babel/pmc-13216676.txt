Syringomyelia and hydrocephalus following tuberculosis are not necessarily caused by mild Chiari I malformation
Authors: Sinda Zarrouk, Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the article by Jamaludin et al. [1] describing a 37-year-old woman with progressive paraparesis and sensory disturbances in the lower extremities, attributed to holocordal communicating syringomyelia secondary to Chiari I malformation. The patient refused surgical correction; however, partial remission was achieved with physical therapy [1]. Although this study is interesting, it has some limitations.

First, Chiari I malformation does not adequately explain holocordal syringomyelia. Although Chiari I malformations are often associated with syringomyelia, they are usually limited to a small section of the spinal cord. In the index patient, the Chiari I malformation was mild. Therefore, alternative causes of syringomyelia should be considered and excluded before attributing it solely to Chiari I malformation. Potential causes include genetic factors, tethered cord, trauma, infectious or immunological diseases, vasculitis, paraneoplasia, and neoplasia [2]. Given the patient’s recent history of tuberculosis, which is rarely associated with syringomyelia [3], it is essential to exclude tuberculosis as a cause. It is important to know in which organ tuberculosis manifested, which antibiotics were prescribed, and whether adherence to therapy was confirmed. Furthermore, which cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests were performed to exclude Mycobacterium tuberculosis? Were Löwenstein cultures or polymerase chain reaction analyses performed?

Second, a discrepancy exists in the description that the patient was able to stand but was unable to move around. This discrepancy should be addressed. Did the patient use a walking aid or stick for standing?

Third, it is unclear why paraparesis affected the lower but not the upper extremities, despite increased tendon reflexes in the upper extremities. Given that magnetic resonance imaging of the spine showed that syringomyelia was more pronounced in the cervical spinal cord than in the lumbar spinal cord, upper extremity paraparesis was expected.

Fourth, no information was provided regarding the CSF pressure elevation during the lumbar puncture. Was the Queckenstedt test performed? CSF flow measurements are essential to assess whether Chiari I malformation contributes to increased intracranial pressure.

Fifth, no explanation was provided for the absence of classic symptoms of Chiari I malformation, such as headache, neck pain, dizziness, balance disorders, numbness or tingling, dysphagia, or ataxia in the index patient [4].

Sixth, the patient refused surgery but benefited from physical therapy [1]. As no explanation was provided as to why the patient’s condition improved with physical therapy, it is necessary to determine whether the imaging abnormalities improved. If a causal relationship exists between paraparesis and syringomyelia, syringomyelia is expected to regress on imaging. Additionally, it was not reported whether the sensory disturbances improved with the physical therapy. Does physical therapy improve paraparesis or sensory disturbance?

In summary, before attributing syringomyelia in a patient with recent tuberculosis to Chiari I malformation, all alternative causes, including tuberculosis, must be ruled out. Partial improvement in physical therapy requires comprehensive explanation. A case report of progressive paraparesis should not be titled “foot drop,” as this misrepresents the clinical presentation.
