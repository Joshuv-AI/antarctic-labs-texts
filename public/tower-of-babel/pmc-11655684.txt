Characterization and z-score calculation of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging parameters in patients after the Fontan operation: A Fontan Outcome Registry using Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance Examinations study
Authors: Tarek Alsaied, Runjia Li, Adam B Christopher, Mark Fogel, Timothy C Slesnick, Rajesh Krishnamurthy, Vivek Muthurangu, Adam L Dorfman, Christopher Z Lam, Justin D Weigand, Jong-Hyeon Jeong, Joshua D Robinson, Laura J Olivieri, Rahul H Rathod, the FORCE Investigators
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) offers valuable hemodynamic insights post-Fontan, but is limited by the absence of normative single ventricle data. The Fontan Outcomes Registry using CMR Examinations (FORCE) is a large international Fontan-specific CMR registry. This study used FORCE registry data to evaluate expected CMR ventricular size/function and create Fontan-specific z-scores adjusting for ventricular morphology (VM) in healthier Fontan patients.Methods“Healthier” Fontan patients were defined as patients free of adverse outcomes, who are New York Heart Association class I, have mild or less valve disease, and <30% aortopulmonary collateral burden. General linear modeling was performed on 70% of the dataset to create z-scores for volumes and function. Models were tested using the remainder (30%) of the data. The z-scores were compared between children and adults. The z-scores were also compared between “healthier” Fontan and patients with adverse outcomes (death, listing for transplantation, or multiorgan disease).ResultsThe “healthier” Fontan population included 885 patients (15.0 ± 7.6 years) from 18 institutions with 1156 CMR examinations. Patients with left ventricle morphology had lower volume, mass and higher ejection fraction (EF) compared to right or mixed (two ventricles) morphology (p < 0.001 for all pairwise comparisons). Gender, body surface area, and VM were used in z-scores. Of the “healthier” Fontan patients, 647 were children <18 years and 238 were adults. Adults had lower ascending aorta flow (2.9 ± 0.7 vs 3.3 ± 0.8 L/min/m2, p < 0.001) and ascending aorta flow z-scores (−0.16 ± 1.23 vs 0.05 ± 0.95, 0.02) compared to children. Additionally, there were 1595 patients with adverse outcomes who were older (16.1 ± 9.3 vs 15.0 ± 7.6, p < 0.001) and less likely to have left VM (35 vs 47%, p < 0.001). Patients with adverse outcomes had higher z-scores for ventricular volume and mass and lower z-scores for EF and ascending aorta flow compared to the “healthier” Fontan cohort.ConclusionThis is the first study to generate CMR z-scores post-Fontan. Importantly, the z-scores were generated and tested in “healthier” Fontan patients and both pediatric and adult Fontan patients. These equations may improve CMR-based risk stratification after the Fontan operation.Keywords: Fontan, Congenital heart disease, Z-scores, Normal values

Introduction

The Fontan operation has improved the lives of patients with single ventricle congenital heart disease, offering them long-term survival while improving their quality of life [1]. It is estimated that a total of 70,000 individuals worldwide are living with the Fontan circulation [2]. As Fontan patients age, they face an increasing number of morbidities and exercise intolerance [3], [4], [5]. To help identify those at risk for adverse outcomes, it is essential to use high-fidelity diagnostic testing for risk stratification following the Fontan procedure.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is an important tool for surveillance and management after the Fontan operation [6]. CMR is the gold standard for evaluating ventricular volume, mass, and function and is valuable in predicting adverse clinical outcomes in Fontan patients [7], [8], [9], [10]. However, interpreting measured ventricular parameters compared to expected values in the Fontan population remains difficult as normative reference standards do not exist. Traditionally, ventricular measurements are indexed using body surface area (BSA) or BSA1.3 as these have been shown to correct for heart rate, age, and body size throughout the lifespan in patients with biventricular hearts [11], [12], [13], [14]. Those assumptions may not hold true in patients with single ventricle physiology and may lead to misinterpretation of ventricular volumes and function given the heterogeneity of ventricular morphology (VM).

The “Fontan Outcomes Registry using CMR Examinations” or FORCE initiative was founded in 2020 as an effort to share data of patients after the Fontan operation. FORCE is a multi-institutional research platform across 36 institutions in the Americas and Europe. Patients must have at least one technically adequate CMR examination to be included in the registry. Clinical history, surgical history, testing data, and clinical outcomes are also recorded as part of the registry data collection.

The objective of this study was to use the FORCE dataset to evaluate the expected CMR-derived parameters of single ventricular size and function for patients after the Fontan operation. The study created parameter-specific z-scores for patients with single ventricle anatomy, including adjustments for relevant clinical factors to aid in the interpretation of observed vs expected results in this population.

Methods

Patients

This study was performed using data from the FORCE registry. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Boston Children’s Hospital. Participating FORCE centers either relied on the Boston Children’s Hospital IRB or had the study approved by their local IRB or ethics review committee. The study proposal and this article were approved by the FORCE Data Governance and Publications Committee and the FORCE Investigators. This study included all the CMR examinations in the FORCE registry as of December 2022. Data for this article are available upon request.

CMR acquisition

CMR studies were performed on various vendor magnets (Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen Germany, and GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, Wisconsin). Protocols varied by institution, but generalized Fontan CMR guidelines have been published previously [15], [16]. Ventricular assessment was performed by an electrocardiographically-gated cine steady-state free precision technique in ventricular short-axis planes, encompassing the cardiac apex through the atria, reconstructed 20–30 phases per cardiac cycle. Only patients with technically adequate images to calculate ventricular size and function were included in the FORCE dataset.

CMR data analysis

Quantitative CMR parameters were abstracted from the clinical reports and data entered into the FORCE registry by the centers and were not measured by a core lab. Ventricular volumes were measured by manual tracing of endocardial and epicardial borders at end-diastole (maximal volume) and end-systole (minimal volume) as previously described and were recorded in the registry [8], [16]. Trabeculations and papillary muscles in right and left ventricles were excluded from the ventricular volumes. In patients with two identifiable ventricles that contributed to the systemic circulation, their volumes and masses were summated, consistent with prior publications [16]. To better account for differences in body size, single ventricle end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and stroke volume (SV) [8], [11] measurements were indexed to BSA and BSA1.3. BSA was calculated using the Mosteller equation. Ascending aorta (AAo) flow measured through two-dimensional phase contrast was also reported and was indexed to BSA. In patients with an aortopulmonary anastomosis (e.g, Damus Kaye Stansel) with antegrade native and neoaortic flow, the AAo flow was obtained above the level of the anastomosis or the sum of the native and neoaorta flows was used. The VM was defined as right ventricular (RV) dominant, left ventricular (LV) dominant, or mixed morphology. Mixed morphology was defined a priori if the smaller ventricle contributes to the systemic circulation and if the smaller ventricle EDV is ≥25% of the combined ventricular EDV.

Clinical data

Clinical and demographic data, including underlying diagnoses and type of single ventricle, were abstracted from the medical records. Arrhythmia history was compiled by review of Holter monitors, electrocardiograms, electrophysiology catheterizations, and clinic notes that were submitted to the FORCE database. Other variables included a history of congestive heart failure requiring treatment, protein-losing enteropathy (PLE), stroke, thrombus, or seizures.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed to demonstrate the baseline characteristics of patients and the CMR parameters of the study cohort. For continuous variables, we report mean and standard deviation, as well as the interquartile range (IQR) when appropriate. For categorical variables, we report frequencies and percentages. The CMR characteristics of patients with different VM are presented, and the significant differences between groups were tested using one-way analysis of variance test.

Z-score calculation procedures

To create Fontan-specific z-scores, we used linear mixed models to predict the CMR volumetric measurements while accounting for a subject-specific random effect. Sex, VM, and BSA were identified as independent variables related to the volumetric measurements and were thus included in the models. Age was not included in the final model since it is highly correlated with BSA (ρ=0.717). Typically, z-scores are generated using “normal” healthy patients. While Fontan physiology is inherently abnormal, z-score regressions were generated on “healthier” Fontan patients who met the following criteria at the most recent follow-up: free of the outcome of death, listing for transplantation, cardiac arrest not in the perioperative period, sustained ventricular tachycardia, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or greater, PLE, plastic bronchitis, history of prolonged or chylous chest tube output >30 days post-Fontan, history of thrombus, stroke, hepatocellular carcinoma, hemoptysis, renal dysfunction, need for cardioversion, the need for a pacemaker or defibrillator. Patients with more than mild valve disease (aortic or atrioventricular valve) were defined as regurgitant fraction >20% and patients with aorticopulmonary collateral burden >30% which is defined as [AAo flow − (caval flow)]/AAo flow] >30% were excluded. Data from those patients with healthier Fontan physiology were split into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets to evaluate and compare the performance of models. The training set was used to fit models and calculate the Akaike information criterion and R2, and then applied the models to the test set to predict and calculate the root mean square error (RMSE). Models with the best predicting performance were selected mainly based on RMSE, which reflects how far the predictions fall from true values and is commonly used for evaluating the quality of predictions. R2 was used to compare models and was reported to present how the model fits in the training subsets.

The z-scores were also compared between the groups with adverse outcomes and healthier Fontan. Patients with adverse outcomes were defined as NYHA class ≥2 functional status or with any of the following: PLE, plastic bronchitis, history of thrombus, ascites, hepatocellular carcinoma, renal dysfunction, the outcome of death, listing for transplantation, or pacemaker insertion. Z-scores were also compared between children (<18 years) and adults in the healthier Fontan group. Data management and statistical analysis were completed using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

At the time of this analysis, the FORCE registry and entire study cohort consisted of 2480 unique Fontan patients, 3834 CMR examinations from 18 institutions. There were 885 patients with 1156 CMR examinations who met the criteria for the healthier Fontan and were used for the z-score regression. The mean age at CMR was 15.0 ± 7.6 years. Comparison of z-score values in children and adults

Of the “healthier” Fontan 647 were children <18 years and 238 were adults. Adults were more likely to have LV morphology in our cohort compared to children (53 vs 45%, p = 0.04). Adults have lower AAo flow (2.9 ± 0.7 vs 3.3 ± 0.8 L/min/m2, p < 0.001) and AAo flow z-scores (−0.16 ± 1.23 vs 0.05 ± 0.95, p = 0.02) compared to children (Table 6). Otherwise, there was no difference in any of the z-scores between children and adults (Table 6).

Comparison of z-score values in patients with adverse outcomes to healthier Fontan

There were 1595 patients with adverse outcomes. Patients with adverse outcomes were older (16.1 ± 9.3 vs 15.0 ± 7.6, p < 0.001), less likely to have LV morphology (35 vs 47%, p < 0.001) and more likely female compared to “healthier” Fontan (43 vs 38%, p < 0.001) (Table 7). Patients with adverse outcomes had higher z-scores for EDV, ESV, and mass and lower z-scores for EF and AAo flow (Graphical abstract).

Discussion

This study summarizes the observed CMR volumetric values for patients with the Fontan circulation using the large multi-institutional FORCE registry and introduces regression equations to calculate Fontan-specific z-scores. To date, this is the largest analysis of any kind in patients after the Fontan operation. The magnitude of this dataset allowed for the calculation of z-scores for single ventricle CMR-derived volumetric parameters adjusting for gender, VM, and BSA in “healthier” Fontan patients. The analysis also found that 1) patients with adverse outcomes had higher z-scores for EDV, ESV, and mass with lower z-scores for AAo flow and EF and 2) adults have lower AAo flow z-scores compared to children.

Z-scores derived from volunteers with biventricular physiology may not apply to patients with single ventricles, given the considerable heterogeneity in VM and the modifications in ventricular preload and afterload. This study introduced a new framework to enable the computation of z-scores in Fontan patients by leveraging a large CMR Fontan dataset. To further validate these z-score calculations, they were generated in a healthier group of Fontan patients and were tested in another subset with good performance. One exception was the performance of z-scores calculation for EF. Although EF had a significant correlation with BSA, gender, and VM, the model explained only ∼6% of the variability of EF in this population. This is not surprising as EF is a derived parameter where there is likely propagation of ventricular volume measurement error. EF is also affected by numerous factors, including surgical history, genetic factors, environmental factors, and alterations in preload and afterload. Thus, similar to the population without congenital heart disease, the use of z-scores for EF may be less clinically relevant and the absolute values should continue to be used [12], [13].

This study showed important differences in ventricular volumetric measurements and mass based on VM. The LV morphology has lower volume and mass and higher EF compared to the RV or mixed morphology. This might be due to the systemic RV dilation and hypertrophy that occurs in single ventricles, consistent with previous echocardiographic studies [17], [18].

Our study also showed that patients who are free of any adverse outcomes have smaller ventricles, and higher EF, and AAo flow. This is consistent with multiple previous studies showing that dilated and dysfunctional ventricles are associated with worse clinical outcomes after the Fontan operation [8], [10]. Ventricular dilation and dysfunction are likely the common pathway of several pathological changes in single ventricle, including valve regurgitation, chronic volume overload, and sinus node dysfunction leading to adverse remodeling and ventricular dysfunction. We also observed a male predominance in our population, with females being less likely to be in the healthier Fontan group. This finding further emphasizes the importance of including sex in the z-score equations and considering it in the risk stratification of Fontan patients. Additionally, we found that adults have lower AAo flow z-scores which suggests that the cardiac output decreases as these patients age. This may explain the gradual deterioration noted in the adults as the cardiac index decreases.

It is noteworthy that the z-scores utilized in this study were generated from a comprehensive dataset that included only healthier patients with Fontan. This approach offers two major strengths: 1) it improves comparability and benchmarking against a large cohort of patients with Fontan, and 2) the z-scores are more robust against outliers, which is a prevalent issue with z-scores generated in small samples of healthy people without congenital heart disease where slight variations in the volume above a certain point can substantially impact the z-score. However, utilizing the new z-score also presents a downside, namely, the larger standard deviation that results from including Fontan patients, leading to “more abnormal values” to attain a z-score ≥2 or ≤−2. This quandary is illustrated in the following example. A recent analysis of a large Fontan CMR dataset comprising 416 patients demonstrated that EDVi ≥156 mL/BSA1.3 is associated with worse transplant-free survival. For a female patient with systemic LV and BSA of 1.5 (BSA1.3 = 1.69), this equates to an EDVi of ≥176 mL/m2 [(156 × 1.69)/1.5]. Using the z-scores from our study, this corresponds to a z-score ≥3.1. In contrast, using the z-scores from Buechel et al., this volume would translate to a z-score ≥8.2 [13]. Thus, these new z-scores will need to be interpreted differently from the traditional z-scores. While in very general terms, a z-score in the range of 2–4 is considered only mildly increased EDV using traditional z-scores, the same z-score may be associated with transplant-free survival using the new z-scores. To address this dilemma, we have planned a future study to compare these z-scores adjusted to sex, VM, and BSA against parameters indexed by BSA alone in predicting adverse outcomes after the Fontan operation and detect thresholds that may correlate with adverse outcomes.

Limitations

As a CMR-centric study, some patients with pacemakers or defibrillators may have been excluded as these devices are a relative contraindication to CMR. The use of pacemakers and defibrillators is common, seen in ∼13% of patients after the Fontan operation [19]. There is also a potential for a selection bias with more symptomatic patients getting CMR, although this is less likely as most centers now use CMR as a routine surveillance tool [20]. Additionally, we used the “healthier” Fontan to generate the z-scores and it is possible that some pediatric patients may be free of the criteria listed at the time of the study although may develop some of these criteria as they age. Finally, this study used real-world clinically reported data from multiple institutions. There may be differences in imaging protocols and post-processing techniques between institutions. To address this limitation, core lab values are being currently generated and will be compared to the institutional values.

Conclusion

This is the first study to generate z-scores specific to patients with single ventricle physiology in healthier Fontan patients. The z-score equations adjust for gender, BSA, and VM. These derived z-scores may prove to be more clinically meaningful for risk stratification and/or decision-making than indexing to BSA alone. The study found that Fontan patients who are free of any adverse outcomes have smaller ventricles with lower mass and higher EFs and AAo flow. Importantly, the z-scores performed well both in children and adults and showed that adults have lower AAo flow z-scores. These equations may have added value in clinical practice and when reporting CMR data in patients after the Fontan operation.
