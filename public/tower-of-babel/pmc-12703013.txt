Role of Pain in Adults Undergoing Soft Tissue and Maxillomandibular Advancement Surgery for Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Ghedak Ansari, Jeanne A. Darbinian, Samuel A. Collazo, Nikolas R. Block‐Wheeler, Megan L. Durr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveIn adult patients who undergo soft tissue and maxillomandibular advancement (MMA) sleep surgery for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), we identify preoperative pain diagnoses and postoperative pain medication dispensing patterns by surgery type.MethodsThis retrospective cohort chart review study examines adults who underwent sleep surgery for OSA at an integrated healthcare delivery system (2009–2017). Data obtained from electronic sources included demographics, surgery type, presentation to the emergency department, hospital readmission, and postoperative medication dispensings.ResultsA total of 2287 adult patients were identified. The average age at surgery was 40.8 ± 12.5 years. Surgeries performed included (n, %) uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (1312, 57.3%) tonsillectomy (577, 25.2%), genioglossus muscle advancement (GA) alone or combined with MMA (108, 4.7%) and MMA alone (170, 7.4%), adenoidectomy (60, 2.6%), tongue and palate surgery (54, 2.4%), and tongue reduction (6, 0.3%). A total of 811 (35.5%) had a preoperative pain‐related diagnosis, and 336 (14.7%) had a preoperative diagnosis of depression. Of the total cohort, 912 (39.9%) had a preoperative opioid prescription. In our cohort, 91.7% were prescribed outpatient opioid medication postoperatively. This varied by surgery type, with the highest proportion of opioid prescription dispensings observed in the soft tissue surgery group (non‐MMA ± GA) and the lowest in the maxillofacial surgery group (MMA ± GA). In contrast, the maxillofacial surgery group had the highest proportion of nonopioid prescriptions dispensed, while the soft tissue surgery group had the lowest.ConclusionsNon‐hypoglossal nerve stimulation (non‐HGNS) sleep surgery for OSA is associated with high (nearly 92%) postoperative opioid dispensings. Adult patients undergoing non‐HGNS sleep surgery are likely at increased risk of opioid complications given the high prevalence of preoperative opioid use, chronic pain, and depression.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is rapidly rising in prevalence, currently affecting an estimated 24% of men and 9% of women in the United States [1]. OSA is related to several comorbidities, including obesity, coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, depression, fatigue, chronic pain, and automobile accidents [2]. Additionally, it can influence quality of life due to psychosocial side effects, including fatigue and impaired cognitive function [1]. The gold standard treatment for adult OSA is continuous positive airway pressure machine (CPAP), but CPAP compliance is poor, with studies estimating a 46% to 83% intolerance rate [3, 4]. Poor compliance with CPAP has created an increased need for alternative OSA treatment methods such as oral appliances and sleep surgery.

Sleep surgery, which can include tonsillectomy, modified or traditional uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP), maxillomandibular advancement (MMA), lingual tonsillectomy, and midline glossectomy, is generally associated with significant postoperative pain requiring hospital admission and opioid usage in the postoperative period [5]. While not assessed in this study, hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS) is also a surgical treatment option for OSA that has been shown to be safe and effective [6]. HGNS is generally associated with lower postoperative pain when compared with other sleep surgeries, but it is not an option for all patients [7].

Studies have found that tonsillectomy has the highest oral morphine equivalents prescribed postoperatively compared to other otolaryngologic surgeries [8, 9]. There has also been an increase within the United States of opioid‐involved overdose deaths, which has led to important initiatives to minimize opioid usage [10]. Furthermore, postoperative prescribing accounts for an estimated 36.5% of all opioid prescriptions in the United States, and recent studies have shown that, after the first prescription, about 6% of opioid‐naïve patients will go on to develop long‐term opioid dependence [11, 12]. In 2017, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that the duration of the first opioid prescription has a direct correlation with long‐term opioid use [13]. Moreover, the association between depression, pain, and pain management is complex; a previous study suggests that depression is a moderator of the relationship among opioid use, pain severity, and physical functioning [14]. Additionally, depression is a known comorbidity in OSA patients, and untreated OSA may worsen depression symptoms [15]. An important step towards decreasing opioid usage after sleep surgery involves examining pain medication use trends and comorbidities that may place patients at higher risk for opioid consumption. The primary objective of this study is to compare the prevalence of chronic pain and/or depression diagnoses and the postoperative medication dispensing patterns among adult patients who undergo different types of non‐HGNS sleep surgery to treat underlying OSA. Identifying factors associated with higher pain levels and/or opioid requirements post‐sleep surgery allows otolaryngologists to develop better multimodal pain management plans. Recent studies have shown success with multimodal analgesia after benign oropharynx surgery [16].

Methods

Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) electronic databases (with clinical, pharmacy, administrative, and utilization data) were used to identify the OSA cohort. This study was approved by the KPNC Region Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent due to the study's design. The cohort was identified by selecting patients with at least two separate encounter‐linked diagnoses of OSA or patients with at least one encounter‐linked OSA diagnosis and at least one OSA diagnosis listed in the subject's medical problem list. The diagnosis of OSA was based on ICD‐9 (327.23) or ICD‐10 (G47.33) diagnosis codes. We selected the first diagnosis linked to a qualifying clinical encounter that occurred between 2009 and 2016, for subjects aged 18–89 years.

The KP Health Connect (KPHC) OpTime Module (an operating room management system with perioperative records), and procedure codes linked to hospitalization discharge records were used to identify OSA‐related surgeries performed on the cohort. We selected non‐nasal surgeries that were performed on or after the first qualifying OSA diagnosis through 2017. Surgeries were selected from a list of relevant ICD‐9, ICD‐10, CPT‐4, and KPHC OpTime procedure codes (confirmed by operating room log descriptions). Members of the OSA cohort who underwent adenoidectomy, genioglossus muscular advancement (GA), MMA surgery, palate or tongue base radiofrequency ablation, tonsillectomy, lingual tonsillectomy, midline glossectomy, and UPPP were included and grouped as shown in Table 1.

If subjects had qualifying sleep surgeries performed on different days, we selected the first surgery date. Thus, the final cohort was comprised of unique individuals with one surgery type and one surgery date. Demographic and clinical utilization data, encounters, encounter‐linked diagnosis codes for comorbid pre‐existing conditions, and medications were obtained from KPNC electronic databases. Patient demographics included sex, age (at OSA diagnosis and index surgery), race, and ethnicity. Pre‐existing conditions that were selected included depression, bleeding‐related, and acute or chronic pain based on any encounter‐linked diagnosis that occurred within the year before surgery.

Postoperative outcomes that were identified included hospital length of stay, presentation to the emergency department (ED), and readmission to the hospital. Outcomes were only included if they occurred between the time of discharge from surgery and 2 months postoperatively. Among those who returned to the ED or were readmitted, primary admitting or discharge diagnoses for these patients were identified and flagged for depression, bleeding, or pain diagnoses. To capture all encounters likely associated with re‐presentation due to pain, diagnoses that included dehydration, syncope, and dysphagia were included.

As shown in Table 2, medications that were identified included opioid medications, nonopioid medications to treat pain, glucocorticoids, and selected antidepressants. Formulations, administration methods, and amounts administered varied within each medication type or category. Outpatient medications were ascertained from pharmacy dispensing records, and medications administered in the hospital were determined from the medication administration record.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared by surgery type (category) using counts and percentages, means ± standard deviations, and medians and interquartile ranges. Differences between groups were evaluated using Fisher's exact tests for categorical data and t‐tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and non‐parametric measures (Kruskal‐Wallis test) for continuous and ordinal variables. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All tests were two‐sided, and analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc.).

Within the year prior to surgery, 811 (35.5%) patients had a pain‐related diagnosis linked to a clinical encounter, and 336 (14.7%) patients had a depression‐related diagnosis linked to a clinical encounter. Within this same period, a total of 912 (39.9%) patients had an opioid pain medication dispensed by an outpatient pharmacy, and dispensing patterns varied by type of surgery performed (p < 0.01), with those who underwent MMA alone having the highest proportion (68.2%, Table 3).

Of the 2287 patients who underwent surgery to treat their underlying OSA, 2098 (91.7% [95% confidence interval 90.6–92.9]) had ≥ 1 opioid medication prescription dispensed between hospital discharge for the index surgery and up to 2 months after by outpatient pharmacies, and the proportion of these dispensings varied by surgery type (p < 0.001). The tongue and palate, UPPP, and tonsillectomy groups had the highest percentage of dispensed outpatient opioid prescriptions (96.3% [91.3–100.0], 95.1% [93.9–96.2], and 94.3% [92.4–96.2], respectively) followed by patients who underwent adenoidectomy (85.0% [76.0–94.0]), tongue reduction surgery (83.3% [53.5–100]), and patients in the maxillofacial surgery groups, MMA + GA (79.6% [72.0–87.2]) and MMA (66.6% [59.5–73.6]). Among the 2098 patients who received an outpatient opioid medication prescription postsurgical discharge, the majority (63.2%) did not have an outpatient opioid prescription dispensed in the year prior; this proportion was 63.5% (tongue and palate), 63.8% (UPPP), 66.2% (tonsillectomy), 52.9% (adenoidectomy), 80.0% (tongue reduction surgery), 65.1% (MMA + GA), and 43.4% (MMA) across the different surgery types.

Nonopioid pain medications (Table 2) were dispensed to 254 patients (11.1%), with the highest proportions observed among patients who underwent maxillofacial surgeries (27.1%–45.4%) and the lowest seen among UPPP patients (6.9%). Perioperative (defined as the period after leaving the operating room until hospital discharge) glucocorticoid administration occurred to a greater extent among maxillofacial surgeries (MMA ± GA; 79.4%–87.0%), compared to the other surgery types (Table 4). In contrast, postoperative (from hospital discharge until 2 months after the index surgery) glucocorticoid treatment, based on dispensed outpatient pharmacy prescriptions, was observed to the greatest extent among patients who underwent the soft tissue surgeries (non‐MMA ± GA).

For the total cohort, the mean length of hospital stay after sleep surgery was 0.6 ± 0.8 days (Table 5). A total of 507 patients (22.2%) returned to the ED or were readmitted to the hospital within 2 months of surgery. The group of patients who underwent MMA + GA had the highest percentage of direct hospital readmission (18.5%, Table 6). Among the 507 patients who had a postoperative return to the ED or hospital readmission (Table 7), 189 (37.2%) had a principal admitting or discharge diagnosis for a pain‐related condition; there was no statistically significant difference among surgery groups (p = 0.23).

Discussion

Our study identified a high proportion of sleep surgery patients with preoperative opioid prescriptions at 39.9%, compared to a previous study that reported preoperative opioid use prevalence in head and neck surgeries to be approximately 20% [17]. We also found a higher preoperative pain diagnosis (35.5%) within our cohort, compared to the reported 20.4% prevalence of chronic pain within the general American population [18]. Additionally, we identified a higher prevalence of depression diagnosis in our cohort (14.7%), compared to the prevalence of depression within the general adult population (7.8%) according to the National Institute of Mental Health [19]. Moreover, this study's findings complement our group's previous study by examining postoperative opioid prescriptions dispensed (91.7%) and by reporting the prevalence of pain and depression for adult patients who undergo different types of non‐HGNS sleep surgery for OSA [20].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that risk factors for opioid abuse in the postoperative period include preoperative opioid use, history of substance abuse, chronic pain or any physical malady, a mental health history, and the use of sedatives, hypnotics, or antidepressants [21, 22, 23, 24]. These risk factors associated with postoperative opioid abuse are also comorbidities for OSA, including depression and chronic pain. The current literature suggests an overlap between OSA comorbidities and risk factors for opioid abuse and indicates that many of these OSA patients undergoing sleep surgery are already at higher risk for opioid abuse, even before surgery [25, 26, 27]. According to data from the CDC, 70,237 drug overdose deaths occurred in the United States, and of these, opioids contributed to 47,600 deaths (67.8% of all overdose deaths) [13]. Given these trends, there has been a push within the surgical community to identify techniques to minimize postoperative opioid use in non‐OSA surgeries, and this has led to the identification of methods to minimize narcotic usage [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Narcotics can be especially dangerous in patients with OSA, given that they can relax pharyngeal tone, potentially worsening baseline upper airway obstruction, and so methods to minimize narcotic use after sleep surgery are important in this population.

Within our cohort, the soft tissue surgeries had the highest percentage of opioid prescriptions dispensed, while the maxillofacial surgeries had the lowest. Conversely, the maxillofacial surgery groups had the highest proportion of non‐opioid prescriptions dispensed, while the UPPP group had the lowest. One potential explanation for these findings is that there were higher rates of NSAID usage postoperatively after skeletal surgery. In our institution, soft tissue surgeries are performed by otolaryngologists, while skeletal surgeries are performed by maxillofacial surgeons; thus, these findings could also reflect differences in opioid prescribing patterns among surgeons with different training backgrounds. An online survey from the Canadian Society of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery from 2019 found that the surgery with the highest oral morphine equivalents prescribed for both adult and pediatric patients was tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy [9]. A similar study from the United States in 2018 by Schwartz et al. had comparable findings [8]. Moreover, a study by Abdelwahab et al. assessed the incidence and risk factors of chronic opioid use after OSA surgery and found that in opioid‐naïve patients, perioperative opioid prescriptions were a significant predictor for persistent, prolonged, and inappropriate opioid use [37]. Additionally, the study noted that skeletal surgery was associated with significantly less perioperative opioid prescription compared with soft tissue surgery [37]. The findings within our cohort suggest that soft tissue surgeries are the ones most associated with pain, which is consistent with the literature, and further emphasize the importance of multimodal analgesia in these surgeries among this already high‐risk group of patients [38].

Prior studies have evaluated the efficacy of adding nonnarcotic pain medications to ease the postoperative pain from tonsillectomies [39, 40, 41]. One study identified the benefits of tramadol injections into the tonsillar bed to minimize postoperative pain [42]. Others have studied the use of celecoxib, diclofenac, ketorolac, or ketoprofen for post‐tonsillectomy pain control, and there is no consensus in the literature about the possible benefits of these medications [43, 44, 45]. Ketorolac is often avoided due to concern for postoperative bleeding, but a study from 2019 by McClain et al. found that there was no significant difference in postoperative hemorrhage between the groups that received ketorolac and the group that did not [46]. Another described option for nonnarcotic pain control is a “swish and spit” with aspirin [47, 48]. Our study assessed glucocorticoid medication administration and found statistically significant differences in perioperative glucocorticoid administration (highest in maxillofacial surgeries), as well as postoperative outpatient glucocorticoid prescription dispensings (highest in soft tissue surgeries), depending on the type of sleep surgery performed. The maxillofacial surgery group also had the lowest proportion of opioid and the highest proportion of nonopioid prescriptions dispensed postoperatively. This suggests that routine use of nonopioid medications post‐MMA could limit opioid usage. The literature has shown the benefits of using local or systemic steroids, pregabalin, and gabapentin both pre‐ and postoperatively after tonsillectomies [49, 50].

Pre‐ and postoperative counseling is paramount with respect to postoperative pain control. A recent general surgery study found that preoperative counseling, along with the utilization of nonopioid analgesics, can decrease opioid usage while maintaining appropriate pain control [51]. A shift to increase preoperative counseling and nonopioid analgesia is a necessary next step towards minimizing opioid usage after sleep surgery and has been officially recommended in the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery's clinical practice guidelines [52].

In addition to pain, we also assessed postoperative hospital length of stay and readmission or return to ED. The longest average hospital length of stay for the index surgery was among tongue and palate surgeries and MMA surgeries, which is consistent with the literature [53, 54, 55, 56]. In our cohort, the surgery associated with the highest percentage of hospital readmission was MMA + GA (18.5%). We found significant differences between surgery type and postoperative bleeding events, with the highest percentages being among UPPP and tonsillectomy patients, which is consistent with prior studies [57, 58]. However, our study did not find a significant difference between surgery type and pain as a reason for return to ED or readmission. A study from 2015 evaluated rates of readmission after UPPP and found that readmission and re‐presentation to the ED are commonly due to dehydration, postoperative hemorrhage, and acute pain [59].

The present study includes a large cohort within a robust integrated healthcare system with a racially and ethnically diverse patient population, which allows for the assessment of multiple variables over a long period of time. Most of our findings are consistent with those previously described in the literature, and the study adds to a growing body of knowledge by assessing both opioid naïve and non‐naïve patients. However, the study is not without its limitations. First, medications were grouped into categories, and this did not allow for comparisons with individual medications or identification of medications purchased over the counter or at outside pharmacies. Drug dose, days' supply, and pharmacy refills were not available in the analytic dataset. We were also unable to consider the impact of temporal trends in opioid stewardship. Second, our analysis of preoperative medical conditions was limited to depression, bleeding‐related conditions, and acute or chronic pain, but did not include other conditions that can impact the postoperative course, including other psychiatric diseases, alcohol abuse, other drug abuse, or prior surgeries. Moreover, we did not assess the underlying cause of the pain diagnoses, which could have suggested that pain was attributable to a type of surgery as opposed to an underlying condition and could thus be identified as a confounder in pain‐related readmissions. Third, we were unable to verify the accuracy of diagnoses, which might have been miscoded and the diagnostic criteria applied, including for OSA, where severity indicators (e.g., apnea–hypopnea index) were not examined. Fourth, we were unable to track compliance with taking medications, nor could we track different dosages or ensure that medications were being taken as prescribed or intended. Finally, during the time when this study was conducted, only three HGNS surgeries were performed. Thus, the decision was made to exclude this type of surgery from analyses, given the low number of patients and the inability to draw any meaningful conclusions.

Conclusion

One of the steps towards decreasing opioid use after surgery involves examining pain medication use trends and comorbidities that may place patients at higher risk for opioid consumption. Non‐HGNS sleep surgery for OSA is associated with high postoperative opioid dispensings. Adult patients undergoing non‐HGNS sleep surgery may also be at increased risk of opioid complications given the high prevalence of preoperative opioid use, chronic pain, and depression.
