SafePath: Conformal Prediction for Safe LLM-Based Autonomous Navigation
Achref Doula, Max Mühlhäuser, Alejandro Sanchez Guinea

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) show growing promise in autonomous driving by reasoning over complex traffic scenarios to generate path plans. However, their tendencies toward overconfidence, and hallucinations raise critical safety concerns. We introduce SafePath, a modular framework that augments LLM-based path planning with formal safety guarantees using conformal prediction. SafePath operates in three stages. In the first stage, we use an LLM that generates a set of diverse candidate paths, exploring possible trajectories based on agent behaviors and environmental cues. In the second stage, SafePath filters out high-risk trajectories while guaranteeing that at least one safe option is included with a user-defined probability, through a multiple-choice question-answering formulation that integrates conformal prediction. In the final stage, our approach selects the path with the lowest expected collision risk when uncertainty is low or delegates control to a human when uncertainty is high. We theoretically prove that SafePath guarantees a safe trajectory with a user-defined probability, and we show how its human delegation rate can be tuned to balance autonomy and safety. Extensive experiments on nuScenes and Highway-env show that SafePath reduces planning uncertainty by 77% and collision rates by up to 70%, demonstrating effectiveness in making LLM-driven path planning more safer.



1
Introduction

Safety is a fundamental requirement for autonomous vehicles (AVs) operating in complex urban environments, where uncertainty arising from the unpredictable behavior of pedestrians, cyclists, and other vehicles, combined with dynamic interactions, poses significant challenges. In this context, safety refers to the ability of a planning approach to minimize the likelihood of collisions or other harmful events to an acceptable low threshold, thus preventing catastrophic consequences. Achieving such a safe performance is challenging in dynamic urban environments due to their complexity.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently emerged as powerful tools for generating navigation strategies in autonomous driving, thanks to their “reasoning” capabilities over complex traffic scenarios
Chen et al. (2024)
;
Fu et al. (2024)
;
Tian et al. (2024)
. However, their tendency to display overconfidence, produce hallucinated outputs, and generate unreliable uncertainty estimates poses significant safety risks
Xu et al. (2024)
;
Xiong et al. ()
. Such vulnerabilities are especially problematic in real-world domains, where even minor errors can lead to severe consequences.

To ensure the reliable use of LLMs
for path planning in autonomous driving, we propose
SafePath
, the first approach, to the best of our knowledge, to integrate LLM-based path generation with conformal prediction to provide formal safety guarantees on the generated paths.
SafePath consists of three stages. First, the
Path Generation
stage uses an LLM to produce a set of candidate paths by reasoning over the AV goals and environmental cues, such as traffic rules and agent behaviors. Second, in the
Uncertainty-Aware Path Selection
stage, we refine the set of candidate paths and filter out risky paths. To achieve this, we introduce a multiple choice question-answering (MCQA) procedure that leverages a second LLM and conformal prediction (CP)
Shafer and Vovk (2008)
, to estimate uncertainty and guarantee that the refined set of paths includes, with a user-defined probability, at least one path that meets safety requirements.

Third, the
Path Decision
stage determines which path to execute by examining how similar the paths in the refined set from the second stage are. If the set contains a single path, indicating low uncertainty, the path is adopted directly. If the set contains multiple paths that exhibit high similarity, SafePath selects the one with the lowest expected collision risk. Only when paths diverge significantly, indicating high uncertainty, SafePath delegates control to a human to ensure safety.

We provide a theoretical analysis proving that SafePath guarantees, with user-defined probability, that the selected trajectory is safe. To empirically validate our approach, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation across two complementary settings: nuScenes
Caesar et al. (2020)
, which supports open-loop planning from rich, annotated urban scenes, and highway-env
Leurent (2018)
, which enables closed-loop testing with real-time agent interaction. This combination allows us to assess SafePath’s performance in both static reasoning and dynamic control regimes.
The results of our evaluation show that SafePath, under fully autonomous settings, achieves a reduction in planning uncertainty of up to 77% and a drop in collision rates by up to 70% compared to state-of-the-art planning methods.

Related work

Motion planning is a central challenge in autonomous driving, requiring predictive navigation in dynamic, often congested environments. Early solutions predominantly relied on rule-based heuristics that maintained strict adherence to lane-keeping, safety distances, and traffic regulations

Background on Conformal Prediction

Conformal prediction is a statistical framework that transforms the outputs of a deep learning model into prediction sets guaranteed to contain the true outcome with a probability
1-\alpha
, where
\alpha
is user-defined error rate
Vovk et al. (2005)
;
Lekeufack et al. (2024)
. Unlike point estimates, the prediction sets explicitly quantify uncertainty by including all plausible candidates, making CP ideal for safety-critical systems where overconfidence must be mitigated. While we later apply CP to the selection of safe paths in autonomous navigation, we first introduce its general form here.

Constructing Conformal Prediction Sets.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the input space and
\mathcal{Y}
the label space. Suppose we have a trained predictive model
f_{\theta}
that provides (possibly overconfident) scores over
\mathcal{Y}
. Conformal prediction seeks to construct, for any new input
x_{\text{test}}\in\mathcal{X}
, a
prediction set

C(x_{\text{test}})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that the true label
y_{\text{test}}
is included with probability at least
1-\alpha
. Formally,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[y_{\text{test}}\in C(x_{\text{test}})\bigr]\,\geq\,1-\alpha,

(1)

where
\alpha
is a user-specified error rate.
Two key components underlie CP:

•

Calibration Set.
A dataset
D_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
exchangeable
samples, drawn from the same distribution as the training and test data.

•

Non-Conformity Score.
A function
S(x,y)
that measures how “atypical” a label
y
is for the input
x
. Examples include
1-\max(\mathrm{model\;probabilities})
or model-specific loss values.

By computing
S(x_{i},y_{i})
for each
(x_{i},y_{i})\in D_{\text{cal}}
, we obtain an empirical distribution of scores. The
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of these scores, denoted
\hat{q}
, then serves as a threshold for constructing prediction sets at test time.

Constructing Prediction Sets.

Given a new input
x_{\text{test}}
with unknown
y_{\text{test}}
, the
prediction set
is:

C(x_{\text{test}})\;=\;\bigl\{\,y\in\mathcal{Y}:S(x_{\text{test}},y)\,\leq\,\hat{q}\bigr\}.

(2)

A fundamental result, stated in Theorem
3.1
ensures that under mild assumptions, specifically, exchangeability in
D_{\text{cal}}
and the new sample
x_{\text{test}}
, this construction satisfies the coverage criterion in Equation
1
.

Theorem 3.1
(Conformal Prediction)

Let
D_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a calibration set of exchangeable samples, and let
S
be a chosen non-conformity score. For each
(x_{i},y_{i})\in D_{\text{cal}}
, compute
S(x_{i},y_{i})
and let
\hat{q}
be the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of these scores. Then for a new input
x_{\text{test}}
,

C(x_{\text{test}})\;=\;\bigl\{\,y\in\mathcal{Y}:S(x_{\text{test}},y)\,\leq\,\hat{q}\bigr\}

(3)

satisfies
\mathbb{P}\bigl[y_{\text{test}}\in C(x_{\text{test}})\bigr]\,\geq\,1-\alpha.

4
Approach

4.1
Approach Overview

SafePath
ensures the safe use of LLMs in autonomous path planning by providing formal safety guarantees on the output of the LLM-generated paths.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, SafePath consists of three sequential stages: Path Generation, Uncertainty-Aware Path Selection, and Path Decision.

In the
Path Generation
stage, the AV employs a first language model,
LLM_{1}
, to generate a diverse set of candidate paths based on its mission goals and the current environmental state, which includes perception data and predicted movements of surrounding agents. Each candidate path is accompanied by a rationale, enhancing interpretability and transparency in the decision-making process.

The
Uncertainty-Aware Path Selection
stage refines the generated set of paths to address the inherent variability and uncertainty in LLM outputs. This refinement process employs a second LLM,
LLM_{2}
, to select paths from the generated candidates. We frame this selection as a multiple choice question-answering task, where
LLM_{2}
receives the relevant scenario context and chooses the most suitable path among the candidates. The structured and bounded MCQA formulation enables us to apply conformal prediction to calibrate the selection process and account for uncertainty. After conformal prediction, the output of this stage is a prediction set of paths that is guaranteed to include at least one path that meets the required safety criteria, with a user-defined probability.

In the
Path Decision
stage, the autonomous vehicle (AV) evaluates the prediction set obtained from the previous stage to determine the final decision for execution. If the set contains a single path, the AV adopts it immediately. If the set contains multiple paths that are sufficiently similar—indicating a consensus among safe trajectories—the AV will autonomously proceed using the path deemed to have the lowest expected collision risk. In scenarios where the set contains multiple diverging paths, reflecting a high degree of uncertainty, the system defers to human oversight to resolve the ambiguity.

4.2
Path Generation

In the Path Generation stage, the AV employs a fine-tuned LLM that utilizes chain-of-thought reasoning (CoT)
Wei et al. (2022)
to generate a set of candidate paths based on its current state, perception data, and mission goals. Let
x=\{x_{\text{percept}},x_{\text{pred}},x_{\text{goal}}\}
represent the AV’s perception data, predicted movements of other agents, and mission objectives. By leveraging chain-of-thought reasoning, the LLM explores various possible trajectories, considering the AV’s goals and the behavior of surrounding agents.

Formally, the LLM generates a set of
k
candidate paths
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}\}
and their associated rationales
R=\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k}\}
through chain-of-thought reasoning:

\{(p_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}=\text{LLM}_{1}(x)

(4)

Each path
p_{i}
represents a potential trajectory for the AV, while
r_{i}
provides the detailed reasoning process that led to the selection of that path. The CoT rationale enhances transparency and can be used for further analysis or to explain decisions to human operators. Furthermore, several works, such as
Chen et al. (2024)
;
Fu et al. (2024)
;
Wei et al. (2022)
, have demonstrated that generating rationales alongside the output leads to more reliable and interpretable results. We provide the full text of the prompts we use to generate the candidate path in Appendix
A.6
.

4.3
Uncertainty-Aware Path Selection

Despite fine-tuning,
LLM_{1}
, may generate unsafe paths due to overconfident or hallucinated outputs
Gekhman et al. (2024)
.
To address this limitation, SafePath integrates conformal prediction to provide statistical guarantees that the selected path set contains at least one
safe
trajectory with probability
1-\alpha
. However, a critical challenge arises from the unstructured nature of LLM-generated paths, which vary in semantic detail and lack a predefined hypothesis space, both essential for conformal prediction guarantees. We first formalize what we mean by a safe path:

Definition 4.1
(Safe Path)

A path
p
is considered
safe
if it satisfies the following constraints:

Collision Avoidance:

p
ensures no intersections with other agents or static obstacles in the environment.

Rule Compliance:

p
adheres to all traffic laws and operational constraints.

Safety Margins:

p
maintains a safe distance from other agents, given their predicted trajectories.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{x}
denote the set of safe paths given a scenario
x
. For our calibrations and guarantees to hold, we assume:

Assumption 4.1

The driving datasets used to train and calibrate the planning models contain only safe paths.

Assumption
4.1
ensures that the models and calibration processes are based on safe driving behaviors, which is reasonable given that training data for autonomous vehicles are curated to reflect correct and safe operations and used to train models that do so.

To reconcile unstructured LLM outputs with conformal prediction’s requirements, we reformulate path selection as a multiple-choice question-answering (MCQA) task.
Each candidate path
p_{i}\in P
is assigned a distinct label
l_{p_{i}}
(e.g., “A,” “B,” “C”). Then we prompt a second LLM,
LLM_{2}
to select a single letter corresponding to the most suitable path
l^{*}
, given the scenario description
x
, the set of paths
P
, and their rationale
R
:

l^{*}=LLM_{2}(P,R,x).

(5)

Casting path selection as a multiple choice task restricts the response space of
LLM_{2}
to a finite set of options, which enables the computation of logit-based probabilities even for closed-source LLMs through their APIs. By extracting the logits, it is possible to compute non-conformity scores to construct statistically valid prediction sets.
Under Assumption
4.1
, we now define a non-conformity score
s_{p_{i}}
for each path
p_{i}
using the Least Ambiguous Classification (LAC) score
Sadinle et al. (2019)
1
1

1

It is possible to use other non-conformity scores such as RAPS
Angelopoulos et al. (2021)
, and APS
Romano et al. (2020)
. However, as we show in Section
5.4
, LAC yielded the best results.
:

s_{p_{i}}\;=\;1-\mathrm{Softmax}\bigl(\mathrm{logit}(l_{p_{i}})\bigr),

(6)

where
\mathrm{logit}(l_{p_{i}})
is the logit corresponding to the label
l_{p_{i}}
. Using a held-out calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{i},p_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we compute
s_{p_{i}}
for each calibration point, then let
\hat{q}
be the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of these scores:

\hat{q}=\mathrm{Quantile}\Bigl(\bigl\{s_{i}\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n},\,\frac{\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n}\Bigr),

(7)

where
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is the ceiling function. For a new situation
x_{\text{test}}
, we compute the non-conformity score
s_{p_{i}}
for every candidate path
p_{i}
, and collect all paths whose scores do not exceed
\hat{q}
:

C\;=\;\{\,p_{i}\in P:s_{p_{i}}\,\leq\,\hat{q}\}.

This
prediction set

C
contains, with probability at least
1-\alpha
, a correct (and thus safe) path for the new scenario. Concretely, we have:

Theorem 4.1
(Prediction Set Safety Guarantee)

Let
C\subseteq P
be the prediction set generated above at confidence level
1-\alpha
, and suppose Assumption
4.1
holds. Then,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[\exists\,p\in C\,\cap\,\mathcal{S}_{x}\bigr]\;\geq\;1-\alpha.

(8)

Theorem
4.1
states that the prediction set
\mathcal{C}
produced by conformal prediction will, with high probability, contain at least one path that meets the safety requirements of Definition
4.1
. We prove Theorem
4.1
by leveraging the statistical guarantees of conformal prediction, which ensures that the prediction set includes the true path with probability at least
1-\alpha
and datasets are assumed to contain safe paths. The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

4.4
Path Decision

Once we have a prediction set
C
of candidate paths, we determine which path to execute. If
C
contains a single path, i.e.,
|C|=1
, the AV adopts it immediately since it is included with probability
1-\alpha
of being safe. Otherwise, we evaluate how similar the paths in
C
are to each other.

Definition 4.2
(
\delta
-Equivalence)

Two paths
p_{i},p_{j}\in P
are
\delta
-equivalent if their similarity, as measured by a function
\text{Sim}:P\times P\to[0,1]
, satisfies:

\text{Sim}(p_{i},p_{j})\geq\delta,

(9)

where
\delta\in[0,1]
is a predefined threshold. This ensures that paths with small deviations, both in geometry and operational characteristics, are considered interchangeable in terms of safety and functionality
2
2

2

In Definition
4.2
,
Sim
is defined as
\text{Sim}:P\times P\to[0,1]
. In Algorithm
1
, we extend this notation to sets, implying that similarity is computed pairwise for all path pairs.
.

If all pairs of paths in
C
are
\delta
-equivalent (i.e., the set
C
effectively contains similar paths),
the AV selects the path with the lowest expected collision based on predictions in the visual scene description.
We define
\mathrm{Collision}(p)
as the fraction of agents with which
p
intersects out of the total number of agents:

\mathrm{Collision}(p)=\frac{\#\{\text{agents collided with }p\}}{\#\{\text{all agents in the scene}\}}.

(10)

Hence, the final path is chosen according to

p_{\mathrm{final}}=\arg\min_{p\in C}\mathrm{Collision}(p).

(11)

If, on the other hand, there exists at least one pair
(p_{i},p_{j})
with
\mathrm{Sim}(p_{i},p_{j})<\delta
, the AV defers to a human operator to avoid committing to highly divergent or conflicting trajectories.

SafePath Guarantee.

By combining the coverage property from Theorem
4.1
with the final decision mechanism, we obtain:

Theorem 4.2
(SafePath Guarantee)

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the prediction set generated in the Uncertainty-Aware Path Selection (Section
4.3
) at confidence level
1-\alpha
, and suppose

\mathbb{P}\bigl[\exists\,p\in\mathcal{C}\,\cap\,\mathcal{S}_{x}\bigr]\;\geq\;1-\alpha.

Let
p_{\text{final}}
be the path chosen according to the path-decision procedure above. Then,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[\,p_{\text{final}}\in\mathcal{S}_{x}\bigr]\;\geq\;1-\alpha.

(12)

In other words, once the prediction set is guaranteed to contain a safe path with probability at least
1-\alpha
, the additional selection or sampling steps (including possible human intervention) preserve that probability. Hence, SafePath produces a single final path that is safe with the same high probability. We provide a full proof of Theorem
4.2
in Appendix
A.1
.

Implications.

The usage of the path decision stage reduces the prediction set to a size of 1 which has the advantage of reducing uncertainty and providing the planner with a feasible path, rather than a set. The parameter
\delta
icontrols how strictly paths must match in similarity: a higher value prompts more human intervention when predictions diverge, preventing overconfident choices, while a lower
\delta

5
Evaluation

To empirically validate SafePath, we structure our evaluation around three core goals: (1) understanding how delegation can be adaptively controlled, (2) assessing the statistical validity and efficiency of our conformal prediction layer, and (3) benchmarking overall planning safety in diverse settings.

We begin by examining the effect of the path-similarity threshold
\delta
, which governs human delegation and autonomy. Next, we evaluate SafePath’s coverage performance across different confidence levels (
\alpha
) using conformal prediction, and compare it to state-of-the-art conformal methods. Finally, we assess collision-avoidance performance in both open-loop and closed-loop settings: open-loop tests SafePath’s reasoning from static inputs, while closed-loop evaluates SafePath in interactive scenarios where decisions influence future states.

Additional results are in the Appendix, including additional metrics (App.
A.2
), aggregation strategies (App.
A.3
), other LLMs (App.
A.4
), and qualitative examples (App.
A.5
).

5.1
Dataset and Simulation Environments

We evaluate SafePath using two complementary testbeds covering distinct planning modes. For open-loop evaluation, we use nuScenes
Caesar et al. (2020)
, a real-world dataset comprising 1,000 driving scenes, and over 40,000 annotated frames.

For closed-loop evaluation, we use
highway-env

Leurent (2018)
, a simulation environment that supports interactive agent control. It includes scenarios such as highways, intersections, and roundabouts, allowing SafePath to be tested in settings where each decision affects future states.

5.2
Implementation

We use
gpt-3.5-turbo-0125
for both path generation (LLM1) and path selection (LLM2). LLM1 is fine-tuned on nuScenes for one epoch (cost: $120 via OpenAI API), while LLM2 remains frozen. Both LLMs use system prompts with few-shot examples (Appendix
A.6
).

Conformal prediction is calibrated using 5,000 held-out training samples. Path similarity is computed via Sentence-BERT
Reimers (2019)
embeddings and cosine distance.
All LLM queries are made via the OpenAI API. The codebase is implemented in Python. Additional results with alternative LLM2s are in Appendix
A.4
. Our Code will be released upon publication.

5.4
Conformal Prediction Evaluation

We evaluate the conformal prediction step to assess SafePath’s ability to construct prediction sets that reliably include the correct path. For this experiment only, the ground-truth trajectory is added to the candidate set to directly measure coverage. Evaluation is performed across confidence levels (
\alpha
) using
\delta=0.85
(no human delegation).

Baselines.

We compare SafePath against adaptive predictive sets (APS)
Romano et al. (2020)
, which guarantees conditional coverage, regularized adaptive predictive sets (RAPS)
Angelopoulos et al. (2021)
, which aims to reduce set sizes, and “knowNo”
Ren et al. ()
, a planning framework with conformal prediction and human delegation. We run experiments at multiple
\alpha
values, corresponding to different target coverage rates
1-\alpha
.

Metrics.

In this evaluation we use the following metrics that are proper for conformal prediction.

6
Conclusion

This work introduced SafePath, an approach for safety-centric path planning under uncertainty in autonomous driving, by combining LLMs, conformal prediction, and selective human intervention. SafePath provides formal safety guarantees and demonstrated significant improvements in prediction uncertainty, collision rates, and reduced human intervention compared to state-of-the-art methods.

7
Limitations

While SafePath demonstrates strong performance on large-scale datasets, such as nuScenes and simulators such as Highway-env, real-world deployment remains a critical next step. Our results provide a solid foundation, but further validation in controlled environments is essential to evaluate both performance and user acceptance. Controlled experiments, such as those conducted on test tracks or in virtual reality setups
Doula et al. (2023)
, can simulate diverse real-world scenarios, enabling a rigorous assessment of SafePath’s applicability and reliability.
The reliance on LLMs for both path generation and selection introduces computational overhead, raising concerns about real-time feasibility. In our current implementation, we relied on APIs, which reduces computational overhead and provides an interface with closed-source LLM such as
\mathtt{gpt-4.o}
, and on lower precision to perform tests with local LLMs, as we show in Appendix
A.4
.
Furthermore, the theoretical guarantees provided by CP rely on the exchangeability assumption and the representativeness of the calibration dataset
Vovk et al. (2005)
;
Lekeufack et al. (2024)
. While the nuScenes dataset covers challenging scenarios, extending the approach to handle out-of-distribution data (OOD), will enhance its robustness. SafePath is flexible in those terms and is not constrained to a single non-conformity score, making it adaptive to scoring functions that handle OOD
Gibbs and Candes (2021)
;
Kaur et al. (2022)
.

In our experiments, we evaluated the impact of various path decision strategies when the prediction set contains multiple
\delta

1.

Random Sampling
: Randomly selecting a path from the prediction set.

2.

3.

Weighted Average

4.

Best Path
: Selecting the path with the highest softmax probability (i.e., lowest non-conformity score).

5.

Minimum Collision Sampling
: Choosing the path with the minimum expected collision probability based on input prompts.

The
Average

Weighted Average

\alpha
. Specifically, these strategies yield average L2 errors of 0.71 m and 0.72 m at
\alpha=0.3
and
\alpha=0.5
, respectively. This suggests that aggregating paths, whether equally or weighted by confidence scores, leads to more accurate trajectory predictions. The minimal difference between the Average and Weighted Average strategies implies that the softmax confidence scores do not significantly influence the aggregation outcome in terms of trajectory accuracy.

In contrast, the
Minimum Collision Sampling
strategy achieves the lowest collision rates, with average collision rates of 0.20% at
\alpha=0.3
and 0.13% at
\alpha=0.5
. This indicates that explicitly selecting the path with the lowest expected collision probability effectively enhances safety. However, this strategy results in higher L2 errors (average of 0.84 m), suggesting a trade-off between minimizing collision risk and maintaining trajectory accuracy.

The
Random Sampling
and
Best Path
strategies exhibit higher L2 errors and collision rates compared to the averaging strategies and Minimum Collision Sampling. The Best Path strategy, which selects the path with the highest softmax probability, does not perform as well as anticipated. This could be due to calibration issues with the softmax scores or a weak correlation between the confidence scores and actual collision risk. These findings raise concerns about the reliability of using softmax probabilities as indicators of path safety and highlight the need for better calibration of these confidence measures.

These observations highlight a trade-off between trajectory accuracy and collision avoidance. The averaging strategies improve trajectory accuracy by synthesizing information from multiple paths but may not minimize collision risk as effectively as strategies that explicitly consider collision probabilities. Conversely, the Minimum Collision Sampling strategy enhances safety by reducing collisions but may compromise on trajectory precision.

It is important to consider that while averaging paths can produce smoother trajectories, this approach might inadvertently smooth out necessary evasive maneuvers that individual paths would have taken to avoid obstacles, potentially increasing collision risk. Therefore, careful evaluation of how path aggregation impacts safety-critical behaviors is essential.

Overall, the results indicate that no single strategy universally outperforms the others across all metrics. The selection of an appropriate path decision strategy depends on the specific priorities and constraints of the autonomous navigation task. For applications where safety and collision avoidance are highly important, strategies like Minimum Collision Sampling are preferable.
