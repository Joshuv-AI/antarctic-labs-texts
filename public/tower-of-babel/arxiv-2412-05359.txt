Accidental Symmetries, Hilbert Series, and Friends
and Pablo Quílez

Abstract
Accidental symmetries in effective field theories can be established by computing and comparing Hilbert series. This invites us to study them with the tools of invariant theory. Applying this technology, we spotlight three classes of accidental symmetries that hold to all orders for non-derivative interactions. They are broken by derivative interactions and become ordinary finite-order accidental symmetries. To systematically understand the origin and the patterns of accidental symmetries, we introduce a novel mathematical construct — a (non-transitive) binary relation between subgroups that we call friendship . Equipped with this, we derive new criteria for all-order accidental symmetries in terms of friends , and criteria for finite-order accidental symmetries in terms of friends ma non troppo . They allow us to verify and identify accidental symmetries more efficiently without computing the Hilbert series. We demonstrate the success of our new criteria by applying them to a variety of sample accidental symmetries, including the custodial symmetry in the Higgs sector of the Standard Model effective field theory.

Introduction

Symmetry principles play a crucial role in understanding the dynamics and physical predictions of Quantum Field Theories (QFTs). They have a broad application across many disciplines of theoretical and experimental physics. However, not all symmetries need to be fundamental or explicitly imposed. Some symmetries are respected
accidentally
by the allowed interactions in the Lagrangian due to other constraints imposed. They are known as
accidental symmetries
. From the Effective Field Theory (EFT) point of view, they typically hold to a certain order in the EFT expansion, and are broken by higher-order effective operators.

The most prominent examples of accidental symmetries in the Standard Model (SM) are perhaps the conservation of baryon number
B
and of lepton number
L

[
1
]
. They are not imposed as requirements on the SM Lagrangian. However, given the SM field content and insistence on preserving the SM symmetries (the gauged
SU(3)_{c}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
together with Lorentz symmetry), no renormalizable interactions (
i.e.
, operators up to mass dimension four) can be constructed to violate
B
or
L
. In the language of the SM EFT (SMEFT), interactions (effective operators) that violate
L
do not arise until mass dimension five
[
1
]
, and those violating
B
do not arise until mass dimension six
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. The
B

accidental symmetries have important phenomenological consequences in SMEFT, such as explaining the suppression of the proton decay
[
4

Another well-known accidental symmetry in the SM and SMEFT is the custodial symmetry in the Higgs sector, where imposing the electroweak symmetry
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
leads to an enhanced symmetry
O(4)\simeq SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}
that is preserved by the renormalizable interactions of the Higgs fields, and broken by their derivative interactions starting at mass dimension six.

In

2
All-order accidental symmetries

In this section, we highlight a special type of accidental symmetries — they hold to all orders in the EFT expansion when derivative interactions are not considered. We start by making the definition precise.

Consider a global group
G
(which may be a product group in general) and a set of fields
\Phi(x)
that transform as a linear representation (rep) under
G
:

\Phi\quad\longrightarrow\quad g\,\Phi\,,\qquad g\in G\,.

(2.1)

We refer to the fields
\Phi(x)
as
building block fields
in this paper. Without considering derivative interactions, we are focusing on the potential part of the EFT Lagrangian

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}=-V(\Phi)+\order{\partial_\mu}\,,

(2.2)

where the potential
V(\Phi)
is an arbitrary polynomial in the fields
\Phi(x)
.
3
3

3

Operators built out of fermionic fields are also included in the potential, as long as they do not contain derivatives. Therefore, the polynomial algebra in
\Phi
here is in general a Grassmann algebra; some of the
\Phi
components might be Grassmann odd.

Now, as opposed to imposing the full group
G
, let us impose only a subgroup of it
H\subset G
,
i.e.
we only require the theory to respect
H
-invariance. Sometimes, however, due to the special properties of the groups
G
,
H
, and the building block fields
\Phi
, imposing the subgroup
H
will automatically guarantee that the potential
V(\Phi)
is
G
-invariant. When this happens, we say that
G
is an accidental symmetry of the potential
V(\Phi)
obtained by imposing
H
:
4
4

4

In relativistic EFTs, Lorentz invariance is a default symmetry requirement on the EFT potential
V(\Phi)
(as well as the full Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
). For simplicity, and to keep our discussions to the point, in this section we will focus on the building block fields
\Phi(x)
that are Lorentz scalars (even in the several “fermionic” cases; see explanations below). In such cases, Lorentz invariance is trivially satisfied. When it becomes non-trivial,
e.g.
when derivative interactions are accommodated, it will be included as an additional symmetry factor on top of the groups
G
and
H
; see our discussions in later sections,
e.g.
(
3.19
).

H\implies G\quad\text{for}\quad V(\Phi)\,.

(2.3)

Since this holds for a potential that is a polynomial of arbitrary degree, we refer to this as an “all-order” accidental symmetry of the potential in the EFT.

All-order accidental symmetries can be verified by computing the corresponding Hilbert series that encode all the allowed effective operators in the EFT. Specifically, we have the equivalence between the following two statements

\hskip-10.0ptH\implies G\quad\text{for}\quad V(\Phi)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)\,.

(2.4)

We refer the reader to Ref.
[
21
]
for a review on the definition, applications, and calculation techniques of the Hilbert series. Throughout this paper, we follow the same notation system used in Ref.
[
21
]
. In particular,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\Phi}(q)
denotes the Hilbert series for the
G
-invariant
\Phi
polynomials (with the unified grading variable
q
), while
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\Phi}(q)
refers to the
H
-invariant
\Phi
polynomials. The right-hand side is the statement that all polynomials in
\Phi
that are
H
-invariant are also
G
-invariant, so the equivalence in
Eq.

2.4
is basically a tautology, but it invites us to use the language of invariant theory
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
in addressing the question of accidental symmetries.

For the rest of this section, we show three classes of examples of all-order accidental symmetries for the potential term in the EFT Lagrangian. We verify each of them using
Eq.

2.4
and computing the Hilbert series explicitly. We emphasize that this is by no means an exhaustive classification. On the contrary, these are just a few examples that we have been able to identify, and we suspect there are many more.

3
Finite-order accidental symmetries

In
Sec.

2
, we identified three classes of accidental symmetries that hold to all orders in the EFT expansion when derivative interactions are not considered,
i.e.
, when we focus on the potential term
V(\Phi)
in the EFT. We verified them by computing the Hilbert series and checking against the criterion (
2.4
).

One may wonder how these analyses based only on the potential term (
i.e.
, polynomials in the fields
\Phi(x)
) can be useful for actual EFTs, where one cannot simply ignore the derivative interactions. As we will explain in this section, all-order accidental symmetries respected by the potential term
V(\Phi)
will be broken by derivative interactions. However, this breaking typically arises beyond the leading order in the EFT, and as a result they become finite-order accidental symmetries. In such cases, the Hilbert series is still a reliable tool for verifying and analyzing them. To demonstrate this, we first discuss some examples of finite-order accidental symmetries within the potential interactions
V(\Phi)
in
Sec.

3.1
. We then explain how to understand the impacts of derivative interactions in
Sec.

3.2
, taking the custodial violation in SMEFT as an illustrative example. Finally in
Sec.

3.3
, we provide a general criterion of using Hilbert series to verify finite-order accidental symmetries of the full Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
, irrespective of whether the symmetry-breaking term originates from the potential or the derivative interactions.

3.1
Accidental symmetry breaking by non-derivative interactions

Non-derivative interactions can break an accidental symmetry, making it only hold up to a certain order in the EFT potential
V(\Phi)
. To verify this kind of finite-order accidental symmetries, one can readily generalize the criterion in
Eq.

2.4
, by comparing the two Hilbert series up to a given order:

H\implies G\quad\text{for}\quad V(\Phi)\quad\text{up to order}\;k\\[5.0pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\qquad{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)+\order{q^{k+1}}\,.

(3.1)

Note that the
\order{q^{k+1}}
terms are always positive, as all
G
-invariants are
H
-invariants.

Sec.

— the Class I all-order accidental symmetries will break when there are too many flavors in the building block fields, due to the appearance of the baryon-like

H(x)
transforms as
(\mathbf{2},+\frac{1}{2})
. For the purpose of determining the allowed effective operators, one can rescale the hypercharge
+\frac{1}{2}\to+1
. With this, the building block fields
H(x)
and
H^{*}(x)
transform precisely as the
(\varphi,\,\varphi^{*})
pair in
Table

3
, and the general discussions in
Sec.

2.2
will apply. In particular, polynomials in
H(x)
and
H^{*}(x)
that are
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
invariant are generated by the combination

|H|^{2}=H^{\dagger}H=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}+\phi_{3}^{2}+\phi_{4}^{2}\right)\,,\qquad\text{with}\qquad H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \phi_1 + i\phi_2 \\ \phi_3 + i\phi_4 \endlx@physics@matrix)\,,

(3.12)

which enjoys a larger
SO(4)
symmetry acting on the four-dimensional real vector
\phi\equiv(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4})^{T}
--- this is the famous custodial symmetry.
13
13

13

Upon electroweak symmetry breaking, this enhanced
SO(4)\simeq SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}
symmetry is broken to the diagonal
SU(2)_{V}
, which is more often referred to as the custodial symmetry in the literature. In this paper, we use the term to refer to the
SO(4)\simeq SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}
group in the unbroken phase.

Therefore, imposing
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
invariance forces the Higgs potential
V(H,H^{\dagger})
in SMEFT to respect the custodial
SO(4)
symmetry to all orders.

However, the custodial symmetry is broken by derivative interactions in the Higgs sector of SMEFT.
14
14

14

The Yukawa and gauge interactions in SMEFT also break the custodial symmetry. Here we focus on the effects of
{\partial}_{\mu}
only, to keep our discussions to the point.

For example, at mass dimension six, there are two independent effective operators of type
H^{4}{\partial}^{2}
(
i.e.
, containing two powers of the derivatives and four powers of the Higgs fields
H,H^{*}
) that are electroweak invariant
[
3
]
:

Q_{H\Box}=-({\partial}_{\mu}|H|^{2})({\partial}^{\mu}|H|^{2})\,,\qquad Q_{HD}=(H^{\dagger}{\partial}_{\mu}H)^{*}(H^{\dagger}{\partial}^{\mu}H)\,.

(3.13)

The first operator
Q_{H\Box}
respects the custodial symmetry, while the second operator
Q_{HD}
does not (and is hence constrained by the
T
parameter in electroweak precision tests
[
39
]
). One can understand the custodial violation of
Q_{HD}
from the rewriting

Q_{HD}=\frac{1}{4}\left(H^{\dagger}{\partial}_{\mu}H+\text{h.c.}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(H^{\dagger}{\partial}_{\mu}H-\text{h.c.}\right)^{2}\,.

(3.14)

The first term
H^{\dagger}{\partial}_{\mu}H+\text{h.c.}={\partial}_{\mu}(H^{\dagger}H)
is custodial symmetric but the second term is not. The situation here is precisely the same as in our discussions around
Eqs.

2.30
and
2.31
. When derivatives are considered, one can view
({\partial}_{\mu}H\,,\,{\partial}_{\mu}H^{*})
as a second flavor of the
(\varphi,\,\varphi^{*})
pair in
Table

3
. Therefore, we are dealing with (at least) two flavors:

\left(\varphi_{1}\,,\,\varphi_{1}^{*}\right)=\left(H\,,\,H^{*}\right)\,,\qquad\left(\varphi_{2}\,,\,\varphi_{2}^{*}\right)=\left({\partial}_{\mu}H\,,\,{\partial}_{\mu}H^{*}\right)\,.

(3.15)

From the discussions around
Eqs.

2.30
and
2.31
, we learned that as soon as we have two flavors, the Class II all-order accidental symmetries will no longer hold. With the identification in
Eq.

3.15
, we recognize the custodial violating term in
Eq.

3.14
precisely as the antisymmetric combination in
Eq.

2.31
:

\frac{1}{2}\left(H^{\dagger}{\partial}_{\mu}H-\text{h.c.}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varphi_{1}^{\dagger}\varphi_{2}-\text{h.c.}\right)=\varphi_{[1}^{\dagger}\varphi_{2]}\,,

(3.16)

while the custodial preserving term in
Eq.

3.14
is the symmetric combination in
Eq.

2.30
.

As seen from the discussion above, the origin of the custodial violation by derivative interactions can be understood as having multiple flavors. In fact,
Eq.

3.15
is not quite accurate —
({\partial}_{\mu}H,\,{\partial}_{\mu}H^{*})
technically consist of four new flavors of the
(\varphi,\,\varphi^{*})
pair in
Table

3
, because
{\partial}_{\mu}
has four components. Together they form a vector irrep of the Lorentz group, and imposing Lorentz invariance also played a role in determining the allowed effective operators listed in
Eq.

3.13
.

More generally, at higher orders in SMEFT, derivative interactions will get more flavors of the
(\varphi,\,\varphi^{*})
pair in
Table

3
involved, such as
({\partial}_{\mu}{\partial}_{\nu}H,\,{\partial}_{\mu}{\partial}_{\nu}H^{*})
. Eventually, one needs to include the full tower of the “Single Particle Modules” (SPMs)
[
18
]
built out of the Higgs fields
H
,
H^{*}
, and the derivatives:

R_{H}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix H \\ \pd_\mu H \\ \pd_{(\mu} \pd_{\nu)} H \\ \vdots\endlx@physics@matrix)\,,\qquad R_{H^{*}}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix H^* \\ \pd_\mu H^* \\ \pd_{(\mu} \pd_{\nu)} H^* \\ \vdots\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

(3.17)

Here the parentheses around the indices imply that we are only keeping the traceless symmetric components. Antisymmetric components vanish because the partial derivatives commute. The trace components are eliminated in the SPM to take care of the so-called equations of motion (EOM) redundancies.
15
15

15

The origin of the EOM redundancy is the field redefinition equivalence in the Lagrangian formulation of EFTs, that allows one to eliminate operators that contain EOM factors, order by order in the EFT expansion, starting from the next-to-leading order. It should be added, however, that there is no EOM redundancy for operators at the leading order of the EFT, and one needs to restore the trace components in the SPM when enumerating them.

To determine the allowed effective operators that are independent, one considers all the polynomials in these SPM components, and then impose the
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}
invariance, as well as the Lorentz invariance. On top of this, one also needs to address the integration by parts (IBP) redundancies,
i.e.
, to remove operators that are total derivatives.

Although derivative interactions in the Higgs sector will break the custodial symmetry, we note that the breaking only starts at mass dimension six. At the renormalizable level (
i.e.
up to mass dimension four), the allowed Lagrangian terms are

H^{\dagger}H\,,\qquad(H^{\dagger}H)^{2}\,,\qquad({\partial}_{\mu}H^{\dagger})({\partial}^{\mu}H)\,,

(3.18)

which are all invariant under the custodial symmetry, even with derivative interactions included. This can be generally understood from the fact that derivatives have positive mass dimensions. Because of this (and the requirement of Lorentz invariance), accidental symmetry breaking from derivative interactions typically starts only from a certain non-trivial EFT order, below which the accidental symmetry still holds.

3.3
General criterion for finite-order accidental symmetries

Let us summarize a few general lessons that we have learned so far about accidental symmetries:

•

When we focus on non-derivative interactions only,
i.e.
, the potential interactions
V(\Phi)
in the EFT, there may exist accidental symmetries that hold to all orders. Several classes of examples were provided in
Sec.

2
. All-order accidental symmetries can be verified with the criterion in (
2.4
).

•

Some accidental symmetries will hold only up to a certain order in the EFT potential
V(\Phi)
. This was discussed in
Sec.

3.1
. One class of them arises when we have too many flavors of irreps in the building block fields, which turns an all-order accidental symmetry into a finite-order one. Finite-order accidental symmetries in
V(\Phi)
can be verified with the criterion in (
3.1
).

•

Derivative interactions will also break all-order accidental symmetries. Their effects can be understood as getting infinitely many flavors of irreps involved; these are the tower of irreps in the SPMs of the fields (see
e.g.

Eq.

3.17
for the Higgs fields). This was explained in
Sec.

3.2
.

•

When an all-order accidental symmetry is broken by derivative interactions, the breaking typically starts from a non-trivial order in the EFT expansion, because derivatives come with positive orders in the EFT power counting. Therefore, an all-order accidental symmetry respected by the potential term
V(\Phi)
becomes a finite-order accidental symmetry when the full Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
is considered.

In general, to verify a finite-order accidental symmetry in the full Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
, we can use a criterion similar to that in (
3.1
):

H\implies G\quad\text{for}\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}\quad\text{up to order}\;k\\[8.0pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times H,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times G,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)+\order{q^{k+1}}\,.

(3.19)

Compared to criterion (
3.1
), the building block fields
\Phi
are now replaced by their SPMs
R_{\Phi}
, a tower made of the fields and their derivatives, much like the
R_{H}
and
R_{H^{*}}
in
Eq.

3.17
.
Again, not all the derivative components are included in
R_{\Phi}
— we need to eliminate the components that correspond to the EOM factors. They depend on the type of the fields. The SPM in
Eq.

3.17
was specifically for scalar fields. We refer the reader to Ref.
[
18
]
for rigorous definitions of the SPMs for different type of fields, including scalars, Weyl fermions, field strengths, as well as Goldstone bosons.

It is also understood that when computing the Hilbert series in (
3.19
), one needs to impose the Lorentz invariance and address the IBP redundancies. We refer the reader to Ref.
[
18
]
for an elaboration on how to do these carefully, which is crucial for correctly computing the Hilbert series for actual EFTs.

We emphasize that the criterion in (
3.19
) works for verifying general finite-order accidental symmetries, irrespective of whether the symmetry breaking originates from the potential or the derivative interactions. Similar with the criterion in (
2.4
), the equivalence in (
3.1
) and (
3.19
) are essentially tautologies — restating the meaning of the Hilbert series. But again they allow us to study accidental symmetries with the powerful tools of invariant theory, which opens up the possibility for more systematic approaches, as we will discuss in
Sec.

4
. For the rest of this subsection, we give a few examples to demonstrate criterion (
3.19
), focusing on the scenario that symmetry breaking comes from derivative interactions.

Example: custodial symmetry

To demonstrate the criterion in (
3.19
), let us apply it to checking the custodial symmetry in the Higgs sector of SMEFT. We use the SPMs
R_{H}
,
R_{H^{*}}
for the Higgs fields in
Eq.

3.17
, and the SPM
R_{\phi}
for the custodial

vector field

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SO(4),\,R_{\Phi}}(\phi,{\partial})

\displaystyle=1+\phi^{2}+\Big(\phi^{4}+\phi^{2}{\partial}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\phi^{8}+\phi^{6}{\partial}^{2}+2\,\phi^{4}{\partial}^{4}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\order{\text{dim-10}}\,,

(3.20a)

\displaystyle=1+HH^{*}+\Big[(HH^{*})^{2}+HH^{*}{\partial}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\Big[(HH^{*})^{3}+2\,(HH^{*})^{2}{\partial}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\Big[(HH^{*})^{4}+2\,(HH^{*})^{3}{\partial}^{2}+3\,(HH^{*})^{2}{\partial}^{4}\Big]

(3.21a)

We see that these two Hilbert series do satisfy the criterion in (

\order{q^6}

4
Systematic identification of accidental symmetries

In
Sec.

2
, we discussed accidental symmetries that hold to all orders in the EFT potential interactions
V(\Phi)
. In
Sec.

3
, we explained that when derivative interactions are included (
i.e.
when we consider the full Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
), these all-order accidental symmetries will hold only up to a finite order in the EFT. In either case, we highlighted that computing and comparing the Hilbert series is a reliable approach to checking/verifying an accidental symmetry. Specifically, we provided concrete criteria of using the Hilbert series to verify all-order and finite-order accidental symmetries in
(
2.4
), (
3.1
) and (
3.19
).

Ideally, however, we would like to find a way of verifying accidental symmetries without explicitly computing the Hilbert series, because this calculation is often challenging and time-consuming; see
App.

A

\Phi(x)
and the imposed symmetry group
H
. Note that this would be much harder than verifying a postulated accidental symmetry
H\implies G
, because it requires to determine the group
G
from
H
and
\Phi(x)
, as well as its breaking order in the EFT.

In this section, we present some developments towards the above ambitious goals. As a preparation, we first introduce a novel mathematical construct in
Sec.

4.1
— we define a
friendship
relation among pairs of subgroups
H_{1}\subset G
and
H_{2}\subset G
. In
Sec.

4.2
, we explain how to make use of this definition, together with a theorem by Brion
[
37

38
,
21

, to derive an approach to verifying all-order accidental symmetries without computing the Hilbert series. It works when the building block fields are bosonic, and we will demonstrate its application to all the three classes of all-order accidental symmetries discussed in
Sec.

2
. In
Sec.

4.3
, we further generalize this approach to verifying finite-order accidental symmetries, as well as accommodating derivative interactions. We will show how it works for the examples of finite-order accidental symmetries discussed in
Sec.

3

Sec.

4.4
, we will sketch a possible systematic procedure of identifying the accidental symmetries
G
, from a given set of building block fields

and the imposed symmetry group

4.2
Verifying all-order accidental symmetries:
friends

In this subsection, we make use of the friendship relation introduced in
Sec.

4.1
to derive a criterion for telling when the two Hilbert series
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}
are equal, without explicitly computing them. We will apply this criterion to verify the three classes of all-order accidental symmetries discussed in
Sec.

2

Criterion for all-order accidental symmetries:

Let
H_{\Phi}
be the subgroup of
G
that remains unbroken
19
19

19

H_{\Phi}
is also called stabilizer subgroup (of a generic vev), little group or isotropy group.
under a generic vacuum expectation value (vev)

of the bosonic building block fields
\Phi(x)
:

G\;\xrightarrow{\;\;\langle\Phi\rangle\;\;}\;H_{\Phi}\subset G\,,

(4.6qaq)

then for any subgroup

, the following two statements are equivalent

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\text{$H$ is a friend of $H_{\Phi}$}\,.

(4.6qar)

It means that imposing any friend
H
of
H_{\Phi}
on the
\Phi

H
a
sufficient subgroup

The proof of this criterion takes a few steps.

First, because
\Phi(x)
is a
G

G

G

20
20

20

The multiplicity

4.6qau

\Phi^{k}=\Big\{\Phi_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\Phi_{s}^{k_{s}}\,,\quad k_{1}+\cdots+k_{s}=k\Big\}\quad\text{with $s$ the total number of components in $\Phi$}\,.

(4.6qas)

In each of these subspaces, we have a well-defined multiplicity
n_{k}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)
. The total multiplicity
n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Irrep}_{G}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n_{k}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\,q^{k}\,.

(4.6qat)

To make the proof easier to follow, we gloss over these technical details below, and simply call these Hilbert series as multiplicities
n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)
.

\{\Phi^{k}\}=n\big(\text{Inv}_{G}\big)\;\text{Inv}_{G}\oplus\left[\bigoplus_{\text{Irrep}_{G}\neq\text{Inv}_{G}}n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\;\text{Irrep}_{G}\right]\,,

where we have singled out
\text{Inv}_{G}

Next, the polynomial space
\{\Phi^{k}\}
also forms a rep of any given subgroup

H

G

\displaystyle\{\Phi^{k}\}

\displaystyle=n\big(\text{Inv}_{G}\big)\;\text{Inv}_{H}\oplus\bigoplus_{\text{Irrep}_{G}\neq\text{Inv}_{G}}n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\,\Big[\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H}\big)\,\text{Inv}_{H}\oplus\text{non-invariant }\text{Irrep}_{H}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big[n\big(\text{Inv}_{G}\big)+\sum_{\text{Irrep}_{G}\neq\text{Inv}_{G}}n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\;\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H}\big)\Big]\,\text{Inv}_{H}

Collecting all coefficients of

n\big(\text{Inv}_{H}\big)=n\big(\text{Inv}_{G}\big)+\sum_{\text{Irrep}_{G}\neq\text{Inv}_{G}}n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\;\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H}\big)\,.

It follows that

(4.6qax)

,

\rank{\left({}_{\mathbb{r}_\I}\mathcal{M}_{\Irrep_G}^{G,\,\Phi} \right) = \tdim\big( \Irrep_G^{H_\Phi} \big)}\,.

Caveat: fermionic case

As mentioned just before (
4.6qaq
), the above criterion is only guaranteed to work for bosonic building block fields. When fermionic building block fields are involved, there are a few technical complications, which could invalidate the proof presented above:

–

When the building block fields
\Psi
is Grassmann odd, one needs to scrutinize the role that the unbroken subgroup
H_{\Psi}
plays in the theorem by Brion in
Eq.

4.6qay
.

–

For purely fermionic building block fields, the polynomial space
\{\Psi^{k}\}
is finite dimensional, and consequently the Hilbert series are finite polynomials, as seen in
Eqs.

2.9
and
2.41
. Because of this, one need to double check if Brion’s theorem in
Eq.

4.6qay
still holds, especially regarding the meaning of the rank of the module.

–

The equivalence in
Eq.

4.6qaz
no longer holds, whether the Brion’s theorem in
Eq.

4.6qay
still holds or not. As the polynomial space
\{\Psi^{k}\}
is now finite dimensional, we have
n\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}\big)\neq 0
only for a finite number of
G
-irreps, which is not determined by a condition of the type
\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H_{\Psi}}\big)\neq 0
. This failure directly invalidates the proof presented above.

We leave an investigation on these complications to future work.

4.2.4
Summary of advantages

From the discussions above, we see that the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qar
is a very powerful tool. One can exploit group representation theory, in particular, the branching rules, to verify all-order accidental symmetries without explicitly computing the Hilbert series. This provides an alternative approach, which is much easier in some cases, as demonstrated above.

More importantly, this alternative approach enables us to go beyond merely verifying a postulated accidental symmetry. For a given symmetry
G
and the building block fields
\Phi
, one can work out the unbroken subgroup
H_{\Phi}
, and then look for all its friends
F\subset G
; these are all sufficient subgroups for the
G
-invariance of
\Phi
polynomials:

F\implies G\quad\text{for}\quad V(\Phi)\,.

(4.6qcp)

We can therefore find more all-order accidental symmetries in a relatively systematic manner. The key to actually achieve this is to develop an efficient algorithm for finding all the friends of a given subgroup
H_{\Phi}\subset G
.

4.3
Verifying finite-order accidental symmetries:
friends ma non troppo

In
Sec.

4.2
, we explained how to make use of the friendship relation to verify all-order accidental symmetries. Specifically, we presented a criterion in
Eq.

4.6qar
, which could save us the effort of explicitly computing the Hilbert series. This also enables us to analyze all-order accidental symmetries more systematically by making use of the branching rules. In this subsection, we explain how to generalize this more systematic approach to verifying finite-order accidental symmetries, and demonstrate its application with the examples discussed in
Sec.

3
.

We learned from
Sec.

3
that finite-order accidental symmetries can be verified by computing and comparing the Hilbert series. In particular, one can use the criterion in
Eq.

3.1
when restricted to non-derivative interactions, and the criterion in (
3.19
) for general cases.

Let us begin with the relatively easier criterion for non-derivative interactions given in
Eq.

3.1
, where the two Hilbert series,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)
, need not to agree in full, but only up to a certain power in
q
. Our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qar
then implies that
H
and
H_{\Phi}
are not friends — the condition in
Eq.

4.6qag
is violated by some
G
-irreps:

\text{Irrep}_{G}\neq\text{Inv}_{G}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H}\big)\,\text{dim}\big(\text{Irrep}_{G}^{H_{\Phi}}\big)\neq 0\,.

(4.6qcq)

Following the logic of the proof for
Eq.

4.6qar
, in particular,
Eqs.

4.6qaw
and
4.6qaz
, we know that each such friendship-violating
G
-irrep will make a contribution to the difference between the two Hilbert series. Therefore, they cannot appear “too soon” if
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)
still agree up to a certain order. Putting this idea into concrete language, we obtain the following criterion for finite-order accidental symmetries.

Criterion for finite-order accidental symmetries:

Let
H_{\Phi}
be the subgroup of
G
that remains unbroken under a generic vev
\langle\Phi\rangle
of the bosonic building block fields
\Phi(x)
:

G\;\xrightarrow{\;\;\langle\Phi\rangle\;\;}\;H_{\Phi}\subset G\,,

(4.6qcr)

and
H\subset G
be any subgroup. Then for the two Hilbert series to agree up to order
k
:

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)+\order{q^{k+1}}\,,

(4.6qcs)

a sufficient and necessary condition is that all the
(H,H_{\Phi})
friendship-violating
G
-irreps arise at higher order
\order{q^{k+1}}
:

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Irrep}_{G}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)=\order{q^{k+1}}\qquad\forall\qquad\left\{\matrixquantity{\lx@physics@matrix\Irrep_G^{} \ne\I_G^{} \\[5pt]
\tdim\big( \Irrep_G^H \big)\, \tdim\big( \Irrep_G^{H_\Phi} \big) \ne 0 \endlx@physics@matrix}\right.\,.

(4.6qct)

In such cases,
H
and
H_{\Phi}
are friends effectively up to order
q^{k}
, and we say that they are
friends ma non troppo
.

To apply the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
, after identifying the friendship-violating
G
-irreps using branching rules, one needs to compute the Hilbert series
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Irrep}_{G}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)
for these
G
-irreps, in principle. So admittedly, it is not necessarily easier than directly comparing the two Hilbert series for
G
and
H
invariants,
i.e.
checking
Eq.

4.6qcs
. However, in many cases, the condition in
Eq.

4.6qct
can be verified without explicitly computing the Hilbert series, as we will see in a few examples below. This is when our above criterion based on friends ma non troppo is favored.

The criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
was derived specifically for the case of non-derivative interactions in
Eq.

3.1
. However, the same logic holds also for the general cases in (
3.19
), where derivative interactions are accommodated. Specifically, to tell if two Hilbert series
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times H,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times G,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)
agree up to a certain order
k
, one just needs to recast the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
with a few steps:

1.

Replacing the building blocks fields
\Phi
with their SPMs
R_{\Phi}
to accommodate derivative interactions.

2.

Adding Lorentz symmetry into the group
G
as a factor:
G\;\longrightarrow\;\text{Lorentz}\times G
.

3.

Checking the IBP redundancy in the end.

With these recasting, we obtain the following criterion equivalent to (
3.19
):

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times G,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)=\order{q^{k+1}}\\[10.0pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times H,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times G,\,R_{\Phi}}(q)+\order{q^{k+1}}\,.

(4.6qcw)

Here the full group in consideration is
(\text{Lorentz}\times G)
. A generic vev of the building block
\langle R_{\Phi}\rangle
breaks it to the subgroup
H_{R_{\Phi}}\subset(\text{Lorentz}\times G)
. We check the friendship relation between this
H_{R_{\Phi}}
and any postulated sufficient subgroup
(\text{Lorentz}\times H)\subset(\text{Lorentz}\times G)
. As
H_{R_{\Phi}}
is often the trivial subgroup
\{\mathbf{e}\}
(although not always),
H
and
H_{R_{\Phi}}
are typically not friends. Using branching rules, we can identify their friendship-violating irreps. The condition in (
3.19
) is then equivalent to the requirement that these irreps arise at higher order
\order{q^{k+1}}
, once IBP redundancies are also taken into account. We will present some explicit examples below to demonstrate the application of
Eq.

4.6qcw
.

4.3.1
Non-derivative example: Class I with too many flavors

In
Sec.

2.1
, we observed that the Class I all-order accidental symmetries hold when the number of flavors is sufficiently small,
k=\max\left(k_{1},k_{2}\right)<N
, as summarized in
Table

2
. We showed this by explicitly comparing the Hilbert series in
Eq.

2.12
. In
Sec.

4.1
, we gave a more systematic and efficient proof making use of our friendship criterion in
Eq.

4.6qar
.

When there are too many flavors,
i.e.
,
k=\max\left(k_{1},k_{2}\right)\geq N
, we explained in
Sec.

3.1
that they are expected to become finite-order accidental symmetries that hold up to the order
q^{N-1}
(as the baryon combinations arise no earlier than
q^{N}
):

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)+\order{q^N}\,.

(4.6qcx)

This was verified for the specific cases of
k_{1}=N>k_{2}
and
k_{1}=k_{2}=N
using the explicit results in
Eqs.

2.14
and
2.15
. In below, we make use of our systematic approach based on friends ma non troppo,
i.e.
the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
, to prove that
Eq.

4.6qcx
holds for all cases of
k=\max\left(k_{1},k_{2}\right)\geq N
.

To make use of
Eq.

4.6qct
, we first identify all the
G
-irreps that violate the friendship relation between
H
and
H_{\Phi}
. Revisiting our discussions around
Eqs.

4.6qbl
and
4.6qbm
, we see that when
k=\max\left(k_{1},k_{2}\right)\geq N
, the unbroken subgroup elements
h_{\Phi}\in H_{\Phi}
become

h_{\Phi}=g_{N}\,g_{1}\qquad\text{with}\qquad g_{N}=e^{-i\theta}\,\mathbb{1}_{N\times N}\,,\quad g_{1}=e^{i\theta}\,,

(4.6qcy)

with the angle
\theta
constrained as
e^{iN\theta}=1
(the
k=N
case of
Eqs.

4.6qbl
and
4.6qbm
). This is a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
group. For this discrete
H_{\Phi}
,
Eq.

4.6qbk
no longer holds for all
Q\neq 0
(
i.e.
all the
G
-irreps in
Eq.

4.6qbi
), and consequently
H
and
H_{\Phi}
are no longer friends. The following
G
-irreps violate their friendship relation:

\text{Irrep}_{G}=\left(\mathbf{1}\;,\;Q=mN\right)\,,\qquad\text{with $m$ any nonzero integer}\,.

(4.6qcz)

They feed into the difference between
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)
. Nevertheless,
H
and
H_{\Phi}
are friends ma non troppo, as
Eq.

4.6qcz
corresponds to a very small portion of all
G
-irreps.

Now to finish checking the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
, we see that all the friendship-violating
G
-irreps in
Eq.

4.6qcz
are of order
\order{q^N}
, without computing the Hilbert series for them. Because each component in our building block fields
\Phi=(\phi_{i},\phi_{j}^{*})
has charge
\pm 1
, we need at least
N
powers of them to make up to a total charge
Q=mN\neq 0
. Therefore, the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
tells us that
H\implies G
is a finite-order accidental symmetry up to the order
q^{N-1}
, for
arbitrarily large

k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. It is worth emphasizing that directly computing the Hilbert series
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{H,\,\Phi}(q)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{Inv}}^{G,\,\Phi}(q)
at large
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
would be very difficult, so our friends ma non troppo approach here is much favored. Furthermore, we managed to draw a conclusion for all
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
with this approach, demonstrating that our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
is more systematic.

Non-derivative example:

As another demonstration of the criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
, let us revisit the example discussed in

3.1
that was summarized in
Table

6
. In this case, we have
G=SU(6)
and the building block fields
\Phi=(\phi,\phi^{*})
are a pair of fundamental and anti-fundamental irreps. When
\Phi
takes a generic vev
\langle\Phi\rangle
, the unbroken subgroup is
H_{\Phi}=SU(5)
. As this accidental symmetry is not all-order, we know from
Eq.

4.6qar
that the subgroup
H=SU(3)\times SU(2)
in
Table

6
is not a friend of
H_{\Phi}
.

To apply our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qct
, we need to identify the
G
-irreps that break the friendship relation between
H
and
H_{\Phi}
. To this end, we perform an “experimental search” by listing out all the
G
-irreps, in the order of increasing irrep dimension, as shown in
Table

14
generated using the
Mathematica
package LieART
[
41
]
. From the table, we see that the lowest dimension
G
-irrep that violates the
(H,H_{\Phi})
friendship has dimension
405
(given by the Dynkin label
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0.5}(20002)}
, as
highlighted
in the table). Therefore, any friendship-violating
G
-irrep would require at least four powers of
\phi
and/or

, because each of them has only
6
components, and homogeneous polynomials of degree smaller than

do not have enough dimension to contain such an irrep:
6^{3}=216<405
. On the other hand, this irrep is indeed contained in the following tensor product rep:

\text{sym}^{2}(\mathbf{6})\times\text{sym}^{2}(\mathbf{\bar{6}})=\mathbf{405}+\mathbf{35}+\mathbf{1}\,.

(4.6qda)

We therefore conclude using our criterion in

4.6qct
that this accidental symmetry holds up to order

and

we also notice that most

4.3.3
Derivative example: Custodial symmetry in Higgs sector of SMEFT

Now let us move on to some examples of the general case where derivative interactions are included, and demonstrate the application of the criterion
Eq.

4.6qct
recast in
Eq.

4.6qcw
.

Let us first analyze the custodial violation in the Higgs sector of SMEFT discussed in
Sec.

3.2
. To check the condition in
Eq.

4.6qcw
, we list out the relevant groups in this example:

Full group:

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Lorentz}\times G=\text{Lorentz}\times SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}\,,

(4.6qdba)

Unbroken subgroup:

\displaystyle\qquad H_{\Phi}=\{\mathbf{e}\}\,,

(4.6qdbb)

Postulated subgroup:

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Lorentz}\times H=\text{Lorentz}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}\,.

(4.6qdbc)

The subgroups
(\text{Lorentz}\times H)
and
H_{\Phi}
are not friends. As
H_{\Phi}=\{\mathbf{e}\}
, the friendship-violating irreps are all the non-invariant irreps of
(\text{Lorentz}\times G)
that contain an invariant under
(\text{Lorentz}\times H)
. These irreps are given by

\text{friendship-violating irreps}=\left(\mathbf{1}\;,\;\mathbf{1}\;,\;w_{R}\in\text{positive even integers}\right)\,,

(4.6qdc)

where
w_{R}=2j_{R}
is the highest weight of the
SU(2)_{R}
irrep with spin
j_{R}
. To see that
w_{R}
needs to be an even integer, we recall that
U(1)_{Y}
is embedded in the
SU(2)_{R}
as the third generator,
Y=t_{R}^{3}
, which can be diagonalized as

Y=t_{R}^{3}=\text{diag}\left(j_{R},j_{R}-1,\cdots,-j_{R}\right)\,.

(4.6qdd)

We see that for an
SU(2)_{R}
irrep to have a
Y=0
component,
j_{R}
needs to be an integer, and hence
w_{R}=2j_{R}
needs to be an even integer.

With the friendship-violating irreps identified in
Eq.

4.6qdc
, now let us check their appearance, which is encoded in the Hilbert series

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R},\,R_{\phi=(H,H^{*})}}(q)\,.

(4.6qde)

Instead of computing these Hilbert series explicitly, let us try to enumerate the operators that could feed into them.

The Higgs fields
H,H^{*}
have hypercharges
Y=\pm 1/2
, so from the highest weight in
Eq.

4.6qdd
we see that an
SU(2)_{R}
irrep with
w_{R}=2j_{R}
would require at least
w_{R}
powers of the Higgs fields; derivatives would not change the hypercharge. Therefore, irreps with
w_{R}\geq 6
would not arise before mass dimension 6.

For the irrep with
w_{R}=4
, its
Y=j_{R}=+2
state would require at least 4 powers of
H
. However, to satisfy the
SU(2)_{L}
invariance in
Eq.

4.6qdc
, they need to be contracted. Because
\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}H=0
(where
\widetilde{H}\equiv i\sigma^{2}H^{*}
), derivatives need to be involved in such a contraction, and they have to come in pairs to satisfy the Lorentz invariance in
Eq.

4.6qdc
. Therefore, the irrep with
w_{R}=4
cannot arise before mass dimension 6 either.

Finally, we are left with the irrep
w_{R}=2
. Its
Y=j_{R}=+1
state would require at least 2 powers of
H
. In addition, at least two powers of derivatives are needed to make an
SU(2)_{L}
invariant and Lorentz invariant. Note that
({\partial}_{\mu}\tilde{H})^{\dagger}({\partial}^{\mu}H)=0
. So without involving more building blocks, the only option we have is

\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}({\partial}^{2}H)={\partial}_{\mu}(\widetilde{H}^{\dagger}{\partial}^{\mu}H)\,.

(4.6qdf)

However, this is a total derivative operator, which is eliminated by the IBP redundancy. Therefore, we need to involve more powers of the Higgs fields or derivatives. They all come in pairs due to the requirement of
SU(2)_{L}
and Lorentz invariance in
Eq.

4.6qdc
. So the irrep with
w_{R}=2
cannot arise before mass dimension 6.

In summary, we conclude that

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R},\,R_{\phi=(H,H^{*})}}(q)=\order{q^6}\,.

(4.6qdg)

Now applying our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qcw
, it means that custodial symmetry is an accidental symmetry for the Higgs sector of SMEFT holding up to order
q^{5}
. This agrees with our previous discussions; see
e.g.

Eq.

3.21
.

4.3.4
Derivative example: Class I single flavor

Finally, we consider the single flavor case of the Class I all-order accidental symmetries summarized in
Table

1
. We included the derivative interactions, and they become finite-order accidental symmetries, as discussed in
Sec.

3.3
. In below, we apply our general friends ma non troppo criterion in
Eq.

4.6qcw
to verify the order that they start to break.

We begin with listing out the relevant groups in this example:

Full group:

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Lorentz}\times G=\text{Lorentz}\times SU(N)\times U(1)\,,

(4.6qdha)

Unbroken subgroup:

\displaystyle\qquad H_{\Phi}=\mathbb{Z}_{N}\,,

(4.6qdhb)

Postulated subgroup:

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Lorentz}\times H=\text{Lorentz}\times SU(N)\,.

(4.6qdhc)

Note that the unbroken subgroup
H_{\Phi}
in this case is not the trivial group, but
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, as explained in
Sec.

4.3.1
, specifically around
Eq.

4.6qcy
. The subgroups
(\text{Lorentz}\times H)
and
H_{\Phi}
are not friends. Their friendship-violating irreps of
(\text{Lorentz}\times G)
are given by

\hskip-10.0pt\text{friendship-violating irreps}=\left(\mathbf{1}\;,\;\mathbf{1}\;,\;Q=mN\right)\,,\quad\text{with $m$ non-zero integer}\,.

(4.6qdi)

These irreps are encoded in the Hilbert series

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SU(N)\times U(1),\,(R_{\phi},R_{\phi^{*}})}(q)\,.

(4.6qdj)

Instead of computing these Hilbert series explicitly, let us try to enumerate the operators that could feed into them.

For explicitness, let us specify to the case
N=3
. To make an
SU(3)
invariant with
Q=3
, we need the factor

\Big[\epsilon^{ijk}\phi_{i}({\partial}_{\mu}\phi_{j})({\partial}_{\nu}\phi_{k})\Big]\sim q^{5}\,.

(4.6qdk)

To make a Lorentz invariant, we need to contract it with another factor

\Big[({\partial}_{\mu}\phi^{\dagger})({\partial}_{\nu}\phi)\Big]\sim q^{4}\,.

(4.6qdl)

Together, this is precisely the mass dimension 9 operator in
Eq.

3.23
. One can convince themselves that to make any irrep in
Eq.

4.6qdi
for the
N=3
case, this combination gives the lowest mass dimension. We therefore conclude

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SU(3)\times U(1),\,(R_{\phi},R_{\phi^{*}})}(q)=\order{q^9}\,.

(4.6qdm)

Our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qcw
then implies that the accidental symmetry
SU(3)\implies SU(3)\times U(1)
holds up to order
q^{8}
, agreeing with the results in
Eq.

3.22
.

The case
N=4
can be analyzed in a similar manner. One way to make an
SU(4)
invariant with
Q=4
is

\epsilon^{ijkl}\,\varphi_{i}\,({\partial}_{\mu}\varphi_{j})\,({\partial}_{\nu}{\partial}_{\rho}\varphi_{k})\,({\partial}^{\mu}{\partial}^{\nu}{\partial}^{\rho}\varphi_{l})\,,

(4.6qdn)

where different numbers of derivatives have been assigned to each factor
\varphi
. Recall from
Eq.

3.17
that the trace components are not part of the SPM for scalar fields. Therefore, the contraction of Lorentz indices above is the only option. This is precisely the kind of operators in the second line of
Eq.

3.25
, which have mass dimension 10.

An alternative option to make an
SU(4)
invariant with
Q=4
is

\epsilon^{ijkl}\,({\partial}_{\mu}\varphi_{i})\,({\partial}_{\nu}\varphi_{j})\,({\partial}_{\rho}\varphi_{k})\,({\partial}_{\sigma}\varphi_{l})\,.

(4.6qdo)

To make a Lorentz invariant, we can contract the Lorentz indices with an
\epsilon
tensor:

\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,\epsilon^{ijkl}\,({\partial}_{\mu}\varphi_{i})\,({\partial}_{\nu}\varphi_{j})\,({\partial}_{\rho}\varphi_{k})\,({\partial}_{\sigma}\varphi_{l})\,.

(4.6qdp)

However, this would make it into a total derivative, which is eliminated by the IBP redundancy. To survive the IBP, we need to further multiply it by another factor
\varphi^{\dagger}\varphi
:

\Big[\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,\epsilon^{ijkl}\,({\partial}_{\mu}\varphi_{i})\,({\partial}_{\nu}\varphi_{j})\,({\partial}_{\rho}\varphi_{k})\,({\partial}_{\sigma}\varphi_{l})\,\Big]\,(\varphi^{\dagger}\varphi)\,.

(4.6qdq)

This gives the kind of operators in the first line of
Eq.

3.25
, which also have mass dimension 10.

One can convince themselves that to make any irrep in
Eq.

4.6qdi
for the
N=4
case, the above two options give the lowest mass dimension. We therefore conclude

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{friendship-violating irreps},\text{ IBP}}^{\text{Lorentz}\times SU(4)\times U(1),\,(R_{\phi},R_{\phi^{*}})}(q)=\order{q^{10}}\,.

(4.6qdr)

Our criterion in
Eq.

4.6qcw
then implies that the accidental symmetry
SU(4)\implies SU(4)\times U(1)
holds up to order
q^{9}
, agreeing with the results in
Eq.

3.24
.

4.4
A process of identifying accidental symmetries

In
Sec.

4.1
, we introduced a new mathematical construct — a
friendship
relation between two subgroups (
Eq.

4.6qag
). Making use of this new machinery, we derived more systematic criteria for all-order accidental symmetries in
Sec.

4.2
(
Eq.

4.6qar
) and for finite-order accidental symmetries in
Sec.

4.3
(
Eqs.

4.6qct
and
4.6qcw
). We applied them to verifying the accidental symmetries discussed in
Secs.

2
and
3
, and demonstrated the power of this new approach.

With the success in
Secs.

4.2
and
4.3
, we are encouraged to think about the more ambitious goal beyond simply verifying a postulate accidental symmetry
H\implies G
: is it possible to systematically identify all the accidental symmetries
G
from a given set of fields
\Phi(x)
and the imposed symmetry group
H
?

One way of attacking this ambitious task is to carry out a brute-force search. First, we make a list of candidates for
G
. We can collect all the components of
\Phi(x)
into a big vector, and then consider all the linear transformations of this vector that preserves the kinetic term of the Lagrangian. This gives us the maximal global symmetry group
X
(within the construction of linear realizations). We list out all the subgroups of
X
that contains the imposed symmetry group
H
,
i.e.
,
H\subset G_{i}\subset X
, each of which is then a candidate accidental symmetry. Now for each candidate
G_{i}
, the task is converted back to that of verifying a postulated accidental symmetry
H\implies G_{i}
. We can make use of the criteria in
Eqs.

4.6qct
and
4.6qcw
to determine at which order the accidental symmetry
H\implies G_{i}
starts to break. If the breaking order is higher than the leading order of the EFT, then it is a valid accidental symmetry. Running this process over all the candidates
G_{i}
, we can systematically identify all accidental symmetries from a given set of fields
\Phi(x)
and the imposed symmetry group
H
.

The process described above is feasible in principle. However, as the maximal global symmetry group
X
is huge in practice, we will have many candidate accidental symmetries to verify. Therefore, a systematic and efficient program for verifying each postulated accidental symmetry
H\implies G_{i}
is still the key to enable the above brute-force search. From the examples discussed in
Secs.

4.2
and
4.3
, we see that there are two major tasks in verifying a postulated accidental symmetry:

1.

Identify all the friendship-violating
G_{i}
irreps. If there is none, then it is an all-order accidental symmetry.

2.

Work out the EFT order at which the friendship-violating
G_{i}
irreps start to arise.

We know how to handle the first task in a relatively systematic way. There are known branching rules to make use of, and when analytic analysis gets too complicated, one can also switch to an
experimental
search, by systematically listing out all the
G_{i}
irreps, similar to what we did in
Tables

9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
and
14
. On the other hand, the second task above is more difficult. For the examples discussed in
Sec.

4.3
, we sort of did this part on a case-by-case basis. A more systematic approach would be very interesting, and would enable the whole process of identifying accidental symmetries described above.

5
Summary and Outlook

Accidental symmetries are ubiquitous in EFTs and have important phenomenological consequences. A deeper understanding of them, especially a more systematic one, is highly desired and could have far-reaching implications. In this paper, we investigated accidental symmetries in EFTs using the Hilbert series method together with more advanced tools in invariant theory.

In
Sec.

2
, we highlighted that when we focus only on the potential interactions
V(\Phi)
(
i.e.
, polynomials in the fields
\Phi(x)
), there are accidental symmetries that hold to all orders in the EFT. As examples, we showed three classes of such all-order accidental symmetries, each verified by explicitly computing and comparing the Hilbert series. In particular, the famous custodial symmetry in the Higgs sector of SMEFT can be recognized as the
N=2
case of the Class II discussed in
Sec.

2.2
.

In
Sec.

3
, we turned to accidental symmetries that hold only up to a finite order in the EFT expansion, and explained how to verify them by computing the Hilbert series. Derivative interactions are accommodated by considering the full EFT Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{\,EFT}}
, and we elaborated on their impacts on accidental symmetries that are preserved to all orders by the potential interactions. Several demonstration examples were provided.

In
Sec.

4
, we presented a more systematic approach of verifying a postulated accidental symmetry. In particular, we introduced a new mathematical construct that we call
friendship
relation between subgroups. Making use of this new definition, we derived more systematic criteria for verifying all-order and finite-order accidental symmetries, without the need to compute the Hilbert series. In
Sec.

4.2
, we applied the new criterion based on
friends
to prove the example all-order accidental symmetries discussed in
Sec.

2
. In
Sec.

4.3
, we applied the new criterion based on
friends ma non troppo
to the example finite-order accidental symmetries discussed in
Sec.

3
. Encouraged by the success of these applications, we also proposed in
Sec.

4.4
a feasible approach to the ambitious goal of systematically finding all the accidental symmetries from a given set of fields and the imposed symmetry group.

Our investigations in this paper have already much improved our understanding of the origin, as well as the patterns of accidental symmetries in EFTs, especially with the more systematic approaches developed in
Sec.

4
. Along this direction, there are several desired technological advancements that, once achieved, would provide us with an even better handling on analyzing accidental symmetries. First, we believe there are many more all-order accidental symmetries than those three classes presented in
Sec.

2
. A key to find them, as mentioned in the end of
Sec.

4.2
, is to develop an efficient algorithm for finding all the friends of a given unbroken subgroup
H_{\Phi}\subset G
. This would allow us to find all the sufficient subgroups
H
for the
G
invariance, and hence identify new all-order accidental symmetries
H\implies G\text{ for }V(\Phi)
. Second, regarding the ambitious goal of systematically identifying finite-order accidental symmetries, the key missing technology to enable our proposal in
Sec.

4.4
is a systematic way of determining the EFT order at which a concerned friendship-violating
G
-irrep starts to appear. Finally, as emphasized in
Sec.

4
, our
friends
and
friends ma non troppo
approaches to accidental symmetries only apply to the cases of bosonic building block fields. When Grassmann odd fields are involved, there are several technical complications listed in
Sec.

4.2
. It would be interesting to examine through this list, to identify the sources of failure in the fermionic cases, and then fix them to accommodate fermionic fields into our approaches in
Sec.

4
.

Our deeper understanding of accidental symmetries, combined with newly developed methodologies for systematically analyzing them, opens the door to numerous interesting phenomenological applications. Accidental symmetries are frequently relied upon in model building to justify the emergence of (approximate) symmetries and to control their breaking.
Prominent examples include solutions to the hierarchy problem (
e.g.
, within the hyperbolic Higgs scenario
[
44
]
an all-order accidental symmetry like the ones studied in

symmetry to address the quality problem within composite

,

,

,

]
or weak
[

dynamics;
discrete
Goldstone bosons where an exact discrete symmetry accidentally generates a continuous one
[
