Large Language Models for Patent Classification: Strengths, Trade-offs, and the Long Tail Effect
Lorenzo Emer, Marco Lippi, Andrea Mina, Andrea Vandin

Abstract
Patent classification into CPC codes underpins large-scale analyses of technological change but remains challenging due to its hierarchical, multi-label, and highly imbalanced structure. While pre–Generative AI supervised encoder-based models became the de facto standard for large-scale patent classification, recent advances in large language models (LLMs) raise questions about whether they can provide complementary capabilities, particularly for rare or weakly represented technological categories. In this work, we perform a systematic comparison of encoder-based classifiers (BERT, SciBERT, and PatentSBERTa) and open-weight LLMs on a highly imbalanced benchmark dataset (USPTO-70k). We evaluate LLMs under zero-shot, few-shot, and retrieval-augmented prompting, and further assess parameter-efficient fine-tuning of the best-performing model. Our results show that encoder-based models achieve higher aggregate performance, driven by strong results on frequent CPC subclasses, but struggle on rare ones. In contrast, LLMs achieve relatively higher performance on infrequent subclasses—often associated with early-stage, cross-domain, or weakly institutionalised technologies—particularly at higher hierarchical levels. These findings indicate that encoder-based and LLM-based approaches play complementary roles in patent classification. We additionally quantify inference time and energy consumption, showing that encoder-based models are up to three orders of magnitude more efficient than LLMs. Overall, our results inform responsible patentometrics and technology mapping, and motivate hybrid classification approaches that combine encoder efficiency with the long-tail coverage of LLMs under computational and environmental constraints.

Introduction

Patent classification is a foundational task in the organisation and analysis of technological information. In scientometrics and patentometrics, automated classification methods are widely used to map technological domains, trace innovation trajectories, and study technological change and emergence
(
15
;
22
;
29
;
35
)
. Accurate assignment of classification schemes such as the IPC or CPC is essential for patent offices and downstream users—including legal practitioners, technology analysts, and policymakers—to support prior art search, landscape analysis, and monitoring of emerging technologies
(
23
;
28
)
.
The rapid growth of global patent filings, together with the increasing granularity of classification systems, has intensified the need for accurate and scalable automated classification methods capable of handling millions of heterogeneous, multi-label, and hierarchically structured documents, while avoiding systematic biases across technological domains

(
23
;
21
)
.

Historically, automated patent classification has progressed through several methodological phases.
Early approaches relied on sparse bag-of-words representations and classical machine learning algorithms, which capture surface-level lexical patterns but struggle with synonymy, polysemy, and domain-specific terminology

(
8
;
54
)
.
Subsequent work incorporated word embeddings and deep neural architectures such as convolutional and recurrent neural networks, offering more expressive representations of patent text and improved performance over traditional pipelines

(
27
;
15
)
.
More recently, transformer-based pre-trained language models such as BERT and domain-adapted variants like PatentSBERTa have become a strong baseline for patent classification, achieving robust performance on large-scale datasets across different levels of the IPC and CPC hierarchies

(
24
;
2
)
.
These encoder-based models remain comparatively efficient to fine-tune and deploy, making them attractive for large-scale patent analytics and indicator construction.

In parallel, recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have reshaped natural language processing more broadly
(
5
;
4
)
.
LLMs are typically trained on massive text corpora and can be applied to downstream tasks through
prompting
, that is, by providing natural-language instructions and examples rather than task-specific supervised training.
This enables
zero-shot
classification, where no labeled examples are provided, and
few-shot
classification, where a small number of illustrative examples is included in the prompt
(
5
)
.
In addition, LLMs can be combined with external document retrieval mechanisms—commonly referred to as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)—to ground predictions in relevant domain-specific text
(
26
)
.

These capabilities call for a systematic assessment of the benefits that LLMs may offer for patent classification, especially in sparsely populated or weakly institutionalised regions of the classification space, as well as an evaluation of whether these benefits justify their substantially higher computational cost.

Recent studies suggest that, despite their versatility, the effectiveness of LLMs in highly technical, domain-specific, and large-scale multi-label settings such as CPC classification remains uncertain

(
21
;
38
)
.
At the same time, recent work in scientometrics has begun to explore the use of large language models in research evaluation and knowledge mapping, often finding that LLMs can complement traditional indicator-based approaches while also introducing systematic biases and efficiency concerns

(
47
;
49
;
48
;
40
)
.
Moreover, inference with LLMs typically incurs substantially higher costs in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption than encoder-based architectures, posing challenges for large-scale operational deployment

(
34
)
.
While fine-tuning can improve LLM performance on domain-specific tasks, it requires access to open-weight models and introduces additional computational and environmental costs, making its practical value an open empirical question.

This work addresses these issues by conducting a systematic comparison between strong encoder-based baselines (BERT, SciBERT, PatentSBERTa) and a diverse set of open-weight LLMs.
We evaluate multiple inference strategies, including zero-shot prompting, few-shot prompting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and hybrid RAG-plus–few-shot configurations, in order to assess how different forms of contextualisation affect CPC multi-label classification performance across frequent and infrequent subclasses.
In addition, we apply parameter-efficient fine-tuning using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
18
)
to the best-performing LLM to examine whether targeted adaptation can narrow the gap with domain-specific encoder models.
Finally, we quantify computational cost and energy consumption using the CodeCarbon framework
(
9
)
, enabling a principled analysis of the trade-off between predictive performance, scalability, and sustainability. To assess the robustness and generalisability of our findings across institutional contexts, we further conduct an external evaluation on an independent dataset of European Patent Office (EPO) patents, without retraining or adapting the models.

Across these evaluations, we obtain four main results.
First, supervised encoder-based models remain highly competitive on frequent CPC subclasses and achieve superior micro-averaged performance (which aggregates predictions over all labels and is driven by frequent subclasses), but they exhibit substantial performance degradation on rare subclasses, resulting in low macro-level (label-balanced) and hierarchical recall.
Second, LLMs—particularly when augmented with few-shot prompting and lightweight retrieval—provide more balanced coverage across the CPC hierarchy and systematically improve recall on rare and infrequent subclasses.
Third, these gains come at the cost of significantly higher computational and energy requirements, which limit the scalability of LLM-based approaches in large-scale classification settings.
Fourth, no single modelling approach dominates across all evaluation criteria: encoder-based models offer unmatched efficiency and scalability, whereas LLMs provide complementary semantic flexibility that is most valuable in long-tail and exploratory classification scenarios.
This is especially important for scientometric analyses concerned with emerging technologies, as these tend to first appear in the long tail of patent classification systems, where categories are sparse, unstable, or subject to revision
(
53
)
.
By providing a controlled empirical comparison under realistic evaluation settings, this study clarifies the practical advantages and limitations of LLM-based approaches relative to established transformer architectures, and identifies when their use is justified in both research and operational patent classification contexts.

Related Work

This section reviews prior work on automated patent classification by focusing on the evolution of modeling paradigms rather than on specific application domains.
We organize the literature along a methodological trajectory, moving from traditional machine learning pipelines to encoder-based transformer models and, more recently, to large language models.
We also discuss how issues such as label imbalance, hierarchical structure, and evaluation choices have shaped both model development and empirical assessment.

From traditional pipelines to encoder-based models

Early work on automatic patent classification relied on classical text classification pipelines in which patent documents were represented using bag-of-words (BoW) features and classified with supervised learners such as support vector machines (SVMs)
(
55
)
or
k
-nearest neighbours (KNNs)
(
31
)
.
Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF–IDF) has been the dominant BoW scheme for encoding titles, abstracts, or claims into high-dimensional sparse vectors.
These representations were typically used for multi-label prediction of IPC or CPC codes using linear classifiers.
Representative approaches include multi-phase and hybrid pipelines combining TF–IDF, feature selection, and SVM- or KNN-based classifiers, achieving moderate performance on collections of tens of thousands of patents

(
8
;
54
)
.
Despite their scalability and simplicity, BoW-based methods are fundamentally limited by their inability to capture semantic similarity beyond lexical overlap and by their sensitivity to vocabulary drift in rapidly evolving technological domains
(
51
)
.

The introduction of distributed word representations marked a transition from sparse BoW features to dense, low-dimensional embeddings.
Word2vec- and fastText-style embeddings trained on large patent corpora enabled more semantically meaningful representations of technical terminology and were combined with neural architectures such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), gated recurrent units (GRUs), and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks
(
57
;
39
)
.
CNN-based models, in particular, demonstrated improvements over BoW+SVM pipelines by capturing local
n
-gram patterns in patent text

(
27
)
.
Subsequent work extended these architectures to multi-label and hierarchical classification settings

(
15
;
33
)
.
Nevertheless, these models relied on static word embeddings, limiting their ability to handle polysemy, contextual variation, and evolving terminology, and often struggled with the length and structural heterogeneity of patent documents
(
60
)
.

The introduction of transformer-based language models marked a substantial advance in patent classification.
Fine-tuning architectures such as BERT and its variants on patent corpora led to significant performance gains across single-label and multi-label classification tasks and across multiple levels of the IPC and CPC hierarchies

(
24
)
.
Domain-adapted variants trained on large-scale patent collections further improved performance by aligning the pretraining distribution with patent-specific language and stylistic conventions

(
2
;
42
)
.
As a result, encoder-based classifiers have become a de facto standard for automated IPC and CPC assignment, owing to their strong performance on frequent labels and their computational efficiency at inference time
(
29
)
.

From a patentometrics perspective, encoder models are attractive because they scale efficiently to millions of documents and can be seamlessly integrated into large indicator pipelines
(
23
)
.
However, prior studies also note their sensitivity to label imbalance and their tendency to optimize performance on dominant subclasses
(
36
;
32
)
.
Consequently, macro-averaged and hierarchical metrics are often substantially lower than micro-averaged scores, raising concerns about their suitability for analyzing emerging or weakly represented technological areas

(
23
;
28
;
21
)
.

Large language models and prompt-based classification

More recently, LLMs have been explored as an alternative paradigm for patent classification.
Unlike encoder-based systems, LLMs can perform zero-shot and few-shot classification by leveraging instruction-following capabilities acquired during large-scale pretraining.
Initial studies show that LLMs can assign plausible labels without task-specific fine-tuning and that performance can be improved through prompt engineering, few-shot exemplars, and lightweight retrieval augmentation

(
61
;
38
;
58
)
.

Applications of LLMs to patent analysis are still relatively limited in number and scope. Parallel discussions in scientometrics emphasize a similar pattern: LLMs are increasingly explored as assistive tools for evaluation and mapping tasks, but their reliability, bias, and resource requirements remain open concerns
(
49
;
46
;
7
;
40
)
. While recent studies demonstrate promising performance in specific settings—often by integrating LLMs into hybrid or assisted workflows such as active learning—most contributions remain task- or domain-specific and stop short of providing systematic, large-scale benchmarking across datasets, prompting strategies, and evaluation dimensions
(
21
;
58
)
. Moreover, despite their relevance for operational deployment in patent offices and research institutions, the computational cost and energy footprint of LLM-based approaches are rarely analyzed as first-class evaluation criteria.

Challenges and Responsible Use of Automated Patent Classification

Patent classification remains intrinsically challenging.
Patent documents are long, heterogeneous, and densely technical; classification systems are highly granular and hierarchical; and expert human examiners themselves exhibit non-negligible disagreement in code assignment
(
6
)
.
As a result, state-of-the-art automated classifiers often achieve high micro-averaged performance while exhibiting substantially weaker macro-averaged and rare-label performance, particularly at fine-grained subclass levels

(
23
;
28
;
21
)
.
These limitations have motivated research on ensemble methods, explainability, and, more recently, the application of LLMs to patent classification

(
20
;
38
)
.

Beyond predictive accuracy, recent work in scientometrics and patentometrics calls for greater attention to the responsible use of automated methods, emphasizing transparency, robustness, and awareness of bias in indicator construction
(
16
;
52
)
.
At the same time, the environmental impact of machine learning models has become an increasingly salient concern for large-scale text processing
(
41
)
. Recent contributions explicitly discuss these concerns in the context of large language models, highlighting both their analytical potential and the risks associated with opacity, bias, and uncritical adoption in evaluative settings
(
47
;
46
)
.

However, it remains insufficiently understood how differences between encoder-based models and LLMs translate into systematic biases or advantages in downstream scientometric analyses, particularly in the presence of long-tailed and hierarchical classification systems.
Against this background, the present study provides a systematic comparison between encoder-based models and LLMs for CPC multi-label patent classification.
Unlike prior work, we evaluate both paradigms under comparable conditions, explicitly accounting for hierarchical performance, rare-label behaviour, inference-time efficiency, and energy consumption.
By focusing on compact, locally deployable LLMs and parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies, we assess not only predictive performance but also the practical and methodological trade-offs faced by patent offices and scientometric researchers
(
21
)
.

Methodology

This study investigates the effectiveness of supervised encoder models and large language models (LLMs) for
CPC subclass-level multi-label patent classification
, with particular attention to the trade-offs between predictive performance, computational cost, and environmental impact (Figure
1
). To enable a fair and realistic comparison, we adopt an experimental pipeline based on a temporally split patent corpus, preprocessing, standardized evaluation metrics, and systematic tracking of energy consumption and CO
2
emissions. For encoder-based models, we adopt standard fine-tuning and decoding settings commonly used in the patent classification literature, obtaining performance in line with previously reported results
(
24
;
2
)
. In contrast, while the application of LLMs to patent classification has so far been limited and largely focused on predictive accuracy, we evaluate LLMs under a rigorous and controlled experimental protocol that explicitly accounts for prompting strategies, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, inference-time efficiency, and energy consumption—dimensions that have received little systematic attention in prior work.
We first establish strong supervised baselines using Transformer-based encoders fine-tuned on labeled patent data. We then evaluate instruction-tuned LLMs under progressively more informative prompting regimes, ranging from zero-shot inference to retrieval-augmented few-shot prompting that incorporates both labeled examples and structured CPC definitions. Finally, we select the best-performing LLM configuration and adapt it to the task via parameter-efficient fine-tuning using LoRA. This progressive design allows us to disentangle the effects of domain pre-training, prompt-based reasoning, external knowledge injection, and task-specific adaptation, while jointly assessing accuracy, efficiency, and sustainability.

Dataset

We conduct our experiments on the
USPTO-70k
dataset
(
36
)
, a benchmark for CPC subclass-level multi-label patent classification. The corpus comprises
70 250
USPTO patent documents with a chronological split designed to emulate realistic deployment conditions:
50 250
patents from 2006–2017 for training,
10 000
patents from 2018 for validation, and
10 000
patents from 2019 for testing. Each patent record includes a unique identifier, a title, an abstract, and one or more CPC subclass labels. Following standard practice in large-scale patent classification, we use the concatenation of title and abstract as the textual input for all models. We apply minimal text preprocessing, consisting of basic normalization (e.g., lowercasing and whitespace normalization), and ensure consistent formatting across all model families. CPC labels are normalized to the four-character subclass level and encoded as multi-hot vectors. All models are trained and evaluated using the same textual input fields to ensure a fair and controlled comparison.

Supervised encoder baselines

We evaluate three supervised Transformer encoder models as strong non-LLM baselines:

BERT
,
SciBERT
, and
PatentSBERTa
. In all cases, the patent title
and abstract are encoded into a fixed-dimensional document representation, which is mapped
to CPC subclass probabilities via a multi-label classification head.

BERT
(
10
)
is a general-purpose language model, while SciBERT
(
3
)

and PatentSBERTa
(
2
)
are domain-adapted encoders trained on scientific
and patent corpora, respectively. Comparing these models allows us to assess the impact of
domain-specific pre-training on CPC subclass assignment.

Each encoder is fine-tuned using binary cross-entropy loss with independent sigmoid outputs,
enabling multi-label predictions over the full CPC subclass space.
At inference time, predicted subclasses are obtained by thresholding calibrated probabilities,
with the maximum number of predicted labels capped to match the LLM evaluation protocol.
In order to enable a direct comparison with label-space retrieval prompting in LLMs, we additionally
evaluate a constrained decoding variant in which encoder predictions are restricted to a
per-patent subset of candidate CPC subclasses retrieved from CPC definitions. This restriction affects only the set of admissible labels; the final number of predicted subclasses per patent remains capped at seven, as in the unconstrained setting.
This constraint is applied only at inference time and does not affect encoder training. Further technical details on encoder models (training, thresholds, label space) are provided in Appendix
1
.

In the few-shot setting, each prompt includes
5
labeled examples: two static examples
that remain fixed across all inputs, and three dynamically retrieved examples selected

This hybrid approach balances prompt stability (static examples) with instance-specific

In preliminary experiments, purely static and purely dynamic few-shot configurations resulted
in less stable behavior and lower recall across models; we therefore adopt the hybrid setting
as a pragmatic compromise, fixed prior to final evaluation.

In our retrieval-augmented prompting (RAG) settings, we do not retrieve evidentiary passages from external corpora; rather, we retrieve a per-patent allowed CPC subclass set from the official CPC subclass definitions

Model Selection and LoRA Fine-Tuning

Across all LLMs and prompting configurations, Qwen-2.5-7B achieves the strongest validation performance (notably in micro- and hierarchical F1), and is therefore selected for parameter-efficient task adaptation via LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
(
18
)
. LoRA adapts a pre-trained model by adding trainable low-rank updates to selected weight matrices (typically in the attention and/or feed-forward projections) while keeping the base weights frozen. Concretely, for a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, LoRA learns an additive update
\Delta\mathbf{W}
parameterized as a rank-
r
decomposition:

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W},\quad\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A},

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
with
r\ll\min(d,k)
. This substantially reduces the number of trainable parameters and the optimizer state footprint compared to full fine-tuning, enabling efficient adaptation of multi-billion-parameter models. Implementation details are available in Appendix
4
.

Evaluation Metrics and Efficiency Analysis

All models are evaluated on a held-out test set using standard metrics for

multi-label
and
hierarchical
classification, which are widely
adopted in the literature
(
50
;
43
)
and
commonly used in large-scale patent classification tasks.

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of CPC subclasses. For each patent
i
, let

Y_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
be the set of ground-truth labels and

\hat{Y}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
the predicted label set after thresholding
(or top-
k
selection, when specified). True positives (TP), false positives (FP),
and false negatives (FN) are computed by aggregating predictions across all
patents and labels.

We report both micro- and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 scores.
Micro-averaged metrics pool all label decisions across the test set and are therefore dominated by performance on frequent subclasses:

P_{\mu}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\quad R_{\mu}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},\quad F1_{\mu}=\frac{2P_{\mu}R_{\mu}}{P_{\mu}+R_{\mu}}.

(2)

Macro-averaged metrics compute precision, recall, and F1 independently for each
subclass and then average across labels, assigning equal weight to frequent and
rare subclasses. Let
\mathrm{TP}_{c}
,
\mathrm{FP}_{c}
, and
\mathrm{FN}_{c}
denote
the per-label counts. The macro-F1 score is defined as

F1_{M}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}F1_{c}.

(3)

To account for the hierarchical structure of the CPC taxonomy, we additionally
report hierarchy-aware precision, recall, and F1 scores based on label expansion.
Each label is augmented with its ancestors in the CPC hierarchy. Let

\mathrm{anc}(c)
denote the set of ancestors of label
c
(including
c
itself),
and define the expanded label sets:

Y_{i}^{H}=\bigcup_{c\in Y_{i}}\mathrm{anc}(c),\qquad\hat{Y}_{i}^{H}=\bigcup_{c\in\hat{Y}_{i}}\mathrm{anc}(c).

(4)

Hierarchical metrics are computed by augmenting predicted and ground-truth labels with higher-level CPC labels.

Across all models and evaluation settings, we cap the number of predicted CPC
subclasses per patent to at most seven. This reflects the empirical annotation
structure of the dataset: in the training set, 99.5% of patents are annotated
with between one and seven subclasses (median: 2; 99th percentile: 7). The same
constraint is applied uniformly to supervised encoders and LLM-based methods to
ensure comparability and to prevent degenerate over-prediction.

All models are evaluated under deterministic inference settings. Encoder-based
classifiers are evaluated in
eval()
mode, while LLMs use greedy decoding
(
do_sample=False
). Repeated runs with different random seeds yield
identical predictions; therefore, results are reported from a single run per
configuration.

The evaluation of large-scale, multi-label patent classification systems requires careful
interpretation of aggregate performance metrics, particularly in the presence of strong
imbalances in CPC subclass frequencies. We therefore distinguish between overall performance
assessed over the full label space and stratified analyses focusing on frequent and rare
subclasses.

For aggregate metrics computed over all CPC subclasses (e.g., micro- and macro-averaged
precision, recall, and F1), we adopt a primarily descriptive perspective. Point estimates are
complemented with non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals to quantify uncertainty. In
particular, CPC subclasses are resampled with replacement, and the corresponding aggregate
metrics are recomputed over
1 000
bootstrap samples
(
12
)
. This procedure
captures variability across technological categories without relying on distributional
assumptions. Formal hypothesis testing is not applied to overall metrics, as these comparisons
are dominated by frequent subclasses and are not associated with a specific null hypothesis.

To investigate performance differences across technological regimes, CPC subclasses are
partitioned according to their empirical frequency in the evaluation data. Subclasses in the
top 20% of the support distribution are treated as frequent, while those in the bottom 20%
are treated as rare. This stratification enables a focused analysis of model behavior on
well-established versus sparsely represented technologies and supports the interpretation of
macro-averaged results.

For stratified comparisons, statistical significance is assessed using paired non-parametric
tests over CPC subclasses
(
17
)
. For each subclass, per-label
performance scores (e.g., F1) produced by two models form paired observations. We use the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which is robust to non-normal and zero-inflated distributions common
among rare subclasses. Directional (one-sided) hypotheses are applied when theoretically
motivated, for example when testing whether LLMs outperform supervised encoder models on rare
subclasses. This label-level testing strategy is well suited to multi-label classification and
avoids document-level assumptions required by tests such as McNemar’s test.

Moreover, to complement predictive performance with sustainability considerations, we estimate energy usage and associated carbon emissions using
CodeCarbon

(
9
)
. For each experimental setting (encoder fine-tuning, LLM inference under each prompting strategy, and LoRA fine-tuning), we record the estimated energy consumption and CO
2
emissions. This enables a direct comparison of accuracy–cost trade-offs across model families. Recent validation studies
(
13
)
support the reliability of CodeCarbon estimates against external power measurements.

Experimental Setup

All experiments are conducted on the Booster supercomputing system at CINECA, using single-node executions based on the BullSequana X2135 architecture and equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs. For the experimental campaign,
2 000
GPU core-hours were allocated. As discussed above, the selected models and experimental settings are compatible with execution on standard laptop- or workstation-class machines equipped with a single GPU. The use of the CINECA HPC infrastructure is motivated by practical considerations, namely to enable large-scale experimentation, ensure reproducible and interference-free execution, and obtain reliable measurements under controlled conditions.

External dataset construction and evaluation protocol (EPO)

To validate the generalizability of our methodology beyond the USPTO setting, we apply the same experimental pipeline to an external dataset of patents from the European Patent Office (EPO). We randomly select
10 000
EPO patent applications filed between 2019 and 2022 using
ORBIS Intellectual Property

2
2

2

https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/capabilities/company-reference-data/orbis.html
by Moody’s as the data source. Patent texts are processed using the same preprocessing steps adopted for the USPTO dataset, including title–abstract concatenation, CPC subclass normalization at the four-character level, and multi-label encoding.

Importantly, no model is retrained or adapted on EPO data. Encoder-based classifiers trained on USPTO patents are evaluated in a strict out-of-domain setting, while large language models are applied in zero-shot and retrieval-augmented configurations identical to those used in the main experiments. Evaluation is performed exclusively on the EPO test set using the same flat and hierarchy-aware metrics, label-frequency stratifications, and efficiency measurements. This design ensures that performance differences observed on EPO data can be attributed to model generalization properties rather than dataset-specific tuning or additional supervision.

2(c)
reports the distribution of the number of CPC subclasses per patent in both the training and test sets. The two splits exhibit very similar distributions, suggesting the absence of major label-density shifts between training and evaluation data. Further details on the descriptive statistics of the CPC labels in the test and training sets are provided in Appendix
5.1
.

The extreme skewness of the label distribution has important implications for model evaluation. In particular, performance metrics aggregated over all labels (e.g., micro-averaged F1) are dominated by a small set of frequent subclasses, while performance on rare and emerging categories contributes little to the aggregate score. This motivates the use of complementary evaluation perspectives and provides context for the comparison between supervised encoder-based models and large language models, which rely less directly on label frequency during inference.

Energy–Accuracy Trade-off

While classification performance provides one dimension of comparison, Table
3
reveals pronounced differences in computational cost across model families.
Encoder-based models operate in a distinctly low-cost regime, completing full inference runs in 1–2 minutes with an energy consumption of approximately 0.01 kWh.
In contrast, LLM-based approaches require between 30 and 280 minutes per run and consume 0.2–2.1 kWh, implying that encoders are roughly 15–250× faster and 20–200× more energy-efficient at inference time.

Zero-shot LLM configurations are often particularly unfavorable, especially for larger instruction-tuned models, as they combine relatively low classification performance with high computational cost.
For instance, models such as LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct require inference times on the order of hours per run while failing to outperform encoder baselines in Micro-F1, placing them in an inefficient region of the accuracy–energy trade-off space (Figure
4
).

Prompt engineering partially alleviates this imbalance.
Few-shot prompting and retrieval-augmented constraints improve classification performance and recall behavior at a moderate additional energy cost relative to zero-shot inference; however, even under the most efficient prompting regimes, LLMs remain one to two orders of magnitude more expensive than encoder-based models in both time and energy. LoRA fine-tuning offers only modest performance gains in this setting while introducing additional training overhead and leaving the inference cost gap largely unchanged. As a result, any accuracy improvements must be weighed against the associated energy costs, particularly in large-scale or production patent classification.
Overall, these results indicate that encoder-based models occupy a low-energy, high-throughput regime, whereas LLM-based approaches operate in a high-energy regime, achieving competitive performance only under carefully designed prompting and fine-tuning strategies.

10 000

These results confirm that the observed performance trade-offs are robust to domain shift and are not specific to a single patent office or dataset.

Discussion

Long-Tail Effects and Model-Induced Bias in Patent Classification

The results reveal that the choice of classification model systematically shapes which
technological categories become visible in CPC-based analyses, particularly in the
presence of strong label imbalance and hierarchical structure.

Encoder models such as BERT, SciBERT, and PatentSBERTa achieve strong performance in terms of micro-averaged precision and F1; they show, therefore, high effectiveness on high-frequency CPC subclasses that dominate the training data.
However, this advantage largely disappears when macro-averaged and hierarchical metrics are considered. Encoder-based models exhibit very low macro recall and F1, indicating severe difficulty in predicting infrequent subclasses. By contrast, LLMs—despite lower micro-F1 in zero-shot settings—display more balanced behavior across the CPC hierarchy. Few-shot prompting combined with lightweight retrieval augmentation substantially improves LLM performance. These configurations consistently narrow the performance gap with encoder models at the subclass level and, in some cases, outperform them at higher hierarchical levels. By contrast, parameter-efficient fine-tuning via methods such as LoRA yields limited and configuration-dependent benefits in this setting. Recent work suggests that extensive pre-training may reduce the downstream adaptability of large language models, making them harder to fine-tune effectively for specific tasks, as fine-tuning can improve task-specific metrics while degrading broader model capabilities or transfer behavior when the downstream task departs from the original pre-training objectives
(
44
;
45
)
. Overall, encoder-based models remain strong baselines for frequent-label prediction, while LLMs demonstrate greater robustness across the full label space. From a scientometric perspective, these results imply that model choice systematically shapes which technological categories become visible in downstream analyses, rather than merely affecting aggregate classification accuracy.

A central empirical finding of this study is that LLMs outperform encoder-based models in identifying rare CPC subclasses, which often correspond to early-stage or cross-domain technologies
(
53
)
. Patent classification data are strongly long-tailed, with a few subclasses (e.g., G06F, H04L) dominating label assignments while many appear only rarely. Supervised encoder-based models are therefore biased toward frequent subclasses and exhibit low recall on rare labels. In contrast, LLMs rely more on semantic understanding than label frequency, enabling them to assign plausible low-frequency subclasses even in zero-shot settings, with further gains under few-shot prompting. This capability is particularly relevant in the patent domain, where rare subclasses often signal emerging or interdisciplinary technologies, which prior research associates with higher impact and disruptiveness
(
59
;
56
)
. Although this comes at some cost to precision, the improved recall suggests that LLMs can play a complementary role to encoder-based systems focused on established technological domains.

To complement the quantitative results, we analyze a small set of representative classification outcomes (Table
4
). In patents assigned to frequent and well-represented subclasses such as
G06F
(electric digital data processing) or
G01R
(measuring electrical variables), encoder-based models like SciBERT tend to provide accurate and stable predictions, reflecting their strength on head classes with abundant training examples.

Conversely, for patents associated with rare and highly specific subclasses—such as
F42B
, which covers explosive charges and pyrotechnics—the LLM correctly identifies the subclass, while the encoder misclassifies the patent into more frequent but semantically adjacent categories (e.g., energy storage or generic technical collections). This behavior is consistent with the long-tail advantages observed in macro-averaged metrics. Finally, some patents remain challenging for both approaches, as illustrated by the metallurgy example, where both models fail to recover the correct material-focused subclasses (
C21D
,
C22C
) and instead default to overly generic or semantically distant technical categories. These examples confirm that the observed performance differences arise from systematic semantic biases rather than isolated errors, reinforcing the motivation for hybrid encoder–LLM classification pipelines.

Efficiency, Scalability, and Sustainability Constraints

From a computational perspective, encoder-based models are substantially more efficient than LLM-based approaches.
Full inference runs with encoder classifiers complete in approximately 1–2 minutes, whereas LLM inference—depending on model size and prompting configuration—requires between roughly 30 minutes and over 4 hours.
This corresponds to encoder-based systems being approximately
one to two orders of magnitude faster
than LLM-based approaches, with speedups ranging from about 15
\times
in the most favorable LLM settings to over 200
\times
in the most demanding configurations.

Energy consumption exhibits an even larger disparity.
Encoder inference requires on the order of
10^{-2}
kWh per run, while LLM inference consumes between approximately
0.2
kWh and more than
2
kWh, implying a factor difference of roughly 20
\times
to 200
\times
in energy use.
As a result, encoder-based models incur negligible inference-time energy costs and associated emissions, making them particularly well suited for large-scale, high-throughput patent classification pipelines.

These differences imply a fundamental trade-off between efficiency and modeling flexibility.
Encoder-based models are clearly preferable when scalability, latency, and cost efficiency are primary concerns.
LLMs, while significantly more expensive in both time and energy, offer greater semantic flexibility and improved coverage of rare or emerging subclasses. Therefore, their deployment seems to be most appropriate in selective or targeted settings rather than as a universal replacement for encoder-based systems.

Implications

The findings of this study have implications both for operational patent classification and
for downstream research in scientometrics and patentometrics. For intellectual property
professionals and patent offices, the results support a hybrid modeling strategy. Encoder-based
classifiers are well suited for routine, large-scale CPC assignment and for monitoring
well-established technological domains. In contrast, LLM-based systems appear particularly
valuable as decision-support tools, assisting examiners in identifying potentially relevant but
infrequent CPC subclasses and in exploring emerging or cross-domain inventions.

Beyond operational settings, these findings have broader implications for scientometrics and
patentometrics research. Because CPC-based classifications are foundational inputs to indicators
of technological diversity, novelty, and emergence, systematic differences in classifier
behavior translate directly into differences in measured innovation dynamics

(
15
)
. Biases in favour of frequent and well-established CPC subclasses have direct consequences for downstream analyses. Indicators based on subclass
frequencies, technological diversity, or novelty may be distorted if rare subclasses are
systematically missed or misclassified

(
30
;
25
)
. In this respect, our
findings align with recent calls for
responsible scientometrics and patentometrics
, which
emphasize transparency, methodological reflexivity, and awareness of model-induced biases in
quantitative innovation studies

(
47
;
46
;
48
;
49
)
.
The observed advantages of LLMs in capturing infrequent subclasses suggest that these models may
help mitigate long-tail biases, particularly in studies focused on technological emergence,
interdisciplinarity, or early-stage innovation dynamics.

At the same time, the substantially higher computational and energy costs of LLMs raise
important concerns from a responsible research perspective. Large-scale patentometrics studies
often involve millions of documents, making indiscriminate use of LLMs environmentally and
economically impractical. Our results therefore support a selective and purpose-driven adoption
of LLMs, for example as complementary tools to enrich analyses of specific technological niches
or to validate findings derived from encoder-based pipelines. These considerations echo recent
discussions on the role of large language models in research evaluation, which underline both
their potential to enrich analytical workflows and the risks of introducing systematic biases
related to field, publication context, and textual characteristics

(
47
;
46
;
48
;
7
)
.

Overall, this study calls for greater methodological pluralism in patent
classification and patent-based research. Rather than seeking a single “best” model,
researchers and practitioners should consider how model choice interacts with
research objectives, data imbalance, deployment constraints, and sustainability considerations.

Future Work

This study is methodological and comparative in nature, with the primary goal of systematically assessing the behavior of (pre-GenAI) encoder-based models and large language models for patent classification under realistic and controlled conditions. Several observations emerging from this analysis naturally point to promising directions for future research.

First, the comparative evidence presented in this paper indicates complementary strengths of encoder-based classifiers and LLMs across different regions of the CPC label space. In particular, the results suggest the potential usefulness of an innovative hybrid methodology that integrates encoder- and LLM-based approaches to leverage the efficiency and stability of encoders with the flexibility and the ability to focus on emerging subclasses of LLMs. While this insight is directly informed by our findings, the design, implementation, and empirical evaluation of such hybrid systems would be beyond the scope of the present study and are left for future work.

Second, by design choice, our analysis focuses on instruction-tuned LLMs in the 7–8B parameter range. This choice reflects a methodological commitment to fair and meaningful comparison under realistic deployment constraints. Future work may nonetheless explore the performance–cost trade-offs across a broader range of model sizes, considering also approaches based on model reduction such as quantization
(
14
)
.

Third, we discussed how fine-tuning the best-performing LLM did not consistently lead to performance improvements. While, as discussed, this behavior has already been observed in the literature, future work could consider other fine-tuning approaches, including aggressive full fine-tuning, not feasible with the computational resources available for this study.

Finally, while the retrieval-augmented prompting strategies explored in this work rely on official CPC subclass definitions, richer forms of retrieval and interaction may further improve practical usefulness. Promising extensions include adaptive retrieval over heterogeneous knowledge sources and human-in-the-loop evaluation to assess how LLM-generated suggestions are used by patent examiners in real workflows. In this broader context, the development of cost-aware hybrid systems that dynamically balance encoder predictions, LLM assistance, and human oversight represents an especially compelling direction for future research.

Conclusion

This study provides a systematic comparison between supervised encoder-based models and open-source LLMs for CPC multi-label patent classification, with explicit attention to performance across the CPC hierarchy, long-tailed label distributions, and computational sustainability. Rather than identifying a single best-performing approach, the results indicate a consistent trade-off between efficiency and semantic coverage, with direct consequences for how technological domains are represented in downstream analyses.
Encoder-based models remain highly effective for frequent CPC subclasses and large-scale, high-throughput classification tasks, offering strong micro-level performance at minimal computational and environmental cost. In contrast, LLMs—particularly when augmented with few-shot prompting and lightweight retrieval—exhibit improved recall on rare and infrequent subclasses and more balanced coverage across the CPC hierarchy, albeit at substantially higher resource requirements.

These findings have direct implications for both operational patent classification and scientometric analysis. For patent offices and IP professionals, they support hybrid workflows in which efficient encoders handle routine cases while LLMs are selectively deployed to assist with complex or long-tail classifications. For scientometrics and patentometrics research, the results carry important consequences: model choice is not merely a technical detail but a methodological decision. In fact, different classification paradigms can systematically bias the visibility of rare, interdisciplinary, or weakly institutionalised technologies, thereby affecting indicators of technological emergence and change.
Overall, this work shows how the responsible use of machine learning in patent analysis requires aligning methodological choices with analytical objectives, data imbalance, and sustainability constraints. Rather than seeking universal solutions, our results point toward selective, purpose-driven integration of LLMs as a means of mitigating long-tail blind spots while preserving scalability and transparency in large-scale innovation studies.

1
Supervised encoder models: implementation details

1.1
Model architecture and training

Each encoder model (BERT, SciBERT, PatentSBERTa) is fine-tuned for multi-label CPC classification
by attaching a linear classification head that outputs one logit per CPC subclass.
Logits are transformed into independent probabilities via a sigmoid function, and models are
trained using binary cross-entropy loss over the full label set.

All models are trained on the same training split and evaluated on a held-out test set.
Input sequences consist of the patent title concatenated with the abstract and are truncated
to a maximum length of 256 tokens. Training is performed using standard optimization settings,
and the final checkpoint is selected based on validation performance.

1.2
Threshold calibration and label decoding

For each encoder, prediction thresholds are calibrated on a separate development set to maximize
micro-averaged F
1
score.
At test time, a CPC subclass is predicted for a patent if its calibrated probability exceeds
the model-specific threshold.
To ensure comparability with LLM outputs, we additionally cap the number of predicted labels
per patent to at most seven; if no label exceeds the threshold, the highest-probability subclass
is selected. In fact, the number of CPC subclasses assigned per patent is highly sparse.
In the training set (
50 625
patents), the mean number of labels per patent is 1.98 and the
median is 2; 90% of patents have at most four labels, 95% have at most four labels, and
99% have at most seven labels.
Overall, 99.48% of training patents are annotated with between one and seven CPC subclasses,
with a maximum of 18.

The test set exhibits a similar distribution (mean: 2.32; median: 2; 99th percentile: 7),
with 99.21% of patents having between one and seven labels.
Based on this empirical distribution, we restrict model outputs to at most seven subclasses
per patent across all methods.
This constraint is applied uniformly to supervised encoders and LLM-based approaches and
serves to prevent degenerate over-prediction while remaining faithful to the annotation regime.

1.3
Constrained label-space evaluation

In addition to standard evaluation over the full CPC subclass space, we consider a constrained
decoding setting that mirrors label-space retrieval prompting used for LLMs.
For each patent, a per-instance candidate set of CPC subclasses is retrieved by embedding
official CPC subclass definitions with a bi-encoder (E5-base-v2) and ranking them by cosine
similarity to the patent text.

During constrained evaluation, encoder output probabilities for subclasses outside the
retrieved top-
K
set (
K=20
) are masked to zero prior to thresholding and decoding. The value
K = 20
controls only the size of the retrieved candidate label set; after thresholding, the number of predicted CPC subclasses is still capped at seven,
Importantly, this constraint is applied only at inference time and does not influence model
training or parameter estimation.

2
Prompting Strategies and Templates

We evaluate large language models under multiple prompting regimes of increasing informational content and constraint, reflecting common practical deployment scenarios. Specifically, we consider: (i) zero-shot prompting; (ii) few-shot prompting with in-context examples; (iii) constrained prompting based on an allowed CPC label set, implemented via retrieval from a dictionary of CPC definitions (retrieval-augmented prompting); and (iv) combinations of few-shot prompting with label-set constraints. Across all settings, prompts enforce a strict JSON output format to ensure deterministic parsing and consistent evaluation.

2.1
Prompt selection and calibration

Prior to the main experimental evaluation, we conducted a limited prompt calibration
phase on a small validation subset of the training data.
During this phase, we tested several semantically equivalent prompt variants differing
in wording, emphasis on recall versus precision, and degree of explicitness regarding
multi-label prediction.
This preliminary exploration was motivated by empirical observations that large
language models tend to be conservative in multi-label settings, often under-predicting
the number of relevant labels when instructions are underspecified.

Importantly, prompt selection was performed
once
per prompting regime (e.g.,
zero-shot, few-shot, constrained) and exclusively on held-out validation data.
The final prompt templates reported in this appendix correspond to the variants that
yielded the best overall performance in terms of micro-F1 and recall, while preserving
strict output formatting and comparability across models.
No prompt was tuned on the test set, and the same finalized prompts were used unchanged
for all models within each experimental condition.

3
Retrieval and RAG Implementation Details

This appendix documents the implementation details of our retrieval-augmented prompting settings used for CPC multi-label prediction.

3.1
Retrieval corpus: CPC subclass definitions

We construct a retrieval corpus from a dictionary of CPC subclass codes mapped to short textual definitions (denoted
cpc_labels
). Each retrieval item corresponds to one CPC subclass code (4-character level, e.g.,
G06F
) and its definition. Before embedding, we normalize codes to the 4-character subclass level by (i) uppercasing, (ii) removing any subgroup suffix after “/” if present, and (iii) truncating to four characters. Each retrieval item is embedded as a single text string of the form:

CODE: definition

This design ensures that retrieval is performed over the semantic content of the CPC definition while preserving explicit label identity.

3.2
Bi-encoder retrieval model and embeddings

We use a local E5 bi-encoder (
e5-base-v2
) to embed both queries and CPC-definition passages. Following the E5 convention, we apply explicit prefixes:

•

Query embedding:
patent text prefixed with
‘‘query: ’’
.

•

Passage embedding:
CPC definition text prefixed with
‘‘passage: ’’
.

The query text is the concatenation of the patent title and abstract (“
title. abstract
”), with whitespace normalization.
Tokenization uses truncation to a maximum length of 512 tokens. Embeddings are computed via mean pooling over the last hidden states using the attention mask, and then
\ell_{2}
-normalized. Because embeddings are normalized, cosine similarity is equivalent to the dot product.

We embed the CPC-definition corpus once (batch size 64) and reuse these vectors for all patents. For patent queries, we embed in batches (default batch size up to 16, capped by the number of items in a batch). All embedding computations run locally (offline), using GPU.

3.3
Top-
K
retrieval and allowed-label construction

For each patent, we compute cosine similarity between the query embedding and all CPC-definition embeddings and retrieve the top-
K
CPC subclasses, with
K=20
. The resulting list forms the
allowed label set
for that patent. This allowed set is injected into the LLM prompt and also used for strict post-processing: predicted labels not contained in the allowed set are discarded. We fix the size of the retrieved allowed CPC set to
K=20
across all models and experiments.
We considered alternative sizes of the retrieved allowed label set to assess feasibility.
With
K=10
, the retrieval stage frequently omitted relevant CPC subclasses, leading to
under-inclusive predictions. Conversely,
K=50
resulted in substantially longer prompts
that approached context-length limits and increased inference cost without qualitative
benefits. Based on these observations, we fixed
K=20
a priori and did not further tune it.

3.4
Prompt formatting for RAG settings

In RAG prompting, the allowed CPC subclasses are shown together with short definitions and grouped hierarchically to improve readability. Specifically, we group allowed subclasses by CPC
section
(first character) and
class
(first three characters), and list subclasses under each class. Each line is formatted as:

- CODE --- definition

To control prompt length, definitions are truncated to at most 220 characters.

Zero-shot + RAG.

In the zero-shot + RAG condition, the prompt contains only (i) the patent text and (ii) the retrieved allowed set with definitions. The model is instructed to select all relevant subclasses
from the allowed set
and to return a strict JSON object:

{"labels": ["G06F", "H04L"]}

We additionally instruct the model to prefer over-inclusion (high recall) and to output between 1 and 7 labels.

Few-shot + RAG.

In the few-shot + RAG condition, we use the same allowed-set retrieval as above and prepend few-shot examples to the prompt. Few-shot examples come from the training set and are formatted as
PATENT TEXT
followed by the corresponding gold label JSON. We include two types of few-shot examples:

•

Static examples:
fixed training row indices (two examples) used in every prompt.

•

Dynamic examples:
retrieved per test instance using TF–IDF cosine similarity over training texts (two examples), excluding the static IDs.

We experimented with alternative few-shot configurations (static-only and
dynamic-only retrieval), but observed reduced recall and higher variance across
runs; for this reason, these variants were not pursued further.

3.5
Few-shot retrieval for examples (TF–IDF)

For dynamic few-shot retrieval, we build a TF–IDF representation of training texts (title + abstract) using: maximum
50 000
features,
(1,2)
-gram range, and minimum document frequency of 2. For each test patent, we compute cosine similarity between its TF–IDF vector and all training vectors and select the top-2 most similar examples (excluding the static examples). Static examples are selected by fixed training indices
[2, 27]
to stabilize the prompt format across runs.

3.6
LLM inference settings

All LLMs are run locally (offline) using Hugging Face Transformers with
device_map="auto"
. We use bfloat16 on supported GPUs (otherwise float16). We generate deterministically with greedy decoding (
do_sample=False
) and
max_new_tokens=256
. Tokenization uses left padding and truncation to a maximum input length of 2048 tokens.

3.7
Output parsing, label normalization, and constraint enforcement

We enforce structured output by requiring strict JSON in the prompt. In post-processing, we parse the model output as follows:

1.

Attempt to parse the entire generation as JSON and read the
"labels"
list.

2.

If this fails, extract the first JSON-like substring using a regex and attempt JSON parsing again.

3.

Normalize each predicted code to the 4-character subclass level (uppercasing, removing subgroup suffix after “/”, truncating to four characters).

For RAG conditions, we enforce the allowed-label constraint by discarding any normalized label not present in the retrieved allowed set for that patent.

Non-empty fallback.

Because some LLMs may output invalid JSON or produce empty outputs, we optionally enforce non-empty predictions. If parsing yields no valid labels, we (i) search for CPC-like tokens in the raw text and keep the first token that is in the allowed set; otherwise (ii) fall back to predicting the top-1 retrieved allowed label. In all settings, we cap the final prediction to at most 7 labels per patent. We constrain the number of predicted CPC subclasses to be between 1 and 7.
This range reflects the typical sparsity of CPC subclass annotations in our dataset
and prevents degenerate outputs with excessively many labels, while still allowing
over-inclusion in line with our high-recall objective.

3.8
Evaluation outputs

For each model and setting, we compute micro and macro precision/recall/F
1
at the subclass level, together with Acc@1. We also compute hierarchical micro metrics at the section, class, and subclass levels by mapping predicted and gold labels to their corresponding hierarchical prefixes and computing micro precision/recall/F
1
. Additionally, we export per-label (section/class/subclass) tables including support, predicted positives, TP/FP/FN/TN, precision, recall, F
1
, and per-label accuracy.
For reproducibility and statistical analysis (e.g., bootstrap), we always save a
predictions.jsonl
file containing the patent text, gold labels, predicted labels, the full prompt, and retrieval metadata (including the retrieved allowed set and, in the few-shot setting, the IDs of retrieved training examples).

4
LoRA Fine-Tuning Details

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using parameter-efficient Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).
The model is trained with a causal language modeling objective, implemented as token-level cross-entropy loss over the target output sequence.
The task is formulated as structured text generation, where the model outputs CPC subclass labels in a JSON format.

LoRA adapters are applied while keeping all base model parameters frozen.
We use a rank of
r=64
, a scaling factor
\alpha=16
, and a dropout rate of
0.05
, with no bias terms.
Adapters are injected into both attention and feed-forward projection layers, including query, key, value, and output projections in the attention blocks, as well as the gate, up, and down projection layers in the MLP blocks.

Optimization is performed using AdamW with a peak learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
and a cosine learning rate schedule with a warmup ratio of
0.05
.
We apply weight decay of
0.01
.
Training is carried out for three epochs with a per-device batch size of 2 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps, resulting in an effective batch size of 16.
The maximum sequence length is set to 2048 tokens.
Training is conducted in mixed precision, using bfloat16 when supported by the hardware and float16 otherwise.

Model checkpoints are saved every 500 training steps, and validation is performed at the same interval.
All experiments are run on a single GPU without model quantization.
To monitor computational cost, training time and carbon emissions are tracked using the CodeCarbon framework.
All values correspond to standard choices commonly used in LoRA-based fine-tuning of large language models.

Overall, these paired tests reinforce the main takeaway: LLMs provide the clearest benefits on
