Case report – AML of left kidney with fatty embolus Involving the renal vein and IVC
Authors: Aran Muhunthan, Dale Wood, Jonathan Kam, Celi Varol, Zannatoon Nury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 53-year-old female was referred for radical nephrectomy on the background of known Angiomyolipoma (AML) of the left kidney. Serial CT scans demonstrated a 5.3cm fat-containing lesion in the left upper pole of her kidney and a fat density material within the left renal vein with level 1 inferior vena cava (IVC) involvement. Given the concern for pulmonary embolism an elective Robotically Assisted Radical Left Nephrectomy and IVC thrombectomy was performed. Intra-operative findings confirmed a mass on the left kidney and fatty thrombus within the IVC. Histopathological examination demonstrated 100% AML tumour and fatty thrombus with no evidence of malignancy.

Introduction

Renal angiomyolipoma (AML) is a benign mesenchymal neoplasm composed of thick-walled vessels, adipose tissue and smooth muscle. These tumours are often benign and account for 0.3-3% of all renal masses and usually follow an indolent path but can behave aggressively by most classically spontaneously haemorrhaging.1 Intravascular tumour extension is a relatively well recognised feature of renal cell carcinoma; however, AML only rarely extends into the renal vein and less frequently into the inferior vena cava (IVC). A PRISMA-guided literature search identified a total of 90 published cases of AML, of which 76 had venous extension up to January 2020 highlighting the rarity of this presentation.2

This case demonstrates a clinically important, rare example of a left renal AML with fatty tumour extending to IVC. Firstly, AML with venous extension can precipitate life-threatening complication. The ability to recognise radiologically can guide appropriate management. Furthermore, differentiating between AML with fat thrombus extension vs fat mimicking renal malignancies can alter surgical management (radical nephrectomy vs thrombectomy vs nephron sparing).

Hence, we report a case of renal AML with fatty thrombus extending into the IVC.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old female was referred by her general practitioner (GP) for urological evaluation, following concerns of development of fat density component within the renal hilum when compared to a previous CT study in 2015. Past medical history was notable for previous acute haemorrhage of left renal AML treated with embolization.

Cross-sectional imaging with CT chest, abdomen and pelvis showed residual fat density at the left renal hilum measuring 38x26 × 49mm with low attenuation filling defect seen in the left renal vein extending towards IVC and a 40mm linear projection of fat tissue into the left renal vein and IVC (Fig. 1). She subsequently had further imaging in the form of CT intravenous pyelogram (IVP) which showed Mayo level 1 IVC involvement of thrombus (Fig. 2) and an FDG PET which confirmed extension of fat density tissue 30mm into the renal vein and IVC as indicated on recent CT scans and excluded disease elsewhere. (Fig. 3). Minor measurement variability between modalities was noted, likely reflecting differences in imaging planes and reconstruction techniques. Given the risk of progression and potential pulmonary embolism, the patient underwent robotically assisted left radical nephrectomy with renal vein and IVC thrombectomy. da Vinci Xi Robotic Surgical System was used to perform this surgery. Thrombus was visualised by incising the left renal vein, exposing a long string of adipose tissue/thrombus. The thrombus was extracted from the IVC through the proximal renal vein and the surgery continued with radical nephrectomy without intra-operative complication.

Histopathological examination confirmed AML of the left kidney with entirely fat thrombus extending through renal vein and into the IVC without evidence of malignant transformation. The AML measured 54 x 42 × 23 mm and fat thrombus within the left renal vein (Fig. 4A, Fig. 4BA and B). She was followed up in the Urology clinic 1 month after her surgery from which she had recovered well with no post-operative complications. Her renal function had reduced from eGFR of 90 pre-operatively to an eGFR of 58 post operatively. Discussion

Renal angiomyolipoma (AML) with venous extension is an uncommon yet clinically significant diagnosis. Although AMLs are a benign mesenchymal tumour, their uncommon ability to extend intravascularly can not only cause life-threatening complications but also mimic the behaviour of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) which more commonly presents with tumour thrombus. This overlap in differentials can pose diagnostic uncertainty and often warrants radical surgical intervention to avoid an embolic event.

A systematic review by Abdessater et al. identified a total of 90 cases of AML with 76 containing venous extension up to January 2020, highlighting the rarity of this presentation and confirming the mainstay of treatment as radical nephrectomy with thrombectomy.2 Perdue et al. in their case report described a 5cm AML with IVC thrombus and pulmonary embolism which was managed successfully by a multidisciplinary team with radical nephrectomy and thrombectomy emphasising the acute and embolic risk posed by relatively small AML.3 Similarly, a case report by Bouaziz et al. reported a tumour extending from renal vein into the IVC and into the right atrium, with histology confirming 100% benign AML.4 This reinforces the notion that AML can be aggressive and present with varying degrees of severity which can dictate surgical planning and urgency. In addition, this suggests that malignant transformation of AML with intravascular extension is uncommon, however, surgical excision remains warranted given the diagnostic overlap of RCC and significant risk of embolization.

Historically, the mainstay of surgery was an open radical nephrectomy and thrombectomy. However robotic assisted surgery has emerged as a valuable approach in managing complex renal and caval procedures. Robotic surgery can offer surgeons improved dexterity, enhances precision and three-dimensional visualisations of anatomical structures which can prove advantageous when performing meticulous surgeries. A retrospective multi-institutional study by Abaza et al. compared the experience of open surgery versus robotic assisted radical nephrectomy with IVC thrombectomy and reported mean blood loss of 399 cc, mean operating time of 292 minutes and no surgery required conversion to open.5 Our case supports these findings and highlights that robotic assisted radical nephrectomy and thrombectomy is a feasible alternative to a more traditional open approach in selected patients with AML with venous involvement.

Furthermore, guideline evidence remains limited due to the rarity of AML with IVC involvement. The current literature recommends surveillance for small AMLs, but advises active management for symptomatic, large or high-risk tumours.6 Our case aligns with this approach, demonstrating that radical nephrectomy with thrombectomy as an appropriate and effective treatment especially in patients with AML with intravascular involvement.

Overall, this case adds to the limited body of literature on AML with IVC extension, highlighting the importance of recognising this rare but important clinical presentation. This case also reinforces the need for careful radiological evaluation, surgical planning and the developing role of robotically assisted surgery in the management of such cases in the future as a safe and feasible alternative to open surgery.

Conclusion

Renal AML with venous extension into the renal vein and IVC is an exceptionally rare phenomenon that as described can mimic malignant tumours both radiologically and clinically. Despite its benign histology, this case highlights the life-threatening complications that may arise from such a presentation. Ultimately highlighting the need for thorough and detailed radiological assessment and the utility of robotically assisted nephrectomy with thrombectomy as a safe and effective option for surgical treatment.
