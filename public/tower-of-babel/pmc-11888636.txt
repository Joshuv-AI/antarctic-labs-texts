Impact of Chest Compression Fraction on Outcomes in In-Hospital and Emergency Department Cardiac Arrests at a Tertiary Care Center in South India
Authors: K S Chanjal, R Haneendhar, M Senthil Kumar, Jinka Venkata Durgaprasad, B Sudhiersharan, Melvin Dominic, Panneerselvam Periasamy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The study aimed to determine the average chest compression fraction (CCF) and its correlation with influencing factors to optimize blood flow during resuscitation, aiming for a minimum of 60%.Materials and Methods:Prospective observational study on 50 cardiac arrest patients, analyzing chest compression fraction (CCF) and identifying associations using Spearman’s correlation coefficient and individual student t-tests.Results:The study revealed a satisfactory average Chest Compression Fraction (CCF) of 71.66 ± 7.79, with diabetes mellitus being the most prevalent medical condition, and no significant correlation between compression depth, rate, and recoil.Conclusion:The average cardiac arrest (CCF) in ED patients decreased with longer code durations, daytime events, defibrillator use, increased team members, male providers, and chest compressions.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiac arrest, a major cause of death and disability globally, is characterized by complete heart function stoppage, accounting for 10% of overall mortality in developing nations. The emergency medical team is working diligently to improve survival rates following cardiac arrests, a challenge that continues due to the global occurrence of nearly 6 million cases annually.[1]

The goal of treating cardiac arrest is to achieve Return of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC) through emergency response, CPR, defibrillation, advanced resuscitation, and post-arrest care, minimizing survival chances outside medical facilities.[2] To maximize the number of compressions given every minute, it is advised by the American Heart Association to minimize the intervals between chest compressions, emphasizing the importance of high-quality chest compressions.[3]

Chest Compression Fraction (CCF) is the percentage of time during CPR that chest compressions are performed, directly influencing cardiac arrest outcomes. CCF measures chest compressions during resuscitation, aiming to optimize blood circulation and coronary perfusion, with the American Heart Association recommending a minimum pace of 100–120 per minute.[4] Physicians may temporarily halt compressions during resuscitation for essential patient care tasks or unrelated factors like distraction or exhaustion. Interruptions can negatively impact blood flow to the brain and heart in animal models.[5]

Over time, the quality of chest compression by rescuers significantly decreases. CPR feedback devices have been introduced to enhance the quality of BLS compressions.[4] This study aims to identify the chest compression fraction (CCF) in cardiac arrest cases in emergency departments and examine factors affecting CCF, such as healthcare providers involved, CPR depth, rate, recoil, resuscitation duration, and diurnal variations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A study was conducted at Vinayaka Missions Kirupananda Variyar Medical College and Hospitals in Salem, over 18 months. The study focused on patients aged 18 or older who experienced cardiac arrest in the emergency department. The study was approved by the VMKV Medical College and Hospital in Salem’s institutional human ethics committee (VMKVMC&H/IEC/21/026 dated 30.01.2021). Participants provided informed written consent. Data were collected after approval. The study excluded those under 18, trauma-related patients, and pregnant females. Patients met specific criteria and were resuscitated using the American Heart Association’s BLS and ACLS protocols. A CPRmeter2Tm feedback device was used to measure inactivity between chest compressions.

RESULTS

A study involving 50 cardiac arrest patients from 2020 to 2022 found that diabetes mellitus was the most common comorbidity, followed by hypertension, dyslipidemia, and coronary heart disease. Totally, 76% had diabetes, 54% had hypertension, 50% had dyslipidemia, and 33% had coronary heart disease. Some patients had respiratory, chronic kidney, and cerebrovascular accidents, and 28% had a history of previous surgery. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrests were 88% and in-hospital arrests 12%.

DISCUSSION

The study involved cardiac arrest resuscitation in the ED with a feedback device, aiming for a CCF of 60%, with a good mean CCF of 71.66 ± 7.79. Idris et al.’s[6] study suggests that CCF significantly influences survival rates in out-of-hospital ventricular fibrillation patients, particularly those with shockable initial heart rhythms. Edelson et al.[7] found that prolonged compression intervals before the initial shock in patients with ventricular fibrillation (VF) were linked to unfavorable outcomes. In our study, we examined multiple factors that influence CCF and conducted a statistical analysis on them. The study reveals a negative correlation between total code duration and CCF, with most cases having a CCF over 70%, influenced by rescuer fatigue, confusion, and interruptions.

CONCLUSION

The study found that cardiac arrest patients’ CCF in the ED is within AHA recommendations, but decreased with longer codes, daytime use, defibrillator use, team size, male providers, and chest compressions.
