Incidentally Diagnosed Very Well-Differentiated Gastric Adenocarcinoma at Intraoperative Frozen Section
Authors: Takuya Nagasaka, Hirofumi Rokutan, Akiko Komatsu, Nobuo Kanazawa, Tomio Arai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma is a rare subtype that is often overlooked because of minimal cytological atypia and ill-defined gross findings. We report an 82-year-old man who was incidentally diagnosed with this entity in an intraoperative cut-end frozen section during gastrectomy for another adenocarcinoma. The frozen specimen showed irregularly fused glands with characteristic architecture, prompting additional resection to secure negative margins. Immunohistochemistry revealed loss of AT-rich interaction domain 1A expression exclusively in the neoplastic glands. This case highlights the importance of recognizing subtle architectural abnormalities in frozen sections to avoid overlooking clinically undetected gastric neoplasms.

INTRODUCTION

Very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma is a rare gastric cancer subtype with a distinctive morphology. Its neoplastic epithelia exhibit only subtle cytological atypia similar to that in nonneoplastic intestinal metaplasia. The glands branch irregularly, fuse,1–3 and form patterns resembling the letters W, H, Y, or X at low magnification.4 This entity is sometimes missed during endoscopy and is associated with higher rates of incomplete resection during endoscopic submucosal dissection, particularly concerning the horizontal margins,5 because the lesions present ill-defined borders endoscopically. Despite these notorious features, endoscopists and pathologists must pay close attention to this entity because a subset of cases transforms into diffuse-type advanced gastric cancer,6 which has a poor prognosis.

We describe a case of very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma incidentally found and accurately diagnosed in the intraoperative frozen section of a margin specimen during gastrectomy. We in addition confirmed abnormality in AT-rich interaction domain 1A (ARID1A), a protein involved in chromatin remodeling, in the final (permanent) pathology; this molecular finding further supported the neoplastic nature.

CASE REPORT

An 82-year-old man was rushed to our hospital for a fever. He was hospitalized for 15 days and treated with antibiotics for liver abscess and bacteremia caused by Streptococcus intermedius. During that period, thickening of the gastric wall was detected on computed tomography, and a tumor approximately 10 cm in the antrum was detected on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. A biopsy taken at that time revealed papillary/tubular adenocarcinoma.

Distal gastrectomy was performed. The resected tumor (main tumor) is the section delineated by the blue dashed line (Figure 1). Intraoperative frozen diagnosis was performed to evaluate the proximal margin. While the surgeons expected a negative result, atypical glands were incidentally noted in the frozen section. Morphologically, fused glands with shapes resembling the letters W, H, Y, or X (with only subtle cytological atypia; Figure 1) were noted in the mucosa. These atypical glands occupied approximately 20% (1.2 cm in length) of the mucosa in the frozen specimen. Two gastrointestinal pathologists discussed the case and reached a diagnosis of very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma based on frozen sections. These results were then communicated to the surgeons. Following this intraoperative diagnosis, an additional 2 cm of the proximal stomach was resected to obtain a negative margin.

After all resected specimens were assessed via permanent pathology, the diagnosis reached was synchronous double gastric carcinoma, noting the 1-cm distance between the 2 tumors. The tumor detected in the intraoperative frozen specimen of the proximal margin (flat reddish area surrounded by green arrowheads in Figure 1) was ultimately identified as a very well-differentiated adenocarcinoma, with the following final diagnosis: type 0–IIb, 18 × 12 mm, pT1aN0M0 (according to the 8th edition Union for International Cancer Control staging system), negative for lymphovascular invasion, and with negative margins. ARID1A immunohistochemistry was performed using primary antibody (clone EPR13501-73, Abcam, cat# ab182561; dilution 1:200) and Leica Bond autostainer system (Leica Biosystems). Nuclear ARID1A expression was scored as retained or lost. Lymphocytes served as internal positive controls, ensuring that negative staining in tumor cells was not due to technical artifacts. This immunohistochemistry revealed that the expression of ARID1A was lost in neoplastic cells (Figure 1). The final pathology of the resected major (larger) lesion confirmed papillary/tubular adenocarcinoma (Figure 2), staged as pT1bN0M0, with positive lymphatic invasion but negative margins. ARID1A expression was retained in this tumor (Figure 2).

Postoperatively, the patient developed anastomotic leakage and aspiration pneumonia, which improved with conservative treatment. He was discharged on postoperative day 48. Two years postoperatively, the patient remains recurrence-free.

DISCUSSION

Diagnosing very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma is often challenging, not only endoscopically but also pathologically, especially in biopsy specimens,2 which is no different from intraoperative frozen diagnosis. Very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinomas must not be overlooked, as they occasionally transform to diffuse-type advanced gastric cancer. In the present case, we correctly diagnosed a very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma by frozen section, despite subtle cytological atypia. This was mainly due to our knowledge of the entity, which can exhibit complex branching patterns—W-, H-, Y-, or X-shaped glands—and our accurate recognition of such structural atypia in the frozen section.

Intraoperative frozen-section assessment of resected specimens helps identify positive margins, allowing surgeons to resect additional tissue. In daily practice, pathologists should keep in mind that frozen section diagnosis can be more difficult than permanent specimens7 and requires more careful histological assessment due to interpretive and sampling errors. It is helpful to first observe the histological appearance of non-neoplastic and metaplastic mucosa in frozen specimens and then search for areas that differ from them. This approach, together with awareness of various neoplastic morphological patterns, enables detection of mild cytological atypia and identification of fused or irregularly branching glands. When findings are subtle, pathologists should examine additional levels or deeper sections without hesitation, to improve the accuracy of frozen section diagnosis.

This case offers another lesson about multiple concurrent gastric cancers. Older adults tend to have multiple gastric cancers.8,9 Thus, surgical margins should be carefully assessed in older adults, both in permanent and frozen sections, as the present case demonstrates. Furthermore, our case underscores the importance of performing an intraoperative frozen-section analysis of the cut end, even when it appears nonneoplastic macroscopically.

Our frozen diagnosis of the very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma was subsequently affirmed by the detection of loss of ARID1A expression (Figure 1) in the final, permanent specimen. Loss of ARID1A expression indicates ARID1A mutation, which occurs in approximately 11%–12% of very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinomas.6,10 Abnormal ARID1A expression has diagnostic value because, unlike other genetic abnormalities, such as RHOA mutations and CLDN18::ARHGAP fusions, which are also harbored in very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma,6,10 it can be detected by immunohistochemistry.

In summary, we encountered a rare case of very well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma, which had not been detected preoperatively but was identified during intraoperative frozen section. Accurate frozen-section diagnosis ensured negative margins. Final (permanent) pathology revealed loss of ARID1A expression, limited to the neoplastic glands. The morphological structure and degree of nuclear atypia is generally more difficult to assess in frozen than in permanent specimens. Thus, pathologists should be aware of this entity and exercise caution to avoid overlooking clinically undetected gastric neoplasms with minimal atypia, as illustrated in this case.
