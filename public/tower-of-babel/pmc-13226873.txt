Risk Factors for COVID-19 mRNA Vaccine-Associated Exacerbations in Patients With Obstructive Airway Diseases: REsearch for Asthma and COPD ExacerbaTION Caused by COVID-19 mRNA Vaccination (REACTION Study)
Authors: Tatsuro Suzuki, Yoshihiro Kanemitsu, Naoya Sugimoto, Katsunori Masaki, Taisuke Akamatsu, Hiroaki Tsurumaki, Suguru Majima, Keima Ito, Toshiyuki Iwata, Hideyasu Shimizu, Hisashi Wakayama, Yuki Amakusa, Yuta Koizumi, Yuta Mori, Kensuke Fukumitsu, Satoshi Fukuda, Takehiro Uemura, Tomoko Tajiri, Hirotsugu Ohkubo, Tetsuya Oguri, Takeshi Hisada, Toshihiro Shirai, Koichi Fukunaga, Hiroyuki Nagase, Akio Niimi

ABSTRACT
PurposeCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) mRNA vaccines have reduced the severity and mortality of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection. However, several reports of asthma exacerbations following COVID-19 mRNA vaccination have raised safety concerns for patients with obstructive airway diseases. This study aimed to evaluate the risk of mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations and identify associated clinical factors among patients with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).MethodsThis multicenter historical cohort study enrolled 455 patients (387 with asthma, including 30 with COPD overlap and 68 with COPD) from 13 Japanese institutions between September 2022 and September 2024. Demographic data, pulmonary function, biomarkers, and questionnaire responses were collected. Exacerbation was defined as worsening of respiratory symptoms occurring within 1 week after vaccination. Independent risk factors were identified by multivariate logistic regression.ResultsPatients with asthma had a higher COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation rate than those with COPD (14.5% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). Among patients with asthma, exacerbations were more frequent after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination than after influenza vaccination (14.5% vs. 1.8%, P < 0.001). Younger age, atopic predisposition, lower fractional exhaled nitric oxide levels (< 25 ppb), and poor asthma control (asthma control test < 20 and/or frequent exacerbations ≥ 2/year) were identified as independent risk factors (all P < 0.05). Most exacerbations were mild, with no severe outcomes.ConclusionsPoorly controlled atopic asthma in younger individuals was associated with increased risk of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation. Despite this, the overall benefits of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination support its continued use, with an emphasis on optimizing asthma control beforehand in high-risk patients.Trial RegistrationUMIN Clinical Trials Registry Identifier: UMIN000049011

INTRODUCTION

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the virus responsible for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), was first identified in China in late 2019.1 COVID-19 spread globally and was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization on March 11, 2020. Since then, the development of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines has reduced the infection rates, severity, and mortality.2 In Japan, 2 mRNA vaccines were initially approved, namely, Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2), approved on February 14, 2021, and Moderna COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (mRNA-1273), approved on May 21, 2021. These mRNA vaccines provided effective immunity against SARS-CoV-2 and reduced the morbidity and mortality rates.34 However, many serious adverse events have been reported after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, including anaphylaxis, myocarditis, and thrombosis.56

Asthma is characterized by airway hyperresponsiveness and reversible obstruction. Th2- or non Th2-driven immune responses play a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is mainly caused by long-term exposure to tobacco smoke, resulting in peripheral airway damage and alveolar destruction with airflow limitation. Although the pathophysiology of asthma and COPD differs, both are prone to recurrent acute exacerbations, often triggered by viral or bacterial infections.7 Recent evidence indicates that SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with an increased risk of new-onset asthma.89 The effect of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination on asthma is controversial: some reports suggested that it may exert a protective effect against these exacerbations and reduce this risk,9 whereas others reported that COVID-19 mRNA vaccination exacerbates asthma.101112 There have been few reported cases of COPD exacerbations associated with COVID-19 mRNA vaccines. Meanwhile, influenza vaccination has not been reported to increase the risk of asthma or COPD exacerbations.1314 However, there are no direct studies on the risk of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in patients with asthma and COPD.

Based on previous case reports and the pathophysiology of allergic asthma, the following hypotheses were tested: (1) Patients with asthma, particularly those with atopic asthma, would have higher rates of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations compared to patients with COPD, driven by enhanced Th2-mediated immune responses. The fundamental differences in immune profiles between asthma and COPD provide a valuable basis for evaluating the role of allergic mechanisms in mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations; and (2) Among patients with asthma, younger age, atopic predisposition, and poor asthma control would be independent risk factors for mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations due to heightened immune reactivity and persistent airway inflammation.151617 These results are expected to help ensure the safety of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination for patients with obstructive airway diseases, and highlight the risk factors of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in those patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This historical cohort study was based on data collected from medical records and patient-reported questionnaires. Patients with asthma and/or COPD were recruited from 13 institutions that participated in the REACTION study in Japan from September 2022 to September 2024. The patients were eligible for this study if they (1) were aged between 20 and 85 years, (2) had a confirmed diagnosis of asthma and/or COPD, (3) had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (either BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273), and (4) provided written informed consent to participate.

The diagnoses of asthma, COPD, and asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) were made according to the respective Japanese guidelines for each condition.181920 Patients with other significant respiratory diseases, such as lung cancer or interstitial lung disease, were excluded.

This study was approved by the Nagoya City University Ethics Committee (Approval No. 60-22-0055) and by the ethics committees of all participating hospitals. It was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000049011).

Measurements

During enrollment, patient demographic and clinical data were collected from their medical records. All participants underwent blood testing, pulmonary function testing, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) measurement, as per the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society and Japanese Respiratory Society guidelines. Blood eosinophil and neutrophil counts, total immunoglobulin E (IgE), and specific IgE titers against common aeroallergens were measured; patients were considered atopic if any specific IgE level was ≥ 0.35 UA/mL. The ImmunoCAP system (H.U. Frontier Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure specific IgE titers across all 13 participating facilities. However, the list of specific IgE items measured was determined by each facility because this study focused on identifying the presence of atopic predisposition rather than the influence of specific allergens.

Disease control and cough-related quality of life (QoL) were evaluated using validated questionnaires: the Asthma Control Test (ACT), COPD Assessment Test (CAT), and Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ). All patients with asthma completed the ACT, all patients with COPD completed the CAT, and all participants completed the LCQ. Poor asthma control was defined as ACT < 20.21 A CAT score ≥ 10 was considered indicative of symptomatic disease with potential impact on daily functioning.22 A self-administered questionnaire captured clinical history, treatment status, and vaccine-associated respiratory symptoms. Patients were specifically asked to recall any episode of worsening dyspnea, wheezing, cough, or sputum production within 1 week after each COVID-19 mRNA or influenza vaccination. Vaccine-associated exacerbation was defined as the onset or worsening of respiratory symptoms within 1 week after vaccination, as reported in the questionnaire. Exacerbations were classified as mild (self-reported symptom worsening) or severe (requiring unscheduled visits, systemic corticosteroids, or hospitalization). Although no universally accepted timeframe exists for defining vaccine-associated asthma exacerbations, a large randomized trial evaluated exacerbation risk within 14 days of influenza vaccination.14 To minimize recall bias inherent to the retrospective design, a 7-day window was adopted. According to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance and cases reported by Ando et al.10 and Tachibana et al.,12 most systemic reactions begin within 1–3 days after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination and resolve within the same period. These findings support the clinical plausibility of using a 7-day window to capture early-onset exacerbations.

Vaccination

Vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 and influenza virus that are available in Japan were as follows: (1) Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine (BNT162b2) uses mRNA technology to introduce genetic information encoding the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein into the body, thereby inducing an immune response. Lipid nanoparticles containing polyethylene glycol (PEG) are used to stabilize the mRNA, and polysorbate 80 is included as an excipient.

(2) Moderna vaccine (mRNA-1273) is also an mRNA-based vaccine against SARS-CoV-2 that incorporates PEG and polysorbate 80.

Although the AstraZeneca viral vector vaccine was approved, its use was very limited and restricted to specific age groups. Other vaccine, such as the Janssen vaccine, were not also practically used in the public program.

(3) The influenza vaccines used in Japan are inactivated vaccines produced by various pharmaceutical companies. Inactivated vaccines are those in which the virus is chemically treated to inactivate it, typically inducing an immune response against the hemagglutinin and neuraminidase proteins present on the surface of the influenza virus. Vaccines from various manufacturers differ slightly, with some products containing excipients such as polysorbate 80. PEG is not utilized in influenza vaccines. In this study, we did not consider any differences in influenza vaccines produced by different companies.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was to determine whether COVID-19 mRNA vaccination increases the risk of exacerbations in patients with asthma compared to those with COPD. Secondary endpoints included (1) comparing exacerbation rates between COVID-19 mRNA and influenza vaccines in patients with asthma and (2) identifying clinical risk factors for COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in asthma. Patients were classified into asthma (including ACO) and COPD groups for comparison. A similar analysis was performed for influenza vaccination. Patients with asthma were further stratified by the presence or absence of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine–associated exacerbations to explore associated risk factors. Known triggers of asthma exacerbations include allergen exposure, inflammation triggered by infection, and poor asthma control (ACT < 20).151617 Case reports also suggest that younger age and atopic predisposition may contribute to mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation risk.101112 Additionally, obesity and severe asthma, known risk factors for severe COVID-19,23 may influence vaccine uptake behavior.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (SPSS Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and compared using the t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on normality. Categorical variables were presented as number (%) and analyzed using chi χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Non-normally distributed data were log-transformed before analysis. Missing data were treated as a blank. FeNO was missing for 3 patients, all of whom had COPD. In addition, age at asthma onset was missing for 4 patients, age at COPD onset for 5 patients, and family history of asthma for one patient.

A priori sample size estimation was performed to ensure adequate power for multivariate logistic regression. Based on previous reports of influenza vaccine–associated asthma exacerbations (12.7%–28.8%),14 a 20% exacerbation rate for COVID-19 mRNA vaccination was assumed. For logistic regression analysis, a minimum of 10 outcome events per variable was considered necessary.24 With 6 independent variables included in the final model (2 confounders and 4 covariates), at least 60 events were required. Given an assumed exacerbation rate of 20%, a total sample size of at least 300 asthma patients was deemed necessary to achieve an adequate number of exacerbation events. To mitigate recall bias in symptom reporting, exacerbations associated with COVID-19 mRNA and influenza vaccinations were compared within the same individuals. Differential exacerbation rates were assessed using the 2-sided exact McNemar test for discordant pairs. No formal sample size calculation was performed for the COPD group owing to its limited sample size and zero event rate. The exact 95% binomial confidence interval for the COPD exacerbation rate was calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. To evaluate whether patients with asthma were more likely than those with COPD to experience mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations, propensity-based sensitivity analyses were performed. Covariate adjustment was conducted using nearest-neighbor matching (caliper = 0.2 SD of the logit) and inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) % predicted, and atopic predisposition.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics

This study enrolled 455 patients, including 387 with asthma (30 with ACO) and 68 with COPD. Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients at enrollment. Asthma patients were younger than COPD patients (P < 0.001), and the disease duration was comparable between the groups. The proportions of women, never-smokers, and patients with atopic predisposition and history of childhood asthma or family history of asthma as well as BMI were higher in the asthma than in the COPD group. Th2-related comorbidities and mental disorders were more prevalent in the asthma group. This group also had higher Th2 biomarkers, except for FeNO, and predicted FEV1% (P < 0.001).

Regarding disease severity, 75 asthma patients (19.4%) and 11 COPD patients (16.2%) were classified as GINA Step 5 and Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stages 3–4, respectively. In the asthma group, 103 patients (26.6%) had an ACT score of less than 20, whereas in the COPD group, 35 patients (51.5%) had a CAT score of 10 or higher. Thus, our cohort included the patients with severe uncontrolled asthma and COPD. The proportion of patients who experienced 2 or more exacerbations within 1 year prior to enrollment was higher in the asthma than in the COPD group (12.9% vs. 4.4%, P = 0.044). However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of the history of hospitalization or systemic corticosteroid use within 1 year prior to enrollment.

The proportion of patients who received 3 or more doses of the COVID-19 mRNA vaccine was higher in the COPD than in the asthma group (100% vs. 92.5%, P = 0.012). Vaccine brand and regimen distribution were comparable between patients with asthma and COPD (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). No participants received COVID-19 non-mRNA vaccination during study period. This finding suggests that brand exposure was balanced across disease groups. Similarly, the influenza vaccination rates did not significantly differ between the groups.

Comparison of vaccine-associated exacerbation rates

The vaccine-associated exacerbation rate is presented in Fig. 2. The asthma group had significantly higher respiratory exacerbation rates within 1 week after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination than the COPD group (14.5% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). Because no exacerbation events occurred in the COPD group, the exact 95% binomial confidence interval was calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method, indicating that the true event rate in COPD could be as high as 5.3%. Propensity-based sensitivity analyses were conducted to adjust for major demographic and physiological differences between asthma and COPD (n = 56 per group). Although matching was performed, residual imbalance in age and atopic predisposition remained due to inherent disease characteristics; therefore, IPW was also applied. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccine–associated exacerbation rate remained significantly higher in the asthma group than in the COPD group (10.7% vs. 0%, Fisher’s exact P = 0.027), and IPW-adjusted logistic regression confirmed a significant association (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 8.6, 95% CI, 1.7–43.7, P = 0.009). To further reduce recall bias and control for interindividual variability, vaccine-associated exacerbations were then examined within the same individuals who received both COVID-19 mRNA and influenza vaccines (n = 387). Among these patients, 56 (14.5%) experienced exacerbations after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination and 7 (1.8%) after influenza vaccination: 6 had both events, 1 had influenza-only events, and 50 had COVID-19 mRNA-only events. A 2-sided exact McNemar test of discordant pairs (50 vs. 1) demonstrated a matched OR of 50.0 (P = 4.6 × 10−14).

Asthma characteristics

We compared clinical characteristics between patients with and without asthma exacerbations (Table 2). Compared with the patients in the non-exacerbated group, those in the exacerbated group had significantly younger age, higher BMI, earlier asthma diagnosis, lower prevalence of comorbid COPD, and higher prevalence of childhood asthma history and atopic predisposition (all P < 0.05). The exacerbated group had more severe asthma and received more intensive treatment, had worse asthma control and more impaired cough-specific QoL, and experienced more frequent asthma exacerbations, systemic corticosteroid treatments, and hospitalizations within the year prior to enrollment. Furthermore, this group exhibited lower blood eosinophil counts (P < 0.001), higher blood neutrophil counts (P = 0.029), lower FeNO levels (P < 0.001), and higher FEV1 (P = 0.012).

Risk factors: COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations

Multivariate logistic regression analyses identified younger age (models 1 and 2 OR, 0.97), lower FeNO levels (< 25 ppb) (model 1 OR, 2.16; model 2 OR, 2.65), atopic predisposition (model 1 OR, 2.89; model 2 OR, 2.65), and poor asthma control (model 1: ACT < 20 points, OR, 5.46; model 2: frequent exacerbations ≥ 2/year, OR, 4.54) as independent predictors of mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations (all P < 0.05, Table 3). To further confirm the robustness of these findings, a supplementary analysis was conducted in which both ACT < 20 and ≥ 2 exacerbations/year were included simultaneously (Supplementary Table S2). The results were consistent with the main models, supporting the validity and stability of our conclusions.

DISCUSSION

In this REACTION study, the rate of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations was higher in asthma patients than in COPD patients. In asthma patients, the exacerbation rate was higher after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination than after influenza vaccination. Notably, this study identified atopic predisposition as an independent risk factor for COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated asthma exacerbations, in addition to younger age and poor asthma control, highlighting a novel aspect of our findings.

These findings are consistent with previous reports documenting asthma exacerbations following COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, as well as with studies indicating a higher frequency of adverse reactions, including anaphylaxis, after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination compared to influenza vaccination.5101112 Furthermore, they may align with reports suggesting that the risk of anaphylaxis is higher among patients with severe asthma, those who are younger to middle-aged, and individuals with allergic rhinitis.525

Asthma patients are more prone to allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, than COPD patients, and particularly, those with atopic asthma exhibit a persistently elevated Th2 immune response, increasing their susceptibility to excessive immune activation triggered by external stimuli.2526 In addition, atopic asthma patients frequently experience both immediate and late-phase asthma reactions after allergen exposure, characterized by an initial onset of respiratory symptoms soon after exposure and a recurrence of similar symptoms hours later.27 These factors suggest that immune hyperactivation and allergic responses induced by COVID-19 mRNA vaccines have contributed to asthma exacerbations. Furthermore, the prevalence of atopic asthma is higher in younger populations, who generally exhibit stronger innate and adaptive immune responses, increasing their susceptibility to pronounced adverse reactions following COVID-19 mRNA vaccination.2829 This may reflect the natural decline in serum IgE levels and allergic sensitization with increasing age.30 Asthma control status is another critical determinant of allergic reaction risk as poorly controlled asthma causes persistent airway inflammation, leading to increased sensitivity to immune activation and other stimuli.3132 Collectively, these findings indicate that young individuals with poorly controlled atopic asthma may be particularly vulnerable to exacerbations after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination due to an enhanced immune response and increased allergic reactivity.

In our cohort, patients with baseline airflow limitation (FEV1% < 80%) experienced fewer mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations, whereas poor symptom control (ACT < 20) was the dominant predictor. This pattern aligns with previous studies demonstrating discordance between spirometric impairment and symptom-based control, particularly among elderly and female patients.33 These findings suggest that symptom burden may be a more relevant modifier of mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation risk than lung function.

Interestingly, mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations were also more prevalent among patients with low FeNO levels. This paradoxical finding indicates that susceptibility to exacerbation is not confined to individuals with overtly Th2-high profiles. Some patients with low biomarker levels may represent steroid-suppressed Th2 inflammation rather than a true Th2-low endotype. In our cohort, patients who experienced mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations were more likely to receive oral corticosteroids or biologic therapy, suggesting a more severe and treatment-modified disease population. Similar observations have been reported in the UK Severe Asthma Registry, in which patients classified as Th2-low had higher BMI, psychological comorbidities, and frequent oral corticosteroid use patterns consistent with steroid-induced biomarker suppression or underlying neutrophilic inflammation rather than a true Th2-low endotype.34 Moreover, the International Severe Asthma Registry framework has shown that allergic sensitization or atopic predisposition alone does not define Th2-high asthma, and sensitized patients may still present with low Th2 biomarkers.35 Thus, the observed association between low FeNO levels and mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations may reflect, at least in part, a steroid-suppressed, treatment-refractory Th2-high phenotype in which persistent airway inflammation and immune dysregulation continue despite intensive therapy.

Previous studies have demonstrated that both COVID-19 infection and mRNA vaccination can enhance eosinophilic inflammation,3637 suggesting that eosinophilic activation may contribute to post-vaccination events. The lower baseline eosinophil counts in the mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation group likely reflect therapy-induced suppression rather than a true absence of Th2 inflammation. Supporting this interpretation, McDowell et al.38 reported that even patients classified as Th2-low at baseline can exhibit Th2-high inflammatory features during exacerbations, indicating that airway inflammatory phenotypes are variable and context-dependent. These observations imply that eosinophilic inflammation cannot be excluded as a contributor to mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations. A single time-point biomarker measurement may not fully capture the dynamic immune processes underlying these events. Although COPD is traditionally regarded as a neutrophil-dominant disease, eosinophilic inflammation occurs in a subset of patients, particularly during exacerbations. Airway biopsy studies have shown up to a 30-fold increase in eosinophil counts during acute COPD exacerbations compared to the stable phase.39 Furthermore, a systematic review of 21 studies demonstrated that blood eosinophil counts ≥ 300/μL are associated with an increased risk of COPD exacerbations.40 However, in the present cohort, only 11 (16.2%) patients with COPD met this eosinophilic threshold, which may partly explain the absence of mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in this group. The low prevalence of eosinophilic phenotypes and the small COPD sample likely reduced the statistical power to detect mRNA vaccine-associated events. Future prospective studies incorporating serial biomarker measurements before and after mRNA vaccination will be essential for determining whether transient eosinophilic or Th2-mediated activation occurs and how these processes contribute to mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in patients with obstructive airway disease.

Although the mechanisms underlying COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated asthma exacerbations remain unclear, the use of mRNA vaccines—as opposed to conventional inactivated vaccines—may be a contributing factor. The mRNA vaccines are known to stimulate the innate immune system by activating endosomal Toll-like receptors and cytosolic retinoic acid-inducible gene I, resulting in the production of type I interferons (IFNs), including IFN-α.41 Viral infections, which enhance type I IFN responses in airway epithelial cells, are known to worsen asthma symptoms, suggesting that mRNA vaccines may trigger similar immune pathways.42 Moreover, the Th1-biased immune response elicited by mRNA vaccines may interact with the Th2-skewed immune environment of atopic asthma, potentially leading to dysregulated Th1/Th2 crosstalk and exacerbated airway inflammation.43

Another hypothesis is that allergic reactions triggered by mRNA vaccines may also contribute to asthma exacerbation. PEG, an excipient in mRNA vaccines, is known to act as an allergen and may contribute to airway inflammation.44 However, polysorbate 80—an additive found in both COVID-19 mRNA and influenza vaccines—exhibits cross-reactivity with PEG and has been associated with hypersensitivity reactions.45 The observed differences in exacerbation rates between COVID-19 mRNA and influenza vaccines suggest that immediate allergic reactions to PEG alone may not fully explain mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations.

Fragments of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, translated from mRNA vaccines, have also been proposed as potential allergens.46 Cases of asthma worsening several days after mRNA vaccination may reflect delayed allergic responses to these spike protein fragments.101112

Taken together, our findings suggest that younger individuals with inadequately controlled atopic asthma may be at increased risk of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation due to excessive immune activation and allergic responses. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated asthma exacerbations.

This study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective design and reliance on patient self-report for symptom assessment represent major limitations. Although questionnaires were completed within 3 months of vaccination during routine clinical visits, recall bias cannot be fully excluded. Second, defining mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations within a 1-week window may not capture delayed-onset events. As with most retrospective observational studies, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding must also be acknowledged. The temporal link between vaccination and symptom worsening does not establish causality, and some reported exacerbations may simply reflect the natural variability of poorly controlled asthma. Third, as an observational study without an unvaccinated control group, causality cannot be established, and the absence of a comparator limits our ability to isolate the direct effects of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination. Fourth, the baseline differences between asthma and COPD patients may have introduced confounding. In this study, the asthma patients were generally younger, had a higher prevalence of atopy, and exhibited higher BMI than the COPD patients, all of which could affect vaccine responses. Although we hypothesized that COPD patients would be less susceptible to mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations due to the relative absence of Th2-mediated allergic responses, systemic vaccine effects such as fever could still provoke COPD exacerbations through mechanisms like increased respiratory rate and dynamic hyperinflation. The lack of mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations observed in the COPD group in this study may therefore reflect the small sample size (n = 68) and the shorter observation period. Fifth, the study included a high proportion of severe asthma patients (GINA Step 5), which means that the mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbation rates may be lower in the general asthma population. Sixth, the lack of precise data on prior SARS-CoV-2 infections and their timing relative to COVID-19 mRNA vaccination prevents full assessment of their impact on immune responses and exacerbation risk. Given that prior SARS-CoV-2 infections can influence mRNA vaccine responses, exploring their potential influence on mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations represents an important direction for future research. Seventh, although the number and brand of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273) were documented, the questionnaire captured only whether respiratory symptoms worsened within 1 week of any vaccine dose. Consequently, dose-specific or brand-specific associations could not be evaluated. Repeated exposure to mRNA vaccine components, such as PEG, polysorbate 80, or translated spike protein fragments, could theoretically enhance hypersensitivity or immune-priming responses in susceptible individuals. Similarly, manufacturer information for influenza vaccines was unavailable, precluding excipient-level analyses. Future studies should collect dose-level and brand-specific data to better elucidate exposure-related mechanisms. Lastly, sensitization to specific aeroallergens may influence asthma exacerbation following COVID-19 mRNA vaccination. However, we did not evaluate the role of specific allergens including house dust mites or the number of sensitized allergens in these exacerbations. In addition, no participants received non-mRNA COVID-19 vaccination including viral vector vaccines in this study. Therefore, their potential influence remains unclear. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate these relationships. Despite these limitations, the study offers important insights into COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations in patients with uncontrolled asthma.

Despite the identified risk of asthma exacerbations after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, most cases were mild, with no serious outcomes. This is consistent with prior evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines in asthma patients.34 Emerging data also indicate that severe COVID-19 infection is associated with unfavorable long-term asthma trajectories, including increased exacerbation frequency and higher mortality risk.474849 Furthermore, individuals with asthma appear to have a heightened risk of cardiovascular complications, such as ischemic heart disease and stroke, after COVID-19 infection.125051 COVID-19 mRNA vaccination may mitigate these adverse outcomes, and recent findings suggest that vaccination may also reduce cardiovascular and respiratory events in patients who experience reinfection.4852 Given that severe asthma is associated with worse COVID-19 outcomes, COVID-19 mRNA vaccination remains recommended for patients with asthma.53

Our current findings suggest that COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated exacerbations can be prevented and managed by maintaining good asthma control prior to vaccination. As mRNA vaccines are expected to expand beyond COVID-19 to target other pathogens and diseases in the future, addressing potential allergic reactions and airway disease exacerbations becomes increasingly important. Further research should focus on elucidating the immunological mechanisms underlying COVID-19 mRNA vaccine-associated asthma exacerbations.

In summary, COVID-19 mRNA vaccination may be associated with an increased risk of exacerbations in patients with poorly controlled atopic asthma, though most exacerbations were mild and manageable. Given the overall benefits of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, our findings support continued immunization while emphasizing the importance of achieving good asthma control prior to vaccination in high-risk individuals. Nevertheless, the observational design of this study limits causal interpretation. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings and ensure mRNA vaccine safety across diverse patient populations with obstructive airway diseases.
