Abstract
. We call a norm on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} intuitive if for every points p 1 , … , p m p_{1},\ldots,p_{m} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , one of the geometric medians of the points over the norm is in their convex hull. We characterize all intuitive norms.



1.
Introduction

The starting point of our work is the intuitive statement
that the geometric median of a set of points
is in their convex hull.
The geometric median of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is the minimizer of the convex function

\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni\xi\mapsto\sum_{i\in[m]}\|\xi-p_{i}\|_{2};

it is unique when the points are not collinear (see e.g.
[
3
]
and references within).
The geometric median is also known as the Fermat-Weber point,
the Torricelli point, Haldane’s median, and by several other names.
It is important in statistics because its breakdown point in
50\%
;
i.e., we need to change at least half of the mass to send the geometric median to infinity
[
2
]
.
It is important in facility location because it captures minimizing
the cost of transportation.
Consequently,
it has a very rich history
and there are many related algorithmic results
(see e.g.
[
1
]
and the long list of references within).

The geometric median makes sense over general norms.
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body
(i.e., compact with non-empty interior
so that
K=-K
), and
denote by
\|\cdot\|_{K}
the norm defined by
K
:

\|\xi\|_{K}=\inf\{t>0:\xi\in tK\}.

We also allow weights for the points.
For a finite set of points
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
denote by
\Delta_{P}
the collection of probability distributions on
P
.
The geometric median
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)
of
W
over
K

is the set of minimizers of the convex function

F_{K,W}(\xi)=\sum_{p\in P}W(p)\|\xi-p\|_{K}.

The geometric median is always non-empty and convex.
If
K
is strictly convex and the points in
P
are not collinear then
it is a unique point
[
3
]
.

It is intuitively obvious that
the geometric median is in the convex hull
\mathsf{conv}(P)

because norms are convex.
Let us say that
K
is
intuitive
if
there always exists a geometric median is in the convex hull of the data points;
for every
W\in\Delta_{P}
,

\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap\mathsf{conv}(P)\neq\emptyset.

Let us start by describing some intuitive norms.

Observation
.

All centered
\ell_{2}
balls are intuitive.

Proof.

If
\xi
is outside
\mathsf{conv}(P)

then there is a separating hyperplane with a normal
\ell_{2}
unit vector
u

so that
\langle u,\xi-p\rangle>0
for all
p\in P
.
For every
p\in P
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|\xi-tu-p\|_{2}=\sqrt{\|\xi-p\|_{2}^{2}-2t\langle u,\xi-p\rangle+t^{2}}.

Now, if
t>0
is small enough then
the point
\xi-tu

has a smaller
F_{\ell_{2},W}
value than
\xi
.
∎

We can obtain more intuitive norms, because
being intuitive is invariant under linear transformations.

Observation
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is intuitive and
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

is an invertible linear map,
then
TK
is intuitive.

Proof.

For every
\xi
,

\displaystyle F_{TK,W}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in P}W(p)\|\xi-p\|_{TK}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in P}W(p)\|T^{-1}(\xi-p)\|_{K}

\displaystyle=F_{K,W^{\prime}}(T^{-1}\xi),

for
P^{\prime}=T^{-1}P
and
W^{\prime}=W\circ T
.
Because
K
is intuitive,
there is a minimizer
x
of
F_{K,W^{\prime}}
in
\mathsf{conv}(P^{\prime})
.
The point
\xi=Tx
is a minimizer of
F_{TK,W}

and
\xi\in\mathsf{conv}(P)
.
∎

Because linear maps of balls are ellipsoids,
we can conclude that all Euclidean norms are intuitive
(a.k.a. finite dimensional Hilbert spaces).

Corollary
.

All centered ellipsoids are intuitive.

The first theorem we prove is that in the plane
all norms are intuitive.

Theorem 1
.

Every symmetric convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is intuitive.

What about higher dimensions?
Our main result is that in higher dimensions,
the only intuitive norms are Euclidean.

Theorem 2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body for
n\geq 3
.
Then,

K
is intuitive
\iff

K
is an ellipsoid.

Theorem
2
shows, in particular, that polytopes are never intuitive (for
n\geq 3
).
More generally, that if a norm is intuitive then it must be strictly convex
and smooth.

It is also natural to consider a stronger definition
where instead of “there is a geometric median in the convex hull”
we require “every geometric median is in the convex hull”.
Instead of
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap\mathsf{conv}(P)\neq\emptyset
,
we require
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\subseteq\mathsf{conv}(P)
.
This stronger definition never holds when the boundary of
K

has a flat part even just in the case that
P
comprises two points.
In particular, this stronger definition never holds for polytopes.
The reason is that if the boundary of
K
contains a flat part
F

then for every
p
in the interior of
F
,
the geometric median of
P=\{0,p\}
contains an open set
but
\mathsf{conv}(P)
is one-dimensional.
For example, in the plane the geometric median of
(0,1)

and
(1,0)
over the
\ell_{1}
norm is the entire unit square.
Because ellipsoids are strictly convex, however,
Theorem
2
shows that in dimensions at least three,
the two definitions are equivalent.

We can reduce the case
n>3
in Theorem
2
to the case
n=3
as follows.
A section of
K
is a set of the form
K\cap H

for a linear space
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The dimension of the section is the dimension of
H
.
We think of the section as a subset of the Euclidean space
H
.

Observation
.

If
K
is intuitive then every section of
K
is intuitive.

Proof.

Assume
K
is intuitive.
If
H
is a subspace and
\xi\in H
then

\|\xi\|_{K\cap H}=\|\xi\|_{K}
.
So, if
P\subset H
and
W\in\Delta_{P}

then

\emptyset\neq\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap\mathsf{conv}(P)=\mathsf{GM}_{K\cap H}(W)\cap\mathsf{conv}(P).\qed

There are many known characterizations of ellipsoids
(see e.g.
[
5
,
7
]
).
The following well-known lemma provides one such characterization
(a concrete reference is Theorem (3.1) in
[
5
]
).
In Section
4
we mention a second characterization
that we shall use.
The lemma can, e.g., be proved by
Jordan and von Neumann’s result that

K
is an ellipsoid iff the parallelogram law

\|x+y\|_{K}^{2}+\|x-y\|_{K}^{2}=\|x\|_{K}^{2}+\|y\|_{K}^{2}
hold for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
which is a two-dimensional condition.

Lemma
.

A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an ellipsoid iff
every
3
-dimensional section of
K
is an ellipsoid.

The observation and the lemma imply that in order to prove
Theorem
2
it suffices to prove the following.

Theorem 3
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

is intuitive then
K
is an ellipsoid.

Remark
.

The proof of the theorem works for a more general class
of norms than “intuitive norms”.
It works for all symmetric convex bodies
K

so that for all sets
P
of three points and
W\in\Delta_{P}
,
we have that
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap A_{P}\neq\emptyset
,
where
A_{P}
is the affine span of
P
.
All convex bodies that satisfy this condition are ellipsoids.

2.
Sub-gradients

To argue about general norms, we need the language of sub-gradients.
In this section, we recall what are sub-gradients and describe some of their basic properties.
The sub-gradient of
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\partial f(\xi)=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ f(y)\geq f(\xi)+\langle g,y-\xi\rangle\}.

If
f
is convex then
\partial f(\xi)
is non-empty and convex for all
\xi
.
If
f
is also differentiable at
\xi
then the sub-gradient is unique
(and is equal to the gradient).
The sum property says that if
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are convex,
then for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\partial(f_{1}+f_{2})(\xi)=\partial f_{1}(\xi)+\partial f_{2}(\xi),

where
A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}
is Minkowski sum.
For
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we use the notation

F_{K,p}(\xi)=\|\xi-p\|_{K}.

When
K
is clear from the context,
we omit the subscript
K
from
\|\cdot\|_{K}
,
from
F_{K,W}
and from
F_{K,p}

Claim 4
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body.
For all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\partial F_{0}(p)=-\partial F_{p}(0).

Proof.

\displaystyle g\in\partial F_{0}(p)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle g,y-p\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle-g,y+p\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y-p\|\geq\|0-p\|+\langle-g,y-0\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle-g\in\partial F_{p}(0).\qed

Claim 5
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body.
For all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\partial F_{0}(p)=-\partial F_{0}(-p).

Proof.

\displaystyle g\in\partial F_{0}(p)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle g,y-p\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle g,-y-p\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|-p\|+\langle-g,y-(-p)\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle-g\in\partial F_{0}(-p).\qed

Claim 6
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body.
For all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
c>0
,

\partial F_{0}(cp)=\partial F_{0}(p).

Proof.

\displaystyle g\in\partial F_{0}(cp)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|cp\|+\langle g,y-cp\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|cy\|\geq\|cp\|+\langle g,cy-cp\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle g,y-p\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle g\in\partial F_{0}(p).\qed

The boundary of
K
is denoted by
\partial K
;
we have
\partial K=\{p:\|p\|_{K}=1\}
.
For
p\in\partial K
,
a tangent to
K
at
p
is a supporting hyperplane
H
to
K

so that
p\in H
.

Claim 7
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body, and
let
p\in\partial K
.
Every
g\in\partial F_{0}(p)
is a normal to a tangent to
K
at
p
.

Proof.

The hyperplane

\{y:\langle g,y-p\rangle=0\}
contains
p
,
and the vector
g
is normal to it.
Every
y
of norm less than one
satisfies
1>\|y\|\geq\|p\|+\langle g,y-p\rangle
with
\|p\|=1
so

\langle g,y-p\rangle<0
.
∎

3.
The plane

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix a norm
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{K}
on the plane.
Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be finite and
W\in\Delta_{P}
.
Assume towards a contradiction that
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap\mathsf{conv}(P)=\emptyset
.
The sets
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)
and
\mathsf{conv}(P)
are convex
so we can use the convex separation theorem.
We can assume, by translation,
that there is a line
\ell
passing through

0\in\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)
and separating
\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)
and
\mathsf{conv}(P)
.
By rotation, we can assume that
\ell
is the
e_{1}
-axis and that
P

is strictly above the
e_{1}
-axis.
Let
x_{*}
is the intersection point of the
e_{1}
-axis and
\partial K
;
see an illustration in Figure
1
.

e_{1}
e_{2}

•

Figure 1.
A two-dimensional symmetric convex body
K
.
The points
P
are in the upper half plane.
The two marked
points are
x_{*},-x_{*}
. The dotted tangent line is in direction
u
.

First order optimality implies that
0\in\partial F_{W}(0)
.
By the sub-gradient sum property
and by Claim
4
,
we can deduce that for each
p\in P
,
there is
g_{p}\in\partial F_{0}(p)=-\partial F_{p}(0)
so that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}W(p)g_{p}=0.

If we delete
x_{*},-x_{*}
from
\partial K
, then
\partial K

is broken into two connected components.
Let
Q\subset\partial K
be the set of points of the form
\frac{p}{\|p\|}
for
p\in P
.
All points in
Q
belong to the same connected component,
which we denote by
\Gamma
and
is homeomorphic to a line segment.
Let
u
be a direction of a tangent
to
K
at
x_{*}
.
Direct
u
so that its inner product with
e_{2}
is positive
(it cannot be zero because
K
is symmetric and
has non-empty interior).

We claim that

\langle g_{z},u\rangle\geq 0
for all
z\in\Gamma
and
g_{z}\in\partial F_{0}(z)
.
Indeed, let
z\in\Gamma
.
The intersection of
K
and
the line parallel to
u
through
z
contains an open set.
So, for some
\varepsilon>0
we have
z-\varepsilon u\in K
.
As in the proof of Claim
7
,
the body
K
is contained in

\{y:\langle g_{p},y-z\rangle\leq 0\}
.
Therefore,
\langle g_{p},z-\varepsilon u-z\rangle\leq 0

which implies
\langle g_{p},u\rangle\geq 0.

By Claim
6
,
it follows that for all
p\in P
, we have

\langle g_{p},u\rangle\geq 0
.
Equation (
3.1
) now implies that
for all
p\in P
,

\langle g_{p},u\rangle=0.

Let
Z
be the set of
z\in\partial K
so that there is

g_{z}\in\partial F_{0}(z)
so that
\langle g_{z},u\rangle=0
.
The set
Z
is symmetric, closed and consists of two parts
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}=-Z_{1}
.
The set
Z_{1}
can be a point or an interval that is parallel to
u
.
If
Q\subset Z_{1}
, then all vectors in
\{g_{p}:p\in P\}
point in the same direction
so they cannot sum to zero,
which is a contradiction to (
3.1
).
A similar argument holds for
Z_{2}
.
Because
Q\subset Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}
,
it follows that both
Q\cap Z_{1}
and
Q\cap Z_{2}
are non-empty.
Because
Q
is contained in the open upper-half plane,
each of
Z_{1},Z_{2}
has a non-empty intersection with the open upper-half plane.
By symmetry, there is a non-zero intersection also with the open lower-half plane.
It follows that the point
\varepsilon u
for some small
\varepsilon>0

satisfies
\|\varepsilon u-p\|=\|0-p\|_{K}
for all
p\in P
.
Now,
\varepsilon u\in\mathsf{GM}(W)
, which is again a contradiction.
∎

4.
Three dimensions

We describe the proof of Theorem
3
in two stages.
We first consider the smooth case, and only then the general case.
The reason is that the smooth case already contains
the heart of the proof, but the general case involves several
technical complications.
In both cases, we rely on a characterization of ellipsoids by Montejano and Morales
[
4
]
,
which in turn relies on a theorem of Blaschke.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the two-dimensional sphere.
For
u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we denote by
u^{\perp}

the two-dimensional space in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is orthogonal to
u
.
For a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
H=u^{\perp}
,
we think of
H
as
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and of
F_{K\cap H,0}
as a map
H\to\mathbb{R}
.
For
\ell\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
denote by
\mathcal{T}(K,\ell)
the union of all lines
that are tangent to
K
and parallel to
\ell
.
The shadow boundary in direction
\ell
is
\mathcal{S}(K,\ell)=\mathcal{T}(K,\ell)\cap K
.
For a subspace
H
, the boundary of
H\cap K
is denoted by

\partial(H\cap K)\subset H
.

Proposition 8

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a symmetric convex body.
If for every
\ell\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
there is
u=u_{\ell}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
so that
\partial(H\cap K)\subset\mathcal{S}(K,u)

with
H=\ell^{\perp}

then
K
is an ellipsoid.

The following lemma and proposition
are the main steps in the proof of the theorem.

Lemma 9
.

Let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body.
Let
x,y\in\partial L
be so that there are
g_{x}\in\partial F_{0}(x)

and
g_{y}\in\partial F_{0}(y)
that span
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For all
z\in\partial L
,
there is
W\in\Delta_{P}
for
P\subset\{\pm x,\pm y,z\}

so that
W(z)>0
and

0\in\mathsf{GM}_{L}(W).

Proof.

Let
g_{z}\in\partial F_{0}(z)

and write

g_{z}=a_{x}g_{x}+a_{y}g_{y}

with
a_{x},a_{y}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By Claim
5
, we have
-g_{x}\in\partial F_{0}(-x)
and similarly for
y
.
There is therefore
P\subset\{\pm x,\pm y,z\}
of size at most three
and
W\in\Delta_{P}
so that
W(z)>0
and

\sum_{p\in P}W(p)g_{p}=0
.
By Claim
4
, we have

-g_{p}\in\partial F_{p}(0)
.
By the sub-gradient sum property,

\partial F_{W}(0)=\sum_{p\in P}W(p)\partial F_{p}(0)
.
It follows that

0\in\partial F_{W}(0)
and so
0
is a minimizer of
F_{W}
.
∎

Proposition 10
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be intuitive.
Let
\ell\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
let
H=\ell^{\perp}
and
L=K\cap H
.
Assume that
there are
x,y\in\partial L
so that
F_{K,0}

is differentiable at
x,y
and there are
g_{x}\in\partial F_{L,0}(x)

and
g_{y}\in\partial F_{L,0}(y)
that span
H
.
Then,
there is
u=u_{\ell}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
so that

\partial(L)\subset\mathcal{S}(K,u)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
9
with
H
as
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
for all
z\in\partial L
,
there is
W\in\Delta_{P}
for
P\subset\{\pm x,\pm y,z\}

so that
W(z)>0
and

0\in\mathsf{GM}_{L}(W).

Because
K
is intuitive, there is
\xi\in\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W)\cap H
.
It follows that

F_{K,W}(0)=F_{L,W}(0)\leq F_{L,W}(\xi)=F_{K,W}(\xi)

and in particular

0\in\mathsf{GM}_{K}(W).

By first order optimality in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

and the sub-gradient sum property,

0=W(z)G_{z}+\sum_{p\neq z}W(p)G_{p}

where
G_{p}\in\partial F_{K,0}(p)=-\partial F_{K,p}(0)
for
p\in P
.
Because
F_{K,0}
is differentiable,
the sub-gradients
G_{p}
for
p\in\{\pm x,\pm y\}
are fixed (do not depend on
z
).
The two vectors
G_{x},G_{y}
span a vector space
V=V_{\ell}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be orthogonal to
V
.
Because
G_{z}\in V=u^{\perp}
, we know that
u\in(G_{z})^{\perp}

so
z\in\mathcal{S}(K,u)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
smooth case.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be intuitive
and assume that
F_{K,0}
is differentiable.
We are going to prove that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
there is
u=u_{\ell}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
so that

\partial(K\cap H)\subset\mathcal{S}(K,u)
where
H=\ell^{\perp}
.
Once we prove this, Proposition
8
completes the proof.

Fix
\ell
and
H=\ell^{\perp}
.
The set
L=K\cap H
is a convex body in
H
and so
there are
x,y\in\partial L
so that there are
g_{x}\in\partial F_{L,0}(x)

and
g_{y}\in\partial F_{L,0}(y)

H
.

In the proof for the smooth case,
we located points
x,y
where
F_{K,0}
is differentiable
(when we used Proposition
10
).
The reason is that we needed the sub-gradients
G_{x},G_{y}

to not depend on
z
so that the space
V

is “the same for all
z
”.
When
K
is smooth, this is automatic,
but in general we need the following claim.

Claim 11
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a symmetric convex body.
Then, there is a dense set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
so that
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
, there are
x,y\in\partial(H\cap K)
with
H=u^{\perp}

so that
F_{K,0}
is differentiable at
x,y
,
and there are
g_{x}\in\partial F_{K\cap H,0}(x)

and
g_{y}\in\partial F_{K\cap H,0}(y)
that span
H
.

Proof.

Denote by
D
the set of points that
F_{K,0}
is differentiable at.
By homogeneity of norms, if
x\in D
then
rx\in D
for all
r>0
.
If
B
is a centered Euclidean ball of radius one,
then the measure of
B\setminus D
is zero (see e.g.
[
6
]
).
Let
\sigma
be the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
By integration in polar coordinates,

0=\int_{[0,1]}\int_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}r^{2}1_{ru\not\in D}d\sigma dr=\int_{[0,1]}r^{2}\int_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}1_{u\not\in D}d\sigma dr=\frac{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus D)}{3},

and so
\sigma(D\cap\mathbb{S}^{2})=1
