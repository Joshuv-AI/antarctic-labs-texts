Quench dynamics via recursion method and dynamical quantum phase transitions
Ilya Shirokov, Viacheslav Khrushchev, Filipp Uskov, Ivan Dudinets, Igor Ermakov, Oleg Lychkovskiy

Abstract
The recursion method provides a powerful framework for studying quantum many-body dynamics in the Lanczos basis recursively constructed within the Krylov space of operators. Recently, it has been demonstrated that the recursion method, when supplemented by the universal operator growth hypothesis, can effectively compute autocorrelation functions and transport coefficients at infinite temperature. We extend the scope of the recursion method to far-from-equilibrium quench dynamics. We apply the method to spin systems in one, two, and three spatial dimensions. In one dimension we find an evidence that the convergence of the method is limited in time by the dynamical quantum phase transition.



Introduction.

Addressing quantum dynamics by means of the recursion method has a long history

Mori 1965
;
Dupuis 1967
;
Haydock 1980
;
Mattis 1981
;
Joslin and Gray 1986
;
Roldan et al. 1986
;
Viswanath and Müller 2008
;
Lindner and Auerbach 2010
;
Nandy et al. 2024
. The (Heisenberg version of the) method essentially amounts to constructing an orthonormal Lanczos basis within the Krylov space of an evolving quantum observable and harnessing the simplifications this basis brings about. A new impact has been given to the method by the universal operator growth hypothesis
Parker et al. 2019
– a conjecture about a generic form of the evolution generator in the Lanczos basis.

Up to now the practical application of the recursion method to quantum many-body systems has been limited almost exclusively to computing infinite-temperature autocorrelation functions and transport coefficients
Parker et al. 2019
;
Khait et al. 2016
;
de Souza et al. 2020
;
Yates et al. 2020a
;
Yates et al. 2020b
;
Yuan et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Bartsch et al. 2024
;
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
Teretenkov et al. 2024
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2024
;
Füllgraf et al. 2025
. There the method has demonstrated high efficiency. This efficiency is particularly notable in application to two-dimensional systems
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
Teretenkov et al. 2024
;
Bhattacharyya et al. 2024
, where traditional numerical methods often struggle. Another notable advantage of the recursion method is that it allows a straightforward symbolic implementation, when a single computation covers all parameters of the Hamiltonian
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
Teretenkov et al. 2024
.

Formally, the output of the recursion method is the Heisenberg operator of an observable. This suggests that the scope of the method can be much broader than infinite-temperature correlation functions and should cover, in particular, the out-of-equilibrium dynamics after quantum quenches. Addressing the later in systems of dimension higher than one is known to be highly challenging, with limited body of results obtained to date
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Dziarmaga 2022
;
Bernier and Agarwal 2025
;
Begušić and Chan 2025
;
Loizeau et al. 2025a
;
Loizeau et al. 2025b
. Given the success of the recursion method in computing two-dimensional autocorrelation function, it seems tempting to apply it to quench dynamics.

Here we undertake exactly this attempt. The paper is organized as follows. We start from briefly introducing the recursion method and specifying it for addressing quench dynamics. Then we apply it to a paradigmatic one-dimensional mixed-field Ising model. The results are benchmarked by essentially exact numerical simulations. We demonstrate that the recursion method has a convergence domain limited in time by the dynamical quantum phase transition. Then results for the transverse-field Ising model on the square and cubic lattices are presented. We conclude by discussing implications of our results and open questions raised.

Recursion method for quench dynamics.
Consider an observable given by a self-adjoint Shrödinger operator
A
. The same observable in the Heisenberg representation reads
A_{t}=e^{itH}A\,e^{-itH}
, where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system. The Heisenberg operator
A_{t}
evolves within the
Krylov space
spanned by operators
A,{\mathcal{L}}A,{\mathcal{L}}^{2}A,\dots
, where
{\mathcal{L}}\equiv[H,\bullet]
is the
superoperator
referred to as Liouvillian.
A_{t}
satisfies the Heisenberg equation of motion
\partial_{t}A_{t}=i\,{\mathcal{L}}A_{t}
. Within the recursion method
Viswanath and Müller 2008
,
this equation is addressed in the orthonormal Lanczos basis
\{O^{0},O^{1},O^{2},\dots\}
constructed as follows:
O^{0}=A/\|A\|
,
A^{1}={\mathcal{L}}O^{0}
,
b_{1}=\|A^{1}\|
,
O^{1}=b_{1}^{-1}\,A^{1}
and

A^{n}={\cal L}\,O^{n-1}-\,b_{n-1}O^{n-2}
,
b_{n}=\|A^{n}\|
,
O^{n}=b_{n}^{-1}A^{n}
for
n\geq 2
. Here a scalar product in the space of operators is introduced according to
\big(A|B\big)\equiv{\cal N}\,\tr\big(A^{\dagger}B\big)
, where
{\cal N}
is a normalization constant. For systems of
N
spins
1/2
and translation-invariant observables considered here it is convenient to choose
{\cal N}=N^{-1}\,2^{-N}
. The scalar product entails the norm
\|A\|=\sqrt{(A|A)}
.
The coefficients
b_{n}
are referred to as
Lanczos coefficients
. In the Lanczos basis the Liouvillian has a tridiagonal form,

{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{1}&0&0&\dots\\[3.00003pt]
b_{1}&0&b_{2}&0&\dots\\[3.00003pt]
0&b_{2}&0&b_{3}&\dots\\[3.00003pt]
0&0&b_{3}&0&\dots\\[3.00003pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

The solution of the Heisenberg equation for the observable
A_{t}
can be expressed as

A_{t}=\|A\|\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi^{n}_{t}\,O^{n},

(2)

where
\phi^{n}_{t}\equiv(O^{n}|A_{t})/\|A\|
are the solution of the system of coupled equations, which can be written in the matrix form as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}=

\displaystyle i\,{\mathcal{L}}\phi_{t}.

(3)

Here
\phi_{t}=\|\phi^{n}_{t}\|_{n=0,1,2,\dots}
is a column vector and
{\mathcal{L}}
is the matrix (
1
).

In practice, one can explicitly compute only a finite number
n_{\rm max}
of Lanczos coefficients. The rest of the coefficients should be extrapolated. The universal operator growth hypothesis
Parker et al. 2019
(see also precursor work
Liu and Müller 1990
;
Florencio et al. 1992
;
Zobov and Lundin 2006
;
Elsayed et al. 2014
;
Bouch 2015
) states that in a generic quantum many-body system Lanczos coefficients
b_{n}
scale asymptotically linearly with
n
at large
n
(with a logarithmic correction for one-dimensional systems). This asymptotic behavior has been confirmed in various many-body models
Cao 2021
;
Noh 2021
;
Heveling et al. 2022
;
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
De et al. 2024
;
Ermakov 2025
. As a rule, the leading asymptotics can be easily extracted from the first dozen of Lanczos coefficients. However, extrapolating Lanczos coefficients by a linear function is a too rough approximation: the qualitative behavior of the solutions of the Heisenberg equation strongly depends on the subleading asymptotic terms
Viswanath et al. 1994
;
Parker et al. 2019
;
Yates et al. 2020a
;
Yates et al. 2020b
;
Dymarsky and Smolkin 2021
;
Bhattacharjee et al. 2022
;
Avdoshkin et al. 2024
;
Camargo et al. 2023
;
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
Dodelson 2025
;
Gamayun et al. 2025
. Guided by the results of ref.
Gamayun et al. 2025
, we employ the following extrapolation formula:

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\alpha\,n}{\log n}+\gamma+\frac{(-1)^{n}\alpha}{\log n+c}

\displaystyle{\rm for}~~1{\rm D},

(4)

\displaystyle b_{n}

\displaystyle\simeq\alpha\,n+\gamma+\frac{(-1)^{n}\alpha}{\log n+c}

\displaystyle{\rm for}~~2,3{\rm D}.

(5)

The last alternating term is required whenever
\varphi^{0}_{t}
relaxes to a nonzero value at
t\rightarrow\infty

Gamayun et al. 2025
, which is generally the case. Parameters
\alpha,\,\gamma
and
c
are found by fitting
n_{\rm max}
explicitly computed Lanczos coefficients.

The knowledge of the Heisenberg operator
A_{t}
of the observable allows one to address the quench dynamics, i.e. compute the time-dependent value
\langle A_{t}\rangle\equiv\tr\rho_{0}A_{t}
of the observable that evolves after the system is initialized in an out-of-equilibrium state
\rho_{0}
. Within our approach, we compute
\langle A_{t}\rangle
according to

\langle A_{t}\rangle\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{n_{\rm max}}\phi^{n}_{t}\,\langle O^{n}\rangle,

(6)

where
\langle O^{n}\rangle\equiv\tr\rho_{0}O^{n}
. We compute
O^{n}
and
b_{n}
for
n
up to
n_{\rm max}
symbolically directly in the thermodynamic limit, as described in
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
.
\langle O^{n}\rangle
is also computed symbolically. As for functions
\phi^{n}_{t}
, they are obtained by numerically solving coupled linear differential equations (
3
) with the tridiagonal matrix
{\mathcal{L}}
truncated at some large
k
(we use
k=1000
in the examples below). The entries of this matrix for
n>n_{\rm max}
are obtained from extrapolation of the
n_{\rm max}
known Lanczos coefficients according to extrapolation formulae (
4
), (
5
).

It should be emphasized that the sequence
\langle O^{n}\rangle
generally does not have any apparent structure and is not expected to have any particular asymptotic behavior, in contrast to the sequence of Lanczos coefficients
b_{n}
. We further discuss this fact and illustrate it by examples in the Supplement
sup
.

Note that
\phi^{0}_{t}=({\cal N}/\|A\|)\,\tr A\,A_{t}
is the infinite-temperature autocorrelation function of the observable
A
, up to a constant factor. Recently the recursion method supplemented by the UOGH has proven effective in computing such autocorrelation functions
Parker et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Uskov and Lychkovskiy 2024
;
Teretenkov et al. 2024
. The out-of-equilibrium post-quench expectation value
\langle A_{t}\rangle
is, however, a more complex object. Below we explore it in specific models.

Quench in one dimension.
We focus on a paradigmatic nonintegrable one-dimensional spin-
1/2
chain – the mixed-field Ising model. Its Hamiltonian reads

H=\sum\nolimits_{j}\sigma^{x}_{j}\sigma^{x}_{j+1}+h_{z}\sum\nolimits_{j}\sigma^{z}_{j}+h_{x}\sum\nolimits_{j}\sigma^{x}_{j},

(7)

where
\sigma^{x,y,z}_{j}
are Pauli matrices at the
j

h_{x}

h_{z}
are two parameters of the Hamiltonian.
This model is integrable when
h_{x}=0

h_{z}=0

The observable we consider here and throughout the paper is the magnetization in the

A=\sum\nolimits_{j}\sigma^{z}_{j}.

(8)

|{\bf p}|\leq 1

|{\bf p}|=1

Quench in higher dimensions.
In two and three dimensions we consider the transverse-field Ising model on the square and cubic lattices, respectively. The Hamiltonian of the transverse-field Ising model can be written as

H=\sum_{\langle{\mathbf{i}}{\mathbf{j}}\rangle}\sigma^{x}_{{\mathbf{i}}}\sigma^{x}_{{\mathbf{j}}}+h_{z}\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}}\sigma^{z}_{{\mathbf{j}}},

(10)

where
{\mathbf{i}}
and
{\mathbf{j}}
enumerate lattice sites and the first sum runs over pairs of neighbouring sites. The observable is the same as in the previous case – the total magnetization in the
z
-direction,
A=\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}}\sigma^{z}_{{\mathbf{j}}}
.

We compute
23
and
12
nested commutators, respectively. Time evolution of the magnetization is presented in Fig.
3
(b),(c). The results of the recursion method are compared to the outcomes of the sparse Pauli dynamics method
Begušić and Chan 2025
;
Beg
.

One can see that the agreement is good initially but abruptly worsens at some threshold time. We are not able to decisively resolve the origin of this discrepancy at the moment. Of course, the DQPT is again the main suspect. DQPT is known to occur in the 2D Ising model, although with a softer logarithmic singularity
Heyl 2018
. However, we are not able to compute the critical time for this DQPT for two- and three-dimensional models.

Conclusions and discussion.
In this work, we have explored the applicability of the recursion method to computing the time evolution of observables relaxing from out-of-equilibrium initial states. We have applied the method to quantum spin systems in one, two, and three dimensions.

In one dimension, benchmarking is straightforward due to the availability of essentially exact numerical simulations. By comparing the recursion method to these simulations, we have observed that it yields accurate results up to a certain threshold time, beyond which it abruptly breaks down.

The breakdown indicates that the expansion (
6
) of a Heisenberg observable in the Lanczos basis has a finite convergence domain, at least for one-dimensional systems. This fact may limit the scope of concepts and techniques based on this expansion
Parker et al. 2019
;
Chu et al. 2024
;
Loizeau et al. 2025c
.

Notably, the breakdown time turns out to closely coincide with the critical time of a dynamical quantum phase transition (DQPT), suggesting that DQPT fundamentally limits the applicability of the recursion method, at least in one dimension. The underlying mechanism linking DQPT to the breakdown of the recursion method remains entirely unknown and represents a pressing open question. The potential subtlety of this mechanism is highlighted by our observation that the breakdown time coincides not with the first, but with the second (in time) dynamical critical point, implying that not every DQPT necessarily disrupts the recursion method.

In contrast to the one-dimensional case, benchmark data in two and three dimensions is scarce. We have compared our results to those obtained from the sparse Pauli dynamics method
Begušić and Chan 2025
;
Beg
and found that the two approaches diverge abruptly after a certain threshold time, similar to what is observed in one dimension. However, due to the lack of available data on DQPTs in the higher-dimensional models we considered, we cannot unambiguously attribute this divergence to DQPTs. Determining whether DQPTs fundamentally constrain the recursion method’s convergence in higher dimensions remains an important open question.

Finally, in one dimension, we have observed that at long times, the recursion method’s outcome coincides with the time-averaged value of the observable, which, in thermalizing systems, is equal to the equilibrium value. If this observation proves to be correct and generic, it would imply an unusual scenario: the recursion method remains valid at both short and long times but fails at intermediate times. Investigating this phenomenon further presents another promising direction for future research.
