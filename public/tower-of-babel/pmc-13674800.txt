Cardiac arrest as the first sign of thyrotoxicosis: a case report of angiographically proven coronary vasospasm

Introduction

Cardiac manifestations of thyrotoxicosis are common but typically mild, including tachyarrhythmias, exertional dyspnoea, and high-output physiology. Coronary artery spasm (CAS) is a rare but increasingly recognized complication of thyrotoxicosis. Although it usually presents as angina or transient ischaemic electrocardiographic changes, malignant ventricular arrhythmias can occur. Cardiac arrest caused by thyrotoxicosis-induced CAS is exceedingly uncommon, with only a few angiographically documented cases reported.

Awareness of this clinical entity is crucial because the diagnostic and therapeutic approaches differ markedly from atherosclerotic acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Prompt vasodilator therapy and correction of thyroid hormone excess can be lifesaving. Here, we present a rare case of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) due to—angiographically confirmed—thyrotoxicosis-induced CAS.

Case presentation

A 63-year-old male suffered an OHCA at home. The collapse was witnessed by a relative and immediate bystander, who started cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Initial rhythm, after arrival of the medical team, was shockable, and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved with a single defibrillation. The patient was intubated at the scene and transferred to our hospital. His cardiovascular risk profile included arterial hypertension and active smoking. Medical history was notable for a single episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation 10 years prior, with a normal echocardiography at that time.

In the emergency department, the first 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed ST elevation in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF) with reciprocal ST depression (Figure 1A).

Electrocardiogram. (A) On admission. (B) recurrent ischaemia within the first 24 h.

Urgent coronary angiography showed patent coronary arteries, but a remarkable CAS of the right coronary artery (RCA), which resolved after intracoronary injection of nitrates (Figure 2, Supplementary material online, Videos 1 and 2). Initially, the spasm was attributed to the—yet low dose of—continuous adrenaline infusion. Adrenaline was weaned off easily, and the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Coronary angiography before (A) and after intracoronary nitrates (B).

Baseline investigations showed a mildly elevated troponin I of 747 ng/L, normal renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate 83 mL/min/1.73 m2), no electrolyte disturbances, and no acute findings on CT imaging of the brain and thorax/abdomen aside from non-displaced rib fractures consistent with CPR. Laboratory tests also revealed significant hyperthyroidism with free thyroxine (fT4) 61.7 pmol/L (ref. 11.5–22.7 pmol/L), free tri-iodothyronine (fT3) 16.4 pmol/L (ref. 3.5–6.5 pmol/L), and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) 0.01 mU/L (ref. 0.55–4.78 mU/L). The family revealed that he had been treated with thiamazole (10 mg daily) for nearly a decade without further follow-up. In the ICU, thiamazole was uptitrated to 30 mg daily. In the first 24 h, intermittent inferior ST elevations recurred despite discontinuation of adrenaline, supporting thyrotoxicosis as the trigger (Figure 1B). Intravenous isosorbide dinitrate, urapidil, and oral amlodipine and propranolol were initiated. The patient also developed atrial fibrillation, which was managed with rate control during the ICU stay. Before discharge, electrical cardioversion was performed to restore the sinus rhythm.

Recovery was uneventful. After 48 h of temperature management, he regained full consciousness and was extubated. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a preserved left ventricular systolic function. No further CAS occurred, and on day 8, the patient was discharged to the cardiology ward. Upon transfer, intravenous nitrates and urapidil were discontinued, and anti-vasospasm therapy was transitioned to oral amlodipine 5 mg daily, propranolol was switched to nebivolol, and a nitroglycerine patch was added.

The patient’s Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS) score was 30, based on tachycardia and atrial fibrillation, indicating a probable impending thyroid storm (scores 25–44). Heteroanamnesis revealed abdominal pain as the initial complaint, contributing an additional 10 points. However, because fever is also a key parameter in the BWPS, its absence due to post-cardiac arrest temperature management likely led to an underestimation of thyroid storm severity.

Endocrinologic work-up revealed Graves’ disease. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the heart showed no underlying structural heart disease. Following the heart team discussion, an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted. At discharge, vasospasm prophylaxis consisted of amlodipine 5 mg daily and a nitroglycerine patch 10 mg. At 3-month follow-up, exercise testing was normal, and no arrhythmias were detected at 1-year outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but clinically important phenomenon: OHCA caused by thyrotoxicosis-induced CAS in the absence of coronary artery disease. To our knowledge, there are only a few previous reports describing similar cases, and many lacked angiographic confirmations or included confounding factors such as drug-induced vasospasm or electrolyte disturbances.1-7 In contrast, our case demonstrates a clear link, supported by recurrent vasospasm and dynamic ST-segment changes in the context of thyrotoxicosis. Initially, the differential diagnosis of adrenaline-induced vasospasms was made; however, the ECG changes recurred independent of adrenaline administration.

Thyrotoxicosis commonly manifests with cardiovascular symptoms, such as palpitations, tachycardia, exertional dyspnoea, widened pulse pressure, atrial fibrillation, and congestive heart failure.8 Thyroid storm is an extreme clinical manifestation of thyrotoxicosis and can be life-threatening. Thyroid storm is diagnosed clinically because laboratory findings often resemble uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis. The BWPS helps differentiate thyroid storm, impending storm, and uncomplicated cases, although no universal diagnostic criteria exist.9 In our case, the use of temperature management underestimates BWPS severity.

The heart is one of the primary organs affected by thyrotoxicosis. Excess thyroid hormone enhances myocardial contractility and heart rate and reduces systemic vascular resistance, resulting in increased cardiac output. Patients with thyrotoxicosis are prone to chest pain, presumably due to the increase in cardiac oxygen consumption, peripheral oxygen demand, and CAS.8

When CAS occurs in this population, it tends to be more diffuse and more resistant to therapy compared with CAS in euthyroid individuals.10 The precise mechanism by which thyroid hormone excess alters endothelial function and predisposes to CAS remains incompletely understood, but enhanced hyperreactivity of vascular smooth muscle cells is believed to play a key role.8

Thyrotoxicosis-induced CAS is estimated to occur in approximately 7% of patients with hyperthyroidism, with chest pain as the predominant presentation.10 Life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, triggered by CAS, remain a very rare complication of thyrotoxicosis.11

Notably, the 2022 European Society of Cardiology guidelines on ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death do not list thyrotoxicosis as a reversible cause of ventricular arrhythmias, whilst CAS is generally recognized.12 In a report by Lee et al.,7 a patient with CAS was found to have 90% stenosis of both coronary ostia and underwent emergent coronary artery bypass grafting; postoperative diagnosis and treatment of severe thyrotoxicosis led to normalization of the coronary arteries 19 days later, suggesting that surgery may have been avoidable. As management of thyrotoxicosis-induced ventricular arrhythmias differs substantially, increased awareness among critical care specialists and cardiologists is essential.13

Once CAS secondary to hyperthyroidism is diagnosed, prompt reduction of thyroid hormone levels is essential. Definitive treatments such as surgery or radioiodine ablation may transiently worsen thyrotoxicosis and are therefore contraindicated in the acute phase. Nitrates and calcium channel blockers are preferred over β-blockers, as the latter may exacerbate vasospasm through unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation.14

The role of ICD implantation in CAS-related cardiac arrest remains debated. Vasospasm is often considered reversible, yet recurrences can be unpredictable, and malignant arrhythmias may occur despite medical therapy.15 The 2022 ESC guidelines consider ICD implantation reasonable (Class IIa) for survivors of CAS-induced cardiac arrest.12 Although no specific recommendations exist for thyrotoxicosis-related vasospasm, it could be argued that these patients remain at higher risk of recurrence, which is why an ICD implantation was opted for in this case.

This case report underscores the rarity yet clinical significance of this condition. Recognition of hyperthyroidism as a potential precipitant is paramount, as the treatment differs greatly from atherosclerotic ACS/OHCA. Prompt correction of thyroid hormone excess and vasodilator therapy can result in full recovery. The role of ICD implantation in secondary prevention remains unclear and should be evaluated on an individual basis.
