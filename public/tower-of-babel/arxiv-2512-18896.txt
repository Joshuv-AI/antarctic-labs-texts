Universal Categories
Hans Schoutens

Abstract
. The category of models of any theory T T in any first-order language L L has the surprising property that any small category that is elementarily equivalent with it, already embeds in it. The proof uses an abstract argument via ultrapowers, leaving one wonder which concrete categorical axioms, depending on T T and L L , are responsible for this embedding result. We also propose a first-order logic for which equivalent categories are always elementarily equivalent.



1.
Introduction

The key notion of this paper is that of universality: let us call an
L
-structure
universal
, if any elementarily equivalent
L
-structure of lesser cardinality embeds in it. Uncountably categorical structures are examples of universal structures, but beyond that, there seem to be no immediate examples. For instance, the real field is not universal, for their exist countable non-Archimedean real closed fields,
which therefore are elementary equivalent with the reals but can never be embedded in them.

In this paper, we will encounter nonetheless an abundance of examples, actually, one for each first-order theory
T
, to wit, the category of models of
T
, viewed as a first-order structure in the language of categories. Since categories make a distinction between objects and morphisms, a two-sorted language
L_{\text{cat}}
is therefore best suited for the model-theoretic study of categories. Apart from that, one needs a (partial) binary operation for composing morphisms, a unary function assigning to an object its identity morphism, and two unary functions assigning to a morphism its domain and range. The axioms in this language are self-evident and the resulting
L_{\text{cat}}
-theory
\mathbf{CAT}
will be called the
theory of categories
. To see how natural all this is: any
L_{\text{cat}}
-structure is in an obvious way a category, any substructure is a subcategory and the
L_{\text{cat}}
-homomorphisms between categories are just the functors. Moreover, this language is very expressive: the existence of any finite limit or co-limit is now first-order expressible. But the main observation is universality (see Theorem
6.6
):

Main Theorem
.

Any category of the form
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
, for some theory
T
in some first-order language
L
, is universal.

Here we write
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
for the subcategory of models of
T
which belong to a once-and-for-all fixed Grothendieck universe
\mathfrak{U}
(see §
4
for more details). We need to make such an assumption to ensure that the category is small (i.e., is not a class), and therefore a model of
\mathbf{CAT}
.

In this introductory section, let me sketch a proof in one particular case, that of the category of Abelian groups
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T_{\text{Ab}}})
, where
T_{\text{Ab}}
is the theory of commutative groups in the language
L=(+,-,0)
. This argument is how one would like to prove the main theorem, by explicitly giving the axioms. Unfortunately, so far, this is basically the only case where I succeeded in doing so. The general case will be proven using ultrapowers: it turns out that any ultrapower
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})_{\natural}
(with respect to an ultrafilter belonging to the universe
\mathfrak{U}
) of any model category
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
is already a subcategory of the original
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
. Together with a characterization of elementarity using ultrapowers, (Proposition
3.1
, a corollary of the famous Keisler-Shelah criterion, but which can easily be proved directly), this will yield the main theorem. But let me explain what I consider to be a better proof at the hand of the category of Abelian groups.

Abelian groups

The category

2.
The model-theory of categories

While many categories have class-many objects and/or morphisms, technically, they cannot be considered as first-order structures. We will introduce the proper framework to deal with this issue, namely, Grothendieck universes, in §
4
. For now, we simply assume that our categories are small (i.e., have set-many objects and morphisms). Let
L_{\text{cat}}
be a two-sorted language, with the
{\tt o}
-sort for objects and the
{\tt m}
-sort for morphisms, together with a binary function
\circ
on the
{\tt m}
-sort, two unary functions
{\tt dom}
and
{\tt rng}
from the
{\tt m}
-sort to the
{\tt o}
-sort, and a unary function
{\tt Id}
from the
{\tt o}
-sort to the
{\tt m}
-sort subject. The interpretation of these symbols in an arbitrary category
\mathbb{C}
are then respectively the composition
g\circ f
of morphisms
g\colon B\to C
and
f\colon A\to B
, the domain
A
and range
B
of a morphism
f\colon A\to B
, and the identity morphism
1_{A}
on an object
A
. Note that we treat
\circ
as a partial function and we do not care what it does on non-composable morphisms. We take the convention that variables ranging over the
{\tt o}
-sort are capitalized and those over the
{\tt m}
-sort are lower-case, so that we do not need to specify this each time.

2.1 Definition
.

Let
\mathbf{CAT}
be the theory given by the following three axioms

(1)

(\forall x,y)
, if
{\tt rng}(x)={\tt dom}(y)
, then
{\tt dom}(x)={\tt dom}(y\circ x)
and
{\tt rng}(y)={\tt rng}(y\circ x)

(2)

(\forall x,y,z)

{\tt rng}(x)={\tt dom}(y)
and
{\tt rng}(y)={\tt dom}(z)

(3)

(\forall X,y)
, if
{\tt dom}(y)=X
(respectively,
{\tt rng}(y)=X
), then
y\circ{\tt Id}(X)=y
(respectively,
{\tt Id}(X)\circ y=y
);

To conform more to the usual notation, we will adopt the following notational conventions for arbitrary
L_{\text{cat}}
-structures. If

is a morphism with

and
B={\tt rng}(f)

{\tt Id}(A)
on an object

. This is justified by the following fact.

2.2 Lemma
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a model of
\mathbf{CAT}
, then one can associate a category
\mathbb{C}
to it such that as
L_{\text{cat}}
-structures, they are isomorphic. Moreover, if
\mathcal{D}
is a second model of
\mathbf{CAT}
with associated category
\mathbb{D}
, then the

-homomorphisms between
\mathcal{C}

induce precisely the functors between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}

Proof.

Consider the category
\mathbb{C}
by taking for objects, the elements of the
{\tt o}
-sort in
\mathcal{C}
, and for morphisms, the elements of the
{\tt m}
-sort, with composition of morphisms given by

then guarantee that

induces a functor
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}

∎

We will therefore henceforward identify
\mathcal{C}
with its associated category
\mathbb{C}
. In line with common notation, we also let
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)
denote the collection of all

{\tt m}
-sort such that
{\tt dom}(y)=A
and
{\tt rng}(y)=B

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)
as a

-definable subset in
\mathbb{C}
with parameters

2.3.
Limits and co-limits

A major tool in category theory is the notion of limit. As far as finite limits are concerned, they are easily seen to be definable. We quickly review their definition. Given a functor
J\colon\mathbbm{d}\to\mathbb{C}
(here
\mathbbm{d}
is a category merely used for indexing purposes), a
J
-cone
is a pair
(C,\psi)
, where
C
is an object in
\mathbb{C}
, and for each object

in
\mathbbm{d}

\mathbb{C}
-morphism
\psi_{A}\colon C\to J(A)
such that for each arrow

in
\mathbbm{d}
we have

. A
J
-cone

is called a

of

, if it is universal among all
J
-cones, that is to say, given any other
J
-cone
(C,\psi)
, there is a unique morphism

, such that
\varphi_{A}\circ u=\psi_{A}
, for all objects
A
in
\mathbbm{d}
.

Let
\mathbbm{d}
be the category with two objects and two morphisms between them (apart, of course, from the identity morphism on each object); symbolically

2.5 Corollary

Let
L

\mathbbm{Mod}(L)

\mathbbm{Mod}(L)
is complete (i.e., closed under all limits). Moreover,

It is known that if a category admits equalizers and products, then it is closed under all limits (see, for instance,

∎

2.6 Remark
.

Whenever a (co-)limit exists, it is actually computed as in the category of sets. More precisely, the underlying-set functor
D
reflects and preserves (co-)limits, if they exist. In particular,

is a regular (and even exact) category.

Note that the morphism

of the co-equalizer will not be in general strong, so the same result fails for the category of
L
-structures with strong homomorphisms (see §
5
below for this category).

3.
Ultraproducts and elementarity

Recall that an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on a set
X
is a collection of non-empty subsets closed under finite intersection and superset, such that any subset of
X
or its complement belongs to
\mathcal{U}
. One thinks of subsets in
\mathcal{U}
as big and the others are then small. Given any sequence of sets
S_{x}
indexed by
x\in X
, let
S_{\infty}
be their Cartesian product. On
S_{\infty}
, the ultrafilter now induces an equivalence relation: we say that two elements
(a_{x})_{x}
and
(b_{x})_{x}
in
S_{\infty}
are equivalent, if almost all their entries are the same, by which we mean that the set of all
x\in X
such that
a_{x}=b_{x}
lies in
\mathcal{U}
(i.e., is big). We call the resulting quotient space the
ultraproduct
of the
S_{x}
and will often denote it as
S_{\natural}
. If
L
is a first-order language and all
S_{x}
are
L
-structures, then
S_{\natural}
inherits an
L
-structure from the Cartesian product
S_{\infty}
. The key result, called Łos’ Theorem, is that an
L
-sentence
\sigma
holds in
S_{\natural}
if and only if it holds in almost all
S_{x}
(see, for instance,
[
9
, §4.2]
).

If all the
S_{x}
are the same set
S
, so that
S_{\infty}=S^{X}
, then we call
S_{\natural}
the
ultrapower
of
S
. It follows from Łos’ Theorem that
S_{\natural}
is elementary equivalent to
S
. In fact, there is a canonical map
S\to S_{\natural}
sending an element
s\in S
to the equivalence class of the constant map
x\mapsto s
, called the
diagonal embedding
, and one checks that this actually yields an elementary embedding. By a famous theorem of Keisler and Shelah, elementarity can be detected by the ultrapower construction: two
L
-structures are elementary equivalent if and only if they have some isomorphic ultrapowers (possibly with respect to different ultrafilters). Its proof, however, seems to rely on the generalized continuum hypothesis, but for our purposes, the following immediate corollary of it can easily be proved directly.

In the sequel, we will denote
L
-structures, for some first-order language
L
, by script letters
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M},\dots
, and we let
D(\mathcal{A}),D(\mathcal{M})\dots
denote their underlying subsets.

3.1 Proposition
.

Two first-order structures are elementary equivalent if and only if there exists an elementary embedding from one into an ultrapower of the other.

Proof.

One direction is clear, so assume
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are elementary equivalent
L
-structures with underlying sets
A:=D(\mathcal{A})
and
B:=D(\mathcal{B})
. We want to show that there is an ultrapower of
\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathcal{B}
as an elementary submodel. Let
b
be a tuple enumerating all elements of
B
and let
{\tt b}
be a set of new constant symbols of the same size. Let
L({\tt b})
be the language obtained from
L
by adding these new constant symbols. Hence the structure
(\mathcal{B},b)
is an
L({\tt b})
-structure by interpreting
{\tt b}
by
b
.
Consider the theory
\operatorname{Th}_{L({\tt b})}(\mathcal{B},b)
(aka, the
elementary diagram
of
\mathcal{B}
) in this language
L({\tt b})
and let
X
be the collection of all non-empty finite subsets of this theory. Any
\Sigma\in X
is logically equivalent with a sentence of the form
\varphi({\tt b})
such that
(\mathcal{B},b)\models\varphi(b)
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is then a model of
(\exists x)\varphi(x)
, so is
\mathcal{A}
by elementary equivalence. Therefore we can find an
L({\tt b})
-expansion
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\Sigma}:=(\mathcal{A},c_{\Sigma})
which models
\Sigma
, where
c_{\Sigma}
is a tuple of elements that are the interpretation in
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\Sigma}
of
{\tt b}
. For each
\Sigma\in X
, let
\langle\Sigma\rangle
be the set of all
\Psi\in X
with
\Sigma\subseteq\Psi
. Since the collection of all
\langle\Sigma\rangle
for
\Sigma\in X
, satisfies the finite intersection property, it is contained in some ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on
X
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}^{*}
be the ultraproduct of the
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\Sigma}
with respect to this ultrafilter. Hence the
L
-reduct
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}
of
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}^{*}
is the ultrapower of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the same ultrafilter. Let
\sigma
be a sentence in
\operatorname{Th}_{L({\tt b})}(\mathcal{B},b)
. For each
\Sigma\in X
containing
\sigma
, we have
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\Sigma}\models\sigma
, and by construction of
\mathcal{U}
, this therefore holds for almost all
\Sigma
, so that
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}^{*}
satisfies
\sigma
. Hence
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}
is the reduct of a model of
\operatorname{Th}_{L({\tt b})}(\mathcal{B},b)
, so that by the elementary diagram lemma (see, for instance,
[
9
, Lemma 8.2.1]
),
\mathcal{B}
is an elementary substructure of
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}
. In fact, the map
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}_{\natural}
is given by sending
b
to the interpretation of
{\tt b}
in the
L({\tt b})
-structure
\mathcal{A}_{\natural}^{*}
, and it follows easily that this is an elementary embedding.
∎

3.2 Remark
.

Note that
X
, the underlying set of the ultrafilter, has size at most that of the power set of the language
L({\tt b})
.

4.3 Definition

Given a first-order language

For

A faithful representable functor can be thought of as some sort of forgetful functor, and that is precisely what we will do. Given a

Let

Let

4.10 Example

sends, up to a natural isomorphism, an
L
-structure
\mathcal{M}
to its ultrapower
\mathcal{M}_{\natural}
, and homomorphisms to their ultrapower. In general this is not an elementary map, whence neither is

. Indeed, even with
L=\emptyset
, i.e., for the category of sets, this does not hold: the sentence expressing that there is a bijection between

and

is clearly false downstairs, but it is true upstairs since
(2^{\omega})_{\natural}\cong\omega_{\natural}
. In particular, the embedding

might even fail to reflect isomorphisms.

Strong homomorphisms

The category
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})
contains the (non-full) subcategory
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}^{\text{str}}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})
, with the same objects, the
L
-structures, but where the homomorphisms are now assumed to be strong, meaning that for any given relation symbol
{\tt R}
, a tuple satisfies
{\tt R}
if and only if its image under the morphism satisfies
{\tt R}
. Of course, if the language has no relation symbols (like, for instance,
L_{\text{cat}}
), then
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})=\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}^{\text{str}}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})
. In general, let us write
\texttt{fct}(L)
for the restriction of
L
obtained by omitting all predicate symbols (of positive arity).

5.4 Lemma
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be an
\texttt{fct}(L)
-structure and
\mathcal{M}
an
L
-structure. If

is an
\texttt{fct}(L)
-homomorphism, then it induces an
L
-structure on
\mathcal{N}
, denoted
f^{*}\mathcal{M}
, such that
f\colon f^{*}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
is a strong
L
-homomorphism. Moreover,
f^{*}\mathcal{M}
is the only expansion of
\mathcal{N}
for which
f
becomes strong.

Proof.

Let
{\tt R}
be an
n
-ary relation symbol and
P\subseteq D({\mathcal{M}})^{n}
the subset it defines in
\mathcal{M}
. Then we let the interpretation of
{\tt R}
on
D({\mathcal{N}})
be the subset
{f^{-1}(P)}
(where we continue to write
f
for the
n
-fold product
f\colon D({\mathcal{N}})^{n}\to D({\mathcal{M}})^{n}
). By construction,
f
is then a strong
L
-homomorphism. The last assertion is now clear from the definition of strong homomorphism.
∎

As before, let
\mathcal{T}
be the one-variable term algebra. We cannot expand it into an
L
-structure which would work for all
L
-structures, so we instead consider the collection

of all
L
-structures whose reduct to

is

(clearly

).

5.5 Lemma
.

The set
\Theta
forms a family of generators of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}^{\text{str}}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})
. This family is moreover locally unique.

Proof.

Let
f,g\colon\mathcal{M}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{N}
be two distinct (strong) homomorphisms of
L
-structures. Their reducts are
\texttt{fct}(L)
-structures and so by Proposition
5.1
, there exists a morphism
a\colon\mathcal{T}\to\left.\mathcal{M}\right|_{{\texttt{fct}(L)}}
such that

. With
\mathcal{J}:=a^{*}\mathcal{M}
—whence

—, we get the desired (strong) homomorphism

by Lemma

. Moreover, uniqueness now guarantees that
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}^{\text{str}}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})}(\mathcal{J}^{\prime},\mathcal{M})
is empty, for any
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\in\Theta
different from

∎

5.6 Proposition
.

With
\Theta
defined as above, there is a natural isomorphism of functors, such that for each small
L
-structure
\mathcal{M}
, we have

(6)

D({\mathcal{M}})\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}^{\text{str}}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})}(\Theta,\mathcal{M}).

Proof.

Let

-homomorphism

Conversely, suppose

.

Let

6.
Universal categories

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
L
-structure. We call
\mathcal{M}

universal
(respectively,
strongly universal
), if every
L
-structure
\mathcal{N}
which is elementarily equivalent with
\mathcal{M}
and has cardinality less than
\mathcal{N}
, embeds (respectively, elementarily embeds) in
\mathcal{M}
.

Any countable structure is trivially universal according to this definition, so we exclude this case.

6.1 Example
.

Any uncountable algebraically closed field
K
is strongly universal (in the language of rings). This is just a special case of:

6.2 Proposition
.

Let
T
be a complete, uncountable categorical
L
-theory and
\mathcal{M}
a model of
T
of uncountable cardinality
\lambda
. If
\lambda
is at least the size of the language
L
, then
\mathcal{M}
is strongly universal.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a model of cardinality
\beta<\lambda
. By upward Löwenheim-Skolem, there exists an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{N}
of size
\lambda
(see, for instance,
[
9
, Theorem 8.4.3]
), and by categoricity, the former must be isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}
.
∎

Rather than working with cardinalities, the following definition in terms of Grothendieck universes is more suitable for our needs. Recall that
D(\mathcal{M})
denotes the underlying set of an
L
-structure and
small
means that this set belongs to
\mathfrak{U}
.

6.3 Definition
.

An
L
-structure
\mathcal{M}
is called

, if
D(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathfrak{U}
and for any small
L
-structure
\mathcal{N}
elementarily equivalent with it, there is an embedding
\mathcal{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
.

For cardinality reasons, the underlying subset of a universal structure can never be an element of
\mathfrak{U}
, only a subset. Universality implies that small substructures form an elementary class:

6.4 Proposition
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a universal structure in a first-order language
L
, let
T
be its complete
L
-theory, and let
\mathcal{N}
be a small
L
-structure. Then
\mathcal{N}
is (isomorphic to) a substructure of
\mathcal{M}
if and only if it is a model of
T_{\forall}
.

Proof.

One direction is clear by universal preservation (see
[
9
, Lemma 6.2.2]
), so suppose
\mathcal{N}
is a small model of
{T_{\forall}}
. By the the same cited lemma, there exists
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\models T
such that
\mathcal{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
4.2
there is then some small
\mathcal{M}_{0}\models T
with
\mathcal{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}
. By universality, we also have an embedding
\mathcal{M}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
, proving that
\mathcal{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}
.
∎

6.5 Example
.

Let
\emptyset
denote the empty theory in the empty language, that is to say, the theory of sets, so that
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({\emptyset})
is just
\mathfrak{U}
. Let

(respectively,

does not contain a terminal object while

does, so their complete

-theories are not the same.

8

8

None of the

6.6 Theorem
.

Given a language

and an

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a small category that is

-elementary equivalent with

, there is some ultrapower

6.7 Corollary
.

Let
L
be a first-order language and

a collection of
L

closed under taking ultraproducts and isomorphic copies. Let
\mathbbm{E}
be the full subcategory of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({L})
the objects of which are the structures in

. Then
\mathbbm{E}
is universal.

Proof.

Let
\mathbbm{C}
be a small category that is elementarily equivalent with
\mathbbm{E}
. By Remark
3.2
again, there is an ultrafilter in
\mathfrak{U}
such that
\mathbbm{C}
admits an elementary embedding into the ultrapower

. Let

be the embedding given by Corollary

and consider its restriction to the subcategory

. Let

\mathbbm{E}_{\natural}
, given by a sequence

. By assumption, the ultraproduct

of the latter also lies in

. As

, and therefore we get an induced embedding

6.8 Example
.

This yields plenty of examples of universal categories that do not arise as the model category of a first-order theory. For instance, let

be a subset consisting besides

of primes and let
\mathbbm{Fld}_{S}
be the subcategory of

7.
Universalizing theories

To any theory
T
in a first-order language, we can now associate a (complete)
L_{\text{cat}}
-theory
\mathbf{T}
, namely, the theory of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
. However, if
S
is an extension of
T
(e.g., a completion), there is no immediate connection between
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{S}
(as both are complete theories). It is of course still possible that
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{T}
, and although I do not know any examples, disproving equality is not obvious, and at present, I only know ad hoc arguments.

7.1 Example
.

Let
FLD
and
ACF
stand respectively for the theory of fields and the theory of algebraically closed fields in the language of rings, and let
\mathbf{FLD}
and
\mathbf{ACF}
be the corresponding
L_{\text{cat}}
-theories of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({FLD})
and
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({ACF})
. To see that these are not the same, observe that the Cantor-Bernstein property holds for the latter but not for the former. More precisely, let
{\tt CB}
be the sentence expressing that
K\hookrightarrow L
(i.e., there is a monomorphism
K\to L
) and
L\hookrightarrow K
implies
K\cong L
; then
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({ACF})\models{\tt CB}
since
K
and
L
must then have the same characteristic and the same transcendence degree, whence are isomorphic by Steinitz’s theorem. On the other hand,
K
and
K(x)
, with
K
uncountable and algebraically closed and
x
a variable, are mutually embeddable
but not isomorphic as the latter is not algebraically closed, and hence
{\tt CB}
does not belong to
\mathbf{FLD}
.

One way, to tell
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({ACF})
and
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({RCF})
apart: only the latter has an object that embeds in any other object (the field of algebraic reals). Let
ACF_{p}
be all algebraically closed fields of characteristic
p
, which now also has this property, but still the theories
\mathbf{ACF}_{p}
and
\mathbf{RCF}
are distinguishable as only the former is totally ordered: for any two algebraically closed fields
K
and
L
of the same characteristic, either
K\hookrightarrow L
or
L\hookrightarrow K
by categoricity. This fails miserably in
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({RCF})
.

7.2 Remark
.

Since
L_{\text{cat}}
is a countable language, there are at most continuum many complete
L_{\text{cat}}
-theories. On the other hand, there are many more first-order theories in all possible languages in
\mathfrak{U}
. In particular, their will be disjoint languages
L
and
L^{\prime}
and theories
T
and
T^{\prime}
in these respective languages such that
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
. Universality now yields a weak form of definitional equivalence between models of
T
and models of
T^{\prime}
in the following sense. Let
\mathbbm{C}
be a small, elementary substructure of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T})
(by Proposition
4.2
we can even assure it to contain a given small collection of models). Since
\mathbbm{C}
is then also elementarily equivalent to
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T^{\prime}})
, it embeds in it by universality, yielding for each model of
T
belonging to
\mathbbm{C}
a corresponding model of
T^{\prime}
.

Proposition
6.4
shows that as far as universality is concerned, the universal theory suffices. But neither the complete nor the universal theory are easily described in concrete cases. The next definition generalizes this in the hope that we can find some concrete axiomatization, like the case of Abelian groups, to obtain universality.

7.3 Definition
.

Given a language
L\in\mathfrak{U}
, let us call an
L
-theory
U

universalizing
, if admits a model
\mathcal{M}
with
D(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathfrak{U}
, such that any small model
\mathcal{N}\models U
embeds in
\mathcal{M}
.

It follows that the above
\mathcal{M}
is universal, for any small model elementarily equivalent to it, is also a model of
U
, whence embeds in
\mathcal{M}
. We express this by saying that
U
is
universalizing for
\mathcal{M}
.
Clearly the complete theory
T
of a universal structure
\mathcal{M}
is universalizing for that structure, as is
T_{\forall}
, by Proposition
6.4
.

Example
6.5
shows that the same theory can be universalizing for different structures. The following result shows how any two universal structures then must at least compare. Here, given a theory
T
in a language

, we will say that an
L
-sentence

belongs to

, if their exists a
\forall\exists
-sentence
\sigma_{1}
and a
\exists\forall
-sentence

, such that
\mathcal{M}\models\sigma\leftrightarrow\sigma_{1}\leftrightarrow\sigma_{2}
in each model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
.

7.4 Proposition
.

Suppose an
L
-theory

is universalizing for two

-structures
\mathcal{M}
and

\mathcal{M}
and

agree on all

Proof.

We will construct a sequence
\mathcal{M}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{1}\hookrightarrow\dots
, with all
\mathcal{M}_{i}
elementary substructures of

and all
\mathcal{N}_{i}
elementary substructures of
\mathcal{N}
. Assume we have such a chain, let
\mathcal{L}
be their filtered co-limit (i.e., direct limit). Note that
\mathcal{L}
is then also the filtered co-limit of the sub-sequences
\mathcal{M}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}\dots
and
\mathcal{N}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{1}\hookrightarrow\dots
(these chains, however, need not be elementary). Let

be a

-sentence in

which holds in
\mathcal{M}
. Since the
\mathcal{M}_{i}
are elementary substructures,
\sigma
holds in each of them, whence also in

by
[
3
, Theorem 2.4.6]
, since

is
U
-equivalent to a
\forall\exists
-sentence. Suppose

holds in

. Being also in

, that

holds in

, contradiction.

To construct the given sequence, let
\mathcal{M}_{0}
be any small elementary substructure of
\mathcal{M}
. Since it is then a model of
U
and

, we can find a small elementary substructure

, etc.

Let us revisit the theory
\mathbf{AB}
introduced in the introduction: it consist of the sentences
{\tt prod}
,
{\tt null}
and
{\tt gen}
expressing respectively the existence of (finite) products, of a null-object, and of a generator, and then the two sentences (Ab1) and (Ab2) asserting respectively the existence of a unique group arrow and that every morphism is compatible with these group laws.

7.5 Proposition
.

The theory
\mathbf{AB}
is universalizing (for
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T_{\text{Ab}}})
).
As a partial converse, if
\mathbbm{C}
is a small, full subcategory of
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T_{\text{Ab}}})
admitting products and a null-object, then
\mathbbm{C}\models\mathbf{AB}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbbm{C}
be a small model of
\mathbf{AB}
, so that in particular, it admits a generator
I
. Put
\left|G\right|:=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbbm{C}}(I,G)
, for
G
any object in
\mathbbm{C}
. Using the unique group arrow
\mu_{G}\colon G\times G\to G
on
G
, we can define an addition on
\left|G\right|
as follows: for
\alpha,\beta\colon I\to G
, let
\alpha+\beta:=\mu_{G}\circ(\alpha,\beta)
, where
(\alpha,\beta)\colon I\to G\times G
is the unique morphism given by the product. It is now not hard to show that the properties of a group arrow make
\left|G\right|
into a group and any morphism
G\to H
in
\mathbbm{C}
yields a group homomorphism
\left|G\right|\to\left|H\right|
. Since
I
is a generator, the functor
\left|-\right|
therefore yields an embedding into not just
\mathfrak{U}
but already into
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T_{\text{Ab}}})
.

For the second statement, assume now that

is a full embedding and
\mathbbm{C}
satisfies
{\tt prod}
and
{\tt null}
. Let
G
be an object in
\mathbbm{C}
, so that
i(G)
is by assumption an Abelian group with addition
+
. Since embedding preserve cones,
i(G\times G)
is a cone in
\mathbb{M}\!\operatorname{od}_{\mathfrak{U}}({T_{\text{Ab}}})
and hence there is a unique morphism
i(G\times G)\to i(G)\times i(G)
(we do not know whether this is an isomorphism as

might not preserve limits). Composition with

yields a morphism
i(G\times G)\to i(G)
. Since

is full, there must be a morphism
\mu\colon G\times G\to G
in
\mathbbm{C}
such that
i(\mu)
is equal to this composition. It is now not hard to see that
\mu
is a group arrow on
G
and that axioms (Ab1) and (Ab2) hold in
\mathbbm{C}
, showing that it is a model of
\mathbf{AB}
.
∎

8.
Weakly universal categories

In this section, we will construct some “algebraic” categories that have some universal behavior but are not given as the models of a first-order theory.

8.1 Definition
.

Let us call a
\mathfrak{U}
-concrete category

weakly universal

\mathbb{D}
elementarily equivalent to

, there is a functor

which is injective on objects.

In other words,

The following example already exhibits our main technique: the use of cataproducts. Recall that if
(R_{x},\mathfrak{m}_{x})
are Noetherian local rings, then their

is by definition the quotient of their ultraproduct
(R_{\natural},\mathfrak{m}_{\natural})
by the

, that is to say, the intersection of all powers
\mathfrak{m}_{\natural}^{n}
. By

Conclusion

While model-theorist normally consider the equality sign as a logical symbol, one could opt not to do so. Consider then the
homotopic language

L_{\text{homo}}
consisting of the binary predicate
\cong
and the ternary predicate
{\tt QC}
in which atomic formulas (whence general formulas) are built in the usual way with the exception that in stead of
=
, we must use
\cong
(in fact, we can leave out the predicate
\cong
as it is definable from
{\tt QC}
). In other words, instead of term equations, we only have term isomorphisms; note also that the only terms are variables (or morphisms when allowing parameters). Hence the
L_{\text{homo}}
-formulas are now just the homotopic formulas (after replacing each occurrence of the
\cong
symbol by its homotopic definition).

Any category
\mathbbm{C}
can be made into a
L_{\text{homo}}
-structure by choosing some iso-graph
\mathbbm{i}\subseteq\mathbbm{C}
, taking as underlying set the morphisms of
\mathbbm{C}
and letting
\cong
and
{\tt QC}
be defined as above. Theorem
9.5
therefore states that in this language elementary equivalence is the same as equivalence of categories. For skeletal categories, there is no difference:

9.8 Proposition
.

Let
\mathbbm{C}
be a skeletal category viewed as an

Since

For each such morphism
\alpha

L_{\text{homo}}
-structure as the those of the

-sort.

Since isomorphic objects are equal, the domain and range predicates are defined by

{\tt dom}(f)=\left|\alpha\right|\leftrightarrow\ {\tt QC}(\alpha,f;f)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\tt rng}(f)=\left|\beta\right|\leftrightarrow\ {\tt QC}(f,\beta;f)

while by Lemma

, composition is defined by

if and only if
{\tt QC}(f,g;h)
. The unary function

is just

∎
