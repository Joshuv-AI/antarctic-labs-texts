Evaluation of point‐of‐care capillary and venous blood glucose concentrations in hospitalized neonatal foals
Authors: Ana Sofia Dias Moreira, Hsin‐Yi Weng, Laura D. Hostnik, Erin M. Beasley, Simon F. Peek, Amelia S. Munsterman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare glucose measurements from capillary and venous blood samples using a point‐of‐care (POC) glucometer with a standard laboratory (colorimetric, glucose oxidase) assay (LABGLU) in a population of hospitalized, neonatal foals.DesignMulticenter, prospective, experimental study, conducted between March 2019 and June 2020.SettingFour university teaching hospitals and 1 private referral hospital.AnimalsFifty‐four hospitalized neonatal (≤30 days of age) foals.InterventionsSimultaneous capillary (muzzle, POCMUZ) and venous (jugular, POCJUG) blood samples were obtained to determine POC glucose concentrations. Venous samples were also analyzed by LABGLU. Each foal was sampled at the time of enrollment or admission to the hospital and at 1 subsequent point during hospitalization. Indirect mean arterial pressure and hematocrit were concurrently recorded.Measurements and Main ResultsBland–Altman analysis showed a mean bias (95% limits of agreement) of −28.0 (−88.6 to 32.6) mg/dL for comparison of POCJUG with LABGLU, −8.2 (−94.3 to 78.0) mg/dL for POCMUZ and LABGLU, and 18.8 (−44.4 to 82.0) mg/dL for POCMUZ and POCJUG. A total of 63.5% of the POCJUG and 45.2% of the POCMUZ samples exceeded the reference value by ±15 mg/dL (for LABGLU samples <75 mg/dL) or ±15% (for LABGLU samples ≥75mg/dL). Concordance correlation coefficient (95% confidence interval [CI]) indicated a fair agreement between POCJUG and LABGLU (0.75, 95% CI: 0.66–0.82) and between POCMUZ and LABGLU (0.71, 95% CI: 0.58–0.80). Fifty percent (14/28) of hypoglycemic foals on the reference method were incorrectly classified as euglycemic by POCJUG, and 5 of 28 were incorrectly classified by POCMUZ.ConclusionsIn the sampled population, the chosen POC glucometer lacked agreement with the standard laboratory measurement. Limits of agreement were wide for both POCJUG and POCMUZ. Inaccuracies in POC results could impact decision‐making in the clinical management of glycemic control in hospitalized neonatal foals and, importantly, increase the risk of hypoglycemic events being underdiagnosed in critical patients.

INTRODUCTION

Neonatal foals have limited endogenous glycogen stores and high tissue metabolism, factors that combine to impair glucose homeostasis, particularly in critically ill individuals. 1 Glycemic disturbances are therefore common in hospitalized neonates, and both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia at hospital admission have been identified as poor prognostic indicators. 2 Hypoglycemia at admission has also been associated with sepsis, a positive blood culture, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome. 2 , 3 Repeat blood glucose sampling during hospitalization is typically used to guide fluid therapy, insulin administration, and nutritional support. Given the need for frequent blood glucose sampling in foals, point‐of‐care (POC) or stall‐side glucometry has become standard practice in equine neonatal intensive care units. 4 , 5

Most commercially available POC glucometers have been designed specifically for people and use conversion algorithms to report the result as a plasma value, even if a whole blood sample is used. These algorithms assume a constant stable relationship between plasma and whole blood and equilibration between plasma and erythrocyte glucose concentrations. 5 , 6 However, these hemodynamic ratios vary among species, affecting the accuracy of devices targeted for human use when applied to veterinary patients. Glucose and water distribute freely between erythrocytes and plasma, so the molality (mmol/kg H2O), but not the concentration of glucose, is identical in erythrocytes and plasma. 7 In people, the concentration of glucose in plasma and whole blood is not interchangeable, and the concentration of glucose in plasma is, on average, 11% higher than in whole blood. 7 , 8 For a given concentration of glucose in plasma, the concentration of glucose in whole blood depends on the hematocrit because erythrocytes have a lower water concentration than plasma. Provided the hematocrit is normal, a constant factor of 1.11 is used for the conversion of whole blood glucose concentration to plasma concentration in people; a decrease in hematocrit will cause an increase in glucose concentration in whole blood and vice versa. 7 In neonatal foals, the concentration of glucose in plasma is also higher than in whole blood, with an average of 81.5 and 52.5 mg/dL, respectively, at birth. 9 The concentration of glucose within erythrocytes (average 15 mg/dL) is markedly lower than in plasma. Additionally, fructose, rather than glucose, is the primary sugar present in foal erythrocytes at birth (average 77.5 mg/dL). 9 These species’ differences call into question the validity of using human glucometers in the equine neonatal setting. Furthermore, multiple factors may also impact POC glucose measurements, including the site of blood collection (eg capillary, venous, or arterial), volume of blood on the glucometer strip (ie, excess sample results in higher glucose levels), hematocrit (ie, anemia results in higher glucose levels), peripheral hypoperfusion, delay in sample processing, and different substances or drugs present in the sample. Dopamine, acetaminophen, mannitol, lipemia, elevated unconjugated bilirubin and uric acid, maltose, galactose, xylose, and icodextrin have all been shown to interfere with glucose measurement. 10 , 11

In human medicine, capillary (fingerstick) POC measurements were traditionally used for glucose monitoring given the ease of sample collection. More recently, studies have demonstrated the inaccuracy of this method, especially in patients with poor peripheral tissue perfusion and shock. As a result, the technique does not meet the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute criteria. 12 , 13 , 14 The current standard of care in human medicine requires arterial or venous specimens. As is the case with people, capillary samples are easily obtained from neonatal foals and are often used for glucose monitoring because vascular trauma resulting from repeated venipuncture is prevented. Capillary samples are also particularly useful when dextrose solutions are being infused through an intravenous catheter. Together, these issues warrant a prospective investigation of the accuracy of capillary POC samples for glucose monitoring in the critically ill neonatal foal.

The primary objective of this study was to compare glucose measurements obtained by a veterinary POC glucometera using concurrent capillary (muzzle, POCMUZ) and venous (jugular, POCJUG) blood samples acquired by direct venipuncture with a reference sample analyzed using a laboratory‐based plasma chemistry analyzerb (colorimetric, glucose oxidase, LABGLU), measured from the venous blood sample. The goal was to determine if results obtained from the handheld glucometer were in agreement between sampling methods (POCMUZ and POCJUG) as well as with the reference plasma chemistry analyzer (LABGLU) in a population of hospitalized neonatal foals. Secondary objectives of the study were to investigate the effect of hematocrit on the accuracy of POC measurements in whole blood and to evaluate the effect of mean arterial pressure (MAP), as measured indirectly by a tail cuff, on the accuracy of capillary blood samples for glucose measurement with a POC glucometer.

We hypothesized that the POC glucometer would provide accurate blood glucose measurements from capillary blood samples compared with venous blood samples, validating that capillary blood samples can be reliably used for glucose monitoring in hospitalized neonatal foals. In addition, we hypothesized that hematocrit and indirect MAP would negatively correlate with glucose concentrations from the POC glucometer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All procedures were approved by the University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and an informed client consent document was signed by each foal's owner or liaison. Neonatal foals (≤30 days of age) hospitalized at the University of Wisconsin–Madison School of Veterinary Medicine, University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine, The Ohio State University College of Veterinary Medicine, University of Georgia College of Veterinary Medicine, and Anglesey Lodge Equine Hospital were enrolled in this study. Age and hospitalization at these institutions were the only inclusion criteria required for enrollment.

Sample collection and processing

Venous blood samples were collected by direct venipuncture of the jugular vein as a part of the routine admission blood draw and at 1 additional time point according to clinical need during hospitalization. Capillary blood sampling was performed concurrently with venous sampling, with a small incision (<5 mm) at the muzzle, using a 25‐Ga needle. POC glucose measurements were performed stall‐side, within 60 seconds of venous and capillary blood collection, with a device specifically designed for veterinary usea that uses glucose dehydrogenase enzyme and a coulometric electrochemical sensor. The veterinary glucometer internal algorithm associated with code 5 was selected, as previously described. 6

After POC analysis, the remainder of each venous blood sample was transferred to fluoride vacutainer tubes, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes within 1 hour of collection, and the plasma was harvested, stored in a cryotube, and frozen at −4°C. Samples collected off‐site were shipped on dry ice to the University of Wisconsin–Madison. All plasma samples were processed by the chemistry analyzerb at the Clinical Pathology laboratory at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, School of Veterinary Medicine. The chemistry analyzerb uses a glucose oxidase assay for measurement of glucose concentrations. The sample hematocrit was measured using microhematocrit tubes and centrifugation from each venous blood sample obtained at the patient's home institution. Indirect blood pressure measurements were also obtained, at the time of sample collection, with a tail cuff that was one third of the diameter of the circumference of the tail head, while the foal was in left lateral recumbency. 15 Mean blood pressure values were calculated from 3 repeated measurements.

Statistical analysis

Commercial statistical software programsc , d were used for analysis. Samples classified as “LO” (below the limit of detection of the glucometer of 20 mg/dL) on the POC analyzer were arbitrarily assigned a value of 15 mg/dL for analytical purposes. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the data were noted to be normally distributed, mean ± SD were presented; otherwise, descriptive statistics using median and range were presented. Previously defined age‐specific reference intervals (RIs) for blood glucose in neonatal foals were used in order to establish the frequency of dysglycemia in our study population. 16 For this particular purpose, foals were grouped into 3 categories: ≤1 day of age (RI: 121–223 mg/dL), 2–7 days of age (RI: 121–192 mg/dL), and 8–30 days of age (RI: 130–216 mg/dL). Pairwise agreements between glucose concentrations measured with each method were assessed using the Bland–Altman method and Lin's concordance correlation. Bias was calculated as the mean difference with the limits of agreement as bias ± 1.96 SD (of the bias). Additionally, the percentages of paired results that met the 2020 Food and Drug Administration (FDA) hospital‐use clearance criteria for split sample studies were calculated. 17 The FDA acceptable accuracy criteria for POC glucometers used by human healthcare professionals are as follows: at least 95% of all values should be within ±12% of the comparator method for glucose concentrations ≥75 mg/dL and within ±12 mg/dL at glucose concentrations <75 mg/dL; in addition, at least 98% of values should be within ±15% of the comparator method for glucose concentrations ≥75 mg/dL and within ±15 mg/dL at glucose concentrations <75 mg/dL. 17 Furthermore, scatter plots with a regression line were also plotted against the FDA thresholds, and a diagonal line was overlaid that presented a perfect agreement between 2 methods. Finally, a Clarke's error grid analysis was performed, as previously described. 6 , 18 The Clarke's error grid focuses on the clinical significance of the difference between glucose values obtained by the glucometer and the reference method. The standard target range for Clarke's error grid analysis in people was used (blood glucose concentration 70–180 mg/dL). Zone A represents glucose values that deviate by no more than 20% or that are in the hypoglycemic range of <70 mg/dL when the reference is also <70 mg/dL. Values falling within zone A are clinically accurate in that they would lead to clinically correct treatment decisions. Zone B represents values that deviate from reference by >20% but that would lead to benign or no treatment. Zones A and B are considered clinically acceptable. On the other hand, zone C represents values that would result in an overcorrection of blood glucose concentrations, causing blood glucose to diverge outside the established target range. Zone D represents dangerous failure to detect and treat; reference glucose values are outside the target range, but POC values appear to be within the range. Finally, zone E represents POC values that are opposite to reference glucose, and corresponding treatment decisions would therefore be opposite. Values in zones C–E are potentially dangerous and considered clinically unacceptable. 18 Last, the association between hematocrit and indirect MAP and POC measurements was calculated using Pearson's correlation coefficient.

RESULTS

Fifty‐four neonatal (≤30 days of age) foals were initially enrolled—3 from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, 3 from the University of Georgia, 10 from the Ohio State University, 26 from Anglesey Lodge Equine Hospital, and 12 from the University of Illinois. Of those, 4 were excluded from analysis, including 1 from the University of Wisconsin–Madison due to sample mislabeling and 3 from the University of Georgia because the plasma samples thawed on route to the clinical pathology laboratory at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Six foals were considered to be clinically healthy. Healthy foals were owned by the University of Illinois (3), born in the hospital at the University of Illinois (2), and 1 was examined at Anglesey Lodge Equine Hospital. All foals were determined to be healthy based on physical examination findings and immunoglobulin G concentrations, and the foal from Anglesey Lodge Equine Hospital was further confirmed as healthy on the basis of a normal CBC and serum biochemistry analysis. The remaining 44 foals were hospitalized and diagnosed with a variety of conditions, including born in hospital after dystocia, dysmaturity, prematurity, failure of transfer of passive immunity, neonatal maladjustment syndrome, septic arthritis, flexural deformities, pneumonia, sepsis, enteritis/enterocolitis, uroperitoneum secondary to a ruptured bladder, patent urachus, and clostridial myositis. Breeds included Thoroughbred (25), Standardbred (12), American Quarter Horse (5), Belgian (2), Shire (2), and 1 each of the following: Tennessee Walker, Hanoverian, Appaloosa, and Gypsy Vanner. There were 27 fillies and 23 colts, and age on admission ranged from 0 hours (born in hospital) to 30 days, with a median age of 2 days. Implemented medical therapy was determined by the attending clinicians at each institution and not specifically evaluated or directed in this study. No significant trauma or complications were associated with venipuncture or muzzle capillary sampling. One foal developed muzzle edema that prevented capillary sampling at the second time point during hospitalization.

The total number of paired samples was 85 for POCJUG and LABGLU, 84 for POCMUZ and LABGLU, and 86 for POCJUG and POCMUZ. There were only 2 POC capillary samples below the limit of detection of the glucometer, both from the admission time point. LABGLU concentrations ranged from 39 to 315 mg/dL, with a mean of 146.9 ± 55.3 mg/dL, POCJUG from 46 to 351 mg/dL, with a mean of 174.2 ± 56.0 mg/dL, and POCMUZ from 15 to 291 mg/dL, with a mean of 154.4 ± 59.9 mg/dL.

According to age‐specific RI for blood glucose in neonatal foals, 16 euglycemia (as measured by the reference method LABGLU) was more frequent than hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia. Fifty‐three percent (45/85) of foals in this study were euglycemic, 33% (28/85) were hypoglycemic, and 14% (12/85) were hyperglycemic. Only 8 LABGLU samples were <75 mg/dL.

The bias (95% limits of agreement) was −28.0 (−88.6 to 32.6) mg/dL when comparing LABGLU with POCJUG, −8.2 (−94.3 to 78.0) mg/dL for LABGLU and POCMUZ, and 18.8 (−44.4 to 82.0) mg/dL for POCMUZ and POCJUG (Figure 1). By definition, the negative mean bias on the Bland–Altman plots indicated that determinations using POC glucometry, both venous and capillary, were on average higher than the reference laboratory method. 19 Lin's concordance correlation coefficient indicated a fair agreement between POCJUG and LABGLU (0.75, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.66–0.82) and between POCMUZ and LABGLU (0.71, 95% CI: 0.58–0.80). 20

The results of the assessment of agreement between POCJUG, POCMUZ, and LABGLU using the FDA criteria 17 are presented in Tables 1 and 2 and Figure 2. A total of 63.5% of the POCJUG and 45.2% of the POCMUZ samples exceeded the reference value by ±15 mg/dL (for LABGLU samples <75 mg/dL) or ±15% (for LABGLU samples ≥75 mg/dL). Figure 2A demonstrates that the regression line for POCJUG is entirely above the line of perfect concordance. This is in agreement with the overestimation of POC glucometry in relation to the reference method as shown on the Bland–Altman plots (Figure 1). By comparison, the regression line for POCMUZ crosses the diagonal line (Figure 2B), indicating an overestimation of POCMUZ values at lower glucose concentrations and an underestimation of POCMUZ at higher glucose concentrations. This is reflected in a lower mean bias shown on the Bland–Altman plot for POCMUZ compared with POCJUG. The standard Clarke's error grid is shown in Figure 3, with 42.4% of POCJUG results falling within zone A and 97.7% within zones A and B, and 64.3% of POCMUZ results falling within zone A and 96.4% falling within zones A and B.

Hematocrit ranged from 17% to 55%, with a mean of 36.0% ± 7.4%. Indirect MAP ranged from 49 to 134 mm Hg, with a mean of 83.8 ± 17.9 mm Hg. DISCUSSION

This study is not the first to assess the potential agreement between a POC glucometer and a reference plasma analyzer, nor is it the first study to use the AlphaTRAK 2 glucometera. The bias identified in the Bland–Altman analysis in the current study was large, especially for POCJUG, which overestimated LABGLU by a mean of 28.0 mg/dL, and the limits of agreement were wide. 19 The regression line for POCJUG (Figure 2A) confirmed this overestimation of venous glucose across the entire range of values and also suggested that the bias was larger at lower glucose concentrations, introducing the risk of underdiagnosing hypoglycemic events in hospitalized neonatal foals. Fifty percent (14/28) of hypoglycemic foals on the reference method were incorrectly classified as euglycemic by POCJUG, and 12 of 45 euglycemic foals were classified as hyperglycemic. Conversely, the regression line for POCMUZ (Figure 2B) crossed the diagonal line, indicating an overestimation of capillary glucose values at lower glucose concentrations and an underestimation of capillary glucose values at higher glucose concentrations, risking misclassification of both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia as euglycemia. When comparing POCMUZ with the reference method, 5 of 28 hypoglycemic foals on the reference method were incorrectly classified as euglycemic and 1 was incorrectly classified as hyperglycemic, while 9 of 45 euglycemic foals were classified as hyperglycemic. As such, this POC glucometer should be used with caution in the evaluation of a sick neonate, where the consequences of inaccurate diagnosis of hypoglycemia can be life‐threatening. 2 , 21

These results were similar to a previous study showing that the same POC glucometer overestimated a standard laboratory chemistry enzymatic method by 22.18 mg/dL using venous samples from a population of 4 healthy and 4 ill neonatal foals. Moreover, only 29% and 49.5% of POC glucometer measurements were within 10% and 15% of the standard analyzer, respectively. 22 The concordance correlation coefficient in our study indicated a fair agreement between POCJUG and POCMUZ and LABGLU, similar to the concordance correlation coefficient in the aforementioned study for venous samples (0.714, 95% CI: 0.66–0.76). 22 However, it was far inferior to the concordance correlation coefficient (0.953, 95% CI: 0.927–0.979) reported using the first AlphaTRAK model in a population of 25 healthy and 25 critically ill neonatal foals. 6 It is possible that the higher number of ill foals as well as the high frequency of dysglycemia in our study contributed to these differences. The variation may also be related to differences between glucometer models from the same manufacturer, which has been shown in dogs. 23 It is recommended that scientific reports regarding POC glucose measurements clearly state the brand and model for accurate interpretation and comparison of results.

Several studies in horses have investigated the performance of other brands of POC glucometers designed for human patients. In 1 report analyzing venous samples from a comparable population of 19 critically ill neonatal foals, there was an underestimation by POC glucometry (Accu‐Chek Advantage; Roche Diagnostics) in relation to the standard analyzer method, with an average of 20 mg/dL and wide limits of agreement (−51.0 to 10.9). 24 Concordance correlation between the 2 methods was stronger (0.87, 95% CI: 0.80–0.93) than that obtained in our study. 24 The accuracy of another POC glucometer designed for human use (Accu‐Chek Aviva; Roche Diagnostics) was investigated in both whole blood and plasma samples from 50 horses, >1 year of age, admitted on emergency. 4 When compared with the standard chemistry analyzer, venous whole blood POC glucometry was on average 44.7 mg/dL lower, with very wide limits of agreement (−22.7 to 111.9), whereas plasma samples achieved a superior agreement, being on average 10.8 mg/dL higher (−36.7 to 15.1). Lin's concordance correlation was also superior in plasma samples (0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99) than in whole blood samples (0.73, 95% CI: 0.63–0.83), which was similar to the outcomes from whole blood samples in our study. 4 Only 1 other study has investigated the accuracy of capillary blood glucose sampling in 7 critically ill neonatal foals. When comparing capillary POC glucometry with a device designed for human use (Contour Link Analyzer; Bayer) to venous blood glucose obtained by a standard chemistry analyzer, the mean bias was −2.9 mg/dL, with narrower 95% limits of agreement (−32 to 27) than the ones obtained in the current study. The methodology was similar to the one described in our study; however, frozen plasma samples were stored for a maximum of only 48 hours before analysis. 1

It has been reported previously that precision and accuracy can be affected by sample type and storage time when using a human‐specific POC glucometer (Accutrend Plus; Roche Diagnostics) in venous whole blood, anticoagulated venous whole blood, and plasma samples from adult horses. Whole blood samples resulted in poor precision and had the poorest agreement, with only 56.5% of venous whole blood POC glucometry measurements falling within 15% of the glucose measurement determined by the laboratory reference method. 25 In the current study, POCJUG and POCMUZ results were not interchangeable, with POCJUG being on average 18.8 mg/dL higher than POCMUZ (Figure 1C). Therefore, the same sampling method should be considered and kept consistent when monitoring glucose over time.

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to investigate POC glucometry performance in veterinary species following the tabular data presentation delineated by the Guidance for Industry and Food and Drug Administration Staff, 2020. 17 These guidelines for POC glucometers used by healthcare professionals are distinct from the ones pertaining to self‐monitoring blood glucose systems for over‐the‐counter use in people and considerably more stringent, as accurate monitoring of blood glucose is paramount in critically ill patient care settings. As such, POC glucometry devices can only be used in human critically ill patient care settings after being studied and validated in the intended population and approved by the FDA. 17 , 26 Because 63.5% of the POCJUG and 45.2% of the POCMUZ samples exceeded the reference value by ±15 mg/dL (for LABGLU samples <75 mg/dL) or ±15% (for LABGLU samples ≥75 mg/dL), the FDA guidelines confirm that the POC glucometer evaluated in this study should be used with caution for both jugular venous and capillary whole blood samples from similar populations of hospitalized neonatal foals as it may lead to clinical error.

An overestimation of POC capillary glucose measurements in relation to venous laboratory reference values has been demonstrated in various studies in critically ill and hypotensive human patients 13 and in dogs. 23 Several conditions, such as hypothermia, hypotension, shock, and edema, may affect capillary glucose measurements. 26 Currently, only 1 POC glucometer has obtained FDA clearance for use in critically ill human patients. Even though this POC glucometer met the aforementioned FDA criteria for arterial and venous sampling, it fell short on the criteria for capillary sampling, with 86% of capillary POC glucometry values within ±12% of the comparator method for glucose concentrations ≥75 mg/dL, 85% within ±12 mg/dL at glucose concentrations <75 mg/dL, and 92% within both ±15% of the comparator method for glucose concentrations ≥75 mg/dL and ±15 mg/dL at glucose concentrations <75 mg/dL. It was nonetheless considered that the benefits of capillary sampling outweighed the risks and its use is permitted. 26 These results for capillary sampling in critically ill human samples are still significantly superior to the ones obtained in our study, and hence, the same risk‐to‐benefit assessment cannot be inferred for the POC glucometer tested here. The location of capillary blood collection may also influence glucose measurements. In a study in dogs using this POC glucometer, glucose concentration in capillary samples collected from the medial aspect of the pinna was similar to peripheral venous samples, whereas samples collected from the carpal pad and oral mucosa differed significantly. 27

In comparison to the FDA guidelines discussed previously, the Clarke's error grid is more lenient, defining zones A and B as clinically acceptable even though the glucose values may deviate >20% from the reference method. 17 , 18 The main goal of this error grid is focused less on device performance and more on clinical significance and as an educational tool for healthcare professionals and patients. 28 The original Clarke's error grid has undergone adjustments and was modified into the Parke's error grid, with adjusted zone boundaries defined after 100 physicians were surveyed. 28 When evaluating over‐the‐counter human glucometers using the Parke's error grid, it is recommended that at least 99% of results fall within zones A and B. 29 Even though the consensus error grid was not developed for critically ill patients, it is the most common metric used to assess the clinical impact of POC glucometer errors. 30 As a consensus target has not been defined for the equine species and because, to the authors’ knowledge, only the Clarke's error grid has been previously used to evaluate a similar glucometry device in horses, 6 the authors elected to also refer to the Clarke's error grid for data analysis. The percentage of POCJUG results within zones A and B of the Clarke's error grid obtained in our study (97.7%) was lower than previously reported (100%) for an older model of the AlphaTRAK glucometer device in samples from both healthy and hospitalized mature horses and foals, 6 which is also in agreement with the lower concordance correlation obtained in our study. These findings demonstrate a poorer agreement of the AlphaTRAK 2 compared with the previous model and would classify this device as unfit for use according to the standards for over‐the‐counter human glucometer devices.

Similar to earlier findings, 4 there was only a weak negative correlation between hematocrit and POCJUG and POCMUZ. Taking into account the differences in glucose concentration between plasma and erythrocytes, 7 , 8 , 9 a negative association between hematocrit and both POCJUG and POCMUZ would have been expected. Nonetheless, to properly evaluate hematocrit interference in POC glucometry values, a wider range of hematocrits and a larger sample size would have been needed. No association was noted between POCJUG and indirect MAP measurements, and a weak positive association was present between POCMUZ and indirect MAP. A lack of accuracy of capillary (fingerstick) glucose has been previously shown in hypotensive human patients. Capillary glucose values were consistently lower than the laboratory reference in hypotensive human patients, possibly secondary to peripheral vasoconstriction and continued tissue glucose consumption. 12 Unfortunately, 1 or both MAP values were not reported in 17 foals, and only 4 foals were hypotensive, with an MAP <60 mm Hg, which likely limited the power necessary to establish an association between MAP and capillary POC glucometry.

There are several limitations to this study. Perhaps one of the most relevant limitations was the low number of LABGLU samples <75 mg/dL, which prevented a more thorough evaluation of this POC glucometer at lower glucose ranges, wherein many critically ill neonates will lie. Similarly, only a few foals were hypotensive, which limited the ability to investigate the effect of hypotension on capillary blood glucose. The population sampled was not completely homogenous, as 6 healthy hospitalized foals were also included. Different substances, conditions, or drugs, including dopamine, acetaminophen, mannitol, lipemia, and elevated unconjugated bilirubin and uric acid, are known to interfere with POC glucose measurements, 10 , 11 and these were not controlled for in this study. Muzzle edema was a single occurrence in this population but could potentially limit repeated capillary sampling in clinical patients. Sodium fluoride tubes were elected for initial sample processing because fluoride rapidly inhibits enolase, a glycolytic enzyme. Lactate production is immediately halted; however, glucose use continues for the first 90–120 minutes, until ATP depletion occurs. 31 , 32 , 33 Most of the research in glucose stability stems from human samples, and results can be conflicting. 33 , 34 There is a study reporting a similar decrease in glucose in fluoride and heparin tubes in the first hour after collection, with a more rapid decline in heparin tubes thereafter. 35 Another study showed no difference in glucose values between serum separator, fluoride, and heparin tubes after standing for 4 hours at room temperature. 33 Finally, Shi et al found lower glucose values in plasma from sodium fluoride tubes when compared with heparin tubes, with a mean difference of 0.39 mmol/L. 36 When assessing equine samples collected at rest and immediately after exercise, a 6% and 10% decrease in plasma glucose was reported in samples preserved with sodium fluoride compared with lithium heparin. A decrease in erythrocyte volume was also noted in fluoride samples, indicating shrinkage of erythrocytes and dilution of the plasma with intracellular water. 37 To minimize a decrease in glucose concentration, centrifugation, plasma separation, and freezing would have ideally been performed immediately after jugular venipuncture. On the other hand, this could become impractical, especially for samples collected out‐of‐hours. As such, the established study protocol required sample processing as soon as possible and within 1 hour of venous blood collection. In our study, sample storage time was not homogenous because samples were analyzed in 3 separate batches. Samples collected at the University of Wisconsin–Madison and The Ohio State University between March and May of 2019 were stored for up to 9 months, and samples collected at the University of Illinois between February and June of 2020 were stored for up to 7 months before analysis. Sample storage time was longer than initially anticipated, reaching up to 14 months, for plasma samples collected from Anglesey Lodge Equine Hospital in 2020, due to difficulties shipping samples from Europe to the United States. This exceeded the recommended storage time of ≤1 year for glucose analysis from human plasma samples. 38 Finally, even though we presented our results after the tabular data format delineated by the Guidance for Industry and Food and Drug Administration Staff, 2020, these guidelines also recommend testing a POC glucometer with 100–150 neonatal samples, a quantitative threshold we did not reach. 17

In conclusion, the performance of this POC glucometer was suboptimal in the studied population, and POC glucose measurements using the AlphaTRAK 2 glucometera, both venous and capillary, should be interpreted with caution in hospitalized neonatal foals without deriving an RI specifically for this POC glucometer and sampling site. Inaccuracies in the interpretation of POC results could impact clinical decisions in the management of glycemic control in hospitalized neonatal foals and introduce the risk of hypoglycemic events being underdiagnosed. Clinicians should be cognizant of these factors when choosing a POC monitor and sampling site for glucose evaluation.
