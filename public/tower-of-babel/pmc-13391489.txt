Grid maps are disrupted after large-scale arena expansion and recover with experience
Authors: Blanca E. Gutiérrez-Guzmán, J Jesús Hernández-Pérez, Holger Dannenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hexagonal firing pattern of grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) is thought to provide a universal spatial metric supporting vector-based navigation regardless of an environment’s spatial scale. However, how the hexagonal pattern adapts to large changes in the spatial scale of an environment remains elusive. Using single-unit recordings in freely behaving male mice, we show that grid maps are substantially disrupted after large-scale but not medium-scale expansion of the recording arena. The disruption occurs when the scale ratio exceeds the scale ratio of successive grid cell modules, indicating that adaptation of grid cell representations within a module is constrained by a preconfigured spatial scale range of the module. Importantly, grid patterns recover gradually over multiple days of experience in the large-scale expanded arena. These findings challenge the notion of a universal spatial metric by grid cells, instead revealing a flexible system shaped by experience.

Introduction

Grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) contribute to a cognitive map of space by firing at multiple locations that fall on a hexagonal lattice. The spatially periodic spiking activity of grid cells1,2 is widely interpreted as a universal3, preconfigured4,5 and global metric for space3 in support of path integration6–9 and vector-based navigation10. However, more recent experimental data suggest that grid cells provide a local as opposed to a global metric by demonstrating that grid maps expand in novel environments11, appear sheared in asymmetrical environments12,13, overrepresent goal locations14,15, and linearly scale when an enclosure is compressed or stretched along one or two dimensions16. Furthermore, grid cells are organized anatomically and functionally into discretized grid cell modules along the dorso-ventral axis of the MEC, with a scale ratio of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41,$$\end{document}2≈1.41, between modules17. Scaling, distortions and fragmentations of grid maps are permissible within the framework of a malleable universal map18,19 and are consistent with continuous attractor models20–22. However, little is known about the limits, if any, of grid map malleability in response to changes in the spatial scale of an environment. Here, we tested whether the hexagonal firing pattern of grid cells is universal across medium-scale and large-scale expanded arenas. Concretely, we recorded grid cells from mice freely foraging in three differently sized versions of an arena. After exploring a small familiar arena, mice were transferred to either a medium-scale expanded or large-scale expanded arena with floor scale ratios of 1.33 or 1.66 relative to the small familiar arena. The floor dimensions of the small-, medium-, and large-scale arenas were 45×45 cm2, 60×60 cm2, and 75×75 cm2. The scale ratios of 1.33 and 1.66 were selected because they are slightly smaller and larger than the scale ratio of 1.41 observed between successive grid cell modules, respectively. If spatial periodicity in grid cell firing were universal across spatial scales, we would expect either scaling16 or remapping2 of grid maps, dependent on the stoichiometry of internal self-motion cues and external sensory cues in generating spatially periodic firing. If not, we would expect either permanent or transient disruptions of grid maps in the large-scale expanded arena. On a typical recording day, a session was composed of a baseline trial in the small-sized arena, followed by a probe trial in either the medium-scale expanded or large-scale expanded arena, followed by another baseline session in the small-sized arena. Furthermore, we evaluated the effect of the animal’s experience on the tiling of space by grid cells by repeating the experimental protocol over successive days. Finally, to take into account the potential contribution of the walls to the spatial representation of the arena floor, we introduced high- and low-wall versions of the three arenas.

Results

Spatial periodicity in grid cell firing is disrupted in large-scale expanded environments

To our surprise, spatial periodicity in grid cell firing was largely disrupted when an animal transitioned from a small-sized arena with high walls to a large-scale expanded arena with low walls (scale ratio of arena floor: 1.66), quantified as a substantial reduction in the grid score (Fig. 1a, c, h; Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). Because walls can contribute to spatial periodicity in grid cell firing23, we tested whether high walls in the large-scale expanded arena would rescue the disruption in spatial periodicity. We found that grid scores were also substantially reduced in the presence of high walls (Fig. 1b, d, i, Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1), and the extent of the reduction did not differ significantly between the low- and high-wall arenas (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Table 1). Next, we asked whether starting a recording day with the large-scale expanded arena would allow the formation of a grid map representation. However, that was not the case, indicating that the disruption of the grid map was independent of the order of sequential arena exposures (Supplementary Fig. 3). Visual cues and encounters with walls of an environment can correct path integration errors in the grid code9,23–25. We therefore quantified the contribution of wall height to grid scores. We found no significant reduction in grid scores when lowering the wall height of the small-sized arena from 30 cm to 12 cm (Fig. 1e, g, j, l, Supplementary Fig. 2; and Supplementary Table 1). A reduced wall height thus does not explain the substantial reduction in spatial periodicity observed after large-scale expansion of the arena floor. Next, we asked whether the observed grid map disruption is a longer-lasting or a short-term, novelty-related phenomenon. To answer this question, we evaluated changes in grid scores in response to large-scale expansion of the small-sized arena in session blocks two, three, and four, with either high or low walls. As shown in Fig. 1f, k and Supplementary Table 1, the disruption persisted in session blocks 2–4. Despite the disrupted periodicity in the large-scale expanded arena, grid cells continued to exhibit nongrid spatial tuning, with spatial stability and spatial information above chance levels (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Taken together, these results demonstrate that grid cells fail to provide a stable, spatially periodic rate map in large-scale expanded arenas, challenging the notion of universal spatial mapping through the hexagonal firing pattern of grid cells. This finding seems at odds with previous reports that spatial periodicity in grid cell firing is largely invariant across environments1,2, and with the finding that grid maps scale linearly with changes in arena size16. To reconcile our findings with previous reports in the literature on grid map scaling, we next tested whether the observed disruption of grid maps is specific to large-scale arena expansion as opposed to medium-scale expansion. Spatial periodicity is maintained in medium-scale expanded environments

The grid cell representation was largely maintained after animals transitioned from a small-sized arena to a medium-scale expanded arena, irrespective of the wall height (Fig. 2a–e, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2). This result demonstrates that the capability to maintain a grid representation in scaled arenas depends on the scale ratio (Fig. 2f, g, k–i, and Supplementary Table 2). In concordance with a previous report16, grid spacing scaled with the increase in arena floor size (Figs. 2h–j, p and 3j). Concretely, the median changes in grid spacing relative to the expansion in arena size were 75.4% (CI: 26.2–103.3) and 42.3% (CI: 25.2–81.2) after medium-scale expansion to an arena with high and low walls, respectively (Fig. 2p). Interestingly, population-level analyses revealed remapping of grid maps when mice transitioned to the medium-scale expanded arena with low walls (Fig. 2m1, n) but not when they transitioned to the medium-scale expanded arena with high walls (Fig. 2m2, o). These data are in alignment with previous reports that grid maps are anchored to external sensory cues such as environmental boundaries2,12,13,26. Next, we assessed the stability of grid maps across successive recording session blocks 2–4 by evaluating changes in spatial periodicity and grid spacing after transitioning from the small-sized to the medium-scale expanded arena. We found that grid representations remained stable across session blocks 2–4 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Because grid scores can be sensitive to the spatial sampling of the arena, we tested whether the observed difference in grid scores between the large-scale and medium-scale expanded arenas could be explained by a difference in the exploration pattern. The formation of grid maps in large-scale expanded environments depends on experience

Next, we asked whether the disruption of spatially periodic firing of grid cells observed after transitions to large-scaled arenas was permanent or could revert with experience. To answer this question, we continued recordings of grid cell activity in the same mice for up to ~2 months (14 session blocks in four mice). On individual recording days, mice transitioned from the small-sized to the large-scale expanded arena. Results from the last recording session obtained from each mouse showed a re-establishment of the periodic grid cell firing pattern (Fig. 3a–e), with grid scores significantly higher compared to the ones obtained after the first transition session block (S1) (Fig. 3h–i). In addition, spatial information increased with experience in both the small and large-scale expanded arena, and spatial stability increased with experience in the large arena, indicating a progressive strengthening of grid cell spatial tuning (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Intriguingly, the tracking of grid cells across successive recording sessions showed that the spatially periodic firing emerged progressively in the large-scale expanded arena after several days of experience (Figs. 3a–e, h, i l1–l2; Supplementary Fig. 7), irrespective of the wall height of the large-scale expanded arena (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 3). To evaluate the effect of the number of recording sessions performed in the large-scale expanded arena on grid scores, we used a linear mixed-effects model that controlled for random effects of mouse identity, cell identity nested within mouse identity, and the recording date. The model revealed a significant interaction between session number and arena size, indicating a gradual, experience-dependent formation of grid cell representations of the large-scale expanded arena. After mice gained experience with transitions from the small to the large-scale expanded arena, grid cells showed an increase in spacing with respect to the small arena, regardless of the wall height of the expanded arena (Fig. 3f, g, j and Supplementary Table 3). Once grid maps were maintained after large-scale expansion, we could quantify the increase in grid spacing, i.e., the extent to which grid maps scaled. Interestingly, the grid spacing exhibited only a moderate increase after large-scale expansion to arenas with high and low walls (median = 31.4%, CI: 22.9–42.1; median = 24.2%, CI: 12.9–38.8) (Fig. 3k), suggesting that idiothetic self-motion cues exerted a stronger influence on grid cell firing than allothetic landmark cues. Taken together, our data demonstrate that the spatial periodicity in grid cell firing in large-scale expanded environments depends on the animal’s level of familiarity with the environment.

Discussion

Here, we show that the spatially periodic firing of grid cells in mice is substantially disrupted after large-scale expansion of a small familiar arena. These data challenge the supposition that the hexagonal spatial metric conveyed by grid cells is universally maintained across different scaled versions of an environment. However, grid cells continued to exhibit spatially consistent firing patterns, indicating a functional dissociation between spatial periodicity and spatial information of spiking activity (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). The disrupted grid maps resembled firing patterns of nongrid spatial cells reported previously27.

Interestingly, the disruption in the hexagonal firing pattern was only observed if the scale ratio was larger than the scale ratio of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim \sqrt{2}\approx 1.41$$\end{document}~2≈1.41 observed in the geometric progression of grid spacing across grid cell modules (Fig. 1), suggesting that scaling of grid maps in mice is constrained by the spatial scale of the module. While the underlying factors determining the exact value of the scale ratio of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim \sqrt{2}$$\end{document}~2 between modules are unknown, the modular architecture may enable a computationally efficient multiscale representation of space or spatial trajectories28,29. According to these scale-space theories, each module represents a specific spatial scale of the environment. Multi-scale representation by different grid cell modules may thus constrain the extent to which grid spacing in an individual module can adapt to changes in the spatial scale of an arena. Within these constraints, and consistent with previous studies2,16, grid maps can adapt to medium-scale expansion by either rescaling, remapping, or both, with high walls favoring rescaling and low walls favoring both (Fig. 2m–o).

Previous studies report that grid cells’ periodic firing is present in novel arenas similar in size to the large arena used in our study1,12. However, no scaling operations were performed in those studies. In contrast, Barry et al. 16 used an arena of 70 × 100 cm2 in rats to perform a non-uniform expansion along one dimension from 70 cm to 100 cm—a scale ratio of 1.43. This operation is closer to the uniform medium-scale expansion from 45 cm to 60 cm reported here, with a scale ratio of 1.33 in both spatial dimensions. Both the medium-scale expansion performed in this study and the expansion performed by Barry et al. 16 resulted in scaling of grid maps. However, only the large-scale expansion performed in the present study with a scale ratio of 1.66 resulted in severe disruption of grid maps, suggesting that i) scaling of grid maps has a sharp limit, and ii) grid maps cannot be maintained beyond that limit. In our study, the scaled arena versions differed only with respect to the arena dimensions; the materials of the walls, the color- texture of the floor, the distal cues in the recording room, and the recording room itself remained constant. In contrast, studies investigating novelty effects on grid cell representations employ arenas of comparable size that differ in geometry, texture, odor, material, and/or visual cues. If the novel arenas are markedly different from the familiar one, entorhinal grid maps expand11 and re-align—accompanied by global remapping of hippocampal place cells2,30. Previous studies24,31 reported hexagonal grid cell firing in arenas of comparable or larger size than the large arena used in this study. However, mice in those studies were familiar with the recording environment. These findings are thus consistent with our results showing stable grid maps after approximately 14 session blocks of experience. Taken together with those previous findings, our data support the conclusion that it is the large-scale expansion of the recording arena as opposed to the large spatial scale of the arena per se that results in the disruption of the grid map. Experimental data and computational modeling results suggest that grid maps are generated by integrating both idiothetic cues derived from self-motion information and external landmark information32,33. While internal cues provide the primary drive for spatial periodicity, external cues act in an error-corrective manner to stabilize and realign the grid representation34. One explanation for the observed grid map disruption after large-scale expansion of a familiar arena is that the mismatch between path integration information from internal cues and landmark information from external cues becomes too large to be resolved. A previous study investigating the relative contribution of self-motion cues and visual landmark cues on spatial periodicity of grid maps showed that grid cells maintained a spatially periodic firing pattern that was largely uncoupled from the landmark reference frame when optic flow gain was substantially reduced32. Although reduced optic flow gain in a virtual environment is similar to expanding arena size, virtual reality systems lack the physical boundaries that help reset the phase of grid maps in real environments13,23,35. In the present study, encounters with the walls of the environment may have reset the phase of grid maps in a manner that is incoherent with internal and external self-motion cues. An alternative explanation is that mice may engage grid cell modules with small grid spacing to represent small arenas but grid cell modules with large spacing to represent large arenas. Notably, in our study, grid spacing in the small arena was as small as ~18 cm, with a median value of ~22 cm (Figs. 2h, i and 3f, g), which is smaller than a median of ~40 cm previously reported in mice36. There is thus a possibility that additional experience is required to represent large-size arenas by grid cells with small spacing. This would imply a functional dissociation between grid cell modules, which has been observed in rats for the most dorsal grid cell modules17. A third alternative explanation is that the observed disruption is specific to mice. While fundamental properties of grid cells, such as spacing and their systematic increase along the dorsoventral axis of the MEC, are comparable between mice36 and rats1, anatomical and physiological differences in the hippocampal formation have been reported. For instance, mice exhibit decreased long-term stability of place cells37 and tend to form multiple fields with lower spatial information during the exploration of novel routes38, demonstrating differences in spatial coding. While a comprehensive comparison of grid cell responses to different environmental manipulations between mice and rats has not yet been conducted, we cannot rule out species-specific differences in how grid cells respond to environmental manipulations.

Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, the observed disruption of grid maps after large-scale expansion suggests that grid cells do not provide a universal spatial metric a priori of spatial experience as previously suggested4,5. Intriguingly, the longitudinal evaluation of grid cell representations in our study revealed that a spatially periodic firing pattern progressively emerged as animals gained experience over multiple sessions on successive days in exploring the large-scale environment (Fig. 3a–e, h, i, l2). This plasticity may imply a capacity for experience-dependent reorganization of grid cell dynamics, likely mediated by slow synaptic changes in the MEC or its afferent circuits. It would be a worthwhile endeavor for future studies to investigate the detailed physiological mechanisms underlying the disruption and slow recovery of grid cell representations in large-scale expanded environments. Previous studies have shown that grid maps can be reshaped by experience11,16,39,40. However, grid maps in those studies were present at all times across different environments. In contrast, here we show that recovery of grid maps after disruption through large-scale arena expansion requires multiple days of experience. Interestingly, a recent developmental study investigating grid cell firing showed that spatially periodic firing in rats raised in an opaque spherical environment appeared only after multiple days of experience in an arena with clear geometric boundaries41, suggesting experience-dependent mechanisms could underlie both phenomena. This reinforces the view that grid cells integrate both information from internal cues derived from self-motion and information from external cues in the environment. Experience-dependent plasticity may change how external cues change grid cell dynamics driven by internal self-motion cues. Taken together, our findings challenge the universality of the grid code and argue against the notion that grid cells provide an a priori substrate for spatial cognition, instead reinforcing the view of a flexible and learned grid code shaped by geometric structure, boundary constraints, and experience.

Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the regulations stated in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Research Council and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of George Mason University (Protocol No.: 0501, approved on January 24, 2022).

Six male mice (Mus musculus), 3.5–7 months old (four wild type, C57BL/6 J, and two heterozygous transgenic mice (ChAT-Cretg/wt, B6.129S-Chattm1(cre)Lowl/MwarJ; The Jackson Laboratory) were used. Prior to surgery, mice were housed together with their siblings in Plexiglas cages on a 12-h light/dark cycle at a room temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and a 50 ± 20% humidity level, with food and water provided ad libitum. After surgery, mice were housed individually in large cages on a 12-h reversed light/dark cycle. Cages contained a spherical treadmill for physical exercise and as environmental enrichment. All mice were handled and habituated to the experimenter and testing room prior to the start of behavioral experiments and electrophysiological data acquisition. To increase the motivation in mice to explore the arena, bits of cereal were randomly placed on the arena floor, and mice were mildly food-deprived starting one day before the start of behavioral experiments, so that their body weight was ≥95% the weight of their littermates with ad libitum access to food.

Surgery and electrodes

Microdrives were implanted as previously reported24,42. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 1–2% maintenance) and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus. Physiological body temperature (37 °C) was monitored and maintained during the surgery via a heating pad and a homeothermic monitoring system. A craniotomy was performed, and four mice were chronically implanted with an Axona microdrive (Axona, St. Albans, UK) containing four movable tetrodes and two mice with OpenEphys shuttle drive with eight movable tetrodes, made of twisted 17-µm coated platinum wires (impedance adjusted to 180–250 kOhm), targeting the superficial layers of the left MEC at the following stereotaxic coordinates: 3.4 mm lateral to the midline and 0.5 mm anterior to the transverse sinus, angled at a polar angle of six degrees. The tetrode tips were lowered 1 mm from the brain surface. A stainless-steel screw implanted in the skull above the cerebellum contacting the dura mater, served as a ground electrode. The microdrive was secured to the skull using four additional anchoring screws and dental cement. For an additional experimental purpose not reported in this study, two mice were injected with an adeno-associated virus coding for the calcium indicator GCaMP8s into the medial septum and implanted with an optical fiber placed above the medial septum. No differences were found in those two mice with respect to the electrophysiological and behavioral metrics used in this study. Mice were allowed to recover from surgery for one week before the start of behavioral experiments and data acquisition.

Behavioral task

Extracellular recordings of action potentials (spikes) in the MEC were carried out while mice randomly foraged for scattered pieces of cereal bits (Froot Loops, Kellogg Company, Battle Creek, MI, USA) in different sizes of black open-field environments. Recording sessions lasted until animals had sampled a sufficiently large area of the open field, typically 20–45 min. One visual cue card (a white triangle) was placed on one of the walls of the open-field enclosure. All enclosures were centered in the same location on a black floor mat in the same room with the same distal cues, materials of the enclosures, and dim light. To test the effect of scaling the arena size on grid cells, data were acquired while mice explored a sequence of square arenas with different sizes namely a small arena, 45 × 45 cm2, with either 30 cm high or 12 cm low walls (Small-high or Small-low, respectively); a medium arena, 60 × 60 cm2, with 45 cm high or 15 cm low walls (Medium-high or Medium-low, respectively); and a large arena, 75 × 75 cm2, with 45 cm high or 20-cm low walls (Large-high or Large-low, respectively). The height of the walls was adjusted to maintain an approximately constant angle, measured from the arena center to the top of the walls with respect to the ground plane. In a square arena, this is equivalent to keeping the ratio of wall height to arena floor dimension approximately constant. In the small, medium, and large arenas, the ratios were 12 cm/45 cm = 0.27, 15 cm /60 cm = 0.25, and 20 cm /75 cm = 0.27, respectively.

Most frequently, four main sets of sequences of different arenas (session blocks) were used: 1) Small-high to Large-low to Small-high; 2) Small-high to Large-high to Small-high; 3) Small-high to Medium-low to Small-high; and 4) Small-high to Medium-high to Small-high. Each of these sequences started and ended with the small arena with high walls (Small Pre and Small Post, respectively). Less frequently, additional sets of sequences were evaluated (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4c, 6). In between sessions, mice were allowed to rest in their home cages in the same room. The interval between individual sessions was 20–30 min. To compare naïve and experienced mice, we analyzed data from the first session block, when mice initially transitioned from the small to the large-scale expanded arena, and from the final session block, when mice were fully experienced. Between sessions, the floor of the open field was thoroughly cleaned with 70% isopropanol.

Neural recordings

A total of 141 grid cells were recorded in six mice during the entire experimental protocol. The total numbers of cells per mouse were: mouse#1 = 29; mouse#2 = 29; mouse#3 = 8; mouse#4 = 2; mouse#5 = 17; mouse#6 = 56. Each grid cell was assigned an ID: mouse#1 [cell ID 1–29]; mouse#2 [cell ID 51–79]; mouse#3 [cell ID 91–98]; mouse#4 [cell ID 40,42]; mouse#5 [cell ID 101–117]: mouse#6 [cell ID 121–176] (Supplementary Table 4).

Neural recordings and analysis of grid cells24 were performed as follows. Tetrodes were lowered daily in increments of 50 micrometers until multiunit spiking activity was detected, indicating that tetrodes had reached the superficial layer in the MEC. The presence of grid cells was assessed by shorter ~20 min screening sessions using the small arena with high walls. In 1 out of 6 mice, the medium-size arena was used for a total of four screening sessions. Once grid cells were present, the full set of arena sequences (session blocks) was employed in all 6 mice. As long as at least one grid cell was observed in the small-sized arena, recording sessions continued the next day. If grid cells were no longer observed in the small-sized arena, the electrodes were further advanced ~25 µm to find new grid cells. Data acquisition ended when spiking activity indicated that all tetrode tips had reached layer I of the MEC.

Neural signals were pre-amplified by unity-gain operational amplifiers located on each animal’s head (head stages) and AC-wire-coupled to an Axona recording system (Axona Limited, St. Albans, UK). Spikes were threshold-detected and recorded at 48 kHz. The position and head direction of mice were captured using a ceiling-mounted video camera to record the position of invisible infrared LEDs on the animal headstage (at 50 Hz) and tracking software (Axona, UK).

Spikes were clustered into single units using spike clustering software (DacqUSB/Tint, Axona, St. Albans, UK). Further offline analyses were performed using Matlab (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Histology

After data collection, animals were anesthetized deeply with isoflurane, and a transcranial perfusion with DPBS 1X followed by 10% buffered formalin was performed. To visualize tetrode track and MEC recording sites, sagittal brain sections (40 µm) were sliced using a vibratome (Leica VT1000S) and stained with a Cresyl violet solution (Supplementary Fig. 8). Brain sections were imaged using a Leica S9i stereomicroscope equipped with an integrated digital camera. Slides were placed on the stage and illuminated using brightfield illumination. Magnification was set to 3x, and the focus was adjusted to optimize visualization of tetrode tracks and cytoarchitecture. A scale bar calibration was performed using a ruler.

Analysis of single-unit spiking activity

Spatial firing rate maps

The occupancy-normalized spatial firing rate maps were generated by dividing the open-field environment into 1.5 cm spatial bins. For each spatial bin, an occupancy-normalized firing rate was computed by dividing the total number of spikes falling into that bin by the total time the animal spent in that bin. Spatial rate maps were then smoothed by a 2 cm wide two-dimensional Gaussian kernel.

Grid score

The grid score was calculated as “gridness measure 3”, described in detail in Brandon et al. 41. This score quantifies the hexagonal spatial periodicity in the autocorrelation of the spatial rate map after correcting for elliptical eccentricity. The score was computed through the following steps. We extracted a ring that encased the six peaks closest to the center peak, but excluding the central peak. The radius of the inner circle was defined as half of the mean distance to the six peaks closest to center, where none of the six peaks could exceed 1.5 times the distance to the first. If there were fewer than six peaks, only those detected were used. The outer radius was defined as the distance to the farthest detected peak from center plus the width of the center peak, which was the same as the radius of the inner circle. To correct for elliptical eccentricity, we first determined the major and minor axes of the grid based on the six closest fields to the central peak of the rate map autocorrelogram and then compressed the autocorrelogram along the major axis so that it became equal to the minor axis. Large eccentricities where the minor axis was less than half of the major axis were not corrected. From the compressed autocorrelogram, we extracted a ring of the six closest peaks to the center peak. A rotational autocorrelation of this ring was calculated to observe the periodicity in paired pixel correlations across 180 degrees of rotation. The grid score was computed as the difference between the highest correlation observed at 30, 90, or 150 degrees of rotation and the lowest correlation observed at 60 or 120 degrees of rotation. A negative grid score indicates no measurable hexagonal spatial periodicity of the rate map. Cells with a grid score ≥ 0.33 in at least one of the two baseline (pre or post) sessions in the small arena (45 × 45 × 30 cm3) with at least 3 spatial fields were classified as grid cells. A null distribution of grid scores was generated using spike-shuffled data (Supplementary Fig. 10). Spike shuffling was performed as described below.

The major analyses were repeated using an alternative grid score24 (Grid score #2). This score differs from the primary grid score in that it is calculated—via the fast Fourier transform—as the power at 60 degrees in the power spectrum of the rate map autocorrelogram—after applying the same preprocessing steps used for the primary grid score, namely the correction for elliptical distortion and extraction of a ring encasing the six peaks closest to the center. An advantage of this grid score is that it ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 and 1 corresponding to no and perfect six-fold spatial periodicity in the grid map, respectively. The score avoids negative grid scores and—by incorporating correlation values across the full 360 degrees of rotation—reduces the error of falsely assigning excessively high or low grid scores to rate maps. A null distribution of grid scores was generated using spike-shuffled data (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Spike shuffling was performed as described below.

Null distribution and spike-shuffling procedure

We computed null distributions of grid scores, spatial stability, and spatial information using a spike-shuffling approach4,43. To generate the null distributions, we used spike-shuffled data pooled across all sessions from the first session block (S1) in the large and medium arenas (each with high and low walls), as well as all sessions from the final session block in the large arena (with high and low walls). For spatial stability and spatial information, null distributions were computed separately for each arena size. We used 100 permutations per session. For each permutation, the entire spike sequence of the cell was time-shifted by a random interval between 40 s and the total trial length minus 40 s, with the trial wrapped end-to-start to allow circular displacements. This procedure preserves the temporal structure of the spiking data while disrupting the relationship between spikes and spatial position.

Grid spacing

The grid spacing was calculated from the spatial autocorrelogram by identifying the center of mass for each of the six closest fields and calculating the distance of the fields peak from the center peak. Because the autocorrelogram is symmetric, the average distance of the three consecutive peaks to the center was calculated.

Spatial crosscorrelation

The spatial crosscorrelation was calculated to evaluate the similarity of grid maps of the same grid cell across different sessions or environments. This analysis requires that grid maps have the same number of spatial bins. To match the number of spatial bins when comparing grid maps of different arena sizes, we increased the bin size (i.e., reduced the spatial resolution) of the grid map of the larger arena in proportion to the scale ratio. The spatial crosscorrelation was quantified by computing Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the two grid maps. To quantify the phase shift between two grid maps of the same cell, we measured the distance between the central peak in the spatial crosscorrelogram and the origin, which is directly proportional to the phase shift between the two grid maps.

Percent increase in grid spacing relative to change in arena size

To quantify how much grid spacing scales proportionally to arena size, we computed the percent increase in grid spacing normalized to the percentage of change in arena size. A value of 1 (or 100%) means grid spacing scales proportionally to arena size; a value of <1 (or <100%) means grid spacing increases less than arena size (partial rescaling).

Spatial stability

Spatial stability of grid cells was quantified as the correlation (Pearson’s r) between rate maps computed from the first and second halves of each recording session, providing a measure of the reliability of spatial firing for each cell. For each arena size, a null distribution of spatial stability scores was generated using spike-shuffled data (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). Spike shuffling was performed as described above.

Spatial information

Spatial information was computed in bits per spike44 using code from the CMBHOME toolbox. Rate maps were occupancy-weighted and smoothed with a 2 cm wide Gaussian kernel. The spatial bin size for constructing rate maps in the large arena was scaled from 1.5 cm to 2.5 cm. Spatial bins with occupancy <0.25 s were excluded from the analysis. For each arena size, a null distribution of spatial information scores was generated using spike-shuffled data (Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). Spike shuffling was performed as described above.

Statistical analysis

Results of statistical analyses are reported in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 3. The type III fixed-effects analysis using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures, followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests, was performed in GraphPad Prism 8.0—using a compound symmetry covariance matrix and the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. If no values are missing in the repeated measures, this method gives the same p-values and multiple comparisons results as a repeated-measures ANOVA. In the presence of missing values, the results can be interpreted in the same way as a repeated-measures ANOVA. In addition, nonparametric statistical tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test were applied using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1). The linear mixed-effects model associated with data presented in Fig. 3l3 was implemented in MATLAB. All statistical tests were two-sided.
