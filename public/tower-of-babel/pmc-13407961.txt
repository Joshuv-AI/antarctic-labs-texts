Comparative Risk Assessment of Marginal Gingival Thickness and Accumulation of Plaque in Preschool Children: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Krishna Salil, Jesudass Govada, Suneetha Motru, PVM Uday Mohan, Pujari Manasa, Roja Ramani Nutakki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundGingival thickness varies among individuals and teeth, potentially influencing plaque retention and gingivitis risk, yet its impact in young children remains underexplored. This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the relationship between marginal gingival thickness and plaque accumulation in deciduous molars of preschool children aged 3–5 years.Subjects and methodsA total of 360 caries-free deciduous molars (180 maxillary and 180 mandibular) from systemically healthy children were examined. Marginal gingival thickness was measured using the transgingival probing method with a #15 endodontic reamer and digital vernier caliper after applying 2% local anesthetic gel. Plaque scores were assessed using Silness and Löe's plaque index. Independent t-tests compared gingival thickness and plaque scores between left and right molars, while Pearson's correlation analyzed their relationship.ResultsNo statistically significant differences in gingival thickness or plaque scores were observed between left and right maxillary or mandibular first and second molars. A moderately strong positive correlation was found between gingival thickness and plaque scores in the majority of examined teeth. Other teeth showed weak to very weak correlations.ConclusionThe gingival thickness is generally seen as thinner phenotype in primary dentition and observed less plaque accumulation around the teeth. Majority of the observed samples have a very weak to moderately strong positive correlation between gingival thickness and plaque scores.Clinical significanceThe gingival phenotype generally observed in preschool children is a thinner phenotype; thus, more regular oral hygiene interventions should be followed to avoid the risk of gingival diseases.Keywords: Deciduous molar, Plaque, Plaque scores, Preschool children, Primary tooth, Risk assessment

Introduction

Gingiva is a section of mucosal tissue that surrounds the alveolar bone of jaws and environs the cervical portion of teeth. Structurally, it is divided into three components: (1) free gingiva, (2) attached gingiva, and (3) interdental (ID) gingiva—each serving vital protective and supportive functions.1 The thickness of marginal gingiva varies among individuals and also between different teeth. Several factors, including gender, developmental stage, and age, influence gingival thickness (GT).2 In primary teeth, free gingiva is thicker compared to permanent teeth, which can be attributed to more pronounced cervical bulge and narrower cementoenamel junction (CEJ).3 The typical threshold for distinguishing between thick and thin gingival phenotypes is 1 mm.4 Scientific studies have demonstrated that the free gingiva in primary teeth is thicker than permanent teeth because of these anatomical differences—specifically, the noticeable cervical bulge and the restricted CEJ. There are various methods to assess the marginal GT, such as invasive and noninvasive methods.5,6 Marginal gingival region is a common site of plaque accumulation. When plaque accumulates on the marginal gingiva, it leads to an inflammatory response known as gingivitis, characterized by redness, swelling, bleeding on probing, and potential tissue damage. If not managed in time, this inflammation may progress to periodontitis, leading to loss of attachment, development of periodontal pockets, and progressive destruction of tooth-supporting structures. The impact of thickness variation on the accumulation of debris or plaque along the gingival margin in preschool children remains unclear. Research objective was to evaluate and compare risk associated with marginal GT and plaque accumulation in children aged 3–6 years using transgingival probing technique.

Methodology

This was a prospective cross-sectional observational study. Before initiation of the study, written informed consent was taken from parents or guardians of each participant. After anesthetizing gingival margin with a 2% local anesthetic gel, marginal GT was measured via the transgingival probing method. Local anesthetic injections were avoided to prevent a temporary, anesthesia-induced increase in gingival volume. An endodontic reamer #15 was used to perform this procedure. The reamer was inserted into the soft tissue, perpendicular to the gingival margin in midbuccal region, until it encountered hard tissue and resistance was felt. Silicone stopper was fixed onto reamer to prevent any unintended horizontal or vertical displacement and to reduce measurement errors. Once resistance was encountered, reamer was gently withdrawn, and distance from its tip to inner edge of silicone stopper was estimated using digital vernier caliper with precision of 0.01 mm (Figs 1 and 2).

Discussion

According to existing literature, gingival biotype plays a significant role in occurrence of mucogingival issues and influences effectiveness of treatments for recession as well as wound healing. In recent years, gingiva's aesthetic characteristics—particularly its thickness and width—have gained importance from both therapeutic and epidemiological viewpoints. A comprehensive understanding of gingival biotype is essential for clinicians to ensure optimal treatment outcomes.7

According to Shah et al.,8 gingival biotype is classified into two categories: thick and thin. Thick biotype features a thick bone structure and a flat soft tissue contour. Due to its dense and fibrous composition, this type is less prone to gingival recession. In contrast, thin biotype is marked by a slender bony structure, presence of fenestrations, and soft tissue that is delicate and finely textured. It is more vulnerable to inflammation, bleeding, and recession. Gingival or periodontal illnesses are more likely to develop in patients with a thin gingival biotype.7

In the current study, no statistically significant variation in GT was observed among maxillary and mandibular first and second molars, suggesting a consistent GT across posterior teeth. The participants in this study demonstrated a thin gingival phenotype, as average GT recorded was below 1 mm. Research conducted by Shivani and Laxman5 assessed GT based on individual teeth, arches, and entire dentition without separating tooth-wise and arch-wise data. The findings showed that GT was highest in mixed dentition phase, measuring 1.3 mm mesiobuccally (MB) and 2.3 mm interdentally, followed by primary (1.1 mm MB and 1.7 mm ID), and then permanent (1.0 mm MB and 1.3 mm ID). Study concluded that GT rises progressively from primary to mixed dentition stages. Dridi et al.9 in their study revealed that overall, 39% of the children in the study group (those under age of 16 years) had thin gingivae based on the probe visibility technique, with primary and permanent dentition showing 15.3 and 49.3%, respectively. Lambrou et al.,10 when assessing the GT and gingival width in children using ultrasonography, found that the gingiva is noticeably thicker in maxillary teeth than mandibular teeth, permanent teeth than primary teeth, and posterior teeth than anterior teeth.

Present study observed no statistically significant differences in plaque accumulation between maxillary and mandibular first and second molars. Moreover, the study participants had less plaque formation around the teeth. When the correlation between GT and plaque scores were assessed, a very weak to moderately strong positive correlation was observed between GT and plaque scores among the posteriors except left mandibular first and second molars. However, left mandibular first molar and second molar teeth showed weak negative and very weak negative correlation, respectively.

Marginal gingivitis is most prevalent type of periodontal disease that begins in early childhood. Even though a lot of people have mild, curable gingivitis, severe gingivitis in children is quite rare. Bacterial plaque, both calcified and uncalcified, is the primary etiologic factor linked to periodontal disorders and gingivitis.5 The periodontium, dentition, mucogingival complex, and jaw bones all change constantly as a child develops. The development of proper proportions of this intricate component is aided by eruption of permanent teeth and heightening of alveolar process. Significant changes in periodontium's morphological structure and gingival phenotype take place between the ages of 6 and 12 years. The gingiva's physiological thinning appears to make the period between primary and permanent teeth a crucial one. Correct tooth placement in the arch determines the maintenance of a healthy mucogingival complex and periodontium.11

Almost no research has been done to evaluate how gingival phenotype affects plaque buildup. Thus, the current study is a novelty in evaluating the effect of gingival phenotype on plaque accumulation. Thus, further research on this topic is necessary.

Limitations and Future Research

Certain limitations should be considered while interpreting the findings of the present study. The investigation was carried out in a single center with a comparatively restricted sample population, which may limit the applicability of the results to broader pediatric groups. As the study followed a cross-sectional design, the observations represent only a single time-point assessment and do not permit evaluation of temporal changes or causative relationships between gingival thickness and plaque accumulation. Furthermore, inclusion was limited to systemically healthy children with caries-free primary molars, thereby excluding children with different oral or systemic conditions that could influence gingival characteristics. Another limitation is that the transgingival probing technique, although widely used, may be affected by examiner sensitivity and child cooperation.

Future investigations should include multicentric longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse populations. The use of advanced noninvasive diagnostic methods and assessment of additional periodontal parameters may further improve the understanding of the association between gingival phenotype and plaque accumulation in children.

Conclusion

The GT is generally seen as a thinner phenotype in primary dentition, and less plaque accumulation is observed around the teeth. The current study noted no statistical differences in plaque accumulation among the maxillary and mandibular first and second molars. Even though statistical significance was not evident, a very weak to moderately strong positive correlation was observed between GT and plaque scores among majority of the molars studied. More studies with larger sample sizes are required to establish the correlation with marginal GT and plaque accumulation in children.

Clinical Significance

Marginal GT in children is clinically significant as it influences periodontal health and susceptibility to inflammation. Thicker gingiva provides better resistance to mechanical trauma and plaque-induced gingivitis, reducing the risk of periodontal disease progression. Plaque accumulation, a primary cause of gingivitis, is more challenging to manage in children with thinner gingiva due to increased vulnerability to irritation. Effective oral hygiene practices are critical to minimize plaque buildup and maintain healthy gingival tissues in pediatric populations.
