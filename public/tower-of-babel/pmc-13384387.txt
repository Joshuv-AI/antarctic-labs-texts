Hyperadhesive von Willebrand Factor Contributes to Pathogenesis of Preeclampsia
Authors: Yajuan Wang, Chenyu Wang, Katie L. Houck, Xiaoli Gao, Yuanyuan Chen, Xin Xu, Xue Zhao, Miguel A. Cruz, Shu Zhang, Chester Q. Li, Fengxia Xue, Min Li, Swati Shree, Jing-fei Dong, Cha Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Preeclampsia is the most common complication of pregnancy, significantly affecting maternal and fetal health, and is characterized by placental and systemic endotheliopathy. Patients with preeclampsia have elevated levels of VWF (von Willebrand Factor), which is associated with poor clinical outcomes. However, whether VWF serves as a marker for endotheliopathy or contributes to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia remains poorly understood.METHODS:We investigated the role of hyperadhesive VWF in the development of preeclampsia by studying patients, evaluating mouse models, and performing in vitro experiments.RESULTS:We show that patients develop VWF- and fibrin-rich thrombosis in the placenta and have significantly elevated levels of VWF adhesive activity and placenta-derived extracellular vesicles. In mouse models, pregnant wild-type mice infused with hyperadhesive VWF alone, or in combination with placenta-derived extracellular vesicles, developed a preeclampsia-like condition, which was reduced by the VWF-cleaving metalloprotease ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif 13). Furthermore, ADAMTS13-deficient mice with high baseline VWF (ADAMTS13−/−/casa) developed a preeclampsia-like condition spontaneously, with VWF adhesive activity increasing 5.6-fold and placenta-derived extracellular vesicles increasing 2.9-fold during late pregnancy. VWF became hyperadhesive during pregnancy by undergoing conformational changes and promoted preeclampsia-associated endotheliopathy by enhancing the interaction of placenta-derived extracellular vesicles with endothelial cells.CONCLUSIONS:This study demonstrates that hyperadhesive VWF plays a causal role in preeclampsia and is a potential therapeutic target.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The study demonstrates a causal role for VWF (von Willebrand Factor)–ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif 13) imbalance in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia, primarily through the synergistic action of hyperadhesive VWF and placenta-derived extracellular vesicles. The study suggests that baseline levels and adhesive activity of VWF before pregnancy may serve as a predictive marker for preeclampsia. It also suggests that exogenous ADAMTS13 could be considered an adjuvant therapy for patients with preeclampsia, especially those with significantly elevated VWF levels and adhesive activity, or those with reduced ADAMTS13 levels and proteolytic activity.

Preeclampsia is a pregnancy-specific disorder characterized by poor placentation and endothelial dysfunction.1,2 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists defines clinical preeclampsia as hypertension with evidence of other organ dysfunction after 20 weeks of gestation.3,4 Preeclampsia can progress into more severe conditions of eclampsia and hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count syndrome, resulting in convulsions, organ failure, and maternal and fetal death.5 Preeclampsia affects 2% to 8% of pregnancies and causes >70 000 maternal deaths and 500 000 fetal deaths worldwide every year.3,6,7

The clinical conditions of preeclampsia improve significantly after delivery or pregnancy termination, suggesting that placenta-derived factors play a key role in its pathogenesis.8–10 Placenta-derived extracellular vesicles (pcEVs) from injured placentas cause hypertension, proteinuria, and kidney injury in pregnant C57BL/6J mice.11 However, pcEVs are constitutively released during normal pregnancy and are necessary for inducing maternal adaptive changes,12,13 suggesting that they become detrimental when released excessively into the maternal circulation.14,15 It is unknown how pcEVs interact with maternal endothelial cells to induce vascular injury and hypertension, given that syncytiotrophoblasts from the outer layer of placental villi and their derivative pcEVs11 do not express ligands or counter-receptors for endothelial cells.

We have previously reported that patients with preeclampsia have increased levels and activity of the adhesive ligand VWF (von Willebrand Factor) compared with women with normal pregnancies.16The key link between VWF and preeclampsia lies in patients with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because TTP is more common in women,17,18 it is often triggered by pregnancy to develop acute thrombotic crisis,19,20 and its clinical presentations overlap with those of preeclampsia.21 More importantly, TTP is caused by hyperadhesive VWF accumulated in the circulation due to deficiency of the VWF-cleaving metalloprotease ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif 13).22 Patients with preeclampsia also have increased levels and activity of plasma VWF with no or moderately reduced ADAMTS13,23–26 resulting in a significantly reduced ADAMTS13-to-VWF ratio, which is associated with poor clinical outcomes of preeclampsia. One multicenter study associated low ADAMTS13 activity with preeclampsia with an odds ratio of 4.2.27 These clinical observational studies link VWF to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia but are limited in providing mechanistic insight. We designed a mechanistic study to fill this knowledge gap by testing the hypotheses that (1) VWF release is increased from injured placental endothelial cells (and syncytiotrophoblasts28) and is hyperadhesive because of insufficient cleavage by ADAMTS13; (2) hyperadhesive VWF binds to pcEVs to mediate their interaction with endothelial cells, facilitating pcEV-induced endotheliopathy and hypertension; and (3) ADAMTS13 attenuates the severity of preeclampsia by reducing the hyperadhesive activity of VWF.

Patients

Patients with preeclampsia were recruited from Tianjin Medical University, Baylor College of Medicine, and the University of Washington after informed consent was obtained. Preeclampsia was diagnosed based on American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists guidelines,4 with the following inclusion criteria: pregnant women with systolic/diastolic blood pressure of ≥140/90 mm Hg and proteinuria. The exclusion criteria were: (1) pregnancies initiated with assisted reproductive technologies; (2) chronic hypertension before pregnancy; (3) existing hemostatic abnormalities, cancer, diabetes, and cardiovascular, autoimmune, renal or liver diseases; and (4) use of aspirin or anticoagulant prophylaxis during pregnancies. Control samples were from women with normal pregnancies who showed neither clinical alterations nor a history of hypertension (pregnancy control), and age-matched healthy, nonpregnant women during the nonmenstrual period (baseline control).

Maternal blood samples (0.38% sodium citrate anticoagulant) were collected at diagnosis before administration of antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate, or antihypertensive medications. Placental tissues were collected for histopathology after informed consent was received. Cord blood was obtained from the Bloodworks Cord Blood Bank. These studies were approved by the institutional review boards of all 3 institutions.

Mouse Models

Three lines of mice were studied: wild-type C57BL/6J (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME), ADAMTS13 deficiency on C57BL/6J background (ADAMTS13−/−/B6, B6.129-Adamts13tm1Dgi/J, Jackson Laboratory, Figure S1), and ADAMTS13 deficiency on CASA/Rk background (ADAMTS1−/−/CASA/Rk provided by Dr David Ginsburg, University of Michigan). The ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice contained high baseline levels of VWF,29 whereas VWF in the ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice was comparable to that of the C57BL/6J mice. These mice were maintained on LabDiet5K52 diet (LabDiet, Richmond, IN), which contains ground wheat, corn, fish meal, and dehydrated alfalfa meal. They were kept in a pathogen-free vivarium with a 12-hour light-dark cycle at ≈22 °C (4 mice per cage). Pregnancy cages were set up with mice of 11 weeks of age, at a female-to-male ratio of 3:1. Complete blood count of pregnant C57BL/6J mice was measured at baseline, 17 to 19 days postcoitum (dpc), and immediately postpartum (Table S1) using an ABX Micros 60 blood analyzer (Horiba Medical, Montpellier, France).

To validate a role of VWF in preeclampsia without intrinsic ADAMTS13 deficiency, pregnant C57BL/6J mice were infused at 17 to 19 dpc with 5 µg per mouse of rhVWF (recombinant human VWF, Baxalta, West Sacramento, CA, or Neoletix Biological Technologies, Beijing, China), pcEVs (1×107 per mouse) generated from injured placenta (Supplemental Methods),11 or VWF+pcEVs and 2 µg per mouse of rhADAMTS13 (recombinant human ADAMTS13). These VWF and pcEV dosages were chosen to be consistent with their levels in blood samples of patients with preeclampsia.16 The rhVWF used for the study is hyperadhesive (Figure S2) because it is not cleaved by ADAMTS13. It also does not contain coagulation factor VIII, which may influence VWF cleavage and contribute to the hypercoagulable state developed during pregnancy.30 RhADAMTS13 was expressed in HeLa cells transfected with ADAMTS13 cDNA (complementary deoxyribonucleic acid; Supplemental Methods). The rhADAMTS13 dosage was chosen based on dose-titration experiments, in which blood samples from mice receiving different doses of rhADAMTS13 were examined for VWF activity, as measured by VWF collagen-binding (VWF:CB) assay.

For both models, we defined a mouse preeclampsia-like phenotype as hypertension, proteinuria, and kidney injury detected at 17 to 19 dpc,11 for consistency with the clinical classification of preeclampsia.1 We used a noninvasive mouse tail-cuff analyzer (Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT) to measure blood pressure.11,31 Urine samples were collected through urethral catheterization and analyzed for albumin and creatinine to detect proteinuria. Blood samples (100 µL per draw) were collected from the retro-orbital sinus at baseline, 17 to 19 dpc, and 6 hours postpartum (0.38% sodium citrate as anticoagulant) from pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk and ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice, or at 30 minutes after pregnant C57BL/6J mice (17–19 dpc) were infused with rhVWF, pcEVs, or VWF+pcEVs. As a control, some pregnant C57BL/6J mice receiving VWF+pcEVs also received 5 µg per mouse of polyclonal anti-VWF antibody (Dako A0082, Agilent Technologies, West Hills, CA), which prevents VWF+pcEVs from causing endothelial injury in mice32 and blocks the adhesion of human platelets to VWF strings formed on activated human endothelial cells.33 The blood samples were first analyzed for blood counts and platelet markers within 2 hours after the blood draws. The rest was centrifuged at 2000g for 20 minutes at room temperature (RT) to collect cell-free plasma for measuring VWF antigen (VWF:Ag), VWF:CB, coagulation, and levels of placental and platelet extracellular vesicles (EVs). The placenta and kidneys were collected at 17 to 19 dpc, immediately after euthanasia under anesthesia, infused with 50 mL of warmed PBS through cardiac puncture to remove blood from the vasculature, and then processed for histopathology.

PcEVs From Syncytiotrophoblasts

Cells from the clonal BeWo line (ATCC, Manassas, VA), which is derived from human placental epithelial cells of choriocarcinoma that express syncytium-specific proteins,34 were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS until ≈80% confluence. They were then incubated with serum-free medium in 2% O2 for 8 hours and then in 8% O2 for 16 hours (×2 cycles) in a 3-gas culture incubator (Thermo Fisher, Bothell, WA). This serum starvation and hypoxic condition mimicked the placental condition of preeclampsia.35 The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 13 000g for 2 minutes and then at 100 000g for 60 minutes at 4 °C to enrich pcEVs. This method collected membrane EVs, defined by the binding of annexin V and an antibody against human PLAP (placental alkaline phosphatase; LifeSpan BioSciences, Seattle, WA). We used pcEVs from BeWo cells instead of blood samples of patients with preeclampsia or mice because EVs in blood samples are heterogeneous, containing EVs from placenta, endothelial cells, and blood cells. Purifying pcEVs from these samples requires multiple processes of ultracentrifugation and immunoprecipitation, which could drastically alter the structure and activities of EVs.

Interaction Between pcEVs and Endothelial Cells In Vitro

We tested pcEVs for interaction with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; ATCC) using 3 complementary assays. First, confluent HUVECs were incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C with 3×104/µL of pcEVs or VWF+pcEVs that were labeled with the membrane fluorescent dye PKH26 (Sigma, St. Louis, MO).36 The HUVECs were then washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with phalloidin (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) to detect the cytoskeleton and DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Abcam) to stain the nucleus, and examined under a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo).37 The polyclonal anti-VWF antibody (32 ng/mL, Dako-Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) and a synthetic synthetic Arg-Gly Asp peptide that blocks interaction of VWF with integrins (RGD) peptide (5 µg/mL, Guoping Pharmaceutical, China) were tested for blocking the pcEV-endothelial interaction.

Second, we used a transwell system to measure VWF-induced endothelial leakage.38 Briefly, HUVECs were cultured on the transwell membrane (Thermo Fisher, pore size 0.2 µm) coated with gelatin until confluence and then treated with rhVWF and pcEVs either alone or in combination for 3 hours at 37 °C. After washing, the treated HUVECs were incubated with 1 mg/mL of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–dextran (70 kDa, Sigma) for 1 hour at 37 °C. The medium in the bottom chamber was then collected to quantify the FITC fluorescence using a plate reader (BioTek Instruments).

Third, we tested the role of rhVWF in mediating the adhesion of pcEVs to cultured endothelial cells under flow conditions using a microfluidic chamber (Cellix, Dublin, Ireland).33 Briefly, HUVECs were cultured on the surface of the chamber in DMEM medium with 10% FBS until confluence. They were then perfused with 1×107/µL of pcEVs enriched from BeWo cells (labeled with the PKH26 dye) for 5 minutes at 2 flow rates that generated 2 and 10 dynes/cm2 of shear stress to mimic venous and arterial blood flows, respectively. The chamber was then perfused with PBS for 2 minutes to remove unbound pcEVs. The adherent PKH26+pcEVs were quantified by surface fluorescence intensity using the Image J program.

Flow Cytometry

The plasma levels of pcEVs and platelet-derived EVs were quantified using flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA).11,36 Briefly, mouse cell-free plasma was incubated with an FITC-antibody against the syncytiotrophoblast marker syncytin (FabGennix, Frisco, TX) and human samples with an anti-PLAP antibody (LifeSpan BioSciences). PLAP is a membrane-bound enzyme expressed after the 12th week of pregnancy and is a widely used marker for syncytiotrophoblasts.15,39–41 We used syncytin to detect mouse syncytiotrophoblasts because PLAP is not expressed in mouse placenta.42 Both mouse and human samples were also incubated with APC (allophycocyanin)–annexin V (Thermo Fisher), which binds anionic phospholipids, for 20 minutes at RT to quantify syncytiotrophoblast membrane EVs or with a polyclonal anti-VWF antibody (Abcam), which reacts to both mouse and human VWF, to detect VWF-bound pcEVs. The samples were also incubated with a PE–anti-CD41 (cluster of differentiation) antibody (Becton Dickinson) and APC–annexin V (Thermo Fisher) for 20 minutes at RT to detect platelet-derived EVs. The annexin V–binding specificity was determined by EDTA, which chelates calcium to prevent binding (Figure S3). The samples were then diluted with PBS and analyzed at the EV gate set by standard microbeads of 0.5, 0.9, and 3 µm (Biocytex, Marseille, France). Platelet activation and platelet-bound VWF were detected using PE–anti-CD41/FITC–anti-CD62p antibodies (Becton Dickinson) and FITC–anti-VWF (Abcam) and PE–anti-CD41 antibodies (Abcam), respectively.

Histopathology

Human placental tissues were collected and processed within 30 minutes after cesarean delivery. Consistent with standard practice in clinical histopathology staining of surgical, biopsy, and autopsy samples, the placentas were not perfused but were washed extensively with warm PBS before being cut into 2 to 3 tissue blocks of 10×10×10 mm from the region located two-thirds of the distance from the umbilical cord insertion sites. Areas with visual tissue infarction, calcification, or hematoma were carefully avoided. The tissue blocks were immediately rinsed 3 times with ice-cold PBS to remove blood, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, a polyclonal anti-VWF antibody, or phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin. Phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin is widely used in clinical diagnosis of intravascular fibrin deposition to the endothelium and fibrin clots.11,43

The placenta and kidney were also dissected from pregnant ADAMTS13−/− mice at 17 to 19 dpc, or from pregnant C57BL/6J mice 60 minutes after infusion with rhVWF at 17 to 19 dpc after euthanasia under anesthesia.11 The mice were perfused with 50 mL of warm PBS through cardiac puncture to remove blood from the vasculature. The organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (1 hour at RT) and then sectioned. Consistent with histopathology staining of human placenta, we used phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin to stain fibrin. We also stained the kidney tissue with periodic acid–Schiff to detect proteins deposited in the glomeruli. The histopathology was performed at the clinical pathology laboratory of the Tianjin Medical University General Hospital for the human tissue, and at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center Comparative Medicine core laboratory for the mouse tissue. The tissue stains were reviewed by clinical and mouse pathologists who were blind to the experimental conditions.

VWF, ADAMTS13, and Coagulation Assays

ADAMTS13 was measured using a coagulation analyzer (DiaPharma, West Chester, OH). For human and mouse samples, we measured VWF:Ag using ELISA (Abcam). VWF adhesive activity was measured by VWF:CB using ELISA (Abcam) and an ELISA-based ristocetin cofactor activity assay (VWF:RCo, COIBO Biologica, Co, China), which detects VWF binding to GP (glycoprotein) Ib,44 for the human samples. The former measures VWF binding to collagen primarily through the A3 domain, whereas the latter measures VWF-GP Ib interaction through the VWF-A1 domain. The adhesive activity of mouse VWF was measured using VWF:CB only because mouse VWF reacts poorly to ristocetin.

To cross-validate the results, we used 2 complementary assays to measure specific conformational changes between the A1 and A2 domains of VWF.45 First, poly His-tagged VWF-A2 protein (5 µg/mL; Supplemental Methods) was captured on nickel-coated microtiter plates for 60 minutes at RT. After washing, A2-coated plates were incubated with 50 µL of plasma from experimental mice for 60 minutes at 37 °C and washed with Tris-buffered saline. The captured VWF was detected by an HRP (horseradish peroxidase)-conjugated polyclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher). Second, A2 captured by an anti-His antibody (Abcam) coupled to Sepharose-conjugated protein A/G microbeads (Thermo Fisher) was incubated with plasma samples overnight at 4 °C on a parallel shaker and then centrifuged at 20 000g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The precipitated proteins were separated with 4% to 12% gradient SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA), probed with an HRP-polyclonal VWF antibody (Dako-Agilent Technologies), and quantified with densitometry.

We also used 5 tests36,46,47 to define the hypercoagulable state in pregnant mice and nonpregnant mice infused with rhVWF, pcEVs, or VWF+pcEVs: (1) phosphatidylserine-expressing EVs (annexin V binding) using flow cytometry36,46; (2) plasma levels of fibrinogen using ELISA (Abcam); (3) plasma D-dimer using ELISA (LifeSpan Biosciences, Shirley, MA)36; (4) a phosphatidylserine-dependent clotting assay using an STA-Procoag-PPL Kit, which tests the ability of EVs to coagulate the phospholipid-depleted plasma in the presence of 0.02 U/mL of the activated coagulation FXa (factor Xa)36,46,47; and (5) thrombin generation using a FluCa Kit on Thrombinoscope BV (Diagnostica Stago Inc, Parsippany-Troy Hills, NJ).36 For the thrombin generation assay, 70 μL of phospholipid-deficient porcine plasma (IMVS), 20 μL of FluCa solution (Diagnostica Stago), and 30 μL of plasma from mice receiving pcEVs with or without rhADAMTS13 were mixed and incubated at 37 °C. Thrombin generation was quantified on a Thrombinoscope Fluoroskan Ascent reader (Thermo Lab Systems, Helsinki, Finland) at 390 nm excitation and 460 nm emission. Thrombin generation induced by 20 μL of the MP-reagent (Diagnostica Stago) served as a control. The MP reagent contains phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine at a ratio of 20:60:20, providing 4 μM of total phospholipids to the reaction mix.

Calcium Influx

Confluent HUVECs were incubated with 3×104/µL of pcEVs or VWF+pcEVs for 10 minutes, followed by washing with phenol red-free Ca2+ and Mg2+ HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubation with 3 μM of Fluo 3-AM (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc, Japan) for 20 minutes at 37 °C. The cells were washed and incubated with HBSS for 20 minutes to completely deesterify the intracellular AM ester. They were then detached by EDTA and washed with HBSS to measure the intracellular fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry. Controls included polyclonal VWF antibody (Dako), synthetic RGD/RGE (arginine-glycine-glutamic acid) peptide (Guoping), 10 µM of nifedipine (Selleck Chemicals LLC, Houston, TX), and cells incubated with the EGM-2 culture medium specified in individual experiments.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as percentages for categorical variables and as the mean±SEM for continuous variables. Data were first evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. For data defined as normally distributed, homogeneity of variance was assessed using the Brown-Forsythe test. For comparisons among multiple groups, we used a standard 1-way ANOVA if both normality and homoscedasticity assumptions were met. If the variance heterogeneity was detected, Welch ANOVA was used. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis 1-way ANOVA on ranks was used. For 2-group comparisons, a Student t test was used for normally distributed data with equal variance, whereas a Welch t test was used if variances were unequal. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test rank-sum test was used. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were analyzed using SigmaPlot v11.2 (Systat, Palo Alto, CA). We only labeled post hoc pair comparisons of ANOVA analyses or t tests that reached statistical significance for all graphs in the Results section.

Results

VWF Was Hyperadhesive in Patients With Preeclampsia

Consistent with our early report,16 VWF:Ag, VWF:CB, and VWF:RCo from a separate cohort sample were significantly higher in the plasma of pregnant women than in those of nonpregnant women, and they increased further in patients with preeclampsia, with progressively reduced levels of ADAMTS13 antigen, resulting in low ADAMTS13-to-VWF ratios (Table 1). Plasma levels of VWF+pcEVs accounted for 62.7±12.6% of total pcEVs in normal pregnancies but 81.5±8.2% in patients with preeclampsia (Figure 1A), whereas the levels of endothelial EVs (CD144+/phosphatidylserine+) increased from <2% of total phosphatidylserine+EVs in normal pregnancies to 4.8±1.6% in patients with preeclampsia (Figure 1B). These results validate our previous findings,16 indicating greatly increased endothelial injury in patients with preeclampsia. We also examined placental tissues from women with normal pregnancies and patients with preeclampsia through histopathology. VWF was exclusively stained in the vessel walls of placentas from normal pregnancies (Figure 1C and 1G), with minimal intravascular fibrin deposition (Figure 1D and 1H). However, intravascular thrombosis was detected in placental tissues from patients with preeclampsia, with overlapping staining for VWF (Figure 1E and 1I) and fibrin (Figure 1F and 1J). ULVWF (ultralarge VWF) multimers were detected in plasma samples from some but not all patients with preeclampsia, and they were less noticeable than those found in cord blood from women with normal pregnancies (Figure 1K). Together, these results suggest that (1) patients with preeclampsia develop acquired ADAMTS13 deficiency and VWF hyperadhesive activity, validating findings from our clinical observational report16; (2) more VWF+pcEVs and endothelial EVs were detected in patients with preeclampsia than in women with normal pregnancies, and (3) VWF- and fibrin-rich thrombosis develops in the placentas of patients with preeclampsia. Because this clinical study was observational with limited mechanistic insight, we conducted a mechanistic study through in vitro experiments and mouse models.

VWF Had an Active Conformation in Pregnant Mice

We studied the role of VWF in preeclampsia in mouse models to reduce confounding influences associated with clinical studies. We found that VWF:Ag (Figure 2A), VWF:CB (Figure 2B), and their ratio (Figure 2C) increased in plasma samples collected at 17 to 19 dpc from pregnant C57BL/6J mice and returned to baseline 0.5 to 2 days after delivery. VWF:CB measures the adhesive activity of VWF, and its ratio to VWF:Ag defines whether a high VWF:CB is caused by intrinsic hyperadhesive VWF or by higher VWF:Ag. Furthermore, VWF+pcEVs measured at 17 to 19 dpc increased 4.2-fold from baseline and returned to baseline during the early postpartum period (Figure 2D). Levels of VWF-bound platelet EVs (VWF+/CD41+, Figure 2E) followed the same longitudinal changes, with more than 80% of platelets expressing the activation marker CD62p in samples collected at 17 to 19 dpc (Figure 2F). Levels of platelet EVs decreased during the postpartum period but remained higher than baseline. Annexin V+ and annexin V+/VWF+ membrane EVs followed similar longitudinal changes (Figure S4).

The findings that VWF was intrinsically hyperadhesive (Table 1) but ULVWF multimers were less apparent in the plasma of preeclampsia patients (Figure 1K) suggested that VWF became hyperadhesive independent of multimer sizes. To test this hypothesis, we examined the conformation of VWF. The VWF A1 and A2 domains form a complex,48 which prevents A1 from binding to platelets and A2 from being cleaved by ADAMTS13 to maintain VWF in an inactive globular conformation during homeostasis. This A1-A2 complex is formed when the 2 vicinal cysteines C1669-C1670 in the A2 domain are in a reduced (thiol) form (Figure 2G),45,48 but it is disrupted when the 2 vicinal cysteines are oxidized to form a disulfide bond,45 which activates VWF. Oxidative stress closely associated with preeclampsia49,50 could, therefore, activate VWF through the disulfide-bound switch. We detect this conformational change of VWF by measuring the binding of plasma VWF from C57BL/6J mice to a recombinant A2 protein, as we previously described.43 The A2 lacks the 2 vicinal cysteines so that its binding to the exposed A1 domain is not affected by an oxidative environment.43–45 A2 captured VWF from plasma collected at 17 to 19 dpc from pregnant C57BL/6J mice but not from nonpregnant mice, as measured using both capturing ELISA (left, Figure 2H) and immunoblots (Figure 2I). The A2 capturing was blocked by the recombinant A1 (Figure 2H). In contrast, A2 captured VWF from plasma of nonpregnant ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice and increased further with plasma collected at 17 to 19 dpc (right panels; Figure 2H and 2I). The amount of A2-captured VWF was significantly higher in pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice than in pregnant C57BL/6J mice (Figure 2H). These results demonstrate that (1) VWF becomes intrinsically hyperadhesive during late pregnancy in C57BL/6J and ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice by adopting an open conformation, thereby binding to pcEVs and activating platelets and (2) the A2-based assay could detect an open conformation that makes VWF hyperadhesive.

Pregnancy-Induced Placental and Renal Pathologies in ADAMTS13-Deficient Mice

To specifically investigate the role of ADAMTS13 and hyperadhesive (uncleaved) VWF in preeclampsia, we studied ADAMTS13−/−/B6 and ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice. The former had plasma levels of VWF comparable to those of C57BL/6J mice, whereas the latter had significantly elevated plasma VWF (Figure S5).51,52 Under regular breeding conditions, ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice had a low birth rate, whereas the birth rates of ADAMTS13−/−/B6 and C57BL/6J mice were comparable (Table 2). Newborn ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice appeared smaller than C57BL/6J mice with comparable placenta sizes (Figure S6). We were unable to capture newborn ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice because of their low fertility. We did not investigate what caused the low birth rate of ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice in this study.

VWF Promoted a pcEV-Induced Preeclampsia-Like Phenotype in Pregnant Mice

This study was designed to investigate the role of hyperadhesive VWF in preeclampsia in normal subjects, not under the condition of TTP. Therefore, we used an adoptive transfer model to specifically examine the role of hyperadhesive VWF and pcEVs without severe ADAMTS13 deficiency in developing a preeclampsia-like condition in C57BL/6J mice. We generated pcEVs from injured mouse placenta11 instead of purifying them from blood samples of pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk or ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice to ensure species matching and minimize contamination by EVs from other cells. The newly produced pcEVs resembled those found in pregnant mice except that they expressed high levels of anionic phospholipids (Figure S8). These pcEVs rapidly became VWF-bound when incubated with rhVWF in vitro (Figure 5A). The interaction between pcEV and hyperadhesive VWF was further validated by: (1) VWF bound to pcEVs in a dose-dependent manner when they were incubated with citrated plasma from pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/B6 and ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice (Figure S9A), and (2) when plasma samples were fractionated into EV-rich and EV-poor plasma by ultracentrifugation at 100 000g for 60 minutes at 4 °C (twice), VWF was primarily detected in the EV-poor plasma fraction of nonpregnant mice, but it was detected in EV and EV-poor plasma fractions of pregnant C57BL/6J mice (Figure S9B). These results demonstrate that a significant amount of hyperadhesive VWF becomes EV-bound in the blood of pregnant C57BL/6J mice.

ADAMTS13 Ameliorated the pcEV-Induced Preeclampsia-Like Condition in Mice

We then tested whether reducing the hyperadhesive activity of VWF would reverse the preeclampsia-like condition developed in VWF+pcEV-infused mice by simultaneously infusing rhADAMTS13 into these mice. The rhADAMTS13 tested in the study cleaved mouse VWF and reduced VWF-induced platelet microvesiculation (Figure S11). We found that a single dose of rhADAMTS13 (2 µg per mouse) given together with VWF+pcEVs (1×107 per mouse) prevented the hypertension (Figure 6A), renal vascular leakage (Figure 6B), proteinuria (Figure 6C), and thrombocytopenia (Figure 6D) found in pregnant C57BL/6J mice. RhADAMTS13 also decreased placental thrombosis (Figure S12) and prevented the high leukocyte counts without affecting erythrocyte counts, hemoglobin, or hematocrit in these mice (Figure S13).

Consistent with the findings in mice, pcEVs activated cultured HUVECs to release VWF into the medium (Figure 6E) and bound endothelial cells (Figure S14). Both pcEVs and VWF+pcEVs induced calcium influx (Figure 6F) and increased the permeability (Figure S15) of cultured HUVECs, which were blocked by the anti-VWF antibody, a synthetic RGD peptide, or, in the case of calcium influx, by the calcium channel blocker nifedipine. PKH26-labeled pcEVs adhered to cultured HUVECs, and the adhesion was significantly enhanced in the presence of rhVWF but reduced by the anti-VWF antibody and by the RGD peptide (Figure 6G). The control RGE peptide, which does not bind integrin, failed to block VWF from binding to pcEVs and parental BeWo cells (Figure S16). Some of the pcEVs were endocytosed by HUVECs, as can be seen in the z axis (bottom right, Figure 6G).

These results demonstrate that pcEV-bound VWF promotes a preeclampsia-like condition in pregnant C57BL/6J mice but also raise the question of how EV-bound VWF promotes pcEV-induced endotheliopathy. To answer this question, we tested the hypothesis that VWF captures pcEVs onto the endothelium, against the pulling force of blood flow, using a microfluidic chamber that mimics the hemodynamics of blood circulation. PcEVs adhered to cultured HUVECs under static conditions, but the adhesion was significantly reduced under the venous shear stress of 2 dynes/cm2 and nearly eliminated under the arterial shear stress of 10 dynes/cm2. RhVWF promoted pcEV adhesion to endothelial cells (Figure 6H). These results demonstrate that hyperadhesive VWF promotes a preeclampsia-like phenotype in pregnant C57BL/6J mice by capturing pcEVs to the endothelium under flow conditions, and these surface-bound pcEVs induce endothelial injury to increase permeability and release more hyperadhesive VWF. ADAMTS13 reduces the hyperadhesive activity of VWF to prevent the pcEV-induced preeclampsia-like condition in mice.

ADAMTS13 Reduced the Hypercoagulable State Associated With Pregnancy

Endothelial injury contributes significantly to a local and systemic hypercoagulable or prothrombotic state in preeclampsia. We found that nonpregnant C57BL/6J mice infused with pcEVs (1×107 per mouse) developed a systemic hypercoagulable state defined by lower fibrinogen (Figure 7A), higher D-dimer (Figure 7B), shortened clotting time (Figure 7C), and enhanced thrombin generation (Figure 7D), as compared with mice receiving equal volumes of PBS. This hypercoagulable state was reduced in mice receiving pcEVs along with rhADAMTS13 (2 µg per mouse). ADAMTS13 also prevented the hypercoagulable state developed in pregnant C57BL/6J mice, defined by increasing platelet activation (CD62p+; Figure 7E), low plasma levels of fibrinogen (Figure 7F), shortened clotting time (Figure 7G), and high plasma levels of D-dimer (Figure S17).

Discussion

We have investigated the role of hyperadhesive VWF in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia through complementary approaches. We show that hyperadhesive VWF became pcEV-bound and captured circulating pcEVs to the endothelium under flow conditions to facilitate pcEV-induced endotheliopathy.11,25 We made the following novel observations.

First, plasma samples from patients with preeclampsia contained significantly elevated levels of VWF antigen and adhesive activity and moderately decreased levels of ADAMTS13, thereby reducing the ADAMTS13-to-VWF ratio (acquired ADAMTS13 deficiency). These results from a separate patient cohort validate our clinical observational report.16 We further show the presence of VWF- and fibrin-rich microvascular thrombosis in the placenta of patients with preeclampsia. Furthermore, ULVWF multimers were detected in plasma samples from some patients with preeclampsia, but they were less evident compared with ULVWF found in patients with TTP, suggesting that additional factors contribute to VWF hyperadhesive activity. This possibility led us to study conformational changes that could have activated VWF without significantly increasing multimer sizes. It has been reported that the VWF A1 and A2 domains form a complex when the 2 vicinal cysteines C1669-C1670 in the A2 domain are in a reduced (thiol) form.45,48 This complex prevents A1 from binding to platelets and A2 from being cleaved by ADAMTS13 to maintain VWF in an inactive globular conformation during homeostasis.54 This A1-A2 complex is disrupted when the 2 vicinal cysteines are oxidized to form a disulfide bond (Figure 2G),45 activating VWF in an oxidative stress environmentclosely associated with preeclampsia.49,50 In mouse experiments, we have shown that a recombinant A2 protein captured VWF from the plasma of pregnant C57BL/6J and ADAMTS13−/− mice but not from nonpregnant mice (Figure 2H and 2I). This conformational change provides a new mechanism for activating VWF independent of multimer sizes. This mechanism remains to be validated through additional clinical studies.

Furthermore, ULVWF multimers were detected in the cord blood of normal pregnancies, as previously reported,55 which raises the question of whether placental vascular injuries in patients with preeclampsia allow for direct communication between fetal and maternal circulations (which do not directly converge during normal pregnancy), thereby leaking ULVWF from the cord blood into the maternal circulation. This question remains to be answered.

Second, consistent with clinical observations,16,26 VWF:Ag and VWF:CB increased significantly in blood samples collected at 17 to 19 dpc from pregnant C57BL/6J mice and quickly returned to baseline during the early postpartum period, suggesting that VWF is released primarily from placental endothelial cells. ADAMTS13 has been reported to be expressed in villous core fetal endothelial cells and trophoblasts during normal pregnancies and preeclampsia, but its expression is highest during the first trimester, followed by a gradual decline, especially in hypoxic conditions and preeclampsia.56 Increasing the release of placental VWF without a parallel increase in ADAMTS13 results in an acquired ADAMTS13 deficiency, which renders VWF hyperadhesive, as is found in mouse models (Figure 2) and patients with preeclampsia.16 This pregnancy-induced VWF hyperadhesive activity explains why pregnancy often triggers acute thrombotic crisis in patients with TTP,19,20 who have severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.57

Furthermore, ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice that have high baseline levels of hyperadhesive VWF developed a more severe preeclampsia-like condition during pregnancy than ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice did (Figures 3 and 4), although both strains were ADAMTS13 deficient. This observation raises the question of whether women with high baseline VWF are prone to developing preeclampsia during pregnancy. This would require comprehensive clinical studies to answer, but placental thrombosis and ischemic injuries developed during pregnancy may contribute to the low birth rate of ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice (Table 2) and the high rate of miscarriages in patients with TTP.58,59

Third, pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice had a lower VWF:CB-to-VWF:Ag ratio but drastically elevated levels of VWF+pcEVs and platelet EVs at 17 to 19 dpc. In contrast, patients with preeclampsia often have increased VWF:CB-to-VWF:Ag ratios.16 This counterintuitive observation suggests that a substantial amount of hyperadhesive VWF binds to pcEVs and platelets, thus removing itself from the plasma. This notion is supported by increased levels of VWF+pcEVs (Figure 3D), platelet EVs (Figure 3F), and VWF-rich placental thrombosis (Figure 3G) in pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/CASA/Rk mice as compared with pregnant ADAMTS13−/−/B6 mice. It is also consistent with previous reports that (1) large VWF multimers are selectively lost in the plasma samples of patients with von Willebrand disease type 2B, which is caused by hyperadhesive VWF,60 and (2) ULVWF multimers are present in the plasma of patients with TTP during remission but not during thrombotic crisis, which is when VWF-rich microvascular thrombosis and severe thrombocytopenia develop.61

Finally, pregnant C57BL/6J mice infused with VWF+pcEVs developed a preeclampsia-like condition that was more severe than in those receiving either rhVWF or pcEVs alone (Figure 5). Furthermore, rhVWF rapidly bound to pcEVs and greatly enhanced their adhesion to endothelial cells under both venous and arterial flow conditions (Figure 6). These results suggest that the surface-bound hyperadhesive VWF captures pcEVs onto the endothelium against the pulling force of circulation so that adherent pcEVs can cause endothelial injury,11 which is the hallmark of preeclampsia.25,62,63 VWF can bind integrin αvβ3 expressed on syncytiotrophoblast-derived pcEV64,65 and on membrane EVs from endothelial cells66 and platelets. This study focuses on pcEVs, but the role of EVs from other cells cannot be excluded. Although VWF on pcEVs could simultaneously interact with integrin αvβ367 and CD62p68 on the endothelial cells, our focus is on the integrin αvβ3-VWF interaction. This is because the integrin αvβ3-VWF interaction could tether VWF+pcEVs to the resting endothelial cells to activate them. Consistent with this notion, the RGD but not RGE peptide blocked the pcEV-induced activation of endothelial cells (Figure 6G) and VWF binding to pcEVs (Figure S16). In contrast, the CD62p-VWF interaction predominantly occurs on activated endothelial cells so that the effect of pcEVs is less critical. ADAMTS13 selectively cleaves hyperadhesive VWF33 to reduce its ability to couple VWF+pcEVs to endothelial cells.

We did not investigate specifically how pcEVs cause endothelial injury, but they may act through several pathways: (1) phosphatidylserine on pcEVs activates intravascular coagulation (Figures 5 and 7) to deposit fibrin to the endothelium, which increases endothelial permeability69,70; (2) pcEVs may carry angiogenic factors (eg, trophoblast-derived VEGF [vascular endothelial growth factor], PlGF [placental growth factor], angiopoietins71,72), which activate endothelial cells to cause vascular leakage and proteinuria,44 or induce vasoconstriction (hypertension).11

In summary, we demonstrate that VWF becomes hyperadhesive during pregnancy due to acquired ADAMTS13 deficiency. The hyperadhesive VWF plays a causal role in preeclampsia by facilitating pcEV-induced endotheliopathy. Our results suggest that screening for hyperadhesive VWF before or early in pregnancies may predict the development of preeclampsia in late pregnancy, and that hyperadhesive VWF may also be a therapeutic target for preeclampsia. These findings also explain why pregnancy triggers TTP and patients with TTP have a higher risk of pregnancy-associated complications.
