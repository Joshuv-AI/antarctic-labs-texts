The association between family socio-demographic factors, parental mediation and adolescents’ digital literacy: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiaxue Lou, Menmen Wang, Xiaoliang Xie, Feng Wang, Xudong Zhou, Jingjing Lu, Hui Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital technology has a two-sided impact whether it is used appropriately or not. It is crucial to promote the benefits of digital technology and reduce its harms by bolstering adolescents’ digital literacy. The appropriateness of the strategies parents use to manage their children’s digital lives plays a key role. This study aims to investigate the separate effects of family socio-demographic factors and varied dimensions of parental mediation on adolescents’ digital literacy separately and simultaneously to explore the appropriate parental mediation strategies even in the absence of favorable family status.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was utilized with a sample of 1894 Chinese adolescents in junior and senior high schools (mean age = 14.01, 48.94% female), using a set of exam questions to test children’s objective digital literacy online. Family socio-demographic factors (parental marital status, economic status, and parental education levels) and varied dimensions of parental mediation were included as predictors of digital literacy. Hierarchical regression analysis was used to estimate the relation between the variables.ResultsHigher parental education levels were positively associated with adolescents’ parental mediation and digital literacy while better family economic status and parental divorce were negatively associated. Among the dimensions of parental mediation, active mediation, restrictive mediation and supervision were significantly associated with adolescents’ digital literacy, but not for co-use. Active mediation and supervision showed significant positive associations with adolescents’ digital literacy, whereas, co-use showed a significant negative association when all parental mediation dimensions were integrated into the model, The significant association between restrictive mediation and digital literacy disappeared. Parental mediation has diluted the impact of family socio-demographic factors on adolescents’ digital literacy.ConclusionsEffective interventions are needed to promote parental active mediation and supervision to help adolescents develop digital literacy and navigate the digital world, especially for adolescents who do not have highly educated parents or whose parents are divorced.Keywords: Digital literacy, Parental mediation, Family socio-demographic factor, Adolescent

Introduction

‘Digital child’ has become a nationwide phenomenon in China [1] with the recent popularity of modern technological devices such as smartphones, computers, and tablets. As of 2023, China had 1.092 billion Internet users with 202 million of them being minors [2]. Children are surrounded by dazzling digital devices, and consume a variety of traditional and new digital media in their daily lives. Online entertainment activities, represented by playing games and watching short videos, have become an important way for minors to relax, with 67.8% and 54.1% frequently engaged in these activities, respectively. At the same time, the data show that the proportion of underage netizens who often use the Internet to study has reached 88.7% [3]. Numerous studies have confirmed that excessive and inappropriate use of digital devices may lead to children’s Internet addiction and other health risks [4–6]. In addition, a study reported that mainland China has one of the highest prevalence of Internet/gaming addiction among adolescents in six Asian countries [7, 8]. However, when utilized appropriately, digital technology can have a positive impact on children’s early learning, social contact, access to information [9], and adaptability to the environment [10]. Therefore, it is crucial to promote the benefits of digital technology and reduce its harm by bolstering adolescents’ digital literacy [11].

Digital literacy of adolescents

Over time, various terms such as digital media literacy, information and communication technology (ICT) literacy, media literacy, digital competence and digital literacy, have emerged to describe the knowledge, skills, and attitudes necessary to succeed in the digital era [8, 12, 13]. These terms often overlap and are used equivalently in most contexts. However, the concept of digital literacy as a whole has been used in the present study. According to the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), digital literacy refers to the ability to define, access, manage, integrate, communicate, evaluate and create information safely and appropriately through digital technologies and networked devices for participation in economic and social life [11]. This definition views digital literacy as an integration of competencies that adolescents need to thrive in this ‘digital age’. As noted, digital literacy is a central facilitator of adolescents’ academic and career development as digital technologies permeate all sectors of this digitized society [14]. There is evidence that proficiency in Internet use may be associated with online risks, since more proficient users are more likely to engage in online activities, making them more susceptible to online risks [15]. Therefore, users need to have better digital literacy to reduce the risk of inappropriate digital technology use and engage with technology wisely and safely for acquiring new knowledge and broadening social contact and support [8].

Given the essential positive effects of digital literacy, researchers have explored factors that may promote adolescents’ digital literacy. Individual-level factors including gender, age, device ownership, and frequency of digital device use [16], as well as contextual-level factors including digital resources at home, location, household rules around technology use, older family members’ interests and attitudes toward technology, family socio-economic status, parental marital status, and parental mediation [1, 13, 17–19], are all important factors related to adolescents’ digital literacy. Previous studies have also demonstrated that the extent to which parents guide their children’s use of digital technology and the strategies they apply are related to family socio-economic status [4, 20]. These factors holistically define the circumstance for both parents and children to adopt technological devices, choose the types of media content, along with establish proper attitudes towards digital usage [21]. Parents from better-off families are more familiar with media devices, more confident, and have more appropriate attitudes and strategies for guiding their children’s technology use, which equips their young children with more technologies and educational and informative television programming [21]. In addition, less affluent parents, particularly those who are less educated, are themselves less familiar with digital technology, and they tend to feel outsmarted by their often-skilled children and lack confidence in managing their children’s online risks, which leads to the intergenerational transmission of poor digital literacy [4]. Studies also highlighted the importance of parental marital status on children’s performance and development. Children from single-parent families are more likely to have a touch screen or TV in their bedroom and twice as likely to own a computer [21]. Because of the influence of parents’ negative role models, children from families with worse family socio-demographic status have an increased likelihood of unhealthy lifestyles, such as sedentary behavior and excessive recreational screen time [22]. Most adolescents use digital technology primarily for entertainment, but if parents are in the habit of adopting educational apps, few of them will spend online time for entertainment but rather form a literacy to develop and create through the Internet [4].

Parental mediation and adolescents’ digital literacy

These findings emphasize the vital role of parental mediation. Parenting in the digital age has been characterized as one of the most challenging tasks of the modern era due to the constant innovations and complexity of digital technology [23]. The 2016 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) media policy statement, Media Use in School-Aged Children and Adolescents, emphasized the role of parents in adolescents’ media lives [24]. A key role for parents is to act as bridges between new media and their children. As suggested, parents who set and enforce household technology rules based on the key concepts of parental mediation can help their children thrive in this digital age better [16]. Parental mediation refers to parental strategies to support, regulate, and/or supervise their children’s Internet and/or media usage and behavior [25]. In general, parental mediation of digital practices can be divided into five major patterns: active mediation, co-use, restrictive mediation general, restrictive mediation content specific and supervision [26].

Co-use allows family members to engage in media activities together to interact and communicate, while active mediation refers to having parents discuss, comment, interpret, and evaluate their children’s online activities [15, 27]. There is substantial evidence that active mediation and co-use can not only help parents improve their understanding of their children’s online activities, thereby facilitating children’s learning and understanding of media content, promoting critical thinking and reducing the risk of Internet addiction, but also enhance parent-child communication and effectively avoid the potential negative effects of media on children’s behavior and mental health [28, 29]. Thus, these active mediation strategies may be beneficial to improve adolescents’ digital literacy. For restrictive mediation and supervision, one of the most prominent strategies is controlling the time that adolescents spend online, followed by limitations on the interaction on social media and playing games, monitoring their children’s Internet use, restricting their access to Websites through filtering software, or even using a log-in system [15]. While these restrictive mediation strategies are related to minimizing exposure to online risks due to less online engagement, they also appear to limit adolescents’ online opportunities to learn, explore, develop digital skills or build resilience to risk online [4, 30]. Notwithstanding, some researchers argue that parental mediation via active co-use and restrictive mediation had a significant negative relationship with children’s positive Internet use [30]. Some studies conclude that restrictive mediation reduce adolescents’ digital skills [31] and digital literacy [32]. Consequently, more research is needed to clarify the impact of different types of parental mediation on adolescents’ digital literacy.

The current study

Previous studies have documented how family socio-demographic factors and parental mediation affect adolescents’ abilities or behaviors in digital device use, and some have studied the effects of parental mediation strategies on adolescents’ online risks or digital technology use [29, 33–36] but not digital literacy. What’s more, empirical studies in this field are still very limited. Only a few studies have revealed the relationship between parental mediation and children’s digital literacy. And most of these studies have only focused on the effects of only one or two major forms without a comprehensive consideration of distinguishing different dimensions and the overall effect of multiple types of parental mediation [1, 13, 32].

In addition, previous research has used various methods to measure adolescents’ digital literacy levels, of which most are self/parent-perceived efficacy using a Likert scale (e.g., Rodríguez-de-Dios’s Digital Literacy Scale [32]) or performance-based assessments using software applications (e.g., IEA’s International Computer and Information Literacy Study [37]). Given the existence of the ‘digital overconfidence’ effect [38] and the complexity of field implementation, we need more objective and implementable measure tools. Thus, a set of exam questions was developed to test children’s objective digital knowledge, skills and attitudes in the present study.

Yet little is known about whether and how these separate family socio-demographic factors, including income and education levels, as well as parental marital status, work simultaneously in shaping different forms of parental mediation and digital literacy of adolescents in China. What’s more, there is still a paucity of studies investigating the effects of multi-type parental mediation on the association between these family socio-demographic factors and digital literacy.

Objectives

This study aims to explore the separate effects of family socio-demographic factors on four dimensions of parental mediation and digital literacy of adolescents and to further investigate the impact of each type of parental mediation on adolescents’ digital literacy. Hence, the present study generates the following three research hypotheses (Fig. 1):

Relationship between socio-demographic variables, parental meditation and digital literacy

H1

Parents staying married, better family economic status, higher paternal and maternal education levels are positively associated with each type of parental mediation (H1a) and adolescents’ digital literacy (H1b).

H2

Varied dimensions of parental mediation are positively associated with adolescents’ digital literacy, individually or simultaneously. Furthermore, the combined effect of all dimensions of parental mediation was more pronounced.

H3

The impact of family socio-demographic factors on adolescents’ digital literacy can be attenuated by varied dimensions of parental mediation to different extents.

Materials and methods

Procedure and participants

The government of Nanling County, Wuhu City, Anhui Province, China, initiated the Students’ Digital Literacy Promotion Program from August to September 2022. After being notified by the county education department, schools encouraged students to participate in this program by scanning the QR code on the notice at home and completing the questionnaire online with or without parental assistance. According to our previous investigation implementation, students aged 12 or above were eligible to complete the questionnaire on their own, while students aged 11 or below were allowed to complete the questionnaire with the assistance of their parents. Consent for this study was obtained from parents or guardians of all students who participated in the program. This program focused on all students from all educational institutions including kindergartens, primary schools, junior and senior high schools in Nanling County. In each school, participating students were recruited if they met the following criteria: (1) enrolled in grades 7–12 (secondary school) of Nanling county, (2) were 12–17 years old, (3) agreed to participate in this survey, (4) committed to complete the questionnaire independently. After inspection of the data, among 4680 responses, 1894 secondary school students were included in the present study (48.94% female). Of the overall adolescents, the average age was 14.01 years (SD = 1.55).

Measures

Digital literacy

Adolescents completed a 35-item quiz question (10 judgmental questions and 25 multiple-choice questions) to assess their digital literacy. This instrument was designed by a team of experts in adolescent digital media use, based on a Global Framework of Reference on Digital Literacy Skills for Indicator 4.4.2 developed by UNESCO [11], measuring digital literacy in five competence areas including information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety, and problem-solving. The judgmental question was like: “Strong passwords should include capital letters, numbers, and special symbols.” The options were “Yes” and “No”. The multiple-choice question was like: “To promote civilized Internet and healthy life, which of the following behaviors should we not engage in?” The options were “A. Personal attacks on other netizens on the Internet”, “B. Consciously resist false and vulgar content on the Internet, so that harmful information has no place to hide”, “C. Browse legitimate websites, play healthy online games, and influence your friends with your actions”, and “D. Do not believe rumors, do not spread rumors, do not make rumors”. The reliability of paper was 0.817, the overall difficulty was 0.728, and the overall discrimination was 0.333.

Children’s perception of parental mediation

Parental mediation was measured using the Chinese version adapted from the Parental Mediation Scale of Nikken and Jansz [26, 35, 39] which was widely used and had good reliability. The 5-point Likert 20-item scale measures the level of parental mediation in children’s online behaviors among four dimensions: active mediation ( = 0.919), co-use ( = 0.686), restrictive mediation ( = 0.842) and supervision ( = 0.779). The composite index was constructed by the sum of item values. Higher scores indicated higher levels of child-perceived parental mediation of Internet use. The Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was 0.925. The model fit met the recommended criteria: X2 = 1830.845, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.075, CFI = 0.926. The Chinese version of the Parental Mediation Scale has good reliability and validity.

Socio-demographic variables

The socio-demographic variables included age, gender, parental marital status, perceived family economic status, and paternal and maternal education level. Since it would be difficult for children to report their parents’ individual or household annual income, we asked about their perceived comparative wealth in the community: “At what level do you consider your family’s economic conditions to be in your current place of residence? (wealthy, fair, poor)?”

Data analysis

Data were conducted using IBM SPSS 26 and Mplus 8. A common method bias test was performed using the Harman’s single-factor test. The explanatory power of the first common factor was 34.947 (< 40%), indicating that there was no serious common method bias. Descriptive statistics were performed on a sample of 1894 responses, which included sample counting and percentages of discrete variables, mean values, and standard deviations of continuous variables. The internal consistency of these constructs was then checked with Cronbach’s alpha. The convergent validity of the Parental Mediation Scale was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Subsequently, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the Parental Mediation Scale was conducted using Mplus. The relation between family socio-demographic factors, parental mediation, and digital literacy was estimated by hierarchical regression analysis. Informed by previous studies that the age and gender of children were included as covariates in the models [17, 19]. After controlling for adolescent gender and age, there was no significant difference in the results between the two models, so now only the model after controlling for the variables has been retained.

Of the four dimensions of parental mediation, active mediation had a higher average score (M = 22.72, SD = 7.84) compared to restrictive mediation (M = 19.98, SD = 7.01), with both dimensions having a maximum possible score of 35. Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted to explore whether all the family socio-demographic variables and multi-type parental mediation associated with adolescents’ digital literacy. As for the relevant factors of digital literacy, parental divorce, family economic status, paternal education level, and maternal education level were significantly associated with digital literacy with adjustments of covariates. Thus, H1b was confirmed. Among these family socio-demographic factors, higher paternal educational level, β = 7.08, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [4.80, 9.35]), and parental divorce, β = -4.34, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [-6.08, -2.60]) showed strong associations with adolescents’ digital literacy.

When the individual categories of parental mediation were taken into account separately with adjustments of family socio-demographic variables, active mediation, β = 0.31, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [0.24,0.39]), restrictive mediation, β = 0.30, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [0.21,0.39]) and supervision, β = 0.98, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [0.79,1.17]) were significantly associated with adolescents’ digital literacy. The changes in R2 between the multi-type parent mediation indicated that: (1) active mediation and supervision were significant predictors of the variation in digital literacy; (2) supervision could predict 5% of the variety of digital literacy, which was the highest among these four dimensions; and (3) all four dimensions of parental mediation could jointly explain 7% change in digital literacy. These results partly support H2. Meanwhile, the association between family socio-demographic factors and digital literacy remained significant. The results indicated that supervision could dilute the association between family socio-demographic factors and digital literacy more likely, followed by restrictive and active mediation. These results partly support H3. In contrast, co-use was not significant.

It is important to emphasize that when all parental mediation dimensions were integrated into the model, the significant association between restrictive mediation and digital literacy disappeared, whereas, co-use showed a significant negative association, β = -0.75, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [-1.03,-0.48]). Particularly, supervision remained the approach with the strongest association, β = 0.78, p < 0.001 (95% CI = [0.54,1.01]).

Discussion

Through this study, we aimed to explore the effect of varied dimensions of parental mediation on adolescents’ digital literacy separately and simultaneously, while considering the overall effects of separate family socio-demographic factors. As we hypothesized, and in consonance to previous studies, family socio-demographic factors appeared to have varied effects on each dimension of parental mediation and adolescents’ digital literacy. In addition, varied dimensions of parental mediation were positively associated with adolescents’ digital literacy, individually or simultaneously, but not for co-use and restrictive mediation. Furthermore, the combined effect of all dimensions of parental mediation was more pronounced. The impact of family socio-demographic factors on adolescents’ digital literacy can be attenuated by varied dimensions of parental mediation to different extents.

Parental divorce can negatively affect parental mediation and adolescents’ digital literacy. On the one hand, compared with married peers, separated parents appeared to have lower expectations of their children’s education [40]. Without proper parental guidance and support, it is extremely difficult to identify and cope with online risks for children, which can lead to problematic and risky Internet use. On the other hand, it is more difficult to devote as much time and effort to their children without a partner’s support [21]. Adolescents might be inhibited from accessing sufficient resources to realize their full development [41]. Moreover, parental divorce has been reported to be related to emotional instability [42], perceived guilt or blame [43], aggressive, impulsive, lower levels of educational aspiration [40], and risky behaviors including Internet addiction [44], playing truant [45], and lower self-discipline [40], which might all further negatively affect digital literacy.

Parental education attainment is a great influence on adolescents’ digital literacy and parental mediation. Referring to Valcke’s [46] and Nikken’s [20] analyses of Internet parenting styles, parents with higher education levels reported better parental mediation. They also indicate that higher parental education levels can bring better parent-child digital interaction and further promote these adolescents’ development through setting wise rules and providing warm support. Our findings demonstrated that highly educated parents can not only guide their children’s access to digital devices but also socialize their children into the world of modern information technology in a better way. Moreover, less educated parents experience greater insecurity and lower media proficiency [21], which may inhibit them from adopting new media technologies to enhance parent-child communication and parental involvement to benefit their children from online activities. As for the non-significant association between paternal education level and co-use, fathers in Nanling tend to work outside for a long time to earn a living and they are less likely to take on the role of primary caregiver.

Interestingly, and contrary to popular prejudice, family economic status was negatively associated with adolescents’ digital literacy in the current research. It is believed that economic inequities can affect children’s and adolescents’ choices of recreational equipment [20, 47]. In the present study, adolescents from better-off families in Nanling are more likely to have their own mobile phones and affordable Internet access (e.g., the Internet café), leading to increased recreational screen time, which might in turn leads to lower digital literacy [48]. Besides, Anhui is one of the largest migration-out provinces in China. A surprisingly large amount of parents preferred to work in other cities to earn a better living while leaving their children in the hometown to continue their studies [45]. Thus, there is a discrepancy that the majority of these parents had no practical strategies to guide their children to use technologies appropriately [23]. What’s more, family economic status might be inaccurately reported, as it is a subjective assessment perceived by the child, which may also lead to inconsistencies with previous results.

As for the research on parental mediation and adolescents’ digital literacy, the stronger adolescents perceive their parents to engage in the active mediation and supervision of their digital technology lives, the better digital literacy children are equipped. These results suggest that active mediation and supervision are appropriate types of parental mediation. This is similar to the findings of Duerager & Livingstone [31], which found that active mediation is associated with increased digital skills. Conversely, our study shows that the separate effect of co-use on adolescents’ digital literacy is not significant, but negative when it comes to all dimensions of parental mediation taken into account simultaneously. In addition, contrary to previous research [32], the significant effect of restrictive association turns insignificant when considering the four dimensions of parental mediation. One explanation could be that parents are often not digitally literate in Nanling. Parents do not have the proper strategies to apply co-use and restrictive mediation or even do not know what their children do online [31]. Inappropriate parental practices may not be sufficient to allow children to improve digital literacy through interactions with parents and time regulations. Another explanation could be the less engagement in online activities because of parental restrictive mediation, reducing the opportunity to promote digital literacy. When taking multi-types of parental mediation into account simultaneously, the enhanced positive effect of parental mediation on adolescents’ digital literacy revealed that combined parental mediation strategies are more effective. What’s more, the comprehensive strategy could mitigate the impact of family disadvantage on adolescents’ digital literacy development.

On this basis, it is important to promote parental active mediation and supervision in order to help adolescents navigate the digital world. These can be achieved by encouraging parents, especially those who lack resources, confidence, experience or expertise related to digital media, to upgrade their own digital literacy through proven strategies such as adopting parent-child communication strategies, spending more time staying with their children after work, or expanding home-school contact with the support and guidance from schools and communities to narrow the digital divide and ensured that all adolescents have the opportunity to develop the necessary skills and knowledge to use media safely and effectively [4, 49].

Among several limitations of this study noted, the foremost is that causation cannot be determined because this is a cross-sectional study. Therefore, a longitudinal design is required. In addition, the generalizability of our results can be limited because participants were from one county in Anhui Province only. Future studies should recruit participants from Eastern, Central, and Western China to generate more robust conclusions. A further limitation of this study is that the digital literacy assessment tools employed were developed specifically for this study, and thus their validity has not been fully validated in other research contexts. It would be beneficial for future research to prioritize the systematic validation of these tools, in order to ensure the reliability and robustness of the study results. Moreover, this study did not take the role of the school into account, on account of the social-ecological model [50]. While previous studies noted that usage at home offers much more room for action on the Internet than school-focused usage, which leads to digital-related outcomes more worth exploring [41], future studies might better include school factors since adolescents at this age spend the majority of their time at school. Also, potential biases must be acknowledged, this research solely relied on adolescent self-reports of the parental mediation and family socio-demographic factors and didn’t recruit parents or other caretakers, in particular, the family economic status is subjectively perceived by the child, rather than the objective family income level. Future studies collecting data from both parents and children are needed to ensure validity. Nevertheless, for family socio-demographic characteristics, junior and senior high school students have the ability to self-report. Moreover, high consistency between parent- and child-reported measures of parenting behaviors has been observed [34, 36].

Conclusion

Digital literacy is extremely important for the academic and career development of adolescents in a digital society. This study revealed that it is crucial to advocate for parental active mediation and supervision by promoting the utilization of efficacious strategies. These strategies may include the implementation of parent-child communication techniques, the allocation of additional time for parents to interact with their children after work, or the expansion of home-school contact with the assistance of educational institutions and communities. This approach can facilitate the narrowing of the digital divide and guarantee that all adolescents develop digital literacy and prevent online risks.
