Smirk: An Atomically Complete Tokenizer for Molecular Foundation Models
Alexius Wadell, Anoushka Bhutani, Venkatasubramanian Viswanathan

Abstract
Text-based foundation models have become an important part of scientific discovery, with molecular foundation models accelerating advancements in molecular design and materials science. However, existing models are constrained by closed-vocabulary tokenizers which capture only a fraction of molecular space. In this work, we systematically evaluate thirty tokenizers, including 19 chemistry-specific ones, for their coverage of the SMILES molecular representation language, revealing significant gaps. To assess the impact of tokenizer choice, we introduce n-gram language models as a low-cost proxy and validate their effectiveness by training and fine-tuning 18 RoBERTa-style encoders for molecular property prediction. To overcome the limitations of existing tokenizers, we propose two new tokenizers – Smirk and Smirk-GPE – with full coverage of the OpenSMILES specification. Our results highlight the need for open-vocabulary modeling and chemically diverse benchmarks in cheminformatics. The proposed tokenizer framework systematically integrates nuclear, electronic, and geometric degrees of freedom; this facilitates applications in pharmacology, agriculture, biology, and energy storage.

1.1
Existing Chemistry-specific Tokenizers

In order to input a molecule into a text-based transformer model, the input molecule must first be represented as text.
SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) are the most widely used text-based representation for molecules

Tokenization refers to the process of splitting input sequences into individual tokens that a language model can then process.
It is an essential first step to developing any language model and has been studied extensively by the natural language processing (NLP) community

1.2
Smirk

To enable complete coverage of the OpenSMILES specification, we propose fully decomposing the bracketed atoms into their consistent glyphs using a two-staged pre-tokenization scheme.
The first stage uses a regular expression to decompose the molecule into Atom-wise substrings; the second stage further splits the bracketed compounds into constituent glyphs (elements, bonds, or other chemically meaningful symbols).
Both regular expressions are provided in the supporting information.
For example, Smirk would first split

[C@@H]

[OH]

[

O

for example,

O

C

[

O

This eliminates the need for an immense vocabulary to enumerate all possible bracketed atoms.
Our scheme also eliminates the training step of all widely used tokenizers;
the set of glyphs used by OpenSMILES is fixed and documented by the specification
Craig A. James 2016
.
In contrast to
Schwaller et al. 2018

@@
(clockwise chiral) and
@
(anticlockwise chiral) and to tokenize two digit rings as
%

1

2
instead of
%12

The resulting vocabulary consists of 167 tokens and, by construction, can faithfully tokenize any OpenSMILES

encoded molecule (
fig.

1

Like existing Atom-wise tokenizers, Smirk is implemented using a pre-tokenization step to split the input SMILES encoding and a dictionary model to map individual glyphs to integers.
Unlike current Atom-wise tokenizers, our pre-tokenization scheme consists of two steps: first, splitting the SMILES string using a regular expression similar to
Schwaller et al. 2018

The two-step process is necessary to distinguish between, for example,

[Cn]
for Copernicium.
Both regular expressions are described and documented in the supplementary information.
We have implemented the proposed tokenization scheme in Rust using HuggingFace’s Tokenizers

https://github.com/BattModels/Smirk
and on PyPI.
We have also implemented an equivalent tokenization scheme (
Smirk-Selfies
) for SELFIES
Krenn et al. 2020

1.3
Glyph Pair Encoding

The above scheme is not without its drawbacks.
The average number of tokens per molecule, or fertility, is higher for Smirk than current Atom-wise tokenizers; it uses at least three tokens
[

Au

]
instead of one for any bracketed atom.
Recent works have found a correlation between lower fertility and higher model performance, suggesting that Smirk’s increase in fertility is not desirable
Goldman et al. 2024
;
Rust et al. 2021
.
To address this, we implemented
Smirk-GPE
, which further compresses Smirk’s tokenization using a variant of BPE that operates at the glyph level instead of the byte or character level of existing BPE implementations
Hug 2024
;
OpenAI 2024
.
As with BPE, merges between adjacent tokens are replaced with a single meta-token using merge rules learned from a training corpus
Gage 1994
;
Sennrich et al. 2016
.
Unlike BPE, merges rules operate on token IDs and not pairs of strings, thus
\mbox{merge}(\mbox{{C}},\mbox{{n}})\neq\mbox{{Cn}}
.

Li and Fourches 2021
’s SmilesPE tokenizer operates similarly, but similar to existing Atom-wise tokenizers does not subdivide bracketed elements.
As such, SmilesPE avoids the need to differentiate between a merged
C

n
and
Cn
, but risks emitting the unknown token for bracketed atoms.
Similar issues arise between
[OH]
and
[C@OH1]
; the former
OH
is an oxygen-hydrogen bond, while the latter indicates an octahedral chiral center.

The Smirk-GPE tokenizer was trained on 262,035,601 molecules from the training split of our pretraining dataset derived from the Enamine REAL Space dataset
Enamine Ltd. 2024
.
To reduce the number of unique “words” the training algorithm considers, we split SMILES strings on rings, brackets, non-bonds, and bracketed atoms using a regular expression.
We set a target vocabulary size of 50k tokens; however, training stopped at 2.3k tokens after exhausting all possible merges.
We trained a second variant,
Smirk-GPE (NMB)
, that excluded merges with brackets (
[
or
]
) from being considered during the training process.

2.1
Tokenizer Coverage of SMILES

Seventeen tokenizers were evaluated for their ability to tokenize chemical primitives and molecules from two benchmark datasets: MoleculeNet

This initial set was then expanded to include isotopes, chiral symbols (limited to

Oxidation states were used as a proxy for the possible charged states of an atom, balancing chemical permissibility with the OpenSMILES requirement to support any charge from -15 to +15

2.2
N-Grams as a Metric for Tokenizer Performance

Despite the ubiquity of tokenization in language modeling, assessing the contribution of a tokenizer to overall model performance remains a nascent area of research
Batsuren et al. 2024
;
Goldman et al. 2024
;
Ali et al. 2024
;
Mielke et al. 2021
.
This is partly due to the high cost and confounding factors incurred when assessing a tokenizer performance via overall model performance
Goldman et al. 2024
.
To address this, prior works have proposed various intrinsic metrics, primarily targeting sub-word tokenization
Judit Ács 2019
;
Goldman et al. 2024
;
Gowda and May 2020
;
Rust et al. 2021
.
For example, fertility measures the average number of tokens per word
Judit Ács 2019
;
Gowda and May 2020
.
As the cost of dot-product attention grows quadratically with sequence length, reducing fertility directly reduces cost of evaluating the model

Recent studies have found a lower tokenizer fertility is correlated with better downstream performance
Goldman et al. 2024
;
Rust et al. 2021
.

Gowda and May 2020
, recommend limiting the size of the vocabulary such that 95% of tokens have at least 100 occurrences; helping to avoid the negative impact rare tokens can the accuracy of language models

2.3.1
Finetuning

We then finetuned each model on 12 regression and classification datasets from MoleculeNet
Wu et al. 2018
.
MoleculeNet is a widely used benchmark dataset of small molecules for evaluating molecular machine learning models primarily targeting drug discovery.
However, as a dataset MoleculeNet’s coverage of OpenSMILES is rather limited, as evidenced by the discrepancy in coverage shown in
fig.

1
.
Many tokenizers achieving near-perfect coverage of MoleculeNet even while covering only a fraction of the elements.

To address this, we also finetuned models on the tmQM dataset, a benchmark dataset of quantum mechanical properties for 108k transition metal complexes computed using density functional theory
Balcells and Skjelstad 2020
.
We transcode the provided optimized molecular geometries to SMILES strings using RDKit
Greg Landrum 2024
.
The resulting dataset covers 30 transition metal complexes and contains an abundance enhanced stereochemical centers (i.e. Octahedral centers
[Co@OH1]
).
Consequently, existing Atom-wise tokenizers have extremely limited coverage of the dataset (
fig.

1
), providing an ideal benchmark for evaluating the impact of unknown tokens.
We have provided the resulting dataset and transcoding scripts as part of our
data release
.

Model predictions are computed by first computing an embedding vector as the final hidden state of the first token of the tokenized molecule.
The embedding vector is then passed through a two-layer neural network, the task network, to extract task-specific predictions.
Models were finetuned for 100,000 steps using the AdamW optimizer and a max learning rate of
1.6\times 10^{-4}
on a single A40 GPU.
For all but the QM9 models, this was far in excess of what was required to achieve convergence.
All models used an effective batch size of 128, although for some combinations of dataset and model this was split across multiple micro-batches due to memory constraints.
As with pretraining, molecules were transcoded on-the-fly, dropping any molecules that could not be converted.
Practically, this was only a concern for the tmQM dataset, where 54% of the molecules could not be transcoded to SELFIES due to the presence of enhanced stereochemistry.

Once finetuned, we then evaluated each model on the test split using the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (
fig.

5
).
As regression targets spanned over three orders of magnitude, we’ve elected to report the average-
R^{2}
metric for each model for ease of comparison.
Absolute errors for each model using the MoleculeNet specified metric are reported in the supplementary information.
Overall, Smirk and Smirk-GPE match the performance of models trained using tokenizers from the current state-of-the-art (SMI-TED
Soares et al. 2024
, MoLFormer
Ross et al. 2022
).
Notably, tokenizers with complete coverage (Smirk, Smirk-GPE, Llama and ChemBERTa) perform markedly betted than those with limited coverage.
Checkpoints for all finetuned models are included in the
data release
.

2.4
Information Loss from Unknown Tokens

We suspect complete coverage of the OpenSMILES specification is desirable for a chemistry language model.
However, current models have achieved competitive performance despite lacking full support for the specification (
fig.

1
).
Partially, this reflects the current datasets available for chemistry models; for example, our corpus of 2.1B molecules lacks any molecules containing quadruple bonds.
However, given the performance of existing models, the information lost from masking out unknown glyphs in a SMILES string may be negligible.
To quantify this, we used a bidirectional character-level N-gram model as our reference tokenizer
R
and evaluated the KL-divergence in its predictions with and without sections of the SMILES string masked out.
Specifically, we estimated the probability of a token
x_{i}
using from the joint probability of the preceding and succeeding
n-1
tokens:

B_{n}(x_{i}|x_{i-n+1},\dots x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{i+n-1})\propto\\
\frac{C(x_{i-n+1},\dots,x_{i})+1}{C(x_{i-n+1},\dots,x_{i-1})+V}\times\frac{C(x_{i},\dots,x_{i+n-1})+1}{C(x_{i+1},\dots,x_{i+n-1})+V}

(3)

Next, we identify the tokens
\{x_{j}|\ j\in M\}
that would be tokenized as unknown by the tokenizer being evaluated
E
.
By marginalizing
R
’s distribution of
B_{n}
over the tokens that
E
would tokenize as unknown, we obtain
B^{\prime}_{n}
, the distribution for
x_{i}
without knowledge of the unknown tokens.
The KL-divergence between
B_{n}
and
B^{\prime}_{n}
is then the information lost to the unknown tokens:
D_{KL}(B_{n}||B^{\prime}_{n})=\sum B\cdot\left(\log B_{n}-\log B^{\prime}_{n}\right)
.
A detailed derivation of
B_{n}
and
B^{\prime}_{n}
is presented in the supplementary information.

For example, a tokenizer lacking a token for oxygen (
O
) would tokenize
C1CCOC1
as
C

1

C

C

[UNK]

C

1
.
Hypothetically, if oxygen were the only heterocyclic compound, no information would be lost by replacing the
O
with the
[UNK]
token;
resulting in
B^{\prime}_{n}=B_{n}
, and
D_{KL}(B_{n}||B)=0
.
Otherwise,
D_{KL}(B_{n}||B)>0
, reflecting the increased uncertainty in the identity of the masked token.
Notably, this metric reflects the information lost regarding the identity of the unknown token, which may or may not impact predictions for downstream tasks.
Here, uncertainty over the identity of
O
should result in a higher uncertainty about the molecule’s molecular weight but not the number of rings.
Using a reference n-gram model and tokenizer is necessary to provide a baseline for comparison, as the tokenizer under evaluation does not have any concept of masked tokens.
As a data-driven method, the reference n-gram model is limited by the distribution of the training corpus.
We used a character-level 5-gram model as our reference tokenizer, as it enabled us to precisely marginalize the tokens that would be marked as unknown by the tokenizer under evaluation.

As shown in
fig.

6
, information loss remains small for tokenizers with robust coverage of a dataset.
For example, MoLFormer has near complete coverage of MoleculeNet scoring an average information loss of
0.01
nats/molecule on this dataset.
This jumps to
40.3
nats/molecule when moving to the tmQM dataset as unknown tokens increasingly obscure critical information.
Conversely, Smirk, Smirk-GPE and open-vocabulary tokenizers avoid losing information on either dataset;
they reap measurable performance improvements on a challenging downstream benchmark (
fig.

6
).
Using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, we found a statistically significant negative correlation between 5-gram information loss and downstream performance on the tmQM dataset (
\rho=-0.752,df=10,t=-3.223,p=0.0121
).
This highlights the practical downstream benefits of the robust coverage provided by Smirk and Smirk-GPE.

3
Discussion

We introduce two new tokenizers, Smirk and Smirk-GPE, for chemistry language models that can represent the entirety of the OpenSMILES specification.
In contrast to prior works, Smirk splits bracketed atoms into their constituent elements, enabling full coverage with limited vocabulary (
fig.

1
)
Additionally, we’ve implemented a BPE variant, Smirk-GPE, capable of merging frequently co-occurring SMILES tokens into a single meta-token.
While both tokenizers achieve full coverage of the OpenSMILES specification, differences between SMILES flavors can still lead to unknown tokens.
For example, the MoleculeNet’s HIV benchmark includes
OCc1cc[te]c1
, despite
[te]
not being a valid aromatic symbol.
However, the overall information loss from unknown tokens remains negligible, as
[te]
was the sole ambiguity in our corpus of 1.6B molecules.
Ongoing efforts to standardize the SMILES language could help clarify and eliminate these ambiguities
Apodaca 2022
;
Vincent F Scalfani
.

We introduced the use of n-grams as a proxy language model for a transformer-based model, which allows us to assess the impact of a tokenizer’s particular vocabulary directly.
As noted by prior works, establishing a causal link between tokenizer metrics and model performance remains an immensely expensive task
Goldman et al. 2024
;
Gowda and May 2020
;
Rust et al. 2021
.
N-gram language models can serve as an intermediate step between intrinsic metrics and extrinsic evaluations.
While, n-grams have largely been replaced by neural and transformer-based language models
Mielke et al. 2021
;
however, their lack of hyperparameters makes them ideal as a minimally intrusive baseline language model.
Using n-grams as a proxy language model, we have quantified the potential impact of unknown tokens on model performance (
fig.

6
).
The information loss for a particular molecule can be large (
fig.

6
).
However, due to the rarity of unknown tokens, the impact on downstream performance remains small for most tokenizers (
fig.

6
).

Finally, we pretrained eighteen Roberta-style molecular foundation models and finetuned them on 13 downstream benchmark datasets.
We found strong evidence for a correlation between n-gram and transformer cross-entropy loss (
fig.

4
).
For many downstream tasks, the choice of tokenizer appears to be negligible, consistent with all tokenizers having robust coverage of MoleculeNet (
fig.

1
) and highlighting the limitations of extrinsic tokenizer evaluations.
However, performance suffered markedly on downstream task when using tokenizers with limited coverage of the dataset (
fig.

5
).
As a single-point evaluation, our analysis may underestimate the performance that could be achieved with additional hyperparameter tuning.

Regardless, we argue that eliminating unknown tokens is a desirable property for a tokenizer and have demonstrated that Smirk performs nearly identically to existing chemistry-specific tokenizers (
figs.

2
and
5
).
We have neglected the core questions of whether a chemistry-specific tokenizer is necessary or if general-purpose tokenizers are sufficient.

Xu et al. 2023
achieved state-of-the-art performance for polymer property prediction, using a pretrained tokenizer developed for natural language processing
Xu et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2019
.

Sagawa and Kojima 2023
’s unigram tokenizer displays similar performance and is capable of open-vocabulary modeling, provided an expanded starting alphabet is used (
fig.

1
).
Our results suggest that chemistry-specific tokenizers may result in more robust models (
fig.

3
), but not necessarily higher performance (
figs.

3
and
5
).
Atom-wise tokenization does improve interpretability by allowing researchers to prob the interatomic attention maps directly
Schwaller et al. 2021
and avoids the risk of generation artifacts
Scott Lundberg and Marco Tulio Ribeiro 2023
from splitting an element symbol across multiple tokens.
Smirk expands on this by allowing researchers to directly manipulate the rich information encoded within bracketed atoms while eliminating the risk of out-of-vocabulary tokens.

Ultimately, molecular foundation models must deliver reliable and accurate predictions across the entire molecular design space.
Current, chemistry language models
Ross et al. 2022
;
Schwaller et al. 2019
;
Li and Fourches 2021
;
Bagal et al. 2022
;
Irwin et al. 2022
;
Ahmad et al. 2022
;
Schwaller et al. 2021
;
Schwaller et al. 2021
;
Soares et al. 2024
;
Leon et al. 2024
;
Tu and Coley 2022
employ tokenizers that inadvertently mask out entire atoms, triggering a potentially significant loss of information (
fig.

6
).
This risk is not purely theoretical, cisplatin (
fig.

1
) is an effective chemotherapy drug, but its isomer is not
Ghosh 2019
.
Omitting the chiral marker both implies a tetrahedral – instead of square planar – geometry, and eliminates medical relevant stereochemistry.
More broadly, coordination complexes, including the cis-
\text{[}\text{CoCl}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{(}\text{en}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{]}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and trans-
\text{[}\text{CoCl}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{(}\text{en}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{]}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
complexes that lead the development of coordination chemistry
Kauffman 1967
.
Bracketed atoms are found in the SMILES encodings for Vitamin B12, Tricalcium Sillicate (component of cement), Amoxicillin (antibiotic), Diammonium Phosphate (fertilizer)  Vaska’s Complex (homogenous catalyst)
Vaska and DiLuzio 1961
, 18F-Flurodeoxyglucose (radiotracer used for cancer detection)
Scott and Kilbourn 2020
.
A foundation model for chemistry must encode the entire breath of chemical space or risk obscuring critical features.
Mitigating this risk demands transitioning to tokenizers capable of encoding the entirety of chemical space.
Smirk – and numerous other open-vocabulary tokenizers
Liu et al. 2019
;
Dubey et al. 2024
;
OpenAI et al. 2024
;
Team et al. 2024
– meet this threshold, are permissively licensed and well documented.
The impact on model performance appears negligible, with clear advantages to robustness and interpretability.
