Derivation of stochastic Burgers on the line with a Dirichlet boundary condition at the origin
Cédric Bernardin, Ana Djurdjevac, Patrícia Gonçalves, Leander Schnee

Abstract
. We analyze the equilibrium fluctuations of a Hamiltonian chain of oscillators on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with an exponential potential, perturbed by a conservative, symmetric noise. Under the canonical diffusive scaling t ↦ t ​ n 2 t\mapsto tn^{2} and an interaction strength tuned by n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} , the fluctuation field is known to converge to the energy solution of the stochastic Burgers equation (SBE) on the torus [ 1 ] . We introduce a coupled moving heat bath of strength n − δ n^{-\delta} acting on the particle system. We prove that for δ ≤ 1 \delta\leq 1 (the strong-coupling regime ), the equilibrium fluctuation field converges to the energy solution of the SBE with a Dirichlet boundary condition at zero . We provide two distinct analytical characterizations of these boundary solutions, corresponding to different spaces of test functions. Conversely, for δ > 1 \delta>1 (the weak-coupling regime ), the heat bath becomes irrelevant in the scaling limit: the fluctuations converge to the standard SBE on the full line without any boundary condition, reproducing the full-line result of [ 16 ] . Our analysis thus reveals a sharp critical scaling in the coupling strength δ \delta , which dictates the emergence—or absence—of a macroscopic boundary condition from the microscopic perturbation.

3.
Existence and Characterization of limit-points

In this section we prove that
(\mathcal{Y}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has limit-points that solve the stochastic Burgers equation. We first assume that the sequence
(\mathcal{Y}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight with respect to the Skorohod topology of
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\delta}^{\prime})
. Then from Prohorov’s theorem we know that sub-sequential limits exist. Let us denote a limit point of

by
\mathcal{Y}
. The proof of tightness is postponed to Subsection
3.2
. We now characterise the limit
\mathcal{Y}
as an energy solution to the corresponding SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
) depending on the range of the parameter
\delta
that rules the strength of the moving heat bath. Finally, we also show that for
\delta<-1
the additional boundary conditions from Definition
2.8
are fulfilled.

3.1.
Characterization of limit-points

We start by proving condition
i)
of Definition
2.5
. We follow the same steps as
[
28

.
We want to show that
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a white noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
. Thus we need to show that for every
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
that

has Gaussian distribution
N(0,\sigma^{2}\|H\|_{L^{2}}^{2})
. To that end, we consider the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}
and since we are under the invariant state, which is a product measure, we have that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}[\exp(i\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(H)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}[\exp(i\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{0}(T_{c_{n}t}H)]=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}T_{c_{n}t}H(\tfrac{x}{n})\bar{\xi}_{x}\bigg)\bigg].

From Taylor expansion of the exponential, last display becomes equal to

\prod_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}T_{c_{n}t}H(\tfrac{x}{n})^{2}+\mathcal{O}\Big(n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\Big)\bigg).

Taking the logarithm of last display and noting that
\log(1+x)=x+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
, we get that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\log\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}[\exp(i\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(H)]\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{c_{n}t}H(\tfrac{x}{n})^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|H\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Where the last equality uses the approximation of a Riemann-integral for sufficiently smooth
H
.

3.1.1.
The martingale problem

Recall that we want to show that limit points satisfy a martingale problem. To that end, we employ Dynkin’s formula for time-inhomogeneous Markov processes that we state in Appendix
B
. To that end, let
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\mathbb{R})
and note that

(14)

\mathcal{M}^{n}_{t}(H):=\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(H)-\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{0}(H)-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{s}+n^{2}L_{n,s})\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H)ds,

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration of the process. We start by developing the integral term. We start with the contribution of the time derivative. Recalling (
7
), a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H)=c_{n}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H^{\prime})=\frac{2\rho n^{2}\alpha}{n^{3/2+\kappa}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{c_{n}s}H^{\prime}(\tfrac{x}{n}){\bar{\xi}_{x}}(sn^{2}).

Now we compute the term related to the action of the generator
{L}=\alpha n^{-\kappa}A+\gamma S+n^{-\delta}B_{n,t}
. To make the presentation simple, we start by computing the contribution of the Hamiltonian dynamics. Since
A(\xi_{x})=\xi_{x}\xi_{x+1}-\xi_{x-1}\xi_{x}
, by a summation by parts and by properly centring the variables, we obtain

\displaystyle A\mathcal{Y}_{s}^{n}(H)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\nabla_{n}^{+}(T_{c_{n}s}H)(\tfrac{x}{n}){\bar{\xi}_{x}}(sn^{2}){\bar{\xi}_{x+1}}(sn^{2})+\frac{\rho}{n^{5/2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\Delta_{n}(T_{c_{n}s}H)(\tfrac{x}{n}){\bar{\xi}_{x}}(sn^{2})

\displaystyle-\frac{2\rho}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\nabla_{n}^{+}(T_{c_{n}s}H)(\tfrac{x}{n})\bar{\xi_{x}}(sn^{2}),

where

\nabla_{n}^{+}H(\tfrac{x}{n})=n\big(H(\tfrac{x+1}{n})-H(\tfrac{x}{n})\big)

denotes the discrete gradient of
H
. We also remark, that with our notation the discrete gradient commutes with the translation, i.e.
\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}=T_{c_{n}s}\nabla^{+}_{n}
.

On the other hand, for the exchange dynamics, by performing a summation by parts we get

\gamma S\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(f)=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{c_{n}s}H(\tfrac{x}{n})({\bar{\xi}_{x+1}}(sn^{2})+{\bar{\xi}_{x-1}}(sn^{2})-2{\bar{\xi}_{x}}(sn^{2}))=\frac{\gamma}{n^{2}}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(\Delta_{n}H),

where

is the discrete Laplacian of the test function
H
.
Adding up all the terms we obtain

(15)

\begin{split}(\partial_{s}+n^{2}L_{n,s})&\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H)=\gamma\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(\Delta_{n}H)-\alpha n^{1/2-\kappa}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\nabla^{+}_{n}(T_{c_{n}s}H)(\tfrac{x}{n}){\bar{\xi}_{x+1}(sn^{2})}{\bar{\xi}_{x}}(sn^{2})\\

+n^{3/2-\delta}H&\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\lfloor c_{n}s\rfloor}{n}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\right).\end{split}

Lemma 3.1

Recall the notation (

\displaystyle\|T_{t}H\|_{2,n}^{2}

\begin{split}&\|\Delta_{n}(T_{c_{n}s}H)\|_{2,n}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor-1,-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor+1]}\Delta_{n}(T_{c_{n}s}H)(\tfrac{x}{n})^{2}+\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}(1+u^{2})\Delta_{n}H(u)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n^{-1},n^{-1}]}(u)\\
&\lesssim\frac{1}{n}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n^{-1},n^{-1}]}(1+u^{2})\sup_{v\in[u-n^{-1},u+n^{-1}]}H^{\prime\prime}(v)=\mathcal{O}(1).\end{split}

We will now study the remaining terms in (
15
). We start with the contribution of the heat bath. The previous strategy of employing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality will not be enough and in order to control these additive functionals we will use the Kipnis-Varadhan inequality as stated in Corollary
B.1
. It is at this point that we have to consider different spaces of test functions in order to obtain the desired SPDEs.

•

If
\delta>1
and for
H\in\mathcal{S}
, from Corollary
B.1
we have that

(19)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\Bigg[\left(\int_{0}^{t}n^{3/2-\delta}H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\right)\ ds\right)^{2}\Bigg]\\
&\leq n^{3-2\delta}\|H\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\;ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim t^{2}n^{1-\delta}\end{split}

which vanishes when
\delta>1
.

•

For
\delta\in(-1,1]
we will need the additional assumption that
H(0)=0
, thus
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
suffices.
Now we can apply a first order Taylor approximation and Corollary
B.1
to obtain

(20)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}n^{3/2-\delta}H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(n^{2}s)}-\beta\right)\ ds\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leq n^{1-2\delta}\|H^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\;ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim t^{2}n^{-\delta-1}\end{split}

which vanishes when
\delta>-1
.

•

For
\delta\in(-\infty,-1]
we need to take
H\in\mathcal{S}_{0},
so that for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
j\in\{0,1,\cdots,d\}
it holds
H^{(j)}(0)=0
. From a Taylor expansion of order
d+1
and Corollary
B.1
, we obtain

(21)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}n^{3/2-\delta}H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\right)\ ds\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leq n^{3-2\delta-2(d+1)}\|H^{(d+1)}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda}{\xi_{-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}(sn^{2})}-\beta\;ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim t^{2}n^{-\delta-1-2d}.\end{split}

This vanishes if
\delta>-1-2d
, however by taking
H\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
we can take
d\in\mathbb{N}
arbitrarily large, so that the rate can be made negative for all
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
.

We observe that as the scaling of the heat bath changes (i.e. depending on the regime of
\delta
) we were forced to take test functions in the space
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
to make this term vanish as the estimates we have at hands are not sufficient to accommodate all the values of
\delta
. The drawback of this approach is that we use a space of test functions for which we may loose the uniqueness in law of the solution of the martingale problem. For that reason we need to extract the information about the boundary behavior directly from the microscopic system and this is the role of condition (BC).

Now let us move towards the term coming from the exchange dynamics of
S
. From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with the fact that
\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{n}
is a linear continuous functional such that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{n}(H\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\|T_{c_{n}t}H\|_{2,n}^{2}

we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(\Delta_{n}H)-\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(\Delta H)ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim T\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\Delta_{n}(T_{c_{n}s}H)-\Delta(T_{c_{n}s}H)\right\|_{2,n}^{2}ds

and from Lemma
3.1
we get the convergence to zero by separating the discontinuity of
H^{\prime}
like in (
18
) and using the fact that

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}H(u)-H^{\prime\prime}(u)|\lesssim n^{-1}\sup_{v\in[u-n^{-1},u+n^{-1}]}|H^{\prime\prime\prime}(v)|

for
u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n^{-1},n^{-1}]
.

Then for the converging subsequence we have convergence in law

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(\Delta_{n}H)ds\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\Delta H)ds.

Finally, it remains to deal with the rightmost term on the first line of (
15
).
This is the term whose limit depends on the range of the parameter
\kappa
. To characterize the limit we will need the following result known as the
second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
.

Theorem 3.2

(Second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(s,\cdot)\|_{2,n}^{2}ds<\infty.

Then for all
t\in[0,T]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\varepsilon>0
it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\left(s,\tfrac{x}{n}\right)\left(\bar{\xi}_{x}(sn^{2})\bar{\xi}_{x+1}(sn^{2})-\left(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\varepsilon n}_{x}(sn^{2})\right)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon n}\right)\;ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\|\psi(s,\cdot)\|_{2,n}^{2}\;ds\left(\varepsilon+\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}n}\right),

where
\overrightarrow{\xi}_{x}^{\varepsilon n}
is the empirical average on the box of size
\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor
at the right side of
x
given by

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\xi}_{x}^{\varepsilon n}:=\frac{1}{\left\lfloor\varepsilon n\right\rfloor}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\left\lfloor\varepsilon n\right\rfloor}\bar{\xi}_{y}.

We do not present the proof of last result since it is exactly the same as in
[
19
]
. We note that in that proof only the exchange dynamics is used to control the term that we are estimating and we can simply neglect the contribution of the moving heat bath. We leave these details to the reader. Applying the last result, we observe that when
\kappa>1/2
the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}})
-norm of the rightmost term on the first line of (
15
) vanishes as
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, for
\kappa=1/2
, we are able to replace each variable
\xi_{x}
by the average
\overrightarrow{\xi}_{x}^{\varepsilon n}
, and this allows rewriting that term as

(22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\alpha b^{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\nabla^{+}_{n}\left(T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\tfrac{x}{n}\right)\right)\left(\overrightarrow{\xi}^{\varepsilon n}_{x}(sn^{2})\right)^{2}ds

plus terms that vanish as
n\to+\infty
and
\epsilon\to 0
.
Recall (
11
) and note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{{z}/n})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\sum_{y=\left\lfloor z-c_{n}s\right\rfloor+1}^{\left\lfloor z-c_{n}s+\varepsilon n\right\rfloor}\bar{\xi}_{y}(sn^{2}).

Thus, by making a change of variables, we can rewrite (
22
) as

\displaystyle\alpha b^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\nabla^{+}_{n}H\left(\tfrac{z}{n}\right)\left(\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}\Big(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{z/n}\Big)\right)^{2}ds

plus terms whose
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}})
-norm vanishes as
n\to+\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Assuming the convergence of
\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
in distribution on
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\delta}^{\prime})
now clearly leads to

\displaystyle\alpha b^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}H^{\prime}\left(u\right)\left(\mathcal{Y}_{t}(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u})\right)^{2}du\ ds+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

This means that by taking
\varepsilon\to 0
we get in the limit
\alpha b^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{t}(H)
of Definition
2.5
. We note that the above limit exists since the previous term forms a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}
, and by completeness. Thus for limiting points we obtain all the elements of the martingale problem formulation of the SBE.

For the proof of the bound for
\mathcal{Q}_{t}(H)
of
ii)
in Definition
2.5
, we can use the same arguments as in Section 5.3 of
[
16
]
applying the second order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle. We also leave this details to the reader as they are standard now.

We also note that when we consider the time reversed process
(\mathcal{Y}_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
appearing in condition
iv)
of Definition
2.5
we know that it is generated by
(L_{t}^{\ast})_{t\in[0,T]}
, which is the
L^{2}(\nu_{\beta,\lambda})
-adjoint of
(L_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.
We have seen in Section
2
that
S
and
B_{n,t}
are symmetric operators and
A
is antisymmetric, thus
L^{\ast}_{t}=-\alpha n^{-\kappa}A+\gamma S+n^{\delta}B_{n,t}
. Thus, from the same procedure as we outlined above, it is easy to prove that
(\mathcal{Y}_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the martingale problem stated in condition
iii)
with the prefactor
\mathfrak{b}=-\alpha b^{2}
.

In the simpler case
\kappa>\frac{1}{2}
we retain a prefactor
n^{\kappa-1/2}
in front of the previous computations for the asymmetric term. This implies that the limit of the nonlinear term is
0
and that we fall back into the definition of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation, i.e. (
SBE
) with
\mathfrak{b}=0
.

Finally, it remains showing that the martingale appearing in Dynkin’s formula (
14
) converges, but this is the next and final step that we present below. From this, we conclude that

(\mathcal{Y}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a limit point
\mathcal{Y}
that is an energy solution as we stated in Theorem
2.9
.

3.1.2.
Convergence of the martingale to a continuous Gaussian process

We need to show that the martingale
M^{n}_{t}(H)
defined in (
14
) converges to a mean zero Gaussian process in
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
with covariance given by
t\mapsto 2\gamma\sigma^{2}\|H^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}t
.
To prove this we will use Proposition 4.4 of
[
17
]
which originally can be found in
[
26
]
as Theorem VIII.3.11.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
((M^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
L^{2}
-martingales with càdlàg trajectories and let
\Phi:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous increasing function. Assume that

i)

the quadratic variation
\langle M^{n}\rangle_{t}\to\Phi_{t}
in probability as
n\to\infty
for all
t\in[0,T]
;

ii)

the size of the biggest jump of
M^{n}
vanishes in probability, in other words

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{n}_{t}-M^{n}_{t^{-}}|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

Under these conditions, we conclude that
M^{n}\to M
in law as
n\to\infty
in the
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
where
(M_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a continuous martingale of predictable quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle_{t}=\Phi_{t}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

To prove
i)
, we compute the quadratic variation of
M^{n}_{t}(H)
, which is given by (
58
):

\displaystyle\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}=n^{2}\int_{0}^{t}L_{n,s}(\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H))^{2}-2\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H)L_{n,s}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(H)ds.

This can be computed for each part of the generator separately. The computations for
A
and
S
have already be done in
[
1
]
. Here we just need to compute the contribution coming from the dynamics of the moving thermal bath. Putting all together we get:

(23)

\begin{split}\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}&=\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\bar{\xi}_{x+1}(n^{2}s)-\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}s)\right)^{2}\\
&+n^{1-\delta}H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)^{2}ds.\end{split}

For the appropriate test function
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
we have seen in the previous section that the summand due to the heat bath vanishes at a positive rate.
Because
\xi(n^{2}s)\sim\nu_{\beta,\lambda}
it is clear that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[(\bar{\xi}_{x+1}(n^{2}s)-\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}s))^{2}\right]=2\sigma^{2}
. Therefore as an approximation of a Riemann-integral we obtain

(24)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}\right]=2\gamma\sigma^{2}t\|H^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

In the second step, to get the convergence in probability, we will in fact prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[(\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}\right])^{2}\right]=0

from where the convergence in probability follows. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the stationarity of
\xi
and the fact that the heat bath contribution vanishes with positive rate we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\langle M^{n}(H)\rangle_{t}\right])^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim t\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\left(\gamma\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\bar{\xi}_{x+1}(n^{2}s)-\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}s)\right)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}\ \right)^{2}\right]ds

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma t}{n^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)\right)^{2}ds\ \mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[Z_{x+1,x}(\bar{\xi})Z_{y+1,y}(\bar{\xi})\right],

where the random variable
Z_{x,y}(\xi)=(\xi_{x}-\xi_{y})^{2}-2\sigma^{2}
and they are centred and for disjoint indices also uncorrelated. Furthermore when correlated we have
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[Z_{x+1,x}^{2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\bar{\xi}_{x}^{4}\right]+5\sigma^{4}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[Z_{x+1,x}(\bar{\xi})Z_{x+2,x+1}(\bar{\xi})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\bar{\xi}^{4}_{x}\right]+2\sigma^{4}
, from where it follows that they are uniformly bounded. Thus, the last display is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma t}{n^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{y\in\{x-1,x,x+1\}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)\right)^{2}ds\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[Z_{x+1,x}(\bar{\xi})Z_{y+1,y}(\bar{\xi})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\sum_{i\in\{-1,0,1\}}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{\cdot}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\nabla^{+}_{n}T_{c_{n}s}H\left(\frac{\cdot+i}{n}\right)\right)^{2}\right\|^{2}_{2,n}ds\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

where the last bound is obtained by Lemma
3.1
and an estimate similar to (
18
). This estimate combined with (
24
) gives that
\langle M^{n}\rangle_{t}\to 2\gamma\sigma\|H^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}t
in probability as
n\to\infty
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

It remains to prove the condition
ii)
of Proposition
3.3
. Note that we can not replicate the argument of
[
1
]
, which used crucially the conservation of energy, because in our case the Markov process is evolving on
\mathbb{Z}
and not on the finite torus.
We need then to study the expectation of the maximal jump of
M^{n}(H)
, which is equal to the maximal jump of the fluctuation field
\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(H)
because they only differ by an integral in time which, as we will see in the proof of tightness, is almost surely continuous.
To study the jumps of
\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}
we define
t\mapsto y_{t}
to be the process that maps
t
to the index
x
where the last exchange of
\xi_{x}
and
\xi_{x+1}
occurred (due to the dynamic of
S
). This is well-defined because there are only countably many jumps with Exp(
\gamma
) distribution. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\

\displaystyle|(\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(H)-\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t^{-}}(H)|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\left(T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{y_{t}+1}{n}\right)-T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{y_{t}}{n}\right)\right)\left(\xi_{y_{t}+1}(n^{2}t)-\xi_{y_{t}}(n^{2}t)\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{y_{t}}{n}\right)\left(\xi_{y_{t}+1}(n^{2}t)-\xi_{y_{t}}(n^{2}t)\right))\right)^{2}\right].

We then use the convexity of the square
\left(\xi_{y_{t}+1}-\xi_{y_{t}}\right)^{2}\leq 2\xi_{y_{t}+1}^{2}+2\xi_{y_{t}}^{2}
which gives two terms that can be bounded in the same way: using the fact that all
\xi_{x}
are positive we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg(\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{y_{t}}{n}\right)\xi_{y_{t}}(n^{2}t)\bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\bigg|\xi_{x}(n^{2}t)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\bigg|\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}t)+\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\bigg|\rho\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\bigg|\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}t)\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{\rho^{2}}{n}\left\|\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{\cdot}{n}\right)\bigg|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|^{4}_{2,n}.

Now, rightmost term in last line is of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
due to Lemma
3.1
, and therefore vanishes as
n\to+\infty
, we focus on the leftmost term. We use the martingale decomposition for
\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{\cdot}(G_{n})
, with
G_{n}(x):=|\nabla^{+}_{n}H(x)|
, and applying Doob’s martingale inequality to the martingale, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\nabla_{n}^{+}T_{c_{n}t}H\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)\bigg|\bar{\xi}_{x}(n^{2}t)\right)^{2}\Bigg]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{t}(G_{n})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\langle M^{n}(G_{n})\rangle_{T}\right]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{0}(G_{n})+\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{s}+n^{2}L_{n,t})\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(G_{n})ds\right)^{2}\right].

In the next section, while proving tightness, we will show that the initial fluctuation field and the integral of the generators
A
and
S
applied to the fluctuation field are bounded in expectation.
Therefore, last terms vanish at rate
O(n^{-2})
.
The contributions of the heat bath generator
B_{n,s}
have to be analised separately because the function
G_{n}
defined above does not fulfill the required boundary condition at
0
even when
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
. In fact, using the Corollary
B.1
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}B_{n,s}\mathcal{Y}^{n}_{s}(G_{n})ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}n^{2-\frac{1}{2}-\delta}G_{n}\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)W^{\prime}_{\beta,\lambda}(\xi_{x_{n}(s))}(n^{2}t))ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim Tn^{1-2\delta}\|G_{n}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\|W_{\beta,\lambda}(\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor})\|^{2}_{-1,n,s}ds\lesssim n^{-(\delta+1)}

which vanishes for
\delta\in(-1,\infty)
. For
\delta\leq-1
we take
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}=\mathcal{S}_{0}
, and from a Taylor approximation at
0
of order
d\in\mathbb{N}
for
H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor+1}{n}\right)
and
H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)
, we get

\displaystyle G_{n}\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=n\left(H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor+1}{n}\right)-H\left(\frac{c_{n}s-\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}{n}\right)\right)\lesssim n^{1-d}.

Thus the term containing
B_{n,s}
vanishes a arbitrarily high rate when
\delta\in(-\infty,-1]
and
H\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
.
In the same way we can treat the term
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\langle M^{n}(G_{n})\rangle_{T}\right]
where we have calculated the expression in (
23
). The first part given by the generator
S
converges and is bounded, and hence vanishes as
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. The term due to
B_{n,t}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{1-\delta})
for
\delta\in(-1,\infty]
, for
\delta\in(-\infty,-1)
the test function
H\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
gives us a vanishing term at arbitrarily fast rate again.

Thus, from
ii)
follows.
Therefore we can apply Proposition
3.3
so that
(M^{n}_{t}(H))_{t\in[0,T]}
converges in law on
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
to a continuous mean zero martingale
(M_{t}(H))_{t\in[0,T]}
with predictable quadratic variation
2\gamma\sigma^{2}\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}t
.

\mathcal{Y}
be a solution of SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
).
Then there exists a unique process
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}_{s}ds\in\mathcal{\mathcal{}}C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{S}_{Neu}^{\prime})
such that for all
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Neu}}
and all

the following bound holds for any

(39)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}_{s}ds\right)(\psi)-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{D}\left(\mathcal{Y}_{s}\left(\rho(u,\cdot)\right)^{2}-\left\|\rho(u,\cdot)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)\psi(u)du\right)ds\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\lesssim T\left(\left(\int_{D}u^{2}\psi(u)^{4}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}R(\rho)S(\rho)\end{split}

where we set

(40)

\begin{split}R(\rho)&:=\sup_{u\in D}\||u-\cdot|\rho(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\langle\rho(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1\|_{L^{4}_{u}},\\
S(\rho)&:=\Big(\sup_{u\in D}\|\rho(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}+\|\rho(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{2}\Big)\cdot\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Furthermore, recalling the definition of
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u}
given by (

), for all
G\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}

(41)

\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}_{t}(G)&:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u})^{2}G^{\prime}(u)\ duds=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\big(\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\rho^{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}-\|\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big)G^{\prime}(u)\ duds\\
&=\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}_{s}ds\right)(G^{\prime}).\end{split}

A very similar result has been shown first in
[
23
, Proposition 3.15]
on the infinite line
\mathbb{R}
and then in
[
21
, Lemma 6.10]
in
[0,1]
. We will adapt here the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 6.10]
to our setting where
D=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is unbounded.
The proof requires some preliminary theory on functionals of Gaussians using Wiener Chaos theory, which we recall in Appendix
C
.

Proof.

We start by defining

\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho(u,\cdot))=\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\rho(u,\cdot))^{2}-\|\rho(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Then, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Neu}}
, we will construct the process
\big(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}_{s}ds\big)(\psi)
as the limit of the Cauchy sequence of processes in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
:

\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{D}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot))\psi(u)duds\right)_{\varepsilon>0}

for the sequence
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
given by
\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u}(v)
, where
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(\cdot)
has been defined in (
36
).
However, for the moment
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is an arbitrary sequence of elements in
L^{1}(D^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(D^{2})
.

Recall that for any
s\geq 0
, by stationarity,
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
is a space white noise with covariance
\mathfrak{c}/\mathfrak{a}
, which for simplicity we assume to be equal to one. The term
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{s}^{\diamond 2}
can be rewritten in terms of the second Wiener-Itô integral, and, using the stochastic Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}\big(\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\rho(u,\cdot))^{2}-\|\rho(u,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big)\psi(u)du

\displaystyle=\int_{D}W_{2}\big(\rho(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\big)(\mathcal{Y}_{s})\psi(u)du

\displaystyle=W_{2}\bigg(\int_{D}\rho(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\psi(u)du\bigg)(\mathcal{Y}_{s}).

Let us fix two positive real numbers
\varepsilon
and
\delta
. We apply the well-known variance estimate, from Proposition
C.2
together with the linearity of
W_{2}
and Lemma
C.4
, to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot))\psi(u)duds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot))\psi(u)duds\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq T\left\|\int_{D}W_{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\right)(\mathcal{Y})\psi(u)ds-\int_{D}W_{2}\left(\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\right)(\mathcal{Y})\psi(u)ds\right\|_{-1,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T\left\|W_{2}\left(\int_{D}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}-\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\right)\psi(u)ds\right)(\mathcal{Y})\right\|_{-1,\mu}^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq T\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{2})\\
f\text{ symmetric }\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\langle\int_{D}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}-\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}\right)\psi(u)ds,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}}}{2d!\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}}

where
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{2})
is defined in (
66
). To estimate the last term we rewrite the scalar product

(43)

\begin{split}&\left\langle\int_{D}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2}-\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot)^{\otimes 2})\psi(u)\ du\ ,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\\
&=\int_{D^{3}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})\delta_{0}(u-v_{2})\right)\psi(u)f(v_{1},v_{2})\ dudv_{1}dv_{2}\\
&+\int_{D^{3}}\left(\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})\delta_{0}(u-v_{2})-\rho_{\delta}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\delta}(u,v_{2})\right)\psi(u)f(v_{1},v_{2})\ dudv_{1}dv_{2}.\end{split}

Now we only treat the term on the second line of last display because the third one can be handled in the same way by changing
\varepsilon
into
\delta
. We decompose

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})

\displaystyle-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})\delta_{0}(u-v_{2})

\displaystyle=(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})-\delta_{0}(u,v_{1}))\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})+\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{2}))

and again only consider the first term, which is bounded like the second by symmetry. We then add and subtract
f(u,v_{2})
, apply the triangle inequality and use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{D^{3}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1}))\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)f(v_{1},v_{2})dudv_{1}dv_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{D^{3}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)(f(v_{1},v_{2})-f(u,v_{2}))dudv_{1}dv_{2}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\int_{D^{2}}\int_{D}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})\,dv_{1}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)(f(v_{1},v_{2})-f(u,v_{2}))dudv_{2}\right|

(44)

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{D^{3}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)\int_{u}^{v_{1}}\partial_{w}f(w,v_{2})dwdudv_{1}dv_{2}\right|

(45)

\displaystyle+\left|\int_{D^{2}}\int_{D}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1})\,dv_{1}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)f(u,v_{2})dudv_{2}\right|.

Up to here the proof followed the same steps as in
[
21
]
. But, now, we use new bounds because our domain
D
is unbounded.

To treat (
44
), we use the Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, then we apply twice the Jensen’s inequality (as in

(\int fg)^{2}\leq\int f^{2}\|g\|_{1}g
for
g\geq 0
with
g\in L^{1}
), and we conclude using
L^{\infty}
estimates. Thus we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{D^{3}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)\int_{u}^{v_{1}}\partial_{w}f(w,v_{2})dwdudv_{1}dv_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\underset{C.-S.}{\leq}\left(\int_{D^{2}}\left(\int_{D^{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)\mathbbm{1}_{[u,v_{1}]}(w)dudv_{1}\right)^{2}dwdv_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{D^{2}}\partial_{w}f(w,v_{2})^{2}dwdv_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{Jensen}}{\leq}\left(\int_{D^{3}}\left(\int_{D}\mathbbm{1}_{[u,v_{1}]}(w)\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})dv_{1}\right)^{2}\psi(u)^{2}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v_{2})\|_{L^{1}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})dudwdv_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

\displaystyle\underset{Jensen}{\leq}\left(\int_{D^{4}}\mathbbm{1}_{[u,v_{1}]}(w)^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v_{2})\|_{L^{1}}\psi(u)^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})dwdv_{1}dv_{2}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

\displaystyle\underset{L^{\infty}}{\leq}\left(\sup_{u\in D}\||u-\cdot|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}\int_{D^{2}}\psi(u)^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})dv_{2}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in D}\||u-\cdot|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{u\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}.

For (
45
), we use the boundary condition
f(0,v_{2})=0
to use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Then we use Jensen’s inequality,
L^{\infty}
bounds and the Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\int_{D}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{1})-\delta_{0}(u-v_{1}))\ dv_{1}\ \rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)f(u,v_{2})dv_{2}du

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{2}}(\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)\int_{D}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,u]}(w)\partial_{w}f(w,v_{2})dwdv_{2}du

\displaystyle\underset{C.-S.}{\leq}\left(\int_{D^{2}}\left(\int_{D}(\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,u]}(w)du\right)^{2}dwdv_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{Jensen}}{\leq}\left(\int_{D^{3}}(\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1)^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v_{2})\|_{L^{1}(D)}\psi(u)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,u]}(w)^{2}dwdudv_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

\displaystyle\underset{L^{\infty}}{\leq}\left(\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}\int_{D^{2}}(\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1)^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,v_{2})\psi(u)^{2}u\ dv_{2}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

\displaystyle\underset{L^{\infty}}{\leq}\left(\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}\sup_{u\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\int_{D}(\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1)^{2}\psi(u)^{2}u\ du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}

(47)

\displaystyle\underset{C.-S.}{\leq}\left(\sup_{v\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,v)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{u\in D}\|\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1\|_{L_{u}^{4}}\left(\int_{D}u^{2}\psi(u)^{4}du\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}.

Recall the definition of
R,S
given by (
40
). Putting (
46
) and (
4.1.1
) together, enables us to bound the fraction in the supremum in (
4.1.1
) uniformly in
f
, so that we get

(48)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho_{\varepsilon}(u,\cdot))\psi(u)duds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\diamond 2}_{s}(\rho_{\delta}(u,\cdot))\psi(u)duds\right|^{p}\Bigg]\\
&\lesssim T\Big(\left(\int_{D}u^{2}\psi(u)^{4}du\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)\big(R(\rho_{\varepsilon})S(\rho_{\varepsilon})+R(\rho_{\delta})S(\rho_{\delta})\big).\end{split}

To obtain a converging sequence it now suffices to choose a sequence
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
such that

(49)

\displaystyle R(\rho_{\varepsilon})S(\rho_{\varepsilon})\to 0\ \text{ as }\ \varepsilon\to 0.

Indeed for such
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
we obtain a Cauchy sequence in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for all test functions
\psi
, with limit
\big(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}ds\big)(\psi)
. This together with Mitoma’s criterion gives us then the convergence to
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}ds
in distribution in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\text{Neu}})
. We also then deduce that (
39
) holds. Observe that
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}ds
depends a priori of the sequence
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
chosen.

We now show that
(\iota^{\cdot}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot))_{\varepsilon>0}
and
(\rho^{\cdot}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot))_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfy (
49
). Indeed
\|\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}=1=\|\iota_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(v)\|_{L^{1}}
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
, thus we have
S(\iota_{\varepsilon})=1
and
S(\rho_{\varepsilon})=1
. Furthermore we have
\langle\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u}(\cdot),1\rangle_{L^{2}}=1
for all
u\in D
, and additionally,

\displaystyle\|\langle\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u},1\rangle_{L^{2}}-1\|_{L^{4}}^{4}=\int_{D}|\chi_{\varepsilon}(u)-1|^{4}du\leq 2\varepsilon.

Finally for any

\displaystyle\||u-\cdot|\times\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}+\||u-\cdot|\times\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\varepsilon(\|\iota_{\varepsilon}^{u}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}+\|\rho_{\varepsilon}^{u}\|_{L^{1}})\leq\varepsilon\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Thus we have proven that
R(\iota_{\varepsilon})\to 0
and
R(\rho_{\varepsilon})\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which completes our proof.

We thus define the process
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond 2}ds
by using the sequence
(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}=(\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(\cdot))_{\varepsilon>0}
. Then, for any
G\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
, we apply (
39
) with
\psi=G^{\prime}
,
\rho=\iota_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(\cdot)
and deduce, since
R(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(\cdot))S(\iota_{\varepsilon}^{\cdot}(\cdot)))
goes to
0
as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
, the statement (
41
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.2.
Uniqueness for SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{0}
) with boundary conditions (BC)

To prove uniqueness for solutions of SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{0}
) that also satisfy additional boundary conditions given in (BC) on
D=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
we will closely follow the ideas of
[
4
]
which were used to handle the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process on the domain
(0,1)
. The goal can be summarised in the two following results.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Y}
is a solution of SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{0}
) satisfying (BC). Then
\mathcal{Y}
can be uniquely extended into a process
\mathcal{Y}
in
C([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}^{\prime})
solving SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
).

Last theorem enables us to make use of the uniqueness result proved in the previous section.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}
be two solutions to SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{0}
) that additionally satisfy (BC). Then
\mathcal{Y}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}
have the same law.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.6
,
\mathcal{Y}
has a uniquely defined extension that is solution to SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
). Because the solutions to SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
) are unique there can not be any
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}
different from
\mathcal{Y}
satisfying the assumptions of the theorem.
∎

Now that we have seen how to obtain uniqueness, we have to construct the extension of
\mathcal{Y}
solving SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
). We start by defining the Banach-space which will be used for that construction.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{H}
be the Hilbert space of real valued progressively measurable processes
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
in
L^{2}
equipped with the norm
\|X\|_{\mathfrak{H}}=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}X_{s}^{2}ds\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Denote
\mathfrak{B}
as the Banach space of real valued processes
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
with continuous trajectories equipped with the norm
\|X\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{B}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}|^{2}\right]
. Note that
\|X\|_{\mathfrak{H}}\leq\sqrt{T}\|X\|_{\mathfrak{B}}
gives us the continuous embedding
\mathfrak{B}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{H}
.

Then we start with the simple but more general extension.

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
H\in L^{2}(D)
we can uniquely define
(\mathcal{Y}_{t}(H))_{t\in[0,T]}
as a process in
\mathfrak{H}
, allowing this abuse of notation using
\mathcal{Y}
for our limit process and its extension. The extension coincides with our SBE solution as soon as
H\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
. Furthermore, for every
t\in[0,T]
the extension
\mathcal{Y}
is a space white-noise.

Proof.

The proof uses
i)
of Definition
2.5
and the argument of Remark
2.7
.
∎

To extend
\mathcal{Y}
to a solution of SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
) we will use the following intermediate space of test functions that we define as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}:=\big\{H\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(D)\mid H(0^{+})=H(0^{-})=H^{\prime}(0^{+})=H^{\prime}(0^{-})=0\big\}.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
H\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. There exists a sequence of functions
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{S}_{0}
such that
H_{n}\to H
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(D)
, i.e.
\lim_{n\to\infty}H_{n}^{(k)}=H^{(k)}
in
L^{2}(D)
for
k=0,1,2
.

Proof.

For
H\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, the sequence
H_{n}=H\Phi_{n^{-1}}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
as for, where

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(u):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\phi}(u)&u\in[0,n^{-1}],\\
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(-u)&u\in[-n^{-1},n^{-1}],\\
1&u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-n^{-1},n^{-1}],\end{array}\right.

and
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(u):=\int_{0}^{nu}a(v)\ dv
where
a(\cdot)
is given in Definition
4.13
. The proof is analogous to that of Lemma A.4 of
[
4
]
and for that reason it is omitted.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

The continuous extension of
\mathcal{Y}
is a solution to SBE(
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
) in the sense of Definition
2.5
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Lemma 5.3 in
[
4
]
, except that now we need to handle the nonlinear term.
To that end, fix any
H\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. From Lemma
4.10
, we now that there exists a sequence
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with

that converges in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(D)
to

.
One easily sees that

is linear with respect to the test function

which means that the martingale
M
also is, such that we get the convergence of

in
\mathfrak{B}
with a limit

which is a continuous martingale with quadratic variation
t\to\mathfrak{c}t\|H^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}
.
Lemma
4.9
tells us that the extention of
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
i)
and also gives us existence of the
\mathfrak{H}
-limit

.
Moreover we can show using Cauchy-Schwarz for the invariant process
\mathcal{Y}
and the

that

and that the limit must be

in
\mathfrak{B}
(and

For the nonlinear term we note that the proof of the second order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle in Theorem
3.2
holds for any test function

and does not require special boundary conditions because only the exchange dynamics of

are relevant. This implies that for any such

the bound of property

in Definition
2.5

.
In the same way the proof from Section 6.1 of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{Q}_{t}(H-H_{n})\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim t^{\frac{3}{2}}\|H^{\prime}-H_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}.

to

in

. Moreover, we get the same bound (

) for the limit which, through stationary increments and Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem, implies the almost sure pathwise continuity of

.
Combining those convergence results we obtain that

with the equality in

Finally this argument can be repeated analogously for the backwards martingale of

.
∎

Appendix A
Sobolev spaces and core of the Laplacian.

In this section we recall the definitions of Sobolev-spaces and some facts on the core of the Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
D=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Definition A.1
.

We write
\mathcal{H}^{k}(D)
for
k=1,2
to be the space of function
H\in L^{2}(D)
with the
k
-th derivative
H^{(k)}\in L^{2}(D)
given as the unique function
H^{(k)}
satisfying

\displaystyle\forall G\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D):\int_{D}H(u)G^{(k)}(u)\ du=(-1)^{k}\int_{D}H^{(k)}(u)G(u)\ du

For a function

we define the semi-norms
\|H\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{k}}:=\|H^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}
and the full norms on the space given by

\displaystyle\|H\|_{k}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|H\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{i}}.

We also define the space
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(D)
as the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}

with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{k}
.
Furthermore we define the fractional Sobolev norms for
\alpha\in(0,1)
by
\|H\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}:=\|H\|_{L^{2}}+\|H\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}}
setting

\displaystyle\|H\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}}:=\int_{(0,\infty)^{2}}\frac{|H(v)-H(u)|^{2}}{|v-u|^{1+\alpha}}dudv+\int_{(-\infty,0)^{2}}\frac{|H(v)-H(u)|^{2}}{|v-u|^{1+\alpha}}dudv.

Remark A.2
.

Note that by considering the weak derivatives on

we allow for discontinuities of the weak-derivatives at

. Therefore

, but not every function in

is 2-times (weakly-) differentiable on

, e.g. a function

Lemma A.3

Let

(53)

\displaystyle\|\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(G)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-(\beta-\alpha)}\|G\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}},

For

\displaystyle=\int_{D}|\partial_{u}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(G)(u)|^{2}du

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{D}|\partial_{u}\chi_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{2}|\check{\iota}_{\varepsilon}\ast G(u)|^{2}du+\int_{D}|\chi_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{2}|\partial_{u}(\check{\iota}_{\varepsilon}\ast G)(u)|^{2}du

\displaystyle\int_{D}|\partial_{u}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(G)(u)|^{2}du

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{D}|\partial_{u}\chi_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{2}|\check{\iota}_{\varepsilon}\ast G(u)|^{2}du+\int_{D}|\chi_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{2}|\partial_{u}(\check{\iota}_{\varepsilon}\ast G)(u)|^{2}du

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{-2}\sup_{v\in[-2\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]}|G(v)|^{2}du+\|\partial_{u}G\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\partial_{u}G\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality with

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}|\check{\iota}_{\varepsilon}\ast G(u)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}\left|\int_{u}^{u+\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{-1}G(v)dv\right|\leq\sup_{u\in[-2\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]}|G(u)|

Lemma A.5
.

For the Dirichlet-Laplacian
\Delta:\mathcal{D}(\Delta)\to L^{2}(D)

Let us first show that

(and thus

is assumed to be a core, there exists a sequence

of functions

such that

H_{-}

\langle M(f)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}L_{s}f^{2}(s,X_{s})-2f(s,X_{s})L_{s}f(s,X_{s})ds.

For the variance estimate we start by defining for
f\in L^{2}(\tilde{\pi})
the
\mathcal{H}^{-1}_{\tilde{\pi}}
semi-norm

(62)

\|f\|_{-1,\tilde{\pi}}^{2}:=\sup_{g\in L^{2}(\tilde{\pi})}\left\{2\langle f,g\rangle_{\tilde{\pi}}-\langle g,(-\tilde{S})g\rangle_{\tilde{\pi}}\right\},

with
\tilde{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{L}+\tilde{L}^{\ast})
. We note that

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{-1,\tilde{\pi}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sup_{{g\in\mathcal{C}}}2\langle f(t,\cdot),g\rangle_{\pi}-\langle g,(-S_{t})g\rangle_{\pi}dt=:\int_{0}^{\infty}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{-1,\pi,t}^{2}\ dt

which, as an application of (
59
), yields the estimate

(63)

E_{\pi}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(s,X_{s})ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim T\int_{0}^{\infty}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{-1,\pi,t}\ dt.

One special case, given in the following result, will be used to bound the heat bath generator.

Corollary B.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N},0\leq t\leq T
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
let
\varphi_{n}(t,\xi):=-W^{\prime}_{\beta,\lambda}(\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor})=\big(\tfrac{\lambda}{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}-\beta\big)
. Then for the Markov process
\xi
with its invariant measure
\nu_{\beta,\lambda}
defined in (
1
) we have the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}(s,\xi(sn^{2}))\ ds\Big)^{2}\Big]\lesssim T^{2}n^{\delta-2}.

Proof.

We apply (
63
) to the setting with
\pi=\nu_{\beta,\lambda}
and
\tilde{S}=n^{2}(\gamma S+n^{-\delta}B_{n,t})
, then it only remains to compute the norm
\|\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{-1\nu_{\beta,\lambda},s}
.
We have that

(64)

\begin{split}\|\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{-1,n,s}^{2}&=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{2\langle\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot),g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}+n^{2}\left\langle g,(\gamma\mathcal{S}+n^{-\delta}B_{n,s})g\right\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\}\\
&\leq\sup_{g\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{2\langle\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot),g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}+n^{2-\delta}\left\langle g,B_{n,s}g\right\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\}\\
&=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{2\langle\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot),g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}-n^{2}n^{-\delta}\left\langle\left(\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}}g\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\}.\end{split}

By definition of
\nu_{\beta,\lambda}
and
\varphi_{n}
we can integrate by parts and use Young’s inequality

\langle\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot),g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}=\langle 1,\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}}g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\leq\frac{1}{2K}+\frac{K}{2}\|\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}s\right\rfloor}}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Plugging this into (
64
) with
K=n^{2-\delta}
yields

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{-1,\nu_{\beta,\lambda},s}\leq n^{\delta-2},

independently of
s\in[0,T]
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Gaussian Analysis and Estimates

In this section we develop the tools necessary to estimate the variance of functionals of the space white-noise. Building on the variance estimate (
59
) we will use the Wiener-chaos expansion to get a more precise estimate of the variational norm, as in
[
23
, Section 3]
.

Recall the definition in Section
2.4
of the space white-noise on
L^{2}(D)
with
D=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and the corresponding measure
\mu
given in (
10
). We assume that
\mathcal{Y}=(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is an energy solution of SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
) and thus for every
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
can be extended uniquely to a space white-noise mapping functions of
L^{2}(D)
to
L^{2}(\mu)
.

C.1.
The variance estimate

We will restate the variance estimate of Appendix
B
in the setting of the solutions to SBE(
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
) on
L^{2}(\mu)
.
We define the vector space
\mathcal{F}
of
cylinder
functions
F:\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}^{\prime},\quad F(\mathcal{Y})=f(\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{1}),...,\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{d}))

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has at most polynomial growth of all derivatives up to order
2
, and
(\varphi_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}^{d}
are arbitrary. The operator
L_{0}
is defined by its action on such a cylinder function
F\in\mathcal{F}
as

\displaystyle L_{0}F(\mathcal{Y}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}f(\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{1}),...,\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{d}))\mathcal{Y}(\Delta\varphi_{i})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{i,j}f(\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{1}),...,\mathcal{Y}(\varphi_{d}))\langle\nabla\varphi_{i},\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle.

Since, by Lemma
A.5
,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
is a core of the Dirichlet-Laplacian, it follows that the space
\mathcal{F}
is a core of
L_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mu)
, i.e. the domain
\mathcal{D}(L_{0})
is the closure of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to the graph norm
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}+\|L_{0}F\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
(the argument is the same as in
[
23
, Corollary 3.8]
).

We define the following variational norms.

Definition C.1
.

The semi-norms
\|\cdot\|_{\pm 1,\mu}
are defined for
F\in\mathcal{F}
by

\displaystyle\|F\|^{2}_{1,\mu}

\displaystyle:=2\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{Y}_{0})(-L_{0})F(\mathcal{Y}_{0})\right]=2\langle F,(-L_{0})F\rangle_{\mu}\ ,

\displaystyle\|F\|_{-1,\mu}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{2\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{Y}_{0})G(\mathcal{Y}_{0})\right]-\|G\|^{2}_{1,\mu}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sup_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{Y}_{0})G(\mathcal{Y}_{0})\right]}{\|G\|_{1,\mu}}\ .

We introduce an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{F}
by declaring that
F
and
G
are equivalent whenever
\|F-G\|_{1,\mu}=0
. In this way,
\|\cdot\|_{1,\mu}
acts as a norms on the associated quotient space of
\mathcal{F}
. We then define
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}_{0,\mu}
as the closure of the resulting
equivalence classes of elements of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{1,\mu}
. Similarly,
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}_{\mu}
is defined as the closure of these classes under
\|\cdot\|_{-1,\mu}
.

Similarly to
[
23
, Corollary 3.5]
we have:

Proposition C.2
.

Let
F\in L^{2}(\mu)\cap\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}_{\mu}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}F(\mathcal{Y}_{s})ds\right|^{2}\right]\lesssim T\|F\|^{2}_{-1,\mu}.

C.2.
Gaussian Analysis

We now describe Wiener-chaos theory (see
[
33
, Chapter 1]
for more details) which will be useful to estimate the previous
\|\cdot\|_{-1,\mu}
-norm in terms of the variational formula for functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(D^{d})
,
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the
d
-th order Wiener-Itô integral
W_{d}:L^{2}(D^{d})\to L^{2}(\mu)
is defined precisely in
[
33
, Section 1.1.2]
but we just need some of its properties and we omit its definition. It satisfies
W_{d}(\Pi f)=W_{d}(f)
, where
\Pi f
denotes the symmetrisation of
f
, i.e. the real valued function on
D^{d}
defined by

(65)

\forall u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in D^{d},\quad[\Pi f](u)=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}f(u_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,u_{\sigma(d)})\ ,

where
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is the symmetric group on
\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Let
L_{s}^{2}(D^{d})=\{f\in L^{2}(D^{d})\;;\;\Pi f=f\}
be the space of symmetric square integrable functions on
L^{2}(D^{d})
. Then, for any
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
there exists a unique (deterministic) sequence
(f_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\prod_{d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})
, such that the chaos-expansion of
F
is given by the converging series in
L^{2}(\mu)
:

\displaystyle F=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}W_{d}(f_{d})\ .

Furthermore, it holds that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d!}}W_{d}
is an isometry between
L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})
and the
d
-th homogeneous chaos
\Big\{W_{d}(f_{d})\;;\;f_{d}\in L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})\Big\}
, which is also equal to the closure of the span of all random variables
\mathcal{Y}\mapsto H_{d}(\mathcal{Y}(\varphi))\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
such that
\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
, and
H_{d}
is the
d
-th Hermite polynomial
7
7

7

H_{d}(u):=(-1)^{d}e^{\frac{u^{2}}{2}}\partial_{u}^{d}[e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}]
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
.
. In fact it holds that
H_{d}(\mathcal{Y}(\varphi))=W_{d}(\varphi^{\otimes d})
.

In order to approximate elements of
L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})
we define a version of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}
with domain
D^{d}
:

(66)

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d}):=\Big\{g\in C^{\infty}(D^{d})\;;\;&\forall u\in\partial D^{d},\ \alpha=(2k_{1},...,2k_{d})\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\partial_{\alpha}g(u)=0,\\
&\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\|g\|_{\infty,\ell}=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\ i+|j|\leq\ell}\ \sup_{u\in D^{d}}(1+|u|^{i})\partial^{j}_{u}\,g(u)<\infty\Big\}.\end{split}

Now we can express the action of
L_{0}
in terms of the Laplacian for elements of a homogeneous chaos.

Lemma C.3
.

For all symmetric
f_{d}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d})
it holds
L_{0}W_{d}(f_{d})=W_{d}(\Delta f_{d})
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as in
[
23
, Lemma 3.7]
where
\mathbb{R}
there is replaced by
D
, and the integration by parts also hold here because functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d})
vanish at
0
.
∎

We have seen that applying
L_{0}
to elements of
L^{2}(\mu)
is related to the regularity of elements of
L^{2}(D^{d})
, thus we can rewrite the the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\pm 1,\mu}
in terms of elements in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d})
.

Lemma C.4
.

For symmetric
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d})
(i.e. with
\Pi f=f
) we have

\displaystyle\|W_{d}(f)\|_{1,\mu}^{2}=2d!\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\text{ and }\|W_{d}(f)\|_{-1,\mu}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d}),\Pi g=g}\frac{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}}}{2d!\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}}}.

Proof.

Combining definition of
\|\cdot\|_{1,\mu}
, Lemma
C.3
, the isometry of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d!}}W_{d}
, as well as integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\|W_{d}(f)\|_{1,0}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}\left[W_{d}(f)L_{0}W_{d}(f)\right]=-2\mathbb{E}\left[W_{d}(f)W_{d}(\Delta f)\right]

\displaystyle=-2d!\langle f,\Delta f\rangle_{L^{2}}=2d!\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For the dual norm we manipulate the norm in the same way and use the density of symmetric functions of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d})
in
L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})
, the orthogonality of the Wiener chaos expansion and the previously derived equality to obtain

\displaystyle\left\|W_{d}\left(f\right))\right\|_{-1,0}

\displaystyle=\sup_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[W_{d}\left(f\right)G\right]}{\|G\|_{1,0}}=\sup_{g\in L^{2}_{s}(D^{d})}\frac{\mathbb{E}[W_{d}\left(f\right)W_{d}(g)]}{\|W_{d}(g)\|_{1,0}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d}),\Pi g=g}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[W_{d}\left(f\right)W_{d}(g)\right]}{\|W_{d}(g)\|_{1,0}}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{Dir}}(D^{d}),\Pi g=g}\frac{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}}}{2d!\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}}}.

∎

Appendix D
Replacement Lemmas

We prove two classical replacement lemmas for the Markov process defined in Section
2
. The results resemble those for similar particle systems e.g. in
[
4
, Section 6]
. The first relies on the heat bath dynamics, while the second is based on the exchange noise.

Lemma D.1
.

For fixed
T>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
the following inequality holds

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{n}\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}(n^{2}s)ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim n^{\delta-1}.

Proof.

We start by applying the general variance estimate (
63
) to obtain

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{n}{\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}(n^{2}s)}\ ds\right)\right]\lesssim nT\int_{0}^{T}\|\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}(n^{2}t)\|_{-1,\nu_{\beta,\lambda,t}}dt\\
&\lesssim nT\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{g\in L^{2}(\nu_{\beta,\lambda})}2\int g(\xi)\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+n^{2-\delta}\int g(\xi)B_{n,t}g(\xi)\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)\ dt\\
\end{split}

where in the second step we dropped the negative semidefinite part of the exchange dynamics
S
.
Then for
t\in[0,T]
integrating by parts under the measure
\nu_{\beta,\lambda}
we get

\displaystyle\int g(\xi)\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+n^{2-\delta}\int g(\xi)B_{n,t}g(\xi)\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)

\displaystyle=\int g(\xi)\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}\int_{0}^{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}(x-\rho)dx\ \nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)-n^{2-\delta}\int(\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}g(\xi))^{2}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)

\displaystyle=\int A(\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor})\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}g(\xi)\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)-n^{2-\delta}\int(\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}g(\xi))^{2}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\int A(\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor})\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+(K-n^{\delta-2})\int(\partial_{\xi_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}}g(\xi))^{2}d\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)

where in the last step we used Young’s inequality for some
K>0
and to make the integration by parts applicable, we choose
A:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

A(u):=-e^{W_{\beta,\lambda}(u)}\int_{0}^{u}(v-\rho)e^{-W_{\beta,\lambda}(v)}dv.

From tail-bounds of the
\Gamma
-distribution it is not difficult to show that
\|A\|_{L^{2}(\nu_{\beta,\lambda}^{(1)})}<\infty
. Thus choosing
K=n^{2-\delta}
it follows that for all
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\|\bar{\xi}_{\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor}(n^{2}t)\|_{-1,\nu_{\beta,\lambda},t}\lesssim n^{\delta-2},

which finishes our proof.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

For fixed
T>0
, any
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
and a measurable
z:[0,T]\to\mathbb{Z}
(e.g.
z(t)=\left\lfloor c_{n}t\right\rfloor
) it holds that

(67)

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=z(t)+1}^{z(t)+\ell}(\xi_{x}(n^{2}s)-\xi_{z(t)}(n^{2}s))\ ds\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{\ell}{n^{2}}.

Proof.

As a first step we rewrite the left hand side of (
67
) in terms of a telescopic sum, which is then equal to

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=z(t)+1}^{z(t)+\ell}\sum_{y=z(t)}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}(n^{2}s)-\xi_{y}(n^{2}s))\ ds\right)^{2}\right].

Then from the variance estimate (
63
) we bound last display by

(68)

T\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{g\in L^{2}(\nu_{\beta,\lambda})}\int\frac{2}{\ell}\sum_{x=z(t)+1}^{z(t)+\ell}\sum_{y=z(t)}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})g(\xi)\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+n^{2}\langle g,(\gamma S+B_{n,t})g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\ dt.

We will now bound the integrand in
t\in[0,T]
by a constant depending only on
n
and
\ell
. Since we estimate the integrand for each
t\in[0,T]
, let us write to simplify
z(t)=z\in\mathbb{Z}
. By making a change of variables in the first term inside the supremum, we can write the first integral as

\begin{split}\int\frac{2}{\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}&\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})g(\xi)\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)=-\int\frac{2}{\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})g(\xi^{y,y+1})\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)\\
&=\int\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})(g(\xi)-g(\xi^{y,y+1}))\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi).\end{split}

The last display can be bounded with Young’s inequality by

\int\frac{1}{2K\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})^{2}\ \nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)\ +\ \int\frac{K}{2\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(g(\xi)-g(\xi^{y,y+1}))^{2}\ \nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)

for any
K>0
. Then by choosing
K=2\gamma n^{2}
and dropping the (negative definite) operator
B_{n,t}
we can bound the contribution of the symmetric generators

\begin{split}&\int\frac{K}{2\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=z}^{x-1}(g(\xi)-g(\xi^{y,y+1}))^{2}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+n^{2}\langle g,(\gamma S+\mathcal{B})g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\\
\leq&\int\frac{K}{2\ell}\sum_{x=z+1}^{z+\ell}\sum_{y=-{\infty}}^{\infty}(g(\xi)-g(\xi^{y,y+1}))^{2}\nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)+\gamma n^{2}\langle g,Sg\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\\
\leq&\frac{K}{2}\langle g,(-S)g\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}+\gamma n^{2}\langle g,Sg\rangle_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}=0.\end{split}

Thus coming back to the bound of (
68
), we can then compute the variance directly to obtain

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=z(t)+1}^{z(t)+\ell}(\xi_{x}(n^{2}s)-\xi_{\ell}(n^{2}s))\ ds\right)^{2}\right]\\
\lesssim&T^{2}\int\frac{1}{K\ell}\sum_{x=z(t)+1}^{z(t)+\ell}\sum_{y=\ell}^{x-1}(\xi_{y+1}-\xi_{y})^{2}\ \nu_{\beta,\lambda}(d\xi)\\
\lesssim&T^{2}\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{2}\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\beta,\lambda}}\left[\xi_{2}^{2}+\xi_{1}^{2}-2\xi_{2}\xi_{1}\right]\lesssim\frac{\ell}{n^{2}}.\end{split}

This finishes our proof.
∎
