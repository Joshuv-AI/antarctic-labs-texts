Radiographic accuracy of implant positioning in unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, does an imageless robotic system make a difference?
Authors: Alice Montagna, Luca Andriollo, Virginia Cinelli, Marina Marescalchi, Luca Matascioli, Francesco Benazzo, Stefano Marco Paolo Rossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic-assisted technologies and evolving implant designs have been introduced in unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) to improve the accuracy and reproducibility of component positioning. However, the relative contribution of robotic assistance compared with conventional instrumentation to radiographic outcomes in experienced, high-volume centers remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study included 96 consecutive medial UKAs performed at a single high-volume institution. Patients were stratified into three groups: Journey I UKA implanted with NAVIO imageless robotic assistance (n = 38), Journey II Uni UKA implanted using conventional instrumentation (n = 27), and Journey II UK UKA implanted with CORI imageless robotic assistance (n = 31). Standardized standing long-leg weight-bearing radiographs were obtained preoperatively and postoperatively. Radiographic evaluation included hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle, femoral component coronal angle (FCCA), tibial component coronal angle (TCCA), and sagittal alignment parameters. Tibial coronal alignment was interpreted according to the Cartier metaphyseal alignment philosophy. Intergroup comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests.ResultsBaseline demographic characteristics and preoperative radiographic parameters were comparable among the three groups (all p > 0.05). Postoperative mean HKA was 177.1° ± 2.1° in the Journey I NAVIO group, 177.3° ± 2.5° in the Journey II Uni conventional group, and 177.7° ± 2.2° in the Journey II UK CORI group, with no statistically significant differences (p = 0.56). Femoral and tibial component coronal alignment, including TCCA, showed no significant intergroup differences. All cases across the three cohorts were within predefined safe zones for HKA, FCCA, and TCCA. Sagittal alignment parameters were also comparable between groups.ConclusionIn a high-volume center adopting a consistent alignment philosophy, medial UKA achieves equivalent and highly accurate radiographic outcomes regardless of robotic assistance or conventional instrumentation.Keywords: Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, Robotic-assisted surgery, Radiographic alignment, Implant positioning, Coronal alignment

Introduction

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is an established surgical option for the treatment of isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis, offering advantages over total knee arthroplasty such as bone preservation, faster recovery, and maintenance of more physiological knee kinematics [1]. Nevertheless, the success of UKA is highly dependent on accurate restoration of coronal limb alignment and precise component positioning [2, 3].

To address variability associated with manual instrumentation, robotic-assisted UKA systems were introduced, providing computer-guided bone resections, real-time alignment feedback, and intraoperative assessment of limb kinematics [4, 5]. Early clinical and radiographic studies demonstrated encouraging accuracy with robotic assistance [2, 3, 6, 7] and after image based platforms also imageless systems such as NAVIO Smith & Nephew (Memphis, TN, USA) were introduced. More recently, the CORI Surgical System has been developed by the same company as an evolution of imageless robotic technology, incorporating refinements in workflow, computational power, and intraoperative feedback.

In parallel with technological developments, unicompartmental implant designs have evolved along different conceptual pathways. Importantly, contemporary UKA systems do not represent a single linear design evolution. The Journey I unicompartmental system represents an early fixed-bearing design developed specifically for UKA. The Journey II Uni system was conceived as an anatomical evolution of this concept and is implanted using conventional instrumentation. In contrast, the Journey II UK system represents a distinct implant lineage derived from the ZUK (Zimmer Uni Knee acquired by S&N) unicompartmental knee, with different geometric and biomechanical principles.

Despite these parallel developments, few studies have compared different robotic platforms and implant systems within a single institution using standardized radiographic protocols. The relative contribution of robotic assistance versus implant system to coronal radiographic accuracy therefore remains unclear.

The aim of this study was to compare radiographic coronal limb alignment and component positioning among three UKA configurations: Journey I with NAVIO robotic assistance, Journey II Uni with conventional instrumentation, and Journey II UK with CORI robotic assistance. We hypothesized that, when performed in a high-volume center using standardized indications and surgical techniques, all three configurations would achieve comparable radiographic accuracy.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective comparative study analyzed consecutive patients undergoing medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) for isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis at a single high-volume knee arthroplasty center. All procedures were performed by the same experienced surgical team following uniform indications, surgical philosophy, and perioperative protocols.

Patients were stratified according to implant system and surgical technique into three cohorts:

Group 1: Journey I UKA implanted with NAVIO imageless robotic assistance (n = 38) operated between January 2020 and December 2021.

A formal a priori power analysis was not performed due to the retrospective nature of the study. However, the present sample size is comparable to previously published radiographic UKA studies evaluating component positioning accuracy. The relatively limited size of each cohort should therefore be interpreted as a potential limitation when detecting small intergroup differences.

Summary of radiographic outcomes

Across all evaluated parameters, Journey I NAVIO robotic-assisted UKA, Journey II Uni conventionally implanted UKA, and Journey II UK CORI robotic-assisted UKA demonstrated equivalent radiographic accuracy in coronal limb alignment and component positioning. Importantly, consistent reproduction of the Cartier tibial alignment philosophy was achieved independently of the use of robotic assistance.

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study is that accurate and reproducible radiographic alignment in medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) can be achieved independently of an imageless robotic assistance when surgery is performed in a high-volume center adopting a consistent alignment philosophy. Specifically, no statistically significant differences were observed in coronal limb alignment or component positioning among Journey I UKA implanted with NAVIO imageless robotic assistance, Journey II Uni UKA implanted using conventional instrumentation, and Journey II UK UKA implanted with CORI imageless robotic assistance.

Component malpositioning and coronal malalignment have long been implicated as major contributors to early UKA failure, and robot-assisted technologies were developed, in part, to improve precision and reduce radiographic outliers compared with conventional techniques. A meta-analysis of over 2,700 UKA procedures reported that robotic assistance was associated with improved component alignment accuracy and fewer mechanical axis outliers than conventional instrumentation, although functional outcomes did not consistently differ between groups [13].

More recent evidence has shifted attention not only to the presence of robotics but also to the type of robotic guidance used. Systematic review data indicate that image-based robotic UKA systems, which utilize preoperative CT or other advanced imaging to build a detailed three-dimensional anatomical model, are associated with fewer radiologic outliers in hip–knee–ankle axis and joint line height when compared with imageless robotic systems [14]. Similarly, cohort studies have found that medial UKAs performed with image-based robotic assistance demonstrate higher accuracy in restoring intended limb alignment and implant positioning compared with imageless robotic systemswhen assessed with rigorous radiographic criteria [15].

In contrast, imageless robotic systems offer distinct practical advantages, including avoidance of preoperative imaging and associated radiation exposure, lower logistical burden, and adaptability to intraoperative anatomical findings without the need for CT planning. These systems remain widely used in clinical practice and have demonstrated excellent radiographic outcomes in registry and cohort studies [16]. In the present series, both imageless platforms (NAVIO and CORI) delivered highly accurate coronal and sagittal alignment with minimal outliers within predefined safe zones.

A key strength of this study is the uniform adoption of the Cartier alignment philosophy for proximal tibial alignment across all groups [8–10]. By intentionally respecting the native metaphyseal varus rather than enforcing a neutral mechanical cut, consistent tibial component orientation was achieved regardless of technology. This reinforces the concept that alignment intent and surgical philosophy may outweigh the specific robotic modality in determining radiographic accuracy when expertise is high [17].

Although sagittal alignment parameters (e.g., posterior tibial slope and femoral sagittal axis) were measured and found to be equivalent among groups, the present analysis remains limited to static radiographs. Rotational alignment and dynamic soft-tissue behavior were not evaluated, and such subtleties may be better elucidated with three-dimensional imaging or advanced dynamic assessment tools.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the use of consecutive cases, standardized indications, and uniform surgical philosophy reduces selection bias and strengthens internal validity. Second, the three study groups were treated during different time periods.The sequential adoption of implant systems and enabling technologies reflects the natural evolution of surgical practice within the institution. Although surgical indications, alignment philosophy, and radiographic protocols remained unchanged, temporal factors such as increasing surgical experience, improvements in perioperative workflow, and advances in imaging systems may represent potential confounders. Consequently, the absence of radiographic differences between groups should be interpreted with caution, as incremental surgical refinement over time cannot be completely excluded Third, the analysis was intentionally limited to static radiographic parameters obtained from standard weight-bearing long-leg radiographs. Rotational alignment, dynamic soft-tissue behavior, and in vivo kinematics were not evaluated and may reveal differences not captured by conventional radiographic assessment. Finally, clinical outcomes, patient-reported outcome measures, and implant survivorship were not analyzed. While this represents a limitation, it also reflects the focused aim of the study to isolate radiographic alignment and component positioning as a single explanatory factor. The present analysis focuses exclusively on radiographic alignment and component positioning. Radiographic measurements were performed by two independent observers to reduce observer-related bias. Although reliability testing demonstrated good agreement between observers, minor measurement variability inherent to long-leg radiographic assessment cannot be completely eliminated. Although accurate component positioning is considered an important determinant of implant longevity in unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, radiographic alignment alone does not necessarily translate directly into improved clinical outcomes or implant survivorship. Several studies have shown that comparable radiographic alignment may coexist with similar functional outcomes regardless of the surgical technology employed. Therefore, while the current findings support the reproducibility of the alignment philosophy adopted in this series, long-term clinical outcomes and survivorship analyses are necessary to determine the true clinical impact of these radiographic findings.

Conclusions

In a high-volume center adopting a consistent Cartier-based alignment philosophy, medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty achieves equivalent and highly accurate radiographic outcomes with imageless robotic assistance or conventional instrumentation. These findings suggest that surgical experience and alignment intent may outweigh the specific enabling technology in determining radiographic accuracy, while the clinical relevance of differences between robotic platforms requires further investigation.
