When pupillometry indices disagree: does QPi add prognostic information beyond NPi?
Authors: Li-Xia Xu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the prospective multicentre LYNX study by Bouzat et al. [1], which compared the neurological pupil index (NPi) and quantitative pupil index (QPi) in patients with acute brain injury. The study provides important evidence that both indices are associated with neurological outcomes, while QPi is substantially more sensitive to anaesthetic exposure. These findings support the authors’ conclusion that the two indices should not be considered interchangeable. However, an important clinical question remains: when NPi and QPi disagree, does QPi provide additional prognostic information, or does the disagreement primarily reflect differences in measurement susceptibility?

The distinction matters because association with outcome and incremental clinical information are not equivalent. Although the two indices were moderately to strongly correlated across 7,089 paired measurements (r = 0.717), the median proportion of abnormal measurements was substantially higher for QPi than for NPi (12.7% versus 1.2%). Both indices were independently associated with unfavourable six-month neurological outcomes in separate adjusted models. However, these findings do not establish whether QPi identifies patients at greater risk among those whose NPi remains normal. Nor do they indicate whether discordant measurements carry prognostic information beyond that already captured by NPi and conventional clinical assessment.

The paired longitudinal design of LYNX offers an opportunity to examine this distinction more directly. Rather than relying solely on correlations or separate prognostic models, discordant measurements could be examined within patients, particularly episodes in which QPi is abnormal despite a normal NPi. Their frequency, persistence, and relationship to subsequent neurological outcomes could clarify whether discordance represents a potentially informative physiological signal or predominantly device-dependent variation. Importantly, because the abnormality threshold of 3 was established for NPi rather than QPi, such analyses should be interpreted as exploratory comparisons rather than validation of equivalent thresholds.

This question is especially relevant given the pharmacological findings. The marked reduction in QPi following anaesthetic administration demonstrates that discordance cannot automatically be interpreted as additional neurological dysfunction. Conversely, attributing all discordance to pharmacological effects may overlook potentially relevant physiological information. The clinical value of QPi therefore depends not simply on its association with adverse outcomes, but on whether discordance contributes interpretable information beyond NPi under the conditions in which these indices are used.

The principal contribution of LYNX is to demonstrate that apparently related pupillometric indices can behave differently in critically ill patients. A logical next step is to determine whether these differences have consequences for risk interpretation. Establishing when discordance is clinically informative, rather than merely documenting that the indices are not interchangeable, would help define the appropriate role of QPi alongside established pupillometric assessment.
