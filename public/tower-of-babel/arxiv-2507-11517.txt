Gaussian Noise Model of Nonlinear Distortions from Semiconductor Optical Amplifiers
Hartmut Hafermann

Abstract
A Gaussian Noise Model of the nonlinear noise power spectral density is developed for a semiconductor optical amplifier as described by the Agrawal model. A simple closed-form expression is obtained for the nonlinear noise-to-signal ratio of broadband wavelength-division multiplexed signals as a function of the Agrawal model parameters, the amplifier output power and the transmission bandwidth. The accuracy of the closed-form expression and its region of validity is assessed in numerical simulations. The error is smaller than 0.1 dB when the product of bandwidth and gain recovery time 𝐁 × τ 𝐜 \mathbf{B\times\tau_{c}} exceeds 100.



I
Introduction

Semiconductor Optical Amplifiers (SOAs) are a promising amplifier technology for ultra-wideband (UWB) wavelength-division multiplexed (WDM) transmission systems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, or as booster
[
4
]
or pre-amplifiers
[
5
,
6
]
.
Compared to Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs), they offer large transmission bandwidth in excess of 100 nm and advantages in terms of smaller footprint, better energy efficiency and lower cost. Recent advances in SOA design have resulted in noise figures competitive to those of EDFAs
[
5
]
and high saturation powers in excess of 23 dBm
[
7
]
.

The nonlinear gain compression in SOAs and fast response time of the order of a few hundred picoseconds, compared to microseconds for EDFAs, cause signal-dependent nonlinear distortions which are detrimental to system performance. Despite significant progress, nonlinear impairments caused by SOAs remain a concern and should be minimized in SOA design as well as accounted for in system design and performance prediction.

For signal distortions caused by nonlinear optical fibers, analytical models based on perturbation theory have proven invaluable to assess the impact of nonlinear impairments, calculate system margins, develop simple rules for system design, or for fast quality of transmission (QoT) prediction.
Various modeling efforts over the years have culminated in the well-known Gaussian noise model for dispersion unmanaged links
[
8
,
9
]
. A detailed account of the GN model and its applications can be found in
[
10
]
.
The limitations of the GN model are now well understood. Its insensitivity to the modulation
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
led to the development of the enhanced GN (EGN) models
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Ref.
[
17
]
extended the theory to account for nonlinear distortions due to SOAs as line amplifiers.
The theory describes nonlinear noise contributions due to fibers, SOAs and their interactions.

The implementation of (E)GN models is complex and often requires numerical evaluation of multidimensional integrals. They may not necessarily be available to SOA designers.
Even though closed-form GN
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and EGN models
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
have been obtained for QoT prediction and to speed up UWB system optimization, they are not easily interpretable.
Simple closed-form results, on the other hand, are useful to provide general intuition, preliminary performance estimates or to inform general system design considerations.
Closed-form GN model results have, for example, informed the well-known LOGO(N) link optimization strategy
[
25
]
which assumes incoherent nonlinear noise accumulation across spans, as well as the widely used ’3 dB-rule’, which asserts that at optimum launch power, the amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) equals twice the nonlinear noise power
[
10
]
. A closed-form model for SOAs has so far been lacking.

In this paper, we develop the Gaussian noise model for a standalone SOA, as described by the Agrawal model. Its physical interpretation is discussed, highlighting similarities and differences compared to the GN model for fiber links. Simple closed-form expressions for the nonlinear noise-to-signal ratio (NSR) are derived. They clearly expose the dependence of nonlinear impairments on the Agrawal model and system parameters, including SOA output power and transmission bandwidth.
Through detailed error analysis it is shown that for Gaussian signaling, the model shows excellent agreement with numerical simulations, even deep in the nonlinear regime.

To obtain this accuracy requires analysis beyond first-order perturbation theory.
It is shown that first-order perturbation theory underestimates nonlinear NSR by a factor as large as 3 dB at saturation. To fully account for the effect of nonlinear gain compression, it is necessary to sum the deterministic terms in the perturbation expansion of the GN model up to infinite order. In the classic GN model, the corresponding terms are neglected, because they cause an overall phase rotation that does not affect the nonlinear noise.

The resulting dependence of the nonlinear noise PSD on power is more complicated than the
P^{3}
-dependence of fiber links. In addition, the Agrawal model is too simplistic to describe real devices.
It has been shown however, that working point-dependent Agrawal parameters can be measured as a function of SOA output power and within a single setup to provide a realistic quantitative description of modern multi-quantum well SOAs
[
26
,
27
]
. The theory developed here thus remains applicable. The 3 dB-rule, however, no longer holds. Because of gain compression and power-dependent parameters, the optimal launch power must in general be computed numerically. Modulation can be accounted for through a modulation-dependent factor
[
27
]
.

Trends of the Agrawal parameters can also be inferred from measurements on multiple SOA designs
[
5
]
or otherwise be modeled. The relative performance of different SOA designs in a wideband transmission system can thus be linked to SOA design parameters through the closed-form GN model, in order to inform the SOA design optimization and obtain better tradeoffs.

II
Agrawal’s model

To set the stage for the following discussions, we briefly review the main features of the Agrawal model
[
28
]
.
The propagation of the electric field
E(z,t)
in an SOA waveguide can be described by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E(z,t)}{\partial z}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(z,t)-\alpha\right)E(z,t)-j\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{H}g(z,t)E(z,t),

(1)

where
\alpha_{H}
is the linewidth enhancement or Henry factor
[
29
]
,
\alpha
describes the internal losses of the mode and
g(z,t)
is the material gain coefficient. The latter implicitly depends on distance and time through the local time-dependent carrier density,
g(z,t)=g(N(z,t))
.

Fig. 1:
Absolute square of the low-pass filter
H_{c}(f)=1/(1+2\pi i\tau_{c}f)
. The cutoff frequency is defined as
f_{c}=1/(2\pi\tau_{c})
.

Under the assumption that the carrier recombination rate and the material gain coefficient are both
linear
functions of the carrier density, the rate equation for the carrier density can be recast into the following equation governing the dynamics of the material gain coefficient
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(z,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{0}-g(z,t)}{\tau_{c}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\frac{g(z,t)P_{\text{in}}(t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(2)

Here
g_{0}
and
P_{\text{sat}}
are the small-signal gain coefficient and the saturation power, respectively, which govern the strength of the nonlinear gain compression of the amplifier, and
\tau_{c}
is its carrier lifetime. It is commonly assumed that internal losses can be neglected compared to the gain, in which case Eq. (
1
) implies
\partial P/\partial z=gP
. Combining, with (
2
), the dependence on the spatial coordinate can be integrated out. Specifically, defining
h_{0}=g_{0}L
and the integrated gain coefficient
h(t)
,

h(t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\int_{0}^{L}g(z,t)dz,

(3)

the equation describing the nonlinear gain dynamics becomes

\displaystyle\frac{dh(t)}{dt}=\frac{h_{0}-h(t)}{\tau_{c}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\frac{P_{\text{in}}(t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}[\exp(h(t))-1].

(4)

Here
G(t)\equiv\exp\left(h(t)\right)
equals the time-dependent gain of the amplifier, such that
P_{\text{out}}(t)=P_{\text{in}}(t)G(t)
.

It is instructive to consider the effect of the finite carrier lifetime.
For illustrative purposes, consider the case of large gain
G\gg 1
, or
P_{\text{in}}(t)\ll P_{\text{out}}(t)
. These conditions are often satisfied in
applications for optical amplification which require gains in excess of 20 dB, but we emphasize that we do not make such assumption elsewhere in the paper.
Rearranging (
4
), we then obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\tau_{c}\frac{d}{dt}\right)(h(t)-h_{0})=-\frac{P_{\text{out}}(t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(5)

In the static case
(dh/dt=0)
, it follows that the gain compression is described by

\displaystyle G(P_{\text{out}})=G_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right),

(6)

where
G_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\exp(h_{0})
is the small-signal gain.
On the other hand, taking the Fourier transform of (
5
), we find that

\displaystyle\Delta h(f)=-H_{c}(f)\frac{P_{\text{out}}(f)}{P_{\text{sat}}},

(7)

where we have defined
\Delta h(t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}h(t)-h_{0}
and where

\displaystyle H_{c}(f)=\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\tau_{c}f}=\frac{1}{1+if/f_{c}}

(8)

is a low-pass filter determined by the carrier lifetime
\tau_{c}
with cutoff frequency

\displaystyle f_{c}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{c}}.

(9)

The dynamic gain fluctuations
\Delta h(t)
are hence proportional to the low-pass filtered output power. The absolute square of the filter function,
|H_{c}(f)|^{2}
, is depicted in Fig.
1
. It decays from its maximum value of 1 at
f=0
to 0 in the limit
f\to\infty
. It attains a value of
1/2
at the cutoff frequency.

It follows from (
1
) that once the gain dynamics
h(t)
are obtained from (
4
) through numerical integration, the electric field at the SOA output is computed as

\displaystyle E_{\text{out}}(t)=E_{\text{in}}(t)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-j\alpha_{H})h(t)\right).

(10)

Equations (
4
) and (
10
) are commonly referred to as the Agrawal model.
As the gain
h(t)
fluctuates around its average value, the signal experiences amplitude fluctuations as well as phase fluctuations. Gain fluctuations are translated into phase fluctuations due to the Henry factor
\alpha_{H}

[
29
]
.
While an optical signal experiences phase fluctuations in a fiber due to the nonlinear Kerr effect, an SOA causes both amplitude and phase fluctuations. In the GN model these are treated as additive Gaussian noise.

III
GN Model

Under the assumption of Gaussian signal statistics and based on Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), the following Gaussian Noise model expression for the power spectral density (PSD) of the nonlinear interference noise of an SOA can be obtained:

\displaystyle G_{\text{NLI}}(f)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{2}g_{\text{in}}(f_{1})g_{\text{in}}(f_{2})g_{\text{in}}(f_{1}+f_{2}-f)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[|H_{c}(f-f_{2})|^{2}+H_{c}(f-f_{2})H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})\right].

(11)

The derivation is provided in Appendix
C
.
Here,
g_{\text{in}}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}G_{\text{in}}(f)/P_{\text{in}}
is the normalized input power spectral density.
G
denotes the static compressed gain at the average total output power
P_{\text{out}}
and is determined through the implicit relation:

G=G_{0}\exp\left(-\Big(1-\frac{1}{G}\Big)\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right).

(12)

The exact solution
G(P_{\text{out}})
is provided in Appendix
A
.

III-A

Physical interpretation

The interpretation of the GN model formula (
11
) for the Agrawal model bears some similarity to the classic GN model, but also important differences. First consider the spectral origin of the nonlinear noise as described by the second and third line of (
11
). Just like the Gaussian Noise Reference Formula
[
10
]
, Eq. (
11
) contains a double integral over a product of input power spectral densities at three different frequencies. It can be understood as an integral over infinitesimal FWM contributions, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.
Each such contribution is weighted by certain products of two filter functions
H_{c}(f)
in the third line of (
11
).

The first term is the dominant term as will be shown in Sec.
IV
.
Physically, this term describes the beating of two arbitrary spectral tones at
f_{1}
and
f_{1}+f_{2}-f=f1-\Delta f
that induces an oscillation of the carrier density at the beating frequency
\Delta f=f-f_{2}
. This oscillation in turn modulates the carrier density-dependent material gain coefficient at this frequency. The nonlinear noise originates from the interaction of a third spectral tone at
f_{2}
with the modulated gain, which creates a new tone at
f=f_{2}+\Delta f
according to the FWM process. In the GN model, these FWM contributions are integrated and treated as a source of noise.

Because of the finite SOA response time, only beating frequencies of the order of the cutoff frequency or below contribute significantly to the SOA response as described by the filter term
|H_{c}(f-f_{2})|^{2}=|H_{c}(\Delta f)|^{2}
. This term decays with frequency according to Fig.
1
. Therefore this term only contributes significantly if the beating frequency
\Delta f=f-f_{2}
is not much larger than the cutoff frequency
f_{c}
.
For typical values of
\tau_{c}=100\,(200)
ps,
f_{c}\approx 1.6\,(0.8)
GHz, which is much smaller than the typical channel spacing of current transmission systems of
B_{\text{ch}}=75
GHz or more.
Therefore two spectral lines causing significant gain oscillations usually lie within the same channel (or at adjacent edges of neighboring channels). Since the filters in this term do not depend on
f-f_{1}
, pairs of such beating tones at
f_{1}
and
f1-\Delta f
in any part of the spectrum contribute equally and are accumulated in the integral over
f_{1}
in (
11
).

Following the terminology of
[
8
]
, the nonlinear noise contributions may be categorized as either cross-channel interference (XCI), when the spectral components
f_{1}
and
f_{1}-\Delta f
lie in a channel distinct from the channel of interest (COI) containing the frequencies
f
and
f_{2}
as illustrated in Fig.
2
a), or as self-channel interference (SCI), when the three interacting spectral components lie in the same channel as
f
, as shown in Fig.
2
b).
In the case of XCI, only the first term in the third line of (
11
),
|H_{c}(f-f_{2})|^{2}
, contributes significantly, since the second term decays with frequency separation
f-f_{1}
, which is typically much larger than
f_{c}
(unless
f
and
f_{1}
are located at neighboring channel edges).
Note that since the first term does not depend on
f_{1}
, XCI does not decay with increasing channel separation, contrary to the case of fiber. In the case of SCI, both terms in the third line of (
11
) may contribute substantially.

Fig. 2:
Schematic representation of the input power spectral density
G_{\text{in}}(f)
and the spectral origin of the dominant contributions to the nonlinear interference noise power spectral density
G_{\text{NLI}}(f)
. a) Cross-channel interference (XCI). b) Self-channel interference (SCI).

All three spectral lines may also lie in distinct channels, causing multi-channel interference (MCI). In this case however, both
f-f_{2}
and
f-f_{1}
are typically much larger than
f_{c}
, so that both
H_{c}(f-f_{2})
and
H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})
are negligible.
Therefore MCI is typically negligible for SOAs for typical channel spacings, as in fiber
[
10
]
. The underlying mechanisms, however, are different. The GN model for fiber contains phase factors of the form
\exp(j4\pi^{2}(f_{1}-f)(f_{2}-f)\beta_{2}z)

[
10
]
, where
\beta_{2}
is the group velocity dispersion parameter and
z
the distance along the fiber. In regions of large phase mismatch, where both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are far from
f
, the rapid oscillations of the phase factor in the integral tend to cancel out any interference. Only those frequencies for which the phase mismatch is small contribute appreciably to the integral.
There is no analogue of the phase matching condition in an SOA. It is instead the low-pass filtering property due to the finite response time which quenches MCI.

Next consider the prefactor of the GN model formula in the first line of (
11
).
The contribution proportional to
\alpha_{H}^{2}
is due to phase noise, since gain fluctuations translate to phase fluctuations via the Henry factor
\alpha_{H}
according to (
10
). Due to amplitude fluctuations, the noise power is nonzero even in absence of phase fluctuations (
\alpha_{H}=0
).
Typical values for
\alpha_{H}
found in the literature
[
29
,
30
,
26
]
range from
3
to
20
, so that
\alpha_{H}^{2}\gg 1
and phase noise typically dominates significantly over amplitude noise.
The gain factor
1-1/G
accounts for the fact that gain compression and nonlinear response are driven by the total rate of photons generated by the amplifier, which is proportional to
P_{\text{out}}-P_{\text{in}}=P_{\text{out}}(1-1/G)
, rather than to the output power itself. For line amplifiers with a typical gain of the order of 20 dB,
1/G\ll 1
and this factor is close to 1.

Finally considering the power dependence of the nonlinear noise power spectral density, one can note that for an amplifier, the nonlinear noise PSD is naturally a function of output power, while for a fiber it is a function of input power.
For a fiber link, nonlinear noise power
P_{\text{NL}}
is proportional to
P_{\text{in}}^{3}
. The nonlinear NSR can thus be written as
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}=\eta P_{\text{in}}^{2}
, where
\eta
is independent of power
[
10
]
. On the contrary, for an SOA described by the Agrawal model, the analogous expression as a function of the total SOA output power reads

\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}=\eta^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\,P_{\text{out}}^{2},

(13)

where
\eta^{\prime}
has been defined to also be independent of power. Only in the limit of small output power,
P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}\ll 1
,
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}
scales as
P_{\text{out}}^{2}
. Although much less relevant in practice, for
P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}\gg 1
,
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}
becomes proportional to
P_{\text{out}}
.
Note that because of gain compression described by (
12
),
P_{\text{out}}=GP_{\text{in}}
increases more slowly than
P_{\text{in}}
when approaching saturation. This will be discussed in more detail in Sec.
IV-A
.

The factor
1/(1+P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}})
is a direct consequence of nonlinear gain compression.
As shown in Appendix
B
, a perturbative treatment up to
first order
instead results in a factor
1/(1+P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}})^{2}
, which hence
underestimates

[
17
]
nonlinear noise power by a factor
1+P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}
. While this factor varies slowly with power relative to
(P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}})^{2}
,
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}
is larger by a factor of 2 at saturation compared to the result of first-order perturbation theory. Fully accounting for nonlinear gain compression leads to an
increase
in nonlinear noise power.
It is shown in Appendix
C
(see in particular the discussion around Eq. (
97
)),
that nonlinear gain compression arises from the deterministic terms in the perturbation series that are present at
all
orders. The correct power dependence (
13
) is obtained by summing the corresponding perturbation series to infinite order.
This term has no analogue in fiber. In a fiber, the corresponding infinite series of deterministic terms leads to an overall nonlinear phase rotation. It is usually not of interest because it neither affects the nonlinear noise, nor is it relevant for transmission because of carrier phase estimation.

Because of the form (
13
) of the nonlinear
NSR
, the so-called ’3 dB-rule’, which states that at optimum launch power, the
ratio of amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) to nonlinear noise power equals two, no longer applies.
In addition, it has been shown that for modern multi-quantum well SOAs, the conventional Agrawal model should be replaced by an
effective
Agrawal model, whose parameters depend on the amplifier working point, i.e., on
P_{\text{out}}

[
27
]
. The optimum launch power must thus be found numerically.

IV
Closed-form GN model for WDM signals

Fig. 3:
Schematic representation of the idealized WDM signal with flat input power spectral density
G_{\text{in}}(f)
used for the calculation of the nonlinear interference noise, with the channel of interest (COI) indicated.

In order to arrive at a closed-form result for the nonlinear noise PSD, we consider the idealized case of a Gaussian signal with a rectangular spectrum of bandwidth
B
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. Each channel has a frequency spacing
B_{\text{ch}}
that coincides with its symbol rate
R_{s}
(referred to as ideal Nyquist-WDM in Ref.
[
10
]
). We assume the center frequency
f_{0}
of the COI not to be too close to the band edges (i.e., with the distance well exceeding
f_{c}
) to avoid edge effects. This assumption is not very restrictive since, as mentioned previously,
f_{c}
is rather small. Without loss of generality, one may choose
f_{0}=0
to be in the center of the band.
The normalized power spectral density
g_{\text{in}}(f)=G_{\text{in}}(f)/P_{\text{in}}
then equals one for
f\in[-B/2,B/2]
and zero otherwise. We first consider the term in the integrand proportional to
|H_{c}(f_{2})|^{2}
in (
11
). The integral domain for the two-dimensional integral is the elongated hexagon shown in Fig.
4
(gray shaded area), where the product
g_{\text{in}}(f_{1})g_{\text{in}}(f_{2})g_{\text{in}}(f_{1}+f_{2})
is non-zero. To arrive at a closed-form result, the integration domain is extended to the square delimited by dashed lines in the same figure. The factor
|H_{c}(f_{2})|^{2}
is shown at the top of the figure and seen to decay rapidly, even for a moderate value of
B\tau_{c}=20
. Contour lines of this function are overlaid and show that the function only overlaps a very small part of the extended integration domain, and in most of it its value has substantially decayed.
The approximation will be more accurate the larger the bandwidth
B
compared
f_{c}
and the faster the integrand decays, i.e., the smaller
f_{c}\sim 1/\tau_{c}
. Hence the error is smaller the larger the product
B\tau_{c}
.
The approximation is consistent with those made in the derivation of the integral form (
11
) of the Gaussian noise model (see the discussion around (
74
) in Appendix
C
and the derivation of the closed form in Appendix
D
, which assume that
1/(B\tau_{c})\ll 1
, which is typically fulfilled in wideband transmission systems.
The extension of the integral domain therefore leads to only a small error, as will be validated below.

Note that since the filter function is independent of
f_{1}
, XCI contributions are added with equal weight independent of the spectral distance to the COI. As a result this term is dominant and has no analogue in fiber, where XCI decays with increasing frequency separation from the COI.
The second term depends on both frequencies
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Its contour plot in Fig.
5
has a hyperbolic shape reminiscent of the FWM efficiency that arises in the derivation of the GN model
[
8
]
. Also here the integration region can be extended to the square while incurring a small error. Further details on the derivation of the closed-form approximation can be found in Appendix
D
.

Schematic representation of the integration region where the product
g_{\text{in}}(f_{1})g_{\text{in}}(f_{2})g_{\text{in}}(f_{1}+f_{2})

|H_{c}(f_{2})|^{2}

B\tau_{c}=20

Because of the rapid decay of this function, the extension of the integral domain essentially does not change the value of integral. For larger product
B\tau_{c}
the decay is even faster.

We are generally interested in the nonlinear
NSR
for wideband WDM signals. Since edge effects are confined to a narrow band of with
\sim f_{c}
at the band edges, the nonlinear
NSR

for a channel of bandwidth
B_{\text{ch}}
can be computed as
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}\approx G_{\text{NL}}(f_{0})B_{\text{ch}}/P_{\text{ch}}
. Here
N_{\text{ch}}B_{\text{ch}}=B
and
N_{\text{ch}}P_{\text{ch}}=P_{\text{out}}
. This leads to the following expression for
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}
(see Appendix
D
):

\displaystyle\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}\!=

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{4}\!\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\!\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}\!

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{1}{2B\tau_{c}}+\left(\frac{1}{2B\tau_{c}}\right)^{2}\right].

(14)

The first and second term in angular brackets in (
14
) stem from the corresponding terms in the third line of (
11
). From the previous discussion we expect the first term to be dominant, in particular for a wideband WDM signal dominated by XCI. Indeed, for a 6 THz signal and
\tau_{c}=100
ps,
x=1/(2B\tau_{c})=8.3\cdot 10^{-4}
and clearly
x^{2}\ll x
.
Even for a single channel of bandwidth 75 GHz, the second term is more than one order of magnitude (15 times) smaller. Neglecting the second term in angular brackets leads to the final simple result

\displaystyle\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}\!=\!\frac{1}{4}\!\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\!\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}\!\frac{1}{2B\tau_{c}}.

(15)

For a single channel and above parameters, the difference between the two approximations is
10\log_{10}(1+1/15)
or about 0.25 dB. Neglecting a factor
1+1/(2B\tau_{c})

reduces
the NSR so that this simplification is not conservative. While care should be taken when applying the GN model to a single channel or narrowband signals, the error decreases further with increasing bandwidth.
A detailed analysis of the accuracy of the closed-form result is given in Sec.
IV-B
.

\displaystyle\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}^{\text{fib}}\!=\!\frac{8}{27}\gamma^{2}P_{\text{in}}^{2}L_{\text{eff}}^{2}\frac{\text{asinh}(\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}|\beta_{2}|L_{\text{eff}}B^{2})}{\pi|\beta_{2}|L_{\text{eff}}B^{2}},

(16)

where

is the effective length and

V
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper, we have derived the GN model of nonlinear distortions from SOAs as described by the Agrawal model.
It has been shown that to fully capture the effect of nonlinear gain compression, it is necessary to sum the deterministic terms in the perturbation theory up to infinite order.
This result improves the first-order perturbation theory
[
17
]
, which underestimates nonlinear noise power by up to 3 dB at saturation.
A physical interpretation of the general integral expression for the nonlinear noise PSD for a WDM signal is provided along with accurate closed-form expressions.
The region of validity has been determined in numerical simulations. For ideal Nyquist-WDM the formula is very accurate (with error
<0.1
dB) when the product of carrier lifetime and bandwidth
B\tau_{c}
exceeds
\sim 100
.

The closed-form formula clearly exposes the dependence of the nonlinear noise on the Agrawal model parameters, the SOA output power and system bandwidth. It can serve to provide general intuition, inform SOA and system design, and to obtain a fast preliminary estimate of SOA nonlinear penalties, in particular for large bandwidths where simulations are expensive.

For real SOA devices, the assumptions underlying the idealized Agrawal model are questionable. In particular, a logarithmic dependence of the material gain coefficient on carrier density is thought to be more adequate for modern quantum-well SOA designs
[
31
,
32
,
30
]
, but would preclude the formulation of a lumped model. It can be shown, however, that effective, power-dependent Agrawal parameters provide a realistic description
[
26
,
27
]
, so that the present theory remains applicable.
The modulation format can be accounted for via a modulation-dependent correction factor
[
27
]
.

The theory can further be extended, for example to describe the FWM efficiency for continuous-wave input (see Appendix
F
), to study the nonlinear noise in spectral gaps that can affect OSNR measurements, or to include the interaction of nonlinear noise from fibers and SOAs already captured in
[
17
]
. Their effect is small for short links and would be negligible for future hollow-core fiber links with negligible fiber nonlinearity. With chromatic dispersion of those fibers in the low single-digit regime (between 1–4
\text{ps}/\text{km}/\text{nm}

[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
), coherent noise accumulation across SOAs would also be negligible in wide-band operation for typical span lengths of 80 km or more
[
37
]
.

A more important effect in UWB application scenarios is the wavelength dependence of gain compression
[
4
]
, with the nonlinear penalty exhibiting a spread of as much as 3 dB in output power across C and L bands as observed experimentally
[
7
]
and in simulation
[
26
]
. While beyond the scope of the present work, it turns out that the GN model can be generalized to describe the wavelength-dependence of the nonlinear noise, even in closed form.

Appendix A
Gain Compression

In this Appendix we establish the relation governing the compression of the material gain coefficient
g(P)
and provide an analytic formula for the Agrawal model gain curve.

The rate equation for the carrier density
N(z,t)
underlying the Agrawal model
[
28
]
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial N(z,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{I}{qV}-\frac{N(z,t)}{\tau_{c}}-\frac{N(z,t)-N_{0}}{\tau_{c}}\frac{P(z,t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(19)

In the static case (
\partial N/\partial t=0
) and in absence of an input signal, the carrier density reaches its maximum value

\displaystyle N^{*}=\frac{I\tau_{c}}{qV}.

(20)

From the rate equation (
19
) for a time-independent input signal it further follows that

\displaystyle 0=N^{*}-N-(N-N_{0})\frac{P}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(21)

Solving for
N
gives:

\displaystyle N=\frac{N^{*}+N_{0}P/P_{\text{sat}}}{1+P/P_{\text{sat}}}.

(22)

Inserting this into the definition of the material gain coefficient yields:

\displaystyle g(N)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\Gamma a(N-N_{0})=\frac{\Gamma a}{1+P/P_{\text{sat}}}(N^{*}-N_{0}).

(23)

Since
N^{*}
is the undepleted carrier density,
g(N^{*})=\Gamma a(N^{*}-N_{0})
must equal the small-signal gain
g_{0}
, which can also be directly verified from its definition using (
20
) (see
[
28
]
):
g_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\Gamma aN_{0}(I/I_{0}-1)
, where
I_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}qVN_{0}/\tau_{c}
is the current required for transparency.
The compression of the material gain coefficient is hence described by

\displaystyle g(P)=\frac{g_{0}}{1+P/P_{\text{sat}}}.

(24)

The Agrawal model gain curve (
12
) follows directly from this relation.
Using
\partial P/\partial z=(g-\alpha)P\approx gP

[
28
, Eq. (2.16)]
and
g(P)
from above, separating variables and integrating yields

\displaystyle\int_{P_{\text{in}}}^{P_{\text{out}}}dP\frac{1+P/P_{\text{sat}}}{P}=\int_{0}^{L}g_{0}dz

(25)

or

\displaystyle\log(P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}})+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}-\frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}=g_{0}L.

(26)

Taking the exponential, defining
G_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\exp(g_{0}L)
and
G\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}}

yields an implicit relation that determines the Agrawal model gain curve:

\displaystyle G=G_{0}\exp\left(-\Big(1-\frac{1}{G}\Big)\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right).

(27)

For large gain,
1/G\ll 1
, we have
G(P_{\text{out}})\approx\exp(-P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}})
.

The solution to (
27
) can also be expressed analytically considering (
4
) in the static case (
dh/dt=0
):

\displaystyle h=h_{0}-\frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}[\exp(h)-1].

(28)

The solution to (
28
) can be expressed as

h=h_{0}+\frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}-W_{0}\left(\frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\exp\left(h_{0}+\frac{P_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)\right),

(29)

where
W_{0}(x)
is the principal
(k=0)
branch of the Lambert-
W
function
W_{k}(x)
. It is the inverse function of
f(W)=W\exp(W)
and hence known as the product logarithm.
Alternatively, (
28
) can be expressed in terms of
P_{\text{out}}
:

h=h_{0}-\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}[1-\exp(-h)].

(30)

Taking the exponential of this equation using
G=\exp(h)
recovers (
27
). In this case, the solution is

h=h_{0}-\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}+W_{0}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\exp\left(-h_{0}+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)\right).

(31)

The solution
G(P_{\text{out}})
to (
27
) is obtained as
G=\exp(h)
.

Appendix B
Heuristic derivation of power dependence of nonlinear NSR in first-order perturbation theory

In this Appendix the dependence of the nonlinear noise-to-signal ratio on output power is derived based on a perturbative treatment up to first-order. The result shows that contrary to the main result (
14
) of this paper, the first-order result is smaller by a factor
1+P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}
and thus underestimates nonlinear noise. The origin of this discrepancy is gain compression.
The correct result, which requires the summation of a partial perturbation series to infinite order, is derived in Appendix
C
.

In the following, time averages are denoted by a bar, e.g.
\overline{h}
, for clarity.
The gain fluctuations are described by Saleh’s differential equation
[
38
]
, which is equivalent to Agrawal’s equation
[
28
]
and (
4
):

\displaystyle\frac{dh(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{0}-h(t)}{\tau_{c}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\frac{P_{\text{out}}(t)-P_{\text{in}}(t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(32)

Defining the gain fluctuations

\Delta h(t)=h(t)-\overline{h}

(33)

around their average
\overline{h}
, the equation can be brought into the form

\displaystyle\left(1+\tau_{c}\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Delta h(t)

\displaystyle=h_{0}-\overline{h}-\frac{P_{\text{out}}(t)-P_{\text{in}}(t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(34)

For time-independent input
P_{\text{in}}(t)=\overline{P}_{\text{in}}
, the derivative
dh/dt
vanishes and one obtains (
28
):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=h_{0}-\overline{h}-\frac{\overline{P}_{\text{out}}-\overline{P}_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(35)

Using the definition (
33
), the output power can approximately be expressed as

\displaystyle P_{\text{out}}(t)

\displaystyle=P_{\text{in}}(t)\exp(h(t))=P_{\text{in}}(t)\exp(\overline{h})\exp(\Delta h(t))

\displaystyle\approx\overline{P}_{\text{in}}\exp(\overline{h})(1+\Delta h(t))+\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)\exp(\overline{h})

\displaystyle=\overline{P}_{\text{out}}\Delta h(t)+P_{\text{in}}(t)\overline{G}.

(36)

Here the exponential
\exp(\Delta h(t))\approx 1+\Delta h(t)
has been expanded to first order, and a term of order
\mathcal{O}(\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)\Delta h(t))
has been neglected. In the last line it has been used that
\overline{P}_{\text{out}}=\overline{P}_{\text{in}}\overline{G}
and that
\exp(\overline{h})=\overline{G}
is the average, time-independent gain.
The term proportional to
\Delta h(t)
describes the nonlinear gain modulation of
P_{\text{out}}(t)
. This term is responsible for a renormalization of the recovery time.
Inserting this result into (
34
), one obtains

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\overline{P}_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}+\tau_{c}\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Delta h(t)=h_{0}-\overline{h}

\displaystyle-\frac{P_{\text{in}}(t)(\overline{G}-1)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(37)

Defining the input power fluctuations

\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)=P_{\text{in}}(t)-\overline{P}_{\text{in}}

(38)

and using the static solution (
35
), the time-independent terms cancel and one obtains

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\overline{P}_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}+\tau_{c}\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Delta h(t)=-\frac{\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)\left(\overline{G}-1\right)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

With the effective recovery time

\tau_{\text{eff}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\tau_{c}/(1+\overline{P}_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}})
, this becomes:

\displaystyle\left(1+\tau_{\text{eff}}\frac{d}{dt}\right)\Delta h(t)=-\frac{1}{1+\overline{P}_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}}\frac{\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)\left(\overline{G}-1\right)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

By denoting the low-pass filter operation in time-domain as
F_{\text{eff}}[\cdot]
, this can symbolically be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta h(t)=-\frac{1}{1+\overline{P}_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}}\frac{F_{\text{eff}}[\Delta P_{\text{in}}(t)]\left(\overline{G}-1\right)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(39)

Using (
38
), this can be rewritten in the form:

\displaystyle\Delta h(t)=-\frac{1}{1+\overline{P}_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}}F_{\text{eff}}\left[\frac{P_{\text{in}}(t)-\overline{P}_{\text{in}}}{\overline{P}_{\text{in}}}\right]\frac{\overline{P}_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\overline{G}}\right).

(40)

Note that the result of the filtering operation,
F_{\text{eff}}[P_{\text{in}}(t)/\overline{P}_{\text{in}}-1]
, is independent of input power.

It remains to express the nonlinear
NSR
in terms of the gain fluctuations
\Delta h(t)
.
The equation for the electric field at SOA output is given by
[
28
]
:

E_{\text{out}}(t)=E_{\text{in}}(t)e^{\frac{1}{2}(1-j\alpha_{H})h(t)}.

(41)

The dynamic gain
h(t)
is split into its average and fluctuations:
h(t)=\overline{h}+\Delta h(t)
, so that

E_{\text{out}}(t)=E_{\text{in}}(t)e^{\frac{1}{2}(1-j\alpha_{H})\overline{h}}e^{\frac{1}{2}(1-j\alpha_{H})\Delta h(t)}.

(42)

The nonlinear noise power is defined as the average of the absolute square of the fluctuations of the output field relative to the output field in absence of gain fluctuations (i.e.,
\Delta h(t)\equiv 0
):

\displaystyle P^{(1)}_{\text{NL}}

\displaystyle=\langle|E_{\text{out}}(t)-E_{\text{in}}(t)e^{(1-j\alpha_{H})\overline{h}/2}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle|E_{\text{in}}(t)e^{(1-j\alpha_{H})\overline{h}/2}(1-j\alpha_{H})\Delta h(t)/2|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(1+\alpha_{H}^{2})\overline{G}\langle|E_{\text{in}}(t)|^{2}\Delta h(t)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(1+\alpha_{H}^{2})\overline{P}_{\text{out}}\langle\Delta h(t)^{2}\rangle.

(43)

Here the superscript
(1)
emphasizes that the result is valid up to first order,
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes expectation and
\overline{G}=\exp(\overline{h})
as before. Here it has been used that
\Delta h(t)
, which is proportional to the low-pass filtered signal according to (
40
), is virtually uncorrelated with the input power fluctuations:
\langle|E_{\text{in}}(t)|^{2}\Delta h(t)^{2}\rangle\approx\langle|E_{\text{in}}(t)|^{2}\rangle\langle\Delta h(t)^{2}\rangle
.

The nonlinear
NSR
is defined as
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}=P_{\text{NL}}/\overline{P}_{\text{out}}
. Combining (
43
) and (
40
), we finally obtain the
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}^{(1)}
in first-order approximation:

\displaystyle\text{NSR}^{(1)}_{\text{NL}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\overline{G}}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\times\langle F_{\text{eff}}[P_{\text{in}}(t)/\overline{P}_{\text{in}}-1]^{2}\rangle.

(44)

The expectation of the filtered relative power fluctuations in the second line is independent of power and only depends on the normalized signal statistics. Comparing with the main result (
14
) of this paper, Eq. (
44
)
underestimates
the nonlinear noise-to-signal ration by a factor
1+P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{sat}}
and by as much as 3 dB when the SOA is operated at saturation.
The power dependence of this result is consistent with the first-order perturbation theory of Ref.
[
17
]
.
To fully account for gain compression, it is necessary to perform a partial summation of the deterministic terms arising in the perturbation series at all orders, which is accomplished in the next section.

Appendix C
Derivation of the GN-Model

In this section, the GN-theory for the Agrawal model is derived.
The derivation roughly follows
[
39
]
which provides a detailed derivation of the GN model for the NLSE. Important differences are pointed out where adequate.

The following Fourier transform pair is used:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[f(t)](\omega)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dtf(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[f(\omega)](t)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\omega\hat{f}(\omega)e^{j2\pi ft}.

(45)

The equation governing the field in the Agrawal model is given by
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E(z,t)}{\partial z}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(g(z,t)-\alpha\right)E(z,t)-j\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{H}g(z,t)E(z,t),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[(1-j\alpha_{H})g_{0}-\alpha\right]E(z,t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(1-j\alpha_{H})\Delta g(z,t)E(z,t),

(46)

where the gain fluctuation
\Delta g(z,t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}g(z,t)-g_{0}
was defined. The gain dynamics are described by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(z,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{0}-g(z,t)}{\tau_{c}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\frac{g(z,t)P(z,t)}{P_{\text{sat}}}.

(47)

To simplify notation, define the perturbation parameter
\epsilon\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}P_{\text{sat}}^{-1}
and write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta g(z,t)}{\partial\tau}

Note that the
A key assumption of the GN model derivation is that the remaining nonlinear term

Noting that typically

It turns out that most of the products of random variables

\displaystyle P_{\text{in}}e^{g_{0}z}\to P(z)

Appendix D
Derivation of closed-form expression

In the following, a closed-form expression for the nonlinear NSR of a WDM signal with an idealized rectangular input power spectral density (ideal Nyquist-WDM) is derived.
Consider a rectangular power spectral density of bandwidth
B
.
The double integral in the noise power spectral density (
122
) is evaluated at a frequency
f=f_{0}
that is assumed to be not too close to the band edge, i.e., at a distance significantly larger than the cutoff frequency
f_{c}
(
9
).
Without loss of generality, one may choose
f_{0}=0
to be in the center of the band.
The input power spectral density equals
g_{\text{in}}(f)=1/B
for
f\in[f_{0}-B/2,f_{0}+B/2]
and
g_{\text{in}}(f)\equiv 0
elsewhere.
The integral is to be performed over a region with the shape of an elongated hexagon where the product
g_{\text{in}}(f_{1})g_{\text{in}}(f_{2})g_{\text{in}}(f_{1}+f_{2})
is non-zero (shaded region in Figs.
4
and
5
in the main text). The functions
|H_{c}(f-f_{2})|^{2}
,
H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})
and
H_{c}(f-f_{2})
decay rapidly with frequency since the ratio of
f_{c}
to the bandwidth is typically small (equivalently,
B\tau_{c}
is reasonably large). For the same reason, edge effects are confined to within a narrow strip of width of order
f_{c}
around the band edges. Due to this decay, the integration region can be extended to a square to good approximation, for both terms in the last line of (
122
), as illustrated in Figs.
4
and
5
. This extension allows one to obtain the integral in closed form.
With these simplifying assumptions, the double integral in (
122
) becomes

\displaystyle I=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B^{3}}\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}df_{2}|H_{c}(f-f_{2})|^{2}\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}df_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{B^{3}}\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}df_{2}H_{c}(f-f_{2})\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}df_{1}H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1}).

(123)

Using the identities

\displaystyle\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}\frac{1}{1+4\pi^{2}\tau_{c}^{2}(f_{0}-f2)^{2}}df_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi\tau_{c}},

(124)

\displaystyle\int_{f_{0}-B/2}^{f_{0}+B/2}\frac{1}{1\pm 2\pi i\tau_{c}(f_{0}-f2)}df_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi\tau_{c}},

(125)

one obtains

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi B\tau_{c}}+\frac{1}{B}\left(\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi B\tau_{c}}\right)^{2}.

(126)

For a WDM channel of bandwidth
B_{\text{ch}}
, the nonlinear NSR is given by
G_{\text{NLI}}(f_{0})B_{\text{ch}}/P_{\text{ch}}
, where
N_{\text{ch}}B_{\text{ch}}=B
and
N_{\text{ch}}P_{\text{ch}}=P_{\text{out}}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}\!=

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{4}\!\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\!\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}\!

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi B\tau_{c}}+\left(\frac{\arctan(\pi B\tau_{c})}{\pi B\tau_{c}}\right)^{2}\right].

(127)

Using approximation (
75
) leads to (
14
).

Appendix E
Nonlinear NSR for spectrally shaped WDM signals

It is instructive to consider the effect of a non-rectangular spectral shape of the input PSD
G_{\text{in}}(f)
. We assume that the spectral width of the SOA filter is sufficiently narrow. Such approximation is more accurate for larger
\tau_{c}
and high baud rates. It will further be assumed that SOA bandwidth is smaller than the spectral gap between channels.
The integral in (
122
) can be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle I^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{1}\int_{-\tilde{B}/2}^{+\tilde{B}/2}\!\!\!d\Delta fg_{\text{in}}(f_{1})g_{\text{in}}(f-\Delta f)g_{\text{in}}(f_{1}-\Delta f)

\displaystyle\times\left(|H_{c}(\Delta f)|^{2}+H_{c}(\Delta f)H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})\right).

(128)

Here
\tilde{B}
is a fictitious bandwidth which is chosen large enough such that the integral essentially covers the whole SOA bandwidth (i.e., it should exceed the cutoff frequency
f_{c}
) and small enough such that
g_{\text{in}}(\Delta f)
can be considered constant over
\tilde{B}
.
These contradictory requirements can be better fulfilled the larger
\tau_{c}
, which permits a smaller
\tilde{B}
. For sufficiently small
\tilde{B}
,
\Delta f
can be neglected in the arguments of
g_{\text{in}}
and we have

\displaystyle I^{\prime}\approx

\displaystyle g_{\text{in}}(f)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{1}g_{\text{in}}^{2}(f_{1})\int_{-\tilde{B}/2}^{+\tilde{B}/2}\!\!\!d\Delta f

\displaystyle\times\left(|H_{c}(\Delta f)|^{2}+H_{c}(\Delta f)H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})\right).

(129)

The spectral shape of the noise hence approximately follows that of the WDM signal, similarly to the case of fiber nonlinear noise
[
10
]
.
The second term containing
H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})
is generally smaller than the first and significant only in case of self-channel interference, and only when
f_{1}
is close to
f
. In this term we make the additional approximation that the argument of
g_{\text{in}}^{2}(f_{1})
can be approximated by
f
. This yields

\displaystyle I^{\prime}\approx

\displaystyle g_{\text{in}}(f)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{1}g_{\text{in}}^{2}(f_{1})\int_{-\tilde{B}/2}^{+\tilde{B}/2}\!\!\!d\Delta f|H_{c}(\Delta f)|^{2}

\displaystyle+g_{\text{in}}(f)^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!df_{1}H_{c}^{*}(f-f_{1})\int_{-\tilde{B}/2}^{+\tilde{B}/2}\!\!\!d\Delta fH_{c}(\Delta f).

(130)

If we assume the power spectrum of the WDM signals to be raised-cosine shaped with roll-off factor
\beta
, the calculation of the NSR involves the integrals

\rho_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\frac{1}{R_{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\!\!\text{rc}^{k}(f)df=1-\beta\left(1-\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}\sec(k\pi)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-k)\Gamma(1+k)}\right).

(131)

It follows that
\rho_{1}=1
and
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\text{rc}(f)df=R_{s}
. For a signal with
N_{\text{ch}}
channels at center frequencies
f_{c,n}
the normalized input power spectral density reads

g_{\text{in}}(f)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{ch}}}\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{ch}}}\text{rc}(f-f_{c,n}).

(132)

Evaluating
I^{\prime}
similarly to (
123
) and
\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}
as
(1/P_{\text{ch}})\int_{-B_{\text{ch}}/2}^{B_{\text{ch}}/2}dfG_{\text{NLI}}(f)
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}\!=

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{4}\!\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\!\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\left[\mu\frac{1}{2N_{\text{ch}}R_{s}\tau_{c}}+\nu\left(\frac{1}{2N_{\text{ch}}R_{s}\tau_{c}}\right)^{2}\right],

(133)

where
\mu=\rho_{1}\rho_{2}=1-\beta/4
and
\nu=\rho_{3}=1-3\beta/8
.
Compared to the closed-form result (
11
) for ideal Nyquist-WDM, the bandwidth is replaced by the occupied bandwidth
N_{\text{ch}}R_{s}
.
Note that this NSR expression does not account for receiver-side matched filtering. If filtering by
H_{\text{RX}}(f)
is taken into account, the nonlinear NSR is given by

\text{NSR}_{\text{NL}}=\frac{1}{P_{\text{ch}}}\int_{-B_{\text{ch}}/2}^{B_{\text{ch}}/2}dfG_{\text{NLI}}(f)|H_{\text{RX}}(f)|^{2}.

(134)

For a root-raised cosine filter
H_{\text{RX}}(f)=\text{rrc}(f)
, the NSR is still given by (
133
), but with
\mu=\rho_{2}^{2}=(1-\beta/4)^{2}
and
\nu=\rho_{4}=1-29\beta/64
.

For an RRC, the filter changes from maximum to zero within a bandwidth of
2\beta R_{s}
, where
R_{s}
is the sampling frequency. For
R_{s}=68
GHz and roll-off
\beta=0.05
,
2\beta R_{s}=6.8
GHz. This value is not much larger than the typical cutoff frequency (1.6 GHz for
\tau_{c}=100

Appendix F
Closed-form result for FWM efficiency

The GN model formula (
11
) can be interpreted as an integral over infinitesimal FWM products. Interestingly, the FWM efficiency of continuous-wave (CW) light sources can be computed based on this formula, even though the assumption of Gaussian statistics is not satisfied.
In case of degenerate FWM, two CW sources are placed at frequencies
f_{0}
and
f_{0}+\Delta f
at SOA input, where
\Delta f
is the frequency spacing. Ignoring the finite linewidth of actual lasers, the normalized input PSD for this case reads

g_{\text{in}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\delta(f-f_{0})+\delta(f-f_{0}-\Delta f)\right].

(135)

First-order four-wave mixing products are generated at the frequency of the lasers, as well as at
f=f_{0}-\Delta f
and
f_{0}+2\Delta f
. The FWM efficiency is defined as the ratio between the power of the first sideband to the power of the pump. Inserting the PSD into (
11
) and taking the ratio of the sideband power at e.g.
f_{0}+2\Delta f
to the pump power, leads to the following closed-form result for the FWM efficiency

\displaystyle\text{FWM}(\Delta f)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{32}\left(1+\alpha_{H}^{2}\right)\frac{1}{1+\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{G}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\frac{2}{1+(\Delta f/f_{c})^{2}}.

(136)

Here it has been assumed that the power of the first sideband is negligible compared to the power of the pump (which hence remains undepleted). The result is based on first-order perturbation theory and therefore does not describe higher-order sidebands. They must hence be negligible (which is generally the case when the previous assumption is satisfied). Furthermore, the beating of two spectral tones leads to large amplitude oscillations between zero to twice the average power, for which a first-order perturbation theory may be inadequate. For these reasons, one may expect the applicability of the formula to be limited to low power.

(137)

Interestingly, the integrated FWM efficiency is proportional the nonlinear NSR of a WDM signal (
