Aggressive Disease and Poor Clinical Outcome in CEBPα‐Mutated Acute Myeloid Leukemia Patient
Authors: Abdullah Attullah Alshammari, Mohammed O. AlSabbagh, Hasan M. Alshehri, Azizah F. AlSwayyed, Faisal M. Alseraye, Abdul Ali Peer‐Zada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCCAAT/enhancer binding protein α (C/EBPα) mutations occur in about 4%–11% of AML patients and are typically associated with favorable prognosis. Here we present a case of AML with two distinct CEBPα variants exhibiting an unexpectedly poor clinical outcome despite intensive chemotherapy and molecular remission at an early phase of treatment. Routine diagnostic work‐up included in‐house morphological assessment, flow cytometry, molecular and cytogenetics testing, and NGS‐based CNV testing. A 63‐year‐old male with a history of trigeminal neuralgia presented to the ER with fever associated with sore throat, cough and spontaneous bruising accompanied by leucocytosis (WBC, 52.4 × 109/L) and thrombocytopenia (PLT, 26 × 103/μL). Bone marrow evaluation revealed 95% blasts consistent with acute leukemia with flow cytometry confirming the diagnosis. Myeloid NGS panel revealed two distinct likely pathogenic CEBPα variants: c.315del; p. F106Lfs*54 in TAD2 and c.941_946dupTGCTGG; p.V314_L315dup in the bZip domain. The patient received 7 + 3 induction chemotherapy followed by HiDAC consolidation when he achieved remission. He subsequently relapsed with progressive disease accompanied by multiple complications and persistent MRD‐positivity despite multiple salvage regimens and SCT. CEBPα‐mutated AML presents a complex challenge to conventional chemotherapy and may require alternative treatment strategies including transplantation. Additionally, genomics technologies in AML have the potential to uncover known and novel gene variants.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), according to the WHO classification of hematolymphoid malignancies is mainly divided into two separate groups: AML with defining genetic abnormalities and AML defined by differentiation [1, 2]. The former is genetically diverse consisting of multiple molecular aberrations in the form of fusion genes resulting from chromosomal translocations, such as RUNX1::RUNX1T1, PML::RARalpha, CBFB::MYH11, RUNX1::MECOM, and/or gene mutations, such as FLT3‐ITD/TKD, NPM1, and CEBPα, which could occur either in combination with chromosomal abnormality or in cytogenetically normal AML (CN‐AML) [3, 4, 5, 6]. These genetic abnormalities help categorize AML patients into favorable, intermediate, or poor risk stratification. AML patients with CEBPα mutation (also called CEBPα‐mutated AML) belong to the favorable risk group and are a provisional entity in the WHO classification, with the recommendation that all CN‐AML be tested for CEBPα mutation [7]. CEBPα gene mutation testing is part of the NGS‐based myeloid tumor panel, currently a routine practice in the diagnostic work‐up of AML patients.

CEBPα (CCAAT enhancer‐binding protein alpha, OMIM#116897) gene maps to chromosome 19q13.1, is intronless and the deduced 357‐amino acid protein consists of an N‐terminal transcription activation domain 1/2 (TAD1/2) and a C‐terminal basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain that recognizes the CCAAT motif in the promoters of target genes [8]. Two main types of mutations in the CEBPα gene have been reported in AML [9]. One, nonsense mutations (frameshift) in the TAD1/2 of the N‐terminus leading to a dominant negative CEBPα protein, and two, “in‐frame indels” in the bZIP of the C‐terminus leading to the disruption of dimerization and diminished DNA‐binding function of CEBPα protein. Mice with Cebpα mutations lack granulocytes whereas conditional expression of C/EBPα triggers neutrophilic differentiation indicating an important role of CEBPα mutations in the pathogenesis of AML [10].

Here, we report a case of AML with two distinct likely pathogenic CEBPα mutations but associated with aggressive disease behavior and poor clinical response. This case contributes to the evolving understanding of CEBPα‐mutated AML and highlights the potential need for revised prognostic classification and tailored treatment approaches.

Case History

A 63‐year‐old male presented to the ER with fever associated with sore throat, cough, hip pain, constipation, shortness of breath, progressive fatigue, pallor, and spontaneous bruising. Initial workup included peripheral smear with marked neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and no circulating blasts, complete blood count (CBC) with WBC count 0.57 × 109/L, Hb 7.8 g/dL, and platelets 20 × 103/μL. He had a past medical history of right‐sided V2 trigeminal neuralgia 13 years back, and underwent right gasserian ganglion radiofrequency ablation. There is no history of chest pain, palpitation, nausea/vomiting, loss of consciousness, seizure or any abnormal movement. Last recorded vitals are: blood pressure 110/68, pulse 85, temperature 37.2°C (99°F, temperature source oral), respiratory rate 19, height 1.74 m, weight 88 kg, SpO2 96%. Physical examination revealed him to be conscious, alert, not in distress, GCS 15/15, and soft and lax abdomen.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Diagnostic work‐up included routine hematology, biochemistry, coagulation profile analyses, bone marrow aspirate and biopsy for morphological assessment, flow cytometry for immunophenotyping, polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) for FLT3ITD, NPM1, IDH1/2, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for AML panel and NGS‐based myeloid panel for confirmation of diagnosis and prognostification. Myeloid NGS panel or targeted Sanger sequencing was requested at different time points such as diagnosis, post‐induction, at relapse and post‐transplant. The differential diagnosis was AML. The patient was started on chemotherapy protocol (7 + 3 induction: cytarabine+ daunorubicin). The patient received high‐dose cytarabine consolidation (HiDAC). He received salvage therapy post‐relapse (AZA/Venetoclax) followed by allogenic hematopoietic cell transplant (STC, stem cell source: peripheral blood; total cells infused: 7 × 106/KG).

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

CBC auto differential revealed leucocytosis (WBC, 52.4) and thrombocytopenia (PLT, 26) with more than 95% immature‐looking cells. Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy revealed hypocellular aspirate with trilineage dysplasia, containing 95% blasts, hypercellular marrow biopsy (80%–90%) shows diffuse infiltration by immature mononuclear cells, which is consistent with AML (non‐M3 morphology, Figure 1A, upper panel). Bone marrow assessment at first relapse after consolidation revealed hypercellular marrow with erythroid hyperplasia, approximately 2.7% residual disease by flow cytometry, and 4% blasts by morphology in the aspirate (Figure 1A, lower panel). Approximately 90% of cells in the bone marrow show dim CD45 and low side scatter and are positive for CD34, CD117, CD13 dim, CD33, CD58, CD64 partial, CD38, CD11C partial, HLA‐DR, MPO with aberrant expression of CD7 (Figure 1B). They are negative for CD14, CD15, and CD4, and all other T‐ lineage and B‐lineage antigens used in the analysis. FISH panel for AML was unremarkable with no evidence for t(8;21), inv(16), t(15;17), or KMT2A rearrangements and no deletion of 5q31 (EGR1) or 7q31 (D7S522). No mutation was detected in NPM1, FLT3‐ITD/TKD, IDH1/2 by in‐house qPCR at KFMC. NGS myeloid tumor panel revealed two different likely pathogenic variants in the CEBPα gene: c.315del; p. F106Lfs*54 in TAD2, and c.941_946dupTGCTGG; p.V314_L315dup in the bZip domain (Figure 2A). The mutated nucleotide/amino acid (F106 and V314) in the CEBPα gene are conserved across species (Figure 2B) suggesting evolutionary significance. Bone marrow assessment at day 28 follow‐up post‐induction therapy revealed < 5% blasts and MRD negativity by flow cytometry and negative for CEBPα mutation. In the first relapse after consolidation, CBC revealed leukopenia and thrombocytopenia (WBC 0.81 × 10*3/μL Platelet Count 13 × 10*3/μL). Trephine biopsy showed scattered CD34 and CD117 positivity, with the cell percentage reaching 10%. CEBPα mutations reappeared. With salvage therapy, the patient despite achieving transient MRD negativity, relapsed again with progressive disease. Bone marrow biopsy revealed hypocellular marrow with regenerating hematopoiesis, 3% blasts and persistent MRD positivity. This was followed by allogeneic stem cell transplant. Treatment course was accompanied by aggressive disease course and poor outcome. During induction, the patient had short intersphincteric active perianal fistula with a small perianal abscess formation and Pseudomonas aeruginosa from rectal swab, not drainable by GS. During consolidation, he had Klebsiella, probable fungal pulmonary infection, febrile neutropenia, diarrhea and left thigh redness. Other medical comorbidities included bilateral high frequencies sensorineural hearing loss.

Molecular genetic testing using targeted Sanger sequencing and myeloid NGS panel revealed biallelic CEBPα mutation at diagnosis and no CEBPα mutation post‐induction. Biallelic CEBPα mutation reappeared at relapse and was not detected post‐transplant. At second relapse, biallelic CEBPα mutation reappeared with concomitant GATA2 mutation (c.1051A>G; p.Asn351Asp, Variant of Unknown Significance).

Discussion

We present here a rare case of an AML patient with two distinct CEBPα variants, one in TAD2 and the other in the bZip domain, who exhibited an unexpectedly poor clinical outcome despite intensive chemotherapy and molecular remission at an early phase of treatment. Biallelic CEBPα mutations are generally associated with a favorable prognosis and thus, this case underscores the complexity and the challenges it may pose in the use of conventional therapeutic options in CEBPα‐mutated AML. The patient needed clinical interventions during each phase of the induction, consolidation and salvage therapy due to several complications, although he did not require ICU admission.

CEBPα‐mutated AML, biallelic CEBPα mutation (CEBPα‐double‐mutated [CEBPα‐dm]) is a provisional entity in the WHO classification of AML and is particularly associated with CN‐AML [11]. In conjunction with other genetic abnormalities, it is recommended to be tested in patients with AML at diagnosis for prognostication. CEBPα‐mutated AML has favorable prognosis unless it occurs with concurrent NPM1 and FLT3 gene mutations that may impact prognostic outcome [12]. The current case did not reveal NPM1 and FLT3ITD/TKD mutations either by NGS or qPCR. The only genetic abnormality was the presence of two CEBPα mutations.

CEBPα mutations occur typically as either frameshift in CEBPα‐TAD1/2 leading to a truncated protein product and can be germline events with predisposition to AML or in‐frame indels in CEBPα‐bZip leading to altered DNA‐binding and dimerization of CEBPα protein [7]. A germline 1‐bp deletion (212delC) in the CEBPα gene is reported in a family with overt AML [13]. Pabst et al. [14] reported CEBPα mutations in 16% of AML‐M2 patients lacking t(8;21). The authors reported 5 deletions, 2 insertions, and 4 point mutations in the CEBPα gene. Snaddon et al. [15] reported 9 somatic CEBPα mutations clustered toward the C terminus in 8/99 patients. Only two patients revealed biallelic mutations: one homozygous for a 57‐bp insertion at nucleotide 1137 and the other compound heterozygous for a 27‐bp insertion at nucleotide 1096 and a 4‐bp insertion (GGCC) at nucleotide 363. The current case revealed a frameshift in TAD2 (c.315del; p. F106Lfs*54) and an indel in bZip (c.941_946dupTGCTGG; p.V314_L315dup) albeit, with an unfavorable outcome. Generally, AML patients harboring CEBPα‐bZIP mutations (single CEBPαsm or double mutated CEBPαdm) show an improved overall survival (OS). In a study by de la Torre EP et al. [16] patients with in‐frame CEBPα‐bZIP mutation had an estimated 3‐year survival of 83.3% compared with patients having CEBPα other mut (54.3%) and with CEBPαwt (CEBPA‐wild‐type or no mutation 47.2%). Tarlock et al. [17] showed an estimated 5‐year OS of 81% ± 7% for CEBPα‐dm, 89% ± 12% for CEBPα‐bZip and 61% ± 2% CEBPαwt patients. Wakita et al. [18] showed an OS of 53% (hazard ratio of 0.3287) for patients with CEBPα‐bZIP mutation, 62% for CEBPαdm and 89% for s CEBPαsm. Taube et al. [19] showed a higher median OS for CEBPα biallelic patients (103.2 months) and for CEBPαsm (21.9 months). Despite the good prognosis of CEBPα‐mutant patients, a recent study by Yu et al. [20] showed some covariables such as increasing WBC count, lower hemoglobin concentration, non‐intensive induction, and MRD positivity after first consolidation as predictors of poor outcomes in AML patients as in our patient with in‐frame CEBPA‐bZip mutations for which transplantation consideration was suggested. It is also possible that the mutation in TAD2 (site of an initiation codon for CEBPα 30 kDa protein) acts as a dominant negative of the wild‐type CEBPα protein (42‐kDa) [21], completely abrogating its function, and in conjunction with bZip mutation leads to an aggressive disease in the current case.

Treatment protocols including targeted therapeutics in AML may vary depending upon risk stratification based on cytogenetic and molecular genetic abnormalities. We used standard of care induction 7 + 3 with HiDAC consolidation as per institutional protocol. He received salvage therapy post‐relapse (AZA/Venetoclax) followed by allogenic SCT. Cladribine/LDAC/Ven treatment protocol could have been an alternative possibility, since both are lower intensity regimens for unfit, relapsed cases. Unfortunately, the patient relapsed disease shortly after completing induction with 7 + 3 and 3 cycles of HiDAC consolidation. With this in mind and given the infections he developed during induction and consolidation, it was decided to go for Aza/Ven without Cladribine/LDAC. AML treatment protocol guidelines do not recommend allogenic stem cell transplant in first CR for favorable risk patients including those with CEBPα mutations. Given available data and risk stratification, allogenic stem cell transplant is usually reserved for relapsed/refractory cases like the current case, This case thus, highlights the complexity of genetic variants of CEBPα‐mutated AML and the need for collecting these rare cases to help personalize the treatment provided. Whether SCT in CR1 would be of benefit in such cases remains unknown.

In conclusion, this case highlights the complexity of CEBPα‐mutated AML and presents a complex challenge to conventional chemotherapy. There is a dire need to look out for such unique cases which may not necessarily fit the notion of universally favorable outcomes in CEBPα‐mutated AML. Aggressive disease behavior and poor clinical response in CEBPα‐mutated AML also highlight the potential need for revised prognostic classification with tailored treatment approaches. Additionally, genomics technologies in AML have the potential to uncover known and novel gene variants.
