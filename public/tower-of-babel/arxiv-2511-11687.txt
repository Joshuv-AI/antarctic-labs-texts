Generative AI as a Linguistic Equalizer in Global Science
Dragan Filimonovic, Christian Rutzer, Jeffrey T. Macher, Rolf Weder

Abstract
For decades, the dominance of English has created a substantial barrier in global science, disadvantaging non-native speakers. The recent rise of generative AI (GenAI) offers a potential technological response to this long-standing inequity. We provide the first large-scale evidence testing whether GenAI acts as a linguistic equalizer in global science. Drawing on 5.65 million scientific articles published from 2021 to 2024, we compare GenAI-assisted and non-assisted publications from authors in non-English-speaking countries. Using text embeddings derived from a pretrained large language model (SciBERT), we measure each publication’s linguistic similarity to a benchmark of scientific writing from U.S.-based authors and track stylistic convergence over time. We find significant and growing convergence for GenAI-assisted publications after the release of ChatGPT in late 2022. The effect is strongest for domestic coauthor teams from countries linguistically distant from English. These findings provide large-scale evidence that GenAI is beginning to reshape global science communication by reducing language barriers in research.

In this paper, we conduct the first large-scale test of the ”linguistic equalizer” hypothesis. We track the stylistic evolution of 5.6 million English-language publications by authors from non-English-speaking countries from 2021 to 2024. We measure the linguistic similarity of each publication to a U.S. scientific writing benchmark using text embeddings generated with the SciBERT model
[
10
]
. SciBERT is a transformer architecture based on BERT
[
17

We find a pronounced post-2022 convergence of GenAI-assisted publications from non-English-speaking country authors toward U.S. scientific writing, relative to non-GenAI-assisted publications from those countries. The effect is strongest where language barriers are greatest—namely for domestic author teams from countries more linguistically-distant from English. These findings indicate that GenAI is both reshaping scientific communication—acting as a powerful linguistic equalizer—and impacting scientific policy—with implications for editorial practice, research equity, and the global production of knowledge.

This pattern is unlikely to reflect random variation but may indicate that the SciBERT-based measure captures both linguistic and topical dimensions. A plausible explanation is that COVID-19-related research temporarily raised linguistic similarity in 2021-2022. As such research receded in 2023, Life Sciences publications returned to more typical topics, thereby lowering similarity. Calculations that exclude medicine publications from the Life Sciences group support this interpretation, revealing an upward trend.

Aggregated averages alone, however, cannot establish whether the observed linguistic convergence reflects the use of GenAI—which enables authors from non-English-speaking countries to write in a style closer to U.S. scientific English—or whether broader shifts in research topics, field composition or other factors have occurred. We therefore turn to a regression-based event-study framework that explicitly compares linguistic similarity between GenAI-assisted and non-assisted publications over time.

GenAI Triggers Widespread Linguistic Convergence

We use the SciBERT similarity scores at the publication-level as the dependent variable in our regression analysis. The main variable of interest is the interaction between an indicator for GenAI-assisted publications and year, capturing how linguistic similarity evolves following the use of GenAI. All regression models include the non-interacted GenAI indicator, country, field, journal, year, and journal-year fixed effects, as well as other publication-level covariates. Details on the model specification are provided in the Materials and Methods section.

The analysis reveals that the release of ChatGPT coincided with an increasing convergence in scientific writing styles worldwide. Figure

(a) indicates that GenAI-assisted publications from non-U.S. coauthors became significantly more similar to the U.S. linguistic benchmark post-2022 than comparable non-GenAI-assisted publications. This effect is both highly statistically significant and increasing in magnitude: i.e., by 0.15% in 2023 and by 0.4% in 2024, relative to the 2022 baseline. The absence of a pre-trend in 2021 is consistent with the shift emerging post-GenAI launch. Absolute movements on the linguistic similarity scale are small but expected, given that the average similarity already exceeds 0.8 as Figure

indicates. Arguably more important is the clear directional and temporally-concentrated shift that appears post-GenAI diffusion. Because the embedding measure can reflect both language and topical content shifts, we estimate models with country, field, journal, year, and journal-year fixed effects, so comparisons are made within the same year and journal. This design ensures that the observed convergence among GenAI-assisted publications is unlikely to be driven by changes in topical composition. The patterns remain highly consistent across robustness tests that vary both the identification of GenAI-assisted publications and the linguistic similarity specifications—including stricter keyword thresholds, alternative U.S. benchmark corpora, and the exclusion of GenAI-assisted U.S. texts (see Supplementary Materials for full details and estimates). This widespread linguistic convergence raises an important question: what drives it? If GenAI acts as a ”linguistic equalizer”, its impact should be most pronounced for researchers who have historically faced the greatest language-related barriers to publication. Our analyses strongly support this hypothesis.

The convergence effect is significantly larger and rises more sharply for linguistically distant countries, in line with the interpretation that GenAI helps reduce language barriers for those facing greater baseline frictions.

Across panels a-c, GenAI appears to act as a partial substitute for human-based linguistic expertise, with the largest gains among domestically coauthored teams from countries linguistically distant from English.

The results indicate that linguistic convergence is more prevalent in lower-impact journals—where authors may have less access to professional editing services—and less prevalent in top-tier journals—where manuscripts already undergo rigorous language polishing and perhaps face a ceiling effect. Previous evidence shows that peer review in high-impact journals is substantially more thorough—reviews are longer and devote greater attention to substantive aspects of manuscripts compared to lower-impact outlets
[
48
]
. These results suggest that GenAI may disproportionately benefit researchers aiming to meet stylistic standards in outlets where language support is not institutionalized.

Discussion

We document that GenAI-assisted publications from countries where English is not the primary language show measurable convergence in writing style toward a U.S. benchmark. The effect is strongest for authors from more linguistically-distant countries, for coauthor teams without native English-speaking representation, and for publications in lower-impact journals. These patterns suggest that GenAI acts as a linguistic equalizer in global science, with disproportionately larger benefits where linguistic and institutional resources are more limited and relatively more costly.

We believe that this convergence will have far-reaching consequences for the global research ecosystem. Insights from international trade theory offer a useful framework to assess the impact of these shifts across multiple actors
[
38
]
. The most immediate beneficiaries are likely non-native English-speaking researchers who use GenAI tools to overcome language constraints, gaining greater visibility in international and higher-impact journals. Yet the influx of new contributors may intensify competition for native English-speaking incumbents, inducing sorting dynamics akin to firm-level responses to import competition
[
38
,
7
,
36
]
: in particular, relatively ”weaker” native English-speaking researchers may experience reduced success, whereas relatively ”stronger” researchers may respond by further enhancing the quality and originality of their work. Broader participation may also expand the pool of ideas which future research can build on—analogous to improved access to imported ”intermediate inputs”
[
8
]
.

Beyond this micro-level enrichment of the knowledge base, reducing language-related barriers may generate macro-level gains—much like trade openness is associated with aggregate welfare improvements through increased productivity, variety and quality
[
32
,
19
,
38
,
29
]
. A more level playing field may enable a wider range of high-quality ideas to compete and may gradually reduce the dominance of the Anglophone center
[
4
,
41
]
. Over time, such diversification of contributors and ideas may help counteract the reported slowdown in disruptive discoveries and declining rates of new idea generation
[
11
,
43
]
.

Realizing these gains will depend, however, on how journals, reviewers and policymakers respond. For this potential to materialize, we suggest approaches around use and disclosure, equitable access, and editor and reviewer guidance: (i) To preserve trust without discouraging responsible GenAI use, journals could adopt transparent, low friction disclosure of language assistance and model use
[
15
,
33
,
51
]
. For example, authors could clearly state what was used, for what tasks, and who verified the final content. (ii) To broaden access where marginal benefits are likely largest
[
24
]
, policymakers and funding bodies could support equitable access and training for under-resourced institutions, including subsidized tools, shared infrastructure, and responsible use guidance. (iii) To better distinguish language clarity from scientific contribution, journals could update editorial and reviewer guidance during screening and review, with a goal of ensuring that declared language support is treated as independent of scientific merit. In this context, resisting GenAI and leaning on LLM-based detectors to prevent its use risks amplifying existing inequities. Current LLM-based detectors disproportionately flag non-native English writing as “AI-generated,” and simple language improvements in prompting can bypass them, making such tools both unfair and ineffective
[
35
]
. We therefore caution against their use in evaluative settings and emphasize policies that protect non-native authors while promoting transparent and responsible language assistance.

Our study has limitations. First, our GenAI identification strategy relies on field-specific frequency shifts of lexical markers in titles and abstracts. Although we validate empirical robustness using stricter thresholds and alternative benchmarks (e.g., excluding GenAI-assisted U.S. texts, using a 2021 U.S. benchmark, and restricting to U.S. papers published in high-impact journals), full-text stylometry and validated detection tools would further sharpen inference
[
27
]
. Second, we classify authors affiliated with institutions in non-English-speaking countries as non-English, and those in the U.S. as English native. This distinction may not perfectly capture individual language proficiency, potentially introducing measurement noise. Third, similarity to a U.S. benchmark captures movement toward a dominant corpus of scientific English—not necessarily toward better science. Scholars have warned that such convergence may encourage stylistic homogenization and reduce rhetorical diversity
[
22
,
6
,
28
]
. This possibility cannot be ruled out by our analysis and may warrant further investigation. Fourth, our design is observational. Linking author-level adoption to outcomes (e.g., acceptance rates, time to publication, citations, and reviewer text) and exploiting natural experiments (e.g., institutional rollouts and disclosure mandates) would strengthen causal claims. Finally, deeper understanding of field-specific norms, disclosure behavior, and interactions between human editing and GenAI assistance will be essential to assess how language technologies reshape scientific practice. In sum, GenAI may broaden global participation in scientific knowledge production at scale, but realizing this potential will depend on complementary investments, responsible practices, and thoughtful policy choices.

Materials and Methods

Data

We use all scientific publications written in English and indexed in the Scopus database from 2021 to 2024
[
49
,
18
]
. For each publication, we obtain information on the publication year, Scopus pre-defined scientific field, journal impact factor, and the country of all affiliated institutions. For authors with more than one affiliation, we use the first listed one, which typically corresponds to the author’s main institutional affiliation. We start with 5.65 million unique publications that include at least one author affiliated with a non-English-speaking country. We further expand the data so that each publication appears once for every non-English-speaking country represented in its author affiliation, dropping all observations for English-speaking countries. For publications classified into multiple scientific fields, we further split records by field, yielding one observation per country-field pair. Our unit of analysis is therefore the publication × country × field cell. The resulting regression sample comprises 14,315,948 publication-country-field observations.

To improve interpretability in cross-field comparisons, we aggregate the detailed Scopus’s journal-based scientific fields into four broad field groups: Life Sciences, Physical Sciences, Engineering and Technology, and Social Sciences. Multidisciplinary and Arts and Humanities journals are excluded due to their small sample size and ambiguous classification. This dimensionality reduction simplifies cross-disciplinary analysis while retaining the main structural differences between scientific domains. Table
1
shows the mapping of detailed fields to these four categories. Each publication inherits the field assignments of its journal. Journals listed in multiple fields contribute to each corresponding scientific field.

Although English dominates higher education and international scientific communication, its ”distance” to non English languages varies considerably across countries. The linguistic distance between a country’s native language and English thus likely shapes both the initial need for GenAI support and the potential for linguistic convergence. To capture this dimension, we use the Common Language Index (CLI) that aggregates three linguistic components
[
37
]
: (i) common native language (CNL), which measures the probability that two randomly chosen individuals from two countries share the same mother tongue; (ii) common official language (COL), which captures institutionalized support for communication via official status; and (iii) linguistic proximity (LP), which quantifies similarities between basic vocabularies of different languages based on the Automated Similarity Judgment Program (ASJP)
[
55
]
. These components are combined into a normalized (0-1) score that reflects the overall ease of communication between countries due to language ties. Higher (lower) values indicate less (more) distance to U.S. English. We split the sample at the median CLI score: (i) Proximate countries (CLI above median); and (ii) Distant countries (CLI below median). We then assess whether the impact of GenAI adoption on linguistic convergence differs in contexts where researchers face larger inherent language barriers.

Identifying GenAI-assisted Papers

We identify the likely use of GenAI by detecting lexical pattern shifts in publication titles and abstracts before and after the introduction of ChatGPT. Our approach builds on recent evidence that text produced with the assistance of large language models (LLMs) tends to exhibit clear stylistic word features
[
3
]
. We exploit this by using a vocabulary of 65 terms previously linked to GenAI-generated content
[
52
,
20
,
21
]
. Table
2
lists the terms that are applied in stemmed form to detect GenAI assistance in publications.

Recognizing that keyword occurrence may vary by field, we apply a field-specific frequency-based filter. In particular, we restrict the analysis to terms whose relative frequency increased by at least 300%: i.e., at least quadrupled in use from 2021 to 2024 and measured separately within each scientific field. We employ a high threshold as more modest shifts could reflect unrelated field-specific usage rather than any GenAI tool assistance. This approach helps to better isolate those terms whose diffusion is plausibly associated with GenAI adoption. Figure
S1
in the Supplementary Materials presents the final selection of terms (denoted as words achieving at least a four-fold increase) by scientific field, and shows a clear upward trend in the frequency of use from 2021 to 2024. A publication is classified as GenAI-assisted if at least one of the filtered keywords in stemmed form appears in its title or abstract. All remaining publications are treated as non-GenAI-assisted. Our filtering approach is designed to reduce false positives, although some keywords may still capture general changes in scientific language rather than GenAI tool use or adoption. We discuss these limitations in the Supplementary Materials and encourage future work to incorporate full-text stylometry and large language model (LLM)-based detection tools where feasible.

In Scopus, scientific field classifications are assigned at the journal level, and journals may be cross-listed in multiple fields. As a result, a single publication can belong to more than one field. Because linguistic baselines differ across fields, we identify GenAI markers and thresholds within field-year strata. A publication is classified as GenAI-assisted if it is flagged in any of its assigned fields (i.e., an ‘any-field’ rule). This choice reduces missing true cases and ensures a single, consistent label per publication. We verify the robustness of our classification approach by applying different frequency change thresholds to our (300%) baseline. More and less restrictive thresholds change the final term composition used to identify GenAI-assisted papers, but they do not affect our baseline findings by much. The Supplementary Materials provides more discussion around and analysis of our threshold classification approach.

Our classification approach examines titles and abstracts. Titles and abstracts are especially well-suited for detecting stylistic shifts
[
44
]
and carry significant editorial weight
[
30
]
, making them meaningful proxies for GenAI uptake in scientific writing. But we readily acknowledge that GenAI tools are likely used in other parts of a manuscript. As such, our estimates thus represent a lower bound of true GenAI adoption.

Linguistic Similarity Measurement

We use text embeddings to measure the linguistic similarity between publication titles and abstracts written by authors affiliated with institutions in non-English speaking countries and those written by authors affiliated with U.S. institutions only. Text embeddings are vector representations in a high dimensional semantic space, where proximity reflects similarity in language use as well as underlying content. We use SciBERT rather than general BERT because it is pretrained from scratch on scientific text and employs a domain specific vocabulary. This allows SciBERT to represent technical terms more accurately and better model the linguistic patterns typical of scientific writing
[
10
]
.

For each publication, we construct an embedding by concatenating its title and abstract, tokenizing this text with the pretrained SciBERT (scivocab, uncased) model from Hugging Face
[
10
]
, and processing it with SciBERT using its maximum sequence length of 512 subword tokens (shorter texts are padded and longer ones truncated). From the model’s final hidden states, we derive a 768-dimensional mean-pooled text vector (averaging token embeddings across the sequence). This approach is widely used for capturing overall text semantics with BERT models
[
47
,
42
]
. Missing titles or abstracts are replaced with empty strings prior to concatenation to ensure consistent processing. Publications missing both fields are excluded from the analysis. Embeddings are computed one publication at a time without gradient updates or fine-tuning. These embeddings serve as the basis for our subsequent analysis, where we quantify the linguistic proximity of non-U.S. publications to U.S. scientific writing by computing cosine similarities between each non-U.S. publication and every ”pure” U.S. publication (i.e., publications with only U.S. affiliations) from the same field and publication year. For example, a Swiss chemistry publication from 2022 is compared to pure U.S. chemistry publications from 2022. We use L2 normalized embeddings for this calculation and average the resulting pairwise similarities to obtain the publication’s similarity score relative to the U.S. benchmark corpus. Cosine similarity values range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater similarity in both linguistic expression and semantic content. These similarity scores form the basis of our empirical analysis, in which we test whether GenAI-assisted publications from non-English-speaking countries show stronger convergence toward U.S. benchmarks after 2022 than non-GenAI-assisted ones.

For robustness, we repeat the analysis using alternative benchmark sets that preserve the same field and year match but include only pure U.S. publications in top journals (the top 10% by field-specific journal impact factor) or only pure U.S. publications not identified as GenAI-assisted. We further use 2021 as a temporal baseline, comparing each GenAI-assisted non-U.S. publication with pure U.S. publications from 2021 in the same field, as it predates the release of ChatGPT. The corresponding empirical results are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Empirical Model

Our empirical approach follows an event-study Difference-in-Differences (DiD) design with repeated cross-sections, comparing GenAI-assisted and non-assisted texts to a U.S. benchmark corpus across calendar years, controlling for fixed effects to absorb time-invariant heterogeneity. The year 2022 serves as the reference year. We include pre-2023 years when GenAI was not yet available to strengthen our design and create a placebo test: viz., if publications flagged by our linguistic markers over 2021-2022 do not differ from other publications in those years, it demonstrates that the observed shifts only emerge once GenAI is actually in use. The pre-GenAI period thus provides a critical baseline, helping disentangle changes driven by the genuine adoption of GenAI from those that could be explained by the mere presence of certain terms. In other words, the pre-GenAI baseline helps verify that any divergence observed post-2023 reflects actual GenAI adoption rather than pre-existing stylistic preferences between GenAI-assisted and non-flagged papers. Empirical Robustness

We conduct a series of empirical robustness tests to assess the stability of our baseline and subsample estimation results. These tests include alternative keyword thresholds, stricter number of keyword thresholds, and alternative definitions for the corpus of benchmark publications.

Alternative Keyword Thresholds

The baseline estimation identifies GenAI-assisted publications using a field-specific frequency threshold of 300% (i.e., occurrences increase by a factor of four between 2021 and 2024) for GenAI-related terms. We test whether this result is sensitive to this threshold level: i.e., thresholds too low may produce too many false-positives; thresholds too high may produce too many false-negatives. We re-estimate the main specification using looser (i.e., 200% or a three-fold occurrence increase) and stricter (i.e., 400% or a five-fold occurrence increase) thresholds.

Stricter Number of Keyword Thresholds

The baseline estimation classifies a publication as GenAI-assisted if at least one GenAI-linked keyword appears in its title or abstract. This definition maximizes sensitivity to early stylistic shifts. However, it may also capture publications where such language reflects author(s) unqiue writing style rather than genuine GenAI assistance. We address this concern by implement a stricter criterion that requires at least two distinct GenAI-related terms to be present. Under this definition, publications flagged only by a single keyword are excluded from the sample. This robustness test ensures that our findings are not driven by marginal cases where GenAI-linked keywords appear without reflecting systematic adoption of GenAI writing support. Convergence that persists under stricter definitions provides additional evidence that the observed effects capture meaningful GenAI-related shifts in linguistic style rather than noise from spurious single-word matches.

Alternative Corpus Definitions

A critical assumption underlying our baseline estimation is that our set of U.S.-authored benchmark publications serves as an accurate and stable reference for ”standard” scientific English. To ensure that our findings are not mechanically driven by how this benchmark is defined, we consider three alternatives.

These robustness tests help strengthen the credibility of our empirical strategy by demonstrating that the observed convergence of GenAI-assisted texts does not hinge on specific choices regarding the composition of our U.S. benchmark corpus.
