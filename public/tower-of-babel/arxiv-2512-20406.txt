Maximal functions, conjugations and multipliers between Toeplitz kernels
M. Cristina Câmara, C. Carteiro, C. Diogo

Abstract
Toeplitz kernels can be defined by Riemann-Hilbert problems, by maximal functions, or by multipliers acting on model spaces. In this paper we study those different characterisations and their relations, highlighting, on the one hand, the crucial role played by symbol factorisation in obtaining multipliers from a model space onto a Toeplitz kernel, in particular isometric multipliers, and, on the other hand, a deep connection of maximal functions with a naturally defined conjugation on the Toeplitz kernel.

1
Introduction: Sarason’s question

When is a closed subspace of
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
a Toeplitz kernel, i.e., the kernel of a bounded Toeplitz operator? To this question, asked by Sarason in
[
26
]
where he revisits and reformulates the results of Hayashi in
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, various answers can be given by looking at it from different perspectives which complement and clarify one another.

1.1
Toeplitz kernels and Riemann-Hilbert problems

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the unit circle,
L^{\infty}:=L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
,
H^{2}_{+}:=H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
denote the Hardy space of the unit disk and let
H^{2}_{-}:=\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{\bot}=\bar{z}\overline{H^{2}_{+}}
.
For
G\in\left(L^{\infty}\right)^{n\times n}
, consider the Riemann-Hilbert (RH for short) boundary value problem on
\mathbb{T}
with matrix coefficient
G

G\phi_{+}=\phi_{-},\quad\phi_{\pm}\in\left(H^{2}_{\pm}\right)^{n}.

(1.1)

The solutions
\phi_{+}
to the RH problem (
1.1
) constitute the kernel of the Toeplitz operator with symbol
G
, defined by

T_{G}:\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}\to\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n},\quad T_{G}\phi_{+}=P^{+}G\phi_{+},

(1.2)

where
P^{+}
denotes the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}
onto
H^{2}_{+}
, applied componentwise to
G\phi_{+}
.
We have thus the following characterisation:

(i)

a closed subspace
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}
is a Toeplitz kernel if and only if there exists
G\in\left(L^{\infty}\right)^{n\times n}
such that
\mathcal{K}
coincides with the space of solutions
\phi_{+}
of the RH problem (
1.1
).
In that case
\mathcal{K}=\ker T_{G}
.

This characterisation, which is valid both in the scalar and in the matricial cases and can moreover be extended to Toeplitz operators defined in the Hardy spaces
H^{p}\left(\mathbb{D}\right)
,
1<p<\infty
, will be frequently used in this work.
Not only is it very general, as it also allows for purely complex analytic methods to be applied when studying Toeplitz operators.
Model spaces in
H^{2}_{+}
, which are a particularly important class of Toeplitz kernels (
[
14
]
), can be seen as consisting of the solutions
\phi_{+}\in H^{2}_{+}
of the particular RH problem

\overline{\theta}\phi_{+}=\phi_{-},\quad\phi_{\pm}\in H^{2}_{\pm},

where
\theta
is an inner function.

Many important properties of Toeplitz kernels can be obtained by taking this RH approach.
One such property is that Toeplitz kernels in
\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}
are
nearly
\eta
-invariant
, i.e.,

\phi_{+}\in\ker T_{G},\;\eta\phi_{+}\in\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}\implies\eta\phi_{+}\in\ker T_{G},

(1.3)

for
\eta
in a wide class of complex valued functions, including all functions in
\overline{H^{\infty}}
and all rational functions whose poles are in the closed unit disk (
[
6
]
).

As a consequence of this property we can also characterise some subspaces of
\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}
which are
not
Toeplitz kernels.

Proposition 1.1

(
[
6
, Thms. 3.5, 3.6]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subseteq f\cdot\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}
, where
f
is an inner function or a rational function whose zeroes belong to the closed unit disk, then
\mathcal{A}
is
not
a Toeplitz kernel.

In particular, for
\eta=1/z
(i.e.,
\eta=\overline{z}
on
\mathbb{T}
), (
1.3
) is equivalent to

\phi_{+}\in\ker T_{G},\phi_{+}(0)=0\implies S^{*}\phi_{+}\in\ker T_{G},

where
S^{*}=T_{\overline{z}I_{n\times n}}
is the backward shift operator.
This is expressed by saying that Toeplitz kernels are
nearly
S^{*}
-invariant subspaces
of
\left(H^{2}_{+}\right)^{n}
.
In the context of
H^{2}_{+}
, nearly
S^{*}
-invariant subspaces were introduced and characterised by D. Hitt in
[
19
]
.
This study has since attracted great interest and has become a topic of research in its own right (see for instance
[
10
]
and references therein).

1.2
Toeplitz kernels and maximal functions

We now focus on Toeplitz operators defined on
H^{2}_{+}
, with scalar symbols
g\in L^{\infty}
.

Another important property of Toeplitz kernels that can be deduced from the RH description of those spaces is that, for every
f\in H^{2}_{+}
, there exists a Toeplitz kernel containing
\{f\}
, which is contained in every other Toeplitz kernel to which
f
belongs.
It is called the
minimal Toeplitz kernel for
f
and denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
;
f
is said to be a
maximal function
in
\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
.
Moreover, for every non-zero Toeplitz kernel
\mathcal{K}
, one can find some maximal function
f^{M}
and, if
f^{M}=IO
is an inner-outer factorisation of
f^{M}
(with
I
inner and
O\in H^{2}_{+}
outer), we have that

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{min}(f^{M})=\ker T_{\overline{zI}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}

(1.4)

(
[
6
, Thm 5.1]
).
Thus any maximal function in a Toeplitz kernel determines the latter and provides, by inner-outer factorisation, a unimodular symbol for that kernel.

We have thus the following characterisation of Toeplitz kernels in terms of maximal functions.

(ii)

a closed subspace
\mathcal{K}\subseteq H^{2}_{+}
is a Toeplitz kernel if and only if there exists
f^{M}\in\mathcal{K}
with inner-outer factorisation
f^{M}=IO
(
I
inner and
O
outer) such that, for all
\phi_{+}\in H^{2}_{+}
,

\phi_{+}\in\mathcal{K}\iff\frac{\overline{f^{M}}}{O}\,\phi_{+}\in\overline{H^{2}_{+}}.

In that case,
\mathcal{K}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{f^{M}}}{O}}=\ker T_{\overline{zI}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}
.

Maximal functions in a Toeplitz kernel can be described as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
7
, Thm 2.2]
)
.

Let
g\in L^{\infty}\backslash\{0\}
.
Then
f^{M}\in H^{2}_{+}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
if and only if

gf^{M}=\overline{z}\overline{O},\quad\text{ for some }O\in H^{2}_{+}\text{, outer}.

(1.5)

Example 1.3
.

Let
g=\overline{\theta}
, where
\theta
is an inner function, and define, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
,

\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}=\frac{\theta-\theta(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\quad,\quad k^{\theta}_{\lambda}=\frac{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}.

(1.6)

Note that
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in H^{\infty}
and
k^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
(denoting by
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{A}
the group of invertible elements in a unital algebra
\mathcal{A}
), so
k^{\theta}_{\lambda}
is outer and we have

\overline{\theta}\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}=\overline{z}\overline{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}.

Thus,
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
is a maximal function in
K_{\theta}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.

1.3
Toeplitz kernels and multipliers acting on model spaces

Toeplitz kernels can also be described as resulting from multipliers acting on model spaces.
Multipliers between two Toeplitz kernels play an important role in understanding those spaces, especially if one of the Toeplitz kernels is a simple or a well studied space to which the other Toeplitz kernel is reduced, in some sense, by a multiplication operator.
The case where one of the Toeplitz kernels is a model space and we have a representation of the form

\ker T_{g}=wK_{\theta},\quad\theta\text{ inner},

(1.7)

is particularly important.
Indeed Hayashi showed (
[
17
, Thm. 3]
, see also
[
26
]
) that such a representation is possible for every nontrivial Toeplitz kernel and that one may choose the multiplier
w
and the function
\theta
in (
1.7
) such that
\theta(0)=0
and
w
multiplies the model space
K_{\theta}
isometrically onto
\ker T_{g}
.

Hayashi’s characterisation of Toeplitz kernels can be stated as follows.
Let
u\in H^{2}_{+}
be an outer function with
u(0)>0
, let
F
be the Herglotz integral of
|u|^{2}
, let
b=\frac{F-1}{F+1}
,
a=\frac{2u}{F+1}

and write, for
\alpha
inner,
u_{\alpha}=\frac{a}{1-\alpha b}
.
With these notations we have:

Theorem 1.4

(
[
18
, Theorem 5]
)
.

The nontrivial kernels of Toeplitz operators are the subspaces of
H^{2}_{+}
of the form

M=u_{\alpha}K_{z\alpha}

(1.8)

where
\alpha
is inner,
u\in H^{2}_{+}
is outer with
u(0)>0
,
u^{2}
is an exposed point of the unit ball of
H^{1}
, and
u_{\alpha}
multiplies
K_{z\alpha}
isometrically onto
M
.

A function
f
in the unit ball of
H^{1}
is exposed if and only if it is rigid and
||f||_{1}=1
.
Rigid functions can be characterised in terms of Toeplitz operators (
[
26
, Proposition 1]
) as follows.

Proposition 1.5
.

If
O\in H^{2}_{+}
is outer, then

O^{2}\text{ is rigid in }H^{1}\iff\ker T_{\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}=\{0\}\iff\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}=\operatorname{span}\{O\}.

When
f\in H^{2}_{+}
is such that
f^{2}
is rigid in
H^{1}
, we say that
f
is
square-rigid
(
[
3
]
).
In that case, it is easy to see that
f
is necessarily outer (
[
26
]
).

As a consequence of Proposition
1.5
, we have:

Corollary 1.6
.

If
f^{\pm 1}\in H^{2}_{+}
then
f
is square-rigid.

Hayashi’s representation (
1.8
) is unique up to a unimodular constant.
While this representation is important to understand the structure of a Toeplitz kernel, by describing it in terms of a model space and a multiplier that preserves the norm, it may be very difficult to obtain explicitly.
In general, we look for a description of the form (
1.7
), which we call a
model space representation
of
\ker T_{g}
.
The multiplier in (
1.7
) is not necessarily isometric, but (
1.7
) provides a clear description of the Toeplitz kernel, especially if the model space is well understood.
We say that (
1.7
) is an
isometric model space representation
of
\ker T_{g}
if
w
multiplies
K_{\theta}
isometrically onto
\ker T_{g}
.

In this paper we study these various characterisations of a Toeplitz kernel and their relations, highlighting the crucial role played in this study by appropriate symbol factorisations and by conjugations.

The paper is organised as follows.

In Section
2
, which is of a preliminary nature, we present several known and new results highlighting the close connection between multipliers from one Toeplitz kernel onto another, maximal functions, and associated factorisations for the symbol that naturally arise in this context.

In Section
3
, we review and extend the characterisation of several properties of Toeplitz operators and their kernels in terms of certain factorisations of their symbols.
By introducing a generalisation of the classical notion of
L^{2}
-factorisation, we obtain a model space representation for the kernels of Toeplitz operators with piecewise continuous symbols, from which Hayashi’s representation (
1.8
) can be determined.
We also introduce, from the characterisation of Toeplitz kernels in terms of maximal functions, a new notion of maximal function factorisation for the symbols and we establish conditions for the outer factor of a maximal function to be a multiplier from a model space onto the kernel.
We illustrate these results by obtaining two model space representations of
\ker T_{\overline{E}z}
, where
E(z)=\exp(\frac{z+1}{z-1})
, one of which is isometric.

In Section
4
we study, for the first time to the authors’ knowledge, the relations between maximal functions and the natural conjugation in
\ker T_{g}
, denoted
C_{\overline{g}}
(
|g|=1
), using it to establish connections with generators of model spaces and invariant sets for the conjugation.

In Section
5
we apply the results of the previous sections to model spaces, a particularly well studied class of Toeplitz kernels, as an illustration.
In particular, by using maximal function factorisations, we obtain a large class of multipliers between two model spaces, thus recovering and extending results from
[
11
]
.

2
Maximal functions and multipliers between Toeplitz kernels

In what follows,
g
and
h
always denote functions in
L^{\infty}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\ker T_{g},\ker T_{h}\neq\{0\}
.

There is a close connection between maximal functions in Toeplitz kernels and multipliers between Toeplitz kernels.
We start by noting that, while multipliers between model spaces must be in
H^{2}_{+}
(
[
11
, Corollary 4]
), multipliers between general Toeplitz kernels need not be in
H^{2}_{+}
; they must however belong to the Smirnov class
\mathcal{N}^{+}
(
[
25
]
).
So we have that

\displaystyle w\ker T_{h}\subseteq\ker T_{g}

\displaystyle\implies w\in\mathcal{N}^{+}

\displaystyle w\ker T_{h}=\ker T_{g}

\displaystyle\implies w^{\pm 1}\in\mathcal{N}^{+}

(
[
7
, Remark 2.4]
).
These multipliers are characterised as follows.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
7
, Thm. 2.5]
)
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{N}^{+}
.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

w\ker T_{h}\subseteq\ker T_{g}
;

2.

w\ker T_{h}\subseteq L^{2}
and
\displaystyle wg/h\in\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}
;

3.

w\ker T_{h}\subseteq L^{2}
and for some (and hence every) maximal function
\displaystyle f^{M}_{h}
in
\ker T_{h}
we have
\displaystyle wf^{M}_{h}\in\ker T_{g}
.

For
w=1
we obtain from Theorem
2.7
necessary and sufficient conditions for a Toeplitz kernel to be included in another Toeplitz kernel.

Corollary 2.8

(
[
7
, Prop. 2.16]
)
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

\ker T_{h}\subseteq\ker T_{g}
;

2.

g/h\in\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}
;

3.

There exists a maximal function
f^{M}_{h}
in
\ker T_{h}
such that
f^{M}_{h}\in\ker T_{g}
.

Since every Toeplitz kernel can be represented in the form (
1.7
), it is natural to ask when is a product of the form
wK_{\theta}
a Toeplitz kernel and, more generally, when is
w\ker T_{h}
a Toeplitz kernel.
We have the following.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
w^{\pm 1}\in\mathcal{N}^{+}
.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

w\ker T_{h}=\ker T_{g}
;

2.

w\ker T_{h}\subseteq L^{2}
,
w^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq L^{2}
and
\frac{g}{h}=\frac{\overline{w}}{w}\frac{\overline{O}_{1}}{\overline{O}_{2}}
with
O_{1},O_{2}\in H^{2}_{+}
, outer;

3.

w\ker T_{h}\subseteq L^{2}
,
w^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq L^{2}
and, for some (and hence every) maximal function
f^{M}_{h}
in
\ker T_{h}
, we have that
wf^{M}_{h}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
.

Moreover,

\ker T_{g}=w\ker T_{h}\implies\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{h\frac{\overline{w}}{w}}.

(2.1)

For
w=1
, we obtain from Theorem
2.9
necessary and sufficient conditions for two Toeplitz operators with different symbols to have the same kernel.

Corollary 2.10

(
[
7
, Corollary 2.19]
)
.

\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{h}\iff\frac{g}{h}=\frac{\overline{O}_{1}}{\overline{O}_{2}}
with
O_{1},O_{2}\in H^{2}_{+}
outer.

In this case, if
h\in\mathcal{G}L^{\infty}
then
g=h\overline{O_{+}}
, with
O_{+}\in H^{\infty}
, outer; in particular, if
|g|=|h|=1
then
g=\lambda h
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\lambda|=1
.

Corollary 2.11

(
[
8
, Corollary 6.3]
)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{T}
,
g\in L^{\infty}
. Then

\ker T_{(z-a)g}=\ker T_{zg}.

If
\ker T_{h}
is a model space in the previous theorem, then we can say more about the multiplier
w
: it must be in
H^{2}_{+}
and it must be square-rigid.
This is a consequence of the following propositions.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
O\in H^{2}_{+}
be outer.
If
O^{-1}\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}\subseteq L^{2}
then
O
is square-rigid.

Proof.

Let
f_{+}\in\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}
, i.e.,
\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}f_{+}=f_{-}
with
f_{\pm}\in H^{2}_{\pm}
.
If
O^{-1}\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}\subseteq L^{2}
, then
O^{-1}f_{+}\in\mathcal{N}^{+}\cap L^{2}=H^{2}_{+}
and

\underbrace{O^{-1}f_{+}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle\in H^{2}_{+}}}=\underbrace{zf_{-}\overline{O}^{-1}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}\cap L^{2}}}\in H^{2}_{+}\cap\overline{H^{2}_{+}}=\mathbb{C}.

Therefore,
f_{+}=aO
with
a\in\mathbb{C}
and it follows that
\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}=\operatorname{span}\{O\}
, i.e.,
O
is square-rigid.
∎

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
O\in H^{2}_{+}
be outer.
If there exists a function
f\in\ker T_{h}
such that
f\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, and
O\ker T_{h}
is a Toeplitz kernel, then
O
is square-rigid.

Proof.

Let
O\ker T_{h}=\ker T_{g}
.
We have that
Of\in\ker T_{g}
, so
\mathcal{K}_{min}(Of)=\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{Of}}{Of}}\subseteq\ker T_{g}
.
On the other hand,
O^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq H^{2}_{+}
, so
f^{-1}O^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq H^{2}_{+}
.
Therefore

f^{-1}O^{-1}\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{Of}}{Of}}\subseteq f^{-1}O^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq H^{2}_{+}

and, by Proposition
2.12
,
Of
is square-rigid.
Since
f\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, it follows that
O
is square-rigid.
∎

Corollary 2.14
.

If
\ker T_{g}=wK_{\theta}
then
w\in H^{2}_{+}
and
w
is square-rigid.

Proof.

Since
f=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in K_{\theta}\cap\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, the result follows from Proposition
2.13
.
∎

A close connection between multipliers from one Toeplitz kernel into another and maximal functions in those Toeplitz kernels becomes clear from the previous results:
on the one hand, maximal functions completely determine a Toeplitz kernel and can be used as test functions for multipliers;
on the other hand, multipliers from
\ker T_{h}
onto
\ker T_{g}
determine, by multiplication, maximal functions in
\ker T_{g}
from maximal functions in
\ker T_{h}
.

It also becomes clear moreover that, whether we look at a Toeplitz kernel as defined by a maximal function, or we look at a representation of the form (
1.7
), certain factorisations of the symbol naturally appear, either via (
1.4
) or via (
2.1
).
In the following section we study different kinds of symbol factorisations for Toeplitz operators and their relations with maximal functions and multipliers.

3
Toeplitz kernels and symbol factorisation

The study of Toeplitz operators is closely related with various types of factorisation of their symbols (
[
15
,
24
]
).
The purpose of those factorisations may be to simplify the study of the properties of the corresponding Toeplitz operator by reducing it to that of a simpler operator, or to obtain a description of the Toeplitz kernel in terms of a multiplier acting on a model space, as in (
1.7
), or to establish an equivalence between the existence of a certain type of factorisation of the symbol and certain properties of the Toeplitz operator.

The first question that arises when studying Toeplitz kernels is whether they are different from
\{0\}
.
A necessary and sufficient condition for
T_{g}
not to be injective can be given in terms of a certain factorisation of its symbol, as follows.

Proposition 3.15

(
[
2
]
)
.

We have
\ker T_{g}\neq\{0\}
if and only if

g=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\alpha}O^{-1}

(3.1)

with
\alpha
inner, non constant, and
O,\mathcal{O}\in H^{2}_{+}
, outer.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the RH description of
\ker T_{g}
, (
1.1
).
If
f_{+}=I_{1}O_{1}
and
f_{-}=\overline{z}\overline{I_{2}}\overline{O_{2}}
, where
I_{1},I_{2}
are inner and
O_{1},O_{2}\in H^{2}_{+}
are outer, then

g(I_{1}O_{1})=\overline{z}\overline{I_{2}}\overline{O_{2}}\iff g=\overline{z}\overline{I_{1}I_{2}}\frac{\overline{O_{2}}}{O_{1}}.

So
g
admits a representation (
3.1
).
Conversely, if
g=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\alpha}O^{-1}
then, by Proposition
3.17
below,
Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\ker T_{g}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, so
\ker T_{g}\neq\{0\}
.
∎

Corollary 3.16

(
[
23
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

If
|g|=1
then
\ker T_{g}\neq\{0\}
if and only if
g=\overline{\alpha}\overline{O}/O
where
\alpha
is inner, non constant, and
O\in H^{2}_{+}
is outer.

The following result was used in the proof of Proposition
3.15
.

Proposition 3.17
.

If (
3.1
) holds then,
Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha},O\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\ker T_{g}
, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

We have that
k_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, so
Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in H^{2}_{+}
and
g(Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha})=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{z}\overline{\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}}\in H^{2}_{-}
,
therefore
Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\ker T_{g}
.
On the other hand,
\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in H^{\infty}
, so
O\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in H^{2}_{+}
and

g(O\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha})=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\alpha}\overline{\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}}=\overline{z}\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{k_{\lambda}^{\alpha}}\in H^{2}_{-},

(3.2)

thus
O\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\ker T_{g}
.
∎

Proposition
3.17
shows that, from a factorisation (
3.1
) for
g
, not only do we know that
\ker T_{g}\neq\{0\}
, but moreover we can identify families of functions belonging to
\ker T_{g}
and establish lower bounds for the dimension of the Toeplitz kernel.
We have the following, where
FBP
denotes the set of all finite Blaschke products.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
g
satisfy (
3.1
).
Then

1.

if
\alpha\in FBP
, with exactly
k
zeroes in
\mathbb{D}
(counting multiplicities), then
\dim\ker T_{g}\geq k
, and the equality holds if and only if
O
is square-rigid;

2.

if
\alpha\notin FBP
or
\dim\ker T_{\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}=\infty
, then
\ker T_{g}
is infinite dimensional.

Proof.

(i) was proved in
[
7
, Theorem 2.8]
.

(ii) Since
Ok_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\in\ker T_{g}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
by Proposition
3.17
, and, if
\alpha\notin FBP
, any set

.
The last part follows from

∎

We can also formulate a necessary and sufficient condition for a representation of the form (

.

g

where

K_{\alpha}
.
In that case,
\overline{\alpha}\overline{O}/O

.

Proof.

If
g=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\overline{\alpha}O^{-1}
then
O\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}
is a maximal function in

, and
\tilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\alpha}

.
Conversely, if

is a maximal function in

, where

, then
gOf_{\alpha}^{M}=\overline{z}\overline{\mathcal{O}_{1}}
, with

outer;
and, since by Theorem

where

∎

If

, with

and

, then, for any maximal function

in

, but the converse is not true.
Indeed, in Proposition

.
One may then ask when is the factor

) a multiplier from a model space onto

.
We have the following, which is an immediate consequence of Theorem

(iii).

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
f_{\alpha}^{M}
be a maximal function in

, with

inner.
If

, with

\ker T_{g}=OK_{\alpha}\iff OK_{\alpha}\subseteq L^{2}\text{ and }O^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq L^{2}.

(3.3)

Example 3.21
.

Consider
\ker T_{\overline{\alpha}(z+1)^{1/5}}
where

is an inner function which is analytic in a neighbourhood of

.
We have that
f^{M}=\tilde{k}^{\alpha}_{\lambda}(z+1)^{-1/5}
is a maximal function in that kernel, since

.

Some particular types of factorisation of the form (
3.1
) for the symbol of a Toeplitz operator are of special interest, allowing us to characterise its kernel in terms of a multiplier acting on a model space, as in (
1.7
), namely
L^{2}
-factorisation (and its generalisations) and maximal function factorisations.

3.1
L^{2}
-factorisation and Toeplitz operators with piecewise continuous symbols

We say that
g\in L^{\infty}
admits a
L^{2}
-factorisation
(
[
22
, Chapter 2]
) if and only if it can be represented as a product

g=g_{-}z^{n}g_{+}\quad,\text{ with }n\in\mathbb{Z},\;g_{+}^{\pm 1}\in H^{2}_{+},\;g_{-}^{\pm 1}\in\overline{H^{2}_{+}}.

(3.4)

When
n=0
, the
L^{2}
-factorisation is said to be
canonical
.
It is easy to see that the following holds.

Proposition 3.22
.

The factorisation (
3.4
) is unique up to constant factors in
g_{\pm}
.
If such a factorisation exists, then
\ker T_{g}\neq\{0\}
if and only if
n<0
;
in that case
g_{+}^{-1}z^{|n|-1}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
and we have

\ker T_{g}=g_{+}^{-1}K_{z^{|n|}}.

A
L^{2}
-factorisation (
3.4
) such that

g_{-}P^{+}g_{-}^{-1}I:\mathcal{D}\to L^{2}\text{ is bounded}

(3.5)

for some
\mathcal{D}
dense in
L^{2}
(for instance,
\mathcal{D}
may be the space
\mathcal{R}
of all rational functions without poles on
\mathbb{T}
) is called a
Wiener-Hopf factorisation
in
L^{2}
, also known as a
generalised factorisation
in
L^{2}
(
[
24
, Chapter 4, §3]
).
This is a particularly important factorisation of the symbol, as we have the following equivalences (
[
24
]
, see also
[
9
]
).

Theorem 3.23
.

The operator
T_{g}
is Fredholm if and only if
g
admits a Wiener-Hopf factorisation in
L^{2}
;

T_{g}
is invertible if and only if that factorisation is canonical.

We say that
g
has a
bounded factorisation
if and only if, for some inner function
\alpha
,

g=g_{-}\alpha^{n}g_{+}\quad,\text{ with }n\in\mathbb{Z},\;g_{+}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty},\;g_{-}\in\mathcal{G}\overline{H^{\infty}}.

(3.6)

In that case we say that
g\sim\alpha^{n}
(
[
7
, Definition 2.23]
).

It is clear that, if (
3.6
) holds, then
T_{g}=T_{g_{-}}T_{\alpha^{n}}T_{g_{+}}
, where
T_{g_{\pm}}
are invertible Toeplitz operators.
When
\alpha=z
, (
3.6
) is a
bounded Wiener-Hopf factorisation
, where (
3.5
) is necessarily satisfied with
\mathcal{D}=H^{2}_{+}
.

It is known that a Wiener-Hopf factorisation in
L^{2}
exists for a wide class of functions, including all non-vanishing continuous functions on
\mathbb{T}
(
[
15
,
24
]
), and that it is bounded when the functions belong to certain algebras of continuous functions, called decomposing algebras (
[
24
]
).
Examples of such algebras are the Wiener algebra and the algebra
C^{\mu}
of all Hölder continuous functions with exponent
\mu\in]0,1[
, which are particularly relevant in applications in physics and engineering (
[
4
,
5
,
20
,
21
]
).

\mathcal{R}
is a subspace of any decomposing algebra, so any function in
\mathcal{G}
\mathcal{R}
admits a bounded Wiener-Hopf factorisation.
In particular, if
B
is a finite Blaschke product we can write

B=B_{-}z^{n}B_{+},

(3.7)

with
B_{-}=\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}
,
B_{+}\in\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, where
n
is the number of zeroes of
B
in
\mathbb{D}
.

However a non-vanishing piecewise continuous
g
on
\mathbb{T}
, i.e., with
\inf\{|g(t)|:t\in\mathbb{T}\}>0
, may not admit a
L^{2}
-factorisation, even in very simple cases, such as
g=z^{1/2}
(
[
22
, Thm 2.5]
).
Those functions, with discontinuities
c_{k}\in\mathbb{T}
,
k=1,\dots,n
, and
g(z^{\pm})\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
, can be represented in the form

g(z)=h(z)\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z^{\alpha_{k}})_{c_{k}}

(3.8)

where
h\in\mathcal{G}C(\mathbb{T})
,

\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\log\frac{g(c_{k}^{-})}{g(c_{k}^{+})}\quad,\quad-\frac{1}{2}\leq\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{k}<\frac{1}{2},

and
(z^{\alpha_{k}})_{c_{k}}
denotes a branch of
z^{\alpha_{k}}
with branch cut crossing
\mathbb{T}
at
c_{k}
.
The function g admits a
L^{2}
-factorisation if and only if
\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{k}\neq-\frac{1}{2}
for all
k=1,\dots,n
(
[
12
, Lemma 4.1]
).
We say in that case that
g
is 2-regular.
So, if
\operatorname{Re}\alpha_{k}=-\frac{1}{2}
for some
k
(which geometrically means that the line segment with endpoints
g(c_{k}^{-})
and
g(c_{k}^{+})
contains the origin in the complex plane), then
g
does not admit a
L^{2}
- factorisation.

One can however extend the result of Proposition
3.22
to that class of symbols by using the following generalisation of the notion of
L^{2}
-factorisation.

Theorem 3.24
.

Let
g\in L^{\infty}\backslash\{0\}
,
g=g_{-}z^{n}g_{+}^{-1}
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
g_{+},\overline{g_{-}}\in H^{2}_{+}
outer, such that, for some polynomial
P
with
p
simple zeroes (
p\geq 0
) all belonging to
\mathbb{T}
, we have

Pg_{+}^{-1}\in H^{2}_{+}\text{ and }\frac{g_{+}}{z-\lambda}\notin H^{2}_{+}\text{ for every }\lambda\in\mathbb{T}.

(3.9)

Then, if
n\geq 0
, we have
\ker T_{g}=\{0\}
and, if
n<0
, we have

kerT_{g}=g_{+}K_{z^{|n|}}.

(3.10)

Proof.

We have that
\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{\overline{g_{+}}z^{n}g_{+}^{-1}}
by Corollary
2.10
, so
\ker T_{g}
is given by
f_{+}\in H^{2}_{+}
such that
\overline{g_{+}}z^{n}g_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=f_{-}
with
f_{-}\in H^{2}_{-}
.
We have

\displaystyle\overline{g_{+}}z^{n}g_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=f_{-}

\displaystyle\iff z^{n}Pg_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=\frac{P}{\overline{P}}\,\overline{Pg_{+}^{-1}}f_{-}

\displaystyle\iff z^{n}Pg_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=cz^{p}\,\overline{Pg_{+}^{-1}}f_{-}

(3.11)

with
c\in\mathbb{C}
, where we used the equality
\frac{P}{\overline{P}}=cz^{p}
.
If
n\geq 0
we have

\underbrace{z^{n}Pg_{+}^{-1}f_{+}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle\in(H^{2}_{+})^{2}}}=cz^{p}\underbrace{Pg_{+}^{-1}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle\in\overline{H^{2}_{+}}}}\underbrace{f_{-}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle\in H^{2}_{-}}}=Q_{p-1}

where
Q_{p-1}
is a polynomial of degree at most
p-1
(cf.
[
1
]
in the context of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
).
Therefore
z^{n}f_{+}=\frac{g_{+}}{P}\,Q_{p-1}

but, since
\frac{g_{+}}{P}
is not square-integrable in any neighbourhood of any zero of
P
, the polynomial
Q_{p-1}
must vanish at all the
p
zeroes of
P
and it follows that
Q_{p-1}=0
.

If
n<0
, we have from (
3.11
), following a similar reasoning,

Pg_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=cz^{|n|+p}\overline{Pg_{+}^{-1}}f_{-}=Q_{|n|+p-1}

where
Q_{|n|+p-1}
is a polynomial of degree at most
|n|+p-1
.
Since
Q_{|n|+p-1}
must vanish at all zeroes of
P
, we get that

g_{+}^{-1}f_{+}=\tilde{Q}_{|n|-1}\in K_{z^{|n|}}
.
∎

Remark 3.25
.

Similarly to
L^{2}
-factorisation, the factorisation in Theorem
3.24
is unique up to constant factors in
g_{\pm}
.
Note also that a
L_{2}
-factorisation (
3.4
) satisfies conditions (
3.9
) with
P=1
.

Example 3.26
.

Consider
g
such that
g(z)=1
if
z\in\mathbb{T}\cap\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and
g(z)=-1
if
z\in\mathbb{T}\cap\mathbb{C}^{-}
, where
\mathbb{C}^{+}
(respectively,
\mathbb{C}^{-}
) denotes the upper (respectively, lower) halp-plane.
We can write, with the notation of (
3.8
),

g=z(z^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{-1}(z^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{1}=g_{-}zg_{+}^{-1}

with
g_{-}=(1+\overline{z})^{1/2}(1-\overline{z})^{1/2}
and
g_{+}=(z+1)^{1/2}(z-1)^{1/2}
.
Taking
P=(z+1)(z-1)
and applying Theorem
3.24
, we get that
\ker T_{g}=\{0\}
, since
n=1\geq 0
.

Note that, using Corollary
2.11
, one can extend the previous results to some classes of symbols that vanish on
\mathbb{T}
.

We now apply Theorem
3.24
to obtain a model space representation for Toeplitz kernels with piecewise continuous symbols that do not admit a
L^{2}
-factorisation.
Furthermore, we show that one can obtain from (
3.10
) an isometric model space representation of
\ker T_{g}
.

Example 3.27
.

A class of finite dimensional Toeplitz kernels

We now consider the question of characterising the kernels of a class of Toeplitz operators with symbols of the form
\overline{z}^{n/2}
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is odd.
These symbols do not admit a
L^{2}
-factorisation (so the associated Toeplitz operator is not Fredholm), but they admit a factorisation as in Theorem
3.24
.
This yields a representation of
\ker T_{g}
of the form (
3.10
).
We show here how this allows for determining an isometric multiplier from a model space onto the Toeplitz kernel and obtain Hayashi’s representation (
1.7
) in a simple and explicit way.

Let
\overline{z}^{n/2}=\overline{z}^{(n-1)/2}\;\overline{z}^{1/2}
where we take the principal branch of the square-root, so that
\overline{z}^{1/2}
is discontinuous on
\mathbb{T}
at the point
-1
.
We have then the factorisation

\overline{z}^{\frac{n}{2}}=\left(\frac{z+1}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}z^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

(3.12)

where the factors
g_{-}=\left(\frac{z+1}{z}\right)^{1/2}
and
g_{+}=(z+1)^{1/2}
satisfy the conditions (
3.9
) with
P(z)=z+1
.

By Theorem
3.24
,
\ker T_{\overline{z}^{n/2}}=\{0\}
, for
n=1
, and

\ker T_{\overline{z}^{\frac{n}{2}}}=(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{z^{N}},\quad N=\frac{n-1}{2}\text{, for }n\geq 3\text{, odd}.

(3.13)

To obtain an isometric multiplier from a model space onto
\ker T_{\overline{z}^{n/2}}
, we can take advantage of the fact that (
3.13
) provides a very simple description of that Toeplitz kernel and yields a basis

\mathcal{B}=\{(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}},(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}z,\dots,(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}z^{N-1}\},

(3.14)

from which one can easily determine a basis for the subspace of all functions in the Toeplitz kernel which vanish at
0
:

{\mathcal{B}_{0}}=\{(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}z,\dots,(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}z^{N-1}\}.

(3.15)

The isometric multiplier in Hayashi’s representation of
\ker T_{\overline{z}^{n/2}}
will be a constant multiple of the orthogonal vector to
{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
in that kernel (
[
26
]
) which can be obtained by subtracting, from the element
(z+1)^{1/2}\in\mathcal{B}
, its orthogonal projection into the space with basis
{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
.

To illustrate this result we take a particular case, with
n=7
.
We have

\ker T_{\overline{z}^{\frac{7}{2}}}=(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{z^{3}}.

(3.16)

With the notation above,
\mathcal{B}=\{(z+1)^{1/2}z^{j},j=0,1,2\}
and
{\mathcal{B}_{0}}=\{(z+1)^{1/2}z,(z+1)^{1/2}z^{2}\}
.
The function

\mathcal{U}=(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}-P_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}=(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-az)(1-\overline{a}z),

with
a=(1+2i)/5
, is orthogonal to
\operatorname{span}{\mathcal{B}_{0}}=z\ker T_{z\overline{z}^{7/2}}
, where
P_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{span}{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
.
So,

u=\frac{\mathcal{U}}{||\mathcal{U}||_{2}}

is an isometric multiplier from a model space of the form
K_{z\alpha}
onto
\ker T_{\overline{z}^{7/2}}
.

To determine the inner function
\alpha
, we use (
2.1
) in Theorem
2.9
together with the representation (
3.16
):

\ker T_{\overline{z}^{\frac{7}{2}}}=(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{z^{3}}=\frac{\mathcal{U}}{||\mathcal{U}||_{2}}K_{z\alpha}

implies that

K_{z\alpha}=\frac{||\mathcal{U}||_{2}}{\mathcal{U}}(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{z^{3}}=\frac{||\mathcal{U}||_{2}}{(1-az)(1-\overline{a}z)}K_{z^{3}}=K_{zB_{a}B_{\overline{a}}}

with
B_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{z-\lambda}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}
.
Thus, Hayashi’s representation for
\ker T_{\overline{z}^{7/2}}
is given by

\ker T_{\overline{z}^{\frac{7}{2}}}=\frac{(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-az)(1-\overline{a}z)}{||(z+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-az)(1-\overline{a}z)||_{2}}K_{zB_{a}B_{\overline{a}}}.

Remark 3.28
.

Note that the same method can be applied to any finite dimensional Toeplitz kernel to obtain a representation of the form (
1.8
), once a representation of the form
\ker T_{g}=wK_{z^{n}}
has been found.

3.2
Maximal function factorisation of symbols

Any maximal function
f^{M}
in
\ker T_{g}
, with inner-outer factorisation
f^{M}=IO
, where
I
is inner and
O
is outer, induces, by (
1.4
), a factorisation of the symbol

g=\overline{zI}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}\overline{h}\ ,\quad\text{ with }h\in H^{\infty}\text{ outer},

(3.17)

where we used Corollary
2.10
.
We call such a representation a
maximal function factorisation
of
g
.
For
|g|=1
, it takes the form

g=\overline{zI}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}.

(3.18)

This factorisation is unique if we fix the outer factor
O
.

For example, if
g=\overline{\theta}
with
\theta
inner and
\ker T_{g}=K_{\theta}
, then
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{0}
is a maximal function in
K_{\theta}
with inner-outer factorisation
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{0}=IO
where

I=\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{0}}{k^{\theta}_{0}}=\overline{z}\frac{\theta-\theta(0)}{1-\overline{\theta(0)}\theta}

is inner and
O=k^{\theta}_{0}=1-\overline{\theta(0)}\theta
is outer (
O\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
).
It follows that any inner function
\theta
can be factorised as

\theta=\left(z\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{0}}{k^{\theta}_{0}}\right)\frac{k^{\theta}_{0}}{\overline{k^{\theta}_{0}}}.

(3.19)

It may be useful to replace
z
in (
3.18
) by its expression in terms of a Blaschke factor
B_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, using the equality
z=B_{\lambda}\frac{1-\overline{\lambda}z}{1-\lambda\overline{z}}
:

g=\overline{B_{\lambda}I}\frac{\overline{O}(1-\lambda\overline{z})}{O(1-\overline{\lambda}z)}.

(3.20)

We say that (
3.20
) is a
modified maximal function factorisation
of
g
.

If
\ker T_{g}=uK_{z\theta}
is Hayashi’s representation of a nontrivial Toeplitz kernel, then we see that the isometric multiplier
u
is the outer factor of a certain maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
,
f^{M}=\theta u
.
We may then ask: if
f^{M}=IO
is an inner-outer factorisation of a maximal function
f^{M}
in
\ker T_{g}
,
when is the outer factor
O
of the maximal function a multiplier from a model space onto
\ker T_{g}
and, if so, whether there is a relation between that model space and the model space
K_{I}
defined by the inner factor of
f^{M}
.
We have the following.

Proposition 3.29
.

Let
f^{M}=IO
be an inner-outer factorisation of a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
.
If
\ker T_{g}=OK_{\alpha}
for some inner
\alpha
, then
K_{\alpha}=K_{zI}
.

Proof.

We have that
\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{IO}/O}=OK_{\alpha}=\ker T_{\overline{\alpha}\overline{O}/O}
.
So, by Corollary
2.10
,
\overline{zI}=\lambda\overline{\alpha}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\lambda|=1
.
∎

From Proposition
3.20
we also have:

Theorem 3.30
.

Let
f^{M}=IO
be an inner-outer factorisation of a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
.
Then,

\ker T_{g}=OK_{zI}\iff OK_{zI}\subseteq L^{2}\text{ and }O^{-1}\ker T_{g}\subseteq L^{2}.

Corollary 3.31
.

With the same assumptions as in Theorem
3.30
, we have
\ker T_{g}=OK_{zI}
if
O\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, or if
O
is square-rigid and
\dim K_{I}<\infty
.

Proof.

The result is clear if
O\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
, by Theorem
3.30
.
Now, if
\dim K_{I}<\infty
, then
\dim K_{zI}<\infty
, so
K_{zI}\subseteq H^{\infty}
and
OK_{zI}\subseteq H^{2}_{+}
.
On the other hand, since
\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{O}/O}=\operatorname{span}\{O\}
if
O
is square-rigid, it follows from
[
1
, Theorem 6.2]
that
\dim\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{I}\overline{O}/O}=\dim K_{I}+1
.
Since
OK_{zI}\subseteq\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{I}\overline{O}/O}=\ker T_{g}

and

\dim OK_{zI}=\dim K_{zI}=\dim K_{I}+1=\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{I}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}},

we conclude that
\ker T_{g}=OK_{zI}
.
∎

Example 3.32
.

Let
E
be the singular inner function
E(z)=\exp\left(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\right)
and let
g=\overline{E}z
.
By Theorem

, a maximal function in

is

f^{M}=\frac{k^{E}_{0}(0)\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}-\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)k^{E}_{0}}{z},

since

\overline{E}zf^{M}=\overline{z}\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)\overline{k^{E}_{0}}\overline{\left(\frac{k^{E}_{0}(0)}{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)}-\frac{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}}{k^{E}_{0}}\right)},

where

is an inner function,
k^{E}_{0}(0)=1-1/e^{2}
,
\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)=E^{\prime}(0)=1/e
and

k^{E}_{0},\ \frac{k^{E}_{0}(0)}{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)}-\frac{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}}{k^{E}_{0}}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}.

An inner-outer factorisation of

(cf. Section
4
) is
f^{M}=IO
with

I=\overline{z}\frac{k^{E}_{0}(0)\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}-\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)k^{E}_{0}}{\overline{k^{E}_{0}(0)}k^{E}_{0}-\overline{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)}\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}}\ ,\ O=\overline{k^{E}_{0}(0)}k^{E}_{0}-\overline{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)}\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty},

(3.21)

so, by Corollary
3.31
we obtain a model space representation for
\ker T_{\overline{E}z}
as

\ker T_{\overline{E}z}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{I}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}=OK_{zI}.

Noting that
O
is the product of two functions in
\mathcal{G}
H^{\infty}
,

O=\overline{k^{E}_{0}(0)}k^{E}_{0}\left(1-\overline{\left(\frac{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}(0)}{k^{E}_{0}(0)}\right)}\frac{\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}}{k^{E}_{0}}\right)=\overline{k^{E}_{0}(0)}k^{E}_{0}k^{\alpha}_{0},

and, from (
3.21
),
I=\tilde{k}^{\alpha}_{0}/k^{\alpha}_{0}
, we can also write

\ker T_{\overline{E}z}=\ker T_{\underbrace{\overline{z}\overline{\left(\frac{\tilde{k}^{\alpha}_{0}}{k^{\alpha}_{0}}\right)e}\frac{\overline{k^{\alpha}_{0}}}{k^{\alpha}_{0}}}_{\smash[b]{\scriptstyle=\ \overline{\alpha}\text{ by \eqref{eq:theta_max_fact}}}}\frac{\overline{k}^{E}_{0}}{k^{E}_{0}}}=k^{E}_{0}K_{\alpha}

Now, by
[
11
, Theorem 10]
,
m=\sqrt{1-|E(0)|^{2}}/k^{E}_{0}
is an isometric multiplier from
K_{E}
, which contains
\ker T_{\overline{E}z}
, onto
K_{z\tilde{k}^{E}_{0}/k^{E}_{0}}=K_{z\alpha}
, which contains
K_{\alpha}
, so
m
is also an isometric multiplier from
\ker T_{\overline{E}z}
onto
K_{\alpha}
, i.e.,

\ker T_{\overline{E}z}=\frac{k^{E}_{0}}{\sqrt{1-|E(0)|^{2}}}K_{\alpha}

is an isometric model space representation of
\ker T_{\overline{E}z}
.

A crucial role is played in the previous results by the inner-outer factorisation of maximal functions in a Toeplitz kernel.
This turns out to have a surprising connection, which we study in the next section, with conjugations that are naturally defined on Toeplitz kernels.

4
Maximal functions and conjugations

We assume throughout this section that
|g|=1
.
Recall that every nontrivial Toeplitz kernel can be described in terms of a unimodular symbol.

A conjugation on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is an antilinear operator
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{C}^{2}=I
, where
I
denotes the identity operator, and
\langle\mathcal{C}f_{1},\mathcal{C}f_{2}\rangle=\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle
for all
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
.
For
g
with
|g|=1
, a conjugation
\mathcal{C}=C_{\overline{g}}
can be defined by

C_{\overline{g}}f=\overline{g}\overline{z}\overline{f},\text{ for all }f\in\ker T_{g},

(4.1)

which can be seen as the natural conjugation on
\ker T_{g}
(
[
13
]
).
It is clear from (
4.1
) that
|C_{\overline{g}}f|=|f|
for all
f\in\ker T_{g}
.

For two unimodular symbols corresponding to the same Toeplitz kernel, the conjugations defined by (
4.1
) for those symbols differ by a unimodular constant.

Using the conjugation
C_{\overline{g}}
we can reformulate the characterisation of maximal functions presented in Theorem
1.2
, as being the conjugates, by
C_{\overline{g}}
, of the outer functions in
\ker T_{g}
.

Theorem 4.33
.

Let
f\in H^{2}_{+}
;
f
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
if and only if
C_{\overline{g}}f
is outer in
H^{2}_{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{O}
denote an outer function in
H^{2}_{+}
.

C_{\overline{g}}f=\mathcal{O}\iff\overline{gzf}=\mathcal{O}\iff gf=\overline{z}\overline{\mathcal{O}}

which is equivalent by Theorem
1.2
to
f
being a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
.
∎

Remark 4.34
.

If
\theta
is an inner function, we say that
f\in K_{\theta}
generates
K_{\theta}
if and only if

\bigvee\{\left(S^{*}\right)^{n}f:n\geq 0\}=K_{\theta},

where
S^{*}
denotes the backward shift on
H^{2}_{+}
.
By
[
14
, Corollary 8.25]
, if
f\in K_{\theta}
is outer then
C_{\theta}f
generates
K_{\theta}
.
As a consequence of Theorem
4.33
, this is equivalent to saying that every maximal function in
K_{\theta}
generates
K_{\theta}
.

Theorem
4.33
allows us to obtain, in a simple way, an inner-outer factorisation of any maximal function.

Proposition 4.35
.

If
f^{M}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
, then an inner-outer factorisation of
f^{M}
is
f^{M}=IO
, with
I=\frac{f^{M}}{C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}}
and
O=C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}
.
We have that
gf^{M}=\overline{z}\overline{O}
where
O
is the outer factor in an inner-outer factorisation of
f^{M}
.

Proof.

We have
f^{M}=\frac{f^{M}}{C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}}\,\cdot\,C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}
, where
C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}
is outer by Theorem
4.33
and
\frac{f^{M}}{C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}}
is a function in
\mathcal{N}^{+}
, with modulus one, so it is an inner function.
∎

Corollary 4.36
.

If
\mathcal{O}
is outer and
\mathcal{O}\in\ker T_{g}
, then
C_{\overline{g}}
\mathcal{O}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
with outer factor
\mathcal{O}
(up to a unimodular constant).
If two maximal functions have the same outer factor then they differ by a unimodular constant.

Proof.

This follows from
C_{\overline{g}}^{2}=I
, Theorem
4.33
and Proposition
4.35
.
∎

In the case where the Toeplitz kernel is a model space
K_{\theta}
, one can associate to any outer function
F\in K_{\theta}
an inner function,
I_{F}=\frac{C_{\theta}F}{F}
, which is called the associated inner function of
F
with respect to
\theta
(
[
14
, Definition 8.18]
).
It follows from Proposition
4.35
and Corollary
4.36
that
I_{F}
is the inner factor of the maximal function with outer factor
F
(up to a unimodular constant).

Remark 4.37
.

Note that two linearly independent maximal functions can share the same inner factor in an inner-outer factorisation.
For example,
f_{1}(z)=z-1
and
f_{2}(z)=z+1
are both maximal functions in
K_{z^{2}}
, with inner factor
I=1
.

The set of all elements in
\ker T_{g}
with the same outer factor as a given maximal function is characterised as follows, generalising an analogous result for model spaces (
[
14
, Proposition 8.20]
).

Proposition 4.38
.

Let
f^{M}
be a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
with inner-outer factorisation
f^{M}=IO
.
The set of all functions in
\ker T_{g}
with outer factor
O
is

\{\alpha O:\alpha\text{ inner and }\alpha\preceq I\}.

Proof.

If
\alpha O\in\ker T_{g}
, then by (
1.4
)
(\overline{z}\overline{I}\overline{O}/O)(\alpha O)=\overline{z}\overline{\psi_{+}}
with
\psi_{+}\in H^{2}_{+}
, so
\overline{I}\alpha=\overline{\psi_{+}}/\overline{O}\in\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}\cap L^{\infty}=\overline{H^{\infty}}
, implying that
\alpha\preceq I
.
Conversely, if
\alpha\preceq I
, then
(\overline{z}\overline{I}\overline{O}/O)(\alpha O)=\overline{z}\overline{I}\alpha\overline{O}\in H^{2}_{-}
, so
\alpha O\in\ker T_{g}
.
∎

In every nontrivial Toeplitz kernel, there are maximal functions whose outer factor is square-rigid
[
1
]
.
It is thus natural to ask whether there exists a maximal function whose outer factor is
1
(that is, an inner maximal function), or whose inner factor is a unimodular constant (that is, an outer maximal function), or even whether one can construct, from a maximal function, other maximal functions with different outer factors.
Note that, by Corollary
4.36
, a maximal function
f^{M}
is defined, up to a unimodular constant, by its outer factor
C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}
.

The answer to the first question is as follows.

Proposition 4.39
.

There exists a function
f\in\ker T_{g}
such that
C_{\overline{g}}f=1
, i.e.,
\ker T_{g}
has an inner maximal function
f^{M}=\theta
, if and only if
\ker T_{g}=K_{z\theta}
.

Proof.

C_{\overline{g}}\theta=1\iff\overline{gz\theta}=1\iff g=\overline{z\theta}
.
∎

In general a maximal function
f^{M}
is neither inner nor outer, but one can construct from
f^{M}
other maximal functions related by a partial order.
Let
f_{1}^{M}
and
f^{M}_{2}
be maximal functions in
\ker T_{g}
and let
f_{j}^{M}=I_{j}O_{j}
,
j=1,2
, be their inner-outer factorisations (
I_{j}
inner,
O_{j}
outer).

Definition 4.40
.

We say that

f_{1}^{M}\preceq f^{M}_{2}\text{ if and only if }I_{1}\preceq I_{2}

(4.2)

(where
I_{1}\preceq I_{2}
means that
I_{2}\overline{I_{1}}\in H^{\infty}
).
If
I_{1}\prec I_{2}
, i.e.,
I_{2}\overline{I_{1}}\in H^{\infty}
is not a constant, then we say that
f_{1}^{M}\prec f^{M}_{2}
.

The relation defined by (
4.2
) can also be expressed in terms of the outer factors
O_{j}
,
j=1,2
.

Proposition 4.41
.

f_{1}^{M}\preceq f_{2}^{M}\iff\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{O_{2}}/O_{2}}\subseteq\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{O_{1}}/O_{1}}
.

Proof.

Since both
f_{1}^{M}
and
f_{2}^{M}
are maximal functions in
\ker T_{g}
, by (
3.18
) we have that

\overline{I_{1}}\frac{\overline{O_{1}}}{O_{1}}=c\overline{I_{2}}\frac{\overline{O_{2}}}{O_{2}},\text{ with }c\in\mathbb{C},|c|=1,

and
\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O_{1}}}{O_{1}}=(c\overline{I_{2}}I_{1})\,\overline{z}\,\frac{\overline{O_{2}}}{O_{2}}
.
If
f_{1}^{M}\preceq f_{2}^{M}
, then
c\overline{I_{2}}I_{1}\in\overline{H^{\infty}}
, so
\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O_{2}}}{O_{2}}}\subseteq\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O_{1}}}{O_{1}}}
.
Conversely, if the latter inclusion holds, then by Corollary
2.8
we have that

\frac{O_{2}}{\overline{O_{2}}}\frac{\overline{O_{1}}}{O_{1}}\in\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}\implies c\overline{I_{2}}I_{1}\in\overline{\mathcal{N}^{+}}\cap L^{\infty}=\overline{H^{\infty}},

so
I_{1}\preceq I_{2}
.
∎

Noting that
O_{j}=C_{\overline{g}}f_{j}^{M}
,
j=1,2
(up to a unimodular constant) and
\ker T_{\overline{z}\overline{O_{j}}/O_{j}}=\mathcal{K}_{min}(O_{j})
, we see that the relation (
4.2
) between maximal functions can moreover be expressed in terms of the conjugation
C_{\overline{g}}
:

f_{1}^{M}\preceq f_{2}^{M}\iff\mathcal{K}_{min}(C_{\overline{g}}f_{1}^{M})\supseteq\mathcal{K}_{min}(C_{\overline{g}}f_{2}^{M}).

It is easy to see that, if
f_{1}^{M}
is outer (
I_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
) then there exists no maximal function
f^{M}
in
\ker T_{g}
such that
f^{M}\prec f_{1}^{M}
and, if
f_{2}^{M}=I_{2}O_{2}
with
O_{2}
square-rigid, then there exists no maximal function
f^{M}
such that
f_{2}^{M}\prec f^{M}
.

Now we consider the question of obtaining, from a given maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
, other maximal functions related to it by the partial order (
4.2
).
In what follows we assume that
f^{M}
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
, with inner-outer factorisation
f^{M}=IO
.

Proposition 4.42
.

For every inner function
\alpha
such that
\alpha\prec I
, there exists a maximal function
f_{\alpha}^{M}
in
\ker T_{g}
, with inner-outer factorisation
f_{\alpha}^{M}=\alpha\mathcal{O}
, where
\mathcal{O}\in H^{2}_{+}
is outer, given by

\mathcal{O}=O(\lambda+I\overline{\alpha}),\text{ with }\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|=1.

(4.3)

We have then
f_{\alpha}^{M}\prec f^{M}
and
\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathcal{O}}}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\alpha\overline{I}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}\supsetneq\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O}}{O}}
.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\lambda|=1
,
\lambda+I\overline{\alpha}
is outer in
H^{\infty}
, so
\mathcal{O}
is outer and

\displaystyle C_{\overline{g}}(\alpha\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=C_{\overline{g}}\left(\alpha O\left(\lambda+I\overline{\alpha}\right)\right)=\overline{gz}\overline{O}\left(\overline{I+\lambda\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(zI\frac{O}{\overline{O}}\right)\overline{z}\overline{O}\left(\overline{I+\lambda\alpha}\right)=\overline{\lambda}O\left(\lambda+I\overline{\alpha}\right)

where the right-hand side represents an outer function in
H^{2}_{+}
.
Thus, by Theorem
4.33
,
f_{\alpha}^{M}=\alpha O
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
such that
f_{\alpha}^{M}\prec f^{M}
and the last inclusion follows from the equality

\frac{\overline{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathcal{O}}=\frac{\overline{O}(\overline{\lambda}-\overline{I}\alpha)}{O(\lambda-I\overline{\alpha})}=\frac{\overline{O}}{O}\overline{\lambda}\overline{I}\alpha.

∎

Taking
\alpha=1
in Proposition
4.42
, we see that

\mathcal{O}^{M}=O(\lambda+I)

(4.4)

is an outer maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\lambda|=1
.
Thus we get the following

Corollary 4.43
.

Every nontrivial Toeplitz kernel has a maximal function
O^{M}
which is outer and we can write

\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{O^{M}}}{O^{M}}}.

(4.5)

Remark 4.44
.

Hayashi showed in
[
16
, Lemma 5]
that, for every Toeplitz operator
T_{g}
with non-zero kernel, there exists an outer function
h\in H^{2}_{+}
, such that
\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{\overline{h}/h}
.
If we write
z
as a quotient of an outer function by its complex conjugate,
z=(z-\eta)/(\overline{z}-\overline{\eta})
, with
\eta\in\mathbb{T}
, we see that Corollary
4.43
also provides a representation for
\ker T_{g}
in the form
\ker T_{\overline{h}/h}
where
h
, which in general does not belong to the Toeplitz kernel, can be obtained from any maximal function
f^{M}
in a simple way.
From (
4.5
) we can write, for instance,
\ker T_{g}=\ker T_{\overline{h}/h}
with
h=(z+1)(C_{\overline{g}}f^{M}+f^{M})
.

As a consequence of the previous results we have the following.

Corollary 4.45
.

For any maximal function
f^{M}
in
\ker T_{g}
there exist outer functions
O_{1},O_{2}\in\ker T_{g}
, with
O_{2}
square-rigid, such that

O_{1}=C_{\overline{g}}O_{1}\preceq f^{M}\preceq C_{\overline{g}}O_{2}.

From Theorem
4.33
and Corollary
4.43
we see moreover that there are elements of
\ker T_{g}
on which the conjugation
C_{\overline{g}}
acts as the identity or a constant multiple of the identity, such as the outer maximal functions in
\ker T_{g}
.
For those functions we have
C_{\overline{g}}f=\lambda f
,
f\neq 0
, where we must of course have
|\lambda|=1
.
If
C_{\overline{g}}f=\lambda f
, writing
\lambda=a/\overline{a}
with
a\in\mathbb{C}
we have that
C_{\overline{g}}(af)=af
, so
af
is invariant under
C_{\overline{g}}
.
One may then ask which other elements of
\ker T_{g}
are eigenfunctions for some eigenvalue
\lambda
.
The next proposition describes all such functions in terms of maximal functions.

Theorem 4.46
.

Let
f\in\ker T_{g}
and let
f=\alpha O
be an inner-outer factorisation.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

C_{\overline{g}}f=\lambda f
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\lambda|=1
;

2.

\alpha f
is a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
,

3.

\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq\ker T_{g}
and
\ker T_{g}
is the minimal Toeplitz kernel containing
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
.

Proof.

(i)\iff(ii)

We have that

C_{\overline{g}}f=\lambda f\iff C_{\overline{g}}(\alpha O)=\lambda\alpha O\iff C_{\overline{g}}(\alpha^{2}O)=\lambda O

which, by Theorem
4.33
, is equivalent to
\alpha^{2}O
being a maximal function in
\ker T_{g}
.

(ii)\implies(iii)

Since
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq H^{2}_{+}
and
\alpha f\in\ker T_{g}
, by Theorem
2.7
we have that
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq\ker T_{g}
.
Moreover, if
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq\ker T_{h}
, for some
h\in L^{\infty}\backslash\{0\}
, then
\ker T_{h}\supseteq\mathcal{K}_{min}(\alpha f)=\ker T_{g}
, so
\ker T_{g}
is the minimal kernel containing
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
.

(iii)\implies(ii)
Let
(iii)
hold, then
\alpha f\in\ker T_{g}
.
If
\alpha f\in\ker T_{h}
for some
h\in L^{\infty}\backslash\{0\}
, then
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq\ker T_{h}
by Theorem
2.7
, since
f
is a maximal function in
\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
and
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)\subseteq L^{2}
.
Therefore,
\ker T_{g}\subseteq\ker T_{h}
, as
\ker T_{g}
is the minimal Toeplitz kernel containing
\alpha\mathcal{K}_{min}(f)
.
This means that
\mathcal{K}_{min}(\alpha f)=\ker T_{g}
.
∎

Example 4.47
.

Two simple examples in
K_{z^{5}}
:
we see that
(1-z)^{4}
is an outer maximal function and an eigenvector of
C_{\overline{z}^{5}}
(for the eigenvalue 1) since
C_{\overline{z}^{5}}(1-z)^{4}=(1-z)^{4}
;
we also have that
z^{2}(1-z)^{2}
is a maximal function in
K_{z^{5}}
(since
C_{\overline{z}^{5}}z^{2}(1-z)^{2}=z^{2}-1
which is outer), so by Theorem
4.46
(ii) we have that
z(1-z^{2})
is an eigenvector of
C_{\overline{z}^{5}}
.

5
Application to model spaces

We now apply the previous results to model spaces, a particularly well known class of Toeplitz kernels, thus recovering and extending, using a maximal function perspective, some known properties of those spaces.

Let
g=\overline{\theta}
,
\ker T_{g}=K_{\theta}
where
\theta
is an inner function, and let
C_{\theta}
be the usual conjugation on
K_{\theta}
:
C_{\theta}f=\theta\overline{z}\overline{f}
, for every
f\in K_{\theta}
.
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, consider
k^{\theta}_{\lambda}
and
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
as in (
1.6
).
We have
k^{\theta}_{\lambda}
,
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in K_{\theta}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, with
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in H^{\infty}
and
k^{\theta}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
;

\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
is a maximal function in
K_{\theta}
(see Example
1.3
).
Since
C_{\theta}\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}
, an inner-outer factorization of
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
, by Proposition
4.35
, is

\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}=IO,\text{ where }I=\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}\text{ is inner and }O=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}.

(5.1)

Thus
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, is an example of a maximal function with square-rigid outer factor.
If
\theta(0)=0
, then
\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{0}=\overline{z}\theta
is an inner function which is maximal in
K_{\theta}
, illustrating Proposition
4.39
.

We can also obtain maximal functions in
K_{\theta}
which are outer, using Corollary
4.43
:

O^{M}=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}\left(\mu+\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}\right)=\mu k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda},\text{ with }\mu\in\mathbb{C},|\mu|=1.

These functions satisfy
C_{\theta}O^{M}=\overline{\mu}O^{M}
, they are eigenvectors of
C_{\theta}
for the eigenvalue
\overline{\mu}
.
If we take
\mu=1
then we get that
C_{\theta}(k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda})=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}
and we can write

K_{\theta}=\ker T_{\overline{z}\frac{\overline{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}}{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}}=\ker T_{\frac{(\overline{z}+1)(\overline{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}})}{(z+1)(k^{\theta}_{\lambda}+\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda})}}

(5.2)

(see Remark
4.44
).
Note that, if
\dim\ker T_{g}>1
, an outer maximal function cannot be square-rigid.
For example
1+\alpha
is an outer maximal function in
K_{\theta}=K_{z\alpha}
, where we assume that
\alpha\notin\mathbb{C}
, but it is not square-rigid since

\ker T_{\frac{1+\overline{\alpha}}{1+\alpha}}=\ker T_{\overline{\alpha}}=K_{\alpha}.

We now consider maximal function factorisations and their modified versions for the complex conjugate of inner functions.
From (
5.1
) and the modified maximal function factorisation (
3.20
) we have the representation

\theta=B_{\lambda_{1}}\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}\ \frac{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z)}{\overline{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}(1-\lambda_{1}\overline{z})},\quad\lambda,\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{D}.

This can also be seen as a bounded factorisation of the form

\theta=\frac{(\overline{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}})^{-1}}{1-\lambda_{1}\overline{z}}\ \theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}\ (k^{\theta}_{\lambda}(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z)),\ \text{ with }\theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}=B_{\lambda_{1}}\frac{\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}}{k^{\theta}_{\lambda}}.

As a consequence, we have:

Proposition 5.48
.

For all
\lambda,\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{D}
, we have that
\theta\sim\theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}
,
K_{\theta}\cong K_{\theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}}
and

K_{\theta}=k^{\theta}_{\lambda}(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z)K_{\theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}}.

(5.3)

For
\lambda_{1}=0
, (
5.3
) corresponds to Proposition 25 in
[
11
]
.

Let us now see how the multiplier between the two model spaces in (
5.3
) relates the norm of an element of
K_{\theta}
with its image in
K_{\theta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda}}
.
Let
f_{\theta}
be any element in
K_{\theta}
.
Using the equality

(k^{\theta}_{\lambda})^{-1}=(1-\overline{\lambda}z)\left(1+\frac{\overline{\theta(\lambda)}}{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}\theta\right),

we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\frac{(k^{\theta}_{\lambda})^{-1}}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\frac{1-\overline{\lambda}z}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}\left(1+\frac{\overline{\theta(\lambda)}}{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}\theta\right)f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\frac{1-\overline{\lambda}z}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2}+|\theta(\lambda)|^{2}\left|\left|\frac{(k^{\theta}_{\lambda})^{-1}}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2}+

\displaystyle\qquad+2\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\frac{1-\overline{\lambda}z}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}\frac{\overline{\theta(\lambda)}}{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}\theta f_{\theta},\frac{1-\overline{\lambda}z}{1-\overline{\lambda}_{1}z}f_{\theta}\right\rangle.

Choosing
\lambda_{1}=\lambda
, the inner product in the last term is zero.
Thus we get, in that case,

(1-|\theta(\lambda)|^{2})\left|\left|\frac{1}{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2}=\left|\left|f_{\theta}\right|\right|^{2},

meaning that the multiplier in (
5.3
), for
\lambda_{1}=\lambda
, preserves the norm, up to a constant factor depending only on
\theta(\lambda)
.
Using the notation
\theta_{\lambda}:=\theta_{\lambda,\lambda}
, we have then:

Proposition 5.49
.

Let
\theta
be an inner function and let

\theta_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}\tilde{k}^{\theta}_{\lambda}/k^{\theta}_{\lambda}=\frac{\theta-\theta(\lambda)}{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta}
.
Then
\theta\sim\theta_{\lambda}
,
K_{\theta}\cong K_{\theta_{\lambda}}
with

K_{\theta}=M_{\theta,\lambda}K_{\theta_{\lambda}}
where

M_{\theta,\lambda}=(1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta)/(\sqrt{1-|\theta(\lambda)|^{2}})

is an isometric multiplier from
K_{\theta_{\lambda}}
onto
K_{\theta}
.

Remark 5.50
.

We recover here a result by Crofoot in
[
11
, Theorem 10(a)]
.
The inverse of the operator defined from
K_{\theta_{\lambda}}
onto
K_{\theta}
by multiplication by
M_{\theta,\lambda}
, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, was called Crofoot’s transformation, and denoted
J_{\lambda}
, by Sarason in
[
27
]
, where it was proven to define an isometry from
K_{\theta}
onto
K_{\theta_{\lambda}}
in a different way.
For
\lambda=0
, in Proposition
5.49
, we obtain Hayashi’s representation of
K_{\theta}
,
K_{\theta}=M_{\theta,0}K_{\theta_{0}}
.

Remark 5.51
.

Note that other factorisations of an inner function
\theta
are possible, yielding different multipliers onto different model spaces, and one may be more convenient than the other, depending on the purpose.
For instance, if
\theta=B\in FBP
, using (
3.7
), one obtains that

K_{B}=r_{+}K_{z^{n}}.

(5.4)

where
n
is the number of zeroes of
B
and
r_{+}\in\mathcal{G}H^{\infty}
is a rational function.
This may be considered the simplest description of the model space
K_{B}
, since
K_{z^{n}}
consists of polynomials.
It follows from (
5.4
) and Hayashi’s representation in Theorem
1.4
that every non-zero finite dimensional Toeplitz kernel can be represented in the form
\ker T_{g}=wK_{z^{n}}
with
n=\dim\ker T_{g}
.
