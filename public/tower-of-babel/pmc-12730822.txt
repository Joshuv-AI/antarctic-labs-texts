Occupational risk of Parkinson’s disease and employment status following hospital admission: secondary analysis of ICOD-R case–control data in Japan
Authors: Shoko Nakazawa, Kota Fukai, Yuko Furuya, Kosuke Sakai, Keika Hoshi, Noriko Kojimahara, Akihiro Toyota, Masayuki Tatemichi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between occupation and Parkinson’s disease (PD) risk and whether patients with PD change occupation after onset.DesignA matched case–control study using secondary analysis of the Inpatient Clinico-Occupational Survey of the Rosai Hospital Group in Japan.SettingA nationwide multicentre inpatient dataset in Japan from 2005 to 2021.ParticipantsThe PD group comprised 2205 inpatients diagnosed with PD (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision code G20) and 10 436 matched controls without PD, matched for age, sex, year of admission and hospital.Primary outcome measureAssociations between the longest-held occupation—classified by industry (blue-collar, service, white-collar) and occupational class (blue-collar workers, service workers, professionals, managers)—and PD risk.Secondary outcome measureOccupations and industries with increased PD risk; occupational changes before and after diagnosis among participants aged <60 years; and associations of PD risk with chemical exposure, smoking status and sex.ResultsAfter adjustment for smoking and alcohol, professionals in the service (OR=2.01, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.25) and white-collar (OR=1.33, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.61) industries had higher PD risk than service workers. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is an age-related disease, and its incidence and prevalence are rapidly increasing owing to the ageing society. The number of patients with PD was 606 million in 2016, and the prevalence of PD has increased by approximately 1.4-fold since 1990.1 Known risk factors for PD are age ≥50 years, genetics, male sex, brain injury and exposure to environmental toxins.2 For instance, exposure to manganese3 could be an important risk factor for the onset of PD or parkinsonism and workers who are exposed to pesticides,4 radiation5 and organic solvents6 could be at risk of developing PD. However, these results were inconsistent between the study design and region. Frigerio et al7 reported an increased risk among health practitioners, while a decreased risk was observed in engineers and workers in the fields of construction, extractivism and metallurgical production. Dick et al8 showed a reduced risk of PD for transport, communication and processing occupations. Two studies in Korea and Japan involving the survey of the longest-held occupation showed an increased PD risk in agriculture, hunting and forestry.9 Tanaka et al10 showed a decreased risk of PD in professional or technical occupations. However, the sample sizes of these studies were relatively small, and the studies were based separately on industries and occupations.

To date, no study has comprehensively examined the association between the risk of PD and the involvement of the industry and occupation in which participants had been employed for the longest. Therefore, identifying the current and previous occupations of the patient before PD diagnosis is crucial for identifying risk factors for PD. In addition, as PD usually occurs in people aged <65 years, and those over 65 years are generally retired, clarifying whether the patient with PD at working age can maintain their same occupation is crucial.

Thus, in this study, we aimed to (1) investigate the association between the longest-held occupational class (industry plus occupation) and PD to identify the risk for PD and (2) determine whether patients with PD were able to continue working after the onset of PD by examining the employment status and changes in participants’ occupations after diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Study setting

In this nationwide, multicentre, case–control study we used data from the Inpatient Clinico-Occupational Database of Rosai Hospital Group (ICOD-R), a survey conducted by the Japan Organization of Occupational Health and Safety. Details of this survey have been previously described.11,14 Briefly, the ICOD-R investigated the clinical and occupational histories of all inpatients admitted to nationwide facilities belonging to the Rosai Hospital Group (250 000 admissions per year from 34 hospitals). We used the data of inpatients whose admissions were from April 2005 to March 2021. The clinical summary at each admission made by the medical doctors contained basic and medical information (including definitive diagnosis and surgical procedure name). Medical doctors could register up to seven definitive diagnoses, including primary diagnoses. These were coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10).15

Occupational histories were obtained through interviews carried out by trained registrars at each hospital. The registers interviewed inpatients or their families based on a questionnaire that explored the inpatients’ current and past three jobs, which were categorised by job type and industry, and age at the start and end of each job that was coded in accordance with the Japan Standard Industrial Classification (JSIC) and the Japan Standard Occupational Classification (JSOC) published by the Japanese Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications.16 17 The questionnaire also explored habits such as smoking, alcohol consumption, medical history, screen time and sedentary time.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research. The data used in this study were obtained from a retrospective analysis of an existing inpatient database, and thus, direct patient involvement was not feasible. However, the results of this study are intended to inform public health strategies and occupational health policies, ultimately benefiting patients and the general public.

Patients and controls

The participants were aged >20 years at the initial hospitalisation and had information on occupational history. The PD group consisted of participants diagnosed with PD based on the ICD-10 (code G20). The control group consisted of patients with primary diagnoses of diseases of the eye and ear (ICD-10: H00–H95), respiratory system (ICD-10: J00–J99), digestive system (ICD-10: K00–K95), skin and subcutaneous tissue (ICD-10: L00–L99) or genitourinary system (ICD-10: N00–N99). We excluded inpatients with a diagnosis possibly related to mental and behavioural disorders and diseases of the nervous system (ICD-10, codes F and G). We randomly selected five controls for each case, matched for sex (male or female), age (5-year strata), admission date (1-year strata) and admitting hospital (34 hospitals).

Occupational demographics and occupational changes among patients with PDWe examined occupational changes before and after diagnosis among patients diagnosed with PD at age <60 years. We identified the first hospital admission during which PD was diagnosed and, for those admitted multiple times thereafter, extracted the occupations with the maximum and minimum JSOC codes after diagnosis. Occupational change was defined as any change in occupation type, or transitions between employment and unemployment, based on whether the maximum and minimum JSOC codes differed. For example, a patient whose occupation before diagnosis was ‘general clerical worker’ and whose occupation after diagnosis was ‘homemaker’ was classified as ‘employed to unemployed.’ We then categorised occupations before and after diagnosis into four occupational classes—blue-collar workers, service workers, professionals and managers—plus ‘unemployed’.

Covariates

Sex (male or female), age (5-year strata), admission year and hospital of admission were controlled using an exact matching procedure. Smoking status (never, former or current) and alcohol consumption (never, former, current (1–3 times/week) or current (4–7 times/week)) were included as confounding variables in regression models.

Statistical analysis

We estimated the ORs and 95% CIs for PD risk and longest occupational class using conditional logistic regression applied to service workers in service industries as reference because the number of workers in the sector is sufficient and they have few opportunities for exposure to hazardous chemicals. ORs were calculated in model 1 after exact matching procedure for sex (male or female), age (5-year strata), admission date (1-year strata), and admitting hospital (34 hospitals). Alcohol consumption (never, former, current (1–3 times/week) or current (4–7 times/week)) was adjusted in model 2. Additionally, smoking (never, former or current) was adjusted in model 3. This approach allowed us to observe how the inclusion of behavioural covariates influenced the association between occupation and PD risk, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the results.20 21 Information on smoking habit or alcohol consumption was missing for a small proportion of participants. We examined the missing data structure using multivariable logistic regression and confirmed that the patterns were consistent with a missing-at-random mechanism (online supplemental table S1). We conducted multiple imputations for missing data using the variables in the present study with the multiple imputation by chained equations method, as performed in previous studies.19 22 The imputation model included age (5-year strata), sex, admitting hospital, admission date, alcohol consumption, alcohol amount and drinking duration. Five imputed data sets were generated.

In addition, we extracted detailed occupational and industrial information from the high-risk categories which the analysis of association between PD and occupational class showed. The ORs and 95% CIs for PD risk of each hazardous chemical (organic solvent, lead, specific chemicals, dust and any of the above) were examined in the three models. We demographically described occupational changes before and after diagnosis.

In the secondary analysis, we stratified patients according to their smoking status (non-smoking/smoking). We also stratified the sex (male or female) because the incidence and prevalence of PD differ between sexes.2

Because multiple subgroup analyses were conducted, we controlled for the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.23 All p values obtained from logistic regression coefficients were adjusted with an FDR threshold of 0.05. All analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System Software V.9.4 (SAS Institute). To mitigate the risk of reidentification, results with fewer than 10 patients were not reported.

Discussion

Our case–control study showed an association between PD risk and professionals in the service and white-collar industries compared with service workers in the service industry. Among these two industry categories, professionals such as doctors, dentists, veterinarians and pharmacists in medical and other health services had a higher risk for PD than service workers in the service industry. At <60 years of age, 47.1% of patients with PD were continuously unemployed, 19.5% quit their occupation to become unemployed, and 29.9% kept working. In contrast, participants who handled chemicals (including organic solvents, lead, specific chemicals or dust) did not have a higher PD risk than those without chemical handling. In the analysis stratified by smoking status, non-smoking professionals in the service and white-collar industries had a higher PD risk compared with non-smoking service workers in the service industry, whereas professionals with current or former smoking did not have a significantly higher PD risk than service workers with smoking in the service industry. Similarly, blue-collar and service workers in the blue-collar industry who were current or former smokers had a lower PD risk than service workers with smoking in the service industry. In the sex-stratified analysis, no significant differences in PD risk were observed after FDR adjustment among either male or female workers compared with service workers in the service industry.

An association has been observed between the risk for PD and professional occupation,24 consistent with our results. Nalls et al25 investigated the association between intelligence and the risk of PD using genome-wide association; the authors found significant genetic correlations that individuals without a college education might be at less risk for PD. Frigerio et al7 found an association between PD risk and having 13–16 years of education (OR=2.1, 95% CI 1.1 to 4.1) and >16 years of education (OR=2.5, 95% CI 1.1 to 5.9). In Japan, the proportions of university graduates among non-manual and manual workers were 84.0% and 13.2%, respectively.26 Therefore, professionals who are non-manual workers are more likely to have higher educational levels. In the secondary analysis, PD risk was not observed in professionals who smoked when we calculated the ORs stratified by smoking status. A meta-analysis showed that the summarised risk estimate of PD in smokers was 0.57 (0.52 to 0.63).20 Bloem et al27 described that smokers might have higher dopamine levels, preventing smokers from producing parkinsonism.

Conversely, blue-collar workers—typically smokers—showed a decreased risk of PD compared with service workers with smoking. Blue-collar occupations included agricultural and manufacturing workers (online supplemental figure S1). Our results showed no association between PD risk and blue-collar workers such as agricultural workers (handling of pesticides, which is a risk factor for PD) and manufacturing workers. Several studies did not show increased PD risk for agriculture.7 8 28 Shrestha et al29 reported that increased PD risk was associated with terbufos, an organic phosphorus (OP), and the herbicide trifluralin, which is an organofluoride pesticide. A decreased risk for PD was associated with diazinon, which is a type of OP.29 We proposed that pesticides should be changed from OP to other types, such as neonicotinoids, or that organic farming should be adopted. In Japan, the use of insecticides and herbicides has been decreasing.30 Regarding metal exposure among manufacturing workers, two meta-analyses found no significant association with PD risk.31 32 In our study, 44.4% (4/9) of blue-collar workers with PD had resigned their jobs, which may reflect difficulties in continuing work after symptom onset. The absence of a significant association between chemical handling and PD risk may also be due to the small number of exposed participants. Further studies are required to clarify the association between hazardous chemicals and PD risk.

We showed that 19.5% lost their occupation. As the unemployment rate in Japan was 2.5% in 2024,33 that among patients with PD was markedly high. Schrag and Banks reported that 46% had stopped working after a disease duration of 5 years and 82% after 10 years in the UK.34 Armstrong et al35 reported that patients with PD who retired were more likely to be older, female, and have a lower income and a longer PD duration. Koerts et al36 showed that patients with PD retired 4–7 years earlier than did the general population and that 21% of working patients with PD wished for further work adjustments such as changes in the work schedule and type of work as well as the freedom to use more time to complete tasks. In addition, 31% preferred retiring because motor symptoms had an adverse effect on fit to work.36 Darweesh et al reported an association between PD prevalence and the RIASEC model;24 37 the authors hypothesised that patients with PD more frequently had chosen conventional occupations such as banking, tax expert and secretary jobs than artistic occupations such as music, stage directing, dancing and writing as most recent occupations (χ²=14.61, p=0.01). The OR (95% CI) of conventional occupations compared with those of other occupations was 1.26 (0.95 to 1.66).24 We showed that 29.9% of patients with PD could continue their occupation (table 4) and that a third of professionals quit the occupation. We suspected that the risk of PD was high among professionals because they could continue to work after the diagnosis.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study was the number of cases. This multicentre study was shown to be representative of workers in Japan.18 In addition, the information on industries and occupations was reliable. Because the duration of current and three previous jobs was recorded, we could evaluate the influence of occupational exposure. We also examined the transition in occupation when patients with PD were admitted multiple times. Another strength was the diagnostic accuracy recorded by the physicians at the time of discharge.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the participants and controls were inpatients, and a selection bias may have occurred because patients with PD are often admitted to hospital either for the treatment of severe symptoms or for investigations to determine whether they are suitable for surgery. However, this dataset was sufficiently large to calculate the ORs for PD risk. In addition, the patients’ survey indicated rates of 21.4 per 100 000 for all patients with PD and 15.3 per 100 000 (71%) for hospitalised patients with PD in 2023.38 Second, we did not have information on test results, such as MRI. Similarly, we did not know the severity of PD symptoms. To exclude other neurodegenerative diseases, MRI and other tests were performed for a definitive diagnosis. Third, recall bias and exposure misclassification may have occurred because occupational class and chemical use were self-reported. However, since these data were collected by trained interviewers using a structured questionnaire, the potential for bias was minimised. Fourth, because 67.7% of participants were men, sex imbalance could have influenced the results. We therefore conducted a secondary analysis stratified by sex to address this issue. Fifth, as we conducted multiple subgroup analyses, there was a risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons. To reduce this risk, we applied FDR correction and reported adjusted p-values for all main tables. Finally, this hospital-based case–control study may have limited external validity, as the participants were not sampled from the general population. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalising to broader settings.

Conclusions

This study examined the occupations and industry categories of patients with PD. Doctors, dentists, veterinarians and pharmacists had a high PD risk. In addition, approximately 20% of the patients with PD were forced to change their careers. Further studies are needed to clarify the association between occupation and PD risk.
