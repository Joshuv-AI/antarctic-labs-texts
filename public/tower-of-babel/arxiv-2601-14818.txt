Statistical Learning Theory for Distributional Classification
Christian Fiedler

Abstract
In supervised learning with distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup, relevant to applications like learning-based medical screening or causal learning, the inputs (which are probability distributions) are not accessible in the learning phase, but only samples thereof. This problem is particularly amenable to kernel-based learning methods, where the distributions or samples are first embedded into a Hilbert space, often using kernel mean embeddings (KMEs), and then a standard kernel method like Support Vector Machines (SVMs) is applied, using a kernel defined on the embedding Hilbert space. In this work, we contribute to the theoretical analysis of this latter approach, with a particular focus on classification with distributional inputs using SVMs. We establish a new oracle inequality and derive consistency and learning rate results. Furthermore, for SVMs using the hinge loss and Gaussian kernels, we formulate a novel variant of an established noise assumption from the binary classification literature, under which we can establish learning rates. Finally, some of our technical tools like a new feature space for Gaussian kernels on Hilbert spaces are of independent interest.

Introduction

In supervised learning, distributions can appear as inputs, and this scenario has been considered in many works, cf.
(
Muandet et al. 2012
)
and the references therein.
However, in some applications, the distributions acting as inputs are not directly accessible, but only samples thereof.
For example, in the context of artificial intelligence (AI) assisted medical diagnosis, one might want to train on past patient data a binary classifier acting on biomarkers in order to detect an illness or anomaly.
In practice, these biomarkers might not be fully observable, but only samples from them are available, e.g., through repeated measurements.
In turn, we can model the biomarkers as distributions, which are only accessible through samples
(
Szabó et al. 2015
)
.
A concrete instance of this situation is training a classifier to detect atrial fibrillation from electrocardiogram measurements
(
Massiani et al. 2025
)
.
Another example can be found in statistical learning approaches to causal learning, where a classifier for the direction of causality between two random variables is desired, and such a causality classifier can be trained on samples from distributions with known causality structure
(
Lopez-Paz et al. 2015
)
.
The common feature of these examples is the
two-stage sampling
setup.
First, a distribution (the input) and some output is sampled from a population, and then samples from the distribution acting as an input are drawn, and only these samples (and the output) are available during the learning phase.
This setup has received particular attention in connection with kernel methods.
Commonly, the distributions and the samples thereof are first embedded into a Hilbert space,
and then a standard kernel method (now with inputs from a Hilbert space) is used on the transformed data set.
The learned hypothesis can then be used on distributional inputs by composing it with the embedding
(
Szabó et al. 2015
)
.
This strategy has received a lot of attention, especially in the context of regression problems, where a Hilbertian embedding is combined with kernel ridge regression (KRR), and a substantial body of theory is available, including learning rates
(
Szabó et al. 2015
;
Szabó et al. 2016
)
.
However, for some applications, other types of learning problems might be more appropriate, and a theoretical analysis should reflect this.
For instance, the two examples described above are most naturally framed as classification problems, and
(
Massiani et al. 2025
)
actually used a classification SVM instead of KRR, with excellent empirical results.

Providing an analysis tailored to the classification setting is particularly important with regards to the assumptions used therein.
As is well-known, in order to establish learning rates, one has to make distributional assumptions due to the
No Free Lunch-Theorem

(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 6)
.
For regression problems, typically a certain smoothness of the regression function is assumed, which is natural and reasonable in this setting.
However, a smoothness assumption might not be appropriate for classification problems, since it does not necessarily capture the intrinsic difficulty (or simplicity) of a classification problem.
Instead, margin and noise exponent assumptions are more appropriate, cf.
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2007
)
and
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 8)
for an overview and discussion.
In turn, this calls for an analysis that can take these considerations into account.
To the best of our knowledge, consistency and learning rate results for kernel-based distributional classification in the two-stage sampling setup under assumptions natural for the classification setting are still missing.
In this work, we close this gap by providing a thorough theoretical investigation of SVMs with distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup.

Related Work

After its introduction in
(
Póczos et al. 2013
)
, learning in the two-stage sampling setup has been primarily investigated in the context of kernel methods, starting with
(
Szabó et al. 2015
)
, which uses kernel mean embeddings (KMEs) for the Hilbertian embeddings of probability distributions.
Note that
(
Muandet et al. 2012
)
is an earlier work that uses this embedding approach, but not in the two-stage sampling setup.
While primarily KMEs have been used for the embeddings, other Hilbertian embeddings have been considered, like using sliced Wasserstein kernels
(
Meunier, Pontil, and Ciliberto 2022
)
, and a variety of such embeddings are available, with
(
Bonnier, Oberhauser, and Szabó 2023
)
as a recent example, and even learned embeddings
(
Kachaiev and Recanatesi 2024
)
.
The main focus has been on regression, with KRR as the kernel method used after the embedding, and this setting is by now well-understood
(
Szabó et al. 2016
;
Fang, Guo, and Zhou 2020
)
, different algorithmic approaches
(
Mücke 2021
)
, and robust variants
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
.
Despite its practical importance, distributional classification in the two-stage setup has received much less attention.
This setting is considered in
(
Lopez-Paz et al. 2015
)
, though this work focuses on empirical risk minimization.
In
(
Fiedler et al. 2024
)
, first steps towards a more general learning-theoretic analysis are taken, including oracle inequalities for SVMs in the two-stage setups.
In particular, it was recognized that most existing works rely on the integral operator technique
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
, which is limited to KRR (and related spectral regularization techniques for regression) and hence cannot be used to treat the classification setting, using for example SVMs with the hinge loss.
However, no consistency results or learning rates are provided, and the oracle inequalities have technical limitations, requiring significant regularity of the loss function, which excludes the hinge loss, or requiring a suitable discretization of the hypothesis space, which can be problematic due to the Hilbertian embedding.
Finally, we would like to stress that we focus on kernel-based approaches for learning with distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup, and other approaches have been considered in this context like deep learning, cf.
(
Liu and Zhou 2025
)
for an example and further pointers to the literature, and we refer to
(
Kachaiev and Recanatesi 2024
, Section C)
for an overview.

Contributions

As a starting point, we prove a very general oracle inequality (Theorem
9
) for SVMs in the two-stage sampling setup.
In particular, in contrast to the results in
(
Fiedler et al. 2024
)
it can now handle the hinge loss without any discretization of the hypothesis space.
We then prove (universal) consistency for rather general loss functions, which includes classification with the hinge loss, both for generic Hilbertian embeddings (Proposition
11
) and kernel mean embeddings (Proposition
12
).
Furthermore, under a standard assumption we then establish learning rates for rather general loss functions (Theorem
14
), again with classification as a special case.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first such results for distributional classification with SVMs in the two-stage setup.
Finally, we investigate learning rates for distributional classification in the two-stage sampling setting with SVMs using the hinge loss and Gaussian kernels, under assumptions that are natural for classification problems.
For this, we introduce a variant of a well-known geometric noise exponent assumption from the theory of binary classification (Assumption
16
),
which in turn allows us to establish learning rates (Theorem
18
), without any explicit smoothness assumption as used in regression.
To the best of our knowledge, this is again the first such result.
Furthermore, for the proof Theorem
18
we develop a new feature space for Gaussian kernels on Hilbert spaces (Theorem
15
), which is of independent interest.
Due to space constraints, most of the proofs of our results have been placed in the supplementary material.

Setup

In this section, we formalize the precise setting we work with for the remainder of this manuscript.
We first describe the general two-stage learning setup,
then we introduce abstract Hilbertian embeddings, and finally we outline kernel mean embeddings as a concrete examle of suitable Hilbertian embeddings.

Two-stage sampling

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}})
be a measurable space, and
\kappa
a measurable and bounded kernel on
\mathcal{S}
with separable RKHS
H_{\kappa}
.
(i) The map

\Pi_{\kappa}:\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{S})\rightarrow H_{\kappa},\>\Pi_{\kappa}Q=\int\kappa(\cdot,s)\mathrm{d}Q(s)

(4)

is well-defined, and we call
\Pi_{\kappa}Q
the
kernel mean embedding (KME)
of
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{S})
w.r.t.
\kappa
.

(ii) Define
\hat{\Pi}_{\kappa}:\mathcal{S}^{\ast}\rightarrow H_{\kappa}
by

\hat{\Pi}_{\kappa}((s_{1},\ldots,s_{M}))=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\kappa(\cdot,s_{m}).

(5)

For all
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{S})
and
S\sim Q^{\otimes M}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have that

\|\hat{\Pi}_{\kappa}S-\Pi_{\kappa}Q\|_{\kappa}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\|\kappa\|_{\infty}^{2}}{M}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\|\kappa\|_{\infty}\ln(1/\delta)}{M}}

(6)

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

(iii) Let
(\mathcal{S},\tau_{\mathcal{S}})
be a separable topological space, consider
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{B}(\tau_{\mathcal{S}})
, and assume that
\kappa
is continuous, then
\Pi_{\kappa}
is
(\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{S}),\mathcal{B}(\tau_{w}))
-
(H_{\kappa},\mathcal{B}(H_{\kappa}))
-measurable.

4
Consistency and Learning Rates

We will now present our first main results.
Building on a rather general oracle inequality stated in Section
4.1
,
we establish (universal) consistency for SVMs in the two-stage sampling setup in Section
4.2
,
and then learning rates in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Oracle Inequality

As common in statistical learning theory, consistency and learning rates can be derived from an oracle inequality.
The following result will be our central tool for this task, and it is a two-stage sampling variant of
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Theorem 7.22)
.

Theorem 9
.

Consider the two-stage sampling setup outlined in Section
3
.
Let
k
be a kernel on
\mathcal{X}
that fulfills Assumption
7
,
let the Hilbertian embedding fulfill Assumptions
3
and
5
,
and consider a convex, locally Lipschitz-continuous loss
\ell
that can be clipped at
M\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Finally, assume that there exists
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\ell(y,t)\leq B\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},t\in[-M,M]

(7)

holds.
Then there exists a universal constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for all
N\geq 2
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\tau\geq 1
it holds with probability at least
1-4e^{-\tau}
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}(\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda})+\lambda\|f_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda}\|_{k}^{2}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{\ast}\leq 9A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda)

(8)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+9(\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{H_{k}\ast}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{\ast})+C|\ell|_{1,{M}}^{2}\|k\|_{\infty}\frac{\ln N}{N\lambda}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+300\frac{B\tau}{\sqrt{N}}+15\frac{\tau}{N}|\ell|_{1,{C_{\lambda}}}\|k\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda)}{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{3}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{\lambda}\left(B_{\Pi}(M^{(n)},e^{-\tau}/N)\right),

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}=\left(|\ell|_{1,{C_{\lambda}}}\sqrt{A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda)/\lambda}+|\ell|_{1,{M}}\sqrt{B/\lambda}\right)\alpha_{k}.

and

C_{\lambda}=\|k\|_{\infty}\sqrt{A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda)/\lambda}.

On a high level, for the proof we use continuity properties to go from the accessible data set
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}}
to the inaccessible first-stage sampling data set
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\Pi}
, on which existing results can be applied.
While this is the standard strategy for the two-stage sampling setup, going back to
(
Szabó et al. 2015
;
Lopez-Paz et al. 2015
)
and also used by
(
Fiedler et al. 2024
)
, we use a rather advanced oracle inequality for the first stage of sampling, which requires some work.
Similarly as in the proof of
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Theorem 7.22)
,
we first establish an oracle inequality for general approximate RERM schemes,
and then check that SVMs indeed fulfill the necessary assumptions for this class of learning methods.
A detailed proof is provided in the supplementary material.

4.2
Consistency

We now turn to consistency of SVMs in the two-stage sampling setup.
It is clear that for consistency to hold, the hypothesis space
H_{k}
has to be expressive enough.
This is formalized in the next assumption.

Assumption 10
.

It holds that
\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{H_{k}\ast}=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}
.

For simplicity, we consider from now on only globally Lipschitz-continuous loss functions, including in particular the hinge loss
\ell_{\mathrm{h}}
, which is of prime importance for classification.
We are now ready to state the following general consistency result for SVMs with distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup.

Proposition 11
.

Consider the situation of Theorem
9
.
Assume that the loss function
\ell
is globally
L_{\ell}
-Lipschitz continuous, and
assume that for a data set of size
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, for every data point,
M_{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
samples are drawn in the second stage of sampling, so
M^{(1)}=\ldots=M^{(N)}=M_{N}
for
N
samples.
If Assumption
10
holds,
and if
(\lambda_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
(M_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{+}
are sequences such that
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{N}=0
and

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(N)}{N\lambda_{N}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}\alpha_{k}(B_{\Pi}(M_{N},1/N))=0,

(9)

then

(\mathcal{S}^{M_{N}}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N}\ni\mathcal{D}^{(N)}\mapsto\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda_{N}}\circ\Pi

is an
\ell
-risk consistent learning method, so

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda_{N}}\circ\Pi)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}

in probability for
N\rightarrow\infty
, for all data-generating distributions
P
under Assumption
10
.

This result poses two conditions on
(\lambda_{N})_{N}
and
(M_{N})_{N}
.
The first one appears also in the usual statistical learning setup with only a single stage of sampling, cf. the discussion in
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Section 7.4)
.
The second condition arises through the two-stage sampling setup, which is a well-known effect in the case of distributional regression, cf.
(
Szabó et al. 2015
;
Szabó et al. 2016
)
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{f\text{ measurable}}\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(f\circ\Pi)=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{\ast},

and

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda}\circ\Pi)-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}\leq\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}(\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda})+\lambda\|\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda}\|_{k}^{2}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P_{\Pi}}}^{\ast}
.
By definition of
\ell
-risk consistency and using Assumption
10
, it is hence enough to ensure that for fixed
\tau\geq 1
, the righthand side in (
8
) converges to zero for
N\rightarrow\infty
.
Since
\lambda_{N}\rightarrow 0
, we have
A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda_{N})\rightarrow 0
, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Lemma 5.15)
.
Furthermore,
\frac{\ln(N)}{N\lambda_{N}}\rightarrow 0
implies
N\lambda_{N}\rightarrow\infty
, so
15\frac{\tau}{N}L_{\ell}\|k\|_{\infty}\sqrt{A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda_{N})/\lambda_{N}}=15\tau L_{\ell}\|k\|_{\infty}/\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda_{N})/(N\lambda_{N})}\rightarrow 0
.
Finally, the last condition in (
9
) ensures that also the remaining terms converge to zero.
Altogether, this shows that the righthand side in (
8
) indeed converges to zero, establishing consistency.
∎

For concreteness we now consider KMEs as Hilbertian embeddings and we assume Hölder-continuity of
\Phi_{k}
.
In this situation, we can achieve the following consistency result.

Proposition 12
.

Consider the situation of Theorem
9
and assume that the loss function
\ell
is globally
L_{\ell}
-Lipschitz continuous.
Assume that for a data set of size
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, for every data point,
M_{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
samples are drawn in the second stage of sampling, and that KMEs (from Proposition
8
) are used for the Hilbertian embedding.
Furthermore, assume that there exist
C_{k},\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\alpha_{k}(s)=C_{k}s^{\alpha}
.
If Assumption
10
holds,
and if
(\lambda_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
(M_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{+}
are sequences such that
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{N}=0
and

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(N)}{N\lambda_{N}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(N)^{\alpha}}{\lambda_{N}M_{N}^{\alpha}}=0,

(10)

then
(\mathcal{S}^{M_{N}}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N}\ni\mathcal{D}^{(N)}\mapsto\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{\hat{\Pi}_{\kappa}},\lambda_{N}}\circ\Pi_{\kappa}

is a
\ell
-risk consistent learning method for all data-generating distributions
P
under Assumption
10
.

This result follows as an immediate corollary from Proposition
11
, and we provide a detailed proof
in the supplementary material.

4.3
Learning Rates

As is well-known, learning rates can only be derived under distributional assumptions due to the
No Free Lunch Theorem
.
The form of the oracle inequality in
Theorem

9
shows that any distributional assumption must enter through the approximation error function.
The following is a standard assumption for this task, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 6)
and
(
Steinwart et al. 2009
)
.

Assumption 13
.

There exist constants
C_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\beta\in(0,1]
such that
A^{H_{k}}_{\ell,P_{\Pi}}(\lambda)\leq C_{\mathcal{A}}\lambda^{\beta}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

For concreteness, we use this to establish a learning rate in the case of KMEs for the Hilbertian embeddings.
Learning rates for other embeddings can be derived similarly, and
in the supplementary material we provide a corresponding result for generic Hilbertian embeddings.

Theorem 14
.

Consider the situation of Proposition
12
,
assume that
\alpha\in(0,2]
,
and let in addition Assumption
13
hold for
\Pi=\Pi_{\kappa}
from Proposition
8
.
If
(M_{N})_{N}
grows at least as
N^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}
, and
(\lambda_{N})_{N}
decays as
N^{-\frac{1}{\beta+1}}
, then a learning rate of
\ln(N)N^{-\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}
is achieved.

This result can be derived from
Theorem

9

using well-known elementary arguments.

5
Classfication With Gaussian Kernels and the Hinge Loss

We now turn to classification (
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
) using the hinge loss
\ell_{\mathrm{h}}
and the Gaussian kernel
k_{\gamma}
on
\mathcal{X}
, cf. Example
6
.
The theory in Section
4
covers this case already, however, the important Assumption
13
is in general difficult to interpret.
For binary classification, margin and noise exponent assumptions are more intuitive, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 8)
for an overview,
and our goal in this section is to realize this also for distributional classification in the two-stage sampling setup.
For this, we will first introduce a new feature space for Gaussian kernels on (subsets of) Hilbert spaces, which is of independent interest,
and then establish a bound on the approximation error function using a variant of an establish geometric margin condition.

5.1
A New Feature Space

In order to bound the approximation error function for the hinge loss and the Gaussian kernel, we need a convenient feature space.
For the Gaussian kernel on
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can use
L^{2}(X,\lambda_{X}^{(d)},\mathbb{R})
, where
\lambda_{X}^{(d)}
is the Lebesgue measure on
X
, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Section 4.4)
.
However, since we consider the Gaussian kernel on
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is in general infinite-dimensional, we do not have the Lebesgue measure available anymore.
Instead, as common in infinite-dimensional analysis
(
Da Prato 2006
)
, we use the Gaussian measure on a Hilbert space instead.
The next result describes how exactly this can be used to build a feature space-feature map pair for the Gaussian kernel on a separable Hilbert space.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space,
\emptyset\not=X\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, and
k_{\gamma}
the Gaussian kernel on
X
with length scale
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
For all
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
,
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is a (real) feature space of
k_{\gamma}
,

\Phi_{Q}:X\rightarrow L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
defined by

\Phi_{Q}(x)=\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{x}(\cdot))

(11)

is a corresponding feature map,
and the canonical surjection
V_{Q}:L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})\rightarrow H_{\gamma}
is given by

(V_{Q}g)(x)=\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)g(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q).

(12)

We provide a detailed proof of this result in the supplementary material.

5.2
Learning Rates

In order to bound the approximation error function for the hinge loss, we follow the high level strategy from
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2007
)
.
First, we need some preliminaries.
We consider a distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and recall that
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
(later on,
P_{\Pi}
will play the role of this
P
).
Let
\eta:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a version of the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)\sim P}[Y=1\mid X=x]
,
and define

X_{1}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid\eta(x)>\frac{1}{2}\}\quad X_{-1}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid\eta(x)<\frac{1}{2}\}

and

\Delta(x)=\begin{cases}d_{\mathcal{H}}(x,X_{1})&\text{if }x\in X_{-1}\\
d_{\mathcal{H}}(x,X_{-1})&\text{if }x\in X_{1}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
d_{\mathcal{H}}(x,A)=\inf_{x^{\prime}\in A}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
for
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.
Furthermore, define
f_{P}^{\ast}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-1,1]
as

f_{P}^{\ast}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in X_{1}\\
-1&\text{if }x\in X_{-1}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Then
f_{P}^{\ast}
is measurable and achieves the Bayes risk, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}(f_{P}^{\ast})=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}^{\ast}
.

We will now state the central assumption of this section.
It can be interpreted as a variant of the geometric noise exponent assumption from
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2007
)
, adapted to a Hilbert space setting.

Assumption 16
.

P
and
\eta
are such that there exist
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
and constants
C_{Q},\alpha_{Q},\bar{t}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
for all
0<t\leq\bar{t}_{Q}
it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}\left(1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)\leq C_{Q}t^{\alpha_{Q}}.

(13)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{t}\|y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid x,Q)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)\leq C_{Q}t^{\alpha_{Q}}

(14)

While rather technical, the preceding assumption has a clear intuitive interpretation, which we discuss
in the supplementary material.
We are now ready to use this assumption to derive a bound on the approximation error function for Gaussian kernels on separable Hilbert spaces.

Theorem 17
.

Let Assumption
16
hold, then for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
\gamma^{2}<\bar{t}_{Q}
, we have

A^{H_{\gamma}}_{\ell_{\mathrm{h}},P}(\lambda)\leq 2C_{Q}\gamma^{2\alpha_{Q}}+\lambda

(15)

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

The proof uses the strategy from
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2007
, Section 4)
, cf. also
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Section 8.2)
:
An explicit Bayes optimal classifier is embedded into the Gaussian RKHS (here via Theorem
15
), which is interpreted as a smoothing, and the resulting performance degradation, i.e., increase in risk, is bounded using a margin or noise assumption, here Assumption
16
.
A detailed proof is provided
in the supplementary material.

Finally, all of this can be combined to arrive at the following result on learning rates for distributional classification with hinge-loss SVMs with Gaussian kernels in the two-stage sampling setup.
For concreteness, we consider KMEs for the Hilbertian embeddings, but analogous results can be derived in a similar manner for other embeddings.

Theorem 18
.

Consider the situation of Proposition
12
with
\ell=\ell_{\mathrm{h}}
and the Gaussian kernel on
\mathcal{X}
,
and let in addition Assumption
16
hold for
P=P_{\Pi_{\kappa}}
.
If
(M_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{+}
grows at least as
N^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}
,

(\lambda_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
decays as
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
,

(\gamma_{N})_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}
decays as
N^{-\mu}
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
and Assumption
10
holds for all
H_{\gamma_{N}}
,
then
(\mathcal{S}^{M_{N}}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N}\ni\mathcal{D}^{(N)}\mapsto\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}^{(N)}_{\hat{\Pi}_{\kappa}},\lambda_{N}}^{H_{\gamma_{N}}}\circ\Pi_{\kappa}

achieves a learning rate of
N^{-\min\{2\mu\alpha_{Q},\frac{1}{2}\}}
.

This result follows directly from Theorems
9
and
17
.

6
Conclusion

We considered kernel-based statistical learning with distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup, for which we established consistency and learning rates for rather general loss functions, covering the important case of binary classification.
Furthermore, using a novel variant of an establish geometric margin exponent assumption, we were able to prove learning rates for distributional classification with SVMs using the hinge loss and Gaussian kernels.
In particular, we could establish a learning rate without relying on an explicit smoothness assumption as common in regression, since this can be inappropriate for a classification setting.
While we focused primarily on KMEs as Hilbertian embeddings, our results can be easily adapted to other embeddings.
Our work opens up a multitude of interesting directions.
First, by using a supremum bound in an oracle inequality like Theorem
9
, our rates can be further refined, as in the case of single-stage sampling, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 7)
.
Second, using appropriate discretizations, for example via entropy number estimates, is another avenue for refinement of our rates, which requires dealing with a delicate interplay of the embedding map, the (in general infinite-dimensional) embedding Hilbert space, and the kernel used in the SVM.
Third, a closer investigation of Assumption
16
and potential refinements is another interesting aspect for future work.
Finally, extensions to related learning problems like multiclass classification are another line of interesting future work.

Appendix A
Additional background

A.1
On surrogate losses, calibration, and assumptions for learning rates

In this section, we briefly review the concept of surrogate losses and calibration, and discuss some consequences for assumptions used to derive learning rates.

Consider the general setup of statistical learning theory outlined in Section
2
.
The learning task, or rather the task that is to be learned by supervised learning, is described by a
loss function

\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
If a hypothesis
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
achieves a small average loss (the risk)
\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(f)
, then it performs well on the task encoded by
\ell
.
For example, if we want to learn a binary classifier, i.e., a map that assigns a class to an object based on certain features
x\in\mathcal{X}
of the object, then this task is most naturally described by the zero-one loss
\ell_{\mathrm{c}}
, cf. Example
1
.
In this case, the risk is the probability of misclassification by the classifier.

However, the loss function should also allow an algorithmic implementation.
In the context of (regularized) empirical risk minimization, the loss determines (together with the hypothesis class and the regularizer) the properties of the resulting optimization problem, which should be amenable to efficient optimization algorithms.
From this perspective, the zero-one loss is particularly bad, since it is nonconvex and even non-continuous.
This motivates the use of a
surrogate loss

\tilde{\ell}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
in the (regularized) empirical risk minimization.
The idea is that
\tilde{\ell}
has better properties with regards to the final optimization problem, but still encodes the original task to be learned.
For classification, a typical choice of a surrogate loss is the hinge loss
\ell_{\mathrm{h}}
, cf. Example
2
, which can be interpreted as a continuous and convex relaxation of the zero-one loss.

But in which sense does a surrogate loss maintain the original task?
In statistical learning theory, this is described by (loss)
calibration
.
We give only a brief overview sufficient for the remainder of our discussion in this section, and refer to
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapters 2,3)
and
(
Bach 2024
, Chapter 4)
for more details.
The immediate goal of (empirical) risk minimization with the surrogate loss is the minimization of the risk (over the actual data-generating distribution)
\mathcal{R}_{{\tilde{\ell}},{P}}
, or equivalently minimizing the excess risk
\mathcal{R}_{{\tilde{\ell}},{P}}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}
.
To ensure that this leads to good performance relative to the actual task, encoded by
\ell
, we therefore need that a small excess risk w.r.t. the surrogate loss
\tilde{\ell}
implies a small risk w.r.t. the original loss
\ell
.
One way to formalize this in a quantitative way is the ensure the existence of a function
\Upsilon:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is increasing, continuous in 0, and
\Upsilon(0)=0
, such that

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}\leq\Upsilon\left(\mathcal{R}_{{\tilde{\ell}},{P}}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{{\tilde{\ell}},{P}}^{\ast}\right)\quad\forall f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable}

In words, if a hypothesis
f
(e.g., an SVM solution
f_{{\mathcal{D}},{\lambda}}^{H_{k}}
using
\tilde{\ell}
) achieves a small risk w.r.t.
\tilde{\ell}
,
then it also achieves a small risk w.r.t.
\ell
, where the conversion between the two types of risks is done by
\Upsilon
.
In particular, if we can show learning rates w.r.t.
\tilde{\ell}
, then
\Upsilon
allows us to derive learning rates for the original loss
\ell
.

For example, in case of the hinge loss we have for all measurable
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-1,1]
that

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}^{\ast}\leq\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}^{\ast},

cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Theorem 2.31)
.
This means that at least when restricting to hypotheses taking only values in
[-1,1]
(which is not really a restriction since
\ell_{\mathrm{h}}
can be clipped at 1),
the hinge loss is calibrated w.r.t. classification.
Note that this is a slightly imprecise description, and we refer to
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapters 3)
and
(
Bach 2024
, Chapter 4)
for precise statements.

Finally, we connect this to assumptions used to derive learning rates.
In least-squares regression, one uses the
squared loss

\ell_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\ell_{2}(y,y^{\prime})=(y-y^{\prime})^{2}
,
and in the context of SVMs (in the sense of regularized empirical risk minimization over RKHSs), this leads to kernel ridge regression (KRR).
Now, the squared loss is classification calibrated (cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Section 3.4)
and
(
Bach 2024
, Section 4.1)
for details),
and KRR is theoretically well-understood, also in the case of distributional inputs in the two-stage sampling setup, cf.
(
Szabó et al. 2016
;
Fang, Guo, and Zhou 2020
)
,
including learning rates.
Altogether, this means that we can use KRR in the two-stage sampling setup for classification, and we get even learning rates.
However, we argue that this does
not
mean that a dedicated theory for distributional classification is not necessary.
Let us discuss this in the general setting of a loss function
\ell
and a surrogate loss
\tilde{\ell}
, which is calibrated w.r.t.
\ell
.
As is well-known, to get learning rates we need distributional assumptions due to the No-Free-Lunch-Theorem, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 6)
.
To derive learning rates for the learning method using the surrogate loss
\tilde{\ell}
, we need assumptions tailored to the latter.
However, the overall goal is to solve the learning problem w.r.t. to original loss
\ell
, and
assumptions for the surrogate problem might not be appropriate for the original problem
.
For regression problems with the square loss
\ell_{2}
, one usually invokes smoothness assumptions (for the conditional expectation), and in this context this is a very natural type of assumption.
However, in general this might not be appropriate for classification problems, since the inherent difficult of a classification problem is in general not related to the smoothness of the conditional expectation (here, the class probabilities), but rather the geometry of the classification problem (e.g., whether two classes are appropriately separated).
In the context of classification, assumptions like margin or noise exponents are more appropriate instead of smoothness conditions, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapter 8)
for a discussion.
These considerations suggest to tailor a statistical analysis to the actual learning problem at hand, in the present situation mainly classification, and then invoke assumptions appropriate for the specific learning problem.
In this work, we first use a generic distributional assumption in Section
4
, cf. Assumption
13
, and then we replace it for the hinge loss and Gaussian kernels with an assumption tailored to the classification setting, cf. Assumption
16
.
In particular, this latter assumption does not impose any explicit smoothness, and allows an intuitive interpretation in the context of classification, cf. Section
E.3
.

A.2
Background on comparison functions

For the reader’s convenience, we recall some facts about the comparison function formalism as used in control theory, and we refer to
(
Kellett 2014
)
for a thorough overview of this topic.

The main idea of comparison functions is to use classes of functions that model certain behaviours of bounds.
Among the most important of these are class
\mathcal{K}
functions, defined as

\mathcal{K}=\{f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid f\text{ continuous, strictly increasing},f(0)=0\},

and class
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
, defined as

\mathcal{K}_{\infty}=\{f\in\mathcal{K}\mid\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}f(s)=\infty\},

modelling a generic upper bound or lower bound that depends on some scalar quantity, typically a Lipschitz constant or norm.
Similarly, class
\mathcal{L}
functions are defined as

\mathcal{L}=\{f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mid f\text{ continuous, strictly decreasing,}\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}f(s)=0\},

which abstracts a bound like
C\exp(-st)
,
C,s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, with
t
growing (for example, time).
Finally, class
\mathcal{KL}
functions are defined as

\mathcal{KL}=\{\beta:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mid\beta(\cdot,s)\in\mathcal{L}\,\forall s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\>\beta(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\},

and they abstract an upper bound like
(t,s)\mapsto Cs\exp(-ct)
, as appearing in the theory of dynamical systems.
In the present work, a bound as in Assumption
5
is typically also of this form.

Operations and relations on classes of comparison functions are defined pointwise, and these function classes enjoy a rich structure and calculus.
For example, for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\alpha_{1}+\lambda\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
,
and
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}
means
\alpha_{1}(s)\leq\alpha_{2}(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
For an overview of the calculus with comparison functions, we again refer to
(
Kellett 2014
)
and the many references therein.

Finally, we follow
(
Fiedler et al. 2024
)
and call
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in I}
,
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, a
nondecreasing family
if
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{K}
(or
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
) and
\alpha_{s}\leq\alpha_{t}
for
s\leq t
,
s,t\in I
.

Comparison functions are very common in modern nonlinear control, but are rarely used in the theory of machine learning so far.
One main advantage of this formalism is the possibility to quickly derive bounds of a simple, abstract form, and by replacing the various comparison functions with concrete bounds, one can recover specific, precise bounds.
For example, Assumption
7
specifies an abstract, quantitative form of continuity of the canonical feature map, and in Proposition
12
we specialize this to Hölder continuity to derive concrete conditions for consistency.
Similarly, in Theorem
14
we derive learning rates using concrete bounds (leading to a concrete learning rate), whereas in Theorem
24
we use abstract bounds described by comparison functions.

Appendix D
A new feature space for Gaussian kernels on Hilbert spaces

D.1
Technical background

For a measure space
(X,\mu)
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, and
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, we denote by
L^{p}(X,\mu;\mathbb{K})
the usual Lebesgue space of
\mu
-a.e. equivalence classes of
p
-integrable
\mathbb{K}
-valued functions.
We denote by
L^{p}_{\mathbb{R}}(X,\mu;\mathbb{C})
the real Banach space arising from the complex Banach space
L^{p}(X,\mu;\mathbb{C})
when restricting scalar multiplication to the reals.

We now recall a few basic facts related to probability measures on separable Hilbert spaces, following
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk 2002
;
Da Prato 2006
)
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space. Let
L(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of continuous linear maps
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
,
let
L_{1}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of trace-class linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
,
and for
T\in L_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, we denote by
\mathrm{tr}(T)
its trace.
Recall that since
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space, a bounded linear operator is trace-class if and only if it is nuclear.
Furthermore, we denote by
L^{+}(\mathcal{H})
the set of continuous linear operators that are self-adjoint (
\langle Tx,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle x,Ty\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
) and positive (
\langle x,Tx\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
),
and define
L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})=L_{1}(\mathcal{H})\cap L^{+}(\mathcal{H})
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
be a Borel probability measure.
If
\int_{\mathcal{H}}\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty
(as a Lebesgue integral), then the Bochner integral
m=\int_{\mathcal{H}}x\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\in\mathcal{H}
exists and is called the mean of
\mu
.
If
\int_{\mathcal{H}}\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty
, then there exists a bounded linear operator
Q
such that

\langle h_{1},Qh_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\langle x-m,h_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\langle x-m,h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\quad\forall h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}

holds. Moreover, we actually have
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
, and we call
Q
the covariance operator of
\mu
.

Finally, we introduce some facts about Gaussian measures on separable Hilbert spaces.
The following result provides existence and uniqueness of Gaussian measures using the Fourier transform, and it corresponds to
(
Da Prato 2006
, Theorem 1.12)
.

Proposition 26
.

For all
a\in\mathcal{H}
and
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
there exists exactly one Borel probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
with

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\langle h,x\rangle)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\exp(i\langle a,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle Qh,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}

(31)

This probability measure has mean
a
, covariance operator
Q
, and we denote it by
\mathcal{N}(a,Q)
.

D.2
The white noise mapping

We now recall the
white noise mapping
of a Gaussian measure, which is a map from
\mathcal{H}
into
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R})
.
Our exposition closely follows
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk 2002
, Section 1.2.4)
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
. If
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
, then
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space, a vector subspace
A\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is dense if and only if
A^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
Let
x_{0}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{H}
we then have

0=\langle x_{0},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{0},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

which implies that
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{0}=0
, which shows that

Qx_{0}=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{0}=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}0=0,

so
x_{0}\in\ker(Q)
, hence by assumption
x_{0}=0
.
Altogether, we get that
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}=\{0\}
, establishing the claim.
∎

Let
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
, then
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is called the kernel of
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
.
Assume from now on that
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
, so that
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is dense.
Furthermore, we also have
\ker(Q^{\frac{1}{2}})=\{0\}
(if
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x=0
, then
Qx=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}x=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}0=0
, so
\ker(Q^{\frac{1}{2}})\subseteq\ker(Q)=\{0\}
),
so
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
is also injective.
Since the inverse of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
exists on
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
, we can define a linear map

Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R}),\quad h\mapsto[\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}],

(32)

where
[f]
denotes the
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
-nullset equivalence class of a
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
-square integrable real-valued function
f
.
The linearity of this map is clear, and we have for all
h\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{H}}|[\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}|\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\leq\|Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)<\infty

so the range of this map consists of square-integrable function equivalence classes, i.e., its range lies indeed in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R})
.
Furthermore, for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\displaystyle\langle[\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1},\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}][\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{2},\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}[\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}][\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{2},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\langle x,Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\langle x,Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1},QQ^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

where we used
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk 2002
, Proposition 1.2.4, (1.2.6))
for the third equality.
This shows that this map is an isometry between
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R})
, so in particular, it is continuous.
Since
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, we can extend this map in a unique manner to a linear, continuous isometry on all of
\mathcal{H}
,
which we denote by
\mathcal{H}\ni h\mapsto W_{h}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{R})
.
This map is called the
white noise mapping
of the Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
.

D.3
Proof of
Theorem

15
(Feature Space for Gaussian Kernels on Hilbert spaces)

For the proof we need some intermediate results, which follow using slightly modified arguments from the proof of
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk 2002
, Proposition 1.2.7)
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space,
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
The map
\mathcal{H}\ni h\mapsto\exp(iW_{h}(\cdot))\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is well-defined and continuous.

Proof.

Recall that since
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
,
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
. We have

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\lambda Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,\lambda Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle{\overset{(1)}{=}}\exp(i\langle 0,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\lambda Q\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\right),\lambda\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle{\overset{(2)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\langle\lambda Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle{\overset{(3)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q),

where we used for (1) the fact that
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
is self-adjoint and
e^{0}=1
,
in (2) we used the characterization of the Gaussian measure (via the Fourier transform) from Proposition
26
,
and in (3) the definition of

(recall that
h\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
by assumption).
Note that since
|e^{is}|=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(\cdot))\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
.

Next, we show that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})\ni h\mapsto\exp(i\lambda W_{h})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is continuous.
Since
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is a vector space, and
h\mapsto W_{h}
is linear, it is enough to show this for
\lambda=1
.
Let
h_{1},h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
, then

\displaystyle\|\exp(iW_{h_{1}}(\cdot))-\exp(iW_{h_{2}}(\cdot))\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left|\exp(iW_{h_{1}}(x))-\exp(iW_{h_{2}}(x))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\exp(iW_{h_{1}}(x))-\exp(iW_{h_{2}}(x))\right)\overline{\left(\exp(iW_{h_{1}}(x))-\exp(iW_{h_{2}}(x))\right)}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(1)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}1-\exp(iW_{h_{1}}(x)-iW_{h_{2}}(x))-\exp(iW_{h_{2}}(x)-iW_{h_{1}}(x))+1\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(2)}{=}}2-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{1}-h_{2}),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{2}-h_{1}),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(3)}{=}}2-\exp(i\langle 0,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle Q\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{1}-h_{2})\right),\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{1}-h_{2})\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\exp(i\langle 0,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle Q\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{2}-h_{1})\right),\left(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{2}-h_{1})\right)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(4)}{=}}2-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|h_{2}-h_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=2-2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right),

In (1), we used the usual rules
e^{ix}\overline{e^{ix}}=|e^{ix}|^{2}=1

and
e^{x}e^{y}=e^{x+y}
,
and in (2) we used the linearity of the integral, the fact that
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
is a probability distribution, and the definition of
h\mapsto W_{h}
(recall that
h_{1},h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
).
For (3) we used again the characterization of the Gaussian distribution via the Fourier transform from Proposition
26
,
and in (4) we used

\langle Q(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h),(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h),(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h),Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

which holds for all
h\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
.
Observe now that for
h_{2}\rightarrow h_{1}
, the righthand side of the above equality chain converges to zero, showing the continuity of the map

Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})\ni h\mapsto\exp(iW_{h})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
,
and a fortiori of all the maps

Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})\ni h\mapsto\exp(i\lambda W_{h})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})

with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
The result now follows due to denseness of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
in
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space,
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
we have

\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q).

(33)

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
be arbitrary.
By denseness of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
in
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a sequence
(h_{n})_{n}\subseteq Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
such that
h_{n}\rightarrow h
, and we get

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)\right|{\overset{(1)}{\leq}}\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\left|\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(2)}{=}}\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\left|\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(3)}{\leq}}\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\right|+\int_{\mathcal{H}}|\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(x))-\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(x))|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\right|+\|\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(\cdot))-\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(\cdot))\|_{L^{1}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt{\overset{(4)}{\leq}}\left|\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\right|+\|\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(\cdot))-\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(\cdot))\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\rightarrow 0,

where we used the triangle inequality for (1),
for (2) we used the previously established fact that
\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|h_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{n}}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)
(since
h_{n}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
),
in (3) the triangle inequality was used again,
and in (4) we used the fact that the Gaussian distribution is a probability distribution.
Finally, the continuity of
h\mapsto\exp(i\lambda W_{h}(\cdot))
implies convergence above.
Altogether, this establishes the claim.
∎

After these preparations, we can now turn to feature spaces of Gaussian kernels on separable Hilbert spaces.
The following result is already new to the best of our knowledge.

Lemma 30
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space and
k_{\gamma}
the Gaussian kernel on
\mathcal{H}
with length scale
\gamma
.
A feature space for
k_{\gamma}
is given by
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
, and

\Phi_{Q}^{\mathbb{C}}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C}),\quad\Phi_{Q}^{\mathbb{C}}(x)=\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{x}(\cdot)).

(34)

is a corresponding feature map.

Note that although
k
is real-valued, the preceding results gives a complex Hilbert space as a feature space, cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Section 4.1)
for some more details on this issue.

Proof.

Recall that the Gaussian kernel on
\mathcal{H}
is given by

k_{\gamma}(h_{1},h_{2})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}}{\gamma^{2}}\right).

Let now
h_{1},h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
, and note that since
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
is a subvectorspace,
h_{1}-h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
.
Defining
\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}
for brevity, we have

\displaystyle k_{\gamma}(h_{1},h_{2})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}\right)^{2}\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle{\overset{(1)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{1}-h_{2}}(x))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle{\overset{(2)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}(h_{1}-h_{2}),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{1},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\exp(-i\lambda\langle Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{2},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{1}}(x))\overline{\exp(i\lambda W_{h_{2}}(x))}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(x\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=\langle\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{h_{1}}(\cdot)),\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{h_{2}}(\cdot))\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})},

where we used Lemma
29
for (1),
and the fact that
h_{1},h_{2}\in Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
and the explicit formula for the white noise mapping in (2).
Using the density of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{H})
in
\mathcal{H}
and the continuity of
h\mapsto\exp(iW_{h}(\cdot))
,
we can extend this to all of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e., for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
we have

k(x_{1},x_{2})=\langle\exp(i\lambda W_{x_{1}}(\cdot)),\exp(i\lambda W_{x_{2}}(\cdot))\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}.

This shows that indeed
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is a complex feature space of
k
, and
\Phi_{Q}^{\mathbb{C}}
a corresponding feature map.
∎

We are finally ready to introduce the new feature space for the Gaussian kernel on real separable Hilbert spaces,
and we start with the case that the kernel is defined on all of the input Hilbert space.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space and
k_{\gamma}
the Gaussian kernel on
\mathcal{H}
with length scale
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
For all
Q\in L_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker(Q)=\{0\}
,
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is a (real) feature space of
k_{\gamma}
,

\Phi_{Q}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
defined by

\Phi_{Q}(x)=\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{x}(\cdot))

(35)

is a corresponding feature map,
and the canonical surjection
V_{Q}:L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})\rightarrow H_{\gamma}
is given by

(V_{Q}g)(x)=\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)g(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q).

(36)

Proof.

Recall that
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is defined as the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space arising from
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
when restricting scalar multiplication to
\mathbb{R}
.
Furthermore, define for
f,g\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}=\Re\left(\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}\right).

(37)

Now,
(L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})})
is a real Hilbert space,
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is a complex feature space of
k_{\gamma}
with feature map
\Phi_{Q}^{\mathbb{C}}
according to Lemma
30
,
and so
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Lemma 4.4)
ensures that
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
is a real feature space of
k_{\gamma}
, and

\Phi_{Q}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C}),\quad\Phi_{Q}(x)=\exp(i\sqrt{2}/\gamma\cdot W_{x}(\cdot)).

(38)

is a corresponding feature map.
Furthermore, the canonical embedding

V_{Q}:L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})\rightarrow H_{\gamma}
is given by

\displaystyle(V_{Q}g)(x)

\displaystyle=\langle g,\Phi_{Q}(x)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle=\Re\langle g,\Phi_{Q}(x)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle=\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)g(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q),

cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Theorem 4.21)
.
∎

Theorem
15
now follows as an immediate corollary of the preceding result.

Proof of Theorem
15
.

Define the Gaussian kernel on all of
\mathcal{H}
and length scale
\gamma
by

k_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{H}}(h,h^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}}{\gamma^{2}}\right),

then
k_{\gamma}=k_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{H}}\lvert_{X\times X}
.
According to Proposition
31
,
L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})

\Phi_{Q}=\Phi_{Q}^{\mathcal{H}}\lvert_{X}
.

\displaystyle k_{\gamma}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=k_{\gamma}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{Q}^{\mathcal{H}}\lvert_{X}(x^{\prime}),\Phi_{Q}^{\mathcal{H}}\lvert_{X}(x)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{Q}(x^{\prime}),\Phi_{Q}(x)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})},

k_{\gamma}
,
with feature map
\Phi_{Q}
.

Since the feature map for the Gaussian kernel on

Appendix E

E.1
Proof of Theorem

(Bound on the approximation error function)

Defining the candidate hypothesis

Let
f_{P}^{\ast}
be the function defined in Section
5.2
that achieves the Bayes risk, so

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}(f_{P}^{\ast})=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}^{\ast},

and define for

\hat{g}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{y}(z)\right)f_{P}^{\ast}(y)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

(39)

Since the absolute value of the integrand is bounded by 1, and
\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)
is a probability distribution,
\hat{g}(z)
is well-defined.
Furthermore, Fubini’s theorem and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{H}}|\hat{g}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{H}}1\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle=1

\hat{g}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})
with
\|\hat{g}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{N}(0,Q);\mathbb{C})}^{2}\leq 1

Our candidate hypothesis will be
\hat{f}=V_{Q}\hat{g}\in H_{\gamma}

is the canonical surjection from
Theorem

15

For later use, we also record that we have for all

\displaystyle(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)

\displaystyle{\overset{(1)}{=}}\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\hat{g}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle{\overset{(2)}{=}}\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{y}(z)\right)f_{P}^{\ast}(y)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)

For (1) we used the definition of
V_{Q}
,
for (2) the choice of
\hat{f}
,
for (3) the linearity of the integral,

for (4) Fubini and the linearity of the integral again,
for (5) we used (
33

and finally that the integral is real-valued.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\Re\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\hat{g}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\hat{g}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{H}}\left|\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\right||\hat{g}(z)|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(z\mid 0,Q)

where we used in the last step that
\left|\exp\left(-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma}W_{x}(z)\right)\right|,|\hat{g}(z)|\leq 1

, and that
\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
is a probability distribution.

Bounding the pointwise risk

Let now
x\in X_{1}
, then

\displaystyle(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)

\displaystyle{\overset{(1)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)f_{P}^{\ast}(y)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle{\overset{(2)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)(f_{P}^{\ast}(y)+1)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)-\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

where we used the calculations above for (1),

the fact that

\displaystyle|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)-f_{P}^{\ast}(x)|

\displaystyle(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)

\displaystyle{\overset{(1)}{=}}\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)f_{P}^{\ast}(y)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

\displaystyle{\overset{(4)}{=}}-2\int_{X_{-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)+\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

where we used again the calculations from above for (1),
the linearity of the integral for (2),
the nonpositivity of the integrand of the first integral for (3),
the fact that
f_{P}^{\ast}\lvert_{X_{-1}}\equiv-1
for (4),
and finally the fact that
B_{\Delta(x)}(x)\subseteq X_{-1}
(since
x\in X_{-1}
by assumption) for (5).
We now get

\displaystyle|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)-f_{P}^{\ast}(x)|

\displaystyle=(V_{Q}\hat{f})(x)-(-1)

\displaystyle\leq 1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)+\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

Summarizing, for all
x\in X_{1}\cup X_{-1}
we have

|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)-f_{P}^{\ast}(x)|\leq 1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)+\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

Bounding the averaged excess risk

Note that for
x\in X\setminus\{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}\}
we have
2\eta(x)-1=0
, so using the above pointwise bounds in Zhang’s Theorem
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Theorem 2.31)
results in

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}(\hat{f})-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)-f_{P}^{\ast}(x)||2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}|(V_{Q}\hat{g})(x)-f_{P}^{\ast}(x)||2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}\left[1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)+\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}\left(1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\right)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\right)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{1}\cup X_{-1}}\left(1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)\right)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid x,Q)\right)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{Q}\gamma^{2\alpha_{Q}},

where we used Assumption
16
together with
\gamma^{2}\leq\bar{t}_{Q}
in the last inequality.

Bounding the approximation error function

By definition we have for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
that

\displaystyle A^{H_{k}}_{\ell_{\mathrm{h}},P}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P},{\lambda}}^{H_{k}\ast}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}^{H_{k}\ast}

\displaystyle=\left(\inf_{f\in H_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}(f)+\lambda\|f\|_{k}^{2}\right)-\inf_{f\in H_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}(\hat{f})+\lambda\|\hat{f}\|_{k}^{2}-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{h}}},{P}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=2C_{Q}\gamma^{2\alpha_{Q}}+\lambda,

establishing the claim.

Proof of Theorem

(Learning Rates for Gaussian Kernels and the Hinge Loss)

Combining Theorem

with Theorem

, we get that for all
\tau\geq 1
, with probability at least

,
\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}(\bar{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\Pi}},\lambda}\circ\Pi)-\mathcal{R}_{{\ell},{P}}^{\ast}

is upper bounded by

\displaystyle C_{1}\gamma^{2\beta_{Q}}+C_{2}\lambda+C_{3}N^{-1}\ln(N)\lambda^{-1}+C_{4}\tau N^{-1}\gamma^{\beta_{Q}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C_{5}\tau N^{-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\left(C_{6}\gamma^{\beta_{Q}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C_{7}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)M^{-\alpha/2}(1+\sqrt{\ln(N/e^{-\tau})})^{\alpha}+C_{8}\tau N^{-\frac{1}{2}}

for appropriate constants

(independent of

, and

, for a suitable constant

so we can find a constant

(independent of

E.3
Discussion of Assumption
16

Since Assumption
16
is novel and might look somewhat opaque, we provide a detailed discussion of its interpretation here.
Furthermore, we would like to stress that while the particular form of this assumption is new,
conceptually it is very similar to classic notations in the theory of binary classification.
In particular, one can interpet Assumption
16
as variant of the
geometric noise exponent
assumption
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2007
, Definition 2.3)
, adapted to the present setting of subsets of Hilbert spaces as input spaces.

First, we need some preliminary considerations. Recall that in the framework of statistical learning theory, the overall goal in binary classification is to find a hypothesis
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{c}},{P}}(f)=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,1\}}\ell_{c}(y,f(x))\mathrm{d}P(x,y)

is small, where
\ell_{\mathrm{c}}
is the zero-one-loss.
The best one can do is achieving the Bayes risk, and as is well-known,

\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}^{\ast}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\min\{\eta(x),1-\eta(x)\}\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x),

where
\eta:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a version of the conditional probability
P[Y=1\mid X=x]
,
cf.
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
, Chapters 2, 8)
.
Furthermore, defining

f_{P}^{\ast}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\eta(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\\
-1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

we have
\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{c}},{P}}(f_{P}^{\ast})=\mathcal{R}_{{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}},{P}}^{\ast}
,
so this particular hypothesis achieves the Bayes risk.
A short calculation shows that

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x}[f_{P}^{\ast}(x)\text{ is wrong}]=\min\{\eta(x),1-\eta(x)\},

so the risk of
f_{P}^{\ast}
(the Bayes risk) is just the probability of misclassification of a given input
x
, averaged over all inputs according to
P_{X}
.

If
\eta(x)=\{0,1\}
, then classification at
x
is essentially deterministic, i.e., the label
y
at
x
is perfectly predictable.
If
\eta(x)=\frac{1}{2}
, then the label
y
is completely random, and we cannot do better than just guessing, so in this case we have zero predictability of the label
y
at input
x
.

Finally, observe that

|2\eta(x)-1|=1-2\min\{\eta(x),1-\eta(x)\},

so we can interpret
|2\eta(x)-1|
as the
normalized predictability
of the label
y
at input
x
.
Indeed, for
\eta(x)=\frac{1}{2}
(we can only guess, no predictability), we have
|2\eta(x)-1|=1-2\cdot\frac{1}{2}=0
, and for
\eta(x)\in\{0,1\}
(deterministic case, perfect predictability) we have
|2\eta(x)-1|=1
.

Now we can turn to (
16
).
Define weighting terms for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
x\in\mathcal{H}
by

\phi_{t,Q}(x)=1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

(40)

It is reasonable to assume that
\phi_{t,Q}(x)\geq 0
for all
0<t<\bar{t}
(after all,
\exp(-s/t)
is integrated over a bounded set, so
t\searrow 0
forces this term towards zero).
Let us interpret this weighting term.
The term

\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)

is roughly zero, unless
y
is very close to
x
, where close is determined by
t
(a small
t
means that
y
has to be really close to
x
).
The term

\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

measures how much weighted Gaussian mass is contained in
B_{\Delta(x)}(x)
, where the weighting is provided by
\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)
.
We therefore have a double localization.
First, we only consider the
\Delta(x)
ball around
x
, and then we indirectly localize even more using the exponential term, with smaller
t
leading to stronger localization around
x
.
Note that two mechanisms make this term small. If
\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is very large, all the
y
considered inside the integral will have a large norm, hence the Gaussian mass will be very small (due to the fast tail decay of the Gaussian measure).
Similarly, if
x
is close to the decision boundary, so
\Delta(x)
is very small, the ball over which the integral is defined, is very small, and hence has small weighted Gaussian mass.
Altogether, the weighting term

\phi_{t,Q}(x)=1-2\int_{B_{\Delta(x)}(x)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q)

deems
x
important if it has a large norm
\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}
(where large is relative to
Q
),
or if
x
is close to the decision boundary.
Consider now the condition from the assumption above, which we can rewrite as

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\phi_{t,Q}(x)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)\leq C_{Q}t^{\alpha_{Q}}.

It states that the average predictability, weighted by
\phi_{t,Q}
, rapidly approaches 0 for
t\searrow 0
(i.e., if we increase the localization in the weighting).

We now turn to (
16
).
To interpret this condition, define the weighting factor

\psi_{t,Q}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{t}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

The
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{t}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)
is roughly zero for
y
far away from
x
, where far away is relative to
t
.
In particular, for a small
t
, even a moderately close
y
appears to be rather far away and makes the exponential term almost zero.
The weighting term

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{t}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}(y\mid 0,Q).

(41)

therefore measures how much Gaussian mass, reweighted by the exponential term above, is close to
x
.
Note that for very small
t
, this will be rather small (since the effective neighbourhood around
x
that is considered in the integral is very small),
and it will also be small for very large
\|x\|
(since then all
y
close to
x
also have large norm).

We can rewrite the condition from the assumption as

\int_{\mathcal{H}}\psi_{t,Q}(x)|2\eta(x)-1|\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x)\leq C_{Q}t^{\alpha_{Q}},

which means that the average predictability, reweighted by
\psi_{t,Q}(x)
, rapidly goes to zero for
t\searrow 0
.
Note that this is a very mild assumption, since as explained above, in general
\psi_{t,Q}(x)
goes to zero for
t\searrow 0
.
