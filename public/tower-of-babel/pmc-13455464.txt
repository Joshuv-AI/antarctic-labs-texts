Classification of maxillary sinus floor contours and mucosal thickening: a CBCT-based analysis
Authors: Hilal Peker Öztürk, Şükran Ayran, Selma Ece Karabıyıkoğlu, Buğra Şenel, Neşe Oral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe maxillary sinus is a pyramid-shaped cavity located within the facial skull. Comprehensive knowledge of its anatomy and anatomical variations is essential for surgical procedures in the posterior maxilla. Accurate radiological evaluation is therefore critical. This study aims to classify maxillary sinus floor morphology and assess the prevalence of maxillary sinus mucosal thickening in a Turkish subpopulation.MethodsCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images from 203 patients were analyzed. Sinus floor contours were classified as narrow, tapered, ovoid, square, or irregular based on the system described by Niu et al. The presence and degree of mucosal thickening were categorized according to Block’s classification as follows: <2 mm, 2–5 mm, > 5 mm below the ostium, or soft-tissue density extending above the ostium. Chi-square tests were used for statistical analyses.ResultsA total of 180 patients met the inclusion criteria, and maxillary sinus morphology was evaluated at 1,080 tooth sites. No significant association was found between gender and mucosal thickening. Having at least two teeth adjacent to the sinus was significantly associated with mucosal thickening on both the right and left sides. No correlation was observed between age and mucosal thickening. Sinus morphology was also assessed based on the presence or absence of teeth or implants. Tooth loss influenced sinus morphology at multiple molar sites, whereas second premolar regions on both sides were unaffected.ConclusionsPrecise classification and evaluation of maxillary sinus floor morphology are essential for improving surgical planning and reducing complications in dental and maxillofacial procedures, particularly implant placement.

Introduction

The maxillary sinus is a pyramid-shaped cavity located within the facial skull. Its base is adjacent to the lateral nasal wall, while the apex extends toward the zygomatic process [1]. The roof corresponds to the orbital floor, and the sinus floor is associated with the alveolar process [2]. The shape, size, and thickness of the sinus walls vary among individuals, and even between the right and left sinuses of the same person [3]. It is the largest of the paranasal sinuses and the first to develop [4, 5].

At birth, the volume of the maxillary sinus ranges between 6 and 8 mm³ and increases progressively with age. In females, development generally continues until the second decade of life, whereas in males it continues into the third decade. A gradual decrease in sinus dimensions has been reported in later years [6].

Tooth loss leads to resorption and remodeling of the corresponding alveolar bone, resulting in an atrophic edentulous ridge [7]. The vertical reduction in bone height, combined with progressive sinus pneumatization, causes further excavation of the alveolar process [8–10].

All surgical procedures performed in the posterior maxilla require a thorough understanding of maxillary sinus anatomy and its potential variations [11]. Although osseointegrated implants are widely used in the rehabilitation of functional dentition, implant placement in the posterior maxilla can be challenging due to the close relationship between the implant site and the maxillary sinus [7]. Therefore, prior to surgery, detailed radiological evaluation is essential to identify anatomical variations, assess bone quantity and quality, detect sinus pathology, and evaluate the periodontal status of adjacent teeth [12, 13]. Accurate assessment of the internal morphology of the sinus is critical for reducing complications and ensuring predictable clinical outcomes [14–16]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is frequently used for this purpose, as it provides high-resolution three-dimensional evaluation of anatomical structures while reducing surgical risks [15, 17, 18].

Odontogenic maxillary sinusitis accounts for approximately 8–29% of all sinusitis cases [19]. To ensure successful dental interventions and avoid medico-legal complications, patients with maxillary sinus pathology should undergo appropriate treatment prior to implant placement. Thus, radiological assessment is strongly recommended to detect and manage sinus inflammatory conditions [20]. Although numerous classifications exist for alveolar bone conditions in the maxilla, evaluation of the maxillary sinus mucosa, which plays a key role in inflammatory processes, is often overlooked [21–23]. CBCT enables detailed visualization of sinus opacities and provides valuable information on paranasal sinus disease [24].

A clear understanding of maxillary sinus morphology is crucial, as it guides clinicians in selecting appropriate surgical techniques, including sinus lift procedures. The aim of this study is to evaluate maxillary sinus floor morphology and the prevalence of mucosal thickening in a Turkish subpopulation using CBCT, thereby emphasizing the clinical importance of anatomical and pathological variations in the posterior maxillary region.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study, in which patients were not contacted in person or by phone, was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision: 2023/355). CBCT images of 203 patients obtained for various dental indications between January 2021 and December 2023 were evaluated. All scans were acquired using a 3D Accuitomo 170 unit (J. Morita Mfg. Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with exposure parameters of 80 kVp and 2.0 mA, a field of view (FOV) of 100 × 100 mm, and a voxel size of 0.125 mm. The images were assessed by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist with two years of experience.

Patients

Exclusion criteria included insufficient image quality (such as artifacts obscuring the area of interest, positioning errors, and low resolution), large pathological lesions, bone fractures, affected teeth, and cases in which patients were not in the permanent dentition period. Inclusion criteria required the absence of pathological lesions, no severe alveolar atrophy, absence of impacted teeth in the maxilla, and high-quality CBCT images. In total, 180 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study.

Image evaluation

Contours of the maxillary sinus floor and the presence and degree of mucosal thickening were assessed based on previous studies [21, 25]. Evaluations were performed at the second premolar, first molar, and second molar regions on both the right and left sides.

For CBCT assessment, a panoramic reconstruction was generated by drawing a curve along the first slice where the maxillary alveolar buccal and palatal cortical boundaries and tooth pulps were clearly visible. Cross-sectional images were subsequently evaluated at the second premolar, first molar, and second molar sites, with the cursor aligned through the mesiodistal midpoint of each tooth.

Sinus morphology classification

Sinus floor morphology was classified as narrow (morp-1), tapered (morp-2), ovoid (morp-3), square (morp-4), or irregular, following the system described by Niu et al. [21] (Fig. 1). The irregular category was divided into three subtypes: sinus floor in proximity to the tooth root (morp-5), irregular sinus floor not associated with the tooth root (morp-6), and sinus floor with septa or exostosis (morp-7).

Mucosal thickening was measured perpendicularly from the sinus floor to the highest point of the thickened mucosal lining on the relevant cross-sectional CBCT image.

Variables examined

The relationships between sinus morphology, mucosal thickening, gender, tooth presence or absence, and implant presence were evaluated for each examined tooth region.

Statistical analysis

All data were categorical in nature and did not follow a normal distribution. The relationships between sinus floor morphology, mucosal thickening, gender, tooth presence or absence, and implant presence were evaluated using Chi-square tests. For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while values between 0.05 and 0.10 were interpreted as indicating weak significance. Statistical evaluations were performed using standard statistical procedures suitable for categorical data.

A sensitivity power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for the Chi-square goodness-of-fit tests. For the primary contingency table analysis (df = 6), with a total sample size of 180 and an alpha level of 0.05, a power level of 0.80 corresponded to a minimum detectable effect size of W = 0.28, indicating that the study had adequate power to detect small-to-moderate effects.

Discussion

The internal anatomy of the maxillary sinus plays a critical role in oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures, particularly those involving the posterior maxilla [8, 26]. Preoperative imaging is therefore essential for identifying anatomic variations and pathological changes that may influence treatment planning. Three-dimensional imaging allows clinicians to visualize the sinus with improved accuracy, helping guide surgical approaches and enhancing the predictability of pre-prosthetic and implant-related procedures [27].

Previous studies have explored various aspects of maxillary sinus anatomy using CBCT. Christoloukas et al. evaluated the potential of maxillary sinus dimensions and volume (assessed solely through CBCT) as parameters for forensic identification and gender determination [28]. Zeng et al. developed a deep-learning screening model integrating object detection with a simplified classification method to identify maxillary sinus abnormalities on CBCT images [29]. Lee et al. measured maxillary sinus size in South Korean adults using 3D analysis after categorizing subjects according to facial index classifications [5]. Marin et al. examined sinus membrane perforation during sinus floor elevation procedures and reported that the highest perforation risk occurred in narrow-tapered contour types [30]. Although considerable research exists, only a limited number of studies provide classifications based on sinus contour morphology, highlighting the importance of further investigation.

Niu et al. categorized sinus contour types and reported that narrow-tapered and tapering morphologies are most common at the premolar and first molar sites, whereas tapering and ovoid types predominate at the second molar site. They emphasized that narrow-tapered sinuses require a modified lateral sinus floor elevation technique to avoid Schneiderian membrane perforation, whereas both lateral and transcrestal approaches may be suitable for tapering types. For square and irregular types, lateral approaches (often with wider or double windows) were recommended [21]. Other studies also highlight anatomical factors influencing perforation risk. Cho et al. showed that the angle between lateral and medial sinus walls in narrow-tapered types significantly affects perforation frequency [31], while Velloso et al. reported that sharper angles at the second premolar region increase perforation risk compared with the molar region [32].

In the present study, a different parameter was examined: the relationship between sinus contour type and the presence or absence of teeth or implants at the corresponding tooth sites. Sinus morphology was classified as narrow tapered, tapering, ovoid, square, or irregular, with irregular types further subdivided according to root proximity, irregular floor configuration, and the presence of septa or exostoses [21].

Our findings demonstrated that in the second premolar region, tooth or implant presence did not alter sinus morphology, with narrow-tapered contours being most common. This may reflect less pronounced sinus pneumatization adjacent to the second premolar, as these teeth are typically lost later than molars. In contrast, morphology was significantly influenced by tooth or implant status in the first and second molar regions. For example, in the right second molar area, sinus-floor–tooth-root contact (morp-5) was most common when the tooth was present, whereas tapering and narrow-tapered types predominated when the tooth was absent. Similar patterns were observed on the left side, and implant-bearing sites displayed a wider distribution of contour types, particularly in the second molar region.

Several studies have investigated maxillary sinus mucosal thickening, which is frequently associated with odontogenic or inflammatory conditions. Shetty et al. assessed the relationship between mucosal thickening and the accessory maxillary ostium using CBCT [33]. Zhang et al. examined how periodontal status of maxillary molars affects mucosal thickening [34]. Norihito et al. reported significant associations between sinus floor morphology, septa presence, and mucosal thickening [35]. Aksoy et al. found that mucosal thickening greater than 2 mm occurred in 58.5% of maxillary sinuses and was associated with periapical lesions [20]. Di Girolamo et al. further suggested that mucosal thickening < 2 mm is not pathological, that 2–5 mm may warrant observation, and that > 5 mm often reflects odontogenic or ventilation-related pathology requiring treatment [36].

In the present study, the relationship between mucosal thickening and demographic and dental factors was assessed. No association was found between mucosal thickening and gender or age. However, having at least two adjacent teeth was significantly associated with the presence of mucosal thickening on both sides. This suggests that the number of teeth adjacent to the sinus may influence sinus mucosal status, potentially through variations in periodontal or periapical health, even when overt pathology is not present.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study provide several practical considerations for implant planning in the posterior maxilla. The narrow tapered and tapering sinus contour types, which were most frequently observed, may offer more favorable conditions for transcrestal sinus floor elevation, whereas irregular sinus floor morphologies or sinus floors closely related to tooth roots may increase the risk of Schneiderian membrane perforation and therefore may require a lateral window approach with careful preoperative assessment. Additionally, the presence or absence of adjacent teeth influenced sinus floor morphology, suggesting that clinicians should consider the timing of tooth loss and sinus pneumatization patterns when determining implant length, position, and augmentation technique. Recognizing these contour patterns preoperatively may help minimize complications and improve treatment predictability.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the research was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with different anatomical or demographic characteristics. Second, all CBCT evaluations were performed by a single experienced oral and maxillofacial radiologist. Because repeated assessments or second-observer evaluations were not feasible, the possibility of evaluator subjectivity cannot be fully excluded. Nonetheless, all assessments were performed using predetermined and standardized criteria based on previous studies. Additionally, clinical correlation could not be assessed because no standardized clinical symptoms or sinonasal findings were available, which limits the ability to relate radiographic findings to clinical presentation. Finally, although the study included all eligible CBCT scans available during the specified period, the retrospective design inherently limits control over sample size. A sensitivity power analysis demonstrated that, with a total sample size of 180, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80, the minimum detectable effect size for the primary Chi-square analyses was W = 0.28. Therefore, while the study was adequately powered to detect small-to-moderate associations, very small effects may not have been captured and should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Accurate assessment of maxillary sinus floor morphology and mucosal thickening is essential for improving diagnostic precision and optimizing surgical outcomes in the posterior maxilla. This study highlights the prevalence of specific sinus contour types and the influence of tooth presence or absence on sinus morphology, particularly in the molar regions. Additionally, the findings demonstrate that having at least two adjacent teeth is significantly associated with mucosal thickening, whereas demographic factors such as age and gender show no relationship with mucosal changes.

Understanding these anatomical and pathological variations may assist clinicians in selecting appropriate surgical techniques, anticipating potential complications, and tailoring treatment plans (especially in implant dentistry and sinus augmentation procedures). Further studies incorporating clinical correlations and larger multicenter samples are warranted to strengthen the generalizability of these findings.
