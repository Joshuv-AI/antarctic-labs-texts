Constraining burdened PBHs with gravitational waves
and Óscar Zapata

Abstract
We investigate the implications of memory burden on the gravitational wave (GW) spectrum arising from the Hawking evaporation of light primordial black holes (PBHs). By considering both rotating (Kerr) and non-rotating (Schwarzschild) PBHs, we demonstrate that the overproduction of primordial GWs from burdened PBHs could impose stringent constraints on the parameters governing backreaction effects. These constraints, derived from Δ ​ N eff \Delta N_{\rm eff} measurements by Planck and prospective experiments such as CMB-S4 and CMB-HD, offer novel insights into the impact of memory burden on PBH dynamics.

Beyond the semiclassical regime

Let us first briefly discuss the necessary ingredients required to understand the effect of spin and memory burden on PBH dynamics. The mass of the PBH formed due to the gravitational collapse is closely related to the horizon size at the point of formation as
[
46
,
21
]

\displaystyle M_{\text{in}}=\frac{4}{3}\,\pi\,\gamma\,\left(\frac{1}{H\left(T_{\text{in}}\right)}\right)^{3}\,\rho_{\text{rad}}\left(T_{\text{in}}\right)\,,

(2.1)

where
H(T_{\text{in}})
corresponds to the Hubble rate during radiation domination at the time of PBH formation. Therefore, radiation temperature at the point of PBH formation

\displaystyle T_{\text{in}}=\left(\frac{1440\,\gamma^{2}}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{1/2}\,M_{P}\,.

(2.2)

Here,
\gamma
represents the efficiency factor, which defines what fraction of the total mass inside the Hubble radius collapses to form PBHs. Here
g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})
is the relativistic degrees of freedom associated with the thermal bath at the point of formation and
M_{P}=1/\sqrt{8\pi G}\simeq 2.4\times 10^{18}
GeV is the reduced Planck mass. For PBH formation during standard radiation domination
\gamma\simeq 0.2

[
96
]
.
The time of the PBH formation is then given by

\displaystyle t_{\rm in}=M_{\text{in}}/(8\pi\gamma M_{P}^{2})\,,

(2.3)

where we have considered
H(t)=1/(2t)
because of standard radiation domination. We parameterize the initial PBH abundance via a dimensionless parameter

\displaystyle\beta\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm BH}(t_{\rm in})}{\rho_{R}(t_{\rm in})}\,,

(2.4)

which corresponds to the ratio of the initial PBH energy density to the SM energy density at
the time of formation.

The Kerr black hole is axially symmetric but not spherically symmetric (that is rotationally
symmetric about one axis only, which is the angular-momentum axis), and is characterized by two parameters, namely, the PBH mass
M_{\text{BH}}
and the dimensionless spin parameter

\displaystyle a_{\star}=8\,\pi\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,J\,,

(2.5)

where
J
is the angular momentum and
a_{\star}\in[0,\,1]
, where
a_{\star}=0
corresponds to the non-spinning Schwarzschild black hole. In Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, the geometry of a Kerr black hole is described by the metric
[
97
]

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{\Delta}\,dr^{2}+\frac{2\,r_{g}\,r}{\rho^{2}}\,\left(dt-a_{\star}\,r_{g}\,\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}\,d\theta^{2}+\left(r^{2}+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}\right)\,\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\,,

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle\Delta=r^{2}-2\,r_{g}\,r+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\rho^{2}=r^{2}+a_{\star}^{2}\,r_{g}^{2}\,\cos^{2}\theta\,,

(2.7)

with
r_{g}=G\,M_{\text{BH}}
. The coordinates
r,\,\theta,\,\phi,\,t
are called Boyer–Lindquist coordinates. For a given
M_{\text{BH}}
, corresponding entropy
S
and horizon temperature
T_{\rm BH}
, for a spinning PBH is given by

\displaystyle T_{\rm BH}=\frac{2\,M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\,\frac{\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}\,,

\displaystyle S(M_{\text{BH}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{M_{P}}{T_{\rm BH}}\right)^{2}\,.

(2.8)

Note that this reduces to the temperature of the
Schwarzschild black hole for
a_{\star}=0
, and tends to 0 in the extremal limit
a_{\star}\to 1
. The Kerr black hole is also characterized by horizon angular velocity, that reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{\star}=\frac{4\pi\,a_{\star}}{1+\sqrt{1-a_{\star}^{2}}}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{M_{\text{BH}}}\,.

(2.9)

Once PBH is formed, it can evaporate by emitting Hawking radiation
[
1
,
2
]
. The mass and spin loss rate for PBH can be tracked via following set of equations as
[
98
]

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}=-64\,\pi^{2}\,\epsilon(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})\,\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{\star}}{dt}=-64\,\pi^{2}\,a_{\star}\,\left[\gamma(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})-2\,\epsilon(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})\right]\,\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{3}}\,,

(2.11)

where, for a given species
j
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})=\frac{g_{j}}{2\pi^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\,E\,\sum_{l=s_{i}}\,\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\frac{d^{2}N_{ilm}}{dp\,dt}\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})=\frac{g_{j}}{2\pi^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\,\sum_{l=s_{i}}\,\sum_{m=-l}^{l}m\,\frac{d^{2}N_{ilm}}{dp\,dt}\,,

(2.12)

with

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{ilm}}{dp\,dt}=\frac{\sigma_{s_{i}}^{lm}(M_{\text{BH}},\,p,\,a_{\star})}{e^{\chi_{i}}-(-1)^{2s_{i}}}\,\frac{p^{3}}{E_{i}(p)}\,.

(2.13)

Here
l,\,m
are
the total and axial angular momentum quantum numbers, respectively, and
\chi_{i}=(E_{i}-m\,\Omega_{\star})/T_{\rm BH}
. The quantity in Eq. (
2.13
) determines the number of
i
species (with internal degrees of freedom
g_{i}
and spin
s_{i}
) having momenta between
p
and
p+dp
, emitted by a spinning BH within the interval
t
and
t+dt
. Here
\sigma_{s_{i}}^{lm}
stands for the absorption cross-section (equivalently, the greybody factor), which describes the effects of the centrifugal and gravitational potential on the particle emission
[

,
2
,

,
100

Before the onset of memory burden effects, we can reliably assume that the early stage of Hawking evaporation is well described by purely semiclassical approximations. In that regime, for a non-spinning PBH, the time-evolution of PBH mass can be obtained using Eq. (
2.10
),

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}(t)=M_{\text{in}}[1-\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}(t-t_{\rm in})]^{1/3}\,,

(2.14)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}=3\,\epsilon\,M_{P}^{4}/M_{\text{in}}^{3}\quad\text{with}\quad\epsilon=\frac{27}{4}\frac{\pi g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})}{480}\,,

(2.15)

is the decay width associated with the PBH evaporation, and
g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})
encodes the degrees of freedom emitted due to PBH evaporation at a temperature
T_{\text{BH}}
. The analytical approximation is obtained assuming the geometric optics (GO) limit for the greybody factor
\sigma_{s_{i}}^{lm}(M_{\text{BH}},\,p)=27\pi\,G^{2}\,M_{\text{BH}}^{2}
. The lifetime

of the PBH reads

\displaystyle t_{\rm ev}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}}\simeq 2.5\times 10^{-28}\left(\frac{M_{\text{in}}}{1~\rm{g}}\right)^{3}~\rm{s}\,.

(2.16)

To incorporate the effect of memory burden, we consider that the semi-classical regime is valid until

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}=qM_{\text{in}}\,.

(2.17)

Since, because of evaporation, the PBH mass diminishes with time, the above relation indicates
q<1
. Beyond the semi-classical regime, the evolution of the PBH mass, as given by Eq. (
2.10
), modifies to
[
68

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}=-64\,\pi^{2}\,\frac{\epsilon(M_{\text{BH}},\,a_{\star})}{\left[S(M_{\text{BH}})\right]^{k}}\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}},

(2.18)

where
S(M_{\text{BH}})
is defined in Eq. (
2.8
). The exponent
k
simply decides the efficiency due to the backreaction effect, when the semiclassical approximation breaks down, and we consider it as a free parameter with
k\geq 0
. For a Schwarzschild PBH, this happens at a time
[
70
,
71
]

\displaystyle t_{q}=\frac{1-q^{3}}{\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}}\,.

(2.19)

Once the mass of the PBH reaches
q\,M_{\text{in}}
, at
t_{q}
, the quantum memory effect becomes non-negligible. Once the memory effect sets in, total evaporation time for the PBH becomes
t_{\rm ev}=t_{q}+1/\Gamma^{k}_{\rm BH}
, where, for a Schwarzschild PBH
[
70
,
71
]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{k}={2^{k}\,(3+2k)\,\epsilon}\,M_{P}\left(\frac{M_{P}}{qM_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3+2k}\,,

(2.20)

captures the second phase of evaporation.
From Eq. (
2.20
), we find,

\displaystyle t_{\rm ev}^{k}=\frac{1}{\Gamma^{k}_{\rm BH}}\simeq\frac{1}{2^{k}\,(3\,\epsilon)}\,\frac{1}{M_{P}}\,\left(\frac{q\,M_{\text{in}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{3}\,,

(2.21)

for
2k\ll 3
, implying, a larger
k
results in longer PBH lifetime. Finally, as the radiation energy density evolves as
\rho_{R}\propto a^{-4}
, while the PBH energy density scales as
a^{-3}
, similar to non-relativistic matter. Therefore, there exist a critical value
[

,
70

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\frac{1}{q}\,\sqrt{\frac{t_{\rm in}}{t_{\rm ev}}}\,,

(2.22)

above which it is possible that after a time the PBH energy density dominates over the radiation energy density, and a PBH dominated epoch starts until the evaporation completes. For a Schwarzschild PBH of mass 100 g,
\beta_{c}\simeq 4\times 10^{-8}
for
k=0.1

q=0.5

3
Gravitational wave from PBH evaporation

Once PBHs complete their evaporation, the particles they emit are typically thermalized. However, gravitons are a notable exception. Due to their extremely suppressed interactions, gravitons retain their original momentum distribution, meaning the spectrum of gravitational waves (GW) preserves crucial information about the PBHs even after they have evaporated. This preserved spectrum serves as a unique signature, potentially offering insights into the properties and existence of the PBHs. The GW signal for a monochromatic mass spectrum with
M_{\text{in}}\sim 1\,(10^{4})
g is expected to peak at frequencies of order
10^{13}\,(10^{15})
Hz. While there are currently no facilities that are capable of detecting such high-frequency GW, however there are future prospects that can potentially be sensitive to GHz-THz spectrum
[
106
,
107
,
108
]
. A generic characteristic of GW due to graviton production from PBH evaporation is that it is nearly a blackbody spectrum, but features more power in the lower frequency domain.

The fractional energy density in stochastic GW generated by the evaporation of PBHs at present epoch reads (see Appendix.
A
for derivation in case of Schwarzschild black hole)
[
93
,
109
]

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{GW}}(\omega_{0})=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}\,a_{\rm in}^{3}}{6\,\pi\,M_{P}^{2}\,H_{0}^{2}}\,n_{\rm BH}^{\rm in}\int_{a_{\rm in}}^{a_{\rm ev}}\,\frac{da}{a^{2}\,H}\,\mathcal{Q}(a_{\star}(t),\,\omega\,M_{\text{BH}}(t))\,,

(3.1)

where
\omega_{0}=2\pi\,f_{0}
stands for GW frequency and
H_{0}\simeq 100\,h
km/s/Mpc is the Hubble rate at the present epoch. The instantaneous graviton spectrum is related to
\mathcal{Q}
-factor as

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt\,d\omega}=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{Q}(a_{\star}(t),\,\omega\,M_{\text{BH}}(t))\,,

(3.2)

and the initial PBH number density,

\displaystyle n_{\rm BH}^{\rm in}=48\,\beta\,\,\pi^{2}\,\gamma^{2}\frac{M_{P}^{6}}{M_{\text{in}}^{3}}\,,

(3.3)

is obtained from Eq. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). The lower and upper limit of the integration in Eq. (
3.1
) corresponds to the scale factor at the time of PBH formation

\displaystyle a_{\rm in}=a_{\text{ev}}\,\sqrt{\frac{t_{\rm in}}{t_{\rm eq}}}\,\left(\frac{t_{\rm eq}}{t_{\rm ev}}\right)^{2/3}

(3.4)

and that at the time of PBH evaporation

\displaystyle a_{\text{ev}}=a_{\rm BBN}\,\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\rm BBN})}{g_{\star}(T_{\rm ev})}\right)^{1/3}\,\frac{T_{\rm BBN}}{T_{\rm ev}}\,,

(3.5)

respectively. Here,

\displaystyle t_{\rm eq}=\beta^{-2}\,t_{\rm in}\,,

(3.6)

corresponds to the time when the PBH and radiation energy density becomes equal to each other, resulting the onset of a matter-dominated Universe for
t_{\rm eq}<t<t_{\rm ev}
, during which
a(t)\propto t^{2/3}
. Prior to that, i.e., for
t_{i}<t<t_{\rm eq}
, the Universe is radiation dominated with
a(t)\propto t^{1/2}
. Further, we assume that the PBHs evaporate before the onset of BBN and
a_{\rm BBN}=a_{0}\,\left(g_{\star s}(T_{0})/g_{\star s}(T_{\rm BBN})\right)^{1/3}\,\left(T_{0}/T_{\rm BBN}\right)

T_{\rm BBN}\simeq 4

a^{-4}

(3.7)

where

,
\rho_{\nu}
, and

correspond to the photon, SM neutrino, and GW energy densities, respectively, with

\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm eff}=N_{\text{eff}}-N_{\text{eff}}^{\text{SM}}=\frac{8}{7}\,\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}}\,\left(\frac{\rho_{\text{GW}}(T)}{\rho_{\gamma}(T)}\right)\,.

(3.8)

Since

Results and discussions

We begin our discussion by demonstrating the impact of memory burden on the evolution of PBH and radiation energy densities in Fig.
2
, considering Schwarzschild and Kerr PBH. The time evolution of the PBH
(\rho_{\rm BH})
and SM radiation
(\rho_{R})
energy densities is governed by the Friedmann equations

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{\rm BH}}{dt}+3H\rho_{\rm BH}

\displaystyle=

5
Conclusion

When the mass of a black hole (BH) reaches a certain fraction
q
of its initial value, the backreaction can no longer be ignored, and it can potentially reduce the evaporation rate by the inverse power law of its entropy
S^{-k}
. From this point onward the system is affected by what is called the memory burden effect that significantly
enhances the BH lifetime. There is apparently, no as such theoretical constraint on
q
and
k
, apart from the fact that
k>0
and
q<1
. In this work we have shown that the primordial gravitational wave (GW), produced entirely from the evaporation of a burdened primordial black hole (PBH), can provide stringent bound on these parameters. This is attributed to the improved lifetime for PBHs beyond semi-classical regime, that results in an enhanced GW amplitude [cf. Fig.
3
] which overproduces relativistic degrees of freedom around the time of big bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), thus conflicting the
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
constraint. We explore both the case of non-spinning and spinning PBHs, where for the later scenario the probability of getting higher spin particles (for example, graviton) from PBH evaporation is also higher. We find, the bounds are very sensitive to typically the choice of
k
and
\beta
and stronger for heavier PBHs as they live longer [cf. Fig.
5
and
6
]. Our study shows, the bounds on the memory burden parameters are stronger for PBH evaporation during PBH domination, but they become comparatively weaker (but non-negligible) for PBH evaporation during radiation domination. Notably, Planck constraint on
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
has excluded any potential memory burden effect in case of Kerr PBHs with
a_{\star}>0.5
, for PBH domination [cf. right panel of Fig.
4
]. Future CMB observations from experiments, for instance, CMB-S4 or CMB-HD, shall further improve this bound, making the period of memory burden more precise.
