Out-of-pocket expenditure for cataract surgery in rural China: a cross-sectional survey combined with hospital administrative data
Authors: Junling Zhao, Jack Hennessy, Xiaochen Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCataract is the primary cause of blindness in China and has a low surgery uptake rate where financial factors are crucial determinants. We report updated data on the financial protection of patients with cataract surgery after the integration of urban and rural medical insurance.MethodsA population-based survey and hospital administrative data were matched to analyze surgical and total out of pocket (OOP) costs and their burden relative to income; the proportion of total surgical costs paid OOP; and the breakdown of total surgical costs. Multivariate regressions, including key interaction terms, identified socio-demographic factors associated with OOP cost and burden.ResultsAmong 348 cataract patients, the average surgical OOP cost incurred by patients after any reimbursements was RMB 2945 (USD 427) and total OOP costs averaged RMB 3442 (USD 499), accounting for 25.1% of annual household income. Total surgical costs averaged RMB 5,220 (USD 758), with 44.8% paid OOP and the remaining 55.2% covered by insurance. Material expenses made up 41.3% of the total surgical costs. Having supplementary health insurances and being registered as poverty-stricken households significantly reduced OOP expenditure and burden. However, supplementary insurance was associated with a significantly higher financial burden for lower-middle-income households, highlighting the vulnerability of the ‘near poor’.ConclusionsFinancial burden relative to income has decreased after the integration of medical insurance. However, challenges for the ‘near poor’ population remain. Therefore, not only expand coverage but also optimize insurance benefit design are crucial for enhancing financial protection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02732-8.Keywords: Cataract surgery, Financial protection, Health insurance reforms, Out-of-pocket costs, Universal health coverage (UHC)

Introduction

Cataracts are the leading cause of blindness worldwide [1]. In 2020, 17 million people were blind due to cataracts, projected to increase to 40 million by 2025 [2, 3]. Cataract-induced blindness and severe visual impairment impact quality of life, economic productivity, and societal participation [4–7]. The global burden of cataracts will continue to rise over the next 15 years due to aging populations [8]. China has the largest number of persons visually impaired by cataracts in the world [9], with cases projected to rise from 112 million in 2015 to 241 million by 2050 among people aged 45–89 [10].

Cataract surgery is cost-effective and successful in restoring vision [11–14], yet many patients in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remain untreated [15]. In 2015 China’s cataract surgical rate (CSR) was 1,782 per million population, lagging behind neighboring LMICs, particularly in rural areas [16]. Trials have shown that uptake of surgery is only 20–30% in rural China, even when patients have significant vision loss and surgery is offered free of charge [14].

Cost regularly appears as a major barrier to accessing cataract surgery, particularly in poor rural areas [17, 18]. Previous studies in China found patients faced large financial burden, and low financial protection, with surgery costs exceeding annual disposable income in rural areas [19]. As in other LMICs, high out-of-pocket (OOP) spending for cataract surgery imposes a significant economic burden, with some low-income individuals needing to borrow or sell assets to cover costs [20–22]. In 2017, the Chinese government merged the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NCMS) and the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) into the Urban and Rural Residents Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI) to expand financial protection in rural areas [23] with the goal to achieve universal health coverage by 2030 [24]. However, existing research on financial protection for cataract surgery in rural areas was largely based on data collected before this policy change [19, 25]. Additionally, most research on OOP healthcare costs of cataract surgeries within and outside China lacks disease-specific OOP cost data and rely on patient recall [26] or self-designed structured questionnaires [27], which face challenges of unverified and limited insurance information, and long recall periods for health utilization and expenditure data. Finally, existing studies have looked at cataract surgery costs and predictors but few investigated non-medical OOP costs and detailed decompositions of total surgical costs (e.g., material expenses, surgery fees, medicine expenses) limiting the ability of policymakers to more effectively target these large cost components and reduce patients’ OOP expenditures.

To address this research gap, we carried out a population-based household survey combined with hospital administrative data for individuals who underwent cataract surgeries in rural China. This study aims to use contemporary data to comprehensively understand the size, burden, distribution, and financial protection related to cataract surgery in rural China.

Methods

Study design

The design of this study is three-fold (Table 1). First, we conducted a household survey to estimate OOP costs including the surgical OOP costs, non-medical OOP costs, and total OOP costs. To assess the financial burden, we measured the surgical OOP costs, non-medical OOP costs, and total OOP costs as a percentage of household annual income using the mean percentage approach.29 Second, we matched the survey data with hospital administration data to estimate the amount of total surgical costs (the sum of surgical OOP costs and reimbursement for surgical cost). The distribution of total surgical costs by types of services fees (material expenses, surgery fees, medicine expenses, diagnostic and examination costs, treatment fees, accommodation, and nursing costs) were also calculated. Third, we used survey data of participant sociodemographic characteristics including place of residence, sex, age, education, occupation, and health insurance types to estimate the determinants of OOP costs and burden.

Setting and sampling

Data collection took place between April and June 2019 in Yunnan Province. Located in southwestern China, Yunnan province has a population of 47 million and a GDP per capita of 6,617 USD, ranked 23rd among China’s 34 provincial-level regions in 2019 [28]. A recent nationally-representative eye disease survey shows that Yunnan has one of the highest cataract-related severe vision impairment or blindness prevalence rates (7.8%), double the national average (3.9%), and the lowest cataract surgical coverage (CSC) at 43.4%, compared to the national average of 62.7% [29].

Two prefectures (Wenshan and Dali) were included in our sample. In each of the prefectures, three counties were selected: the prefectural central counties (Wenshan and Dali) and two counties (Yanshan and Qiubei) in Wenshan Prefecture and Binchuan and Nanjian (in Dali Prefecture). For the survey, we employed a multi-stage cluster random sampling method to ensure a representative sample. Initially, in each county, four townships were selected using simple random sampling. Within these townships, two clusters were defined at the village level. To refine our sample, villages were stratified based on economic conditions and their proximity to the township seat. From the stratified villages, a list of cataract patients was obtained from the village clinician. Five to ten cataract patients were randomly selected based on the size of each village. Eligibility criteria included patients diagnosed with cataracts who had undergone cataract surgery over the past 24 months. For collection of hospital administrative data, patients were identified both from those who completed the survey questionnaires and from records provided by local county hospitals.

Data collection

Questionnaire

Trained local medical professionals conducted face-to-face interviews to collect questionnaire information. Prior to the survey, we registered participation of all invited patients and recorded basic information to facilitate exclusion. For patients who were in very poor health or not on site, family member(s) or other caregivers helped with the interview; all other interviewees were the patients themselves. The questionnaire included four parts: (1) demographic and societal information (e.g., place of residence, sex, age, education, occupation, and health insurance types); (2) physical health status, including the number of chronic conditions, affected by illness pain or disability over the past 30 days; (3) cataract surgery-related diagnoses and expenditure information (including overall surgical expenditure and detailed non-medical expenditure including meals, transportation, and accommodation); and (4) household wealth, previous-year household income.

Hospital administrative data

Hospital records from four counties in Dali (Nanjian and Binchuan) and Wenshan (Qiubei and Yanshan) were matched with patient survey data using inpatient names and home addresses, and included information on total surgical costs, billing classifications, health insurance types, reimbursement levels, and surgical OOP costs.

Data triangulation and validation

Recognizing the potential for recall and self-reporting bias in survey data, a key strength of this study is its use of hospital administrative data for triangulation. We intentionally combined patient-reported expenditures with official hospital billing records to validate the financial information provided by the participants. Key variables, including total surgical costs, insurance reimbursement amounts, and final OOP costs, were cross-referenced between the two data sources. This triangulation process significantly enhances the methodological rigor and credibility of our cost estimates by mitigating the limitations associated with relying solely on patient recall.

Data analysis

Individual and household level characteristics were presented as counts and percentages for categorical variables and mean ± SD for continuous variables. OOP costs, distribution, and burden were assessed across individual and household levels. Group comparisons were conducted using the Chi-square test for categorical data and the independent samples t-test for continuous data. To assess the financial burden, for each household, we first calculated the ratio of their total OOP costs to their annual income. The overall financial burden was then presented as the mean of these individual percentages, a method known as the mean percentage approach [30]. All expenditure data were converted to the 2019 Chinese Yuan (CNY = 0.145 USD) by the year-specific healthcare consumer price index of China [28].

To ensure the stability of the regression estimates, we formally tested for multicollinearity among the predictor variables in the full model specification (Model 2). We calculated the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each variable. The results showed a mean VIF of 2.75 and a maximum VIF of 7.37, with all values falling well below the conventional threshold of 10 [31], indicating that multicollinearity did not pose a significant problem for our analysis.

Given that our data were collected from two distinct rural regions (Wenshan and Dali), we considered the possibility of regional clustering effects. To address this, we tested a hierarchical linear model (HLM) with a random intercept for region. We calculated the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) to quantify the proportion of variance in OOP costs attributable to regional differences. The ICC was found to be negligible across all four models (ICC < 0.001), indicating that nearly all the variance in OOP expenditure is explained by individual-level factors rather than regional-level clustering. Consequently, the more parsimonious OLS model was deemed both statistically robust and sufficient for this analysis. The full results of the HLM are provided in the Appendix for transparency (Appendix Table S2).

Data were analyzed using STATA (version 17). P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 348 cataract patients were enrolled in the study. Most were female (63.7%), Han Ethnic Group (68.0%), aged 70 years or older (58.6%), had primary school education or below (57.8%), and were currently married (72.0%). Comorbidities were common; 62.3% reported having at least one chronic condition other than cataract and 38.7% had experienced limitations in daily activities due to illness, pain, or disability in the past 30 days. More than two-thirds were not engaged in income-generating activities (68.1%). Almost all participants were covered by at least one health insurance scheme (341 participants, 98%). Among them, 324 participants (93.9%) were enrolled in one of the social health insurance schemes: URRBMI, UEBMI, or socialized medicine. In addition to social health insurance, fourteen participants (4.1%) were also covered by supplementary health insurance schemes, including the medical aid programme and/or catastrophic medical insurance. Additionally, 15.0% were from poverty-stricken households, and 21.5% had to borrow money or sold assets to pay for cataract surgery. Income distribution showed that 25.1% of households fell into the lowest income quartile.

The level and burden of OOP costs of cataract surgery

Comparing the two data sources of surgical OOP costs, we found that there was no significant difference between patient survey data and hospital administrative data (online supplemental figure S1). We therefore report our results based on the data from the patient survey because it collected information of both surgical costs and non-medical costs, allowing us to calculate total OOP costs. The mean level of cataract-related costs is shown in Table 2. The average surgical OOP costs was RMB 2,945 (USD 427), and the average non-medical OOP costs and total OOP costs were RMB 489 (USD 72) and RMB 3,442 (USD 499), respectively. Surgical OOP costs and non-medical OOP costs accounted for 85.8% and 14.2% of the overall OOP expenditure, respectively. The level of OOP cost varied based on patients’ sociodemographic characteristics. Compared to those who lived in Dali region, patients from Wenshan region had significantly higher surgical OOP costs (USD 489 vs. USD 371) and total OOP costs (USD 557 vs. USD 447). The Chi-square test results indicate that individuals covered by supplementary health insurance schemes and those from poverty-stricken households had significantly lower expenditures in surgical OOP costs, non-medical costs, and total OOP costs. Individuals covered under supplementary health insurance only paid RMB 73 (USD 11) for surgical costs, plus RMB 229 (USD 33) for non-medical costs, totaling RMB 302 (USD 44). This amount is less than 5% of the total OOP costs among uninsured patients paying fully out-of-pocket. For poverty-stricken households, the average surgical OOP costs were two-thirds lower than those for non-poverty households (RMB 974 (USD 141) vs. RMB 3,201 (USD 464)). The average total OOP costs for poverty-stricken households were half of those for the non-poverty group (RMB 1,733 (USD 251) vs. RMB 3,698 (USD 537)). Furthermore, patients from higher-income quartiles were more likely to have higher OOP payments than those from lower-income quartiles.

Table 2 reports the burden of OOP costs as a percentage of income. On average, surgical OOP cost and total OOP cost accounts for one fifth (20.8%) and a quarter of patients’ annual income (25.1%). Means and distributions of total surgical costs

Table 3 reports the means of total surgical costs and the distribution by type of fees, based on hospital administrative data. Total surgical costs averaged RMB 5220 (USD 758), with 44.8% paying out of pocket and 55.2% covered by health insurances. OOP expenses, as a proportion of total surgical costs, were lower for those in the Dali region (35.4%), those with supplementary health insurances (19.5%), and poverty-stricken registered households (23.9%). These proportions were greater for those in Wenshan region (51.0%), those without social health insurance (85.3%) and non-poverty alleviation households (51.2%).

Table 3 also disaggregates total surgical costs by type of service fees. Material expenses (IOLs) accounted for the majority (41.3%) of total surgical costs, averaging RMB 5,220 (USD 758). Surgery fees accounted for 28.7% of the total surgical costs, followed by diagnostic and examination costs (17.6% of the total surgical cost). Accommodation and nursing costs, medicine expenses and treatment fees accounted for a small portion (7% or less) of total surgical costs. The service fee accounting for total surgical costs varied by region, and poverty status. In Dali region, material expenses (34.1%) and surgery fees (34.5%) accounted for a similar proportion, whereas in Wenshan region, the proportion of material expenses (46.7%) was higher than surgery fees (24.9%). The proportion of material expenses for cataract surgery in poverty-stricken registered households was lower than in non-poverty households (24.6% vs. 46.0%), yet the proportion of surgery fees were higher in poverty-stricken households than in non-poverty households (41.1% vs. 25.1%). This disparity is likely due to the different costs associated with the types of lenses chosen for surgery. Poverty-stricken households mostly purchased domestically produced rigid lenses, which are cheaper (around RMB 234 (USD 34)), whereas non-poverty households generally bought higher-quality and more expensive imported lenses (up to RMB 4,021 (USD 583)).

Multivariate analysis of socio-demographic determinants on OOP costs

Table 4 provides the results from multivariate ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions, which examine the socio-demographic determinants of four OOP cost and burden outcomes. The main effects models (Columns 1, 3, 5, and 7) reveal that those with supplementary health insurances(β= -2.279, p < 0.01 for log surgical OOP costs) and registered as poverty-stricken households(β ranging from − 0.936 to -1.617, p < 0.001) had lower OOP costs and burden compared to those without social health insurance and non-poverty households, indicating the positive impact of targeted subsidies for vulnerable groups. Surgical OOP costs and total OOP costs as a percentage of household income decreased with the household income level. Compared to the 1st quartile (lowest income), the 4th quartile (highest income) experienced 54.2% and 59.2% reductions in surgical and total OOP burden, respectively(p < 0.001 for both). To further explore these relationships, we introduced interaction terms into the models (Columns 2, 4, 6, and 8). A significant negative interaction was found between the highest income quartile and residence in the Dali region for both the log of surgical OOP costs (β = -1.128, p < 0.01) and the log of total OOP costs (β = -0.969, p < 0.01). This suggests that while higher income is generally protective, its mitigating effect on OOP expenditure is substantially stronger for patients in the Dali region compared to the Wenshan region. Furthermore, we observed significant interactions between income level and insurance coverage. For households in the 2nd income quartile, having supplementary insurance was associated with a significantly higher financial burden (β = 65.651, p < 0.001 for surgical OOP burden).

Discussion

Cataracts are increasingly prevalent among China’s ageing population, posing a significant public health challenge, particularly in rural areas [32]. This study is the first to combine patient-reported data and hospital administrative data to estimate size, burden, and distribution of OOP costs related to cataract surgery within the context of China’s evolving health financing system. Studies have found that the unification of urban and rural medical insurance has made progress towards improved financial risk protection [33]. However, no evidence has been reported on the associated financial protection for cataract surgeries. On average, our study found an average surgical OOP cost of RMB 2,945 (USD 427) and total average OOP cost of RMB 3,442 (USD 499), amounting to one fourth of patients’ annual household income. Although it is challenging to directly attribute the effect of unification of urban and rural medical insurance on the financial protection for accessing cataract surgeries due to the lack of comparable data in previous studies, we found suggestive evidence that burden relative to income has decreased after the integration of health insurance schemes. One 2010 study in rural area of Hubei Province found that the total surgical cost averaged at USD 536 [19], accounting for over 70% of the disposable annual income of rural residents in (USD 740) for that province that year [34]. In contrast, our results show that the average total surgical cost (USD 758) accounted for less than 50% of the disposable annual income of rural residents in Yunnan Province for the year data was collected.

In an international comparison, the out-of-pocket cost for cataract surgery for patients in China is at a moderate level. For example, in India, under the Aravind Eye Care System model, patient out-of-pocket expenses are exceptionally low, with the total cost per surgery averaging around $120 [35]. At other Indian hospitals, the direct and indirect costs for patients undergoing Manual Small Incision Cataract Surgery (MSICS) and phacoemulsification were approximately $12–20, a figure significantly lower than in China [36, 37]. In Bangladesh, patients’ willingness to pay is $93 for SICS and $126 for phacoemulsification, which corresponds to 12 and 16 days of household income, respectively [38]. Conversely, in developed countries such as the United States, the cost for a single-eye surgery is substantially higher. A cost-benefit analysis found that the direct medical cost per cataract surgery for patients was approximately $2,526 in 2018, with Medicare covering a significant portion, but patients still responsible for notable OOP expenses depending on their insurance coverage and the type of intraocular lens selected [39]. Globally, the cost of cataract surgery is closely linked to a nation’s economic level, health insurance coverage, and the surgical models employed. Low and middle-income countries have effectively reduced the financial burden on patients through innovative service models and government subsidies. Although China has made progress in health insurance integration, there is still room for further optimization when compared to the extremely low out-of-pocket costs seen in countries like India.

Our study also found that having supplementary health insurances and being poverty-stricken registered household had a significant effect in reducing OOP expenditure and burden. For those with supplementary health insurance, surgical OOP costs decreased by RMB 3,097 (USD 449) compared to those with only social health insurance. For those in poverty-stricken registered households, surgical OOP costs decreased by RMB 2,227 (USD 323) compared to those who were not. Supplementary health insurances mainly include the catastrophic medical insurance (also called critical illness insurance or Da Bing Yi Bao) and the medical aid programme (also called medical financial assistance or Yi Liao jiu Zhu). Catastrophic medical insurance was initially launched in 2012 and implemented nationally in 2015. It currently covers all patients with critical illness, providing extra financial protection if their OOP expenses are more than the average annual per capital disposable income in the local area. The medical aid programme, which was launched in 2003 in rural areas and expanded to urban areas in 2005, was designed to provide medical aid to the poorest people by paying their medical insurance premiums and reducing out-of-pocket expenses after receiving reimbursement from the basic social health insurance schemes and catastrophic medical insurance [24]. Our results are in line with data from the Sixth National Health Service Survey (NHSS, 2018) indicate that the implementation of catastrophic medical insurance has reduced the incidence of catastrophic health expenditures (CHE) [40]. Similarly, previous studies have found that poverty alleviation efforts in China may have a positive effect in reducing the financial burden associated with illness and health service utilization. For example, showing the share of OOP payments for the poor decreased from 41.9% in 2013 to 31.9% in 2018 [41].

Despite progress, challenges exist for improving the financial protection of a “near poor” population, referring to those low-income individuals not eligible for supplementary health insurance schemes and poverty alleviation programs. In our study, OOP expenditures account for a 20-fold higher proportion of income for lowest income quartile individuals (56.6%) than for highest income quartile individuals (2.8%). Indeed, more than one in five (21.5%) patients in our sample had to borrow or sell assets to finance their cataract surgeries. This proportion is comparable to an earlier study that collected data on financial protection of accessing cataract surgeries prior to the social health insurance scheme integration policy (21.0%) [42] The cost-sharing rate of catastrophic medical insurance is currently uniform across all economic statuses and the medical aid program is relatively small in scale and covers only a small proportion of patients needed [24]. In addition, many low-income Chinese households live just above the poverty line and are not within the scope of the Targeted Poverty Alleviation Campaign. These groups remain particularly vulnerable to financial hardship due to health spending [43].

Our analysis of interaction terms reveals that the determinants of this financial burden are not uniform but are instead highly context-dependent. For instance, the protective effect of high income against OOP costs was significantly stronger in the Dali region than in Wenshan. This suggests that regional health system characteristics-such as variations in local reimbursement ceilings or the density of healthcare providers-likely moderate the economic advantages of patients. Most notably, a paradoxical effect emerged: supplementary insurance was associated with a significantly higher financial burden for lower-middle-income households. This counterintuitive finding suggests a structural vulnerability. Unlike the ‘registered poor’ who benefit from comprehensive Medical Aid that often waives copayments, the ‘near poor’ occupy a precarious middle ground. They are not poor enough to qualify for full subsidies, yet they are not affluent enough to absorb the co-payments. Furthermore, the presence of basic insurance may inadvertently induce supply-side demand for premium services that are inadequately covered. For example, having insurance might signal an ‘ability to pay’ to providers, who then incentivize patients to choose imported intraocular lenses (IOLs) over domestic ones. Consequently, the ‘near poor’ are left exposed to significant costs that the insurance benefit design fails to cushion.

Recent research in China shows that the rural near-poor face a post-health expenditure poverty incidence 8.6 times higher than the non-poor, with health expenses disproportionately pushing them into poverty. Socioeconomic factors such as small household size, unemployment, poor health status, and chronic disease further increase their vulnerability. Policy recommendations include developing a multi-level medical security system, expanding medical assistance, and implementing data-driven monitoring to prevent relapse into poverty for the near-poor [44]. In South Korea, the near-poor—those ineligible for Medical Aid but not affluent enough for comprehensive National Health Insurance—incur higher out-of-pocket costs and face a greater risk of catastrophic health expenditures than both the poorest (who receive aid) and higher-income groups [45]. In the United States, near-poor Medicare beneficiaries face a “coverage cliff,” with substantially higher out-of-pocket costs and reduced care utilization compared to those just below the Medicaid eligibility threshold, underscoring the adverse effects of abrupt subsidy cutoffs [46]. To address this coverage gap, future policies must go beyond simply expanding coverage and instead adopt a multi-pronged strategy. First, it is crucial to expand the centrally financed medical assistance pool to establish a nationally uniform assistance scheme with tiered supports and setting an OOP maximum for different income brackets [47]. Second, it is important to provide clearer guidance on cost-effective treatment options—to mitigate perverse incentives.

Our study also found that material expenses (mainly IOLs) accounted for 41.3% of the total surgical costs. This finding highlights the importance of recent key measures to address rising healthcare costs including the elimination of drug mark-ups, adjustment of fee schedules, and a hospital payment methods reform in China [48] (online supplemental Table S2), particularly the national volume-based procurement (NVBP) policy, implemented since 2018—the year most of the patients in our sample accessed cataract surgeries. The NVBP policy, implemented since 2018, is a pivotal policy designed to directly address the high cost of medical consumables by leveraging the state’s collective purchasing power to negotiate substantial price reductions with manufacturers. By guaranteeing procurement volumes, the NVBP has resulted in average price cuts ranging from 25% to 96% for selected products [49, 50]. After the introduction of NVBP, the proportion of affordable drugs increased significantly for both rural and urban residents, and the mean number of days’ wages required to purchase essential medicines dropped markedly. For instance, the cost of IOLs decreased from RMB 2,697 in 2018 to RMB 2,350 in 2021 (online supplemental Figure S3). By streamlining distribution and ensuring timely payments, this centralized system has significantly improved access and affordability for vulnerable populations, offering a powerful strategy to alleviate the exact financial barriers identified in our study and serving as a model for other low- and middle-income countries.

In addition to procurement reforms, adjustments to hospital payment methods are crucial. Our findings indicate varying degrees of implementation of disease-specific payment methods (DRG/DIP) across Yunnan Province, which plays an important role in reducing the costs of cataract surgery and improving quality, with such methods currently limited to urban employee health insurance schemes. Therefore, it is important to continue promoting the expansion of DRG/DIP reform coverage areas. Meanwhile, promoting health insurance payment methods tailored to different diseases and service characteristics is essential, as is establishing a mechanism for timely adjustments based on actual operations and cost changes.

Strengths of the current study include its population-based nature and a combination of both patient-reported data and hospital administrative data, which allowed us to study the OOP costs, burden, distribution, and determinants of cataract surgery alongside potential impacts of health financing reforms. Additionally, using post-reform data, our study provides the latest evidence on the financial burden associated with cataract surgery following the integration of health insurance schemes in 2017. Unlike previous studies that focused on reimbursement rates and total expenses, our research details non-medical OOP costs and total surgical cost breakdowns (e.g., IOLs, surgery fees, medication), allowing policymakers to target specific cost components to reduce patient expenditures.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, our sampling strategy focused on a small sample size of patients from two specific prefectures within Yunnan province, limiting the broader applicability of findings to other rural regions in China and reducing overall representativeness. Second, the survey did not include indirect costs, such as lost earnings by patients and caregivers during medical care, which can constitute a significant financial burden. Lastly, using cross-sectional data to identify socio-demographic factors associated with OOP costs and their financial burden only allows for correlational examination and cannot imply causation. Nevertheless, this study provides a critical and timely snapshot of the OOP expenditure landscape immediately following a major national health financing reform, establishing an essential baseline that has been heretofore unavailable. This baseline is vital for informing both policymakers in the present and future longitudinal research aimed at tracking the long-term effects of the URRBMI policy. Future studies should focus on the use of panel data and dynamic analysis to better elucidate the impact of health insurance policies on OOP costs in China.

Conclusions

The study describes the financial burden of cataract surgery on patients in rural Yunnan. Our contemporary data analysis found that the burden relative to income has decreased after the integration of urban and rural medical insurance. However, we also found an example of “near poor” population in our setting where low-income individuals who are not eligible for supplementary health insurance schemes or poverty-stricken households programs still face high OOP burdens and poor financial risk protection. China has set a target to reduce the share of out-of-pocket health expenditure in total health expenditure to 25% by 2030 [47]. Our study emphasizes the importance of supplementary health insurances and targeted subsidies for vulnerable populations, optimize insurance benefit design to prevent inducing catastrophic expenditure, and targeted policies to reduce the cost of IOLs to enhance financial protection and achieve UHC, thereby improving access and affordability in rural areas of China.
