Vasculitis syndromes: the pathogenic roles of COVID-19 and related vaccinations
Authors: Saiedeh Razi-Soofiyani, Yalda Rahbar Saadat, Sepideh Zununi Vahed, Seied Hadi Saghaleini, Mohamadreza Jafari Nakhjavani, Sima Abediazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfection by SARS-CoV-2 has contributed to more than four million deaths worldwide. Based on clinical observations, it has been revealed that the virus can easily disturb the function of various organs in the body. Besides its main damage to the respiratory system, the extra-pulmonary manifestations are deemed to be common in affected patients even after COVID-19 vaccination. COVID-19 has been accompanied by various types of skin manifestations, including varicella‐like exanthemas, dengue‐like petechial rashes, or urticarial eruptions. However, not only have viral rashes been related to COVID‐19, but also other types of skin symptoms that are reminiscent of a vascular disease, such as acro‐ischaemic lesions. This literature review aims to provide information on various forms of COVID-19-induced vasculitis and vasculitis following vaccination. It has been hypothesized that various forms of vasculitis can be considered as pathological consequences following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Numerous suggested mechanisms are involved in vasculitis, including the deregulation of the immune system, increased activation of mastocyte, augmented production of proinflammatory cytokines, which in turn lead to indirect endothelial damage, complement system activation, recruitment of neutrophils, and deposition of immune complexes. Based on previous studies, the mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccine is much more implicated in relation to vasculitis. SARS–CoV-2 and COVID-19 vaccination lead to the onset and relapse of different types of vasculitis that should be clinically evaluated by exact monitoring.Keywords: Vasculitis, COVID-19, Vaccine, Inflammation

Background

Vasculitis, a heterogeneous group of pathologies, is the result of vessel inflammation [1, 2]. The clinical/pathological features of vasculitis are different and relate to the site and blood vessel type that is affected by inflammation [3]. More than 30 types of vasculitis have been introduced up to now. Vasculitis is classified into two categories: primary vasculitis and secondary ones [3, 4]. The vasculitis pathology of COVID-19 is an interesting subject. Vasculitis in COVID-19 patients is the extra-pulmonary manifestation of the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Further, SARS-CoV-2 infections probably lead to the activation of severe drug-related vasculitis [5]. Different types of COVID-19-related vasculitis cases were reported in recent papers.

Studies showed that inflammation occurs in the endothelial cells of COVID-19-affected patients. Most of the viruses are bound to the heparan sulfate of the host cells and enter the cells. The highly sulfated groups provide a negative charge and binding capacity of the receptors for various types of positively charged ligands [6, 7]. Varga et al. indicated the direct infection of the endothelial cells. The diffused endothelial inflammation followed by endothelium induction and programmed cell death (PCD) was found in the autopsies of COVID-19 patients [8]. Also, the infiltration of the mononuclear cells into the vascular intima along the lumen of most of the vessels was reported. These findings proposed that the virus can infect the vessels and induce vasculitis [9]. It has been shown that in COVID-19 patients who need intensive care, the T helper 2 (Th2) mediated immune responses are dominant when compared to the COVID-19 patients who experienced mild symptoms [10, 11]. The hypersensitivity mediated by T cells is involved in the pathophysiology of the vasculitis induced by COVID-19. The hypersensitivity is the result of excessive immune complex accumulation inside the tissues, particularly in blood vessels that induce severe inflammation by the action of complement C3a and C5a, stimulating the histamine release from mast cells and phagocytes recruitment and leading to tissue damage [12]. Also, immune complexes deposition inside the vascular walls results in more severe inflammatory responses. IL-6 plays a key in this event [10]. The hypothesized mechanisms for both virus-induced and vaccine-induced vasculitis were shown in Fig. 1.

SARS-CoV-2 can trigger a spectrum of vasculitis through overlapping pathogenic mechanisms. (A) SARS-CoV-2 infection can induce vascular inflammation through a complex interplay of direct viral effects and dysregulated host immune responses that affect small, medium, or large vessels, resulting in a wide range of vasculitis. (B) The development of vasculitis begins with endothelial injury, caused either by the virus entering endothelial cells via ACE2 receptors or by cytokine-driven activation of these cells. The resulting damage disrupts vascular integrity, increases the expression of adhesion molecules, and exposes normally hidden vascular antigens. During severe COVID-19, a cytokine storm dominated by IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ further activates immune cells and intensifies inflammation in vessel walls. Complement activation produces inflammatory molecules such as C3a and C5a and deposits membrane attack complexes (C5b-9), causing additional tissue injury and small-vessel thrombosis. At the same time, molecular mimicry and bystander B-cell activation can lead to the production of autoantibodies that drive systemic vasculitic features. Simultaneously, neutrophil hyperactivation and release of NETs contribute to endothelial damage and serve as a potent source of autoantigens such as MPO and PR3. These autoantigens nurture ANCA autoantibody formation and thereby explaining the emergence of ANCA-associated vasculitis in some post-COVID cases. In IgA vasculitis and cutaneous leukocytoclastic vasculitis, immune complexes containing viral antigens and IgA or IgG antibodies can deposit in vessel walls, activating complement, and promoting further vascular inflammation and injury. NETs: neutrophil extracellular traps; MPO: myeloperoxidase; PR3: proteinase 3; COVID-19: Coronavirus disease 2019; SARS‑CoV‑2: severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2

The vasculitis cases following COVID-19 infection/vaccination involved both cases of denovo or relapsed vasculitis. The vasculitis patients presented the vasculitis symptoms between 1 and 15 days after vaccinations (the median time is 7 days after the first vaccination and 1 day after the second vaccination) and COVID-19 patients experienced vasculitis between 15 days to one month after their positive test for COVID-19 [13, 14]. Most of the vasculitis patients associated with COVID-19 were discharged from the hospital with supportive care and their problems were resolved. Here, we review the various types of vasculitis induced by COVID-19 that have been reported recently.

Urticarial vasculitis

Urticarial vasculitis is a rare type of cutaneous leukocytoclastic vasculitis that presents by urticarial skin lesions in combination with other clinical, immunological, and histological features [15]. Urticarial vasculitis is a heterogeneous disorder associated with different severity from mild lesions to severe ones by organ-specific or systematic manifestations which usually occurs in young to middle-aged women [16, 17]. Infections, drugs, autoimmunity, and cancers can cause urticarial vasculitis. While urticarial vasculitis is typically asymptomatic, the lesions can be associated with a burning feeling, pain, or fever [18]. The urticarial vasculitis induced by COVID-19 was reported in 2020. The manifestations may be accompanied by COVID-19 or initiate afterward. The exact etiology of urticarial vasculitis development is unknown. The drugs frequently administered in COVID-19 patients may be the main cause of drug-related urticarial vasculitis. Also, immune complex deposition and complement system activation are involved in urticarial vasculitis following COVID-19 [19–21]. Activation of the complement system and formation of membrane attack complex (MAC) causes microvascular endothelial cell damage [22]. The complement pathway activation in vasculitis pathogenesis of viral infection has been reported [19]. In this regard, Nasiri et al. reported the case with urticarial vasculitis following SARS-CoV-2 infection recovery in Iran. The patient was treated with hydroxychloroquine (200 mg BD) and azithromycin 250 mg/day for 5 days. However, nearly a month after recovery, the patient experienced weakness, malaise, anorexia, and the onset of generalized skin lesions. Careful cutaneous and histopathological examinations provided evidence of leukocytoclastic vasculitis consistent with urticarial vasculitis. Antihistamine treatment led to the skin lesions clearance after a week. They stated that the exact etiology of urticarial vasculitis development is unknown in their case study, however, they proposed that complement pathways activation and MACmediated microvascular endothelial cell injury are deemed to be responsible for the development of urticarial vasculitis [19]. Also, two cases of COVID-19-induced urticarial vasculitis have been reported in Spain. One of the cases was treated with hydroxychloroquine, lopinavir/ritonavir, and azithromycin for 5 days, and the other one received hydroxychloroquine and azithromycin as therapeutic agents. They suggested that the vascular involvement in remote tissues may develop various manifestations, as observed in studied patients, who were immunologically predisposed individuals; urticarial vasculitis appeared as a result of SARS-CoV-2 infection [18]. Dardas et al. reported the first case of urticarial vasculitis in a pregnant woman with COVID-19 with mucin deposition in the upper dermis that which might be a newly found histological feature in COVID‐19 [23]. Another study indicated that urticarial vasculitis might be associated with COVID‐19, and that omalizumab could be a safe therapeutic option for pediatric cases of urticarial vasculitis [24]. Also, urticarial vasculitis was reported in two asymptomatic SARS‐CoV‐2 brazilian women with positive SARS‐CoV‐2 serology test. Therefore, the clinicians must be vigilant and do SARS-CoV-2 serology test, along with cutaneous biopsies and immunohistochemical analysis for SARS-CoV-2 antigens in the skin, where possible. A cutaneous eruption may be merely visiable sign of hidden COVID-19 infection [25].

Large vessel vasculitis (LVV)

Aortitis -inflammation of the aorta- is an uncommon, however life-threatening disease that is attributed to infection with SARS-CoV-2 rather than vasculitis [26]. An incidental case of large vessel vasculitis (LVV) diagnosed on [18F]-Fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) associated COVID-19 pneumonia was reported by Dávila et al. [27]. This case was a 69-year-old female which diagnosed of basal ganglia ischemic stroke with no respiratory symptoms. The blood tests showed left-shift leukocytosis and a high level of acute phase reactants. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging result was in agreement with cerebral vasculitis and a computed tomography angiography indicated the left subclavian artery narrowing. Considering the case was clinically suspected to be a systemic vasculitis, an 18F-FDG PET/CT study was carried out, the result of the examination showed a diffuse enhancement of glucose metabolism in the wall of main arterial vessels that proposed a large vessel vasculitis. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the findings in this case were interpreted that she was COVID-19 suspicious and the PCR test for COVID-19 was positive for this person [27]. Recently, Sollini and colleagues demonstrated that LVV was deemed to be a complication of SARS-CoV-2 infection in patients with persistent symptoms after recovery from COVID-19. They reported a higher target-to-blood pool ratio in different vascular parts, including the thoracic aorta, right iliac artery, and femoral arteries in the recovered COVID-19 group compared to the control group, which in turn indicated the role of SARS-CoV-2 in vascular inflammation induction [28]. It has been suggested that in affected individuals, the ascending aorta, arch of aorta, descending aorta, and ilio-femoral are among the common arteries that demonstrate augmented uptake [26]. Oda and colleagues, for the first time, reported the case of adult LVV associated with COVID-19 in a 71-year-old Japanese man. After a month of NSAIDs treatment, the improved inflammatory indices were observed on CT and 18F-FDG PET/CT. They deduced that patients with a susceptibility to developing vasculitis may develop LVV due to SARS-CoV-2 infection. They proposed that endothelial cell infection and subsequent endotheliitis may result in vasculitis [29].

Data from the literature suggest that COVID-19 has been accompanied with LVV development and it is deemed to be more severe in patients with LVV and the rates of hospitalization and lethality were high in these patients [30–32].

ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV)

Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a group of immune-mediated disorders characterized by small and medium vessels inflammation, affecting all systems. Inflammation is driven by pathogenic autoantibodies directed against neutrophil cytoplasmic antigens, primarily myeloperoxidase (MPO) and proteinase 3 (PR3).

These autoantibodies activate primed neutrophils, leading to endothelial injury, complement activation, and the formation of pauci-immune necrotizing vasculitis, which clinically manifests as rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, pulmonary hemorrhage, or systemic small-vessel disease. COVID-19 and AAV share pathogenic pathways where excessive NET (neutrophil extracellular trap) release drives tissue inflammation, complement activation, and vascular damage.

The alternative complement pathway, particularly the C5a–C5aR1 axis, acts as a key amplifier of these processes. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, an increasing number of case reports have described relapses of pre-existing AAV and new-onset AAV developing during or after SARS-CoV-2 infection [33–36].

SARS-CoV-2 may act as an autoimmune trigger through mechanisms such as excessive neutrophil activation and NET formation, molecular mimicry, and dysregulated immune responses leading to loss of tolerance against self-antigens like MPO and PR3 [37]. Patients with AAV are at increased risk of severe outcomes from SARS-CoV-2 infection, with reported mortality rates around 20–25%. Risk factors for severe COVID‑19 include older age, active vasculitis, underlying kidney or lung disease, high-dose glucocorticoids, and use of B-cell depleting therapy such as rituximab [38].

Overlapping pathways such as NET formation, complement activation, and thrombosis may contribute to the heightened vulnerability of this population [39]. Pandemic-related care disruptions, including delayed diagnosis and postponed therapy, have been associated with worse vasculitis outcomes.New-onset AAV has also been reported following SARS-CoV-2 infection [33–36] or after vaccination [40–43]. A new onset of anti-MPO vasculitis in a patient who tested positive for COVID-19 IgG antibodies (suggestive of previous asymptomatic infection) was reported by Powell and coworkers [44].

Two adult cases developing de novo AAV during or soon after COVID-19 were reported in the USA (2020). These individuals presented with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, often concurrent with active SARS-CoV-2 infection. ANCA serology revealed both MPO- and PR3-positivity, and kidney biopsies confirmed pauci-immune necrotizing and crescentic lesions. Management followed standard AAV protocols using high-dose corticosteroids with either cyclophosphamide or rituximab, or plasma exchange for severe pulmonary-renal involvement [33].

Izci Duran et al. (2021) described two young adults who developed MPO-positive AAV within weeks of recovering from COVID-19. Both received pulse methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide, with one also undergoing plasma exchange due to severe renal failure [34]. Another instance of MPO-ANCA vasculitis is reported in a 54-year-old woman following COVID-19. Immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents led to gradual improvement, and plasmapheresis was used in parallel [35]. With the emergence of COVID-19, researchers believed that immunosuppressed patients with AAV might be at a higher risk of COVID-19 infections compared to healthy individuals [45]. However, the primary document did not show any correlation between immunosuppressive drugs and the high risk of respiratory complications of COVID-19. Silvia Suárez et al. showed that AVV patients treated with rituximab had mild COVID-19. Rituximab can modulate the immune system via various mechanisms (i.e. antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and induction of apoptosis). Therefore, B-cell depletion therapy may not increase the risk of severe COVID-19, however, favors a milder course of the disease and results in the generation of specific antibodies. Patients with systemic vasculitis or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) may be at a higher risk of hospitalization from COVID-19 [46]. Rituximab is a licensed therapy for remission induction of AAV. Quartuccio and coworkers assessed 100 patients (53 females; 47 males) diagnosed with granulomatosis with polyangiitis, granulomatosis eosinophilic with polyangiitis, or microscopic polyangiitis (MPA). Two cases were infected by SARS-CoV-2. Both SARS-CoV-2 affected cases were administrated RTX as remission maintenance. One of the cases was a 73-year-old woman with ANCA-PR3 positive granulomatosis with polyangiitis. She was treated with RTX as remission induction. After SARS-CoV-2 infection, she was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and received glucocorticoids, piperacillin/tazobactam, enoxaparin, and oxygen therapy. However, she died 25 days after the COVID-19 diagnosis. The second case was a 74-year-old woman diagnosed with ANCA-MPO-positive MPA. She was on maintaining remission with RTX. The timing of RTX and IgG levels were different between the two cases, this might affect the outcome greatly. Therefore, patients in need of RTX therapy, seem to be in prioritization for SARS-CoV-2 vaccination [47]. A 17-year-old male was diagnosed with COVID-19 pneumonia with pulmonary nodules, atypical for COVID-19. Elevated inflammatory markers, negative infectious testing, and positive ANCA were detected and seemed to be the signs of AAV. Symptoms and laboratory markers were enhanced after steroids and RTX administration. After a six-month follow-up, lesions were highly ameliorated, and laboratory markers met the normal levels. Overall, it can be suggested that COVID-19 induces autoimmunity in the pediatric population [48]. Hussein et al. for the first time reported ANCA-positive granulomatosis with polyangiitis and COVID-19 [49]. Vacchi et al. reported a SARS-CoV-2-affected 73-year–old man with a history of type II diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and ischemic coronary disease. They also reported an arterial thrombosis in the splenic hilum, which was related to a neutrophilic vasculitis process. Their findings were alike the acute stage of polyarteritis nodosa (PAN), a medium-sized vessel vasculitis associated with viral infections. His clinical condition ameliorated following anticoagulant therapy but not immunosuppressive treatment. Overall, they proposed that a short course of glucocorticoid administration and/or plasma exchange could represent a therapeutic alternative in SARS-CoV-2 infection and PAN-like disorder [50]. It has been suggested that the prescription of glucocorticoids during active SARS-CoV-2 infection following standard induction regimens after COVID-19 recovery can be considered a beneficial approach in AAV patients with severe vasculitis and concomitant SARS-CoV-2 infection [51].

IgA vasculitis

IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) is characterized as small-vessel vasculitis mediated by immune-complexes deposits (i.e. IgA). Numerous microorganisms contribute to IgA vasculitis. The main clinical symptoms are non-thrombocytopenic purpura, arthritis/arthralgia, as well as abdominal pain [52]. As evidence indicated, SARS-CoV-2 may lead to IgA vasculitis. During the first days, the anti-SARS CoV-2 IgA is the first immunoglobulin that has been detected in COVID-19 [26, 53]. In a 30-year-old man, the development of de novo glomerulonephritis as a renal manifestation of SARS-CoV-2 infection was detected. Preserved renal function with dysmorphic hematuria and nephrotic range proteinuria were among his symptoms. Renal biopsy showed focally crescentic and segmentally necrotizing IgA nephropathy. Thus, it has been proposed that IgA vasculitis may be a rare complication of COVID-19 [54]. Moreover, Suso et al. described a patient who developed IgA vasculitis and nephritis following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Though, it has been evident that SARS-CoV-2 might trigger IgA nephritis [52]. Walker et al. reported the 39-year old male with a persistent rash which firstly emerged 8 days after COVID-19 PCR positive test with no past medical history of vasculitis [55].

The result of a systematic review identified 13 patients with IgA nephropathy and IgAV that were connected with SARS-CoV-2 infection. Moreover, four cases had IgA nephropathy following COVID-19 vaccination; suggesting the function of IgA in immune hyperactivation as the main facilitator of the disease process in COVID-19 patients [56].

The kidney is one of the organ systems where COVID-19’s viral components have been shown to cause endotheliitis and microvascular disruption. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that COVID-19 can cause immune complex deposition in vascular beds by intensifying a type 3 hypersensitivity reaction following a Th2 immune response. The endothelial damage risk factors of advanced age, male sex, and hypertension may increase the susceptibility of these patients to IgA vasculitis through one or both of these processes [55].

Cerebral vasculitis

Cerebral vasculitis is composed of some inflammatory vasculitis that leads to stenosis, occlusion, and rupture of the central nervous system vessels. Furthermore, the peripheral nervous system is affected by these diseases. Intracranial vasculitis may result in thrombosis of arteries/veins, including the dural sinuses. The granulomatous meningeal involvement may be caused by the disease and neurologic dysfunction is the result of cytokine release. Peripheral neuropathy is the neurologic presentation of vasculitis [57]. Viral infections, like Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), HIV, and HCV can induce cerebral vasculitis. SARS-CoV-2 infection has been correlated with neurological dysfunctions at brain MRI for critical illness-related encephalopathy. It has been suggested that central nervous system (CNS) damage induced by COVID-19 is mainly mediated by inflammatory response involving cytokines like IL-6 [58].

The severe viral infection pathology is tightly associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome development (SIRS). In severe pneumonia caused by COVID-19 infection, SIRS could be atypically initiated, though early anti-inflammatory intervention could effectively preserve organ failure resulting in immune responses and declining the risk of nervous system injury [59].

COVID-19 infection persistence and its potential to infect macrophages, microglia, and astrocytes in the CNS is so important. The neurotropic virus could activate glial cells and make a pro-inflammatory state. IL-6 is one of the main pro-inflammatory cytokines which is positively associated with the severity of COVID-19 symptoms induced by cytokine storm. The primary glial cells infected with COVID-19 secret a large amount of inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-12, IL-15, and TNF-α). The result of immune cell activation in the brain would be chronic inflammation and brain damage.

Lersy et al. reported cerebral vasculitis of medium-sized vessels in COVID-19 patients [60]. Also, the CNS involvement composed of acute hemorrhagic necrotizing encephalopathy, acute transverse myelitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and meningitis was reported in COVD-19 patients [61]. COVID-19 infects the neurons and cerebral endothelial cells through interaction with the ACE2 receptor [61]. de Sousa and coworkers reported neurological symptoms as the main consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic. In this line, a 28-years-old male patient who underwent MRI demonstrated hyperintense lesions in cortical and subcortical regions, possibly due to vasculitis [62]. In a 69-year-old Caucasian man, cerebral vasculitis secondary to SARS-CoV-2 infection was detected. It has been proposed that headache might be considered as a warning sign of cerebral vasculitis in COVID-19-affected individuals [63].

The affinity of SARS-CoV-2 for ACE2 receptors in the cerebral arterial wall results in local inflammation initiation, inducing a vasculitic process. ACE2 receptors are expressed in endothelial and arterial smooth muscle cells in the brain. The virus damages the intracranial arteries, which in turn leads to the rupture of the vessel wall and subsequent hemorrhagic stroke in COVID-19. High amounts of cytokine release or secondary hemorrhagic transformation of an ischemic stroke are considered possible causes of hemorrhagic stroke in COVID-19 [64]. During pregnancy, the pro-inflammatory state is usually observed in the first and third trimesters, and maternal immunity is affected by alterations in hormonal function. The occurrence of cerebral vasculitis among COVID-19 patients was reported, however, there is no publication regarding COVID-19 and cerebral vasculitis in a postpartum patient. Vasculitis, hypercoagulable state, as well as cardiomyopathy, are among the factors that contribute to the occurrence of ischemic strokes in COVID-19 [64]. In this regard, Chua et al. reported a case of a postpartum with COVID-19 who experienced cerebral vasculitis. Pregnancy and COVID-19 exert a double pro-inflammatory state that increases the chance of having a vasculitic type of stroke [64]. Ghoush et al. reported a unique neurologic complication of COVID-19 in a patient (a 66-year-old man) who experienced extensive cerebral small-vessel ischemic lesions similar to cerebral vasculitis. Although the neurologic complications of COVID-19 could be more debilitating than the respiratory illness itself, particularly in patients, especially for those patients who survived the complicated course of mechanical ventilation [65]. Furthermore, Timmons et al. described neurologic complications (cerebral lesions) in a case of biopsy-confirmed CNS vasculitis after SARS-CoV-2 infection. In this case, it has been proposed that young patients with mild SARS-CoV-2 infection develop a risk for neurologic complications [66]. In ten autopsy cases of COVID-19, the early stage of type 3 hypersensitivity vasculitis was observed. In type 3 hypersensitivity vasculitis, PMN activation is the main vascular damage effector along with hyper-cytokinemia [67]. Brun et al. described demyelination or small-vessel CNS vasculitis in a 54-year-old woman as an uncommon complication of sedated patients with COVID-19 [68]. Also, Reyes et al. in 2024 reported a 42-year-old male patient infected by COVID-19 experienced non-fluent aphasia and right-sided hemiparesis. He treated with high-dose intravenous steroid and cerebral blood flow was improved and any ischemic infarcts were not seen on follow-up examination. There for, high-dose intravenous steroids may be an effective therapeutic approach in prevention of further stroke load when used in conjunction with vasopressor support [69].

Cutaneous vasculitis

Due to COVID-19 pandemic, patients with several dermatological manifestations like chilblain-like lesions, livedo reticularis, urticaria, varicella-like, petechial and maculopapular exanthems were referred to hospitals [70]. COVID-19 could induce cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis. Cutaneous vasculitis, an inflammatory disease that affects the dermal blood vessel walls, encompasses the various types of skin vasculitis through activating the complement system [71]. The clinical manifestations of cutaneous vasculitis are dependent on the affected vessels’ location and size. Sohail et al. reported a case with skin and oral lesions secondary to infection with COVID-19 [72]. Recently, Schenker and colleagues described the onset of arthritis and vasculitis in a 65-year-old Caucasian woman 10 days after recovery from acute SARS-CoV-2 infection. After COVID-19-related symptoms termination, symmetric polyarthritis of ankles, wrists, and knee joints, as well as palpable purpura of both calves were observed, while the aforementioned case showed no history of rheumatic diseases before. Therefore, they concluded that COVID-19 might induce an autoimmune response which in turn causes reactive arthritis and vasculitis [73]. A severe case of a COVID-19 patient, with vasculitic acro-ischemic lesions in the right hand was reported. Biopsy of the hand lesion suggested COVID-19 vasculitis based on evidence of necrosis and residual scar at the dorsum of the hand secondary to a liveoid lesion. The histologic section of the hand lesion biopsy that was stained by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) indicated infiltration of perivascular inflammation at dermal vessels that was accompanied by vessel wall thickening, lumen sclerosis, and fibrin thrombus. However, microsurgical auto-transplant was successful in the patient [74]. Also, Iraji et al. analyzed 799 hospitalised COVID-19 patients for cutaneous vasculitis development. Direct immunofluorescence and skin biopsies were used to confirm the suspected patients’ cutaneous vasculitis. 24 out of 799 COVID-19 patients was confirmed as cutaneous vasculitis cases. The study resukts indicate that the development of cutaneous vasculitis was significantly correlated with male sex, advanced age, C-reactive protein level, and the comorbidities existence and the severity of COVID-19 infection [75].

Leukocytoclastic vasculitis (LCV)

Leukocytoclastic vasculitis (LCV) is a type of cutaneous vasculitis of dermal capillaries/venules and is characterized by leukocytoclasis [76, 77]. The LCV can be idiopathic or can be secondary to infections, neoplasms, connective tissue disorders, autoimmune diseases, and drugs. The main features of LCV are palpable purpura on the lower extremities, which may be accompanied by pain and pruritus [76, 77]. Ulcers, pustules, and vesicles are among other cutaneous manifestations of LCV. In the case of internal organs (i.e. gastrointestinal tract (GIT), kidneys, and joints) involvement, systemic symptoms, including arthralgia, arthritis, myalgia, abdominal pain, fever, diarrhea, melena, cough, hematochezia, etc. appeared [77]. The exact mechanism underlying LCV remains unknown; however, deposition of the immune complex exerts an important role in LCV pathophysiology [77]. The LCV needs to be validated by skin biopsies; the presence of vascular-perivascular infiltration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes with nuclear dust formation (leukocytoclasis), extravasation of erythrocytes, and fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel walls are common features of the LCV [78, 79]. Emerging data suggest that cutaneous vasculitis may manifest during the course of or following COVID-19 infection [80]. Case reports of cutaneous vasculitis observed in the post-COVID-19 syndrome encompass both newly developed cases and exacerbations of pre-existing disease. In this line, Mayor-Ibarguren et al. reported an 83-year-old woman with LCV whose serum IgM and IgG against COVID-19 is positive, but the oropharyngeal swab PCR is negative. This finding indicates that LCV could develop as a COVID-19 late-onset manifestation [79]. Moreover, it is believed that COVID-19 antigens may trigger antibody production and antigen–antibody complexes formation that target the vascular endothelium of the skin and provoke the LCV [81].

Like other skin lesions in COVID-19 patients, there is not enough evidence about the COVID-19 role in the ethiopathogenic mechanism of the skin lesions. A study indicated the viral particles presence in the endothelium of COVID-19 patients with chilblains and the histological evidence of vascular damage [82].

Numerous lines of evidence indicated that the most common form of cutaneous involvement is a generalized macular or maculopapular rash, followed by papulovesicular rash and urticarial [77, 83–86]. The LCV secondary to COVID-19 has been reported in 49 males and 83 females following SARS-CoV-2 infection [78, 79]. Furthermore, a 49-year-old man developed skin lesions on the lower limbs as well as the trunk and normal complement levels, two weeks after COVID-19 diagnosis. Histopathologic findings proposed that the lesions were cutaneous LCV. It has been thought that ACE2 receptors present in the skin were probably responsible for dermatologic complications of COVID-19 [77]. Camprodon Gómez and colleagues, for the first time, reported the presence of SARS-CoV-2 in a skin biopsy from LCV lesions [81]. Ozturk et al. reported a case of LCV in a 47-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis in remission, treated with adalimumab, who received a five-day course of favipiravir for COVID-19. Clinical manifestations appeared three days after receiving favipiravir treatment, raising suspicion that the drug may have triggered the vasculitis. However, the authors considered this interval insufficient to initiate the vasculitic immune mechanism, suggesting that favipiravir might not be the definitive cause. In this case, COVID-19 led to severe and diffuse LCV, which resulted in amputation, highlighting the potential for COVID-19 to cause severe vasculitic complications [87]. Another study reported a 93-year-old man in Switzerland who recovered from COVID-19 later developed a severe and rapidly progressing form of LCV, which caused extensive skin and tissue necrosis, ultimately leading to his death. In this case, LCV was considered a secondary manifestation of COVID-19, despite the absence of SARS-CoV-2 in the skin biopsy [88]. In a 41-year-old male patient with type 2 diabetes mellitus who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2, histopathological examination revealed features consistent with LCV. Although the initial chest radiograph was unremarkable, the patient subsequently developed severe bilateral pneumonia accompanied by progression of the cutaneous lesions, culminating in extensive necrosis of the left foot and dry gangrene of the left hallux. In response, targeted COVID-19 therapy was initiated in conjunction with repeated surgical debridement, antibiotic administration, and eventual amputation of the affected great toe [89]. Moreover, a 41-year-old male patient with a background of poorly controlled diabetes mellitus presented with SARS-CoV-2 infection accompanied by follicular cutaneous rashes. Notably, antigen testing for COVID-19 conducted on the skin lesions produced a positive result. The patient received treatment following the nationally approved anti–COVID-19 therapeutic protocol. Over a 4‑week course, he exhibited substantial clinical and radiological improvement and was discharged with a peripheral oxygen saturation of 100% on ambient air. His cutaneous manifestations had fully resolved, except for residual areas of hypo- and hyperpigmentation [90]. da Cruz Gouveia and colleagues reported a case of extensive cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis in a 27-year-old male patient with mild COVID-19 infection in Brazil. After 5 days of systemic prednisone therapy, his skin lesions demonstrated marked improvement [91]. The accumulating clinical data point toward a multifactorial etiology of COVID-19–associated LCV, in which immune complex deposition, and host inflammatory response act synergistically. Elucidating these mechanisms could have important implications for understanding post-viral autoimmune phenomena and optimizing management strategies in affected patients.

Vasculitis following vaccination

Natural infection will usually provoke lifelong immunity; however, in the case of life-threatening diseases, vaccination is a crucial way to boost natural immune responses by preventing fatal diseases. Minor symptoms (i.e. fever, body aches, headaches, as well as injection site soreness) are common indications following vaccination, which demonstrates that the immunity is built up [92]. COVID-19 vaccine-associated vasculitis

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) expeditiously approved various COVID-19 vaccines [92]. Vasculitis associated with COVID-19 vaccination is an uncommon but recognized adverse event for which no standardized therapeutic approach has yet been established [107].

Shakoor et al. reported a case of renal-limited AAV in a 78-year-old woman following the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccination. Acute kidney injury (AKI), proteinuria, and microscopic hematuria with dysmorphic red blood cells in the urine were reported in the patient. Following steroids and rituximab treatment, kidney function was enhanced. They suggested that the immune response to the COVID-19 vaccine might trigger AAV; however, this case cannot define whether there is a causal relationship between COVID-19 vaccination and AAV or not [92]. Okuda and colleagues described a 37-year-old Japanese patient who developed AAV after receiving an mRNA-based anti-COVID-19 vaccine. She had been taking propylthiouracil for Graves’ disease. Signs of erythema appeared on her forearm on the 12th day, fever on the 13th day, as well as redness and swelling of her left auricle on the 25th day post first shot of the vaccine. The serum MPO-ANCA and proteinase 3-ANCA levels were increased whereas they were negative before the Graves’ disease treatment. It has been proposed that propylthiouracil was the main cause of AAV onset, however, assessing the safety of the vaccine seems to have priority [108]. Furthermore, Schierz et al. reported vascular and bursal findings after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination in a 78-year-old female. She presented cephalgia, cervicalgia, ostealgia, and pain in multiple large joints and muscles 2–3 weeks after the first shot of the COVID-19 vaccine and deteriorated after the second shot. [18F] FDG positron emission tomography/computed tomography ([18F] FDG-PET/CT) exhibited enhanced tracer uptakes in the large arteries of the legs. Additionally, they distinguished a moderate increase within pelvic bursae accompanied by ligamenta flava intense FDG uptakes. Also, a significant rise in the vertebral arteries V3 segment was reported which was associated with arterial wall thickening. Briefly, the reported case is the first to show infrequent bursal and vascular findings following mRNA vaccination of COVID-19 [109]. Although researchers should be cautious when linking vaccine side effects to the findings, as they may be coincidental rather than causal.

In another case report, a 76-year-old man with previous liver cirrhosis developed an immune complex vasculitis 12 days after the second shot of the mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccine. Following a short period of oral steroids administration, all clinical manifestations (pruritic purpuric macules on hands and feet, flexor and extensor parts of both legs as well as thighs and lower abdomen, and bloody diarrhea), in addition to inflammatory markers, were resolved [110]. A 78-year-old woman with a history of IgA vasculitis with LCV, renal and gastrointestinal involvement, was in remission status. A week after receiving an mRNA-based vaccine, she developed diarrhea and diffuse abdominal pain with acute onset. Various tests demonstrated elevated levels of anti-spike IgG, IgA, and IgM antibodies after vaccination. It has been proposed that the elevated levels of anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike IgA and the reactivation of pre-existing IgA vasculitis following vaccination might be linked together [111].

New-onset LCV was observed in a 33-year-old man after administration of the COVID‐19 mRNA vaccine [95]. In an 84-year-old woman with CKD and depressive disorder, a few hours following the first dose of the mRNA-based vaccine, burning pain in the distal part of one leg and foot was observed. Her serum tests were positive for VZV IgM and IgG antibodies. Thus, an uncommon manifestation of herpes zoster infection after the COVID-19 vaccine results in small-vessel vasculitis. It has been proposed that the age of the patient and her immune system reaction to the vaccine might lead to immunosuppression, which in turn causes virus reactivation [112]. In a reported case involving a 47-year-old woman, acute renal failure developed a few days after administration of the first dose of the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine; however, the condition was managed with corticosteroid therapy in combination with azathioprine [113]. A cohort mainly involved female patients, with biopsy-confirmed AAV characterized primarily by kidney involvement. Most cases developed symptoms after the second Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine dose. Glucocorticoid therapy led to generally positive outcomes [114]. Moreover, a 74-year-old woman developed otitis media associated with AAV after receiving her third dose of an mRNA COVID-19 vaccine. She experienced general fatigue and hearing impairment in both ears for about a month following vaccination. Laboratory analysis showed elevated myeloperoxidase-ANCA levels, and audiometric testing indicated moderate mixed hearing loss bilaterally. Administration of corticosteroids combined with rituximab resulted in improved hearing and decreased myeloperoxidase-ANCA concentrations [115]. Another case-study involving a 43-year-old male was identified as the first documented case of isolated popliteal giant cell arteritis (GCA) occurring after both COVID-19 mRNA vaccination and SARS-CoV-2 infection. According to Gabrielli et al., the enhanced immune activation induced by the vaccine may have precipitated GCA in this patient, who had underlying susceptibility factors and a history of repeated microtrauma to the popliteal fossa [116]. Although adverse events are rare, the advantages of COVID-19 vaccination surpass its potential risks, underscoring the critical role of immunization initiatives [117].

A case of cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis (cSVV) with asymmetrical distribution, following a second dose of inactivated COVID-19 vaccine in a 31-year-old woman, was reported. It might be a result of molecular mimicry and immunological cross-reactivity due to vaccine antigens [118]. A peculiar cutaneous eruption (maculopapular rash) following anti-COVID-19 vaccination in a 51-year-old female patient was reported which was characterized by lymphocytic vasculitis [96]. Dash et al. reported for the first time a 27-year-old man with itchy reddish lesions following inactivated COVID-19 vaccination. Elevated levels of inflammatory response after vaccination result in vascular inflammation and urticarial vasculitis [93]. Additionally, LCV (cutaneous purpuric dermatitis) with IgA deposition after the first shot of the inactivated COVID-19 vaccine was observed [95]. IgA vasculitis was also reported two weeks after injection of adenoviral vector vaccine in a 72-year-old-man [111].

COVID-19 vaccination generated T cell response, Interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-2, and IgG1 and IgG3 subclass production. These immune responses might contribute to a recurrence of glomerular disease or podocytopathies [119]. Following mRNA-based vaccination, kidney biopsies demonstrated AAV, IgA nephritis, and anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease. An acute granulomatous vasculitis has developed in a man receiving the first dose of the AstraZeneca vaccine [120]. The exact mechanism of de novo AAV after COVID-19 vaccination is still unknown; however, a neutrophilic immune response to the S protein or mRNA of SARS-CoV-2 in predisposed individuals is suggested. Vaccination results in the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF and IL-1β, which in turn leads to neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation. Persistent NETs and exposure to their contents cause tolerance disruption and formation of antibodies to myeloperoxidase and proteinase 3 which subsequently may result in triggering an AAV [119]. Five cases of cSVV were observed following administration of the ChAdOx1 COVID-19 vaccine among female patients aged 44 to 68 years. Clinical manifestations generally emerged within two days post-immunization. In most cases, cutaneous involvement was limited to the lower extremities, whereas one patient exhibited lesion extension to the upper limbs. All affected individuals responded well uncommon dermatologic adverse reaction associated with both COVID-19 infection and vaccination. Although the majority of reported cases are self-limiting and resolve without lasting sequelae, more severe manifestations appear to occur predominantly in the context of COVID-19 infection rather than vaccination [121]. A 77-year-old female patient undergoing hemodialysis was hospitalized due to the appearance of purpura on her limbs, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) as well as D-dimer levels, and an episode of syncope. Despite initial management, cSVV progressively worsened. The purpura began to improve following the initiation of prednisolone therapy. It was subsequently discovered that the patient had received her seventh dose of a COVID-19 vaccine prior to the onset of symptoms. This case underscores that cSVV may potentially occur even after multiple COVID-19 vaccinations [122]. CSVV represents a relatively of vasculitis was observed around seven days following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination [123].

McGonagle et al. proposed several mechanisms, including pulmonary microembolisation, systemic RNAaemia with arteriolar and venous thrombosis, and other factors attributed to the thrombotic phenotypes that can mimic vasculitis. Virus interaction with the ACE2 receptor results in elevated angiotensin II concentrations, which in turn may induce endothelial inflammation and in situ immunothrombosis [14, 124]. Organs like the brain and kidneys, which are dependent on high amounts of cardiac output, particularly suffer from the detrimental effects of systemic embolization. The microscopic vascular thrombosis in the lungs and peripheral tissues of patients affected with severe COVID-19 demonstrated the deposition of alternative and mannose-binding lectin complement pathway proteins adjacent to thrombosis, which proposed local complement activation. The diffuse organ damage in severe COVID-19 patients has been linked to microangiopathic hemolytic anemias, including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and hemolytic uremic syndrome, which demonstrates the role of complement activation [14].

It has been hypothesized that both SARS-CoV-2 infection and mRNA-based vaccines may trigger autoimmune responses, potentially mediated by molecular mimicry or cross-reactivity between vaccine constituents and host self-antigens [123]. Some reports described the emergence of autoimmune and autoinflammatory disorders after COVID-19 vaccination [125]. In this regard, Matsouda et al. encountered three patients who developed new-onset rheumatic conditions post-vaccination: rheumatoid arthritis, AAV, and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Symptoms began within a few days after immunization. The AAV and SLE cases responded well to glucocorticoid therapy, whereas the patient with rheumatoid arthritis died as a result of COVID-19 [126]. A 75-year-old female patient developed AAV and lupus glomerulonephritis following administration of the second AstraZeneca vaccine dose. The patient exhibited marked renal function deterioration after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy and subsequently developed a new COVID-19 infection, which was associated with fatal complications [127].

A systematic review demonstrated newly developed ANCA-associated small-vessel vasculitis following COVID-19 vaccination. The study population was composed mainly of women. Biopsy results confirmed the diagnosis of AAV, with renal impairment being the most common clinical presentation. In the majority of patients, AAV symptoms appeared following the second vaccine dose, and the Pfizer-BioNTech formulation was the one most frequently administered. Glucocorticoids served as the primary therapeutic approach, generally yielding a positive outcome [128]. A recent systematic review conducted by Kalantari et al. proposed a potential, although not yet verified, relationship between COVID-19 vaccination and the emergence of vascular lesions such as petechiae, purpura, and ecchymosis. Most reported cases occurred following the initial doses of the mRNA vaccines (i.e. Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna). These manifestations may act as early clinical indicators of serious conditions necessitating immediate medical attention [129]. Another systematic review revealed that cases of vasculitis following COVID-19 vaccination predominantly occurred among individuals with a median age of 57 years. The incidence was comparable between sexes, showing a near-equal female-to-male ratio of 1.1:1, indicating no significant gender predilection [123]. Notably, their findings indicated that the majority of vasculitis cases were associated with mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines, accounting for approximately 65% of reported instances. Among these, the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna vaccines were most commonly implicated. This pattern may be explained by factors related to the vaccine formulations themselves or by their extensive use within the general population. On average, the onset to treatment with systemic methylprednisolone, antihistamines, and topical corticosteroids [130]. The growing body of evidence suggests that while vasculitic events following COVID-19 vaccination are clinically significant, they remain rare compared to the vast number of administered doses. The data collectively support the concept that vaccination may unmask or exacerbate autoimmune phenomena in predisposed individuals rather than directly initiating disease. Therefore, continuous post-vaccination surveillance and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify pathways and ensure vaccine safety without undermining their critical public health benefits.

Conclusion

Accumulating clinical evidence indicates that SARS-CoV-2 infection and, in rare instances, COVID-19 vaccination can act as potential triggers for various forms of vasculitis. The underlying mechanisms are complex and appear to involve immune complex deposition, complement activation, endothelial injury, and cytokine-mediated inflammation, which together contribute to vascular and tissue damage. COVID-19–associated vasculitis represents a significant extra-pulmonary manifestation that reflects the systemic nature of SARS-CoV-2 infection and its capacity to dysregulate immune homeostasis. Although vasculitic events following vaccination remain rare in comparison to the vast number of doses given worldwide, their occurrence highlights the need for ongoing pharmacovigilance and mechanistic studies to better understand immune-mediated vascular injury in this context. Early recognition and appropriate management are crucial for favorable outcomes; as most reported cases respond well to corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy. Thus, advancing our understanding of COVID-19–related vasculitis—whether arising from infection or vaccination—will enhance clinical awareness, guide diagnostic approaches, and support the development of preventive strategies that preserve the essential public health benefits of vaccination while minimizing the risk of immune complications.
