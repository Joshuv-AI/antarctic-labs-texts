Cognitive-behavioral therapy in people with Parkinson's disease and caregivers: A mixed method systematic review on clinical applications and therapeutic outcomes
Authors: Francesca Proietti, Simone Migliore, Chiara Longo, Francesca Lea Saibene, Giuseppe Curcio, Maria Chiara Malaguti, Mario Meloni, Edoardo Bianchini, Massimo Marano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has emerged as a promising intervention in Parkinson's disease (PD), although the available evidence remains heterogeneous in design and quality.ObjectiveThe present mixed method systematic review aims to synthesize the evidence on CBT in people with PD (PwPD) and caregivers, focusing on clinical applications and therapeutic outcomes across symptom domains.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane databases was conducted for studies published between 1980 and 2025, following PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies evaluated clinical applications and therapeutic outcomes of CBT-based interventions in PwPD and/or caregivers and included randomized controlled trials, observational studies, case series and single-case reports. Findings were synthesized narratively, and certainty of evidence was assessed using a simplified GRADE approach.ResultsFifty-four studies met inclusion criteria. The most robust evidence was observed for depression, with several RCTs supporting the efficacy of CBT, resulting in moderate certainty of evidence. Evidence for anxiety and sleep disorders suggests potential benefit; however, certainty was low due to heterogeneity and small sample sizes. Evidence for impulse control disorders, cognitive outcomes, motor symptoms, and caregiver -related outcomes remain limited, with low to very low certainty, Effects on motor symptoms were inconsistent and likely indirect.ConclusionsCBT represents a flexible and transdiagnostic intervention for treating non-motor symptoms in PwPD and for caregivers. While moderate-certainty evidence supports CBT use for depression, evidence for other domains remains limited. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and further large-scale, well designed RCTs are needed to strengthen the evidence base and clarify optimal therapeutic targets and delivery format.Keywords: psychology, psychotherapy, depression, anxiety, non-motor symptoms, cognitive-behavioral therapy, Parkinson's disease

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the fastest-growing neurodegenerative disease worldwide, with numbers rising steadily over the past decade - from approximately 6.1 million people in 2016 to an estimated 14 million by 2040 (1). PD is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder accumulating disability and substantial individual and societal burden along the disease course.1,2 It is characterized by a motor syndrome, whose hallmarks are bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor and a variable degree of postural abnormalities and gait impairment. Although motor manifestations are mainly caused by the nigrostriatal dopaminergic denervation, PD is now recognized as a multisystem disorder involving also serotonergic, noradrenergic and cholinergic pathways, as well as limbic and cortical regions.3,4 Such networks play a key role along with the dopaminergic one in the pathogenesis of non-motor manifestations, that are now recognized as core features of PD, often emerging during the prodromal phase and years before motor onset. 5

Mood changes, sleep issues and cognitive impairment are among the most reported non-motor manifestations of PD. Epidemiology studies indicate that anxiety and depression affect approximately 30–50% of people with PD (PwPD), yet these symptoms frequently remain under-recognized and undertreated. Sleep disturbances affect up to 80% of PwPD over the course of the disease, with insomnia being the most prevalent condition. The prevalence of insomnia increases with disease progression, ranging from approximately 30% in early stages up to 80% in advanced PD, and is associated with poorer quality of life, longer disease duration, depression, and cognitive decline. 6 Likewise, depression and anxiety have been linked to faster motor progression and higher risk of dementia, emphasizing the need for effective interventions. 7 Also, cognitive impairment is a prevalent non-motor symptom of PD. The most frequently involved domains include executive functions, attention, processing speed, visuospatial abilities and memory.5,6 Its clinical spectrum ranges from mild cognitive impairment to dementia, which develops in up to 80% of PwPD with long disease duration. Cognitive deficits have a significant impact on daily functioning, quality of life, and caregiver burden, and frequently coexist with other non-motor symptoms such as sleep disturbances. 6 Beyond their emotional burden, affective, cognitive and sleep issues significantly contribute to functional disability, cognitive decline and reduced quality of life, sometimes exerting a greater impact than motor impairment.3,7

Given the strong interrelationship between mood disturbances, sleep dysfunction and cognitive symptoms in PD, interventions targeting maladaptive thoughts and behavioral patterns should be in the spotlight. By promoting adaptive coping strategies, improving sleep related behaviors, and enhancing cognitive flexibility, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can alleviate affective symptoms, indirectly support cognitive functioning, and improve overall quality of life in individuals with PD and their caregivers.

CBT is one of the most empirically supported and conceptually integrated models of psychotherapy. It emerged from the integration of behavioral and cognitive approaches and became formally established as a unified psychotherapeutic model in 1980s. The behavioral approach is rooted in learning theory and focused on observable change, whereas the cognitive model emphasizes the role of distorted or maladaptive thinking patterns in emotional and behavioral dysfunction. 8 Within this theoretical framework, CBT conceptualizes psychological distress as arising primarily from maladaptive interpretations of internal or external events rather than from events themselves. Therapeutic change is therefore achieved by identifying and modifying dysfunctional cognitions and behaviors, with the aim of reducing emotional distress and maladaptive response.9–11 As a structured, goal-oriented, and theory-driven psychotherapy model, CBT employs a range of evidence-based techniques derived from its foundational principles. Core interventions include: cognitive restructuring, which targets maladaptive beliefs and automatic thoughts; behavioral activation, aimed at increasing engagement in meaningful and rewarding activities; and exposure-based techniques, designed to reduce avoidance and anxiety through systematic confrontation with feared stimuli (Supplementary Table 1).12,13 In addition, contemporary CBT has incorporated mindfulness-based techniques, which emphasize present-moment awareness and more adaptive relationships with thought and emotions, and acceptance and metacognitive awareness. 8 Importantly, the CBT framework has also led to the development of disorder-specific and broad-based treatment protocols tailored to specific clinical conditions. Among these, CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I) represents a well-established, manualized intervention that applies CBT principles and techniques to target maladaptive sleep-related cognitions and behaviors and is currently considered a first-line treatment for chronic insomnia.14,15 By targeting dysfunctional thinking and emotional regulation, CBT may help normalize this imbalance, improving both anxiety and depressive symptoms.

Within its technical evolution, CBT application to neurodegenerative diseases including PD has grown.3,16–18 However, despite increasing empirical support, the current literature is still poorly structured and heterogeneous. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have primarily focused on the efficacy of CBT on specific outcomes, particularly depression and anxiety in PD,4,7 providing a relatively focused perspective and do not fully address the broader range of potential clinical applications of CBT in PwPD. The present review extends the existing literature by adopting a broader, transdiagnostic perspective, examining CBT across multiple symptom domains, including sleep disturbances, impulse control disorders (ICDs), cognitive and functional (e.g., activities of daily living) outcomes, as well as motor and caregiver-related variables. By including heterogeneous study designs, it also provides a more comprehensive overview of both established and emerging clinical evidence.

Therefore, the present review aims to systematically synthesize through a mixed method approach the available evidence on the use of CBT in PD, summarizing its clinical applications and therapeutic outcomes across different symptom domains. By integrating findings of the available literature, this review aims to clarify the current state of research and highlight the potential of CBT as a transversal therapeutic instrument in PD, providing an updated guide on the use of CBT in PD.

Methods

We conducted a systematic search of Pubmed, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane between October 2025 and January 2026 of articles published between 1980 and 2025. The search strategy uses the following terms: (“Parkinson's disease”) AND (“cognitive behavioral therapy” OR CBT) AND (depression OR anxiety OR apathy OR “sleep disorder” OR pain OR “neuropsychiatric symptom” OR “motor” OR gait OR tremor OR psychological OR behavioral).

Studies were included if they involved individuals diagnosed with PD (PwPD) and/or PD caregivers, regardless of disease stage, age, or clinical setting. Interventions were considered eligible if CBT techniques represented a central component of intervention, even when delivered alongside other therapeutic elements. Eligible studies were required to evaluate interventions based on or including CBT, including traditional CBT or adapted CBT approaches. We considered a wide range of study designs, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, qualitative studies, case series and single-case reports, to provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence. Studies were excluded if they did not involve participants with PD or if the intervention was not grounded in CBT principles. We also excluded secondary literature, such as review articles, editorials, conference abstracts and book chapters as these sources do not provide primary empirical data. Four reports represented secondary analyses of already included studies and were therefore grouped as single studies in the final synthesis.

A review protocol was not prospectively registered. However, the review methodology (including eligibility criteria, search strategy and outcomes) was predefined by the authors and conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Given the inclusion of heterogeneous study designs (randomized, observational, and case-based studies), a convergent segregated approach was adopted (i.e., mixed method). Quantitative evidence was summarized descriptively according to study outcomes, while qualitative and case-based evidence were synthesized narratively. Findings were then integrated at the level of symptom domains (mood including depression and anxiety, sleep, impulse control disorders, motor, cognitive and activity of daily living) to provide a comprehensive interpretation of CBT effects in PwPD and caregivers. Moreover, a formal GRADE assessment was applied in a simplified manner to evaluate the overall certainty of evidence across domains. Evidence derived from RCT was considered higher certainty, while observational studies and case-based evidence were interpreted with lower certainty. No studies were excluded based on the results of the critical appraisal, as the aim of this review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence across different study designs. Finally, the methodological quality of included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias 2.0 tool by two independent reviewers (CL, FS); any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (SM). When multiple publications referred to the same randomized controlled trial, they were considered a single study together with the main one. All relevant outcome domains (e.g., depression, anxiety, sleep) were extracted to provide a comprehensive overview of the reported findings. When multiple measures were used to assess the same outcome within a study, only one measure per outcome per RCT was included in the synthesis to avoid data duplication. The selected measure corresponded to the primary outcome of the study, when specified, or otherwise to the most widely used and validated instrument within that domain.

Extracted data have been summarized in tables including information on study design and sample size, adopted CBT technique, duration and delivery format (i.e., individual, group, remote, use of technologies), presence and type of comparator, patient demographic and clinical features (e.g., age, sex, disease duration and motor scores), involvement of caregivers, main study outcomes, analysis of limitations and strengths.

Results

A total of 1175 records were identified through database searches (PubMed = 237; Web of Science = 283; Scopus = 525; Cochrane = 130). After removing 371 duplicates, 804 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 724 were excluded as they consisted of book, book chapter, editorials, letter, correspondence, study protocols, review, meta-analysis or other non-eligible publication types. The remaining 80 articles were evaluated for eligibility, leading to the exclusion of 21 studies (6 full text not retrieved, 15 not in English language). Consequently, 54 studies were included in the review (Figure 1). These studies are presented and grouped according to the specific symptoms addressed by CBT interventions in PD: affective symptoms (anxiety and depression), sleep disorders, behavioral symptoms (impulsive-compulsive disorders, ICDs), cognitive and functional symptoms, and CBT intervention for PD caregivers. The main characteristics and findings of these studies are summarized in the accompanying tables.

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies showed substantial heterogeneity in terms of design, populations, interventions, and outcome measures. A total of 54 studies were included in the synthesis, comprising RCTs (n = 24),19–42 case series (n = 8),43–50 single case (n = 5),51–55 open label randomized (n = 1), 56 open label uncontrolled (n = 11),24,57–66 open label controlled (n = 5).67–71 Four post-hoc analysis were included in the narrative review but not in the synthesis.72–75 Two publications were identified as deriving from the same randomized controlled trial and were therefore treated as a single study in the synthesis to avoid double counting of participants.30,76 The studies were conducted across different geographical regions, including Europe,20,23,24,32,33,35,37,39,41,42,46,48,49,51,54,58,68,71,74,77 North America,19,22,25,26,38,45,47,52,53,56,57,60,61,72,73,75 Asia,21,27,30,31,34,40,50,59,65,69,70,76 Oceania,28,29,36,44,62–64,66,67 reflecting a broad international interest in CBT interventions for PwPD. Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from single-case reports to larger randomized controlled trials including over 350 participants. However, interventional samples were generally smaller, as the largest study included 350 PD patients but only 139 received CBT. A total of approximately 1850 paricipants were included across all studies.

Overall, the review included both PwPD and, in several studies, their caregivers, either as participants or as part of the intervention. Particularly, caregivers were included in a subset of studies, either as a co-participants in patient-focused interventions,19,20,25,26,32,36,39,45,47,51,52,54,60–64,71 or as the primary target to CBT-based interventions.42,55,66

Participant characteristics were heterogeneous, with wide variability in age (ranging from a mean of 51.68 ± 12.02 to 74.1 ± 6.0), disease duration (ranging from a mean of 3.5 ± 2.3 to 15.7 ± 2.6), and disease severity (Hoehn and Yahr scale ranging from a mean of 1.3 ± 0.5 to 3.6 ± 1.1). Control conditions, when present, included waitlist controls,27,30,31,33,36,42,67,77 Clinical Monitoring Only (CMO),19,32,35 Enhanced Usual Care (EUC), 22 Health Enhancement Program (HEO), 20 psychoeducational intervention,34,38,56,68 Tretment As Usual (TAU).25,26,29,39 As primary or secondary outcomes, the included studies addressed a range of symptom domains, most commonly depression (n = 31)19–31,43–46,51,56–63,67–69,72,73,76 and anxiety (n = 10),32–36,47,52,53,64,70 followed by sleep disorders (n = 6),37,38,48–50,65 impulse control disorders (n = 4),39,54,71,74 cognitive and functional outcomes (n = 6),40,41,62,70,75 and caregiver-related outcomes (n = 3).42,55,66 Motor outcomes were typically assessed as a secondary endpoint in a minority of studies (n = 5).19,41,58,64,68

Study duration and follow-up periods varied substantially, ranging from brief interventions lasting approximately 4 weeks to longer programs with follow-up assessments up to 12 months.

CBT interventions were delivered using a wide range of formats, including individual and group sessions, in-person, telephone-based, internet-based, and telehealth modalities, often with adaptations specific to PD and, in some cases, with caregiver involvement (Figure 2).

Distribution of CBT studies in Parkinson's disease by clinical domain and intervention delivery format. Red circles, RCTs with a high risk of bias; yellow circles, RCTs with a moderate risk of bias; green circles, RCTs with a low risk of bias; grey circles, non-RCT studies.

A wide variety of outcome measures were used across studies, including clinician-rated scales (e.g., Hamilton Depression rating scale and Hamilton Anxiety rating scale, respectively HAM-D and HAM-A; Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale, UPDRS and its MDS sponsored version; Clinical Global Impression scale, CGI; Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA; Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE; Neuropsychiatric Inventory, NPI,), self-report questionnaires (Geriatric Anxiety Inventory, GAI; Geriatric Depression Scale, GDS-30; Beck Depression Inventory, BDI; Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7, GAD-7; Patient Health Questionnaire 9, PHQ-9; Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, HADS; Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire 39, PDQ-39), and disease-specific instruments (PDQ-39; Parkinson's Disease Sleep Scale, PDSS; Parkinson's Disease Anxiety Scale, PAS; Parkinson's Disease-Cognitive Rating Scale, PD-CRS; Questionnaire for Impulsive-Compulsive Disorders in Parkinson's Disease, QUIP), further contributing to methodological heterogeneity.

Given this substantial heterogeneity across study designs, interventions, and outcome measures, a quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate.

CBT for affective symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease focusing on depression

Former studies of CBT in PD were primarily designed for exploring its feasibility and efficacy in affective disorders. Pilot experiments by Cole and Vaughan 43 and Feeney et al., 44 demonstrated that both individual and group-based CBT interventions could be beneficial in PD, reducing depressive symptoms. In Cole and Vaughan, 43 GDS scores decreased from a range of 5–13 at baseline to 1–8 post treatment, alongside reductions in BDI-II scores (e.g., 32% reduction from 34 to 23), while Feeney et al., 44 reported clinically meaningful improvements in depression with shifts toward milder severity levels on the BDI-II.

Despite their small sample size and uncontrolled design (i.e., case series, before after design), both studies offered valuable proof-of-concept, showing that behavioral activation and cognitive restructuring can potentially improve abnormal mental processing and behavioral withdrawal, that characterize PwPD with depression.

In this landscape, two more case series supported a refinement of CBT content, setting and delivery.45,46 Noteworthy, these works emphasized the importance of caregiver inclusion into individualized CBT programs, improving patient compliance and therapeutic outcomes on depression, with 50% reductions in HAM-D and BDI scores, 45 and on depressive symptom severity and psychosocial adjustment (e.g., Self-Rating Depression Scale scores from 48 to 37). 46 Successive structured clinical trials confirmed such early results, supporting the therapeutic engagement of caregivers in CBT protocols as a key predictor of outcome.19,57 In the main RCT by Dobkin et al., CBT produced greater reductions in depressive symptoms (HAM-D mean from 7.35 to 0.05 at week 10). Post-hoc analyses identified caregiver participation and patient executive functioning as predictors of treatment response. At week 10, caregiver participation was the only significant predictor of CBT response (p = 0.012), while at week 14 both caregiver participation (p = 0.008) and executive functioning (Trail Making Test A-B: p = 0.029) predicted response. 72 Beyond patient selection, a subsequent secondary analysis of the same randomized trial further refined this framework by showing that CBT response differed across depressive clusters, and that stabilized antidepressant use moderated CBT-related improvement specifically in somatic depressive symptoms (HAMD somatic subscale Cohen's d = 0.77, p < 0.01).

The first controlled study for group-based CBT, was conducted by Troeung and colleagues 67 who conducted a waitlist-controlled trial, demonstrating large and sustained improvements in both depression and anxiety up to six months compared with clinical monitoring alone. At post-treatment, CBT participants showed greater reductions in depressive symptoms (DASS: mean change −2.45 vs +0.29; d = 1.12, p = 0.011) and anxiety (DASS: Cohen's d = 0.89, p = 0.0025), with effects increasing at 6-months follow-up (depression Cohen's d = 2.07; anxiety Cohen's d = 2.26).

Beyond mood symptoms, group CBT has also been associated with improvements in stress, emotional well-being, and non-motor symptoms, as shown in uncontrolled real-world studies 58 and in controlled comparisons versus psychoeducational interventions and other active health enhancement programs.20,68 Improvements have been reported in depressive and anxiety symptoms (appromiximately 29–30% reduction in HAMD and HAMA scores), along with redcutions in motor symptom severity (19% decrease in UPDRS scores). These findings are further supported by controlled comparisons with active interventions: group CBT has been shown to significantly reduce psychiatric symptoms (e.g., HAMA −30%, HAMD −22%) and improve non-motor symptoms relative to psychoeducational programs, with no comparable changes in control conditions and to improve stress and quality of life compared to health enhancement programs (e.g., PDQ-39 improvement, p = 0.03).

Noteworthy, group-based CBT demonstrated to transcend linguistic and cultural barriers while maintaining therapeutic integrity,21,69 with significant reductions in depression (BDI) and anxiety (BAI) (n = 90: F = 96.05, p < 0.05; n = 60: F = 94.22, p < 0.001), and sustained effects over time. Similarly, a culturally adapted CBT program showed high feasibility (95% completion rate) and large reductions in depressive symptoms (Hedges’ g = 1.02 post-treatment; −1.40 at 3-month follow-up), with 42% of participants achieving ≥ 50% reduction in GRID-HAMD scores). 59

Moreover, CBT proved effective even in frail PD population with elevated risk of reduced mobility (i.e., advanced PD). 51 To such extent, several studies adopted telephone or internet-based interventions with the aim of broadening CBT accessibility yet including caregivers in their study designs. For instance, in a 10-week telephone-based CBT program, Dobkin et al., 60 supplemented their intervention with caregiver support, achieving significant improvements in depressive symptoms (mean HAMD change −7.91 points; Cohen's d = 1.21, p < 0.001), along with reductions in anxiety and negative thinking, with high treatment adherence (95% completion). Internet-based CBT was associated moderate improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms (e.g., HADS: Cohen's d = 0.79, p < 0.05), 22 whereas others reported limited maintenance of benefits at follow-up (e.g., 3-month in guided self-help interventions.23,24

Nevertheless, RCTs on telephone- and video-based CBT and on their adaptation to scalable digital formats, provided class I evidence for CBT efficacy in PD depression and anxiety,25,26,56,61,62,73 with telephone-based CBT showing greater reductions in depressive symptoms than usual care (HAM-D: −6.53 vs. −0.27; p < 0.0001) and video-based CBT demonstrating significant improvements across depression outcomes (p < 0.001), with sustained effects over 6 months. Growing evidence, including open-label 63 and RCT27,28 studies, supported the use of videoconferences for the treatment of depression, and of anxiety as a secondary outcome. In particular, an iCBT RCT 27 showed that the intervention group improved significantly more than the control group, with greater reductions in depression (PHQ-9: 3.07 points, Hedges’g = 0.62) and anxiety (GAD-7:1.87 points, Hedges’ g = 0.41; p < 0.001), maintained at 3- month follow-up. Likewise, group CBT 28 was associated with a larger reduction in depressive symptoms (BDI: 9.86 to 5.06 vs. 9.45 to 8.87; p ≤ 0.01). Digital CBT delivery also enabled scalable and standardized interventions for neurological disorders, with open trial 29 reporting large within-group reductions in depression (Cohen's d = 0.93) and anxiety (Cohen's d = 0.66).

More recently, the integration of new technologies (e.g., immersive virtual reality) into CBT protocols was associated with significant improvements in emotional well-being and quality of life compared with control conditions, including reductions in emotional distress (HADS total score: −7.2 points, p < 0.001) and improvements in quality of life (PDQ-39: + 12.5%, p = 0.002). 76 Similarly, other interventions showed significant reductions in depression (BDI: 9.86 to 5.06 vs. 9.45 to 8.87; p ≤ 0.01) and anxiety (HADS: F = 17.8, p < 0.001) alongside improvements in quality of life.30,31

CBT for affective symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease focusing on anxiety

A multicenter RCT demonstrated greater improvements with CBT compared to clinical monitoring, with a reduction in Parkinson Anxiety Scale (PAS) scores of 9.9 points in the CBT groups versus 5.2 points in controls (p = 0.012), with effects maintained at 3- and 6-month follow-up. 32 A modified mindfulness-based cognitive therapy did not lead to a significant reduction in anxiety at the group level (F = 0.688; p = 0.411). However, reliable change analyses showed that a greater proportion of participants experienced individual improvement in anxiety compared with the waitlist control (47% vs. no change). 33

Case-based study combining CBT with cognitive enhancement reported reductions in anxiety (HAM-A 34 to 24 post-treatment), with sustained improvement at follow-up. 52 Similarly, a case series study reported significant reductions in anxiety with decreases of 6 points on the Overall Anxiety Severity and Impairment Scale (OASIS), 10 points on the BAI, and up to 30 points on the STAI-S based on the reliable change indices (95% CI), with effects maintained at 6-week follow-up. 47

Finally, the concomitant use of non-invasive brain stimulation reported greater improvements than psychological treatment alone, with anxiety scores (HAMA) -decreasing from 22.10 ± 1.02 to 3.11 ± 0.36 in the combined group versus 21.21 ± 0.64 to 8.02 ± 0.11 in controls (p < 0.001), alongside higher overall response rates (92% vs 70%). 34

Several studies reported additional improvements in secondary domains. Significant post-treatment improvements were observed in motor disability (t = 2.41, p = 0.04) and cognitive functioning (t = −2.92, p = 0.01), alongside a reduction in caregiver burden (t = 2.68, p = 0.03), with effects maintained at follow-up. 64 Improvements in quality of life were also reported compared clinical monitoring alone when CBT was delivered as an add-on to standard levodopa treatment. 70 CBT-related anxiety improvements was also associated with functional brain changes in PD-related anxiety circuits compared with clinical monitoring alone (F = 4.58; p = 0.04). 35

Qualitative case-based evidence suggested concurrent clinically relevant improvements in sleep and speech difficulties associated to anxiety (PA: 13 to 2). 53 Reductions in caregiver emotional burden were also reported, with small-to-moderate post-treatment (GDS-15: Cohen's d = 0.90; GAI: d = 0.36).36,64

CBT for sleep disorders

The first proof-of-concept study on CBT for insomnia in PD was provided by Yang H. and Petrini M. 65 Authors evaluated an individuallydelivered CBT intervention for insomnia in PD, improvements in subjective sleep quality and insomnia severity. These included reductions in sleep-onset latency (48.0 to 27.2 min), wake after sleep onset (53.1 min to 29.1 min) and increases in total sleep time (232.7 min to 300.5 min) and sleep efficiency (46% to 66.1%), maintained at 3-month follow-up (p < 0.01).

A subsequent RCT compared CBT-based intervention (CBT combined with bright light therapy), low-dose doxepin, and placebo. 37 While pharmacological treatment yielded the strongest short-term improvements, CBT-based therapy also produced significant reductions in insomnia severity (Insomnia Severity Index, ISI: −7.8 vs, −2.0; p = 0.03) and improvements in clinician-rated global impression (p = 0.006), although no significant changes were observed in other subjective sleep measures or actigraphy outcomes. Importantly, none of them resulted in a significant change in objective sleep metrics, highlighting a subjective-objective dissociation that would recur across subsequent studies. This limitation was partially addressed by Lebrun et al., 48 who employed a single-case experimental design, demonstrating a clear, treatment-linked reduction in insomnia severity (ISI: 18.4 to 8.1 post-treatment; 6.0 at follow-up), sleep efficiency (70.1% to 89.6%), and total wake time (128.3 to 34.3 min), alongside reductions in anxiety (PAS: 20.0 to 12.5) and depressive symptoms (BDI: 13.2 to 8.5), with effects maintained at 3-month follow-up.

Other work focused on feasibility and format of delivering. Case series, such as the one reported by Humber et al., 49 reported improvements in sleep efficiency (p = 0.02) and reductions in nocturnal awakenings (p = 0.02) despite clinical profiles of enrolled subjects being heterogeneous. In contrast, the randomized pilot study by Patel et al., 38 demonstrated that computerized CBT improved insomnia among treatment completers (ISI: −7.9 vs. −3.5; p = 0.03), whereas the intention-to-treat analysis was not significant (−4.5 vs. −3.3; p = 0.48), likely reflecting the high dropout rate in the intervention group (43%). More recently, Osawa et al., 50 evaluated a brief two-session CBT protocol tailored to PD, reporting improvements in subjective insomnia severity (Sheehan Disability Scale, SDS: 13.9 to 8.82; p = 0.01) while objective sleep measures remained largely unchanged.

CBT and impulse control disorders (ICDs) in Parkinson's disease

In their study, Jiménez-Murcia et al., 71 observed that PwPD with pathological gambling could engage in a structured CBT program, and compared their treatment outcomes with matched pathological gamblers without PD. Authors found no significant differences between PD with pathological gambling and matched pathological gamblers without PD in response to CBT, including dropout and relapse rates, despite PD patients being older, showing later gambling onset, and more frequently reporting bingo playing.

The first controlled evidence in PD-specific ICD treatment was provided by Okai et al., 39 whose RCT showed that CBT significantly reduced the global severity compared with standard medical care alone (CGI mean: 4.0 to 2.5 vs. 3.7 to 3.5; p < 0.001) with parallel improvement in neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPI total score: p = 0.03. At 6 months, 44% of treated patients no longer met QUIP criteria for any ICB, compared with 29% of controls, and anxiety and depression also improved, whereas caregiver burden and distress did not significantly change.

In a subsequent post hoc analysis, 74 regression models identified fewer ICDs, lower dopaminergic medication load, better social functioning, and lower severe neuropsychiatric severity. These predictors explained about 21.5% of (Clinical Global Impression- global clinical outcome domain) CGI-C variance and 22.3% of NPI change variance.

More recent exploratory work has emphasized innovation and personalization. In the case report by Mena-Moreno et al., 54 after 20 CBT sessions plus 15 e-Estesia sessions, the patient showed improved emotion regulation, as shown by reductions in difficulties in emotion regulation (DERS impulse-control scores decresing from t = 73 to t = 56) and increased use of adaptive strategies (ERQ reappraisal increasing from t = 75 to t = 53), while psychological regulation improved, with mean heart rate decreasing from 92 to 83.38 bpm and the Heart Rate Variability (HRV) increasing from 38.86 to 41.38 ms.

CBT for treatment of cognitive symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease

Analysis of affective-focused CBT trials have shown improvements of cognitive measures adopted as secondary outcomes. Dobkin et al., 75 showed that improvement in depression over 10 weeks was accompanied by gains in memory and executive functioning including total recall (p = 0.009) and recognition (p = 0.007), as well as executive functioning, measured by inhibition (p = 0.02). More recently, Dhanusia et al., 40 extended CBT-based interventions to PwPD and dementia, reporting pre-post improvements on global cognitive measures (MMSE: 18.47 ± 2.29 to 27.13 ± 2.60; p=<0.001) and PD-specific cognition (PD-CRS: 86.47 ± 3.96 to 97.00 ± 4.97; p = 0.001)) in the CBT group. The aerobic exercise group also improved on MMSE (18.14 ± 2.77 to 23.00 ± 2.52; p < 0.001) and PD-CRS (88.07 ± 4.71 to 92.07 ± 5.12; p = 0.034), while post treatment between-group differences favored CBT for both MMSE and PD-CRS (p < 0.001).

CBT for treatment of motor symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease

In a RCT primarily targeting depression, Dobkin et al., 19 reported a significant between-group difference in PD symptom ratings, measured with the UPDRS total score, favoring CBT (mean change −5.57 vs. + 1.64; p = 0.02; Cohen's d = 0.41).

In an 8-week inpatient multidisciplinary rehabilitation program, Monticone et al., 41 reported improvements in both groups on MDS-UPDRS part III, with greater improvement in the experimental group (between-group difference of 25 points) maintained at 12-month follow-up. Subsequent CBT-focused studies showed reported mixed findings on motor symptoms. In an uncontrolled study, Dissanayaka et al., 64 observed before-after improvements in motor disability (p = 0.04) with benefit maintained at follow-up occurring at 3 and 6-months. In an open group-CBT study, Berardelli et al., 58 reported an approximately 19% reduction in total UPDRS III scores after 12 weeks of group CBT, which did not reach statistical significance. These findings were not replicated in a subsequent controlled study, in which Berardelli et al., 68 found no between-group differences in UPDRS Part III in either the CBT group (18.1 ± 7.9 to 17.1 ± 7.4) or the psychoeducational group (18.4 ± 3.6 to 19.5 ± 5.9).

CBT for daily functioning and quality of life in people with Parkinson's disease

In their multidisciplinary rehabilitation setting, Monticone and colleagues, 41 showed that an 8-week program combining physical, occupational and cognitive-behavioral strategies, produced sustained improvements in activities of daily living and quality of life, with superior outcomes in the experimental group compared to controls. Functional Independence Measure (FIM) scores showed between-group differences of 19 points after treatment and 25 points at follow-up. Similarly, PDQ-39 domains showed mean improvements in the experimental group, including activities of daily living (−19.6 to −24.9), mobility (−14.1 to −15.4), and emotional well-being (−14.8 to −19.8), also improved significantly in favor of the experimental group.

A more targeted functional approach was adopted by Kraepelien et al., 77 who tested individualized internet-delivered CBT with daily functioning as the primary outcome. iCBT significantly improved Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS) scores compared with standard medical treatment alone (between-group difference −4.56; Hedges’ g = 0.69; p = 0.001). Approximately one-third of treated participants met responder criteria, compared with 10% of controls. Similarly, Gandy et al., 62 integrated CBT with compensatory cognitive rehabilitation strategies in a transdiagnostic neurological sample (including PD) with an online program. In this open trial, significant within-group reductions in disability (≥23%) were observed at post-intervention and maintained at follow-up, alongside improvements in perceived cognitive (PDQ) difficulties, particularly in attention (mean: 2.24 to 2.07 at post-treatment and 1.95 at follow-up), planning (mean: 2.02 to 1.88 and 1.79) and prospective memory (mean:1.63 to 1.47 and 1.50).

More recently, Ji et al., 70 reported that, CBT combined with clinical monitoring led to greater improvements compared with clinical monitoring alone. Anxiety and depression scores decreased (HAM-A: 23.70 ± 6.55 to 11.88 ± 3.90; HAM-D: 19.19 ± 5.21 to 11.08 ± 3.37), while cognitive measures and quality of life improved (MMSE: 13.70 ± 7.78 to 22.10 ± 4.50; MoCA: 10.88 ± 3.39 to 24.27 ± 3.67; SF-36: 30.00 ± 10.49 to 63.22 ± 12.80) . Additionally, fewer falls were reported during a 5-month follow-up in the CBT group.

CBT for caregivers of people with Parkinson's disease

Preliminary randomized controlled trial 42 reported that an individual CBT intervention delivered over approximately 3 months led to substantial reductions in global psychological distress, compared with no-treatment control (GHQ-28 total score: −20.7 ± 14.5 vs −6.8 ± 13.9; p < 0.01), as well as decreases in caregiver burden (CBI: −29.8 ± 20.1 vs −1.9 ± 14.6; p < 0.001) and caregiver strain (CSI:−4.1 ± 4.2 vs −0.3 ± 1.3; p < 0.01), with improvements largely maintained at 3-month follow-up. In a more recent uncontrolled pilot study, Mosley et al., 66 evaluated an 8-session manualized CBT program for spousal caregivers of PwPD treated with deep brain stimulation, reporting significant reductions in caregiver burden (ZBI: 41.1 ± 10.6 to 33.6 ± 13.6; p = 0.017) and anxiety (GAI 8.8 ± 6.1 to 5.8 ± 5.1; p = 0.020) at post-treatment and maintained at 3-month. In contrast, improvements in caregiver quality of life (PDQ-Carer: 45. ± 15.8 to 38.0 ± 15.0) were not sustained over time.

Finally, Plys and colleagues 55 described a single-case study of a spousal caregiver of a patient with advanced PD treated with an individual CBT-based intervention integrated within a Caregiver Family Therapy framework. The intervention consisted of over 20 weekly sessions, followed by two additional sessions at 6 weeks. Although findings are not generalizable, CBT was associated with clinically meaningful reductions in caregiver burden (ZBI-12 = −20), depressive symptoms (GDS-30 = −10) and anxiety (GAI = −6) across treatment and booster sessions.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 2). Overall, the risk of bias across studies was variable.

While some trials were judged to have a low risk of bias in specific domains, a substantial proportion of studies presented either some concerns or high risk of bias. The most frequent limitations were related to the randomization process, which was often insufficiently described, and to deviations from the intended interventions, particularly due to the inherent challenges of blinding in psychological interventions such as CBT. Additional concerns included small sample sizes and incomplete outcome data in several studies. In contrast, the risk of bias in outcome measurement was generally lower, although still variable across studies.

Overall, these methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may have influenced the estimated effects. No studies were excluded based on the results of the critical appraisal, as the aim of this review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence. Studies of varying methodological quality were retained and interpreted with appropriate caution, and these limitations were reflected in the GRADE assessment. Overall, according to this approach, CBT studies showed the most robust strength of evidence for depression (moderate certainty supported by RCTs). Studies on anxiety, sleep, ICD, quality of life, and caregiver outcomes were featured by inconsistent evidence of low certainty, being of very low quality for cognitive and motor outcomes, mainly due to small samples, heterogeneous designs, and sparse data.

Discussion

This review summarizes over two decades of research on CBT in PD, documenting its evolution from early feasibility studies to structured, technology-supported and mechanism-informed interventions.19,40,43,44,54,62 Noteworthy, progresses of CBT showed that the theoretical switch from a disease-specific intervention to cross-cutting, system-oriented models has resulted in flexible and clinically meaningful approaches addressing the psychological, behavioral and relational complexity of PD across symptom domains.3,17,18 CBT protocols included caregivers, implemented the adoption of group formats and embraced strategies of digital delivery across various level of evidence (Figure 2; Supplementary Table 3).19,39,62,77 Such a stratified approach may enhance the personalization of care and, in some cases, contribute to the optimization of complex treatment plans by addressing behavioral and emotional drivers of symptom's burden.62,70,77 These findings also reflect the increasing diversification of CBT delivery formats, including digital, group-based, and caregiver-inclusive interventions, aimet at improving accessibility and real-world applicability.

Even if several questions are still open, it is already possible to contextualize CBT interventions in PD, identifying which patients, when and how they can benefit of such an approach.

Who should be selected for receiving CBT?

The reviewed literature identified CBT targets and prognostic factors in PD, including determinants of treatment adherence. Although early studies primarily focused on depression and anxiety, CBT benefits were often observed beyond the primary symptom domain.48,64,75 The frequent co-occurrence of anxiety, depression and insomnia supports the relevance of CBT approaches targeting shared mechanisms that address maladaptive illness beliefs, avoidance behaviors and emotional dysregulation in PD.3,5 Addressing multiple symptom domains within a unified therapeutic framework may enhance clinical efficiency and relevance compared with strictly disorder-specific protocols. Accordingly, CBT techniques can be combined to tackle symptoms of PwPD from different perspectives (Figure 3; Supplementary Table 4). CBT is typically delivered as an adjunct therapy to pharmacological management and multidisciplinary care, enabling psychological interventions to be tailored to symptom profiles, cognitive resources and disease stage.39,41,60,77 Moreover, treatment response may vary across depressive symptom clusters and may be influenced by concurrent pharmacological treatment, suggesting potential interactions between psychological and pharmacological approaches.

CBT techniques and intervention programs across clinical domains.

Adherence and efficacy can be partly anticipated. Evidence from a seconday analyses suggests that caregiver participation and patient exectuvie functioning may act as key predictors of treatment response, highlighting the relevance of both cognitive resources and relational context in optimizing CBT outcomes. Particularly, preserved executive functioning and caregiver involvement have been identified as modifiers of treatment response, whereas reduced cognitive flexibility or prominent apathy may interfere with engagement in cognitively demanding or self-initiated CBT strategies.40,67,72 Nevertheless, adapted CBT-informed interventions appear feasible even in cognitively impaired or advanced patients.40,51 However, greater and more stable effects are reported in patients with earlier disease stages or in samples with lower neuropsychiatric complexity, particularly for anxiety, depression and impulse control disorders.32,60,72,74

Across studies, reduced adherence and drop-outs were associated with disease burden, cognitive difficulties, apathy, mobility limitations and technological barriers.23,24,38,77 Therapist guidance, flexible formats, caregiver involvement, and technology-supported delivery may improve engagement and sustain effects.22,23,54,60 Technology-enhanced approaches, including immersive and digital CBT formats, may further improve accessibility and clinical effectiveness.30–32,76 In particular, findings from internet-based interventions suggest that therapist guidance and caregiver involvement may play a role in sustaining long-term treatment effects.22–24

PD is inherently relational and caregivers often act as therapeutic partners supporting behavioral change,19,39,67,71 while also experiencing psychological burden that can be reduced by CBT-based and psychoeducation interventions.42,55,66,78 These findings support the inclusion of caregiver-focused strategies within stratified and integrated CBT-based models of PD care. Although evidence remains limited, available studies suggest that CBT may reduce caregiver distress and burden, supporting the integration of caregiver-focused interventions within the PD management.42,55,66

Which is the right moment to prescribe CBT to a person with PD?

Available evidence suggests that CBT is not restricted to a specific stage of PD but may fulfill different clinical functions across the disease trajectory. In earlier phases, CBT primarily targets affective symptoms, maladaptive illness beliefs and anticipatory threat, thereby supporting psychological adjustment to diagnosis.5,60 In more advanced stages, therapeutic goals tend to shift toward emotional regulation, coping with disability and maintenance of daily functioning, often within caregiver-inclusive or multidisciplinary care frameworks.41,77

Although current evidence does not allow firm conclusions regarding the optimal timing of CBT, the available studies suggest that CBT may be relevant beyond affective symptom management, addressing psychological processes associated with diagnostic uncertainty and disease progression. While no reviewed studies explicitly focused on de novo PD or specific disease milestones such as the onset of motor fluctuations, interventions targeted mechanisms that are particularly salient during early and transitional phases, including anticipatory threat, maladaptive illness interpretations, behavioral withdrawal, and impaired coping.51,60 In this context, CBT may help patients to organize their experience and to build up psychological meaning around emerging symptoms, facilitating adaptation rather than symptom reduction alone.5,68

How long does the effect of CBT last?

Follow-up periods are heterogeneous and generally limited, available studies suggest that CBT-related improvements in depression and anxiety are typically maintained at short-to medium-term follow-up (3–6 months).19,32,60 CBT appears to buffer the psychological impact of worsening motor and non-motor symptoms over time.20–37,47,48,51–53,56,59–65,68–70,73,76 Nevertheless, evidence on long-term maintenance and relapse prevention remains limited, highlighting the need for booster strategies and longitudinal designs.

How does CBT work in PD?

CBT efficacy in PD appears to be mediated by its effects on transversal disease mechanisms, including maladaptive illness beliefs, avoidance behaviors, emotional dysregulation, and reduced self-efficacy. 3 Early studies highlighted the role of behavioral activation and cognitive restructuring in addressing maladaptive cognitive processing and behavioral withdrawal in patients with PD and depression.43,44

This transdiagnostic framework is particularly relevant in PD, where non-motor symptoms frequently co-occur rather than presenting in isolation.3–5 By targeting dysfunctional symptom interpretations, anticipatory threat and maladaptive behavioral responses, CBT may disrupt self-reinforcing cycles linking anxiety, depression, insomnia and functional withdrawal.19,50,51 This may also explain the observed improvements across secondary domains, including sleep, cognitive functioning and caregiver-related outcomes.37,41,48,62,64,65,77 Consistently with this transdiagnostic perspective, preliminary evidence suggests that combining CBT with cognitive enhancement strategies may further improve anxiety outcomes. 47

From a mechanistic and translational perspective, connectomics research provides a promising framework for contextualizing CBT effects in PD. In non-PD populations, functional connectivity within large-scale brain networks has been shown to predict individual response to CBT in mood and anxiety-related disorders.79,80 In PD, cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms are consistently associated with alterations in prefrontal, limbic, hippocampal and basal ganglia networks and their interaction with motor systems.81,82 Emerging connectome-based approaches further suggest that individual symptom profiles and cognitive status in PD may be inferred from patterns of network organization and connectivity. 83

Evidence for CBT effects beyond affective symptoms highlights the broad clinical applicability of CBT across multiple non-motor domains, including functional (e.g., activities of daily living) outcomes and caregiver burden.41,42,55,62,64,66,77,78 CBT for insomnia reliably improves subjective sleep quality and daytime functioning, despite limited effects on objective sleep measures were observed, highlighting its role in reducing sleep-related distress and maladaptive beliefs.37,48,50,65 These findings support the role of maladaptive thoughts and behaviors in maintaining sleep disturbances in PD.

In ICDs, CBT appears feasible and clinically meaningful as an adjunct therapy to pharmacological treatment, particularly when implemented early in time and with caregiver involvement, with stronger effects observed in patients with lower disorder complexity and dopaminergic burden.39,71,74

Regarding CBT effects on cognitive outcomes in PD, improvements in cognition – reported as a secondary outcome in affective-focused CBT trials – suggest that affective burden may limit cognitive efficiency, and that targeting emotional processes may indirectly support cognitive functioning in PD. 54 More recent CBT-informed interventions have begun to directly target cognition in advanced PD populations although findings remain preliminary. 40 When embedded within multidisciplinary programs, CBT may also contribute to improvements in daily functioning and perceived cognitive difficulties, likely through indirect pathways involving emotional regulation, self-management and behavioral adaptation, but also including through technology-enhanced and multidisciplinary interventions.41,62,77 Consistent with its theoretical framework, CBT does not appear to exert direct effects on motor symptoms and observed functional gains likely reflect downstream psychological and behavioral mechanisms rather than motor change per se, and findings across studies remain inconsistent.58,64,68 Consistently, evidence on motor outcomes remains heterogeneous and less robust compared to non-motor domains.

Conclusion

From a clinical perspective, CBT represents a feasible and adaptable intervention for addressing non-motor symptoms in PD, particularly depression, for which the most consistent evidence is available. Clinicians may consider CBT as a part of a multidisciplinary management strategy for PwPD and caregivers including its delivery in individual, group, or remote formats, and with the involvement of caregivers when appropriate.5,19,32,68

However, given the variability in the certainty of evidence across domains,5,19,32,62,68,77 CBT should be applied with caution for symptoms such as anxiety, sleep disturbances, ICDs and cognitive outcomes, where evidence remains limited. Tailoring interventions to patient characteristics, disease stages and specific symptom targets is likely to enhance clinical effectiveness.

Looking forward, emerging approaches within the broader framework of third-wave behavioral therapies, such as acceptance and commitment therapy and mindfulness-based interventions, may offer complementary strategies by targeting psychological flexibility, acceptance, and values-based behavior. 84 Future research should explore the integration of these approaches with traditional CBT to optimize outcomes in PwPD.
