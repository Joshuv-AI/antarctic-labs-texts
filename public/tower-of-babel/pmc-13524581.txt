Endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery for staghorn calculus in a horseshoe kidney with double collecting system: A case report
Authors: Daniel Amartya, Wiliam Kristofer Gilbert Prionggo, Kevin Yuwono, Widi Atmoko, Dyandra Parikesit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Horseshoe kidney (HSK) with a duplicated collecting system and a bifid ureter is a unique surgical challenge. We report the case of a 43-year-old male patient with a left HSK, duplicated collecting system, bifid ureter, staghorn calculus in the lower moiety, and proximal ureteral stone. The procedure of mini-endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery (mECIRS) with mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (mini-PCNL) was successfully performed, resulting in minimal blood loss and no intraoperative complications. This case exemplifies the feasibility of employing mECIRS with a single-tract, tubeless mini-PCNL, combined with flexible ureteroscopy, to achieve complete stone clearance in a rare and challenging anatomical context.

Introduction

Horseshoe kidney is the most common congenital kidney fusion anomaly, occurring in approximately 1 in 400 to 600 individuals, with a higher prevalence in males. It results from the fusion of the lower poles of the kidneys during fetal development and is frequently associated with abnormal kidney rotation, altered vascular anatomy, and variations in ureteral insertion. These anatomical deviations can impair urinary drainage, predisposing patients to complications such as hydronephrosis, urinary tract infections, and nephrolithiasis. The incidence of kidney stones in patients with horseshoe kidneys is reported to be significantly higher than in the general population, reaching up to 60%.1,2 In even rarer instances, a horseshoe kidney may be accompanied by a duplicated pelvicalyceal system or bifid ureter, further complicating urinary flow and presenting unique challenges in both diagnosis and surgical management.3

We report a case involving a patient with a left horseshoe double collecting system kidney, complicated by a large staghorn calculus occupying the lower moiety and a proximal ureteral stone. Although both horseshoe kidney and duplicated collecting systems have been reported individually in the literature, their coexistence is exceedingly rare. Only a few case reports have described this anatomical combination, and even fewer have discussed its implications in the context of stone disease.4,5 The use of mini-endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery (mECIRS) for stone clearance in this setting has not been previously documented to our knowledge. The decision to proceed with mECIRS in the Galdakao-modified supine Valdivia position was made to allow simultaneous antegrade and retrograde access, providing a strategic advantage in managing the complex anatomy and achieving complete stone clearance without the necessity of having multiple punctures in a single session.

This case is of particular interest because it demonstrates a safe and effective surgical approach for managing complex urolithiasis in a patient with a rare congenital anomaly. It also highlights the practical value of mECIRS in resource-limited settings, where access to advanced robotic or laparoscopic techniques may be unavailable. According to the 2025 European Association of Urology (EAU) Guidelines on Urolithiasis, the management of stones in anomalous kidneys should be individualized and guided by detailed imaging, taking into account the altered anatomy.6 ECIRS has been increasingly recognized as a versatile and effective approach in managing complex stones, especially in cases requiring dual access for complete clearance. While PCNL remains the standard for large-volume kidney stones, the combination with flexible ureteroscopy in ECIRS is gaining traction for its superior stone-free rates and lower complication profile in complex cases.7 This case adds to the limited body of literature and supports the role of mECIRS as a viable and adaptable technique in managing stone disease in patients with complex kidney anatomy. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist.8

Case presentation

A 43-year-old Indonesian male patient presented to our Urology Clinic with a 4-month history of intermittent left flank pain and recurrent urinary tract symptoms. Pain was non-radiating and occasionally associated with gross hematuria. There was no fever, dysuria, or relevant past medical history. He denied smoking, alcohol, or drug use, and family history was non-contributory. On examination, he was hemodynamically stable, afebrile, and in no acute distress. Abdominal and flank examinations were unremarkable. Urinalysis showed microscopic hematuria without pyuria; serum creatinine and electrolytes were normal. The patient was positioned in the Galdakao-modified supine Valdivia position under general anesthesia. Ureteroscopy using a 6 Fr semi-rigid ureteroscope was performed on the left ureter, revealing stones in the proximal ureter consistent with preoperative CT findings. A guidewire was placed and left as a safety wire. Retrograde pyelography with a double-lumen catheter demonstrated duplication of the left collecting system at the L3 level (Fig. 2). A guidewire was advanced into the lower moiety, and an 11/13 Fr ureteral access sheath (ClearPetra®, Well Lead Medical Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) was introduced to the proximal ureter (Fig. 3). Flexible ureterorenoscopy through the sheath confirmed multiple stones in the kidney pelvis. Initial dusting was performed with a Holmium:YAG laser (Litho EVO, Quanta System S. p.A., Samarate, Italy; dusting mode 0.3–0.5 J, 30–50 Hz), reducing stones into fine fragments. Percutaneous access to the upper pole of lower moiety of the left kidney was then obtained with a 17.5 G needle under combined fluoroscopic, ultrasonographic, and visual guidance from the retrograde side. The tract was sequentially dilated to 15 Fr, and Mini-PCNL was performed using a disposable cysto-nephroscope system (Guangzhou Redpine Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) with an 18 Fr ClearPetra® suction-evacuation sheath. The collecting system needed to be dilated using irrigation pressure from the RIRS side to create artificial hydronephrosis and facilitate puncture, as there is no hydronephrosis. The puncture also needed to be slightly angulated inferior-anteriorly since the space on the lower moiety is small (due to the pelvic bone and upper moiety). The same flexible ureteroscope was alternately used in retrograde and antegrade fashion, enabling access to both upper and lower moieties (Fig. 4). Due to the limited space within the collecting system and the weight of the upper moiety, the system collapsed immediately upon stone removal, resulting in a reduction of the available space and decreased navigability. Furthermore, the anteroinferior calyx of the lower moiety remained inaccessible because of the markedly acute infundibulopelvic angle and medially oriented calyceal anatomy. Fragmentation of larger stones was performed with the Ho:YAG laser in fragmentation mode (1.0–1.5 J, 15–20 Hz), supplemented with pneumatic lithotripsy as needed. Stone fragments were evacuated using suction and forceps. At the conclusion of the procedure, a 6 Fr double-J stent was placed in the left ureter. The procedure was completed tubeless without nephrostomy placement. Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable with mild flank pain (VAS 1–2) and complete resolution of hematuria by postoperative day one. A 24-h postoperative kidney–ureter–bladder radiograph showed a left double-J stent in situ, with no visible residual radiopaque stone and no nephrostomy tube, consistent with a tubeless procedure (Fig. 5A). Leukocytosis (20,640/μL) was noted in the absence of fever or other signs of infection and was managed conservatively with continued intravenous ceftriaxone and close monitoring. The patient was ambulating independently, tolerating oral intake, and voiding spontaneously by postoperative day one, and was discharged the same day in stable condition with oral antibiotics and analgesics. At the one-month follow-up, a non-contrast CT scan demonstrated residual fragments, with decreased stone burden in the left lower moiety (up to 7 mm) and no evidence of ureteral calculi (Fig. 5B and C). Discussion

Horseshoe kidney (HSK) is among the most common kidney fusion anomalies, whereas complete duplication of the collecting system is far rarer, occurring in only 0.2% of live births.1,9 HSK is strongly associated with urolithiasis, with a meta-analysis reporting a 36% incidence of kidney stones, predominantly in males. Stone formation is attributed to both anatomical and metabolic factors. Anatomical factors, including abnormal ureteral insertion, a high ureteropelvic junction, and medullary sponge kidney, impair urinary drainage and promote urinary stasis. Metabolic abnormalities, including hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia, and low urine volume, further increase the risk of stone formation.10 Moreover, duplication of the collecting system also associated with vesicoureteric reflux, ureteric obstruction, and renal scarring which may also elevate the risk of stone formation.11 The present case is unique for the exceedingly rare combination of HSK, a completely duplicated collecting system, bifid ureter, and staghorn calculi. Only a few such cases have been documented, and most were managed with conventional PCNL or URS rather than combined endourological strategies 3, 4, 5. This rarity forms the basis for reporting.

Traditionally, PCNL has been the preferred treatment for large calculi in HSK, whereas URS and ESWL are reserved for smaller stones.2 Yet, HSK anatomy complicates treatment: the pelvis is flatter, intrarenal spaces are narrower, the infundibulopelvic angle is sharper, and ureteral insertion is higher.12 Aberrant vasculature further complicates percutaneous access, with bleeding requiring transfusion reported in up to 20% of cases.13 Posterior upper-pole puncture is often the safest choice, providing alignment with the kidney pelvis and broad calyceal access, while minimizing pleural risk with a subcostal approach.14 In our patient, due to the nature of the duplicated system, the uppermost calyx of the lower moiety was chosen for access, while lower pole puncture was avoided due to its anteromedial displacement and vascular proximity.15

Endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery (ECIRS) combines antegrade and retrograde access and is most commonly performed in the Galdakao-modified supine Valdivia position.7 Compared with standard prone PCNL, it facilitates management of stones involving multiple moieties while avoiding multiple percutaneous tracts.5,16 In complex kidney stone cases, such as those involving anatomical anomalies, ECIRS can provide superior stone clearance with lower morbidity compared to PCNL alone. In this patient, ECIRS was particularly suitable because the stone burden extended across the upper calyx to the lower calyx of the lower moiety, necessitating dual access to achieve complete clearance while allowing access to most of the calyces in a single procedure.

Torricelli and Monga reported a similar case of a 2 cm renal stone in a horseshoe kidney with a right partial duplex collecting system. Complete stone clearance was achieved using similar approach.17 In contrast, our patient presented with a staghorn calculus occupying multiple calyces of the lower moiety, representing a substantially greater stone burden. Nevertheless, mECIRS enabled effective stone removal through a single percutaneous tract despite the increased complexity.

Our technique involved mini-PCNL with an 18 Fr suction sheath and a 35 W holmium laser. Studies has found that using mini-PCNL in anomalous kidney has a similar stone free rate while less bleeding complication, reduced postoperative pain and has a shorter hospital stays compared to the standard PCNL.18 A flexible and navigable ureteral access sheath was placed retrogradely under guidewire guidance to maintain low intrarenal pressure, provide continuous irrigation, and facilitate efficient fragment clearance. The sheath also allowed the “self-popping” evacuation technique, accelerating stone removal. The entire stone burden was managed through a single approximately 1 cm percutaneous incision and the procedure was completed tubeless without nephrostomy placement. The operation was performed as a two-surgeon mECIRS, but rather than simultaneous instrumentation, the surgeons alternated use of the equipment. The same flexible ureteroscope was used both retrograde through the ureteral access sheath and antegrade through the percutaneous tract. This cooperative strategy enabled access to nearly all calyces of the lower moiety, enhanced visualization, and facilitated fragment relocation via the “pass the ball” maneuver.19 A small residual stone was anticipated because the anteroinferior calyx could not be reached owing to its markedly acute infundibulopelvic angle and medially oriented anatomy. Historically, PCNL in HSK has been associated with complication rates up to 14%, including hematuria, fever without sign of sepsis, hydrohemothorax, and even colonic injury.20 In contrast, our patient had no significant complications. Postoperative events were classified using the Clavien–Dindo system, with no events beyond grade I (mild postoperative pain, no blood transfusion needed, and minimal residual stone), further highlighting the safety of this tailored approach. This case highlights the need for future guidelines to consider mECIRS as a preferred approach for complex stones in anomalous kidneys, given its superior stone clearance and lower morbidity compared to conventional PCNL.

Conclusion

This case highlights the successful management of a rare combination of horseshoe kidney, a duplicated collecting system, a bifid ureter, and staghorn calculi using mECIRS. Using the Galdakao-modified supine approach, mini-PCNL, a suction sheath, and a cooperative two-surgeon technique, effective stone clearance was achieved through a single tract without major complications. mECIRS offers a safe and effective alternative to multi-tract PCNL in anatomically complex cases, demonstrating the value of flexible, combined endourological strategies for optimizing outcomes.
