Coccidioidomycosis Testing Among Patients Hospitalized With Community-Acquired Pneumonia
Authors: Saima N Tisekar, Yehia S Aboalmaaty, Gretchen E Taylor, Janis E Blair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong 1141 inpatients from Arizona with community-acquired pneumonia, 865 (75.8%) were tested for Coccidioides infection and 85 (9.8%) had positive results. Patients more commonly underwent testing if they had concerning radiographs, fever, dyspnea, or fatigue. Patients with night sweats, rash, headache, or eosinophilia more commonly had positive results.

Coccidioidomycosis (also known as valley fever) is an infection caused by a dimorphic fungus, Coccidioides, and is endemic to the southwestern United States, Washington state, and Central and South America [1–3]. Coccidioidomycosis is an important cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), accounting for 15% to 30% of cases in endemic regions [4, 5]. Its symptoms often are indistinguishable from that of other causes of CAP, which can delay diagnosis and appropriate treatment [2, 6]. Coccidioidomycosis is often asymptomatic or self-limited; nevertheless, patients with immunosuppression or diabetes, and even otherwise healthy individuals, have risk of severe and disseminated disease [2, 7].

Although the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report 10 000 to 20 000 coccidioidomycosis cases annually, the true incidence is likely much higher because of (1) state-based differences in reporting practices and (2) probable missed diagnoses [8]. Recent quality improvement efforts have focused on increasing the rates of coccidioidomycosis testing among outpatients presenting to emergency departments [4], ambulatory settings [7], and urgent care facilities [9]. However, testing rates for hospitalized patients remain poorly characterized. We aimed to determine the rate and temporal trends of coccidioidomycosis testing and positivity among adult patients hospitalized for CAP at a tertiary care center in Maricopa County, Arizona, a highly endemic region.

METHODS

This study was exempted by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board because of its noninterventional nature and retrospective design. Only patients who authorized use of their health records for research were included in the study. All data were handled in accordance with institutional policies to ensure patient privacy and confidentiality.

We retrospectively reviewed records of adult inpatients with CAP, hospitalized from November 1, 2024, through October 31, 2025, at Mayo Clinic Hospital (Phoenix, Arizona), a tertiary care center. Patients were identified through an electronic search of diagnoses listed on the hospital admission sheet. Admissions with a text diagnosis of “pneumonia” at the time of hospital admission were eligible for the study. This approach was designed to identify the majority of patients hospitalized with unspecified CAP at their initial presentation. We included all adults (age ≥18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of CAP, defined as clinician-documented pneumonia (determined by clinical and radiographic findings). We excluded patients who did not have pneumonia.

We conducted a detailed review of a subgroup of sequential patients hospitalized between May 1 and October 31, 2025. From their health records, we abstracted admission dates, patient demographic characteristics, immunocompromised status, comorbid conditions, laboratory test results at hospital admission, antifungal treatments, and hospital length of stay (LOS). Eosinophilia was defined as an absolute eosinophil count of 480/µL or higher. We determined whether patients had a history of coccidioidomycosis and their current testing status (plus results, if available) for coccidioidomycosis and other microbiology tests; we accepted Coccidioides serologic testing performed by any method (including enzyme immunoassay for immunoglobulin [Ig] M and IgG, immunodiffusion screen [IgG or IgM], and complement fixation [IgG]). We abstracted the history and duration of symptoms before hospitalization, including fever, night sweats, headache, cough, shortness of breath, rash, chest pain, arthralgia, malaise, anorexia, and fatigue [3, 10]. We noted any consistent radiologic findings, such as consolidation with or without lymphadenopathy, cavitation, nodules, or effusions [11].

We used descriptive statistics to summarize patient characteristics and microbiologic findings. We compared categorical variables with χ2 or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. We summarized continuous variables as median (IQR) and compared them using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We assessed temporal trends in testing rates and positivity using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend. We used univariate logistic regression to evaluate associations between symptoms and coccidioidomycosis positivity among tested individuals; results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. We used multivariate logistic regression to study the association between LOS and Coccidioides test rate and between LOS and Coccidioides test positivity. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed with R, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

From November 1, 2024, through October 31, 2025, our hospital admitted 1141 patients for pneumonia (median [IQR] age, 73 [62–82] years); 56% were male. Of these patients, 865 (75.8%) underwent testing for coccidioidomycosis. Positivity rates for Coccidioides testing were highest from November to December 2024 and October 2025 (range, 13.0%–21.1%) and in March 2025 (15.3%), consistent with known seasonal variation of coccidioidomycosis [7]. Testing rates increased with time, ranging from 63.4% to 90.8% per month (P = .02) (Figure 1). A sensitivity analysis (excluding data from October 2025, which appeared to be an outlier) showed no linear trend (P = .36). Of the 865 patients tested, 85 (9.8%) were positive for coccidioidomycosis. No linear trend in test positivity was observed (P = .40).

Patients more commonly underwent coccidioidal testing when chest images suggested possible fungal or granulomatous infection (96.1% vs 77.2%; P = .002) (Supplementary Table 2) and when patients had fever (P = .008), shortness of breath (P = .01), or fatigue (P = .04) (Supplementary Table 3). Coccidioidal test positivity was more common for patients with a history of coccidioidomycosis (34.4% vs 6.5%; P < .001) (Supplementary Table 4) and when radiologists had concerns about a fungal infection (34.7% vs 5.0%; P < .001) (Supplementary Table 5). Patients with night sweats, rash, or headache were more likely to have a positive test result for Coccidioides (Supplementary Table 6), and median eosinophil counts were higher in coccidioidomycosis vs noncoccidioidal illness (0.17 [IQR, 0.09–0.36] cells/μL vs 0.04 [IQR, 0.01–0.15] cells/μL; P < .001).

Longer LOS was independently associated with higher odds of testing (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.10]). Patients with positive test results for coccidioidomycosis had a shorter LOS than those with negative results (OR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.02–1.81]).

DISCUSSION

According to a 2025 CDC study [8], the true number of coccidioidomycosis cases may be 10- to 18-fold higher than reported, owing to underreporting, misdiagnosis, and patients not seeking care; these factors potentially contribute to an estimated disease burden of $385 000 000. In the present study, we showed that undertesting may be an additional factor that obscures the true count of coccidioidal cases. Given the large gap between the reported and estimated incidence, an optimized testing strategy is crucial to ensure that cases are recognized. In our cohort, 75.8% of patients with CAP underwent coccidioidal testing; this rate is substantially higher than testing rates reported in outpatient, urgent care, and emergency department settings (range, 2%–21.5%) [7, 9]. The variability in coccidioidal testing across settings and institutions may reflect disease severity, institutional practices, educational interventions, and physician- or institution-specific factors [12].

We noted that 10% of tested patients had positive test results for Coccidioides. Other studies have reported even higher positivity rates, ranging from 15% to 29% [4, 7]. About a quarter of patients were not tested, and assuming a similar prevalence of coccidioidomycosis in this cohort, we estimate that 25 to 30 patients with coccidioidomycosis may have been missed. Missed diagnoses can potentially prolong symptoms and hospitalization, as well as unnecessarily exposing patients to antibacterial therapy or invasive diagnostic testing that may contribute to patient anxiety [7].

In our cohort, when Coccidioides was identified early as the cause of CAP, the patient's hospital LOS was shorter than that of a patient with noncoccidioidal CAP, even when accounting for other comorbid illnesses (other than diabetes, which can increase the severity of coccidioidal infections) [13]. A shorter LOS could be an institutional incentive to test all patients with CAP for coccidioidal infection.

Because Coccidioides was the most commonly identified CAP-causing organism in our cohort, we recommend that all patients with CAP undergo coccidioidal testing. However, one-third of our patients with coccidioidomycosis also had a second organism identified during their evaluation, suggesting that Coccidioides infection cannot be definitively ruled out when a viral or bacterial pathogen is detected. Consistent with these findings, the 2025 American Thoracic Society guidelines suggest empiric antibiotic treatment for adult inpatients with CAP (severe or nonsevere), even those with a confirmed viral respiratory infection [14]. Our data reflect this approach, with common pneumonia-causing organisms being identified less frequently, likely because patients were treated empirically with antibiotics. Nevertheless, in a similar study, confirmation of a Coccidioides infection did not decrease the duration of antibiotic treatment [15].

Some studies suggest that systemic symptoms, such as fatigue, arthralgias, or erythema nodosum, are much more predominant in coccidioidomycosis than other causes of CAP, and these symptoms may aid in recognition of this infection [3]. In our cohort, patients with fever, shortness of breath, or fatigue were more likely to get tested, and patients with night sweats, rash of any type, headache, hypoxia, or eosinophilia were more likely to have positive test results. These symptoms thus may aid in pretest identification of potentially infected patients.

We acknowledge several limitations that may affect generalization of findings. This study was conducted at a tertiary care facility, which treats many older patients with complex comorbid conditions. Our predominantly White population may not resemble the patient population at other institutions. Because of the observational nature of this study, causality between patient factors, testing, and positivity rates cannot be established. In addition, by primarily searching the hospital admission records for the text word “pneumonia” to identify those patients hospitalized for pneumonia, we may have missed identifying patients whose pneumonia was identified after they were admitted.

In summary, among patients hospitalized for CAP in an endemic region, Coccidioides was the most commonly identified pathogen. Future efforts should focus on further increasing testing rates.
