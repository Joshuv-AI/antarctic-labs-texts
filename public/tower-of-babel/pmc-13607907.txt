Do revision lumbar spine procedures lead to worse postoperative outcomes? A systematic review with a meta-analysis of patient-reported outcomes
Authors: Brendon Wang, Jake Lee, Irene Wang, Riya Vasantha Karne, Mohamed Zaazoue, Mark J. Lambrechts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Revision lumbar spine surgery is increasingly performed for recurrent disc herniation, adjacent segment disease, and pseudarthrosis, yet comparative outcomes with primary procedures remain incompletely characterized. This systematic review compared perioperative complications, patient-reported outcomes (PROs), and reoperation risk between primary and revision lumbar surgery.Materials and Methods:A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses-compliant systematic review of PubMed was conducted through July 28, 2025. Studies published after 2000 comparing adult patients undergoing primary versus revision lumbar surgery were included. Data extraction and quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale were performed independently by reviewers. Random-effects meta-analyses were conducted for outcomes reported in at least three studies.Results:Thirty-six studies met inclusion criteria, including lumbar fusion (n = 21), discectomy (n = 11), and complex deformity surgery (n = 4). Revision patients consistently had worse baseline pain and disability. In fusion cohorts, both groups improved postoperatively, but revision surgery was associated with worse absolute PROs and greater perioperative morbidity. Meta-analysis demonstrated a small advantage for primary surgery in 6-month Oswestry Disability Index scores (mean difference: 2.9, 95% confidence interval: 0.42–5.38), which was not maintained at 12 months. Revision discectomy was associated with higher complication and reoperation rates and poorer long-term PROs. Across procedure types, revision surgery demonstrated higher rates of infection, dural tears, thromboembolic events, and blood transfusion.Conclusions:Revision lumbar surgery is associated with greater perioperative risk and lower absolute functional outcomes than primary procedures, although most patients achieve meaningful postoperative improvement. Careful patient selection and preoperative expectation management remain essential.Level of Evidence:III

INTRODUCTION

Low back pain represents a substantial global health burden, with surgical intervention frequently required for conditions including lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and degenerative spondylolisthesis. While primary lumbar surgery has demonstrated efficacy in carefully selected patients, with sustained improvements in pain and function extending to 8 years postoperatively, a significant proportion of patients require revision procedures.[1234] The incidence of revision surgery ranges from 10% to 40% following lumbar laminectomy, with recurrent disc herniation, adjacent segment disease, pseudarthrosis, and recurrent stenosis representing the most common indications.[15]

The comparative outcomes between primary and revision lumbar surgery remain incompletely characterized, with existing literature presenting conflicting findings regarding complication rates, patient-reported outcomes (PROs), and reoperation risk. Several studies have reported that revision procedures are associated with increased surgical site infections, neurologic complications, unfavorable discharge, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism, while others found comparable complication profiles between cohorts.[678910] However, the magnitude of these differences and their clinical significance across different surgical procedures and patient populations requires systematic evaluation.

Understanding the different outcomes between primary and revision lumbar surgery carries important implications for patient counseling, surgical decision-making, and healthcare resource allocation. Patients undergoing revision procedures may face distinct risk profiles for perioperative complications, including surgical site infections, dural tears, and thromboembolic events. In addition, the extent to which revision surgery achieves meaningful improvements in pain, disability, and quality of life compared to primary procedures remains an area of active investigation, with some studies suggesting comparable outcomes while others demonstrate inferior results in the revision cohort.[57811121314]

This systematic review aims to comprehensively evaluate whether patients undergoing revision lumbar surgery experience worse outcomes than those undergoing primary lumbar surgery. By synthesizing the available evidence, this review seeks to provide clinicians with evidence-based guidance for preoperative risk stratification, informed consent discussions, and optimization of surgical outcomes in patients requiring revision lumbar surgery. A meta-analysis of PROs will also better define patient’s experiences following primary and revision lumbar surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, but was not registered in PROSPERO, and did not require institutional review board approval because only previously published studies were analyzed. A comprehensive literature search of PubMed was performed from database inception through July 28, 2025 using the following query: (lumbar) AND (surgery OR fusion OR decompression OR laminectomy OR discectomy) AND (primary OR “index surgery”) AND (“revision surgery” OR reoperation OR redo). Studies published prior to 2000 were excluded to ensure contemporary surgical practices. Reference lists of all eligible studies were also manually reviewed to identify any additional manuscripts that satisfied the criteria.

Study eligibility

Studies were included if they evaluated adult patients undergoing primary lumbar spine surgery and reported at least one relevant postoperative outcome involving revision surgery, reoperation, or redo procedures. Eligible study designs consisted of randomized controlled trials, prospective comparative studies, and retrospective cohort studies or case series with at least 10 patients per treatment group. Studies were excluded if they were case reports, single-arm studies without a comparison group, conference abstracts or posters lacking full-text availability, investigations with fewer than 10 patients per group, studies involving skeletally immature patients, or animal or cadaveric models. Articles not written in or translated into English were also excluded. All studies underwent independent screening by two reviewers (B.W., J.L.) using Covidence, and discrepancies were resolved via a tiebreaker (B.W., J.L.). Data collection and quality assessment Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (B.W., J.L.) using standardized data templates, and all disagreements were validated and resolved by a third reviewer (I.W.).

Data collection and quality assessment

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (Blinded for Review) using standardized data templates, and all disagreements were validated and resolved by a third reviewer (Blinded for Review). Extracted data included study design, cohort characteristics, surgical procedure type, follow-up duration, and clinical outcomes. Additional clinical variables were collected when available. Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies, with a Newcastle–Ottawa score of six or greater considered indicative of high quality.

Given the marked variability in study populations, outcome measures, and surgical techniques across the included literature, only a restricted meta-analysis was feasible. A meta-analysis was planned when at least three studies reported the same outcome using comparable definitions. When appropriate, pooled estimates were generated using random-effects models, and statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

The initial database search yielded 1046 records for screening. After title and abstract screening, 1003 studies were excluded as irrelevant, leaving 43 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Of these, 7 studies were excluded. Ultimately, 36 studies met inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis [Figure 1]. Lumbar fusion

Twenty-one studies compared outcomes following primary and revision lumbar fusion [Table 2]. Revision cohorts demonstrated greater baseline pain, disability, and deformity severity across nearly all series. Significant postoperative improvement occurred in both groups in most investigations, although revision surgery was commonly associated with inferior absolute PROs at follow-up. Smaller institutional and propensity-adjusted cohorts frequently reported comparable perioperative outcomes and similar magnitudes of improvement, whereas large administrative datasets more consistently identified increased morbidity and resource utilization among revision procedures.

Evidence from prospective and matched analyses indicates that meaningful clinical benefit remains achievable after revision fusion. In a multicenter study of 722 primary and 333 revision cases, Carreon et al.[15] observed significant improvement in Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), 36-Item short-form health survey physical component summary, and back and leg pain at 1 year in both cohorts (P < 0.0001), with similar minimum clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds. Mohanty et al., using propensity matching in transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) for degenerative spondylolisthesis, likewise found no differences in 1-year pain, disability, complication, or reoperation rates.[12] Comparable findings were reported in minimally invasive populations. Khechen et al. demonstrated similar perioperative complications and postoperative PROs between 26 primary and 26 revision Minimally invasive surgery with a TLIF patients despite worse baseline disability in the revision group.[13] Kang et al. observed equivalent 1-year ODI and visual analog scale (VAS) improvement,[24] although durotomy occurred more frequently in revisions, and Selznick et al. reported similar operative time and blood loss with higher minor complication rates in revision surgery driven primarily by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak.[28]

Despite similar relative gains, multiple studies demonstrated persistent differences in outcome status. With a mean 5-year follow-up after minimally invasive posterior lumbar interbody fusion, Hentenaar et al. reported inferior ODI (30.4% vs. 14.4%, P = 0.003) and EuroQol-5 dimensions scores (P = 0.04) in revision patients despite comparable pain reduction.[21] Jung et al. found worse ODI and VAS outcomes at 6 and 12 months after revision oblique lumbar interbody fusion (ODI: 10.27 vs. 14.68 at 6 months, P < 0.001; 8.97 vs. 12.74 at 12 months, P < 0.001; VAS leg pain: 2.47 vs. 3.79 at 6 months, P < 0.001), with revision status independently associated with poorer disability.[23] In the CHARITÉ lumbar disc arthroplasty investigational device exemption cohort, Geisler et al. demonstrated smaller improvements in ODI (mean change: −53.0% primary vs. −12.7% revision) and pain VAS (−59.1% vs. −23.4%) at 2 years among patients requiring revision (P < 0.0001).[20] In a multicenter registry of more than 4600 cases, Lambrechts et al. identified revision surgery as an independent predictor of worse postoperative PRO measures (PROMs), including higher 12-month ODI (32.6 vs. 23.6, P < 0.001), higher VAS leg pain (3.6 vs. 2.62, P < 0.001), and higher VAS back pain (4.31 vs. 3.01, P < 0.001) in revision patients, as well as smaller improvements and greater likelihood of further surgery.[5] Montenegro et al. similarly found lower probability of achieving ODI MCID at 6 months in revisions (odds ratio [OR]: 1.85, P = 0.01), with functional decline more common after revision (23% vs. 12.3%, P = 0.0014).[27]

In extensive deformity reconstructions, revision cohorts often demonstrated large absolute improvements reflecting more severe preoperative pathology. Fu et al. reported similar final radiographic outcomes after long fusion to the sacrum/pelvis and higher Scoliosis Research Society scores in revisions (3.65 vs. 3.14, P = 0.005).[14] Kim et al. likewise observed greater overall improvement with comparable final ODI (19.3 primary vs. 19.5 revision) and VAS (2.1 vs. 2.3) at 2 years following combined anterior–posterior procedures.[25]

Perioperative risk patterns were more uniform than PRO findings. National analyses by Kalakoti et al. and Mahamid et al. demonstrated higher rates of infection, wound complications, neurologic and thromboembolic events, transfusion, prolonged length of stay, and nonroutine discharge in revision fusion, while mortality differences were minimal or inconsistent.[78] Dalton et al., in a propensity-matched study of 206 primary and 206 revision cases, found no association between revision status and postoperative opioid requirements (P = 0.900), with persistent use correlating with preoperative exposure (P < 0.001).[16]

Because outcome definitions, reporting conventions, and follow-up intervals varied substantially across the literature, opportunities for formal meta-analysis were limited. Only three studies reported ODI data in a format permitting quantitative synthesis at both 6 and 12 months.[122327] At 6 months, pooled analysis demonstrated a small but statistically significant difference (mean difference [MD]: 2.9, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.42–5.38; P = 0.022) [Figure 2]. By 12 months, however, the estimate widened considerably (MD: 3.22, 95% CI: −3.67–10.11; P = 0.36) [Figure 3]. Both analyses were marked by extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 96.5% and 98.6%), indicating that between-study variability dominated the pooled effect and limiting confidence in the precision or generalizability of the summary measure.

Lumbar discectomy

Eleven studies compared outcomes following primary and revision lumbar discectomy or microdiscectomy [Table 3]. Compared with the fusion literature, findings were more homogeneous. Revision surgery was consistently associated with greater technical demands, higher complication rates, increased risk of additional procedures, and less favorable PROs, even though most patients improved relative to baseline.

Large longitudinal cohorts demonstrated elevated downstream surgery rates. Dalal et al. identified significantly higher risks of repeat discectomy and conversion to fusion after revision procedures (P < 0.01),[29] and Roiha et al. similarly reported greater reoperation frequency and shorter time to further intervention among previously operated patients.[36] Revision operations were also associated with more demanding perioperative courses. Ahn et al. reported longer operative time, longer hospitalization, and higher narcotic requirements, with worse early pain that largely converged by later follow-up.[10] Liang et al. found longer surgery, greater blood loss, longer stay, and increased rates of transient neurologic irritation and CSF leak (all P < 0.05),[32] while Hoang et al., using the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP), confirmed higher infection (2.1% vs. 1.4%, P = 0.002) and transfusion risk (1.3% vs. 0.7%, P < 0.001).[30]

Although improvement from baseline was typical, the magnitude of benefit was often reduced compared with primary surgery. Registry analyses by Fritzell et al. demonstrated smaller gains in leg pain, function, and quality of life and lower satisfaction in revision cohorts.[11] Mehren et al.[6] and Nolte et al.[33] likewise reported worse ODI and VAS scores at extended follow-up, with revision status independently associated with inferior results. Specifically, Mehren et al.[6] found ODI of 22.4 versus 11.6 at 12 months, while Nolte et al.[28] reported final ODI of 49.33 versus 29.52 and VAS back of 7.13 versus 3.81.[633] Even in series reporting comparable magnitudes of change, such as those by Papadopoulos et al. and Patel et al., revisions continued to demonstrate higher durotomy rates and more persistent symptoms.[3435]

Overall, revision discectomy provided symptomatic relief but was associated with worse average outcomes and a greater likelihood of future surgery.

DISCUSSION

Revision lumbar spine surgery presents a distinct clinical challenge, characterized by higher baseline disease severity, increased perioperative morbidity, and lower absolute PROs, despite often achieving meaningful clinical improvement. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from 36 studies comparing primary and revision lumbar procedures, highlighting consistent patterns that inform surgical decision-making and patient counseling.

Patients undergoing revision surgery consistently present with higher baseline pain, disability, and deformity severity, reflecting inherent selection bias in these populations.[578] These differences likely contribute to the persistent gap in postoperative outcome measures, despite similar relative improvements. Large administrative datasets show that revision patients tend to be younger, suggesting that earlier surgical intervention or more aggressive disease progression may predispose to revision procedures.[741] For example, Mahamid et al. reported a mean age of 53.9 years for revision fusion patients versus 61.9 years for primary fusion (P < 0.001).[7] Independent predictors of reherniation requiring revision include younger age, lack of sensory or motor deficits, and higher baseline ODI scores.[2] Recognizing this selection bias is critical, as revision patients often start from a more compromised functional baseline, limiting their ultimate recovery potential.

Despite these challenges, revision lumbar fusions and discectomy generally yield meaningful clinical improvement.[5113335] Djurasovic et al. demonstrated significant improvements in back pain, leg pain, and ODI across all revision indications, with 49% of postdecompression patients, 38% with adjacent segment disease, and 29% with nonunion achieving MCID for ODI.[42] Smaller studies or propensity-matched studies often report comparable improvements to primary surgery, yet larger administrative datasets consistently show lower absolute PROs and a higher likelihood of subsequent surgery.[58] For instance, Mohanty et al. found no differences in 1-year pain, disability, complication, or reoperation rates between primary and revision TLIF cohorts,[12] though long-term follow-up indicates revision patients generally achieve lower final functional status.[51042] Lambrechts et al., in a multi-surgeon single institution database of over 4600 cases, identified revision surgery as an independent predictor of worse postoperative outcomes, smaller improvements, and increased likelihood of further surgery.[5] Minimally invasive approaches may mitigate some disparities, though complications such as durotomy remain more frequent.[24] Percentage-based MCID thresholds (30%–66%) appear to better predict patient satisfaction, particularly in those with extreme baseline disability.[434445]

Revision procedures are associated with higher perioperative risk, including infection, wound complications, thromboembolic events, transfusions, and longer hospital stays.[2710] Large databases demonstrate elevated odds for surgical site infections (OR: 27.10), urinary tract infections (OR: 2.15), dural tears (OR: 2.06), deep venous thrombosis (OR: 2.35), and pulmonary embolism (OR: 1.72) compared to primary procedures.[78] Kalakoti et al. reported higher rates of unfavorable discharge, prolonged hospitalization, and increased hospital charges among revision patients.[8] Mortality differences are minimal, and perioperative opioid requirements are generally comparable when adjusted for baseline exposure.[9] Smaller institutional series or minimally invasive cohorts often show operative metrics similar to primary surgery, reflecting the importance of surgical expertise.[91046] Basques et al. found no differences in 30-day complications or readmissions for revision posterior lumbar fusion after controlling for patient and operative factors,[9] while Ahn et al. observed longer procedural times, hospital stays, and postoperative narcotic use for minimally invasive revision discectomy, though pain outcomes converged at follow-up.[10] Differences between administrative databases and smaller institutional series likely reflect inadequate sample size or inappropriate power in smaller studies to fully categorize differences in outcomes between primary and revision surgery.

This review has several notable limitations. Heterogeneity in study design, patient selection, follow-up duration, surgical technique, and outcome measurement limits the ability to draw robust, generalizable conclusions. Pooled ODI and pain outcomes show very high statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 95%), reflecting differences in baseline severity, surgical indications, and postoperative rehabilitation. Most studies report short-to intermediate-term follow-up, potentially underestimating late complications, reoperations, or functional decline. Surgical experience and institutional volume, which influence outcomes, are inconsistently reported. Many studies rely on retrospective data or administrative datasets, which are susceptible to coding errors and unmeasured confounding, including psychological and functional status. Collectively, these factors underscore the need for cautious interpretation of current literature and highlight gaps requiring further investigation.

Future research should prioritize standardization of outcome reporting, including MCID, VAS, and ODI, and incorporate long-term follow-up to assess durability. Prospective, multicenter, propensity-matched studies could clarify absolute versus relative benefits and better define perioperative risk profiles. Studies evaluating surgical techniques and perioperative protocols that minimize morbidity, such as minimally invasive approaches, enhanced recovery pathways, and strategies to manage epidural scarring, may improve both functional outcomes and patient satisfaction. Further, identifying patient-specific predictors of successful revision surgery, including psychological health, smoking status, preoperative opioid use, and baseline functional reserve, could inform individualized patient selection and preoperative optimization. Integrating these approaches promises to refine surgical decision-making, enhance expectation management, and ultimately improve the quality of care for patients undergoing revision lumbar spine surgery.[3940414243]

CONCLUSIONS

Revision lumbar surgery is consistently associated with greater baseline disease burden and higher perioperative risk of complications, and although most patients achieve meaningful symptomatic improvement, absolute outcomes and the likelihood of additional procedures generally remain less favorable than after primary operations. Careful patient selection, transparent expectation setting, and continued efforts to standardize outcome reporting and optimize surgical strategies are essential to maximizing benefit in this complex population.
