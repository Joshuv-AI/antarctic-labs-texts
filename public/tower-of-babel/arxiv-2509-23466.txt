Abstract
. In mathematical physics it is of interest to study Schrödinger equations with friction and possessing an invariant measure. The focus of this paper is the Cauchy problem for the Schrödinger equation ∂ t f − i ​ ℒ ​ f = 0 \partial_{t}f-i\mathscr{L}f=0 , where ℒ = Δ − ⟨ x , ∇ ⟩ \mathscr{L}=\Delta-\langle x,\nabla\rangle is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator. We use this as a model to stimulate interest in a new class of possibly degenerate dispersive equations which cannot be treated by the existing theory.

1.
Introduction

The Cauchy problem for the free Schrödinger equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f-i\Delta f=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ f(x,0)=\varphi(x),

occupies a central position in quantum mechanics, and it represents a fundamental model for a class of equations known as dispersive. The name comes from the following estimate fulfilled by a solution
f(x,t)
of (
1.1
): for any
1\leq p\leq 2
, one has for some universal
C(m,p)>0
,

(1.2)

||f(\cdot,t)||_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\leq\frac{C(m,p)\ }{t^{m(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}||\varphi||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

As in the theorem of Hausdorff-Young for the Fourier transform, (
1.2
) fails for
p>2
, see e.g.
[
30
, Lemma 1.2]
or
[
13
, Proposition 2.2.3]
. One important application of (
1.2
) is the
T^{\star}T
approach of Ginibre & Velo to prove mixed norms inequalities, known as Strichartz estimates, for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, see
[
31
]
or
[
13
, Section 2.3]
.

In mathematical physics it is of interest to study Schrödinger equations with friction, but with a principal part that can be possibly degenerate. For instance, the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,\infty)

(1.3)

\partial_{t}f=i\left(\partial_{xx}f-2(x+y)\partial_{x}f+x\partial_{y}f\right),\ \ \ \ \ f(x,y,0)=\varphi(x,y),

arises from the Smoluchowski-Kramers’ approximation of Brownian motion with drift, see
[
10
]
and
[
26
]
. One would like to know whether for (
1.3
) there exist dispersive estimates such as (
1.2
), local or global. However, since the diffusive term
\partial_{yy}u
is missing in (
1.3
), such equation cannot be treated by ad hoc modifications of the existing theory for (
1.1
), and one has to develop new tools.
Equations such as (
1.3
) fall into a class of open problems whose investigation is the background motivation of the present work.

To describe the questions we have in mind, given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the second order partial differential operator in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

(1.4)

\mathscr{L}f=\operatorname{tr}(Q\nabla^{2}f)+\langle Bx,\nabla f\rangle.

In (
1.4
) we have denoted by
Q
and
B
two
m\times m
matrices with real constant coefficients. We assume that
Q=Q^{\star}\geq 0
, but this matrix can a priori be degenerate. With
\mathscr{L}
as in (
1.4
), in the opening of his fundamental work
[
33
]
, Hörmander considered the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times(0,\infty)

(1.5)

\partial_{t}f-\mathscr{L}f=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ f(x,0)=\varphi(x),

and proved that the PDE in (
1.5
) is hypoelliptic if and only if, for at least one
t>0
, the following condition holds for the covariance matrix generated by
Q
and
B
,

(1.6)

Q(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds>0.

He did so by showing that, under the assumption (
1.6
), the problem (
1.5
) admits the “heat kernel”

(1.7)

G(x,y,t)=\frac{(4\pi)^{-m/2}}{\sqrt{\det Q(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{\langle Q(t)^{-1}(y-e^{tB}x),y-e^{tB}x\rangle}}{4}\right),

which is easily checked to be
C^{\infty}
outside the diagonal. It is worth noting here that the condition (
1.6
) identified by Hörmander is also necessary and sufficient for the controllability of a first-order system, see
[
56
]
.

The present work is motivated by the following broad question: to study the Cauchy problem

(1.8)

\partial_{t}f-i\mathscr{L}f=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ f(x,0)=\varphi(x),

when the Hörmander’s condition (
1.6
) is satisfied. This is a problem which, at the moment, is unchartered territory: here, we present some initial progress by focusing the attention on the specific model (
1.19
) below.

To put our results in the proper perspective, we note that the task of understanding (
1.8
) is somewhat ambiguous since the class (
1.4
) encompasses equations with a very diverse physical background. To explain this point we note that the model problem (
1.3
) presents two distinctive features:

(i)

an underlying complex Lie group invariance;

(ii)

the existence of an invariant measure.

While (i) is shared by the general Schrödinger equation (
1.8
), property (ii) depends on the drift matrix
B
and it is not always true.
Concerning (i), we mention that if one defines the left-multiplication operator

\sigma_{(x,s)}(y,t)=(x,s)\circ(y,t)=(y+e^{-tB}x,t+s),

then it is not difficult to verify the following commutation property for the PDE in (
1.5
)

(\partial_{t}-\mathscr{L})(\sigma_{(x,s)}f)=\sigma_{(x,s)}[(\partial_{t}-\mathscr{L})f].

This invariance, which is reflected in the expression (
1.7
), was first observed in the paper
[
37
]
. The problem (
1.8
) displays a related, yet very different property. If in fact one considers the complex non-commutative group law

(1.9)

\tau_{(x,s)}(y,t)=(x,s)\circ(y,t)=(y+e^{-itB}x,t+s),

then it is not difficult to verify that

(\partial_{t}-i\mathscr{L})(\tau_{(x,s)}f)=\tau_{(x,s)}[(\partial_{t}-i\mathscr{L})f].

The Lie group action (
1.9
) links the analysis of (
1.8
) to some interesting unexplored aspects of conformal geometry. In such broader perspective, a motivation for (
1.8
) is connected to the study of the Schrödinger equation
\partial_{t}u-i\mathscr{P}u=0
, where
\mathscr{P}
is an “hybrid” operator as defined in
[
29
]
. Yet another motivation is provided by the study of the Schrödinger equation
\partial_{t}u-i\Delta_{H}u=0
, where
\Delta_{H}
indicates a sub-Laplacian in a group of Heisenberg type. In this direction, important contributions concerning mixed restriction inequalities, or dispersive estimates, were given in the works
[
40
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
. Strichartz estimates in complex semisimple Lie groups were obtained in
[
14
]
, whereas various forms of uncertainty principles were established in the works
[
45
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
38
]
.

Concerning (ii), we recall that a probability measure
\mu
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called invariant with respect to
\mathscr{L}
in (
1.4
) if for every
u\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathscr{L}u\ d\mu=0.

Now, it is known that an invariant measure exists if and only if all the eigenvalues of the drift matrix
B
have strictly negative real part, i.e.,
the spectrum
\sigma(B)
satisfies the assumption

(1.10)

\max\{\Re(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\sigma(B)\}\ <\ 0.

It is also known that (
1.10
) is necessary and sufficient for the following integral

(1.11)

Q_{\infty}\overset{def}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds

to converge to a well-defined positive matrix.
We observe here that, from the definition of the covariance matrix
Q(t)
in (
1.6
), it is easy to check the following monotonicity property

(1.12)

Q(t+s)=Q(t)+e^{tB}Q(s)e^{tB^{\star}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ s,t>0.

Note that (
1.12
) implies that (
1.6
) holds for one
t>0
if and only if it does so for every
t>0
, and that furthermore the function
t\to Q(t)
is strictly increasing in the sense of quadratic forms, so that it always makes sense to consider the formal limit
\underset{t\nearrow\infty}{\lim}Q(t)=Q_{\infty}
. For these results one should see the monographs
[
17
, Section 6]
and
[
8
]
.

For instance, for the Smoluchowski-Kramers’ model (
1.3
) one has

(1.13)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}-2&-2\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Since the eigenvalues of
B
are
\lambda=-1-i
and
\bar{\lambda}=-1+i
, we infer that (
1.10
) is verified, and we refer to
[
23
, Sec. 5]
for the computation of the relevant invariant measure. Furthermore, Hörmander’s condition (
1.6
) holds, so that the equation in (
1.5
) is hypoelliptic. One has in fact for any
t>0

\operatorname{det}Q(t)=\frac{e^{-2t}}{16}\left(\cosh(2t)+\cos(2t)-2\right)>0,

see
[
28
, Sec. 3.1]
. However, as we have mentioned above, invariant measures do not always exist. Consider, in fact, the equation of Kolmogorov from Brownian motion and the kinetic theory of gases

(1.14)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{v}u-\langle v,\nabla_{x}u\rangle=0,

first introduced in
[
35
]
(see also the discussion in
[
47
]
and the book
[
48
, Sec. 7.4]
). In (
1.14
) we have indicated the spatial variables
v,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that, with
m=2n
, the PDE acts on functions defined in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times(0,\infty)
. Despite the degenerate character of (
1.14
) (note the missing diffusive term
\Delta_{x}u
), the equation is hypoelliptic. This had already been proved by Kolmogorov himself in
[
35
]
since he did write the following explicit fundamental solution (actually, he only discussed the case
n=1
, in which case
m=2
)

G(x,y,\bar{x},\bar{y},t)=\frac{3^{\frac{n}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{n}}t^{-2n}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4t}\left(|x-\bar{x}|^{2}+12|\frac{y-\bar{y}}{t}+\frac{x+\bar{x}}{2}|^{2}\right)\right\}.

To see the hypoellipticity using Hörmander’s theorem, observe that one has for (
1.14
)

(1.15)

Q=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&O_{n}\\
O_{n}&O_{n}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \ \ \ B=\begin{pmatrix}O_{n}&O_{n}\\
I_{n}&O_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
B^{k}=O_{m}
for every
k\geq 2
, a simple computation gives

(1.16)

Q(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{sB}Qe^{sB^{\star}}ds=\begin{pmatrix}tI_{n}&\frac{t^{2}}{2}I_{n}\\
\frac{t^{2}}{2}I_{n}&\frac{t^{3}}{3}I_{n}\end{pmatrix},

and therefore the assumption (
1.6
) holds, since one has for
t>0

(1.17)

\det Q(t)=c(n)t^{4n}=c(m)t^{2m}>0.

From (
1.15
) it is clear that the condition (
1.10
) fails (and in fact (
1.16
) shows that
Q_{\infty}
is not defined), we thus conclude that (
1.14
) does not admit an invariant measure.

This discussion should convince the reader that the Cauchy problem (
1.8
) requires a different approach, depending on whether (
1.10
) is valid or not. We mention that, when (
1.10
) is satisfied, and therefore
Q_{\infty}
in (
1.11
) is well-defined, the invariant measure for
\mathscr{L}
is given by

(1.18)

d\gamma_{\infty}(x)=\frac{(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}}{(\det Q_{\infty})^{1/2}}e^{-\frac{\langle Q_{\infty}^{-1}x,x\rangle}{4}}dx,

see e.g.
[
11
, Proposition 2.3]
. Since in such case the operator
\mathscr{L}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma_{\infty})
, from Stone’s theorem (see e.g.
[
55
, Theorem 1, p. 345]
) we conclude that, associated with (
1.8
), there exists a strongly-continuous group
e^{it\mathscr{L}}
of unitary operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma_{\infty})
. As a first objective, one would like to obtain for such group a dispersive estimate similar to the classical one in (
1.2
).

This paper provides an initial contribution to this program by considering the model case
Q=I_{m}
and
B=-I_{m}
in (
1.4
), so that

\mathscr{L}f=\Delta f-\langle x,\nabla f\rangle,

and (
1.8
) presently becomes

(1.19)

\partial_{t}f-i\left(\Delta f-\langle x,\nabla f\rangle\right)=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ f(x,0)=\varphi(x).

Note that for this problem the assumptions (
1.6
) and (
1.10
) are obviously satisfied, and in fact
a simple calculation shows that we presently have

Q(t)=\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}\ I_{m}\ \nearrow\ Q_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}I_{m}.

One thus obtain from (
1.18
) the invariant measure

d\gamma(x)=d\gamma_{\infty}(x)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}dx,

i.e., the standard normalised Gaussian measure.
The parabolic counterpart of the PDE in (
1.19
) is the well-known Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation
introduced in
[
41
]
, and further developed in
[
53
]
. Such equation plays a significant role in probability, especially in connection with the calculus of Malliavin, see
[
44
, Chap. 2]
. For a historical account up to the year 2018 the reader is also referred to the survey
[
9
]
.

Our first result concerning (
1.19
) is Proposition
1.1
, which provides a representation formula for its solution. In order to state it, we introduce a basic functional class which is reminiscent of the Bargmann representation in a Fock space, see
[
25
, Chap. 1 & 5]
. In what follows, given a function
\varphi
, we indicate with
\psi
the function

(1.20)

\psi(x)=e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}\varphi(x),\ \ \ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Notice from (
1.20
) that

\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma),

and that

(1.21)

||\psi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}=||\varphi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma)}.

We denote by

(1.22)

\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\mid\psi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\}.

It should be clear to the reader that
\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma)
. Throughout this paper we let
J^{+}=(0,\pi)
,
J^{-}=(\pi,2\pi)
, and denote

J=J^{+}\cup J^{-}.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the function

(1.23)

f(x,t)=\begin{cases}\frac{(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}e^{\frac{imt}{2}}}{e^{\frac{i\pi m}{4}}(\sin t)^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{i\frac{|e^{it/2}y-e^{-it/2}x|^{2}}{4\sin t}}\varphi(y)dy,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ t\in J^{+},\\
\\
\frac{(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}e^{\frac{imt}{2}}}{e^{\frac{i3\pi m}{4}}|\sin t|^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-i\frac{|e^{it/2}y-e^{-it/2}x|^{2}}{4|\sin t|}}\varphi(y)dy,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ t\in J^{-},\end{cases}

and
f(x,0)=\varphi(x)
, solves (
1.19
) in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times J
.

The next result extends to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck group
e^{it\mathscr{L}}
the well-known connection between the Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}
and the free Schrödinger group
e^{it\Delta}
. We note that the definition of
\mathscr{F}
adopted in this paper is (
2.18
),
so that Plancherel theorem gives
||\mathscr{F}u||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}=||u||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}
.
Henceforth, when
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma)
, we will write
f(x,t)=e^{it\mathscr{L}}\varphi(x)
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Then for every
t\in J^{+}
the function
f(x,t)=e^{it\mathscr{L}}\varphi(x)
is given by the formula

(1.24)

e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}f(x,t)=(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\frac{e^{\frac{imt}{2}}}{e^{\frac{i\pi m}{4}}(\sin t)^{\frac{m}{2}}}e^{i\frac{\cot t|x|^{2}}{4}}\mathscr{F}\left(e^{i\frac{\cot t|\cdot|^{2}}{4}}\psi\right)(\frac{x}{4\pi\sin t}),

where
\psi
is defined by (
1.20
).

Remark 1.3
.

We explicitly note that it follows from (
1.24
) that

(1.25)

e^{it\mathscr{L}}:\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\ \longrightarrow\ \mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m}).

To state the next proposition, we stress that for Schrödinger equations such as (
1.8
), or even the model problem (
1.19
), restriction inequalities are at the moment unchartered territory. As a consequence, dispersive estimates such as (
1.2
) acquire an even stronger relevance in the implementation of the above mentioned approach of Ginibre-Velo to Strichartz estimates. In this connection, we have the following local result.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and
f(x,t)=e^{it\mathscr{L}}\varphi(x)
with
t\not=k\pi
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
1\leq p\leq 2
one has

(1.26)

||e^{-\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{4}}f(\cdot,t)||_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\leq\left(\frac{p^{1/p}}{{p^{\prime}}^{1/p^{\prime}}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\ \frac{1}{(4\pi|\sin t|)^{m(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}\ ||e^{-\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{4}}\varphi||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})},

where
1/p+1/{p^{\prime}}=1
. The estimate (
1.26
) is optimal, in the sense that it cannot possibly hold in the range
2<p\leq\infty
. Moreover, equality is attained in (
1.26
) by initial data
\varphi(x)=e^{-\alpha|x|^{2}+\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}
,
\Re\alpha>0
.

Note that as
t\to 0
the estimate (
1.26
) displays the same behaviour
t^{-m(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}
as that in (
1.2
) for the free Schrödinger group
e^{it\Delta}
.
It is interesting to compare (
1.26
) with the following dispersive estimate for the Schrödinger equation with friction (see (
1.14
) above)

(1.27)

\partial_{t}v-i\Delta v+\langle x,\nabla v\rangle=0.

The PDE (
1.27
) is very different from (
1.19
), as one can readily surmise from its invariance group of (real) left-translations

\sigma_{(x,s)}(y,t)=(x,s)\circ(y,t)=(y+e^{t}x,t+s).

As a special case of the result in
[
27
, Theorem 4.1]
, one obtains for the semigroup
\{\mathcal{T}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
associated with the Cauchy problem for (
1.27
)

(1.28)

||\mathcal{T}(t)\varphi||_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\leq C(m,p)\ \frac{e^{\frac{m}{p^{\prime}}t}}{(1-e^{-2t})^{m(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}||\varphi||_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

The qualitative behaviour as
t\to 0^{+}
in (
1.26
) and (
1.28
) is the same, but because of the presence of
e^{-\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{4}}
in the former, the two estimates are incomparable.

Our final result is Proposition
1.5
which represents a form of uniqueness for the group
e^{it\mathscr{L}}
: a certain
L^{2}
decay in Gaussian measure at two different times completely determines the solution. This kind of result is reminiscent of Hardy’s uncertainty principle for the Fourier transform in his classical work
[
32
]
. Nowadays, this subject is vast, and there exist many beautiful and important results scattered in the literature which is impossible to entirely quote here. The reader can see
[
16
]
,
[
45
]
,
[
25
]
and
[
52
]
for an interesting account which covers up to 2004. Subsequently, the subject received a new impulse with the fundamental works
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
22
]
, in which a whole new program of uncertainty inequalities for equations of Schrödinger type was developed. One should also see the recent paper
[
24
]
. The reader familiar with the subject will surmise that the next proposition is one more manifestation of the ideas of Escauriaza, Kenig, Ponce and Vega in the above mentioned works.

Proposition 1.5
.

Assume that for some
a,b>0
one has

(1.29)

||e^{a|\cdot|^{2}}f(\cdot,0)||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma)}+||e^{b|\cdot|^{2}}f(\cdot,s)||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},d\gamma)}<\infty.

If
ab\sin^{2}s\geq\frac{1}{16}
, then
f\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}
.

We note explicitly that the assumption
ab\sin^{2}s\geq\frac{1}{16}
automatically excludes the possibility that
s=k\pi
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This discrete set of points is where the complex covariance matrix
Q(t)=U(t)I_{m}
, where
U(t)
is defined in (
2.3
) below, becomes singular and the representation (
1.23
) of
e^{it\mathscr{L}}
ceases to be valid. The proof of Proposition
1.5
combines our formula (
1.24
) in Proposition
1.2
with the
L^{2}
version in
[
15
]
of the above cited works
[
19
]
-
[
22
]
. We note that coupling Proposition
1.2
with the uncertainty principle for the Fourier transform due to Hardy, see
[
32
]
or also
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
, we obtain the following
L^{\infty}
version of Proposition
1.5
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Suppose that for some
C,a,b>0
one has for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(1.30)

|e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}f(x,0)|\leq Ce^{-a|x|^{2}},\ \ \ \ \ \ |e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}}f(x,s)|\leq Ce^{-b|x|^{2}}.

If
ab\sin^{2}s\geq\frac{1}{16}
, then
f\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}
.

In closing we mention that there exists a deep link between (
1.19
) and the Cauchy problem for the quantum mechanics harmonic oscillator (
3.1
). For the problem (
1.8
) such link becomes tenuous and it is all but clear (at least, to this author) what should replace the harmonic oscillator in such general framework. Since the present work, with the underlying Wiener space and invariant measure, serves as a model for the problem (
1.8
), we have not emphasised this link in the course of the paper, but have provided a brief (purely PDE) account in the appendix in Section
3
. The reader interested in more theoretical aspects should consult e.g.
[
49
]
,
[
50
]
,
[
7
]
, and especially the masterful lectures of Pauli
[
42
]
. Using the results in Section
3
, one can derive from the dispersive estimate in Proposition
1.4
a corresponding result for the solution to (
1.19
). In the opposite direction, Propositions
1.5
and
1.6
are closely connected to results for the harmonic oscillator available in the literature, see the works
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
, and the more recent
[
36
]
and
[
43
]
, where the authors prove a conjecture by Vemuri, respectively up to an exceptional arithmetic progression, and in full. However, as far as we are aware of, in none of these papers the link with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator and its invariant measure has been explicitly highlighted.

The present work is organised as follows. In Section
2
we establish the representation formula (
1.23
) in Proposition
1.1
and we derive its main consequence, Proposition

2.
The Schrödinger equation

In this section we solve the Cauchy problem (
1.19
) by constructing a representation of the solution operator. Our main result is the proof of (
1.23
) in Proposition
1.1
, which we then use to prove (
1.24
) in Proposition
1.2
and (
1.26
) in Proposition
1.4
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

We begin with a simple, but critical observation. Suppose that
v
and
f
are connected by the relation

(2.1)

v(x,t)=f(e^{it}x,t).

Then,
f
is a solution of the Cauchy problem (
1.19
) if and only if
v
solves the problem

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v-iU^{\prime}(t)\Delta v=0,\\
v(x,0)=\varphi(x),\end{cases}

where we have let

(2.3)

U(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2is}ds=\frac{1-e^{-2it}}{2i}=e^{-it}\frac{e^{it}-e^{-it}}{2i}=e^{-it}\sin t.

To prove that
v
solves (
2.2
), we argue as follows. The chain rule gives from (
2.1
)

v_{t}(x,t)=ie^{it}\langle x,\nabla f(e^{it}x,t)\rangle+f_{t}(e^{it}x,t).

On the other hand, the PDE in (
1.19
) gives

f_{t}(e^{it}x,t)=i\Delta f(e^{it}x,t)-ie^{it}\langle x,\nabla f(e^{it}x,t)\rangle.

Combining the latter two equations, we infer that
v
solves

v_{t}(x,t)=i\Delta f(e^{it}x,t).

Next, differentiating (
2.1
) we find

\Delta v(x,t)=e^{2it}\Delta f(e^{it}x,t).

We thus conclude that

v_{t}(x,t)=ie^{-2it}\Delta v(x,t)=iU^{\prime}(t)\Delta v(x,t),

where in the second equality we have used (
2.3
).
Summarising, the function
v
solves the problem (
2.2
).
To find a representation formula for the latter, we use the Fourier transform.
Supposing that
v
be a solution, we define

(2.4)

\hat{v}(\xi,t)=\mathscr{F}(v)(\xi,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}v(x,t)dx.

Then (
2.2
) is transformed into

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\hat{v}+4\pi^{2}iU^{\prime}(t)|\xi|^{2}\hat{v}=0,\\
\hat{v}(\xi,0)=\hat{\varphi}(\xi),\end{cases}

whose solution is given by

(2.6)

\hat{v}(\xi,t)=\hat{\varphi}(\xi)e^{-4\pi^{2}iU(t)|\xi|^{2}}.

Note that, with
U(t)
as in (
2.3
), for every
t\in J
the matrix
Q(t)=U(t)I_{m}
is invertible. Moreover, we have

(2.7)

iQ(t)=iU(t)I_{m}=i(\cos t-i\sin t)\sin t\ I_{m}=\sin^{2}t\ I_{m}+i\frac{\sin{2t}}{2}I_{m}.

We now invoke
[
34
, Theorem 7.6.1]
, which we formulate as follows: Let
A\in G\ell(\mathbb{C},m)
be such that
A^{\star}=A
and
\Re A\geq 0
. Then

(2.8)

\mathscr{F}\left(\frac{(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{det}A}}e^{-\frac{\langle A^{-1}\cdot,\cdot\rangle}{4}}\right)(\xi)=e^{-4\pi^{2}\langle A\xi,\xi\rangle},

where
\sqrt{\operatorname{det}A}
is the unique analytic branch such that
\sqrt{\operatorname{det}A}>0
when
A
is real.
If in (
2.8
) we take

A=iQ(t)=iU(t)I_{m}=ie^{-it}\sin t\ I_{m},

with
t\in J^{+}
, then (
2.7
) gives
\Re A=\sin^{2}t\ I_{m}\geq 0
, and

A^{-1}=-i\frac{e^{it}}{\sin t}I_{m}.

We thus find

(2.9)

e^{-4\pi^{2}iU(t)|\xi|^{2}}=\mathscr{F}\left(\frac{e^{\frac{imt}{2}}}{e^{\frac{i\pi m}{4}}(\sin t)^{\frac{m}{2}}}(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}e^{ie^{it}\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{4\sin t}}\right)(\xi).

From (
2.6
) and (
2.9
) we conclude that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
t\in J^{+}

(2.10)

v(x,t)=\frac{e^{\frac{imt}{2}}}{e^{\frac{i\pi m}{4}}(\sin t)^{\frac{m}{2}}}(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{ie^{it}\frac{|y-x|^{2}}{4\sin t}}\varphi(y)dy.

Finally, keeping (
2.1
) in mind, after some elementary algebraic manipulations, we obtain the representation (
1.23
) when
t\in J^{+}
. The part corresponding to
t\in J^{-}
follows by elementary changes if one observes that now
A=e^{i\frac{3\pi}{2}}e^{-it}|\sin t|I_{m}
.

∎

for the solution of the Cauchy problem for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

If now

Appendix: The imaginary harmonic oscillator

As said at the end of the introduction, for the benefit of the reader unfamiliar with the link between the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator and the quantum mechanics harmonic oscillator, in this brief appendix we show how to pass from one PDE to the other, and back. The main tool is the nonlinear Schrödinger equation of Riccati type (
3.3
) in Lemma
3.1
. Consider the harmonic oscillator

H=\Delta-|x|^{2}

Lemma 3.1
