Establishing a Multidisciplinary Medical Imaging Clinical Educator Team Across a Large Tertiary Health Network: A Workforce Education Model
Authors: Ilona Lavender, Roman Blagin, Luke Monsour, Venkatesh Vinay Mudumbi, David Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionClinical educator roles support workforce capability, training consistency, and quality and safety across allied health professions. Large multi‐site medical imaging services face challenges in delivering consistent education and credentialing across diverse craft groups. In 2024, an operationally funded multidisciplinary medical imaging clinical educator (CE) team was established across a tertiary health network; the aim of this study was to evaluate the short‐term outcomes of this workforce education model.MethodsA multidisciplinary CE model was implemented across radiography, sonography, nuclear medicine, nursing, and administrative roles. Education initiatives were organised across four interrelated domains: training and credentialing, cross‐disciplinary collaboration, learning culture, and innovation. Outcomes were evaluated using participation metrics, credentialing data, and anonymous pre‐ and post‐education confidence surveys. Evaluation was conceptually aligned with Kirkpatrick's framework, with reported outcomes reflecting Levels 1 (engagement) and 2 (self‐reported learning), and emerging Level 3 indicators.ResultsEducation reach and engagement increased following implementation of network‐wide communication, digital infrastructure, structured CPD delivery, and simulation‐based education. Simulation programs demonstrated improved self‐reported confidence, including first‐trimester communication (mean confidence increased from 2.7 to 3.5 on a 4‐point Likert scale). These findings indicate early engagement and learning gains, with emerging behavioural indicators.ConclusionAn operationally embedded multidisciplinary medical imaging CE model can strengthen education visibility, workforce engagement, and early credentialing outcomes across a large multi‐site service. Although longer‐term organisational (Level 4) outcomes require further evaluation, this model provides a scalable and structured approach to workforce education delivery in complex medical imaging environments.

Introduction

Clinical educators are widely recognised as a critical component of the allied health workforce capability, providing structured supervision, supporting competency development, facilitating professional development, and strengthening quality and safety through consistent training practices [1]. Across a range of healthcare professions, education‐focused roles have been shown to enhance workforce capability by improving clinical supervision, supporting workplace skill development, and promoting reflective practice and professional identity formation [1, 2]. These roles also contribute to safer clinical care by enabling structured training, reinforcing consistent standards, and supporting teams to learn from practice through feedback and continuous improvement [3, 4]. In complex clinical environments, clinical educators can support staff engagement and confidence by fostering psychological safety and embedding a culture where learning is prioritised alongside service delivery [5, 6].

Medical imaging services are increasingly complex, requiring staff to maintain high‐level clinical and technical skills while adapting to evolving equipment, protocols, patient acuity, and interdisciplinary workflows [7]. Despite this complexity, medical imaging departments have not consistently adopted dedicated clinical educator structures across all craft groups, and education delivery may remain variable and locally dependent [8].

Our organisation is a large tertiary teaching hospital network spanning multiple sites, with a diverse medical imaging workforce including radiography, sonography, nuclear medicine, nursing, and administrative teams (approximately 550 staff). Although this scale enables significant service delivery capacity, it also presents challenges in ensuring consistent education practices, equitable access to training opportunities, and effective cross‐site networking and collaboration. Before 2024, education delivery varied across sites based on staffing resources, local priorities, and service pressures.

In 2024, operational funding was obtained to establish a network‐wide medical imaging clinical educator team. The purpose of this initiative was to develop structured training and credentialing processes while also strengthening cross‐site consistency, collaboration, and connectivity across the medical imaging workforce. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first operationally funded multidisciplinary medical imaging clinical educator models in Australia, incorporating radiographers, nuclear medicine technologists, sonographers, nursing, and administrative staff.

The objective of this study was to describe the implementation of an operationally funded multidisciplinary medical imaging clinical educator model and to evaluate its early workforce education outcomes, including engagement, infrastructure adoption, credentialing activity, and participant‐reported confidence.

Methods/Team Model Description

This project was conducted as a quality‐improvement workforce education evaluation within a tertiary multi‐site medical imaging service. This initiative was implemented within a large tertiary medical imaging service operating across multiple hospital sites within a single health network. The scale and geographical distribution of the workforce created challenges for standardisation, communication, and equitable access to education.

Team Structure and Governance

The medical imaging clinical educator (CE) team was established as an operationally funded unit embedded within routine service delivery. The team was governed through the medical imaging operational structure, with the Lead Clinical Educator reporting to the Operations Manager to support alignment between workforce education priorities and service requirements across the network.

Educator roles included protected education responsibilities embedded within routine service delivery to support scalable education while maintaining clinical service capacity.

The medical imaging clinical educator team included educators from radiography, sonography, nuclear medicine, nursing, and administration. The team provided modality‐specific expertise while supporting coordinated, network‐wide education delivery and cross‐site consistency in education priorities (Figure 1).

Evaluation and Data Collection

Outcomes were mapped to Kirkpatrick's training evaluation framework. Early outcomes reported in this manuscript primarily represent Level 1 (participation and engagement) and Level 2 (self‐reported confidence changes following simulation). Further work will evaluate Level 3 (behaviour change in workplace practice) and Level 4 (organisational and service outcomes including credentialing completion rates, escalation behaviours, and workforce capability indicators).

The four implementation domains (training and credentialing, cross‐disciplinary collaboration, learning culture, and innovation) were conceptually aligned with Kirkpatrick's four‐level evaluation framework [9]. Training and credentialing activities primarily supported Level 2 outcomes (knowledge and confidence development) and emerging Level 3 behaviours (credentialing completion and expanded scope activities). Learning culture initiatives such as CPD visibility and newsletter engagement aligned with Level 1 outcomes (reaction and participation). Innovation initiatives, including simulation and distributed BLS supervision, contributed to Levels 2 and 3 outcomes, while cross‐disciplinary collaboration was designed to support longer‐term Level 4 organisational alignment and workforce capability indicators.

Ethics Approval

This study was conducted as a low‐risk quality improvement research project and received institutional ethics approval (Reference: QA/125981/MonH‐2026‐517049(v1)). The evaluation utilised routinely collected, anonymous workforce education data obtained as part of routine education evaluation and quality improvement activities. No identifiable staff information was collected, and individual consent was not required due to the anonymous and aggregated nature of the data collection.

Basic Life Support (BLS) Superusers and Train‐the‐Trainer Model

A BLS train‐the‐trainer model was implemented to support scalable delivery of practical BLS training across the multi‐site medical imaging service. Seventeen BLS superusers were established, and 97 staff were credentialed through the program, representing early progress toward workforce‐wide BLS credentialing.

Learning Culture

Strengthening learning culture was central to improving engagement, access, and sustainability of education across a busy clinical service. The focus was on providing consistent, visible, and structured CPD offerings to normalise learning as part of routine work rather than an optional add‐on.

Education resources were designed to support equitable access across sites and shift patterns through recorded sessions and centralised resources. This enabled staff who were unable to attend live sessions due to workload, part‐time employment, or rostering constraints to participate.

Structured CPD sessions were developed across multiple modalities, with improved visibility through education communications and SharePoint scheduling, thereby increasing staff awareness and access to learning opportunities.

Simulation‐based training was introduced as a targeted education strategy to support areas of practice that are difficult to address through routine clinical exposure. Unlike other allied health and nursing disciplines within the organisation, simulation‐based education had not previously been embedded within the medical imaging workforce at this network. Simulation‐based education is increasingly recognised within medical radiation sciences as a modality to support clinical preparedness and safe skill acquisition [10].

Simulation sessions were delivered in a psychologically safe environment, enabling participants to practise complex clinical and communication scenarios that are not easily replicated during routine service delivery.

The deteriorating patient simulation program was evaluated using a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = not confident, 5 = very confident), with pre‐simulation confidence scores generally in the 2.7–3.3 range (low–moderate confidence) and post‐simulation scores increasing to approximately 4.0–4.4 (high confidence) across scenarios including early clinical deterioration, airway obstruction, hypoxia, shock, medical emergency team (MET) activation, Code Blue response, contrast reactions, and anaphylaxis (this is seen in Figure 2). The most significant improvements were reported in MET calls and Code Blue responses, with substantial gains also seen in the management of anaphylaxis, contrast reactions, airway obstruction, and hypoxia. Qualitative free‐text feedback supported the acceptability and perceived impact of simulation‐based education, with participants describing the sessions as realistic and highly valuable for learning.

The first‐trimester communication simulation was evaluated using an anonymous pre‐ and post‐session 4‐point Likert confidence survey (1 = not confident, 4 = very confident) and demonstrated improved confidence across all communication domains, with mean confidence increasing from 2.7 pre‐simulation to 3.5 post‐simulation Figure 3. Improvements were observed in clearly explaining ultrasound findings, communicating uncertain or inconclusive results, managing patient distress or emotion, using appropriate language and empathy, structuring conversations effectively, and concluding and summarising discussions. Collectively, these findings suggest that simulation‐based education is a promising modality for strengthening preparedness for high‐risk clinical events and emotionally challenging communication scenarios within medical imaging. Simulation provides a controlled and psychologically safe environment in which staff can practise infrequent but high‐consequence situations that are difficult to rehearse during routine service delivery. Beyond the scenarios described in this study, simulation may be applied to a range of medical imaging contexts, including medical emergencies in magnetic resonance imaging environments, contrast reactions in computed tomography, paediatric deterioration, and procedural complications. Simulation also supports interprofessional escalation and communication during acute clinical events. As such, simulation represents a scalable and adaptable education strategy for improving confidence and readiness across diverse imaging cohorts.

Automation and Sustainable Systems

Several automated processes were implemented to strengthen education sustainability and reduce duplication of administrative tasks, including streamlined CPD certificate generation and improved systems for credentialing storage and tracking. The time saving has been substantial, with certificate automation reducing manual processing by approximately 3–5 min per certificate; across 651 certificates issued to date, this equates to an estimated 33–54 h of administrative time saved.

Expanded Credentialing Access

Credentialing initiatives were expanded to support workforce flexibility and advanced skill development. Radiographers' access to PICC lines for contrast injections was introduced or strengthened through structured credentialing processes, with 37 radiographers credentialed to use them.

This initiative enhanced clinical capability and supported more efficient and responsive care delivery for appropriate patient cohorts.

Discussion

Structured clinical educator models have been recognised internationally as a critical mechanism for strengthening workforce capability and aligning education with health system priorities [1, 2]. Embedding educators within service delivery while maintaining protected education responsibilities may enhance supervision quality, training consistency, and workforce sustainability across complex, multi‐site services. Similarly, simulation‐based education is increasingly utilised in medical radiation sciences to support safe skill acquisition, team communication, and high‐stakes clinical preparedness [10]. The present findings align with this broader literature and extend it by demonstrating how a coordinated, multidisciplinary educator structure can support both implementation and structured evaluation of workforce education initiatives within a tertiary medical imaging network.

Using Kirkpatrick's four‐level framework as a conceptual lens, most reported outcomes in this study represent early indicators of Level 1 (reaction and engagement) and Level 2 (learning and confidence development). Improvements in CPD participation, broad engagement with the medical imaging education newsletter, utilisation of the SharePoint platform, and attendance at multimodality workshops demonstrate positive workforce response and engagement with the educator model. Increased visibility and accessibility of education initiatives appear to have strengthened participation and perceived value of structured education activities [11].

Level 2 outcomes are reflected in participant‐reported confidence following simulation activities and structured training initiatives. Simulation‐based education, including deteriorating patient and communication scenarios, demonstrated measurable improvements in self‐reported confidence, consistent with literature supporting simulation as a mechanism for safe skill acquisition and workforce preparedness [10]. Structured credentialing programs, expanded PICC line access, and distributed BLS training also support learning and capability development within defined scopes of practice.

Emerging Level 3 (behavioural) indicators are suggested through credentialing completion, expansion of scope activities, and establishment of distributed supervision models such as the BLS train‐the‐trainer approach. The integration of educator roles within routine service delivery enabled practical skill application and local reinforcement of education processes across sites. Structured clinical supervision remains a critical component of safe workforce development, and dedicated educator roles may enhance supervision quality and behavioural consistency across complex services [12]. Although formal observation of sustained workplace behaviour change was beyond the scope of this evaluation, these indicators suggest progression beyond reaction and learning alone.

Direct Level 4 (organisational) outcomes, including workforce retention, clinical performance metrics, and patient‐level outcomes, were not formally assessed in this study. However, the multidisciplinary structure of the educator team and strengthened cross‐disciplinary collaboration may support longer‐term organisational alignment and system‐level capability [13, 14, 15]. Strengthening workforce capability through structured education aligns with broader calls for transformation in health professional education to meet evolving system demands [9]. Future evaluation will examine sustained credentialing rates, compliance indicators, workforce stability, and selected service metrics to determine whether early engagement and learning gains translate into measurable organisational impact.

Importantly, the four interrelated implementation domains training and credentialing, cross‐disciplinary collaboration, learning culture, and innovation provided a practical structure through which Level 1–3 outcomes could be observed. Foundational infrastructure such as the newsletter, SharePoint platform, and hybrid workshops supported scalable delivery across sites, addressing known challenges in dispersed services [6, 8, 16]. Early BLS credentialing outcomes (97 staff) and expansion of simulation initiatives demonstrate feasibility within a large workforce context and provide a foundation for longitudinal evaluation.

Overall, this educator model demonstrates a structured and scalable approach to workforce education within a multi‐site medical imaging service. Although longer‐term organisational outcomes remain to be evaluated, early findings support the feasibility and value of an operationally funded, multidisciplinary clinical educator team in strengthening engagement, learning, and emerging behavioural indicators across a complex healthcare environment.

Limitations

A key limitation is that several outcomes reported represent early indicators aligned primarily with Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2, including workforce engagement (e.g., attendance, views, participation) and participant‐reported confidence. Although confidence is relevant to workforce readiness and psychological safety, the study did not include direct assessment of competence, sustained workplace behaviour change (Level 3), or robust organisational and service‐level outcomes (Level 4), such as workforce retention metrics, clinical performance indicators, or patient‐level impact measures. Although early credentialing expansion and distributed supervision models suggest emerging behavioural and system‐level effects, formal evaluation of sustained behavioural change and organisational outcomes remains limited. Future evaluation will incorporate workplace‐based assessments, audit measures, credentialing compliance data, and selected service indicators to assess longer‐term Level 3 and Level 4 impact. As a service‐level evaluation, the study prioritised feasibility and workforce implementation outcomes rather than hypothesis testing.

Conclusion

Establishing an operationally funded, multidisciplinary medical imaging CE team across an extensive tertiary multi‐site health network enabled improved education consistency, enhanced collaboration, a stronger learning culture, and practical innovation. Education infrastructure initiatives, including the imaging newsletter, the Education SharePoint platform, and hybrid multimodality workshops, supported scalable, inclusive access to learning across sites and craft groups. This model provides a transferable approach for medical imaging departments seeking sustainable workforce education structures aligned with operational service delivery.
