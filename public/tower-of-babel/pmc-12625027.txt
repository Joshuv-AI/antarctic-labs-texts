Patrilineages of ethnolinguistically diverse populations reveal multifactorial influences on Chinese paternal population stratification
Authors: Ting Yang, Yunhui Liu, Shuang Zou, Xiangping Li, Zhiyong Wang, Lintao Luo, Renkuan Tang, Chao Liu, Liping Hu, Guanglin He, Shengjie Nie, Mengge Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge-scale Y-chromosome genetic resources provide critical insights into human evolutionary history. However, the limited high-density Y-chromosomal data from ethnolinguistically diverse Chinese populations hinder the reconstruction of fine-scale population stratification and the exploration of its complex influencing factors.ObjectivesWe report microarray-based high-density Y-SNP data from 5311 unrelated males in the pilot phase of the 10K Chinese People Genomic Diversity Project (10K_CPGDP) and merge it with spatiotemporally high-coverage reference data from both ancient and modern individuals from public sources and the Huaxi Biobank dataset.ResultsWe identified clear north–south and west–east genetic substructures among Chinese populations, reflecting distinct regional genetic origins and migration patterns. We illuminated how multiple cultural and demographic factors, including subsistence strategies, language barriers, and geographic isolation, have shaped the Chinese paternal population dynamics via genetic diversity analysis coupled with phylogenetic and phylogeographic analyses. Paternal genetic diversity followed complex patterns, with a haplogroup frequency spectrum and a variation-based phylogenetic tree indicating that more than 95% of paternal lineages belong to haplogroups O, C, N, D, and Q. The phylogeographic analysis revealed distinct regional distributions of haplogroups linked to subsistence strategies and ancestral population dispersal. The predominance of Neolithic farmer-related lineages suggested that the spread of ancestral agricultural populations promotes population differentiation between ancient northern and southern East Asians. We observed significant lineage sharing between Han Chinese and ethnic minority groups, with the northwestern paternal gene pool contributing lineages associated with farming and herding. Spatial autocorrelation and principal component analyses revealed genetic connections between the Han Chinese and ethnic minorities, highlighting paternal population substructures shaped by complex admixture and migration patterns that align with geographical and linguistic divisions.ConclusionsThese findings support the influence of the farming-language dispersal hypothesis on Chinese paternal lineage formation and underscore the role of geographic and linguistic isolation in shaping the genetic landscape. This study demonstrates the unique value of large-scale Y-chromosome data in uncovering human evolutionary complexity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02442-1.

Background

East Asia is characterized by rich genetic, linguistic, and cultural diversity, comparable to that of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia [1, 2]. As the most populous country in East Asia, China presents distinct population substructures and long-term population continuity not typically seen elsewhere [3–5]. Over the past two decades, extensive genetic studies have revealed a clear north–south differentiation among Chinese populations, based on various genetic markers including autosomal single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and short tandem repeats (STRs), Y-chromosome haplogroups, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) lineages [6–8]. Genome-wide analyses have consistently demonstrated genetic substructure within the Han Chinese population and between Han and minority groups, with the Qinling–Huaihe line marking this genetic and cultural distinction [9–15]. Similarly, studies focusing on Y-chromosomal variations [7, 16, 17], mtDNA diversity [18, 19], and ancient DNA [1, 3, 5, 11, 20] have further supported the notion of deep-rooted north–south divergence in China. While this north–south differentiation has been well-documented, genetic differentiation along the east–west axis remains largely unexplored. Nevertheless, China features substantial east–west differences in geographic environment [21], linguistic distribution [22], and subsistence patterns [14]. The terrain of China slopes from west to east in a three-step staircase pattern: the high-altitude Qinghai-Xizang Plateau in the west, followed by the Loess Plateau and its associated basins, and finally the eastern plains. These ecological and cultural contrasts suggest the possibility of underlying genetic differences along the east–west axis. However, to date, there has been a lack of systematic genetic investigations addressing this dimension of population structure in China.

Despite these findings, prior studies have predominantly focused on genome-wide autosomal SNPs or maternal lineages [9, 15], whereas comprehensive and in-depth investigations of paternal lineages remain limited. The Y chromosome, inherited exclusively through the paternal line, serves as a robust marker for reconstructing male-specific population history and structure due to its non-recombining nature and relatively stable lineage-specific variants [7, 14, 23–27]. Over the past two decades, population genetic studies based on limited Y-chromosomal SNP or STR loci have revealed pronounced genetic differences between northern and southern Chinese populations [28, 29], with southern Chinese groups exhibiting higher genetic diversity, supporting a south origin of East Asians followed by subsequent northward migration [30, 31]. Recent Y-chromosome studies have documented fine-scale population structure and notable demographic patterns, including paternal lineages linked to imperial ancestry [26, 27]. Furthermore, large-scale Y-chromosome sequences from non-East Asian populations have shed light on Paleolithic and Neolithic demographic events, including severe male-specific bottlenecks that resulted in the loss of specific lineages and contributed to a skewed female-to-male effective population size ratio [32, 33]. These studies have also documented extensive paternal expansions and admixture events, often associated with shifts in social organization and subsistence strategies [34]. Although previous research has demonstrated that geographic and linguistic factors strongly influence Y-chromosome variations [35, 36], the extent to which complex demographic processes have shaped paternal genetic diversity and fine-scale population structure within China remains poorly understood. In particular, the combined effects of geography, language affiliation, and subsistence mode on the composition and differentiation of Chinese patrilineages have yet to be systematically explored [8, 37, 38]. Clarifying these patterns is crucial for reconstructing the demographic history of Chinese populations and understanding the social and environmental forces that shape their paternal genetic landscape.

To fill the gap in understanding the paternal landscape of Chinese populations and to explore the factors leading to paternal population stratification, genome-wide Y-SNP data were generated from 5311 newly genotyped samples from the 10K Chinese People Genomic Diversity Project (10K_CPGDP) [15], and complementary haplogroup information was obtained from the Huaxi Biobank (HXB). The former was used to reconstruct the basic patterns of Chinese paternal history, and the latter to explore the possible phylogeographical origins of the identified founding lineages. The 10K_CPGDP genomic resource comprised individuals from 91 populations across 34 provinces in China, representing 24 ethnic groups and five prominent linguistic families: Sinitic, Tibeto-Burman, Altaic, Hmong-Mien, and Tai-Kadai. Furthermore, we collected published Y-chromosome haplogroup data from both ancient and modern sources, including ancient DNA data from previous Chinese studies and contemporary data from other Asian populations [39–45]. By integrating spatiotemporal, linguistic, and cultural dimensions, we observe extensive paternal genetic diversity and genetic substructures among Chinese populations, as well as significant lineage sharing between Han Chinese and ethnic minority groups. We examine distinct haplogroup distribution patterns associated with ancient populations that employed varying subsistence strategies and explore the multiple factors contributing to Chinese paternal genetic diversity. Collectively, our findings reveal distinct lineage stratification patterns shaped by geography, language, and subsistence strategies, offering new insights into the demographic history of East Asian populations.

Results

Genetic landscape and diversity of Chinese paternal lineages

We analyzed high-density Y-SNP data from 5311 unrelated male individuals in the 10K_CPGDP cohort, representing 91 populations across China, encompassing 34 provinces and 24 ethnic groups (Fig. 1A–C, Additional file 1: Table S1). Compared with previous Y-chromosome studies in East Asian populations [14, 26, 27, 46], which often focused on specific regions or ethnic groups or used lower-resolution genotyping methods, our study significantly expanded both the geographic and ethnic coverage and enhanced phylogenetic resolution. Additionally, we integrated haplogroup data from the HXB and other Asian populations. We compiled haplogroup data from published Chinese ancient DNA studies (Additional file 1: Table S2), enabling a fine-scale reconstruction of paternal lineage structure within a broader spatiotemporal framework [39–45]. Using the ISOGG 2019–2020 Y-DNA haplogroup tree (version 15.73), we assigned samples to 111 intermediate or terminal haplogroups (Additional file 1: Table S3). Over 95% of individuals belonged to five major haplogroups (O, C, N, D, and Q), consistent with earlier findings on paternal diversity among Han Chinese groups [7], with O being the most prevalent lineage in East Asia. However, our large-scale sampling allowed for a more refined quantification of major subclades: haplogroup O2a2b accounted for 28.92%, forming the most considerable portion of the paternal gene pool, followed by O2a1b (14.33%), O1b1a (11.69%), O1a1a (11.22%), C2b1 (5.89%), O2a2a (5.48%), N1b2 (2.64%), D1a1a (2.47%), and Q1a1a (2.09%) (Fig. 1C, Additional file 1: Table S3). In addition, we detected several rare haplogroups E, F, G, H, I, J, L, R, and T at frequencies below 2%, likely reflecting subtle yet historically significant gene flow between Chinese populations and neighboring Eurasian populations or some deep genetic legacy of early Asians, such as the identification of the F lineage [47]. To investigate the genetic relationships between Chinese populations and other Asian groups, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) and genetic distance assessments within a broader Asian context. To explore patterns of Chinese paternal genetic diversity, we first excluded populations with fewer than 20 individuals to reduce sampling bias, retaining 41 representative groups (Additional file 1: Table S4). We calculated haplogroup diversity (HD), nucleotide diversity (Pi), and Tajima’s D across diverse ethnic, geographic, and linguistic groupings, as well as among Han Chinese populations stratified by geographic regions (Fig. 1D, Additional file 1: Table S5). We observed generally high haplogroup and nucleotide diversity across most groups, with particularly elevated values in the northwestern provinces of Qinghai, Ningxia, Gansu, and Xinjiang (Additional file 1: Table S5). This may reflect the complex admixture history in these areas in the context of large-scale Trans-Eurasian cultural and population exchanges. In contrast, lower diversity was observed in more isolated populations, such as those in Hainan (Additional file 1: Table S5), which may be attributed to founder or island effects [48]. Among language family-defined groups, southern Sinitic, northern Sinitic, and Altaic-speaking populations exhibited higher genetic diversity than those speaking Tibeto-Burman, Hmong-Mien, and Tai-Kadai languages (Additional file 1: Table S5), supporting a scenario of multiple regional centers of origin. These centers were likely associated with distinct subsistence strategies—rice agriculture in the Yangtze River Basin (YZRB), millet farming in the Yellow River Basin (YRB), and pastoralism on the northern steppe [11, 49, 50]. Notably, Tajima’s D values were predominantly negative across all groupings, suggesting a strong signal of recent population expansion. However, none of the values reached statistical significance (P > 0.05, Additional file 1: Table S5). Among Han Chinese subpopulations (Additional file 1: Table S5), regional diversity patterns largely mirrored those observed at the broader geographic scale. For example, northwestern Han populations (e.g., Gansu, Qinghai, and Ningxia) exhibited higher haplogroup and nucleotide diversity, whereas southeastern coastal populations (e.g., Fujian and Hainan) showed relatively reduced diversity. These results indicate that geographic location and historical gene flow have played major roles in shaping the fine-scale paternal structure within Han Chinese populations [9, 10, 28, 51].

Genetic divergence between southern and northern Chinese populations

Characterizing the population structure of the Han Chinese, the world’s largest ethnic group, and China’s ethnic minorities via denser markers is essential for understanding the genetic diversity of East Asian populations. To this end, PCA was performed based on haplogroup frequencies (Additional file 1: Table S6). The first principal component (PC1), explaining 49.38% of the variation, was aligned along an east–west cline. In contrast, the second principal component (PC2), accounting for 27.33%, corresponded to a north–south cline (Fig. 2A). To explore the relationship between genetic substructure and geographical variation, we calculated the correlation coefficient between the positional indices of the principal components, geographical coordinates (latitude and longitude), the pairwise genetic distance (Φst) matrix of 41 populations, and the frequencies of major fifth-level haplogroups across Chinese populations (Fig. 2B). PC2 effectively distinguished the southern and northern Chinese Sinitic-speaking populations, as supported by a significant positive correlation between PC2 values and geographic latitude (Pearson’s r = 0.55, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A, B, Additional file 2: Fig. S3). Strong correlations were identified between PC1 and longitude and latitude, suggesting the possible existence of west–east paternal genetic differentiations. The frequencies of haplogroups O1a1a (R = − 0.62, P < 0.001) and O1b1a (R = − 0.66, P < 0.001) correlated negatively with latitude, indicating that their frequencies decreased from south to north. Conversely, the frequencies of haplogroups O2a2b (R = 0.61, P < 0.001) and O2a1b (R = 0.42, P < 0.01) correlated positively with latitude. The significant correlations in pairwise genetic distances among the studied populations suggested complex interactions and exchanges between these groups. We reported that the frequency of dominant paternal lineages strongly correlated with the population genetic distance matrix (Fig. 2B). This north–south differentiation pattern was further supported by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis, a neighbor-joining (N-J) phylogenetic tree, and a genetic distance matrix heatmap based on Φst values (Fig. 2C, D, Additional file 1: Table S7, Additional file 2: Fig. S4). We found that the genetic substructure is consistent with geographic or linguistic proximity, with northern Sinitic-speaking Chinese being more closely related to the Altaic and Tibeto-Burman people. In contrast, southern Sinitic speakers are more closely related to the Hmong-Mien and Tai-Kadai groups. These patterns suggested that cultural factors may influence paternal genetic substructures, and the connections among linguistically different populations also indicated a genetic interaction between Han Chinese individuals and neighboring ethnically and linguistically close groups. Furthermore, the N-J tree revealed that southern Sinitic-speaking populations (except Han_Hainan) clustered within a single clade, while northern Sinitic-speaking populations were distributed across two distinct clades (Fig. 2D). Notably, Hainan Han individuals formed a distinct branch in the N-J tree, away from other southern Sinitic-speaking Chinese individuals in PCA and NMDS, suggesting unique genetic traits in this isolated group. The haplogroup frequency spectrum (HFS) of the 41 studied populations revealed significant geographic and ethnic differences in haplogroup composition (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). To investigate the correlation between Y-chromosome haplogroup distribution and population structure, we performed a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Consistent with the results from PCA and N-J tree, paternal genetic substructure was found to be closely correlated with linguistic affiliation. Haplogroups such as O2a, D1a, O1b, and O1a showed the strongest correlation with population differentiation (vector lengths > 0.85, Additional file 1: Table S8), indicating that these haplogroups are key drivers of the observed genetic substructure. The vectors for O1a and O1b pointed mainly toward southern Sinitic groups, while O2a pointed to the northern Sinitic groups (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). To further explore genetic connections across populations, we analyzed haplogroup sharing across linguistically, geographically, and ethnically distinct groups. Among linguistically classified groups, the largest intersection (size = 15) was observed between northern and southern Sinitic groups (Fig. 2E, left panel). They also exhibited substantial haplogroup overlapping with multiple non-Sinitic groups (intersection size = 10), indicating widespread paternal lineage continuity or historical gene flow across these linguistically distinct groups (Fig. 2E, left panel). Across geographically diverse groups, a total of 111 haplogroups were detected, of which 70 (63.1%) were shared between northern and southern populations, while 19 (17.1%) and 22 (19.8%) were unique to northern and southern populations, respectively (Fig. 2E, middle panel). This demonstrates both extensive shared paternal ancestry and regional differentiation. At the ethnic level, the Han population harbored the highest number of unique haplogroups (intersection size = 53), substantially exceeding those observed in minority groups (Fig. 2E, right panel). In contrast, the number of haplogroups shared between the Han and minority groups remained low (intersection size < 10), indicating that the Han possess a distinct set of paternal lineages with limited overlap with neighboring minority populations. To elucidate the geographic and linguistic factors that shape the paternal genetic divergence between northern and southern Chinese, we conducted an analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) (Additional file 1: Table S9–10). When all 41 populations were treated as a single group, genetic variation among populations accounted for 3.47% of the total. We also explored variations within all Han Chinese populations, as well as within northern and southern Chinese populations. We found significant variations within populations in each of the tested groups. Among-population variation within all southern Chinese populations was the highest compared to other groups, suggesting that individual-level variation contributed most to overall genetic diversity. Additionally, southern Chinese populations exhibited greater heterogeneity than northern Chinese populations (4.46%, P < 0.05 and 0.13%, P > 0.05, respectively) (Additional file 1: Table S9). We further explore variations among different populations categorized via geographical boundaries at various levels. A significant differentiation (grouping 5, 1.25%, P < 0.05) was observed when populations were classified into southern and northern groups based on the Qinling–Huaihe line. Similar patterns of differentiation were detected when Han Chinese populations were categorized along the same north–south divide (grouping 6, 1.53%, P < 0.05), suggesting distinct genetic variations between northern and southern China or between Han Chinese populations. We also observed that the latter showed fewer within-group differences, suggesting that geographically distinct Han Chinese populations are relatively homogeneous compared to geographically and ethnolinguistically diverse Chinese populations. In addition, we found that when populations were divided according to the seven geographical regions (grouping 7), the among-group differences were relatively small (0.43%, P < 0.05). In contrast, the within-group differences are substantial (3.09%, P < 0.05). Compared to the two-region grouping (northern vs. southern, grouping 5), which showed a higher among-group variation (1.25%), the reduction suggested that finer-scale geographic divisions represent visible but relatively minor barriers to gene flow and admixture among Chinese populations. We next examined the effect of cultural boundaries on population differentiation. Grouping based on Han versus minority status and language family revealed the most remarkable genetic differences (5.23%, P < 0.05 and 4.36%, P < 0.05, respectively). Notably, language-based grouping resulted in lower within-group genetic variation (0.90%, P < 0.05), highlighting a strong link between linguistic affinity and Y-chromosome genetic legacy. These results suggest that both geographical and linguistic factors contribute to genetic differences among populations, with linguistic differences playing a more pronounced role than geography does.

Archaeological evidence suggests that millet farming originated in northern China, whereas rice farming originated in southern China [52–54]. It was hypothesized that patrilineal segmentary systems, that is, social structures organized around male lineage descent [34], often involving hierarchical segmentation of clans or lineages, were associated with different subsistence strategies, and that these systems contributed to highly differentiated haplogroup compositions across geographically and linguistically diverse populations. This suggests that the independent origins of subsistence-related founding lineages have contributed to the deep paternal genetic stratification observed. To assess the impact of subsistence strategies on paternal genomic diversity in both modern and ancient Chinese populations, we calculated haplogroup frequency differences across contemporary groups. A threshold of 0.025 was applied to identify highly differentiated paternal lineages, the top 10 haplogroups showing the greatest frequency differences between northern and southern populations. A Manhattan plot of these frequency differences revealed 41 haplogroups with marked differentiation, primarily within the C and O clades (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Table S11). Northern Chinese populations were enriched for haplogroups C2b1, O2a2b, O2a2b1a2a, and O2a1b, with statistical support from Mann–Whitney U tests (C2b1: Z = 215, P < 0.001; O2a2b: Z = 192.5, P < 0.05; O2a2b1a2a: Z = 183, P < 0.05; O2a1b: Z = 148.5, P > 0.05), while southern populations exhibited higher frequencies of O1a1a, O1a1a1a1a1a, O1b1a, and O2a2a (O1a1a: Z = 12, P < 0.001; O1a1a1a1a1a: Z = 34, P < 0.01; O1b1a: Z = 19, P < 0.001; O2a2a: Z = 56, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Table S12). These observations supported significant regional divergence in the distribution of major founding paternal lineages between southern and northern Chinese populations (Fig. 3E, Additional file 2: Fig. S7). To further illustrate whether these patterns persisted over time, we collected published ancient Chinese DNA and compared the third-level haplogroup compositions between ancient northern and southern Chinese populations. Genetic divergence between eastern and western Chinese populations

PCA and correlation analysis consistently revealed the existence of an east–west genetic differentiation among Chinese populations (Fig. 2A, B). To investigate the impact of geographical and linguistic differences on the stratification of eastern and western Chinese paternal lineages, we divided the population into eastern and western groups using China’s three-step topographic staircase, which refers to the well-established division of China’s topography into three descending steps from west to east. Specifically, we followed the boundary line formed by the Greater Khingan, Taihang, Wushan, and Xuefeng Mountains, marking the boundary between the second and third topographic steps. AMOVA results indicated statistically significant but low levels of genetic differentiation between eastern and western Chinese populations (Additional file 1: Table S9, groupings 10 and 11). Although the proportion of variation among groups was low, a discernible east–west genetic structure was detected. Based on a frequency difference threshold of 0.025 in the Manhattan plot, we identified 22 highly differentiated loci between eastern and western populations, including haplogroup O, which predominated in eastern China, and haplogroup D, which was confined mainly to western regions (Fig. 3B, Additional file 1: Table S13). Notably, a significant negative correlation was observed between longitude and the frequency of haplogroup D1a1a (R = − 0.42, P < 0.01), consistent with its predominance in western China (Fig. 2B). To further resolve patterns beyond the major O and D lineages, we conducted an additional fine-scale Manhattan analysis after excluding O and D haplogroups (Fig. 3C). We found that lineages C, J, N, Q, and R exhibited greater frequency variation and were predominantly distributed in western China (Fig. 3C). Among them, only haplogroups J showed statistically significant geographic distribution (Z = 54, P < 0.05), whereas C, N1a1a, Q1, and R did not (C: Z = 78, P > 0.05; N1a1a: Z = 61, P > 0.05; Q1: Z = 58, P > 0.05; R: Z = 72, P > 0.05) (Additional file 1: Table S12). The maximum and average values in boxplots supported regional differentiation for these haplogroups (Fig. 3F, Additional file 2: Fig. S7). Ancient DNA evidence supported the widespread distribution of haplogroup C across both eastern and western China, the predominant distribution of paternal lineages D, J, Q, and R in western China, whereas lineages N and O were more frequently found in eastern China (Additional file 2: Fig. S8B).

Haplogroup frequency differences were next examined across linguistically distinct groups and identified 42 highly differentiated loci between Tibeto-Burman- and non-Tibeto-Burman-speaking populations within the O and D haplogroups (Fig. 3D, Additional file 1: Table S14). Specifically, paternal lineage D and its downstream sublineages were observed at high frequencies in Tibeto-Burman-speaking populations, whereas lineage O and its sublineages were predominant among non-Tibeto-Burman groups. Although these patterns did not achieve statistical significance in Mann–Whitney U tests (D1a1a: Z = 136, P > 0.05; O1a1a: Z = 47, P > 0.05) (Additional file 1: Table S12), the differential distribution of haplogroups among linguistically distinct populations remained apparent (Fig. 3G). In addition, PCoA further supported this trend, with the D1a vector oriented toward Tibeto-Burman groups (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Because linguistic affiliation in China often corresponds to ethnic identity, genetic relationships were further examined by grouping populations according to ethnic labels. Third-level haplogroup compositions, PCA, and genetic distance assessments were used to characterize inter-ethnic genetic structure (Additional file 2: Fig.S9–11). We found that ethnic groups belonging to the same language family tended to share similar haplogroup compositions. For example, Zhuang and Shui populations exhibited high frequencies of haplogroups O1a and O1b. Certain haplogroups were also enriched in specific groups, such as D1a in Tibetan and Mosuo populations, and N1a in the Mosuo population (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). Moreover, ethnic groups within the same language family generally showed closer genetic relationships (Additional file2: Fig. S10–11). These findings revealed that the haplogroup composition of certain ethnic groups plays a decisive role in shaping the population substructures associated with linguistic affiliations.

Phylogeographic characteristics of modern Chinese paternal lineages

Phylogeographic analysis of major founding lineages identified population-specific haplogroups that strongly correlate with geography-driven population substructures (Fig. 4). Haplogroup O2a2b, the primary lineage in northern China, was found at the highest frequencies in Hebei (43.36%), Liaoning (38.51%), and Shanxi (36.84%) (Additional file 1: Table S15), exhibiting apparent north–south differentiation. Its prevalence in both modern and ancient northern genomes supports a northern origin. The presence of O2a2b in southern Chinese populations suggests the impact of millet farmers’ southward migration. The key subhaplogroups O2a2b1a1a1 and O2a2b1a2a occurred at relatively high frequencies across Chinese populations (Additional file 1: Table S3). Haplogroup O2a1b was prevalent among Altaic-speaking groups in northern China (Additional file 1: Table S16). Haplogroup O1b1a, common among Tai-Kadai speakers in southern China, was frequent in Guangxi (23.84%), Guizhou (21.16%), and Hainan (23.21%) (Additional file 1: Table S15–16). Higher frequencies in southern Sinitic groups, along with a negative correlation with latitude (Fig. 2B), indicate south-to-north expansion, with O1b1a1a1 as the primary founding subhaplogroup (Additional file 1: Table S3). The haplogroup O1a1a showed a north–south gradient, with higher frequencies in southeastern coastal populations, particularly among southern Sinitic, Hmong-Mien, and Tai-Kadai groups (Additional file 1: Table S16). The frequency also decreased from the coastal to the central and western regions (Table S17), with O1a1a1a1a1a1 being the dominant subhaplogroup (Additional file 1: Table S3). Haplogroup C2b1 was found predominantly in northeastern and northern China (Additional file 1: Table S18), especially in Liaoning (12.84%), Shandong (12.58%), Hebei (11.89%), and Heilongjiang (11.85%) (Additional file 1: TableS15), and was more common among northern than southern Sinitic-speaking populations (Additional file 1: Table S16). This lineage likely entered Siberia during the Neolithic [14]. Conversely, haplogroup O2a2a was concentrated in southern China, especially among Hmong-Mien and Tai-Kadai speakers in Guangxi (13.58%) and Guizhou (11.20%) (Additional file 1: Table S15–16). Although O-M122 likely originated in southern China, its downstream haplogroups, O2a2a and O2a2b, show distinct regional distributions, likely shaped by early northward migration followed by subsequent isolation or later southward dispersal [31]. Haplogroup N1b2 was concentrated in southwestern and northwestern China (Additional file 1: Table S18), whereas D1a1a displayed high frequencies on the Qinghai–Xizang Plateau and surrounding regions, notably in Sichuan (9.09%) and Ningxia (6.98%) (Additional file 1: Table S15). Discussion

Genetic relationships and structure within Chinese populations

Previous studies on the paternal genetic structure of Chinese populations have primarily focused on Han Chinese populations or specific ethnic minority groups, using low-density SNP or STR markers, resulting in an incomplete understanding of the broader paternal landscape across China [7, 28, 30]. To address this gap, we reported a dataset of 5311 unrelated males from 34 provinces and 24 ethnic groups, which was used to map the fine-scale paternal genetic structure of Chinese populations (Fig. 1A–C). Our findings confirmed that haplogroups O, C, N, D, and Q dominate the paternal landscape, with haplogroup O accounting for more than 75% of the total population [17, 57]. The patterns of haplogroup diversity highlighted the influence of geographical factors and demographic histories in shaping the fine-scale paternal genetic structure within Chinese populations (Fig. 1D). PCA, correlation analysis, and genetic distance measurements revealed genetic connections and stratification within Chinese populations (Fig. 2A–D). Haplogroup sharing patterns further demonstrated widespread paternal lineage sharing across diverse Chinese populations (Fig. 2E). In addition to the well-established north–south genetic divide, our results revealed multiple genetic substructures associated with language- and geography-related groupings, reflecting the evolutionary histories and migration patterns of culturally specific Chinese groups (Additional file 2: Fig.S3–5). Previous studies have suggested that southern Sinitic-speaking populations originated from prehistoric southward migrations of northern millet farmers [7, 42], followed by historic migrations associated with events such as the An Shi Rebellion, the Jinkang Incident, and the Yi Guan Nan Du. Subsequent intermixing with indigenous southern populations likely contributed to the minimal genetic divergence observed between these groups [28]. Ancient DNA evidence also suggested a temporal reduction in north–south genetic differentiation [5], likely driven by increased population mobility. Furthermore, we identified a weak but detectable east–west genetic differentiation associated with both geographical and linguistic divisions (Fig. 3B–D, Additional file 1: Table S9).

Multiple factors jointly shaped the north–south stratification of paternal lineages

The AMOVA results highlighted the significant influence of both geographic and linguistic factors in shaping the paternal genetic substructure of Chinese populations (Additional file 1: Table S9). Specifically, geographic subdivisions, defined by the traditional north–south boundary, the east–west division, and even finer-scale regional classifications, all contributed significantly to the observed genetic differentiation (Additional file 1: Table S9: grouping 5, 7, 10, 11). Similarly, linguistic affiliations, including distinct language families and subgroups, also played a substantial role in structuring paternal genetic variation (Additional file 1: Table S9: grouping 6, 9, 33, 34). The PCoA findings highlighted strong associations between specific haplogroups and linguistically defined groups. For example, haplogroups O1a and O1b were associated with southern Sinitic Chinese populations, O2a with northern Sinitic Chinese populations, and D1a with Tibeto-Burman-speaking groups (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). The haplogroup composition further highlighted the high frequencies of O1a and O1b among the Tai-Kadai-speaking Zhuang and Shui populations in southern China, as well as the elevated frequency of D1a among the Tibeto-Burman-speaking Tibetan and Mosuo populations (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). The Manhattan plot analysis identified highly differentiated loci associated with geographic and linguistic structure, with significance supported by statistical tests (Fig. 3, Additional file 1: Table S11–14, Additional file 2: Fig. S7). Notably, lineages O and C exhibited pronounced north–south differentiation, while clades O, D, C, J, N, Q, and R displayed marked east–west structuring (Fig. 3A–C). Additionally, phylogeographic patterns revealed strong associations between paternal founding lineages and geographically defined population substructures (Fig. 4). Specifically, lineages O2a2b, O2a1b, and C2b1 were more frequently distributed in northern China, while O1b1a, O1a1a, and O2a2a showed high frequencies in southern and southeastern coastal regions. D1a1a is predominantly distributed in western China (Fig. 4, Additional file 1: Table S11). Patterns of haplogroup frequency distribution in Chinese ancient DNA corroborated these findings, indicating long-term genetic continuity across Chinese populations (Additional file 2: Fig. S8).

Agricultural innovations have played a crucial role in the origin, migration, expansion, and admixture of early farmers and their descendants [11]. Ancient DNA from key Neolithic transition sites in East Asia suggests a broad division between northern millet farmers and southern rice agriculturalists [5], highlighting the long-term genetic stability of agricultural centers or genetic continuity in specific geographic regions [3, 4]. Mitochondrial genome studies from China have suggested that genetic divergence between river valley-defined populations was driven by the expansion of ancient agricultural populations along river basins, with rice farmers along the YZRB, tropical agricultural groups along the Pearl River, and millet farmers along the Yellow River [58]. Ancient genomic studies further revealed that rice-farming populations primarily spread lineage O1 from the YZRB in southern China, whereas millet farmers from the YRB in northern China contributed to the spread of lineage O2 [4, 59, 60]. Integrating the farming-language dispersal hypothesis, phylogeographic evidence, and the concept of long-term genetic continuity, our results linked haplogroups O1a1a and O1b1a to southern rice farmers, and O2a2b to northern millet farmers. Notably, correlation analysis revealed significant associations between haplogroup frequencies and latitude. Haplogroups O1a1a and O1b1a exhibited strong negative correlations with latitude, supporting their southern origin and expansion likely linked to the spread of rice agriculture. In contrast, haplogroup O2a2b showed a significant positive correlation with latitude, suggesting a northern origin associated with millet farming (Fig. 2B). Three downstream haplogroups exhibited distinct spatial distributions that align with their proposed centers of origin. O1a1a1a1a1a-F533 showed high frequencies along the southeastern coast, with spatial autocorrelation analyses identifying a hotspot in the middle and lower reaches of the YZRB (Fig. 5A). O1b1a1a1a1a-F2524 showed high frequencies in southwestern China, with a likely origin in the Pearl River Basin (Fig. 5B). In contrast, haplogroup O2a2b1a2a2-F743 exhibited a widespread distribution in northern China, with its origin center inferred in the middle and lower reaches of the YRB (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, the frequency distribution pattern of O2a2b1a2a2-F743 closely mirrors the historical migration event known as "Chuang Guandong", during which large-scale populations from northern China migrated to the northeastern region in recent history [61, 62], suggesting this haplogroup may have played a dominant role in that demographic expansion. Further investigation with higher-resolution data is warranted. The latitudinal frequency patterns of these three downstream haplogroups are consistent with those of their upstream lineages (Fig. 5D). These findings highlight the regional distribution and differentiation of paternal lineages linked to subsistence patterns and their role in shaping the genetic stratification between southern and northern Chinese populations.

East–West paternal genetic substructure among Chinese populations

Based on the boundary between the second and third steps of China’s three-step topographic staircase, populations were divided into eastern and western groups. Manhattan analysis revealed a high frequency of haplogroup O1a1a in eastern China, whereas haplogroups D1a1a, C, J, N1a1a, Q1, and R were more prevalent in the west (Fig. 3B). This geographic pattern was further supported by a significant correlation between the frequency of haplogroup D1a1a and longitude (Fig. 2B), as well as phylogeographic analyses of haplogroups O1a1a and D1a1a (Fig. 4). To investigate this pattern further, available ancient DNA data were analyzed, and haplogroups J and R were found to be significantly more frequent in ancient individuals from western China than those from the east (Additional file 2: Fig. S8B). These results are consistent with previous genetic and archaeological evidence indicating the western Eurasian origin of haplogroups J and R, and their association with the dispersal of Yamnaya-related steppe herders [14], which aligns with the identification of a relatively high frequency of western Eurasian ancestry in Hexi corridor populations [15]. These findings support the hypothesis that historical west-to-east gene flow contributed to the paternal genetic structure of western China, underscoring the influence of western Eurasian-related lineages in shaping the observed east–west genetic stratification among present-day Chinese populations (Fig. 3F, Additional file 2: Fig. S7). In addition, haplogroups O1a1a and D1a1a are predominantly found in non-Tibeto-Burman and Tibeto-Burman-speaking populations, respectively (Fig. 3D). The HFS, PCoA, and haplogroup composition analyses consistently revealed the high frequency of D1a1a among Tibetan populations, underscoring its importance as a major lineage within Tibeto-Burman speakers (Additional file 2: Fig. S5, S6, S9). Notably, haplogroups associated with the east–west geographic division overlapped substantially with those related to linguistic differentiation, with O1a1a primarily distributed among non-Tibeto-Burman groups in the east and D1a1a enriched among Tibeto-Burman groups in the west. In our study, eastern populations were predominantly characterized by O lineages (Fig. 3B), particularly those associated with the spread of agricultural populations. In contrast, western populations frequently carried haplogroups D1a1a, C, J, N1a1a, Q1, and R (Fig. 3B, C), which have been historically linked to high-altitude adaptation, pastoralism, and foraging lifestyles [14, 26]. China’s topography is characterized by a high west, low east gradient, with elevated plateaus and mountains in the west and expansive plains and hills in the east. Agricultural subsistence, particularly rice and millet cultivation, has historically dominated the eastern regions, while herding and foraging have been prevalent in the west [63–66]. This genetic differentiation closely mirrors the geographic distribution of subsistence strategies, suggesting that the genetic divergence between eastern and western Chinese populations has been shaped by both geographic and linguistic barriers, as well as long-term differences in subsistence modes. Taken together, these findings highlight the complex interplay between geography, language, and subsistence strategies in shaping the paternal genetic landscape of China.

This study utilizes array-based genotyping to elucidate the multifactorial determinants of paternal genetic stratification in Chinese populations, providing a framework for future research on paternal population history. However, several limitations remain. First, microarray-based genotyping relies on a predefined set of SNP loci, which constrains the comprehensive resolution of Y-chromosomal variation, particularly in detecting rare variants and de novo mutations. In addition, although this study incorporates ancient haplogroup data to assess regional differences in haplogroup distributions, detailed phylogeographic or phylogenetic analyses of ancient Y-chromosomal sequences were not performed, which could have offered direct spatiotemporal insights into past demographic dynamics. Finally, integrated analyses combining Y-chromosomal, autosomal, and mitochondrial genomes are needed to reconstruct a more comprehensive demographic history of East Asia [8, 37]. Future efforts employing large-scale whole-genome sequencing and high-precision telomere-to-telomere Y-chromosome assemblies will enable more precise detection of rare and complex structural variants, facilitating precise haplogroup assignment and phylogenetic reconstruction. Furthermore, integrating ancient DNA sequences alongside more detailed geographic metadata and comprehensive phylogeographic analyses will advance our understanding of the paternal genetic history of East Asia, shedding light on migration trajectories and population dynamics across different temporal and demographic contexts.

Conclusions

We present extensive Y-chromosome genomic data from ethnolinguistically and geographically diverse Chinese populations during the pilot phase of the 10K_CPGDP program. Fine-scale paternal genetic substructures were identified, highlighting dominant lineages among geographically and linguistically distinct populations that shape the complex Chinese paternal genetic landscape. Comprehensive population genetic analyses and correlation tests indicate significant influences of cultural and geographical factors, including natural barriers between northern and southern China, as well as between eastern and western regions, and language boundaries tied to paternal founding lineages. These results shed light on ancient human migration patterns, reflecting the co-dispersal of languages and millet and rice farming in early Chinese societies. These findings provide critical insights into the genetic profiles of the Han Chinese and ethnic minority groups, revealing genetic diversity and distribution patterns within the Chinese population. They also support complex farming-language dispersal models linked to diverse subsistence strategies and emphasize the role of geographic and cultural isolation in shaping genetic structure. However, limitations inherent to genotyping arrays restricted the detection of certain variants and the resolution of haplogroup assignment. Future phases of the 10K_CPGDP are expected to overcome these constraints through expanded sequencing strategies.

Methods

Sample collection

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (2024–1425). Sample collection and experimentation were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Human Genetic Resources Administration of China and the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [67]. A total of 5508 male saliva samples were collected from 34 administrative provinces and 24 ethnic groups across China. All participants provided informed consent. For individuals with limited proficiency in Mandarin Chinese, local translators who were fluent in both Mandarin and the local dialect were employed to ensure clear communication throughout the consent process. Community engagement was facilitated through collaboration with local health workers and community leaders, who introduced the study objectives and procedures to potential participants. To minimize the confounding effects of recent migration and ensure genetic representativeness, participants were recruited only if their parents and grandparents were born in the same geographic area (or neighboring counties) and spoke the same native language or dialect. Additional data from the HXB resource were incorporated to refine analyses of the evolutionary history and potential origin centers for three putative founding lineages in the Chinese population. This dataset included 13,373 samples belonging to the O-F533 haplogroup, spanning 34 provinces and 316 prefecture-level cities. Additionally, it comprised 4012 samples linked to the O-F2524 lineage from 32 provinces and 282 prefecture-level cities, and 2479 samples associated with the O-F743 lineage from 33 provinces and 277 prefecture-level cities. Modern Y-chromosome haplogroup data from Asian populations were also integrated from the 1000 Genomes Project [68], the Human Genome Diversity Project [47], and three other published studies [69–71]. To support and strengthen the prehistoric interpretations, ancient Y-chromosome data were collected from previously published Chinese ancient DNA studies [3, 4, 11, 20, 39–45, 60, 72–84].

DNA extraction, genotyping, and quality control

DNA was extracted and purified via the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). Quantitative analysis was performed using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Genotyping of amplified DNA was performed using an Affymetrix microarray, yielding approximately 5162 informative sites on the Y chromosome. Quality control was conducted using PLINK v.1.90, applying thresholds of greater than 95% genotyping success rate and greater than 5% minor allele frequency [85]. Variants with a missing rate greater than 5% and all indels were filtered using BCFtools v1.8 [86]. Only biallelic variants were retained. Samples with a missing genotyping rate greater than 5% were filtered using VCFtools v0.1.16 [87]. A total of 2613 SNPs were retained for the final analysis. KING software was used to identify and exclude samples with close kinship up to the third degree based on autosomal variations. After removing related individuals and those with unclear geographic origins, a final dataset of 5311 male samples was retained. The geographical position and sample size of this dataset are presented in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Haplogroup allocation

Paternal haplogroups were classified via the hGrpr2 Python package within HaploGrouper [88], based on the ISOGG Y-DNA Haplogroup Tree 2019–2020 (https://isogg.org/tree/index.html). Genotyping data generated from the Affymetrix microarray, which includes enough phylogenetically informative Y-chromosome SNPs, were used for haplogroup classification. Although hGrpr2 is originally designed for whole Y-chromosome sequencing data, its Bayesian probabilistic framework enables robust haplogroup classification even from high-density SNP array data. Previous studies have shown that such data allow classification accuracies exceeding 95% for major clades [11, 33]. Haplogroup assignment scores were generated per sample (Additional file 1: Table S19). The highest-confidence calls recommended by hGrpr2 were adopted for all subsequent population genetic analyses. Final haplogroup classifications are available in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Genetic diversity and structure

We excluded populations with fewer than 20 individuals, retaining 41 populations out of 91 geographically and ethnically defined groups to minimize statistical bias caused by small sample sizes or merging them based on geographical proximity (Additional file 1: Table S4). Y-chromosomal diversity parameters were calculated based on groupings defined by geographic region, ethnicity, and language family, with Han Chinese further subdivided by geographic location (Additional file 1: Table S5). Pi values of each population were calculated using VCFtools v0.1.16 with a sliding window approach (window size = 200 Mb, step size = 200 Mb), automated via a custom Bash script (Additional file 1: Table S20). HD and Tajima’s D values were estimated using Arlequin 3.5.2. Haplogroup frequencies for each population were calculated via an internal Python script (Additional file 1: Table S20), and HFS was plotted at three levels via the “pheatmap” package in R v4.4.2 (http://cran.r-project.org/) [89]. PCA based on haplogroup frequencies was conducted via the ClusterHaplogroup module in Y-LineageTracker [90]. We conducted an additional PCA analysis by retaining populations with more than 20 individuals and/or excluding haplogroups with frequencies below 1%. Pairwise Φst values were calculated using Arlequin 3.5.2 based on the genotype data, and a genetic distance matrix heatmap was generated (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). By incorporating reference populations from other Asian groups, we performed PCA and genetic distance matrix heatmap analyses based on haplogroup data using Y-LineageTracker (Additional file 2: Fig.S1–2). The same analyses were repeated for newly genotyped samples grouped by ethnicity (Additional file 2: Fig.S10–11). NMDS was performed via R based on the pairwise Φst matrix. AMOVA was conducted via Arlequin 3.5.2, with groupings defined by geographic or linguistic categories. Each test used 1000 permutations (default setting). An unrooted N-J phylogenetic tree for the 41 populations was constructed via MEGA 11 based on Φst values, and annotated with iTOL.

Phylogenetic relationships

Correlation analyses were conducted to explore relationships between PCs, latitude/longitude, pairwise genetic distances, and fifth-level haplogroup frequencies across 41 populations. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed using the cor() function in base R, and p-values were obtained using the cor_pmat() function from the “ggcorrplot” package. Correlation matrices were visualized using the “corrplot” package. Venn analyses of shared and unique haplogroups among linguistically distinct populations, as well as northern and southern populations (divided by the Qinling–Huaihe line), and ethnically diverse groups, were performed using the “ggvenn” and “upset” R packages. Haplogroup frequency differences at each hierarchical level were estimated using Python scripts (Table S20) and visualized through Manhattan plots, with a frequency difference threshold of 0.025. To ensure robust identification of significant differences, both the Mann–Whitney U test and Welch’s t-test were performed to compare highly differentiated clades across geographically or linguistically distinct populations. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg correction using the p.adjust function in R (method = 'BH'). A haplogroup was considered significantly differentiated only when both adjusted p-values were below 0.05. All raw and adjusted p-values are reported in Additional file 1: Table S12. Significant haplogroup differences were visualized using boxplots, with medians marked by horizontal lines, boxes representing interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3), and whiskers extending 1.5 times the interquartile range. Using the “pairwise_distances” function from the “scikit-learn” library and “scipy.linalg.eigh” in Python, PCoA was performed based on third-level haplogroup frequencies. Stacked bar charts were generated using the “ggplot2” package in R to display third-level haplogroup compositions among ancient Chinese populations grouped by geographic regions and newly genotyped samples categorized by ethnic groups.

Phylogeographic characteristics

A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree of 5311 Chinese individuals was constructed via the PhyloHaplogroup module in Y-LineageTracker and annotated with iTOL [90]. The phylogeographic characteristics of founding lineages were visualized with Golden Software Surfer 23. Haplogroup frequencies across various regions were interpolated using the Ordinary Kriging algorithm, with a 1000 × 1000 grid and coordinate limits determined automatically. An external polygon boundary was used to constrain the interpolation area using the “blanking” function, ensuring that no data were assigned outside the geographic range of interest. All other Kriging parameters were set to the software’s default values. Regional frequency heatmaps for the three founding lineages were generated using ArcGIS software, and spatial autocorrelation analyses were performed via hotspot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*), identifying high-value clusters as potential diffusion or origin centers for specific haplogroups. Correlations between haplogroup frequencies of the three founding lineages and latitude/longitude coordinates were assessed via the “corrplot” R package.
