Preoperative diagnosis of Meckel diverticulitis with and without perforation in adult: Two case reports

Introduction

Although incomplete obliteration of the omphalomesenteric duct, called the Meckel's diverticulum (MD), is considerably common, patients are often diagnosed only when complications occur [1]. Although this condition predominantly affects children, fewer cases have been reported in adults [1,2]. The clinical presentation of MD spectrum can be highly variable, posing challenges to radiologists for accurate diagnosis. Prompt and accurate diagnosis is necessary to guide appropriate surgical intervention, especially in situations of threatening complications. The use of advanced imaging modalities, such as high-frequency ultrasound and CT scans, can facilitate the differential diagnosis in this scenario. Careful detection of subtle signs on CT images could improve the accuracy of preoperative detection of MD complications. In this report, we present two cases of MD with complications of diverticulitis and perforation in adults, diagnosed primarily through imaging modalities and confirmed via surgical exploration (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Case report

Case 1

A 40-year-old male patient presented with progressively worsening atypical epigastric pain and fever for one day with no significant medical history. Physical examination revealed focal tenderness in right lower quadrant, raising concern for acute appendicitis. Laboratory results showed no electrolyte imbalances but indicated infection including WBC: 10.3 G/L, neutrophils %: 87.2 %, CRP hs: 17.08 mg/L, other biological markers varied in normal limit. Imaging modalities were performed to find infectious target. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an isolated digestive structure adjacent to the small intestine near the umbilicus. Subsequent CT identified a small intestinal loop tethered to the distal ileum with surrounding fat stranding, suggesting Meckel's diverticulitis. Also, free intraperitoneal gas was noted, raising suspicion of diverticular perforation. Punctual laparoscopic exploration confirmed an inflamed, congested, blind-ending intestinal pouch with adjacent purulent fluid. Histopathological analysis verified an infected MD with perforation. The patient underwent successful surgical intervention was discharged in stable condition within 5 days and uneventful 1-month follow-up.

Case 2

A 42-year-old male presented with vague, progressively worsening right plank pain for 6 h, accompanied by diarrhoea. Initial investigations suggested a gastrointestinal infection, supported by abnormal blood tests results—WBC: 14.9 G/L, neutrophils %: 79 %, CRP hs: 0.8 mg/L. Subsequently, imaging study was ordered to identify the source of infection. While abdominal sonography ruled out appendicitis, it failed to pinpoint an inflamed organ. A consequent CT scan revealed a distended, fluid- and gas-filled mimic intestinal loop communicating with the distal ilium, raising suspicion of Meckel's diverticulitis. This rare entity in adults complicated the initial diagnosis. Given the diagnostic uncertainty, laparoscopy was performed within an hour to clarify and address the urgent situation. Histopathology confirmed and infected MD. The patient recovered well, was discharged in stable condition after one week, and demonstrated good health at a one-month follow-up.

Discussion

Meckel's diverticulum, a congenital abnormality of the small intestine, arises from incomplete obliteration of the omphalomesenteric duct. This condition was first officially published by anatomist Johann Friedrich Meckel in 1809 [4]. This entity is known to be prominent in children [1] while there have been <100 synthesized and sporadic case reports in adult patients since 2000 in Pubmed [2,[5], [6], [7], [8]]… In among, case reports from Asian institutions seem to be rare [9,10]. This article also means to enrich the literature in this population.

Overall, the ‘rule of 2s’ characterising of this condition has been widely used: occurs in 2 % of infants, measures about 2 in. length, is located 2 ft from the ileocecal valve, contains two types of ectopic tissue (gastric and pancreatic), and is twice as prevalent in males compared to females [11].

Pathologically, MD can manifest as a fibrous band, an omphalomesenteric duct cyst, or a 3–10 cm blind pouch on the anti-mesenteric border of the small bowel. This pouch could be lined with all the layers of the intestinal wall or contain ectopic tissue from the gastric mucosa or pancreatic tissue [12].

Although the majority of cases are asymptomatic, complications arise in 4.2–6.4 % of patients, including haemorrhage from ectopic tissue, bowel obstruction due to intussusception or adhesions, and symptoms mimicking appendicitis, ulceration, perforation, or neoplasms [4,13].

The broad spectrum of clinical presentations necessitates the use of advanced imaging modalities for an accurate preoperative diagnosis. In worldwide literature, abdominal CT is regarded as the first-line diagnostic tool owing to its high spatial resolution and capability for multi-planar reconstruction [14] so far. The normal MD is similar to normal bowel loop while complicated diverticulum images vary in differently depending on the complication [15]. The general appearance is a blind-ending triangular or tubular outpouching from anti-mesenteric margin of distal ileum [15]. Nuclear medicine, employing technetium-99 m, is considered the most sensitive diagnostic test for identifying ectopic gastric mucosa within the diverticulum and is more sensitive and specialised in children (80–90 % and 95–98 %, respectively) than in adults, with a sensitivity of 62.5 % and a positive predictive value of 60 % [15]. Angiography can be useful in detecting significant bleeding (>0.5 mL/min) [11], while high-frequency ultrasound is effective in diagnosing bleeding Meckel diverticulitis in paediatric cases as the first imaging modality [15]. In adult patients, ultrasonography serves as a valuable tool to rule out other acute conditions, such as appendicitis or colonic diverticulitis, and to identify the source of pain for further evaluation through CT.

Symptomatic MD generally necessitates surgical excision via laparoscopic or open techniques [1]. Meanwhile, the management of asymptomatic cases remains controversial, although prophylactic surgery may be warranted in cases with a high risk of future complications [16]. The research in 2005 of the MayoClinic attributed one of following details as risk factors to combine the risk of MD complication: under 50-year-old, male, length of MD over 2 cm, presence of mucosal heterotopy in macroscopic specimen [13]. In the presence of one to four details could qualify patients to prophylactic excision depending on different authors or case-by-case [13,16].

According to the synthesis on 27 adult cases of Hernández et al. [2], most of hospitalized interval is about 7 days in a range from 2 to 24 days. One early idiopathic complication occurred in ninth postoperative day due to aponeurosis tear, and other 2 late complications within 3 years due to ileitis and intestinal obstruction.

In our cases, both adult patients presented with abdominal pain, clinical signs and blood tests revealed the infectious status. First-hand ultrasonography followed by CT revealed Meckel diverticulitis with and without perforation in patients 1 and 2, respectively. Prompt surgical intervention corroborated the diagnosis, underscoring the critical role of imaging in effective management of this condition. Both patients discharged safely without early secondary complication. By those cases, authors extract 3 proper key points in assessment. The first key point is the process of first hand high-frequency ultrasound as a reliable tool for excluding urgent conditions and guiding further diagnosis, subsequent CT imaging as advanced modality to facilitate the key to diagnose MD complications which is the location and anti-mesenteric communicating to distal ileum, disregard for the diameter and content of the pouch. The second key point is the awareness of overlapped complications, which means intra-abdominal gas might be perforation from diverticulitis. The third key point is the probability of MD complication occurred in adults; this should not be ignored in differential diagnosis.

Conclusion

Meckel's diverticulum, although a relatively rare congenital anomaly, can lead to significant complications such as diverticulitis with and without perforation, posing substantial health risks if not promptly diagnosed and treated. The authors suggest the process of using high-frequency ultrasonography followed by CT Scan to navigate and narrow the targets of differential diagnosis. Moreover, all the subtle signs on CT imaging should alert the awareness of one to multiple MD complications despite their rarity in comparison to other emergency situations. This cautiousness of radiologists could enhance accurate early detection which, in turn, could enable the appropriate management in varying scenarios. In other words, timely surgical intervention based on accurate preoperative diagnosis can significantly improve patient outcomes.

Besides, authors would like to contribute to the worldwide literature of MD complication in adults among Asian population.
