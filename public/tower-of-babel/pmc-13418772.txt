Self‐Reported Driving Problems Predict Future Traffic Crashes in Older Men: Prevalence and Determinants of Unrecognized Imminent Risk
Authors: Ji Won Han, Tae Hui Kim, Kyung Phil Kwak, Bong Jo Kim, Shin Gyeom Kim, Jeong Lan Kim, Seok Woo Moon, Joon Hyuk Park, Seung‐Ho Ryu, Dong Woo Lee, Seok Bum Lee, Jung Jae Lee, Jin Hyeong Jhoo, Ki Woong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOlder drivers exhibit elevated crash risk per distance driven, yet accurately identifying high‐risk individuals without unjustly restricting mobility remains challenging. Whether older drivers can accurately assess their own driving‐related risks remains controversial, and educational attainment—as a proxy for cognitive reserve—may influence this capacity for self‐assessment.MethodsThis population‐based prospective cohort study included 1673 male drivers aged 60 years and older from the Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD). Participants were classified into four risk phenotypes based on concordance between baseline self‐reported driving concerns and subsequent crash occurrence: Drivers with inherent safety (DIS), drivers with regulated safety (DRS), drivers with recognized risk (DRR), and drivers with unrecognized risk (DUR). Self‐reported and informant‐reported driving problems were assessed using the driving risk questionnaire (DRQ). Crash records were obtained from the Korean National Police Agency (KNPA) over a 2‐year follow‐up.ResultsOf 290 drivers involved in crashes, 179 (61.7%) had reported baseline concerns (DRR), while 111 (38.3%) had not, representing ‘silent’ at‐risk drivers—those who reported no driving concerns at baseline but subsequently experienced crashes (DUR). Lower baseline Self‐Informant Discrepancy (mean SID: −0.05 in the unrecognized risk group vs. 3.15 in the recognized risk group; p < 0.001) characterized the unrecognized risk group, while informant reports showed a floor effect (94.3% zero) and did not predict crashes, necessitating objective screening. Among drivers without self‐reported concerns, lower education (≤ 9 years) combined with slower TMT‐B performance (≥ 180 s) identified a high‐risk subgroup with a crash rate of 20.9% (OR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.29–3.22; p = 0.002).ConclusionsSelf‐reported driving concerns demonstrate predictive validity for future crashes, but a substantial minority of crash‐involved drivers remain undetected by self‐report. Education and TMT‐B performance may serve as clinically actionable markers for identifying these “silent” at‐risk drivers.Keywords: driving safety, older adults, self‐report validity, trail making test, unrecognized risk

Introduction

Driving is a critical determinant of autonomy, social engagement, and quality of life [1, 2, 3] among older adults. However, older drivers exhibit higher rates of fatal crash involvement per distance driven compared with middle‐aged drivers [4, 5, 6]. This creates a clinical dilemma: how to accurately identify high‐risk drivers without unjustly restricting the mobility of those who remain safe. Currently, the field relies heavily on cognitive testing. While the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) [7], Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [8], Trail Making Test (TMT) [9], or Stroop test [10] are standard, their ability to predict real‐world crashes remains inconsistent [11, 12]. This gap likely exists because safe driving requires a dynamic integration of behavioral and compensatory skills that cannot be adequately captured by static cognitive tests [13, 14, 15].

To bridge this gap, clinical attention is shifting toward self‐assessment. Indeed, evidence suggests that some older adults possess valid insight into their declining abilities and self‐regulate accordingly [16, 17, 18]. However, reliance on self‐report is complicated by the insight paradox: while some drivers accurately self‐limit, others exhibit profound overconfidence [19, 20]. Specifically, the discrepancy between a driver's self‐report and an informant's observation (self‐informant discrepancy; SID) has been proposed as a proxy for insight [21]. Yet, the neurocognitive and sociodemographic mechanisms underlying why some drivers maintain insight while others lose it remain poorly understood.

A critical, yet often overlooked, factor in this insight equation is educational attainment. In the context of cognitive aging, education serves as a primary proxy for cognitive reserve and health literacy [22]. According to the cognitive reserve hypothesis, individuals with higher education possess more robust neural networks and superior metacognitive monitoring abilities. This enables recognition of subtle performance changes and behavioral adaptation. Conversely, older adults with lower education may experience functional decline but lack the reserve capacity to recognize it. We hypothesize that the failure to recognize driving risk is not solely a function of hardware failure (raw cognitive deficits like slow processing speed) but rather a software failure (lack of cognitive reserve to monitor those deficits), heavily influenced by educational background [23].

From a public safety perspective, the most dangerous driver is not necessarily the one with the worst cognitive scores, but the one who is unaware of their deficits. We identify a specific phenotype of concern: drivers who report no problems at baseline but subsequently experience crashes. We term this phenomenon “Unrecognized Imminent Risk.” Unlike classical anosognosia, which denies established deficits, this represents a failure of prospective insight (risk awareness)—an inability to recognize the warning signs that presage a crash.

To capture these subjective perceptions, standardized items from the Driving Risk Questionnaire (DRQ)—originally developed within the American Academy of Neurology's practice parameter [24] for driving evaluation in dementia—were adapted. Although designed for dementia populations, the items assessing self‐reported tickets, crashes, and at‐fault crashes are relevant to broader older driver populations. Validating these items in a non‐demented older driver cohort constitutes a key objective of the present study.

This prospective study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalences of Recognized and Unrecognized Imminent Risk among drivers who subsequently experience crashes; (2) evaluate the predictive validity of cognitive tests and self‐reported driving problems (DRQ‐Self) for the Imminent Risk; and (3) investigate the determinants of Unrecognized Imminent Risk, specifically determining whether lower education (cognitive reserve) and domain‐specific executive dysfunction operate through interactive or distinct additive pathways to precipitate this high‐risk phenotype.

We focused exclusively on male drivers because, according to the Korean Road Traffic Authority [25], men accounted for approximately 85% of licensed drivers aged 60 years and older during the study period (2010–2012). Furthermore, traffic crash records from the Korean National Police Agency (KNPA) were not available for female participants in our cohort, precluding their inclusion.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD), a nationwide, population‐based investigation of community‐dwelling older adults [26, 27]. From the parent KLOSCAD cohort (N = 6818), 3919 female participants were excluded—as KNPA crash records were available only for male participants—leaving 2899 males. Of these, 831 did not hold a valid driver's license, yielding 2068 licensed male participants. Among them, 253 had ceased driving, resulting in 1815 actively driving licensed males. After excluding 142 participants with missing DRQ data (either self‐ or informant‐report), the final analytic sample comprised 1673 male drivers (Figure 1).

Participant flow diagram. Flow diagram showing the sequential exclusion of participants from the Korean Longitudinal Study on Cognitive Aging and Dementia (KLOSCAD) parent cohort (N = 6818) to the final analytic sample (N = 1673). DRQ, driving risk questionnaire.

Traffic crash records were obtained from the Korean National Police Agency (KNPA) database through an official data‐sharing agreement. Under Article 54 of the Korean Road Traffic Act, drivers involved in traffic crashes resulting in death, injury, or property damage are mandated to report the incident to the police. The KNPA crash database follows the Traffic Accident Analysis System (TAAS) criteria, which define a traffic crash as an incident involving personal injury (death, serious injury requiring ≥ 3 weeks of treatment, minor injury requiring 5 days to < 3 weeks, or slight injury requiring < 5 days) on a road as defined by Article 2 of the Road Traffic Act. Property‐damage‐only crashes without personal injury are excluded from the TAAS statistical database. For each crash, the KNPA designates the at‐fault driver (the party with greater fault) versus the victim party. Participants were identified through the KNPA database as the at‐fault driver in at least one injury‐involved crash during the 2‐year follow‐up period.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (B‐0912/089–010), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Baseline Driving Assessment and SID

Subjective driving behaviors were assessed using items adapted from the Driving Risk Questionnaire (DRQ), originally included in the American Academy of Neurology practice parameter for driving evaluation in dementia [24]. Specifically, we used the unsafe driving behavior items encompassing self‐reported tickets, crashes, and at‐fault crashes with a 3‐year recall period. The exact item wording was: (1) ‘How many times have you been stopped or ticketed for a traffic violation in the last three years?’; (2) ‘How many accidents have you been in, or caused, within the last three years?’; and (3) ‘In how many accidents were you at fault in the last three years?’ Response options for each item were 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4+. Other components of the original algorithm, such as screening for sleep disorders, sedating medications, and alcohol use, were not assessed in the present study.

The DRQ was administered independently to the participant (DRQ‐Self) and their informant (DRQ‐Informant), typically the spouse or adult child. DRQ‐Self‐Unsafe and DRQ‐Informant‐Unsafe were calculated as the sum of reported tickets, accidents, and at‐fault accidents. Because the at‐fault crash item is a subset of the total crash item, an at‐fault crash contributed 2 points to the composite score (intentional double‐weighting reflecting additional risk). The SID score was calculated as follows: SID = DRQ‐Self‐Unsafe − DRQ‐Informant‐Unsafe.

Classification of Risk Recognition Phenotypes

All participants were classified into four mutually exclusive phenotypes based on concordance between baseline DRQ‐Self‐Unsafe score and subsequent crash records: (1) Drivers with inherent safety (DIS): no driving problems reported and no crashes; (2) drivers with regulated safety (DRS): driving problems reported but no crashes; (3) Drivers with Recognized Risk (DRR): driving problems reported and crashes occurred; and (4) drivers with unrecognized risk (DUR): no driving problems reported despite subsequent crashes (“Silent Risk”).

Neuropsychological and Diagnostic Assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease Neuropsychological Assessment Battery (CERAD‐K‐N) [28, 29], including verbal fluency test, modified boston naming test, word list memory/recall/recognition tests, constructional praxis/recall tests, and trail making test A/B. We defined objective cognitive impairment as performing −1.5 standard deviation (SD) or below on any neuropsychological tests compared to age, sex, and education‐adjusted norms [29, 30, 31, 32]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was diagnosed according to International Working Group consensus criteria [33] and dementia according to DSM‐IV criteria [34].

Statistical Analysis

We employed two complementary analytic approaches. First, for predictive validity analysis, we used hierarchical (sequential) binary logistic regression with crash occurrence as the dependent variable. Models were constructed incrementally to quantify the independent contribution of each predictor class: Model 1 included demographic variables (age, education) only; Model 2 added cognitive measures (VMS, VFT, TMT‐B); Model 3 added self‐reported and informant‐reported driving concerns (DRQ‐self‐unsafe, DRQ‐informant‐unsafe); and Model 4 replaced the separate self‐ and informant‐reports with the SID score. Model discrimination was evaluated using the C‐statistic, which is equivalent to the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and ranges from 0.5 (no discrimination) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), with values of 0.6–0.7 generally considered ‘poor‐to‐fair’ and 0.7–0.8 ‘acceptable’. Incremental improvement in discrimination was assessed by comparing C‐statistics between nested models. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), where lower values indicate better fit. Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate the statistical significance of model improvement at each step.

Second, for unrecognized risk analysis, we performed stratified analyses by separating drivers who reported driving concerns at baseline (DRQ‐self‐unsafe ≥ 1) from those who reported none (DRQ‐self‐unsafe = 0). This stratification was motivated by the clinical hypothesis that predictors of crash risk may differ between drivers who can self‐identify risk factors (potentially amenable to self‐report‐based screening) and those who cannot (requiring alternative screening strategies). Within each stratum, logistic regression models were fitted with the same predictor variables. Finally, among drivers without self‐reported concerns, we constructed risk profiles using education and TMT‐B cut‐off values to identify clinically actionable subgroups for targeted objective screening. All analyses used Python 3.9 with scikit‐learn and statsmodels. Two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The sample comprised 1673 male drivers with mean (SD) age 68.0 (5.4) years and mean education 11.9 (4.4) years (Table 1). Of these, 1206 (72.1%) had normal cognition and 467 (27.9%) met criteria for MCI. The mean DRQ‐Self‐Unsafe score was 1.3 (1.9), and mean DRQ‐informant‐unsafe score was 0.1 (0.7). A notable floor effect was observed for informant reports: 1577 participants (94.3%) had DRQ‐informant‐unsafe scores of zero compared to 876 (52.4%) with DRQ‐self‐unsafe scores of zero.

During the 2‐year follow‐up, 290 participants (17.3%) experienced at least one police‐reported traffic crash. Critically, among these drivers with imminent risk, 38.3% (111/290) had not reported any driving concerns at baseline, representing DUR. Among the 1383 drivers without crashes, 618 (44.7%) had reported driving concerns at baseline but did not experience crashes during the 2‐year follow‐up period (DRS).

The four risk phenotypes differed significantly in baseline characteristics (Table 1). Educational attainment was notably lower in at‐risk groups (DRR and DUR) compared to safe groups (DIS and DRS), with DUR showing the lowest mean education of 10.4 years (p < 0.001). DRQ‐Informant‐Unsafe scores were higher in DRS and DRR compared to DIS (p < 0.001), but notably, informant reports did not differentiate DUR from DIS, confirming that informants also failed to recognize the risk in this group. DUR exhibited the longest TMT‐B completion times of 191.1 s, indicating executive dysfunction (p < 0.001). The prevalence of MCI was numerically highest in DUR (33.3%) but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.114).

Prediction of Imminent Risk

Hierarchical logistic regression models were constructed to identify predictors of imminent risk (Table 2). In Model 1 (demographics only), lower education predicted crash risk (odds ratio [OR], 0.93 per year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.90–0.96, p < 0.001). The C‐statistic for this model was 0.59. Adding cognitive measures in Model 2 did not improve prediction (C‐statistic, 0.60; Δ, +0.01; likelihood ratio test p = 0.066). Among cognitive variables, only TMT‐B reached significance (OR, 1.002 per second; p < 0.05).

In contrast, Model 3, which added self‐reported and informant‐reported driving concerns, substantially improved prediction (C‐statistic, 0.675; Δ, +0.071 from Model 2; likelihood ratio test p < 0.001). Self‐reported concerns predicted imminent risk (OR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.20–1.36; p < 0.001), whereas informant‐reported concerns did not contribute independently to prediction (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.87–1.19; p = 0.854). In summary, self‐reported driving concerns predicted crash risk beyond demographic and cognitive variables, while informant reports did not contribute independently.

Stratified Analysis by Self‐Reported Concern

Among the 797 drivers who reported driving concerns at baseline, 179 (22.5%) had traffic crashes during the 2‐year follow‐up (DRR), while 618 (77.5%) remained crash‐free despite their concerns (DRS). Only educational attainment predicted crash occurrence (OR, 0.95 per year; 95% CI, 0.91–0.99, p = 0.027; Table 3; C‐statistic of 0.604).

The 876 drivers who reported no driving concerns represented a critical clinical challenge, as self‐report (the strongest predictor) was unavailable for risk stratification. Among them, 111 (12.7%) subsequently experienced crashes (DUR) despite reporting no baseline concerns, while 765 (87.3%) remained crash‐free (DIS). Similarly, only education predicted crashes (OR, 0.94 per year; 95% CI, 0.89–0.99, p = 0.016; Table 3). Both groups had near‐zero informant scores (DIS: 0.04, DUR: 0.05), confirming that informants did not detect driving problems even in drivers who would subsequently crash.

The high‐risk profile showed more than doubled crash risk (adjusted OR = 2.04; 95% CI, 1.29–3.22; p = 0.002) after controlling for age, MCI status, and prior violations. When the most restrictive cut‐off was applied (education ≤ 6 years + TMT‐B ≥ 180 s), 91 drivers showed a crash rate of 25.3% (RR = 2.77 vs. reference).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study provides novel evidence for both the utility and limitations of driving self‐assessment among older adults. Our findings demonstrate that baseline self‐reported driving problems predict future crashes (OR = 1.28) and meaningfully improve prediction beyond demographics (C‐statistic Δ = +0.071). At the same time, the high prevalence of unrecognized risk (38.3% of crash‐involved drivers) highlights that some at‐risk drivers fail to recognize their difficulties at baseline. While the final model's discrimination (C‐statistic = 0.675) falls within the “fair” range, this must be interpreted within the stochastic, multifactorial nature of traffic crashes [35]. The primary metric of clinical utility is effective risk stratification: this simple, two‐minute clinical screen (Education + TMT‐B) identifies a “silent” high‐risk group with doubled crash risk (OR = 2.04; Figure 2).

A striking finding was the poor predictive performance of informant reports (p = 0.854), which starkly contradicts Western dementia literature where collateral history is often considered the gold standard [36, 37]. We observed a severe floor effect, with 94.3% of informants reporting zero driving problems. We interpret this as a “Cultural False Negative” phenomenon rather than mere measurement error. In this cohort of older Korean men, informants were predominantly non‐driving wives. Within traditional Confucian values, spouses may avoid reporting deficits that could lead to license revocation and loss of the husband's autonomy [38]. Cross‐tabulation confirmed this pattern: only 24 participants (1.3%) had informant‐reported concerns without corresponding self‐reports, versus 707 (37.2%) who self‐reported problems their informants did not, regardless of MCI status. This carries critical implications for practice in Asian or patriarchal cultural settings: absence of informant‐reported concerns should not confirm driving safety.

Classical anosognosia refers to denial of established deficits [39]. In contrast, our drivers lacked prospective insight (risk awareness). The finding that lower baseline SID strongly predicts unrecognized risk group membership (SID, −0.05 vs. 3.15; p < 0.001) suggests that “silent” at‐risk drivers may be resistant to driving modification recommendations because they do not perceive a problem. Clinical interventions targeting insight enhancement may be warranted for this group, potentially including objective driving evaluations, simulator assessments, or structured feedback sessions.

Although the absolute crash rate was lower in the self‐report‐negative group (12.7% over 2 years) than in the self‐report‐positive group (22.5%), the clinical significance of the DUR phenotype lies not in its absolute crash rate but in its implications for screening and prevention. Drivers who report concerns can be identified and targeted through standard clinical assessment. In contrast, DUR entirely bypass self‐report‐based screening mechanisms—the strongest available predictor—making them invisible to conventional assessment. This is why objective risk stratification (Education + TMT‐B) is critical specifically for this subgroup.

Our risk stratification analysis suggests a mechanistic integration best understood through a “Hardware vs. Software” framework. TMT‐B slowing represents the ‘Hardware Failure’—an objective decline in processing speed and executive control required for complex driving maneuvers [9, 40]. Conversely, education serves as a proxy for the “Software” [41, 42]—specifically, the cognitive reserve and health literacy required for metacognitive monitoring [22]. We note that TMT‐B is a multi‐domain test tapping visual search, task switching, processing speed, and working memory; its association with crash risk may reflect deficits across multiple cognitive domains relevant to driving rather than a specific executive function impairment. This broader interpretation is consistent with evidence that posterior (occipital/parietal) dysfunction, in addition to frontal impairment, can contribute to driving difficulties in older cohorts. We recommend that clinicians consider drivers with ≤ 9 years of education as candidates for objective driving screening using TMT‐B. With the more restrictive cut‐off (≤ 6 years), crash rates reached 25.3% (RR = 2.77 vs. reference).

Self‐reported driving concerns capture risk information beyond demographics, cognitive tests, or informant reports provide, but 38.3% of at‐risk drivers lack this self‐awareness. The non‐significant contribution of informant reports in Model 3, combined with the predictive value of SID in Model 4, suggests that the discrepancy between self‐perception and external observation is the critical factor. The 618 drivers who reported concerns but did not crash may reflect successful compensatory self‐regulation, although longer follow‐up could reveal additional crashes. Given the substantial limitations of both self‐report and informant reports, clinicians conducting fitness‐to‐drive evaluations should consider supplementing subjective reports with objective records such as official police traffic records.

Several limitations warrant discussion. The two‐year crash rate in our sample (17.3%, or approximately 8.7% per year) is higher than the commonly cited 4%–5% annual rate for US drivers aged 65 and older [43, 44]. Several factors may contribute: the Korean KNPA database records all police‐reported crashes involving personal injury (including minor injuries requiring less than 5 days of treatment), which may capture a broader range of incidents than crash definitions used in some US studies [43, 45, 46]; a prior analysis of this same KLOSCAD cohort [6] reported a consistent age‐standardized rate of 38.7 per 1000 person‐years among Korean male drivers aged 60 and older; and differences in driving environments, traffic density, road infrastructure, and crash reporting thresholds between Korea and the US may also contribute. To contextualize, according to the TAAS database in 2012, at‐fault drivers aged 61–64 years accounted for 20,223 injury crashes and those aged ≥ 65 years for 42,369, out of a national total of 196,349—a smaller proportion than drivers aged 41–50 (32,535 crashes) or 51–60 (43,312), though older drivers' crashes carry disproportionately higher fatality rates (3.1% for drivers ≥ 65 years vs. 0.9% for younger drivers, TAAS 2010 data). According to TAAS, the proportion of older drivers (≥ 65 years) among all at‐fault drivers increased from 5.0% in 2011 to 8.4% in 2015, reflecting both an aging population and increased driving exposure among older Koreans. These findings support the clinical relevance of screening older Korean drivers despite the relatively modest absolute crash rate. Nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted within this Korean context, and generalizability to other driving environments requires further investigation.

We used educational attainment as the sole proxy for cognitive reserve; other established proxies, including occupational complexity, should be explored in future studies. Our definition of active driving (having driven within the past 2 years) is relatively liberal and may have included individuals with minimal driving exposure, potentially attenuating observed associations; future studies should consider frequency‐based definitions (e.g., driving at least once per week). Additionally, the DRQ does not distinguish moving from non‐moving violations (e.g., parking tickets).

Our restriction to male drivers limits external validity, particularly given evidence that women experience higher driving anxiety [47], which may influence self‐reported driving problems and risk perception differently. As older female driver populations grow in rapidly aging societies, future studies should include female cohorts to examine sex‐specific patterns.

Conclusions

Self‐reported driving problems demonstrate predictive validity for future traffic crashes in older male drivers, outperforming informant reports and cognitive testing. However, approximately 40% of crash‐involved drivers had not recognized their risk at baseline, and lower baseline SID prospectively identifies this unrecognized risk group. These findings support a two‐pronged clinical approach: self‐reported problems should be taken seriously as a marker of elevated risk [48], but absence of self‐reported concerns should not be reassuring. Clinical driving evaluations should incorporate self‐assessment while recognizing its limitations, and should consider objective assessment for drivers who report few concerns. For drivers without self‐reported concerns, risk stratification by education (≤ 9 years) and TMT‐B performance (≥ 180 s) identifies a high‐risk profile (OR = 2.04) warranting formal driving assessment regardless of their self‐reported confidence.
