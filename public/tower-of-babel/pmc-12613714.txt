The safety of cabotegravir in pregnancy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amber Jasper, Willem Fourie, Sarentha Chetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV remains an important global public health crisis. Antiretroviral treatment has been essential to suppress viral replication, and in at-risk individuals, antiretroviral pre-exposure prophylaxis drugs (PrEP) lower transmission rates. Unfortunately, current oral regimens require stringent daily adherence to be effective. Long-acting intra-muscular cabotegravir potentially averts the issue of poor patient adherence. This formulation has the potential to cause a breakthrough in mitigating the spread of HIV; however, safety in pregnancy is still to be established. This systematic review aimed to assess the data relating to the safety and prevalence of adverse effects of cabotegravir in pregnancy.Methods and analysisThe research followed the PRISMA guidelines and Cochrane Handbook. Keyword and MeSH term combinations were utilised. Studies matching the inclusion criteria published between 2016 and 2023 through EMBASE, PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, Medline, and Web of Science were reviewed. Studies were screened by title and abstract, followed by full-text screening by two independent investigators. Duplicate studies were removed. Relevant data was extracted from the selected studies. Critical appraisal and the risk of bias were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) SUMARI program. The I2 test was used to assess statistical heterogeneity.Results and outcomesOf the 12,357 studies identified, 6 articles were included in the review. This review investigated 41 known pregnancies in females on cabotegravir, of which 25 live babies were born, 8 were not born due to spontaneous/elective abortion, and 8 were lost to follow-up. No congenital abnormalities, birth defects, or foetal adverse effects were reported.ConclusionThe small sample size currently precludes a definitive conclusion on the safety profile of cabotegravir use during pregnancy. More research in this area is required to assess this drug’s role in utero.Systematic review registrationThis systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD42023449146.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11817-2.Keywords: Cabotegravir, Rilpivirine, HIV, Prevention, PrEP, Pregnancy, Safety

Introduction/background

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection remains a significant global public health crisis. To date, the disease has infected between 65 and 113 million people worldwide and taken between 32.9 and 51.4 million lives. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), 39 million people were living with HIV at the end of 2022. Despite the availability of effective treatments, HIV still carries a high disease burden and mortality rates, with over 650,000 people dying of HIV-related illnesses annually on a global scale [1]. Countries in Sub-Saharan Africa are the most affected by HIV, having the highest number of infections and deaths worldwide. In these countries, HIV accounts for up to 28% of the overall mortality rates [2].

Treatment options

There is currently no cure or approved vaccine for HIV. It is a lifelong disease; however, disease progression can be managed if diagnosed early, and patients comply with treatment [3]. There are a wide variety of different antiretroviral medications which can also be used to treat HIV once infected. These medications maintain viral suppression by keeping the viral load low, thereby limiting transmission [1]. These may consist of oral drug combinations or newer long-acting agents such as cabotegravir in combination with rilpivirine.

Oral prevention methods

One way of preventing the transmission of HIV between an infected individual and a high-risk exposed individual is through the use of pre-exposure prophylaxis, known as PrEP [4]. Forms of PrEP include devices such as the dapivirine vaginal ring, which slowly releases the drug for up to 28 days [5], and oral drug combinations [6]. Emtricitabine and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or emtricitabine and tenofovir alafenamide are the two most common oral drug combinations used [7]. By decreasing transmission, these drugs significantly improve morbidity and mortality associated with HIV [6]. According to the WHO guidelines, PrEP is offered to individuals who are most at risk of contracting HIV [2]. These guidelines may differ slightly between countries [7]; however, these high-risk individuals generally include sex workers, transgender people, serodiscordant couples, individuals with multiple sexual partners, men having sexual relations with men, incarcerated individuals, and intravenous drug users [8]. After more than a decade of research and several clinical trials, oral PrEP has been shown to be between 44% − 86% effective in preventing HIV transmission globally [9]. This large discrepancy is due to the varying levels of drug compliance [10]. Across the globe, specifically in Southern African countries, the number of people consistently taking PrEP is still too low to control the virus’s spread fully [11]. The oral HIV prevention strategies are not implemented adequately enough to combat the high incidence rates of the virus in some countries. This is due to poor adherence and healthcare facilities having inadequate medication stocks [7].

Adherence issues

Advances in modern medicine have enabled the number of new HIV cases to decrease from 2.8 million people in 2000 to 1.3 million people in 2022 [9]. However, despite the availability of effective oral medications for prevention, the global HIV burden continues to rise by about 5000 new cases each day [12]. Poor adherence is a problem [4]. Daily pills require intentional effort on the part of the patient to take them, as well as healthcare facilities having adequate stock present, which is a problem faced by many poorer sub-Saharan countries. Treatment interruptions can also fuel mutations in the viral genome, leading to the emergence of drug-resistant HIV strains [13].

Cabotegravir

Cabotegravir is a long-acting intra-muscular injectable requiring administration every one to two months [14]. It is an HIV-1 integrase inhibitor. It can be used in both HIV prevention and treatment. This pharmacological agent can be administered less often than oral medication, reducing problems associated with adherence significantly [15]. IM cabotegravir can be given alone or in a combined formulation with rilpivirine for added benefit. Rilpivirine is a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor that enhances HIV virologic suppression when given with cabotegravir [12]. IM cabotegravir is mostly used for HIV prevention, whilst oral cabotegravir is used to assess tolerance prior to shifting to injectables for HIV treatment [14].

It is believed that over the next 20 years, long-acting injectables of cabotegravir have the potential to prevent a large number of new HIV infections, particularly in Southern African countries [16]. Researchers have estimated that cabotegravir can prevent up to 42,000 infections annually in South Africa, compared to the 16,300 infections prevented by oral pills [17]. Cabotegravir has the potential to improve HIV prevention strategies because of the many advantages it offers over oral PrEP. It has a significantly increased half-life. Cabotegravir has a half-life of 41 h when given orally but 5.6–11.5 weeks when given intramuscularly. Rilpivirine has a half-life of 45 h when given orally but 13–28 weeks when given intramuscularly [14]. This is because IM administration allows for the drug to be released slowly from the muscle over a prolonged period. Muscles have a rich vasculature, allowing the drug to be easily absorbed into the systemic circulation [18]. IM drug administration avoids first-pass metabolism and the potential for certain drug-drug interactions. The oral formulation of the drug is usually given before initiating the IM route to ensure patient tolerability. The oral form of cabotegravir can also be given as a top-up dose in the event of a missed appointment if taken within a week of a scheduled dose [14]. The longer-acting benefits of the injectable formulation also allow for increased patient privacy due to the requirement for fewer clinic visits [15]. Fewer clinic visits may improve adherence as patients do not feel as exposed or stigmatised as they may on monthly collections of daily oral pills [19].

Cabotegravir rollout

This drug was first approved for use in HIV prophylaxis in Canada in March 2020, and then Europe and the United States followed suit in December 2020. It was approved for HIV treatment in January 2021 [14]. South Africa registered and approved cabotegravir injectables in December 2022 and began piloting its use in early 2023 [16]. Researchers advise that the drug formulation needs to become more cost-effective for Southern African countries before it can be viable as a countrywide HIV prophylaxis option [20]. However, cabotegravir has the potential to prevent between 15 and 28% of new HIV infections when compared to the current oral treatment of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine [7]. This means that it could potentially be more cost-effective in the long run as economic costs associated with hospitalisations and management of HIV-associated infections are reduced. Innovative financial plans will need to be created to help with the short-term costs, eventually resulting in long-term savings [17].

Aims and objectives

Despite the availability of a wide range of HIV medication, the number of new infections continues to rise, demonstrating that the epidemic is still prevalent [12]. Due to the nature of HIV being a sexually transmitted disease, serodiscordant couples or women with multiple sexual partners may be on a HIV drug regimen for PrEP. If they fall pregnant while on cabotegravir, healthcare workers need to understand the implications and steps required to mitigate adverse effects. Rilpivirine is generally well tolerated in pregnancy, although further studies are needed [21]. However, limited data is available investigating the safety of cabotegravir during pregnancy. Some of the concerns hypothesized from the data are the prevalence of birth defects, miscarriages, or other harm to the foetus or mother [22]. This review aimed to evaluate the literature available on the potential risks of this drug during pregnancy. The primary objective was to investigate the safety of cabotegravir in pregnancy. This included foetal viability and teratogenicity. The secondary objective was to investigate possible adverse effects on the mother and foetus while on cabotegravir.

Methods

Review reporting

This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported based on PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis) statements [23]. Recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook were also used while performing the research.

Literature search strategy

Eligible studies were retrieved from 7 databases: PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE. All literature published in English from 1 January 2013 to 31 December 2023 was searched without restrictions on study type or country of study. 2016 was when the first cabotegravir drug trials were conducted; thus, the chosen date range ensured a comprehensive search of the literature. Medical subject headings (MESH terms) were used to enhance the retrieval of relevant data (Table 1). Different keyword combinations linked with Boolean operators were also used (Table 6, Supplementary paper).

Research question

Is the HIV integrase inhibitor cabotegravir safe to use in pregnancy?

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were based on a PICOS statement:

P (Population): Pregnant females.

I (Intervention): Cabotegravir or cabotegravir and rilpivirine.

C (Comparison): Control groups receiving oral ARVs, such as tenofovir, emtricitabine, or lamivudine or a placebo drug.

O (Outcome): The primary outcome was the safety of cabotegravir in pregnancy. The secondary outcome was the types and frequency of adverse effects.

S (Study): Observational studies, case-control, cohort, quasi-experimental studies, RCT and non-RCT studies.

Study screening and selection

All identified studies were exported to the Zotero version 6.0.26 reference manager. The duplicates were removed, and the remaining articles were exported to Excel. Studies were initially screened by title and abstract, followed by a full-text review by two independent investigators. This was done to assess whether the studies adequately addressed the research question and adhered to the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, a manual screening of reference lists of the included studies was carried out. Any conflict was resolved through discussion or involvement of a third investigator. Reasons for study exclusion were documented. The study selection process followed the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram showing the results of the literature searching, screening, and inclusion of articles

Study quality evaluation

Two independent investigators evaluated the quality of the studies. The JBI’s critical appraisal tools were used to quantify the risk of bias (Figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary paper). The program provided a series of questions to assess the quality of evidence regarding the selection, measurement, and analysis bias seen in the study. Answers of either Y/N/Unclear or N/A were provided to the questions. The program provided a different set of questions based on the type of study design. Articles then received a total score indicating how good the quality was. Each trial’s risk of bias assessment was formatted into a table, displaying the high, low, or unclear risk for each of the recommendations mentioned above (Table 3). Any conflicting views were discussed with an attempt at resolution. The final systematic review was compared to the PRISMA Checklist Tool to ensure all necessary components were present (Table 5, Supplementary paper).

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using the JBI SUMARI program. The odds ratio and weighted mean difference were calculated for the binary outcomes. This software also calculated the standardised mean, effect size, heterogeneity, and 95% confidence intervals. The data was presented with descriptive statistics and pooled estimates of the parameters. Statistically significant results were taken as having P values of less than 0.05. Data was expressed as proportions and compared between groups using risk ratios. Heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic. An I2 statistic of less than 50% is low, 50–75% is moderate, and above 75% is a high level of heterogeneity [24]. A P value of less than 0.1 and the chi-squared value were also used to indicate heterogeneity [25]. In cases where heterogeneity was present, possible causes were explored, and a subgroup analysis was done. A meta-analysis was conducted, and forest plots were drawn to compare similar studies. A funnel plot was also created to visualise the heterogeneity.

Results

Article inclusion

The initial search across 7 databases with the specified search terms yielded 12,357 results. Of the articles retrieved, 11,120 were duplicates, leaving 1,237 articles to be screened. After titles and abstracts were reviewed by two independent investigators, 1,131 articles were excluded. Of the 106 full-text articles reviewed, 5 were selected for inclusion in the analysis. A manual search of the reference lists of included articles led to a further full-text review of 6 articles, one of which was included in the study analysis. Some of the reasons for exclusion were that studies were review articles (n = 41), did not address the safety of the drug in pregnancy (n = 21), or were in-vitro/lab studies (n = 7). This search strategy led to 6 articles being used in this systematic review, with 2 included in the meta-analysis. The results of the screening process are presented in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

All included articles were published between 2018 and 2023. Of the included studies, five were RCTs, and one was a quasi-experimental study. These included the HPTN084 trial [26], HPTN077 trial [27], ATLAS-2 M trial [28], SOLAR trial [29], FLAIR trial [30] and a compassionate drug use program [31]. The studies either used cabotegravir alone (n = 2) or cabotegravir in combination with rilpivirine (n = 4). The studies compared the intervention of interest to a control of either IM saline placebo or other standards of care, such as tenofovir alafenamide, bictegravir, or emtricitabine. The quasi-experimental study did not have a control as this study was a compassionate drug use program and not a formal clinical trial. All five RCT outcomes were to assess the general safety or efficacy of the drug or both. The studies excluded pregnant women and required participating women to be on contraceptives. These articles were all multi-centre studies occurring in 4 to 14 countries. Table 2 summarises the outcomes, study design, and pregnancy results extracted from these articles.

Publication bias

The JBI critical appraisal tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies. Table 3 shows the quality of evidence that each of these studies possessed. The 5 RCTs received scores of between 7 and 11 out of a maximum of 13. This shows a low level of bias, meaning that the study is of good methodological quality. The quasi-experimental study scored 3 out of 9, indicating a high risk of bias. Since this was not a trial but rather a compassionate drug use program, there were no comparators and no strict protocol to follow. Each patient was reviewed on a case-by-case basis, with their personal physician required to report any evidence.

Adverse effects on the mother

Three of the studies reviewed reported that the mother experienced adverse effects in pregnancy. HPTN084 reported 9 incidents of adverse effects experienced in pregnancy, while on cabotegravir. These included hyperemesis gravidarum (n = 2), pregnancy-induced hypertension (n = 1), pre-eclampsia (n = 1), oligohydramnios (n = 1), premature membrane rupture (n = 2) and incomplete abortion (n = 2). This was compared to the TDF-FTC arm of the study, where the only pregnancy adverse effect recorded was hyperemesis gravidarum (n = 2). HPTN077, HPTN084 and the compassionate use program each had one pregnancy occur in their trial where the participant had pre-eclampsia. In the pregnancies where pre-eclampsia was present, the babies were delivered via caesarean section. (This occurred in the HPTN077 study at 38 weeks, the compassionate use trial at 37 weeks and was not recorded for HPTN084).

Adverse effects on the foetus

No congenital abnormalities, birth defects, or ectopic pregnancies have been recorded while mothers were pregnant and on cabotegravir. No information was reported of any adverse effects on the foetus.

Meta-analysis

A random effects model meta-analysis was created using JBI (Fig. 2). The Mantel Haenszel method was used to form a forest plot. The odds ratio was used as the effect size for this dichotomous data. The meta-analysis compared the live births on cabotegravir vs. the live births in the control. Only two studies were included in the meta-analysis. The other 4 studies in this systematic review could not be included as they did not have all comparable results available. This was due to protocol-specified pregnancy study withdrawal or poor follow-up practices. This meta-analysis showed that there was an overall 95% confidence interval of [0.60–8.24]. The summary statistic (diamond) that displays the weighted average and overall pooled value crosses the line of null effect, indicating that the results may not be statistically significant. This means that any differences in outcome between the control and the intervention are likely due to chance. There is an effect size of Z = 1.19 with a P value of 0.233. A P value of 0.23 indicates a high probability that the effects observed were due to chance alone. An I2 value of 0% shows that there is no heterogeneity, meaning there are low levels of difference in the methods and interventions used between the two studies. A large P value above 0.1 with a χ² value of 0.23 further indicates homogeneity. Due to this large P value and small sample size, no subgroup or sensitivity analysis tests were required. The results of this meta-analysis should be considered with caution due to the small sample size of pregnancies and the relatively small number of articles included.

Discussion

Cabotegravir represents a pinnacle development in the prevention of HIV,32 providing long-lasting viral protection without the stringent requirement of daily adherence. This systematic review aimed to analyse the literature available on women who became pregnant while on cabotegravir.

The meta-analysis demonstrates that it is not yet possible to draw a conclusive answer on the safety of cabotegravir in pregnancy. This is because of the current small sample size. With only 41 women found from the available literature to have reported pregnancies whilst on the drug, to date, there is not enough information available.

The drug has been licensed and approved; however, there is no conclusive data for pregnancy. Most clinical trials require eligible women to be on effective contraception and have no current fertility desires. This means that often, in the development of new antiretroviral drugs, drugs begin to be used in real-world settings without enough data being available for the pregnant population. This creates a degree of uncertainty, on the best choice of treatment options for this critical patient population. This potentially means that the older, less advanced products may be preferred to the newer, more effective agents due to a lack of clinical evidence [32]. Pharmacovigilance data in the post-licensing period on women who fall pregnant whilst on the drug is thus crucial to ensure further evidence is provided for the safety of the drug in pregnancy. This includes the effective collection, analysis, and monitoring of adverse effects in this population. Gathering this critical data will aid in making educated decisions for the population of people taking the drug.

Another issue that has been highlighted is the need for more information from follow-ups carried out post clinical trials [33]. Many women were withdrawn from the respective studies if they became pregnant, but no further information has been reported on the outcomes of mother and baby in these pregnancies. Information is, therefore, unclear or missing. This can be seen in the ATLAS-2 M, FLAIR and HPTN084 study, where no birth outcomes were reported for 8 pregnancies- this accounts for 19.5% of the cabotegravir pregnancies found. Most of the studies also had no information about the adverse effects experienced by mothers during pregnancy. Due to study protocol-specified regular pregnancy tests, women were withdrawn on early discovery of their pregnancy. This means that no data is available for cabotegravir use in the second or third trimester of pregnancy [34]. To date, on the Vigiaccess database, less than 1% of the adverse events reported in pregnancy are for patients on cabotegravir. This systematic review had 3 preeclampsia-reported pregnancies out of 41 pregnancies (A 7.3% incidence rate) whilst on cabotegravir. According to the WHO, preeclampsia occurs in 2–10% of pregnancies so this was within normal limits [2].

Furthermore, the uncertainty of the drug’s effects gives rise to worry and anxiety. The prominent issue found in the research available is that some women in cabotegravir clinical trials who become pregnant choose to have an elective abortion. Between 4.9% and 17% of women chose this option. The fear and lack of knowledge surrounding the drug may play a significant role in human decision-making. This shows the importance of ensuring that women understand the current literature on cabotegravir and the implications of its use so that they can make informed decisions.

In the discussion of cabotegravir’s safety in pregnancy, there are two conflicting discussions among academia:

Some scientists advocate that cabotegravir is safe based on its structural similarity to dolutegravir [35]. Since cabotegravir is an analogue of dolutegravir, it is appropriate to look at their comparisons and draw knowledge from that. Dolutegravir is a widely used integrase strand transfer inhibitor in pregnancy, which the WHO has suggested as a first-line agent [2]. It has a low 0.2% relative risk increase of neural tube defects and a good overall safety profile [36]. Based on cabotegravir’s resemblance to this drug, many hypothesize that it is a safe option in pregnancy. It is also advocated for because its potential benefits may outweigh the risks. With an 89% HIV risk reduction compared to oral tenofovir in the HPTN084 study, many people believe that the unknown chance of a pregnancy adverse effect is a smaller probability than the potential good that this drug can provide [37].

However, many other academics feel that cabotegravir is not safe in pregnancy. Cabotegravir studies have shown that the drug has a prolonged half-life and a long pharmacokinetic tail [38]. In some instances, the drug is still detectable in the systemic circulation 12 months following cessation. In the phase 3 clinical trials, women were switched from cabotegravir to oral PrEP on the discovery of pregnancy, and cabotegravir remained detectable (at levels above 0.5 µg/mL) throughout pregnancy in two participants- 40 to 52 weeks after the last injection. In the HPTN077 study, 42% of women still had detectable cabotegravir levels at 72 weeks after their last injection [27]. This prolonged tail data is similar in those adults who are not pregnant [39]. The exposure to the drug for a long period of time may harm pregnant mothers and foetuses, so caution is advised [40].

While there is a need to understand the drug’s effect on human pregnancy, animal models can help provide some beneficial foundational knowledge. In one animal study, cabotegravir was given orally to rats at a 0/0.5/5/100 mg/kg/day dose [41]. This was done from day 6 of gestation to 21 days after birth. The rats which received low doses of cabotegravir displayed no foetal abnormalities, malformations, or negative effects on viability. However, a lower birth weight was observed. The rats which received 1000 mg/kg/day (28 times greater than the recommended human dose) displayed an increased number of stillbirths and neonatal deaths. These results highlighted the negative correlation between high dosages of cabotegravir and adverse effects in animal pregnancies [41].

Certain small vertebrates with a high degree of genetic similarity to humans are used to understand the toxicological effects of many drugs. Zebrafish that were given cabotegravir displayed significant changes in neuronal differentiation and brain development. This occurred at therapeutic and even sub-therapeutic doses. This study’s impairment in embryo neurogenesis emphasises the importance of similar long-term studies to be carried out in humans to assess the neurological development in children exposed to cabotegravir in utero [42].

Since pregnancy yields many metabolic changes in women, which affects the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of drugs, it is vital to understand the safe dosage of cabotegravir in pregnancy. Increased hormones such as progesterone and cortisol cause increased levels of UGT1A9 and UGT1A1. These enzymes metabolize cabotegravir, and thus, the drug may be metabolized faster. Many hemodynamic changes that occur in pregnancy may alter the tissue perfusion and plasma volume, changing the drug’s clearance and volume of distribution [43]. One possible way to overcome this issue is through therapeutic drug monitoring in pregnant women [44]. Blood from the pregnant mother can be taken at specific intervals to assess the cabotegravir levels in the systemic circulation. Knowing and understanding this can help determine the proper dosage to maximise clinical effects whilst minimizing adverse effects [45].

Currently, there is not enough good-quality data regarding the drug in human pregnancy to make a conclusive decision about the safety of cabotegravir in pregnancy [46]. However, a few initiatives are underway to obtain possible answers.

In expansion, some of these plans are:

Healthcare workers in the US have been encouraged to register their patients with the Antiretroviral Pregnancy Registry (ARP). This independent board monitors the outcomes of women on cabotegravir who become pregnant [40]. Doctors and patients are encouraged to report any adverse effects experienced, even if unsure if they relate to the study product. Unfortunately, it may take a few years before the adverse effects reported on the APR are in large enough quantities to draw meaningful conclusions [47].

There are currently also several post-approval studies opening up to assess the effects of cabotegravir in pregnancy. The HPTN084 trial has expanded into an open-label extension study whereby women who get pregnant may choose to continue on cabotegravir if they wish [48]. This will hopefully provide beneficial additional data.

The IMPAACT (International Maternal Paediatric Adolescent AIDS Clinical Trials) network has also set up a trial following 45 pregnant women and their infants on cabotegravir in the US and SA. The result’s release date is still to be determined. With more and more trials planned for cabotegravir, ending between 2024 and 2026, evidence of its use in pregnancy will slowly become more available [47].

The results of this study should be considered with caution and expanded on in future studies. The gaps in gathering clinical data for pregnant women are concerning and should be treated as a priority [49]. For now, a cautious approach should be taken to the use of cabotegravir in pregnant women. The potential risks versus potential benefits should be weighed, and the patient’s overall health should be considered [50].

Women seeking PrEP should undergo thorough pre-conception counselling and be informed of all the concerns surrounding cabotegravir. For now, if the long-acting intramuscular drug is the best HIV prevention option, women should be placed on effective contraception. However, if a woman does have fertility desires, the gold standard of oral tenofovir continues to be a safe and effective option in this population until more knowledge about cabotegravir is obtained [22].

Limitations

The main limitation of this study was the small number of women found to have become pregnant on cabotegravir. As this drug was recently licensed, the only source of information currently available is a few trials where women have become pregnant. There are significant ethical issues involving pregnancy in clinical trials; thus, safety data is not widely accessible. A few studies withdrew pregnant women from participating in the respective study and did not document any follow-up. The compassionate use program’s data was collected by each individual’s physician, meaning it was not as standardised as a formal clinical trial and had a high risk of bias. The exclusion of studies in languages other than English meant that some important findings could have been missed. There was a small number of articles that fit the inclusion criteria of this study. Some of the studies included in this review were not blinded, causing an increased risk of bias. There may be selection bias. There are no long-term studies that have followed up on mothers and children. There is no reported data of cabotegravir in the second and third trimester of pregnancy. The lack of sufficient data limited the conclusions that could be drawn.

Conclusion

HIV continues to affect many people in the world today. Scientific advances and modern-day medicine have enabled humans to control the spread of the virus through various pharmacological agents. However, adherence to these drugs remains a barrier. Long-acting intra-muscular cabotegravir can combat this issue as it requires less intentionality from the patient to remember to take a daily pill. However, there is not enough evidence assessing the safety of this drug in pregnancy. Due to the ethics involved in clinical trials in pregnant women, there is currently no conclusive answer to the question. The sample size of women who have become pregnant on cabotegravir is too small to give a definitive answer about its safety. Follow-up, further research, and ongoing surveillance are of vital importance for knowledge of this drug’s safety in pregnancy.
