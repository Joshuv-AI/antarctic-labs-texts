Retrospective analysis of a failed introduction of intrathecal morphine in elective orthopaedic surgery
Authors: Aart Jan W Teunissen, Robert Jan Stolker, Joep Kitzen, Puck Engelhard, Seppe JHSA Koopman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite advancements in perioperative pain treatment for elective orthopaedic surgery, significant postoperative pain remains. Postoperative pain is a factor that influences recovery and hospital stay after prosthetic orthopaedic surgery. Treatment modalities, such as nerve blocks, local infiltration analgesia, and opioids, have been attempted. Each treatment modality has its own distinct advantages and disadvantages. Intrathecal morphine was administered in accordance with PROSPECT recommendations.ObjectivesGiven the discrepancies between our findings and the recommendations, we retrospectively analysed the effectiveness and side effects of intrathecal morphine in patients undergoing hip or knee replacement.DesignA single-centre retrospective analysis.SettingLarge non-university teaching hospital.ParticipantsA total of 854 patients who underwent spinal anaesthesia for knee or hip replacement surgery between March 15, 2022, and August 31, 2023, with or without intrathecal morphine. Data, registered as part of standard clinical practice, were collected from 854 patients, of whom 414 had received intrathecal morphine.Main outcome measuresWe collected pain scores on the day of the operation (day 0) and on the two days thereafter (days 1 and 2). Furthermore, side effects, including urinary retention requiring catheterization, nausea, vomiting, and itching, were studied.ResultsThe pain scores were modestly, but significantly, lower on the day of the operation in patients receiving intrathecal morphine. However, on day two, the pain scores were modestly and significantly higher, in patients receiving intrathecal morphine. Additionally, patients in the spinal morphine group experienced more nausea, vomiting, and itching. Furthermore, they had an increased risk of catheterization in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05). After correction, there was an increased risk of catheterization in males only (odds ratio, 8.75; 95% confidence interval: 3.16-24.20, P<0.001).ConclusionsThe side effects of intrathecal morphine, with only a modest decrease in pain scores on the operation day, prompted us to stop administering intrathecal morphine for elective knee and hip replacement.Clinical trial number and registryThis trial was registered in the Open Science Framework registry (https://osf.io/rt57a).Keywords: Intrathecal morphine, Knee or hip replacement surgery, Postoperative pain, Pruritus, Urinary retention

Introduction

Postoperative pain after knee or hip replacement surgery hampers patient comfort and mobilisation [1]. Despite advancements in perioperative pain treatment, postoperative pain remains a problem [1, 2]. Several treatment modalities have been tried, such as nerve blocks, local infiltration analgesia and opioids. Each treatment modality has its own distinct advantages and disadvantages. Ideally, postoperative pain medication should be highly effective with minimal side effects and no effect on motor function, enabling rapid mobilisation in postoperative patients. Early ambulation and physical therapy improve the success of surgery, reduce hospitalisation duration, and reduce the risk of long-term immobilisation and possible chronic pain [3]. Since nerve blocks may affect motor function, their use is limited in orthopaedic surgery [2, 4–8]. Previous literature and the PROSPECT recommendations reported lower pain scores and opioid consumption after intrathecal morphine [1–3, 9–11]. In our hospital, we have favourable experiences after the introduction of intrathecal morphine in robot-assisted prostate surgery and endoscopic colon surgery [12, 13]. Encouraged by our experiences, available PROSPECT recommendations and literature, we introduced intrathecal morphine as standard care in all patients undergoing elective hip or knee replacement surgery [3, 9, 13].

After the introduction, the nursing staff in the ward reported an increase in nausea, pruritus, and urinary retention despite no significant improvement in pain management. We clinically evaluated the introduction and decided to discontinue the use of intrathecal opioids after thorough consideration. In this study, we retrospectively analysed the effects of introducing intrathecal morphine on urinary retention in patients undergoing elective hip or knee replacement. We compared these patients to a similar, historical cohort of patients who did not receive intrathecal morphine. This primary endpoint was chosen since this was the main adverse effect reported by the nursing staff.

Methods

Study design

This study was a single-centre retrospective analysis of the data, registered as part of standard clinical practice, extracted from electronic patient records. We gathered data retrospectively for these two periods. Firstly, we gathered data from the period during which intrathecal morphine was administered, from March 15, 2022, to December 6, 2022. Secondly, data from the cohort after cessation of spinal morphine administration, specifically from December 7, 2022, to August 31, 2023, were collected to make comparisons possible. This is about the same time period. All consecutive adult patients were included without any inclusion or exclusion criteria. The main reason why we stopped using intrathecal morphine in elective orthopaedic surgery was the higher frequency of postoperative urinary catheterizations. We therefore choose so postoperative urinary retention (POUR) as the primary endpoint. The secondary outcomes were postoperative pain, itching, nausea, and vomiting (PONV).

Standard care

In our hospital, the standard care pathways for knee and hip are highly standardised. This enables us to make changes as part of standard care which are closely adhered to. Medication prior to the operation (premedication) and postoperative medication on the ward are prescribed using standard combination prescriptions including all medication. These combinations are specific for knee and hip replacement surgery. This ensures a (near) total adherence. Any changes are made in close collaboration with the orthopaedic surgeons and the orthopaedic ward further ensuring maximal adherence. As part of standard care, these patients are closely monitored. Results of this monitoring are discussed periodically with a multidisciplinary team consisting of an anaesthesiologist, orthopaedic surgeon, nurses, physiotherapists and physician assistants of the orthopaedic surgery ward. As part of standard care, all patients received paracetamol 1000 mg, metamizole 1000 mg and 2 g tranexamic acid orally before the operation.

Dexamethasone (8 mg) and magnesium sulfate (2000 mg) were administered intravenously as multimodal pain treatments. Patients received 2000 mg cefazoline (body mass index (BMI) ≤ 40) or 3000 mg cefazoline (BMI > 40).

In the intrathecal morphine cohort, attending anaesthetists injected four to five ml bupivacaine 2.5 mg.ml-1 with morphine (25 mcg.ml-1. Before the spinal injection, 4 mg of ondansetron was injected intravenously to prevent itching and PONV [14, 15]. In the second cohort (no spinal morphine), the anaesthetists injected 10–12.5 mg hyperbaric bupivacaine 5 mg.ml-1 0.5% intrathecally.

Patients who underwent total or uni-knee replacement received local infiltration analgesia (LIA) with ropivacaine 2 mg.ml-1. Patients weighing < 80 kg received 100 (ml), and those weighing ≥ 80 kg received 150 ml. The injections were performed systematically into all traumatised tissues by injecting into the posterior capsule and collateral ligaments after the bone cuts had been made and before insertion of the prosthesis. After the prosthesis was inserted, ropivacaine was injected into the capsule incision, quadriceps tendon, infrapatellar ligament, posterior cruciate ligament, and subcutaneously [16].

Postoperatively, patients received paracetamol 1000 mg and metamizole 1000 mg four times daily, magnesium gluconate 1000 mg twice daily, ondansetron 4 mg intravenously up to three times daily if needed, and droperidol 0.0625 mg intravenously up to four times daily if needed. The intrathecal morphine group received oxycodone 5 mg immediate-release six times daily, if needed. The other group received slow-release oxycodone 10 mg twice daily and immediate-release oxycodone 10 mg six times daily, if needed, conforming to the pain protocol we used before the introduction of intrathecal morphine. When no spinal morphine was administered, the patient received extended-release oral oxycodone 10 mg before leaving the recovery room.

All patients were postoperatively stimulated to start ambulation as quickly as possible to accelerate recovery under the supervision of physiotherapists in a joint care program. Nurses performed mobilisation stimulation every 2 h, scheduled postoperatively during waking hours.

Data collection

We collected data from our data-management system that were registered as part of standard clinical care. The outcome measurements had been added to the patient data management system by the orthopaedic surgeons or the attending nurses unaware that we would conduct a study. The data were extracted by one of the authors (PE). We collected the following data: age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologist (ASA) score [17], perioperative opioid use during the first three days (days 0–2), verbal rating pain scores (measured thrice daily by the attending nurse) [18], nausea and vomiting, use of anti-emetics dichotomously per day, and urinary retention resulting in frequency of catheterization. Patients were catheterized if there was no urination within six hours postoperatively, or if the bladder contained more than 500 ml of urine measured with an ultrasound scan of the bladder. Indwelling catheters were only used after consultation with orthopaedic surgeons.

Statistical analysis

Since this was a retrospective study, no sample size was calculated. Data are presented as means and standard deviations, or as medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the normal distribution. Data were compared using the Mann Whitney U test and multivariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to control for potential confounders. These confounders were selected based on those reported in the literature, those with biological plausibility, or those identified by statistical analysis. The primary endpoint was the need for ≥ 2 catheterizations. One-time catheterization could also be caused by bupivacaine in both groups and not solely by morphine. Unfortunately, we did not have any information regarding the timing of catheterization. Multivariate regression analysis was performed separately for males and females. Furthermore, pain and opioid usage were studied separately in males and females. A sensitivity analysis was performed, excluding patients (n = 9) who received unilateral knee replacement, as these patients might differ in terms of postoperative pain and recovery from those who received total knee replacement. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Data were analysed using SPSS (IBM, version 29.020, 2023).

Results

Baseline characteristics

From March 15, 2022, to August 31, 2023, data were collected from 854 patients, of whom 414 received spinal morphine. Baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, and comorbidities, did not differ between patients receiving spinal morphine and those who did not (Table 1). Patients in the intrathecal morphine group received less bupivacaine (10 versus 12.5 mg, P < 0.001). Fewer patients in the intrathecal morphine group underwent half-knee replacement, reflecting a change in preference among our orthopaedic surgeons (one versus nine patients, P < 0.011; Table 1).

Primary endpoint

More patients in the intrathecal morphine group had postoperative urinary retention requiring more than two intermittent catheterizations (10.2% in the intrathecal group vs. 3.7%, P = 0.005, Table 2). There were no differences in the use of indwelling catheters between groups. When analysing the need for catheterization between sexes, only males were more prone to repeat catheterization after the use of intrathecal morphine (OR, 8.75) 95% CI: 3.16-24.20, P<0.001; Table 3). The sensitivity analysis, excluding unilateral knee replacement, did not alter the results.

Secondary endpoints

Pain scores were lower on the day of the operation (day 0) in the intrathecal morphine group (P < 0.001) but higher in the intrathecal group on day 2 (P < 0.001) (Table 2). Although the difference in pain scores was statistically significant, it was only ≤ 1 on days 0 and 2 (Table 2). When analysing the pain scores between sexes, women tended to have more pain and used more rescue medication in the groups with and without spinal morphine (Supplemental Table 1).

Patients in the intrathecal morphine group experienced more itching than those in the control group (10.1% vs. 1.1%, respectively; P < 0.001). Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was more prevalent in the intrathecal morphine group (37% vs. 13.2%, P < 0.001; Table 2).

Oxycodone use differed according to the design of postoperative pain treatment; therefore, it is difficult to point to relevant differences.

Discussion

Introduction of intrathecal morphine for elective knee or hip replacement surgery did result in lower pain scores postoperative the first 24 h. Although statistically significant, the clinical relevance of the difference in pain scores is questionable.

Pain scores were lower in the intrathecal morphine group on the day of the operation (day 0), but higher on day 2. Patients receiving intrathecal morphine experienced more itching and nausea. Males were at an increased risk of multiple catheterizations when receiving intrathecal morphine.

A recent review also demonstrated that intrathecal morphine was effective in reducing postoperative pain and opioid use on the day of the surgery. However, when patients were treated with local infiltration and multimodal pain management, this difference was often not clinically relevant [2]. Another explanation could be that intrathecal morphine is probably more effective in operations with visceral than somatic pain [19]. Oral intake of oxycodone was lower when intrathecal morphine was used because of the different standards of care protocols between the groups. When we compared our findings to studies that measured pain on postoperative day 2 and included local infiltration analgesia (LIA) and multimodal pain management, we found also similar results [20–22].

Our research revealed that women tended to experience higher levels of pain and required more rescue medication in knee and hip replacement surgery than their male counterparts did. This observation aligns well with existing literature [23, 24].This insight emphasises the need for tailored pain management approaches that consider the unique experiences of different patient populations.

Patients receiving intrathecal morphine experienced more itching and nausea. Side effects such as nausea, vomiting, pruritus, and urinary retention can be a burden for patients and are unacceptable if intrathecal morphine does not substantially reduce the pain scores [25].

Males were at an increased risk of multiple catheterizations when receiving intrathecal morphine. Comparing studies on postoperative urinary retention is difficult because of the differences in the definitions. 26, 27 We used the definition of urinary retention as the need for repeat single catheterizations to prevent bladder overdistention. Some catheterizations are to be expected because of age, procedure, and the use of intrathecal anaesthesia with bupivacaine. The higher than usual frequency of catheterization in the orthopaedic ward without a clinical benefit on comfort was the reason for stopping the use of intrathecal morphine for knee and hip replacement surgery. Our finding that the increased risk of urinary catheterization is sex-dependent is in line with the reviews by Bracey et al. and Karani et al., who investigated urinary retention during orthopaedic surgery [26, 27]. Bracey et al. found an incidence of postoperative urinary retention ranging from 5.5 to 46,3%. Risk factors included increasing age, male sex, benign prostatic hyperplasia, use of bupivacaine and intrathecal morphine during spinal anaesthesia, glycopyrrolate use, and volume of intraoperative intravenous fluid administered [26]. Karani et al. found that the frequency of postoperative urinary retention ranged from 8% to 55%, with the risk of retention increasing by 1.4 times per decade. Male sex, joint replacement surgery, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were identified as the risk factors [27].

Patients receiving intrathecal anaesthesia are already at risk for a single catheterization because of the long duration of bladder function inhibition [26, 28]. The suppression of detrusor contractility and decreased sensation of urge is even longer if intrathecal morphine is used [26, 29]. Postoperative urinary retention can, if not recognised, result in bladder overdistention, The treatment of urinary retention can result in urinary tract infections and catheter-related complications [30]. Some patients already have medical reasons for indwelling catheters, and a substantial number of patients have trouble urinating after surgery due to pain or prone positioning [26]. To prevent urinary retention, it is possible to use alpha-1 adrenergic blocking drugs [30, 31]. This could lead to more orthostatic hypotension, which is undesirable during postoperative mobilisation of patients [32].

Limitations

This study was a retrospective single-centre study, which may have introduced bias. To minimise this, we corrected for known confounders and those identified in the literature during the analysis. No randomisation or blinding was possible, which could have led to residual confounding factors. Since spinal morphine was clinically introduced, all consecutive patients were administered spinal morphine for their knee of hip replacement surgery. After cessation, no spinal morphine was administered for these operations. The risk of selection bias is therefore not present. Furthermore, no relevant differences in baseline characteristics were observed. The nurses and orthopaedic surgeons reporting outcomes could not know that we would conduct this retrospective analysis and were in this way blinded. One of the limitations in our study is the difference in long acting opioids prescribed in both cohorts. Long acting opioids were given in patients with only intrathecal bupivacaine to provide adequate pain treatment if the spinal anesthesia wears off. Given the long duration of action of spinal morphine, we choose not to give long acting opioids to these patients to prevent opioid intoxications. Other than this change, which was part of the introduction of spinal morphine, no other changes were made during this study period in the standard treatment of patients undergoing knee or hip replacement surgery.

The procedures performed at our hospital may differ from those at other hospitals, potentially influencing the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the timing of catheterization in the ward was documented only in nurse shift reports; therefore, the exact timing remained unknown. Knowing the catheterization time would have been helped assess the risks of catheterization over time.

Nurses in the ward were used to routine postoperative complaints after major orthopaedic surgery and had to adjust to the new postoperative effects of intrathecal morphine. The heightened frequency and thus awareness of catheterization may have coloured their view, making it less favourable. We cannot rule out the possibility of significant observer bias. We attempted to minimise this by having a research assistant, who was not involved in the analysis of this project, extract all relevant data. Despite these limitations, our results were valid.

Conclusion

Intrathecal morphine administration reduced pain and opioid intake on the operation day but had too many side effects, making it a less valuable contribution to hip and knee replacement surgery when multimodal pain treatment and local infiltration anaesthesia are already given. This is especially true taking in mind the wish to perform this surgery in daycare.
