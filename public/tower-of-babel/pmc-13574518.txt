Dacron vascular grafts: a long-term solution for short renal vein challenges in living donor kidney transplantation in a retrospective case series
Authors: Ara Cho, Sungsin Cho, Ahram Han, Sangil Min, Jongwon Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeShort renal veins in living donor kidney transplantation present technical challenges during graft placement and vascular anastomosis. Artificial graft use raises concerns about infection and thrombosis. We share our experiences using Dacron vascular grafts (Hemagard, Maquet) for renal vein extension with long-term safety and efficacy.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 11 cases at Seoul National University Hospital (2016–2024) where Dacron vascular grafts were used for renal vein extension. Patient demographics, comorbidities, graft size, and postoperative antiplatelet therapy were analyzed. Imaging follow-up was performed with sonography before discharge.ResultsPatients had a median age of 56 years and a median body mass index of 25.0 kg/m2. Eight grafts involved the right kidney and three involved the left kidney. The median graft diameter was 12 mm. At discharge, median serum creatinine was 1.2 mg/dL and median estimated glomerular filtration rate was 63.7 mL/min/1.73 m2. Median follow-up was 46 months. One patient required reoperation due to bleeding unrelated to the graft. No thrombosis or stenosis was detected. Vascular patency was excellent in all cases during follow-up, aside from 1 death (pneumonia) and 1 graft failure from immunoglobulin A nephropathy recurrence.ConclusionDacron vascular grafts for renal vein extension in living donor kidney transplantation are a reliable and effective surgical option, demonstrating favorable long-term safety and patency without increased thrombotic or infectious complications under antiplatelet management.

INTRODUCTION

Living donor kidney transplantation (LDKT) is the ultimate treatment for patients with end-stage renal disease. While left kidneys are usually preferred in LDKT due to longer renal veins facilitating easier anastomosis, there are cases where the right kidney must be used. The right kidney may be necessary for donor safety or anatomical considerations. Additionally, even left kidneys can have short renal veins depending on the recipient’s anatomical condition (for example, when performing anastomosis to the left common iliac vein). The right renal veins are typically shorter, making venous anastomosis technically challenging and increasing the risk of vascular complications.

To address the short renal vein issue, several methods have been proposed. One is using autologous veins such as the gonadal vein, femoral vein, or internal jugular vein [12]. However, in many kidney transplant recipients, the use of a saphenous vein graft is not favored because it requires a separate incision, and its size may not be suitable. Therefore, a more time-consuming procedure is needed to modify the vein graft for size matching. Another option is using cryopreserved vascular allografts, such as iliac vein, renal vein, or inferior vena cava from deceased donors [3]. However, many centers in Korea do not have immediate access to cryopreserved vascular tissues, and these tissues are expensive. Other technical approaches include mobilizing the external iliac vein to make it flexible for anastomosis or performing an end-to-end anastomosis to the internal iliac vein. Finally, artificial grafts made from materials like polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or polyethylene terephthalate (Dacron) are also considered for extending short or damaged renal veins [45]. Although artificial grafts present concerns such as infection, immunological issues, bleeding from systemic heparin use, and increased thrombosis risk, they offer distinct advantages like standardized size, immediate availability, and resistance to shrinkage or aneurysmal dilatation.

In this study, we present our experience using a Dacron vascular graft (Hemagard, Maquet) for renal vein extension in LDKT. We aim to evaluate the long-term safety, efficacy, and vascular patency of this technique. By providing detailed surgical outcomes and follow-up data, this study contributes to the ongoing discussion regarding the feasibility of artificial grafts in kidney transplantation. While synthetic grafts have been used in vascular reconstruction, data on their long-term application in LDKT remain limited. This study offers insight into their clinical utility and durability in this context.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study has been approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Seoul National University Hospital (No. 2505-071-1639) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective study, informed consent from the patients was waived.

Patient selection

This retrospective study included LDKT patients who underwent renal vein extension with Dacron vascular grafts at Seoul National University Hospital from 2016 to 2024. Patient demographics, perioperative characteristics, and long-term outcomes were reviewed.

Donor kidney laterality criteria

All donors undergo preoperative CT angiography for vascular/parenchymal assessment and 99mTc-DTPA scintigraphy for differential function. Donor safety guides kidney selection, with the left kidney preferred for its longer renal vein, which facilitates easier anastomosis. The right kidney is selected if the left has multiple renal arteries complicating reconstruction, shows inferior function on split scintigraphy, or has favorable vascular/collecting system anatomy.

Surgical techniques for donor renal vein resection

Donor nephrectomy is performed using a hand-assisted laparoscopic approach or a pure laparoscopic approach in our center. The renal vein is meticulously dissected to maximize its length, and vascular control and resection are achieved using a hem-o-lock clip or laparoscopic vascular stapler. The renal vein is divided as close as possible to the inferior vena cava.

Surgical techniques for renal vein extension

The Dacron graft length was individualized according to the anatomical relationship between the renal vein stump and the recipient’s external iliac vein, ensuring tension-free anastomosis while minimizing excess length. The Dacron graft diameter was matched to the renal vein diameter. An end-to-end anastomosis was performed between the renal vein and a 10–14 mm Dacron vascular graft using 6-0 Prolene sutures (Ethicon Inc.) with a 13 mm needle (Fig. 1). The graft was then anastomosed end-to-end to the recipient’s external iliac vein (Fig. 2). The lumen of the vascular graft was flushed with heparin-mixed saline (10,000 International Units of heparin in 1 L of normal saline) without systemic heparinization.

Postoperative management

Prophylactic antibiotics were used preoperatively and for 3 days postoperatively. Patients were instructed to take single antiplatelet therapy (sarpogrelate, clopidogrel, or aspirin) starting on postoperative day 1, and the specific agent was selected according to each patient’s preoperative medication history and underlying comorbidities. In patients who were already receiving anticoagulation therapy, the anticoagulation medication was maintained, and an additional antiplatelet agent was not prescribed. These agents were administered lifelong, beginning immediately after surgery. All patients underwent kidney Doppler ultrasonography within 7 days after surgery to evaluate the patency of the vascular grafts and intrarenal perfusion. Thereafter, if kidney function remained stable, routine Doppler surveillance was performed annually.

RESULTS

A total of 11 patients underwent renal vein extension using Dacron vascular grafts during LDKT at Seoul National University Hospital from 2016 to 2024. The cohort included 6 male and 5 female patients, with a median age of 56 years (interquartile range [IQR], 43–63) and a median body mass index of 25.0 kg/m2 (IQR, 23.4–26.6) (Table 1). Among the cases, 8 involved the donor’s right kidney, while 3 involved the left kidney. The renal vein was extended using Dacron grafts from 10 to 14 mm, selected based on anatomical requirements. The median cold ischemic time (CIT) was 71 minutes (IQR, 55–74), and the warm ischemic time remained less than 5 minutes in all cases (Table 2). During surgery, an end-to-end anastomosis was performed between the Dacron graft and the donor renal vein, followed by anastomosis to the recipient’s external iliac vein. All patients received perioperative prophylactic antibiotics, and none required systemic heparinization. At the time of discharge, the median serum creatinine level was 1.2 mg/dL (IQR, 1.0–1.3), and the median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration [CKD-EPI] equation) was 63.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR, 58.1–69.4). The median follow-up duration was 46.0 months (IQR, 19.5–56.0). The detailed prescriptions of medication are described for each case (Table 3).

Postoperative Doppler ultrasonography was conducted for all patients within the first postoperative week. The Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated a venous flow pattern of the renal graft, with color Doppler imaging showing patency of the intrarenal vessels. Spectral Doppler pattern of the renal vein showed continuous, low-velocity monophasic flow with a peak velocity of 40 cm/sec without evidence of turbulence or flow reversal, consistent with the absence of renal vein thrombosis or stenosis (Fig. 3). Vascular patency remained excellent throughout the follow-up period in all patients except for 1 case of graft failure due to immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) recurrence at 46 months and 1 patient’s death due to pneumonia at 18 months. Only 1 patient required reoperation on postoperative day 2 due to bleeding. Reoperation was performed due to bleeding from retroperitoneal fat, and the bleeding was unrelated to the graft itself.

The long-term patency of Dacron vascular grafts in LDKT was outstanding. The patency probability was 100% with no additional loss of vascular integrity up to 98 months of follow-up, excluding events unrelated to vascular infection, such as patient mortality due to pneumonia and IgAN recurrence. Throughout the entire follow-up period, no cases of thrombosis, graft occlusion, or infection were observed, supporting the hypothesis that Dacron vascular grafts provide durable and reliable venous reconstruction in LDKT.

DISCUSSION

The results of this study indicate that Dacron vascular grafts offer durable and reliable outcomes for renal vein extension in LDKT. Despite long-standing concerns associated with synthetic grafts, such as thrombosis, infection, and graft occlusion, our experience demonstrated excellent long-term patency without any graft-related complications during the follow-up period.

Autologous vein, such as the saphenous or gonadal vein, has traditionally been the preferred choice for renal vein extension due to its natural endothelial lining and reduced thrombogenic potential. However, their availability can be limited, and their use often requires additional surgical dissection, leading to prolonged CIT and potential donor site complications. Some studies reported the use of PTFE grafts in transplantation. Delpín [4] described 2 cases in which PTFE grafts were successfully used to lengthen short donor renal veins in 2 deceased-donor kidney grafts. Also, Kamel et al. [5] described donor renal artery reconstruction in 2 deceased-donor kidney grafts and 1 donor renal vein reconstruction in a renal autotransplant using a PTFE graft. In both reports, the PTFE grafts for vein or arterial lengthening remained patent for 1 and 2 years of follow-up [5]. However, in our report, a Dacron graft was used because only an 8 mm-sized PTFE graft was the largest available size at our center. Several reports have compared the effectiveness of the Dacron and PTFE grafts in venous reconstruction. These reports covered the patency in the portal vein, superior mesenteric vein, and peripheral vein, but not the renal vein, which yielded no significant differences in primary patency rates [67]. Our study demonstrates that Dacron grafts maintain high patency rates with no thrombosis in long-term follow-up.

In countries such as Korea, where deceased organ donation remains infrequent and access to cryopreserved vascular allografts is limited, synthetic grafts serve as a practical and efficient alternative. Dacron grafts offer consistent sizing, immediate availability, and eliminate the need for additional tissue harvesting, ultimately reducing CIT and simplifying the surgical workflow. These advantages are particularly meaningful in resource-constrained environments, where options for vascular reconstruction may be limited.

To achieve tension-free mobility of the external iliac vein for anastomosis with a short renal vein, the vein is thoroughly dissected and mobilized laterally. This maneuver effectively extends the reachable venous segment, thereby enabling tension-free anastomosis and minimizing the risk of anastomotic stretching or rupture. When necessary, the internal iliac vein is ligated and divided to further enhance the external iliac vein mobility, facilitating anterior retraction and optimal repositioning. However, this approach may not be possible in all cases and would impair the venous outflow when the renal artery compresses the renal vein. Interposition grafting with Dacron facilitates tension-free alignment, mitigates the risk of venous compression, and supports optimal hemodynamics.

In our center, patients were instructed to take single antiplatelet therapy from postoperative day 1, considering the comorbidities of each patient. Other case reports involving artificial grafts for renal vein extension did not mention the use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, indicating the need for further consensus on this matter [45]. Similar to portal vein reconstruction in hepatobiliary-pancreatic cancers, synthetic grafts for renal vein elongation may require long-term anticoagulation [8]. However, a systematic review of portal vein reconstruction found no standardized approach to anticoagulation therapy [9]. In our study, we did not observe any thrombotic events with single antiplatelet therapy, but further studies are necessary to establish a consensus on this issue. The results in our cases showed good long-term results of the Dacron graft under lifelong antiplatelet therapy. However, long-term results and randomization or systematic review are needed to obtain more conclusive findings.

The median CIT in our cohort was 71 minutes, which is comparable to other studies [5]. According to a study investigating the association between CIT and delayed graft function (DGF) in LDKT recipients, a CIT of less than 4 hours was significantly associated with a lower risk of DGF [10]. Based on these findings, the CIT observed in our study appears to be within an acceptable range. Moreover, the use of Dacron grafts is expected to reduce operative time compared to harvesting and utilizing other autologous or homologous vascular grafts, which may contribute to improved graft outcomes.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of direct comparison with cryopreserved vascular allografts due to the specific situation in Korea. Despite these constraints, Dacron grafts demonstrated no graft-related complications, favorable long-term patency, and safety as a practical alternative in settings where cryopreserved vascular allografts are unavailable. Further prospective, multicenter trials are warranted to validate these findings and define the role of synthetic conduits in complex kidney transplantation cases.

Dacron vascular grafts offer a reliable and effective solution for renal vein extension in LDKT, demonstrating excellent long-term patency with no thrombotic complications. As artificial grafts continue to evolve, their role in transplantation surgery should be further explored to optimize outcomes and expand surgical options for complex cases.
