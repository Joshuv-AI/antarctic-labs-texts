Clinician Led Point of Care Ultrasound Workshops for Anatomy Instruction: A Pilot Study
Authors: Dylan Ledbetter, John T Mumber, Luke B Strong, Daniel Cawley, Jordan Nickols, Joseph Edison, Nathan T Douthit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPoint-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) has an expanding role in clinical practice and medical education. However, evidence for the impact of clinician-led POCUS training on anatomy comprehension and student comfort with POCUS remains limited.AimWe aimed to determine whether clinician-led POCUS training in pre-clinical medical school curricula improved students’ performance on anatomy assessments and confidence using ultrasound.MethodsThis staggered cohort study was granted exempt status by the VCOM IRB (# 2023–052). Pre-clinical students were randomized into 2 groups. Both groups underwent a baseline test and an “Introduction to POCUS” session, followed by staggered sessions on abdominal, shoulder, and cardiac POCUS including didactic and hands-on components. After each of these staggered sessions, quizzes compared the knowledge of students who received POCUS against those who had not. Finally, the students repeated the baseline test and subjective evaluations of the use of POCUS. Multiple “free scan” workshops were available during the study. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare the knowledge assessments, and Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test was used to compare ordinal scores.ResultsBetween-group differences in percent correct were statistically significant in 2 questions (curved probe indication and probe marker orientation). The Total Score results achieved statistical significance (Pre: 4.41 ± 1.66, post: 6.41 ± 1.97, p = 0.0005). Q10a, an ordinal question that gauged participants’ comfort in utilizing ultrasounds in clinical practice, also achieved statistical significance (Pre: 2.31 ± 1.20, post: 3.69 ± 0.87, p = 0.0018).ConclusionIntegrating clinician-led POCUS workshops into pre-clinical curricula was associated with greater comfort using ultrasound in clinical settings and a trend toward improved anatomy knowledge even though gains were inconsistent. These findings support earlier POCUS exposure in pre-clinical education; longer studies and larger cohorts are needed to assess the durability of learning and transfer of skills to clinical settings.

Introduction

Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) has become a mainstay in clinical practice, especially amongst doctors in internal medicine, emergency medicine, and family medicine, due to its portability, ease of use, and ability to provide rapid diagnostic information.1 POCUS enables visualization of anatomic pathology at the bedside, facilitating timely diagnoses at a lower cost than other imaging modalities.2 Conditions such as pneumothorax, pleural effusion, joint pathology, cardiopulmonary abnormalities, and hydronephrosis can be identified using POCUS with less formal training, demonstrating only a subset of its extensive clinical applications.1,3

The utilization of POCUS is steadily increasing, driven by well-substantiated clinical and educational factors.4 POCUS has been especially beneficial in rural medicine, providing a convenient and cost-effective diagnostic option that enables results to be obtained and communicated in real time.5 This capability reduces the financial burden of extensive diagnostic tests and ameliorates the risk of patient loss to follow-up when further appointments for diagnostic testing and debriefing would otherwise be necessary.5 In urgent clinical situations, POCUS delivers rapid diagnostic information that facilitates timely decision-making in critically ill patients.6,7 In fields such as hospital medicine and emergency medicine, where the difference between a patient living and dying could be a matter of minutes, the immediate diagnostic clarity offered by POCUS represents a significant advancement in patient care.7 In pediatrics or outpatient medicine, POCUS is especially beneficial for patients unable to tolerate long imaging studies or who encounter obstacles to returning for additional diagnostic studies, as imaging could be completed within minutes.8

With the expanding clinical role of POCUS, early exposure during medical training is increasingly recognized as essential for preparing students to provide high-quality care across clinical settings. By 2021, ultrasound curricula had been adopted by more than 70% of medical schools, and many programs had fully integrated ultrasound instruction into basic science coursework.9,10 There is growing expectation that medical students will graduate with not only familiarity in ultrasound use but also with foundational competency, as bedside ultrasound is becoming a standard component of clinical practice.11 Despite increasing adoption, variability persists in depth, structure, and clinical integration across institutions.10

Multiple educational approaches have been developed to introduce POCUS during pre-clinical training. Prior studies have demonstrated the incorporation of ultrasound into physical examination, clinical skills, and anatomy courses.12,13 Rather than replacing traditional gross dissection, ultrasound has typically been incorporated alongside anatomy lab to complement gross dissections.13,14 This integration provides students with an additional method of visualizing anatomical structures and spatial relationships that may not be fully appreciated through dissection alone.12,13 Studies evaluating longitudinal integration of POCUS within anatomy courses have shown improved performance on their written exams.3,15 Additionally, ultrasound allows students to visualize both normal and abnormal findings, further reinforcing clinical relevance and supporting early scholarly activities.16

Despite the increasing adoption of ultrasound curricula, significant gaps remain in how POCUS is delivered during pre-clinical education.17 While many medical schools report offering ultrasound instruction, clinically focused, hands-on exposure during the pre-clinical years remains limited at some institutions.9 Consequently, students may begin clinical training with insufficient familiarity and confidence in the use of ultrasound clinical settings.18 These challenges highlight the need for educational strategies that offer early, clinically focused POCUS learning opportunities.

To address these limitations, a clinician-led series of structured POCUS workshops integrating ultrasound and anatomy instruction was offered as stand-alone sessions. These workshops combined didactic instruction with supervised hands-on scanning sessions focused on shoulder, cardiac, and abdominal anatomy, aiming to provide early exposure to ultrasound technology, reinforce anatomical knowledge, and enhance familiarity with clinical applications.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate whether integrating clinician-led POCUS workshops improves students’ understanding of shoulder, cardiac, and abdominal anatomy. Secondary objectives included assessing whether participation in these workshops improves students’ knowledge of ultrasound technology and its clinical applications, as well as increases students’ confidence and perceived educational value of ultrasound training. We hypothesized that students participating in clinician-led POCUS workshops would demonstrate improved understanding of anatomy, ultrasound technology, and clinical applications, while developing increased appreciation and confidence regarding ultrasound use.

Methods

This research involved a quasi-experimental staggered cohort study with pre/post assessment. Inclusion criteria for this study were enrolled OMS I or OMS2 students at VCOM-Auburn Campus in good academic standing (GPA > 3.2 and no active academic remediation) who were voluntarily interested in participation for the purposes of POCUS education. No cap was placed on recruitment; all interested and qualifying students were able to participate. There were 35 students from the OMS-I (class of 2028) and 34 students from the OMS-II (class of 2027). Students were randomly allocated to one of two cohorts that received the three POCUS modules in different sequences, so that at each interim assessment one had completed a given module while the other had not yet. In the end, both cohorts received identical content. This project was reviewed by VCOM’s IRB and declared exempt in February of 2025 (#2023-052). Informed consent was obtained through an individual form and was signed by each student on the first day of the project lectures before starting the material.

All participants first underwent a 12-item pre-test to assess their baseline hands-on skills using gel models (Figures 1 and 2) with hidden objects inside (Figures 3–6) as well as their pre-existing knowledge of POCUS (Figure 7). Each item was scored as correct or incorrect with a maximum possible score of 12 points. The pre-test also included Likert-style ordinal questions rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) to assess participant’s confidence, perceptions, and attitudes toward ultrasound use. They then all underwent an initial “Introduction to POCUS” session that involved didactics as well as hands-on experience. They were then split randomly into two staggered groups. One group underwent a session (including didactic and hands-on components) on “Cardiac POCUS” and a pre-recorded lecture on relevant abdominal anatomy. The other group received a session on “Abdominal POCUS” and standardized educational materials on cardiac anatomy. Each group also received quizzes on both organ systems’ anatomy prior to the following session (Appendix 1). These further sessions completed the missing POCUS sessions for each group and introduced “Shoulder POCUS” in a similar staggered fashion (Figure 8). Sessions were completed every 2–4 weeks, alternating between free scans and specific didactic modules. The groups went through these didactic modules in staggered fashion in order that each group could serve as a control group for the other. After completing all POCUS sessions, both groups then completed a “post-test” consisting of the same questions as the initial pre-test, with an additional subjective component regarding their overall evaluation of the utility of POCUS in pre-clinical medical education across each of the tested organ systems.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined based on an anticipated analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) framework, in which post-intervention outcomes would be compared between groups after adjustment for baseline values. Under that framework, a sample of 40 subjects, divided randomly into 2 groups, achieved 80% power to detect a Cohen’s f of 0.46 (a medium effect), assuming baseline to post-intervention correlation of 0.5 and a type I error rate of 0.05. Under the same assumptions, 69 subjects would provide 80% power to detect group differences, explaining 13% of the variability in post-intervention outcomes. Because the observed outcomes were dichotomous (correct/incorrect answers) or ordinal (Likert-style comfort ratings), rather than continuous, nonparametric tests were substituted for ANCOVA in the analysis, but the a priori power calculation was retained as the basis for the sample size target.

Prior to conducting the study, the biostatistician provided a permuted variable block size randomization scheme, with varying block sizes of 2, 4, and 6 subjects. Students were allocated to the two cohorts based on this scheme.

Collected demographics of the groups included OMS year and number of POCUS sessions attended. Analyses began by summarizing demographics and outcomes using proportions for categorical variables (OMS year and number of POCUS sessions attended) and sample means and standard deviations for continuous outcomes. The distribution of continuous outcomes was examined using histograms and boxplots. Following this, three distinct analyses were performed.

Analysis 1: (Baseline Comparison Between Classes)

To evaluate whether classes differed in baseline POCUS knowledge prior to the modules, pre-test results were compared between classes (regardless of group assignment). Pearson chi-square tests were used for dichotomous items, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for ordinal items and total scores.

Analysis 2: (Between-Cohort Comparisons at Staggered Time Points)

Because each cohort received the organ specific modules at different times, each interim assessment compared one cohort that had completed a specific module and one that had not. These comparisons served as the primary test of module-specific effects, as they minimize the influence of concurrent anatomy coursework that was assumed to affect both cohorts equally. Pearson chi-square tests were used for dichotomous items, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for ordinal items and total scores.

Analysis 3 (Within Subject pre-Post Change)

To assess change in knowledge and comfort in individuals from the start to the end of the intervention, each participant who completed both the pre-test and the post-test was analyzed for comparison between the two. McNemar’s test of paired proportions was used for dichotomous items, and the sign test was used for ordinal items and total scores. These within-subject comparisons were considered secondary given the potential for confounding by concurrent anatomy coursework.

Not all subjects attended every session and completed every assessment. Sample sizes reported in each analysis reflect the number of participants with complete data for that specific analysis. No imputation was performed for missing data. Because both cohorts received the full intervention, the between-cohort comparisons at staggered time points served as the primary test of module-specific effects on anatomical education. Pre-post change served as a marker of POCUS knowledge and comfort as well as a secondary analysis of subjective anatomy education benefit. All tests used a Type I error rate of 0.05 and analyses and graphs were conducted using SAS 9.4 or R 4.3.0.

Discussion

The integration of POCUS into undergraduate medical education has expanded considerably in recent years; however, variability persists in the structure, implementation, and evaluation of ultrasound curricula across institutions.9,17,19,20 While ultrasound training has traditionally been introduced during the clinical years, the growing emphasis on early clinical skill development has created opportunities to incorporate ultrasound into the pre-clinical phase. Previous studies have demonstrated that ultrasound enhances visualization of spatial relationships, strengthens connections between anatomical knowledge and clinical applications, and supports ultrasound and physical exam skill development.12,15,21,22 In response to the need for structured early exposure, this study implemented clinician-led, stand-alone POCUS workshops to evaluate their impact on anatomical and ultrasound knowledge as well as clinical confidence.

In this study, participation in clinician-led POCUS sessions was associated with significant improvements in foundational ultrasound knowledge and learner confidence. Students exhibited greater accuracy in selecting the appropriate probe for liver evaluation, correctly orienting the probe marker for the parasternal long-axis view, and effectively using ultrasound to identify hidden objects within premade phantom models. These skills contributed to higher total knowledge scores. Additionally, participants reported a significant increase in comfort using ultrasound in clinical practice. The observed improvement in ultrasound knowledge likely reflects the combined effects of hands-on learning, immediate visual feedback, and clinician-guided reinforcement, all of which are established drivers of skill acquisition in procedural education. Perceptions of ultrasound as a valuable clinical tool and interest in further ultrasound training were high at baseline and remained unchanged, suggesting pre-existing positive attitudes toward ultrasound education. Overall, these findings suggest that clinician-led POCUS instruction is effective at improving ultrasound knowledge, technical skills, and confidence in ultrasound application.

Baseline POCUS knowledge and comfort with ultrasound use were significantly higher among OMS-I students compared to OMS-II students. These differences likely reflect a recent curricular change that integrated ultrasound into the OMS-I anatomy curriculum, which provided earlier, structured exposure to POCUS concepts. In contrast, OMS-II students did not receive comparable early training. This suggests that early integration of ultrasound into pre-clinical education may improve baseline knowledge.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating the value of early ultrasound exposure in improving ultrasound knowledge and confidence in its use in clinical settings. Khoury et al reported that preclerkship medical students demonstrated significantly increased confidence and improvement in ultrasound knowledge after undergoing a structured 3-hour cardiac ultrasound training session23 Zeitouni et al found that following three 30-minute hands-on ultrasound sessions, student familiarity with ultrasound techniques increased from 38% to 99%, confidence in acquiring ultrasound images improved from 26% to 94% and comfort with 3D anatomical visualization rose from 65% to 86%.12 Peticca et al reported that attendance at a single-day point-of-care ultrasound symposium led to improvements in students’ ultrasound knowledge and confidence in performing the FAST exam, increasing from 3.3 to 6.7 on a 10-point scale.24 These findings parallel our results, in which students demonstrated improved ultrasound knowledge and reported greater confidence in clinical ultrasound use. Together, these findings contribute to growing evidence that structured ultrasound exposure, even of short duration, enhances knowledge acquisition and clinical confidence and may improve learner preparedness for clinical application.

In our study, participants who completed POCUS modules did not show significant differences in shoulder, cardiac, or abdominal anatomy quiz performance compared to those who did not complete the modules. Prior studies have reported mixed effects of ultrasound instruction on anatomy knowledge, often varying by organ system. Van Iderstine et al conducted a randomized controlled study in which pre-clinical medical students attended a single 2-hour session on neck, upper limb, and abdominal anatomy using either hands-on ultrasound or cadaver-based instruction.21 Both groups demonstrated immediate improvement in anatomy knowledge after the session but declined at 1-week and 1-month, with no significant differences between groups, indicating similar anatomy retention. Students in the ultrasound group retained ultrasound-specific knowledge at 1 month and demonstrated improvement on cadaveric questions in addition to ultrasound-based questions. Similarly, Kondrashov et al reported significant improvements in anatomical knowledge among pre-clinical osteopathic medical students after participation in a clinical ultrasound elective, particularly in heart, abdominal, and extremity anatomy; however, there was little improvement in neck and eye anatomy, which the authors attributed to less instructional focus and ultrasound integration.25 Haidar et al reported that participation in a voluntary longitudinal pre-clinical ultrasound curriculum resulted in a small but statistically significant improvement in cardiovascular anatomy scores, whereas no significant improvement was observed in gastrointestinal or neurology anatomy. The authors attributed the cardiovascular improvement to the strong alignment between echocardiography and cardiac anatomy. In contrast, the lack of improvement in neurology and gastrointestinal content was likely due to ultrasound’s limited relevance to neuroanatomy topics and the curriculum’s focus on luminal gastrointestinal structures.

Several factors may account for the lack of improvement in anatomy knowledge observed in this study. The 6-week interval between the POCUS sessions and quiz administration, combined with the staggered design in which scanning sessions were spaced 2 to 4 weeks apart, likely reduced retention compared with studies that administered assessments immediately after instruction or implemented a more formal curriculum.21,25,26 Despite this, students who participated in the POCUS sessions demonstrated similar anatomical knowledge compared with those who received prerecorded anatomy lectures, suggesting that ultrasound exposure may support retention of previously learned material.21,27 These findings highlight several important considerations for the design of ultrasound learning experiences. Implementing shorter intervals between instruction and assessment, and providing repeated exposure, may enhance retention.28 Additionally, aligning ultrasound instruction with anatomically relevant regions and reinforcing content over time may facilitate improvements in POCUS knowledge, anatomical understanding, and technical skill development.29,30

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of participants completing both pre- and post-test, likely reduced statistical power and increased the risk of Type II error. The single-center design restricts generalizability to institutions with different curricula or student populations. Voluntary participation in optional “free scan” sessions may have introduced self-selection bias, as students with greater interest in ultrasound may have been more likely to participate and attend optional sessions. Although staggered groups were utilized, the absence of a true control group without any POCUS exposure limits the ability to isolate the independent effect of the intervention. Additionally, curricular changes implemented for the OMS-I cohort, including integration of POCUS into anatomy lab sessions, likely contributed to baseline differences between groups. Despite these limitations, clinician-led POCUS sessions resulted in measurable improvement in ultrasound knowledge and confidence, supporting the integration of early, structured ultrasound sessions into pre-clinical curricula.

There are several directions for future research in this area. Study design that could overcome our limitations would be a multi-center study or a single-center study with a true control group who does not receive ultrasound education, although the heterogenous nature of medical education and the widely held belief of POCUS benefit in medical education makes this less feasible. Longer term follow-up to see improvement in standardized testing or clinical performance would also give a more robust dataset. Such studies would benefit from a standardized competency assessment to allow comparison across different POCUS training models.

Conclusion

Over the course of the study, participants demonstrated a clear improvement in overall POCUS knowledge (total score improved from pre: 4.41 to post 6.41, p = 0.0005) and comfort with POCUS (Q10a: 2.31 to 3.69 (p = 0.0018)). Anatomy knowledge did not show a statistically significant improvement between the staggered groups. Ultrasound knowledge improved, and students found its use subjectively valuable in anatomical education. Beyond their test scores, students consistently reported that the ultrasound sessions helped them better visualize anatomy in a dynamic context. Future directions include expanding this model to larger cohorts with more robust study designs and longer-term outcome evaluation. Our study supports the feasibility, perceived value, and POCUS knowledge benefits of clinician-led integration into pre-clinical curricula while also showing that effect on anatomy knowledge will require larger and longer studies.
