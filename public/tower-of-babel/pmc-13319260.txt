Real-Time Detection of Colorectal Cancer Using Topical Application of a pH-Activatable Fluorophore
Authors: Daan G J Linders, Jinhui Ser, Md Shamim, Aida Baibussunova, Jasmijn C J Loggers, Gilbert Noordam, Davey van den Burg, Gijs J D van Acker, Onno R Guicherit, Marinke Westerterp, Peter J K Kuppen, Andreas W K S Marinelli, Denise E Hilling, Elke E M Peters, Satoshi Kashiwagi, Maged Henary, Hak Soo Choi, Alexander L Vahrmeijer

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStandard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer involves neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision, but this approach carries significant morbidity and often results in incomplete resections owing to poor intraoperative tumor visualization. For patients with complete response to nCRT, a watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy can spare surgery, but current imaging techniques inadequately identify complete responders, leading to regrowth in ~ 30% of cases. To improve nCRT response assessment and guide resections, we developed PH10, a topically applied, pH-activatable near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent probe for rapid, tumor-specific imaging.MethodsPH10, a small-molecule cyanine analog, was tested in murine models and on human tumor specimens (n = 11). Fluorescence activation, tumor specificity, and tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) were evaluated in vivo and ex vivo within clinically relevant timeframes.ResultsPH10 enabled rapid and specific tumor visualization, achieving a median TBR of 3.2 within 1 min in vivo in murine models and 2.2 within 10 min ex vivo on human samples. The probe demonstrated high specificity for tumor tissue within the acidic tumor microenvironment without requiring systemic administration or prolonged incubation.ConclusionsPH10 is a promising, fast-acting topical NIR agent for real-time tumor detection during colorectal cancer endoscopy and surgery. Its simplicity and rapid kinetics support potential clinical translation. Moreover, its pH-activatable mechanism may extend its utility to other solid cancers with acidic microenvironments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40291-026-00853-6.

Key Points

PH10, a pH-activatable near-infrared probe, enabled fast tumor visualization with high specificity, achieving a tumor-to-background ratio of 3.2 in 1 min in murine models and 2.2 in 10 min in human samples.PH10’s fast activation and simplicity make it a promising tool for real-time colorectal cancer detection during fluorescence-guided interventions, with potential for use in other cancers with acidic microenvironments.

Introduction

Curative-intent treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer consists of neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision, a procedure associated with significant postoperative complications and morbidity, such as anastomotic leakage, stoma-related issues, urinary incontinence, impotence, and bowel dysfunction [1–5]. The complex anatomy of the distal rectum and nCRT-induced fibrosis complicate the accurate identification of tumor boundaries during surgery, resulting in tumor-positive surgical margins in up to 20% of cases [6, 7]. Such incomplete resections significantly increase the risk of local recurrence, negatively affect overall survival, and often necessitate additional treatments, leading to increased patient morbidity and higher healthcare costs [6, 8, 9].

Approximately 15–20% of patients with rectal cancer who undergo nCRT achieve a pathological complete response (pCR), defined as no residual tumor cells found in the resected specimen [10, 11]. For these patients, an organ-preserving watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy offers a potential alternative to surgery, aiming to maintain quality of life [12–14]. However, accurately identifying pCR preoperatively remains challenging owing to the limitations of conventional imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and endoscopy. This discrepancy between clinical complete response (cCR) and pCR contributes to local regrowth rates of up to 38% in patients managed with W&W and underscores the need for improved methods to assess treatment response [12–16].

Advanced imaging techniques are needed to address two critical stages in rectal cancer management: (1) endoscopic evaluation of treatment response after nCRT, to identify candidates for W&W, and (2) intraoperative detection of residual tumors during surgery, to prevent incomplete resections in patients who do not achieve a complete response. Tumor-targeted near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging (FI) has emerged as a promising modality to enhance tumor visualization in these contexts. By combining tumor-specific fluorescent agents with NIR light (700–900 nm), this technique enables real-time optical imaging during both fluorescence molecular endoscopy (FME) and fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) [17, 18]. FME could improve the accuracy of identifying pCR during nCRT response evaluation, while FGS could aid in detecting residual tumors intraoperatively, facilitating more complete resections.

Despite its potential, current NIR agents for colorectal cancer imaging are limited by poor specificity, off-target fluorescence, and the need for intravenous administration or prolonged incubation after parenteral or topical application, which impedes clinical adoption [18–24]. To overcome these limitations, we developed PH10, a pH-activatable, fluorescently quenched imaging agent optimized for topical application. This approach offers several advantages, including minimal nonspecific background signals, reduced risk of systemic side effects, and streamlined integration into surgical and endoscopic workflows [18]. The acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) represents a universal and accessible target for activatable imaging agents, as it is a hallmark of solid cancers driven by altered cancer cell metabolism (Warburg effect) [25–28]. While normal extracellular pH is approximately 7.5, the TME exhibits mild acidity, with pH values ranging from 6.4 to 7.1 [29]. However, most existing pH-sensitive probes activate only at relatively low pH values (< 5.0), making them unsuitable for detecting the mildly acidic TME, potentially resulting in false negatives and missed residual tumors [30].

Yokomizo et al. recently reported a rapidly acting, topically applied pH-sensitive cyanine analog fluorophore that highlighted submillimeter peritoneal tumors via organic anion transporter peptides (OATP) [31]. Armed with this study, we designed PH10, a cyanine analog modified with a proximal amino group and a cyclohexathione ring to confer pH sensitivity. This structure allows electron delocalization in mildly acidic environments, reducing intramolecular fluorescence quenching. To further optimize the probe for cancer imaging, we incorporated sulfur into the ring structure, enhancing its sensitivity to mildly acidic pH and improving solubility, thereby increasing its tumor-to-background ratio (TBR). PH10 has already been shown to brightly, rapidly, and specifically label thyroid cancer in mouse models by spraying, indicating its potential use in endoscopic evaluation of treatment response in colorectal cancer and intraoperative detection of residual tumors during surgery [32].

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This study aimed to develop, validate, and clinically translate a pH-sensitive fluorophore (PH10) for rapid intraoperative detection of (residual) human cancer through topical application. Initial preclinical experiments were conducted using PH10 and a control fluorophore (PH09) in both in vitro and murine models to assess targeting specificity, including antibody-blocking studies. Subcutaneous and peritoneal disseminated tumor-bearing mice (n = 3) were used to simulate clinical tumor resection scenarios. Researchers were not blinded to experimental conditions, and no outliers were excluded from the analysis. Owing to the exploratory nature of this work, predefined power calculations were not performed. Given the specific objectives of each experiment, randomization was not applied, and operators were aware of sample conditions. In the clinical phase, freshly resected tissues were collected from ten patients diagnosed with colon cancer to evaluate the fluorescence activation of PH10 after topical application ex vivo. Detailed study protocols and methodologies are provided in subsequent sections. Animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (protocol no. 2016N000136). The clinical study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC)/Haaglanden Medical Center (HMC) (N22.117), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Chemicals and Synthesis

All reagents and solvents used for the synthesis and purification of fluorophores were of American Chemical Society (ACS) grade or higher and were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA), Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA), Combi-Blocks (San Diego, CA, USA), and Acros Organics. Reaction progress was monitored using silica gel 60 F254 TLC plates (Merck EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Purification of the final fluorophores was performed via flash column chromatography using 60–200 μm, 60 Å silica gel (Dynamic Adsorbents, Norcross, GA, USA). Both 1H− and 13C− nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained using high-quality Kontes NMR tubes (Kimble Chase, Vineland, NJ, USA) rated to 500 MHz and were recorded on a Bruker Avance (400 MHz) spectrometer using CDCl3 or DMSO-d6 containing tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an internal calibration standard set to 0.0 ppm. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm), and signals were designated as singlets (s), doublets (d), triplets (t), multiplets (m), or broad signals (br), with coupling constants (J) provided in Hertz (Hz). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis)/NIR absorption spectra were measured using a Varian Cary 50 spectrophotometer, and melting points were determined with a calibrated Mel-temp apparatus.

Measurement Photophysical Properties

Absorbance and fluorescence emission spectra of the series of NIR fluorophores were measured using fiber optic HR2000 absorbance (200–1100 nm) and USB2000FL fluorescence (350–1000 nm) spectrometers (Ocean Optics, Dunedin, FL, USA). NIR excitation was provided by a 655 nm red laser pointer (Opcom Inc., Xiamen, China) set to 5 mW cm−2 and coupled through a 300 μm core diameter, NA 0.22 fiber (Fiberguide Industries, Stirling, NJ, USA). To assess pH-dependent fluorescence activation, optical measurements were conducted in buffered solutions with pH adjustments made using NaOH or HCl. Fluorescence quantum yields (QY) were determined using indocyanine green (ICG) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (QY = 2.7%) as a calibration standard, with matched absorbance at 760 nm [33]. In silico calculations of physicochemical properties, including the partition coefficient (LogP, LogD at pH 7.4 and pH 5.0), molecular surface charge, hydrogen bond acceptors/donors (HBA/HBD), acid dissociation constant (pKa), and total polar surface area (TPSA), were performed using Marvinsketch and JChem calculator plugins (ChemAxon, Budapest, Hungary).

Stability Studies of Squaraine Fluorophores

Photostability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 760 nm over a 3-h time period. Measurements were taken intermittently at the indicated time points. The light exposure condition involved a custom-built real-time intraoperative NIR imaging system [34]. A black 96-well plate containing 10 μM of PH09 and PH10 in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS solution was placed 15 cm away from the light source and exposed to 760-nm laser excitation with white light at a fluence rate of 2 mW cm−2 at room temperature. The intensity of the fluorescence signal was analyzed using ImageJ and calculated images at each indicated time point divided by 0 min image.

Fluorescence Live-Cell Imaging

Colorectal cancer MC38 and fibroblast NIH3T3 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Ovarian cancer cells (ID8 cells) were procured from the laboratory of Dr. Wang at Massachusetts General Hospital’s Vincent Center for Reproductive Biology (MGH). The cells were cultivated and kept in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), which was supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). For fluorescence live-cell imaging, cells were then washed with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) and then performed using BioTek Cytation 5 (Winooski, VT, USA) with a Cy7 filter set (Chroma Technology, Brattleboro, VT, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a BioTek Cytation 5 system with a 20 × objective, providing resolution sufficient for organelle-level localization but not for suborganelle analysis. The fluorescence intensity of each cell was measured using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) based on the raw, linear image data before any postprocessing. All NIR fluorescence images for a particular fluorophore were normalized identically for all conditions during an experiment. That is, for each NIR fluorophore, the image with the strongest signal was optimized for brightness, contrast, and gamma, and then these identical settings were applied to all other images in the same group. Critically, these nonlinear adjustments (gamma correction) were used for visualization purposes only and were not applied to the quantitative intensity measurements. For intercellular imaging using different pH media and colocalization imaging, NIH3T3 and MC38 cells were incubated at a density of 10,000 cells/well in a 6-well plate in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in the air. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the presence of 1 µM of PH09 and PH10 in different pH media adjusted using HCI. After being washed three times with HBSS, nuclei, mitochondria, and lysosomes were then stained with NucBlue, MitoTracker Green FM (0.5 μM), or LysoTrackerTM Green (0.5 μM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in HBSS for 30 min as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Cellular Uptake Mechanism

To determine the cellular uptake mechanism of PH09 and PH10 using an OATP inhibitor and cold conditions, cells were seeded on a 24-well plate (Corning, cat. no. 3524) at a density of 10,000 cells per well and incubated in growth media for 48 h. For the inhibition condition, cells were preincubated with 250 μM of BSP (OATP inhibitor; MedChemExpress, cat. no. HY-D0217), well known as an OATP inhibitor in phenol red-free Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 14025092) at 37 °C in the incubator for 20 min. For cold conditions, cells were preincubated at 4 °C for 30 min. Then, 1 μM of PH09 and PH10 in HBSS was added to each cell in both conditions and incubated for an additional 20 min.

Mouse Model of Peritoneal Dissemination of Ovarian Cancer

Female C57Bl/6 mice (no. 000664), 7 weeks old, were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed in an AAALAC-certified facility at Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH). All animal procedures were performed following the Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care of Laboratory Animals and approved by the MGH IACUC (protocol no. 2016N000136). To establish a murine model of peritoneal dissemination of ovarian cancer, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 3 × 106 in 100 μL PBS. In this model, macroscopic metastatic intraperitoneal nodules appear reproducibly after approximately 90 days.

Subcutaneous Xenograft Mouse Model of Colorectal Cancer

Female athymic nude mice (no. 490), 4 weeks old, were purchased from the Charles River (Wilmington, MA, USA) and housed in a MGH AAALAC-certified facility. All animal procedures were performed following the Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care of Laboratory Animals and approved by the MGH IACUC (protocol no. 2016N000136). To establish a murine model of subcutaneous xenograft of colorectal cancer, mice were injected subcutaneously with 3 × 106 MC38 cells in 100 µL of PBS with Matrigel (cells: Matrigel = 1:1). For experiments conducted on established tumors, an approximate tumor volume average of 100 mm3 is used as the experiment starting size.

Preclinical NIR Fluorescence Imaging

Mice were fed chlorophyll-free mouse chow (VWR International) at least 5 days before imaging to minimize autofluorescence. For in vivo imaging of ovarian cancer, animals were anesthetized with 100 mg kg−1 ketamine and 10 mg kg−1 xylazine subcutaneously (Webster Veterinary, Fort Devens, MA, USA). Following anesthesia, a midline incision was made to expose the abdominal cavity. All images were obtained using the same exposure time, and the excretion route of the fluorophore was investigated using the whole abdominal image. The chest cavity was also opened by incising the chest wall and the diaphragm to examine the distribution of compounds in the chest cavity. After in vivo imaging, mice were euthanized with CO2 inhalation, and tumor tissues and the major organs were removed for ex vivo imaging and histological examination. For the NIR fluorescence imaging, we used a custom-built real-time NIR imaging system (the FLARE imaging system) [35, 36]. In this study, a 760 nm excitation was used at a fluence rate of 2 mW cm−2, with white light (400–650 nm; 40,000 lux). Color and NIR fluorescence images were acquired simultaneously with custom software at rates up to 15 Hz over a 10-cm-diameter field of view (FOV). Pseudo-colored lime green was used for NIR fluorescence in the color-NIR merged images. The imaging system was positioned at a distance of 9 inches from the surgical field. For all real-time intraoperative imaging, a standardized imaging protocol was used during and after the operation. Real-time fluorescence signals in tumors (Tu) and tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) compared with neighboring tissue were obtained over the period of imaging. The TBR was calculated using the same formula, with IT representing the intensity of the tumor tissue and IB representing the signal intensity of the surrounding tissue over the imaging period.

General FOV (5 cm in diameter [dia.]) was used to include the pancreas, duodenum, liver, and kidneys of a mouse, while closeup FOV (3.3 cm in dia.) included the pancreas, duodenum, and liver. Color and NIR fluorescence images were taken simultaneously. All images were obtained using the same exposure time. To ensure objective quantification of the tumor-targeting efficiency in this complex model, the regions of interest (ROIs) for tumor and background were standardized on the basis of anatomical tumor margins in white-light images and adjacent normal tissues, respectively.

Preclinical Intraoperative Fluorescence-Guided Surgery in Ovarian Cancer Mouse Model

To perform preclinical fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) with peritoneal dissemination in the ID8 ovarian cancer C57BL/6 mouse model, we used the FLARE imaging system. For the peritoneal dissemination mouse model, we injected 20 nmol of PH09 and PH10 in 100 µL of 5% wt/vol BSA in saline into the mouse intraperitoneally 4 h before the surgery. The abdominal cavity was opened with a midline incision under isoflurane inhalational anesthesia. Peritoneal dissemination was resected under the 760-nm channel of NIR and white light. Mice were euthanized 4 h post-injection with CO2 inhalation, and tumor tissues and the major organs, including the heart, lung, liver, pancreas, spleen, kidney, duodenum, intestine, muscle, and adnexa, were removed for ex vivo imaging and histological examination.

In Vivo Tumor Targetability of PH09 and PH10 in Colorectal Cancer Mouse Model

To determine the tumor targetability of pH-sensitive probes with a subcutaneous xenograft MC38 colorectal cancer nude mouse model, we used the FLARE imaging system. We sprayed nude mice with 100, 250, and 500 μM of PH09 and PH10 in 30 μL of 5% wt/vol BSA in saline and waited for 1 min to perform in vivo image-guided surgery on tumors under white light and a 760 nm laser. Then, to distinguish the margin of the tumor area, we washed tumor tissue surrounded by normal tissue with saline three times and resected the tumor for ex vivo imaging. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was analyzed by ImageJ using images of tumor tissue divided by normal tissue.

Histological Analysis of the Tumor-Targetability of the NIR Fluorophore

To determine the tissue distribution of the NIR fluorophore, tumor-included tissue was removed from MC38 colorectal cancer-bearing mice sprayed with 500 µM of PH09 and PH10 in 30 µL of 5% wt/vol BSA in saline and washed with saline three times. Then we trimmed dissected tissues and embedded them in the Tissue-Tek optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA) without a prefixation step, and the tissue block was frozen at − 80 °C. Then, we cut the 10-µm-thick frozen sections using a cryostat (Leica, Germany). The slides were subjected to fluorescence analysis first, then stained for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Fluorescence and brightfield images were acquired on Cytation5. Exposure times were adjusted to obtain a similar maximum fluorescence value for each fluorescence image. Brightfield images of H&E-stained slides from a matching field of view were also obtained.

Patients

Patients with biopsy-confirmed colon cancer scheduled to undergo surgery at the Haaglanden Medical Center (HMC), The Hague, the Netherlands, were enrolled in the study. Patients that underwent a low-anterior resection or abdominoperineal resection were excluded, owing to a different pathological work-up (fixation before tissue slicing) of these specimens. To prevent the enrollment of patients that did not have any tumor in the resected specimen, patients with a history of polypectomy were also excluded. Additionally, to prevent interference of the fluorescence signal, patients that received another NIR fluorophore perioperatively were excluded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained before any study-related procedure was performed. Institutional review board (IRB) approval was waived by the Ethics Committee of the HMC owing to the ex vivo methodology of the study.

Then, 5 mL of the pH-activatable NIR fluorescent probe PH10, dissolved in PBS, with 5% (volume/volume) DMSO, was topically applied onto the luminal side of the specimen. Then, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min after topical application, visible light and NIR fluorescence images of the tumor and surrounding tissue were obtained with the Pearl® Trilogy Imaging System (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). After 10 min of incubation, superfluous probe was washed off up to three times with saline, and after each washing step, the specimen was imaged again. Histology: Human Specimens

The two 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices were cut into smaller parts that were fixed with formalin and embedded in paraffin (FFPE). All FFPE tissue blocks (n = 40) were cut into sequential 4-μm-thick tissue sections that were mounted on adhesive slides. The first 4-μm tissue section of each FFPE tissue block was reserved for microscopic NIR fluorescence analysis. The next sequential section was stained for routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histology. Tumor-positive regions were delineated by a board-certified pathologist (EEMP) blinded for the fluorescent signal. All H&E slides were scanned using the Philips IntelliSite Pathology Solution (Philips Electronics, Eindhoven, the Netherlands).

NIR Fluorescence Signal Analysis of Human Specimens

All macroscopic NIR fluorescence images of the longitudinally opened colon resection specimen and of the 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices on which PH10 was topically applied, acquired with the Pearl® Trilogy Imaging System, were analyzed using the QuPath software version 0.2.3 (University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, Scotland, Great Britain) and MeVisLab software, version 3.4.1 (MeVis Medical Solutions AG, Bremen, Germany), as described previously [37, 38]. The original NIR fluorescence images of the whole resection specimen and the two tissue slices were imported in QuPath. In the whole resection specimens (i.e., the images acquired before tissue slicing), tumor-positive areas were manually annotated on the basis of macroscopy. Using the expand annotation function, the 1-cm border area around the tumor was automatically computed as background. Additionally, all healthy mucosa in the whole specimen was annotated to prevent the standardized 1-cm background rim from protruding outside the tissue. In the tissue slices, tumor-positive areas and background areas with healthy mucosa were annotated on the basis of the corresponding H&E slides in which the pathologist annotated tumor-positive regions. The annotated areas (tumor, background, and all mucosa in the specimen) were exported into the MeVisLab network. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), defined as the total fluorescent signal divided by the number of pixels, was calculated for each annotated area. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was defined as the MFI of the tumor area divided by the MFI in the background rim area. An example of the used NIR fluorescence signal analysis is shown in Supplementary Fig. S6. In line with clinical fluorescence-guided surgery trials, tumors that showed a fluorescence signal with a TBR ≥ 1.5 were defined as fluorescence-positive and tumors that had a TBR < 1.5 as fluorescence-negative.

Immunohistochemistry: Human Specimens

One sequential 4-μm tissue section of each FFPE tissue block of the tissue slice on which PH10 was topically applied (n = 20) was immunohistochemically stained for Ki-67 (clone MIB-1, GA626, Agilent, ready-to-use, mouse monoclonal) using the Dako Omnis IHC automated staining system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The number of 4-μm tissue sections of each tumor stained for Ki-67 is presented in Supplementary Table S1. All slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, dehydrated with alcohol and xyleen on the Tissue-Tek Prisma Plus Automated Slide Stainer (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA), then mounted with Tissue-Tek coverslipping film (Sakura Finetek). All immunohistochemically stained slides were scanned using the Philips IntelliSite Pathology Solution (Philips Electronics, Eindhoven, the Netherlands).

Quantification of Immunohistochemical Staining of Human Specimens

The expression of Ki-67 in tumor and adjacent healthy tissue was quantified as a mean immunohistochemical staining intensity (MSI) by means of a previously described objective scoring method using the ImageJ software (version 1.53h, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) [39, 40]. In each slide, one region of interest (ROI) containing the tumor tissue and one containing all the healthy tissue was drawn on the basis of the annotations by the pathologist on the H&E slides. The MSI of the ROIs was measured as follows: (1) the plugin colour_deconvolution2 was used to remove hematoxylin staining, to make sure only the DAB staining was analyzed, (2) the image was converted to grayscale, and (3) the image was inverted. ImageJ subsequently calculated an MSI value between 0 (black, no staining signal) and 255 (white, maximal staining signal) for each ROI and determined its area. The MSI in tumor and normal tissue from the individual 4-µm slides of each tissue slice were combined and corrected for area to create a weighted average MSI per tissue slice (n = 11). For each tissue slice, the MSI ratio was calculated by dividing the weighted average MSI in tumor tissue by the weighted average MSI in healthy tissue. Consequently, the fluorescence signal intensities in the tissue slices could objectively be compared with the Ki-67 expression in the same tissue slice.

Microscopic NIR Fluorescence Imaging and Penetration Depth Measurement on Human Specimens

The first cut, plain 4-µm tissue section of each FFPE tissue block of both 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices of all patients (n = 45) was deparaffinized and scanned using the NIR fluorescence-sensitive Odyssey CLx (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Subsequently, these slides were mounted with EverBrite Hardset with DAPI (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA) and scanned with the ZEISS Axio Scan.Z1 fluorescence slide scanner (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). In all patients, the maximal penetration depth of topically applied PH10 was measured macroscopically and microscopically. For this purpose, the NIR fluorescence Pearl® images of the tissue slice on which no PH10 was topically applied at the cutting edge (macroscopic), and the corresponding Odyssey Clx images, (microscopic) were analyzed in QuPath. A linear annotation was drawn from the mucosal border of the specimen to the NIR fluorescent spot furthest from the mucosal border (i.e., the “deepest” signal). The shortest route possible from the deepest signal to the mucosal border of the specimen was used for the annotation. QuPath automatically calculated the length of the annotation. The same location was used for the macroscopic and microscopic annotation. In short, penetration depth of PH10 was quantitatively assessed by measuring the shortest linear distance from the mucosal surface to the deepest detectable fluorescence signal using standardized macroscopic (Pearl®) and microscopic (Odyssey CLx) image annotations in QuPath. This measurement reflects the maximal detectable fluorescence depth rather than a continuous penetration front and therefore represents focal tissue labeling within the analyzed sections.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 software (SPSS, IBM Corporation, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). For all preclinical experiments, statistical analysis was carried out using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Results were presented as mean ± standard errors of the mean (s.e.m.) for all the image analyses on the FLARE system and fluorescence microscopy. In the human specimens, to investigate differences in MFI between tumor tissue and adjacent healthy colon tissue in both the whole specimen and tissue slices, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The same test was used to investigate differences in Ki-67 expression between tumor tissue and normal mucosa. The Mann–Whitney U test was performed to investigate differences in TBR between the two different probe concentration groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was determined to investigate the correlation between the MFI and the Ki-67 MSI in tumor tissue in the tissue slices. All statistical tests were performed as two-sided tests, and results were considered statistically significant at the level of P < 0.05: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. For the human data, descriptive statistics are depicted as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Data are summarized in graphs generated by GraphPad Prism 10. Figures were generated with biorender.com.

Results

Synthesis and Optophysicochemical Properties of pH-Activatable NIR Probes

To develop a rapidly acting, topically applied, pH-sensitive fluorophore for clinical use, we leveraged our previous strategy of incorporating an N-N cyclostructure adjacent to the conjugated double bonds of a heptamethine cyanine scaffold. This design reduces intramolecular fluorescence quenching under low-pH conditions (Supplementary Scheme S1) [31]. To modulate pH sensitivity, we synthesized heptamethine cyanines containing a cyclohexathione ring in place of the conventional cyclohexenyl ring within the polymethine backbone. The cyclohexathione ring, bearing a reactive meso-chlorine atom (PH09), was synthesized using established methods [41, 42]. The sulfur atom in the ring was expected to increase electron density within the cyanine backbone and enhance molecular solubility. PH09 served as a control fluorophore lacking a functional moiety for pH activation. The pH-activatable fluorophore, PH10, was synthesized via the SNR1 reaction between the meso-chloride of PH09 and a secondary amine, a moiety suitable for detecting acidic environments (Fig. 1a andSupplementary Figs. S1 and S2) [43]. The crude products were then purified by precipitation using DMSO/diethyl ether or methanol/diethyl ether or by column chromatography using 5–10% methanol in DCM [31].

The physicochemical and optical properties of the probes were then evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1a, PH10 exhibited a marked decrease in logD value from 1.92 at pH 7.4 to 0.44 at pH 5.0. PH10 displayed modest absorbance (ε at 589 nm = 46,600 M−1cm−1) at neutral pH, but an increase in absorbance (ε at 760 nm = 62,000 M−1cm−1) accompanied by a red shift to 641 under acidic conditions (pH = 5.0). PH09 also showed an increase in absorbance at pH 5.0 (ε at 589 nm = 97,600 M−1cm−1) compared with that at pH 7.4 (ε = 14,600 M−1cm−1) but without any red shift. With the red shift in an absorbance peak toward a longer wavelength, PH10 displayed higher fluorescence intensity at pH 5.0 relative to pH 7.4 (Fig. 1b). The normalized fluorescence intensity of PH10 at pH 5.0, which is similar to pH of the tumor environment, was nearly twofold higher than that at pH 7.4 (Fig. 1b). Fluorescence imaging of PH10 solutions confirmed brighter signals at pH 5.0, resembling the TME, whereas PH09 showed no significant fluorescence change between the two pH conditions (Fig. 1c). These results were consistent with the measurements of the absorbance ratios in Fig. 1b.

Next, photostability was evaluated under biologically relevant imaging conditions by incubating PH09 and PH10 in 5% BSA/PBS and exposing them to continuous NIR excitation (760 nm) and white light (2 mW cm−2) for 3 h. While designed for rapid and topical applications, both probes demonstrated moderate photostability under these conditions, supporting their suitability for short-term intraoperative imaging applications. At pH 5.0, PH09 and PH10 retained 27% and 35% of their fluorescence intensity, respectively, after laser exposure. At pH 7.4, both probes retained 27% of their intensity (Fig. 1d). Fluorescence intensity at pH 5.0 was consistently higher than at pH 7.4 over 150 min, indicating greater stability in mildly acidic conditions.

To understand the effect of changes in electron density distributions in response to pH changes, possible charge states, the optimized molecular structures, and frontier molecular orbitals (FMO) for compound PH10 in their deprotonated forms, PH10+ and PH102+were calculated on the basis of density functional theory (DFT) with 6–311 G basis set. We further performed quantum chemical calculations on the protonated forms, PH09+ and PH102+, using spartan 18. The energy gap decreases from 2.28 to 1.7 eV for PH10+ and PH102+, which explains the absorption wavelength change of PH09 in different pHs at 7.4 and 5.0. There are no distinct differences between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) for all the possible species of the optimized molecular structures. At low pH, PH10 undergoes protonation, resulting in a reduced energy gap and a general lowering of orbital energy compared with neutral pH (Fig. 1e). These results indicate that the change in electron density distributions and rapid reduction in intramolecular quenching occurs in the acidic TME and lysosomes of cancer cells for PH10, consistent with our previous study [31].

Cancer Cell Targetability of pH-Activatable NIR Probes

To investigate the cancer-targeting mechanism of pH-activable probes, we conducted a cell uptake assay using the colon cancer cell line MC38. Our previous study demonstrated that pH-activatable heptamethine cyanines were predominantly uptaken by OATPs [31]. On the basis of this result, we determined whether PH09 and PH10 enter cells via OATPs using an OATP inhibitor bromsulphthalein (BSP) or under cold conditions, which inhibits transporter activity in the membrane. Both probes showed significantly reduced fluorescence intensity when incubated with BSP or under cold conditions, compared with the control, indicating that their uptake occurs via OATPs (Fig. 2a, the BSP versus the no inhibitor control group: ****P < 0.0001, the cold condition versus the no inhibitor control group: ****P < 0.0001). Consistently, similar results were obtained in the previously explored ovarian cancer cell line ID8 [31], further confirming OATP-mediated uptake of the pH-activable probes PH09 and PH10 in cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. S3a).

Retention Mechanism in Cancer Cells

To determine the retention mechanism of PH09 and PH10 in cancer cells, live-cell imaging was conducted. Upon incubation with the probes, PH09 accumulated significantly more in normal fibroblast NIH3T3 cells than in colon cancer MC38 cells (Fig. 2b, P < 0.0001). In contrast, PH10 showed markedly higher uptake in MC38 cells compared with normal cells (Fig. 2b, P < 0.0001). Similar uptake patterns were observed in ovarian cancer ID8 cells, where PH09 accumulated more in NIH3T3 cells than in ID8 cells, while PH10 exhibited greater accumulation in cancer cells compared with normal cells (Supplementary Fig. S3b). To determine the subcellular localization of the probes within cancer cells, colocalization of the probe with MitoTracker or LysoTracker Green was examined. Fluorescence patterns were evaluated in relation to MitoTracker and LysoTracker signals to assess preferential organelle-associated localization rather than nanoscale colocalization. Both PH09 and PH10 predominantly accumulated in mitochondria (62% and 67%) rather than lysosomes (24% and 32%) in MC38 cells (Fig. 2c). Especially, PH10 displayed higher accumulation in both organelles compared with PH09 (Fig. 2c). This is consistent with our previous observations that cations with a heptamethine backbone accumulate in mitochondria and lysosomes [31]. Consistently, in ID8 cells, PH09 showed slightly greater mitochondrial accumulation, whereas PH10 exhibited higher lysosomal accumulation (Supplementary Fig. S3c). The difference in the subcellular distribution in mitochondria and lysosomes may explain the distinct targetability of PH10 to cancer cells, but the elucidation of the precise mechanisms of action is warranted. Together, these results suggest that the pH-activatable probes are retained in cellular organelles, enabling durable imaging.

Cytotoxicity of pH-Activatable NIR Probes

To assess the potential toxicity of PH09 and PH10, we incubated MC38 cells with a range of probe concentrations from 0.4 to 200 µM. PH09 and PH10 exhibited a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 8.0 and 2.4 in MC38 cells, respectively (Fig. 2d). The assays conducted on normal NIH3T3 cells and ovarian cancer ID8 cells similarly revealed IC50 values of 6.7 with PH09 and 5.5 with PH10 in normal cells, while PH10 demonstrated slightly higher toxicity in ID8 cells with an IC50 value of 4.7 (Supplementary Fig. S3d,e).

In Vivo Efficacy of Topically Applied pH-Activable Probes

The tumor-targeting ability of the pH-activatable probes PH09 and PH10 was evaluated in vivo using a subcutaneous mouse model bearing MC38 tumors. Once the tumors reached the desired size, the skin overlying the tumor was removed, and the probes were applied using a spray method. To optimize experimental conditions, TBR was evaluated under varying washing protocols (up to three washes; Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S4b,c) and waiting times (Supplementary Fig. S4a) after spraying to reduce background signals caused by unbound probes. Thus, we concluded that a 1-min topical application of PH10 with three washings would generate the best imaging result. After spraying 500 µM of PH09 and PH10 onto the tumor area and allowing a 1-min incubation, the site was washed three times with saline, and the exposed tumor was then imaged on the custom-built NIR imaging system [44]. Then, 1 min after the application of PH10, the tumors were clearly visualized under the system with a high TBR (≈ 3.0) against the surrounding normal tissue (Fig. 2e). In contrast, PH09 did not detect the tumor tissue with a low TBR (≈ 1.0), indicating that this probe is not suited for tumor imaging. The TBR of PH10 increased following washing, reaching a value of 3.2, which was significantly higher than the TBR of 0.9 observed for PH09 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S4b,d). Interestingly, a high signal was observed in peritumor regions for both probes, indicating that the probes detected low pH in the TME. To explore the optimal dose of pH-activatable probes, we tested various concentrations of PH10 using the same imaging procedure. Although the highest TBR of 3.2 was consistently observed with 500 µM PH10, and the TBR showed a trend of decreasing with lower concentration, the final TBR value for the 250 µM group was not significantly smaller than that of 500 µM (Fig. 2f). Ex vivo analysis confirmed that the 500 µM PH10 group exhibited a significantly higher signal than the 50 µM group, with the difference being greater than that observed with the 250 µM group (Fig. 2g). Histological analysis with H&E staining of tumor tissue treated with 500 µM PH10 revealed that the strong fluorescence signals of PH10 were detected in the top several layers of tumor nodules, tumor stroma, and peri-tumor capsule (Fig. 2h). These results are consistent with our previous work showing that the low molecular weight, along with the modest LogD value of PH10, supported efficient diffusion into tumor tissue and that its pH sensitivity resulted in the high fluorescence signal in an acidic TME and lysosomes inside tumor cells [31]. Together, these results suggest that this novel pH-activatable NIR probe PH10 is well suited for clinical application.

Patient and Tumor Characteristics in the Ex Vivo Validation Study

Following encouraging results in vitro and in animal models, PH10 was evaluated ex vivo for colorectal cancer imaging in clinical settings. Patient and tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of ten patients with 11 histologically confirmed colon tumors undergoing surgery at the Haaglanden Medical Center, The Hague, the Netherlands, were enrolled. The median age of patients at surgery was 67 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 53–73), with seven female patients. The majority of tumors (91%) were adenocarcinomas, while one (9%) was a mucinous carcinoma. Surgical procedures included three sigmoid resections, four right-sided hemicolectomies, and three left-sided hemicolectomies. Nine tumors (82%) were well or moderately differentiated (low grade), and two were poorly or undifferentiated (high grade). Pathological tumor (pT) staging revealed one pT1 tumor, three pT2 tumors, five pT3 tumors, and two pT4a tumors. The median tumor size, as determined by pathological examination, was 40 mm in the greatest diameter (IQR: 28–45).

Topically Applied PH10 Detects Colon Cancer Ex Vivo with Adequate Contrast to Healthy Tissue

The workflow of the ex vivo validation study on human colon cancer tissue is shown in Fig. 3a. After we removed the tumor, we topically applied 50–500 µM PH10 onto the macroscopic tumor and surrounding healthy colon mucosa and performed NIR imaging up to 10 min. We then washed the probe off up to three times and imaged them after each washing step again. Topically applied PH10 enabled the detection of colon cancer and resulted in a sharp demarcated fluorescent signal in tumor tissue in the longitudinally opened colon resection specimen and on the 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices of the tumor (Fig. 3b). Histological analysis of the corresponding hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue, Ki-67-stained tissue, and microscopic fluorescence image of sections of the tissue confirmed that the macroscopic and microscopic fluorescence signal on the tissue slices matches the annotated tumor-positive area on the H&E slides (Fig. 3c). After the final washing step with saline, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was significantly higher in tumor tissue compared with adjacent healthy tissue (Fig. 3d, ***P < 0.001). The median TBR after the final washing step in all 11 tumors was 2.2 (IQR 1.8–2.9). The TBRs were significantly higher for the 500 µM dosing group (2.5) compared with the 50 µM dosing group (1.4) (Fig. 3e, *P = 0.012). All 11 colon tumors showed a fluorescence signal with a TBR > 1. Two out of three tumors in the 50 µM dosing group had a TBR < 1.5. All tumors in the 500 µM dosing group (n = 8) had a TBR ≥ 2.0. Colon tumors could also be visualized with a clinical fluorescence camera system (Supplementary Fig. S5). Importantly, these human ex vivo and the animal in vivo models reflect tissue-level contrast influenced by microenvironmental factors following topical exposure; accordingly, these approaches are mechanistically connected but not spatially equivalent to the live-cell experiments, which were designed to resolve intracellular uptake and subcellular distribution.

Consecutive Washing Steps Increase TBRs

Ten tumors were washed with saline three times after 10 min of incubation with PH10, one tumor was only washed two times (Supplementary Table S1). The median MFI in tumor tissue and adjacent healthy colon tissue (or background) both decreased with each washing step (Fig. 3f). However, because the background signal had a relatively larger decrease, the median TBR increased with each washing step (Fig. 3g). After 10 min of incubation and zero, one, two, and three washing steps, the median TBR was 1.4 (IQR 1.3–1.9), 1.8 (IQR 1.3–2.6), 1.9 (IQR 1.5–2.6), and 2.2 (IQR 1.9–3.1), respectively. In Fig. 3h, the effect of multiple washing steps on the NIR fluorescence signal is shown for one tumor. These results suggest that consecutive washing to reduce background signal is critical to improving TBR and imaging outcomes for topically applied pH-activatable probes.

Correlation between PH10 Fluorescence Intensity and Ki-67 Expression Levels

On one 3–5-mm-thick tissue slice per tumor (n = 11), the TBR was calculated on the NIR fluorescence image acquired after 10 min of incubation and three washing steps. The histologically proven viable tumor in the 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices showed a significantly higher fluorescence signal intensity compared with adjacent healthy colon tissue (P < 0.001). The median TBR on the tissue slices was 2.9 (IQR 2.5–4.8). To investigate whether the fluorescence signal colocalized and correlated with areas of high cell proliferation, 4-µm sections of the tissue were stained for Ki-67. An example of a Ki-67-stained 4-µm section is shown in Fig. 3c. The tumor area shows diffuse high Ki-67 expression, while adjacent healthy mucosa only shows moderate Ki-67 expression in the deeper parts of the crypts of the villi intestinalis. The median mean staining intensity (MSI) of Ki-67 in tumor tissue and adjacent healthy mucosa in all tissue slices on which PH10 was topically applied (n = 11) was 28.9 (IQR 15.8–35.7) and 17.1 (IQR 13.0–18.4), respectively. Ki-67 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissue compared with adjacent healthy mucosa (P = 0.01). The median Ki-67 MSI ratio between tumor and healthy mucosa was 1.9 (IQR 0.9–2.5). The tumor Ki-67 MSI, as determined by immunohistochemistry, correlated significantly with the fluorescence signal intensity, defined as MFI, in the same tumor areas on the tissue slices (Fig. 3i, r = 0.65, P = 0.031). This indicates that tumors with a higher cell proliferation rate show a more intense fluorescence signal.

Microscopic Fluorescence Imaging Demonstrates Efficient PH10 Penetration in Human Tissue

Figure 3c shows an example of the microscopic NIR fluorescence signal in a 4-µm section. The tumor area shows a fluorescence signal, while adjacent healthy mucosa does not. The penetration depth of PH10 in colon cancer tissue was measured macroscopically on the NIR fluorescence images of the 3–5-mm-thick tissue slices and microscopically on the NIR fluorescence scans of 4-µm slides. The median penetration depth on the tissue slices and microscopic fluorescence scans was 4.0 mm (IQR 2.4–5.1) and 2.4 mm (IQR 1.9–4.5), respectively. These results consistently demonstrate that PH10 shows efficient diffusion deep enough to image human tumors and is well suited for FGS in a clinical setting

Discussion

This study demonstrates the in vivo efficacy of a rationally designed, tumor-targeted, pH-activatable cyanine fluorophore, PH10, for colorectal cancer imaging. PH10 shows changes in electron density distributions in the acidic TME and lysosomes of cancer cells and rapid reduction in intramolecular quenching, resulting in robust detection of colorectal cancer within 1 min in a murine model and 10 min in surgical specimens of topical application with a high TBR. We improved the previously explored probe [31] with a cyclohexathione ring that increased solubility and sensitivity to respond to a small pH change in the tumor tissue. Specifically, this structural modification allows PH10 to undergo efficient protonation in acidic environments, leading to a significant red shift in its fluorescence peak and enhanced brightness within the NIR window (800 nm), as evidenced by our DFT calculations and orbital energy analysis. Additionally, PH10 retains an excellent affinity to OATPs, while it shows efficient diffusion into tumor tissue, robust fluorescence signal due to its high sensitivity to a mildly acidic TME, and targetability to the mitochondria and lysosomes.

Although the relative contributions of extracellular versus intracellular pH were not quantitatively dissected, the observed fluorescence patterns are consistent with a combined mechanism involving extracellular activation and intracellular retention. While the lipophilic cationic backbone facilitates general uptake into mitochondria, the preferential accumulation of PH10 in acidic lysosomes further triggers its pH-dependent activation. Subcellular localization differences between cell lines likely reflect cell-context-dependent partitioning driven by variations in mitochondrial potential, lysosomal content, and intracellular pH. Importantly, PH10 consistently exhibits relatively increased lysosomal localization compared with PH09. Our data confirm that even at identical concentrations, PH10 exhibits a nearly twofold increase in fluorescence intensity at pH 5.0 compared with pH 7.4, indicating that its superior contrast is driven by pH responsiveness rather than simple accumulation. These mechanisms enable rapid and durable real-time imaging with high TBR, which together overcome historical shortcomings of activatable fluorescence probes, such as a long waiting time after injection and short imaging window [45], making it ideal for the specific detection of colorectal cancer.

Rationally designed PH10 adds value by combining a small-molecule NIR readout, topical administration, rapid activation within 1 min, and sufficient retention and real-time visualization in cancer. This differentiates it from previously explored pH-sensing probes, including ONM-100, which is an intravenously administered pH-activatable nanoprobe used with a preoperative dosing interval and gGlu-HMRG, which is a visible-spectrum, enzyme-activated topical [27, 46]. While CypH-11 and PH08 also exemplify a sprayable NIR pH-responsive strategy, the novelty of PH10 in the present work lies in rapid activation within 1 min, followed by sufficient retention for real-time visualization of cancer, conferring faster reported labeling, strong contrast, and direct relevance to intraoperative tumor imaging [31, 47].

In clinical contexts, FME has the potential to improve neoadjuvant treatment response assessment in patients with rectal cancer after nCRT, to correctly select patients for a W&W strategy and avoid unnecessary surgery. FGS could help to achieve complete tumor resection and therefore prevent local regrowth and improve patient outcomes. Topically applied, activatable imaging agents in FME and FGS could offer advantages in terms of patient logistics, safety, and cost-effectiveness, all while ensuring adequate contrast between cancerous and healthy tissues. This validation study demonstrates that the topically applied, pH-activatable fluorescent imaging agent PH10 allows rapid and highly specific visualization of human colorectal cancer, with a median TBR of 2.2, which is well above the arbitrary minimal value of 1.5 that is considered to be required for satisfactory tumor visualization [48]. It should be noted that the ex vivo and in vivo models reflect tissue-level contrast; these are mechanistically linked to the live-cell experiments, but not spatially equivalent.

The fluorescence signal intensity in the tumor correlated with the level of Ki-67 expression, a marker of tumor cell proliferation, suggesting that PH10 would be especially suitable for detecting fast growing, aggressive tumors [49, 50]. Of note, Ki-67 was included as a marker of tumor cell proliferation and viability rather than as a surrogate for acidic conditions; thus, the observed correlation reflects preferential fluorescence in viable tumor regions and does not constitute direct validation of low-pH-dependent activation.

Overall, 82% (9 out of 11 samples) of the tumors in this study archived a TBR ≥ 1.5, with significantly higher TBRs observed in the 500 µM dosing group compared with the 50 µM group, likely due to stronger fluorescence signals. In the 500 µM group, all tumors achieved TBRs ≥ 2.0, though animal studies indicated that 250 µM is also sufficient for adequate visualization. Future studies should adopt 250–500 µM as the starting concentration for optimal imaging outcomes. It was confirmed that washing with saline is necessary to achieve an adequate TBR. This is in line with other topically applied fluorescent imaging agents [51, 52]. Washing is performed to flush away excess imaging agent that tends to accumulate owing to gravity, causing a false-positive fluorescence signal, even when quenched. As washing only takes a minute, this would be feasible during FME or FGS. Additionally, PH10 demonstrated a penetration depth of 4 mm in human tissue, potentially sufficient for detecting submucosal tumors. Differences between macroscopic and microscopic penetration depths can be attributed to postresection tissue shrinkage from formalin fixation [53, 54].

These results are promising and suggest that PH10 could effectively detect residual tumors (or confirm its absence) during FME and FGS. However, this validation study has some limitations. First of all, owing to a different work-up at the Pathology Department (i.e., formalin fixation before tissue slicing), freshly resected rectal cancer specimens could not be used to test topically applied PH10. Instead, colon cancer specimens were used. While (distal) colon tumors biologically closely resemble rectal tumors, they are very seldom treated with neoadjuvant therapy [55, 56]. Therefore, it was not possible to confirm that PH10 can still visualize (residual) cancer after nCRT. Second, manual delineation of tumor-positive areas introduces some variability in MFI and immunohistochemistry intensity calculations. To minimize this, tumor annotations from H&E slides were carefully transferred onto NIR fluorescence images. Background areas were generated automatically, but minor discrepancies could arise from manual tumor delineation. Third, as this study focused on proof-of-concept topical application, comprehensive evaluation of biocompatibility, toxicology, and phototoxicity was beyond its scope and should be addressed in future studies, together with expanded stability testing in complex biological environments. Finally, potential spillover of PH10 during sectioning might have overestimated penetration depth. However, extensive washing before tissue slicing likely mitigated this risk. Still, removing blood and mucus before topical application of PH10 could also have overestimated tissue penetration in vivo.

The next steps toward the clinical translation of topically applied PH10 for FME and FGS comprise Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) production of the imaging agent, toxicology testing, and, if successful, a subsequent phase I/II clinical trial in healthy volunteers and patients with rectal cancer. In healthy volunteers, the imaging agent could be tested on drawn blisters to investigate local (and systemic) safety and irritability and its effect on wound healing. In patients with rectal cancer, PH10 could be applied in vivo during FME or directly in the surgical cavity during FGS to evaluate its safety and feasibility in detecting residual tumor tissue.
