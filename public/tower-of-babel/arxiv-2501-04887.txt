Uniform nonlinear Szemerédi theorem for corners in finite fields
Zi Li Lim

Abstract
. Let P ⁡ ( t ) , Q ⁡ ( t ) ∈ ℚ ⁡ ( t ) P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t) be rational functions such that P ⁡ ( t ) , Q ⁡ ( t ) P(t),Q(t) and the constant function 1 1 are linearly independent over ℚ \mathbb{Q} , we prove an asymptotic formula for the number of the corner configurations ( x 1 , x 2 ) , ( x 1 + P ⁡ ( y ) , x 2 ) , ( x 1 , x 2 + Q ⁡ ( y ) ) (x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y)) in the subsets of 𝔽 p 2 \mathbb{F}_{p}^{2} .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Introduction

The monumental paper of Bergelson and Leibman
[
BL96
]
provides a satisfactory qualitative understanding of the sets that do not contain polynomial progressions, i.e., configurations of the form

x,x+P_{1}(y),x+P_{2}(y),...,x+P_{l}(y)

where
P_{1},P_{2},...,P_{l}
are polynomials. Meanwhile, the quantitative understanding of this subject is still formative. Recent breakthroughs of Peluse
[
Pel19
,
Pel20
]
and of Peluse and Prendiville
[
PP24
]
bring major progress on quantitative estimates for sets lacking one dimensional polynomial progressions, but the theory for higher dimensional polynomial progressions is far from complete.

There are significant new difficulties for higher dimensional progressions, which could be found in Peluse’s paper on L-shaped configurations
[
Pel24
]
. Further evidence regarding high dimensional difficulties are recorded in Austin’s work
[
Aus14
]
.

In this paper, we consider the following two dimensional configurations in finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{p}

(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))

where
P,Q
are rational functions. We call these configurations
corners generated by
P,Q
. Here and throughout, we use the notation
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}
for expectations, and it will exclude the poles when the summands of
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}
involve rational functions. Besides, we will often abbreviate
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}
as
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
. Our main result is the following asymptotic formula for counting operator for corners generated by
P,Q
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions such that
P(t),Q(t)
and the constant function
1
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, then we have the asymptotic formula

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O_{P,Q}\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{40960}}\bigr)

for all
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 1.2
.

We have not attempted to optimize the power-saving exponent
1/40960
. The precise numerical value of the power-saving exponent can be ignored for a first reading.

Remark 1.3
.

With more effort, it can be shown that the implied constant of the error term in Theorem
1.1
actually only depends on the degrees of
P,Q
, where we define the degree of a rational function to be the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator. Also, we refer to Section
2
for more details regarding our asymptotic notation convention.

As usual, one could derive an upper bound for subsets
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
that do not contain corners generated by
P,Q
. To see that, let
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
be the characteristic function
1_{A}
, and note that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(1_{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}1_{A}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}1_{A}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)\gg\Bigl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}1_{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\Bigr)^{3}

by the pigeonhole principle (see
[
HLY21
, Lemma 3.1]
for details). This leads to the following power-saving bound.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions such that
P(t),Q(t)
and the constant function
1
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
A
be a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with density
\delta=\frac{|A|}{p^{2}}\gg p^{-\frac{1}{122880}}
, then
A
contains corners generated by
P,Q
. In fact, there are plenty of corners generated by
P,Q
in
A
, that is

\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},y)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}:\text{The corner}\;(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\;\text{is in}\;A\}\gg p^{3}\delta^{3}.

As a remark, the linear independence assumption of
P,Q
and constant function
1
is not restrictive, it includes the cases that
P,Q
are linearly independent polynomials with zero constant terms, which are the common assumptions for quantitative polynomial Szemerédi theorem. For example, the configurations we consider include

(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+y,x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+y^{2})

and

(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+y^{3},x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+y^{3}-y^{2}+y)

and even strange examples like

(x_{1},x_{2}),\left(x_{1}+\frac{y^{2}}{y^{7}-5y^{3}},x_{2}\right),\left(x_{1},x_{2}+y^{17}+\frac{1}{y^{13}+19}\right).

Compared to previous results, Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.4
is new in the following two aspects.

•

Qualitatively, Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.4
allows
P,Q
to be rational functions while the previous results are only applicable to polynomials.

•

Quantitatively, the power-saving exponent
1/122880
is an improvement upon previous results when
P,Q
are polynomials and their degrees are the same and greater than
2
. Moreover, the power-saving exponent
1/122880
is uniform in the degrees of
P,Q
; it does not decay exponentially as the degrees grow.

To be more specific, previously Han, Lacey and Yang
[
HLY21
]
proved that if
P,Q
are linearly independent polynomials with zero constant terms and
distinct
degree or linearly independent quadratic polynomials with zero constant terms, and
A
is a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with density
\delta\gg p^{-1/16}
, then

\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},y)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}:\text{The corner}\;(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\;\text{is in}\;A\}\gg p^{3}\delta^{3}.

On the other hand, Kuca
[
Kuc24a
,
Kuc24
]
proved that if
P,Q
are linearly independent polynomials with zero constant terms, then there exists a constant
c
depending on the degrees of
P,Q
such that

\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},y)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}:\text{The corner}\;(x_{1},x_{2}),(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\;\text{is in}\;A\}\gg p^{3}\delta^{3}

for all subsets
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with density
\delta\gg p^{-c}
. The method of Han, Lacey and Yang is Fourier analytic while the method of Kuca is based on higher order Fourier analysis and degree lowering. The power-saving exponent
c
in Kuca’s results is unspecified in his papers, but it could be made explicit in principle, although it would be fairly small due to the astronomical amount of application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in degree lowering. For this same reason, the power-saving exponent
c
would also decay quite quickly as the degrees of
P,Q
grow. Therefore, our power-saving exponent
1/122880
is an uniform numerical improvement only in the cases not covered by the results of Han, Lacey and Yang.

The new difficulties when moving to rational function progressions from polynomial progressions are that Weyl differencing is no longer useful and techniques based on PET induction are no longer available for obtaining Gowers norm control. The main reason why we can allow rational functions in Theorem
1.1
is that we use algebraic geomerty to bypass Weyl differencing to obtain Gowers norm control for counting operators. As a side note, the Gowers norm control we obtain is of fairly low degree, and this explains the improvement on power-saving exponent in the error term.

The approach of this paper comes from four different papers
[
HLY21
,
HL24
,
KW24
,
Kuc24
]
. To elaborate, Hong and the author
[
HL24
]
developed an algebraic geometry version of PET induction that could bypass Weyl differencing to obtain Gowers norm control for one dimensional three term rational function progressions. To generalize the algebraic geometry PET induction to the case of corners, we incorporate the method with the Fourier analytic approach due to Han, Lacey and Yang
[
HLY21
]
. However, the “naive” generalization of algebraic geometry PET induction does not work, since it would discard too much cancellation among exponential sums, and lead to degeneracy of certain varieties. Luckily, we could borrow an idea from the work of Kavrut and Wu
[
KW24
]
on improving the nonlinear Roth theorem to amplify the cancellation among exponential sums and hence obtain Gowers norm control. After obtaining Gowers norm control, it is natural to apply the degree lowering pioneered by Peluse
[
Pel19
]
to complete the proof. In fact, we need a different version of degree lowering that works for directional Gowers norms, and this version of degree lowering was developed by Kuca
[
Kuc24
]
.

To draw parallel comparisons, we remark that corners could be viewed as a two dimensional generalization of one dimensional three term progressions. For results on one dimensional three term progressions, see work of Bourgain and Chang
[
BC17
]
, work of Peluse
[
Pel18
,
Pel19
]
, work of Dong, Li and Sawin
[
DLS20
]
, and work of Hong and the author
[
HL24
]
. In another direction, for polynomial Szemerédi theorem for corners in integers, see work of Peluse, Prendiville and Shao
[
PPS24
]
and work of Kravitz, Kuca and Leng
[
KKL24
]
.

2.
Convention

2.1.
Notation

Throughout, let
p
be a prime and
\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the finite field with
p
elements. For any finite set
\mathcal{A}
and function
f:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{A}}f(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}}f(x)
be the average of
f
over
\mathcal{A}
. When the summation variables are in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, we would often omit the subscript
\mathbb{F}_{p}
in the summation notation and write
\mathbb{E}_{x}
instead. By abuse of notation, we also use
\mathbb{E}
to denote averaging excluding the poles of the (rational) functions being averaged. For example, if
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
are rational functions and
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
are functions, then

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))=\\
\frac{1}{p^{3}}\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\;\text{and}\;y\neq\text{poles}}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y)).

Furthermore, we would often omit the subscript
P,Q
in Vinogradov’s notation
\ll,\gg
and Landau’s notation
O,o
since the implied constants would usually depend on the rational functions
P,Q
.

Moreover, we would use boldface letters to denote vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
, for example
\mathbf{h},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}
would mean
(h_{1},h_{2}),(n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})
respectively.

2.2.
Fourier Analysis Notation

Let
d
be a positive integer, for any function
f:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, denote the
L^{r}
-norm with respect to the uniform probability measure on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
by
\lVert f\rVert_{r}=\bigl(\frac{1}{p^{d}}\sum_{x}\lvert f(x)\rvert^{r}\bigr)^{1/r}
and the
l^{r}
-norm with respect to the counting measure on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
by
\lVert f\rVert_{l^{r}}=\bigl(\sum_{x}\lvert f(x)\rvert^{r}\bigr)^{1/r}
. Denote
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
,
e_{p}(x)=e^{2\pi ix/p}
and Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}f(x)e_{p}(-x\xi)
. In this convention, Parseval’s identity reads
\lVert f\rVert_{2}=\lVert\widehat{f}\rVert_{l^{2}}
and Fourier inversion reads
f(x)=\sum_{\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)e_{p}(\xi x)
.

2.3.
Gowers Norm Notation

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Denote the difference operator by
\Delta
, that is
\Delta_{h}g(x)=g(x)\overline{g}(x+h)
for any functions
g
on
G
and any elements
h\in G
. Let
s
be a positive integer and
f:G\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a function, the
U^{s}(G)
-Gowers norm is defined to be

\lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}(G)}^{2^{s}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in G}\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{s}\in G}\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}...\Delta_{h_{s}}f(x).

More generally, let
H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}
be subgroups of
G
, the Gowers box norm with respect to
H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}
is defined to be

\lVert f\rVert_{H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}}^{2^{s}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in G}\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{1}\in H_{1},h_{2}\in H_{2},...,h_{s}\in H_{s}}\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}...\Delta_{h_{s}}f(x).

When
H=H_{1}=H_{2}=...=H_{s}
, we would write
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{s}(H)}
for
\lVert f\rVert_{H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}}
.

Suppose
K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{s}
are subgroups of
H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}
respectively, then we have

\lVert f\rVert_{H_{1},H_{2},...,H_{s}}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{s}}

for all functions
f
.

2.4.
Algebraic Geometry Terminology

By (classical) varieties, we mean any classical affine, quasi-affine, projective or quasi-projective varieties. We do not require the varieties to be irreducible, hence our affine varieties and projective varieties may be algebraic sets in some references. By abuse of terminology, we also refer to schemes that are separated and of finite type over
\mathrm{Spec}\;k
for some field
k
as a variety. We would often use
Y(\mathbb{C}),Z(\mathbb{F}_{p}),...
to denote classical varieties and
Y,Z,...
to denote schemes. If
X
is a scheme over some field
k
, we also write
X(k)
as
k
-valued points
\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(\mathrm{Spec}\;k,X)
.

If
X
is a variety or scheme, denote the dimension of
X
as a variety (or scheme) by
\dim_{\mathrm{Var}}X
(or
\dim_{\mathrm{Sch}}X
), which is the supremum of the length of the chains of irreducible closed subsets in the underlying topological space.

3.
Algebraic geometry PET induction

In this section, we would establish the framework of algebraic geometry PET induction. The main purpose is to upper bound the counting operator for corners generated by rational functions
P(t),Q(t)
by a Gowers norm, modulo the point-counting estimates for certain varieties. The precise statement is in Theorem
3.2
, and the corresponding point-counting estimates would be handled in Section
4
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
. Define the
Roth variety
for corners associated to
P(t),Q(t)
to be the (quasi-affine) variety cut out by the following ten equations in sixteen variables
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{16}
.

(1)

\displaystyle P(y_{1})-P(y_{3})

\displaystyle-P(y_{9})+P(y_{11})=0

(2)

\displaystyle P(y_{2})-P(y_{4})

\displaystyle-P(y_{10})+P(y_{12})=0

(3)

\displaystyle P(y_{5})-P(y_{7})

\displaystyle-P(y_{13})+P(y_{15})=0

(4)

\displaystyle P(y_{6})-P(y_{8})

\displaystyle-P(y_{14})+P(y_{16})=0

(5)

\displaystyle Q(y_{1})-Q(y_{2})

\displaystyle-Q(y_{9})+Q(y_{10})=0

(6)

\displaystyle Q(y_{3})-Q(y_{7})

\displaystyle-Q(y_{11})+Q(y_{15})=0

(7)

\displaystyle Q(y_{4})-Q(y_{8})

\displaystyle-Q(y_{12})+Q(y_{16})=0

(8)

\displaystyle Q(y_{5})-Q(y_{6})

\displaystyle-Q(y_{13})+Q(y_{14})=0

(9)

\displaystyle P(y_{9})-P(y_{10})-P(y_{11})+P(y_{12})

\displaystyle-P(y_{13})+P(y_{14})+P(y_{15})-P(y_{16})=0

(10)

\displaystyle Q(y_{9})-Q(y_{10})-Q(y_{11})+Q(y_{12})

\displaystyle-Q(y_{13})+Q(y_{14})+Q(y_{15})-Q(y_{16})=0

The Roth variety could be regarded as a classical variety or a scheme over various fields, for example, the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
or finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})
be the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-points of the Roth variety for corners associated to
P(t),Q(t)
. Then, we have the following Gowers norm control

\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert\\
\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{4}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{4}^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})}^{\frac{1}{4}}\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{6}}|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\biggr)^{\frac{1}{16}}

for all
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Heuristically, the Roth variety for corners is cut out by
10
constraints in
16
variables, hence the intuitive expectation suggests that the dimension of the Roth variety is
16-10=6
and the size of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-points on the Roth variety is about
p^{6}
when the
10
constraints are “sufficiently independent”. In this case, Theorem
3.2
would indeed be providing control by the Gowers norm.

The ingredients of the proof of Theorem
3.2
are divided into three propositions, namely Propositions
3.3
,
3.5
and
3.6
. The strategy is to generalize the algebraic geometry PET induction for one dimensional three term rational function progressions developed by Hong and the author
[
HL24
]
to two dimensional corners. We would proceed with Fourier analytic method, and transform the exponential sums into point-counting estimates for the corresponding Roth variety to avoid difficult multidimensional exponential sum estimates. The Fourier calculations in the first half (up until Equation (
17
)) of the proof of Proposition
3.3
are same as the Fourier calculations in the work of Han, Lacey and Yang
[
HLY21
]
on corners generated by polynomials. It seems plausible that combining the ideas from
[
HL24
]
and
[
HLY21
]
would lead to the desired estimates, however, there is a new obstacle–the “naive” generalization of algebraic geometry PET induction produces varieties with wrong expected dimensions. Fortunately, this obstacle could be tackled by ideas in the work of Kavrut and Wu
[
KW24
]
on improving the nonlinear Roth theorem.

The Proposition
3.3
is an intermediate step for algebraic geometry PET induction; it has the correct geometric term
|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|
but messy Fourier terms
F_{1},F_{2}
. The Fourier terms would be simplified in Proposition
3.5
and
3.6
. In the statement and proof of Proposition
3.3
, we would frequently use boldface letters to denote vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
, for example
\mathbf{h},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}
would mean
(h_{1},h_{2}),(n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})
respectively. Also, recall that the difference operator
\Delta
is defined as
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}g(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})\overline{g}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{h})
for any functions
g
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})
be the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-points of the Roth variety for corners associated to
P(t),Q(t)
. For any functions
f_{1},f_{2}
, define

\displaystyle F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n_{2}}\Delta_{-\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2}),

where the order of operators is taking Fourier transform first and then difference operator for
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}
. Then, we have

\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert\\
\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/2}\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/4}\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/8}\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{6}}|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\biggr)^{1/16}

for all functions
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

For notational convenience, denote

\Lambda_{0}\coloneqq\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert

to be the quantity we would like to estimate.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
x_{1},x_{2}
, we have

\begin{split}\Lambda_{0}&=\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert\\
&\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\Biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Fourier expand the functions
f_{1},f_{2}
to obtain

(11)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n})e_{p}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x})e_{p}(n_{1}P(y))\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})e_{p}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x})e_{p}(m_{2}Q(y))\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})e_{p}((\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{m})\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}e_{p}(n_{1}P(y)+m_{2}Q(y)).\end{split}

Denote
K(a,b)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}e_{p}(aP(y)+bQ(y))
and perform change of variables
\mathbf{n}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}
, then the expression (
11
) is equal to

(12)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})e_{p}((\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{m})\cdot\mathbf{x})K(n_{1},m_{2})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})e_{p}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x})K(n_{1}-m_{1},m_{2})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\biggl(\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})K(n_{1}-m_{1},m_{2})\biggr)e_{p}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x})\end{split}

Combining (
11
) and (
12
), Parseval’s identity says

(13)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\Biggl\lvert\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})K(n_{1}-m_{1},m_{2})\Biggr\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

Expanding the squares and performing change of variables
\mathbf{n}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{m}
, the expression (
13
) becomes

(14)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})\overline{\widehat{f}_{1}}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{h})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})\overline{\widehat{f}_{2}}(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{h})K(n_{1}-m_{1},m_{2})\overline{K}(n_{1}-m_{1}-h_{1},m_{2}+h_{2})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n})\overline{\widehat{f}_{1}}(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{h})\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})\overline{\widehat{f}_{2}}(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{h})K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{K}(n_{1}-h_{1},m_{2}+h_{2})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{h}}\Delta_{-\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{n})\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(\mathbf{m})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2}).\end{split}

To clarify, for the notation
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}
, the order of operators is applying Fourier transform first and then difference operator.

Note that in the last line of (
14
), some variables decouple, hence we can write (
14
) as

(15)

\sum_{\mathbf{h},n_{1},m_{2}}\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\Biggl(\sum_{n_{2}}\Delta_{-\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(n_{1},n_{2})\Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})\Biggr).

To ease the notations, define

\displaystyle F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n_{2}}\Delta_{-\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2}),

so that the expression (
15
) can be rewritten more succinctly as

(16)

\sum_{\mathbf{h},n_{1},m_{2}}F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2}).

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in variables
\mathbf{h},n_{1}
, we deduce that

\begin{split}\eqref{eqs: PET6}&=\sum_{\mathbf{h},n_{1}}F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\sum_{m_{2}}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\\
&\leq\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}\Biggl(\sum_{\mathbf{h},n_{1}}\sum_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime}}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Bigg)^{1/2}.\end{split}

For notational convenience, denote

\Lambda_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{h},n_{1}}\sum_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime}}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime}).

To sum up, so far we have obtained

(17)

\Lambda_{0}\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/2}\Lambda_{1}^{1/4}

and we have Cauchy–Schwarz away the functions
f_{0},f_{1}
. It is tempting to apply Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to
\Lambda_{1}
in variables
\mathbf{h},m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime}
to get rid of
f_{2}
. However, in this way, we would discard too much cancellation and the resulting variety has degeneracy, that is, the variety is defined by six equations in eight variables, but its dimension is three. To fix this, we use a technique due to Kavrut and Wu
[
KW24
]
to iterate the exponential sums one more time in order to capture more cancellation. Hence, we apply Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to
\Lambda_{1}
in variables
\mathbf{h},m_{2}
to obtain

(18)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{1}&=\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\sum_{n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime}}\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\\
&\leq\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}\\
&\quad\times\bBigg@{4}(\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime}\\
n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\bBigg@{4})^{1/2},\end{split}

where
m_{2}^{\prime\prime},n_{1}^{\prime}
are the copies of
m_{2}^{\prime},n_{1}
respectively.

Once again, for notational convenience, denote

\begin{split}\Lambda_{2}&\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime}\\
n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\end{split}

and (
18
) can be rewritten as

(19)

\Lambda_{1}\leq\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}\Lambda_{2}^{1/2}.

Now, we have kept enough cancellation, and we can apply Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to
\Lambda_{2}
in variables
\mathbf{h},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}
to get rid of
F_{2}
and transform exponential sums into point-counting on variety. Hence, we have

(20)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{2}&=\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\sum_{n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}}\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\\
&\leq\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h}}}\\
&\quad\times\bBigg@{4}(\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}\\
N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime},M_{2}\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(N_{1},M_{2})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(N_{1}^{\prime},M_{2})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(N_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(N_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\bBigg@{4})^{1/2},\end{split}

where
N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime},M_{2}
are the copies of
n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}
respectively.

Finally, expanding the exponential sums, we have

(21)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{h},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}\\
N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime},M_{2}\end{subarray}}\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(n_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(n_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(N_{1},M_{2})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(N_{1}^{\prime},M_{2})\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K(N_{1},m_{2}^{\prime})\overline{\Delta_{(-h_{1},h_{2})}K}(N_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}\\
y_{5},y_{6},y_{7},y_{8}\\
y_{9},y_{10},y_{11},y_{12}\\
y_{13},y_{14},y_{15},y_{16}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},h_{2}\\
n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}\\
m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime},M_{2}\end{subarray}}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad e_{p}\Bigl(+n_{1}P(y_{1})+m_{2}Q(y_{1})\quad-(n_{1}-h_{1})P(y_{9})-(m_{2}+h_{2})Q(y_{9})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,-n_{1}^{\prime}P(y_{2})-m_{2}Q(y_{2})\quad+(n_{1}^{\prime}-h_{1})P(y_{10})+(m_{2}+h_{2})Q(y_{10})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,-n_{1}P(y_{3})-m_{2}^{\prime}Q(y_{3})\quad+(n_{1}-h_{1})P(y_{11})+(m_{2}^{\prime}+h_{2})Q(y_{11})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,+n_{1}^{\prime}P(y_{4})+m_{2}^{\prime\prime}Q(y_{4})\quad-(n_{1}^{\prime}-h_{1})P(y_{12})-(m_{2}^{\prime\prime}+h_{2})Q(y_{12})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,-N_{1}P(y_{5})-M_{2}Q(y_{5})\quad+(N_{1}-h_{1})P(y_{13})+(M_{2}+h_{2})Q(y_{13})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,+N_{1}^{\prime}P(y_{6})+M_{2}Q(y_{6})\quad-(N_{1}^{\prime}-h_{1})P(y_{14})-(M_{2}+h_{2})Q(y_{14})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,+N_{1}P(y_{7})+m_{2}^{\prime}Q(y_{7})\quad-(N_{1}-h_{1})P(y_{15})-(m_{2}^{\prime}+h_{2})Q(y_{15})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,-N_{1}^{\prime}P(y_{8})-m_{2}^{\prime\prime}Q(y_{8})\quad+(N_{1}^{\prime}-h_{1})P(y_{16})+(m_{2}^{\prime\prime}+h_{2})Q(y_{16})\Bigr)\end{split}

which is equal to

(22)

\begin{split}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}\\
y_{5},y_{6},y_{7},y_{8}\\
y_{9},y_{10},y_{11},y_{12}\\
y_{13},y_{14},y_{15},y_{16}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},h_{2}\\
n_{1},n_{1}^{\prime},N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}\\
m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime},M_{2}\end{subarray}}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad e_{p}\Bigl(n_{1}(P(y_{1})-P(y_{3})-P(y_{9})+P(y_{11}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+n_{1}^{\prime}(-P(y_{2})+P(y_{4})+P(y_{10})-P(y_{12}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+N_{1}(-P(y_{5})+P(y_{7})+P(y_{13})-P(y_{15}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+N_{1}^{\prime}(P(y_{6})-P(y_{8})-P(y_{14})+P(y_{16}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+m_{2}(Q(y_{1})-Q(y_{2})-Q(y_{9})+Q(y_{10}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+m_{2}^{\prime}(-Q(y_{3})+Q(y_{7})+Q(y_{11})-Q(y_{15}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+m_{2}^{\prime\prime}(Q(y_{4})-Q(y_{8})-Q(y_{12})+Q(y_{16}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+M_{2}(-Q(y_{5})+Q(y_{6})+Q(y_{13})-Q(y_{14}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+h_{1}(P(y_{9})-P(y_{10})-P(y_{11})+P(y_{12})-P(y_{13})+P(y_{14})+P(y_{15})-P(y_{16}))\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,+h_{2}(-Q(y_{9})+Q(y_{10})+Q(y_{11})-Q(y_{12})+Q(y_{13})-Q(y_{14})-Q(y_{15})+Q(y_{16}))\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{p^{6}}|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\end{split}

where the last line of (
22
) follows from the orthogonality of characters and the definition of the Roth variety.

To complete the proof, we combine (
17
), (
19
), (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
), and conclude

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/2}\Lambda_{1}^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/2}\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/4}\Lambda_{2}^{1/8}.

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/2}\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/4}\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{h}}}^{1/8}\biggl(\frac{1}{p^{6}}|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\biggr)^{1/16}.

∎

.

n_{1}
for
\Lambda_{1}
to exploit the cancellation among exponential sums since we want to avoid difficult multidimensional exponential sums with rational function phase functions.

The next proposition is the first part of simplifications of Fourier coefficients in Proposition
3.3
. Similar calculations also appear in
[
HLY21
]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be functions, define

F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})=\sum_{n_{2}}\Delta_{-\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{1}(n_{1},n_{2}),\quad F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})=\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2}),

then we have

\lVert F_{1}(n_{1},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{n_{1},\mathbf{h}}}\leq\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{4}^{2},\quad\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}\leq\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{4}^{2}.

Proof.

We would prove the estimate for
F_{2}
and the estimate for
F_{1}
follows similarly by symmetry. By definition of
F_{2}
and Fourier transform, we have

(23)

\begin{split}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})&=\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})\overline{\widehat{f}_{2}}(m_{1}+h_{1},m_{2}+h_{2})\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}f_{2}(\mathbf{x})e_{p}(-\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x})\overline{f_{2}}(\mathbf{z})e_{p}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z})e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z}).\end{split}

Summing over variable
m_{1}
to utilize the orthogonality of characters, the expression (
23
) becomes

(24)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(m_{2}(z_{2}-x_{2}))e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z}-m_{2}x_{2})\end{split}

where the last line of (
24
) follows from change of variables
x_{2}\leftrightarrow x_{2}+z_{2}
.

Combining (
23
) and (
24
), Parseval’s identity says

(25)

\lVert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}}^{2}=\sum_{m_{2},\mathbf{h}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}.

Note that

(26)

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}\biggr)\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}\biggr)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{2}\end{split}

Finally, applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the inner sum of the last line of (
26
), we have

(27)

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{2}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{2}}\bigl\lvert f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\bigr\rvert^{4}=\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{4}^{4}.

Combining (
25
), (
26
) and (
27
) would complete the proof.
∎

The next proposition is the second part of simplifications of Fourier coefficients in Proposition
3.3
. Compared to the Proposition
3.5
, its proof is more complicated and involves Gowers norms.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a
1
-bounded function and define
F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})=\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})
. Then, we have

\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}}}\leq\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})}^{2}.

Proof.

By definition of
F_{2}
and Fourier transform, we have

(28)

\begin{split}F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})&=\sum_{m_{1}}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}}\widehat{f}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})\overline{\widehat{f}_{2}}(m_{1}+h_{1},m_{2}+h_{2})\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}f_{2}(\mathbf{x})e_{p}(-\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x})\overline{f_{2}}(\mathbf{z})e_{p}(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z})e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z}).\end{split}

Summing over variable
m_{1}
to utilize the orthogonality of characters, the expression (
28
) becomes

(29)

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},z_{1},z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(m_{2}(z_{2}-x_{2}))e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1},z_{2}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z})\biggr)e_{p}(-m_{2}x_{2})\end{split}

where the last line of (
29
) follows from change of variables
x_{2}\leftrightarrow x_{2}+z_{2}
.

Combining (
28
) and (
29
), Parseval’s identity says

(30)

\begin{split}\sum_{m_{2}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z})\biggr\rvert^{2}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})e_{p}(-\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{z}^{\prime}).\end{split}

Note that the quantity we would like to upper bound for this proof has the following decomposition:

(31)

\begin{split}\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}}}^{2}&=\sum_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{h}}\Biggl(\sum_{m_{2}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{m_{2}^{\prime}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Biggr)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{h}}\Biggl(\sum_{m_{2}}\bigl\lvert F_{2}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Biggr)^{2}.\end{split}

By (
31
), (
30
) and Parseval’s identity, we have

(32)

\lVert\overline{F_{2}}(m_{2},\mathbf{h})F_{2}(m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h})\rVert_{l^{2}_{m_{2},m_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{h}}}^{2}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},\mathbf{z}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\biggr\rvert^{2}.

Note that the inner sum of (
32
) is nonnegative since

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},\mathbf{z}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2}+z_{2})\overline{\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2},\mathbf{z}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\overline{\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\biggr)\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{2}}\overline{\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\biggr)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{z_{1}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\Delta_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}f_{2}(z_{1},x_{2})\biggr\rvert^{2}\\
&\geq 0\end{split}

Nonnegativity of the inner sum of (
32
) and the
1
-boundedness of
f_{2}
allow us to deduce that

(33)

The main theorem in this section is Theorem
4.2
. The proof of Theorem
4.2
is quite technical and long, but the main idea is actually fairly intuitive and direct. The strategy is similar to the one that Hong and the author
[
HL24
]
used for the Roth variety for one dimensional progressions. We would decompose the Roth variety into smooth part and singular part. The smooth part could be dealt with complex analytic technique, while the singular part has extra constraints for free. Furthermore, we would repeat this process several times, hence we would have “smooth part of the singular part” and “singular part of the singular part” and so on. After enough decompositions, there would be sufficient constraints that only depend on a few variables, and this is very helpful for point-counting estimates.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
P(t),Q(t)
and constant function
1
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})
be the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-points of the Roth variety for corners associated to

|Y(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\ll p^{6}.

Proof.

We would break down the proof into eight steps. Here is a roadmap of the proof. The main steps are Steps
1,2,3,4,6

•

Step
1
: Bounding the generic part of the Roth variety via complex analytic techniques. The readers are recommended to glance through this step and skip the technical details.

•

Step
2
: Decomposing the Roth variety further to divide and conquer. This step is mostly the definitions and constructions.

•

Steps
3,4
: Showing that certain constraint is nontrivial. These two steps are the key calculations.

•

Step
6
: Bounding the special part of the Roth variety. This step is quite representative and the same method would also be used in Steps
5,7,8
.

•

Steps
5,7,8
: Dealing with the degenerate cases. These three steps can be skipped on a first reading.

While reading the proof, the readers are encouraged to take

as polynomials, in fact, taking

\dim_{\mathrm{Sch}}Y_{\mathrm{gen}}\times_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p}=\dim_{\mathrm{Sch}}Y_{\mathrm{gen}}\times_{\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. Hence, we have
\dim_{\mathrm{Var}}Y_{\mathrm{gen}}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=6
for all but finitely many
p
. Now, the Lang–Weil bound
[
LW54
]
(also see
[
Tao
, Lemma 1]
) implies that
|Y_{\mathrm{gen}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\ll p^{6}
. (Note that one does not need the irreducibility of the variety.)

(
As a side note, we pass to complex numbers since it is convenient to work with algebraic geometry over characteristic zero and rational functions over complex numbers. That being said, the machinery of algebraic geometry is robust enough for one to only work over finite fields. Therefore, the approach we chose is just one possible way to proceed.
)

Step
2
: Decomposing
Y_{\mathrm{sp}}

This step is mostly the definitions and constructions. In this step, we would decompose
Y_{\mathrm{sp}}
and isolate the main difficulty.

Let

R(y)=\frac{P^{\prime}(y)}{Q^{\prime}(y)}

and rewrite
J(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{10})
as

\begin{split}&\Bigl(-P^{\prime}(y_{9})Q^{\prime}(y_{10})+Q^{\prime}(y_{9})P^{\prime}(y_{10})\Bigr)\\
&\times\Bigl(Q^{\prime}(y_{1})Q^{\prime}(y_{2})Q^{\prime}(y_{3})Q^{\prime}(y_{4})Q^{\prime}(y_{5})Q^{\prime}(y_{6})Q^{\prime}(y_{7})Q^{\prime}(y_{8})\Bigr)\\
&\times\Bigl(R(y_{1})R(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})-R(y_{2})R(y_{3})R(y_{5})R(y_{8})\Bigr).\end{split}

Define

\displaystyle Z=Y\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{16})\in\mathbb{A}^{16}:R(y_{1})R(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})-R(y_{2})R(y_{3})R(y_{5})R(y_{8})=0\}

\displaystyle Y_{i}=Y\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{16})\in\mathbb{A}^{16}:Q^{\prime}(y_{i})=0\}\quad\text{for}\;1\leq i\leq 8

\displaystyle Y_{9,10}=Y\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{16})\in\mathbb{A}^{16}:-P^{\prime}(y_{9})Q^{\prime}(y_{10})+Q^{\prime}(y_{9})P^{\prime}(y_{10})=0\}.

Note that
Y_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=Z(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Y_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Y_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup...\cup Y_{8}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Y_{9,10}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. Informally, the varieties
Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{8},Y_{9,10}
are of low dimension due to the strong constraints
Q^{\prime}(y_{i})=0
for
1\leq i\leq 8
and
-P^{\prime}(y_{9})Q^{\prime}(y_{10})+Q^{\prime}(y_{9})P^{\prime}(y_{10})=0
, and they can be handled more easily later at Steps
7,8
. The main difficulty lies in the variety
Z
.

To bound the size of
Z(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, we would define an auxiliary variety
Z^{\prime}
. Let
Z^{\prime}
be the variety cut out by the following three equations in eight variables
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{8}
.

(35)

\displaystyle P(y_{1})-P(y_{2})-P(y_{3})+P(y_{4})-P(y_{5})+P(y_{6})+P(y_{7})-P(y_{8})=0

(36)

\displaystyle Q(y_{1})-Q(y_{2})-Q(y_{3})+Q(y_{4})-Q(y_{5})+Q(y_{6})+Q(y_{7})-Q(y_{8})=0

(37)

\displaystyle R(y_{1})R(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})-R(y_{2})R(y_{3})R(y_{5})R(y_{8})=0

Note that
Z^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
lives in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{8}
. The first two defining equations of
Z^{\prime}
do not come out of nowhere; the points on the Roth variety
Y
actually satisfy those two equations. To see this, we manipulate the defining equations of the Roth variety algebraically; consider adding the equations
\eqref{eqs: roth1}-\eqref{eqs: roth2}-\eqref{eqs: roth3}+\eqref{eqs: roth4}+\eqref{eqs: roth9}
and
\eqref{eqs: roth5}-\eqref{eqs: roth6}+\eqref{eqs: roth7}-\eqref{eqs: roth8}+\eqref{eqs: roth10}
. Therefore, the varieties
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are closely related: for any
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{16})\in Z(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, we have
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{8})\in Z^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

The main goal would be showing
|Z^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\ll p^{5}
. To do that, once again we decompose
Z^{\prime}
into smooth part and singular parts. Let
J_{Z^{\prime}}(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
be the determinant of the first, fourth and sixth columns of the Jacobian matrix of the defining equations of
Z^{\prime}
, that is

J_{Z^{\prime}}(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})=\begin{vmatrix}P^{\prime}(y_{1})&P^{\prime}(y_{4})&P^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})&Q^{\prime}(y_{4})&Q^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
R^{\prime}(y_{1})R(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})&R(y_{1})R^{\prime}(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})&R(y_{1})R(y_{4})R^{\prime}(y_{6})R(y_{7})\\
\end{vmatrix}.

Let
S(y)
be the logarithmic derivative of
R(y)
, i.e.,

S(y)=\frac{R^{\prime}(y)}{R(y)}

and

D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})=\begin{vmatrix}P^{\prime}(y_{1})&P^{\prime}(y_{4})&P^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})&Q^{\prime}(y_{4})&Q^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
S(y_{1})&S(y_{4})&S(y_{6})\\
\end{vmatrix}

so that we could rewrite

J_{Z^{\prime}}(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})=R(y_{1})R(y_{4})R(y_{6})R(y_{7})D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6}).

Define

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen}}=Z^{\prime}\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{8})\in\mathbb{A}^{8}:J_{Z^{\prime}}(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})\neq 0\}

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{i}=Z^{\prime}\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{8})\in\mathbb{A}^{8}:R(y_{i})=0\}\quad\text{for}\;i=1,4,6,7

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sp}}=Z^{\prime}\cap\{(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{8})\in\mathbb{A}^{8}:D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})=0\}.

Note that
Z^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p})=Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Z^{\prime}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Z^{\prime}_{4}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Z^{\prime}_{6}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Z^{\prime}_{7}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cup Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. The variety
Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen}}
is a generic part of
Z^{\prime}
that lies in the smooth component of
Z^{\prime}
. The associated complex analytic space of
Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen}}(\mathbb{C})
is a
5
-dimensional complex manifold since the Jacobian matrices have full rank and we can apply the implicit function theorem. By similar arguments to Step
1
, we could compare various dimensions and conclude that
|Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gen}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|\ll p^{5}
. The varieties
Z^{\prime}_{1},Z^{\prime}_{4},Z^{\prime}_{6},Z^{\prime}_{7}
are of low dimension due to the strong constraints
R(y_{i})=0
for
i=1,4,6,7
, and they can be handled more easily later at Step
5
. We would proceed to bound
|Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|
in Steps
3,4
.

Step 3: Showing that
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
is nonzero

In this step, we would show that
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
is a nonzero rational function, hence
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})=0
is a nontrivial constraint for
Z^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sp}}
. This step crucially relies on the assumption that
P,Q
and constant function
1
are linearly independent.

Expanding
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
with respect to the first column, we have

(38)

\begin{split}&D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})\\
&=P^{\prime}(y_{1})\begin{vmatrix}Q^{\prime}(y_{4})&Q^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
S(y_{4})&S(y_{6})\end{vmatrix}-Q^{\prime}(y_{1})\begin{vmatrix}P^{\prime}(y_{4})&P^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
S(y_{4})&S(y_{6})\end{vmatrix}+S(y_{1})\begin{vmatrix}P^{\prime}(y_{4})&P^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
Q^{\prime}(y_{4})&Q^{\prime}(y_{6})\end{vmatrix}.\end{split}

Note that

\begin{split}S(y)&=\frac{R^{\prime}(y)}{R(y)}\\
&=\frac{1}{R(y)}\biggl(\frac{P^{\prime}(y)}{Q^{\prime}(y)}\biggr)^{\prime}\\
&=\frac{Q^{\prime}(y)}{P^{\prime}(y)}\frac{Q^{\prime}(y)P^{\prime\prime}(y)-P^{\prime}(y)Q^{\prime\prime}(y)}{(Q^{\prime}(y))^{2}}\\
&=\frac{P^{\prime\prime}(y)}{P^{\prime}(y)}-\frac{Q^{\prime\prime}(y)}{Q^{\prime}(y)}\end{split}

hence
S(y)
is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of
P^{\prime}(y)
and
Q^{\prime}(y)
.

If the poles of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
are different, then
S(y_{1})
would at least have a simple pole since it is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
. Meanwhile, the poles of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
cannot be simple poles since they are derivatives of meromorphic functions. Therefore, in the expression (
38
), the poles of
S(y_{1})
could not be cancelled out by the poles of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
, and hence
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
is nonzero.

Similarly, if the zeroes of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
are different, then
S(y_{1})
would at least have a simple pole since it is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
. Once again, the poles of
S(y_{1})
could not be cancelled out by the poles of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
, and hence
D(y_{1},y_{4},y_{6})
is nonzero.

If the poles and zeroes of
P^{\prime}(y_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(y_{1})
are exactly the same, then
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
would only differ up to a scalar, and this would contradict the linear independence of
P,Q
and constant function
1
.

As an ending remark, we do have a little detail not checked yet, that is, we need to check that
\bigl\lvert\begin{smallmatrix}P^{\prime}(y_{4})&P^{\prime}(y_{6})\\
Q^{\prime}(y_{4})&Q^{\prime}(y_{6})\end{smallmatrix}\bigr\rvert

is nonzero so that it could not cancel out the poles of
S(y_{1})
. Suppose the said determinant is zero, then
P^{\prime}(y_{4})/Q^{\prime}(y_{4})=P^{\prime}(y_{6})/Q^{\prime}(y_{6})
, hence
P^{\prime}/Q^{\prime}
would be a constant and this contradicts the linear independence of
P,Q
and constant function
1
. This completes the proof of Step
3
.

LW54
]

choices

LW54
]

5.
Degree lowering

In this section, we would put everything together to prove Theorem
1.1
. In Sections
3
and
4
, we obtain Gowers norm control for counting operator for corners generated by
P(t),Q(t)
, hence we could apply the degree lowering pioneered by Peluse
[
Pel19
]
to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Actually, we need a different version of degree lowering that works for directional Gowers norms, and this version of degree lowering is developed by Kuca
[
Kuc24
]
in his work on multidimensional Szemerédi theorem in finite fields. However, we do not need full strength of Kuca’s work since we start degree lowering with
U^{2}
-norm as opposed to general
U^{s}
-norm, for this reason, and for keeping track of the power-saving exponents and the sake of completeness, we reproduce Kuca’s arguments in this section. In this section, we closely follow Kuca’s paper
[
Kuc24
, Section 6]
.

Definition 5.1

(Eigenfunctions)
.

(1)

A function
\chi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the first coordinate if there exists a subset
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
and phase functions
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p},\psi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\chi(x_{1},x_{2})=1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})x_{1})e(\psi(x_{2}))

for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
.

(2)

A function
\chi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate if there exists a subset
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
and phase functions
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p},\psi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\chi(x_{1},x_{2})=1_{E}(x_{1})e_{p}(\phi(x_{1})x_{2})e(\psi(x_{1}))

for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
.

The following proposition could be regarded as an inverse theorem for
U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)
-norm and
U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})
-norm.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a
1
-bounded function.

(1)

Suppose
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)}\geq\delta
, then there exists an eigenfunction with respect to the first coordinate
\chi
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq\delta^{4}

and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq 0
for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

(2)

Suppose
\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})}\geq\delta
, then there exists an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate
\chi
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq\delta^{4}

and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq 0
for all
x_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Proof.

The proofs of Proposition
5.2
.(1) and
5.2
.(2) are similar, hence we would only prove
5.2
.(2).

For any
x_{1}
, let
g_{x_{1}}(x_{2})=f(x_{1},x_{2})
. Note that

\begin{split}\delta^{4}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})}^{4}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},h,k}f(x_{1},x_{2}+h+k)\overline{f}(x_{1},x_{2}+h)\overline{f}(x_{1},x_{2}+k)f(x_{1},x_{2})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}\lVert g_{x_{1}}\rVert_{U^{2}}^{4}\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}\lVert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}\rVert_{l^{4}}^{4}.\end{split}

Observe that

\begin{split}\lVert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}\rVert_{l^{4}}^{4}=\sum_{\xi}\lvert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}(\xi)\rvert^{4}&\leq\lVert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{\xi}\lvert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\\
&=\lVert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert g_{x_{1}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
&\leq\lVert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}\rVert_{\infty}.\end{split}

Hence, for every
x_{1}
, there exists a frequency
\xi_{x_{1}}
such that

\delta^{4}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}\lvert\widehat{g_{x_{1}}}(\xi_{x_{1}})\rvert=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})e_{p}(-\xi_{x_{1}}x_{2})\biggr\rvert.

Choosing phase functions
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p},\psi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})e_{p}(-\xi_{x_{1}}x_{2})\biggr\rvert=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{1})x_{2})e(\psi(x_{1}))

completes the proof. (Note that in the definition of eigenfunctions, there is a characteristic function
1_{E}
for some subset
E
, here we can choose
E=\mathbb{F}_{p}
.)
∎

The following proposition is an asymptotic formula for two term progressions, which is needed for induction.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
P(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be a rational function over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(1)

For all
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\Bigr)+O\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr).

(2)

For all
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}+P(y))=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(x_{1},a)\Bigr)+O\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr).

Proof.

The proofs of Proposition
5.3
.(1) and
5.3
.(2) are similar, hence we would only prove Proposition
5.3
.(1).

Note that after fixing
x_{2}
, the counting operator reduces to the one dimensional case, and we can invoke the results
[
HL24
, Proposition 5.1]
for one dimensional progressions. Hence, we have

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\Bigl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})\Bigr)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\Bigl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})+O\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\Bigr)+O\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)\end{split}

∎

The following theorem contains the induction process for degree lowering. Essentially, we would induct on the number of the eigenfunctions in the counting operators. That being said, we choose to write the proof not in the exact format of an induction so that the notations could be less dense. Also, note that Theorem
5.4
.(3) is same as Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
P(t),Q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(t)
be rational functions such that
P(t),Q(t)
and the constant function
1
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be
1
-bounded functions.

(1)

Suppose
f_{1}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the first coordinate and
f_{2}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate, then we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O_{P,Q}\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr).

(2)

Suppose
f_{2}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate, then we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O_{P,Q}\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{640}}\bigr).

(3)

For all
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{1}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O_{P,Q}\bigl(p^{-\frac{1}{40960}}\bigr).

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.(1).

This would be the base case for the induction for Theorem
5.4
. Since
f_{1},f_{2}
are eigenfunctions, there exist subsets
E,F\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
, phase functions
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p},\alpha:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\psi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\beta:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})x_{1})e(\psi(x_{2}))

\displaystyle f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=1_{F}(x_{1})e_{p}(\alpha(x_{1})x_{2})e(\beta(x_{1}))

for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
.

Expanding the counting operator, we have

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})x_{1})e(\psi(x_{2}))1_{F}(x_{1})e_{p}(\alpha(x_{1})x_{2})e(\beta(x_{1}))\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})P(y)+\alpha(x_{1})Q(y))\end{split}

By Bombieri’s bound on single variable exponential sums in finite fields with rational function phases
[
Bom66
]
(also see
[
BC17
, Proposition 2.2]
), we have

\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})P(y)+\alpha(x_{1})Q(y))\biggr\rvert\ll p^{-1/2}

when
\phi(x_{2})\neq 0
or
\alpha(x_{1})\neq 0
.

Therefore, the counting operator is equal to

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})1_{E}(x_{2})e(\psi(x_{2}))1_{\phi(x_{2})=0}1_{F}(x_{1})e(\beta(x_{1}))1_{\alpha(x_{1})=0}+O(p^{-1/2}).

To complete the proof, note that the directional averages of eigenfunctions have a special form, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})x_{1})e(\psi(x_{2}))=1_{E}(x_{2})e(\psi(x_{2}))1_{\phi(x_{2})=0}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}1_{F}(x_{1})e_{p}(\alpha(x_{1})x_{2})e(\beta(x_{1}))=1_{F}(x_{1})e(\beta(x_{1}))1_{\alpha(x_{1})=0}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.(2).

First, we would like to de-mean
f_{2}
with respect to variable
x_{2}
by splitting
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=(f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a))+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)
. The term
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)
contributes to the main term since by Proposition
5.3
.(1), we have

\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)\Bigr)f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)\Bigr)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{1}(b,x_{2})+O(p^{-1/2})\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{1}(b,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)\Bigr)+O(p^{-1/2}).\end{split}

By replacing
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
by
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)
, we can assume the directional average of
f_{2}
with respect to
x_{2}
is zero, that is
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
. (Also, note that
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}f_{2}(x_{1},a)
is still an eigenfunction with respect to the second variable.)

Now, given
1
-bounded functions
f_{0},f_{1},f_{2}
where
f_{2}
is an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
, let

\delta=\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\biggr\rvert

be the quantity that we would like to upper bound. Define the dual function

F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{0}(x_{1}-P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1}-P(y),x_{2}+Q(y)).

Note that

\delta=\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\biggr\rvert\leq\biggl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\lvert F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}.

Expanding
\lvert F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rvert^{2}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\overline{F_{1}}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\geq\delta^{2}.

By algebraic geometry PET induction (Theorem
3.7
) and the dimension estimates for the Roth variety (Theorem
4.2
), we deduce that

\lVert F_{1}\rVert_{U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)}^{1/4}\gg\delta^{2}.

By inverse theorem for
U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)
(Proposition
5.2
), there exists an eigenfunction with respect to the first coordinate
\chi
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{32}

and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq 0
for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Let

U=\{x_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{32}/2\},

then by the pigeonhole principle, we have
\frac{|U|}{p}\gg\delta^{32}
. Hence, we deduce that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}1_{U}(x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{64},

which is

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})1_{U}(x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\gg\delta^{64}.

By induction on Theorem
5.4
.(1) for the functions
f_{0},\chi 1_{U},f_{2}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}(\chi 1_{U})(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O(p^{-1/2})\gg\delta^{64}.

Note that the desired estimate in Theorem
5.4
.(2) already holds when
p^{-1/2}\gg\delta^{64}
, since we are in the mean zero case. Thus, without loss of generality, we may eliminate the error term
O(p^{-1/2})
in the above estimate. By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in variable
x_{2}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}(\chi 1_{U})(a,x_{2})\biggr\rvert^{2}\gg\delta^{128}.

Since
\chi
is an eigenfunction with respect to the first coordinate, there exists a subset
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}
, phase functions
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p},\psi:\mathbb{F}_{p}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\chi(x_{1},x_{2})=1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})x_{1})e(\psi(x_{2}))
. Therefore, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}1_{E}(x_{2})e_{p}(\phi(x_{2})a)e(\psi(x_{2}))1_{U}(x_{2})\biggr\rvert^{2}\gg\delta^{128},

which is

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl\lvert 1_{E}(x_{2})1_{U}(x_{2})1_{\phi(x_{2})=0}\biggr\rvert^{2}\gg\delta^{128}.

By definition of
U
, we deduce that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}1_{E}(x_{2})1_{U}(x_{2})1_{\phi(x_{2})=0}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{160},

which is

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}1_{E}(x_{2})1_{U}(x_{2})1_{\phi(x_{2})=0}e(\psi(x_{2}))\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{160}.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
x_{2}
, we have successfully passed from
U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)
-norm to
U^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)
-norm and lowered the degree, i.e.,

\delta^{320}\ll\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}\biggl\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\biggr\rvert^{2}=\lVert F_{1}\rVert_{U^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)}^{2}

Finally, expanding the squares
\lvert\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1}}F_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rvert^{2}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}\overline{F_{1}}(a,x_{2})\Bigr)\gg\delta^{320},

By the asymptotic formula for two term progressions (Proposition
5.3
.(2)) for the functions
f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}\overline{F_{1}}(a,x_{2})
and
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
, we conclude

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}\Bigl(f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{a}\overline{F_{1}}(a,x_{2})\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b}f_{2}(x_{1},b)\Bigr)+O(p^{-1/2})\gg\delta^{320}

The main term is zero since we already eliminated the mean of
f_{2}
with respect to variable
x_{2}
in the beginning of the argument, hence we have
\delta^{320}\ll p^{-1/2}
which is equivalent to
\delta\ll p^{-1/640}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.(3) and Theorem
1.1
.

The proof of Theorem
5.4
.(3) is similar to the proof of Theorem
5.4
.(2), hence we would only briefly indicate the changes. Basically, we reverse the roles of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and induct on Theorem
5.4
.(2). Here is a list of the changes.

•

We would de-mean
f_{1}
with respect to
x_{1}
.

•

The dual function would be
F_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2}-Q(y))f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2}-Q(y))
.

•

We have
\lVert F_{2}\rVert_{U^{2}(0\times\mathbb{F}_{p})}
instead of
\lVert F_{1}\rVert_{U^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\times 0)}
since we would invoke Theorem
3.2
.

•

We would invoke Proposition
5.2
.(2) to get an eigenfunction with respect to the second coordinate
\chi
.

•

The definition of
U
would be
\{x_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{2}}F_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2})\gg\delta^{32}/2\}
.

•

We would induct on Theorem
5.4
.(2) instead of Theorem
5.4
.(1) for the averages

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{1},x_{2},y}f_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})f_{1}(x_{1}+P(y),x_{2})\chi(x_{1},x_{2}+Q(y))1_{U}(x_{1})\gg\delta^{64},

hence we would get an error term
O(p^{-1/640})
instead of
O(p^{-1/2})
, this would lead to the bound
\delta\ll p^{-1/(640\cdot 64)}=p^{-1/(40960)}
.

∎
