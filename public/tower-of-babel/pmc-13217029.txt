Estimating 5-year absolute risk of cardiovascular disease using routinely collected electronic medical records from Australian general practices
Authors: Nicholas I-Hsien Kuo, Sebastiano Barbieri, Clare Arnott, Blanca Gallego, Ziba Gandomkar, Shahana Ferdousi, Kirsty Douglas, Mark Woodward, Louisa Jorm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent cardiovascular risk equations from the USA and United Kingdom use routinely collected electronic medical records (EMRs), while current equations used in Australia (AusCVDRisk) have not been validated locally. We assessed the feasibility and performance of using routinely collected EMRs from Australian primary care software systems to predict absolute risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD).MethodsWe used primary care EMR data from the New South Wales Health Lumos programme, covering 680 general practices, linked with hospital and death records. Individuals aged 30–74 years on 1 January 2017 with no prior CVD history and at least one record for an anthropometric measurement or pathology test were included. Sex-specific Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate 5-year risk of a fatal or non-fatal CVD event. Predictors included demographics, smoking, chronic conditions, clinical variables and medications. Modelling used a 5×2 cross-validation approach. Discrimination, calibration and reclassification performance were assessed.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 4.91 years, 33 578 CVD events were recorded in 850 216 patients. Full models with 28 predictors had Harrell’s C of 0.803 (95% CI 0.801 to 0.804) for females and 0.772 (95% CI 0.770 to 0.773) for males. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator models with 12–15 predictors performed similarly. Models were well calibrated across age, socioeconomic and smoking strata. Geographic (internal–external) validation across 10 Primary Health Networks confirmed consistent discrimination (C-index range 0.747–0.788 for males; 0.772–0.827 for females). Percentile-based net reclassification improvement showed enhanced event detection compared with a model based on AusCVDRisk variables (event-Net Reclassification Improvement up to 0.185).ConclusionsCVD risk equations based on routine Australian primary care data performed strongly and generalised well across diverse settings. These models offer potential for automated integration into general practice software to support real-time CVD risk assessment.

Introduction

Primary prevention guidelines universally recommend the use of multivariable equations to assess the absolute risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. These risk equations are pivotal in guiding clinical decision-making, targeting preventive measures and optimising resource allocation for individuals at high risk of CVD. The AusCVDRisk1 calculator, launched in mid-2023, adapts the New Zealand (NZ) PREDICT-1 equation2 to estimate 5-year risk of first-time CVD events (fatal or non-fatal). AusCVDRisk was modified from PREDICT by applying weights based on the relative age-specific and sex-specific rates of CVD deaths (not total events) in the Australian and NZ (excluding Maori and Pasifika) populations, while retaining the hazard ratios (HRs) from PREDICT.3 4 A recent adaptation, AUS-PREDICT, used a similar recalibration approach and reported good performance when applied to a modified NZ cohort adjusted to reflect the Australian population structure.5 However, neither AusCVDRisk nor AUS-PREDICT has been validated using Australian data, and their full model coefficients remain unpublished, limiting independent evaluation. This is an important gap, given the potential differences in demography, health behaviours and incidence and case-fatality of CVD events.

In contrast, the USA released new PREVENT equations in late 2023,6 which predict 10-year and cumulative 30-year risk of fatal and non-fatal CVD events. These were derived and validated using a large, comprehensive set of electronic medical record (EMR) data as well as data from multiple cohort studies. Additionally, they incorporate novel risk biomarkers, such as the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), which are not included in traditional risk models.6

Similarly, new United Kingdom (UK) QR4 equations released in 2024 were developed using primary care EMRs.7 Like PREVENT, QR4 predicts the 10-year risk of both fatal and non-fatal events. However, rather than incorporating new biomarkers, QR4 includes a range of additional health conditions (including cancer types, learning disability and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)).

The use of EMR data offers several advantages: it allows for the inclusion of a wide range of variables, accurately reflecting the complexity of CVD risk factors and facilitates the integration of real-world, routinely collected health data, enhancing the equations’ relevance and applicability to diverse populations.7

Here, we report the feasibility and performance of assessing absolute risk of CVD using routinely collected EMRs from Australian primary care. Our primary aim was to develop new prediction models based on a broader set of variables routinely captured in general practice, with the goal of improving risk prediction tailored to the Australian population and enabling automated implementation through clinical software systems. As a secondary aim, we also evaluated the performance of a model using the same variables as AusCVDRisk, to assess how well this variable set performs when applied to Australian data. This approach has the potential to improve CVD risk prediction by tailoring to the Australian population and facilitating automation through general practice (GP) software systems.

Methods

Data source

We sourced data from the Lumos program,8 9 a partnership of the New South Wales (NSW) Ministry of Health and NSW Primary Health Networks (PHNs) made possible through the participation of General Practices across NSW. The Lumos program has created a retrospective cohort comprising all patients who attended participating general practices at least once after January 2012. Twice yearly, extracts are taken from the EMR systems of these general practices and linked by the Centre for Health Record Linkage10 to a range of other NSW Health data collections. Consumers are actively involved in the governance of Lumos through representation on the Lumos Data Governance and Stakeholder Committees.

We used general practice EMR data and linked data from the NSW Admitted Patient Data Collection (APDC)11 and Cause of Death (COD) unit record file12 from the August 2024 Lumos extract. This extract includes EMR data from January 2010 onwards for 5 564 678 patients who attended one of 680 participating NSW general practices at least once since January 2012. COD information for this extract was available up to 24 December 2021.

Study cohort

The study cohort comprised individuals aged 30–74 years on 1 January 2017 with no prior history of CVD and at least one prior EMR record for a measured clinical variable (anthropometric measurement or pathology test). This ensured that at least a minimum of EMR information was available for prediction. A complete cohort selection description is given in online supplemental A. Prior history of CVD was ascertained from chronic disease flags in general practice EMR data and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM)13 codes recorded in principal or additional diagnosis fields of APDC records (online supplemental B).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the first fatal or non-fatal CVD event, defined as either death with an ICD-10 code for CVD recorded as the underlying cause in the COD unit record file or hospitalisation with an ICD-10-AM code for CVD recorded as a principal or additional diagnosis in the APDC (online supplemental B).

Predictors

To assess risk prediction using routinely collected EMR data, predictor variables were limited to those available in the Lumos GP EMR data extract. Variables were selected a priori based on their availability in the dataset and clinical relevance, informed by established cardiovascular risk models including QR4,7 PREVENT6 and PREDICT.2

Predictors were ascertained as at 1 January 2017 and included age, area-based socioeconomic deprivation (national quintile of Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD)14), smoking status, chronic conditions (including diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, atrial fibrillation (AF), asthma, COPD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), depression, serious mental illness and other mental illness, and most recently recorded values for clinical variables (body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), ratio of total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TC/HDL), eGFR, urine albumin to creatinine ratio (UACR), triglycerides, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)) and prior use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering or antithrombotic medications (online supplemental C). Chronic conditions were identified using flags derived from structured diagnosis or problem list fields in GP EMRs, based on coded or free-text entries routinely maintained by GPs and harmonised across clinical information systems.

Individuals with missing smoking status were classified as non-smokers. This approach was informed by evidence that smoking status in primary care data is typically not missing at random, with current smokers generally well documented and missing data most likely representing non-smokers or long-term ex-smokers.15 To assess the impact of this assumption on model performance, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using random forest-based imputation as an alternative strategy (full details are in online supplemental D).

Missing values for the IRSD were imputed using the most frequent value within the patient’s PHN. Clinical (continuous) variables were preprocessed with Box-Cox transformation,16 rescaled and imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE).17 Imputation was performed in an ‘event-aware’ manner to account for variable differences across patient risk profiles. Imputation of height and weight incorporated external age-specific medians from the Australian Bureau of Statistics National Health Survey18 and missing clinical data itself, often indicative of health status,19 20 was also considered in the MICE process. Full details of handling of missing values and imputation are given in online supplemental D.

The age variable was transformed by subtracting 30 (the minimum age) from all values. This transformation allows for straightforward interpretation of model coefficients, representing the change associated with each additional year beyond 30. Additionally, we explored incorporating non-linear splines and knots21 for eGFR, urine albumin, HbA1c, BMI, SBP and lipid measures, given their known or potential non-linear associations with diabetes and CKD.22 However, across multiple exploratory models, these non-linear transformations provided minimal improvements in predictive performance, and in many cases introduced convergence issues. Accordingly, we treated continuous predictors as linear terms in the final models. Furthermore, we incorporated clinically plausible interaction terms among our variables.

Statistical analysis

We used sex-specific Cox proportional hazards23 models to estimate the 5-year risk of a fatal or non-fatal CVD event. We followed individuals from 1 January 2017 until the earliest of the following: a hospital admission or death due to CVD, death from other causes or the end of the observation period on 31 December 2021. We built three main models for each sex: a model incorporating all potential predictors (‘Full’), a parsimonious model utilising the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (‘LASSO’)24 (details are provided in online supplemental E).

As the full regression coefficients for the original AusCVDRisk model are not publicly available, we refitted a model using the same variable set (excluding ‘Moderate-severe chronic kidney disease’ and ‘Familial hypercholesterolaemia’, which were unavailable in our data). This ‘Refitted AusCVDRisk’ model enabled direct comparison with our Full and LASSO models within our Australian dataset.

The analysis was performed using Lifelines,25 a Python26 library designed for survival analysis, which supports the fitting and evaluation of Cox proportional hazards models. We adopted a 5×2 cross-validation approach27 throughout our modelling process. For final HR calculations, models were fitted using the complete dataset.

Model performance evaluation

We compared the performance of the Full, LASSO and Refitted AusCVDRisk models by evaluating their discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C28 (an estimate of the probability that two randomly selected subjects will have correctly ordered risk predictions); and calibration was assessed using calibration plots and calibration slopes (details in online supplemental F).

Reclassification analysis

We assessed improvement in individual risk ranking over the Refitted AusCVDRisk model using two Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) approaches. First, a percentile-based NRI analysis quantified how well the new models reclassify event and non-event cases, without relying on fixed risk thresholds.29 This method captures improvements in rank ordering across the risk spectrum and is less sensitive to threshold effects. We computed NRI across multiple percentile binning schemes (5, 10 and 20 quantiles) (details in online supplemental G).

Second, we performed a threshold-based NRI analysis using risk cut-offs defined by current Australian CVD prevention guidelines.1 These guidelines recommend a 5-year CVD risk threshold of <5% (low risk), 5%–<10% (intermediate risk), and ≥10% (high risk) to inform the intensity of preventive interventions.1 We calculated NRI based on upward and downward reclassification across these thresholds. Details of both NRI approaches are presented in online supplemental G.

Geographic validation

To evaluate our Full and LASSO models’ generalisability to new settings and populations, we conducted a geographic, (or ‘internal–external’30), validation. This was performed by partitioning the data into geographic clusters based on PHNs, then iteratively holding out one PHN as the test set while training the model on the remaining PHNs. This process was repeated across each of the 10 NSW PHNs (details in online supplemental H).

Results

In both females and males, most of the traditional risk factors included in the AusCVDRisk calculator, including age, smoking, IRSD, AF, CKD, diabetes, SBP, TC/HDL, anti-hypertensives and antithrombotics had significantly elevated HRs in both the Full and Refitted AusCVDRisk models. Significant predictors in the Full model for females that are not used in the AusCVDRisk calculator included depression (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.20), other mental health conditions (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.19), asthma (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.28), COPD (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.55 to 1.81), cancer (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.15), osteoporosis (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.19), HbA1c (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.44), BMI (HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.03) and triglycerides (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.08), while eGFR (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 0.99), alanine transaminase (ALT) (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 0.99) and haemoglobin (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98 to 0.99) had significant protective effects.

Significant predictors in the Full model for males were similar to those for females and included depression (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.21), COPD (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.32 to 1.51), HbA1c (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.44), BMI (HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.02) and triglycerides (HR 1.01, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.02), with ALT (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 0.99) and haemoglobin (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 0.99) having significant protective effects. Unlike females, the HRs associated with asthma (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.12), other mental health conditions (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.10) and eGFR (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.00) did not attain significance, while serious mental illness (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.26) and liver disease were associated with a significantly increased risk (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.23).

The LASSO models for both males and females retained six of the 11 variables used in the AusCVD risk calculator: age, smoking, AF, SBP, TC/HDL and antihypertensives. In contrast, the AusCVD risk variables IRSD, CKD, diabetes and lipid-modifying agents were dropped from the LASSO models. In both females and males, additional variables retained in the LASSO models included depression, COPD, HbA1c, BMI and eGFR, while other mental health conditions, coeliac disease and triglycerides were included in the female LASSO model only.

Reclassification analysis

Online supplemental tables G1 and G2 present the results of the percentile-based NRI analysis. The Full model consistently demonstrated positive total NRI values across all binning resolutions, with the highest improvement observed in the male cohort using 20 bins (event-NRI=0.185). While there was a small negative NRI for non-events (e.g., –0.077), this was offset by improved identification of true cases. The LASSO model showed smaller but still positive NRI values. Results were similar in the female cohort.

Online supplemental tables G3–G7 present the results of the threshold-based NRI analysis.

For males, the Full model reclassified 3.6% of those who went on to experience a CVD event into a higher risk category (event-NRI=0.036), and 3.7% of those who did not experience an event into a lower category (non-event NRI=0.037), resulting in a total NRI of 0.073. The LASSO model showed smaller NRI values.

For females, the Full model demonstrated the largest gains, reclassifying 7.1% of event cases into a higher risk category (event NRI=0.071) and 1.8% net of non-events into a lower category (non-event NRI=0.018), for a total NRI of 0.089. The LASSO model achieved more modest improvements.

Geographic validation

Online supplemental tables H1 and H2 show Harrell’s C and calibration error across all 10 NSW PHNs. For males, the Full model C-index ranged from 0.747 to 0.788, comparable to 0.772 in the main analysis. The LASSO model showed a similar range (0.743–0.783), relative to 0.765. For females, the Full model C-index ranged from 0.772 to 0.827, vs 0.803 in the main analysis. The LASSO model ranged from 0.764 to 0.820, aligning with the overall 0.797. The lowest Harrell’s C values (PHN G) were observed in a socioeconomically disadvantaged rural PHN contributing <5% of patients, while the highest (PHN A) occurred in a less disadvantaged metropolitan PHN contributing >25% of patients (online supplemental tables H3–H7).

Calibration error for Full and LASSO models for males ranged from 0.0311 to 0.1550 and 0.0134 to 0.134, respectively. Comparable ranges of Full and LASSO models for females were 0.0220 to 0.228 and 0.0043 to 0.236. PHN B exhibited the largest calibration error across all models; this was a low-disadvantage metropolitan PHN contributing <5% of all patients to the dataset (online supplemental tables H3–H7).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility and performance of CVD risk prediction models developed using routinely collected EMRs from Australian primary care. Both a Full Model including a comprehensive range of EMR-derived predictors, and a parsimonious model developed using regularisation and automated variable selection (LASSO) showed superior discrimination and similar calibration compared with a model fitted using only the variables included in the AusCVDRisk calculator1 derived from the PREDICT study of NZ.2 The inclusion of a wider range of traditional and non-traditional risk factors in our models aligns with contemporary approaches like the PREVENT and QR4 equations recently developed in the US and UK, respectively.6 7

Our Full Model demonstrated strong discrimination, with Harrel’s C of 0.803 (SD 0.0015) for females and 0.772 (SD 0.0014) for males. While direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in underlying populations, predictor sets and outcome definitions, our results are broadly comparable to those reported for the PREVENT total CVD model (0.789, SD 0.011 for females; and 0.747, SD 0.020 for males)6 and appear to outperform both PREDICT (0.73, SD 0.01 for both sexes)2 and AUS-PREDICT (0.75, SD 0.005 for females; and 0.74 SD, 0.003 for males).5 Although QR4 reported higher C values (eg, for England, 0.864 SD 0.002 for females; and 0.849 SD 0.002 for males),6 the broader age range (18–84 years) likely inflated its discrimination, capturing more low-risk and high-risk individuals. Additionally, the UK primary care datasets used to develop QR4 are subjected to comprehensive validation and quality checks31 and have been extensively used for research, with improvements in data quality driven by incentives such as the Quality and Outcomes Framework.32

Our models diverge from PREDICT, from which AusCVDRisk is derived, by incorporating a broader range of clinical variables. AusCVDRisk collects information on HbA1c, eGFR, UACR and BMI exclusively for individuals with diabetes. In contrast, our models identified eGFR, BMI and HbA1c as significant CVD predictors in a general population. This wider applicability aligns with PREVENT, which includes eGFR in its total CVD model but excludes BMI and treats HbA1c as an optional predictor. We also expanded the range of chronic conditions considered, while PREVENT includes only diabetes. Our models are further distinguished by incorporating COPD, asthma and depression as key predictors of CVD risk, which were not fully explored in other models like QR4.

Comparatively, the QR4 model includes a narrower set of clinical variables, focusing on SBP and TC/HDL ratio and introduces a wider range of health conditions such as cancer types, learning disabilities and pre-eclampsia, many of which are not available in our dataset. Like QR4, we found COPD to be significantly associated with CVD risk, particularly among females. We also identified asthma as female-specific predictor—possibly reflecting both biological factors and diagnostic misclassification, as COPD is often recorded as asthma in older adults.33 Unlike QR4 which only examined postnatal depression, our models identified depression as a significant predictor of CVD for both sexes.

Our LASSO models incorporating automated variable selection prioritised measured clinical variables (HbA1C, eGFR) over the corresponding chronic disease flags (diabetes, CKD), suggesting that these measured clinical values had better predictive value than the binary flags. Furthermore, the LASSO models retained measured SBP, TC/HDL and triglycerides, while of the three medication classes included, only antihypertensives were retained. This again suggests that measured clinical values may be particularly powerful predictors, while a binary flag indicating that medications have been prescribed conflates the underlying condition with its management.

Our percentile-based NRI analysis provided further evidence that our EMR-derived models more accurately rank individuals by future CVD risk than the AusCVDRisk variable set. The Full and LASSO models consistently improved the identification of true cases, with only a modest increase in false positives. These results highlight improved individual-level risk stratification—particularly relevant in real-world settings, where risk-based decision-making increasingly often extends beyond fixed thresholds.

Our threshold-based NRI analysis indicates that the Full model can meaningfully improve clinical classification at decision-relevant thresholds, particularly in female patients. Specifically, it would have identified an additional 7% of females and 4% of males who went on to experience cardiovascular events as being at higher risk—potentially leading to earlier or more intensive intervention under current guidelines. The reclassification of non-event cases into lower risk categories also suggests a potential reduction in overtreatment, especially in males. However, we note that threshold-based NRI is sensitive to the choice of cut-points and may exaggerate the clinical value of small changes near boundaries.

To assess generalisability, we conducted geographic (‘internal-external’) validation using PHNs as natural clusters, a recommended approach in the prediction modelling.30 In the absence of a suitable external Australian datasets with linked outcomes, this method provides a robust alternative. Both Full and LASSO models maintained good discrimination across PHNs, with C-indices similar to 5×2 cross-validation. Slightly lower performance and higher calibration error were seen in low-volume or socioeconomically disadvantaged PHNs, suggesting the need for ongoing monitoring and possible recalibration in under-represented settings.

By incorporating a broader range of biomarkers and health conditions, our models offer greater relevance to Australian clinical data compared with the AusCVDRisk calculator, which is adapted from NZ equations. Importantly, our models were developed using predictors routinely captured in general practice software, enabling the potential for automated, real-time risk prediction directly within clinical information systems.

To enable a transparent and scientifically robust comparison, we also refitted a model using the same predictor variables as the AusCVDRisk calculator on our Australian real-world dataset. This provided a meaningful benchmark optimised to local data distributions and recording practices. Unlike a simple recalibration of the original AusCVDRisk equations, our refitting approach allowed for full re-estimation of predictor effects, which is likely to yield at least equivalent—if not superior—performance in this context. Additionally, it supports full transparency through publication of model coefficients, facilitating reproducibility, independent validation and potential clinical adaptation.

This creates two primary use cases for future implementation. First, the models could act as prescreening tools, flagging patients who may benefit from formal assessment through Medicare-funded Heart Health checks.34 Second, with further validation and clinical consultation, the models could ultimately support direct risk estimation in practice, offering performance comparable to existing calculators without the need for manual data entry.

While AusCVDRisk supports reclassification based on additional factors—such as family history and ethnicity—our models already include several of these elements, including depression, other mental health conditions and eGFR. Inclusion of eGFR allows for more granular risk estimation rather than binary reclassification based on thresholds. This may offer greater flexibility and precision—particularly for patients whose eGFR values fall above but near the high-risk cut-off. A reclassification step could still be incorporated in future deployments to refine decision-making for specific patient groups.

Although data quality challenges persist, our approach leverages information already present in EMRs and does not rely on clinician-initiated risk calculation. Even if risk scores could only be calculated automatically for one-third of patients, as in our Lumos sample, this would represent a substantial advance over current practice. For instance, fewer than 10% of patients aged 45–74 years currently have a recorded CVD risk score in their general practice record.35

Moreover, digital health initiatives—such as the growing adoption of clinical decision support tools, standardised templates and quality improvement programmes—are expected to improve data completeness over time. These trends suggest that our models are well-positioned for broader implementation as EMR quality improves.

Limitations

The focus on 5-year risk only limits comparability with models such as PREVENT and QR4, which predict 10-year risk. However, our study’s focus on 5-year risk may offer more immediate clinical utility for patients, compared with longer term predictions.

Our cohort was limited to individuals aged 30–74 years, consistent with Australian guidelines for absolute CVD risk assessment at the time of extraction. Validation in older adults (75+ years) is important given Australia’s ageing population but presents challenges due to increased competing mortality risks and changes in the predictive value of some risk factors. Future work should explore model performance and utility in this age group.

Importantly, our dataset lacked ethnicity information, a key predictor in both PREDICT and QR4, and did not include family history of CVD, which is also used in these models. Data quality also posed challenges, particularly for variables like BMI and smoking status, which had high missingness due to selective recording in general practice. This informative missingness complicates imputation and model implementation. We addressed this by including comissingness indicators to retain predictive signal, but real-time use may still be affected. Future models could focus on more consistently recorded variables or explicitly model missingness.

Our study used data from NSW, which accounts for approximately one-third of Australia’s population and is broadly representative in terms of age distribution, chronic disease prevalence and patterns of general practice use and medication dispensing.18 36 However, differences in healthcare access and behaviours across states warrant further validation to support broader implementation.

Conclusion

The performance of our cardiovascular risk equations developed using routinely collected EMR data from Australian general practices compares favourably with the other contemporary equations including PREDICT and PREVENT. Since our equations were developed using data collected through routine Australian general practice clinical software, they are potentially suitable for incorporation into this software to automate estimation of absolute cardiovascular risk. Automation of risk prediction obviates the requirement to specifically enter risk factor data, and thus presents the potential to identify patients-at-risk who might otherwise be missed and prompt early initiation of preventive interventions.
