Excess photon-assisted noise of Majorana and Andreev bound states
Qiang Lin, Ying-Xin Liang, Ke He, Zhan Cao

Abstract
Photon-assisted tunneling arises under an ac bias, with the drive frequency setting the photon energy. The excess photon-assisted noise is defined as the difference between the shot noise under a combined dc and ac bias and that under a dc bias alone. We investigate this quantity in tunneling into Majorana or Andreev bound states, which are of great interest in the search for topological superconductors. Under a harmonic bias V ⁡ ( t ) = V dc ​ [ 1 − cos ⁡ ( Ω ​ t ) ] V(t)=V_{\mathrm{dc}}[1-\cos(\Omega t)] , the excess photon-assisted noise exhibits distinct behaviors: for Majorana or quasi-Majorana bound states, it undergoes multiple sign reversals as V dc V_{\mathrm{dc}} increases and vanishes at nonzero integer values of e ​ V dc / Ω eV_{\mathrm{dc}}/\Omega (with e e the elementary charge), whereas for zero-energy Andreev bound states—particularly those producing nearly quantized zero-bias conductance peaks—it remains strictly negative over the entire V dc V_{\mathrm{dc}} range.



I
Introduction

Majorana bound states (MBSs)
Read and Green 2000
;
Kitaev 2001
, predicted to appear at the boundaries or vortex cores of topological superconductors
Alicea 2012
;
Sato and Fujimoto 2016
, are promising for realizing topological quantum computation
Kitaev 2003
;
Nayak et al. 2008
;
Das Sarma et al. 2015
. An isolated MBS resides at zero energy as an equal-weight electron–hole superposition, enabling resonant Andreev reflection
Law et al. 2009
and thereby producing a quantized zero-bias conductance peak (ZBCP)
Law et al. 2009
;
Flensberg 2010
;
Wimmer et al. 2011
. Owing to their topological protection, MBS-induced ZBCPs are expected to remain robust against variations in experimental parameters, manifesting as stable quantized conductance plateaus.

Among the various candidates for topological superconductor
Mandal et al. 2023
, iron-based superconductors
Chen et al. 2019
;
Zhu et al. 2020
and semiconductor–superconductor hybrid nanowires
Wang et al. 2022
have exhibited nearly quantized conductance plateaus.
Numerical simulations, however, indicate that such plateaus may also arise from zero-energy Andreev bound states (ABSs)
Das Sarma and Pan 2021
;
Pan et al. 2021
or quasi-MBSs (QMBSs)
Moore et al. 2018a
;
Vuik et al. 2019
; the latter reproduce local features of MBSs but lack intrinsic topological protection
Vuik et al. 2019
. These topologically trivial states typically originate from disorder
Liu et al. 2012
;
Ahn et al. 2021
;
Das Sarma et al. 2023
;
Pan and Das Sarma 2020
or spatially varying physical parameters
Kells et al. 2012
;
Fleckenstein et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Moore et al. 2018b
;
Cao et al. 2019
;
Stanescu and Tewari 2019
;
Pan and Das Sarma 2020
in realistic hybrid nanowires. To achieve an unambiguous identification of MBSs in hybrid nanowires, the “topological gap protocol” has been proposed
Pikulin et al. 2021
, wherein electrodes are attached to opposite ends of a grounded nanowire to measure both local and nonlocal conductances. This approach tests whether ZBCPs appear simultaneously in the local conductances following a bulk gap closing and reopening observed in the nonlocal conductance—signatures expected at a topological superconducting transition
Lutchyn et al. 2010
;
Oreg et al. 2010
. While a recent experiment on InAs–Al hybrid nanowires
Aghaee et al. 2023
reported consistency with this protocol, the data quality was deemed insufficient for a definitive identification of MBSs
Kouwenhoven 2025
. Moreover, the robustness and reliability of this protocol remain controversial
Hess et al. 2023
;
Das Sarma and Pan 2024
;
Hess et al. 2024
;
Legg 2025
.

In superconducting vortex systems expected to host MBSs, such as topological insulator–superconductor heterostructures
Fu and Kane 2008
and iron-based superconductors
Wang et al. 2015
, nonlocal transport measurements remain unrealized despite several proposed protocols
Bolech and Demler 2007
;
Sbierski et al. 2022
, likely due to experimental challenges. Instead, various STM–based local probes have been employed in these systems. For example, spin-polarized STM tips
Sun et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2021
probe MBS-induced spin-dependent tunneling
He et al. 2014
, while superconducting tips provide tunneling spectroscopy with enhanced energy resolution compared to metallic tips
Ge et al. 2023
.

Superconducting tips have also been used for shot noise measurements in iron-based superconductors
Ge et al. 2023
. The extracted Fano factor approaches unity in the large-bias, low-temperature limit, consistent with theoretical predictions for MBSs
Perrin et al. 2021
. Subsequent theoretical work
Cao et al. 2023a
suggested, however, that zero-energy ABSs can exhibit the same feature. Differential shot noise has also been proposed as an identifier to distinguish zero-energy ABSs from MBSs and QMBSs, as they produce distinct noise signatures near zero bias
Cao et al. 2023a
;
Wong et al. 2022
. Yet, low-bias shot noise, which scales with the bias, can be obscured when significant background noise is present
Liang 2022
, thereby limiting the practical applicability of this protocol.

In this work, we investigate photon-assisted shot noise in tunneling into MBSs, QMBSs, or zero-energy ABSs under a time-dependent bias. Applying an ac bias at frequency
\Omega
allows charge carriers to absorb or emit photons of energy
\hbar\Omega
(hereafter we set
\hbar=1
), a process known as photon-assisted transport
Platero and Aguado 2004
. Photon-assisted shot noise has previously been observed in coherent normal systems
Schoelkopf et al. 1998
;
Reydellet et al. 2003
;
Gasse et al. 2013
;
Dubois et al. 2013a
;
Gabelli and Reulet 2013
as well as in diffusive normal-metal–superconductor junctions
Kozhevnikov et al. 2000
, where it typically displays features at a sequence of dc bias values separated by
\Omega
or
\Omega/2
. It is therefore natural to expect that zero-energy bound states should likewise generate distinct photon-assisted shot noise signatures at large bias, where the influence of background and/or thermal noise is substantially reduced. In practice, we specifically focus on the
excess photon-assisted noise
, defined as

S^{\textrm{exc}}(V_{\textrm{dc}})=S^{\textrm{dc+ac}}(V_{\textrm{dc}})-S^{\textrm{dc}}(V_{\textrm{dc}}),

(1)

where
S^{\textrm{dc+ac}}
and
S^{\textrm{dc}}
denote the shot noise under a combined dc and ac bias and under a dc bias alone, respectively, with
V_{\textrm{dc}}
the dc bias voltage. Excess photon-assisted noise has proven to be a powerful probe in mesoscopic physics. In normal tunnel junctions
Dubois et al. 2013a
;
Gabelli and Reulet 2013
, it has been employed to detect single-electron excitations above the Fermi sea without accompanying electron–hole pairs. For periodic Lorentzian voltage pulses of frequency
\Omega
, such excitations yield
S^{\textrm{exc}}=0
at zero temperature when
eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega
equals nonzero integers
Keeling et al. 2006
;
Dubois et al. 2013b
. In normal-metal–superconductor junctions dominated by Andreev tunneling,
S^{\textrm{exc}}=0
is predicted when
eV_{\mathrm{dc}}/\Omega
equals half-integers
Belzig and Vanevic 2016
;
Bertin-Johannet et al. 2022
, reflecting the effective charge
2e
transferred in Andreev processes.

Here, we focus on photon-assisted Andreev tunneling mediated by the aforementioned zero-energy states in the parameter regime
k_{B}T\lesssim\Gamma\ll\Omega
, where
k_{B}T
denotes the thermal energy and
\Gamma
the tunneling strength. Under a harmonic bias
V(t)=V_{\mathrm{dc}}[1-\cos(\Omega t)]
, we find that the excess noise
S^{\mathrm{exc}}
associated with MBSs or QMBSs exhibits multiple sign reversals with increasing
V_{\textrm{dc}}
and vanishes at nonzero integer values of
eV_{\mathrm{dc}}/\Omega
. By contrast, for zero-energy ABSs, particularly those yielding nearly quantized ZBCPs,
S^{\mathrm{exc}}
remains strictly negative over the entire bias range. These results are supported by both effective-model analyses and numerical simulations of semiconductor–superconductor hybrid nanowires.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces the effective model Hamiltonian, scattering matrix, transport formulas, and analytical results. Section
III
presents numerical results of the effective model together with simulations of hybrid nanowires. A summary and discussion are provided in Sec.
IV
, and technical details are included in the Appendices.

II
Model and formalism

II.1
Effective Hamiltonian

We consider a tunneling model described by the effective Hamiltonian
H=H_{P}+H_{B}+H_{T}
with

\displaystyle H_{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\sigma}[\varepsilon_{k}+eV(t)]c_{k\sigma}^{{\dagger}}c_{k\sigma},

(2)

\displaystyle H_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\varepsilon_{B}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},

(3)

\displaystyle H_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{mk\sigma}(\lambda_{m\sigma}c_{k\sigma}^{\dagger}-\lambda^{\ast}_{m\sigma}c_{k\sigma})\gamma_{m}.

(4)

Here,
H_{P}
describes a metallic probe, such as an electrode or STM tip with dispersion
\varepsilon_{k}
. The fermionic operator
c_{k\sigma}
(
c_{k\sigma}^{\dagger}
) annihilates (creates) an electron with momentum
k
and spin
\sigma=\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
. A time-dependent bias
V(t)
is applied to the probe
Jauho et al. 1994
;
Maciejko et al. 2006
. The term
H_{B}
describes an ABS at energy
\varepsilon_{B}
deep inside the superconducting gap. It is expressed in terms of two hybridized Majorana operators,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, which are self-conjugate (
\gamma_{m}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{m}
) and obey the anticommutation relation
\{\gamma_{m},\gamma_{n}\}=\delta_{mn}
. Equivalently, the ABS Hamiltonian can be written as
\varepsilon_{B}c_{B}^{\dagger}c_{B}
, where the conventional complex fermion operator
c_{B}
is related to
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
via
c_{B}=(\gamma_{1}+i\gamma_{2})/\sqrt{2}
.

H_{T}
describes the tunnel couplings between the probe and the two Majoranas. The tunneling amplitudes can be parameterized as
Cao et al. 2023a
;
Prada et al. 2017

\displaystyle\lambda_{1\uparrow}=\lambda_{1}\sin(\theta_{1}/2),\qquad\lambda_{1\downarrow}=-\lambda_{1}\cos(\theta_{1}/2),

(5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2\uparrow}=-i\lambda_{2}\sin(\theta_{2}/2),\qquad\lambda_{2\downarrow}=-i\lambda_{2}\cos(\theta_{2}/2),

where
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
accounts for the spin degree of freedom of the ABS. A schematic representation of this effective tunneling model is shown in Fig.
1
(a). While this model neglects Bogoliubov quasiparticles outside the superconducting gap, it captures subgap transport faithfully and allows for transparent analytical insights.

Since the probe is unpolarized, the spin quantization axis can be rotated such that
\gamma_{1}
couples exclusively to the spin-down channel. We therefore set
\theta_{1}=0
and retain
\theta_{2}
as a free parameter. This choice is consistent with the finding that only the average angle
\theta=(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2
enters the expressions of current and shot noise under a dc bias
Cao et al. 2023a
. For later convenience, we define the tunneling strengths
\Gamma_{1,2}=2\pi\rho\lambda_{1,2}^{2}
, where
\rho
is the probe density of states at the Fermi level. Without loss of generality, we take
\Gamma_{2}\leq\Gamma_{1}
and introduce
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}
together with a ratio

r=\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma.

(6)

As elucidated below, zero-energy ABSs are characterized by
r>0
, whereas both MBSs and QMBSs are characterized by
r=0
.

To connect the effective model with a representative platform, we consider an electrode tunnel-coupled to one end of a semiconductor-superconductor hybrid nanowire, which can host ABSs, MBSs, or QMBSs. All three may occur at zero energy, but they are distinguished by the properties of their wave functions in the Majorana basis. As illustrated in Fig.
1
(b), the two Majorana components of an ABS strongly overlap, whereas in an MBS or QMBS they are spatially separated. QMBSs mimic MBSs near one end of the wire, which motivates their designation; however, unlike MBSs, their wave functions expressed in the electron-hole basis typically exhibit unequal electron and hole weights in the tail extending toward the opposite end
Cao et al. 2022a
.
When the probe couples to an ABS, both Majorana components
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
couple to the probe with finite strengths
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, yielding
r>0
. By contrast, when the probe couples to an MBS or QMBS
\gamma_{1}
, its partner
\gamma_{2}
is far from the probe and has negligible wave-function weight at the probed end of the wire, leading to
\Gamma_{2}=0
and thus
r=0
. Distinguishing MBSs from QMBSs requires an additional probe at the opposite end of the wire to determine whether
\gamma_{2}
resides there
Moore et al. 2018b
. Such a two-probe configuration lies beyond the scope of the present work. The parameter
\theta
accounts for the spinor structure of the wave functions, which is not depicted in Fig.
1
(b). Since the local probe under consideration cannot distinguish between MBSs and QMBSs, we use the notation “MBSs/QMBSs” to denote either case hereafter.

II.2
Scattering matrix

Our main findings of excess photon-assisted noise are associated with a distinctive property of the scattering matrix associated with MBSs/QMBSs and zero-energy ABSs, as elaborated below. The scattering matrix takes the general form

\textbf{s}(E)=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{s}^{ee}(E)&\textbf{s}^{eh}(E)\\
\textbf{s}^{he}(E)&\textbf{s}^{hh}(E)\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where the submatrix
\textbf{s}^{\alpha\beta}(E)
contains the scattering amplitudes of the processes where a
\beta
-type carrier with energy
E
injected from the probe is reflected as an
\alpha
-type carrier. For later use, we define
\textbf{T}^{\alpha\beta}(E)\equiv\textbf{s}^{\alpha\beta{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\beta}(E)
. The explicit expression of the scattering matrix of our effective model is presented in Appendix
A
.

To set the stage, we briefly review the dc transport properties of the effective model. As analyzed in Ref.
Cao et al. 2023a
, at zero temperature, the differential conductance
G
and differential shot noise
P
are given by

\displaystyle G(V_{\textrm{dc}})=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\sum_{s=\pm}\tau_{s}(eV_{\textrm{dc}}),

(8)

\displaystyle P(V_{\textrm{dc}})=\frac{8e^{3}}{h}\sum_{s=\pm}\tau_{s}(eV_{\textrm{dc}})[1-\tau_{s}(eV_{\textrm{dc}})],

(9)

where the transmission coefficients
\tau_{\pm}(E)
correspond to the eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix
\textbf{T}^{he}(E)
. In what follows, we take
\varepsilon_{B}=0
to focus on zero-energy ABSs and MBSs/QMBSs. In this case,

\displaystyle\tau_{\pm}(E)=\frac{\Lambda\pm|E|(\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2})\Pi}{2(E^{2}+\Gamma_{1}^{2})(E^{2}+\Gamma_{2}^{2})},

(10)

with

\displaystyle\Lambda=E^{2}(\Gamma_{1}^{2}+\Gamma_{2}^{2}-2\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}\cos^{2}\theta)+2\Gamma_{1}^{2}\Gamma_{2}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta,

(11)

\displaystyle\Pi=\sqrt{E^{2}(\Gamma_{1}^{2}+\Gamma_{2}^{2}-2\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}\cos 2\theta)+(\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}\sin 2\theta)^{2}}.

(12)

Substituting Eq. (
10
) into Eq. (
8
) yields the zero-temperature, zero-bias conductance,

G(0)=\frac{4e^{2}}{h}\sin^{2}\theta.

(13)

The dependence of
G(0)
on
(r,\theta)
is shown in Fig.
2
(a). Qualitatively, ABSs can yield nearly quantized ZBCPs over a broad and continuous parameter range. As shown in Ref.
Cao et al. 2023a
, finite temperature introduces an
r
dependence in
G(0)
and enlarges the parameter space supporting nearly quantized ZBCPs. Quantitatively, both MBSs/QMBSs and ABSs with
\theta=0.25\pi
produce quantized ZBCPs, but they arise from distinct transmission probabilities:
\tau_{+}(0)=1
for MBSs/QMBSs, and
\tau_{+}(0)=\tau_{-}(0)=1/2
for ABSs. According to Eq. (
9
),
P(V)
exhibits a zero-bias dip in the former case and a zero-bias peak in the latter, thereby providing a diagnostic to distinguish between MBSs/QMBSs and zero-energy ABSs. However, because the shot noise of a zero-energy state scales with the dc bias amplitude, observing this low-bias feature is experimentally challenging if significant background noise exists in certain setups
Liang 2022
;
not
.

(a) Zero-temperature, zero-bias conductance
G(0)
(in units of
2e^{2}/h
) and (b)
\Delta Y

Now we turn to elucidate how our main findings of excess photon-assisted noise are connected to the scattering matrix. To this end, we define two integrals

\displaystyle Y_{1}=\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,\tau_{s}^{2}(E),

(14)

\displaystyle Y_{2}=\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,\tau_{s}(E)\,[1-\tau_{s}(E)],

(15)

and their difference

\Delta Y\equiv Y_{1}-Y_{2}.

(16)

Using the expressions of
\tau_{\pm}(E)
from Eq. (
10
), one finds
Y_{1}=\pi\Gamma(1-F)^{2}/2
and
Y_{2}=\pi\Gamma(1-F^{2})/2
, with
F=4r(1-r)\cos^{2}\theta
, leading to

\Delta Y=-\pi\Gamma F(1-F).

(17)

The
(r,\theta)
dependence of
\Delta Y
is shown in Fig.
2
(b). Notably, (i)
\Delta Y=0
for MBSs/QMBSs and (ii)
\Delta Y\simeq 0
for zero-energy ABSs in both the upper and lower-right regions. These ABSs can nevertheless be readily distinguished by their zero-bias conductance, which approaches either
0
or
4e^{2}/h
, as shown in Fig.
2
(a). Taken together, Figs.
2
(a) and
2
(b) demonstrate that
\Delta Y
provides a criterion to differentiate the ABSs with nearly quantized ZBCPs from MBSs/QMBSs.

In Sec.
II.4
, we establish an analytical connection between
\Delta Y
and the excess noise
S^{\textrm{exc}}
, defined in Eq. (
1
), at zero temperature. In particular,
\Delta Y=0
implies that
S^{\textrm{exc}}
vanishes at nonzero integer values of
eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega
, independent of the functional form of the ac bias. For a harmonic drive
V(t)=V_{\textrm{dc}}[1-\cos(\Omega t)]
, this prediction is confirmed in Sec.
III
by numerical calculations of the full
S^{\textrm{exc}}(eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega)
dependence, which further highlight a sharp distinction between MBSs/QMBSs and zero-energy ABSs with nearly quantized ZBCPs: while
S^{\textrm{exc}}
for MBSs/QMBSs undergoes multiple sign reversals as
V_{\textrm{dc}}
increases, it remains strictly negative for zero-energy ABSs over the entire
V_{\textrm{dc}}
range.

II.3
Photon-assisted current and current-noise

We consider a time-dependent bias composed of dc and ac components,

V(t)=V_{\textrm{dc}}+V_{\textrm{ac}}(t)
,
where
V_{\textrm{ac}}(t)=V_{\textrm{ac}}(t+\mathcal{T})
and averages to zero over a period
\mathcal{T}
,

\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\,V_{\textrm{ac}}(t)=0
.
The associated frequency
\Omega=2\pi/\mathcal{T}
sets the photon energy quantum.

The scattering theory of photon-assisted transport in normal systems was established in Ref.
Pedersen and Büttiker 1998
.
Within this framework, we derive the photon-assisted current and current noise for the superconducting model introduced in Sec.
II.1
, as detailed in Appendices
B
and
C
. The two experimental observables, period-averaged current
J
and zero-frequency current noise
S
, are formally expressed as

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{h}\sum_{m}|p_{m,e}|^{2}\int dE\,\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{T}^{he}(E)]

(18)

\displaystyle\times\big[f_{e}(E-m\Omega)-f_{h}(E+m\Omega)\big],

and
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
, with

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\textrm{Re}\sum_{mm^{\prime}n}p_{n,e}^{\ast}p_{m,e}p_{m+m^{\prime},e}^{\ast}p_{n+m^{\prime},e}\int dE

(19)

\displaystyle\times K_{1,m^{\prime}}(E)\Big\{f_{e}(E-n\Omega)\,[1-f_{e}(E-m\Omega)]

\displaystyle+f_{e}(E-m\Omega)\,[1-f_{e}(E-n\Omega)]\Big\},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\textrm{Re}\sum_{mm^{\prime}n}p_{n,e}^{\ast}p_{m,h}p_{m+m^{\prime},h}^{\ast}p_{n+m^{\prime},e}\int dE

(20)

\displaystyle\times K_{2,m^{\prime}}(E)\Big\{f_{e}(E-n\Omega)\,[1-f_{h}(E-m\Omega)]

\displaystyle+f_{h}(E-m\Omega)\,[1-f_{e}(E-n\Omega)]\Big\},

where

\displaystyle K_{1,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{T}^{he}(E)\textbf{T}^{he}(E+m^{\prime}\Omega)],

(21)

\displaystyle K_{2,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{s}^{he{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{hh}(E)\textbf{s}^{hh{\dagger}}(E+m^{\prime}\Omega)\textbf{s}^{he}(E+m^{\prime}\Omega)],

and
f_{\alpha}(E)=1/\big[e^{(E-\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)eV_{\textrm{dc}})/k_{B}T}+1\big]
is the Fermi distribution of
\alpha
-type carriers in the reservoir. Photon-assisted transport processes manifest themselves through the appearance of
\Omega
in the energy arguments.

II.4
Analytical insights

We are now in a position to demonstrate that the quantity
\Delta Y
, defined in Eq. (
16
), is accessible from the excess noise
S^{\textrm{exc}}
at nonzero integer values of
eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega
. In Fig.
3
, we show a set of curves of the functions
K_{1,m^{\prime}}
and
K_{2,m^{\prime}}
appearing in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) for various values of
m^{\prime}
and
\Gamma/\Omega
. These curves exhibit characteristic peaks around
E/\Omega=-m^{\prime}
, with the peak heights decreasing as
|m^{\prime}|
increases. As the reduced tunneling strength
\Gamma/\Omega
increases, the peaks broaden and the peak heights for
m^{\prime}\neq 0
tend to approach those for
m^{\prime}=0
. Consequently, for
\Gamma/\Omega\ll 1
, it is reasonable to retain only the
m^{\prime}=0
contribution in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
). At zero temperature, this yields

\displaystyle S_{1}=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\sum_{mn}X_{m,n}\bigg|\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{(m+v)\Omega}^{(n+v)\Omega}dE\,\tau_{s}^{2}(E)\bigg|,

(23)

\displaystyle S_{2}=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\sum_{mn}X_{m,n}\bigg|\sum_{s=\pm}\int_{-(m+v)\Omega}^{(n+v)\Omega}dE\,\tau_{s}(E)[1-\tau_{s}(E)]\bigg|,

(24)

where
v=eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega
and
X_{m,n}=|p_{m,e}p_{n,e}|^{2}
. Importantly, when
v
is an integer
q
, it can be eliminated from the integration bounds via the substitutions
m\to m-q
and
n\to n-q
. Furthermore, for
\Gamma/\Omega\ll 1
, the integrands in Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
) decay rapidly with
|E|
, allowing the integration bounds to be safely extended to
\pm\infty
or
0
. As a result, the integrals defined in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) enter the expressions for
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively.

Analytically, for
eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega=q\neq 0
, we obtain (see Appendix
E
for details)

\displaystyle S^{\textrm{exc}}=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\Big(\kappa\,\Delta Y-|p_{-q,e}|^{4}\,\overline{Y}\Big),

(25)

where
\kappa=\sum_{mn<0}X_{m-q,n-q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{mn=0}X_{m-q,n-q}
and
\overline{Y}=(Y_{1}+Y_{2})/2
.
Evidently,
S^{\textrm{exc}}
scales linearly with
\Delta Y
up to a small fourth-order correction, offering a direct experimental test of whether
\Delta Y=0

III.2
Simulations of hybrid nanowires

The results in the preceding section are obtained from a platform-independent effective model. To verify their validity, we calculate the excess noise of MBSs and zero-energy ABSs in semiconductor–superconductor hybrid nanowires, which constitute one of the most promising platforms for topological quantum computation
Plugge et al. 2017
;
Karzig et al. 2017
. The hybrid nanowire can be modeled by the one-dimensional Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian
Lutchyn et al. 2010
;
Oreg et al. 2010

H_{\textrm{wire}}=\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\Psi^{{\dagger}}(x){\cal H}\Psi(x)
,
where
\Psi^{{\dagger}}=(\psi_{\uparrow}^{\dagger},\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger},\psi_{\downarrow},-\psi_{\uparrow})
and

{\cal H}=\left[-\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2m^{\ast}}-\mu+V_{\textrm{pot}}(x)+i\alpha\sigma_{y}\partial_{x}\right]\tau_{z}+V_{Z}\sigma_{x}+\Delta(x)\tau_{x}
.
Here
L
,
m^{\ast}
,
\mu
,
V_{\textrm{pot}}
,
\alpha
,
V_{Z}
, and
\Delta
denote the wire length, effective electron mass, chemical potential, electrostatic potential, Rashba spin–orbit coupling, Zeeman energy, and proximity-induced superconducting pairing potential, respectively, in the semiconductor. The Pauli matrices
\sigma_{x,y,z}
(
\tau_{x,y,z}
) act in spin (electron–hole) space. In realistic tunneling spectroscopy experiments, a short portion of the wire adjacent to the electrode serves as the tunnel junction controlled by a nearby gate. Consequently, the electrostatic potential and superconducting pairing potential near the junction region may differ from those farther away, depending sensitively on device details. These nonuniformities are captured by the position dependence of
\Delta(x)
and
V_{\textrm{pot}}(x)

Liu et al. 2017
;
Fleckenstein et al. 2018
.

In numerical simulations, we use the parameters
m^{\ast}=0.026\,m_{e}
,
\alpha=30\,\text{meV\,nm}
, and
L=2\,\mu
m, typical for InAs–Al hybrid nanowires
Lutchyn et al. 2018
. For an isolated nanowire, diagonalization of
H_{\textrm{wire}}
on a lattice with spacing
a=10
nm yields the energy spectrum and wave functions. When the left end of the wire is attached to an electrode, we compute the scattering matrix to evaluate the differential conductance and current noise, as outlined in Sec.
II
. We set the hopping amplitude between the electrode and the leftmost lattice site of the wire to
\lambda=\hbar^{2}/2m^{\ast}a^{2}
, with electrode density of states
\rho=0.01/\lambda
. As will be shown later, these parameters yield ZBCP widths of
\Gamma\simeq 0.01
meV. To satisfy the condition
\Gamma/\Omega\ll 1

\Omega=0.05
meV (
\sim
12 GHz), within the experimental range in photon-assisted noise measurements
Schoelkopf et al. 1998
;
Reydellet et al. 2003
;
Gasse et al. 2013
;
Dubois et al. 2013a
;
Gabelli and Reulet 2013
;
Kozhevnikov et al. 2000
. The dc bias amplitude is further restricted to values well below the superconducting gap, ensuring that quasiparticles outside the gap can be neglected and the formulas in Sec.
II.3
remain valid. Notably, in recently developed PbTe–Pb hybrid nanowires
Cao et al. 2022b
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023

IV
Summary and discussions

We have investigated photon-assisted shot noise in tunneling into zero-energy ABSs or MBSs/QMBSs under a combined dc and ac bias, providing insights that complement earlier theoretical studies
Golub and Horovitz 2011
;
Perrin et al. 2021
;
Cao et al. 2023a
;
Wong et al. 2022
restricted to dc bias. The ac drive enables photon-assisted transport processes, with frequency
\Omega
(in the GHz range) setting the photon energy. We assume the energy hierarchy
k_{B}T\lesssim\Gamma\ll\Omega
and focus on the regime where Andreev tunneling dominates while quasiparticle tunneling is suppressed. Within an effective tunneling model, we analytically demonstrate that the excess photon-assisted noise
S^{\mathrm{exc}}
of MBSs/QMBSs vanishes at nonzero integer values of
eV_{\mathrm{dc}}/\Omega
, independent of the specific form of the ac drive. For a harmonic bias
V(t)=V_{\mathrm{dc}}[1-\cos(\Omega t)]
, numerical calculations confirm this prediction and further reveal that
S^{\mathrm{exc}}
exhibits multiple sign reversals as
V_{\mathrm{dc}}
increases. By contrast, for zero-energy ABSs—particularly those producing nearly quantized ZBCPs—
S^{\mathrm{exc}}
remains strictly negative over the entire
V_{\textrm{dc}}
range. These results are further corroborated by numerical simulations of semiconductor–superconductor hybrid nanowires hosting either MBSs or zero-energy ABSs.

Since our predictions for excess photon-assisted noise rely solely on a single local probe, they can, in principle, be tested on any platform where tunneling conductance has been measured using an electrode or an STM tip. We emphasize, however, that local measurements of tunneling conductance and excess photon-assisted noise cannot unambiguously distinguish QMBSs from MBSs, particularly when QMBSs exhibit robustness against variations in experimental parameters. Nevertheless, such measurements provide a practical means of discriminating trivial zero-energy ABSs—typically arising from disorder or spatial inhomogeneity—from MBSs/QMBSs. This capability enables a meaningful assessment of platform quality by ruling out zero-energy ABSs. In sufficiently clean systems with advanced nanofabrication, unambiguous identification of MBSs can then be pursued through complementary measurement schemes in more sophisticated device architectures
Pikulin et al. 2021
;
Bolech and Demler 2007
;
Nilsson et al. 2008
;
Akhmerov et al. 2011
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Valentini et al. 2022
;
Sbierski et al. 2022
;
Smirnov 2022
;
Cao et al. 2023b
;
Dmytruk and Trif 2023
;
Ren et al. 2024
.

Appendix A
Scattering matrix

The scattering matrix of the effective tunneling model under consideration can be obtained by the Mahaux–Weidenmüller formula
Mahaux and Weidenmüller 1969

\textbf{s}(E)=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{s}^{ee}(E)&\textbf{s}^{eh}(E)\\
\textbf{s}^{he}(E)&\textbf{s}^{hh}(E)\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{I}-2\pi i\,\textbf{W}^{{\dagger}}\textbf{G}^{r}(E)\textbf{W},

(26)

where
I
is the identity matrix,
\textbf{G}^{r}(E)
is the dressed retarded Green’s function of the bound state, and
W
describes the coupling between the probe and the bound state. Explicitly, they are given by

\displaystyle\textbf{G}^{r}(E)=\big(E-\textbf{H}_{B}+i\pi\textbf{W}\textbf{W}^{{\dagger}}\big)^{-1},

(27)

\displaystyle\textbf{H}_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&i\varepsilon_{B}\\
-i\varepsilon_{B}&0\end{bmatrix},

(28)

\displaystyle\textbf{W}=\sqrt{\rho}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1\uparrow}^{\ast}&\lambda_{1\downarrow}^{\ast}&-\lambda_{1\uparrow}&-\lambda_{1\downarrow}\\
\lambda_{2\uparrow}^{\ast}&\lambda_{2\downarrow}^{\ast}&-\lambda_{2\uparrow}&-\lambda_{2\downarrow}\end{bmatrix}.

(29)

The unitarity of the scattering matrix,
\textbf{s}^{\dagger}(E)\textbf{s}(E)=\textbf{I}
, leads to the following relationships for the submatrices in Eq. (
26
):

\displaystyle\textbf{s}^{\alpha\alpha{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\alpha}(E)+\textbf{s}^{\bar{\alpha}\alpha{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\bar{\alpha}\alpha}(E)=\textbf{I},

(30)

\displaystyle\textbf{s}^{\alpha\alpha{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(E)=-\textbf{s}^{\bar{\alpha}\alpha{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\bar{\alpha}\bar{\alpha}}(E).

(31)

Similarly, considering
\textbf{s}(E)\textbf{s}^{\dagger}(E)=\textbf{I}
, one obtains

\textbf{s}^{\alpha\alpha}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\alpha{\dagger}}(E)+\textbf{s}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}{\dagger}}(E)=\textbf{I}.

(32)

Combining Eqs. (
30
) and (
32
) yields the trace identities

\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\textbf{I}-\textbf{T}^{\alpha\alpha}(E)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\textbf{T}^{\bar{\alpha}\alpha}(E)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\textbf{T}^{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(E)\right].

(33)

In the Nambu basis
\{\hat{a}_{e\uparrow},\hat{a}_{e\downarrow},\hat{a}_{h\uparrow},\hat{a}_{h\downarrow}\}^{T}
, the intrinsic particle–hole symmetry of the BdG Hamiltonian is expressed as
\Xi H_{\textrm{BdG}}=-H_{\textrm{BdG}}\Xi
, with
\Xi=\tau_{x}K
, where
K
denotes complex conjugation and
\tau_{x}
is the first Pauli matrix in the electron–hole space. Consequently, the scattering matrix satisfies
\Xi\textbf{s}(E)=\textbf{s}(-E)\Xi
, which implies

\displaystyle\textbf{s}^{\alpha\beta}(E)=\textbf{s}^{\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}\ast}(-E),

(34)

\displaystyle\textbf{T}^{\alpha\beta}(E)=\textbf{T}^{\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}\ast}(-E).

(35)

These relations are subsequently employed in Appendices
B
and
C
to derive the expressions for the current and current noise presented in the main text.

Appendix B
Derivation of period-averaged current

In the presence of an ac bias, an electron (
e
) or hole (
h
) emitted from the reservoir connected to the probe acquires an additional time-dependent phase while propagating toward the scattering interface between the probe and the bound state. On top of its plane-wave form
\exp[i(kx-Et)]
, the carrier picks up a phase

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}(t)=\exp\!\left[-i\,\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\,e\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,V_{\textrm{ac}}(t^{\prime})\right],

(36)

with
\alpha=\{e,h\}
,
\operatorname{sgn}(e)=1
, and
\operatorname{sgn}(h)=-1
. Since
V_{\textrm{ac}}(t)
averages to zero over one period
\mathcal{T}
,
\Phi_{\alpha}(t)
is periodic and can be expanded in a Fourier series,
\Phi_{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{m}p_{m,\alpha}\exp[-im\Omega t]
. The wave function then takes the form
\sum_{m}p_{m,\alpha}\exp\{i[kx-(E+m\Omega)t]\}
, representing a superposition of plane waves with shifted energies. Each term corresponds to a process in which a carrier with energy
E
absorbs (
m>0
) or emits (
m<0
)
|m|
photons, with
p_{m,\alpha}
denoting the associated probability amplitudes. This leads to a relation

\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}(E)=\sum_{m}p_{m,\alpha}\,\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\prime}(E-m\Omega),

(37)

where
\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}
and
\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\prime}
denote annihilation operators for incoming
\alpha
-type carriers with spin
\sigma
near the scattering interface and in the reservoir, respectively.

Tunneling between the probe and the bound state is governed by the scattering matrix in Eq. (
26
), which relates incoming and outgoing states,

\hat{b}_{\alpha\sigma}(E)=\sum_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\textbf{s}^{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(E)\,\hat{a}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(E),

(38)

where
\hat{b}_{\alpha\sigma}
annihilates an outgoing
\alpha
-type carrier with spin
\sigma
near the scattering interface.

The difference in occupation number between incoming and outgoing carriers gives the charge current operator

\displaystyle\hat{J}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2h}\sum_{\alpha\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\int dE\int dE^{\prime}\,e^{i(E-E^{\prime})t}

(39)

\displaystyle\times\big[\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}^{{\dagger}}(E)\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}(E^{\prime})-\hat{b}_{\alpha\sigma}^{{\dagger}}(E)\hat{b}_{\alpha\sigma}(E^{\prime})\big],

which forms the basis for deriving two experimental observables: the period-averaged current
J
and zero-frequency current noise
S
.

Using Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
),
\hat{J}(t)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{J}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2h}\sum_{\alpha}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\int dE\int dE^{\prime}\sum_{\gamma\varsigma n}\sum_{\beta\chi m}e^{i(E-E^{\prime})t}p_{n,\gamma}^{\ast}

(40)

\displaystyle\hskip-32.72049pt\times p_{m,\beta}\hat{a}_{\gamma\varsigma}^{\prime{\dagger}}(E-n\Omega)\hat{a}_{\beta\chi}^{\prime}(E^{\prime}-m\Omega)\big[\textbf{A}_{\gamma\beta}(\alpha,E,E^{\prime})\big]_{\varsigma\chi},

where

\textbf{A}_{\gamma\beta}(\alpha,E,E^{\prime})=\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\textbf{I}-\textbf{s}^{\alpha\gamma{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\alpha\beta}(E^{\prime}).

(41)

For brevity, we denote
\textbf{A}_{\gamma\beta}(\alpha,E,E)
as
\textbf{A}_{\gamma\beta}(\alpha,E)
below.

We now consider the period-averaged current:

J=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\,\langle\hat{J}(t)\rangle.

(42)

Using Eq. (
40
), the statistical average of
\hat{J}(t)
reduces to the evaluation of the reservoir state averages:

\displaystyle\quad\big\langle\hat{a}_{\gamma\varsigma}^{\prime{\dagger}}(E-n\Omega)\hat{a}_{\beta\chi}^{\prime}(E^{\prime}-m\Omega)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{\gamma\beta}\delta_{\varsigma\chi}\delta(E-n\Omega-E^{\prime}+m\Omega)f_{\gamma}(E-n\Omega),

(43)

where
f_{\gamma}(E)=1/\big[e^{(E-\operatorname{sgn}(\gamma)eV_{\textrm{dc}})/k_{B}T}+1\big]
is the Fermi distribution function of the
\gamma
-type carrier in the probe. Substituting Eqs. (
40
) and (
43
) into Eq. (
42
) gives

J=\frac{e}{2h}\sum_{\alpha\gamma m}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)|p_{m,\gamma}|^{2}\int dE\,\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\alpha,E)]f_{\gamma}(E-m\Omega).

(44)

Performing the explicit sum over
\alpha
and
\gamma
in Eq. (
44
) and using Eq. (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle J=\frac{e}{h}\sum_{m}\int dE\,\operatorname{Tr}

\displaystyle[\textbf{T}^{he}(E)]\big[|p_{m,e}|^{2}f_{e}(E-m\Omega)

(45)

\displaystyle-|p_{m,h}|^{2}f_{h}(E-m\Omega)\big].

Finally, making use of the relation
p_{m,h}=p^{\ast}_{-m,e}
, Eq. (
45
) immediately reduces to Eq. (
18
).

Appendix C
Derivation of period-averaged zero-frequency current noise

The time-symmetric current-current correlation operator is defined as

\hat{S}(t_{1},t_{2})=\Delta\hat{J}(t_{1})\Delta\hat{J}(t_{2})+\Delta\hat{J}(t_{2})\Delta\hat{J}(t_{1}),

(46)

with
\Delta\hat{J}(t)=\hat{J}(t)-\langle\hat{J}(t)\rangle
. This symmetrization ensures that
\hat{S}(t_{1},t_{2})
is Hermitian. The statistical average of
\hat{S}(t_{1},t_{2})
is

S(t_{1},t_{2})=\langle\{\hat{J}(t_{1}),\hat{J}(t_{2})\}\rangle-2\langle\hat{J}(t_{1})\rangle\langle\hat{J}(t_{2})\rangle,

(47)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denoting the anticommutator.
Inserting Eq. (
40
) into Eq. (
47
) and making use of Wick’s theorem
Haug and Jauho 2008
, we find

\displaystyle S(t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{4h^{2}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\sum_{\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}n_{2}}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha_{1})\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha_{2})p_{n_{1},\gamma_{1}}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},\delta_{1}}p_{n_{2},\delta_{1}}^{\ast}p_{m_{2},\gamma_{1}}\int dE_{1}\int dE_{1}^{\prime}e^{i(E_{1}-E_{1}^{\prime})(t_{1}-t_{2})}

(48)

\displaystyle\times e^{i(n_{1}+n_{2}-m_{1}-m_{2})\Omega t_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{A}_{\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}}(\alpha_{1},E_{1},E_{1}^{\prime})\textbf{A}_{\delta_{1}\gamma_{1}}(\alpha_{2},E_{1}^{\prime}+(n_{2}-m_{1})\Omega,E_{1}+(m_{2}-n_{1})\Omega)]

\displaystyle\times\big\{f_{\gamma_{1}}(E_{1}-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\delta_{1}}(E_{1}^{\prime}-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{\delta_{1}}(E_{1}^{\prime}-m_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\gamma_{1}}(E_{1}-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\},

where we have employed the statistical average in Eq. (
43
).

The period-averaged zero-frequency noise power
S
is defined as
Pedersen and Büttiker 1998

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\int d\tau S(t+\tau,t).

(49)

Substituting Eq. (
48
) into Eq. (
49
) and making use of the integral relationships

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,e^{i(n_{1}+n_{2}-m_{1}-m_{2})\Omega t}=\delta(n_{1}+n_{2}-m_{1}-m_{2}),\quad\int d\tau\,e^{i(E_{1}-E_{1}^{\prime})\tau}=2\pi\delta(E_{1}-E_{1}^{\prime}),

(50)

we obtain

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{4h}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}}\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)p_{n_{1},\gamma}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},\delta}p_{m_{1}+m_{2},\delta}^{\ast}p_{m_{2}+n_{1},\gamma}\int dE\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{A}_{\gamma\delta}(\alpha,E)\textbf{A}_{\delta\gamma}(\beta,E+m_{2}\Omega)]

(51)

\displaystyle\times\big\{f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\delta}(E-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{\delta}(E-m_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\}.

Summing over
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
yields
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
with

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{4h}\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}}p_{n_{1},\gamma}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},\gamma}p_{m_{1}+m_{2},\gamma}^{\ast}p_{m_{2}+n_{1},\gamma}\int dE\big\{f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\gamma}(E-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{\gamma}(E-m_{1}\Omega)

(52)

\displaystyle\qquad\times[1-f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\}\operatorname{Tr}[(\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\gamma,E)-\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\bar{\gamma},E))(\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\gamma,E+m_{2}\Omega)-\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\bar{\gamma},E+m_{2}\Omega))],

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{4h}\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}}p_{n_{1},\gamma}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},\bar{\gamma}}p_{m_{1}+m_{2},\bar{\gamma}}^{\ast}p_{m_{2}+n_{1},\gamma}\int dE\big\{f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{\bar{\gamma}}(E-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{\bar{\gamma}}(E-m_{1}\Omega)

(53)

\displaystyle\qquad\times[1-f_{\gamma}(E-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\}\operatorname{Tr}[(\textbf{A}_{\gamma\bar{\gamma}}(\gamma,E)-\textbf{A}_{\gamma\bar{\gamma}}(\bar{\gamma},E))(\textbf{A}_{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(\gamma,E+m_{2}\Omega)-\textbf{A}_{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(\bar{\gamma},E+m_{2}\Omega))].

The operators under the trace can be expressed explicitly making use of Eqs. (
30
), (
31
), and (
41
):

\displaystyle\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\gamma,E)-\textbf{A}_{\gamma\gamma}(\bar{\gamma},E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\textbf{T}^{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(E),

(54)

\displaystyle\textbf{A}_{\gamma\bar{\gamma}}(\gamma,E)-\textbf{A}_{\gamma\bar{\gamma}}(\bar{\gamma},E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\textbf{s}^{\bar{\gamma}\gamma{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\bar{\gamma}\bar{\gamma}}(E),

(55)

\displaystyle\textbf{A}_{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(\gamma,E)-\textbf{A}_{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(\bar{\gamma},E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\textbf{s}^{\bar{\gamma}\bar{\gamma}{\dagger}}(E)\textbf{s}^{\bar{\gamma}\gamma}(E).

(56)

Substituting Eq. (
54
) into Eq. (
52
) and summing over
\gamma
leads to
S_{1}=S_{1a}+S_{1b}
with

\displaystyle S_{1a}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{h}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}}p_{n_{1},e}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},e}p_{m_{1}+m_{2},e}^{\ast}p_{m_{2}+n_{1},e}\int dE\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{T}^{he}(E)\textbf{T}^{he}(E+m_{2}\Omega)]

(57)

\displaystyle\times\big\{f_{e}(E-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{e}(E-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{e}(E-m_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{e}(E-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\},

\displaystyle S_{1b}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{h}\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}n_{1}}p_{n_{1},h}^{\ast}p_{m_{1},h}p_{m_{1}+m_{2},h}^{\ast}p_{m_{2}+n_{1},h}\int dE\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{T}^{eh}(E)\textbf{T}^{eh}(E+m_{2}\Omega)]

(58)

\displaystyle\times\big\{f_{h}(E-n_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{h}(E-m_{1}\Omega)]+f_{h}(E-m_{1}\Omega)[1-f_{h}(E-n_{1}\Omega)]\big\}.

Using the relations
p_{m,\alpha}^{\ast}=p_{-m,\bar{\alpha}}
,

Appendix D
Excess photon-assisted noise of a normal metal-superconductor junction

Our expressions Eqs. (
18
)–(
20
) are applicable to a normal metal-superconductor tunnel junction in the subgap transport regime where the bias amplitude is well below the superconducting gap. In the absence of subgap bound states, the scattering matrices are energy-independent, such that
\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{T}^{he}\textbf{T}^{he}]=2\tau_{A}^{2}
and
\operatorname{Tr}[\textbf{s}^{he{\dagger}}\textbf{s}^{hh}\textbf{s}^{hh{\dagger}}\textbf{s}^{he}]=2\tau_{A}(1-\tau_{A})
, where
\tau_{A}
denotes the Andreev reflection probability. We then obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092ptJ=\frac{4e}{h}\tau_{A}\sum_{n}|p_{n,e}|^{2}(eV_{\textrm{dc}}+n\Omega)=\frac{4e^{2}}{h}\tau_{A}V_{\textrm{dc}},

(59)

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092ptS_{1}=\frac{8e^{2}}{h}\tau_{A}^{2}k_{B}T,

(60)

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092ptS_{2}=\frac{4e^{2}}{h}\tau_{A}(1-\tau_{A})\sum_{n}P_{n}(2eV_{\textrm{dc}}+n\Omega)\coth\frac{2eV_{\textrm{dc}}+n\Omega}{2k_{B}T},

(61)

where
P_{n}=\Big|\sum_{m}p_{n-m,e}(v)p_{m,e}(v)\Big|^{2}=|p_{n,e}(2v)|^{2}
. The resulting current
J
and current noise
S\equiv S_{1}+S_{2}
coincide with the expressions obtained via the Keldysh Green’s function formalism
Bertin-Johannet et al. 2022
.

In the zero-temperature limit, from Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
) we obtain the excess noise, defined in Eq. (
1
), as

S^{\textrm{exc}}=\frac{4e^{2}}{h}\tau_{A}(1-\tau_{A})\bigg[\sum_{n}P_{n}|2eV_{\textrm{dc}}+n\Omega|-|2eV_{\textrm{dc}}|\bigg],

(62)

which was previously derived within the framework of full counting statistics
Belzig and Vanevic 2016
. When
eV_{\textrm{dc}}/\Omega
equals a half-integer, the excess noise exhibits a local minimum for periodic harmonic or square pulses, while it is fully suppressed (i.e.,
S^{\textrm{exc}}=0
) for periodic Lorentzian pulses
Bertin-Johannet et al. 2022
, reflecting the effective charge
2e
of Cooper pairs.
