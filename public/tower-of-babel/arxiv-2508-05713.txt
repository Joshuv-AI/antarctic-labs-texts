Dynamical systems with bounded condition and C ∗ C^{*} -algebras
Takehiko Mori

Abstract
By introducing the totally uniqueness condition for maps, we establish a one-to-one correspondence between the family of invariant sets for the q ​ x + d qx{+}d -function (e.g. the 3 ​ x + 1 3x{+}1 -map, also known as the Collatz map), where q q and d d are arbitrary positive odd numbers, and the family of reducing subspaces for the associated C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. This extends the connection between the Collatz conjecture (also known as the 3 ​ n + 1 3n{+}1 -problem) and the irreducibility of its associated C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. We also introduce homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded conditions that preserve the structures of these dynamical systems. We prove the existence of an isomorphism between their associated C ∗ C^{*} -algebras is proven for every isomorphism between dynamical systems with bounded condition.



Introduction

The interaction between dynamical systems and operator theory is an active and long-standing area of study.
In particular, a great deal of interesting work has been carried out on linking dynamical systems to the theory of
C^{*}
-algebras.
A
C^{*}
-algebra is a
*
-algebra consisting of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space that is given the topology of the operator norm.
In their pioneering work
[
6
]
, T. Giordano, I. F. Putnam and C. F. Skau proved that two minimal homeomorphisms on the Cantor set are strongly orbit equivalent if and only if their associated crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras are isomorphic.
Giordano, H. Matui, Putnam and Skau later generalized this result to minimal
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-actions in
[
5
]
.
M. Boyle and J. Tomiyama have studied the relationship between orbit equivalence for topologically free homeomorphisms on compact Hausdorff spaces and their associated crossed product
C^{*}
-algebras (see
[
2
]
).

Another important example is the Cuntz-Krieger algebra, which is a universal
C^{*}
-algebra that arises from a topological Markov shift.
A universal
C^{*}
-algebra is defined by its generators and relations.
The Cuntz algebra, denoted by
\mathcal{O}_{k}
, is a well-known example of a universal
C^{*}
-algebra.
It is determined by a set of isometries that satisfy the Cuntz relation, which can be described as follows. Further details about universal
C^{*}
-algebras can be found in
[
1
, Section 1]
, for example.

Definition 0.1

(
[
3
]
, The Cuntz algebras
\mathcal{O}_{k}
)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}
, the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{k}
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by isometries
S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k}
which satisfy

S_{1}S_{1}^{*}+S_{2}S_{2}^{*}+\cdots+S_{k}S_{k}^{*}=I.

(1)

In
[
3
]
, J. Cuntz shows that a
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a set of isometries that satisfy the Cuntz relation (
1
) is isomorphic to the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{k}
and simple as a
C^{*}
-algebra.
Building on this, Cuntz and W. Krieger discuss the connection between the properties of dynamical systems and their associated
C^{*}
-algebras in
[
4
]
.
These
C^{*}
-algebras are known as the Cuntz-Krieger algebras.
In
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, K. Matsumoto introduced the
C^{*}
-algebras associated with general subshifts and computed their
K
-groups.
In
[
10
]
, Matsumoto and Matui classified topological Markov shifts up to continuous orbit equivalence based on the concept of the Cuntz-Krieger algebras.

For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the set of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
A subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is said to be a reducing subspace for a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
when
T\mathcal{M},T^{*}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
T\in\mathcal{S}
.
In this paper, we will only refer to a subspace as ‘reducing’ if it is a topologically closed.
A reducing subspace is called trivial if it is either
\{0\}
or
\mathcal{H}
.
For a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is irreducible when there are no non-trivial reducing subspaces for
\mathcal{A}
.

In
[
11
]
, we presented a method of formulating the Collatz conjecture in terms of dynamical systems and operator theory.
The Collatz conjecture, also known as the
3n{+}1
-problem, the Syracuse problem, Hasse’s algorithm, Kakutani’s problem or Ulam’s problem, concerns positive integers and is named after L. Collatz, who first described it in an informal lecture at the International Mathematical Congress in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1950
[
7
]
.
The Collatz map
f
is defined for positive integers by setting
f(n)
equal to
3n{+}1
when
n
is odd and to
n/2
when
n
is even.
The conjecture states that, starting from any positive integer
n
, some iteration of
f
will eventually yield the value
1
.
We constructed
C^{*}
-algebras associated with dynamical systems induced by maps satisfying the bounded conditions and obtained the following result regarding the Collatz conjecture.

Theorem 0.2

(
[
11
, Theorem 4.2.7]
)
.

Let
f
be the Collatz map and
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
be the associated
C^{*}
-algebra. The following are equivalent:

(i)

C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
is irreducible;

(ii)

The Collatz conjecture holds.

Theorem 0.3

(
[
11
, Proposition 4.3.4, Theorem 4.3.10]
)
.

Let
f
be the Collatz map,
N_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\equiv 1,5\pmod{6}\}
, and
N_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\equiv 4,16\pmod{18}\}
.
If
P
is the first-return map for
f
on
N_{1}\cup N_{2}
and
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
is the associated
C^{*}
-algebra, then
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
is isomorphic to the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{2}
and the following are equivalent:

(i)

C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
is irreducible;

(ii)

The Collatz conjecture holds.

Building on the above study, this paper establishes new connections between dynamical systems and
C^{*}
-algebras.
By introducing the totally uniqueness condition for maps, we demonstrate a one-to-one correspondence between the family of invariant sets for the
qx{+}d
-function, where
q
and
d
are arbitrary positive odd numbers, and the family of reducing subspaces for the associated
C^{*}
-algebra (Proposition
3.4
, Theorem
3.7
, and Corollary
3.8
).
This extends the connection between the Collatz conjecture and the irreducibility of the associated
C^{*}
-algebra.
We also introduce homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition, proving that isomorphisms between such dynamical systems induce isomorphisms between their associated
C^{*}
-algebras (Theorem
4.12
).

The paper is organized as follows.
Section 1 provides the concept of invariant sets in dynamical systems by maps, bounded condition for maps, and theorems revealed in previous studies.
Section 2 defines the uniqueness condition for maps.
It is then shown that the tuple of parities of a period for the Collatz map is aperiodic.
Section 3 discusses the correspondence between the families of invariant sets for a map satisfying the bounded condition and the associated
C^{*}
-algebra’s reducing subspaces.
Assuming the totally uniqueness condition for the map implies that the former correspondence is one-to-one.
Section 4 defines homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition and proves that every isomorphism between such dynamical systems induces an isomorphism between their associated
C^{*}
-algebras.

The cardinality of any set

1
Dynamical systems with bounded condition and associated
C^{*}
-algebras

In this section, we will provide the definitions of invariant sets in dynamical systems by maps, the bounded condition for maps, and their associated
C^{*}
-algebras, and the theorems proven in
[
11
]
.
For any set
X
and any map
f
on X, we denote
f^{m}
as the
m
-th iterate of
f
, where
m\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
f^{0}=\id_{X}
and
f^{m+1}=f\circ f^{m}
, and we call
(X,f)
a (discrete) dynamical system.

For
x\in X
, the (forward) orbit of
x
, denoted by
\orb(x;f)
, is defined as follows:

\orb(x;f)=\{f^{n}(x)\mid n\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}\}.

We define the equivalence relation on
X
by
f
as follows:
For
x,y\in X
,

x\sim y\iff\orb(x;f)\cap\orb(y;f)\neq\emptyset,

in other words, there exist
l,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{l}(x)=f^{m}(y)
.
We use this equivalence relation to define total orbits.

Definition 1.1

(Total orbits)
.

For
x\in X
, the total orbit of
x
, denoted by
\Orb(x;f)
, is defined as

\Orb(x;f)=\{y\in X\mid x\sim y\}.

Hereinafter, we will assume that
X
is a countable set.

Definition 1.2

(Invariant sets)
.

Let
K\subseteq X
.
When
f(K),f^{-1}(K)\subseteq K
, we say that
K
is a
f
-invariant set.
When
K=\emptyset
or
K=X
, it is called trivial.
We say that
(X,f)
is minimal when there are no non-trivial
f
-invariant sets.

The following lemma characterizes invariant sets and minimality of dynamical systems.

Lemma 1.3
.

For every
x\in X
,
\Orb(x;f)
is the smallest
f
-invariant set containing
x
and
(X,f)
is minimal if and only if
x\sim y
for every
x,y\in X
.

Proof.

Let
y\in\Orb(x;f)
and assume that
z\in f^{-1}(y)
.
Since
f(y),z\sim y
and
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
X
, we have that
f(y),z\sim x
.
Therefore,
f(y),z\in\Orb(x;f)
.
It follows that
f(\Orb(x;f)),f^{-1}(\Orb(x;f))\subseteq\Orb(x;f)
, and so
\Orb(x;f)
is a
f
-invariant set.

Suppose that
K\subseteq X
is a
f
-invariant set containing
x
.
If
y\in\Orb(x;f)
, then there exist
l,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{l}(x)=f^{m}(y)
.
This implies that
y\in f^{-m}(f^{l}(x))\subseteq f^{-m}(f^{l}(K))\subseteq K
.
Hence,
\Orb(x;f)\subseteq K
.
Therefore, the smallest
f
-invariant set containing
x
is equal to
\Orb(x;f)
.

According to the previous discussion, if
(X,f)
is minimal, then
\Orb(x;f)=X
for every
x\in X
.
This implies that
x\sim y
for every
x,y\in X
.
Conversely, assume that
x\sim y
for every
x,y\in X
.
Then,
X=\Orb(x;f)
for every
x\in X
.
Since
\Orb(x;f)
is the smallest
f
-invariant set containing
x
, the minimality of
(X,f)
holds.
∎

Next, we define the bounded condition of
f
.
In
[
11
]
, we presented the definitions of the bounded condition and separating condition of
f
, as well as the following results for the case in which
|X|=\aleph_{0}
.
The same arguments can be applied to cases where
|X|<\aleph_{0}
.
Therefore, we can assume that
X
is a countable set for the rest of this section.

Definition 1.4

(
[
11
, Definition 5.1.1]
, Bounded condition)
.

When there exist mutually disjoint subsets
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{k}
of
X
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

(i)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}=X
;

(ii)

f|_{X_{i}}
is injective, where
1\leq i\leq k
,

we say that
f
satisfies the bounded condition.

This condition plays an important role in this study.
From this point on, we will assume that
f
satisfies the bounded condition.
For
(X,f)
, we let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space on
\mathbb{C}
with
\dim\mathcal{H}=|X|
and let
\{e_{x}\}_{x\in X}
be a completely orthonormal system (abbreviated as C.O.N.S.) for
\mathcal{H}
.
Define
k
partial isometries
T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by:

\displaystyle T_{i}e_{x}=\begin{cases}e_{f(x)},&x\in X_{i},\\
0,&x\notin X_{i},\end{cases}

where
1\leq i\leq k
.
This setting leads rise to the following lemma and theorem.
For detailed proofs, please refer to
[
11
]
.

Lemma 1.5

(
[
11
, Lemma 5.1.7]
)
.

For every
x\in X
,

\overline{C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})e_{x}}=\overline{\Span\{e_{y}\mid y\in\Orb(x;f)\}}.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
11
, Corollary 5.1.9]
)
.

If
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
is irreducible, then
(X,f)
is minimal.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma
be a cyclic permutation of
\{1,2,\cdots,m\}
defined by:

1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3\mapsto\cdots\mapsto m-1\mapsto m\mapsto 1.

For a
m
-tuple of numbers
(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})
, we say that it is aperiodic if

(i_{\sigma^{j}(1)},i_{\sigma^{j}(2)},\cdots,i_{\sigma^{j}(m)})\neq(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})

for every
1\leq j<m
.
Secondly, we define the separating condition of
f
.

Definition 1.7

(
[
11
, Definition 5.2.1]
, Separating condition)
.

Let
x\in X
.
When the following conditions hold:

(i)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
f^{m}(x)=x
;

(ii)

For the minimum
m
as above, the
m
-tuple
(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
, such that
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{i_{j}}
where
1\leq j\leq m
, is aperiodic,

satisfies the separating condition for

.

This condition implies that the converse of the relationship described in Theorem

is true.

Theorem 1.8

(
[
11
, Theorem 5.2.4]
)
.

Assume that
f
satisfies the separating condition for some point in
X
.
If
(X,f)
is minimal, then
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
is irreducible.

Example 1.9
.

(i)

(The Collatz map)
The Collatz map
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by:

f(n)=\begin{cases}3n+1,&n:\text{odd},\\
n/2,&n:\text{even}.\end{cases}

Let
X_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{odd}\}
and
X_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{even}\}
.
Since
X_{1}\cup X_{2}=\mathbb{N}
and
f|_{X_{1}},f|_{X_{2}}
are injective,
f
satisfies the bounded condition.
It follows that
f^{3}(1)=1,1\in X_{1},f(1)=4\in X_{2},f^{2}(1)=2\in X_{2}
, and
(1,2,2)
is aperiodic.
Hence, the Collatz map
f
satisfies the separating condition for
1
.

(ii)

(
qx{+}1
-function)
Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
be an odd number.
We define the
qx{+}1
-function
f_{q}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as follows:

f_{q}(n)=\begin{cases}qn+1,&n:\text{odd},\\
n/2,&n:\text{even}.\end{cases}

By setting
X_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{odd}\},X_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{even}\}
,
f_{q}
satisfies the bounded condition.

Assume that
q
be a Mersenne number, i.e.,
q=2^{m}-1
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
1\in X_{1},f(1)=2^{m}\in X_{2},f^{2}(1)=2^{m-1}\in X_{2},\cdots,f^{m}(1)=2\in X_{2}
, and
f^{m+1}(1)=1
.
Since
(1,2,\cdots,2)
is aperiodic,
f_{q}
satisfies the separating condition for
1
.

(iii)

(
3x{+}d
-function)
Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be an odd number. We define the
3x{+}d
-function
f_{d}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as follows:

f_{d}(n)=\begin{cases}3n+d,&n:\text{odd},\\
n/2,&n:\text{even}.\end{cases}

By setting
X_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{odd}\},X_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{even}\}
,
f_{d}
satisfies the bounded condition.
It follows that
d\in X_{1},f_{d}(d)=4d\in X_{2},f_{d}^{2}(d)=2d\in X_{2}
, and
f_{d}^{3}(d)=d
.
Since
(1,2,2)
is aperiodic,
f_{d}
satisfies the separating condition for
d
.

2
Uniqueness condition

In this section, we define the notion of the uniqueness condition for
f
.
For
1\leq j\leq k
, we let
f_{j}
denote
f|_{X_{j}}
.
For
x\in X
, if there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{m}(x)=x
, then
x
is called a periodic point.
In particular, when
m=1
,
f(x)=x
is called a fixed point of
f
.
Consider the set of indices,
\{i_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
satisfying
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{i_{j}}
where
1\leq j\leq m
and
i_{j}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
,

I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}

is uniquely determined for
x
.

We use the notation
f_{I}
and
T_{I}
for the map
f_{i_{m}}\circ f_{i_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1}}
(if defined) and the bounded linear operator
T_{i_{m}}T_{i_{m-1}}\cdots T_{i_{1}}
for any
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})\in\{1,2,...,k\}^{*}
.
The following are then equivalent:

(i)

f
has a periodic point;

(ii)

f_{I}
has a fixed point for some
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
.

In this regard, we define the uniqueness condition for
f
.

Definition 2.1

(Uniqueness condition)
.

When
f_{I}
(if defined) has at most one fixed point for every
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
,
we say that
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition.

Example 2.2
.

(i)

(
qx{+}d
-function)
Let
q,d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd numbers.
We define the
qx{+}d
-function
f_{q,d}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as follows:

f_{q,d}(n)=\begin{cases}qn+d,&n:\text{odd},\\
n/2,&n:\text{even}.\end{cases}

By setting
X_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{odd}\}
and
X_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{even}\}
,
f_{q,d}
satisfies the bounded condition.
Since
qn{+}d
and
n/2
are linear functions and
d\neq 0
,
(f_{q,d})_{I}(x)
is a linear function for every
I\in\{1,2\}^{*}
satisfying
(f_{q,d})_{I}(x)\not\equiv x
(
x\in\mathbb{N}
).
Therefore, the equation
(f_{q,d})_{I}(x)=x
has at most one solution where
x
is a natural number.
Consequently,
f_{q,d}
satisfies the uniqueness condition.

(ii)

(
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
-system)
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{k-1}),\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{k-1}
.
The function
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by:

f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(n)=\begin{cases}a_{i}n+b_{i},&n\equiv i\pmod{k},\ 1\leq i\leq k-1,\\
n/k,&n\equiv 0\pmod{k}.\end{cases}

By setting
X_{i}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\equiv i\pmod{k}\}
where
1\leq i\leq k
,
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
satisfies the bounded condition.
Since
a_{i}x+b_{i}
and
x/k
are linear functions and
b_{i}\neq 0
for every
1\leq i\leq k
,
(f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}})_{I}(x)
is a linear function for every
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
satisfying
(f_{a,b})_{I}(x)\not\equiv x
(
x\in\mathbb{N}
).
Therefore, the equation
(f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}})_{I}(x)=x
has at most one solution where
x
is a natural number.
Consequently,
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
satisfies the uniqueness condition.

The uniqueness condition of a map can be used to prove a property relating to its periodic points.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition.
For every
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

f_{I}
has a fixed point;

(ii)

f_{I}^{m}
has a fixed point.

In particular, if
f
has a periodic point, then there exists an aperiodic tuple
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
satisfying that
f_{I}
has a fixed point.

Proof.

If
x\in X
is a fixed point of
f_{I}
, then
f_{I}^{m}(x)=x
.
Hence,
(i)\implies(ii)
holds.

Assume that
x\in X
is a fixed point of
f_{I}^{m}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle f_{I}^{m}(f_{I}(x))=f_{I}(f_{I}^{m}(x))=f_{I}(x).

Thus,
f_{I}(x)
is a fixed point of
f_{I}^{m}
.
By the uniqueness condition of
f
,
f_{I}^{m}
has at most one fixed point, and hence
f_{I}(x)=x
.
Therefore,
x
is a fixed point of
f_{I}
and
(ii)\implies(i)
.
∎

The following immediately follows from the above theorem:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
q,d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd numbers and let

be the

Suppose that

.

2,&f_{q,d}^{j-1}(x):\text{even}.\end{cases}

In the above settings,
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})
is aperiodic.
In particular, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a periodic point for the Collatz map
f
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the minimum positive integer such that
f^{m}(n)=n
, then the

-tuple

((-1)^{n},(-1)^{f(n)},\cdots,(-1)^{f^{m-1}(n)})

is aperiodic.

Proof.

We will show that a contradiction arises if we assume that
I
is periodic.

Assume that
0<l<m
and that
J=(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{l})\in\{1,2\}^{*}
such that

I=(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{l},j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{l},\cdots,j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{l}).

It follows that

\displaystyle x=f^{m}(x)=f_{I}(x)=f_{J}^{m/l}(x).

According to Theorem
2.3
, it holds that
f^{l}(x)=f_{J}(x)=x
.
However, this contradicts the assumption that
m
is the minimum positive integer such that
f^{m}(x)=x
.
Therefore,
I
is aperiodic.

When
q=3
and
d=1
,
f_{q,d}
is equal to the Collatz map.
Suppose
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a periodic point of
f_{3,1}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the minimum positive integer such that
f_{3,1}^{m}(n)=n
.
Define
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})
as the statement and
J=((-1)^{n},(-1)^{f_{3,1}(n)},\cdots,(-1)^{f_{3,1}^{m-1}(n)})
.
Since, for
1\leq j<m
,
i_{j}=1
if and only if
f_{3,1}^{j-1}(n)
is odd, it follows that
f_{3,1}^{j-1}(n)=1
if and only if
(-1)^{f_{3,1}^{j-1}(n)}=-1
.
Similarly,
i_{j}=2
if and only if
(-1)^{f_{3,1}^{j-1}(n)}=1
.
This implies that
I
is aperiodic if and only if
J
is aperiodic.
As we have already demonstrated that
I
is aperiodic, it follows that
J
is too.
∎

The following relationship can be seen to hold for the uniqueness and separating conditions of a map.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that
x\in X
is a periodic point of
f
.
If
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition, then it also satisfies the separating condition for
x
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Definition
1.7
and Theorem
2.3
.
∎

The next result applies to any map that satisfies the bounded condition, regardless of whether the uniqueness condition is assumed.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
.
If
f_{I}
has a fixed point, then
T_{I}
has a non-zero fixed point.

Proof.

Let
x
be the fixed point of
f_{I}
.
Then,

0\neq T_{I}e_{x}=e_{f_{I}(x)}=e_{x}.

Thus,
T_{I}
has a non-zero fixed point.
∎

The uniqueness condition of a map leads that the converse of the above lemma holds.

Theorem 2.7
.

Assume that
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition and that
I\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
satisfies the condition that
T_{I}\neq 0
.
If
T_{I}
has a non-zero fixed point, then
f_{I}
has a fixed point.
Furthermore, if
x\in X
is the fixed point of
f_{I}
, then the set of all fixed points of
T_{I}
is
\{\alpha e_{x}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathcal{H}\backslash\{0\}
be a fixed point of
T_{I}
.
Since
\left\lVert T_{I}\right\rVert\leq 1
, it holds that
\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert a\right\rVert
, and so,

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a-a\right\rVert^{2}=\left\langle T_{I}^{*}a-a,T_{I}^{*}a-a\right\rangle=\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\langle T_{I}^{*}a,a\right\rangle-\left\langle a,T_{I}^{*}a\right\rangle+\left\langle a,a\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\langle a,T_{I}a\right\rangle-\left\langle T_{I}a,a\right\rangle+\left\langle a,a\right\rangle=\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\langle a,a\right\rangle-\left\langle a,a\right\rangle+\left\langle a,a\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\langle a,a\right\rangle=\left\lVert T_{I}^{*}a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}=0.

We obtain that
T_{I}^{*}a=a
.
Let
x\in X
satisfying
\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle\neq 0
.
Then,

\displaystyle 0\neq\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle(T_{I}^{*})^{m}a,e_{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle a,T_{I}^{m}e_{x}\right\rangle

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
T_{I}^{m}e_{x}\neq 0
and
T_{I}^{m}e_{x}=e_{f_{I}^{m}(x)}
.
Since

\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{y\in X}\left\lvert\left\langle a,e_{y}\right\rangle\right\rvert^{2}<+\infty,

the set
\{e_{f_{I}^{m}(x)}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is finite.
Hence, there exist
M<N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f_{I}^{M}(x)=f_{I}^{N}(x)
.
Let
m=N-M
.
Then,
f_{I}^{m}(f_{I}^{M}(x))=f_{I}^{N}(x)=f_{I}^{M}(x)
.
Thus,
f_{I}^{m}
has a fixed point.
Similarly,
f_{I}^{m}(f_{I}^{M+1}(x))=f_{I}^{m+1}(f_{I}^{M}(x))=f_{I}(f_{I}^{M}(x))=f_{I}^{M+1}(x)
.
By the uniqueness condition of
f
,

f_{I}^{M}(f_{I}(x))=f_{I}^{M+1}(x)=f_{I}^{M}(x).

Since
f_{I}^{M}
is injective,
f_{I}(x)=x
.
Hence,
x
is the fixed point of
f_{I}
.

Conversely, assume that
x\in X
is a fixed point of
f_{I}
.
Then,
e_{x}
is a fixed point of
T_{I}
.
Let
a\in\mathcal{H}\backslash\{0\}
be a fixed point of
T_{I}
.
If there is
y\in X\backslash\{x\}
satisfying that
\left\langle a,e_{y}\right\rangle\neq 0
, then it holds that

\displaystyle 0\neq\left\langle a,e_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle(T_{I}^{*})^{m}a,e_{y}\right\rangle=\left\langle a,T_{I}^{m}e_{y}\right\rangle=\langle a,e_{f_{I}^{m}(y)}\rangle

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
And similarly, it also holds that
f_{I}(y)=y
.
Since
f_{I}(x)=x
, by using the assumption of the uniqueness condition of
f
, we obtain that
x=y
which contradicts to the assumption that
x\neq y
.
Thus,
a\in\Span\{e_{x}\}=\{\alpha e_{x}\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\}
.
∎

3
Totally uniqueness condition

Fix
x\in X
.
For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there uniquely exists
i_{j}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
such that
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{i_{j}}
.
We define
\hat{x}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

\hat{x}=(i_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\cdots).

Definition 3.1

(Totally uniqueness condition)
.

When the map
X\ni x\mapsto\hat{x}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is injective, we say that
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.

Let us consider the
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
-system described in Example
2.2

(ii)
.
If
k=2
,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1})=(q)
,
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1})=(d)
where
q,d\in\mathbb{N}
are arbitrary odd numbers, then
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
is the
qx{+}d
-function.
At this time, it hold that
a_{1}=q
is relatively prime to
k
and,
\{n,f_{q,d}(n)\}
includes a multiple of
2
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
k\geq 2
, in what follows, we assume the following conditions:

(i)

a_{i}
is relatively prime to
k
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

(ii)

\{n,f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(n),\cdots,f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}^{k-1}(n)\}
includes a multiple of
k
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
f=f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
and set
X_{i}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\equiv i\pmod{k}\}
where
1\leq i\leq k
.
Next, let us consider the ring

X=\projlim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{Z}/k^{n}\mathbb{Z}.

We let
\pi_{n}:X\to\mathbb{Z}/k^{n}\mathbb{Z}
denote the canonical ring homomorphism.
The map
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
naturally extends to
a continuous map
f:X\to X
, namely

f(x)=\begin{cases}a_{i}x+b_{i}&\pi_{1}(x)\equiv i\pmod{k},\ 1\leq i\leq k-1,\\
x/k&\pi_{1}(x)\equiv 0\pmod{k}.\end{cases}

Since
a_{i}
is relatively prime to
k
for every
i=1,2,\dots,{k-1}
,
one can verify that
f
becomes a local homeomorphism.

For
x\in X
, we define
\hat{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z})^{\mathbb{N}}
by

\hat{x}(j)=\pi_{1}(f^{j-1}(x))

where
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x,y\in X
and let
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose
i:=\pi_{1}(x)=\pi_{1}(y)
and
\pi_{j}(f(x))=\pi_{j}(f(y))
.

1.

When
i\neq 0
, we have
\pi_{j}(x)=\pi_{j}(y)
.

2.

When
i=0
, we have
\pi_{j+1}(x)=\pi_{j+1}(y)
.

Proof.

(i)
One has
f(x)=a_{i}x+b_{i}
and
f(y)=a_{i}y+b_{i}
by the definition of
f
.
Since
a_{i}
and
k
are coprime,

\pi_{j}(f(x))=\pi_{j}(f(y))
implies
\pi_{j}(x)=\pi_{j}(y)
.

(ii)
We have
kf(x)=x
and
kf(y)=y
by the definition of
f
.
This, together with
\pi_{j}(f(x))=\pi_{j}(f(y))
,
implies
\pi_{j+1}(x)=\pi_{j+1}(y)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

The map
x\mapsto\hat{x}
, where
x\in X
, is injective.

Proof.

Assume that there exist
x,y\in X
such that
\hat{x}=\hat{y}
.
For any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\pi_{1}(f^{j-1}(x))=\hat{x}(j)=\hat{y}(j)=\pi_{1}(f^{j-1}(y))

holds.
Take
m\in\mathbb{N}
arbitrarily.
The cardinality of the set

\left\{j\in\{1,\dots,mk\}\mid\pi_{1}(f^{j-1}(x))=0\right\}

is not less than
m
,
because
\{n,f(n),f^{2}(n),\cdots,f^{k-1}(n)\}
includes a multiple of
k
for every natural number
n
.
Hence, starting from
\pi_{1}(f^{mk}(x))=\pi_{1}(f^{mk}(y))
and
applying the lemma above inductively,
we obtain
\pi_{1+l}(x)=\pi_{1+l}(y)
for some
l\geq m
.
As
m
was arbitrary, one can conclude
x=y
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

In the above settings,
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.
In particular, for every odd number
q,d\in\mathbb{N}
, the
qx{+}d
-function satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.

Proof.

By identifying sets
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
and
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
, the map in Lemma
3.3
can be regarded as an extension of the map
n\mapsto\hat{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since the former map is injective, so is the latter.
∎

As we will show below, the totally uniqueness condition is stronger than the uniqueness condition.

Proposition 3.5
.

If
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, then
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition.

Proof.

Suppose
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{m})\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{*}
satisfies that there are
x,y\in X
such that
f_{I}(x)=x
and
f_{I}(y)=y
.
Then, it follows that
\hat{x}=\hat{y}
.
By the totally uniqueness condition of
f
, it holds that
x=y
.
Therefore,
f
satisfies the uniqueness condition.
∎

Given the inclusion order of the sets, the family of all
f
-invariant sets can be regarded as a complete lattice, as can the family of all reducing subspaces for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
The next theorem applies to any
f
that satisfies the bounded condition, regardless of whether totally uniqueness condition is assumed.

Theorem 3.6

(Injections)
.

There exists an injective, order-preserving map from

\{K\subseteq X\mid\text{$K$ is a $f$-invariant set}\}

to

\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\mid\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a reducing subspace for $C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})$}\}.

Proof.

For a trivial
f
-invariant set
K=\emptyset\subseteq X
, let
\mathcal{H}_{K}=\{0\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
as a trivial reducing subspace.

Let
K(\neq\emptyset)\subseteq X
be a
f
-invariant set and
\mathcal{H}_{K}=\overline{\Span\{e_{x}\mid x\in K}\}
.
For two
f
-invariant sets
K\neq K^{\prime}\subseteq X
, by the above definition, it holds that
\mathcal{H}_{K}\neq\mathcal{H}_{K^{\prime}}
.

We will see that
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is a reducing subspace for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
Let
e_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
.
By the definition of
T_{i}
,

T_{i}e_{x},T_{i}^{*}e_{x}\in\Span\{e_{y}\in\mathcal{H}\mid y=f(x)\text{ or }y\in f^{-1}(x)\}

for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Since
K
is a
f
-invariant set,
f(x)\in K
and
f^{-1}(x)\subseteq K
.
Therefore,
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})e_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{K}
.
It follows that
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})\mathcal{H}_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{K}
, and hence,
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is a reducing subspace for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
∎

When
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, we will show that the order-preserving map above is bijective.
Hence, for such a
f
, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the families of all
f
-invariant sets and all reducing subspaces for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.

Theorem 3.7

(Bijections)
.

Assume that
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.
Then, there exists a bijective, order-preserving map from

\{K\subseteq X\mid\text{$K$ is a $f$-invariant set}\}

to

\{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\mid\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a reducing subspace for $C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})$}\}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the map
K\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{K}
in Theorem
3.6
is surjective.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a reducing subspace for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
If
\mathcal{M}=\{0\}
, then it is the image of
\emptyset\subseteq X
by the map as above.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}\neq\{0\}
.
Let
a\in\mathcal{M}\backslash\{0\}
and
x\in X
satisfy
\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle\neq 0
.
Let
\hat{x}=(i_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
By the totally uniqueness condition, for every
y\in X\backslash\{x\}
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{i_{j}}
and
f^{j-1}(y)\notin X_{i_{j}}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define

P_{m}=T_{i_{1}}^{*}T_{i_{2}}^{*}\cdots T_{i_{m}}^{*}T_{i_{m}}\cdots T_{i_{2}}T_{i_{1}}\in C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k}).

Then,
P_{j}e_{x}=e_{x}
and
P_{j}e_{y}=0
.
This implies that

P_{m}a=P_{m}(\sum_{y\in X}\left\langle a,e_{y}\right\rangle e_{y})\to\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle e_{x}\text{ }(m\to\infty).

Therefore,
e_{x}\in\overline{C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, and hence

\overline{\Span\{e_{x}\mid x\in X,\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle\neq 0\text{ for some }a\in\mathcal{M}\}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}.

The converse of this inclusion relationship is also true.
Thus,

\mathcal{M}=\overline{\Span\{e_{x}\mid x\in X,\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle\neq 0\text{ for some }a\in\mathcal{M}\}}.

Let
K=\{x\in X\mid\left\langle a,e_{x}\right\rangle\neq 0\text{ for some }a\in\mathcal{M}\}
.
It is easy to check that
K
is a
f
-invariant set, because
\mathcal{M}
is a reducing subspace for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
This implies
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

Therefore, the map in Theorem
3.6
is surjective.
∎

The next corollary follows from the previous theorem.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
q,d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd numbers and
f_{q,d}
be the
qx{+}d
-function.
Set
X_{1}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{odd}\}
and
X_{2}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n:\text{even}\}
.
Let
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
be the associated
C^{*}
-algebra.
Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
is irreducible;

(ii)

(\mathbb{N},f_{q,d})
is minimal.

Proof.

According to Proposition
3.4
,
f_{q,d}
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.
This implies that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the families of all
f_{q,d}
-invariant sets and all reducing subspaces for
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})
.
In particular,

is irreducible if and only if
(\mathbb{N},f_{q,d})
is minimal.
∎

4
Homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition

Throughout this section, let
Y
be a countable set and
g
be a map on
Y
.
Assume that there exist mutually disjoint subsets
Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots,Y_{k}
of
Y
such that:

(i)

\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}Y_{j}=Y
;

(ii)

g|_{Y_{j}}
is injective, where
1\leq j\leq k
.

We introduce the notion of homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition.

Definition 4.1

(Homomorphisms)
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a map.
When the following conditions hold:

(i)

\varphi\circ f=g\circ\varphi
;

(ii)

\varphi(X_{i})\subseteq Y_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
,

we call
\varphi
a homomorphism from
(X,f)
to
(Y,g)
.

The following lemmas show the properties of homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a homomorphism from
(X,f)
to
(Y,g)
and let
x\in X
.

(i)

\varphi(\orb(x;f))=\orb(\varphi(x);g)
.

(ii)

\varphi(\Orb(x;f))\subseteq\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
.

(iii)

(\varphi(X),g|_{\varphi(X)})
is a dynamical system with bounded condition.

(iv)

\varphi^{-1}(L)
is a
f
-invariant set for every
g
-invariant set
L\subseteq Y
.

Proof.

(i) Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\varphi\circ f=g\circ\varphi
,

\displaystyle\varphi(f^{n}(x))=(\varphi\circ f)(f^{n-1}(x))=(g\circ\varphi)(f(f^{n-1}(x)))=g(\varphi(f^{n-1}(x)))=\cdots=g^{n}(\varphi(x)).

Hence,
\varphi(\orb(x;f))=\orb(\varphi(x);g)
.

(ii) Let
w\in\Orb(x;f)
, i.e.,
w\in X
satisfying
w\sim x
.
There exist
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(w)=f^{l}(x)
.
By the former discussion, it follows that
g^{k}(\varphi(w))=\varphi(f^{k}(w))=\varphi(f^{l}(x))=g^{l}(\varphi(x))
.
Thus,
\varphi(w)\sim\varphi(x)
.
This implies that
\varphi(\Orb(x;f))\subseteq\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
.

(iii) For every
x\in X
,
g|_{\varphi(X)}(\varphi(x))=(g\circ\varphi)(x)=(\varphi\circ f)(x)=\varphi(f(x))
.
Thus,
g|_{\varphi(X)}
is a map on
\varphi(X)
.
Since
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
, it holds that
\varphi(X)=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(X_{i})
.
By the definition of homomorphisms,
\varphi(X_{1}),\varphi(X_{2}),\cdots,\varphi(X_{k})
are mutually disjoint subsets of
\varphi(X)
.
Because of the bounded condition of
g
, for every
1\leq i\leq k
,
g|_{Y_{i}}
is an injection.
It follows that

g|_{\varphi(X_{i})}=(g|_{Y_{i}})|_{\varphi(X_{i})}

is injective for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
Therefore,
g|_{\varphi(X)}
satisfies the bounded condition.

(iv) Assume that
x\in\varphi^{-1}(L)
.
It holds that
\varphi(f(x))=g(\varphi(x))\in g(L)\subseteq L
, and so,
f(x)\in\varphi^{-1}(L)
.
If
y\in f^{-1}(x)
, then

g(\varphi(y))=\varphi(f(y))=\varphi(x),

and

\varphi(y)\in g^{-1}(\varphi(x))\subseteq g^{-1}(L)\subseteq L.

Hence,
y\in\varphi^{-1}(L)
.
This implies that
\varphi^{-1}(L)
is a
f
-invariant set.
∎

Let
(Z,h)
be a dynamical system with bounded condition and
Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{k}
be its associated mutually disjoint subsets.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
,
\psi:Y\to Z
, and
\tau:Y\to X
be homomorphisms.

(i)

\psi\circ\varphi
is a homomorphism.

(ii)

id_{X}
is a homomorphism satisfying
\varphi\circ\id_{X}=\varphi
and
\id_{X}\circ\tau=\tau
.

Proof.

(i) By the definition of homomorphisms,

(\psi\circ\varphi)\circ f=\psi\circ(\varphi\circ f)=\psi\circ(g\circ\varphi)=(\psi\circ g)\circ\varphi=(h\circ\psi)\circ\varphi=h\circ(\psi\circ\varphi)

and it holds that
(\psi\circ\varphi)(X_{i})=\psi(\varphi(X_{i}))\subseteq\psi(Y_{i})\subseteq Z_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
This implies that
\psi\circ\varphi
is a homomorphism.

(ii) Since
f\circ\id_{X}=\id_{X}\circ f
and
\id_{X}(X_{i})=X_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
,
\id_{X}
is a homomorphism.
∎

From the above lemma, we can see that homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition give morphisms in the category whose class of objects consists of such dynamical systems.
Consequently, the definition of an isomorphism between such dynamical systems is provided:
\varphi:X\to Y
is an isomorphism if and only if
\varphi
is a bijective homomorphism, and
\varphi^{-1}
is also a homomorphism.
It immediately follows that the relation that a dynamical system with bounded condition is isomorphic to another one is an equivalence relation.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a homomorphism from
(X,f)
to
(Y,g)
.
For isomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition, the following property holds.

Lemma 4.4
.

\varphi
is an isomorphism if and only if
\varphi
is bijective.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\varphi
is an isomorphism when
\varphi
is bijective.

Suppose
\varphi
is bijective.
It follows that
\varphi|_{X_{i}}:X_{i}\to Y_{i}
is bijective for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
Thus,
\varphi^{-1}(Y_{i})=X_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
Let
y\in Y
.
Since
\varphi\circ f=g\circ\varphi
,

\displaystyle\varphi(f(\varphi^{-1}(y)))

\displaystyle=(\varphi\circ f)(\varphi^{-1}(y))=(g\circ\varphi)(\varphi^{-1}(y))

\displaystyle=g(y)=(\varphi\circ\varphi^{-1})(g(y))=\varphi(\varphi^{-1}(g(y))).

Hence,
f(\varphi^{-1}(y))=\varphi^{-1}(g(y))
.
Therefore,
\varphi^{-1}\circ g=f\circ\varphi^{-1}
and this implies that
\varphi^{-1}
is a homomorphism.
∎

Next, we introduce the notion of symbolic dynamics of dynamical systems with bounded condition.
We will write
\hat{X}
as the set of all
\hat{x}
where
x\in X
.
Let
x\in X
and
\hat{x}=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\cdots)\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
By the definition of
\hat{x}
, it follows that
\widehat{f(x)}=(i_{2},i_{3},i_{4},\cdots)
.
This observation is used to define the following symbolic dynamics:

Definition 4.5

(Symbolic dynamics)
.

Let
\hat{f}:\hat{X}\to\hat{X}
defined by:

\hat{f}((i_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(i_{j+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},

and set
\hat{X_{i}}=\{\hat{x}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid x\in X_{i}\}
where
1\leq i\leq k
.
Then,
\hat{f}
satisfies the bounded condition and we call
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
the symbolic dynamics of
(X,f)
.

The following canonical homomorphism is derived from the symbolic dynamics.

Lemma 4.6
.

The map
X\ni x\mapsto\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
is a surjective homomorphism from
(X,f)
to
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\hat{X}
, surjectivity holds directly.
Let
x\in X
.
If
x\in X_{i}
, then
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}_{i}
.
Hence,
\varphi(X_{i})\subseteq\hat{X}_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
For
\hat{x}=(i_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, it holds that
\widehat{f(x)}=(i_{j+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=\hat{f}(\hat{x})
.
Therefore, the map
X\ni x\mapsto\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
is a homomorphism.
∎

The next proposition holds for every
(X,f)
with bounded condition, regardless of whether the totally uniqueness condition of
f
is assumed.

Proposition 4.7
.

\hat{f}
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.

Proof.

For
\hat{x}=(i_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we let
\hat{x}_{j}
denote
i_{j}
.
We will check that
\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x})\in\hat{X}_{\hat{x}_{j}}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
.

Since
\widehat{f(x)}=\hat{f}(\hat{x})
,

\widehat{f^{j-1}(x)}=\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x}).

This implies that
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{i}
if and only if
\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x})\in\hat{X}_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
On the other hand,
\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x})\in\hat{X}_{i}
if and only if
i=\hat{x}_{j}
.
Therefore,

\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x})\in\hat{X}_{\hat{x}_{j}}.

Let
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\hat{X}
.
If
\hat{x}\neq\hat{y}
, then there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying that
\hat{x}_{j}\neq\hat{y}_{j}
.
Then,
\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{x})\in\hat{X}_{\hat{x}_{j}}
and
\hat{f}^{j-1}(\hat{y})\notin\hat{X}_{\hat{x}_{j}}
.
Thus,
\hat{f}
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition.
∎

The following theorem can be proven based on the results obtained so far:

Theorem 4.8
.

If
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, then
(X,f)
is isomorphic to
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
.

Proof.

If
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, then the map
X\ni x\mapsto\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
is injective.
According to Lemma
4.6
, it is a surjective homomorphism.
Furthermore, the restriction of the map on
X_{i}
is a bijective map from
X_{i}
to
\hat{X}_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
By Lemma
4.4
, the inverse map
\hat{X}\ni\hat{x}\mapsto x\in X
is also a homomorphism.
Therefore, the map is an isomorphism and
(X,f)
is isomorphic to
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
.
∎

According to Proposition
4.7
and the former theorem, we can obtain the next corollary.

Corollary 4.9
.

(\hat{\hat{X}},\hat{\hat{f}})
is isomorphic to
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
.

homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition induce injective homomorphisms between their associated symbolic dynamics.

Theorem 4.10
.

For every homomorphism
\varphi
from
(X,f)
to
(Y,g)
, there exists an injective homomorphism
\hat{\varphi}
from
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
to
(\hat{Y},\hat{g})
satisfying
\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x})=\widehat{\varphi(x)}
for every
x\in X
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and
\hat{x}_{j}
denote the index satisfying
f^{j-1}(x)\in X_{\hat{x}_{j}}
where
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\varphi
is a homomorphism, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

g^{j-1}(\varphi(x))=\varphi(f^{j-1}(x))\in\varphi(X_{\hat{x}_{j}})\subseteq Y_{\hat{x}_{j}}.

It follows that
\widehat{\varphi(x)}=(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=\hat{x}
.
If
z\in X
satisfies
\hat{x}=\hat{z}
, then
\widehat{\varphi(x)}=\hat{x}=\hat{z}=\widehat{\varphi(z)}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{\varphi(x)}
is uniquely determined for
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
regardless of how
x
is chosen.
Hence, we can define a map
\hat{\varphi}:\hat{X}\to\hat{Y}
by:

\hat{\varphi}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}.

For every
(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\hat{X}
,

(\hat{g}\circ\hat{\varphi})((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=\hat{g}(\hat{\varphi}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}))=\hat{g}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(\hat{x}_{j+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

and

(\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{f})((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=\hat{\varphi}(\hat{f}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}))=\hat{\varphi}((\hat{x}_{j+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(\hat{x}_{j+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}.

Thus, it holds that
\hat{g}\circ\hat{\varphi}=\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{f}
.
It is easy to see that
\hat{\varphi}(\hat{X}_{i})\subseteq Y_{i}
, for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
Therefore,
\hat{\varphi}
a homomorphism.
Injectivity of
\hat{\varphi}
directly holds from its definition.
∎

From the setting above, we can obtain a functor from the category of dynamical systems with bounded condition to the category of symbolic dynamics.

Corollary 4.11

(Functoriality)
.

Let
F((X,f))=(\hat{X},\hat{f})
and
F(\varphi)=\hat{\varphi}
for every dynamical system
(X,f)
with bounded condition and homomorphism
\varphi
between such dynamical systems.
Then,
F
is a covariant functor from the category of dynamical systems with bounded condition to the category of symbolic dynamics.

Proof.

Let
(X,f),(Y,g)
and
(Z,h)
be dynamical systems with bounded condition and let
\varphi:X\to Y
and
\psi:Y\to Z
be homomorphisms.
We will check that
F(\id_{X})=\id_{\hat{X}}
and
F(\psi\circ\varphi)=F(\psi)\circ F(\varphi)
.

Since
\id_{X}(X_{i})=X_{i}
,

\widehat{\id_{X}}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

for every
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
.
It follows that
F(\id_{X})=\widehat{\id_{X}}=\id_{\hat{X}}
.

For every
(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\hat{X}
.

\displaystyle F(\psi\circ\varphi)((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\psi\circ\varphi}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=(\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=\hat{\psi}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})=\hat{\psi}(\hat{\varphi}((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}))

\displaystyle=F(\psi)(F(\varphi)((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}))=(F(\psi)\circ F(\varphi))((\hat{x}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}).

Therefore,
F(\psi\circ\varphi)=F(\psi)\circ F(\varphi)
.
∎

At the end of this section, we discuss how to induce
*
-homomorphisms between
C^{*}
-algebras from homomorphisms between dynamical systems with bounded condition.
In particular, we prove that every isomorphism between such dynamical systems induce an isomorphism between their associated
C^{*}
-algebras.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a Hilbert space with
\dim\mathcal{K}=|Y|
and let
\{h_{y}\}_{y\in Y}
be a C.O.N.S. for
\mathcal{K}
.
For
1\leq j\leq k
, define
S_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
by:

\displaystyle S_{j}h_{y}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}h_{g(y)},&y\in Y_{i},\\
0,&y\notin Y_{i}.\end{cases}

Theorem 4.12

(Isomorphisms)
.

For every isomorphism
\varphi
from
(X,f)
to
(Y,g)
, there uniquely exists an isomorphism
\varphi^{*}
from
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
to
C^{*}(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k})
satisfying
\varphi^{*}(T_{i})=S_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi:X\to Y
is bijective, there uniquely exists an isomorphism
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
such that
Ue_{x}=h_{\varphi(x)}
for every
x\in X
.
We will check that
UT_{i}U^{-1}=S_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

Let
y\in Y
.
It follows that there uniquely exists
1\leq j\leq k
satisfying
y\in Y_{j}
.
Since
\varphi^{-1}
is a homomorphism,
\varphi^{-1}\circ g=f\circ\varphi^{-1}
and
\varphi^{-1}(y)\in X_{j}
.
If
j=i
, then

UT_{i}U^{-1}h_{y}=UT_{i}e_{\varphi^{-1}(y)}=Ue_{(f\circ\varphi^{-1})(y)}=Ue_{(\varphi^{-1}\circ g)(y)}=h_{g(y)}=S_{i}h_{y}.

If
j\neq i
, then
UT_{i}U^{-1}h_{y}=UT_{i}e_{\varphi^{-1}(y)}=0=S_{i}h_{y}
.
Therefore,
UT_{i}U^{-1}h_{y}=S_{i}h_{y}
for every
y\in Y
and this implies that
UT_{i}U^{-1}=S_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.
Since
U
is an isomorphism, that is,
U
is bijective and
U^{-1}=U^{*}
, it holds that

UT_{i}^{*}U^{-1}=UT_{i}^{*}U^{*}=(UT_{i}U^{*})^{*}=S_{i}^{*}

for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Hence, for every
2k
-variable polynomial
p
,

\displaystyle Up(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k},T_{1}^{*},T_{2}^{*},\cdots,T_{k}^{*})U^{-1}

\displaystyle=p(UT_{1}U^{-1},UT_{2}U^{-1},\cdots,UT_{k}U^{-1},UT_{1}^{*}U^{-1},UT_{2}^{*}U^{-1},\cdots,UT_{k}^{*}U^{-1})

\displaystyle=p(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k},S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*},\cdots,S_{k}^{*}).

Because

\{p(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k},T_{1}^{*},T_{2}^{*},\cdots,T_{k}^{*})\mid p:\text{a $2k$-variable polynomial}\}

is dense in
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
,

UTU^{-1}\in C^{*}(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k})

for every
T\in C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
.
Similarly, it holds that
U^{-1}SU\in C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
for every
S\in C^{*}(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k})
.
If
UTU^{-1}=0
, then
T=U^{-1}(UTU^{-1})U=U^{-1}0U=0
.
Thus, the map
\varphi^{*}:C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})\ni T\mapsto UTU^{-1}\in C^{*}(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k})
is a bijection.
It is easy to see that
\varphi^{*}
is a
*
-homomorphism.
Therefore,
\varphi^{*}
is an isomorphism.
∎

For
(\hat{X},\hat{f})
, let
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space with
\dim\hat{\mathcal{H}}=|\hat{X}|
and
\{e_{\hat{x}}\}_{\hat{x}\in\hat{X}}
be a C.O.N.S. for
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
.
For
1\leq i\leq k
, define
\hat{T_{i}}\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})
by:

\hat{T_{i}}e_{\hat{x}}=\begin{cases}e_{\hat{f}(\hat{x})},&\hat{x}\in\hat{X_{i}},\\
0,&\hat{x}\notin\hat{X_{i}}.\end{cases}

By Theorem
4.8
and
4.12
, the following holds immediately.

Corollary 4.13
.

If
f
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, then
C^{*}(T_{1},T_{2},\cdots,T_{k})
is isomorphic to
C^{*}(\hat{T_{1}},\hat{T_{2}},\cdots,\hat{T_{k}})
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a homomorphism.
We will consider the condition that, for every
x\in X
,
\varphi(\Orb(x;f))=\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
.
This condition always holds when the homomorphism is a bijection, i.e. an isomorphism.

Lemma 4.14
.

If
\varphi(\Orb(x;f))=\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
for every
x\in X
and

satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, then
\hat{\varphi}(\Orb(\hat{x};\hat{f}))=\Orb(\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x});\hat{g})
for every
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{x}\in\hat{X}
.
Assume that
\hat{y}\in\Orb(\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x});\hat{g})
, that is, there exist

such that
\hat{g}^{k}(\hat{y})=\hat{g}^{l}(\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x}))
.
Because of Corollary
4.11

\widehat{g^{k}(y)}=\hat{g}^{k}(\hat{y})=\hat{g}^{l}(\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x}))=\widehat{g^{l}(\varphi(x))},

regardless of the choice of
x
and
y
.
Since
g
satisfies the totally uniqueness condition, it follows that
g^{k}(y)=g^{l}(\varphi(x))
for every
x
and
y
as such, and then,
y\in\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
.
By the assumption for
\varphi

\varphi(\Orb(x;f))=\Orb(\varphi(x);g)
.
Therefore, there exists

such that
\varphi(w)=y
.
According to Lemma
4.2
and
4.6
, we can obtain that

\hat{w}\in\Orb(\hat{x};\hat{f}).

Hence,

\hat{y}=\widehat{\varphi(w)}=\hat{\varphi}(\hat{w})\in\hat{\varphi}(\Orb(\hat{x};\hat{f})).

Thus,

.

The converse of the former inclusion relation follows from Lemma

.

\varphi

.
