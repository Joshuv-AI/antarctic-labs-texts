Problem-based learning for functional otolaryngology in postgraduate education
Authors: Yi Fang, Jian Chen, Peijie He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the role of problem-based learning (PBL) in the education of otolaryngological postgraduates.MethodsPostgraduates who took the functional otolaryngology course completed the pre-course, after-course questionnaires and closed-book test, the results were compared and analyzed.ResultsA total of 33 students who had completed the functional otolaryngology course and 33 students who did not take the course filled out the questionnaires. Background

In Chinese medical education, otolaryngology is a compulsory course that medical students are first exposed to in their fourth year of a five-year undergraduate program. The curriculum covers the anatomical foundations of otolaryngology, symptomatology, and the pathophysiological processes of common diseases. Following this, undergraduates are required to participate in clinical ENT internships and complete a two-week internship rotation. These experiences provide a solid foundation for students pursuing graduate studies in otolaryngology.

In China, graduate education in otolaryngology is an academically-oriented pathway designed for students pursuing master’s or doctoral degrees. Its primary focus is on developing research skills. In contrast, residency training provides comprehensive 36-month clinical preparation for specialist practice. Graduates with a bachelor’s, master’s, or doctoral degree are all eligible for the standardized residency training program. Upon obtaining their professional qualification as a physician in otolaryngology, they become fully qualified otolaryngologists and can join a hospital staff. Not all otolaryngologists hold doctoral degrees, academic hospitals often require PhDs and professional qualification, community practitioners may qualify with bachelor’s or master’s degrees combined with residency completion.

However, the current state of otolaryngology postgraduate education still has room for improvement. research-oriented master’s and PhD candidates are often busy with research projects and cell biology experiments. They need to rely on reading otolaryngology textbooks and participating in clinical rotations for several months to acquire clinical knowledge. Therefore, the training process for young otolaryngologists is relatively long.

There has been limited research addressing the improvement of otolaryngology postgraduate education. Dimitrov et al. analyzed 417 questionnaires regarding ENT exposure among general practitioners in England and found that ENT training was insufficient for both undergraduate and postgraduate practitioners [1]. Similarly, Liu et al. explored the use of a near-peer teaching workshop in the education of medical students pursuing a postgraduate medical degree [2]. Six junior doctors shared management strategies for acute otolaryngology/ENT surgery conditions presenting as emergencies, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach in enhancing medical student education.

Amid advancements and updates in otolaryngology teaching concepts, problem-based learning (PBL) and the role of functional preservation have been highly valued. PBL is a “student-centered and problem-oriented” teaching model guided by instructors, which encourages students to independently gather information, identify issues, and develop solutions through group discussions. This fosters critical thinking, independent learning, and innovation skills among students [3]. The PBL approach has been recognized for its positive impact in fields such as radiography [4], psychopharmacology [5] and nursing education [6]. However, its application in otolaryngology education has not received much attention or been widely reported. While the principle of functional preservation has gained prominence in clinical practice, its effective implementation requires early reinforcement of fundamental anatomical and physiological knowledge during foundational medical education. To bridge this educational gap, we developed an integrative curriculum that leverages institutional expertise in symptom-oriented pedagogy. Through problem-based learning (PBL) modules incorporating functional preservation concepts, we aim to cultivate clinical reasoning competencies in otolaryngology trainees, enabling them to contextualize theoretical knowledge within evidence-based, organ-sparing therapeutic frameworks.

Methods

Research design

This research was approved and registered by the Ethics Committee of the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University (No. 2025051). All participants were fully informed about the research protocol through online and verbal notifications, regardless of their enrollment status in the course.

The postgraduate course “Functional Otolaryngology Theory and Practice” taught by the researchers was conducted as part of the research. A total of two semesters of elective students participated in the study. Participants completed questionnaires before the first class and after the final session of the course. Additionally, students who did not attend the course were invited to complete the questionnaire as a control group.

Characteristics of participants

All participants in the course were master’s and PhD degree candidates in the medical school of our medical unit. The inclusion criteria for the experimental group were as follows: (1) voluntary registration in the course according to selection requirements; (2) completion of undergraduate ENT coursework with corresponding credits earned; (3) cooperation in completing the questionnaire; (4) participation in all teaching activities of this course and completion of the classroom, after-class, and final assessments; (5) no violations of regulations or discipline during the course. The exclusion criteria for the experimental group were as follows: (1) students who had taken this course multiple times; (2) students who failed to complete this course; (3) students who did not complete the questionnaire in whole or in part. For the control group, master’s and PhD candidates who had not participated in this course could join if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) voluntary completion of the questionnaire; (2) completion of the theory courses and internships in otolaryngology at the undergraduate level.

Participants in the experimental group uniformly took courses offered by the researchers in this project. The course consists of 12 sessions, each lasting 3 class hours (45 min per class hour). Participants worked in groups of 4 to complete course assignments and discussions. All courses were taught using the PBL format. Teachers assigned literature readings one week before class. During the course, a case from an outpatient, emergency, or ward setting was used as an example. The patient’s basic information, chief complaint, and simple physical examination results were provided. Students were then asked to further inquire about the medical history, order additional examinations, and provide preliminary diagnoses and treatment plans. This process required students to comprehensively apply their professional knowledge, testing the integrity of their knowledge framework. The teacher would supplement information regarding changes in the patient’s condition after treatment, which could involve improvement or worsening, to further assess the students’ ability to manage and respond to changes in the condition. Teaching effectiveness was evaluated through multiple-choice questions and short-answer questions during breaks and after class.

Interventions and comparisons

The interventions in this research consisted of the teaching content of the courses, and the subjective scoring data of the participants were compared. Students completed the online questionnaire by scanning a WeChat quick response code (as shown in Table 1).

The questionnaire was developed based on the research purpose and course objectives and was discussed and finalized by the project leader and the teachers. The questionnaire content focused on students’ evaluations/expectations of the course and their self-assessed abilities. To validate the questionnaire, multiple students were invited to complete the same questionnaire twice in the same environment in advance to evaluate the reproducibility of the results. Several teachers were also invited to evaluate the content validity, construct validity, and criterion validity of the questionnaire. Finally, additional students were recruited to pilot-test the questionnaire. After no obvious errors were identified, the questionnaire was officially used in the study.

The questionnaire included the following: years of exposure to otolaryngology; understanding and self-evaluation of thinking training in otolaryngology, functional otolaryngology, and the PBL teaching model; knowledge of the anatomy and symptoms of each otolaryngology subspecialty; understanding of ENT emergencies; and self-evaluation of competence in frontline work in otolaryngology. The exposure period was divided into < 1, 1–3, 3–6, and > 6 years, corresponding to scores 1–4. Other indicators were divided into a 1–10 point scale and subjectively scored by participants. The 10-point scale represents students’ quantitative subjective evaluation of the assessment items, with higher scores indicating higher evaluation. Specifically, 1–3 points, 4–6 points, 7–9 points, and 10 points were divided into four levels.

The course concluded with a closed-book exam lasting 2 h. The assessment consisted of two parts: one open-ended question based on an authentic clinical scenario (20 points), designed to evaluate clinical humanistic competency, and five case analysis questions (80 points) derived from PBL-based lessons, and five case analysis questions (80 points) presented in the course using the PBL teaching model. The latter included common cases from ear, nose, throat, and head and neck specialties, which had been confirmed not to overlap with cases used in the courses. The test results were recorded on a 100-point scale. Students in the control group completed the test under the same conditions and were quantitatively scored.

Data analysis and statistical methods

Except for the exposure years, all other indicators are statistically analyzed based on the before and after comparison as self-control. The participants’ pre-class scores were used for baseline analysis with the control group, and the former’s post-class scores were used for external control analysis with the control group. Normal distribution tests on the data have been applied before employing the t-test. The baseline comparison and test scores were analyzed using independent sample t-test, the experimental group’s pre-class and after-class data were analyzed using paired-samples t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. * indicated P < 0.05, ** indicated P < 0.01, *** indicated P < 0.001.

Results

A total of 33 scientific postgraduate students took the course and completed the questionnaire in this study, including 18 PhD candidates and 15 master’s degree candidates. Among them, 8 had less than 1 year of experience in otolaryngology, 10 had 1–3 years, 7 had 3–6 years, and 8 had more than 6 years. Another 33 students who did not take the course filled out the questionnaire, including 5 with < 1 year, 11 with 1–3 years, 12 with 3–6 years, and 5 with > 6 years. There was no statistical difference in exposure-year distribution between the two groups (p = 0.432).

As shown in Table 2, when the data from each section were directly compared between the two groups, baseline analysis showed differences in diagnostic thinking improvement (p = 0.035), PBL model role (p = 0.008), and self-ability confidence (p = 0.002), but not in functional otolaryngology understanding (p = 0.176), in the comparison between the control group and the students before the course. Except for functional otolaryngology understanding (p = 0.007), there was no statistical difference in diagnostic thinking (p = 0.514), PBL role (p = 0.968), or work confidence (p = 0.841) in the comparison of students before and after the course. Similarly, no statistical differences were found in the questionnaires on otology, rhinology, or laryngology anatomy and treatment based on the comparison of numbers between groups (Table 3).

When the pre-class and post-class data of the same subjects were analyzed, it was found that 45.5% (15/33) of students believed that their diagnostic thinking had improved, 51.5% (17/33) had a deeper understanding of functional otolaryngology, and 93.9% (31/33) believed that the PBL model played an important role. Additionally, 39.4% (13/33), 36.4% (12/33), and 33.3% (11/33) of students showed improved awareness of otology, rhinology, and laryngology, respectively, and 48.5% (16/33) of students strengthened their confidence in their ability to perform ENT work. There were significant differences in diagnostic thinking improvement (F = 5.318, p < 0.001), functional otolaryngology understanding (F = 3.373, p = 0.005), PBL model role (F = 7.051, p < 0.001), and self-ability confidence (F = 12.52, p < 0.001) (as shown in Fig. 1). Significant differences were also found in all subspecialty knowledge accumulation, including otology anatomy (F = 10.17, p < 0.001), otology disease (F = 5.815, p < 0.001), rhinology anatomy (F = 4.017, p < 0.001), rhinology disease (F = 6.419, p < 0.001), laryngology anatomy (F = 5.887, p < 0.001), and laryngology disease (F = 7.488, p < 0.001) (as shown in Fig. 2).

Discussion

The self-assessment analysis of course participants demonstrated that this curriculum positively contributed to reinforcing students’ conceptual understanding and specialized knowledge in otolaryngology. In the ENT concepts domain (Table 2), the most significant cognitive enhancement emerged in functional otolaryngology comprehension, evidenced by statistically significant distribution ratio changes (p = 0.007). Baseline comparisons between participants and non-participants revealed divergent evaluation patterns: non-enrolled students provided moderately positive ratings (scale 4–9), possibly reflecting social desirability bias, whereas course participants offered more nuanced assessments with greater score dispersion. Although statistical differences were observed between groups, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential confounding factors. Notably, participants had higher expectations and recognition of the PBL teaching model (p = 0.008), which served as the course’s foundational instructional framework. In ENT specialized knowledge acquisition (Fig. 2), self-assessment data indicated improvements in students’ understanding of otological, rhinological, and laryngological anatomy/pathology. However, comparative analyses with control groups showed no significant between-group differences in knowledge retention (Table 3), suggesting that substantial enhancement of professional knowledge bases cannot be realistically achieved through a single course intervention. This finding underscores the necessity for sustained knowledge integration through didactic instruction and self-directed learning.

Clinical case simulations revealed superior diagnostic performance in course participants compared to controls, indicating PBL’s efficacy in cultivating clinical reasoning skills among postgraduate trainees. While no statistical differences emerged in open-response items assessing liberal competencies, the persistent educational emphasis on patient-centered values, particularly respect and empathy, remains imperative and will continue to constitute a core curricular component in subsequent iterations.

We termed this innovative curriculum “Functional Otolaryngology”. Although this name has rarely appeared in previous literature reports, we believe that it fully encapsulates our understanding of advancements in otolaryngology. It emphasizes that functional protection of organs such as the ear, nose, and throat must be fully considered in the context of otolaryngology theory, specific diseases, surgical treatment, and pharmacotherapy. The two-semester program was exclusively designed for research-oriented postgraduate scholars, comprising 18 academic-track PhD candidates and 15 clinical MD trainees. Based on their subjective ratings, PhD candidates performed better in the course compared with MD candidates. This phenomenon is consistent with the current state of otolaryngology graduate education in China, as doctoral students have already accumulated a certain amount of otolaryngology knowledge and completed several months of frontline clinical work during their master’s degree training. From the feedback and suggestions submitted, students accepted the PBL teaching model but expressed a desire for the teaching content to place greater emphasis on explaining basic knowledge related to diseases, which also places higher demands on the instructors’ teaching methods.

In addition to the above-mentioned student feedback, the opinions and suggestions of other course participants can be summarized and analyzed in the following aspects: (1) Students advocated for a dual-focused didactic approach that strategically bridges fundamental pathophysiological principles with cutting-edge therapeutic advancements. This requirement reflects that students are interested in disciplinary developments in addition to gaining a comprehensive understanding of diseases to meet clinical needs. (2) Students expressed a desire for a more spacious teaching environment. Initial course delivery in a 100-seat lecture hall transitioned to a compact round-table configuration during the second semester to accommodate PBL requirements. This spatial reorganization, while presenting initial ergonomic challenges, effectively operationalized key PBL success factors through proximity-enhanced learning dynamics. Therefore, we plan to retain this setup in the teaching arrangements for the next semester.

Extensive empirical evidence documents the efficacy of the PBL model in graduate medical education, especially postgraduate education [7, 8]. However, little educational work has focused on the role of functional science in otolaryngology graduate education. Emphasizing the role of functional protection in the treatment of otolaryngology diseases represents an important sign of medical progress, grounded in advancements in organ pathophysiology, disease pathogenesis, and treatment methods. Specifically, the protection of hearing, balance, smell, voice quality, and swallowing function during disease treatment—especially during surgical intervention—is among the key approaches to improving patients’ quality of life and embodying medical humanistic care [9, 10]. Therefore, we believe that relevant concepts should be introduced to students as early as possible in postgraduate education so that they can pay sufficient attention to functional preservation during the formation of diagnostic and therapeutic thinking.

This research had the following shortcomings that need to be improved: (1) Although students who had not taken the course served as the control group, the comparison was designed to assess the overall impact of the course—which incorporates the PBL model—rather than the effect of PBL alone. This limitation stems from the scarcity of dedicated theoretical otolaryngology courses available to postgraduate students and the challenge of identifying graduates who have undergone a conventional otolaryngology curriculum. (2) The restricted sample size (n = 33) constrained statistical power, reflecting inherent challenges in otolaryngology postgraduate recruitment pools. As this study was a questionnaire survey centered around a postgraduate course, it was difficult to include a large sample within two semesters given the size of otolaryngology graduate enrollment. This course will continue to be developed and improved, and further statistical analysis will be conducted after expanding the sample size to verify the current conclusions. (3) This study relied on questionnaire-based analysis and evaluation. These data are subjective and may be subject to potential bias. (4) Single-center sampling introduced inherent in-group bias, and the scoring data obtained may not be as accurate as those derived from blinded analysis. This may also explain why pre-course baseline scores were more evenly distributed among course participants compared to non-participants. In future studies, we will expand enrollment and adopt a multicenter design by involving faculty from other institutions to implement a blinded study design.

Conclusion

The PBL teaching model might help to improve otolaryngology postgraduates’ understanding and learning of functional otolaryngology. Further studies with larger sample sizes and more evaluation methods are needed to verify PBL model in otolaryngology teaching work.
