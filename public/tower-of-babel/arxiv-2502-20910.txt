On diophantine properties for values of Dedekind zeta functions
Jerson Caro, Fabien Pazuki, Riccardo Pengo

Abstract
. We study the Northcott and Bogomolov property for special values of Dedekind ζ \zeta -functions at real values σ ∈ ℝ \sigma\in\mathbb{R} . We prove, in particular, that the Bogomolov property is not satisfied when σ ≥ 1 2 \sigma\geq\frac{1}{2} . If σ > 1 \sigma>1 , we produce certain families of number fields having arbitrarily large degrees, whose Dedekind ζ \zeta -functions ζ K ​ ( s ) \zeta_{K}(s) attain arbitrarily small values at s = σ s=\sigma . On the other hand, if 1 2 ≤ σ ≤ 1 \frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1 , we construct suitable families of quadratic number fields, employing either Soundararajan’s resonance method, which works when 1 2 ≤ σ < 1 \frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma<1 , or results on random Euler products by Granville and Soundararajan, and by Lamzouri, which work when 1 2 < σ ≤ 1 \frac{1}{2}<\sigma\leq 1 . We complete the study by proving that the Dedekind ζ \zeta function together with the degree satisfies the Northcott property for every complex s ∈ ℂ s\in{\mathbb{C}} such that Re ⁡ ( s ) < 0 \mathrm{Re}(s)<0 , generalizing previous work of Généreux and Lalín.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{N}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of number fields. For every
s\in\mathbb{C}
, we define the
Dedekind height
:

(1)

\displaystyle h_{s}\colon\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle[K]

\displaystyle\mapsto\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert,

where
\zeta^{\ast}_{K}(s)
denotes the
special value
of the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(z)
at
z=s
, which is classically defined as the first non-vanishing coefficient appearing in the Laurent series expansion of the meromorphic function
\zeta_{K}(z)
around the point
z=s
, as we recall in
Section

2.6
.
We are particularly interested in studying the topology of the set
h_{s}(\mathcal{N})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and in particular to know if
h_{s}
satisfies the
Northcott property
(
i.e.
if for each
B\geq 0
the set
\{[K]\in\mathcal{N}\colon h_{s}([K])\leq B\}
is finite) or the
Bogomolov property
(
i.e.
if the infimum of
h_{s}(\mathcal{N})
is isolated, and whether this infimum is a minimum).

1.1.
Historical remarks

When
s=n
is an integer, the function
h_{n}
was considered by the second and third-named authors of the present paper
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
, who proved that
h_{n}
has the Northcott property if
n\leq 0
, and does not have this property when
n\geq 1
.
In particular, when
n=0
this result is an immediate consequence of the celebrated Brauer-Siegel theorem
[
1
, Theorem 2]
, which compares the size of
\zeta^{\ast}_{K}(0)
to the size of the discriminant of
K
.

Généreux and Lalín
[
5
]
also considered the function
\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by
[K]\mapsto\lvert\zeta_{K}(s)\rvert
, which coincides with
h_{s}
for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that either
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
or
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
and
s\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
.
They have proven that this function does not have the Northcott property when
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
, but has this property for
s
lying in certain regions of the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
.

More generally, the last two authors of the present paper developed a research framework around the Northcott and Bogomolov properties of special values of
L
-functions
[
12
]
. From their first results, it seems that, for some natural families of
L
-functions, the Northcott property holds at integers on the left of the critical strip, and does not hold at integers on the right of the critical strip. What happens when
s\in\mathbb{C}
is not an integer, and in particular when
s
lies inside the critical strip, remains intriguing.

In the special case of the Dedekind zeta functions associated with function fields in positive characteristic, this analysis was extended to special values taken at any complex point
s\in\mathbb{C}
by Généreux, Lalín, and Li
[
6
]
. Moreover, Généreux and Lalín
[
5
]
considered the Northcott property for the special values at any complex number
s\in\mathbb{C}
of the Dedekind zeta functions associated to number fields, and proved in particular in
[
5
, Theorem 1.6]
that the Northcott property for the function
h_{s}
defined in (
1
) is not satisfied in the region
\frac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(s)<1
. Furthermore, they showed that the validity of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for the Dedekind zeta functions
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})}(s)
associated to real quadratic fields of prime discriminant implies that for every
B>0
and every
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(s)<1
the set
h_{s}(\mathcal{N})\cap[0,B]
is infinite, and in particular that
h_{s}
does not satisfy the Bogomolov property in the region
\frac{1}{2}<\mathrm{Re}(s)<1
. Let us note once again that these results do not deal with the heights
h_{s}
associated with complex numbers
s\in\mathbb{C}
lying on the critical line
\mathrm{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
, which however plays a special role, as the center of symmetry of all the functional equations satisfied by the Dedekind zeta functions
\zeta_{K}(s)
, which we recall in (
25
).

The aforementioned results, and in particular the interest in studying the difference between the values and the
special
values of Dedekind zeta functions, date back a long time. Indeed, in a short paper published in the first volume of Acta Arithmetica in 1935, Chowla
[
3
]
indicates that one can expect a non-vanishing result for certain values of the
L
-functions associated with non-trivial real characters
\chi
. More precisely, one can expect that
L(s,\chi)>0
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and Chowla proves a criterion to obtain this result. Chowla returns to this question in page xv and page 82 of
[
4
]
, pointing out that, thirty years after his original work
[
3
]
, this question was still open. We refer the interested reader to
[
10
]
for a more recent study. These results of Chowla help compare special values
\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)
with the actual values
\zeta_{K}(s)
, and therefore can be viewed as precursors of the present study, in a way.

1.2.
Main results

At present, we focus on the Northcott and Bogomolov properties
on the real line
. Concerning the Bogomolov property, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\sigma\in[\frac{1}{2},+\infty)
.
The function
h_{\sigma}\colon\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by
h_{\sigma}([K]):=\lvert\zeta^{\ast}_{K}(\sigma)\rvert
does not have the Bogomolov property. More precisely, for every
\sigma\in[\frac{1}{2},+\infty)
and every
B\in(\inf(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N})),+\infty)
the intersection
h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N})\cap(0,B)
is infinite.

The proof is divided into several tasks corresponding to the four intervals
\{\frac{1}{2}\}
(the center of the critical strip),
(\frac{1}{2},1)
(the interior of the critical strip),
\{1\}
(the boundary of the critical strip), and
(1,+\infty)
(the region of absolute convergence), each requiring different methods of study. More precisely:

•

on the critical line,
i.e.
when
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
, we prove
Theorem

1.1
by studying the second moment of the resonator coefficients constructed by Soundararajan
[
14
]
for
L
-functions attached to quadratic characters;

•

inside the critical strip, but not on the critical line,
i.e.
when
\sigma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, we prove
Theorem

1.1
either by adapting Soundararajan’s resonator method or by employing previous work of Lamzouri
[
9
]
, concerning the similarities between the distribution of values of quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions and random Euler products;

•

on the boundary of the critical strip,
i.e.
when
\sigma=1
, we use previous work of Granville and Soundararajan
[
7
]
, which proves once again that special values of quadratic
L
-functions at
\sigma=1
are distributed like random Euler products;

•

in the region of absolute convergence,
i.e.
when
\sigma\in(1,+\infty)
, we prove
Theorem

1.1
by constructing explicit families of number fields
\{K_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
[K_{d}\colon\mathbb{Q}]\to+\infty
and
\zeta_{K_{d}}(\sigma)\to 1
as
d\to+\infty
. We do so by imposing that many rational primes stay inert in each field
K_{d}
.

Let us be more precise. To prove
Theorem

1.1
in
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
, we focus on quadratic fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
, where
d
is a fundamental discriminant. By Artin’s formalism, for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
and every fundamental discriminant
d
we have that

(2)

\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}(s)=\zeta(s)\cdot L(s,\chi_{d}),

where
\zeta(s)=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}}(s)
denotes Riemann’s zeta function, while
L(s,\chi_{d})
denotes the
L
-function associated to the quadratic Dirichlet character
\chi_{d}
, whose definition is recalled in
Section

2
. This decomposition shows that, in order to prove that
h_{\frac{1}{2}}
does not have the Bogomolov property, it suffices to show that the image of the function
d\mapsto\left\lvert L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\right\rvert
, defined over the set of fundamental discriminants, accumulates towards zero.
This is precisely what we do in
Section

3
, where we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem for
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
)
.

There exists an absolute constant
c_{0}
such that for every
X\geq c_{0}
there exists a fundamental discriminant
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X\leq d\leq 2X
and

(3)

0<\left\lvert L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\right\rvert\leq\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right),

where
\chi_{d}
denotes the real primitive character associated with the fundamental discriminant
d
. In particular
\inf(h_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{N}))=0
, and the function
h_{s}
defined in (
1
) does not have the Bogomolov property when
s=\frac{1}{2}
.

This theorem implies the existence of a family of fundamental discriminants whose associated Dirichlet
L
-functions attain strictly positive values at
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
that tend to
0
. This implies that
0
is the infimum and an accumulation point of the image of
h_{\frac{1}{2}}
. Therefore, this function does not have the Bogomolov property.

The strategy to prove
Theorem

1.2
is based on the resonators method used by Soundararajan
[
14
]
. Our main contribution is provided by
Theorem

3.2
, where we compute the second moment associated with a resonator function.

Remark 1.3
.

Soundararajan already studied the function
d\mapsto L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)
in his work
[
14
]
. However,

[
14
, Theorem 2]
only implies that for
X
sufficiently large, there exists a fundamental discriminant
d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
X\leq|d|\leq 2X
and

(4)

L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right).

While Soundararajan originally expressed this bound with the absolute value
\lvert L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\rvert
on the left-hand side of (
4
), the authors of the present paper noticed that the formulation without the absolute value is the correct implication of
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. Furthermore, Soundararajan suggested in private communication that the authors of the present paper explore the second moment associated with the resonator function to strengthen the analysis. Soundararajan also noted that assuming Chowla’s conjecture would lead to
Theorem

1.2
. Building on these insights, we unconditionally prove
Theorem

1.2
, as explained in
Section

3
.

In
Section

4
, we extend the resonator method for every
\sigma
in the interval
\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. This allows us to prove an analogous result to
Theorem

1.2
, which, by (
2
), implies
Theorem

1.1
in this interval, that is, that
h_{\sigma}
does not satisfy the Bogomolov property for
\sigma\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
.

Theorem 1.4

(Main Theorem for
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
)
.

For every
\sigma\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
there exists a constant
c_{1}(\sigma)
such that for every
X\geq c_{1}(\sigma)
there exists a fundamental discriminant
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
X\leq d\leq\frac{5}{2}X
and

0<|L(\sigma,\chi_{d})|\leq\exp\left(-\left(4\sigma+o(1)\right)\frac{\log^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2\sigma}}X}{\log\log X}\right),

where
\chi_{d}
denotes the real primitive character associated with the fundamental discriminant
d
.
In particular, for every real number
\sigma\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
, we have that
\inf(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N}))=0
, and the function
h_{s}
defined in (
1
) does not have the Bogomolov property when
s=\sigma
.

Remark 1.5
.

The natural questions concerning the effectivity of the bounds provided by
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.4
still have to be explored.

When
\sigma=1
, we employ a result of Granville and Soundararajan
[
7
]
, recalled in
Proposition

4.10
, to show that
h_{\sigma}=h_{1}
does not have the Bogomolov property.
Finally, if
\sigma\in(1,\infty)
, we have that
\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(\sigma)=\zeta_{K}(\sigma)\geq 1
for every number field
K
. In
Section

5.1
, we construct a family of number fields depending on
\sigma
, such that the values of the Dedekind zeta function at
\sigma
tend to
1
.

Theorem 1.6

(Main Theorem for
\sigma>1
)
.

For every
\sigma\in(1,+\infty)
there exist two families of number fields
\{K_{d,\sigma},K_{d,\sigma}^{\prime}\}
such that
\zeta_{K_{d,\sigma}}(\sigma)\to 1
and
\zeta_{K_{d,\sigma}^{\prime}}(\sigma)\to+\infty
as
d\to+\infty
. In particular, the function
h_{s}
defined in (
1
) does not have the Bogomolov property when
s=\sigma
is a real number such that
\sigma>1
.

Let us now turn to the Northcott property. Généreux, Lalín, and Li
[
6
]
considered the analogous problem for isomorphism classes of function fields
K
with constant base field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In particular, they proved that
h_{s}
has the Northcott property when
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
. In contrast, Généreux and Lalín
[
5
]
demonstrated that the Dedekind zeta function (not its special value, simply its value at the considered point, but note that the two coincide for
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
and
s\not\in\mathbb{Z}
) exhibits different behavior for
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
. Specifically, they showed that close to the negative integers,
h_{s}
does not have the Northcott property, whereas far from these points, it does. In this context, we observe that
h_{s}
has the Northcott property if one bounds furthermore the degrees of the number fields involved.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
s
be a complex number with
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
. Then, the function

[K]\mapsto\left(\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert,[K\colon\mathbb{Q}]\right)

has the Northcott property.

In
Section

5.2
, we prove
Theorem

1.7
by directly analyzing the functional equation satisfied by this function.

Remark 1.8
.

In fact, the proof of
Theorem

1.7
shows that if
\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert
is bounded then the
root discriminant

\delta_{K}:=\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert^{\frac{1}{[K\colon\mathbb{Q}]}}
is also bounded. This entails that the only families of number fields violating the Northcott property of the Dedekind height
h_{s}
when
\mathrm{Re}(s)<0
are built from towers of number fields having bounded root discriminant, as those considered by Généreux and Lalín in
[
5
]
.

2.
Notation

The aim of the present section is to gather several pieces of notation that will be used in the main body of the present paper.

2.1.
Numbers

We let
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
denote the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{Z}
denote the ring of rational integers,
\mathbb{Q}
denote the field of rational numbers,
\mathbb{R}
denote the field of real numbers and
\mathbb{C}
denote the field of complex numbers.
Given a complex number
s\in\mathbb{C}
we let
\sigma=\mathrm{Re}(s)
be its real part, and we let
t=\mathrm{Im}(s)
be its imaginary part, so that
s=\sigma+it
, where
i
denotes the imaginary unit.

2.2.
Asymptotics

Let
S
be a topological space and
f,g\colon S\to\mathbb{C}
be two functions. Then:

•

given
s_{0}\in S
, we write
f(s)=O(g(s))
for
s\to s_{0}
, or equivalently
f(s)\ll g(s)
for
s\to s_{0}
, if and only if there exists an open subset
U\subseteq S
such that
s_{0}\in U
, and a real number
C>0
such that
|f(s)|\leq C|g(s)|
for every
s\in U
;

•

given
s_{0}\in S
, we write
f(s)=o(g(s))
for
s\to s_{0}
if there exists an open subset
U\subseteq S
such that
s_{0}\in U
, and a function
\varepsilon\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
, such that
f(s)=\varepsilon(s)g(s)
for every
s\in U
, and
\displaystyle{\lim_{s\to s_{0}}\varepsilon(s)=0}
;

•

given
s_{0}\in S
, we write
f(s)\sim g(s)
for
s\to s_{0}
if there exists an open subset
U\subseteq S
such that
s_{0}\in U
, and a function
h\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
, such that
f(s)=g(s)(1+h(s))
for
s\in U
, and
h(s)=o(1)
for
s\to s_{0}
.

When
S=\mathbb{R}
or
S=\mathbb{C}
, these definitions readily generalize to include
s_{0}=\infty
. Moreover, if
f,g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
then
f(n)\ll g(n)
as
n\to+\infty
if and only if there exists
C>0
such that
\lvert f(n)\rvert\leq C\lvert g(n)\rvert
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case, we will avoid writing
n\to+\infty
and simply write
f(n)\ll g(n)
.

2.3.
Constants

We say that a real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
appearing in some expression that bounds a function
f
is an
absolute constant
if it does not depend on any of the variables or parameters featured in the definition of
f
. In the same context, we will say that a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{R}[x]
appearing in some expression bounding
f
is an

if none of its coefficients depends on any of the variables or parameters featured in the definition of
f
.

2.5.
Arithmetic functions

\mathrm{gcd}(a,b)=1

\mathrm{gcd}
denotes the greatest common divisor. Furthermore, we say that
f
is
completely multiplicative
if
f(ab)=f(a)f(b)
for every
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
. Analogously, a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is
additive
if
f(ab)=f(a)+f(b)
for every
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\gcd(a,b)=1
, and
f
is
completely additive
if
f(ab)=f(a)+f(b)
for every
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the present paper, we let
\mu
denote the
Möbius function
, the unique multiplicative function defined on prime powers as

(13)

\mu(p^{k}):=\begin{cases}1,\ &\text{if}\ k=0\\
-1,\ &\text{if}\ k=1\\
0,\ &\text{if}\ k\geq 2\end{cases}

for every rational prime
p
and every integer
k\geq 0
. Moreover, we let
\tau
denote the number of divisors function, which is the multiplicative function defined as

(14)

\tau(n):=\#\{m\in\mathbb{N}\colon m\mid n\}=\sum_{m\mid n}1

for every natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, we let
\sigma
denote the
sum of divisors
function, which is the multiplicative function defined as

(15)

\sigma(n):=\sum_{m\mid n}m

for every natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Finally, we will let
\omega
be the additive function that counts the number of distinct prime factors of an integer, defined as

\omega(n):=\#\{p\ \text{prime}\colon p\mid n\}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}1

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We will also need a number of non-standard arithmetic functions. First of all, we denote by
h
the unique multiplicative function defined as

(16)

h(p^{k})=1+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{4}{p(p+1)},

for every rational prime
p
and every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, given
D>0
we denote by
\epsilon_{D}
the multiplicative function defined as

(17)

\epsilon_{D}(n):=D^{\omega(n)}

for every integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We need as well a completely multiplicative function
f
that depends on two fixed real numbers
L>0
and
\sigma\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. When
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
, we define

(18)

f(p):=\begin{cases}\frac{L}{\sqrt{p}\log p},\ &\text{if}\ L^{2}\leq p\leq\exp(\log^{2}X),\\
0,\ &\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for every rational prime
p
. On the other hand, when
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
, we define

(19)

f(p):=\begin{cases}Lp^{-\sigma},\ &\text{if}\ p\geq L^{1/\sigma}\\
0,\ &\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for every rational prime
p
. Since
f
is completely multiplicative, these definitions determine it completely.

We need three more multiplicative functions
F,G
and
H
, defined as

(20)

\displaystyle F(p^{k})

\displaystyle:=1+\frac{f(p)^{2}}{h(p)}-4\frac{f(p)\sqrt{p}}{h(p)(p+1)}

(21)

\displaystyle G(p^{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\log p}{p^{2}}\frac{f(p)^{2}}{h(p)F(p)}

(22)

\displaystyle H(p^{k})

\displaystyle:=-4\log^{k}p\frac{f(p)\sqrt{p}}{h(p)(p+1)F(p)},

for every rational prime
p
and every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
These functions are defined in analogy with
[
14
, page 481]
. Moreover, the functions
G
and
H
are supported on natural numbers whose prime factors lie in the interval
[L^{2},\exp(\log^{2}L)]
, where
L
is the same parameter used in the definition of
f
. Finally, we will need the arithmetic function
M
defined as

(23)

M(n)=\frac{1}{\omega(n)}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{1,1}(p)}{p}\right)H(n),

for every integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1,1}
is the function appearing in (
12
).

2.6.
Dedekind zeta functions

Let
K
be a number field. The
Dedekind zeta function

\zeta_{K}(s)
is defined for complex numbers
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma>1
by the series:

(24)

\zeta_{K}(s)=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})^{s}},

where the sum is over all nonzero ideals
\mathfrak{a}
of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
in
K
, and
\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})
denotes the index
[\mathcal{O}_{K}\colon\mathfrak{a}]
.
Let
\Delta_{K}
denote the absolute discriminant of
K
, and let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
denote the number of real and complex places of
K
, respectively.
The Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(s)
extends to a meromorphic function on the complex plane with a simple pole at
s=1
, and satisfies the functional equation

(25)

\zeta_{K}(s)=\zeta_{K}(1-s)\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(1-s)}{\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)}\right)^{r_{1}}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(1-s)}{\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s)}\right)^{r_{2}}|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}-s}.

The following function

\zeta_{K}^{*}(s):=\lim_{z\to s}\frac{\zeta_{K}(z)}{(z-s)^{\mathrm{ord}_{z=s}(\zeta_{K}(z))}},

which assigns to every complex number
s\in\mathbb{C}
the
special value
of the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}
at
s
, plays the main role in the present paper.

Finally, given a quadratic fundamental discriminant
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\chi_{d}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\{\pm 1\}
be the quadratic Dirichlet character defined as

\chi_{d}(n):=\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\left(\frac{d}{\cdot}\right)
denotes the Kronecker symbol. We can associate to
\chi_{d}
the
Dirichlet
L
-function

L(s,\chi_{d})
, defined for complex numbers
s
with
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
by the following series:

(26)

L(s,\chi_{d})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{d}(n)}{n^{s}}.

This Dirichlet series extends to an entire function
L(s,\chi_{d})
, defined for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
. By employing the Artin formalism, we obtain the following relation for a quadratic field
K
with discriminant
\Delta_{K}=d
:

\zeta_{K}(s)=\zeta(s)\cdot L(s,\chi_{d}),

where
\zeta(s):=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}}(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

We will, in particular, be interested in several moments associated with these
L
-functions associated with Dirichlet characters. For instance, we will study the asymptotic behavior of the function

(27)

M_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(l;X):=\sum_{d=1}^{+\infty}\mu(2d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{1},\chi_{8d}\right)L\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{2},\chi_{8d}\right)\chi_{8d}(l)\Phi\left(\frac{d}{X}\right)

when
X\to+\infty
. This function
M_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(l;X)
was introduced by Sono in
[
13
]
, and depends on two parameters
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. Moreover, the notation
\Phi
featured in (
27
) refers to the function
\Phi
defined in (
7
). In particular, the sum appearing in (
27
) is always finite.

3.
On the critical line

The aim of this section is to employ the resonance method of Soundararajan
[
14
]
to show that the function

\displaystyle h_{\frac{1}{2}}\colon\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle[K]

\displaystyle\mapsto\left\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\rvert

does not have the Bogomolov (or Northcott) property, thereby proving

.
To do so, let us first define

(34)

R(8d):=\sum_{l\leq N}r(l)\chi_{8d}(l),

where

is a parameter to be specified later. This function

is called a “resonator”, of the kind used in
[

.
The coefficients

are to be chosen in such a way that

“resonates” with the large values of

More precisely, we will take

(35)

r(l):=\mu(l)f(l),

where

), and

is the multiplicative function introduced in (

), where the parameter

[

[

The resonator

\displaystyle M_{1}(R,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right),

\displaystyle M_{2}(R,X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)^{2},

in the same spirit as

The asymptotic behavior of these two moments when

can be determined explicitly if one chooses the parameters

and

this has been done by Soundararajan, who obtained the following result.

Theorem 3.1

(Soundararajan)
.

Let
X>0
be large,
N\leq X^{\frac{1}{20}-\varepsilon}
be large, and set

. Let

M_{1}(R,X)\sim c_{4}X(\log X)\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

for some positive absolute constant
c_{4}
, where
M_{1}(R,X)
is defined as in (
36
).

On the other hand, the following asymptotic for the second resonated moment

is the main technical result of the present section.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X>0
be large,
N\leq X^{\frac{1}{20}-\varepsilon}
be large, and set
L=\sqrt{\log N\log\log N}
. Let
f
be defined as in (
18
),
r
be defined as in (
35
), and
R
be defined as in (

). Then, as

we have that

(39)

M_{2}(R,X)\sim c_{5}X(\log X)^{3}\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(1+f(p)^{2}-4\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

where
c_{5}
is the absolute constant defined in (
29
) and
M_{2}(R,X)
is defined as in (
37
).

Before we proceed with the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, let us see how this result can be combined with
Theorem

3.1
in order to obtain a proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Suppose by contradiction that there exists some
X>0
large such that for no fundamental discriminant
d
with
X\leq d\leq 2X
, the two inequalities portrayed in (
3
) hold true.
This is obviously equivalent to assuming that there exists some large
X>0
such that for every fundamental discriminant
d
with
\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}
we have that either

\left\lvert L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)\right\rvert>\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right)

or
L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)=0
. Under this assumption, we would have that

(40)

\displaystyle{\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)^{2}}>\exp\left(-\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right)\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}\\
L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}},

which would negate the asymptotics provided by
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
. To see this, we will use the elementary inequality

(41)

\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{b_{1}}+\dots+\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{b_{n}}\geq\frac{(a_{1}+\dots+a_{n})^{2}}{b_{1}+\dots+b_{n}},

which is valid for any real numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and any positive real numbers
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
. Note that (
41
) follows easily from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and is known under the names of Sedrakyan’s inequality, Engel’s form, Bergström’s inequality, or Titu’s lemma.
Applying (
41
), we see that:

\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}\\
L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}}\geq\frac{\displaystyle{\left(\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)\right)^{2}}}{\displaystyle{\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}\Big|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)\Big|^{2}}}=\frac{M_{1}(R,X)^{2}}{M_{2}(R,X)},

which implies that

(42)

\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)^{2}}}{\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}\\
L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{d}\right)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}}}\leq\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X)}{M_{1}(R,X)}\right)^{2}.

Combining (
42
) with (
40
) we see that if the conclusion of
Theorem

1.2
were false we would have that

(43)

\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X)}{M_{1}(R,X)}\right)^{2}>\exp\left(-\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right)

for some
X>0
large.
This, however, contradicts the conclusions of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
, which imply that

\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X)}{M_{1}(R,X)}\right)^{2}\sim\frac{c_{5}^{2}}{c_{4}}(\log^{4}X)\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(\frac{1+f(p)^{2}-4\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)^{2}

for every
X>0
large, and for every
N\leq X^{\frac{1}{20}-\varepsilon}
which is also large.
Indeed, notice that

(44)

\begin{split}(\log^{4}X)\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(\frac{1+f(p)^{2}-4\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)^{2}&=(\log^{4}X)\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(1-\frac{2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)^{2}\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X+2\sum_{p\ \text{prime}}\log\left(1-\frac{2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)\right)\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sum_{p\ \text{prime}}\frac{\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{f(p)}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
L^{2}\leq p\leq\exp(\log^{2}L)\end{subarray}}\frac{L}{p\log p}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sqrt{\log N\log\log N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
L^{2}\leq p\leq\exp(\log^{2}L)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p\log p}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\right),\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the prime number theorem.
Given that this computation will recur in several forthcoming results, we take this first occurrence as an opportunity to present it in full detail. More precisely, let
\pi(t):=\#\{p\ \text{prime}\colon p\leq t\}
be the prime counting function, let
\mathrm{Li}(t):=\int_{2}^{t}\frac{du}{\log u}
be the offset logarithmic integral function, and let
R(t):=\pi(t)-\mathrm{Li}(t)
. Then, we have that

(45)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
L^{2}\leq p\leq\exp(\log^{2}L)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p\log p}

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{d\pi(t)}{t\log t}=\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{d\mathrm{Li}(t)}{t\log t}+\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{dR(t)}{t\log t}

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{dt}{t\log^{2}t}+\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{dR(t)}{t\log t}

where the integrals appearing in these formulas are taken in the sense of Riemann-Stieltjes. Now, an elementary calculation shows that

(46)

\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{dt}{t\log^{2}t}=\frac{1}{2\log L}-\frac{1}{\log^{2}L}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log N},

because
L=\sqrt{\log N\log\log N}
.
Moreover, if we apply integration by parts to the last integral appearing in (
45
) we see that

(47)

\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{dR(t)}{t\log t}=\frac{R(\exp(\log^{2}L))}{\exp(\log^{2}L)\log^{2}L}-\frac{R(L^{2})}{2L^{2}\log L}+\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}R(t)d\left(-\frac{1}{t\log t}\right).

Now, the prime number theorem asserts that
R(t)\ll_{A}\frac{t}{\log^{A}t}
for every
A>0
, as explained for example in
[
8
, Section 2.4]
. Using this upper bound, we see easily that

(48)

\frac{R(\exp(\log^{2}L))}{\exp(\log^{2}L)\log^{2}L}-\frac{R(L^{2})}{2L^{2}\log L}\ll\frac{1}{\log^{2}L}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log^{2}N}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log N}

Finally, we can apply the prime number theorem again to see that

(49)

\displaystyle\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}R(t)d\left(-\frac{1}{t\log t}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{R(t)(\log t+1)}{t^{2}\log^{2}t}dt

\displaystyle\ll\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}L)}\frac{\log t+1}{t^{2}\log^{3}t}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\log^{2}L}+\frac{1}{2\log L}-\frac{1}{2\log^{4}L}-\frac{1}{\log^{2}L}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\log\log N}.

Plugging (
48
) and (
49
) into (
47
), we now see that

(50)

\int_{L^{2}}^{\exp(\log^{2}(L))}\frac{dR(t)}{t\log t}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log N}.

Moreover, plugging (
50
) and (
46
) into (
45
) we finally see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
L^{2}\leq p\leq\exp(\log^{2}(L))\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p\log p}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log N},

which implies the last equality portrayed in (
44
).
Taking
N=X^{\frac{1}{20}-\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X)}{M_{1}(R,X)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(4\log\log X-(4+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(4+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log\log X}}\right),

for every
X>0
large. This shows that (
43
) cannot be true, and therefore concludes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

]
and

]

Proof of

By

L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi_{8d}\right)^{2}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\chi_{8d}(n)\cdot W_{2}\left(\frac{n\pi}{8d}\right),

where

Substituting the explicit expression for

given by (

given in (

given in (

Therefore, we see that

\displaystyle M_{2}(R,X,\sigma)

\displaystyle\sim c_{18}(\sigma)X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,r,s\\
ar,as\leq X\\
(a,r)=(a,s)=(r,s)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(a)^{2}f(a)^{2}\frac{\mu(r)f(r)}{r^{\sigma}}\frac{\mu(s)f(s)}{s^{\sigma}}\sum_{m\text{ odd}}\frac{\tau(rsm^{2})}{m^{2\sigma}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
p\mid mars\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{p}{p+1}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{19}(\sigma)X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,r,s\\
ar,as\leq X\\
(a,r)=(a,s)=(r,s)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(a)^{2}f(a)^{2}\frac{\tau(r)\mu(r)f(r)}{r^{\sigma}}\frac{\tau(s)\mu(s)f(s)}{s^{\sigma}}\eta(2\sigma;a^{2}rs),

where the second equality follows by an application of (
55
) with
w=\sigma-\frac{1}{2}
, and therefore
c_{19}(\sigma)=c_{18}(\sigma)\cdot\zeta(2\sigma)^{3}
.
To conclude, note that the asymptotic behavior of the last sum does not change if we extend it over all the integers
a,r,s
, thanks to
Lemma

4.1
applied with
D=2
, which shows that the tail of this sum is little-
o
of the right-hand side of (
100
).
Moreover, recall that
\eta(\alpha;l)\leq 1
for every integer
l
and every
\alpha>1
, as follows from the definitions (
10
).
Therefore, there exist two positive constants
c_{16}(\sigma)
and
c_{17}(\sigma)
such that for every
X\geq c_{17}(\sigma)
we have that

\displaystyle M_{2}(R,X,\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq c_{16}(\sigma)X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,r,s\\
(a,r)=(a,s)=(r,s)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(a)^{2}f(a)^{2}\frac{\tau(r)\mu(r)f(r)}{r^{\sigma}}\frac{\tau(s)\mu(s)f(s)}{s^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=c_{16}(\sigma)X\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)\tau(p)}{p^{\sigma}}\right)=c_{16}(\sigma)X\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1+f(p)^{2}-4\frac{f(p)}{p^{\sigma}}\right),

which allows us to conclude.
∎

Final step:
The following corollary shows that for every
\sigma\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
the function
h_{\sigma}
does not have the Bogomolov property.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Using (
41
) we see that

\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}L(\sigma,\chi_{d})^{2}}}{\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{X}{16}\leq d\leq\frac{X}{8}\\
L(\sigma,\chi_{d})\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(2d)^{2}R(8d)^{2}}}\leq\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X,\sigma)}{M_{1}(R,X,\sigma)}\right)^{2}.

By
Theorem

4.4
and
Proposition

4.6
we have that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{M_{2}(R,X,\sigma)}{M_{1}(R,X,\sigma)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{16}(\sigma)}{c_{14}(\sigma)}\left(\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{1+f(p)^{2}-4\frac{f(p)}{p^{\sigma}}}{1+f(p)^{2}-2\frac{f(p)}{p^{\sigma}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{16}(\sigma)}{c_{14}(\sigma)}\exp\left(-\left(8\sigma+o(1)\right)\frac{\log^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2\sigma}}X}{\log\log X}\right),

which gives the desired result.
∎

4.2.
Random Euler products

In the previous subsection we have proven that for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
, the restriction of the Dedekind height
h_{\sigma}\colon\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}
to the subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
of isomorphism classes of quadratic fields does not satisfy the Bogomolov property. In this particular case, such a result suffices to conclude that
h_{\sigma}
does not satisfy the Bogomolov property
tout court
, because
h_{\sigma}([K]):=\lvert\zeta_{K}(\sigma)\rvert\geq 0
for every
[K]\in\mathcal{N}
, and we have proven in the previous section that
h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Q})
accumulates towards zero. This implies in particular that for every
B>0
, the set
\{K\in\mathcal{Q}\colon h_{\sigma}(K)<B\}
is non-empty. Since this happens
for every

B>0
, these sets are, in fact, infinite. However, such a set could have density zero inside the set of all quadratic fields
\mathcal{Q}
, ordered by discriminant. The following proposition, proven by Lamzouri
[
9
]
, shows that this is not the case and gives an expression for the aforementioned density, which is completely explicit up to a logarithmic error term.

Proposition 4.7

(Lamzouri)
.

Fix
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to+\infty}\frac{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X,\ h_{\sigma}(K)\leq B\}}{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X\}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-c_{20}(\sigma)\log\left(\frac{\zeta(\sigma)}{B}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}\log\left(\log\left(\frac{\zeta(\sigma)}{B}\right)\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}\left(1+O\left(\log\log\left(\frac{\zeta(\sigma)}{B}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right),

as
B\to 0
, where
c_{20}(\sigma)
is the constant defined in (
31
).

Proof.

Lamzouri showed in
[
9
, Theorem 1.6]
that the distribution of the
L
-values
L(\sigma,\chi_{d})
as
d
varies amongst all the fundamental discriminants resembles that of a random Euler product

L(\sigma,\mathbf{X}):=\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{X}_{p}}{p^{\sigma}}\right)^{-1},

where
\{\mathbf{X}_{p}\}_{p}
denotes a family of independent random variables, each of which assumes the values
1
and
-1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. This resemblance is particularly true for the moments of these distributions, as proven in
[
9
, Proposition 5.2]
, and therefore for the tails of the absolute values of these distributions. Since the random variables
\mathbf{X}_{p}
are symmetric,
i.e.

\mathbf{X}_{p}
and
-\mathbf{X}_{p}
are identically distributed, the upper and lower tails of the random variable
\lvert L(\sigma,\mathbf{X})\rvert
coincide, as observed in
[
9
, Remark 4]
, and their asymptotic value is determined by
[
9
, Theorem 1.9]
. In fact, this result, as well as
[
9
, Theorem 1.6]
, is more precise, providing as well an explicit error term for the distribution of the truncated Euler products

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\ \text{prime}\\
p\leq y\end{subarray}}(1-\mathbf{X}_{p}p^{-\sigma})^{-1},

for any fixed
y>0
. Putting everything together, and letting
y\to+\infty
, we obtain a proof of
Proposition

4.7
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

As we already observed, we have that
\sup(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Q}))=+\infty
for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<1
. Moreover,
[
9
, Theorem 1.6]
shows that

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to+\infty}\frac{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X,\ h_{\sigma}(K)\geq B\}}{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X\}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-c_{20}(\sigma)\log\left(\frac{B}{\zeta(\sigma)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}\log\left(\log\left(\frac{B}{\zeta(\sigma)}\right)\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}\left(1+O\left(\log\log\left(\frac{B}{\zeta(\sigma)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right)\right),

for every
B>0
which is sufficiently big with respect to
\sigma
.

Remark 4.9
.

We underline that
Proposition

4.7
does not assume the validity of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, even though other parts of Lamzouri’s article
[
9
]
do.

What happens when
\sigma=1
? In this case, the sets
\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon h_{1}(K)\leq B\}
have again positive density, as was shown by Granville and Soundararajan
[
7
]
.

Proposition 4.10

(Granville & Soundararajan)
.

When
B\to 0
one has that

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to+\infty}\frac{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X,\ h_{1}(K)\leq B\}}{\#\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert\leq X\}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-c_{21}\exp\left(\frac{\zeta(2)}{Be^{\gamma_{0}}}\right)\left(\frac{Be^{\gamma_{0}}}{\zeta(2)}\right)+O\left(\exp\left(\frac{\zeta(2)}{Be^{\gamma_{0}}}\right)\left(\frac{Be^{\gamma_{0}}}{\zeta(2)}\right)^{2}\right)\right),

where
c_{21}
is the absolute constant defined in (
32
), while
\gamma_{0}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, defined by setting
j=0
in (
33
).

Proof.

This result can be obtained by combining
[
7
, Proposition 1]
, which computes the distribution of some random Euler products evaluated at
s=1
, together with
[
7
, Theorem 2]
, which implies, letting
x\to+\infty
, that the values of Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(1,\chi_{d})
have exactly the distribution of the random Euler products considered in
[
7
, Proposition 1]
.
∎

To conclude this section, let us note once more that combining
Proposition

4.7
and
Proposition

4.10
gives an alternative proof of
Theorem

1.4
, which moreover has the advantage of showing that the density of the set of quadratic fields
K
such that
\lvert\zeta_{K}(\sigma)\rvert\leq B
is always positive, for every
B>0
and every real number
\sigma
such that
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma\leq 1
.

Remark 4.11
.

Note that

\lim_{\sigma\to\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\log(\cosh(x))}{x^{1+\frac{1}{\sigma}}}dx=\lim_{\sigma\to 1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\log(\cosh(x))}{x^{1+\frac{1}{\sigma}}}dx=+\infty,

which implies that
c_{20}(\sigma)\to 0
as
\sigma\to\frac{1}{2}
, while
c_{20}(\sigma)\to+\infty
as
\sigma\to 1
, where
c_{20}(\sigma)
is the constant defined in (
31
), which appears in
Proposition

4.7
. This does not imply that the sets
\{[K]\in\mathcal{Q}\colon h_{1/2}(K)\leq B\}
have density zero,
but it is just another indication of the fact that Lamzouri’s proof cannot be “continuously deformed” to
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
or to
\sigma=1
.

5.
Outside the critical strip

This section aims to use explicit bounds for the Dedekind
\zeta
-functions
\zeta_{K}(s)
in the region of absolute convergence, as well as their functional equations, to prove that for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
the Dedekind height
h_{s}
defined in (
1
) does not have the Bogomolov (or Northcott) property if
s=\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>1
. On the other hand, we will show that if we restrict this function to classes of number fields having bounded degree, then
h_{s}
has even the Northcott property if
s\in\mathbb{C}
is any complex number such that
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leq 0
. This generalizes some results of
[
12
]
, which dealt with integers
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, and some results proven by Généreux and Lalín
[
5
]
.

5.1.
The right of the critical strip

In this section, we study the Diophantine properties of the special values of Dedekind zeta functions at real numbers
\sigma>1
. Since the Euler product defining
\zeta_{K}(\sigma)
converges absolutely on this half line, we have
\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(\sigma)=\zeta_{K}(\sigma)
for every
\sigma>1
. In this case,
Theorem

1.6
claims that the Dedekind height
h_{\sigma}
defined in (
1
) does not satisfy the Bogomolov property, henceforth generalizing
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
, which proved that it does not have the Northcott property. We devote the rest of this sub-section to giving proof of this result.

Proof of
Theorem

1.6
.

Note first of all that

h_{\sigma}([K])=\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(\sigma)\rvert=\lvert\zeta_{K}(\sigma)\rvert=\zeta_{K}(\sigma)=1+\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\#\{I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\colon\lvert\mathcal{O}_{K}/I\rvert=n\}}{n^{\sigma}}

for every
\sigma>1
and
[K]\in\mathcal{N}
. This shows immediately that
h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>1}
, as claimed.

Let us now show that
\inf(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N}))=1
, by constructing a sequence of number fields
\{K_{d}\}_{d=1}^{+\infty}
such that

\zeta_{K_{d}}(\sigma)\approx\zeta(d\sigma)

as
d\to+\infty
, which allows us to conclude because
\zeta(d\sigma)\to 1
as
d\to+\infty
.
To construct the number fields
K_{d}
, let
p_{1}=2,p_{2}=3,\dots
be the sequence of rational prime numbers. Then, for every pair of integers
d,n\geq 1
, there exists a monic polynomial
\psi_{d,n}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
which has degree
d
and is irreducible modulo every prime
p_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. To construct
\psi_{d,n}
, it suffices to choose some monic polynomials
\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}[x],\dots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p_{n}}[x]
which have degree
d
and are irreducible. Then, applying the Chinese remainder theorem coefficient-wise, we can construct a monic polynomial
\psi_{n,d}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
which has degree
d
and is congruent to
\beta_{j}
modulo each prime
p_{j}
. This polynomial
\psi_{d,n}
is necessarily irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
and defines a number field
F_{d,n}:=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\psi_{d,n})
of degree
d
, such that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are inert in the extension
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq F_{d,n}
. This implies that

\zeta_{F_{d,n}}(\sigma)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{d\sigma}}\right)^{-1}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\left(\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p_{j}\mathcal{O}_{F_{d,n}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{f(\mathfrak{p}/p_{j})\sigma}}\right)^{-1}\right)\right),

where
f(\mathfrak{p}/p_{j})
denotes the inertia index of the prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{F_{d,n}}
over the prime ideal
p_{j}\mathbb{Z}
. This shows that
\zeta_{F_{d,n}}(\sigma)\to\zeta(d\sigma)
when
n\to+\infty
and
d
is fixed.
More precisely, using the fact that
\left\lvert\log\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)\right\rvert<\frac{1}{x-1}
for every
x>1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\log\left\lvert\frac{\zeta_{F_{d,n}}(\sigma)}{\zeta(d\sigma)}\right\rvert\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\left(\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{d\sigma}}\right\rvert-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p_{j}\mathcal{O}_{F_{d,n}}}\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{f(\mathfrak{p}/p_{j})\sigma}}\right\rvert\right)\right\rvert\leq(d+1)\sum_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{p_{j}^{\sigma}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d+1}{(\sigma-1)n^{\sigma-1}\log n},

for every
n,d\geq 1
. Setting
\alpha:=\lfloor\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\rfloor+1
and
K_{d}:=F_{d,d^{\alpha}}
, we see that

\lim_{d\to+\infty}h_{\sigma}(K_{d})=\lim_{d\to+\infty}\zeta_{K_{d}}(\sigma)=\lim_{d\to+\infty}\zeta(d\sigma)=1,

and therefore that
\inf(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N}))=1
, as we wanted to show.

In order to conclude the proof of this proposition, we need to show that
\sup(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N}))=+\infty
. To do so, fix two integers
n,k\geq 1
, and let
q_{1,n},\dots,q_{k,n}\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest
k
-many primes such that

q_{j,n}\equiv 1\ \text{mod}\ 4p_{1}\dots p_{n}

for every
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, which exist thanks to Dirichlet’s theorem on the existence of infinitely many primes in any given arithmetic progression
[
8
, Section 2.3]
. Then, the quadratic fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{q_{1,n}}),\dots,\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{q_{k,n}})
are all linearly disjoint, and the primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
split in each of these quadratic fields. Therefore, we constructed a number field
L_{k,n}:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{q_{1,n}},\dots,\sqrt{q_{k,n}})
of degree
[L_{k,n}\colon\mathbb{Q}]=2^{k}
such that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
split completely in
L_{k,n}
.
This implies that

\zeta_{L_{k,n}}(\sigma)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{\sigma}}\right)^{-2^{k}}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\left(\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p_{j}\mathcal{O}_{L_{k,n}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{f(\mathfrak{p}/p_{j})\sigma}}\right)^{-1}\right)\right)

and therefore we see that
\zeta_{L_{k,n}}(\sigma)\to\zeta(\sigma)^{2^{k}}
when
n\to+\infty
and
k
is fixed. More precisely, using once again the fact that
\left\lvert\log\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)\right\rvert<\frac{1}{x-1}
for every
x>1
, we see that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\log\left\lvert\frac{\zeta_{L_{k,n}}(\sigma)}{\zeta(\sigma)^{2^{k}}}\right\rvert\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\left(2^{k}\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{\sigma}}\right\rvert-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p_{j}\mathcal{O}_{L_{k,n}}}\log\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{p_{j}^{f(\mathfrak{p}/p_{j})\sigma}}\right\rvert\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(2^{k}+1)\sum_{j=n+1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{p_{j}^{\sigma}-1}\leq\frac{2^{k}+1}{(\sigma-1)n^{\sigma-1}\log n}.

Setting
\alpha:=\lfloor\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\rfloor+1
and
M_{k}:=L_{k,2^{\alpha k}}
, we have that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}h_{\sigma}(M_{k})=\lim_{k\to\+infty}\zeta_{M_{k}}(\sigma)=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\zeta(\sigma)^{2^{k}}=+\infty,

which finally allows us to conclude that
\sup(h_{\sigma}(\mathcal{N}))=+\infty
.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.6
shows that if
K
is any number field of degree
[K\colon\mathbb{Q}]=d
inside which the primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
split completely, then

\left\lvert\log\left\lvert\frac{\zeta_{K}(\sigma)}{\zeta(\sigma)^{d}}\right\rvert\right\rvert\leq\frac{d+1}{(\sigma-1)n^{\sigma-1}\log n}.

In fact, for any fixed
d
and
n
, such a number field always exists, and can even be constructed as an abelian extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Indeed, the theorem of Grünwald and Wang
[
11
, Theorem 9.2.8]
allows one to construct such an extension if
d
is odd. On the other hand, if
d=2^{k}
we can take
K=L_{k,n}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{q_{1,n}},\dots,\sqrt{q_{k,n}})
as above. In the general case, one can write
d=d_{0}2^{k}
, fix an abelian extension
K^{\prime}
of degree
d_{0}
inside which all the primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
split completely, and then take some primes
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}
such that
\ell_{j}\equiv 1\ \operatorname{mod}\ 4p_{1}\cdots p_{n}
and
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\ell_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{\ell_{n}})
is linearly disjoint from
K^{\prime}
. This allows one to take
K=K^{\prime}(\sqrt{\ell_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{\ell_{n}})
.

5.2.
Left of the critical strip

In this section, we study the diophantine properties of special values of Dedekind zeta functions for those
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leq 0
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.7
.

By
[
5
, Lemma 3.1]
, for every
s
with
\sigma=\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
we have that:

(101)

\frac{1}{\zeta(\sigma)^{d_{K}}}\leq|\zeta_{K}(s)|\leq\zeta(\sigma)^{d_{K}},

where
d_{K}=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. In addition, by
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
we have that

(102)

\Gamma_{m}(s)^{d_{K}}\leq\left|\frac{\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(1-s)^{r_{1}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(1-s)^{r_{2}}}{\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)^{r_{1}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s)^{r_{2}}}\right|,

where
\Gamma_{m}
is the function defined in (
5
).
By
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
we know that if
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
is a non-positive integer,
h_{s}
satisfies the Northcott property. In particular, the function
[K]\mapsto\left(\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert,d_{K}\right)
has the Northcott property. Thus, we can assume that
s
is not a negative integer. Then, we have that
\Gamma_{m}(s)>0
.

Applying (
101
) and (
102
) to the functional equation (
25
), we obtain that for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\sigma<0
, we have

|\zeta_{K}(s)|\geq\left(\frac{\Gamma_{m}(s)}{|\zeta(1-\sigma)|}\right)^{d_{K}}|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma}.

Let
B
be a positive number. We assume that
d_{K}\leq B
and aim to prove that
[K]\mapsto\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert
has the Northcott property.
Since the discriminant function
[K]\mapsto\lvert\Delta_{K}\rvert
has the Northcott property, thanks to a celebrated theorem of Hermite’s, for all but finitely many isomorphism classes
[K]
, we have that

|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{4}}\geq\left(\frac{\Gamma_{m}(s)}{|\zeta(1-\sigma)|}\right)^{B}.

Therefore, for all but finitely isomorphism classes of number fields
[K]
with
d_{K}\leq B
, we have

|\zeta_{K}(s)|\geq|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{4}-\sigma}.

Consequently, since
\sigma<0
, the function
[K]\mapsto\left(\lvert\zeta_{K}^{\ast}(s)\rvert,d_{K}\right)
has the Northcott property.
∎
