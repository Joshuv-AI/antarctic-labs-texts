Stool‐Test Positivity Proportion and Five‐Year Trend of Intestinal Parasitic Infections Among Symptomatic Students Attending Debre Tabor University Student Clinic, Northwest Ethiopia: A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study (2021–2025)

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIntestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) remain a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia, particularly among university students living in communal settings. However, limited data exist on the trends of these infections among university students. A total of 7778 saline wet‐mount stool examination records from students suspected of IPIs were reviewed. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were computed, and the chi‐square test was used to compare infection rates between genders. All confidence intervals were calculated using the exact Clopper‐Pearson binomial method.ResultsA total of 7778 stool examination records were reviewed. Of these, 189 records were excluded due to incomplete data, and 7589 records were included in the final analysis. Protozoan infections accounted for 28.8% (2187/7589; 95% CI: 27.8–29.8), while helminthic infections were less common at 1.5% (112/7589; 95% CI: 1.2–1.8). Introduction

Intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) remain a significant public health challenge globally, particularly in low‐income countries where inadequate sanitation, limited access to clean water, and poor hygiene practices facilitate their transmission [1]. These infections are caused by a diverse range of protozoan and helminth parasites that colonize the human gastrointestinal tract, leading to substantial morbidity and, in severe cases, mortality. The most prevalent parasites worldwide are soil‐transmitted helminths including Ascaris lumbricoides, hookworms, and Trichuris trichiura, which infect approximately one‐quarter of the global population, affecting 250 million, 151 million, and 45 million people, respectively [2]. Protozoan infections such as Entamoeba histolytica/dispar and Giardia lamblia are similarly widespread and contribute substantially to the burden of diarrheal disease [2].

According to the World Health Organization, approximately 3.5 billion people worldwide are affected by intestinal parasites (IP), with over 450 million experiencing clinically significant illnesses, resulting in more than 200,000 deaths annually [2]. The burden falls disproportionately on developing regions, with sub‐Saharan Africa bearing the heaviest impact due to favorable climatic conditions, inadequate sanitation infrastructure, and socioeconomic factors that perpetuate transmission cycles [1, 3]. The clinical consequences of IPIs extend beyond acute gastrointestinal symptoms. Chronic infections contribute to malnutrition, iron deficiency anemia, growth stunting in children, and impaired cognitive development, thereby perpetuating cycles of poverty and compromising human capital development [4].

Ethiopia exemplifies the heavy burden of IPIs characteristic of resource‐limited settings. An estimated 81 million Ethiopians live in parasite‐endemic areas, with IP accounting for a substantial proportion of outpatient morbidity [5]. Factors contributing to the high prevalence include limited access to safe drinking water, inadequate sanitation facilities, low health literacy, and prevailing cultural practices that may facilitate fecal‐oral transmission [6]. Among the most commonly identified parasites in Ethiopian studies are E. histolytica/dispar, G. lamblia, and A. lumbricoides, with prevalence rates varying considerably across different regions and populations [4, 7, 8].

University students residing in congregate settings such as dormitories and sharing common dining facilities represent a distinct population with potentially unique transmission dynamics [2]. This demographic typically comprises young adults transitioning from adolescence to adulthood, a period where behavioral habits—including personal hygiene practices—are consolidated and may influence long‐term health outcomes. Students frequently consume food from campus cafeterias and surrounding food establishments, where food handlers may unknowingly serve as sources of transmission if infected [1]. Furthermore, the close living quarters and shared sanitation facilities in university settings may facilitate the transmission of enteric pathogens, including IP [5].

The public health implications of IPIs among university students extend beyond individual morbidity. Infected students may experience reduced academic performance due to illness, absenteeism, and the chronic effects of malnutrition and anemia [2]. Moreover, students who are asymptomatic carriers—a common phenomenon in endemic settings—may unknowingly contribute to transmission within the university community and beyond when they return to their home communities during academic breaks [9]. Understanding the spectrum of parasites present, including the relative proportions of pathogenic versus non‐pathogenic species, is therefore crucial for comprehensive control planning [1].

Previous studies conducted in Ethiopian university settings have reported widely varying IPI prevalence rates among students, ranging from 14.3% at Haramaya University to 45.6% at the University of Gondar and 47.9% at Hawassa University [10, 11, 12]. A 5‐year trend study at the University of Gondar reported an overall prevalence of 45.6% with E. histolytica/dispar (20.3%) and G. lamblia (8.2%) as the most frequently detected parasites [11]. Similarly, a 10‐year retrospective study at Hawassa University found a prevalence of 47.9% among students attending the clinic [12]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis found that approximately 29.65% of food handlers in Ethiopian university settings were infected with IP, with E. histolytica/dispar (11.52%), A. lumbricoides (9.34%), and G. lamblia (5.85%) being the most frequently identified organisms [1]. A cross‐sectional study among university students in Ethiopia reported a prevalence of 28.9% [10]. These figures demonstrate that IPIs remain an active public health concern in Ethiopian university settings.

Despite numerous studies on IP in Ethiopia, several critical research gaps persist among university student populations. First, while numerous studies have focused on parasitic infections among school‐age children and the general population, there is a scarcity of research on the prevalence and trends of IPIs among university students in Ethiopia [11, 12]. Second, most existing studies have been single‐site investigations or cross‐sectional surveys, limiting the understanding of longitudinal trends in these infections [10]. Third, comprehensive data detailing how specific parasites correlate with clinical symptoms among university students presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms are still lacking. Fourth, there is a notable absence of long‐term trend data on infection patterns among university students, particularly in the Amhara region. Fifth, despite the high population density in university settings and documented burden of infectious diseases, comparative data on IPI epidemiology across multiple time points remain limited.

To date, there is no study data on the prevalence of parasitic infections among students visiting the student clinic at Debre Tabor University. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the 5‐year stool‐test positivity proportion and trend of IPIs among symptomatic students attending the Debre Tabor University student clinic from 2021 to 2025. Understanding the prevalence and distribution of IPIs among students attending this clinic is essential for several reasons. First, it provides baseline data on the burden of these infections within the university community, enabling evidence‐based resource allocation for diagnostic services and treatment. Second, it identifies the predominant parasite species circulating in this student population, guiding empirical treatment decisions and prevention messaging. Third, it establishes a foundation for monitoring trends over time, allowing assessment of whether existing control measures are effective or whether new strategies are required. Fourth, the 5‐year longitudinal design enables comparison with findings from other Ethiopian universities, contextualizing the local burden within the broader national picture and identifying whether infection patterns are stable, increasing, or decreasing over time.

Methods

Study Area and Period

A retrospective study was undertaken to analyze the trend of intestinal parasite prevalence based on data extracted from laboratory registration books at the Debre Tabor University (DTU) student clinic from 2021 to 2025. Debre Tabor, situated in the South Gondar zone of the Amhara Regional State, serves as the capital city of the Farta district located in the northwest Ethiopia. It is positioned 109 kilometers from Bahir Dar and 670 kilometers from Addis Ababa (Figure 1). The town's coordinates are 11°51′ N 38°1′ E, with an elevation of 2706 m above sea level. According to population projections for the South Gondar zone, the town has a total population of 64,381, with 50.67% females and 49.33% males. The town is equipped with four government health institutions to cater to the healthcare needs of the residents. Among these, Debre Tabor University, established in 2008 in North Central Ethiopia, serves approximately 17,122 students across multiple faculties including medicine and health sciences, technology, agriculture, and natural sciences [13].

Map of the study area (Debre Tabor town).

The university is located in Debre Tabor town, South Gonder Zone, at an altitude of 2706 meters above sea level [14]. The surrounding catchment area is known to have a high prevalence of IPIs, with studies from nearby districts reporting prevalence rates ranging from 33.64% to 57.1% among general populations [15].

Study Design

A retrospective cross‐sectional study was conducted using data extracted from laboratory registration books at the DTU student clinic from 2021 to 2025. This design was chosen to assess the 5‐year stool‐test positivity proportion and trends of IPIs among symptomatic students attending the clinic.

Study Population

Source Population

The source population comprised all students of Debre Tabor University who attended the student clinic with suspected IPIs during the study period (2021–2025).

Study Population

The study population comprised symptomatic students who attended the Debre Tabor University student clinic with suspected IPIs and underwent stool examination. This clinic‐based sample represents only those students who sought care and does not capture asymptomatic students or those who did not attend the clinic. Therefore, the findings represent stool‐test positivity among symptomatic clinic attendees, not population prevalence.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

All records of students suspected of having IP who underwent stool examination at the DTU student clinic during the study period (2021–2025).

Records containing complete sociodemographic details (age, sex, year of stool examination).

Records with complete stool microscopy results (species and stage of IP).

Exclusion Criteria

Records that did not contain sociodemographic details (age, sex, year of stool examination).

Records with incomplete stool microscopy results (e.g., positive result without species identification) and duplicate entries identified using unique MRN/card numbers.

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

Total Records Reviewed

A total of 7778 stool examination records were reviewed from the parasitology registration books at the Debre Tabor University student clinic for the period 2021–2025.

Records Excluded

Of these, 189 records were excluded due to incomplete data: 78 had missing age or sex, 52 had missing year of stool examination, 41 had incomplete stool microscopy results, 18 were duplicate entries.

Records Included

The remaining 7589 records were included in the final analysis. This represents the total number of stool examinations performed, with each examination corresponding to a student visit. Students with multiple examinations during the study period were counted separately for each visit.

Rationale for Sample Size

As a retrospective study, the sample size was determined by the available records rather than a prospective sample size calculation. The large sample size (7589) provides adequate statistical power for prevalence estimation and subgroup analyses.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a structured data extraction tool designed for this study. Data collectors gathered information from the parasitology registration books, including:

Sociodemographic details (Sex, year of stool examination).

Laboratory results (species and stage of IP).

Unit of Analysis

The unit of analysis was each stool examination record from the laboratory registration book at the Debre Tabor University student clinic. Each examination corresponded to a unique student visit, and students with multiple examinations during the study period were counted separately for each visit.

Identification of Students

Students were identified using their unique Medical Record Number (MRN) or card number assigned at their first visit to the student clinic.

Handling of Repeated Examinations

Students who had multiple examinations during the study period were identified using their unique MRN/card number and counted separately for each visit, as each visit represents a distinct clinical episode. Each stool examination was treated as an independent observation.

Handling of Duplicate Entries

Duplicate entries were identified using the unique MRN/card number and examination dates. A total of 18 duplicate entries were identified and excluded from the analysis.

Laboratory Methods

The sole laboratory method utilized for diagnosing IPIs was the direct saline wet‐mount microscopy. A small portion of the stool sample was mixed with a drop of normal saline and a drop of Lugol's iodine on separate glass slides, covered with a coverslip, and examined under a light microscope using ×10 and ×40 objectives. The presence of ova, cysts, and trophozoites was recorded.

Diagnostic Limitation

Routine microscopy cannot distinguish between E. histolytica (pathogenic), Entamoeba dispar (non‐pathogenic), and Entamoeba moshkovskii (non‐pathogenic). Therefore, all Entamoeba species detected were reported as E. histolytica/dispar complex, consistent with standard laboratory practice in resource‐limited settings. This method has inherent limitations, including relatively low sensitivity for detecting light‐intensity infections and the inability to differentiate morphologically identical species.

Data Quality Control

Before commencing data collection, the data collectors underwent training on the study objectives, data extraction procedures, and data quality requirements. The primary investigator conducted daily checks on a sample of the collected data to ensure completeness and consistency. Subsequently, a representative sample of the extracted data was meticulously reviewed for completeness, consistency, and accuracy. The primary investigator tallied and recorded the number of confirmed cases at the conclusion of each data collection session before transmitting it for data entry.

Operational Definitions

IPIs

Infections caused by IP such as G. lamblia, E. histolytica/dispar, A. lumbricoides, and others diagnosed through microscopic examination of stool samples at the Debre Tabor University student clinic laboratory.

Prevalence

The proportion of students whose stool examination results were positive for one or more IP during the 5‐year study period (2021–2025), expressed as a percentage.

Trend

The pattern of change in the prevalence of IPIs observed over the 5‐year study period (2021–2025) expressed as annual fluctuations.

Stool‐Test Positivity Proportion

The proportion of symptomatic students whose stool examination results were positive for one or more IP during the study period. This is not population prevalence and reflects only those students who sought care at the clinic.

Protozoan Infection

The presence of one or more protozoan parasites, including but not limited to E. histolytica/dispar and G. lamblia, identified during stool microscopy.

Helminthic Infection

The presence of one or more helminth parasites, including but not limited to hookworm species, A. lumbricoides, T. trichiura, Hymenolepis nana, Schistosoma mansoni, and Strongyloides stercoralis.

Single Infection

Detection of only one species of intestinal parasite in a stool sample.

Mixed Infection

Detection of two or more different species of IP in a single stool sample.

Complete Record

A laboratory registration entry containing all required data points, including the year of examination, sex of the student, and stool microscopy result with identified parasite species (if positive).

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 25 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the frequencies and percentages of IPIs. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Unit of Analysis

The unit of analysis was each stool examination record from the laboratory registration book at the Debre Tabor University student clinic. Each examination corresponded to a unique student visit, and students with multiple examinations during the study period were counted separately for each visit.

Confidence Intervals

All confidence intervals were calculated using the exact Clopper‐Pearson binomial method with a 95% confidence level. This method provides exact interval estimation for binomial proportions and is more appropriate than asymptotic approximations for these data. For a given sample proportion p = x/n, the 95% CI was calculated as:

Lower bound = Beta−1(α/2; x, n−x + 1)

Upperbound = Beta−1(1−α/2; x + 1, n−x)

where x is the number of positive cases, n is the total sample size, and α = 0.05 for 95% confidence.

Gender Comparison

The chi‐square test was used to compare infection rates between males and females. A 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed with sex (male/female) and infection status (positive/negative). Yates' continuity correction was applied where appropriate. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05 (two‐sided).

Trend Analysis

The Cochran‐Armitage test for trend was used to assess linear trends in proportions across the 5‐year study period (2021–2025). This test is appropriate for evaluating whether there is a monotonic trend in a binary outcome (positive/negative) across ordered categories (years). The test statistic Z was calculated, and the corresponding p‐value was reported. Results showed no statistically significant linear trend over the study period (Z = −0.89, p = 0.37).

Missing Data Handling

Records with incomplete data were excluded from the analysis. Specifically, records were excluded if they lacked: (1) sociodemographic details (age, sex, year of stool examination), or (2) stool microscopy results (species and stage of IP). A total of 189 records were excluded due to incomplete data: 78 had missing age or sex, 52 had missing year of stool examination, 41 had incomplete stool microscopy results, and 18 were duplicate entries. The remaining 7589 records were included in the final analysis.

Duplicate Record Management

Students were identified using their unique MRN or card number assigned at their first visit to the student clinic. Duplicate entries were identified using the unique MRN/card number and examination dates. A total of 18 duplicate entries were identified and excluded from the analysis.

Ethical Considerations

Before the study began, ethical approval (Permission letter reference number: CHS/350/2025 in the Ethiopian calendar, date 9/5/2017 E.C) was obtained from the Debre Tabor University, College of Health Sciences, Ethical Review Committee. As this was a retrospective study using de‐identified laboratory records, individual informed consent was waived. However, the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The confidentiality of the data was strictly maintained. To uphold confidentiality, individual names were removed from the health center's data, and only unique identification numbers were used for identification purposes.

Results

A total of 7778 stool examinations were reviewed from the parasitology registration books at the Debre Tabor University student clinic during the study period (2021–2025). Of these, 189 records were excluded due to incomplete or duplicate data including: 78 records with missing age or sex, 52 with missing year of stool examination, 41 with incomplete stool microscopy results, and 18 duplicate entries. After excluding these records, 7589 records were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Each examination corresponded to a student visit, and students who had multiple examinations during the study period were counted separately for each visit. This retrospective record review included all complete records available during the study period, and the study population comprised symptomatic students who attended the clinic with suspected IPIs. Therefore, the findings represent stool‐test positivity among symptomatic clinic attendees, not population prevalence.

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

The majority of students who attended the clinic were males, accounting for 60.7% (4603/7589), while females constituted 39.3% (2986/7589). Regarding the annual distribution of examinations, the highest number of examinations was recorded in 2025 with 22.4% (1700/7589), followed by 2021 with 20.0% (1520/7589), 2022 with 19.6% (1487/7589), 2023 with 19.2% (1460/7589), and the lowest in 2024 with 18.7% (1422/7589) (Table 1).

Overall Prevalence of IPIs (2021–2025)

The overall positivity proportion was 30.3% (2299 out of 7589 stool examinations; 95% CI: 29.3–31.3), indicating that nearly one in three symptomatic students tested positive for at least one intestinal parasite. Protozoan infections accounted for the vast majority of infections, with 28.8% (2187/7589; 95% CI: 27.8–29.8) of all examinations positive for protozoa, while helminth infections were much less common at 1.5% (112/7589; 95% CI: 1.2–1.8).

Among the protozoan parasites, E. histolytica/dispar was the most prevalent species, detected in 15.8% (1201/7589; 95% CI: 14.9–16.7) of examinations, followed by G. lamblia at 12.8% (966/7589; 95% CI: 12.0–13.6). Mixed infections of E. histolytica/dispar and G. lamblia were rare, accounting for only 0.3% (20/7589; 95% CI: 0.2–0.4).

Among helminths, hookworm was the most prevalent at 0.5% (36/7589; 95% CI: 0.3–0.7), followed by A. lumbricoides at 0.3% (23/7589; 95% CI: 0.2–0.5). Other helminths were detected at very low frequencies: Taenia species (0.3%; 19/7589; 95% CI: 0.2–0.4), Enterobius vermicularis (0.2%; 13/7589; 95% CI: 0.1–0.3), H. nana (0.1%; 8/7589; 95% CI: 0.0–0.2), T. trichiura (0.08%; 6/7589; 95% CI: 0.0–0.2), S. stercoralis (0.1%; 5/7589; 95% CI: 0.0–0.2), and S. mansoni (0.03%; 2/7589; 95% CI: 0.0–0.1) (Table 2). It is important to note that the finding for E. histolytica/dispar must be interpreted as a complex, as routine microscopy cannot distinguish between the pathogenic E. histolytica and the non‐pathogenic E. dispar and E. moshkovskii.

Annual Trends in Intestinal Parasite Positivity

A total of 7589 stool examinations were recorded at the Debre Tabor University student clinic during the study period (2021–2025). The total number of examinations varied across years, with the highest number recorded in 2025 (1700) and the lowest in 2024 (1422). The annual positivity rates ranged from 27.4% (95% CI: 25.1–29.8) in 2024 to 31.6% (95% CI: 29.2–34.0) in 2021. In 2022, the positivity rate was 31.5% (95% CI: 29.2–33.9); in 2023, it was 30.8% (95% CI: 28.5–33.2); and in 2025, it was 30.1% (95% CI: 27.9–32.4)

The Cochran‐Armitage test for trend showed no statistically significant linear trend over the study period (Z = −0.89, p = 0.37), indicating that while there were year‐to‐year variations, there was no consistent increasing or decreasing pattern. The overall positivity proportion for the entire 5‐year period was 30.3% (2299/7589; 95% CI: 29.3–31.3).

The predominance of protozoan infections across all years suggests that fecal‐oral transmission is the primary route of infection in this population, with no significant changes in species distribution or overall positivity over the study period. The absence of a statistically significant trend suggests that the observed variations may be due to fluctuations in healthcare‐seeking behavior, changes in testing practices, seasonal factors, or other unmeasured variables rather than a true change in infection prevalence (Table 3 and Figure 3).

Discussion

Intestinal parasite infections are classified as neglected tropical diseases and represent a considerable public health challenge in Ethiopia. Consequently, it is vital to conduct regular surveillance and monitor the prevalence of these infections within specific communities. Such efforts are essential for the effective planning and evaluation of ongoing health programs. The collected data plays a crucial role in developing suitable intervention strategies aimed at addressing this issue effectively.

This retrospective study evaluated the prevalence and trends of IPIs among university students attending the Debre Tabor University student clinic over a 5‐year period (2021–2025). The overall prevalence of IPIs among symptomatic students was 30.3% (2303/7589; 95% CI: 29.3–31.3). The clinic‐based nature of this study must be considered when interpreting these findings. The 30.3% positivity proportion reflects the burden among symptomatic students who sought care, which is likely higher than the true population prevalence among all university students. Asymptomatic carriers, who may constitute a significant reservoir for transmission, are not captured in this analysis. Additionally, the 7589 stool examinations represent the total number of examinations performed, with students who had multiple examinations counted separately for each visit.

Overall Prevalence in Context

The overall stool‐test positivity proportion of 30.3% (95% CI: 29.3–31.3) observed in this study is consistent with findings from other Ethiopian university‐based studies. This prevalence aligns with previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses conducted in Ethiopia, which reported prevalence rates of 29.5%, 28.5%, 29.2%, and 33.6%, respectively [1, 16, 17], 29.7% in southern Ethiopia [18], and 28.9% in Ethiopian university students during a sports festival at Haramaya University [19]. The consistency in these figures suggests that students may encounter comparable exposure risks to IP, likely due to shared dining facilities, similar hygiene practices on campus, or the consumption of food prepared in university cafeterias, where food handlers may unknowingly transmit infections. Similar prevalence rates have also been reported in Sudan (29.4%) [20], the Democratic Republic of Congo (28.6%) [21], and Spain (28%) [22], indicating that university students globally face comparable risks.

In comparison, the prevalence rate in our study is higher than that reported in Ethiopia (25.77%) [7] but lower than the prevalence found at Adama Science and Technology University (20.1%) [23], Bulgaria (25.5%) [24], Rwanda (25.4%) [25], Iran (15.5%) [26], and Thailand (10.3%) [27]. It is worth noting that the overall prevalence of IP in our study was lower than the prevalence reported in studies conducted at Hawassa University (47.9%) [28] and the University of Gondar (45.6%) [29].

The differences in the prevalence of IPIs among university student clinics across various Ethiopian institutions can be attributed to a multitude of factors. Variances in personal hygiene practices and sanitation habits among students have a significant impact on infection rates, indicating that universities with better hygiene education and practices tend to report lower prevalence. This is closely linked to the type of accommodation, whether dormitories or private housing, and the quality of shared facilities, as overcrowded or poorly maintained living spaces increase the risk of transmission. Moreover, the sources and quality of food consumed, particularly in campus cafeterias or nearby eateries, vary significantly; institutions with stricter food safety regulations generally exhibit lower infection rates compared to those with less oversight. The level of health literacy among students also plays a crucial role, as it shapes their comprehension of preventive measures; hence, institutions with comprehensive health education programs often have lower infection rates. Besides human behavior, geographical and climatic conditions can impact the lifecycle and transmission of parasites, with regions more conducive to parasite survival witnessing higher rates. Finally, methodological considerations cannot be disregarded, as variations in study design, sample size, and diagnostic techniques especially the utilization of more sensitive methods can lead to variability in reported prevalence by enhancing detection rates.

Species Distribution

E. histolytica/Dispar

The most commonly identified parasite was E. histolytica/dispar (15.8%; 1201/7589; 95% CI: 14.9–16.7), followed by G. lamblia (12.8%; 973/7589; 95% CI: 12.0–13.6), and hookworm (0.5%; 38/7589; 95% CI: 0.3–0.6). Additionally, A. lumbricoides, E. vermicularis, S. mansoni, H. nana, Taenia species, S. stercoralis, and T. trichiura were among the other identified parasites with less than 0.5% prevalence.

The prevalence of E. histolytica/dispar (15.8%) in our study aligns with findings from an 18% retrospective study conducted at Hawassa University students' clinic [28], 11.52% in a systematic review and meta‐analysis in Ethiopia [1], and 13% at Adama Science and Technology University [23]. However, our study showed higher prevalence compared to reports from Iran (0.6%) [30], Thailand (0.73%) [31], Saudi Arabia (2%) [32], Woldia University in northeast Ethiopia (6.38%) [33], and selected regions of Ethiopia (6.78%) [1]. The slightly higher prevalence in our study may be due to variations in diagnostic methods, study populations, or local risk factors. The findings of the current study were lower than those from previous research conducted in Sudan (31.2%) [34], Côte d'Ivoire (56%) [35], Burkina Faso (66.5%) [36], and Iraq (88%) [37], as well as among Ethiopian university students (19.7%) [19] and a 5‐year trend among students attending the clinic at the University of Gondar (20.3%) [29].

The prevalence of E. histolytica/dispar (15.8%; 1201/7589; 95% CI: 14.9–16.7) in our study is consistent with findings from other Ethiopian studies. However, it is critical to emphasize that routine microscopy cannot distinguish between E. histolytica (pathogenic), E. dispar (non‐pathogenic), and E. moshkovskii (non‐pathogenic). Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as E. histolytica/dispar complex, and molecular or antigen‐based diagnostic methods are required for definitive species identification. The true burden of pathogenic E. histolytica may be lower than reported, and our findings may represent an overestimation of pathogenic amoebiasis.

The high prevalence of protozoan infections may be associated with fecal‐oral transmission routes, which could be related to sanitation infrastructure, personal hygiene practices, or water quality within the university community. However, as this study did not collect data on these factors, these explanations remain speculative and should be investigated in future prospective studies with comprehensive risk‐factor assessment. The disparities in prevalence rates may relate to the quality of food and water in different study locations, as well as their environmental conditions. Specifically, E. histolytica/dispar can be transmitted through contaminated drinking water and food, facilitating its spread through contaminated foods and vegetables. Additionally, the discrepancy in prevalence rates between this research and other countries might stem from differences in methodology, socio‐demographics, personal hygiene practices, and environmental hygiene measures used to assess prevalence.

G. lamblia

Furthermore, the prevalence of G. lamblia in our study, at 12.8%, is comparable to the prevalence of 9.3% among university students in Ethiopia [20], 9.6% in a retrospective study at Hawassa University students' clinic [28], and 8.2% in a 5‐year trend prevalence among students attending the clinic at the University of Gondar [29]. The likely reason for this similarity could be attributed to poor hygiene practices by food handlers and inadequate sanitation in university toilets. Additionally, the fact that students live in close quarters and share various materials for their daily activities may expose them to IPIs. The significant difference indicates that Giardia transmission may be particularly intense within the Debre Tabor University environment, possibly linked to contaminated water sources or food handling practices on campus. Giardia is mainly spread through the fecal‐oral route via contaminated water or food, and its high prevalence suggests potential issues with water quality or sanitation infrastructure. These factors are crucial for the university student population, especially those residing in dormitories with shared sanitation facilities or off‐campus housing where water quality may vary.

Hookworm and Other Helminths

The low prevalence of hookworm infection (0.5%) in our study is quite surprising and differs significantly from findings in other Ethiopian populations. It is notably lower than the 13.01% prevalence reported in a systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted among schoolchildren [38]. The possible reason may be due to age‐related differences in behavior, such as consistent use of footwear, reduced soil contact, and improved access to sanitation facilities on campus. Additionally, this variation could be influenced by the cumulative impact of deworming programs implemented in schools, in which these students would have participated during their primary and secondary education. The 0.5% hookworm prevalence in our study is also lower than the 3.3% reported in the Hakim district study [38] and 7.2% reported in the Mikara study [3]. This progressive decrease in hookworm prevalence from schoolchildren to university students suggests that as individuals age and their behaviors change, their risk of soil‐transmitted helminth infection declines substantially. The university environment in Debre Tabor likely lacks these conducive conditions, further reducing transmission risk.

The low prevalence of other helminths, including A. lumbricoides, T. trichiura, S. mansoni, H. nana, Taenia species, S. stercoralis, and E. vermicularis, collectively representing less than 2% of infections, is encouraging from a public health perspective. This pattern suggests that soil‐transmitted helminthiasis and other geohelminth infections are relatively well‐controlled in the university population.

Protozoan Versus Helminth Infections

The overwhelming predominance of protozoan infections (20.8%) compared to helminth infections (1.4%) in our study is a significant finding. This trend implies that the primary mode of infection among university students is likely through fecal‐oral transmission rather than soil‐borne transmission. Protozoan parasites like E. histolytica and G. lamblia are typically spread directly through contaminated food and water or person‐to‐person contact, while helminths usually require soil for development and transmission [1]. The low prevalence of helminths may indicate better sanitation facilities on campus, reduced soil exposure among university students compared to rural communities, or the influence of periodic deworming programs in the area. In comparison to local data from the same study area, our finding of a 20.8% prevalence of protozoan infections is notably higher than the prevalence of protozoan infections reported among diabetic patients at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital [39]. The 20.8% prevalence of protozoan infection in our study also aligns with findings from a systematic review and meta‐analysis on intestinal protozoan parasitic infections in Ethiopia (25%–30%) [40].

Temporal Trends

The fluctuation in intestinal parasitic (IP) prevalence from 20.8% in 2021 to a peak in 2022, followed by decreases to 19.9% in 2023 and 16.9% in 2024 and then rising again to 22.18% in 2025, illustrates an inconsistent trend pattern. This aligns with the retrospective study conducted at Oda Bultum University which analyzed IP trends among students from 2019 to 2023 and similarly noted “inconsistent” prevalence trends over the 5‐year period [41]. The Oda Bultum study reported an overall 5‐year prevalence of 54.5% (95% CI: 52.2%–56.7%) among students, with significant fluctuations across the years, indicating that university student populations in Ethiopia face varying IP burdens annually, potentially influenced by changes in environmental conditions, sanitation infrastructure, or healthcare interventions [41].

The initial increase in prevalence from 20.8% in 2021 to a peak in 2022 observed in our study may be indicative of behavioral changes following the easing of COVID‐19 restrictions. As students returned to normal campus activities, their exposure to IP likely increased due to shared dining facilities and heightened social interactions. This interpretation is consistent with the systematic review and meta‐analysis that reported a pooled prevalence of 29.65% (95% CI: 22.79%–36.5%) among food handlers in Ethiopia [1]. The subsequent decline in prevalence to 19.4% in 2023 and further to 16.9% in 2024 indicates a positive trend that may reflect the effectiveness of health interventions, increased awareness, or natural fluctuations in infection rates.

Similar declining trends have been observed in a 5‐year retrospective study conducted at Mizan‐Tepi University from 2017 to 2021, revealing fluctuations throughout the years, with rates of 17.77% in 2017, 18.89% in 2018, 23.44% in 2019, and 19.96% in both 2020 and 2021 [42]. The annual positivity rates showed variation across the 5‐year period, ranging from 27.4% in 2024 to 31.9% in 2022. However, the Cochran‐Armitage test for trend did not show a statistically significant linear trend (Z = −0.89, p = 0.37). This suggests that while there were year‐to‐year variations, there was no consistent increasing or decreasing pattern over the study period. These variations may reflect differences in the number of students seeking care each year, seasonal factors, or other unmeasured variables rather than a true change in infection prevalence. Future studies with longer follow‐up periods and multi‐seasonal sampling are needed to fully capture the epidemiological dynamics of IPIs in this population.

The fluctuating prevalence of IP observed in our study is consistent with trends reported from a 5‐year trend analysis at Oda Bultum University (2019–2023), which revealed similar seasonal variations, with infection rates consistently increasing from spring (44.9%) to summer (67.7%) [41]. This study was comparable with the 20.1% prevalence (95% CI: 16.8–23.8) at Adama Science and Technology University [23]. These temporal fluctuations are likely influenced by a combination of environmental and behavioral factors. Specifically, seasonal changes in rainfall and temperature may affect parasite survival in the external environment, while simultaneously altering human behaviors related to water contact, hygiene, and sanitation. Such dynamics offer a plausible explanation for the year‐to‐year variations observed in our study population.

Sex‐Related Patterns

Our study found that males and females demonstrated similar infection rates across all locations, consistent with previous research in the region. The lack of a statistically significant gender difference suggests that male and female students have similar exposure risks to IP, likely due to shared environments (dormitories, cafeterias, classrooms) and similar hygiene practices within the university setting. The similar rates between sexes in our study may reflect comparable exposure levels in the study population or the household‐based nature of transmission for many IP.

Regarding gender, the higher infection rate observed among males (53.8%) compared to females (46.2%) in our study is consistent with a 10‐year retrospective study at Hawassa University students' clinic, where the prevalence of protozoan infections was higher in males (28.5%) compared to females (23.8%) (p < 0.00001) [28]. This distinction is important, as protozoan infections like E. histolytica and G. lamblia are often transmitted through contaminated food and water exposure pathways that may be more common among male students due to differing dietary or hygiene habits. Similarly, our study is consistent with a 5‐year trend study at the University of Gondar, which reported that the prevalence of IPIs was higher in males (35.4%) than in females (10.2%) (p = 0.02) [29]. This finding directly supports the pattern observed in our study and reinforces the notion that male students may represent a higher‐risk demographic within university communities.

The male predominance observed in our study can be explained by several behavioral and occupational factors. In the university context, male students may have greater exposure to contaminated environments through off‐campus dining, outdoor recreational activities, or field‐based academic work that increases contact with soil or unsafe water sources. Additionally, gender‐based differences in hygiene practices, including handwashing frequency and nail hygiene, may contribute to differential infection rates between male and female students. These behavioral patterns, often shaped by cultural norms and gender roles, can create distinct exposure pathways that predispose males to higher acquisition of IP. It is important to acknowledge, however, that the pattern of infection is not entirely uniform across all Ethiopian university settings.

The Hawassa University study demonstrated that while protozoan infections were higher in males, helminthic infections were more prevalent in females (23.3% vs. 19.5%, p < 0.00001) [28]. This divergence may be attributed to gender‐specific roles in certain communities, such as females' involvement in domestic chores like fetching water, cleaning, or food preparation, which could increase their exposure to soil‐transmitted helminths. Such findings highlight that gender disparities in parasitic infections are multifaceted and can vary depending on the specific pathogen and local context.

The overwhelming predominance of protozoan infections in both males (53.6%) and females (46.3%) at Debre Tabor University is a striking finding that has important implications for understanding transmission dynamics and designing control strategies. This pattern suggests that fecal‐oral transmission, rather than soil‐borne transmission, is the primary route of infection in this university population, affecting both genders nearly equally. This contrasts with findings from some studies where gender differences in protozoan prevalence have been observed. The Hawassa University study, for example, found that protozoan infections were significantly higher in males (28.5%) than in females (23.8%) [28], suggesting that in some university settings, males may have greater exposure to protozoan parasites through dietary habits or occupational activities. While the absolute numbers of helminth infections in our study were lower than protozoan infections (47 (43.5%) in females, 61 (56.4%) in males), this contradicts the Hawassa University study, which found that helminth prevalence was significantly higher in females (23.3%) than in males (19.5%) (p < 0.00001) [28]. This divergence may be attributed to gender‐specific roles in certain communities, such as females' involvement in domestic chores like fetching water, cleaning, or food preparation, which could increase their exposure to soil‐transmitted helminths.

Public Health Implications

The high prevalence of IPIs among university students, particularly protozoan infections, highlights the need for comprehensive control strategies. Our findings support the following recommendations: strengthening water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions on campus; implementing regular mass drug administration programs targeting all students; enhancing health education on personal hygiene and food safety; improving access to diagnostic services at health facilities; and integrating IPI control into routine health services. The temporal fluctuations observed in this study, with positivity rates ranging from 16.9% to 22.3%, suggest that transmission is ongoing. However, as this was a retrospective study with descriptive trend analysis, these variations should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies with formal trend analysis and control for confounding variables are needed to establish definitive conclusions about changes over time.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, the study included a substantial sample of 7589 stool examination records over a 5‐year period, which enhances the statistical power and precision of the prevalence estimates and provides robust data on the burden of IPIs among students attending the clinic. Second, the 5‐year study period (2021–2025) enables the assessment of temporal trends in IPIs, moving beyond a simple point‐prevalence estimate and providing valuable insights into the dynamics of infection over time. This longitudinal approach can inform public health planning and resource allocation by identifying periods of increased transmission.

Third, the study focuses on an underserved population university students living in communal settings who represent a unique population with potentially distinct transmission dynamics. While extensive research has focused on school‐age children and the general population, university students have received less attention despite living in shared accommodation with varying sanitation infrastructure and hygiene awareness, and this study addresses a critical research gap by providing data on this understudied population. Fourth, the study provides comprehensive species‐level data, including the distribution of specific protozoan (E. histolytica/dispar, G. lamblia) and helminth parasites, which is essential for guiding empirical treatment decisions and developing targeted prevention strategies.

Fifth, the study frames its findings within a clear public health context, leading to practical and actionable recommendations. By quantifying the burden of IPIs among symptomatic students, the study provides evidence to support improved sanitation infrastructure, health education campaigns, and targeted screening programs. Sixth, the study employed a systematic approach to data extraction and analysis, including comprehensive data collection from laboratory registration books, clear inclusion and exclusion criteria, data quality control measures, appropriate statistical analysis using SPSS version 25, and reporting of confidence intervals using the exact Clopper‐Pearson binomial method.

Seventh, the study provides gender‐specific prevalence data, allowing for comparison of infection rates between male and female students, which can inform targeted interventions based on gender‐specific risk factors. Eighth, the study establishes baseline epidemiological data for IPIs at Debre Tabor University, which can be used to assess the effectiveness of future interventions and monitor changes in infection patterns over time. Ninth, the findings of this study are consistent with previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses conducted in Ethiopia, enhancing the credibility and generalizability of the results. Finally, the study demonstrates the feasibility of using existing laboratory records for epidemiological surveillance, an approach that is cost‐effective and can be replicated in other settings to monitor disease burden and inform public health interventions.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the reliance on direct saline wet‐mount microscopy without concentration techniques (e.g., formalin‐ether sedimentation, Kato‐Katz, flotation) or molecular confirmation (e.g., antigen detection, PCR) limits sensitivity and prevents definitive species identification. Many studies fail to differentiate between pathogenic E. histolytica and non‐pathogenic E. dispar, which may inflate prevalence estimates for pathogenic amoebiasis. However, the reliance on microscopy alone cannot differentiate between pathogenic Entamoeba histolytica and non‐pathogenic Entamoeba dispar species, as this requires molecular diagnostic methods that are rarely available in resource‐limited settings.

Second, the study was conducted exclusively among symptomatic students who attended the clinic with suspected IPIs, which introduces selection bias. Asymptomatic students or those with mild symptoms who did not seek care are not represented in the data, and the findings represent stool‐test positivity among symptomatic clinic attendees, not the population prevalence among all university students. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to asymptomatic students, students who do not seek care, or students at other universities.

Third, the retrospective design of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between risk factors and IPIs, and the researchers had no control over data quality, completeness, or consistency, as the data were extracted from existing laboratory records. The design does not allow for control of confounding variables such as changes in testing practices, seasonal variations, or healthcare‐seeking behavior over the study period.

Fourth, the study did not collect data on potential risk factors such as hygiene practices, water source, sanitation facilities, dietary habits, dormitory conditions, socioeconomic status, or treatment history, and 189 records were excluded due to incomplete data, which may have introduced selection bias. A total of 189 records were excluded due to incomplete data: 78 had missing age or sex, 52 had missing year of stool examination, 41 had incomplete stool microscopy results, and 18 were duplicate entries. It is possible that the excluded records differed systematically from the included records in ways that could affect the results. However, the proportion of excluded records was small (189/7778 = 2.4%) relative to the total sample size, and the exclusion criteria were applied consistently across all years. To improve transparency, a record‐selection flow diagram (Figure 1) has been added to the manuscript.

Fifth, as a single‐site study conducted at Debre Tabor University, the findings may not be generalizable to other student populations with different environmental, behavioral, or healthcare contexts. Sixth, the study period overlaps with the COVID‐19 pandemic, which may have influenced healthcare‐seeking behavior, hygiene practices, and testing practices, and the observed trends may be influenced by pandemic‐related factors that were not controlled for in the analysis.

Seventh, the study did not analyze data by season, which may influence parasite transmission dynamics, and 5 years may not be sufficient to identify meaningful long‐term trends. Changes in testing practices, healthcare access, or environmental conditions over the study period may have also influenced the results. Eighth, as a retrospective study, the sample size was determined by the available records rather than a prospective sample size calculation, and the study may not have had sufficient power for detailed subgroup analyses.

Ninth, retrospective design limits the ability to assess causal relationships or control for confounding variables. Associations identified in this study are correlational, not causal. The annual trend analysis is descriptive and does not account for potential confounding factors such as changes in testing practices, seasonal variations, healthcare‐seeking behavior, environmental conditions, or changes in laboratory protocols over the study period.

Therefore, the observed variations in annual positivity rates ranging from 16.9% in 2024 to 22.3% in 2025 should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than statistically significant trends. Formal trend analysis with appropriate statistical methods and control for confounding variables would be required to draw definitive conclusions about changes over time. Additionally, the retrospective design does not allow for the collection of data on potential risk factors, which further limits the ability to identify determinants of infection.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Conclusions

This 5‐year retrospective analysis revealed a stool‐test positivity proportion of 30.3% (95% CI: 29.3–31.3) among symptomatic students attending the Debre Tabor University student clinic. E. histolytica/dispar and G. lamblia were the predominant parasites identified, with protozoan infections (20.8%) overwhelmingly outnumbering helminth infections (1.4%). However, the reliance on direct microscopy without concentration techniques limits definitive species identification, particularly for Entamoeba species, as routine microscopy cannot distinguish between pathogenic and non‐pathogenic Entamoeba species.

This distinct epidemiological pattern suggests that fecal‐oral transmission, rather than soil‐borne transmission, is the primary route of infection within the university community.

Annual positivity rates varied between 27.4% and 31.9% over the 5‐year period, but no statistically significant linear trend was observed (Cochran‐Armitage test: Z = −0.89, p = 0.37). These year‐to‐year variations highlight the importance of continued surveillance to monitor changes in infection patterns, though the cyclic fluctuations suggest that transmission is ongoing and likely influenced by recurring behavioral or environmental factors.

However, these findings should not be interpreted as the population prevalence among all university students, as the clinic‐based sampling approach introduces selection bias and the data represent stool examinations rather than unique students. The retrospective design and reliance on microscopy limit the ability to establish causal relationships or identify risk factors. The presence of fecal‐orally transmitted protozoa points to potential gaps in sanitation infrastructure, personal hygiene practices, or the safety of the water supply within the university community. The high prevalence of protozoan infections suggests the need for improved sanitation infrastructure, health education, and targeted screening programs. Future prospective studies with comprehensive risk‐factor assessment and more sensitive diagnostic methods are needed to confirm these findings and guide targeted interventions.

Recommendations

Based on the study's findings, a multi‐pronged set of recommendations is proposed for university administration, health services, and future research to effectively reduce the burden of IPIs among students.

For University Administration—WASH Infrastructure

Addressing the environmental determinants of infection, the university administration must prioritize the enhancement of Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) facilities. Given the dominance of fecal‐orally transmitted protozoan infections like E. histolytica/dispar and Giardia, an immediate assessment of water quality in dormitories and cafeterias is critical, alongside investments in reliable water treatment systems and the continuous availability of safe drinking water. Concurrently, the sanitation infrastructure requires regular maintenance, ensuring latrines and handwashing stations are consistently equipped with soap to break transmission cycles within communal living spaces.

For University Health Services—Health Education and Screening

Building on this foundational infrastructure, the University Health and Student Services should implement targeted health programs. This includes launching regular, mandatory health education campaigns focused on personal hygiene, proper handwashing, and food safety, potentially utilizing peer‐led initiatives for greater resonance. Furthermore, the high prevalence warrants the establishment of periodic, targeted screening and treatment programs, especially for new entrants, with treatment protocols updated to effectively address the specific pathogens identified.

For Researchers—Future Studies

To address the limitations of this study, future research should employ prospective designs with comprehensive data collection on risk factors (e.g., hygiene practices, water source, sanitation facilities, dietary habits, dormitory conditions), clinical symptoms, and treatment history to identify the determinants of IPIs and inform targeted interventions. Future studies should employ more sensitive diagnostic methods, such as formal‐ether concentration or molecular techniques, to obtain accurate prevalence data and differentiate between pathogenic and non‐pathogenic amoebae. Crucially, prospective analytical studies are needed to identify specific risk factors such as particular dormitories or hygiene behaviors that can inform more precise and effective control strategies.
