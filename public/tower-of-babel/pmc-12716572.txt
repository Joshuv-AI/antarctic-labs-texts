CD300e is a driver of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and colorectal cancer progression via macrophage reprogramming
Authors: Annica Barizza, Stefania Vassallo, Laura Masatti, Mattia Laffranchi, Sofia Giacometti, Silvia Lonardi, Mattia Bugatti, Sara Coletta, Chiara Della Bella, Mario Milco D’Elios, Simone Pizzini, Antonio Rosato, William Vermi, Matteo Fassan, Gaya Spolverato, Silvano Sozzani, Enrica Calura, Roberta Sommaggio, Gaia Codolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) progression is shaped by the tumor microenvironment, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which often adopt immunosuppressive functions. CD300e, a myeloid receptor involved in immune regulation, has an uncharacterized role in CRC.MethodsFunctional studies were conducted in azoxymethane/dextran sodium sulfate and MC38 murine CRC models using CD300e systemic and myeloid-specific CD300e knockout mice, and adoptive transfer experiments assessed macrophage-intrinsic effects. Human studies included analysis of CD300e expression in matched tumor and normal tissue from patients with CRC and in vitro co-culture of patient-derived colon tumor organoids with monocytes to study CD300e induction and TAM polarization.ResultsIn vivo, CD300e deficiency led to reduced tumor burden, enhanced major histocompatibility complex expression on TAMs, and improved T-cell responses. CD300e-deficient macrophages exhibited increased phagocytic activity, antigen presentation, and support for T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. Adoptive transfer confirmed that macrophage-intrinsic CD300e expression is sufficient to suppress T-cell function and promote tumor growth. In patients with CRC, CD300e is selectively upregulated in tumor-infiltrating monocytes and macrophages, driving a suppressive phenotype marked by impaired antigen presentation. Tumor-derived signals in vitro induce CD300e expression and promote a protumorigenic macrophage profile.ConclusionsOur findings identify CD300e as a critical regulator of macrophage-mediated immune suppression in CRC and a potential target for reprogramming TAMs to enhance immunotherapy.Keywords: Colorectal Cancer, Macrophage, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Immune modulatory

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the most common malignancy of the gastrointestinal tract and the second most common cause of death related to cancer.1 2

A key component of CRC progression is the tumor microenvironment (TME), a dynamic and heterogeneous ecosystem composed not only of cancer cells but also of immune cells, stromal components, vasculature, and secreted factors.3 4

Within this complex ecosystem, immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes and myeloid cells, play a dual role, either promoting tumor elimination or enabling immune escape and tumor growth, depending on their functional orientation.

Recent studies have revealed a strong correlation between the immune landscape of the TME and clinical outcomes in patients with CRC.5 6 The development of the Immunoscore system, which quantifies cytotoxic T-cell infiltration in tumor tissue, has underscored the prognostic value of immune composition in CRC, often surpassing conventional tumor, node, metastases staging in predicting patient survival.7 8 Despite advances in screening and therapy, a large proportion of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, and responses to immunotherapy remain limited, suggesting the presence of dominant immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME that inhibit effective antitumor immunity.

Among the immune populations that support cancer growth in the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent a dominant myeloid population and play a dual role in tumor immunity.9,11 Although TAMs can support antitumor responses, in CRC they are often reprogrammed by tumor-derived cues into an immunosuppressive, tumor-promoting phenotype.12 13 These reprogrammed TAMs impair cytotoxic T lymphocyte function, promote angiogenesis, and facilitate metastasis, and their abundance correlates with poor patient outcomes.14 High TAM infiltration in CRC has been consistently associated with worse clinical outcomes, poor responses to immunotherapy, and tumor resistance to conventional treatments.15,18

Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying TAM reprogramming is crucial for the development of effective therapies. Several attempts to deplete or reprogram TAMs have shown limited success in the clinic, likely due to the functional heterogeneity and plasticity of these cells.19,21 Therefore, identifying specific molecular pathways that drive TAM-mediated immune suppression remains a priority.

One emerging candidate is CD300e, a myeloid-specific receptor whose role in cancer is poorly defined.22 23 Although originally classified as an activating receptor,24 CD300e engagement has been shown to inhibit antigen presentation and T-cell activation in macrophages, suggesting an immunosuppressive function.25 26 Moreover, upregulation of CD300e has been linked to a regenerative or immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype in various pathological contexts.27,29 Despite its expression in several tumor types, including CRC, the functional role of CD300e in tumor immunity remains uncharacterized. Intriguingly, elevated CD300e expression correlates with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types, positioning it as a potential immune regulatory checkpoint.30 31

Here, we demonstrate that CD300e promotes tumor growth by enforcing an immunosuppressive program in TAMs. Using two murine CRC models—azoxymethane/dextran sodium sulfate (AOM/DSS)-induced and MC38-derived tumors—we show that CD300e deficiency enhances antigen presentation, phagocytosis, and T-cell activation, leading to reduced tumor burden. CD300e-deficient macrophages were more immunostimulatory and supported cytotoxic T-cell responses. Adoptive transfer of CD300e−/− macrophages into tumor-bearing hosts confirmed that CD300e expression on macrophages alone is sufficient to drive immune suppression and tumor progression. These in vivo findings are complemented by human data showing CD300e upregulation in tumor-infiltrating monocytes/macrophages and induction by tumor-derived signals in vitro. Tumor-associated CD300e+ macrophages displayed low major histocompatibility complex (MHC) expression and poor antigen-presenting capacity, further reinforcing the immunosuppressive role of CD300e in human CRC.

Materials and methods

Animals

The mice were housed at the Department of Biology, University of Padua Animal Facility. The mice were housed in a temperature (22–24°C) and humidity-controlled (50%) colony room and maintained on a 12 hours light/dark cycle with standard food and freely available water and environmental enrichment.

CD300e total knockout (KO) and CD300e conditional KO mouse models were generated on a C57BL/6J background at the Transgenic Core Facility of the University of Copenhagen via the CRISPR/Cas9 system in mouse zygotes. Heterozygous male mice carrying a deletion encompassing exon 2 of the Cd300e gene in all body tissues were used as founders of the new CD300e−/− mouse line. Similarly, heterozygous male mice with LoxP site insertions flanking exon 2 were used as founders for generating the new CD300efl/fl mouse line, which was crossed with LysM Cre mice to generate a conditional KO mouse lacking CD300e expression on myeloid cells (CD300eLysM). Wild-type (CD300e+/+) C57Bl/6J mice were used as controls for CD300e−/− mice, and CD300efl/fl mice were used as controls for CD300eLysM.

All experiments were conducted on age-matched and sex-matched 8–10 weeks old mice housed in the same animal facility.

Colitis-associated CRC and subcutaneous mouse experimental models are described in the online supplemental methods.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry samples were acquired via an LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA), and the data were analyzed via FlowJo V.10.3 (TreeStar, Ashland, Oregon, USA). A detailed description of samples processing and staining is reported in the online supplemental methods.

Immunosuppressive activity assay

The immunosuppressive activity of F4/80+ tumor-infiltrating cells was evaluated by determining the proliferation of allogenic CellTrace-labeled splenic T cells. Briefly, T cells were stained with 0.5 µM CellTrace Violet Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen, Molecular Probes) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, activated by coating with 1 µg/mL anti-CD3 and 5 µg/mL soluble anti-CD28 (BioLegend) mAb and co-cultured in flat-bottom 96-well plates at a 1:3 ratio with F4/80+ macrophages isolated from CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− tumors with anti-F4/80 MicroBeads UltraPure following the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec). After 4 days at 37°C and 5% CO2, T-cell proliferation was assessed by staining the cells with an anti-CD3 APC, anti-CD4 BV650 and anti-CD8 PerCP-Cy5.5 mAbs (BioLegend) for flow cytometry analysis and analyzing them with a BD LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The T-cell proliferation index was analyzed with FlowJo V.10.3 and calculated as the total number of divisions divided by the number of cells that went into division.

Bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has been performed on bone marrow (BM) monocytes isolated from CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mice. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) and data are available in onlinesupplemental tables 14.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on CD11b+ cells isolated from CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mouse adenomas of mice subjected to AOM/DSS tumorigenesis. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. Data are available in onlinesupplemental tables 515.

The human scRNA-seq dataset was downloaded from the GEO (GSE178341) and was provided by Pelka et al32; data analysis is available in onlinesupplemental tables 1620.

The detailed methodologies for sequencing and analysis are available in online supplemental methods.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism V.8.0 software via one-way or two-way analysis of variance followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test or Tukey’s test, as appropriate. For two-group comparisons, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed. All the data are presented as the means±SDs, unless otherwise stated, and differences between groups were considered statistically significant at p<0.05. *p≤0.05; **p≤0.01; ***p≤0.001; ****p≤0.0001.

All detailed methodologies are available in the online supplemental methods. Primers sequences are detailed in online supplemental table 21.

Results

CD300e supports colitis-associated colon cancer development

To assess the in vivo function of CD300e in CRC, we generated a systemic CD300E-deficient mouse (online supplemental figure 1A) and characterized its phenotype in both steady state and tumor conditions. In CD300e+/+ tissues, CD300e was expressed primarily in the blood, BM, and liver, which are enriched with monocytes/macrophages, and at lower levels in the heart, muscle, lymph nodes, lungs, and spleen (online supplemental figure 1B). Efficient gene deletion was confirmed by assessing gene expression across different tissues (online supplemental figure 1C). Notably, CD300e deletion did not affect leukocyte populations in the blood or BM in either sex (online supplemental figure 1D,E), nor did it alter the phenotype of peritoneal macrophages (online supplemental figure 1F). Histological analysis revealed no significant differences between CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mice under homeostatic conditions (online supplemental figure 1G).

To gain molecular insight into CD300e function, we performed bulk RNA-seq on BM-derived monocytes from CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mice. Several genes were differentially expressed in the absence of CD300e (online supplemental figure 1H). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of genes involved in antigen presentation, immune receptor activity, cytokine signaling, and chemokine-related processes (online supplemental figure 1I). In particular, CD300e−/− monocytes presented upregulation of MHC class II genes (H2-Ab1, H2-Aa, H2-Eb1, and Cd74) and proinflammatory/immunostimulatory genes (Tnfrsf1b, Il17ra, and Ccr7), whereas immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting genes such as Ccl233 and Trem234 were markedly downregulated (online supplemental figure 1J and online supplemental table 2). These data suggest that CD300e shapes monocyte identity by promoting a transcriptional program associated with immunosuppression and tumor support, which is consistent with its regulatory role in human monocyte activation and function.26

CD300e contribution to CRC development was first evaluated in a colitis-associated CRC model using AOM and DSS.35 Although not statistically significant, CD300e+/+ mice tended to have increased mortality, particularly during the first DSS cycle (figure 1A). CD300e−/− mice experienced significantly less weight loss than their CD300e+/+ counterparts did (figure 1B), despite having comparable baseline weights (online supplemental figure 2A) and similar weight gains in untreated controls (online supplemental figure 2B). Colon shortening, a hallmark of DSS-induced injury, was significantly attenuated in CD300e−/− mice (figure 1C), accompanied by reduced intestinal permeability (online supplemental figure 3A). The baseline colon length in the untreated mice was equivalent between the genotypes (online supplemental figure 2C).

Importantly, CD300e deficiency led to a significant reduction in tumor burden, as evidenced by fewer neoplastic lesions and a smaller tumor area (figure 1D), which was confirmed histologically (online supplemental figure 3B). Myeloid cell infiltration within adenomas was modestly reduced in CD300e−/− mice, mainly due to a lower number of infiltrating neutrophils, which was consistent with the AOM/DSS model (online supplemental figure 3C). Although monocyte/macrophage infiltration was limited overall, tumor-associated F4/80+ cells in CD300e−/− mice presented slightly increased MHC-I and MHC-II expression (online supplemental figure 3D). This prompted scRNA-seq analysis of adenoma-infiltrating myeloid cells to evaluate potential shifts in their transcriptional profiles.

We observed lower overall myeloid infiltration in CD300e−/− mice, with neutrophils comprising the predominant population (online supplemental table 6). Among the nine identified clusters, two represented monocytes/macrophages present at similar frequencies across genotypes, although absolute numbers were greater in CD300e+/+ mice (figure 1E). Hallmark pathway analysis revealed that CD300e−/− monocytes downregulated several inflammatory and tumor-associated pathways (figure 1F). Overall, CD300e+/+ monocytes/macrophages expressed higher levels of genes associated with immunosuppression and tumor support, including Cd36, Spp1, Il6, Cd274, Ccl5, Ccl17, Ccl22, and Ccl24 (figure 1G and online supplemental figure 3E).36

Although tumor-infiltrating T cells were present at a low frequency, their CD4+ and CD8+ proportions were similar between genotypes (online supplemental figure 3F). Moreover, to further characterize the tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte compartment, we analyzed the activation and exhaustion profiles of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The frequencies of CD69+ and CD44+CD62L⁻ cells were comparable between CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mice, indicating similar activation status (online supplemental figure 3G,H). However, the proportion of programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)+ T cells was significantly reduced in both CD4+ and CD8+ subsets from CD300e−/− mice compared with their controls (online supplemental figure 3I), suggesting a decrease in T-cell exhaustion.

This was further substantiated by the fact that CD300e⁻/⁻ mice presented a significantly greater frequency of interferon (IFN)-γ-producing CD8+ T cells in mesenteric lymph nodes (figure 1H), indicating enhanced systemic antitumor immunity in the absence of CD300e.

No differences in T-cell activation were detected in untreated animals (online supplemental figure 2D,E).

To confirm that the observed effects were due to myeloid-intrinsic CD300e, we generated conditional knockout (cKO) mice in which CD300e deletion was restricted to the myeloid compartment (indicated as CD300eLysM) (online supplemental figure 4A). These cKO mice recapitulated the phenotype observed in global knockouts, showing reduced weight loss, decreased tumor burden, increased frequencies of IFN-γ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and reduced frequency of exhausted T cells (figure 2A–D and online supplemental figure 4B–F).

Lack of CD300e enhances antitumor immunity by reprogramming tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells and increasing T-cell function

To further investigate the role of CD300e in shaping the tumor immune landscape, we employed a subcutaneous CRC model by injecting the MC38 colon cancer cell line into CD300e−/− and CD300e+/+ mice—an established model in which tumor progression is known to rely on the activity of tumor-infiltrating macrophages.37 While MC38 tumors grew progressively in CD300e+/+ mice, their growth was significantly impaired in CD300e−/− mice (figure 3A,B), in line with the findings from the AOM/DSS CRC model. To characterize the immune microenvironment, we performed flow cytometry analysis of MC38 tumors 21 days postinjection (the gating strategy is shown in online supplemental figure 5). CD11bhi myeloid cells were less abundant in CD300e−/− tumors because of a reduction in both neutrophils (figure 3C) and monocytes (CD11b+Ly6C+Ly6G−) and monocyte-derived macrophages (CD11b+Ly6CloF4/80+) (figure 3D,F; online supplemental figure 6). Despite their lower abundance, monocytes and macrophages infiltrating CD300e−/− tumors presented significantly higher expression levels of MHC-II and MHC-I molecules (figure 3E,G), suggesting a greater immunostimulatory profile in the absence of CD300e. Given the overall reduction of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in CD300e−/− tumors, we next sought to determine whether this decrease reflected an intrinsic defect in the migratory capacity of CD300e-deficient monocytes. To address this, we performed an in vivo migration assay in which BM-purified CD300e+/+ or CD300e−/− monocytes were adoptively transferred into CD45.1 MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Contrary to the hypothesis of impaired migration, CD300e−/− monocytes displayed significantly higher migratory capacity than their CD300e+/+ counterparts (online supplemental figure 7A). Thus, the reduced myeloid infiltration observed in CD300e−/− tumors cannot be attributed to defective migration, but rather reflects the smaller tumor size and the lower production of myeloid-attracting chemokines in the absence of CD300e (online supplemental figure 7B). Collectively, these results indicate that CD300e deficiency enhances both the immunostimulatory phenotype and the intrinsic motility of monocytes, while limiting the establishment of a myeloid-enriched TME.

Given that elevated expression of antigen-presenting molecules is often associated with improved T-cell activation, we next analyzed the lymphoid compartment. Compared with those from CD300e+/+ control mice, tumors from CD300e−/− mice presented a greater proportion of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (figure 3H; gating strategy detailed in online supplemental figure 8). In addition, CD300e−/− tumors contained increased frequencies of activated and effector memory T cells within both the CD4+ and CD8+ subsets (figure 3I,J), indicating enhanced T-cell activation. Functional profiling revealed a significant increase in the number of IFN-γ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs) in the CD300e−/− mice (figure 3K). This heightened activation was accompanied by greater cytotoxic potential, as CD8+ TILs from CD300e−/− tumors expressed higher levels of granzyme B and perforin (figure 3L). Given the differences observed in T-cell infiltration, we also analyzed the regulatory T-cell compartment in the MC38 subcutaneous model. CD300e−/− tumors showed a significantly lower proportion of Treg cells compared with CD300e+/+ counterparts, further supporting the establishment of a more immunostimulatory environment (figure 3M). Moreover, analysis of PD-1 expression revealed a marked reduction in exhausted CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in CD300e−/− tumors (figure 3N), strengthening the evidence for restored T-cell functionality in the absence of CD300e.

These findings were corroborated in cKO; similar to those in full knockouts, CD300e cKO mice presented reduced tumor growth, decreased myeloid infiltration, increased MHC expression on myeloid cells, and an enhanced T-cell response characterized by elevated IFN-γ production and CD8+ cytotoxic activity (figure 4).

To directly compare the functional properties of macrophages from CD300e+/+ and CD300e−/− mice, we performed an ex vivo phagocytosis assay on F4/80+ tumor-infiltrating macrophages. CD300e−/− macrophages exhibited significantly greater phagocytosis capacity (figure 5A), which is consistent with the notion that TAMs adopt a less phagocytic, immunosuppressive phenotype during tumor progression.38 We next evaluated the ability of tumor-infiltrating macrophages to promote T-cell activation by co-culturing isolated F4/80+ macrophages with autologous CellTrace-labeled T cells. CD300e−/− macrophages induced markedly stronger T-cell proliferation than CD300e+/+ controls (figure 5B). Subset analysis revealed that this enhancement was predominantly confined to CD8+ T cells, as the proliferation rate of CD4+ T cells was not significantly affected (figure 5B).

Together, these results demonstrate that CD300e deficiency promotes the acquisition of a more immunostimulatory macrophage phenotype, which in turn enhances T-cell activation, expansion, and cytotoxicity in the TME.

CD300e expression on macrophages is sufficient to promote tumor growth and suppress the antitumor T-cell response

To determine whether the tumor-promoting effects of CD300e are directly mediated by its expression on macrophages, we performed adoptive transfer experiments using tumor-infiltrating macrophages isolated from CD300e−/− and CD300e+/+ mice. These macrophages were co-injected at a 1:3 ratio with MC38 tumor cells into CD300e+/+ recipient mice (figure 6A). Notably, tumors from mice that received CD300e−/−-derived macrophages exhibited significantly slower tumor growth than those from mice that received CD300e+/+ macrophages (figure 6B,C). Although the overall infiltration of monocytes and macrophages within tumors was comparable between the groups, the TIL profile recapitulated the immune phenotypes observed in CD300e−/− hosts. Specifically, recipient mice injected with CD300e−/− macrophages presented increased frequencies of IFN-γ-producing T cells (figure 6D) and greater cytotoxic potential among CD8+ lymphocytes (figure 6E).

To confirm these findings in an independent system, we repeated the co-injection experiment using BMDMs polarized with interleukin (IL)-4 to mimic the TAM phenotype. When CD300e+/+ recipient mice were co-injected with MC38 cells and either CD300e+/+ or CD300e⁻/⁻ BMDMs at a 1:3 ratio (online supplemental figure 9A), mice receiving CD300e⁻/⁻ BMDMs exhibited reduced tumor growth (online supplemental figure 9B,C). This effect was accompanied by a greater proportion of macrophages expressing MHC-I and MHC-II (online supplemental figure 9D) and increased infiltration of activated T cells (online supplemental figure 9E,F).

Conversely, when tumor-isolated macrophages from CD300e+/+ or CD300e−/− donors were transferred into CD300e−/− recipient mice (online supplemental figure 10A), those receiving CD300e+/+ macrophages presented enhanced tumor growth (online supplemental figure 10B,C), coupled with reduced frequencies of IFN-γ-producing T cells and diminished CD8+ cytotoxic activity (online supplemental figure 10D–F), confirming that macrophage-intrinsic CD300e expression is sufficient to dampen antitumor immunity even in a CD300e-deficient environment.

To further distinguish donor-derived from recipient-derived macrophage response, we performed adoptive transfer experiments using CD45.1 wild-type recipient mice co-injected with MC38 cells and tumor-derived macrophages isolated from either CD300e+/+ or CD300e−/− CD45.2 donors. As shown in online supplemental figure 10G–I, CD300e−/− donor macrophages retained higher expression of MHC-I and MHC-II molecules within tumors, confirming their enhanced immunostimulatory profile. Notably, recipient macrophages in mice receiving CD300e−/− donor cells also upregulated antigen-presentation markers compared with those receiving CD300e+/+ macrophages, suggesting that CD300e-deficient macrophages can propagate an activating signal within the myeloid compartment.

Taken together, these data provide direct evidence that CD300e expression on macrophages is sufficient to drive tumor progression by dampening antitumor T-cell responses, highlighting its central role in orchestrating an immunosuppressive TME.

Colorectal cancer promotes CD300e expression in dysfunctional tumor-infiltrating macrophages

To translate our findings to human CRC, we analyzed matched tumor and normal colon mucosa from patients with CRC. Compared with matched normal tissue, tumor mucosa presented significantly increased expression of CD300e (figure 7A), associated with reduced MHC-II expression, accompanied by significant downregulation of CIITA and STAT1, the latter being a key transcription factor in the IFN-γ-CIITA axis (figure 7B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the increase in CD300e expression within CRC tissue reflects enhanced infiltration of CD163+ macrophages co-expressing CD300e. Importantly, the proportion of CD300e+/MHC-IIlow macrophages was significantly elevated in tumor tissue relative to matched normal mucosa (figure 7C), supporting a role for CD300e in fostering a suppressive myeloid compartment within the CRC microenvironment.

To further characterize the phenotype and functional properties of CD300e+ myeloid cells in CRC, we leveraged a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset (GSE178341)32 from primary tumor and adjacent normal samples from untreated patients with CRC. Given that CD300E is predominantly expressed by myeloid cells, we focused our analysis on this compartment, and we identified 20 distinct clusters organized in granulocytes, monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells based on their transcriptional profiles and the previous authors’ classification (figure 7D and online supplemental table 3). A comparison of cluster distributions between normal and tumor tissues revealed increased monocyte infiltration in tumor samples (figure 7E and online supplemental table 3). Whereas monocytes and macrophages account for approximately two-thirds of the myeloid compartment in normal tissues, their proportion is notably reduced in tumors, resulting in a more heterogeneous myeloid composition. CD300E expression was enriched in several monocyte clusters (Mono_a, Mono_b, Mono_C and Mono_d) and in one macrophage cluster characterized by an active IFN gene signature (Macro_IFN). Although monocytes and macrophages were the most abundant cell types in normal samples, CD300E expression was upregulated in tumor-infiltrating cells compared with their normal counterparts. Among these clusters, the Mono_a cluster presented significantly increased mean expression of CD300E in tumor tissues (figure 7F). A cross-species comparison revealed that Mono_a cluster shared a transcriptional signature with the murine monocytes (online supplemental figure 11), supporting functional conservation. Given the positivity for CD300E of tumor-derived monocyte and selected macrophage clusters, and by considering that the majority of tumor-infiltrating macrophages differentiate from recruited circulating monocytes, we performed a pseudotime trajectory analysis restricted to monocytes and macrophages, which revealed the expected progression from monocyte to macrophages (online supplemental figure 12A,B). Notably, as shown in online supplemental figure 12C, the expression of CD300E decreased along the monocyte maturation trajectory and terminated approximately when there was a switch from Macro_IFN to Macro_c. In summary, in CRC, the CD300E expression is largely confined to tumor-infiltrating monocytes and early monocyte-derived macrophages.

To investigate the clinical relevance of CD300e expression, we analyzed transcriptomic data from a large CRC cohort.39 This analysis confirmed that CD300E expression is significantly higher in tumor tissues compared with matched normal counterparts, and that this upregulation is independent of tumor stage and patient sex (online supplemental figure 13A). Consistent with our findings, high CD300E expression was associated with poor overall survival, confirming its negative prognostic value (online supplemental figure 13B). Patients with elevated CD300E levels also displayed higher predicted infiltration of monocytes and macrophages; notably, individuals with high monocyte or macrophage M1 infiltration exhibited a greater risk of death (HR<1), suggesting that these populations may contribute to the adverse prognostic impact of CD300E (online supplemental figure 13C). Although CD300E expression did not correlate directly with total monocyte or T-cell abundance, multivariate analysis controlling for potential confounders confirmed CD300E as an independent prognostic factor. Together, these data reinforce the notion that CD300E expression defines a macrophage-dominated, immunosuppressive TME associated with reduced patient survival.

The colorectal tumor cells drive the differentiation of immunosuppressive CD300e+ macrophages

Given that CD300e+ monocyte-derived macrophages in CRC tumors display an immunosuppressive profile, we sought to determine whether this phenotype could be directly instructed by the tumor epithelium. To this end, we established an in vitro co-culture system using patient-derived tumor colon organoids (TCOs) and matched normal colon organoids (NCOs), which were incubated with peripheral blood monocytes (figure 8A).

As previously described in the literature,25 40 and as shown in figure 7D–F, CD300e is highly expressed in circulating monocytes and is progressively downregulated during differentiation into macrophages, unless sustained by specific stimuli. In agreement with these reports, monocytes isolated from peripheral blood expressed markedly higher CD300e levels compared with their differentiated macrophage counterparts (online supplemental figure 14A). After 5 days of co-culture, monocytes exposed to both NCOs and TCOs acquired a macrophage-like phenotype (figure 8B), with TCO-exposed cells showing distinct immunological features. Notably, exposure to TCOs restored the expression of CD300e (figure 8C), reducing MHC-II and CD86 expression, and increasing expression of the anti-inflammatory markers CD206 and CD163 relative to HCO-exposed cells (figure 8D and online supplemental figure 14B). Functionally, these cells also secreted higher levels of IL-6 and IL-10, two cytokines associated with tumor-promoting inflammation (figure 8E).

To directly evaluate the functional consequences of CD300e modulation, transient CD300e-silenced monocytes were exposed to NCO-conditioned medium (NCO-CM) or TCO-conditioned medium (TCO-CM) prior to co-culture with autologous T cells.

As shown in figure 8F, T cells co-cultured with monocytes exposed to TCO-CM exhibited markedly reduced proliferation compared with those cultured with monocytes exposed to NCO-CM, consistent with the immunosuppressive nature of tumor-derived factors. CD300e silencing partially restored T-cell proliferation under TCO exposure.

Together, these findings demonstrate that colorectal tumor-derived factors sustain CD300e expression in monocyte-derived macrophages, promoting their acquisition of an immunosuppressive phenotype that limits T-cell activation. This establishes CD300e as a key mediator of tumor-driven myeloid reprogramming in the CRC microenvironment.

Discussion

CD300e is a relatively understudied member of the CD300 family, originally described as an activating molecule selectively expressed by cells of the myeloid lineage. While emerging evidence has implicated CD300e in modulating immune response during chronic inflammation and infection,25 26 its role in cancer immunity has remained unexplored.

In this study, we provide the first in vivo evidence that CD300e is a functional regulator of tumor macrophages behavior and a key determinant of the immunosuppressive TME in CRC.

Using complementary murine and human models, we show that CD300e expression enforces macrophage-mediated immune suppression, restrains antigen presentation, and limits effective T-cell responses.

In both AOM/DSS and MC38 tumor models, genetic ablation of CD300e significantly reduced tumor burden and enhanced antitumor immunity.

CD300e-deficient mice displayed increased expression of MHC-I and MHC-II molecules both on tumor-infiltrating monocytes and macrophages, elevated frequencies of IFN-γ-producing and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, and decreased PD-1 and Treg accumulation, collectively pointing to the restoration of an immunostimulatory TME. The conditional deletion of CD300e in myeloid cells and adoptive transfer experiments confirmed that CD300e functions in a macrophage-intrinsic manner to restrain immune activation.

Mechanistically, CD300e-deficient macrophages exhibited increased phagocytic and antigen-presenting capacity. These features are hallmarks of macrophages that support productive antitumor immunity.41 Adoptive transfer experiments further demonstrated that macrophage-specific CD300e expression was sufficient to drive tumor growth and suppress cytotoxic T-cell responses, reinforcing the conclusion that CD300e is not merely a marker of TAMs but a key driver of their suppressive function.

Together, these findings identify CD300e as an upstream regulator of macrophage-T-cell crosstalk, capable of dictating whether the TME evolves toward immune activation or suppression.

Our murine findings were substantiated by human data. CD300e expression was elevated in tumor-infiltrating monocytes and early monocyte-derived macrophages from patients with CRC. Notably, CD300e+ cells exhibited reduced expression of MHC-II and marked downregulation of key IFN-γ effectors, including STAT1 and CIITA, the latter being the master regulator of MHC-II transcription, pointing to compromised antigen-presenting capacity. This finding is consistent with our previous observations26 and suggests dysregulated or suppressed IFN-γ response within the TME, leading to impaired antigen presentation and contributing to local immune suppression. Our data position CD300e not merely as a marker of immunosuppressive TAMs, but as a functional regulator that acts upstream of the transcriptional downregulation of the STAT1–CIITA axis. This mechanistic link provides a functional explanation for earlier observations in CRC,42 where MHC-IIlow/CD206high TAMs failed to support effective T-cell activation.

Pseudotime analysis revealed that CD300e expression is highest at early stages of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation, declining as cells mature. This pattern supports the idea that CD300e may act as an early molecular checkpoint skewing differentiation toward a suppressive phenotype.

Functional co-culture experiments with patient-derived tumor organoids demonstrated that tumor-derived signals are sufficient to induce CD300e expression in monocytes and instruct the acquisition of an immunosuppressive phenotype. These tumor-educated macrophages exhibited reduced MHC-II and CD86 expression and secreted higher levels of IL-6 and IL-10, consistent with a protumor profile.

Silencing CD300e in primary human monocytes restored T-cell activation even under tumor-conditioned conditions, confirming that CD300e signaling actively restrains antigen-specific T-cell proliferation.

Our findings suggest that CD300e acts as a molecular sensor integrating tumor-derived inflammatory and metabolic cues within the TME. In line with this, tumor organoids and in vivo CRC tissues are enriched in IL-10, IL-6, and oxidized or apoptotic cell-derived lipids, which have been shown to engage other CD300 family receptors and promote tolerogenic macrophage polarization.2338 43,45 Although direct evidence for lipid binding to CD300e is still limited, these observations support the idea that converging tumor-derived signals activate CD300e to enforce macrophage immunosuppression. We propose that CD300e signaling establishes a feed-forward loop in which tumor-derived factors sustain receptor expression, and CD300e engagement, in turn, inhibits macrophage activation and antigen presentation through interference with STAT1–CIITA–MHC-II pathways. Such a circuit may represent a previously unrecognized mechanism of immune evasion in CRC.

From a translational perspective, our data identify CD300e as a macrophage-centered immune checkpoint. Although macrophage infiltration has often been associated with favorable outcomes in CRC, emerging evidence,21 including our findings, emphasizes that macrophage phenotype, rather than abundance, dictates their contribution to tumor progression. CD300e-deficient tumors showed reduced macrophage infiltration but stronger antitumor immunity, underscoring the relevance of macrophage polarization over total myeloid content. This highlights CD300e not only as a marker of suppressive TAMs but also as a driver of their tumor-promoting behavior. Its blockade could restore macrophage antigen-presenting capacity and relieve T-cell suppression, thereby complementing existing immunotherapies that primarily target lymphocytes. Combination approaches that concurrently target CD300e and checkpoint pathways or integrate CD300e blockade with therapies modulating myeloid compartments (eg, CSF1R or TREM2 inhibitors) could achieve synergistic reactivation of antitumor immunity.

In conclusion, this study identifies CD300e as a previously unrecognized orchestrator of immune suppression in CRC. By enforcing macrophage reprogramming toward a tolerogenic phenotype, CD300e limits antigen presentation and T-cell activation. Therapeutic targeting of CD300e may thus represent a promising strategy to reprogram the myeloid compartment and enhance the efficacy of existing immunotherapies.

Future work should therefore focus on testing selective CD300e inhibitors or blocking antibodies in preclinical CRC models and exploring their combination with existing immunotherapies to enhance antitumor efficacy.

In addition, the identification of tumor-derived factors responsible for CD300e induction may reveal metabolic or cytokine pathways that could be co-targeted to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Together, these translational perspectives position CD300e as a tractable macrophage-centered checkpoint whose blockade could synergize with existing immunotherapies to overcome resistance and improve patient outcomes.
