Approaches to a crisis in early pregnancy: an explorative qualitative study of medical students and doctors in training in Ireland, using a story completion model
Authors: Mary Higgins, Sharon Cooley, Deirdre Hayes-Ryan, Brendan Dempsey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA crisis in early pregnancy can be due to an unplanned pregnancy or a suspected abnormality. Pregnant people have the right to unbiased and comprehensive advice of all options from healthcare providers. Using story completion models (SCM), the aim of this qualitative study was to explore the attitudes of medical students and doctors in training towards crisis pregnancy, specifically two scenarios: early unplanned pregnancy and fatal fetal abnormality (anencephaly). Participants were invited from medical students attending University College Dublin (UCD) and trainees in Obstetrics and Gynaecology at the Royal College of Physicians of Ireland (RCPI) from July to December 2022. SCM involves giving the consenting participant an introduction to a hypothetical situation that acts as the beginning of a story and asking them to complete it. Stories were compiled and analysed using thematic analysis. Research Ethics Committee approval was given by both UCD and the RCPI. The standards for reporting qualitative research guidelines were followed. Eight doctors in training and six medical students consented to participate in the study; all but two medical students completed both stories to the required word count, giving 25 stories for analysis. For both situations, stories described a variety of approaches, all of which were based on the person's, or couple's, wishes, from continuing in pregnancy to deciding to end the pregnancy. SCM allowed detailed analysis of potentially sensitive subjects such as pregnancy options. This study showed that participating medical students and doctors in training recognise that pregnant people have the right to all choices in crisis pregnancy.

Introduction

A crisis may occur in pregnancy for many reasons. The pregnancy may have been unplanned,1 unintended,2 or unwanted (a term that is unpopular with women themselves),3 or a complication such as a fetal abnormality may occur in a planned or now wanted pregnancy.4 These can represent very different concepts and with overlapping outcomes.

Unplanned pregnancy rates vary worldwide from 1.1% to 14% of all pregnancies,5 and may be as high as 50%.6 The concept of planned versus unplanned pregnancy may not be a dichotomous one but in fact, a spectrum where most people lie somewhere in between.1,7 In a qualitative study based in the United Kingdom, women described a pregnancy as “planned” only if it met four specific criteria: intending to become pregnant, stopping contraception, being at the right time in their life to have a pregnancy, and partner agreement.3 In contrast, “unplanned” pregnancy could mean a variety of situations, including where some but not all of the four criteria would be met, or where pregnancy was a result of mistake or accident, or unintended. Equally importantly, with advances in pregnancy screening and diagnosis, genetic or anatomical abnormalities may be suspected and then diagnosed, even in early pregnancy. The diagnosis of a fatal fetal abnormality in pregnancy at any stage can be devastating. People in this situation can grieve the loss of a “normal” pregnancy, a healthy baby, and the interruption in the pathway of becoming a parent.8,9 With the diagnosis of a fetal abnormality, depending on the severity of the abnormality, parental wishes, and legal circumstances, between 8% and 60% of parents will choose to continue in the pregnancy.10–13

While many women may choose to continue a crisis pregnancy, not all will, and women who are uncertain often wish to openly discuss all pregnancy options available to them.14 Options can include choosing to continue a pregnancy, to end the pregnancy, or to consent for adoption after the birth. In all situations, women and pregnant people have a right to and deserve unbiased and comprehensive advice of all options15 and healthcare professionals (HCPs) play an imperative role in this process. Even where women have the legal right to all options, HCPs can act as facilitators or barriers to such care.16 Not only do HCPs need to be aware of clinical practice and national legislation17 but also of the ethical issues and their own conscious or unconscious personal values on pregnancy options.18,19 Consideration of both continuing the pregnancy and ending it is important, and HCPs should not presume that one is a more legitimate option than the other.20

The role of HCPs in supporting those experiencing a crisis pregnancy also extends beyond pregnancy options counselling. When caring for people with an unplanned pregnancy, HCPs’ role also includes providing and co-ordinating person-centred care based on the decision of the woman, as well as non-judgemental or non-coercive prevention efforts aimed at avoiding future unplanned pregnancies.21 For those choosing to continue a pregnancy affected by a fetal abnormality, relationships with HCPs are fundamental to this process,22–24 allowing “support, comfort, guidance and connection” for families.25 Though some research papers have highlighted the lack of defined pathways of care for those choosing to continue a pregnancy with a fetal abnormality,9,26 the goodwill and good intentions of HCPs are often apparent22,26 as well as support for models of perinatal palliative care.26 For those who choose to end the pregnancy affected by fetal anomalies, HCPs can be cognisant of the unique grieving process of termination, and their role as an information provider.27 While parents can report that they felt pressurised to decide, HCPs wish to give time to make the decision that is right for them: “…they have time, they don't need to hurry”.28 Procedures that they may consider routine, such as signing a consent form, can have deep symbolic meaning for the parents9 and supporting the parents to find words and descriptions that work for their situation is crucial for HCPs.20

Pregnancy options counselling is a fundamental part of healthcare, especially for those working in General Practice or Obstetrics and Gynaecology. Using story completion models, the aim of this study was to explore how medical students and doctors in training approached pregnancy options counselling specifically for two scenarios: early unplanned pregnancy and fatal fetal abnormality. Our a priori hope was that these stories would include all options for both pregnancy options, respecting the moral, ethical and now legal rights in Ireland for pregnant people experiencing a crisis in early pregnancy. For context, it is important to recognise that abortion was legalised in Ireland in 2019, allowing abortion without reason under 12 weeks or after this gestation if there was a fatal fetal abnormality, significant maternal health issue or in an emergency.29

Methods

Story completion models

Given the potential sensitivity of the subject area, the most appropriate qualitative method considered by the research team was story completion models (SCM). As the name suggests, SCM involves giving the consenting participant an introduction to a hypothetical situation that acts as the beginning of a story and asking them to complete it as they see appropriate. Initially used in clinical psychoanalysis as a projective technique, SCM has been used in research for sense and meaning making, especially to explore areas which may be more vulnerable to social desirability bias. As well as exploring meanings behind the stories, SCM can also be used with a social constructivist lens where the researchers “interpret these stories as reflecting contemporary discourses upon which subjects draw in making a sense of experience”.30 Non-personal story telling makes it useful for potentially sensitive topics31 such as pregnancy counselling.

This projective nature allows researchers to explore preconceptions and assumptions rather than experience. This is a benefit as it reduces issues with self-report methods, like giving socially desirable responses and lack of personal experience.32 In completing their stories about unplanned pregnancies, participants may impart conscious and unconscious stereotypes they hold about topics including: what it would be like to be in each situation; how each woman became pregnant; who the “stereotypical woman” (or pregnant person) who finds herself in this situation is; and what options are most appropriate in each case, e.g. carry the pregnancy to term, adopt the child or raise the child, abortion, or provide palliative care (e.g. for the case of anencephaly).

Practically, a story introduction for SCM is usually one to four sentences long and introduces the participant to a character and a situation that is designed to encourage them to explore and discuss their assumptions and preconceptions on a topic of interest.30 Topics previously covered using SCM to date include eating disorders,33 health problems,32 disability,34 sexuality,35,36 chronic pain,37 suicidal ideation,38 experiences of the COVID-19 pandemic,39,40 evangelical Christianity,41 and consumer representation.42,43 While word count for stories is not always reported, those that do report stories vary from three to 1252 words. With this, we decided to exclude any stories with less than three words, as these less rich, shorter, thinner stories may represent participants “refusing” the task.44

Recruitment

We invited medical students attending University College Dublin (UCD) and trainees in Obstetrics and Gynaecology at the Royal College of Physicians of Ireland (RCPI) to participate. UCD is one of six medical schools in the Republic of Ireland and has two options for training: an “undergraduate” five-to-six-year programme direct from secondary level education or a “graduate” four-year programme for those with a primary honours’ degree in another area. Medical students were eligible to participate in this study if they completed their obstetrics and gynaecology rotation in their penultimate year of medical school. After they had completed this rotation, a message advertising the study, along with a link to access an information sheet and the online survey, was displayed on their Brightspace to 80 medical students (a virtual learning environment used by the University to co-ordinate all learning and assessment materials). RCPI is the training body for basic and specialist training for medical specialities, including Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and for continuing professional development for trained specialists. All 75 doctors completing RCPI training in Obstetrics and Gynaecology in the Republic of Ireland were eligible and invited to participate by email through the training scheme coordinator as outlined in the research ethics application.

Procedure

Data were collected using a survey between July to December 2022, which was designed and hosted on Qualtrics. After informed consent had been collected, participants were introduced to two stories and asked to complete them as they saw appropriate. One of the scenarios (see Supplementary material) reflected a common early pregnancy scenario, where “Laura” discovers she is seven weeks gestation with an unplanned pregnancy. The other was a rarer complication of pregnancy where “Hannah”, who is 14 weeks pregnant, has discovered that the fetus has anencephaly, a significant fetal abnormality where the skull has not developed, which is likely to end in either fetal intrauterine death or very early neonatal death. A pilot study was conducted to develop and refine the story stems and instructions used for both scenarios. All data collected during this pilot were destroyed prior to data collection commencing for the current study. In order to reduce potential bias based on the order in which these stories were presented, their position in the survey was randomised for each participant. Of note, we also checked to ensure that there were no students or trainees with these names (“Laura” or “Hannah”) before the invitation to participate was circulated.

After participants had completed both stories, basic demographic information was requested. Given the potential sensitivity of the subject area, reflecting on potential power differentials, and with the small population of trainees and medical students, demographic information requested was both optional and limited. All participants were asked their gender, age, and how often they engaged in religious practices as these may be factors that would affect decision-making. Medical students were asked whether they would be willing to engage in abortion care following training in the area, whether they were undergraduate or graduate students, the time remaining to their graduation, and whether they were interested in pursuing a career in Obstetrics and Gynaecology or General Practice (these were deliberately chosen as abortion care is provided mainly within these specialities in the Republic of Ireland). Medical students were also asked if they would be willing to perform abortion care as part of their future career. This question was deliberately chosen to allow a potential comparison with the findings of a previous questionnaire, to see if there was a change in numbers following the 2018 referendum changing the Irish constitution which facilitated the introduction of legal expanded abortion care.29,45 Doctors in training were asked about their grade (level of training, whether Senior House Officer, Registrar or Specialist Registrar) and whether they were involved in abortion care.

Ethics Committee approval was given by both UCD (29th of October 2020; LS-62-Dempsey-Higgins) and the RCPI (20th of October 2020; RCPI RECSAF 135). As this was qualitative research, the standards for reporting of qualitative research guidelines were followed.46

Analysis

In this study, we wanted to explore if the participants referred to abortion care as an appropriate option for either woman or if so, how it was referred to. In addition to describing how the participants discussed the two crisis pregnancies, we also aimed to compare how they discussed the above details between the two cases to compare if certain preconceptions are more prominent for either case. We hypothesised that participants may be more supportive of abortion as an option for women with a pregnancy affected with a fatal fetal abnormality than an early pregnancy.

The completed stories were then compiled in a university secure database and analysed (using a form of Thematic Analysis47,48) to identify themes in how participants used their stories to make meaning of the characters and the situation that they were in. Analysis is mostly considered to be horizontal or vertical.49 Horizontal analysis explores broad themes across the stories and is most like traditional qualitative methods, whereas vertical analysis explores themes in how the stories unfold, i.e. convergences and divergences in the beginning, middle, and end. Story completion models, unlike many qualitative methods, also allow for direct comparison within the data if participants are asked to complete multiple stories.

For qualitative research, it is important to report the researcher characteristics, reflectivity, and reflexivity.50,51 Within the research team MH, SC and DHR are consultant obstetricians each working in tertiary level units in Ireland providing the full range of obstetrics care, including abortion and fetal medicine. MH and SC are maternal-fetal medicine sub-specialists. All three have completed MD/PhD research work. MH is also an academic within UCD, so to reduce the possibility of coercion, students were only invited to participate in the study once they had completed the modules within which she teaches and assesses. We acknowledge that the potential power differential, and hierarchy of clinical care, may have affected the research – for example, consultant involvement may have encouraged some to participate or discouraged others, even though responses were anonymous. BD was a PhD graduate from UCD at the time of the research, who completed a thesis exploring providers’ experiences following the 2018 referendum and legal introduction of expanded abortion care. BD provided a non-clinical interpretation of results. All four researchers supported the introduction of expanded abortion care; MH and SC openly advocated for expanded abortion care in the 2018 referendum. We acknowledge also that our pro-choice stance may have influenced analysis and that the addition of another researcher with different opinions may have changed recruitment or interpretation.

Stories were mostly several paragraphs in length, with a median word count of 148 words for Laura’s scenario (range five to 514 words) and 163 words for Hannah’s scenario (range one to 539 words). The one-word response was excluded giving a final sample of 25 stories for analysis. Scenario one: “Laura”, unplanned pregnancy at seven weeks gestation

Laura’s life circumstances

For “Laura”, some chose to describe a person presumably close to their own situation: a medical student close to graduation with “plans of Australia and intern year that I have carefully crafted over the years” (P1, student), a woman with a “full-time job, busy life” (P11, doctor), or as a “31 years old and in a stable relationship” (P10, doctor). Others picked a character seemingly different to their own personal circumstances, either as a woman who is 41 years old with a “creaking back” (P3, student), or as a 38-year-old with “three beautiful children already” where the “middle one has developmental issues and I'm already stretched thin” (P4, student).

Some described the existence of a partner, even if they were uncertain of their support “I mean is he even ready? Can he help me provide for this baby?” (P2, student) though some were optimistically hopeful of their “loving husband. Loving, but not exactly pro-child. His idea of hell is an afternoon spent at my sisters with her 4-year-old twin girls … But I'm sure he'll love his own child!” (P3, student). Some showed apparent exasperation with the partner “Does my husband have to know?” (P4, student) and “ … dang fool man seducing me that one night out, the only time we got away from the family!” (P6, doctor). Others saw the partner as a source of support “my partner and I will talk through things” (P5, doctor) and “I love my partner and he is fully supportive of the pregnancy” (P8, doctor). For most stories, “Laura” had a partner, though three did not mention a partner (nor lack of one). Where a partner was mentioned, they were always male, and often a husband.

No story mentioned assault or non-consensual intercourse, though there were some exasperated comments about contraception: “Why didn’t we use a condom?” (P6, doctor) or “Was I meant to be [using] more contraception than condoms? They'd always worked before.” (P4, student).

Factors influencing Laura’s decision

Most stories reflected the conflict that a woman may feel in the early stages of an unplanned pregnancy. Two, however, were quick to make decisions, with one defiantly saying “Unplanned, but not unwanted Doctor! Even if my mother thinks I'm too old” (P3, student) and another opting immediately for “termination and respect patient’s autonomy” (P9, doctor). Of the other stories, conflict ranged widely with women reflecting on issues that would affect their decision. These included their own education which was “incomplete” (P1, student), and career prospects, which ranged from “this is too early in my career to start a family” (P2, student) to “This couldn't come at a better time … I'm at the most secure in my career than I've ever been” (P3, student). Readiness varied from those who were not yet ready to start a family “ … a child is the end to my childhood and my ongoing youth” (P1, student) to those who thought that their families were complete “I thought this having babies bit of my life was over” (P4, student). Some had reservations “Ultimately, we have the means and happy family home to support a family. I just don't know how I will manage with two under one” (P7, doctor), or “I do want to have children but thought it would be when I was married and that I would plan it for a few years’ time” (P8, doctor). Others considered their existing children: “personally unable to terminate pregnancy in view of having 3 very much loved and wanted children, how would this child be any different?” (P11, doctor). Some were ready for a pregnancy, even if unplanned “The only reason we ever put off having kids in the first place was because we usually travel so much, but that won't be on the cards again for who knows how long!” (P3, student).

Apart from their partner (if mentioned), other people who were important in the decision-making included friends and family “My parents won’t be happy, I'm sure … . get upset or even disown me” (P2, student) and “the reactions of my friends and (inevitable disappointment) of my parents” (P1, student) and “what will my mother say?” (P7, doctor). Some compared themselves to others who would be in this situation “I have the means and support and lots of people don't and still continue” (P7, doctor).

Practical issues were noted in some of the stories, including how to access abortion care “How much time do I have to decide? Do I have to see other doctors to make these decisions?” (P3, student) or “ … different options of termination including medical & surgical as well as contraception afterwards. What impact a TOP (termination of pregnancy) would have on my future pregnancies & future fertility” (P12, doctor). Some, choosing to continue with the pregnancy, thought about antenatal care “ … . starts me on folic acid, the first medication I've ever had to take in my life and sends me on my way with some warnings about my blood pressure or something … ” (P3, student) and “go through the booking into a hospital process and how that works” (P5, doctor). Some worried about possible complications in pregnancy “ … concerns regarding pregnancy at advanced age re complications for mother and/or baby” (P11, doctor) and “I think I will do tests to make sure the pregnancy is healthy and take it from there” (P8, doctor).

Laura’s final decision

Apart from the two stories which had immediate decisions, most went through a period of conflict before making a final decision or proceeding with uncertainty. Of these, four were clear in continuing the pregnancy, including one that considered adoption, one story decided on abortion, and five did not resolve on a final decision. One unresolved story wished for a miscarriage that could make the decision for her, wondering “is there a chance that I could lose the baby on my own? I'm not sure I would be able to live knowing I chose to end a life” (P4, student), another considering adoption wondered if “I could give him up for adoption but I’m not sure my heart will let me … .” (P2, student).

One story projected a year into the future where the mother of a young child, uncertain how she would cope with “two under one” but confident that they have the “means and happy family home to support a family” decides to continue in the pregnancy as “I have to continue. I can't not” and finds that “life is hectic. Crazy. I haven't slept in months. But we are happy. Our hearts are full” though “I tell you one thing; he won't be coming near me again any time soon.” (P7, doctor). No one who terminated their pregnancy projected the story into the future, but one woman trying to decide considers in the story “ … what if in ten years’ time, I am desperate for a child and struggling through fertility treatments and this is my one and only chance of having a baby? To terminate this pregnancy is also not without risks and it is something I would carry with me forever. A dark and sad secret never aired. I don't think I could tell many people and I don't know if I would be able to put it behind me for a long time.”

Scenario two: “Hannah”: a pregnancy diagnosed with anencephaly at 14 weeks gestation

Hannah’s life circumstances

Hannah was described in multiple ways, from an apparently single person “she couldn't quite mask the tiny look she threw at the empty chair beside me” (P1, student) to someone who had struggled to come to terms with an unexpected pregnancy “7 weeks since my world had collapsed. My dreams sank … ” and now resented continuing in the pregnancy only to be diagnosed with a fetal anomaly “ … it seemed like all had been for nothing”. Most, however, referred to a woman planning a “’perfect’ pregnancy” (P2, student), one with a husband or partner and a “ … family (that) has been so supportive of our pregnancy. There are so many expectations on me to make the right choice as a mother. So much excitement for the first grandchild” (P4, student) or a “much wanted baby to expand our family” (P7, doctor).

All clearly described shock at the diagnosis, both for the woman who struggles to “understand how this happened” (P6, doctor) as well as their “husband is devastated and is finding this even more difficult than I am” (P5, doctor) or a partner who “doesn't get it, and keeps pressing this idea of hope” (P4, student). Caring for her husband also in the middle of a devastating diagnosis, one describes a partner who “doesn’t know what to do. He wants this baby” (P7, doctor).

While two stories made quick clear decisions for “termination” care (P9, P10, doctors), most described conflict as Hannah struggled to decide. While one was factual and unemotional in consideration of the issues: “how long would the baby survive after birth What would be involved continuing with pregnancy … What could I have done to prevent it … What does terminating the pregnancy involve … What’s the risk of it recurring?” (P12, doctor), others truly struggled in considering options. Some reverted to a religious background “Funny how I suddenly feel Catholic. Is it bad to choose me over the baby? But the baby has no chance of survival” (P8, doctor) or spirituality “I feel like I will be held accountable by my conscience for ending this child’s life” (P2, student). Many stories reflected on the baby in this scenario, thinking of the impact of termination and the option of continuing the pregnancy “Can I keep the baby? If I wanted to, could I carry (carry) her, deliver her, and hold her?” where “I see the heart flicker and it seems so cruel” and “she will always have been surrounded by love, by her mother. I will have held her for her whole life”. (P4, student).

Another wondered “how can I be pregnant and not bring a child home” (P6, doctor). Others reflected on the impact of continuing the pregnancy would have on their families “my husband, who I am trying to be strong for. He doesn't know what to do” (P7, doctor) and themselves: “having to explain to all friends/family/work/society regarding the circumstances of your pregnancy as it becomes visible/obvious to others, I would find that very hard and exhausting” (P11, doctor).

Of those who considered abortion, some reflected on the inevitable fatal nature of the condition to help make their decision “What difference was there between a 14-week-old baby in the grave and a 40-week-old baby in the grave?” (P1, student) and that “I believe that in this circumstance it’s the right thing to do” (P2, student). Another reflected that they “feel reassured that this (abortion) is part of normal healthcare, and many others would make the same choice” (P5, doctor), though they strongly mourned the pregnancy loss “‘Does Friday suit?’ ‘Yes’, my baby shall die on Friday.” (P1, student)

The doctor in this scenario is painted in different ways – one was inpatient to complete a consultation who “blurted facts and figures and cited sections of the constitution with a frantic ferocity” then sneaking “an almost imperceptible glance at the clock and smothered a rising sigh” (P1, student), though most were supportive. One story described where Hannah “ … looked back up into the eyes of the doctor and saw what I had been looking for. Understanding” (P3, student). Others describe doctors that were “informative and helpful”, or “quite kind and approachable” who “ask about arrangements for our toddler and sympathise with the practical aspects” (p4-6 doctors).

Hannah’s final decision

For this story, only two participants came to a definitive conclusion, with both deciding to terminate the pregnancy. Another four participants came to a probable conclusion, with two implying that they would continue with the pregnancy despite the diagnosis, while the other two implied that they would terminate the pregnancy. The remaining eight participants failed to conclude what decision Hannah would take regarding the pregnancy, focusing instead on the negative impact that the diagnosis would have on her and her family.

One story reflected on the future, and a new pregnancy “One year later, I am pregnant again. But every day I think about what I have done to my last little baby and the fear and nerves of something happening to this baby is heightened. I can't relax.” (P7, doctor).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the attitudes of medical students and doctors in training towards crisis pregnancy using story completion models. Two scenarios were introduced: a crisis due to an unplanned pregnancy and a second due to a fatal fetal abnormality.

The focus of the unplanned pregnancy stories was on deciding, rather than on dwelling too much on the emotions that came with the crisis in pregnancy. Participants reflected on the practical factors, i.e. partner/family reactions, other children, work stability, income, etc., and whether “Laura” would be able to adequately care for the child. There was also much more variety in Laura’s life circumstances, either being single or in a relationship, and on good terms with the father or not. Unique to Laura’s stories were instances of stigma related to her unplanned pregnancy, i.e. some wrote that Laura pre-empted negative experiences from family and friends. Also, for Laura, most came to a definite conclusion about what they would do about the pregnancy, that is to continue or terminate the pregnancy.

Comparatively, for Hannah’s story, the focus of the stories was more on the devastating emotional impact of the diagnosis on Hannah and, often, her partner and family. Their life circumstances were much more focused towards a happily married couple who wanted to have a baby and would be devastated by loss of the baby. Many also focused on the emotional impact influencing their final decision, such as telling their partner and family, and how devastating it would be to carry a pregnancy and not have a healthy child. Despite this, relatively few came to a finalised decision to what Hannah would do, choosing instead to leave her in ambiguity or leaning either towards continuing the pregnancy or choosing abortion. Some also evoked religion or religious beliefs here whereas that didn’t happen in Laura’s story.

While we had hypothesised that Hannah’s scenario – a fetal abnormality that inevitably ends in the death of the fetus – would be more likely to have abortion as an option, in fact the option of abortion was included in stories for both scenarios, along with a full range of other options, i.e. continuing in the pregnancy, adoption, and indeed not deciding, which can also be a decision. This was particularly important given recent changes in pregnancy care within the Republic of Ireland, where abortion, which had always been a moral and ethical right, is now a legal right. This recent expansion of “free, safe, and legal” options for pregnancy care occurred following a referendum in 2018,29,52 which removed a previous constitutional amendment allowing the right to life for the unborn as equal to that of the pregnant person. For the first time in the history of the state, individuals can legally access abortion care “without specific indication” if the pregnancy is under 12-weeks’ gestation. Three other legal criteria exist to end a pregnancy: maternal risk to health or life, a fetal abnormality likely to cause death in pregnancy or within 28 days after birth or maternal risk to health in an emergency.29,53 In the first five years following introduction, between 4577 and 10,033 legal abortions were recorded annually, the majority occurring within the first trimester and performed within the community by general practitioners.54–57

Abortion care is part of the Obstetrics and Gynaecology curriculum for medical students in most Irish universities and is part of both the Basic Specialist and Higher Specialist training curricula for doctors in training in the speciality.52,58,59 Medical students and doctors in training are in an interesting position, where they will be asked to act on their beliefs. During the referendum, young people were change leaders, with those aged between 18 and 34 overwhelmingly voting in favour of repeal (87% of 18–24 and 83% of 25–34 year olds).60 The majority of medical students and doctors in training fall within this age range and will soon emerge into the workforce. It could be postulated that they hold the same beliefs as their non-medical peers. Indeed, studies published prior to the referendum reported that 73% of medical students were moderately or strongly pro-choice, 90.5% believed that abortion should be available in limited circumstances or upon request, and most obstetric doctors in training (>80%) were in favour of abortion in specific circumstances.45,61 Many new doctors will provide care to people who may request abortion as an option, so understanding their preconceptions of abortion and the people that access care is crucial to preparing them for the situation and to ensure the individuals accessing care are not stigmatised by their healthcare providers. In parallel, future doctors should be aware of how to deal with their own personal moral and ethical beliefs while still providing respectful care or referral.58 Eliciting their views on unplanned pregnancies from the patient’s perspective, as in this study, provides insights into these stereotypes and knowledge of these may inform teaching at both medical student and trainee level to identify and unlearn stereotypes, ensuring that staff is capable of providing patient-focused care to all who access care.

The stories, and particularly differences in how these were constructed, offer insights into some of these stereotypes that the participants held. Laura was practical and relatively unemotional in deciding on the most appropriate option and there was wide variety in the descriptions of her private life and the circumstances in which she became pregnant. Hannah, on the other hand, was so devastated she failed to make a final decision in most stories and was in most circumstances married and trying to have a child. These clear differences could perhaps be due to unconscious bias, which may be informed by experiences since expanded provision of care, or the cultural messaging of the referendum. With the 2018 referendum, it was noted that many of the stories about unplanned/unwanted pregnancies were rooted in practical issues of travel and access to care, whereas stories on pregnancies affected by fatal fetal abnormalities were often used to create emotion for the undeniable need for compassionate care in Ireland.62 The responses could harken to that. It also shows that even if the stereotypes could be rooted in prevailing messaging from the referendum, participants didn’t default to choosing abortion. It shows that while abortion is now an option, it may not be a prevalent option and that the women (+/- partner) are central to decisions. Additionally, some discussed the stigma associated with crisis pregnancy and abortion care in Irish society, i.e. negative reactions from family and friends as a result of unplanned pregnancy and the woman’s own shame or guilt if they chose to access abortion care for either scenario. While these comments do not inherently show that participants endorsed these stigmatised views63 we note that providers may themselves stigmatise the women who access care, and acknowledging stereotypes they may hold is imperative to providing person-centred care.64

This is our first experience working with Story Completion Models (SCM) as a qualitative research method, and indeed may be the first time this has been used in research on pregnancy. The numbers that participated were relatively small compared to more traditional quantitative research and indeed when compared to other studies using SCM, which range from 20 to 200 items.44,49 Despite these small numbers, the stories that were generated were a good length, rich in data, and alternative endings. Many of the stories were well thought out, showing humour, realism and perhaps the HCPs’ or students’ experiences, either personal or professional. It may be that the sample size was too small and unrepresentative to make broad claims and instead the focus could be on what these stories may tell us about Irish society with extended abortion care, rather than what they tell us for future students and trainees. These are stories developed by people who lived and worked during and after the referendum and most had experience of providing or at least knowledge of the procedure. Their answers are influenced by public messaging and also professional knowledge of the services. We did note that some people consented to participate in the study but did not complete the stories, perhaps as they did not understand what was involved in the work or changed their minds about participation once presented with the task.

A second potential limitation of this study is that only those medical students who support options of care participated. All of the medical students hoped to specialise in areas of healthcare where abortion care is practised (General Practice or Obstetrics/Gynaecology) and all aimed to participate in abortion care. The number of participating students is too small to meaningfully compare to a previous study from Ireland where most were supportive of abortion care, and it is reasonable that only those interested in providing such care would participate in the study. Different results may of course have been obtained had others participated who are not as supportive of all options of care. There was more variety amongst the doctors in training, with one participant not involved in abortion care and two preferring not to say. Of note, these three participants still acknowledged abortion as a legitimate option depending on the persons wishes.

Future work will aim to build on these results and as such this study may act as an exploration of using SCM in building on a larger and more encompassing study on attitudes towards crisis pregnancy.

Conclusion

The aim of this explorative qualitative study was to study the attitudes of medical students and doctors in training towards crisis pregnancy, specifically two scenarios: early unplanned pregnancy and fatal fetal abnormality (anencephaly). There were noticeable differences between the stories presented. In the case of “Laura”, who was experiencing an unplanned pregnancy, the focus was placed on practical factors that might influence her final decision. In the case of “Hannah”, who was experiencing a pregnancy affected by fatal fetal abnormality, the focus was placed on the devastating emotional impact of the diagnosis on Hannah and, often, her partner and family. Additionally, while most brought Laura’s story to a conclusion by stating what she decided to do regarding her pregnancy, most left Hannah in a state of ambiguity regarding her decision. Participants didn’t default to choosing abortion but instead relied on the wishes of the woman, plus her partner if present. These stories may provide a glimpse into Irish society that now has extended abortion care. While participant numbers were not sufficient to extrapolate findings, the stories developed provide a valuable insight into Irish society following the extension of abortion care. Further research should continue to explore the values and attitudes of healthcare workers following legalised abortion in new jurisdictions.
