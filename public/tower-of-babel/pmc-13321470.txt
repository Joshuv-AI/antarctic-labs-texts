Pre-delivery anaemia and associated factors among women in Kikuube District, Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Julius Businge, Miisa Nanyingi, Mathias Lwenge, Juliet Ndibazza, Rovina Alimpa, David Agaroi, Scovia Mbabazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnaemia in pregnancy is a global public health concern, especially in low and middle-income countries, where it is linked to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Despite various antenatal care interventions to prevent anaemia in pregnancy, a considerable proportion of pregnant women still present with anaemia at delivery. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, severity, and factors associated with pre-delivery anaemia among women at Kyangwali Health Centre IV (KHCIV).MethodsA health facility-based cross-sectional analytical study was conducted. A simple random sampling technique was used to select 422 pregnant mothers at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation admitted for delivery. Data on socio-demographic, medical, and obstetric characteristics were collected using an interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire and analyzed using STATA version 15. Anaemia was defined as a hemoglobin (Hb) level less than 11 g/dl. We performed a modified Poisson regression analysis to determine the factors associated with pre-delivery anaemia at a 95% confidence level and 5% statistical significance using multivariable analysis.ResultsThe overall prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia was 30.1% (95% CI, 29.90-30.27%). The prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe anaemia was 71.7%, 22.8%, and 5.5%, respectively. Attendance of more than four Antenatal Care (ANC) visits (APR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.24–0.73, p = 0.003), malaria prevention with Fansidar (APR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04–0.54, p = 0.004), and prior use of contraceptives (APR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.34–0.74, p < 0.001) were associated with a lower prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia. On the contrary, treatment for malaria during pregnancy was associated with a higher prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia (APR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.08–2.46, p < 0.015).ConclusionAnaemia remains a significant maternal health burden. A third of the pregnant women admitted for delivery in Kyangwali HCIV had pre-delivery anaemia, albeit the majority had mild anaemia. The findings highlight the protective role of ANC attendance and use of antimalarial chemoprevention during pregnancy, and prior contraceptive use in reducing the risk of anaemia. However, a history of malaria treatment during pregnancy was associated with increased anaemia, suggestive of inadequate prevention, underscoring the need for additional malaria preventive strategies. Strengthening ANC services to ensure uptake of a comprehensive package of preventive interventions may help reduce the prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09223-w.Keywords: Pre-delivery anaemia, Malaria, Pregnancy anaemia, Maternal anaemia, Kikuube District

Introduction

Pre-delivery anaemia refers to the anaemia present at the time of delivery, characterized by a hemoglobin (Hb) concentration level of less than 11 g/dL [1–3]. It is a global public health concern affecting approximately 36% (34–39%) of pregnant women worldwide [4]. Anaemia in pregnancy is associated with increased risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, with moderate to severe anaemia conferring a higher likelihood of intrapartum and postpartum complications [5–8]. There is a strong independent relationship between severe anaemia and maternal death, especially when diagnosed at the time of delivery. Unaddressed anemia that persists until the time of labor and delivery potentiates maternal complications, including prolonged labor, post-partum hemorrhage, post-partum anemia [5], and puerperal sepsis, as well as increased admissions to neonatal intensive care units, neonatal deaths, and stillbirths [5]. Maternal anemia persisting at the time of delivery is an independent risk factor for transfusion and maternal death regardless of the delivery blood loss [9]. Various studies in sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated that anaemia in pregnancy is a common occurrence, with a pooled prevalence of 36-51.2% [9] and has been associated with factors such as intestinal worm infestation [10, 11], iron and folic acid deficiency [12–14], third-trimester pregnancy [11, 12, 15], and dietary deficiencies [12, 16]. Factors associated with anemia in pregnancy vary from individual to community levels and often interact with socio-demographic, nutritional, and residential factors [17]. Several of these contributors are related to parity [15, 18], short inter-birth intervals [15], and the number of antenatal care contacts or visits [19]. Deficiencies of micronutrients in the diet as well as increased demand during pregnancy for iron, folate, vitamin C, and vitamin B12, contribute to the development of anemia [19–21]. Other factors that have been associated with anaemia include malaria infection [22], Diabetes Mellitus, Tuberculosis, and HIV, as well as drugs such as alcohol and caffeine [23].

In the health sector, there is a broad recognition of the various factors contributing to anaemia and the importance of targeted interventions. As a result, services such as malaria presumptive treatment, iron and folic acid supplementation, nutrition education, and screening for anaemia during antenatal care are a package of services [3]. The assumption is that if women receive these services, the risk of anaemia at the time of delivery will be reduced, and therefore all the complications associated with anaemia during pregnancy will be reduced [24]. Hence, in Uganda, these services are made available at Health Center IIIs, IVs, hospitals, and as targeted service packages during outreaches and health campaigns [25]. Efforts have been made to eliminate barriers to accessing antenatal care services, including free iron and folic acid supplementation, presumptive treatments of malaria with Fansidar, nutrition education and screening for anaemia in all government health facilities [26, 27]. However, numerous challenges persist, such as limited capacity for hemoglobin testing [28] and pregnant women continue to present for delivery with anaemia, putting them at risk of anaemia-related complications.

In the Kikuube District local government, several efforts have been made to address and prevent anaemia during the preconception period. These include promoting birth spacing through contraceptive use, nutrition education, and preventing and treating infectious diseases [29]. During the antenatal period, interventions include focused antenatal care, routine hemoglobin screening for case detection and management. Additional measures are maternal nutrition and education, iron and folate supplementation, deworming, and continued promotion of contraception [1, 26, 30] .Implementation of a standard antenatal anemia treatment plan is associated with improvements in pre-delivery hemoglobin and reduces the need for maternal blood product transfusions and length of hospital stay at delivery [3, 27, 30]. Despite all these efforts to deliver maternal services to reduce anaemia at the time of delivery, the problem persists. Studies have documented this persistence due to limited capacity for hemoglobin testing, staffing, and other resource challenges [28]. The consequence of this persistence has been associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. These have been documented to include poor health conditions for women, premature rupture of membranes, postpartum birth asphyxia, and included likelihood of blood transfusion and maternal death [4, 8].

District Health information records (unpublished routine Health Information Management System data from the Kikuube Health Office), indicated that between January- March 2022, of the 903 women admitted for delivery, 62.2% had undergone anaemia screening during Antenatal care, 6.6% had severe anaemia (Hb < 7 g/dl). Additionally, 18 (1.99%) required blood transfusion before delivery, consuming 86 units of blood and 12 (1.32%) received blood transfusion due to post-partum anemia, even though there was no significant blood loss during the delivery process.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a health facility-based, analytical cross-sectional study conducted among pregnant women admitted for delivery at Kyangwali HC IV(KHCIV), Kikuube District in Uganda. The study was conducted between September and October 2024. Kikuube district is located in western Uganda in the Bunyoro Sub-region, 104 km west of Hoima city and Hoima Regional Referral Hospital and 8 km from Lake Albert and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) border. It has a population of 379,547, with a refugee population of 54,419, about 16.3% of the total population. It is one of the 10 districts in Uganda with the highest number of refugees. The district population is unstable due to the influx of refugees from the Democratic Republic of Congo, which gives it characteristics of a humanitarian setting. It also has an additional influx of people from the neighboring districts due to petroleum production in the Bunyoro sub-region. The unemployment rate is 15.3%, with 48% of the population who are not in education or training, and 45.3% of households that have moderate to severe food insecurity [31]. The district has no hospital but operates only one Health Centre IV of KHCIV. KHCIV serves as the main referral facility for the local and refugee population and is the only health facility in Kikuube that provides comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care (CEmONC), which includes the essential signal functions such as parenteral antibiotics, anticonvulsants, oxytocics, as well as the more complex procedures like caesarean sections and blood transfusions. The maternal health services are delivered through multi-sectoral collaboration with integration of district health services with services for refugee and local communities [30, 32]. The facility has a diagnostic laboratory where hematological tests such as complete blood counts, including hemoglobin estimation, blood tests for malaria parasites and other blood disorders, are carried out.

Study participants

The study enrolled 422 pregnant women at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation, admitted for delivery to the maternity unit at Kyangwali HC IV, Kikuube District. Pregnant women with a history of blood transfusion in the preceding three months, or admitted with antepartum hemorrhage, and the critically ill were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was estimated using a single proportion formula based on the prevalence of anaemia in pregnancy at the time of delivery of 50%, in a humanitarian setting [13], with a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 10% non-response rate, giving a final sample of 422. The Kyangwali health center IV was purposively selected because it was the only referral health facility in the district. A systematic random sampling technique was used to select the study participants over one month. Based on an estimated total of 982 admissions in three months, and a sample size of 422, a sampling interval (k) of two was used. The first participant was selected at random using the rotary method. Thereafter, every second eligible pregnant woman was included in the study until the required sample size was achieved.

Study variables

The dependent variable was pre-delivery anaemia, defined as a hemoglobin (Hb) level of Hb < 11 g/dl. Hemoglobin concentration of capillary blood was estimated on admission, two milliliters (mls) of venous blood were drawn at the cubital fossa of the non-dominant upper limb by a trained laboratory assistant and collected in an ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) tube. The vacutainer was labeled with each participant’s identification number and immediately transported, at room temperature (25°c), to the laboratory. The laboratory technologist used a Haematology analyzer (Sysmex-XN-550, Va-Q-Tec, Germany) to measure the hemoglobin level. Women who had an Hb less than 11 g/dl were considered to have anaemia using the WHO protocols [2]. Further classification of anaemia was made using the WHO cut-offs: mild = 9.0–10.9 g/dl, moderate = 7.0–8.9 g/dl, and severe = less than 7 g/dl. Participants received the results from the blood test, and those who had anemia were treated using the anaemia pregnancy management protocol [27, 33]. The independent variables included in the study were socio-demographic factors (age, education level, residence, marital status, family size, referral status, and occupation); medical related factors (HIV, TB, Diabetes, Sickle cell disease, chronic hypertension, and malaria treatment during pregnancy); and obstetric related factors (gravidity, gestation age, inter-birth interval, Antenatal Care attendance (ANC) and contraceptive use. Antenatal care and medication use were verified using clinical records from the maternal and child health card. Gestation age was further categorized as follows: preterm defined as birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation; term define as birth at and beyond 42 completed weeks of gestation [34].

Data collection approach and tool

Data on socio-demographic, medical, and obstetric characteristics were collected through face-to-face interviews using structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed based on a review of existing literature [8, 22]. Clinical information on Hb level and antenatal attendance were obtained from maternal and child health care. Where available, medical records were used to verify and supplement other self-reported information provided by the participant.

Data quality control

The research assistants were trained on the administration of the standardized questionnaire by JB, the study lead and specialist in obstetrics and Gynecology. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Runyoro and Kiswahili and pretested to ensure clarity, appropriate wording, and consistency. Research assistants were also trained in obtaining informed consent from eligible participants and in specimen collection in accordance with WHO-UNICEF guidelines [35]. Blood sample collection was supervised by DA, a laboratory technician. Completed questionnaires were reviewed daily by JB to ensure accuracy and completeness. The hematology analyzer was pretested and calibrated before use and every 25th sample was submitted to the reference laboratory for external quality control to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results.

Data management and analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using STATA version 15 (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics were used to present the frequency of the participants’ characteristics and to estimate the prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia. We used a modified Poisson regression with robust error variance procedure to examine the factors associated with pre-delivery anaemia [36]. All biologically plausible independent variables and those with a p-value less than 0.2 during bivariate analysis were later added to a final multivariable model as a measure of adjusting for confounding. Adjusted Prevalent Ratios with 95% Confidence Intervals were reported for variables with a p-value of ≤ 0.05.

Ethical approval and consent to participate in the study

Ethical approval to collect data was obtained from Lira University Research and Ethics Committee (LUREC 2023-34). Administrative clearance was obtained from Uganda Martyrs University and Kikuube District Local Government through the institutional and local research approvals. Permission was also sought from the Health Center IV. Voluntary informed written consent was obtained from participants of consenting age, and informed consent for participants of non-consent age was obtained from their legal guardians and assent was obtained from each of them before data collection. The participants who were non-consenting and did not have parents or guardians were taken as mature and emancipated minors and a close in the guidelines for assent and parental consent in research was used to obtain informed consent from this group [37].

Proportion of pre-delivery anaemia

Out of 422 participants enrolled in the study, 127 (30.1%, 95% CI: 25.9–34.7) women had anaemia; of which 7 (1.6%) had severe anaemia, 29 (6.9%) had moderate anaemia, and 91 (21.6%) had mild anaemia. The mean hemoglobin (Hb) concentration was 11.79 g/dl (standard deviation ± 1.97), minimum of 6.0 g/dl and a maximum of 19.5 g/dl.

Factors associated with pre-delivery anaemia

At the bivariate analysis, age, inter-birth interval, number of ANC antenatal care visits, contraceptive use before pregnancy, Fansidar, iron supplementation, and mebendazole use during pregnancy were associated with a lower likelihood of pre-delivery anaemia. Malaria treatment during pregnancy was associated with an increased likelihood of pre-delivery anaemia (Table 2).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia and associated factors among women admitted for delivery in Kikuube District, Uganda. Findings show that three in every ten (30.1%) women had anaemia, with relative contributions of mild (21.6%), moderate (6.9%), and severe (1.6%) cases. The prevalence is lower than in other studies with broadly similar contexts, in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), in population-based studies with pooled estimates of 44.3% from 21 Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries using the most recent nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey [9], 48.3% from seven SSA countries [22]. The lower prevalence observed in this study could partly be attributed to the facility-based design; the study population may have been healthier, more health-conscious, of higher socio-economic status, and with better access to healthcare. There is also a possibility that this outcome could occur in contexts where maternal nutrition awareness is high and healthier sub-groups are present, such as refugee communities benefiting from programmes that improve livelihoods and promote self-sufficiency [38]. In addition, the lower prevalence is could be because this comparison is made in reference to studies that are large-scale and incorporate multiple demographic and health surveys from several Sub-Saharan African countries, providing pooled prevalence estimates. These discrepancies could be attributed to differences in methodological approaches, geographical differences, disease burden, nutrition, and health interventions. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of 64.8% and 50.6 reported in contexts with similar mixed, stable, and emergency populations, with similar methodological approaches, being facility-based cross-sectional studies in Somalia and Ethiopia [5, 14] could be attributed partly to improved multi-sectional collaboration between the implementing partners, the district, and ministry health to deliver services in humanitarian settings [32].

The prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia in this study is higher than reported in studies conducted in northern and western Uganda and Ethiopia [11–13]. The finding of this study warrants attention, particularly since evidence shows that reducing anaemia during pregnancy and at the time of delivery is essential for ensuring maternal safety and minimizing complications of anaemia in pregnancy [8]. Despite substantial investment in healthcare infrastructure and resources to support women during pregnancy and delivery across regions, the persistence of anaemia could point to the inequalities in the utilisation of the continuum of maternal and quality of antenatal care services provision [25], and it also underscores the complex sequelae, driven by factors that extend beyond healthcare access alone. In addition, measurement of anaemia during labour is not routinely practiced in some Ugandan health facilities. Evidence shows that about 20% of women admitted for labour in sampled mid-range facilities had their hemoglobin measured, and this largely depended on the discretion of individual clinicians’ acumen, rendering the process inconsistent and subjective [28].

The distribution of anaemia severity among the participants in this study indicated 71.7% had mild anaemia, followed by moderate anaemia (22.8%), while severe anaemia was observed in 5.5% of the study participants. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Ghana, and India, where the majority of women presented with mild anaemia, followed by moderate to severe forms [23, 39]. In contrast, in emergency-related contexts such as Somalia, most women were reported to present with moderate anaemia at the time of delivery [40]. This consistent pattern across diverse geographical locations could be attributed to common underlying factors. These may include shared dietary habits, socio-economic challenges, and higher regional prevalence of conditions such as iron deficiency, with differences in the protocols for its classification and management [3].

In this study, malaria treatment during pregnancy was associated with increased prevalence of pre-delivery anaemia, which is consistent with other studies elsewhere in Uganda and other sub-Saharan African countries. In addition, malaria prevention with Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP-Fansidar) use during pregnancy was associated with reduced prevalence of anaemia at the time of delivery. Individuals with malaria experience a breakdown of red blood cells (erythrocytes), which predisposes them to malaria. Furthermore, there is a direct destruction and sequestration of erythrocytes by the spleen, as well as decreased red cell production due to substances released during inflammation, which interfere with the hormone erythropoietin that normally stimulates the production of red blood cells [41]. Moreover, in pregnant women, placental sequestration of malaria-infected erythrocytes induces oxidative stress, which further contributes to the reduction of hemoglobin concentration [42]. In many contexts [43], malaria continues to be reported as an independent contributor to anaemia prevalence during pregnancy despite the implementation of the World Health Organization recommendation for intermittent preventive treatment with Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine [30]. This persistence highlights gaps in the continuum of care and quality of prenatal care, underscoring the need to re-examine the current approaches to malaria prevention during pregnancy [25].

Four or more antenatal visits and contraceptive use were associated with reduced prevalence of anaemia at the time of delivery. It is noteworthy that high service uptake for the pregnancy-related preventive services, possibly due to having maternal community health workers from the community and refugee population, was noted as a facilitator in educating women about maternal health [32].

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the focus on the pre-delivery anaemia, a clinically vital and programmatical relevant outcome that directly influences maternal and neonatal health. Assessing the anaemia at the time of delivery provides a practical picture of the cumulative effects of the continuum of care for maternal health. Another strength is the identification of modifiable, actionable determinants that offer clear programmatic levers that the system can act on. Furthermore, it was conducted in a setting where the population is unstable, which increases the relevance of the findings for the programming and monitoring of health services’ effectiveness in resource-constrained health care contexts. The study had some limitations. A facility-based study may not accurately represent pre-delivery anaemia, especially in situations with unstable populations and resource constraints at the household level. This, however, could be offset by a high percentage (80.6%) of women who delivered in the health facilities in the Bunyoro sub-region [44]. Being a single-centre study conducted at a referral facility may have led to an overestimated the prevalence of anaemia and limits the generalizability of the finding to wide region in Bunyoro Sub-region.

Conclusion

Three in every 10 pregnant women admitted for delivery had pre-delivery anaemia with the majority having mild anaemia. Attending more than 4 ANC visits, malaria prevention with Sulfadoxine-Pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP-Fansidar), and using contraceptives, were the factors associated with the prevention of pre-delivery anaemia. Therefore, employing interventions to prevent malaria during pregnancy and to improve contraceptive use, among other interventions, through maternal care services can go a long way in preventing pre-delivery anaemia.
