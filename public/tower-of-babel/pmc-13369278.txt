Institutional experience and pooled survival analysis of chemotherapy based multimodal management in poorly differentiated chordoma
Authors: Jingyu Xing, Hao Zhang, Zijie Yuan, Xinghao Cao, Tao Tan, Jinbo Hu, Wenlan Zhi, Chenglong Zhao, Cheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoorly differentiated chordoma (PDC) is an exceptionally rare bone tumor with aggressive behavior and early recurrence. Evidence on the role and integration of chemotherapy remains limited. We aimed to share our institutional management experience and identify clinical prognostic factors in PDC.MethodsFive consecutive patients with axial PDC treated at our spinal tumor center (June 2021–June 2025) received surgery and anthracycline–ifosfamide (AI) chemotherapy. Neoadjuvant response was assessed by RECIST 1.1 and overall survival (OS) by Kaplan–Meier. We also reviewed histologically confirmed PDC cases reported in the literature with eligible treatment and outcome data, and compared OS by chemotherapy exposure and other factors using log-rank tests.ResultsThe mean age in our series was 21.4 years; all tumors were mobile-spine PDC with INI-1 loss and brachyury positivity. Neoadjuvant AI-based combination therapy achieved a partial response (PR) in one patient. Median progression-free survival was 13 months and median OS was 21 months. In the pooled cohort (n = 30), there was a female predominance. Prior recurrence was associated with inferior OS. In descriptive comparisons, patients receiving chemotherapy-based multimodality therapy had longer observed OS than those receiving local therapy without chemotherapy, including among recurrent cases. Whereas radiotherapy did not show a comparable survival benefit, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the likelihood of substantial selection bias and the palliative role of RT in many recurrent cases.ConclusionsAI-based chemotherapy, particularly in the neoadjuvant setting, was feasible and showed preliminary antitumor signal in selected patients. However, the patient who achieved a partial response also received apatinib; thus, the specific contribution of AI chemotherapy cannot be isolated. In the pooled exploratory analysis, patients who received chemotherapy had a longer observed median OS than those receiving local therapy alone. However, due to the broad definition of chemotherapy and high risk of selection bias, this finding is presented as purely descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and does not imply a meaningful association between chemotherapy and improved OS.Keywords: Poorly differentiated chordoma, Chemotherapy, Multimodal therapy, SMARCB1/INI1, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Chordoma is an aggressive bone tumor originating from embryonic notochord remnants, characterized by notochordal differentiation and a predilection for the axial skeleton. Chordoma accounts for approximately 1–4% of all bone tumors and has a population-based incidence rate of 0.8/1,000,000 [1]. It is classified as conventional chordoma (including the chondroid chordoma subtype), dedifferentiated chordoma, and poorly differentiated chordoma (PDC) [2], among which PDC is an ultra-rare subtype accounting for only 2–5% of all chordoma cases [3]. Its pathological features include pleomorphic epithelioid-to-rhabdoid cells arranged in cohesive sheets, which are positive for keratin and brachyury, and exhibit loss of SMARCB1 (INI-1) protein [4]. Thus far, only a limited number of cases have been reported [5–7]. Compared with conventional chordoma, PDC occurs more frequently in children and young adults, most commonly involving the clivus and cervical spine [8]. In clinical practice, treatment of PDC still relies primarily on maximal surgical resection combined with local radiotherapy [9, 10]. But given the complex anatomy of these regions, it is often difficult to achieve a R0 resection and apply safe radiotherapy [11]. Consequently, this more aggressive chordoma subtype has a higher risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis, with local control rates reported to be below 30% [3]. Recurrent PDC is often refractory and associated with poor prognosis.

Although conventional chordoma is generally considered chemoresistant [12], recent clinical reports suggest that chemotherapy may have potential therapeutic value in PDC. Isolated cases have described marked shrinkage of the primary lesion after chemoradiotherapy and durable disease control without recurrence for up to 3 years following chemotherapy-based multimodal treatment [13]. Nevertheless, these observations come mainly from scattered case reports and small series, with limited patient numbers, no standardized longitudinal management, and little exploration of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, leaving substantial gaps in the current evidence for PDC. Moreover, although the poor prognosis of PDC is widely recognized, its specific risk factors and practical frameworks for prognostic stratification remain unclear, which hampers optimization of treatment strategies and the design of prospective studies. In this context, we report our institutional experience in managing PDC in attempt to explore a neoadjuvant chemotherapy approach for providing structured longitudinal care of patients receiving chemotherapy. In parallel, we conducted a literature review to examine potential prognostic factors, hoping that these findings could help inform future therapeutic decision-making and multidisciplinary management in patients with this rare and aggressive disease.

Materials and methods

General information

Included in this retrospective study were five PDC patients who received at least one surgical procedure and perioperative chemotherapy at our spinal tumor center between June 2021 and June 2025 (Table 1). Patient demographics, radiological findings, comprehensive treatment details, and pathological results are listed in Table 1. All patients were followed up at 3-month intervals during the first year and every 6 months thereafter. Disease status was assessed at each follow-up visit based on clinical evaluation and available imaging, and recorded as local recurrence, metastatic disease, or no evidence of disease. For patients who were unable to attend in-person follow-up, survival status and interval events were ascertained by telephone interviews with the patient and/or caregivers, supplemented by outside medical records when available. Patients were categorized at last follow-up as no evidence of disease (NED), alive with disease (AWD), or dead of disease (DWD). Prior to the initiation of the study, approval was obtained from the hospital ethics committee, along with written informed consent from all patients.

Treatment and assessment

All enrolled patients received antitumor chemotherapy at our institution in accordance with the NCCN and ESMO Sarcoma Clinical Practice Guidelines and administered in a 3-weeks cycle [14, 15]. Based on our prior treatment experience, apatinib was added in one patient during the neoadjuvant chemotherapy phase [16]. Treatment response was assessed every 2–3 standard cycles through scheduled disease surveillance, which routinely included contrast-enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the tumor site and chest Computed Tomography (CT), as well as additional imaging of other sites, or Positron Emission Tomography–Computed Tomography (PET-CT) in patients who were clinically indicated. The efficacy of preoperative therapy was evaluated according to RECIST version 1.1 and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Patients who continued with adjuvant chemotherapy postoperatively followed the same surveillance schedule until completion of the treatment. Two clinicians reviewed the serial images independently to determine the disease status. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval from initiation of chemotherapy to the first evidence of local progression, newly developed metastatic disease, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up.

Literature search strategy and case selection

A literature search of the PubMed database was performed to identify eligible studies published from 2005 to the present using the keyword “poorly differentiated chordoma” without additional restrictions to obtain comprehensive results and minimize the risk of missing relevant studies. After screening titles and abstracts, basic research articles, review articles, and purely pathological studies without detailed clinical information were excluded. The remaining clinical treatment reports on PDC constituted the initial pool for subsequent patient-level screening. In addition, the reference lists of all eligible articles were manually reviewed to identify any additional relevant studies. Flow diagram of literature search, study selection, and patient inclusion for the pooled analysis of PDC

In this pooled analysis, “chemotherapy” was defined as receipt of any systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy during the disease course, regardless of timing relative to surgery or recurrence. Based on the available case-level information, chemotherapy could have been delivered in neoadjuvant, adjuvant, salvage, or palliative settings. Regimen composition, line of therapy, and treatment timing were not consistently reported across all published cases, and some patients also received additional targeted or other non-cytotoxic therapies during the disease course. Therefore, the chemotherapy variable in the pooled cohort should be interpreted as a heterogeneous historical systemic-treatment exposure rather than a uniform regimen or a category directly equivalent to our institutional AI-treated cohort. Because treatment timing was incompletely reported, separate survival analyses according to neoadjuvant, adjuvant, or post-recurrence chemotherapy were not feasible.

A pooled analysis was performed by including studies that reported PDC cases arising in the axial skeleton. Tumor diagnosis was required to be confirmed by histopathology and immunohistochemistry. Eligible studies were restricted to English-language reports involving human subjects and providing sufficient patient-level information on treatment and outcomes. Patients were excluded if the primary tumor site was unknown; the tumor was located in the extremities; survival outcomes were not reported; survival was reported but survival time was not specified; or information on treatment modality (chemotherapy/radiotherapy/surgery) was insufficient. Two investigators independently screened studies according to the predefined eligibility criteria and extracted data from each included report. Given that the literature component of this study was conducted as a literature review and was based mainly on case reports and small case series, a formal standardized risk-of-bias assessment was not performed. Potential sources of bias, including publication bias, selection bias, reporting bias, and clinical heterogeneity, were considered when interpreting the findings.

Statistical strategy and method

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.1; GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Continuous variables are summarized as medians with ranges, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and presented as survival curves. Univariable survival analysis was conducted according to clinically relevant factors (e.g., age, tumor location, treatment pattern, or other covariates), and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical features

Between June 2021 and June 2025, five patients who were ultimately diagnosed with PDC and received chemotherapy at our institution were included in this study, comprising two males and three females, with a mean age at diagnosis of 21.4 ± 12.1 years (median, 18 years; range, 9–44 years). All primary tumors arose in the mobile spine, including four in the cervical spine and one in the lumbar spine. The patients mainly presented with limb pain, numbness, weakness, and varying degrees of functional limitation. The diagnosis of PDC was confirmed by postoperative histopathology in all five patients, characterized by loss of INI-1 expression and positive brachyury staining (Fig. 2). Notably, the initial biopsy diagnoses were discordant with the final diagnosis in two cases: one was first interpreted as epithelioid angiosarcoma owing to strong epithelial marker expression and microscopic features suggestive of endothelial differentiation despite INI-1 loss; the other was initially diagnosed as metastatic carcinoma based on strong positivity for epithelial markers such as CKpan and CAM5.2.

Treatment details and outcomes

All five patients received doxorubicin plus ifosfamide (AI) chemotherapy administered in 3-week cycles as part of multimodal management. Two patients (Patient 1 and 2) were treated with neoadjuvant AI before definitive resection, and the remaining three received AI in the adjuvant or recurrent setting. Four patients completed the planned eight cycles, and one patient (Patient 5) discontinued after a single induction cycle because of diagnostic uncertainty. The mean follow-up duration for the cohort was 17.4 ± 8.4 months (median, 20 months; range, 5–25 months). During follow-up, three patients developed local recurrence and underwent repeat resection; four patients received postoperative local radiotherapy after the second surgery for additional local control. One patient died from rapidly progressive metastatic disease, and the remaining four were alive at the last follow-up. The mean PFS was 10.8 ± 4.2 months (median, 13 months; range, 5–15 months), and the mean OS was 24.8 ± 8.0 months (median, 21 months; range, 5–54 months).

Case presentation

Patient 2 was a 17-year-old male who presented with insidious onset of neck and shoulder pain radiating to the upper limb. Imaging revealed a cervical spine mass, which was confirmed by biopsy as PDC. Chest CT showed no evidence of distant metastasis. Given the complex cervical anatomy, extensive tumor involvement, and large tumor burden, upfront radical resection was considered high risk, and a multidisciplinary team recommended initial neoadjuvant systemic therapy. In accordance with the NCCN and ESMO guidelines for bone and soft-tissue sarcomas, the patient received doxorubicin plus ifosfamide (AI regimen), and apatinib was added based on our institution’s prior clinical experience. After four cycles of neoadjuvant therapy, the best response by RECIST 1.1 was a partial remission, with the maximum tumor diameter decreasing from approximately 9.3 cm to 4.2 cm and imaging evidence of extensive intratumoral necrosis (Fig. 3A–F). The patient achieved initial radiologic disease control after resection of the cervical lesion and postoperative adjuvant AI chemotherapy. Approximately three months after completion of adjuvant therapy, surveillance imaging revealed local recurrence. A CDK4/6 inhibitor was initiated based on the published experience, but the disease continued to progress. Guided by tumor genomic profiling, treatment was subsequently switched to the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat. Follow-up imaging demonstrated reduction in the cervical lesion, permitting a second debulking surgery, after which tazemetostat was continued as maintenance therapy. About three months later, the cervical lesion progressed again, and a third local debulking procedure was performed (Fig. 3I). Throughout this period, pulmonary lesions remained radiologically stable. After the third surgery, tazemetostat was maintained and cervical radiotherapy was administered with the aim to improve local control. At the most recent follow-up, cervical and pulmonary disease remained stable.

Literature review and prognosis analysis

Among the 25 previously reported patients with PDC identified in our literature review, the median age at diagnosis was 7.0 years (mean, 9.7 ± 8.5 years; range, 0.25–32 years). Females were more frequently affected than males (F: M ratio, 16:9; approximately 1.8:1). Most tumors arose in the upper axial skeleton, with cervical spine and/or clival involvement in 16 (64%) of the 25 patients. Systemic chemotherapy was not uniformly administered in these historical cohorts. Overall, 13 of the 25 patients received systemic chemotherapy, and the other 12 patients were managed without chemotherapy, typically with surgery with or without radiotherapy as described in the original reports. PFS could not be reliably estimated because PFS data were incompletely reported in a substantial proportion of cases. In contrast, OS was available for most patients. In an exploratory pooled analysis restricted to cases with clearly documented treatment and OS, patients with recorded exposure to systemic chemotherapy during the disease course had longer OS than those without documented chemotherapy exposure: mean OS 26.2 ± 17.4 months (median, 24 months; range, 0.3–76 months) versus 10.0 ± 9.7 months (median, 8.5 months; range, 1–38 months), respectively. Given the heterogeneity in treatment timing and indication across published reports, this finding should be interpreted as a descriptive observation only, and does not provide evidence of a survival benefit or a meaningful association attributable to chemotherapy.

Corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals derived from univariable Cox proportional hazards regression models are presented in Table 3. In the pooled cohort combining our institutional cases with previously reported patients, neither sex nor age (dichotomized at the median of 7 years) was associated with OS (p = 0.97 and p = 0.622, respectively). By contrast, recurrence was significantly associated with inferior survival (p = 0.029). Given that this comparison is clinically expected and primarily reflects baseline disease severity, it should be interpreted as an exploratory descriptive finding rather than an independent clinically actionable result. In this exploratory pooled analysis, a descriptive difference in median OS was observed between patients treated with multimodality regimens incorporating chemotherapy (plus radiotherapy and/or surgery) and those receiving local therapy without chemotherapy (p = 0.041). Given the heterogeneous nature of chemotherapy exposure across included studies, the lack of standardized treatment timing, and the high likelihood of selection bias, this finding should be considered purely descriptive and hypothesis-generating. It should not be interpreted as a survival benefit or as evidence of an association between chemotherapy and improved OS. Within the subgroup of patients with recurrent disease, radiotherapy showed no descriptive OS difference (p = 0.843), whereas receipt of chemotherapy showed a descriptive OS difference (p = 0.009). Among patients who received chemotherapy, OS did not differ between cervical and other axial primary sites (p = 0.126), and a similar lack of site-specific difference was observed among those treated with radiotherapy (p = 0.172).

Discussion

Poorly differentiated chordoma is an exceptionally rare and aggressive chordoma subtype, and our data further underscore its predilection for younger patients and the axial skeleton. The mean age at diagnosis of the five patients in our series was 21.4 ± 12.1 years (median 18 years; range 9–44 years) versus 7.0 years (mean 9.7 ± 8.5 years; range 0.25-32 years) in the pooled literature cohort of 25 previously reported cases. This age distribution confirms that PDC disproportionately affects children, adolescents, and young adults [8], but our experience also highlights that it can present in fully grown adults and should not be overlooked in this population. A female predominance was observed in both cohorts (three in our series, and a F: M ratio of 16:9 in the literature), suggesting a potential but as-yet unexplained sex bias. With respect to anatomic distribution, all tumors in our series arose in the mobile spine (4 cervical and 1 lumbar), while most reported cases involved the upper axial skeleton, with cervical and/or clival lesions accounting for 64% of tumors in the literature [17]. Taken together, these findings reinforce that PDC has a strong axial predilection but can occur along the entire cranio-spinal axis, including the mobile spine.

The pathological diagnosis of PDC presents several challenges. First, PDC often lacks classic morphological features of conventional chordoma, such as a myxoid extracellular matrix [18, 19]. This absence broadens the spectrum of differential diagnosis, and conventional immunohistochemical results can sometimes be misleading. PDC typically exhibits cohesive epithelioid morphology with positive keratin staining, which can lead to misdiagnosis as metastatic carcinoma [20]. For instance, in Patient 5 with a lumbar lesion in our series, the initial biopsy suggested a mesenchymal neoplasm and dedifferentiated sarcoma could not be excluded; subsequent multidisciplinary review favored metastatic carcinoma, and surgery was performed. Retrospectively, strong CKpan and CAM5.2 expression likely confounded the differential diagnosis and contributed to this pitfall [21]. Second, multi-omics studies have demonstrated that PDC is characterized by loss of SMARCB1/INI1 expression [22–24]. Although two cases in a study by Antonelli et al. were reported as PDC despite retained SMARCB1/INI1 expression, the loss of SMARCB1/INI1 is still widely regarded as a specific diagnostic marker for PDC [25]. The presence of SMARCB1/INI1 deficiency serves as a critical diagnostic “switch,” indicating a specific subset of malignant tumors. However, it is important to note that SMARCB1/INI1 loss is not entirely unique to PDC and can also occur in epithelioid sarcoma and atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumors (AT/RT) [26–28]. Indeed, beyond the cases in our center, other institutions have also reported initial misdiagnoses—often on small biopsy specimens—as epithelioid sarcoma or AT/RT due to overlapping histological features. A key distinguishing feature is that PDC retains brachyury expression, which is absent in both epithelioid sarcoma and AT/RT [29]. Compared to conventional chordoma, PDC exhibits distinct clinical and immunohistochemical profiles, and its morphological characteristics necessitate broad differential diagnosis, including metastatic carcinoma and various sarcomas. Regardless of morphology, PDC should be considered in the differential diagnosis for all axial skeletal tumors.

The poor prognosis of PDC remains a significant challenge in clinical management. As is the case with conventional chordoma, surgery aiming for R0 resection remains the cornerstone of treatment [30, 31]. However, it is often difficult to achieve adequate margins in axial locations involving the cervical spine and clivus because of close proximity to the spinal cord, brainstem, and major neurovascular structures. In the series reported by Angela R. Shih et al., 50% patients (5/10) with axial PDC developed local recurrence or distant metastasis within 1 year of diagnosis, including four recurrences occurring within 6 months after surgery, with a cumulative rate of recurrence or metastasis approaching 80% at 5 years [18]. Consistent with these findings, the five patients in our series experienced local recurrence during the disease course, underscoring that surgery alone is unlikely to provide durable local control in axial PDC. In this context, radiotherapy becomes an essential component of local multimodal management [32]. High-dose, conformal radiotherapy can help eradicate microscopic residual disease in anatomically constrained regions where wide surgical margins are rarely achievable [33, 34]. Although in our pooled analysis radiotherapy did not show a statistically significant OS benefit in the recurrent subgroup, this result is likely influenced by the small sample size and selection bias and does not negate its key role in local control. Prior studies have also suggested that cervical or clival PDC may have worse outcomes than tumors in more caudal locations, further illustrating the challenges of local treatment in these sites [35]. Taken together, current evidence supports a strategy that systematically integrates high-quality radiotherapy with maximal safe resection, rather than relying on surgery alone [36].

The present analysis provides additional support for a prognostic role of chemotherapy in PDC, based primarily on OS. Because PFS data were incompletely reported in a substantial proportion of published cases, reliable estimates of time to progression could not be generated in the pooled dataset. In contrast, OS was available for most patients and demonstrated a clinically meaningful separation between those who did and did not receive chemotherapy: patients treated with chemotherapy had a mean OS of 26.2 ± 17.4 months (median 24 months; range 0.3–76 months), compared with 10.0 ± 9.7 months (median 8.5 months; range 1–38 months) in patients managed without chemotherapy. In the pooled cohort combining our institutional series with previously reported patients, a history of recurrence was associated with inferior survival. However, this finding is largely clinically expected and mainly reflects underlying disease severity rather than a modifiable treatment-related factor. We therefore retained this comparison as part of the overall exploratory prognostic framework, but it should not be overinterpreted as a stand-alone clinically decisive result. Within this adverse prognostic context, treatment intensity emerges as a key modifiable factor. In our pooled analysis, patients receiving chemotherapy-based multimodality therapy had longer OS compared with those treated with surgery and/or radiotherapy alone. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the historical “chemotherapy” group did not represent a uniform treatment cohort and was subject to considerable selection bias and clinical heterogeneity. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Notably, even in the subgroup of patients who had experienced recurrence, a descriptive difference in OS continued to be observed between those who did and did not receive chemotherapy [36]. However, this observation is subject to the same, if not greater, selection bias as the primary analysis, and no meaningful association can be inferred. In contrast, radiotherapy was not associated with improved OS in this pooled retrospective analysis; however, this finding is likely confounded by substantial selection bias, because RT in recurrent cases was often used for palliation or in patients who were not candidates for further surgery or chemotherapy, and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence against its role in local control. Although derived from a small and heterogeneous cohort, these findings collectively support the concept that incorporating systemic chemotherapy into multimodality strategies may partially offset the negative impact of recurrence and improve long-term outcomes in this highly aggressive subtype. Further analysis suggests that systemic chemotherapy may have potential clinical value in selected scenarios, including tumor downstaging in locally advanced or borderline unresectable disease and multimodality treatment intensification in high-risk patients. However, because the pooled literature-based cohort included heterogeneous treatment settings and incompletely reported treatment timing, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence supporting a specific survival benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy after non-R0 resection.

Beyond its role in the recurrent or metastatic setting, our experience also highlights the potential value of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in the multidisciplinary management of PDC. Given the rarity of this entity and the limited size of our institutional cohort, robust survival comparisons for neoadjuvant use are not feasible; however, case-level data provide important qualitative signals. In selected patients, we integrated neoadjuvant chemotherapy into the treatment pathway and observed radiologically evident tumor shrinkage and symptomatic improvement, which facilitated surgical planning and spinal cord decompression in anatomically constrained regions. To the best of our knowledge, reports explicitly incorporating neoadjuvant chemotherapy into PDC management are exceedingly scarce, with most existing studies focusing on adjuvant or palliative systemic therapy [20]. Our findings therefore offer preliminary real-world support for considering neoadjuvant chemotherapy in carefully selected patients with high tumor burden, borderline resectability, or critical neural compromise. While these observations should be interpreted with caution in light of the small sample size, retrospective design, and potential selection bias, they suggest that chemotherapy may not only prolong survival when embedded within multimodality strategies but also improve local control and operative conditions when deployed in the neoadjuvant setting, warranting further evaluation in prospective, multicenter studies.

Targeted therapy has shown promising advances in the treatment of recurrent and refractory chordomas. Previous clinical trials at our center demonstrated that apatinib extended the median PFS to 18 months in patients with advanced chordoma [16]. Based on this experience and considering the large tumor size, we combined chemotherapy with the TKI apatinib during the neoadjuvant phase for Patient 2. The outcome was encouraging, with imaging revealing extensive intratumoral necrosis; however, because apatinib was administered concurrently, the specific contribution of AI chemotherapy to the observed tumor shrinkage cannot be determined. The characteristic SMARCB1/INI1 deficiency in PDC confers susceptibility to EZH2 inhibitors. Clinical studies, such as that by Mrinal M Gounder, have reported that tazemetostat improves PFS in patients with PDC [37]. Following local recurrence after chemotherapy, Patient 2 received tazemetostat targeted therapy and achieved a PFS of approximately 8 months. Subsequent MRI demonstrated a reduction in tumor volume and decreased internal blood supply. These positive outcomes suggest that molecular targeted therapy may prove to be a powerful tool in managing PDC in the future. Further clinical studies are warranted to validate and expand upon these findings. Beyond TKIs and epigenetic agents, SMARCB1/INI1 loss has also been linked to an “immune-hot” microenvironment and potential sensitivity to immune-checkpoint inhibition [38]. Recent studies have reported on INI1-deficient pediatric PDC described a clinical and radiographic response to nivolumab, supported by CD8-positive T cell infiltration and brachyury-directed immune recognition [39, 40]. Although we did not use immunotherapy in our cohort, these emerging data indicate that immunotherapy deserves further investigation in PDC.

Although our findings are statistically meaningful, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design inevitably introduces selection bias, as patients with higher risk or more advanced disease were more likely to receive intensified regimens (including combined chemotherapy), which may influence the interpretation of survival differences between treatment groups. Second, as the previously reported PDC cases included in our pooled analysis were largely derived from small case series or individual case reports, they generally lack standardized full-course management pathways and complete, harmonized clinical data (such as detailed systemic treatment information, longitudinal imaging, and consistent PFS definitions), thereby limiting the reliability of some subgroup analyses. Finally, Some hazard ratios (e.g., for prior recurrence history: HR 4.414, 95% CI 1.166–16.71) exhibit wide confidence intervals, reflecting considerable uncertainty due to the small sample size (N = 20 for this variable). While p < 0.05 suggests significance, the broad confidence intervals indicate fragile estimates. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as small sample sizes lead to less stable estimates and increased susceptibility to random fluctuation. Nevertheless, because survival definitions in historical case reports were not always described with complete uniformity, the pooled survival analysis should still be interpreted cautiously. These limitations highlight the need for multicenter, prospective registries and standardized care pathways to more clearly define the optimal integration of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy in the management of PDC.

Conclusion

This study reinforces PDC as a biologically distinct, SMARCB1/INI1-deficient chordoma subtype that predominantly affects young patients, shows a strong axial skeletal predilection, and carries a high risk of early recurrence and death. By integrating systematically managed cases from our institution with carefully curated reports from the literature, we show that surgery alone is rarely sufficient for durable control of axial PDC, and that high-quality radiotherapy should be regarded as a core component of local treatment rather than an optional adjunct. Our pooled survival analyses do not allow for any conclusion about an association between chemotherapy and longer OS. The descriptive observation of longer OS in the chemotherapy-exposed group is presented as an exploratory finding only, and must be interpreted with extreme caution due to significant bias and heterogeneity in the definition of “chemotherapy.” These hypothesis-generating observations require confirmation in prospective, standardized cohorts. Neoadjuvant use may downstage bulky or borderline-resectable tumors and facilitate spinal cord decompression in anatomically constrained regions. Alongside these findings, emerging data on SMARCB1-directed epigenetic therapy, anti-angiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and early immunotherapeutic approaches suggest that biology-driven systemic strategies may further expand the therapeutic options for this otherwise refractory malignancy. Although limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and reliance on heterogeneous literature-derived data, this study provides practice-informing evidence that rational, biology-based multimodal therapy can meaningfully alter the natural history of PDC. These results underscore the urgent need for prospective, multicenter, pathway-driven studies to refine risk stratification and define the optimal integration of surgery, radiotherapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and targeted or immunologic agents in this rare and highly aggressive tumor.
