Assessing differences in epigenetic aging markers between sexual or gender minority and straight cisgender adults using the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults cohort
Authors: Adovich S Rivera, Bridget R Slone, Grace V Avila, Brian Joyce, Yinan Zheng, Lifang Hou, Mercedes Carnethon, Ankeet S Bhatt, James M Shikany, Cora E Lewis, Pamela J Schreiner, Lauren B Beach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarginalized individuals like sexual and gender minority (SGM) people may experience accelerated biologic aging due to stigma and structural discrimination. We used Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) cohort data to assess this hypothesis. We first identified SGM individuals using Year 35 sexual orientation and gender identity data, then compared Year 15 (2000–01) or 20 (Y20, 2005–06) epigenetic aging biomarkers between SGM and straight cisgender participants using linear models adjusting for socio-demographics and behavior. Epigenetic markers included intrinsic epigenetic aging acceleration (IEAA), extrinsic EAA, PhenoAge acceleration, and GrimAge acceleration. Our sample (n =817) was 18.4% SGM. SGM and straight cisgender participants had similar age, sex, and race distributions. Unadjusted and adjusted analyses showed no significant differences between groups except for SGM adults having higher Y20 IEAA in adjusted models compared to straight cisgender adults. Longitudinal models also showed higher rate of change in IEAA in SGM adults. Given the potential of selection bias driving significant results, we used simulations to explore which scenarios can produce observed findings. Our simulations suggest that the observed Y20 IEAA difference is unlikely if there was indeed no disparity. It is also possible that the estimate was an underestimate of the true disparity due to selection from differential survival between the two groups. Our analysis showed no strong evidence of accelerated aging in SGM. Future studies to address limitations are needed.Keywords: Aging, epigenetic aging, Sexual and gender minorities, CARDIA, Adulthood

Introduction

Sexual minority stress theory describes how stress from discriminatory processes lead to adverse health outcomes in sexual and gender minority (SGM) individuals(Lick et al., 2013; McKetta et al., 2024). While developed for sexual minorities, this theory can explain the health issues faced by gender minorities (e.g., non-binary or transgender individuals), intersex individuals, and people who have same-sex attraction but do not report sexual minority identity(Oginni et al., 2023; Rosenwohl-Mack et al., 2020; Testa et al., 2015; Zeeman et al., 2019).

One mechanism by which minority stress may “get under the skin” to contribute to poor health and health disparities in SGM populations is stress-induced chronic inflammation and immune dysfunction which can contribute to accelerated aging(Chung et al., 2019; Flentje et al., 2020; Lick et al., 2013). Distal minority stressors (e.g., discrimination) can further compound on this by increasing stress-related coping behaviors and hindering access to social and health resources(Correro and Nielson, 2020; Streed et al., 2021).

With advancements in epigenetics and the addition of measures of sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, and gender identity (SOGI) to research studies, it is now possible to empirically examine if SGM individuals are experiencing accelerated biologic aging. A recent large-scale study showed supportive evidence of faster aging in sexual minority men compared to heterosexual men based on telomere lengths (Rivera et al., 2024). However, telomere length has been found to have mixed associations with risk of chronic diseases (L. Smith et al., 2019b). An alternative approach is the use of epigenetic aging measures which rely on the idea that DNA methylation patterns change over time as a function of a person's actions, experiences, and environment(Galkin et al., 2023; Joyce et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020; Lim et al., 2022; Simons et al., 2021; Yannatos et al., 2023). While the first few epigenetic clocks (Horvath, Hannum) were calibrated to chronologic age, the newer ones (PhenoAge, GrimAge) were developed to predict remaining healthy lifespans(Chen et al., 2016; Simons et al., 2021). Several studies have shown associations of epigenetic measures with risk of chronic diseases and, even time to death(Chen et al., 2016; Duan et al., 2022; Hillary et al., 2020).

Many studies have investigated how behavior and socioeconomic conditions are associated with epigenetic aging(Forrester et al., 2024; Galkin et al., 2023; Joyce et al., 2021; Simons et al., 2021). Importantly, cohort studies are uncovering disparities in epigenetic aging and are investigating “weathering” or the earlier onset of health decline in marginalized populations(Roetker et al., 2018; J. A. Smith et al., 2019a). Most studies have focused on disparities by race, sex/gender, or class (Crimmins et al., 2021; Forrester et al., 2024; George et al., 2021; Schmitz et al., 2022; Vick and Burris, 2017), and disparities by sexual orientation or gender identity is relatively unexplored. We found one study that recently demonstrated an association with higher sexual minority stress and epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) in SGM men with HIV(Ghanooni et al., 2022).

To address this knowledge gap, we compared epigenetic aging of SGM and heterosexual cisgender participants of the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study. We hypothesize that SGM individuals would exhibit accelerated aging compared to heterosexual cisgender counterparts. Further, we used simulations to explore how the temporality of measurements and survivor bias could influence our findings given that SGM status was measured 15 to 20 years after epigenetic aging samples were collected.

Methods

Data source

We used Y7 (1992–93), Y10 (1995–96) Y15 (2000–01), Y20 (2005–06) and Y35 (2020–22) data from the CARDIA cohort and the Cardiovascular Health Associations with Minority stress: Behavioral Evaluations and Self-Reported Sociopsychological Outcomes by SOGI Status (CHAMBERS) sub-study.(Division of Preventive Medicine University of Alabama at Birmingham, 2024)

CARDIA began in 1985–6 with a group of 5115 Black and White men and women aged 18–30 years recruited from Birmingham, AL; Chicago, IL; Minneapolis, MN; and Oakland, CA. All sites used community-based sampling except Oakland which recruited from an integrated healthcare system. At recruitment, participants were selected so that there would roughly equal number of people within subgroups of race, gender, education, and age. To date, there have been 10 waves of data collection (Table 1). CHAMBERS is a Y35 sub-study which collected information on SOGI, sexual behavior, and attraction. Full data collection forms (1 – Telephone interview, 3 - Sociodemographics, 8 – Medical History, 36 - Feelings, 44 - Discrimination, 130 – Sexual and Gender identity) are in the CARDIA website.22

For the primary analysis, we included people who participated in Y15, had at least one Y15 or Y20 epigenetic aging measure, and had Y35 SOGI data. All participants provided written informed consent to participate in CARDIA and CHAMBERS. CARDIA received institutional review board (IRB) approval at each field center (University of Alabama at Birmingham, Northwestern University, University of Minnesota, and Kaiser Permanente). Outcome

Epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) measures difference between a person's chronologic age and their biologic age as calculated from levels of DNA methylation in their genome(Zavala et al., 2024). DNA methylation naturally occurs over time and can lead to lower expression of certain genes. The rate and pattern of DNA methylation has been used to generate “epigenetic clocks” that correlate with lifespan and health outcomes(Kusters and Horvath, 2025). In this study, we analyzed four EAA measures from Year 15 and 20 blood samples: Intrinsic Epigenetic Age Acceleration (IEAA), Extrinsic Epigenetic Age Acceleration (EEAA), DNAm PhenoAge acceleration (PAA), and DNAm GrimAge acceleration (GAA). As these are acceleration measures, the values used were residuals from regression of the raw measure (e.g., PhenoAge) to chronologic age at time of the sample or survey wave as well as technical variables (aka batch adjustment) with some additional covariates for IEAA and EEAA.

In CARDIA, IEAA's regression used Horvath DNAm age on chronological age and blood cell count estimates. Meanwhile, EEAA is based on regressing an enhanced Hannum DNAm age on calendar age. Enhanced Hannum combined Hannum with quantities of naïve cytotoxic T cells, exhausted cytotoxic T cells, and plasmablasts. Full details on measurement and processing described elsewhere(Joyce et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2022).

Comparison group

We compared SGM to heterosexual cisgender adults. Any person who reported identity other than heterosexual and cisgender, were intersex, or reported same-sex attraction or behavior were considered as SGM. A report on this constructed measure is forthcoming.

Key questions include: (a) SGM identity: “Do you consider yourself to be: straight/heterosexual, lesbian/gay, bisexual, other, donť know, prefer not to respond?”, “Do you identify as asexual?”, “Do you consider yourself to be transgender?”), (b) Assigned sex at birth and current gender (“What sex were you assigned at birth, meaning on your original birth certificate?”, “Do you currently consider yourself to be:” Man, Woman, Non-binary, Other), (c) Intersex condition (“Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have a difference of sexual development (DSD) or an intersex condition?”), and (d) same-sex attraction/behavior (e.g., “People are different in their sexual/romantic attraction to other people. Which best describes your feelings, even if you have not taken any action based on your feelings? Only to women, mostly women, equally to women and men, mostly men, only to men, never to anyone, not sure”, “Have you ever felt sexually/romantically attracted to someone of the same sex?”, “In your lifetime, whom have you had sex with? Men, women, people of other genders”).

For sensitivity analysis, we used two alternatives of classification. First was just using the SGM identity questions from Form 130 – Sexual and gender identity. The second was based reporting Y7 discrimination due to sexual orientation (Form 44 - Discrimination, “Have you ever experienced discrimination, been prevented from doing something, or been hassled or made to feel inferior in any of the following eight situations because of your sexual preference (hetero/bi/homosexual): in your family? at school? Getting a job? Getting housing? At work? At home? Getting Medical care? On the street or in a public setting?”). The advantage of the second measure is that it was measured before EAA samples were taken, however, we are unable to disaggregate sexual minority adults who did not experience discrimination from heterosexual adults due to lack of SSOGI measures at this survey wave.

Covariates

Covariates for outcome analysis included binary sex/gender, race (Black, White), CARDIA site, income decile, educational attainment (less than college, college, graduate degree), self-reported chronic disease (with or without), Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression (CES-D) scale (≤7 low, 7–15 intermediate, ≥16 high) (Davidson et al., 2000; Lewinsohn et al., 1997), and Life's Simple 7 score (Lloyd-Jones et al., 2010). Chronologic age was already included in generating the outcomes so it was not included in the outcome modelling.

Binary sex/gender, race, and center were obtained from Y0 (Form 1 – Telephone Interview) and other covariates were from Year 10, 15 and 35. Binary sex from the parent CARDIA cohort was included as a covariate to adjust for sex differences. Due to the small number of gender minority participants (only 2 individuals included in CHAMBERS reported they were transgender and only 1 individual in CHAMBERS reported accessing gender affirming care), it was not possible to adjust for both sex and gender in study analyses. Individuals who reported they identified as transgender or nonbinary, as identifying as intersex or as having a difference of sex development, or who reported a different sex and gender identity were classified as SGM. Chronologic age at time of the survey were calculated based on Year 0 age. Income decile (“Which of these categories best describe your total combined family income for the past 12 months?”, see Table 1 for responses harmonized to Y10) and education (“What is the highest degree you earned?” less than college/trade school/others, completed college, graduate degree) were based on Form 3 – Sociodemographic questionnaire responses (see Table 1).

CES-D categories were based on Form 36 - Feelings. Comorbidities from Form 8 – Medical History (“Has a doctor or nurse ever said you had any of the following?”) included: hypertension, high cholesterol, heart disease, kidney disease, liver and gallbladder disease, migraine, peripheral vascular disease, cancer, gout, asthma, emphysema, bronchitis, and multiple sclerosis. Life's Simple 7 score is a measure of cardiovascular health that incorporates health behaviors (diet, physical activity, and smoking status) and health factors (body mass index, blood pressure, total cholesterol, and glucose). This index has been shown to predict various health outcomes like inflammation, atherosclerosis, and vascular aging (Aguayo et al., 2025). This was a derived variable obtained from the CARDIA data coordinating center.

Main statistical analysis

For the main analysis, we first calculated unadjusted differences in epigenetic age measures between SGM and heterosexual cisgender as defined above. We then calculated standardized differences adjusting for covariates using inverse propensity treatment weighting implemented using WeightIt(Greifer, 2024) and survey packages. Model 1 adjusts only for age, sex, race, and center, Model 2 additional adjusts for income and educational attainment, and Model 3 additional adjusts for chronic disease, depressive symptoms and Life's Simple 7 score. The choice of covariates were informed by a theoretical directed acyclic graph that links SGM status to EAA with Model 1 adjusting for potential confounders and likely estimates the overall disparity. Models 2 and 3 adjusts for potential mediators (2 – socioeconomic, 3 – clinical) and probably estimate any disparity that are due to unobserved pathways (Fig. 1).

All analyses (including bias and additional analyses below) were conducted in R 4.3.2/RStudio 2024.04.0. (See Supplement for R Code) Since we assessed multiple markers, we also assessed if p-values remained significant after correcting for multiple testing (Bonferroni adjusted p-values).

Additional statistical analysis

To address missing data, we use multiple imputation (m = 50, max iterations = 10) implemented via mice(van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) and WeightThem (Pishgar et al., 2021). For this analysis, we included everyone with at least one epigenetic aging measure even if their SGM categorization was missing. The imputation model included age, race, sex, survey center, income, education, depressive symptoms, discrimination (year 7), comorbidity status, Life's Simple 7, SGM indicators, and epigenetic aging outcomes.

To account for non-random participation in various aspects of the study, we used inverse propensity attrition weights(Metten et al., 2022). Attrition weight models adjusted for the following covariates: age, sex/gender, race, income, education, depressive symptoms, and Life's Simple 7 score. For outcome modelling, Treatment and attrition weights were multiplied and the result was truncated at 10. Attrition weights were calculated using logistic regression except for Year 35 attrition weights which used Bayesian additive regression trees.

Finally, we also conducted longitudinal analysis using mixed effects models with random intercept terms for the individual via lme4(Bates et al., 2015). This was done to test if the rate of change in EAA from Y10 to Y15 was different by SGM status. Instead of using weights, we used covariate adjustment. To facilitate interpretation, we used post-estimation using emmeans(Lenth, 2024).

Simulation of selection bias

Our analysis is hampered by the fact that we do not have SOGI data at or before the time that the EAA markers were measured. Ideally, we want Y10/Y15 SOGI data but only Y35 data is available. We thus need to make a strong and untestable assumption of limited SOGI mutability: Y35 SOGI is highly correlated with Y10 SOGI, and that misclassification is minimal. A second limitation is that we only have SOGI data for Y35 participants or those who survived to Y35. By conditioning on Y35 participation, we likely will detect an association between being SGM and EAA markers (called collider bias) even if the truth is that there is no association(Holmberg and Andersen, 2022). We thus need to assess the direction of bias given two truth scenarios: Scenario A - No disparity between Y10 SGM and Y15 epigenetic aging; and Scenario B - Y10 SGM individuals have lower Y15 epigenetic aging (Disparity: − 1.5) (Fig. 2).

Set-up of Simulation for Influence of Selection Bias on Results

Note: Selection bias arose from the data only having measured Sexual and Gender minority status for those who participated in Year 35. We assess the difference between the observed disparity and true disparity at different levels of selection bias driven by how SGM status and year 15 epigenetic age can predict participation at Year 35.

We conducted simulations focused on selection (the second limitation). We do not include confounders for simplicity. The general steps of the simulation analysis are: (1) Generate the EAA outcome for the two groups with incorporation of the true disparity value. (2) Calculate the probability of Y35 participation based on SGM and EAA. (3) Estimate an EAA outcome model limited using Y35 participants only. (4) Assess the difference in the estimated disparity and the true disparity. (5) Repeat with different seeds. We run these models across a range of strengths of selection bias (− 1 to 0). A coefficient of − 1 means being SGM (b_sgm) or a 1-unit increase in EA (b_ea) is associated with lower odds (OR: 0.36) of participating in Y35 while 0 means no influence on Y35. Code for the simulation is included in the Supplement.

Results

We included 817 participants with Y15 or Y20 epigenetic aging markers in the main analysis (Fig. 3). In this sample, 150 (18%) were SGM. SGM and heterosexual cisgender adults had similar chronologic age, sex, and income distributions. A higher proportion of SGM adults had post-Bachelor degrees and were Black. Average epigenetic ages were also similar between SGM and heterosexual cisgender adults (Table 2). We found those with missing Y35 SGM data had significantly higher Y15 and Y20 GAA than those with SGM data (Table 3). Y15 participants who were excluded from the main analysis had a lower proportion of Black participants but was comparable in terms of mean Y15 age and frequency distribution by center, and sex.

EAA differences

In complete case analysis, we found no significant difference in the Y15 markers across all models. Analyzing Y20 markers, we observed significantly higher Y20 IEAA in SGM adults in models adjusting for socio-demographic variables. Sensitivity analysis accounting for attrition showed significantly higher Y20 IEAA in SGM adults in all the adjusted models (Table 4, Fig. 4). However, these differences were not significant after correcting for multiple testing. No other significant differences in Y20 markers were noted.

Simulation of selection bias

In scenario A (truth is no disparity), in the absence of selection (b_sgm = 0 & b_ea = 0), the disparity model leads to a correct estimate of the disparity (estimated disparity = − 0.034, % with null p-values = 97%) (Fig. 8). However, with increasing association of SGM or EAA on Y35 participation (aka selection), the estimated disparity moves towards the negative values (darker blue) i.e., SGM have better EAA than heterosexual cisgender adults. Most scenarios lead to null or nonsignificant disparities. While the probability of correctly getting null results decreases with increasing strength of selection, the proportion with significant p-values remain below 40% for all tested conditions. If significant, the result is much more likely to suggest a negative disparity (i.e., SGM has lower EAA and this is not expected). If scenario A is true, selection bias does not matter too much because it will still likely produce null findings. A finding where SGM is paradoxically better off could be attributable to selection bias.

Bias analysis in the setting of no disparity (Scenario A). Note: EA – epigenetic age, SGM – sexual and gender minority.

From scenario B simulations (truth is SGM has higher EAA set at 1.5), we can see that the true disparity is recovered if there is no selection bias. Also, the probability of null findings is <20% (Fig. 9). With increasing selection bias (e.g., Y10 SGM are less likely to reach Y35, higher Y15 EA is less likely to reach Y35), the estimated disparity goes towards zero and is underestimated. The degree of bias in measuring the disparity also increases with selection bias. Additionally, the probability of null findings also increases while the probability of significant positive disparity decreases. Finally, we can see that it is highly improbable (<4%) to detect a significant disparity where SGM has lower EAA than heterosexual cisgender adults, and this occurs only at high selection bias.

Bias analysis in the setting of a true disparity (Scenario B). Note: EA – epigenetic age, SGM – sexual and gender minority.

In summary, if the truth is that there is no disparity in accelerated aging between SGM and heterosexual participants, our analysis will likely show null findings even in the presence of selection bias. Alternatively, if the truth is that there is a SGM individuals experience accelerated aging, our analysis can show null findings due to selection bias. The observed disparity in IEAA is likely robust to selection bias, but the null findings can be observed regardless of the truth.

Discussion

Our empirical analysis showed largely no differences in epigenetic aging acceleration markers between SGM and heterosexual cisgender adults who participated in the CARDIA Y35 Exam. We detected only one marker, IEAA, with a potential signal of difference. Y20 IEAA was higher in SGM adults in the complete case attrition weighted analysis and there are differences in rate of change with the longitudinal models. However, the significance did not persist after adjusting for multiple testing. Based on simulations, a potentially significant Y20 IEAA difference is probably not due to selection bias. Our result was plausible only in scenario B (disparity is present) but not in scenario A (no disparity). Additionally, our observed IEAA difference is also likely an underestimate of the true disparity if there is a survival difference between groups. Finally, simulations suggested that our null empirical findings (for the other markers) can occur in either scenario. Our null findings are thus consistent with a scenario where there is no true difference between SGM and non-SGM adults, or a scenario where the difference was overwhelmed by the selection bias (e.g., differential Y35 participation by SGM status) leading to null results.

If the IEAA disparity is present, this finding lends support to the theory which links minority stress to biologic aging acceleration and weathering(Chung et al., 2019; Flentje et al., 2020; Geronimus et al., 2006; Lick et al., 2013). Among epigenetic aging measures, IEAA is considered a “multitissue measure of cell aging” derived from Horvath epigenetic age and viewed to be less affected by disease and risk factors (Joyce et al., 2021). IEAA would thus likely be affected by an exposure like minority stress which is expected to have broad impacts on the body due to its proposed embodiment mechanisms. These mechanisms include: alteration of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, immune dysregulation, and exaggerated autonomic nervous system reactivity (Lick et al., 2013).

The impact of IEAA on health is still a growing area of research. A systematic review showed that most studies focused on cardiovascular health with mixed findings(Fransquet et al., 2019). Several studies have demonstrated that cardiovascular health scores predict IEAA and that IEAA predicts cardiovascular risk and organ damage(Crimmins et al., 2021; Pottinger et al., 2021; Roetker et al., 2018). But, there are also studies with null findings(Beydoun et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2024). Within CARDIA, higher IEAA has been found to be associated with lower clinical cardiovascular health scores in Black or male participants(Joyce et al., 2021).Another analysis found that Year 20 EAA markers were not associated with incident CVD events after inclusion of a clinically-derived age acceleration score(Forrester et al., 2024). In terms of other chronic health conditions, increased IEAA has been found to be associated with breast cancer and B-cell lymphomas but not other cancers(Ambatipudi et al., 2017; Dugue et al., 2018). Finally, one study did not observe correlation between baseline IEAA and rate of change in frailty scores among older adults. on follow-up(Seligman et al., 2022).

The rest of our findings suggest that SGM and heterosexual cisgender Y35 participants had similar immune aging (EEAA), and similar predicted healthy lifespan (GAA, PAA) at Y15 and Y20. As our simulation showed this can be due to selection bias. Even if we assume fixed sexual or gender identity throughout all CARDIA exams, we have available SOGI data only for those who survive to Y35. Healthy survivors with better Y15/Y20 epigenetic aging markers were more likely to be included in our dataset. (e.g., those with available Y35 had significantly lower Y20 GrimAge scores, see Table 3)

Thus, and supported by our simulations, we expected null findings because (1) the healthiest individuals will be more likely to participate in Y35, (2) healthier individuals have better Y15 or Y20 epigenetic age, and (3) SGM individuals are less likely to be healthy (not necessarily aging faster) due to minority stress. We employed two approaches to address this selection. Analysis with attrition weights found null results except for significant a Y20 IEAA difference (before adjusting for multiple testing). Meanwhile, using multiple imputation (with or without attrition weights) led to null results for all markers. Since these methods for addressing selection may not always work better compared with complete case analysis, additional work with other cohorts less affected by healthy survivor bias is still needed(Kristman et al., 2005; Metten et al., 2022).

Crucially, our null findings could also be interpreted more positively within a resilience framework(Colpitts and Gahagan, 2016; Testa et al., 2015). SGM individuals have often found ways to cope with stressors and systems of oppression. Our sample included middle-aged adults who have survived adolescence and young adulthood (often characterized as vulnerable life stages of SGM individuals). These survivors may have gained ability to cope with stress and have overcome biologic impacts of stress through adopting new behaviors, gaining access to supportive environments or forming protective relationships. Studies focused on how individuals successfully maintain health in the face of adversity are important to support equity efforts for SGM populations.

Limitations

In addition to possible selection bias, our study has other limitations. Generalizability is an issue given this cohort focused only on Black and White adults from selected generations and geographic regions in the US. The modest sample size, especially for the SGM group, likely limited our power to detect differences and made it unfeasible to examine outcomes for specific categories within SGM as well as intersectional categories (e.g., male SGM vs male heterosexual participants). We also were not able to include measures of minority stress for comparisons. This requires the assumption that all SGM individuals experience minority stress and discrimination. Additional studies are needed to show a more concrete link between minority stress, discrimination, and biologic aging. Our results can help design these studies including sample size calculations. Finally, we do not assess how these disparities translate to differences in chronic disease risk in SGM individuals.

Conclusion

Despite limitations, our analysis showed no strong evidence of accelerated aging during middle adulthood in a group of SGM adults that survived until a median age of 60 years. Future work is needed to confirm findings, investigate mechanisms, and identify implications on SGM chronic disease risk.
