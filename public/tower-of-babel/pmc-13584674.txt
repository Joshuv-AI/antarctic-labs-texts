Three‐Dimensional Airway Reconstruction and Computational Spray Simulation to Guide Bronchoscopic Amphotericin B Delivery in Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis: An Exploratory Prospective Cohort Study With Historical Controls
Authors: Yonghui Cheng, Hao Zhu, Guoshi Wan, Enguo Chen, Riqing Chen, Chenhui Su, Hong Xu, Xiongjun Fu, Wenwen Kong, Jisong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo initially evaluate the feasibility and potential clinical value of high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT)‐based three‐dimensional (3D) airway reconstruction combined with computational spray simulation for guiding bronchoscopic amphotericin B delivery in patients with chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA).MethodsThis multicenter, prospective cohort study with an expanded historical control was conducted. Patients in the prospective reconstruction cohort received HRCT‐based 3D airway reconstruction combined with computational spray simulation to guide bronchoscopic amphotericin B delivery. Historical controls consisted of eligible CPA patients with complete clinical, imaging, treatment, adverse‐event, and 6 month follow‐up data who had received conventional bronchoscopic amphotericin B administration before implementation of the reconstruction‐guided approach.ResultsA total of 10 patients in the reconstruction cohort and 30 historical controls were included. Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between groups, although fatigue was more frequent in the historical control cohort. At 6 months after treatment, the reconstruction cohort showed lower total St. George's respiratory questionnaire (SGRQ) scores than the historical control cohort. And SGRQ scores decreased significantly after treatment in each group, with a decrease of well over four points from baseline. More favourable clinical and radiographic responses and fewer adverse events were observed in the reconstruction cohort.ConclusionThe integrated reconstruction‐guided strategy may provide a feasible strategy for optimizing bronchoscopic amphotericin B delivery in CPA. Given the historical‐control design, small intervention cohort and potential residual confounding, these preliminary findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger prospective controlled studies.Keywords: 3D airway reconstruction, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, computational spray simulation, SGRQ

Introduction

Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is a progressive infection caused by Aspergillus in patients with underlying chronic lung disease, affecting over 3 million people worldwide [1, 2]. The prognosis of CPA is poor, with 5‐year survival rates ranging from 15% to 60%, influenced by comorbidities and patient age [3, 4]. Current management relies on prolonged systemic antifungal therapy (minimum 6 months), using voriconazole and itraconazole as first‐line agents [5, 6, 7]. However, treatment outcomes remain suboptimal; after 6–12 months of therapy, only 47%–50% of patients exhibit improvement, while approximately 32% experience clinical deterioration [8]. Surgical intervention, although an alternative, is contraindicated in many patients due to risks of postoperative bleeding, infection, and fungal dissemination [9, 10]. Surgical recurrence rates can be as high as 33% within 3 years [11].

Bronchoscopic local antifungal therapy has emerged as a potential alternative or adjunct to systemic treatment, offering the theoretical advantage of targeted high‐dose drug delivery with minimized systemic exposure [12], as evidenced by several case reports in pulmonary fungal infections [13, 14]. Compared to systemic administration, targeted local delivery may enhance efficacy, reduce total drug dosage, and shorten treatment duration [15]. CPA often occurs secondary to pre‐existing pulmonary structural damage, which manifests as structural airway disorders, including lumen stenosis or obstruction [16]. Conventional bronchoscopic topical drug delivery lacks precision, resulting in uneven drug distribution within lesions and making it difficult to determine the extent of diffusion. Existing methods of bronchoscopic drug delivery are also difficult to cover relatively dispersed target lesions [17].

Given these limitations, we established three‐dimensional (3D) airway reconstruction derived from chest high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) combined with computational spray simulation to guide precise bronchoscopic delivery of amphotericin B via a disposable atomizing microcatheter. In this study, we initially explored the efficacy of this technique for drug administration in CPA treatment to optimize targeted drug delivery, and thus potentially improve therapeutic outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This multicentre prospective cohort study with an expanded historical control cohort was conducted at three hospitals (the First People's Hospital of Wuyi County, the Second People's Hospital of Yuhang District, and Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University). Patients in the prospective reconstruction cohort were enrolled between December 2023 and June 2025. Historical controls were identified from CPA patients treated at the same participating centres before the implementation of HRCT‐based 3D airway reconstruction and computational spray simulation. The study was designed as an exploratory historical‐control comparison. No individual matching or propensity‐score matching was performed. Approved by the respective Institutional Review Boards (Approval Nos.: 2023WY002, 2024–015, and 2024–2310‐01) and adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki, the study obtained written informed consent from the prospective cohort participants, with a waiver granted for the historical control due to the retrospective design.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria of the Prospective Cohort Study

Patients were eligible only if they had not received prior systemic antifungal agents (e.g., itraconazole, isavuconazole, or micafungin). CPA was diagnosed according to established criteria [5]: (1) the presence of respiratory symptoms (e.g., cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis) and/or systemic symptoms (e.g., fever, weight loss, and fatigue) lasting for at least 3 months; (2) the characteristic cavitary lesions, intracavitary mass, infiltrative changes, or nodules of CPA by chest imaging; (3) positive mycological evidence, including a positive Aspergillus‐specific IgG antibody test, a positive galactomannan assay, or confirmation via molecular diagnostics, culture, or histopathology; and (4) exclusion of other diagnoses such as tuberculosis, nontuberculous mycobacteria, and histoplasmosis.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe allergy to the drugs used in the study; (2) history of bone marrow or organ transplantation, or use of immunosuppressive agents; (3) long‐term use of glucocorticoids; (4) received radiotherapy or chemotherapy within the past 3 months; (5) severe liver and kidney failure, respiratory failure, heart failure, persistent asthma or malignant arrhythmia; (6) elevated specific IgE; and (7) unable to tolerate bronchoscopy as assessed by the investigators.

Historical Control Selection

Because CPA is a relatively uncommon disease and complete retrospective data were limited, the historical control cohort included all patients who fulfilled the same diagnostic criteria, met the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, received systemic antifungal therapy and conventional bronchoscopic amphotericin B administration, and had complete baseline, treatment, imaging, SGRQ, adverse‐event, and 6 month follow‐up data. As a result, 30 eligible historical controls were finally included.

Treatments

All patients received systemic antifungal therapy throughout the study period. Oral voriconazole (Vfend; Pfizer Italia S.r.l., Italy; 200 mg/tablet) was administered with a loading dose of 400 mg every 12 h on the day prior to the initial bronchoscopic procedure, followed by a maintenance dose of 200 mg twice daily. A stock solution of amphotericin B (2.5 mg/mL) was prepared by dissolving 25 mg of lyophilized powder (North China Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., China) in 10 mL of sterile water for injection. Two hours before each bronchoscopic treatment, all patients received 5 mL of this solution (12.5 mg) via jet nebulizer for inhalation. In the prospective cohort, the target for local therapy was selected based on 3D airway reconstruction and simulated deposition patterns of nebulized amphotericin B. Prior to administration, a 12.5‐mg dose (5 mL) of amphotericin B solution was prepared and delivered via the disposable endoscopic spray catheter (BRS‐GYG‐20, Broncus Medical Inc., Hangzhou, China) directly to the identified site. In the control group, the target lesion was first identified by conventional assessment of chest CT images. Following identification, the same amphotericin B solution was administered bronchoscopically using a dedicated spray catheter (WP‐18/1800, Micro‐Tech Medical Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China).

Bronchoscopic local amphotericin B administration was performed every 2 weeks. All patients were planned to receive at least four treatment sessions. Up to two additional sessions, for a maximum of six sessions, could be recommended when the lesion burden was large, residual lesions remained substantial, or the clinical/radiographic response was considered insufficient after the initial sessions, provided that the patient tolerated the procedure. The 4–6‐session treatment window was based on institutional practice and experience with bronchoscopic local drug instillation, as no standardized number of bronchoscopic local antifungal treatment sessions has been established for CPA. The cumulative dose of amphotericin B administration was limited to 10–25 mg per session. All procedures were performed by pulmonologists with > 10 years of interventional bronchoscopy experience. A standardized procedural protocol was applied across participating centres, including pre‐procedural nebulization, amphotericin B dose preparation, bronchoscopic localization, catheter placement, local drug administration, and post‐procedural monitoring.

3D Airway Reconstruction and Simulation

HRCT images of the chest were imported into LungPro software (Broncus Medical Inc., Hangzhou, China) to generate a 3D reconstruction of the bronchial tree. For small airways where the automated reconstruction was suboptimal, the 3D model was manually supplemented based on the original HRCT images. Subsequently, a spray simulation was performed to analyse the distribution of drug particles emitted from the nebulizing catheter within the airways. Outcomes

The outcomes included overall efficacy, St. George's respiratory questionnaire (SGRQ), imaging evaluation, and adverse events (AEs) at 6 months after treatment. SGRQ, a standardized self‐administered questionnaire for airways diseases, assesses three domains: symptoms (eight items, frequency and severity of respiratory symptoms), activity (16 items, activities that cause or are limited by breathlessness), and impacts (26 items, social functioning and psychological disturbances due to airway disease) [20, 21]. Scores for each subscale and the total questionnaire range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating worse quality of life. A decrease of ≥ 4 points on the SGRQ is considered a clinically meaningful threshold for treatment benefit [22]. The treatment benefit was defined as: (1) significant improvement: clinical symptoms and signs disappeared; (2) improvement: remission of clinical symptoms and signs; (3) no improvement: persistent or almost no improvement in clinical symptoms and signs; (4) deterioration: exacerbation and progression of clinical symptoms and signs. Imaging outcomes were categorized as follows: (1) complete remission: complete resolution of the aspergilloma and pericavitary consolidation/infiltration, plus a > 50% reduction in pleural thickening or cavity size; (2) partial remission: reduction in aspergilloma size and partial resolution of pericavitary consolidation/infiltration, with a < 50% decrease in pleural thickening or cavity size; (3) stable disease: no significant imaging changes; (4) progressive disease: increase in the size or number of aspergillomas, cavities, solid nodules, or pleural thickening. Overall treatment efficacy was assessed based on clinical and radiographic responses, and was categorized as follows: (1) cure: significant improvement of clinical symptoms, along with complete remission of imaging findings (excluding pre‐existing lesions); (2) improvement: clear improvement in clinical symptoms, with no radiographic disease progression; (3) ineffective: absence of clinical improvement or worsening of symptoms, accompanied by radiographic disease progression. Patients achieving a status of ‘Cure’ or ‘Improvement’ were considered favourable treatment response.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with quartiles [M (Q1, Q3)], as appropriate. Between‐group comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test or Welch's t‐test for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed continuous variables. Within‐group comparisons of SGRQ scores between baseline and 6 months after treatment were performed using paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or Chi‐square test. Effect‐size estimates were additionally calculated for outcomes to improve the interpretability of the exploratory analysis. For binary outcomes, risk differences (RDs) and risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the historical control cohort as the reference group. RDs were calculated as the event rate in the reconstruction cohort minus the event rate in the historical control cohort. RRs were calculated as the event rate in the reconstruction cohort divided by the event rate in the historical control cohort. For continuous SGRQ outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were calculated as the mean value in the reconstruction cohort minus that in the historical control cohort. ΔSGRQ was calculated at the individual‐patient level as the 6 months total SGRQ score minus the baseline total SGRQ score. No formal adjustment for multiplicity was performed because this was an exploratory study. All statistical analyses were carried out using R version 4.6.0 (2026‐04‐24 ucrt). Two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistical significance.

Treatment Efficacy

The comparison of outcomes in the two cohorts was presented in Table 4. All patients in the reconstruction cohort benefited from treatment, with 70.00% (7/10) achieving significant clinical improvement and 30.00% (3/10) showing improvement. Conversely, in the control cohort, 13.33% (4/30) had significant improvement and 20.00% (6/30) improved, while 66.67% (20/30) derived no benefit. These non‐responders mainly presented with persistent fatigue, frequent cough, and exertional dyspnea. On imaging assessment, a complete or partial remission was observed in 90% (9/10) of the reconstruction cohort, whereas only 26.67% (8/30) of the control cohort achieved such a remission, with 6 patients showing disease progression. The overall efficacy was significantly higher in the reconstruction cohort than in the control cohort (p < 0.001). In the reconstruction cohort, all 10 patients responded (5 cured, 5 improved). In the historical control cohort, 10 patients responded (2 cured, 8 improved), while the remaining 20 showed no improvement. Representative imaging findings from three patients in the reconstruction group were shown in Figure 4. In Patient 3, pre‐treatment imaging revealed obstructive pneumonia and atelectasis in the right upper lobe, with yellowish‐white necrotic debris occluding the lobar orifice (Figure 4a–c). After four treatment cycles, complete resolution of the pneumonia was observed, accompanied by lobar re‐expansion and bronchiectasis; the necrotic obstruction was cleared, and the lumen was patent (Figure 4d–f). For Patient 6, a thick‐walled cavity in the right apical segment (Figure 4g) resolved completely after six treatment cycles (Figure 4h). In Patient 8, a cavitary aspergilloma in the left apical segment and associated significant inflammatory mucosal changes in the left upper lobe bronchus were observed prior to therapy (Figure 4i,j). Following four cycles of treatment, the aspergilloma size decreased by more than 50% (indicating complete remission), and the bronchial mucosa returned to a normal appearance (Figure 4k,l).

AEs

AEs were summarized in Table 5. Any AE was observed less frequently in the reconstruction cohort than in the historical control cohort [1/10 (10.00%) vs. 20/30 (66.67%), p = 0.003]. In the reconstruction cohort, only one patient developed tachypnea, and no fever, chest pain, or cough was reported. In the historical control cohort, the most frequently observed adverse events were tachypnea [19/30 (63.33%)], cough [14/30 (46.67%)], and fever [12/30 (40.00%)]. Compared with the historical control cohort, the reconstruction cohort showed lower incidences of fever, tachypnea, and cough, whereas the difference in chest pain was not statistically significant.

Effect Estimates for Outcomes

Effect estimates for outcomes were summarized in Table 6. Using the historical control cohort as the reference group, the reconstruction cohort showed a higher overall treatment efficacy rate than the historical control cohort (100.00% vs. 33.33%; RD = 66.7%, 95% CI: 33.6% to 80.8%; RR = 3.00, 95% CI: 1.81 to 4.98). Imaging remission was also more frequently observed in the reconstruction cohort than in the historical control cohort (90.00% vs. 26.67%; RD = 63.3%, 95% CI: 28.1% to 78.3%; RR = 3.38, 95% CI: 1.80 to 6.33). Any AE was less frequently observed in the reconstruction cohort than in the historical control cohort (10.00% vs. 66.67%; RD = −56.7%, 95% CI: −73.0% to −21.4%; RR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.98). At 6 months after treatment, the reconstruction cohort had a lower total SGRQ score than the historical control cohort (32.57 ± 6.61 vs. 45.15 ± 13.28; MD = −12.58, 95% CI: −19.10 to −6.06). The mean ΔSGRQ was −20.30 ± 10.37 in the reconstruction cohort and −8.39 ± 4.94 in the historical control cohort, corresponding to a between‐group MD of −11.91 points (95% CI: −19.45 to −4.37), indicating a reduction in total SGRQ score in the reconstruction cohort.

Discussion

In this exploratory cohort study with an expanded historical control cohort, an integrated reconstruction‐guided strategy comprising patient‐specific 3D airway reconstruction, computational spray simulation, individualized procedural planning, and atomizing‐microcatheter delivery was associated with favourable preliminary clinical, radiographic, patient‐reported, and safety outcomes in patients with CPA. These findings suggest that individualized airway reconstruction and spray simulation may help optimize local drug delivery. However, due to the historical‐control design, small intervention cohort, and potential residual confounding, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Conventional bronchoscopic drug delivery, which relies on manual localization using chest imaging, is limited by accuracies. This is particularly problematic in CPA, where structural abnormalities such as cavities and traction bronchiectasis distort airway anatomy, leading to suboptimal targeting. To address this, we employed a novel, image‐guided approach in our prospective cohort. First, 3D airway reconstruction from chest HRCT provided detailed visualization of airway morphology and lesion extent, enabling precise anatomical localization of target bronchi and cavities. Second, the LungPro software platform was used to plan an optimal bronchoscopic pathway, assisting clinicians in selecting an optimal access route and injection site, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of local therapy.

Conventional bronchoscopic drug delivery, involving perfusion or injection, is limited by uncontrolled distribution. Due to gravity and airway resistance, the liquid drug often spills and flows uncontrollably into adjacent airways, leading to uneven deposition and compromised local efficacy. In the control group, we observed substantial drug reflux during administration, which not only prevented accurate assessment of drug distribution at the target site but also reduced the effective intralesional drug concentration, thereby affecting the efficacy. We utilized computational spray simulation to model the diffusion and deposition patterns of aerosolized drug particles within the target airways and lesions. The simulation results suggested that a relatively broad and spatially distributed aerosol pattern within the reconstructed airway model, without apparent reflux in the modelled setting (Figures S1 and S2). These model‐derived patterns support the potential procedural value of simulation‐assisted catheter positioning and spray‐direction planning.

At 6 months after treatment, we observed that the two cohorts showed improvement in clinical symptoms and radiographic findings. However, no significant intergroup difference was found in measures of activity limitation on daily life, potentially due to the prevalent underlying lung pathologies (e.g., structural lung damage, impaired function) in the two cohorts. Additionally, the AEs in the historical control cohort comprised fever, tachypnea, chest pain, and cough, whereas the single AE in the reconstruction cohort was tachypnea. The lower frequency of AEs in the reconstruction cohort may be regarded as a preliminary safety signal rather than definitive evidence of superior safety. In this current study, all patients received concomitant systemic voriconazole therapy during the study period. Therefore, the independent effect of HRCT‐based reconstruction and spray simulation cannot be fully isolated. The observed differences should be interpreted as the potential added value of optimized local amphotericin B delivery in addition to standard systemic antifungal therapy.

The additional effect‐size analysis may provide a more clinically interpretable estimate of the magnitude and uncertainty of the observed between‐group differences. Compared with the historical control cohort, the reconstruction cohort showed higher absolute and relative rates of overall treatment response and imaging remission, lower post‐treatment total SGRQ scores, greater reductions in total SGRQ scores, and fewer adverse events. These findings were directionally consistent across clinical, radiographic, patient‐reported, and safety outcomes, suggesting a potential clinical signal associated with the reconstruction‐guided approach.

This study has several limitations. First, efficacy was evaluated using a composite of SGRQ scores, clinical symptoms, and imaging changes, lacking precise quantitative criteria. Although bronchoalveolar lavage fluid galactomannan testing showed a positivity rate of 48.7%, serological tests (serum galactomannan positivity: 18.4% [23]) were excluded from the primary assessment due to their limited specificity and sensitivity. Second, all patients received concomitant systemic voriconazole to ensure adequate blood concentrations and treatment of all infection sites. The potential positive therapeutic effect of voriconazole on CPA needs to be considered when evaluating efficacy. Moreover, a pre‐procedural amphotericin B nebulization was administered to acclimate the airways and prevent acute adverse reactions, which may influence the generalizability of the local treatment effect. Third, the intervention cohort remained small, and the study was not powered for definitive efficacy testing. Fourth, residual confounding and temporal bias cannot be completely excluded due to the non‐randomized design and the use of historical controls [24]. Fifth, the number of bronchoscopic treatment sessions varied between four and six according to lesion burden, clinical/radiographic response, and patient tolerance. However, the exact number of completed sessions was not systematically recorded and therefore could not be compared between groups or included as an adjustment variable. Potential differences in treatment exposure may have influenced the observed outcomes. Future prospective studies should prespecify the number of bronchoscopic treatment sessions or systematically record treatment exposure so that the potential influence of treatment intensity can be evaluated. Sixth, the reconstruction‐guided strategy consisted of multiple concurrently implemented components, and the present study could not distinguish the independent contribution of 3D reconstruction, computational simulation, procedural planning, or the dedicated atomizing microcatheter. In addition, the simulated aerosol distribution has not yet been validated against direct in vivo measurements of amphotericin B deposition or local tissue drug concentration. The model relied on simplified assumptions regarding airflow, particle transport, and airway‐wall interactions. The use of only 100 simulated particles may limit the realism of the model. Therefore, the simulation outputs should be interpreted as qualitative guidance for target selection, catheter positioning, and spray‐direction planning rather than as quantitative estimates of actual in vivo drug deposition. Future studies should validate predicted deposition patterns against in vivo measurements or experimentally validated patient‐specific airway models and should employ more physiologically realistic aerosol models and larger particle numbers.

Conclusion

The integrated reconstruction‐guided strategy showed promising preliminary clinical signals and may help optimize bronchoscopic amphotericin B delivery in CPA. Given the historical‐control design, small intervention cohort, absence of randomization, and potential residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Larger prospective controlled studies are required to confirm the efficacy and safety of this approach.
