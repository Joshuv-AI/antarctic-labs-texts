From pre-lamination to foliated Plane
Christian Bonatti, Théo Marty

Abstract
To a singular foliation on the plane corresponds a circular boundary at infinity endowed with a pre-lamination on the circle. We solve the converse direction. We determine which pre-lamination on the circle are boundary at infinity of a foliation, and we build the corresponding (unique) foliation. We consider both regular foliations and a singular foliations with prong singularities.



Keywords:
foliations on the plane, pre-laminations on the circle.

AMS classification:
57R30, 37C86.

Introduction

1.1

Short presentation of the questions and results

Foliations on the plane and their relations with pre-laminations on the circle recently saw interesting applications, for instance, to the characterization of pseudo-Anosov flows by their bi-foliated planes.
For almost a century, it has been known that foliations on the plane are characterized by their leaf spaces
[
4
,
5
]

1

The generalized construction allows at most countable families of (singular) foliations with some mild transversality assumptions, and states the uniqueness of the compactification under some natural conditions.

The circle at infinity is naturally endowed with a pre-lamination as follows. Denote by

In this article, we complete the picture by solving the converse question:

Question 1

More precisely, starting from a pre-lamination

The uniqueness of the compactification in

1.2

Statement of our results: the non-singular case

Let us now present our results, first in the non-singular cases.
Theorem

S^{1}
and with no more than countably many leaves ending at a given endpoints, and that satisfies two additional conditions. We express these conditions using

Additionally, when it satisfies the previous condition, the foliation is unique up to a homeomorphism of

Later on, a pre-lamination satisfying the hypotheses in Theorem

Let

Preliminary notions

In this section, we introduce our terminology about ramified cover (used as a tool later on) and about foliations. We also state precisely the compactification of foliations on the plane.

2.1

Ramified cover

Let
S
be a connected surface which is not homeomorphic to a sphere,
X\subset S
be a closed discrete subset, and
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
be any function.
As

is closed and discrete, we can fix a family of disjoint compact discs

centered at

, pairwise disjoint.

Fix an origin
\mathbf{x_{0}}\in S\setminus\bigcup_{x\in X}D_{x}
. Let
G(X,\varphi)
be the normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(S\setminus X,\mathbf{x_{0}})
generated (as a normal subgroup) by the generators
\{(\gamma_{x})^{\varphi(x)}\}_{x\in X}
where
\gamma_{x}
is a loop around

(the boundary of
D_{x}
). Clearly, each
\gamma_{x}
depends on the choice of a path between the origin
\mathbf{x_{0}}
and

but, as we consider the normal subgroup, the group

does not depend on the choices.

A normal cover only depends on a normal subgroup of the fundamental group. Thus

determines a normal cover
\Pi_{X,\varphi}\colon\widetilde{(S\setminus X)}_{\varphi}\to S\setminus X
. Any connected component of
\Pi_{x,\varphi}^{-1}(D_{x}\setminus\{x\})
, for
x\in X
, is a punctured disc, and
\Pi_{x,\varphi}
induces on that punctured disc a cyclic cover or degree
\varphi(x)
over
D_{x}\setminus\{x\}
. Let us consider the completion of
\widetilde{(S\setminus X)}_{\varphi}
obtained by adding all the punctures of these punctured discs. It yields a new surface
\widetilde{S_{X,\varphi}}
and the projection
\Pi_{X,\varphi}
extends in a unique way as a ramified cover

\Pi_{X,\varphi}\colon\widetilde{S_{X,\varphi}}\to S,

ramified over
X
and which is a cyclic ramified cover of degree
\varphi(x)
on each connected component of

\Pi_{X,\varphi}^{-1}(D_{x})
.

The fundamental group of
\widetilde{(S\setminus X)}_{\varphi}
is by construction the group

and is generated by the loop around the punctured discs, connected components of
\Pi_{x,\varphi}^{-1}(D_{x}\setminus\{x\})
, for
x\in X
. Adding the punctures vanishes all this generators, so that the surface

is connected, simply connected and not compact, and so a plane.

Definition 2.1
.

The projection

3

Planar structure

We introduce here the notion of planar structure: roughly speaking, a non-Hausdorff 1-manifold with order data that encode a (maybe singular) foliation on the plane.

3.1

Tame non-Hausdorff
1
-manifold

A
a non-Hausdorff
1
-manifold

The example below is what we don’t want to consider:

Example 3.1

Consider the usual diadic Cantor set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Let

be a homeomorphism of

. Let

be the quotient of

obtained by identifying

.
Then

is a non-Hausdorff 1-manifold, and every point in

If
\mathcal{L}
is a non-Hausdorff
1
-manifold and

. A

is a germ at

of connected component of

is an open interval that contains

define the same side as a connected component of
J\setminus\{x\}
if they coincide in a neighborhood of

A non-Hausdorff
1
-manifold

which are not separated one from the other there are charts

is not separated from

on their common side.

On tame non-Hausdorff

of

is an equivalence class of the non-separating relation on a given side. More precisely: consider the set of pairs

where

, and

is a side of

are non separated on their side

be an equivalence class of this relation

.
A point

may belong to at most

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a tame non-Hausdorff 1-manifold.
Then the set of non-separated points of
\mathcal{L}
is at most countable.

Proof.
From the tame definition, every non-separated pair of points in
\mathcal{L}
is contained in a pair of intervals containing no other non-separated pair of points. One may choose these intervals in a countable basis of the topology of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, these set of pairs of intervals is countable, and (a fortiori) so is the set of pairs of non-separated points. Hence the set of non-separated points is at most countable

Branching have been used to characterize non-separated leaves of (non-singular) foliations. We introduce a similar notion to characterize separatrices of

-foliations.
A

•

•

given

One denotes by

The

An

We introduce singular planar structure. Think of them as the leaf space of the

4.4.2
Bad accumulations

Let

be a shell/star pre-lamination of

. Recall that, among complementary region with one boundary of length at least

, all but finitely many have well-defined two long edges (see Lemma

A

of

is a sequence
(\Delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of complementary shells that converges toward a geodesic

of
\Int(D^{2})

A

of

of complementary stars that converges toward a geodesic

of

are not adjacent.

The lack of bad accumulations has a simple characterization:

Lemma 4.14
.

Let

be a shell/star pre-lamination. Then

1.

has no bad accumulations of shells if and only if for all
\epsilon>0
, for all but maybe finitely many of the complementary shell
\Delta
that have an edge of length at least
\epsilon
, the root of
\Delta
is one of its two long edges.

2.

has no bad accumulations of stars if and only if for all
\epsilon>0
, for all but maybe finitely many of the complementary star
\Delta
that have an edge of length at least
\epsilon
, the two long edges of
\Delta
are adjacent.

.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a pA-foliation.

Proof.
We reason by contradiction.

4.5

Few common ends

The end pre-lamination of a foliation satisfies a last property.
Let

be a pre-lamination. Given

is either empty or ordered as a sub-interval of

(that is finite, ordered as
\mathbb{N}
,
-\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
). Here the set

is naturally ordered by its endpoint other than

in
S^{1}\setminus\{\theta\}
.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let

be a pA-foliation of
\Int(D^{2})
. Then

Lemma
is stated and proved in
[

, Lemma 4.6]
] for non-singular foliations, and the proof holds in the pA-setting with no changes. We reproduce it here for completeness.

Proof.
Up to conjugation, we may assume that
\partial D^{2}
is the boundary at infinity of
\mathcal{F}
.
Consider two leaves (or face of singular leaves)

of
\mathcal{F}
sharing a same end

. It is enough to prove that only finitely many of them are between

and

For that, one considers a simple path

where the segments

are transverse to
\mathcal{F}
and the

are leaf-arcs (possibly

and

). We denote by

the sub-disc of

bounded by

and

and

If a regular leaf of

would be uncountable. Thus a ray of regular leaf entering

.

Any element in

and should have both ends at

We will prove by induction on

and

Assume now

Assume that the assertion holds up to

. Let

and

and consider the ray

(density condition)

2.

5.4

Ordering the branchings

We equip branchings and cyclic branchings of
\Leaf^{*}(L)
with orders.

Let
\Delta

\nu_{\Delta}

\preceq^{\circlearrowleft}_{\nu_{\Delta}}

Let now

\Leaf^{*}(L)
on a branching

. Let

on the leaves in

-leaves that bound

are not order that way. Take a boundary component

of

a singular planar structure, which we say

6

Universal foliations and planar structures

Kaplan prove that for any planar structure, there is a foliation on the plane that induces that planar structure. We need to prove the same result for singular planar structure. For that, we develop the notion of universal (singular or not) foliation, that contains all other foliations of the plane. We reprove Kaplan’s result in the process.

6.1

Universal foliations

We say that a co-oriented foliation
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
a universal foliation
if, given any co-oriented foliation
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

•

\mathcal{G}=\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{F})

•

\mathcal{F}

•

\psi

\mathcal{G}

In other words,
\mathcal{G}
is conjugated to the restriction of
\mathcal{F}
to a connected simply connected,
\mathcal{F}
-saturated region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

We define now a foliation which we prove universal later.
Let

be the standard dyadic Cantor set.
We define a set

as the union

K=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\\
|n|\geq q\end{subarray}}\left(C+2n,\tfrac{p}{q}\right).

It can be described as: for all
\tfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
, the intersection of
K
and of the line

is the union of
\mathbb{Z}
-copies of the Cantor set

, all shifted by a multiple of 2, with a hole centered at

of size

.
The condition

is chosen so that the following holds.

Lemma 6.1
.

is closed inside
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.
Let

be a sequence in

7.2

Completing pre-laminations

We answer the following question: given a pre-lamination
L_{1}
on the circle, is there a pre-lamination

that satisfies the condition of ?THM?
, meaning it is the end pre-lamination of a foliation

, and so that

corresponds to a dense subset of leaves of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
. Recall that the two long boundary components of a complementary region with diameter larger than some

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
L_{1}
be a pre-lamination of
S^{1}
. Then there exists a
\mathfrak{P}
-pre-lamination

, so that the image of
L_{1}
into
\Leaf^{*}(L_{2})
is dense if and only if

satisfies the following properties:

•

is a dense pre-lamination,

•

any complementary region of
L_{1}
has at least one boundary component which is not a leaf of
L_{1}
,

•

L_{1}
has no bad accumulation,

•

L_{1}
has few common ends.

Next example explains why we use here of the ”few common ends” condition instead of the ”at most countably many leaves shares the same endpoint”.

Example 7.4
.

Take
L=\{(1,\theta=e^{2q\pi})\in S^{1}\times S^{1},q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap]0,2\pi[\}
. Then
L
is a dense pre-lamination, the boundary of each complementary component consists in two accumulated geodesics,
L
has no bad accumulations, and the set of leaves having a common end is countable as
L
is countable. However,
L
has not few ends, and
L
is not contained in a
\mathfrak{P}
-pre-lamination.

Proof of the direct implication..
We build
L_{2}
by adding leaves to
L_{1}
. First add to
L_{2}
all the geodesics that are complementary regions with empty interior of
L_{1}
. Then for any complementary region
\Delta
of
L_{1}
with non-empty interior, add to
L_{2}
all but one the boundary component of
\Delta
. We can choose the excluded boundary component to be one of the two long boundary components, expect for at finitely many possible
\Delta
. Then
L_{2}
is a dense pre-lamination. It has no accumulation since the complementary regions of
L_{2}
are exactly the complementary regions of
L_{1}
with non-empty interior, with maybe some boundary components removed. Additionally,
L_{2}
has no star component. Note that by construction, all the leaves in
L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
are accumulated by leaves in
L_{1}
.

We prove that
L_{2}
has few common ends, which implies that it is a
\mathfrak{P}
-pre-lamination with no star. Take
\theta\in S^{1}
and recall that
E_{L_{i}}(\theta)
is the set of leaves of
L_{i}
that ends on
\theta
.
Take
l^{-},l^{+}
two distinct leaves of
L_{2}
that have a common endpoint

.

We prove by contradiction that there are at most finitely many leaves of
L_{2}
that separates the
l^{-}
and
l^{+}
. So let us assume that it is not the case.
Any leaf

that separates
l^{-}
and
l^{+}
is either a leaf of
L_{1}
, or accumulated by leaves of
L_{1}
. In the second case, it is accumulated by leave of
L_{1}
that separates
l^{-}
and
l^{+}
, so they also are in
E_{L_{1}}(\theta)
. It follows that
E_{L_{1}}(\theta)
is not finite.

Note that
E_{L_{1}}(\theta)
is ordered as an interval of
\mathbb{Z}
, and it is not finite by assumption. Let us then denote by

these leaves, with
i
in
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, so that

is increasing. The sequence

is monotonous and bounded by either

or

, so it converges when

, and its length is bounded below away from zero. Note that

bound a common complement region

goes to

, the area of

goes to zero, so the length of all its boundary components outside

and

go to zero. Hence, for

and

are the two long edges of

. It follows that

is a bad accumulation of

, which contradicts the hypothesis. Therefore, there are at most finitely many leaves between

and

, so

has few common ends.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

In the previous proof, we only add to
L_{2}
some accumulated boundary of complementary regions. This is not the only possibility. For instance if a complementary region

has four boundary components, two of which are not leaves of
L_{1}
, we can add one diagonal of
P
that separates the two accumulated boundary components of

. When

has more boundary components, wilder completion are possible.

This remark makes the converse trickier to prove that one may expect.

Proof of the converse.
Assume that such a pre-lamination
L_{2}
exists. Then
L_{1}
has few common ends since it is a subset of
L_{2}
.

Assume (arguing by contradiction) that there is a complementary region
\Delta
of
L_{1}
, with non-empty interior and whose boundary geodesic are all leaves of
L_{1}
. Since
L_{2}
satisfies
\mathfrak{P}
with no stars,
\Delta
is not a complementary region of
L_{2}
. So there exists a leaf

of

which lies in the interior of

. Let
\mathcal{I}
be an open

-interval containing

. By definition of open

-intervals,
\mathcal{I}
contains another leaf

of
L_{2}
contained in

. Then the

-interval

inside

has non-empty interior and is disjoint from the image of

. It contradicts the density assumption on the leaves of
L_{1}
. Thus, all complementary region of

with non-empty interior have at least one accumulated boundary.

We now prove that

has no bad accumulation. Assume (arguing by contradiction) that there exist

and a sequence of
\epsilon

\Delta_{n}
of

whose two long boundary components are leaves of

, that accumulates on a geodesic

-interval

contains all the leaves close enough to

contains the two large boundary components of

for all large enough

inside a common region

(

. And similarly to above, the interval of leaves of

between them does not contain any leaf of

∎

Singular planar structures and

-foliations

In this section we consider
pA
-foliations. Next remark motivates our hypothesis of non-existence of connection between singular points.

8.1

The singular planar structure of a
pA
-foliation

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
pA
-foliation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\Sigma
its singular set. We denote by
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
the set of leaves of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
, equipped with the quotient topology. Given an open curve
\sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
transverse to
\mathcal{F}
, every leaf of
\mathcal{F}
cuts
\sigma
in at most
1
point (see Lemma
2.2
). It yields an embedding from
\sigma
to
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
. As the images of such curves cover the whole space
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
, this proves:

Lemma 8.1
.

\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
is a connected non-Hausdorff
1
-dimensional manifold.

We regroup simple facts about
pA
-foliations:

1.

If two leaves
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
of
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
are non-separated, then there are segments

\sigma_{i}\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
,
i=1,2
transverse to
\mathcal{F}
, so that
\sigma_{i}(0)\in\ell_{i}
, and so that
\sigma_{1}(t)
and
\sigma_{2}(t)
lie on the same leaf for
t\in[-1,0[
, and no leaf cuts both
\sigma_{1}(]0,1])
and
\sigma_{2}(]0,1])
. This implies that
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
is a tame
1
-dimensional manifold (see Definition
3.2
).

2.

Every non-singular leaf
l
of
\mathcal{F}
cuts
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
in two connected components. Thus
l
cuts
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
in two connected components.

3.

Let
\ell_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,k
be the separatrixes of a singular point. Then
\ell_{i},\ell_{i+1}
are on a same branching
\mu_{i}
, and
\mu_{i-1}\neq\mu_{i}
. Furthermore,
\ell_{i-1}\cup\ell_{i+1}
disconnects
\ell_{i}
from the other separatrixes. One deduces that
\ell_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,k
form a cyclic branching.

4.

As a leaf in a cyclic branching does not disconnect
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
, a leaf that is not a separatrix cannot belong to a cyclic branching.

Every branching
\mu
of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
is ordered as follows: let
R
be the (unique) connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(\mu\cup\Sigma)
that contains a sequence of leaves that converges on all the leaves of
\mu
. Denote by
l
a (non-separatrix) leaf in
R
and
R^{\prime}
the components of
R\setminus l
that is adjacent to
\mu
. For two leaves
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mu
, we set
f_{1}\preceq f_{2}
if
(l,f_{1},f_{2})
is clockwise cyclically ordered as boundary components of
R^{\prime}
(or if
f_{1}=f_{2})
. It yields a total order
\preceq_{\mu}
on
\mu
, independent of the choice of the leaf

, and called the

.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
pA
-foliation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The cyclic branchings of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
are the sets of separatrixes of the singularities, ordered clockwise or anti-clockwise.

In the proof we will use the following observation.

Lemma 8.3
.

For any cyclic branching
\nu=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}
and any compact subset
K\subset\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
disjoint from
\nu
, there exists a continuous loop
\gamma\subset\Leaf(\mathcal{F})\setminus K
that intersects each point in
\nu
exactly once, and transversely.

Proof.
To build
\gamma
, simply concatenate small enough segments around the points in
\nu
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
.
We take
\nu=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}
a cyclic branching of
\mathcal{L}
. By definition, no leaf
x_{i}
disconnects
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
, so they all are separatrixes of
\mathcal{F}
.

Denote by
p\in\Sigma
the based point of the separatrix
x_{i}
, and let
y
be a separatrix of
\mathcal{F}
based on
p
. If
y
doesn’t belong to
\nu
, Lemma
8.3
provides a continuous loop
\gamma
that intersects every point in
\nu
exactly once and transversely, and that avoids

.
But then
\gamma\setminus\{x_{i}\}
connects the two distinct connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(x_{i}\cup y\cup\{p\})
. This contradiction implies that all separatrixes based at
p
are contains in
\nu
.

Since
x_{i}
is separated from all

,
j\neq i
, excepts for
x_{i-1}
and
x_{i+1}
, these two separatrixes are adjacent to
x_{i}
. And by induction on

,
\nu
is exactly the set of separatrixes of
p
in clockwise or anti-clockwise order.
∎

As a
pA
-foliation does not admit saddle connections, it follows that:

Corollary 8.4
.

The cyclic branching of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
are pairwise disjoint.

Observe that since two adjacent separatrixes, at a common singularity
p
, are not separable from one side, they are ordered for the left/right order on the branching that contains them.
For any cyclic branching
\nu
of
\mathcal{F}
, the anti-clockwise cyclic ordering of its separatrixes is compatible with the left/right order on branchings of
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
pA
-foliation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The set
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
equipped with the left-right order on branching and the anti-clockwise cyclical order on cyclic branching is a singular planar structure.

Proof.
It follows from ?THM?
that the cyclic branching in
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
are disjoint and of order at least 3. Any non-separatrix leaf disconnects
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
in two connected components. So it also disconnects
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
in two connected components. Similarly, every cyclic branching
\nu
separates
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
into
|\nu|
connected components, and therefore
\Leaf(\mathcal{F})
, in
|\nu|
connected components.
∎

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
pA
-foliation, and

be a continuous map that is locally injective. Then

is injective.

Proof.
A classical argument implies that a locally injective map

can be realized as a path
\widetilde{f}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Sigma
topologically transverse to

. Now the lemma follows from Lemma

.

, so that
f(\ell_{1})=\ell_{2}
. We choose lifts
\widetilde{\ell}_{1},\widetilde{\ell}_{2}
of
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
on
\Leaf(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1})
and
\Leaf(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2})
, respectively. Now the map
f
lifts uniquely on the universal cover as a map

\widetilde{f}\colon\Leaf(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1})\to\Leaf(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2})
so that
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{\ell}_{1})=\widetilde{\ell}_{2}
and this map is a homeomorphism which is increasing for the order, that is, is an equivalence of the planar structures of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
. According to Kaplan theorem, there is a homeomorphism
\widetilde{\Phi}\colon P_{1}\to P_{2}
with
\Phi(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
and so that
\Phi
induces
\widetilde{f}
on the planar structures.

For every singular point
p
of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
one chooses a non-origin separatrix
\ell_{p}
, and thus
f(\ell_{p})
is a non-origin separatrix for
f(p)
. Notice that:

\Delta_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{p\in\Sing(\mathcal{F}_{1})}\ell_{p}

is connected, and simply connected, that is, is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\widetilde{\Delta}_{1}
be the lift of
\Delta_{1}
on
\mathcal{P}_{1}
that contains
\mathbf{x}_{1}
.
Note that its boundary is the union of two lifts of each of the leaves
\ell_{p}
.

Let
\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}=\Phi(\widetilde{\Delta}_{1})
be the corresponding set on
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
It is a connected, simply connected, and its contains exactly one lift of each leaf of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
excepts for the leaves
f(\ell_{p})
. Thus,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}
projects down on the set
\Delta_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{p}f(\ell_{p})
.

The restriction
\widetilde{\Delta}_{1}\xrightarrow{\Phi}\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}
induces a homeomorphism
h_{0}\colon\Delta_{1}\to\Delta_{2}
that conjugates the foliations
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. It has no reason to extend continuously on the whole set
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To extend it, we first deform
h_{0}
in neighborhoods of the leaves
f(\ell_{p})
.

Denote by
\ell^{+}_{p},\ell^{-}_{p}
the lifts of
\ell_{p}
that bound
\widetilde{\Delta}_{1}
. Since they lie above the same separatrix on the base space, there is an element
\gamma_{p}
in the fundamental domain of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F}_{1})
that satisfies
\gamma_{p}\cdot\ell^{+}_{p}=\ell^{-}_{p}
. The map
f
induces an isomorphism
f^{*}
between the fundamental groups of
\Leaf(\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
\Leaf(\mathcal{F}_{2})
. So we have
f^{*}(\gamma_{p})\cdot\Phi(\ell^{+}_{p})=\Phi(\ell^{-}_{p})
.
We can extend continuously
h_{0}
to the leaf
\ell_{p}
if and only if

\Phi\circ\gamma_{p}
coincides with
f^{*}(\gamma_{p})\circ\Phi
on
\ell^{+}_{p}
. These two maps are isotopic through homeomorphism from
\ell^{+}_{p}
to
f(\ell^{-}_{p})
.
Corollary
asserts that the opposite separatrixes
\ell_{p}
, for all singular point
p
, admits disjoint neighborhoods
U_{p}
. Let
\widetilde{U}_{p}
be the preimage of
U_{p}
inside
\widetilde{\Delta}_{1}
. Then one can build a homeomorphism
g_{p}
of
\widetilde{U}_{p}
which is equal to the identity on
\partial\widetilde{U}_{p}\setminus\ell^{+}
, which preserves the leaves of the foliations, and so that
\Phi\circ\gamma_{p}\circ g_{p}
coincides with
f^{*}(\gamma_{p})\circ\Phi\circ g_{p}
on
\ell^{+}_{p}
.

We define
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
to be equal to the projection of
\Phi\circ g_{p}
inside
U_{p}
, for all singularity
p
, to be equal to
h_{0}
on
\Delta_{1}\setminus\cup_{p}U_{p}
, and that sends the singularity of
\ell_{p}
to the singularity of
f(\ell_{p})
. From the discussion above, it is well-defined. It is a homeomorphim which conjugates
\mathcal{F}_{1}
to
\mathcal{F}^{2}
, and it induces
f
on the leaf spaces.

Lemma 8.19

A subset

Proof.
We denote by

that contains a separatrix of

is the set of leaves of

First assume that

Let
