Artificial intelligence in preclinical nonhuman primate xenotransplantation: bridging the gap from data complexity to clinical precision
Authors: Seung Jae Jason Kim, Sangil Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nonhuman primate (NHP) preclinical models remain an indispensable gateway for translating xenotransplantation into human clinical trials. These models generate high-dimensional immunological, physiological, behavioral, and histopathological datasets that are difficult to interpret with conventional analytical approaches and that limit the precision of translational inference. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) may help address this complexity: computer vision can support continuous noninvasive behavioral phenotyping and pain assessment; anomaly detection algorithms can extract early warning signals from biosignal streams; multiomics integration can identify xenograft-specific biomarker signatures; digital twin frameworks may enable hypothesis-generating simulations of prospective human recipient responses; and explainable AI (XAI) can make model outputs more transparent and regulatory defensible. This review synthesizes current evidence across these domains, highlights methodological limitations, and proposes a research agenda through which AI-augmented NHP experimentation can become a bridge from preclinical data complexity to clinical precision in xenotransplantation. The framework also supports the ethical mandate of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), aligning scientific rigor with animal welfare imperatives.

INTRODUCTION

The Complexity Crisis in Nonhuman Primate Research

Organ shortage remains one of the defining crises of modern transplantation medicine. Over 100,000 patients in the United States and hundreds of thousands globally await life-saving organ transplants, yet the supply of deceased-donor allografts satisfies fewer than one in four waiting patients [1]. Xenotransplantation, transplantation of genetically engineered porcine organs into humans, represents the most advanced strategy to resolve this disparity, offering a theoretically inexhaustible organ supply. The modern era of xenotransplantation has been defined by revolutionary advances in CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, enabling the creation of multigene-edited (GE) pigs that overcome the principal immunological barriers of hyperacute and early antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) [2,3].

These scientific advances have now translated into early clinical reality. In 2022, Griffith and colleagues [3] at the University of Maryland performed the first genetically modified pig-to-human cardiac xenotransplantation under U.S. Food and Drug Administration expanded access, with the recipient surviving 60 days. Kawai et al. [2] subsequently reported genetically engineered porcine kidney transplantation into a living human recipient with end-stage kidney disease, achieving immediate xenograft function and dialysis independence before the patient died from sudden cardiac causes on day 52. These milestones confirmed technical feasibility but simultaneously exposed the magnitude of the remaining translational gap: nonhuman primate (NHP) preclinical results, however impressive, do not yet reliably predict human clinical outcomes.

NHPs (predominantly baboons [Papio spp.] for cardiac models and cynomolgus macaques [Macaca fascicularis] or rhesus macaques [Macaca mulatta] for renal models) remain the gold-standard preclinical recipients because they carry preformed anti-pig xenoreactive antibodies and mount immunological responses that are more closely analogous to humans than those of nonprimate models [4–7]. Singh et al. [5] demonstrated life-supporting cardiac xenograft survival of up to 225 days (mean, 128±36 days) in baboons using 10-GE donors with nonischemic continuous perfusion preservation, providing important supportive evidence for subsequent clinical translation.

Yet NHP research carries profound limitations. Ethical scrutiny and high costs restrict cohort sizes, typically to fewer than 5 to 10 animals per experimental arm, severely constraining statistical power [8]. The datasets generated (encompassing single-cell transcriptomics, proteomics, telemetric biosignals, serial biopsy histopathology, and immunosuppressive pharmacokinetics) are simultaneously high-dimensional, heterogeneous, longitudinally irregular, and frequently incomplete. Conventional statistical models struggle to capture the nonlinear, high-order immunological interactions that govern graft fate. Prior syntheses of NHP renal xenotransplantation experiments have also highlighted an important paradox: NHP xenograft survival and immune injury patterns do not map cleanly onto either clinical allotransplantation benchmarks or early human xenotransplantation experience, underscoring fundamental limitations in interspecies translational inference [7].

Artificial intelligence (AI) offers a transformative solution to this complexity crisis. By extracting hidden patterns from high-dimensional, multimodal datasets at scales that exceed human analytical capacity, AI can enhance the predictive precision of NHP research, objectively monitor animal welfare in real time, integrate multiomics layers into coherent immunological signatures, and simulate prospective human recipient responses through digital twin modeling. This review comprehensively examines these applications across the preclinical xenotransplantation pipeline (Fig. 1), evaluates current evidence and methodological limitations, and delineates the research agenda that will transform NHP preclinical data complexity into clinical precision medicine. HISTORICAL CONTEXT AND THE TRANSLATIONAL CHALLENGE

The evolution of xenotransplantation from a scientific concept to a clinical reality spans more than 6 decades. Following the early NHP-to-human transplantation attempts of the 1960s, the modern field shifted decisively toward porcine donors in the 1990s, driven by ethical, physiological, and infectious safety considerations [4]. The advent of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing accelerated the pace of genetic modification, enabling the creation of donors carrying 69 genomic edits that simultaneously eliminate xenoantigens through disruption of GGTA1, CMAH, B4GALNT2; inactivate porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERVs); and overexpress human protective transgenes, including CD46, CD55, THBD, PROCR, CD47, TNFAIP3, and HMOX1 [6]. The resulting GE pigs represent the culmination of decades of translational iteration guided primarily by NHP preclinical studies.

The critical importance of the NHP model was reinforced by two landmark multiomics studies. Loupy et al. [9] performed digital spatial transcriptomics and whole-transcriptome profiling of GE pig kidney xenografts transplanted into human decedents, identifying early patterns of AMR characterized by monocyte/macrophage activation, natural killer (NK) cell infiltration, and endothelial activation in the glomeruli, biomarker signatures invisible to standard histology. Schmauch et al. [10] applied integrative multiomics profiling to two human decedents receiving 10-GE pig heart xenografts, revealing heterogeneous immune and metabolic responses between recipients and identifying NF-κB activation as a central driver of cardiac xenograft failure. These studies established that molecular monitoring technologies, directly paralleling AI-amenable data streams, are essential to characterize xenograft rejection mechanisms and guide the next generation of clinical trials.

A particularly informative dataset was reported by Schmauch et al. [11] in Nature, who performed large-scale multiomics profiling including spatial transcriptomics (Xenium 5,100-gene panel), single-nucleus RNA sequencing, and flow cytometry over a 61-day pig-to-human decedent kidney xenotransplantation. Three temporally distinct immune rejection waves were identified at postoperative day (POD) 21 (innate), POD 33 (macrophage/AMR), and POD 45–49 (T cell/cell-mediated rejection). This longitudinal granularity (precisely the type of high-dimensional, time-resolved dataset that AI is best positioned to analyze) exemplifies the data complexity challenge that motivates the present review.

Fig. 2 summarizes the parallel evolution of xenotransplantation and artificial intelligence-enabled transplantation research [12–18]. Recent pig-to-human kidney xenotransplantation studies have demonstrated that xenograft injury is highly dynamic and cellularly heterogeneous, with single-cell and spatial analyses revealing early activation of innate immune, cytotoxic lymphocyte, macrophage, and endothelial pathways that are not fully captured by conventional histology alone [19]. The human decedent model has further emerged as a critical translational platform for de-risking first-in-human xenotransplantation by generating human-relevant immunological and physiological data under controlled experimental conditions [20]. In parallel, the xenotransplantation literature has rapidly expanded, with recent updates highlighting accelerating advances in donor genetic engineering, immunosuppressive strategies, organ preservation, and clinical translation [21].

At the same time, artificial intelligence has become increasingly relevant to transplantation, as emphasized by recent reviews and consensus discussions on emerging technologies, digital twins, and artificial intelligence-driven decision support [22–24]. In kidney allotransplantation, dynamic artificial intelligence models have already shown that deeply phenotyped longitudinal data can improve renal survival prediction, while machine learning and interpretable modeling approaches have demonstrated feasibility for graft and patient outcome prediction [12–14]. Together, these converging milestones support the central rationale of this review: the next phase of xenotransplantation will require integration of molecular, histological, physiological, and clinical data through artificial intelligence-enabled analytical frameworks to improve translational inference from preclinical models to human clinical trials.

REAL-TIME MONITORING AND BEHAVIORAL PHENOTYPING

Artificial Intelligence Computer Vision for Objective Clinical Scoring

Postoperative welfare assessment in NHP xenotransplantation relies on trained observers conducting periodic clinical examinations, generating subjective scores for pain, lethargy, and behavioral abnormalities that serve as proxy endpoints for graft function and systemic rejection. This approach is constrained by inter-rater variability, circadian blind spots, and the inherent subjectivity of species-specific behavioral interpretation. Computer vision-based behavioral analysis offers a paradigm shift toward continuous, objective, 24-hour surveillance.

Vogg et al. [25] demonstrated that state-of-the-art multi-animal tracking and action recognition frameworks can achieve reliable individualized behavioral phenotyping in complex primate settings, encompassing detection, pose estimation, individual re-identification, and fine-grained action classification. Three widely validated tools (DeepLabCut [15], SLEAP [16], and Multi-animal DeepLabCut [17]) provide markerless skeletal pose estimation that quantifies locomotion, postural changes, and social interaction patterns from standard video input, requiring no physical contact with the animal.

Broomé et al. [26] comprehensively surveyed computer vision approaches to automated animal pain recognition, encompassing facial action unit analysis, body posture classification, and spatiotemporal video modeling. Their analysis specifically addressed automated adaptation of the Macaque Facial Action Coding System (MacFACS) for NHPs, identifying convolutional neural network (CNN) and ConvLSTM architectures as best-performing for pain intensity classification. In a xenotransplantation context, such systems could flag signs of early systemic rejection (lethargy, hunched posture, reduced grooming, facial grimacing) hours before biochemical confirmation, enabling timely biopsy or pharmacological intervention while simultaneously reducing the frequency of invasive physical examinations.

A 2024 Frontiers in Veterinary Science narrative review catalogued CNN-based and geometric landmark AI approaches to automated pain recognition across veterinary species, reinforcing that species-specific training datasets remain a critical bottleneck [27]. For NHP xenotransplantation programs, this implies a clear priority: prospectively annotated behavioral video datasets from NHP xenotransplantation experiments, curated against expert MacFACS scoring and paired with contemporaneous immunological and histopathological rejection data, constitute a foundational resource investment that would unlock transformative AI welfare monitoring capabilities.

Automated Biosignal Anomaly Detection

Telemetric monitoring implants in NHP xenotransplantation subjects provide continuous streams of cardiac rhythm, blood pressure, body temperature, and respiratory rate data. These physiological time series may encode early signatures of rejection episodes: complement-mediated cardiovascular collapse in hyperacute rejection, progressive hemodynamic deterioration in acute humoral rejection, and subtle chronic dysfunction characteristic of chronic active AMR.

Shibanoki et al. [28] proposed a dual-modality monitoring system combining behavioral information extracted from video with internal physiological state information (heart rate, respiration), integrated via a neural network anomaly detection framework. This architecture demonstrated superior specificity for welfare state discrimination compared to single-modality approaches. Noninvasive remote photoplethysmography for heart rate estimation in chimpanzees has been validated by Wang et al. [29], enabling camera-based physiological monitoring entirely without contact, a critical refinement advantage in postoperative NHP subjects. Machine learning anomaly detection algorithms, including isolation forests, variational autoencoders, and transformer-based time-series models, could provide personalized, real-time alerts; however, a fixed preoperative physiologic baseline is unlikely to remain valid after transplantation. Continuous sedation or anesthesia, vasoactive or immunosuppressive regimens, mechanical ventilation, and evolving graft function can substantially alter physiological signals. Because these fluctuations can exceed baseline differences between subjects, anomaly-detection models should utilize phase- and treatment-aware dynamic baselines. Recalibrating these baselines after transplantation and major changes in medication or organ-support therapy is essential to accurately distinguish true immunological graft injury from pharmacologic artifacts and the high intra-individual physiological variability characteristic of the posttransplant period.

MULTIOMICS INTEGRATION FOR PRECISION IMMUNOLOGY

Deciphering High-Dimensional Xenotransplantation Immunology

The immunological barrier to xenotransplantation is multilayered and temporally dynamic: preformed xenoreactive antibodies, complement activation, NK cell cytotoxicity, adaptive T cell and B cell responses, and endothelial activation each contribute to graft injury in complex, interdependent cascades [21]. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies from NHP and human decedent xenotransplantation models have delineated these pathways with unprecedented precision, generating datasets of a dimensionality that demands AI-powered analytical frameworks (Fig. 3).

Wu et al. [30] applied high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) to peripheral blood transcriptomics from kidney transplant acute rejection recipients, constructing a noninvasive ML diagnostic model based on hub gene modules that distinguished acute rejection from stable graft function. This methodological approach is directly applicable to NHP xenotransplantation, where repeated tissue biopsy is procedurally burdensome and peripheral blood biomarkers would enable noninvasive real-time rejection monitoring.

Tripathy et al. [31] introduced the graph neural network-derived representation alignment and integration (GNNRAI) framework, an explainable graph neural network architecture that integrates transcriptomic and proteomic data with biological knowledge graphs for biomarker identification in small-sample settings. The GNNRAI approach specifically addresses a critical limitation of NHP xenotransplantation research: the need to derive robust, biologically meaningful conclusions from cohorts of fewer than 20 animals. By leveraging biological prior knowledge encoded in protein interaction networks and pathway databases, GNNRAI can partially compensate for statistical under-powering through structural regularization, enabling the discovery of candidate xenograft-specific multiomics biomarker signatures that would remain difficult to detect with conventional analysis.

Pan et al. [19] characterized cellular dynamics in pig-to-human kidney xenotransplantation via single-cell RNA-sequencing, demonstrating that cytotoxic T lymphocyte and NK cell activation are critical early drivers of xenograft injury, findings that, when integrated with contemporaneous NHP data, define specific immunological targets amenable to ML-guided monitoring in preclinical studies. Spooner et al. [32] benchmarked ensemble ML algorithms for multiclass, multiomics integration in clinical outcome prediction, identifying gradient boosting and random forest ensembles as consistently high-performing across diverse dataset configurations, a directly applicable methodological recommendation for NHP xenotransplantation multiomics pipelines.

Machine Learning for Graft Survival Prediction

The translation of multiomics complexity into actionable graft survival predictions requires ML models capable of handling high-dimensional, longitudinal, incomplete, and nonlinearly interacting data. In allotransplantation, Raynaud et al. [12] demonstrated that an AI model trained on deeply phenotyped kidney transplant recipients from 18 international centers achieved dynamic survival predictions that continuously improved as longitudinal data accrued, with superior performance to traditional regression-based risk scores across external validation cohorts. Naqvi et al. [13] applied ML classification with deep autoencoder-based dimensionality reduction to 50,000 kidney transplant records, achieving areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.82 to 0.89 across three temporal prediction horizons.

Salaün et al. [14] developed interpretable ML models for simultaneous prediction of graft and patient outcomes after kidney transplantation, demonstrating that interpretable gradient boosting architectures with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) value attribution achieved comparable predictive performance to black-box deep neural networks while preserving clinical interpretability. In NHP xenotransplantation, where cohort sizes preclude conventional large-scale model training, transfer learning from allotransplantation ML models (fine-tuned on NHP xenotransplantation-specific datasets incorporating donor gene-edit profiles, xenoreactive antibody titers, and immunosuppressive regimen variables) represents the most pragmatic near-term pathway to graft survival prediction models with genuine translational relevance.

BRIDGING THE TRANSLATIONAL GAP: DIGITAL TWINS AND IMMUNOSUPPRESSION OPTIMIZATION

Digital Twin Concepts in Xenotransplantation

The digital twin (DT), a continuously updating computational patient-in-silico model calibrated on real-world data, has emerged as a central enabling technology for precision medicine [18,33–35]. Laubenbacher et al. [33] delineated the field of mechanistic immune digital twins in immunology, demonstrating that multiscale ordinary differential equation-based computational models calibrated on individual patient immunological data can simulate responses to therapeutic perturbations with biological plausibility. Sadée et al. [18] articulated five essential components of the medical digital twin (patient representation, data connection, patient-in-silico model, interface, and twin synchronization), providing a regulatory and technical roadmap for clinical adoption. The potential application to xenotransplantation is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Niarakis et al. [36] reviewed immune digital twin development for complex human pathologies, outlining critical prerequisites for clinical implementation: multiscale mechanistic modeling, longitudinal multiomics calibration data, and prospective validation against independent cohorts. In the xenotransplantation context, a mechanistic immune DT calibrated on NHP xenotransplantation longitudinal multiomics data could in principle generate patient-in-silico simulations that predict how a prospective human recipient (with a specific xenoreactive antibody profile, human leukocyte antigen sensitization status, and comorbidity burden) would respond to a defined GE porcine organ under a specific immunosuppressive regimen. This translational simulation capability directly addresses the central challenge identified in the clinical xenotransplantation literature: the unpredictability of human immune responses from NHP preclinical data [4,20].

Bordukova et al. [37] reviewed the role of generative AI in empowering digital twins in drug discovery and clinical trials, identifying generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders as tools for simulating synthetic trial populations from limited real-world data. Applied to NHP xenotransplantation, this approach could support sensitivity analyses, cohort-enrichment strategies, and power simulations without increasing the number of animal experiments; however, synthetic subjects should be used for hypothesis generation and design optimization, not as substitutes for biological validation. The Transplant AI Symposium provided an early proof-of-concept for DT-guided transplant decision support, reporting long short-term memory ensemble and digital twin simulation approaches for posttransplant outcome prediction in clinical lung transplantation [23].

Artificial Intelligence-Optimized Immunosuppressive Protocol Design

Immunosuppressive regimen design represents perhaps the most immediately actionable AI application in NHP xenotransplantation. Current regimens, combining costimulation blockade (anti-CD40 or anti-CD154 monoclonal antibodies), calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites, and complement inhibitors, have been developed through empirical iteration spanning decades, constrained by NHP species-specific pharmacodynamic differences [21,38]. The International Xenotransplantation Association position paper on kidney xenotransplantation specifically identified immunosuppressive regimen optimization as a priority research area requiring systematic preclinical investigation [38].

Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning algorithms are well-suited to multidimensional immunosuppressive protocol optimization. By constructing pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic models from NHP drug concentration-biomarker-outcome data, these algorithms can navigate the complex parameter space of drug combinations, timing, and dosing to identify regimens that minimize rejection risk while avoiding immunosuppression-related toxicity. The Transplant AI Symposium demonstrated SHAP-identified predictors of long-term graft outcome (including time since transplantation, donor-recipient age, disease etiology, and standard laboratory values) that could serve as the feature basis for reinforcement learning reward functions in immunosuppression optimization models [23]. He et al. [39] provided a 30-year bibliometric analysis of AI in kidney transplantation, confirming immunosuppression personalization as the fastest-growing application domain in the field.

ETHICAL AND PRACTICAL CONSIDERATIONS

The 3Rs Framework and Artificial Intelligence Integration

Russell and Burch's 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement, first articulated in 1959) provide the foundational ethical framework for all animal research [40]. Recent regulatory developments and nonanimal methodology literature have increasingly encouraged AI-based and in silico approaches as components of biomedical research packages [41]. AI supports all 3Rs dimensions in NHP xenotransplantation: Reduction through power calculations informed by ML simulation, enabling smaller cohorts without compromising statistical validity; Refinement through AI-driven continuous welfare monitoring with automated humane endpoint detection; and longer-term Replacement through digital twin models that may enable some experimental questions to be explored in silico before animal studies are undertaken.

Grimm et al. [42] provided a comprehensive framework for advancing the 3Rs through innovation, implementation, ethics, and societal engagement, emphasizing that emerging technologies including AI constitute priority 3Rs tools. Fröhlich and Loizou [43] specifically highlighted that in silico modeling approaches, including physiologically based kinetic models enhanced by ML, are increasingly accepted by regulatory authorities as components of 3Rs-compliant preclinical packages. Gangwal and Lavecchia [44] extended this framework to digital twins and organ-on-chip technologies, arguing that AI-augmented preclinical platforms may become increasingly important elements of regulatory submissions once biological fidelity, reproducibility, and validation standards are established.

In the xenotransplantation domain, where NHP experiments carry particularly high ethical visibility and public scrutiny, demonstrating robust 3Rs compliance through AI integration will be essential for regulatory and societal acceptance of the clinical translation pathway. Critser and Locke [40] argued that the emergence of AI and high-throughput in vitro systems has created ethical pressure to revise the 3Rs framework toward stronger replacement obligations, reinforcing the importance of proactively integrating AI into NHP research program design.

Explainability, Regulatory Compliance, and Federated Learning

The opacity of high-performance deep learning models constitutes a persistent barrier to clinical and regulatory adoption. In transplantation, Assis de Souza et al. [45] conducted a systematic review of explainable ML in kidney transplantation, concluding that model-agnostic interpretability techniques (SHAP and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations) are currently the most pragmatic pathway to explainable AI (XAI) compliance in transplant clinical decision support. Abbas et al. [46] synthesized studies of XAI in clinical decision support systems, identifying visualization-based methods (gradient-weighted class activation mapping and attention maps) and model-agnostic techniques as dominant approaches, while critically noting gaps in real-world evaluation of explanation fidelity and clinician trust calibration.

In NHP xenotransplantation, XAI is required at multiple levels: rejection prediction models must produce biologically interpretable feature attributions; behavioral monitoring systems must generate actionable, clinician-readable welfare alerts; and pharmacokinetic optimization algorithms must provide transparent dosing rationales. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (2024) classifies healthcare AI as high-risk and mandates transparency, risk management, and human oversight requirements [47] that must be proactively addressed in the design of AI tools intended for xenotransplantation clinical trial and Investigational New Drug (IND) applications.

Federated learning, which allows ML models to be trained across multiple institutional datasets without centralizing raw animal research data, offers a pathway to aggregating NHP xenotransplantation datasets at scales sufficient for generalizable model training while respecting data governance and institutional proprietary constraints [24]. The AI and Machine Learning in Transplantation review by Vivek and Papalois [24] identified federated learning as a critical translational infrastructure requirement alongside XAI and data governance frameworks, specifically noting its applicability to small-dataset transplant domains. Yoo et al. [48] demonstrated that AI-based histopathological classification of kidney allografts achieved diagnostic performance competitive with international expert panels, providing a directly translatable proof of concept for federated NHP xenotransplantation digital pathology platforms.

CURRENT LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH PRIORITIES

Despite compelling theoretical and early empirical support for AI integration in NHP xenotransplantation, several critical challenges require explicit acknowledgment. First, the training data problem: all AI models are bounded by the quality and representativeness of their training data. NHP xenotransplantation datasets are characterized by small cohort sizes (typically n<10 per arm), high heterogeneity across experimental sites, and nonstandardized data collection protocols, conditions that risk model overfitting, algorithmic bias, and poor external validity. Systematic multicenter data collection initiatives with harmonized protocols, analogous to the iBox consortium in allotransplantation [12], represent a foundational research infrastructure investment for the xenotransplantation community.

Second, interspecies translational validity remains a major challenge. Differences between Old World NHPs and humans in xenoreactive antibody specificities, NK cell repertoires, and complement activation and regulation may limit the direct transfer of AI models and multiomics signatures [7,8]. NHP-derived signatures should therefore be considered candidate biomarkers requiring human validation. The human decedent model, as described by Montgomery et al. [20], offers a complementary calibration stage rather than a definitive bridge. Although it generates human immune data, brain death and associated factors (including neurologic injury, endocrine and cytokine disturbances, vasoactive support, and mechanical ventilation) may alter immune and hemodynamic trajectories independently of xenograft biology. Models calibrated on these data may therefore learn brain-death-specific patterns that do not generalize to living recipients. Decedent data should be treated as a distinct biological domain, with cause and duration of brain death, organ-support settings, and treatment exposures explicitly modeled. Translation should follow prespecified stages: development and external validation in NHP datasets, validation and recalibration in human decedent cohorts, and prospective validation in living recipients. Each transition should use prespecified, task-specific performance thresholds, with model revision and repeat validation when these thresholds are not met.

Third, the validation and regulatory integration problem: AI tools intended for IND application evidence packages must satisfy regulatory standards for analytical validation, bias assessment, and performance benchmarking that have not yet been established for xenotransplantation-specific AI applications. The 2024 Transplant AI Symposium (Frontiers in Transplantation) addressed this gap directly, proposing a framework for clinical AI implementation in transplantation that encompasses federated learning infrastructure, prospective validation studies, and XAI compliance review [23]. The xenotransplantation community must engage proactively with regulators to define the evidence standards for AI-supported IND applications, ideally through consortia analogous to the International Xenotransplantation Association [38].

CONCLUSION

The Path to Clinical Precision

Nonhuman primate preclinical xenotransplantation stands at a pivotal inflection point. The first clinical xenotransplantations have proven the concept [2,3,5,49], yet the translational gap between NHP experimental success and reliable human clinical outcomes remains insufficiently bridged. The root cause is a data complexity crisis: the immunological, physiological, and behavioral signals that determine graft fate in NHP experiments are too high-dimensional, too nonlinear, and too temporally dynamic for conventional analytical approaches to fully decode.

Artificial intelligence provides a comprehensive response to this crisis across every stage of the preclinical research pipeline. Computer vision transforms subjective behavioral scoring into continuous, objective phenotyping [25–28]; biosignal anomaly detection converts physiological time series into early rejection alerts [29]; multiomics integration frameworks identify xenograft-specific biomarker signatures from high-dimensional molecular data [9–11,30–32]; graft survival prediction models leverage the full complexity of NHP experimental data for statistically powered translational inference [12–14]; and digital twin simulations bridge the interspecies gap by modeling prospective human recipient immune responses in silico [18,33,36]. XAI frameworks ensure that these analytical advances are transparent, interpretable, and regulatory-defensible [45–47].

The synergy between rigorous NHP experimentation and AI-driven data science is now technically achievable. Loupy et al. [22] positioned AI as a transformative pillar alongside gene editing and novel immunosuppression in reshaping transplantation medicine, with xenotransplantation explicitly identified as a priority application domain. The 3Rs framework provides an ethical mandate that aligns perfectly with this AI-augmented research agenda: fewer animals, greater precision, and stronger translational evidence [40–43].

Realizing this vision demands coordinated action: establishment of international multicenter NHP xenotransplantation data repositories with harmonized protocols; development of species-specific training datasets for behavioral AI welfare tools; prospective validation of multiomics ML biomarker pipelines; codesign of digital twin platforms with xenotransplantation clinical teams; and proactive regulatory engagement to establish AI evidence standards for IND applications. The path to clinical precision in xenotransplantation now runs through the intersection of biology and machine intelligence, and the field is rapidly approaching that intersection.
