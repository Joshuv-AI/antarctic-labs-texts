Stochastic Bilevel Optimization with Heavy-Tailed Noise
Zhuanghua Liu, Luo Luo

Abstract
This paper considers the smooth bilevel optimization in which the lower-level problem is strongly convex and the upper-level problem is possibly nonconvex. We focus on the stochastic setting that the algorithm can access the unbiased stochastic gradient evaluation with heavy-tailed noise, which is prevalent in many machine learning applications such as training large language models and reinforcement learning. We propose a nested-loop normalized stochastic bilevel approximation (N 2 SBA) for finding an ϵ \epsilon -stationary point with the stochastic first-order oracle (SFO) complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( κ 7 ​ p − 3 p − 1 ​ σ p p − 1 ​ ϵ − 4 ​ p − 2 p − 1 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa^{\frac{7p-3}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\epsilon^{-\frac{4p-2}{p-1}}\big) , where κ \kappa is the condition number, p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] p\in(1,2] is the order of central moment for the noise, and σ \sigma is the noise level. Furthermore, we specialize our idea to solve the nonconvex-strongly-concave minimax optimization problem, achieving an ϵ \epsilon -stationary point with the SFO complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( κ 2 ​ p − 1 p − 1 ​ σ p p − 1 ​ ϵ − 3 ​ p − 2 p − 1 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa^{\frac{2p-1}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\epsilon^{-\frac{3p-2}{p-1}}\big) . All above upper bounds match the best-known results under the special case of the bounded variance setting, i.e., p = 2 p=2 .

Introduction

We consider the following smooth bilevel optimization problem

\begin{split}\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}&\varphi(x)\coloneqq f(x,y^{*}(x)),\\
{\rm s.t.}~&y^{*}(x)\coloneqq\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}g(x,y),\end{split}

(1)

where both the upper-level function
f(x,y)={\mathbb{E}}[F(x,y^{*}(x),\xi)]
and lower-level function
g(x,y)={\mathbb{E}}[G(x,y,\zeta)]
are jointly continuous differentiable.
In this paper, we focus on the nonconvex-strongly-convex setting where the lower-level function
g(x,y)
is smooth and strongly convex with respect to
y
, while the hyper-objective function
\phi(x)
is smooth but possibly nonconvex.
This formulation is ubiquitous in a wide range of machine learning applications, including reinforcement learning

, hyperparameter optimization

There has been a growing research interest in developing stochastic algorithms for solving the large-scale bilevel problem

Earlier studies

have utilized Hessian–vector product (HVP)-based methods, combined with random samples of gradients and Hessians, to directly approximate the hyper-gradient of
\varphi(x)
, which can be explicitly characterized as

\nabla\varphi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,y^{*}(x))-\nabla^{2}_{xy}g(x,y^{*}(x))(\nabla^{2}_{yy}g(x,y^{*}(x)))^{-1}\nabla_{y}f(x,y^{*}(x)).

(2)

Leveraging established convergence results of stochastic gradient descent for smooth nonconvex objectives, these methods are able to obtain an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\varphi(x)
using
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-4})
HVP calls.
However, these approaches require access to the Jacobian and the inverse of the Hessian of the lower-level function, which can be computationally prohibitive in large-scale bilevel optimization problems.

In contrast to the aforementioned HVP-based approaches, more recent studies
[
35
,
4
]
have proposed fully stochastic first-order methods for solving bilevel optimization problems, without requiring any second-order information.
Specifically, these methods leverage the following penalty reformulation of the bilevel problem (
1
):

{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}(x,y)\coloneqq f(x,y)+\lambda(g(x,y)-g^{*}(x)),

(3)

where
\lambda>0
is the penalty parameter.
Moreover,
Kwon et al. 2023
introduce the auxiliary function

{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\coloneqq{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}(x,y_{\lambda}^{*}(x)),~{\rm where}~y_{\lambda}^{*}(x)=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}(x,y),

(4)

and
Chen et al. 2025
demonstrate that, by setting
\lambda=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})
, an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
corresponds to an
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
-stationary point of
\varphi(x)
.
Notably, the gradient of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
can be computed using only first-order information from
f
and
g
, given by

\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,y_{\lambda}^{*}(x))+\lambda(\nabla_{x}g(x,y_{\lambda}^{*}(x))-\nabla_{x}g(x,y^{*}(x))).

(5)

This reformulation effectively reduces the problem of finding an approximate stationary point of
\varphi(x)
to that of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
, thereby enabling the development of fully stochastic first-order methods.
We note that the convergence analysis of these methods typically relies on the assumption that the stochastic gradients of both the upper-level and lower-level problems have bounded variance.

However, recent insights in machine learning indicate that the bounded variance assumption is restrictive.
The empirical evidence from scenarios like image classification
[
58
]
, large language model (LLM) training
[
63

1
]
, and policy optimization in reinforcement learning
[

suggests that stochastic gradients often follow heavy-tailed distributions.
These findings suggest adopting a weaker noise model which only requires the
p

p
-BCM) of the stochastic gradient such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\nabla F(x,y;\xi)-\nabla f(x,y)\right\|^{p}]\leq\sigma_{f}^{p}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\nabla G(x,y;\zeta)-\nabla g(x,y)\right\|^{p}]\leq\sigma_{g}^{p}
, for some
\sigma_{f}
,
\sigma_{g}>0
and
p\in(1,2]

When
p=2

[
48

18

For

Although existing works

35

4
]
establish non-asymptotic convergence guarantees for stochastic methods in bilevel optimization under the bounded variance assumption, the convergence behavior of such methods in the presence of more general heavy-tailed noise remains unclear.

To guarantee the convergence of our algorithm under the

\kappa

2
Related Work

In this section, we provide a review of related work on stochastic methods for bilevel optimization, along with stochastic minimization and minimax optimization problems in the presence of heavy-tailed noise.

2.1
Stochastic methods for Bilevel and Minimax Optimization

To address the stochastic bilevel optimization problem, earlier HVP-based approaches
[
19
,
28
,
30
]
construct the stochastic hyper-gradient estimator explicitly according to the update (
2
).
These methods employ iterative algorithms to solve the associated linear system, thereby avoiding the explicit computation of the costly Hessian inverse.
In particular,
Ghadimi and Wang 2018
proposed the bilevel stochastic approximation (BSA) method based on single-sample gradient and Hessian estimates. Building on this,
Hong et al. 2020
developed a two-timescale stochastic approximation (TTSA) algorithm that achieves a better trade-off between the complexities of the inner and outer optimization loops.
Later,
Ji et al. 2021
designed a more sample-efficient hyper-gradient estimator using the Neumann series.
Very recently,
Hao et al. 2024
,
Gong et al. 2024a
,
Gong et al. 2024b
established high-probability convergence guarantees of HVP-based methods for stochastic bilevel optimization with unbounded smoothness under the light-tailed noise assumption.

The gradient-based stochastic methods
[
35
,
4
]
leverage inexpensive gradient oracles while avoiding reliance on costly HVP oracle evaluations, thereby enhancing their scalability to large-scale bilevel optimization problems.
Observing that
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
in equation (
5
) involves only first-order information,
Liu et al. 2022
,
Shen and Chen 2023
proposed deterministic algorithms that converge to a stationary point of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
, but without establishing any convergence guarantees for
\varphi(x)
.
Building on this,
Kwon et al. 2023
established the relationship between
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
and
\varphi(x)
, and introduced the Fully First-order Stochastic Approximation (F
2
SA) method to obtain an approximate stationary point of
\varphi(x)
.
More recently,
Chen et al. 2025
incorporated a two-timescale update to enhance F
2
SA, achieving a stochastic first-order (SFO) complexity of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\kappa^{11}\sigma^{2}\epsilon^{-6})
.

For stochastic minimax optimization,
Jin et al. 2020
proposed the stochastic gradient descent with max-oracle (SGDmax) method, which adopts a nested-loop framework: the inner loop approximately solves the maximization subproblem
\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}f(x,y)
, and the obtained solution is then utilized in the outer loop to perform an approximate stochastic gradient descent update on
x
.

Lin et al. 2020a
demonstrated that the problem (
6
) can be solved by directly extending stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to the stochastic gradient descent ascent (SGDA) method, which performs gradient descent on
x
and gradient ascent on
y
.
In contrast to SGDmax, which relies on an inner maximization loop, SGDA updates both variables simultaneously.
They further established that SGDA requires
{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{3}\sigma_{f}^{2}\epsilon^{-4})
stochastic gradient evaluations to obtain an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\Phi
in expectation.

2.2
Minimization and Minimax Optimization under Heavy-Tailed Noise

Classical in expectation and high probability convergence guarantees for convex problems are derived under the assumption of light-tailed noise distribution
[
33
,
48
,
17
]
.

Nazin et al. 2019
established the first high-probability complexity bounds for convex optimization under the assumption of bounded gradient variance.

Gorbunov et al. 2020
introduced the technique of accelerated gradient clipping, leading to improved complexity bounds for both convex and strongly convex settings.
Subsequent work by
Gorbunov et al. 2024
,
Sadiev et al. 2023
extended these approaches to the problem with Hölder-continuous gradients under weaker heavy-tailed noise assumptions.

Compared with the convex problem, the study of nonconvex problems under heavy-tailed noise is relatively scarce.

Zhang et al. 2020
,
Sadiev et al. 2023
,
Nguyen et al. 2023
studied in-expectation and high probability convergence guarantee of Clip-SGD under
p
-BCM noise assumption.
However, these studies employ an increasing clipping parameter, which results in suboptimal convergence guarantees.

Cutkosky and Mehta 2020
,
Liu and Zhou 2023
attained improved complexity bounds by leveraging a combination of gradient normalization and clipping under some restrictive assumptions.
Very recently,
Hübler et al. 2024
,
Liu and Zhou 2025
investigated the normalized stochastic gradient descent method, demonstrating that it attains an optimal complexity bound under the
p
-BCM noise assumption without the gradient clipping technique.

For the minimax optimization problem,
Juditsky et al. 2011
established the first in-expectation and high probability complexity bound for solving monotone variation inequality (VI) assuming the noise is light-tailed.

Gorbunov et al. 2022
derived high probability complexity results of stochastic methods for solving monotone VI problems under the bounded gradient variance assumption.
Later,
Sadiev et al. 2023
extended their result to the
p
-BCM noise distribution setting.
While both studies address the non-monotone star-cocoercive variational inequality problems, their results do not generalize to the nonconvex-strongly-concave setting.
Very recently,
Laguel et al. 2024
provided high probability complexity results for the nonconvex-Polyak-Lojasiewicz problem under the light-tailed noise assumption.

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we present the assumptions used throughout the paper, and then we introduce the definitions of stationarities of the bilevel and minimax optimization problems.

3.1
Approximate Stationary Points of Bilevel Optimization

We begin by outlining the assumptions necessary for finding first-order stationary points of the hyper-objective
\varphi(x)
.

Assumption 3.1
.

Suppose that

(a)

g(x,y)
is
L_{g}
-gradient Lipschitz,
\rho_{g}
-Hessian Lipschitz, and
\mu
-strongly convex in
y
.

(b)

f(x,y)
is
C_{f}
-Lipschitz in
y
,
L_{f}
-gradient Lipschitz, and two-times continuous differentiable;

(c)

\varphi(x)
is lower bounded,
i.e.

\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}\varphi(x)>-\infty
;

The above assumptions are standard and essential for non-asymptotic analyses of bilevel optimization
[
19
,
30
,
4
]
.
As established in Theorem 3.2 of
Chen et al. 2024
, bilevel problems are generally intractable when
g(x,\cdot)
is not strongly convex. Consequently, existing non-asymptotic analyses of bilevel optimization typically adopt the lower-level strong convexity assumption.
Under this assumption, the hyper-gradient
\nabla\varphi(x)
can be expressed in terms of
\nabla_{x}f(x,y)
,
\nabla_{y}f(x,y)
,
\nabla_{xy}^{2}g(x,y)
and
\nabla_{yy}^{2}g(x,y)
as shown in Equation
2
.
We define the condition number of the problem (
1
) as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, we define the largest smoothness constant
\ell:=\max\{C_{f},L_{f},L_{g},\rho_{g}\}
and the condition number of problem (
1
) as
\kappa:=\ell/\mu
.

From the above assumptions, it follows that
\nabla\varphi(x)
is uniquely defined and Lipschitz continuous, as formally stated below.

Proposition 3.1

(
L
emma 2.2)
.

ghadimi2018approximation]

Under Assumption
3.1
,
the hyper-gradient
\nabla\varphi(x)
is uniquely defined by Equation
2
, and
the hyper-objective
\varphi(x)
is
L_{\varphi}
-gradient Lipschitz, where
L_{\varphi}={\mathcal{O}}(\ell\kappa^{3})
.

Since the above proposition guarantees that the gradient of the hyper-objective
\varphi(x)
is well-defined, we define the notion of
\epsilon
-first-order stationary points as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a differentiable function
\varphi(x):{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,
we call
\hat{x}
an
\epsilon
-first-order stationary point of
\varphi(x)
if
\|\nabla\varphi(\hat{x})\|\leq\epsilon
.

We note that evaluating the hyper-gradient
\nabla\varphi(x)
requires computing both the Jacobian and the inverse of the Hessian, which can be prohibitively expensive in large-scale bilevel optimization.

Kwon et al. 2023
,
Chen et al. 2025
observed that the gradient
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
can be evaluated using only first-order information, and further established its relationship with
\nabla\varphi(x)
, as stated below.

Lemma 3.1

(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for
\lambda\geq 2L_{f}/\mu
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|y^{*}_{\lambda}(x)-y^{*}(x)\right\|\leq{C_{f}}/{(\lambda\mu)},~~~~|L_{\lambda}^{*}(x)-\phi(x)|\leq D_{0}/\lambda,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla L_{\lambda}^{*}(x)-\nabla\phi(x)\right\|\leq D_{1}/\lambda,~~~\left\|\nabla y^{*}(x)-\nabla y_{\lambda}^{*}(x)\right\|\leq D_{2}/\lambda,

where we define

\displaystyle D_{0}\coloneqq\left(C_{f}\!+\!\frac{C_{f}L_{g}}{2\mu}\right)\frac{C_{f}}{\mu}={\mathcal{O}}(\ell\kappa^{2}),~~~D_{1}\coloneqq\left(L_{f}\!+\!\frac{\rho_{g}L_{g}}{\mu}\!+\!\frac{C_{f}L_{g}\rho_{g}}{2\mu^{2}}\!+\!\frac{C_{f}\rho_{g}}{2\mu}\right)\frac{C_{f}}{\mu}={\mathcal{O}}(\ell\kappa^{3}),

\displaystyle D_{2}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{2L_{g}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\left(L_{f}+\frac{C_{f}\rho_{g}}{\mu}\right)={\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{3}).

Based on Lemma
3.1
, by setting
\lambda=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})
, any
\epsilon
-stationary point of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)
corresponds to an
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)
-stationary point of
\varphi(x)
.

3.2
Approximate Stationary Points of Minimax Optimization

We now state the assumptions required to establish first-order stationary points of the minimax optimization problem.

Assumption 3.2
.

Suppose that the objective function satisfies

a.

f
is
\ell
-smooth and
f(x,\cdot)
is
\mu
-strongly concave.

b.

f
is uniformly lower bounded, i.e.,
\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}\max_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}f(x,y)>-\infty
.

The above assumptions are standard and essential for non-asymptotic analyses of the nonconvex-strongly-concave minimax optimization
[
40
,
41
,
46
]
.
For the ease of notation, we define

\Phi(\cdot)=\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}f(\cdot,y).

(7)

We observe that the minimax optimization problem (
6
) can be equivalently reformulated as the minimization of the objective function
\Phi(x)
.
In particular, when
f(x,y)
is nonconvex in
x
and strongly concave in
y
, the resulting function
\Phi(x)
is guaranteed to be smooth.
To make this precise, we formally state the following technical lemma, which establishes the structural properties of
\Phi(x)
.

Proposition 3.2

(
Lin et al. 2020a
)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
\Phi(x)
is
(\ell+\kappa\ell)
-smooth with
\nabla\Phi(\cdot)=\nabla_{x}f(\cdot,y^{*}(\cdot))
where
\kappa\coloneqq\ell/\mu
is the condition number and
y^{*}(\cdot)=\argmax_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}f(\cdot,y)
.
In addition,
y^{*}(\cdot)
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz.

Since the function
\Phi(x)
is nonconvex, finding its global minimum is generally NP-hard.
Consequently, our objective is to seek its approximate stationary points instead.
Formally, we define the notion of an
\epsilon
-first-order stationary point as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

We call a point
\hat{x}
an
\epsilon
-stationary point of a differentiable function
\Phi(\cdot)
if it satisfies
\left\|\nabla\Phi(\hat{x})\right\|\leq\epsilon
.

3.3
Stochastic Gradient Assumption

Before concluding this section, we formally introduce the
p
-th bounded central moment (
p
-BCM) assumption on the stochastic gradient oracle, which generalizes the conventional bounded variance assumption.

Assumption 3.3
.

We access the gradients of the bilevel problem in equation (
1
) via the unbiased gradient oracle
\nabla F(x,y;\xi)
and
\nabla G(x,y;\zeta)
such that

{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(x,y;\xi)]=\nabla f(x,y),~~~{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\nabla F(x,y;\xi)-\nabla f(x,y)\right\|^{p}]\leq\sigma_{f}^{p},

(8)

{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla G(x,y;\zeta)]=\nabla g(x,y),~~~{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\nabla G(x,y;\zeta)-\nabla g(x,y)\right\|^{p}]\leq\sigma_{g}^{p},

(9)

where

The algorithm incorporates an auxiliary variable

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}},y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}\left\{f(x,y)+\lambda\left(g(x,y)-\min_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}g(x,z)\right)\right\}\overset{(\ref{eq:L-lambda})}{=}\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{x}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x).

\displaystyle{\rm clip}(x,\tau)=\begin{cases}\min\left\{1,{\tau}/{\left\|x\right\|}\right\}x,&x\neq 0,\\

For the outer loop, however, applying gradient clipping alone to the stochastic gradient at

[

To address this, we instead perform a single step of normalized stochastic gradient descent on
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x)

In contrast to classical nonconvex optimization, bilevel optimization does not provide access to an unbiased gradient estimator of the outer-level function, which motivates us to normalize the inexact stochastic gradient estimator and perform a single gradient descent step with respect to

Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we propose a stochastic algorithm called N
2
SBA for solving nonconvex–strongly-convex bilevel optimization problems in the presence of heavy-tailed noise.
We establish both in-expectation and high-probability convergence guarantees for N
2
SBA under the
p
-BCM gradient noise assumption. The stochastic first-order (SFO) complexity of our method matches that of the best-known stochastic gradient–based algorithms under the bounded-variance assumption.
Furthermore, we introduce N
2
SGDA for addressing nonconvex–strongly-concave minimax optimization problems with heavy-tailed noise, achieving nearly optimal dependence on
\sigma
and
\epsilon
.
Numerical experiments on both synthetic and large-scale real-world datasets confirm the effectiveness and practical advantages of the proposed algorithms.

Studying the lower bound for stochastic first-order algorithms in solving nonconvex–strongly-convex bilevel optimization problems under heavy-tailed noise is an interesting direction for future work.
Another promising avenue is to extend our methodology to handle constrained bilevel and minimax optimization problems.

We now prove the equation (
25
) by induction.
For the base case
t=0
, it holds that
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\hat{z}_{0,0}-y^{*}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}]\leq R_{0}^{2}\leq R_{z}^{2}
by the parameter setting of
R_{z}
and the initialization that
\hat{z}_{0,0}=\hat{y}_{0,0}
.
Let equation (
25
) holds for
t=0,\ldots,\tilde{t}-1
, we consider the case
t=\tilde{t}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t},0}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{2L_{g}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|x_{\tilde{t}}-x_{\tilde{t}-1}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{2L_{g}^{2}\eta_{x}^{2}}{\mu^{2}},

where the first inequality follows from
\left\|a+b\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|a\right\|^{2}+2\left\|b\right\|^{2}
, and the second inequality is due to Lemma
B.2
.
According to the induction hypothesis (
25
), we can apply Lemma
D.2
to show the following convergence result:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq 2R_{z}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu K}{30L_{g}}\right)+\frac{2*5400^{\frac{2}{p}}\sigma_{g}^{2}(\ln(B_{z,\tilde{t}-1}))^{2}}{\mu^{2}K^{\frac{2(p-1)}{p}}},

According to the parameter choice that

\displaystyle K=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\kappa+\frac{\ell^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\kappa^{\frac{4p}{p-1}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}}\right)~~~{\rm and}~~~\eta_{x}=\frac{\epsilon}{\ell\kappa^{3}},

we can show that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{z}_{\tilde{t},0}-y^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{4}}{\ell^{4}\kappa^{6}}+\frac{2L_{g}^{2}\eta_{x}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\leq\frac{2\epsilon^{4}}{\ell^{4}\kappa^{6}}+\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\ell^{2}\kappa^{4}}\leq R_{z}^{2}.

Therefore, we complete the induction.

Similarly, to apply Lemma
D.2
on
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\hat{y}_{t,K}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}]
, we can show that

{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\hat{y}_{t,0}-y^{*}_{\lambda}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}]\leq R_{y}^{2}\coloneqq 4R_{0}^{2}+\frac{2\epsilon^{4}}{\ell^{4}\kappa^{6}}+\frac{32\epsilon^{2}}{\ell^{2}\kappa^{4}}

(26)

for
t=0,\ldots,T-1
by induction.
For the base case
t=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\hat{y}_{0,0}-y^{*}_{\lambda}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}]\leq 2{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\hat{y}_{0,0}-y^{*}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}]+2\left\|y^{*}(x_{0})-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}\leq 2R_{0}^{2}+2R_{0}^{2}=4R_{0}^{2},

where the first inequality follows from
\left\|a+b\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|a\right\|^{2}+2\left\|b\right\|^{2}
, and the second inequality is due to Lemma
3.1
,
R_{0}\geq\left\|\hat{y}_{0,0}-y^{*}(x_{0})\right\|
, and the choice of
\lambda
.
Let equation (
26
) holds for
t=0,\dots,\tilde{t}-1
, we consider the case
t=\tilde{t}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t},0}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{32L_{g}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|x_{\tilde{t}}-x_{\tilde{t}-1}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{32L_{g}^{2}\eta_{x}^{2}}{\mu^{2}},

where the first inequality follows from
\left\|a+b\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|a\right\|^{2}+2\left\|b\right\|^{2}
, and the second inequality is due to Lemma
B.2
.
According to the induction hypothesis equation (
26
), we can apply Lemma
D.2
and
B.5
to show the following convergence result:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t}-1,K}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}-1})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq 2R_{y}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu K}{120L_{g}}\right)+\frac{32*5400^{\frac{2}{p}}(\sigma_{f}+\lambda\sigma_{g})^{2}(\ln(B_{y,t}))^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}K^{\frac{2(p-1)}{p}}}

With the specified choices of
K
and
\eta_{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\hat{y}_{\tilde{t},0}-y_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{\tilde{t}})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{4}}{\ell^{4}\kappa^{6}}+\frac{32L_{g}^{2}\eta_{x}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\leq\frac{2\epsilon^{4}}{\ell^{4}\kappa^{6}}+\frac{32\epsilon^{2}}{\ell^{2}\kappa^{4}}\leq R_{y}^{2},

We complete the proof of induction.

Combining equation (
23
) and the convergence guarantee of
\hat{y}_{t,K}
and
\hat{z}_{t,K}
by applying Lemma
D.2
, we can find an
\epsilon
-first-order stationary point of

by choosing the following set of parameters

\displaystyle\eta_{x}=\frac{\epsilon}{\ell\kappa^{3}},~~~T={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Delta\ell\kappa^{3}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),~~~M={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\ell^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\kappa^{\frac{3p}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}}\right),~~~K=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\ell^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\kappa^{\frac{4p}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}}\right),

where
\sigma=\max\{\sigma_{f},\sigma_{g}\}
.
The total SFO complexity is

\displaystyle TM+TK=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\Delta\ell^{\frac{2p-1}{p-1}}\kappa^{\frac{7p-3}{p-1}}\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{4p-2}{p-1}}}\right).

∎

B.2
High-Probability Convergence Guarantee

In this subsection, we present the proof of the high-probability convergence guarantee of the N
2
SBA method for solving the nonconvex-strongly-convex bilevel optimization problem.
We start by providing a high-probability convergence guarantee of N
2
SBA with a general gradient estimator
g_{x,t}
.
Throughout this section, we denote the natural filtrations of our method by
{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\sigma(g_{x,0},\dots,g_{x,t})
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x,y,z)=f(x,y)+\lambda\left(g(x,y)-g(x,z)\right)
. Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.3
, the iterates generated by N
2
SBA satisfy

\begin{split}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}&\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq\frac{2({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T}))}{\eta_{x}T}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{4{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|g_{x,t}-\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})\right\|\right]}{T}+D_{3}\eta_{x}+\\
&\quad\quad\quad\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{4\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|}{T}+12\left(\frac{\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|}{T}+\eta_{x}D_{3}\right)\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right).\end{split}

(27)

Proof.

Let
\nu_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t}),g_{x,t}\rangle}{\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\left\|g_{x,t}\right\|}
denote the cosine angle between
\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})
and
g_{x,t}
.
Then according to (
19
), one has

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t+1})\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})-\eta_{x}\left\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t}),\frac{g_{x,t}}{\left\|g_{x,t}\right\|}\right\rangle+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})-\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\nu_{t}+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}}{2}.

Telescope the above inequality from
t=0
to
T-1
, and rearrange the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\nu_{t}\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}T}{2}.

Next, we use the fact that
\nu_{t}
is bounded and hence sharply concentrates around its expectation.
Formally, let
\psi_{t}\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}[\nu_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]
and denote that
D_{t}\coloneqq-\eta_{x}(\nu_{t}-\psi_{t})\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Furthermore, noting that

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{D_{t}^{2}}{4\eta_{x}^{2}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{(\nu_{t}-\psi_{t})^{2}}{4}\right)\leq e,

which implies that we may apply Lemma
A.8
.
Doing so yields, for all
\chi>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{x}(\psi_{t}-3\chi\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|)\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}T}{2}+\frac{1}{\chi}\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.
By the smoothness of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|+D_{3}t\eta_{x},

and hence choosing
\chi\coloneqq 1/(6(\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|+C_{T}D_{3}))
where
C_{T}=\eta_{x}^{2}T
yields, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{x}\left(\psi_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}T}{2}+6(\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|+C_{T}D_{3})\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right).

By applying Lemma
A.1
, we get

\displaystyle\psi_{t}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\geq\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|-2{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|g_{x,t}-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|].

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})+2\eta_{x}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|g_{x,t}-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|]+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}T}{2}

\displaystyle+6(\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|+C_{T}D_{3})\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})+2\eta_{x}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|g_{x,t}-\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})\right\|\right]+\frac{D_{3}\eta_{x}^{2}T}{2}

\displaystyle+2\eta_{x}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|+6(\eta_{x}\left\|\nabla{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})\right\|+C_{T}D_{3})\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right).

where we define
\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x,y,z)=f(x,y)+\lambda\left(g(x,y)-g(x,z)\right)
, and we use triangle inequality in the last step.
Dividing both sides by
\eta_{x}T/2
leads to the desired result.
∎

Under an appropriate choice of
\eta_{x}
, we can derive the following high-probability lemma.

Lemma B.7
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.3

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(7+50\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)\frac{\sqrt{\Delta D_{3}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{8\sigma_{f}+16\lambda\sigma_{g}}{M^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{4\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|}{T},

Proof.

Let
\xi_{i}^{t}
,

where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality, and the second inequality is due to Lemma

and Assumption

.
Plugging the above inequality into (

) now yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T}))}{\eta_{x}T}+\frac{8\sigma_{f}+16\lambda\sigma_{g}}{M^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{4\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|}{T}

Note that
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{0})-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{T})\leq 3\Delta
and we choose

, then with probability at least

\displaystyle\left(7+50\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)\frac{\sqrt{\Delta D_{3}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{8\sigma_{f}+16\lambda\sigma_{g}}{M^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{4\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\lambda}(x_{t},y_{t},z_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|}{T},

Based on the above lemmas, we can establish the high probability convergence guarantee of N
2

B.2.1
Proof of Theorem

Proof.

B.7
, we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}(x_{t})\right\|\\

with probability

where the inequality is due to Assumption

Similarly, we can use induction to show that

We want to prove
{\mathbb{P}}(E_{t})\geq 1-t\delta/(2T)
for all

.
The base case of the induction is trivial:
For

\left\|\hat{y}_{0,0}-y^{*}(x_{0})\right\|^{2}\leq R_{0}^{2}\leq R_{y}^{2}
with probability 1.
Next, assume that for

{\mathbb{P}}(E_{T^{\prime}-1})\geq 1-(T^{\prime}-1)\delta/(2T)
.
Given this, we now consider the case

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime},0}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}})\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}-1})\right\|^{2}+2\left\|y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}-1})-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}-1})\right\|^{2}+2\kappa^{2}\left\|x_{T^{\prime}-1}-x_{T^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}-1})\right\|^{2}+2\kappa^{2}\eta_{x}^{2},

where the first inequality is due to
\left\|a+b\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|a\right\|^{2}+2\left\|b\right\|^{2}
, the second inequality is due to Proposition
3.2
, and the last inequality follows from the update of
x_{T^{\prime}}
.
According to the induction hypothesis,
R_{y}^{2}\geq\left\|\hat{y}_{l,0}^{2}-y^{*}(x_{l})\right\|^{2}
holds for all
l=0,1,\dots,T^{\prime}-1
with probability
1-(T^{\prime}-1)\delta/(2T)
.
We further apply Lemma

\hat{f}(x,y)=-f(x,y)

\hat{\delta}=\delta/(2T)

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime}-1,K}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}-1})\right\|^{2}\leq 2\hat{R}^{2}\max\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{K}{400\kappa\ln\left({4K}/{\hat{\delta}}\right)}\right),\frac{1}{B_{K}}\right\}\leq\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{\ell^{2}},

with probability

, where the last inequality follows from the parameter setting of

According to the parameter setting of
\eta_{x}
, we further have
\left\|\hat{y}_{T^{\prime},0}-y^{*}(x_{T^{\prime}})\right\|^{2}\leq 10\epsilon^{2}/\ell^{2}\leq R_{y}^{2}
.
Applying the union bound, then we obtain
{\mathbb{P}}(E_{T^{\prime}})\geq 1-T^{\prime}\delta/(2T)
.
The proof of induction is complete.

Using the union bound on
E_{T}
and the event of equation (
37

1-\delta

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\right\|\leq\epsilon
with the parameter setting of

\displaystyle T={\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(1+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)^{2}\frac{\Delta_{\Phi}\kappa\ell}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),\quad M={\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right),\quad\eta_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{\Phi}}{(\kappa+1)\ell T}},

\displaystyle K={\mathcal{O}}\left(\kappa\ln\left(\frac{\ell R_{y}}{\epsilon}\right)\ln\left(\frac{T\kappa}{\delta}\right)+\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\ell^{2}}{\mu^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2(p-1)}}\left(\ln\left(\frac{\ell R_{y}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\ln\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2(p-1)}}\right)\right),

The total SFO complexity is

\displaystyle TM+TK=

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\Delta_{\Phi}\kappa^{\frac{2p-1}{p-1}}\ell\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}{\epsilon^{\frac{3p-2}{p-1}}}+\frac{\Delta_{\Phi}\kappa^{2}\ell}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

Appendix D
Convergence Analysis of Clipped-SGD

In this section, we examine the convergence of clipped-SGD for solving strongly convex functions under heavy-tailed noise, providing both in-expectation and high-probability guarantees.
This result serves as a key building block for tackling our bilevel and minimax optimization problems.

D.1
In-Expectation Convergence Analysis

We observe that clipped-SGD satisfies the following recursive relation, as established in Lemma D.3 in
Gorbunov et al. 2022
.
For completeness, we include the proof here.

Lemma D.1
.

Assume the function
\hat{f}(x,y)={\mathbb{E}}[\hat{F}(x,y;\xi)]
is
\mu
-strongly concave in
y
and
\ell
-smooth, running the stochastic gradient descent on
\hat{f}(x,y)
,

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k+1}=\hat{y}_{k}-\eta_{y}\hat{g}_{y,k},

where
\hat{g}_{y,k}
is a stochastic gradient estimator of
\hat{f}(x,y)
for
k=0,1,\dots,K
.
Then the following statement holds true with
\eta_{y}\leq 1/\ell

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{K+1}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K+1}\left\|\hat{y}_{0}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K}(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K-k}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\eta_{y}\sum_{k=0}^{K}(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K-k}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle.

where
\omega_{k}=\hat{g}_{y,k}-\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})
and
y^{*}(x)=\argmin_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{y}}}\hat{f}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Using the update rule of
\hat{y}_{k}

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k+1}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-\eta_{y}\hat{g}_{y,k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x),\hat{g}_{y,k}\rangle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\hat{g}_{y,k}\right\|^{2}.

Define
\omega_{k}=\hat{g}_{y,k}-\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k+1}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x),\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})\rangle-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x),\omega_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})\right\|^{2}+2\eta_{y}^{2}\langle\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x),\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})\rangle-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})\right\|^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x),\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k})\rangle-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle+\eta_{y}^{2}\ell\langle\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\rangle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle-2\eta_{y}(1-\eta_{y}\ell/2)\langle\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\rangle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle-2\eta_{y}\mu(1-\eta_{y}\ell/2)\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}.

The second inequality is due to Lemma
A.5
, and the third inequality follows from Lemma
A.6
.
Choosing
\eta_{y}\leq 1/\ell

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{k+1}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{y}\mu)\left\|\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{y}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle+\eta_{y}^{2}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}.

Summing up

from

to

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{y}_{K+1}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K+1}\left\|\hat{y}_{0}-y^{*}(x)\right\|^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K}(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K-k}\left\|\omega_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\eta_{y}\sum_{k=0}^{K}(1-\eta_{y}\mu)^{K-k}\langle\hat{y}_{k}-y^{*}(x)-\eta_{y}\nabla_{y}f(x,\hat{y}_{k}),\omega_{k}\rangle.

∎
