Does More Mean Better? Treatment Intensity and Predictors of Clinically Significant Change in Suicidal Ideation During Dialectical Behaviour Therapy
Authors: Maya E. Amestoy, Nathan Kolla, Judith Levy‐Ajzenkopf, Ryan Klein, Amanda A. Uliaszek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study examined whether DBT treatment intensity predicts clinically significant change (CSC) in suicidal ideation, whether maladaptive personality traits moderate this relationship and whether early symptom improvements in suicidal ideation from baseline to midtreatment predicted CSC at post‐treatment, above and beyond the effects of treatment intensity and personality traits. Adults (n = 105) in a hospital‐based DBT day treatment programme were triaged into three DBT intensity tracks over 12 weeks: low intensity (weekly DBT skills group), mid intensity (weekly skills group plus individual therapy) and high intensity (two weekly skills groups, individual therapy). Suicidal ideation was assessed using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI), and maladaptive personality traits were assessed using the Personality Inventory for ICD‐11 at baseline, Week 6 and Week 12. Early symptom improvement was operationalized as change in BSSI scores from baseline to Week 6. CSC at Week 12 was defined as a reliable improvement from baseline to Week 12 and a BSSI score of ≤ 6 at Week 12. Suicidal ideation decreased significantly over time, with no differences across treatment intensity levels or maladaptive personality traits. Early symptom improvement predicted higher odds of achieving CSC at Week 12, whereas Disinhibition predicted lower odds of achieving CSC. Suicidal ideation improved across DBT intensity levels, with early reductions in suicidal ideation and Disinhibition, but not treatment intensity, emerging as key predictors of clinically significant recovery at post‐treatment.

Introduction

Suicide remains a leading cause of preventable death worldwide and a persistent public health concern, ranking among the top causes of mortality across early and middle adulthood (National Alliance on Mental Illness 2024; WHO 2023). Suicidal ideation, defined as thoughts about, consideration of or planning for suicide, is a critical clinical target, as it reflects both a source of profound psychological distress and a well‐established predictor of suicide attempts and death (Nock et al. 2008). Contemporary models conceptualize suicidal ideation along a continuum of risk, with evidence indicating that its presence, persistence and severity significantly increase the likelihood of transition to suicidal behaviour (Klonsky et al. 2016; May and Klonsky 2016). Longitudinal research further suggests that while many individuals experience transient suicidal thoughts, a subset develops chronic or escalating ideation associated with markedly elevated risk (Franklin et al. 2017). Accordingly, identifying effective interventions and clarifying the mechanisms and conditions under which they reduce suicidal ideation remains a central priority in clinical research and practice.

Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) has emerged as one of the most empirically supported interventions for reducing suicidal ideation and behaviour. Originally developed by Linehan (1993) for individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD), DBT is a comprehensive cognitive‐behavioural treatment that integrates behavioural principles, dialectical philosophy and elements of mindfulness derived from Zen practice. DBT is grounded in the biosocial model, which conceptualizes chronic emotion dysregulation as arising from the interaction between biological vulnerability and invalidating environmental contexts.

A substantial body of evidence supports the efficacy of DBT in reducing suicidal and self‐injurious behaviours. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that DBT leads to significant reductions in suicide attempts, nonsuicidal self‐injury and treatment dropout relative to treatment‐as‐usual and other expert‐delivered comparison conditions (Linehan et al. 2006; Linehan et al. 2015). For example, Linehan et al. (2006) found that individuals with BPD receiving DBT were approximately 50% less likely to attempt suicide over the treatment period compared with those receiving community treatment by experienced clinicians, alongside fewer self‐injurious behaviours and psychiatric hospitalizations. DBT has additionally been associated with improved treatment retention, an especially important outcome in high‐risk populations characterized by elevated attrition (McCauley et al. 2018).

These findings are reinforced by meta‐analytic evidence demonstrating moderate to large effects of DBT on reducing suicide attempts across clinical populations (DeCou et al. 2019), consistent with earlier reviews showing reductions in self‐harm and treatment attrition (Kliem et al. 2010). More recent syntheses extend this evidence base, indicating that DBT also produces broader improvements in emotion regulation, depressive symptoms and psychosocial functioning (Valentine et al. 2020). Collectively, this literature supports DBT as a first‐line, evidence‐based intervention for individuals at elevated risk for suicide. Further, a growing body of research supports the transdiagnostic applicability of DBT for a range of psychological conditions characterized by emotion dysregulation, including mood disorders, substance use disorders and post‐traumatic stress disorder (Panos et al. 2014; Ritschel et al. 2015).

Treatment Duration and Intensity in Psychotherapy and DBT

The concept of dose in psychotherapy has historically been operationalized primarily in terms of treatment duration, such as the number of sessions or weeks of engagement. Foundational dose–response research suggests that the majority of therapeutic gain occurs early in treatment, with diminishing returns over time (Howard et al. 1986). Subsequent work has similarly shown that substantial symptom improvement often occurs within the first several sessions, with smaller incremental benefits observed beyond approximately 26 sessions (Hansen et al. 2002). Although this literature has commonly used the term dose, treatment exposure can vary along at least two distinct dimensions: duration, reflecting the overall length of treatment, and intensity, reflecting the frequency or amount of therapeutic contact delivered within a given period. Distinguishing between these dimensions is important because treatments of equivalent duration may involve substantially different levels of therapeutic contact. This distinction has important implications for clinical decision‐making and the organization of mental health services, particularly in resource‐limited settings where understanding the amount and intensity of treatment required for meaningful improvement may inform the efficient allocation of treatment resources.

Within DBT, research examining treatment duration has begun to challenge the assumption that longer treatment necessarily produces superior outcomes. For example, the Feasibility of a Shorter Treatment and Evaluating Responses (FASTER) trial is among the only study to directly investigate treatment duration in DBT, comparing 6 versus 12 months of treatment for individuals with BPD (McMain et al. 2022). Both conditions produced significant improvements in suicidal behaviour, emotion regulation and overall symptom severity. These findings suggest that shorter DBT programmes may be sufficient for some individuals and highlight the importance of identifying the amount of treatment required to achieve clinical improvement and which individuals may benefit most from reduced treatment length.

Treatment intensity represents a related but distinct consideration within DBT. Comprehensive DBT traditionally involves multiple concurrent treatment modalities, including individual therapy, group‐based skills training, between‐session telephone coaching and therapist consultation. However, DBT has increasingly been implemented in modified formats that vary in the number and frequency of treatment components provided. In particular, stand‐alone DBT skills training has been examined as a lower intensity alternative to comprehensive DBT, with reviews providing preliminary support for its utility across a range of clinical populations and outcomes (Valentine et al. 2015, 2020). These adaptations may be particularly relevant in clinical settings in which comprehensive DBT is difficult to implement because of resource or service constraints. Variation in DBT intensity is also consistent with stepped‐care approaches, in which individuals initially receive the least intensive intervention expected to meet their clinical needs, with treatment intensity increased when indicated by symptom severity, risk or insufficient treatment response (Babeva et al. 2020). Applied to DBT, such an approach raises an important empirical question—whether all individuals require comprehensive, high‐intensity treatment or whether some may achieve comparable outcomes with fewer treatment components or less frequent therapeutic contact.

Despite its clinical relevance, treatment intensity has received considerably less empirical attention than treatment duration in DBT. Existing research has predominantly focused on treatment duration, despite the important distinction between the overall length of treatment and the frequency of therapeutic contact delivered within a given period. This distinction is particularly relevant in real‐world clinical settings, such as hospital‐based programmes, where triage decisions may result in individuals receiving different frequencies of therapeutic contact over the same overall treatment period. Accordingly, treatment intensity represents an important but understudied dimension of treatment exposure, with implications for resource allocation, clinical triage and treatment planning.

Individual Differences: Maladaptive Personality Traits

Maladaptive personality traits may represent an important source of individual variability in psychotherapy engagement and outcomes. Within BPD specifically, patient characteristics such as impulsivity have been associated with higher likelihood of treatment dropout, although findings regarding predictors of treatment response have been mixed (Barnicot et al. 2012). Greater baseline clinical severity has also been associated with larger symptom reductions in some studies, potentially reflecting greater scope for improvement among individuals entering treatment with more severe symptoms (Barnicot et al. 2012).

The ICD‐11 dimensional model of personality disorder provides a framework for capturing individual differences in maladaptive personality traits. This model includes five trait domains: Negative Affectivity (emotional lability, anxiousness and depressed mood), Detachment (social withdrawal, restricted affect and anhedonia), Dissociality (disregard for others and lack of empathy), Disinhibition (impulsivity, risk‐taking and poor behavioural control) and Anankastia (rigidity, perfectionism and perseveration; WHO 2019). This framework closely parallels Criterion B of the DSM‐5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD), which similarly conceptualizes personality pathology dimensionally through maladaptive trait domains (American Psychiatric Association 2022).

Emerging evidence suggests that these maladaptive personality traits may be relevant to suicide‐related processes. In a longitudinal sample of individuals with BPD, Negative Affectivity moderated the association between interpersonal dysfunction and suicidal ideation, such that greater interpersonal dysfunction was more strongly associated with suicidal ideation among individuals higher in Negative Affectivity (Allen et al. 2022). Disinhibition has similarly been shown to moderate the association between suicidal thoughts and subsequent suicidal attempts, such that suicidal thoughts were more strongly associated with subsequent attempts among individuals higher in Disinhibition (Allen et al. 2022). Together, these findings suggest that maladaptive personality traits may contribute to individual differences in suicide‐related outcomes and may therefore be relevant when examining treatment response among individuals experiencing suicidal ideation.

There is also preliminary evidence to suggest that these traits may differentially interact with DBT treatment mechanisms. For instance, individuals high in Disinhibition may benefit from DBT's emphasis on behavioural control and distress tolerance skills, whereas those high in Negative Affectivity may respond more to emotion regulation and mindfulness components (Neacsiu et al. 2010). Conversely, elevated Detachment or Anankastia may pose barriers to treatment engagement, given DBT's reliance on group‐based skills training and behavioural flexibility. However, direct tests of these maladaptive personality traits as moderators of DBT treatment response remain limited. More broadly, existing evidence suggests that personality pathology influences both the trajectory and magnitude of treatment response (Keefe et al. 2020), highlighting the need for further research examining personality‐informed variation in DBT outcomes.

Clinically Significant Change (CSC) and Early Symptom Response

Across psychotherapy research, early symptom change, typically occurring within the first 25%–30% of treatment, is among the strongest predictors of outcome (Lutz et al. 2001; Ilardi and Craighead 1994). Meta‐analytic and longitudinal evidence indicates that individuals who exhibit early response are significantly more likely to achieve remission and maintain gains, whereas those who do not are at increased risk for poor outcomes, chronicity and treatment dropout (Delgadillo et al. 2014). This pattern has been observed across a range of disorders and treatment modalities, including CBT for depression and anxiety (e.g., Fournier et al. 2009), as well as eating disorders (Vall and Wade 2015), suggesting that early change reflects a general process of treatment responsiveness rather than a modality‐specific mechanism. Consistent with this, more recent meta‐analytic research on sudden gains indicates that large, early symptom improvements are robustly associated with better post‐treatment and follow‐up outcomes across a range of disorders and treatment modalities (Shalom and Aderka 2020). Despite its robust predictive value, early symptom change has been relatively understudied in DBT, particularly in relation to suicidal ideation and clinically significant outcomes. This represents an important gap, given the structured, time‐limited nature of many DBT programmes and the potential utility of early change as a decision‐making indicator in clinical practice.

The Present Study

The present study is a secondary analysis of data drawn from a larger naturalistic, nonrandomized trial (Uliaszek et al., under review) examining outcomes in a transdiagnostic sample of adults participating in a 12‐week hospital‐based integrated day treatment (IDT) programme. Within this context, three nonrandomized DBT intensity tracks—differing in the frequency and type of therapeutic contact while holding treatment duration, skills content and overall programme structure constant—provided a unique opportunity to examine outcomes across varying levels of treatment intensity. Four a priori aims guided the study. First, we examined whether rates of CSC in suicidal ideation differed across DBT treatment intensity tracks. Second, we evaluated whether greater treatment intensity was associated with larger reductions in suicidal ideation over time. Third, we tested whether maladaptive personality traits moderated the relationship between treatment intensity and changes in ideation. Fourth, we assessed whether early reductions in suicidal ideation from baseline to midtreatment predicted CSC at post‐treatment, above and beyond the effects of treatment intensity and personality traits.

Method

Participants

Participants were consecutively recruited adults entering the IDT programme at a large tertiary mental health hospital in Toronto, Canada. The IDT programme is a hospital‐based day service for individuals who would benefit from intensive programming for mood and anxiety, trauma and addictions. Accordingly, programme eligibility required a primary diagnosis of at least one of the following: major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorder or post‐traumatic stress disorder. Participants could also present with comorbid conditions, including personality disorders, substance use disorders or trauma‐related disorders. Participants entered the programme from either an inpatient setting following hospital discharge or from an outpatient referral source. Study inclusion criteria were (1) enrolment in the IDT programme, (2) age 18 years or older, (3) fluency in English and (4) capacity to provide informed consent. Given the naturalistic design of the study, participants were recruited consecutively upon entry to the programme and were not required to meet a predetermined threshold of suicidal ideation for study inclusion, thereby retaining the range of suicidal ideation observed within the clinical population. Treatment

All participants received DBT as the primary treatment modality within the IDT programme. Clients were triaged by clinical staff into one of three treatment intensity tracks based on clinician‐rated acuity. Treatment intensity track was determined through clinical assessment of each patient's acuity and treatment needs rather than according to a predetermined cut‐off or formal triage algorithm. Triage decisions considered symptom presence and severity, functional impairment, suicidal ideation, previous treatment history and response and recent utilization of acute mental health services (e.g., inpatient hospitalization or emergency services). Given the complexity and heterogeneity of patients presenting to the service, these factors were considered collectively through clinical judgement rather than as discrete criteria for assignment to a particular treatment intensity level. As such, although suicidal ideation was considered as part of this assessment, it was not the sole determinant of treatment intensity. Following triage, an orientation meeting with a clinician confirmed the appropriateness of the assigned track based on clinical presentation, scheduling capacity and willingness to engage in treatment demands. All three tracks shared identical DBT skills training content over 12 weeks, facilitated by IDT clinical staff.

Treatment intensity was operationalized according to the frequency and type of DBT therapeutic contacts provided within the 12‐week treatment period. The low‐intensity track consisted of one weekly DBT skills group (12 sessions total). Participants in this track were also required to maintain contact with an external care provider at least twice monthly while enrolled in the programme. The type and extent of care provided externally were not systematically documented. The mid‐intensity track consisted of one weekly DBT skills group plus a 1‐h individual therapy session per week (12 group sessions and 12 individual sessions). The high‐intensity track consisted of two weekly DBT skills groups, resulting in two full rounds of the 12‐session group protocol, plus a 1‐h individual therapy session per week. Clients across all treatment streams also had access to consultation with clinical staff as needed, and all clients received at least one psychiatric consultation for medication management during the programme. Treatment intensity varied systematically across the three tracks in terms of the frequency and type of therapeutic contact provided within the IDT programme, while treatment duration remained constant 12 weeks.

Procedure

Treating clinicians obtained verbal consent from participants during orientation sessions to be contacted by the research team. Formal informed consent was subsequently obtained by research assistants via remote methods (telephone, email and videoconferencing). Participants completed a questionnaire package through the Qualtrics online survey platform while remaining on video conference with a research assistant. This procedure enabled real‐time clarification of questions and immediate risk assessment in cases where suicide‐related items were endorsed. The questionnaire package was administered at three timepoints: pretreatment (baseline), midtreatment (Week 6) and post‐treatment (Week 12).

Four validity items were embedded within the questionnaire package to identify careless or invalid responding (e.g., ‘I am the president of the United States’ and ‘I work 14 months in a year’). Participants endorsing any validity item incorrectly were excluded from analyses. Of the 105 participants who completed the baseline assessment, three were excluded from the analyses after failing validity checks.

Measures

Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI)

Suicidal ideation was assessed using the BSSI (Beck et al. 1979), a 21‐item self‐report measure assessing the intensity of current suicidal ideation. Consistent with the scoring manual, only the first 19 items were included in the total score, yielding a possible range of 0–38, with higher scores indicating greater ideation severity. The BSSI has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.87–0.90) and adequate test–retest reliability (Beck 1993). Across all three timepoints, the BSSI demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach's α = 0.89–0.93).

Personality Inventory for ICD‐11 (PiCD)

Maladaptive personality traits were assessed using the PiCD (Oltmanns and Widiger 2018), which operationalizes the five maladaptive trait domains of the ICD‐11 alternative model of personality disorder: Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition and Anankastia. Higher scores on each domain indicate greater expression of maladaptive traits. The PiCD has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties across clinical and community samples (Oltmanns and Widiger 2018). The PiCD was administered at all three timepoints and showed acceptable to good internal consistency across domains (Cronbach's α = 0.75–0.80).

CSC

A binary indicator of CSC in suicidal ideation was constructed following the framework of Jacobson and Truax (1991). CSC was defined as meeting two criteria simultaneously: (1) reliable improvement from baseline to post‐treatment, operationalized as a reliable change index exceeding the standard error of measurement of the BSSI, and (2) a post‐treatment BSSI score at or below the established clinical cut‐off of ≤ 6, indicating movement into the functional range (Kliem et al. 2017). Participants meeting both criteria were coded as having achieved CSC (coded 1); all others were coded as not having achieved CSC (coded 0).

Data Analytic Plan

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 31. All descriptive statistics are reported for the full sample (n = 105) unless otherwise noted, whereas all inferential analyses were conducted using the analytic sample (n = 102). Preliminary analyses were conducted to examine baseline differences across treatment intensity groups (low, mid and high). One‐way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) indicated no significant differences in baseline suicidal ideation, F(2, 99) = 0.39, p = 0.68, η 2 = 0.01. Similarly, no significant group differences were observed for any PiCD domains, including Negative Affectivity, F(2, 99) = 2.17, p = 0.12, η 2 = 0.04; Detachment, F(2, 99) = 0.99, p = 0.37, η 2 = 0.02; Dissociality, F(2, 99) = 0.02, p = 0.98, η 2 = 0.00; Disinhibition, F(2, 99) = 0.72, p = 0.49, η 2 = 0.01; and Anankastia, F(2, 99) = 0.56, p = 0.57, η 2 = 0.01. The assumption of homogeneity of variance was met for all variables except Negative Affectivity, for which Levene's test was significant, F(2, 99) = 5.12, p = 0.01.

To address Aim 1, crosstabulation and a chi‐square test of independence were used to examine whether CSC rates differed by treatment intensity track. A binary logistic regression was then conducted with treatment intensity as the predictor and CSC as the binary outcome.

To address Aims 2 and 3, linear mixed models were used to examine changes in BSSI scores across the three timepoints, with fixed effects of time (linear and quadratic), treatment intensity and the time * intensity interaction. PiCD domains were subsequently added as covariates and tested as moderators by including PiCD domain * time interaction terms. Random intercepts were included at the participant level and models were estimated using maximum likelihood.

To address Aim 4, a binary logistic regression was conducted with CSC at post‐treatment as the outcome. Predictors were entered simultaneously and included: early improvement in suicidal ideation (operationalized as the change in BSSI score from baseline to midtreatment), treatment intensity and all five PiCD domain scores. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals, Wald statistics and overall model classification accuracy are reported.

Results

Aim 1: Treatment Intensity and CSC

CSC in suicidal ideation was achieved by 31.3% of participants in the low‐intensity track, 23.7% in the mid‐intensity track and 9.1% in the high‐intensity track (see Table 2). Although the CSC rates were descriptively higher in the low‐intensity group, a chi‐square test of independence indicated these differences were not statistically significant, χ 2(2, N = 76) = 3.05, p = 0.22. A binary logistic regression analysis further examined whether treatment intensity predicted CSC. The model was not statistically significant, χ 2(2, N = 76) = 3.35, p = 0.19, Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.07, indicating that treatment intensity did not significantly predict the likelihood of achieving CSC (see Tables 3 and 4).

Aim 4: Predictors of CSC at Post‐Treatment

A binary logistic regression examined early improvement in suicidal ideation, treatment intensity and PiCD domains predicted CSC at post‐treatment. The overall model was statistically significant, χ 2(8, N = 78) = 34.28, p < 0.001, explaining 36% (Cox & Snell R 2) to 60% (Nagelkerke R 2) of the variance in CSC. The model demonstrated an overall classification accuracy of 91%.

Early improvement in suicidal ideation (baseline to midtreatment) was the strongest and most robust predictor of CSC, B = 0.44, SE = 0.13, Wald χ 2(1) = 10.87, p < 0.001, OR = 1.55, 95% CI [1.19, 2.01], such that each unit increase in early symptom improvement was associated with a 55% increase in the odds of achieving CSC. Treatment intensity was not a significant predictor of CSC in this model.

Among the PiCD domains, Disinhibition was the only significant predictor, B = −1.20, SE = 0.53, Wald χ 2(1) = 5.07, p = 0.024, OR = 0.30, 95% CI [0.11, 0.86], indicating that higher Disinhibition was associated with lower odds of achieving CSC. The remaining PiCD domains were not significant predictors. Results are presented in Table 6.

Discussion

The present study examined treatment intensity, maladaptive personality traits and early symptom change as predictors of clinically significant recovery in suicidal ideation among adults enrolled in a hospital‐based DBT programme. Four principal findings emerged. First, CSC rates did not significantly differ across treatment intensity levels. Second, suicidal ideation decreased significantly and linearly across the 12 weeks of treatment, with no difference in trajectory by intensity or personality traits. Third, higher Disinhibition predicted substantially lower odds of achieving CSC, independent of treatment intensity. Fourth, early improvement in suicidal ideation was the strongest predictor of CSC at post‐treatment, with 91% overall classification accuracy, and this effect held above and beyond the effects of treatment intensity and maladaptive personality traits. Together, these findings advance our understanding of for whom and under what conditions DBT produces clinically meaningful reductions in suicidal ideation.

Treatment Intensity and CSC

Contrary to what was expected, rates of CSC did not significantly differ across treatment intensity levels. CSC was achieved by 31.3%, 23.7% and 9.1% in the low‐, mid‐ and high‐intensity tracks, respectively. Although these rates were descriptively different, the relatively small number of participants achieving CSC, particularly in the high‐intensity track, limits the conclusions that can be drawn regarding between‐group differences. Accordingly, the absence of a statistically significant effect should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent effectiveness across treatment intensity tracks.

The relatively modest rates of CSC should also be considered in light of the stringent definition used in the present study. Following the widely used Jacobson and Truax (1991) framework, participants were required to demonstrate both reliable improvement in suicidal ideation from baseline to post‐treatment and a post‐treatment BSSI score within the functional range (≤ 6). Participants who experienced reductions in suicidal ideation but did not satisfy both criteria were therefore classified as not achieving CSC. Indeed, when suicidal ideation was examined continuously, BSSI scores decreased significantly over the 12‐week treatment period, suggesting that the binary CSC outcome may not fully capture the extent of symptom improvement observed during treatment. The clinical complexity of this transdiagnostic, hospital‐based sample may also have further contributed to the modest proportion of participants reaching this threshold.

Although the present findings do not establish equivalence across treatment intensity levels, they contribute to broader literature examining whether meaningful outcomes can be achieved with less intensive or abbreviated forms of DBT. Previous research has demonstrated favourable outcomes for shorter versus longer DBT programmes (McMain et al. 2018), while a growing literature supports the potential utility of a stand‐alone DBT skills training. The present results extend this question from treatment duration to treatment intensity, raising the possibility that lower intensity formats may be sufficient for some individuals. Given the naturalistic design, relatively small group sizes, and limited number of participants achieving CSC, determining whether low‐intensity DBT produces outcomes comparable with more intensive formats will require further investigation.

The nonrandomized allocation of participants to treatment tracks is particularly important when interpreting these findings. Treatment intensity was determined through clinical triage, with greater clinical complexity or acuity warranting more intensive treatment. Thus, the absence of significant differences across tracks may partly reflect the matching of treatment intensity to clinical need rather than an absence of benefit associated with greater treatment intensity. Although groups did not differ significantly at baseline on suicidal ideation or maladaptive personality traits, these measures may not have captured the full range of factors informing treatment assignment. Clients were triaged through a routine interdisciplinary case review process involving programme management and a clinician, informed by chart review and overall clinical presentation rather than predetermined quantitative thresholds. As such, treatment allocation may have reflected clinically meaningful factors not directly assessed in the present analyses, including recent hospitalization, suicide risk history, functional impairment, treatment readiness or complexity of comorbid presentations. Consequently, unmeasured differences in clinical complexity may also have contributed to the descriptive variation in CSC rates across treatment tracks, including the lower rate observed in the high‐intensity track.

Differences in treatment exposure outside of the programme provide an additional consideration. Participants in the low‐intensity track were required to maintain contact with an external care provider at least twice monthly, and the nature and extent of this treatment were not systematically documented. Ancillary treatment may therefore have reduced the effective difference in therapeutic contact across tracks. Future randomized studies that more precisely characterize treatment exposure and factors informing treatment allocation are needed to determine whether treatment intensity independently influences outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the absence of a detected treatment intensity effect remains clinically relevant and warrants further investigation, particularly for settings where the resources required for full‐model DBT, including individual therapy, consultation teams and telephone coaching, are unavailable.

Suicidal Ideation Trajectories

The significant linear reduction in BSSI scores across the 12 weeks of treatment is consistent with the established DBT efficacy literature (DeCou et al. 2019; Kliem et al. 2010; Linehan et al. 2006; Linehan et al. 2015). Consistent with the larger study, change in suicidal ideation followed a linear trajectory, with no significant quadratic effect, indicating steady improvement across the treatment period. In contrast, the larger study found BPD symptoms demonstrated a significant quadratic pattern, characterized by slower improvement early in treatment followed by more rapid gains in the latter half (ref). This divergence in trajectories may suggest that suicidal ideation responds more consistently across treatment, whereas broader BPD symptomatology may require more time to demonstrate accelerated change.

The non‐significant interaction between time and treatment intensity confirms that, at the group level, all three intensity tracks produced similar rates and trajectories of improvement. This finding is consistent with the dose–response literature more broadly, which has repeatedly demonstrated that the relationship between treatment quantity and outcome is not linear, particularly for common mental health outcomes (Hansen et al. 2002). The present findings suggest this principle extends to suicidal ideation outcomes in DBT across varying intensity levels.

Personality Traits as Moderators

The non‐significant moderation effects for most PiCD domains are notable in the context of the broader personality and psychotherapy literature. Despite theoretical reasons to expect that Negative Affectivity and Detachment would moderate the trajectory of suicidal ideation, through their associations with emotional intensity and social withdrawal, respectively, neither emerged as significant moderators. This may reflect the relatively brief observation window (12 weeks) adopted in this study or the transdiagnostic nature of the sample, in which variance in personality traits may reflect diagnostic heterogeneity.

The significant independent effect of Disinhibition on CSC in the logistic regression model, however, is an important finding. Individuals with higher Disinhibition, characterized by impulsivity, risk‐taking and poor behavioural regulation, had substantially lower odds (OR = 0.30) of achieving clinically significant recovery in suicidal ideation, even after accounting for treatment intensity and early symptom change. This finding aligns with theoretical formulations suggesting that impulsivity may undermine the conditions necessary for skills‐based treatments to achieve their full effect. DBT explicitly targets Disinhibition‐related features through distress tolerance and emotion regulation modules; yet high levels of these traits at baseline may interfere with homework completion, consistent group attendance and the cognitive and behavioural practice required to consolidate skills.

These results have direct implications for treatment planning and triage. Routine assessment of maladaptive personality traits, using dimensional instruments such as the PiCD, at intake could identify individuals at elevated risk for non‐response, enabling proactive treatment modification. Possible augmentation strategies for high‐Disinhibition individuals might include additional individual therapy sessions with a focus on behavioural activation and structure, more intensive between‐session support such as telephone coaching or adjunctive pharmacotherapy targeting impulsivity. Future research may seek to systematically evaluate whether Disinhibition‐targeted modifications to standard DBT improve outcomes for this subgroup.

Early Symptom Change as a Predictor of Recovery

The finding that early improvement in suicidal ideation was the strongest predictor of CSC at post‐treatment, with an OR of 1.55 and 91% overall classification accuracy, is both empirically robust and clinically actionable. Each unit of improvement on the BSSI from baseline to Week 6 increased the odds of achieving CSC at Week 12 by 55%, and this effect was maintained above and beyond the contributions of treatment intensity and personality traits. This finding is consistent with the broader early response literature (Lutz et al. 2001) and extends it to DBT for suicidal ideation in a transdiagnostic sample.

The practical implications of this finding are considerable. The Week 6 midtreatment assessment in a 12‐week programme represents a natural decision point at which clinicians can evaluate whether individuals are on a trajectory towards meaningful recovery. Individuals who have not demonstrated early improvement in suicidal ideation by midtreatment are substantially less likely to achieve CSC, suggesting that this is an appropriate juncture to consider treatment modification. Possible responses to early non‐response include intensifying treatment (e.g., adding individual therapy for a patient in the low‐intensity track), augmenting with crisis support and modifying the skills training focus.

These findings also align with the emerging framework of measurement‐based care, which advocates for routine outcome monitoring throughout treatment as a driver of clinical decision‐making (Scott and Lewis 2015). The present results provide specific empirical support for using the BSSI at Week 6 as a decision‐relevant clinical indicator in the context of DBT. Importantly, the high classification accuracy of the model (91%) suggests that early BSSI change is not merely a statistical predictor but suggests potential utility as a clinical indicator for identifying individuals who may be unlikely to achieve meaningful recovery under current treatment conditions.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgement. First, the nonrandomized triage of participants into intensity tracks limits causal inference about the effects of treatment intensity. In addition, the absence of a no‐treatment or alternative‐treatment control condition precludes conclusions regarding whether the observed reductions in suicidal ideation were attributable specifically to DBT rather than factors such as natural symptom fluctuation, regression to the mean or non‐specific treatment effects. Although the primary aim of the present study was to compare outcomes across levels of DBT treatment intensity rather than to establish the efficacy of DBT itself, future research incorporating an appropriate comparison condition would permit stronger conclusions regarding the source of observed symptom change. Although baseline comparisons revealed no significant differences on the BSSI or PiCD domains across tracks, the possibility of unmeasured confounders, including clinical severity variables not captured by the study measures, cannot be excluded. Randomized designs assigning individuals to different DBT intensity conditions over identical time periods would allow stronger causal conclusions regarding the effects of treatment intensity and whether outcomes differ across lower and higher intensity formats. Studies examining the mechanisms through which early change occurs in DBT, including skills acquisition, therapeutic alliance and changes in emotion regulation, would also be beneficial to clarify whether early BSSI reduction reflects these processes or is primarily a proxy for baseline severity.

Second, the complexity of the treatment environment introduces ambiguity in the interpretation of the treatment intensity. Treatment intensity was not independent of treatment composition, as the tracks differed in both the frequency and type of therapeutic contact provided. Specifically, the mid‐ and high‐intensity tracks included individual therapy in addition to group‐based skills training, whereas the low‐intensity track did not. Consequently, the independent effects of treatment modality, frequency of therapeutic contact and overall therapeutic exposure cannot be disentangled in the present design. Future studies employing more balanced treatment conditions, for example, comparing skills training alone, individual therapy alone and combinations of these modalities at varying frequencies, would help isolate the relative contributions of these treatment characteristics to clinical outcomes. Moreover, although DBT was the primary intervention within the IDT programme, it was not the only therapeutic modality available, and participants in the low‐intensity track were required to maintain contact with an external care provider at least twice monthly. The type and extent of this external care were not systematically documented; consequently, participants' overall therapeutic exposure may not have been fully captured by their assigned treatment track. Future studies should systematically document all therapeutic contacts, including those occurring outside the primary treatment programme, to more accurately quantify treatment intensity. The relatively small sample sizes within each treatment intensity track, coupled with the small number of participants who achieved CSC, may have limited statistical power to detect between‐group differences in this outcome. Third, the study lacked a measure of DBT treatment adherence or fidelity. It is unknown whether DBT group leaders delivered the treatment consistently and to protocol across tracks, and variation in treatment fidelity could confound intensity comparisons.

Fourth, the sample was predominantly White and female and was drawn from a single tertiary care hospital in a major Canadian city, limiting the generalizability of findings to other demographic groups and clinical settings. Additionally, outcomes were not examined according to specific diagnostic profiles or patterns of comorbidity. Although participants met eligibility criteria based on primary mood‐, anxiety‐ or trauma‐related disorders, many also presented with co‐occurring personality pathology or substance use disorders. Consequently, it remains unclear whether particular diagnostic subgroups responded differently to treatment intensity. Future research examining outcomes across more clearly defined diagnostic subgroups and more diverse populations would help clarify the extent to which diagnostic composition influences response to varying DBT treatment intensities.

Fifth, the 12‐week observation period may have been insufficient to capture personality‐related differences in longer term treatment trajectories, particularly for higher order outcomes. Accordingly, extended follow‐up studies are needed to determine whether CSC achieved within a 12‐week programme is maintained over time, and whether treatment intensity is associated with differential longer term outcomes, even when significant differences are not detected over the short term.

Conclusions

The present study represents the first systematic examination of DBT treatment intensity in relation to suicidal ideation outcomes and the first evaluation of PiCD personality domains as moderators of change in suicidal ideation. Suicidal ideation decreased significantly across all three intensity levels over 12 weeks of treatment, with no significant differences in trajectories across treatment intensity levels. Although rates of CSC also did not differ significantly across treatment intensity levels, the relatively small number of participants achieving CSC limits conclusions regarding differences in clinically meaningful recovery across tracks. These findings highlight the need for further research examining whether lower intensity DBT formats may be appropriate for some individuals, with potential implications for treatment accessibility and resource allocation. Disinhibition emerged as a significant barrier to clinically meaningful recovery, suggesting that personality‐informed treatment planning and targeted augmentation may enhance outcomes for individuals with high impulsivity and behavioural dyscontrol. Moreover, early symptom improvement was the strongest predictor of CSC, with clinically meaningful classification accuracy, highlighting the importance of routine outcome monitoring at midtreatment as a driver of real‐time clinical decision‐making. Together, these findings advance our understanding of the conditions under which DBT produces clinically meaningful recovery from suicidal ideation and may provide a foundation for more individualized, data‐driven approaches to DBT delivery.
