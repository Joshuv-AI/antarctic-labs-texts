Passive objects in a chiral active bath: from micro to macro
Cory Hargus, Federico Ghimenti, Julien Tailleur, Frédéric van Wijland

Abstract
We present a detailed derivation of the Langevin dynamics obeyed by a massive rigid body immersed in a chiral active bath. We show how the antisymmetric nature of the noise leads to an unusual relationship between the Langevin equation describing stochastic trajectories and the Fokker-Planck equation governing the time-evolution of the probability density. The chirality of the bath endows the object dynamics with odd diffusivity, odd mobility, and rotational ratchet effects that depend on the object symmetries. For rotationally-symmetric objects, we show that a hidden time-reversal symmetry leads to separate effective equilibrium descriptions for the translational and rotational degrees of freedom. Finally, starting from the bath dynamics, we construct a multipole expansion to quadrupolar order that allows predicting the far-field current and density modulation induced by the object on the bath.

II
Effective Langevin equation for the object

II.1
Setting the stage

Given a rigid object of arbitrary shape immersed in a two-dimensional bath of chiral active particles, we build a stochastic description of the object dynamics by integrating out the bath degrees of freedom in the adiabatic limit of large object mass
M
and moment of inertial
I
. To do so, we consider the Newtonian dynamics of the object given by

\begin{split}\dot{\bm{R}}&=\frac{1}{M}{\bm{P}}\,,\\
\dot{\bm{P}}&={\bm{F}}\,,\\
\end{split}\quad\begin{split}\dot{\Theta}&=\frac{1}{I}L\,,\\
\dot{L}&=\Gamma\,.\end{split}

(1)

Here,
{\bm{R}}
is the position of the object’s center of mass,
{\bm{P}}
its momentum,
\Theta
its orientation with respect to the
x
-axis, and
L
its angular momentum.
{\bm{F}}
and
\Gamma
are the total force and torque exerted on the object due to pairwise interactions between the object and the bath particles. We model the latter as chiral active Brownian particles, which evolve as

\begin{split}\gamma\dot{\bm{r}}_{i}&={\bm{F}}_{i}+f_{0}{\bm{u}}(\theta_{i})\,,\\
\dot{\theta}_{i}&=\omega_{0}+\sqrt{2D_{r}}\xi_{i}\,,\end{split}

(2)

where
\gamma
is the friction from the substrate,
f_{0}{\bm{u}}
is the self-propulsion force oriented along
{\bm{u}}(\theta_{i})=[\cos(\theta_{i}),\sin(\theta_{i})]^{T}
,
\omega_{0}
is a constant angular drift,
D_{r}
is the rotational diffusivity, and
\xi_{i}
is a centered, unit-variance Gaussian white noise.
Finally,
{\bm{F}}_{i}
is the force on bath particle
i
due to interactions with the object, satisfying
\sum_{i}{\bm{F}}_{i}=-{\bm{F}}
. In the absence of collisions with the object, the bath particles self-propel with speed
v_{0}=f_{0}/\gamma
, and are characterized by two active lengthscales. The persistence length
\ell_{p}=\frac{f_{0}}{D_{r}\gamma}
describes how far the particle travels before losing memory of its direction, while the gyroradius
\ell_{g}=\frac{f_{0}}{|\omega_{0}|\gamma}
describes the average curvature of trajectories due to chirality.

The evolution of an observable

O(t)=O\big({\bm{R}}(t),{\bm{P}}(t),\Theta(t),L(t),{\bm{r}}(t)^{N},\theta(t)^{N}\big)\,,

(3)

which depends on the object and bath degrees of freedom can be computed using the Itō formula. Following
[
66
]
, it can be written in terms of the linear equation

\partial_{t}O(t)=\left[\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}\right]O(t)\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}
are evolution operators acting respectively on the object and the bath degrees of freedom:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}&=\frac{{\bm{P}}}{M}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}+{\bm{F}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{P}}}+\frac{L}{I}\partial_{\Theta}+\Gamma\partial_{L}\,,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}&=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\gamma}[{\bm{F}}_{i}+f_{0}{\bm{u}}(\theta_{i})]\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}\\
&+\sum_{i}[D_{r}\partial_{\theta_{i}}+\omega_{0}+\xi_{i}(t)]\partial_{\theta_{i}}\,.\end{split}

(5)

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}
depends on time through the realizations of the noise
\xi_{i}(t)
. The Laplacian terms
\partial_{\theta_{i}}
appearing in the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}
are a consequence of Itō’ s lemma. This formalism is analogous to the one involving Liouville operators for Hamiltonian dynamics in the original Mori-Zwanzig formalism
[
5
,
6
]
, where the noise is treated as an external time-dependent force.

Writing the evolution of
O(t)
by means of linear operators allows us to give a formal solution to Eq. (
4
), namely

O(t)=\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}](t,0)O(0)\,,

(6)

where the initial condition
O(0)
is propagated in time by means of the evolution operator
\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}](t,0)
. The latter is defined as

\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}](t,0)\equiv\exp\left[\mathcal{T}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}(\tau)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}]\right]\,,

(7)

where
\mathcal{T}
is a time-ordering operator, placing earliest times to the right and latest ones to the left
[
67
]
. This is consistent with, Eq. (
6
) being a solution to Eq. (
4
). Within this formalism, the equations of motion of the translational and angular momentum of the object become

\begin{split}\dot{\bm{P}}(t)&=\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}](t,0){\bm{F}}(0)\\
\dot{L}(t)&=\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}](t,0)\Gamma(0)\,.\end{split}

(8)

In the spirit of Mazur and Oppenheim
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
and van Kampen
[
7
]
, we now integrate out the bath degrees of freedom in Eq. (
8
), transforming it into a closed non-Markovian equation of motion for the object. We work in the regime where the motion of bath is fast compared to motion of the object, such that the timescales associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}
are well-separated. In this regime, we will show that the non-Markovian equation takes the form of a stochastic differential equation, where the action of the bath on the object is decomposed into average forces and torques, an effective friction, and fluctuating forces and torques with Gaussian statistics. As the separation of timescales becomes very large, the effect of the bath on the object is completely determined by the instantaneous configuration of the latter. The effective Langevin equation then becomes Markovian. In this regime, the friction coefficient and the statistics of the random force can be computed from the long-time dynamics of the bath particles with the object held fixed.

To formalize this adiabatic regime, we introduce the dimensionless parameter

\epsilon\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{MD_{r}}}\,,

(9)

which we will make small by taking
M
large to reach the adiabatic limit
1
1

1

We can define an effective mass of an achiral ABP as follows: an ABP with self-propulsion speed
v_{0}=f_{0}/\gamma
transfers a momentum
\Delta p=\gamma v_{0}/D_{r}
during a collision with an obstacle
[
71
]
, which suggests and effective mass of the bath particles
m_{\text{b}}\equiv\Delta p/v_{0}=\gamma/D_{r}
, and thus
\epsilon\equiv\sqrt{\frac{m_{\text{b}}}{M}}
.
.
We assume that
M\propto I
, so that adiabaticity in the translational motion of the object coincides with adiabaticity in its rotational motion.
We work for convenience with units such that
\gamma=D_{r}=1
. We then introduce a rescaled time
t^{*}\equiv\epsilon^{2}t
, a rescaled mass
M^{*}\equiv\epsilon^{2}M
, a rescaled momentum
{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})\equiv\epsilon{\bm{P}}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})
, a rescaled angular momentum
L^{*}(t^{*})\equiv\epsilon L(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})
, and a rescaled moment of inertia
I^{*}=\epsilon^{2}I
. With these rescaled variables Eq. (
8
) becomes

\begin{split}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t^{*}}{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})&\equiv\dot{\bm{P}}^{*}=\epsilon^{-1}\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},0){\bm{F}}(0)\\
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t^{*}}L^{*}(t^{*})&\equiv\dot{L}^{*}=\epsilon^{-1}\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},0)\Gamma(0)\,.\end{split}

(10)

with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}=\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}}{M^{*}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}+{\bm{F}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{P}}^{*}}+\frac{L^{*}}{I^{*}}\partial_{\Theta}+\Gamma\partial_{L^{*}}
. Equation (
10
) is the starting point of our coarse-graining procedure. At the end of the analysis we will revert back to the unscaled variables. Sections
II.2
through
II.5
contain a pedagogical derivation of our results and the reader is invited, in a first reading, to jump directly to Sec.
II.6
, where the resulting dynamics for the object is presented.

II.2
Projection operator formalism

To obtain an effective Langevin equation from Eq. (
10
) we need to integrate out the degrees of freedom of the active chiral bath. To proceed, we note that the observable
O(t)
defined in Eq. (
3
) can be seen using Eq. (
6
) as a function of
{\bm{R}}(0),{\bm{P}}(0),\Theta(0),L(0),{\bm{r}}(0)^{N},\theta(0)^{N}
at an initial time
t=0
and of the realization of the noises
\xi_{i}(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}\in[0,t]
. We then define an operator
\mathcal{P}
which acts on
O(t)
as

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}O(t)&\equiv\Biggl\langle\int\text{d}{\bm{r}}^{N}\text{d}\theta^{N}\rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N},\theta^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta)O(t)\Biggr\rangle\\
&\equiv\langle O(t)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,\end{split}

(11)

where
\rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N},\theta^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta)
weights the bath initial conditions at
t=0
and the average
\langle\ldots\rangle
is carried over the realizations of the noises
\xi_{i}(t^{\prime}\in[0,t])
. As a result,
\mathcal{P}O(t)
is a function of
{\bm{R}}(0),{\bm{P}}(0),\Theta(0),L(0)
.
We choose
\rho_{\text{b}}
such that it corresponds to the steady-state distribution of the bath for a fixed position of the object, i.e.

\langle\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)\rho_{\text{b}}\rangle=0\,,

(12)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)
is the operator adjoint to
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}
. Further, the operator
\mathcal{P}
is a projector because
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}
. Noting that

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}=0\,,

(13)

we see that
\mathcal{P}
can be physically interpreted as a projector along a direction orthogonal to that of the evolution of the bath degrees of freedom induced by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{Q}
the projection operator orthogonal to
\mathcal{P}
, defined by
\mathcal{Q}\equiv 1-\mathcal{P}
.

With this choice of
\mathcal{P}
, the projected observable behaves as though the bath relaxes instantaneously to its steady state distribution with the object degrees of freedom effectively frozen. The space over which
\mathcal{P}
projects is thus tailored to capture the dynamical regime with widely separated timescales.

Our goal is to transform Eq. (
10
) into an effective Langevin equation for the object. Using the operator identity
[
67
]

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{A}](t,0)&=\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}](t,0)\\
&-\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{A}](t,\tau)\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}](\tau,0)\,,\end{split}

(14)

with
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}
,
\mathcal{B}=-\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}(t)^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger})=-\epsilon\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})=\epsilon^{-1}{\bm{F}}^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})

(15)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}{\bm{F}}^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\,,

and

\displaystyle\dot{L}^{*}(t^{*})=\epsilon^{-1}\Gamma^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})

(16)

\displaystyle+\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}\Gamma^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\,.

We have thus transformed the dynamics of
\dot{\bm{P}}^{*}
and
\dot{L}^{*}
into closed non-Markovian ones, involving a projected force
{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}})](\tau,0){\bm{F}}(0)
and a projected torque
\Gamma^{+}(\tau)\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}})](\tau,0)\Gamma(0)
. The projected force and torque can be thought of as stochastic variables, whose randomness stems both from the bath initial condition and from the realizations of the noises acting on the bath in
[0,t]
.

The statistics of
{\bm{F}}^{+}
and
\Gamma^{+}
are very complicated in the general case. However, in the adiabatic limit, we will show that the instantaneous statistics of
{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)
and
\Gamma^{+}(\tau)
become identical to the statistics of
{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}](\tau,0){\bm{F}}(0)
and
\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}](\tau,0)\Gamma(0)
, which are respectively the force and the torque exerted by the bath on the object when the latter is held fixed and the bath degrees of freedom are allowed to evolve for a time interval
\tau
. The breaking of time-reversal and parity symmetry by the bath allows a nonzero average value of
{\bm{F}}_{0}
and
\Gamma_{0}
, yielding translational and rotational ratchet effects. In the adiabatic limit, we will show that the fluctuations of the stochastic force and torque around these mean values are Gaussian and that the second term in Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) are friction terms. We now substantiate this picture with the calculation of the statistics of
{\bm{F}}^{+}
and
\Gamma^{+}
in the adiabatic limit.

II.3
The projected force

The deviation of the projected force
{\bm{F}}^{+}
from the force acting on a fixed object
{\bm{F}}_{0}
can be systematically expanded in powers of the adiabaticity parameter
\epsilon
. The details of this derivation are given in Appendix
A
. To lowest order in
\epsilon
, we find

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{F}}^{+}(t)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}(t)\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)\\
&=\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}[\Theta(0)]+O(\epsilon)\,,\end{split}

(17)

where we have made explicit that
\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}
solely depends on the orientation of the object at time
0
, due to translational invariance.
We also note that
\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}[\Theta(0)]
does not depend on the noises
\xi(t^{\prime}\in[0,t])
so that
\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}[\Theta(0)]
reduces to a steady-state average computation of
{\bm{F}}_{0}
with the object held fixed.
The average value of
{\bm{F}}^{+}(t)
thus matches the average, steady-state force exerted on the object by the bath when the object is held fixed. We obtain similarly, for the torque,

\langle\Gamma^{+}\rangle_{\text{b}}=\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)\,\,,

(18)

where we note that
\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}
is independent of both
\Theta(0)
and
{\bm{R}}(0)
.

To characterize the fluctuations of
{\bm{F}}^{+}(t)
and
\Gamma^{+}
, it is useful to introduce

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv{\bm{F}}_{0}-\left\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\right\rangle_{\text{b}},\qquad\delta\Gamma_{0}\equiv\Gamma_{0}-\left\langle\Gamma\right\rangle_{\text{b}}

(19)

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}

\displaystyle\equiv{\bm{F}}^{+}-\left\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\right\rangle_{\text{b}},\qquad\delta\Gamma^{+}\equiv\Gamma^{+}-\left\langle\Gamma_{0}\right\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.

(20)

The two-point correlator of the random force fluctuations
\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}
is shown in Appendix
A
to obey

\langle\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)\,.

(21)

Similar results hold for higher-order moments of
\delta{\bm{F}}
, for
\delta\Gamma^{+}
, and for their cross-correlations.

The results presented here rely on the assumption that the dynamics of the bath around a fixed object is ergodic, and that the decay of correlation functions of physical observables happens sufficiently fast. This assumption breaks down in the thermodynamic limit of an infinitely large bath, due to the slow relaxation of its hydrodynamic modes
[
72
,
73
]
. We thus expect our theory to apply when the large-mass limit is taken before the large-system-size limit.

The effective dynamics of Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})&=\epsilon^{-1}\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}(\Theta(0))+\epsilon^{-1}\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})+\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}{\bm{F}}^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\,\\
\dot{L}^{*}(t^{*})&=\epsilon^{-1}\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}+\epsilon^{-1}\delta\Gamma_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})+\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}\Gamma^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\,.\end{split}

(22)

The statistics of the random force
\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}
and torque
\delta\Gamma_{0}
in the adiabatic limit is what we determine next.

II.4
Statistics of the random force in the adiabatic limit

By definition,
\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})\rangle_{\text{b}}=\mathbf{0}
. The two-point correlation functions evaluated at different times
t_{1}^{*}
and
t_{2}^{*}
are, in the adiabatic limit,

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*}_{1})\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*}_{2})\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}(t^{*}_{2}-t^{*}_{1}))\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}\otimes\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}=\mathbf{0}\,.\end{split}

(23)

Here we made use of the stationarity of the dynamics of the bath and of the assumption that for widely separated times the correlations in the bath vanish. Note that the last equality is valid as long as the relaxation time of the bath diverges slower than
\epsilon^{-2}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. This is trivially true in our case, since the adiabatic limit is implemented through a large object mass so that the bath relaxation time remains finite in this limit.

Equation (
23
) shows that the two-point correlator of
\epsilon^{-1}\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}
vanishes when evaluated at different times in the adiabatic limit. In addition, integrating the autocorrelation functions over the intervals
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,+\infty)
gives

\begin{split}\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\equiv\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\,,\\
\epsilon^{-2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}\equiv\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\,.\end{split}

(24)

This defines the noise correlation matrix
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
, which need not be symmetric in general so that one may have
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\neq\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
. Equations (
23
) and (
24
) can be written in a compact form as

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\delta_{+}(\tau)+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\delta_{-}(\tau)\,.

(25)

Here the distributions
\delta_{\pm}(t)
are the two halves of the Dirac distribution. With a test function
f(t)
which is continuous at
t=0
, these can be defined by

\displaystyle 2\delta(\tau)

\displaystyle=\delta_{-}(\tau)+\delta_{+}(\tau)\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\delta_{+}(\tau)f(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}\tau\,\delta_{-}(\tau)f(\tau)=f(0)\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}\tau\,\delta_{+}(\tau)f(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\delta_{-}(\tau)f(\tau)=0\,.

(28)

Analogously, we can compute the torque correlations and the cross correlations

\begin{split}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta\Gamma(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\delta\Gamma_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\lambda_{LL}\delta(\tau)\,,\\
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\delta\Gamma(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}\delta_{+}(\tau)+\bm{\lambda}_{L{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\delta_{-}(\tau)\,,\end{split}

(29)

with the noise correlations
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}
,
\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}_{L{\bm{P}}}
, and
\lambda_{LL}
defined as

\begin{split}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\delta\Gamma_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,\\
\bm{\lambda}_{L{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,\\
\lambda_{LL}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\delta\Gamma_{0}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.\end{split}

(30)

To express the fluctuating force and torque in a compact way we introduce a stochastic noise
\epsilon^{-1}\bm{\xi}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)
in the extended space of the object’s linear and angular momentum

\begin{split}\epsilon^{-1}\bm{\xi}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)&\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon^{-1}\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\\
\epsilon^{-1}\delta\Gamma(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon^{-1}\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\\
\epsilon^{-1}\xi_{L}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\end{bmatrix}\\
&\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}^{*}_{\bm{P}}(\tau)\\
\xi^{*}_{L}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\equiv\bm{\xi}^{*}(\tau)\,,\end{split}

(31)

with correlations

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\langle\epsilon^{-2}\bm{\xi}(0)\otimes\bm{\xi}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\bm{\lambda}\delta_{+}(\tau)+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}\delta_{-}(\tau)\,,\\

(32)

which define the diffusivity matrix
\bm{\lambda}
as

\bm{\lambda}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}&\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}\\
\bm{\lambda}_{L{\bm{P}}}&\lambda_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(33)

Finally, in the adiabatic limit the statistics of the noise
\bm{\xi}
is Gaussian as shown in Appendix
B
. Note that Eq. (
26
) shows that when
\bm{\lambda}
is symmetric,
\bm{\xi}
is a standard Gaussian white noise. With the statistics of
\bm{\xi}
at hand, we now show that, in the adiabatic limit, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
22
) contributes a friction term to the object dynamics.

II.5
The friction matrix

We now consider the time integral appearing in Eq. (
22
) for the translational momentum
{\bm{P}}^{*}
, namely

\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\epsilon^{-2}t^{*},\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}{\bm{F}}^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\,.

(34)

In Appendix
C
, we show that, in the adiabatic limit, this integral becomes

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}

\displaystyle\big[\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))-\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}(\Theta^{*}(0))\big]

\displaystyle-\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})}{M^{*}}-\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))\frac{L^{*}(t^{*})}{I^{*}}\,,

(35)

where
\Theta^{*}(t^{*})\equiv\Theta(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})
is the orientation of the object measured in rescaled time, and the linear momentum and torque friction coefficients
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}
are given by Agarwal formulae

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\;.

(37)

Using this and Eq. (
31
), we rewrite Eq. (
22
) as a Langevin equation for the linear momentum:

\begin{split}\dot{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})&=\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))-\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})}{M^{*}}-\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}\frac{L^{*}(t^{*})}{I^{*}}\\
&+\bm{\xi}^{*}_{{\bm{P}}}(t^{*})\;.\end{split}

(38)

A similar analysis can be carried out to study the memory term in the angular-momentum dynamics, to find

\dot{L}^{*}(t^{*})=\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}-\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{L{\bm{P}}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})}{M^{*}}-\zeta_{LL}\frac{L^{*}(t^{*})}{I^{*}}+\xi^{*}_{L}(t^{*})\,,

(39)

where the friction coefficients
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}
and
\zeta_{LL}
read, respectively

\begin{split}\zeta_{LL}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.\end{split}

(40)

In the next section, we revert to the original coordinates of the system and we summarize the results of the projection operator approach.

II.6
Final result

We reintroduce the original momenta
{\bm{P}}(t)=\epsilon^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{*}(\epsilon^{2}t)
,
L(t)=\epsilon^{-1}L^{*}(\epsilon^{2}t)
, mass and moment of inertia
M=\epsilon^{-2}M^{*}
,
I=\epsilon^{-2}I
and time
t\equiv\epsilon^{-2}t^{*}
. We drop the index
0
from the stochastic force
{\bm{F}}_{0}
and torque
\Gamma_{0}
. We can thus rewrite Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
) as

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\bm{P}}(t)\\
\dot{L}(t)\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\end{bmatrix}-\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}&\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}\\
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}&\zeta_{LL}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\zeta}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{M}{\bm{P}}\\
\frac{1}{I}L\end{bmatrix}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}(t)\\
\xi_{L}(t)\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\xi}}\,,\end{split}

(41)

where
\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}
and
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}
are the steady-state average force and torque exerted on an object held fixed.

The noise
\bm{\xi}(t)
is a Gaussian noise with mean
\langle\bm{\xi}(t)\rangle=\mathbf{0}
and correlations

\langle\bm{\xi}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\bm{\lambda}\delta_{+}(t-t^{\prime})+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}\delta_{-}(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(42)

where the functions
\delta_{\pm}(t)
have been defined below Eq. (
25
). As shown in Sec.
II.4
, the noise correlations are characterized by a
3\times 3
momentum diffusivity matrix
\bm{\lambda}
given by the Green-Kubo formula
[
74
,
75
]

\bm{\lambda}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\begin{bmatrix}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\delta\Gamma(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
\langle\delta\Gamma(\tau)\delta{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&\langle\delta\Gamma(\tau)\delta\Gamma(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(43)

Likewise, as shown in Sec.
II.5
the
3\times 3
friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}(\Theta)
is given by an Agarwal formula
[
11
]

\bm{\zeta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\text{d}\tau\begin{bmatrix}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
\langle\delta\Gamma(\tau)\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}&\langle\delta\Gamma(\tau)\partial_{\Theta}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\end{bmatrix}\hskip-2.84526pt\,.

(44)

Note that the average force
\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}(t)
, and the friction and momentum diffusivity matrices depend on the object orientation at time
t
,
\Theta(t)
.

The effective Langevin equation that describes the adiabatic dynamics of an object in a chiral active bath given by Eq. (
41
), together with the expressions of the friction and diffusion matrices in terms of correlation functions, is one of the main results of this paper.

Let us now discuss the properties of the probability current of the object’s degrees of freedom, and the evolution of their associated probability density predicted by our theory.

II.7
Noise, currents and probabilities

The noise described by Eq. (
42
) differs from the usual Gaussian white noise appearing in the classical derivation of Brownian motion
[
68
]
. This is due to the chiral nature of the bath, and requires some careful considerations when the adiabatic limit is taken, as also happens when considering the overdamped limit of equilibrium Langevin dynamics in an external magnetic field
[
76
]
. The correlator of Eq. (
42
) is to be understood as the Markovian limit, implemented by sending the parameter
\epsilon
in Eq. (
9
) to 0, of a Gaussian process
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)
:

\langle\bm{\xi}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\rangle\,.

(45)

The memory kernel of
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)
decays over a time scaled by
\epsilon^{2}
, consistently with the scaling adopted in the projection operator approach. In many cases, computations have to be carried out using a finite
\epsilon
, before taking the
\epsilon\to 0
limit. This is for instance the case when computing the entropy production rate as done in Section
VI
. We expect our results to be independent of the specific form of the correlations of
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}
, as long as Eq. (
45
) is respected.

Next, from Eq. (
41
), we can define and derive a probability current. We introduce a generalized coordinates system encompassing both translational and rotational degrees of freedom, defining the momentum
{\bm{W}}=[{\bm{P}},L]^{\mathrm{T}}
, force
\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle=[\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}},\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
, and mass matrix
\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{diag}([M,M,I])
.
Equation (
41
) is then expressed as

\begin{split}\dot{\bm{W}}&=\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}-\mathcal{M}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}\cdot{\bm{W}}+\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\\
&=\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}-\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}\cdot{\bm{W}}+\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\,,\end{split}

(46)

where in the second line we absorb the mass matrix into the friction matrix, defining
\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}\equiv\mathcal{M}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}
. Let
\bm{\nu}(t)\equiv[{\bm{R}}(t),\Theta(t),{\bm{W}}(t)]^{\mathrm{T}}
denote the set of degrees of freedom of the object. We define the current of the system
{\bm{J}}(\bm{\nu},t)
as

{\bm{J}}(\bm{\nu},t)\equiv\langle\dot{\bm{\nu}}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle\,,

(47)

The current
{\bm{J}}(\bm{\nu},t)
contains a term of the form
\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle
, which can be computed using the Novikov relation
[
77
]

\begin{split}\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle&=-\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\rangle\\
&\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{W}}}\left\langle\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)}\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\right\rangle\,.\end{split}

(48)

We have introduced the notation
\left[\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)}\right]_{ij}\equiv\frac{\delta W_{i}(t)}{\delta\xi_{\epsilon,j}(\tau)}
. Using the fact that in the adiabatic limit the statistics of
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}
converges to the one of
\bm{\xi}(t)
, we show in Appendix
D
that, as
\epsilon\to 0
, Eq. (
48
) reduces to

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle=-\bm{\lambda}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{W}}\rho_{\text{o}}(\bm{\nu},t)\,,

(49)

where
\rho_{\text{o}}(\bm{\nu},t)\equiv\langle\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t)\rangle
is the probability density of the object degrees of freedom.
The current associated with Eq. (
41
) in the adiabatic limit is thus

{\bm{J}}(\bm{\nu},t)\equiv\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{P}}/M\\
L/I\\
\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}-\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}{\bm{W}}-\bm{\lambda}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{W}}\end{bmatrix}\rho_{\text{o}}(\bm{\nu},t)\,.

(50)

In situations with Gaussian white noise with symmetric correlation matrices, the current
{\bm{J}}
is readily related to the probability density
\rho_{\text{o}}
through a Fokker-Planck equation,
\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{o}}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{\nu}}\cdot{\bm{J}}
. In this case, however, the singular nature of the adiabatic limit demands a more careful treatment. To obtain a Fokker-Planck equation for
\rho_{\text{o}}
we consider a time-coarse graining of the effective Langevin equation (
46
)

\overline{\dot{\bm{W}}}=\overline{\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}}-\overline{\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}{\bm{W}}}+\overline{\bm{\xi}(t)}\,,

(51)

where, for any time dependent vector
\text{\bf v}(t)
, we have introduced a time-coarse grained vector
\overline{\text{\bf v}(t)}\equiv\lim_{\delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{2\delta t}\int_{t-\delta t}^{t+\delta t}\text{d}\tau\text{\bf v}(\tau)
. Using Eq. (
42
), we see that the correlations of the time-coarse grained noise are given by

\langle\overline{\bm{\xi}(t)}\otimes\overline{\bm{\xi}(t^{\prime})}\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\left[\bm{\lambda}+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\equiv 2\delta(t-t^{\prime})\bm{\lambda}_{S}\,,

(52)

The time-coarse grained noise has a symmetric correlation matrix
\bm{\lambda}_{S}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left[\bm{\lambda}+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]
. We can thus write a Fokker-Planck equation
[
78
]
for the probability density associated with the time-coarse grained-variables

\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{o}}(\overline{\bm{\nu}},t)=-\bm{\nabla}_{\overline{\bm{\nu}}}\cdot\overline{{\bm{J}}}(\overline{\bm{\nu}},t)\,,

(53)

where the coarse-grained probability current is defined as

\overline{{\bm{J}}}(\overline{\bm{\nu}},t)\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\overline{{\bm{P}}}/M\\
\overline{L}/I\\
\overline{\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}}-\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}\overline{{\bm{W}}}+\bm{\lambda}_{S}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{W}}\end{bmatrix}\rho_{\text{o}}(\overline{\bm{\nu}},t)\,.

(54)

Only the symmetric part of the correlation matrix thus contributes to the Fokker-Planck equation. This loss of information is a byproduct of the time-coarse-graining procedure. The Fokker-Planck equation (
53
) correctly describes the evolution of the probability density of the object degrees of freedom. However, since the dynamics of the object is, in essence, non Markovian, the correct expression for the probability current is instead given by Eq. (
50
). We note again that these subtleties are very similar to those reported in
[
76
]
for the study of an underdamped charged particle in a magnetic field.

With the Fokker-Planck equation and an expression for the current of the system at hand, we now have a complete framework to study the motion of the object.

III
How object symmetries impact transport parameters

We now discuss the impact of the object shape on its dynamics, explaining under what circumstances certain terms in Eq. (
41
) vanish due to symmetry, as summarized in Table
1
.
A circular object (i.e. a disk) is isotropic, being invariant under rotation by any angle. These rotations form the special orthogonal group
SO(2)
.
The steady-state distribution of the bath is isotropic surrounding a disk, implying that
\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Moreover, in the absence of tangential contact forces (which are not considered in our model), the bath exerts no torque on a disk, and thus its angular variables are irrelevant. Its motion is then entirely described by
{\bm{R}}
and
{\bm{P}}
, with the
2\times 2
friction and force correlation matrices

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\langle{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\langle{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes{\bm{F}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.

(56)

The most general isotropic form of these matrices is

\begin{split}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}&=\zeta_{\parallel}\mathbf{1}+\zeta_{\perp}\text{\bf A}\,,\\
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}&=\lambda_{\parallel}\mathbf{1}+\lambda_{\perp}\text{\bf A}\,,\end{split}

(57)

with
\mathbf{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}

and
\text{\bf A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
.
Here,
\zeta_{\perp}
is the odd friction, causing the object to deflect in the direction perpendicular to its motion, and
\lambda_{\perp}
is the odd momentum diffusivity.
These can be seen from Eqs. (
55
) and (
56
) to require breaking time-reversal and parity symmetries, as satisfied by the chiral active bath. By the same symmetry considerations, objects exhibiting nonzero
\zeta_{\perp}
also exhibit odd diffusivity
D_{\perp}
and odd mobility
\mu_{\perp}
, as described in the following two sections.

For other shapes, the angular variables
\Theta
and
L
are also needed to fully characterize the state of the object. We specifically identify two relevant symmetries. The first is an
n
-fold rotational symmetry, denoted by
C_{n}
, and meaning the object is invariant to rotations of angles
\frac{2\pi}{n}
, with
n\geq 2
an integer. The second is parity (i.e. reflection) symmetry, which we denote by
\Pi
. Examples of objects obeying or breaking these symmetries are portrayed in Table
1
, together with a characterization of their dynamics.

Objects in
C_{n}
exhibit odd dynamics in a chiral active bath. In the adiabatic limit, they have fully independent translational and rotational degrees of freedom:
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}
,
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}
,
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}
, and
\bm{\lambda}_{L{\bm{P}}}
vanish by symmetry. Such objects have no polarity, and thus do not behave as translational ratchets, exhibiting
\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}=0
, but do receive a nonvanishing torque
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\neq 0
from a chiral active bath. Moreover, when a
C_{n}
symmetric object is chiral (i.e. belongs to
C_{n}
but not
\Pi
), it can exhibit odd dynamics and a net torque whether or not the bath is chiral. Thus, to achieve odd dynamics and ratchet torques, the breaking of parity symmetry can be supplied either by the chirality of the object or by the chirality of the active bath.

Objects breaking
C_{n}
symmetry exhibit a net force
\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}
due to their polarity, whether or not the bath is chiral. If they preserve the
\Pi
symmetry and the bath is achiral, the decoupling of rotational and translational degrees of freedom survives the breaking of
C_{n}
symmetry in the adiabatic limit. This is, for instance, the case of a wedge in a bath of active Brownian particles.

Finally, objects with neither
C_{n}
nor
\Pi
symmetries are expected to exhibit the most general form of the dynamics, with odd dynamics, a net torque and force, and all cross-couplings, regardless of the chirality of the bath.

To establish these symmetry claims, we observe that for an object with
C_{n}
symmetry, the steady-state bath probability distribution respects

\rho_{\text{b}}\left({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta+\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)=\rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta)\,,

(58)

with
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
Here, we have defined the marginalized steady-state bath probability density
\rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta)=\int\text{d}\theta^{N}\ \rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N},\theta^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta)
, since the orientations
\theta^{N}
do not affect bath-object interactions in our model, and can thus be ignored in the following treatment.
What follows, however, straightforwardly generalizes to cases where the orientations
\theta^{N}
do affect the interaction force and torque.

With these considerations in mind, the ratchet force then evaluates to

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}=\int\text{d}{\bm{r}}^{N}\ \rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta){\bm{F}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int\text{d}{\bm{r}}^{N}\ \rho_{\text{b}}\left({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta+\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right){\bm{F}}\left({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta+\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}\int\text{d}{\bm{r}}^{N}\ \rho_{\text{b}}({\bm{r}}^{N}|{\bm{R}},\Theta){\bm{F}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}\langle{\bm{F}}\rangle_{\text{b}}=\mathbf{0}\,.

(59)

The second line follows from the isotropy of the bath and holds for an arbitrarily-shaped object.
On the third line, we have used the identity
{\bm{F}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta+\frac{2\pi i}{n})=\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}{\bm{F}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta)
, which holds for any object, where we define the set of
n
rotation operators
\{\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}\}
with
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
Finally, inserting the identity from Eq. (
58
), the ratchet force is seen to vanish for
C_{n}
symmetric objects.

Unlike the force
{\bm{F}}
, the torque
\Gamma
is invariant under rotations by
\frac{2\pi i}{n}
with respect to the bath; that is,
\Gamma({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}},\Theta+\frac{2\pi i}{n})=\Gamma({\bm{r}}^{N})
. Thus, while
C_{n}
objects experience no net force, they do receive a net torque if either the bath or the object is chiral.

By the same principle, dynamical correlations between the fluctuating force and torque are seen to vanish at all times as

\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(t)\delta\Gamma(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(t)\delta\Gamma(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\bm{0}\,,

(60)

because, as in Eq. (
59
), fluctuations in the force vanish statistically when summing over rotations. These considerations are illustrated in Fig.
1
.

As a consequence, the Green-Kubo relations in Eq. (
43
) implies that
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}L}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{L{\bm{P}}}=\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
An identical argument for the correlation functions
\langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}
and
\langle\delta\Gamma(\tau)\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}
appearing in Eq. (
44
) leads to the same result for the frictional cross couplings, i.e.
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}=\mathbf{0}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}=\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}
. For similar reasons, these cross-couplings also vanish for achiral objects. In this case, reflecting the object over its symmetry axis preserves the fluctuation in the force but changes the sign of the torque, so that the correlator vanishes statistically when summing over the original configuration and the (statistically identical) mirror image.

Figure 1:
Three equally probable configurations of an object with
C_{3}
symmetry (blue) interacting with a bath particle (yellow), related by rotations of
\frac{2\pi}{3}
. Averaging over the three configurations yields a net torque but no net force. The same principle also applies to fluctuations of the torque and force, so that the force-torque time correlations vanish and, consequently, translational and rotational degrees of freedom decouple from one another.

This leaves the transport matrices
\bm{\zeta}(\Theta)
and
\bm{\lambda}(\Theta)
with the block structure

\begin{split}\bm{\zeta}(\Theta)&=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}&\zeta_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\,,\\
\bm{\lambda}(\Theta)&=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}&\lambda_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\,.\end{split}

(61)

As such, the translational motion becomes independent of the rotational motion.

Finally, we discuss the effect of inverting the chirality of the bath
\omega_{0}\to-\omega_{0}
on the ratchet effects and the transport matrices. Achiral objects, which can be superimposed on their mirror image, experience no net torque when the active bath is achiral (
\omega_{0}=0
), and thus their direction of rotation is determined solely by the bath chirality.
That is,

\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\xrightarrow{\omega_{0}\rightarrow-\omega_{0}}-\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.

(62)

If the object has both parity and rotational symmetries, inverting the bath chirality leads necessarily to a sign change in the off-diagonal elements of the friction and noise correlation matrices

\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\xrightarrow{\omega_{0}\rightarrow-\omega_{0}}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,,\quad\quad\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\xrightarrow{\omega_{0}\rightarrow-\omega_{0}}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,.

(63)

These relations are invoked in Section
VI
to investigate the time-reversal symmetry behavior of rotationally-symmetric objects.

IV
Diffusion, mobility, and Einstein relations

IV.1
Effective temperatures of rotationally-symmetric objects

In this section, we show that an effective equilibrium steady state exists for isotropic or
C_{n}
objects, such as a disk or a rod, in the adiabatic limit.

For a disk in a chiral active bath, the Fokker-Planck equation (
53
) reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)=\big[-M^{-1}{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\\
&+\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot(\zeta_{\parallel}\mathbf{1}+\zeta_{\perp}\text{\bf A})M^{-1}{\bm{P}}+\lambda_{\parallel}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}^{2}\big]\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}})\,,\end{split}

(64)

where
\lambda_{\perp}
plays no role due to its antisymmetry. In the presence of periodic boundary conditions, the steady state is uniform in
{\bm{R}}
and takes the Boltzmann form:

\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}\propto\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2MT_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}}|{\bm{P}}|^{2}}\,,

(65)

with the translational effective temperature given by:

T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\equiv\frac{\lambda_{\parallel}}{\zeta_{\parallel}}=\frac{1}{2M}\big\langle|{\bm{P}}|^{2}\big\rangle\,.

(66)

Here, and in the following,
\langle\ldots\rangle
indicate an average over
\rho_{\text{o}}
.

We next consider, more broadly, all objects with
C_{n}
rotational symmetry, for which the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},\Theta,L,t)=

\displaystyle\bigg[-\frac{{\bm{P}}}{M}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}+\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)}{M}{\bm{P}}+\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{I}\partial_{\Theta}-\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\partial_{L}+\partial_{L}\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}L+\lambda_{LL}\partial_{L}^{2}\bigg]\rho_{\text{o}}\,.

(67)

In the steady state, the independence of translational and rotational degrees of freedom make
\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}
factorize. The second and third lines of Eq. (
67
) vanish separately and
\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}
is given by the product of two Boltzmann distributions:

\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},\Theta,L)\propto\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2IT_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}}(L-I\Omega)^{2}}\times\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2MT_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}}|{\bm{P}}|^{2}}\,.

(68)

The rotational effective temperature
T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}
is defined by

T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}\equiv\frac{\lambda_{LL}}{\zeta_{LL}}=I^{-1}\big\langle|L-I\Omega|^{2}\big\rangle\,,

(69)

where we have introduced the steady state angular velocity
\Omega
, defined by

\Omega=\frac{\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}}{\zeta_{LL}}\,.

(70)

The steady state solution in Eq. (
68
) requires the symmetric part of the friction and noise correlation matrices
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)
and
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)
to obey the fluctuation-dissipation relation

T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\left(\bm{\zeta}(\Theta)+\bm{\zeta}(\Theta)^{\mathrm{T}}\right)=\left(\bm{\lambda}(\Theta)+\bm{\lambda}(\Theta)^{\mathrm{T}}\right).

(71)

For objects with a
C_{n}
symmetry, the decoupling of translational degrees of freedom from the rotational ones thus gives rise to two independent effective temperatures. In Section
V
, we confirm the thermodynamic role of these temperatures by considering the dynamics of objects trapped in external potentials.

IV.2
Disk diffusivity and mobility

The translational diffusivity matrix of the passive object can be computed from the Green-Kubo relation

\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{transl}}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}t\ \langle{\bm{P}}(t)\otimes{\bm{P}}(0)\rangle\,.

(72)

For disk, isotropy imposes
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{transl}}=D_{\parallel}\mathbf{1}+D_{\perp}\text{\bf A}
, where
D_{\perp}
is the odd diffusivity
[
44
]
.
The Langevin equation for the disk reduces from Eq. (
41
) to

\dot{\bm{R}}=M^{-1}{\bm{P}}\,,\hskip 11.38109pt\dot{\bm{P}}=-M^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}+\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}\,,

(73)

which is solved by

{\bm{P}}(t)=\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}t}{\bm{P}}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \text{e}^{-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(t-\tau)}\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}(\tau)\,.

(74)

The disk momentum autocorrelation function appearing in Eq. (
72
) is then

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{P}}(t)\otimes{\bm{P}}(0)\rangle=\text{e}^{-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}t}\left\langle{\bm{P}}(0)\otimes{\bm{P}}(0)\right\rangle\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \text{e}^{-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(t-\tau)}\left\langle\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}(\tau)\otimes{\bm{P}}(0)\right\rangle\\
=MT_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}e^{-\frac{\zeta_{\parallel}}{M}t}{\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\frac{\zeta_{\perp}}{M}t)&\sin(\frac{\zeta_{\perp}}{M}t)\\
-\sin(\frac{\zeta_{\perp}}{M}t)&\cos(\frac{\zeta_{\perp}}{M}t)\end{bmatrix}}\end{split}

(75)

for all
t>0
. On the final line we have identified
T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}
using Eq. (
66
).
We also exploit the structure of the friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}
given by Eq. (
57
) together with the useful identity

\exp\left({-\text{\bf A}t}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(t)&\sin(t)\\
-\sin(t)&\cos(t)\end{bmatrix}\,.

(76)

The momentum autocorrelation decays through dampened oscillations, and its behavior is entirely characterized by the friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
and the object mass. The even friction coefficient
\zeta_{\parallel}
determines the rate of the envelope decay, while the odd friction coefficient
\zeta_{\perp}
sets the frequency of the oscillations. In Figure
2
we use numerical simulations (numerical details available in companion letter
[
65
,
79
,
80
]
) to show that as the adiabatic limit is approached, the momentum autocorrelation functions converge towards the prediction of Eq. (
75
).

Inserting the result of Eq. (
75
) into Eq. (
72
), we obtain the following expression for the diffusivity matrix:

\begin{split}\text{\bf D}_{\text{transl}}&=\frac{T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}}{M}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}t\ e^{-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}t}\\
&=T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}=\frac{T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}}{\zeta_{\parallel}^{2}+\zeta_{\perp}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{\parallel}&\zeta_{\perp}\\
-\zeta_{\perp}&\zeta_{\parallel}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(77)

Figure 3:
Even and odd thermometers
(
\ell_{p}=10
,
\ell_{g}=2
). The effective temperature
T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}
of a disk can be approximated using both even and odd Einstein relations (Eq. (
80
)), and the even FDT (Eq. (
100
)). All of these measurements agree in at large
M
but differ for smaller masses. In the adiabatic limit, however, the ratio
\lambda_{\perp}/\zeta_{\perp}
still does not measure the effective temperature, due to the absence of an odd FDT for
\lambda_{\perp}
. This mismatch is an odd signature of the bath nonequilibrium nature.

We now turn to the computation of the mobility tensor. When pulling the disk through the chiral active bath with a weak external force
{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, the evolution equation becomes

\dot{\bm{P}}=-M^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}+{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}+\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}\,.

(78)

Averaging over the steady state in the presence of
{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, denoted by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}}
, the disk mobility
\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}
is defined by

M^{-1}\langle{\bm{P}}\rangle_{{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}}=\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}\equiv\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{transl}}{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{ext}}\,.

(79)

Finally, a comparison with the result of Eq. (
77
) yields the Einstein relation

\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{transl}}=T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{transl}}\,,

(80)

holding for both the even and odd parts. Figure
3
shows that, in the adiabatic limit, Eqs. (
66
) and (
80
) indeed allow defining the same translational effective temperature.
By convention, our numerical simulations set
\omega_{0}<0
, so that the bath particles rotate clockwise. In response, the disk then acquires counterclockwise random motion with
D_{\perp}>0
,
\mu_{\perp}>0
, and
\zeta_{\perp}<0
.

IV.3
Diffusion and mobility of
C_{n}
objects

Let us now turn to the relation between diffusivity and mobility matrices for objects with
C_{n}
symmetry. For such objects, the
3\times 3
diffusion and mobility matrices assume the block form

\text{\bf D}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{\bf D}_{\text{transl}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}&D_{\text{rot}}\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\quad\bm{\mu}_{\text{rot}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\mathrm{T}}&\mu_{\text{rot}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(81)

Here,
\text{\bf D}_{\text{transl}}
and
\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}
are the
2\times 2
translational diffusion and mobility matrices defined in the previous subsection, while the scalar
D_{\text{rot}}
and
\mu_{\text{rot}}
are the rotational diffusion and mobility constants.
Despite the angular dependence of the friction coefficient, the long-timescale translational motion of
C_{n}
objects is formally identical to that of the disk.
This can be seen by integrating Eq. (
67
) over
\Theta
and
L
, which
recovers Eq. (
64
), but with
\bm{\zeta}
and
\bm{\lambda}
replaced by
\bar{\bm{\zeta}}
and
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
, defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\zeta}}\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}})\equiv\iint\text{d}\Theta\text{d}L\ \bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},\Theta,L)\,,

(82)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}})\equiv\iint\text{d}\Theta\text{d}L\ \bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta)\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},\Theta,L)\,.

(83)

The translational motion of
C_{n}
objects thus obeys the Einstein relation

\text{\bf D}_{\text{transl}}=T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}\,,

(84)

analogous to Eq. (
80
), with
\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}=\bar{\bm{\zeta}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}
.

Meanwhile, the rotational diffusion coefficient is

D_{\text{rot}}=\frac{1}{I^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}t\ \left\langle[L(t)-I\Omega][L(0)-I\Omega]\right\rangle,

(85)

where we recall that
\Omega=\frac{\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}}{\zeta_{LL}}
is the average angular velocity in steady state. The equation of motion of the angular momentum is

\dot{L}=\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}-I^{-1}\zeta_{LL}L+\xi_{L}(t)\,,

(86)

which is solved by

\begin{split}L(t)-I\Omega&=\text{e}^{-\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}t}\left[L(0)-I\Omega\right]\\
&+\sqrt{2T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}\zeta_{LL}}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \text{e}^{-\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}(t-\tau)}\xi_{L}(\tau)\,.\end{split}

(87)

From Eq. (
85
), the rotational diffusivity is then

D_{\text{rot}}=\frac{1}{I\zeta_{LL}}\langle|L(0)-I\Omega|^{2}\rangle=\frac{T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}}{\zeta_{LL}}\,,

(88)

where
T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}
is defined in Eq. (
69
).

Finally, analogous to Eq. (
79
), the rotational mobility
\mu_{\text{rot}}
in response to an external torque
\Gamma^{\mathrm{ext}}
is defined by

I^{-1}\langle L\rangle_{\Gamma^{\mathrm{ext}}}=\zeta_{LL}^{-1}\Gamma^{\mathrm{ext}}\equiv\mu_{\text{rot}}\Gamma^{\mathrm{ext}}\,,

(89)

where
\langle\ldots\rangle_{\Gamma^{\text{ext}}}
is an average over the steady state in the presence of a weak external torque
\Gamma^{\text{ext}}
. Comparing Eq. (
88
) with Eq. (
89
)yieldis the Einstein relation

D_{\text{rot}}=T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}\mu_{\text{rot}}\,,

(90)

involving the effective rotational temperature
T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}
.

Our calculations thus predict that rotational and translational degrees of freedom obey Einstein relations, albeit with different temperatures, in line with the simluation results presented in
[
65
]
.

V
Density and flux in a confining potential

To test whether the effective temperatures defined in the previous section indeed play a thermodynamic role, we now study the steady state of objects whose translational and rotational degrees of freedom are confined by an external potential.

V.1
Translational confinement

When the passive disk is confined by an external potential
U({\bm{R}})
, Eq. (
64
) becomes

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)=\big[-M^{-1}{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\\
&+M^{-1}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\cdot{\bm{P}}+\lambda_{\parallel}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}^{2}+\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}U)\big]\rho_{\text{o}}\,.\end{split}

(91)

Eq. (
91
) is solved in steady state by the Boltzmann distribution

\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}})\propto\text{e}^{-\big(\frac{|{\bm{P}}|^{2}}{2M}+U({\bm{R}})\big)/T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}}\,,

(92)

which confirms the interpretation of
T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}
as a temperature.

We now define the number density
\rho_{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}},t)=\int\text{d}{\bm{P}}\ \rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)
. Integrating Eq. (
91
) over
{\bm{P}}
then yields the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{\bm{R}}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{J}_{\bm{R}}\,,

(93)

where
\bm{J}_{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}},t)=M^{-1}\int\text{d}{\bm{P}}\ {\bm{P}}\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)
.
Furthermore, taking the first moment of Eq. (
91
) with respect to
{\bm{P}}
yields

\partial_{t}\bm{J}_{\bm{R}}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}-M^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\cdot\bm{J}_{\bm{R}}-M^{-1}\rho_{\bm{R}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}U\,,

(94)

where
\bm{\sigma}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\int\text{d}{\bm{P}}\ {\bm{P}}\otimes{\bm{P}}\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)
.
In the steady state,
\bm{\sigma}=M^{-1}T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\rho({\bm{R}})\mathbf{1}

and the flux becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{J}_{\bm{R}}^{\mathrm{ss}}({\bm{R}})&=-\mathbf{D}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\rho_{\bm{R}}-\rho_{\bm{R}}\bm{\mu}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}U\\
&=\left[T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\bm{\mu}_{\text{transl}}-\mathbf{D}_{\text{transl}}\right]\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\rho_{\bm{R}}=\bm{0}\,,\end{split}

(95)

where the equality to zero in the final line results from the Einstein relation in Eq. (
80
).
Thus, in the adiabatic limit a disk exhibits no circulation in position space in a confining potential: the current vanishes due to an exact cancellation between the flux driven by diffusion and that driven by the mobility, as observed in Fig.
4
b
.

A nonzero net circulation appears however in momentum space. Defining the momentum density
\rho_{\bm{P}}({\bm{P}},t)=\int\text{d}{\bm{R}}\ \rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)
and marginalizing Eq. (
91
) with respect to
{\bm{R}}
yields the continuity equation in momentum space

\partial_{t}\rho_{\bm{P}}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}\,,

(96)

where the momentum-space flux is defined by

\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}=-[M^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}+\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}]\rho_{\bm{P}}-\int\text{d}{\bm{R}}\ (\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}U)\rho_{\text{o}}\,.

(97)

Inserting
\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}
from Eq. (
92
) yields

\int\text{d}{\bm{R}}(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}U)\rho_{\text{o}}({\bm{R}},{\bm{P}},t)=-T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\int\text{d}{\bm{R}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}=\mathbf{0}\,,

(98)

and finally

\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}^{\mathrm{ss}}=\left[T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}-\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\right]\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\rho_{\bm{P}}\,.

(99)

The steady state condition
\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\cdot\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}^{\mathrm{ss}}=0
imposes a fluctuation-dissipation theorem

\lambda_{\parallel}=T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\zeta_{\parallel}\,.

(100)

This leaves the steady-state momentum flux as:

\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}^{\mathrm{ss}}=\left[T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}\zeta_{\perp}-\lambda_{\perp}\right]\text{\bf A}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{P}}\rho_{\bm{P}}\,.

(101)

Note that, while the Einstein relation (
80
) holds for both the odd and even parts of
\text{\bf D}_{\rm transl}
, the FDT (
100
) holds only for the even part:
\lambda_{\perp}/\zeta_{\perp}
is in general not given by
T_{{\bm{R}}}^{\text{eff}}
, even in the adiabatic limit, as shown in Fig.
3
. An important consequence is that Eq. (
101
) predicts the mismatch between
\lambda_{\perp}
and
\zeta_{\perp}
to drive a steady-state circulation
\bm{J}_{\bm{P}}^{\mathrm{ss}}
. This is confirmed in Fig.
4
c
, even in the adiabatic limit.

V.2
Rotational confinement

We next impose a confining external potential
U({\bm{R}},\Theta)
on a
C_{n}
object such as a rod. So long as this potential decomposes as
U({\bm{R}},\Theta)=u({\bm{R}})+w(\Theta)
, the translational and rotational degrees of freedom remain independent. As long as the potential traps the object within a finite range
[\Theta_{\text{min}},\Theta_{\text{max}}]
, the rotational degrees of freedom admit the steady-state distribution

\rho_{\text{o}}^{\text{ss}}\propto\exp\bigg\{-\frac{I^{-1}\big(L-I\Omega\big)^{2}+\big(w(\Theta)-\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}\Theta\big)\big]}{2T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}}\bigg\}\,.

(102)

This validates the interpretation of
T_{\Theta}^{\text{eff}}
as an effective temperature. Note, however, that Eq. (
102
) breaks down for a periodic, nonconfining potential
w(\theta)
. This shows that, for
C_{n}
objects, the equilibrium regime for the angular degrees of freedom is more fragile than for the translational degrees of freedom, due to the non-vanishing average torque
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}
.

V.3
Comparison with an equilibrium Lorentz bath

The mismatch between
\zeta_{\perp}
and
\lambda_{\perp}
that drives momentum circulation in Eq. (
101
) has no equivalent among even transport coefficients. It is a signature of the nonequilibrium nature of the chiral active bath and it would not be present in a passive chiral bath. To show this, we consider bath particles subject to an equilibrium Langevin dynamics with a Lorentz force orthogonal to their direction of motion:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\bm{r}}_{i}&=\bm{v}_{i}\,,\\
m\dot{\bm{v}}_{i}&={\bm{F}}_{i}+f_{\text{Lorentz}}\text{\bf A}\bm{v}-\gamma\bm{v}+\sqrt{2T\gamma}\bm{\eta}_{i}\,.\end{split}

(103)

Here,
m
is the mass of a bath particle,
\gamma
is the friction,
T
is the temperature, and
\bm{\eta}_{i}(t)
are Gaussian white noises satisfying
\langle\bm{\eta}_{i}(t)\otimes\bm{\eta}_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\mathbf{1}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
Equation (
103
) can be thought as describing thermalized charged particles moving in the plane normal to a magnetic field.
As in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), bath particles interact with the passive object through a potential
V^{\mathrm{int}}
(i.e.
{\bm{F}}=-\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}V^{\mathrm{int}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bm{F}}_{i}
).

f_{\text{Lorentz}}
is a Lorentz force acting perpendicular to the motion of a bath particle, responsible for breaking parity symmetries.
f_{\text{Lorentz}}
also breaks time-reversal symmetry if its sign is not flipped under time reversal
[
81
]
, which results in nonzero
\zeta_{\perp}
and
\lambda_{\perp}
. The steady state distribution is, however, given by the equilibrium Boltzmann weight

\rho_{\text{b}}\propto e^{-V^{\mathrm{int}}({\bm{r}}^{N},{\bm{R}})/T}\,.

(104)

Equation (
44
) then evaluates to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{R}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}s\ \langle\delta{\bm{F}}(\tau)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}/T\\
&=\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}/T\,,\end{split}

(105)

where the final equality is made through identification with Eq. (
43
).
Thus, we recover
a FDT for both the even and odd parts of
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
.
As a result, the momentum-space flux in Eq. (
101
) is seen to vanish, even when
\zeta_{\perp}
and
\lambda_{\perp}
are individually nonzero, as shown numerically in Fig.
4
f
.
This stands in contrast to the case of a chiral active bath, where such circulation persists, indicating the non-equilibrium nature of the bath.

VI
Time-reversal symmetry and comparison with a charged particle in a magnetic field

In this Section, we address the irreversibility of the trajectories of the object. We focus on the disk, whose symmetry properties allow for analytical progress and we show that the entropy production contains a contribution that diverges in the adiabatic limit. We then observe that a hidden symmetry exists, corresponding to simultaneous reversal of time and the bath chirality, under which the entropy production of the disk vanishes. This symmetrized entropy production is also found to vanish in the contribution from rotational motion of objects with both
C_{n}
and
\Pi
symmetries. These findings rationalize the existence of equilibrium-like steady states for such objects, as discussed in the previous Sections.

VI.1
Entropy production rate

The irreversibility of the object’s dynamics is quantified by the entropy production rate

\sigma\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\ln\frac{\mathscr{P}[\big\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\big\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]}{\mathscr{P}[\big\{\tilde{\bm{\Gamma}}(t-\tau)\big\}\lvert\tilde{\bm{\Gamma}}(t),\omega_{0}]}\right\rangle_{\text{path}}\,,

(106)

where
\big\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\big\}=\big\{{\bm{R}}(\tau),\Theta(\tau),{\bm{P}}(\tau),L(\tau)\big\}
is a trajectory through phase space for
\tau\in[0,t]
and
\big\{\tilde{\bm{\Gamma}}(t-\tau)\big\}=\big\{{\bm{R}}(t-\tau),\Theta(t-\tau),-{\bm{P}}(t-\tau),-L(t-\tau)\big\}
is the time-reversed trajectory.
The quantity
\mathscr{P}[\big\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\big\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]
is the probability to observe a trajectory
\big\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\big\}
beginning at
\bm{\Gamma}(0)
at time
\tau=0
in a chiral active bath with the rotational frequency
\omega_{0}
.
The brackets

\langle\ldots\rangle_{\text{path}}\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\ldots\mathscr{P}[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)|\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}\}]

(107)

denote the average over the ensemble of paths
\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}
.
As discussed at the beginning of Sec.
II.4
, path probabilities and entropy production must be computed at finite
\epsilon
, and the adiabatic limit
\epsilon\to 0
must be taken only afterwards. This procedure is implemented using the non-Markovian Gaussian process
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}
, which recovers the statistics of
\bm{\xi}
in the adiabatic limit, as described by Eq. (
45
). We also use the generalized coordinates system introduced in Eq. (
46
). The probability density of a trajectory that solves Eq. (
46
) is given by

\mathscr{P}[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\propto\exp\left[-S[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\right]\,,

(108)

where the action
S[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]
is defined as

S\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\left(\dot{\bm{W}}(\tau)-\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}+\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}\cdot{\bm{W}}(\tau)\right)\cdot{\bf T}_{\epsilon}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\left(\dot{\bm{W}}(\tau^{\prime})-\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}+\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}\cdot{\bm{W}}(\tau^{\prime})\right)\,.

(109)

The operator
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is the inverse of the noise-noise correlation function at finite
\epsilon
given by Eq. (
45
), such that

\int\text{d}\tau\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\rangle{\bf T}_{\epsilon}(\tau,t^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(110)

Finding an explicit expression of
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}
for arbitrary object shapes is challenging, because the correlation function
\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\rangle
depends on the evolution of the object orientation.
To allow for analytical progress, we focus on the disk and, as mentioned in Section
II.7
, we introduce a regularized explicit form for the correlations of
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)
. We use the following memory kernel that decays over a time scaled by
\epsilon^{2}
:

\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{e}^{-\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}(t-t^{\prime})/\epsilon^{2}}&\text{ if $t\geq t^{\prime}$}\\
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{e}^{-(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}(t^{\prime}-t)/\epsilon^{2}}&\text{ if $t<t^{\prime}$}\end{cases}

(111)

which indeed recovers Eq. (
42
) as
\epsilon\to 0
.

In the case of the disk, the operator
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is a
2\times 2
operator matrix which depends only on the time difference
\tau-\tau^{\prime}
. We denote this operator by
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
. Using the expression of the noise-noise correlation given by Eq. (
111
) we find, after some algebra detailed in Appendix
E
the following expression:

\begin{split}{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}(t)&=\frac{1}{2}[(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\\
&+\epsilon^{2}\left((\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}-\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\right)\partial_{t}\\
&+\epsilon^{4}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\partial_{t}^{2}]\delta(t)\\
&=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{\parallel}}[\mathbf{1}+2\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{\perp}\text{\bf A}\partial_{t}+\epsilon^{4}(\lambda_{\parallel}^{2}+\lambda_{\perp}^{2})\mathbf{1}\partial^{2}_{t}]\\
&\equiv\frac{1}{2}[{\bf T}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}{\bf T}^{(1)}\partial_{t}+\epsilon^{4}{\bf T}^{(2)}\partial^{2}_{t}]\delta(t)\,.\end{split}

(112)

where
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left[\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}+\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\right]
is the symmetric part of the matrix
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
. In the second equality we used the explicit form of
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
for the disk. The last line defines three matrices
{\bf T}^{(n)}
(
n\in\{0,1,2\}
) multiplying the operators
\epsilon^{2n}\partial_{t}^{n}\delta(t)
. The symmetric matrices
{\bf T}^{(0)}
and
\epsilon^{4}{\bf T}^{(2)}\partial_{t}^{2}
are even under time reversal, while the antisymmetric matrix
\epsilon^{2}{\bf T}^{(1)}\partial_{t}
is odd under time reversal.

The path probability for a disk is given by

\begin{split}&\mathscr{P}[\{{\bm{P}}(\tau)\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\propto\exp\Biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\ \bigg(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)\\
&+\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau)\bigg)\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\bigg(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau^{\prime})+\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau^{\prime})\bigg)\Biggr]\,.\end{split}

(113)

Using the expression of
{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}
given by Eq. (
112
) and its symmetry properties under time reversal, we obtain

\begin{split}\sigma_{\text{disk}}&=-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\ \Biggl\langle\dot{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{(0)}\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}}{M}{\bm{P}}\\
&+\epsilon^{4}\dot{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{(2)}\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}}{M}\ddot{\bm{P}}\\
&+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\left[\dot{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{(1)}\ddot{\bm{P}}+\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}}{M}{\bm{P}}\cdot\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}}{M}\dot{\bm{P}}\right]\Biggr\rangle_{\text{path}}\,.\end{split}

(114)

To identify the leading contribution to Eq. (
114
) in the adiabatic limit, we observe that the integrand contains correlations among time derivatives of various orders of the disk momentum. Their contribution can be estimated, using the effective Langevin equation Eq. (
41
) and the noise correlations in Eq. (
111
), as

\begin{split}&\left\langle\frac{\text{d}^{n}{\bm{P}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau^{n}}\otimes\frac{\text{d}^{m}{\bm{P}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau^{m}}\,\right\rangle_{\text{\tiny{path}}}\\
&\sim\left\langle\frac{\text{d}^{n-1}\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau^{n-1}}\otimes\frac{\text{d}^{m-1}\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau^{m-1}}\right\rangle\sim\epsilon^{-2(n+m-1)}\,.\end{split}

(115)

Using this scaling, we see that the leading order to the entropy production
\sigma_{\text{disk}}
is given by the term
\epsilon^{2}\langle\dot{\bm{P}}{\bf T}^{(1)}\ddot{\bm{P}}\rangle
, which is of order
O(\epsilon^{-2})
, while all the other terms are of higher order in
\epsilon
. The entropy production
\sigma_{\text{disk}}
is thus, to leading order in
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma_{\text{disk}}\approx-\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\left\langle\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)\cdot{\bf T}^{(1)}\ddot{\bm{P}}(\tau)\right\rangle_{\text{path}}\\
&=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\Tr\left[{\bf T}^{(1)}\left\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\otimes\dot{\bm{\xi}}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\right\rangle\right]\\
&=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\Tr\left[{\bf T}^{(1)}\left(\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}\text{e}^{-\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})/\epsilon^{2}}\right)\biggr\rvert_{\tau^{\prime}=\tau}\right]\\
&=\epsilon^{-2}\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\Tr\left[{\bf T}^{(1)}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{2}}\Tr\left[{\bf T}^{(1)}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}\right]\\
&=\frac{2\lambda_{\perp}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{\parallel}(\lambda_{\parallel}^{2}+\lambda_{\perp}^{2})}\,.\end{split}

(116)

In the second equality, we made use of the fact that
{\bf T}^{(1)}
is antisymmetric. This expression clearly shows that the adiabatic limit being singular is a consequence of the chirality of the bath. Interestingly, the leading term in
\sigma_{\text{disk}}
depends only on the properties of the force-force correlation matrix
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
, and not on the friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
. It can be shown that treating the friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
as the adiabatic limit of a non Markovian process does not change the singular contribution to the entropy production rate.

VI.2
Hidden time-reversal symmetry for the disk

In this section, we show that the disk obeys a hidden time-reversal symmetry under which the entropy production vanishes, when the chirality of the bath is flipped upon time reversal.
To do so, we consider the
symmetrized
entropy production rate

\sigma_{s}\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\ln\frac{\mathscr{P}[\big\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\big\}\lvert\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]}{\mathscr{P}[\big\{\tilde{\bm{\Gamma}}(t-\tau)\big\}\lvert\tilde{\bm{\Gamma}}(t),-\omega_{0}]}\right\rangle_{\text{path}}\,,

(117)

where
\omega_{0}
has been flipped in the time-reversed trajectories.

Let us compute the symmetrized entropy production rate for the disk,
\sigma_{s,\text{disk}}
. We recall that inverting the bath chirality has the effect of transposing the force-force correlation and friction matrices
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
,
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
, see Eq. (
63
). Using this fact, we can see that the operator
{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}
defined in Eq. (
112
) is even under simultaneous reversal of time and the chirality of the bath. The symmetrized entropy production of the disk is thus

\begin{split}\sigma_{s,\text{disk}}&=-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\Biggl\langle\left(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)+\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau)\right)\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\left(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)+\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau)\right)\\
&\hskip 76.82234pt-\left(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau)\right)\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\left(\dot{\bm{P}}(\tau)-\frac{1}{M}\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}(\tau)\right)\Biggr\rangle_{\text{path}}\\
&=-\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{Mt}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\Biggl\langle\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S}{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\dot{\bm{P}}+\dot{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S}{\bm{P}}\\
&\hskip 76.82234pt+\frac{1}{2M}\left(\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{P}}-\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bf T}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}_{\epsilon}\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}\right)\Biggr\rangle_{\text{path}}\hskip-8.53581pt,\end{split}

(118)

where
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}+\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}})
. In Appendix
F
, we show that all terms in the integrand entering Eq. (
118
) either vanish by symmetry or contribute boundary terms that vanish as
1/t
, so that

\sigma_{s,\text{disk}}=0\,.

(119)

For
C_{n}
-symmetric objects, the friction and noise-correlation matrices depend on the object orientation and evolve in time. As a consequence, computing the entropy production rate in a controlled way as the adiabatic limit is approached becomes challenging. We can nevertheless show that the contribution to the entropy production due to the net rotational motion of objects with both
C_{n}
and
\Pi
symmetries once again vanishes when simultaneously reversing time and the bath chirality. The noise correlation function
\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle
for such objects has a block matrix structure, with a
2\times 2
block associated with the translational motion and a
1\times 1
block
\langle\xi^{L}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\otimes\xi^{L}_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle
for the rotational motion. As for the disk, we choose for the latter an exponential decay:

\langle\xi^{L}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\otimes\xi^{L}_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{e}^{-\lambda_{LL}|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|/\epsilon^{2}}\,.

(120)

The operator
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}(t)
defined in Eq. (
110
) has a block matrix structure as well, with a
2\times 2
block associated with translational motion and a
1\times 1
block
T_{\epsilon}^{LL}(t)
associated with the rotational one. The latter can be explicitly computed using Eq. (
110
) and Eq. (
120
), yielding,

T_{\epsilon}^{LL}(t)=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{LL}}\left(1+\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{LL}\partial^{2}_{t}\right)\delta(t)\,.

(121)

The action defined in Eq. (
109
) thus decomposes into two contributions

\begin{split}S[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}|\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]&=S_{\text{transl}}[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}|\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\\
&+S_{\text{rot}}[\{\bm{\Gamma}(\tau)\}|\bm{\Gamma}(0),\omega_{0}]\,,\end{split}

(122)

where the rotational contribution
S_{\text{rot}}
is defined as

S_{\text{rot}}[L(\tau)|L(0),\omega_{0}]\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\left(\dot{L}(\tau)-\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}+\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}L(\tau)\right)T_{\epsilon}^{LL}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\left(\dot{L}(\tau^{\prime})-\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}+\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}L(\tau^{\prime})\right)\,.

(123)

The symmetrized entropy production rate can be decomposed accordingly,

\sigma_{s}\equiv\sigma_{s,\text{transl}}+\sigma_{s,\text{rot}}\,.

(124)

While an analytical computation of the translational contribution
\sigma_{s,\text{transl}}
is not straightforward (for reasons described following Eq. (
110
)), the rotational contribution is readily evaluated as

\begin{split}\sigma_{s,\text{rot}}&=-\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\bigl(S_{\text{rot}}[L(\tau)|L(0),\omega_{0}]\\
&\hskip 51.21504pt-S_{\text{rot}}[\tilde{L}(t-\tau)|L(t),-\omega_{0}]\bigr)\\
&=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{LL}t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\Biggl\langle\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}(\dot{L}+\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{LL}\dddot{L})\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt-\frac{\zeta_{LL}}{I}(\dot{L}L+\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{LL}\ddot{L}\dot{L})\Biggr\rangle_{\text{path}}\\
&=0\,.\end{split}

(125)

In the second equality we have used that
\langle\Gamma\rangle_{\text{b}}
changes sign under reversal of the bath chirality (see Sec.
III
) and that the operator
T_{\epsilon}^{LL}
given by Eq. (
121
) is symmetric upon reversal of time and chirality of the bath. The final equality to zero then follows from the fact that all terms in the integrand contribute boundary terms that vanish as
1/t
. Note that since the noise correlations in Eq. (
120
) are time-reversal symmetric, the computation of
\sigma_{s,\text{rot}}
can equally be done by taking
\epsilon=0
directly in Eq. (
121
).

The hidden symmetries in Eq. (
119
) and Eq. (
125
) can be understood through analogy with the motion of a charged particle in an external magnetic field. Just as that system is invariant under simultaneous reversal of time and the magnetic field
[
4
,
81
]
, the dynamics of these rotationally-symmetric objects are invariant under simultaneous reversal of time and the bath chirality. The presence of such a symmetry rationalizes the finding, discussed in Sec.
IV.1
that the stationary distribution of the disk has a Boltzmann form.

VII
Multipole description of far-field current

VII.1
Dynamics of the chiral active bath

In the previous sections, we developed an adiabatic theory for the motion of passive objects by integrating out the bath degrees of freedom. This allowed to us to determine the impact of the object shape on its dynamics. We now complement this perspective with the impact that the object shape has on the bath itself.
The dynamics of the bath are given by Eq. (
2
), which corresponds to the Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}\psi=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big[v_{0}\psi{\bm{u}}(\theta)-\mu\psi\bm{\nabla}V\big]-\partial_{\theta}\big[\omega_{0}\psi-D_{r}\partial_{\theta}\psi\big]\,.

(126)

Here,
\psi({\bm{r}},\theta,t)
is the probability of any of the non-interacting bath particles existing at position
{\bm{r}}
with orientation
\theta
at time
t
. We define
\mu=\gamma^{-1}
as the mobility of the bath particle and
v_{0}=\mu f_{0}
as its self-propulsion speed.
The first three angular moments of
\psi({\bm{r}},\theta,t)
define the density, polar order, and nematic order, respectively, as

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\theta\ \psi({\bm{r}},\theta,t)\,,

(127)

\displaystyle\bm{m}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\theta\ \psi({\bm{r}},\theta,t){\bm{u}}(\theta)\,,

(128)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}\theta\ \psi({\bm{r}},\theta,t)\left({\bm{u}}(\theta)\otimes{\bm{u}}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\right)\,.

(129)

From Eq. (
126
), we then obtain evolution equations for the density and polar fields as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big[v_{0}\bm{m}-\mu\rho\bm{\nabla}V\big]\,,

(130)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\bm{m}&=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big[v_{0}(\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\mathbf{1})-\mu\bm{m}\otimes\bm{\nabla}V\big]\\
&\hskip 25.60747pt+\big[\omega_{0}\text{\bf A}-D_{r}\mathbf{1}\big]\bm{m}\,.\end{split}

(131)

in steady state (blue streamlines) induced by a wedge,

j_{\phi}={\bm{r}}\times\bm{j}/|{\bm{r}}|
. In an achiral bath
(left)
the dipole moment
\bm{p}
(blue arrow and dotted line) lies on the symmetry axis of the object (yellow line), which coincides with the symmetry axis of the bath flux (dashed magenta line). In chiral bath

\bm{p}
is rotated with respect to the symmetry axis of the object. The flux field is then further rotated with respect to

for

,

. (Figure adapted from the companion Letter
[

]
.)

Recalling the definition
{\bm{u}}(\theta)=[\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)]^{\text{T}}
and differentiating yields the identities
\partial_{\theta}{\bm{u}}=-\text{\bf A}{\bm{u}}
and
\partial^{2}_{\theta}{\bm{u}}=-{\bm{u}}
.
Equation (
130
) is a continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{j}
, where the bath current is
\bm{j}\equiv v_{0}\bm{m}-\mu\rho\bm{\nabla}V
.
Solving for the steady-state value of
\bm{m}
in Eq. (
131
) and inserting the result into Eq. (
130
) then yields for the steady-state current

\bm{j}=-\mu\rho\bm{\nabla}V-\mathbf{D}^{\text{b}}\bm{\nabla}\rho-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big[2\mathbf{D}^{\text{b}}(\mathbf{Q}-\frac{\mu}{v_{0}}\bm{m}\otimes\bm{\nabla}V)\big]\,.

(132)

The three terms on the right-hand side account for the current driven directly by the bath-object interaction potential
V
, the diffusive flux, and the divergence of a stress-like term due to orientational order near the object.

\mathbf{D}^{\text{b}}
is the diffusivity of the bath particles defined by

\mathbf{D}^{\text{b}}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2(D_{r}^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2})}(D_{r}\mathbf{1}+\omega_{0}\text{\bf A})\equiv D^{\text{b}}_{\parallel}\mathbf{1}+D^{\text{b}}_{\perp}\text{\bf A}\,,

(133)

with
D^{\text{b}}_{\parallel}
and
D^{\text{b}}_{\perp}
the even and odd diffusivity of the bath particles, respectively.

VII.2
Multipole expansion

Placing a passive object into an active bath induces density modulations and currents in the bath which can extend far away from the object
[
82
]
. Their large-scale structures has been shown to be well captured within a multipole description
[
83
,
84
]
. Here we extend this approach to characterize the bath density and current fields when the bath is also chiral, revealing how the bath chirality induces large-scale modifications of the far-field flow and density fields.

Far from the object, the bath flux is well described by its diffusive approximation

\bm{j}_{D}=-\mathbf{D^{\text{b}}}\bm{\nabla}\rho\,.

(134)

Close to the object, it deviates from this expression by an amount
\bm{\delta j}=\bm{j}-\bm{j}_{D}
. Requiring that
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{j}=0
in the steady state leads to the Poisson equation

D_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{b}}\nabla^{2}\rho=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\delta j}\,,

(135)

which admits the solution

\rho({\bm{r}})=\rho_{0}+\frac{1}{2\pi D_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{b}}}\int\text{d}\bm{r^{\prime}}\ln|{\bm{r}}-\bm{r^{\prime}}|\ \bm{\nabla^{\prime}}\cdot\bm{\delta j}(\bm{r^{\prime}})\,.

(136)

Here,
\bm{\nabla^{\prime}}
denotes the gradient with respect to
\bm{r^{\prime}}
and
\ln|{\bm{r}}-\bm{r^{\prime}}|
is the Green’s function for the Laplacian in two dimensions, which can be expanded when
|{\bm{r}}|\gg|\bm{r^{\prime}}|
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\ln|{\bm{r}}-\bm{r^{\prime}}|=\frac{1}{2}\ln\big[({\bm{r}}-\bm{r^{\prime}})\cdot({\bm{r}}-\bm{r^{\prime}})\big]\\
&=\ln|{\bm{r}}|-\frac{{\bm{r}}\cdot\bm{r^{\prime}}}{r^{2}}-\bm{r^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{2{\bm{r}}\otimes{\bm{r}}-r^{2}\mathbf{1}}{2r^{4}}\cdot\bm{r^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})\,.\end{split}

(137)

Inserting this expansion into Eq. (

VIII
Conclusion

We have constructed a detailed theory for the dynamical properties of a passive object immersed in a chiral active bath. Our derivations show how the chiral nature of the bath endows the resulting Langevin dynamics with unusual properties that are reflected in the non-standard relationship between the Langevin and Fokker-Planck equations. This derivation provides a microscopic grounding of many observations presented in our companion Letter
[
65
]
.
In particular, we show that broken symmetries in the bath intersect with broken symmetries of the object to give rise to odd transport properties and rotational ratchet currents.
The corresponding odd diffusivity and odd mobility are connected by an Einstein relation, but no equivalent odd fluctuation-dissipation theorem exists between the noise correlations and the friction.
The effective equilibrium regimes reported in our companion letter are here rationalized by the existence of hidden time-reversal symmetries of the object dynamics when the bath chirality is flipped upon time reversal.
Finally, we develop a multipole expansion to predict the far-field structure and flows induced in the chiral bath and we connect these effects to the ratchet force and torque exerted on the object.

A number of intriguing directions are suggested by these results, including investigating interactions between passive objects mediated by a chiral active bath, with consequences for tunable self-assembly. Another direction of interest would be to consider the limit of large bath before the adiabatic limit is taken. Then, hydrodynamics modes lead to long-time tails
[
72
]
that can have important consequences in active systems
[
73
]
. How they would manifest in chiral active bath remains an interesting open question. On a more mathematical side, odd Langevin dynamics have now arisen in two different contexts: for a passive tracers in a chiral bath, here, and for a Brownian charged particle in a magnetic field in Ref
[
76
]
. Much remains to be done to fully characterize the stochastic calculus emerging from these equations and its physical consequences (at
\epsilon=0^{+}
).

Appendix A
Statistics of the projected force

In this Appendix we show that the statistics of the projected force
{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)=\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}](\tau,0){\bm{F}}(0)
matches the statistics of the force
{\bm{F}}_{0}
, exerted by the bath on a fixed object. By iteratively applying the operator identity in Eq. (
14
), with
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{B}=\epsilon\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}
, to
{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)
, we construct the Dyson series

{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)={\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\epsilon^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\text{d}\tau_{1}\,\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\text{d}\tau_{2}\,\ldots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}\text{d}\tau_{n}\,\mathcal{O}(\tau,\tau_{1})\mathcal{O}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\ldots\mathcal{O}(\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}){\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau_{n})\,,

(148)

where we defined an operator
\mathcal{O}(t_{1},t_{2})\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}](t_{1},t_{2})\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}
and
{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\equiv\mathcal{U}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{b}}](\tau,0){\bm{F}}(0)
is the force exerted on the object by the bath, after evolving the latter for a time
\tau
with the object held fixed in its configuration at time
0
.

Averaging Eq. (
148
) over the bath degrees of freedom and using the triangle inequality we obtain

\lvert\langle{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}-\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}\rvert\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\epsilon^{n}c_{n}\,,

(149)

with the coefficients
c_{n}
given by

c_{n}\equiv\lim_{s\to+\infty}\int_{0}^{s}\text{d}\tau_{1}\,\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\text{d}\tau_{2}\,\ldots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}\text{d}\tau_{n}\,\bigl\lvert\left\langle\mathcal{O}(s,\tau_{1})\mathcal{O}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\ldots\mathcal{O}(\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n}){\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau_{n})\right\rangle_{\text{b}}\bigr\rvert\,.

(150)

We now use that the bath dynamics for a fixed object is ergodic, and assume that the decay of correlation functions of physical observables happens sufficiently fast, so that all the coefficients
c_{n}
are finite.
Note that this is expected to hold when the adiabatic limit is taken before the large-bath limit, so that the bath hydrodynamic modes also have time to relax
[
72
,
7
]
. We also assume that the series
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\epsilon^{n}c_{n}
converges. Using the stationarity of the bath dynamics we then have, to lowest order in
\epsilon
,

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{F}}^{+}(t)\rangle_{\text{b}}&=\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}(t)\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)=\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

(151)

The average value
{\bm{F}}^{+}(t)
matches the average, steady-state force exerted on the object by the bath when the object is held fixed. As discussed in the main text, the average force
\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}
can be different from
\mathbf{0}
. We thus introduce the fluctuations of the force and the torque on the object as

\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}\equiv{\bm{F}}_{0}-\left\langle{\bm{F}}_{0}\right\rangle_{\text{b}},

(152)

and analogous fluctuating quantities for
\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}
.

The two-point correlation function of
\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}
can also be expanded in terms of the small adiabaticity parameter
\epsilon
. From Eq. (
148
), using
\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(0)=\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)
, we obtain

\lvert\langle\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}-\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}\rvert\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}d_{n}\epsilon^{n}\,,

(153)

with the coefficient
d_{n}
given by

d_{n}\equiv\lim_{s\to+\infty}\int_{0}^{s}\text{d}\tau_{1}\,\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\text{d}\tau_{2}\,\ldots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}\text{d}\tau_{n}\,\bigl\lvert\left\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\mathcal{O}(s,\tau_{1})\mathcal{O}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\ldots\mathcal{O}(\tau_{n-1},\tau_{n})\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau_{n})\right\rangle_{\text{b}}\bigr\rvert\,.

(154)

If all the coefficients
d_{n}
are finite, which follows again from our assumption of an ergodic bath of finite size we obtain

\langle\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}=\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(0)\otimes\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{b}}+O(\epsilon)\,.

(155)

The proof for higher moments of
\delta{\bm{F}}^{+}
proceeds in a similar fashion
[
68
]
. The statistics of the projected force
{\bm{F}}^{+}
and of
{\bm{F}}_{0}
are thus identical in the adiabatic limit. A similar derivation can be performed for the projected torque.

&-\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*}-\tau)}{M^{*}}\\
&-\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\frac{L^{*}(t^{*}-\tau)}{I^{*}}\,.\end{split}

(163)

In the adiabatic limit the first memory term becomes

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*}-\tau)}{M^{*}}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t^{*}/\epsilon^{2}}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*}-\epsilon^{2}\tau)}{M^{*}}\\
&=\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\right]\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})}{M^{*}}\\
&\equiv\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})}{M^{*}}\,.\end{split}

(164)

The last line defines the linear momentum friction matrix
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))
. In the adiabatic limit it thus satisfies an Agarwal-like formula
[
11
]

\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\Theta^{*}(t^{*}))=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\otimes\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,

(165)

computed by letting the bath evolve while the object is held in fixed position
{\bm{R}}^{*}(t^{*})
and orientation
\Theta^{*}(t^{*})
. An analogous manipulation shows that

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\frac{L^{*}(t^{*}-\tau)}{I^{*}}\\
&=\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\langle\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\right]\frac{L^{*}(t^{*})}{I^{*}}\\
&\equiv\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}\frac{L^{*}(t^{*})}{I^{*}}\end{split}

(166)

where the friction coefficients
\bm{\zeta}_{{\bm{P}}L}
couple the linear momentum with the angular one. Plugging Eq. (
164
) and Eq. (
166
) into Eq. (
163
), and introducing the rescaled noise
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{P}}^{*}(t^{*})\equiv\epsilon^{-1}\delta{\bm{F}}_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})
, we obtain Eq. (
38
) of the main text.

A similar analysis can be carried out to study the memory term in the equation of the angular momentum. Through a similar analysis, we can show that the projected random torque reads

\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\text{o}}^{*\dagger}\Gamma^{+}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)=\\
&-\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)(\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0))\rangle_{\text{b}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}}{M^{*}}\\
&-\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}\tau)(\partial_{\Theta}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0))\rangle_{\text{b}}\frac{L^{*}}{I^{*}}\,.\end{split}

(167)

Note that, in this case, there is no extra angular ratchet term, since
\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}=0
. The memory terms in Eq. (
22
) can be studied as in the derivation for the linear momentum, leading to the following dynamics for
L^{*}

\dot{L}^{*}=\langle\Gamma_{0}\rangle_{\text{b}}-\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}\cdot\frac{{\bm{P}}^{*}}{M^{*}}-\zeta_{LL}\frac{L^{*}}{I^{*}}+\epsilon^{-1}\delta\Gamma_{0}(\epsilon^{-2}t^{*})

(168)

where the friction coefficients
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}
and

respectively read

\begin{split}\zeta_{LL}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\partial_{\Theta^{*}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,,\\
\bm{\zeta}_{L{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}&\equiv\int_{0}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\delta\Gamma_{0}(\tau)\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{R}}}\ln\rho_{\text{b}}(0)\rangle_{\text{b}}\,.\end{split}

(169)

Upon introducing the rescaled noise

Appendix D
Derivation of Eq. (
49
)

In this Appendix we derive Eq. (
49
) starting from Eq. (
48
). We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)\rangle\\
&\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{W}}}\left\langle\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\right\rangle\,\\
&=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}s\bm{\lambda}\delta_{+}(t-s)\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{{\bm{W}}}\left\langle\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\right\rangle\\
&=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{s\to t_{-}}\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{W}}\left\langle\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\right\rangle\,.\end{split}

(170)

In the second equality, we used the fact that, in the adiabatic limit, the correlations of the noise
\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}
recover the correlations of
\bm{\xi}
, given in Eq. (
42
). The functional derivative
\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}
can be computed expressing
{\bm{W}}(t)
as
{\bm{W}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\dot{\bm{W}}(\tau)+{\bm{W}}(0)
and using the effective Langevin equation (
46
). We obtain

\begin{split}\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}&=\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\left[\frac{\delta\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}-\frac{\delta\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}{\bm{W}}(\tau)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\right]+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\frac{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\\
&=\int_{s}^{t}\text{d}\tau\,\left[\frac{\delta\langle{\bm{G}}\rangle_{\text{b}}}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}-\frac{\delta\tilde{\bm{\zeta}}{\bm{W}}(\tau)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}\right]+\mathbf{1}\,,\end{split}

(171)

where the second equality follows from causality. Taking the limit
s\to t_{-}
on both sides, we get

\lim_{s\to t_{-}}\frac{\delta{\bm{W}}(t)}{\delta\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(s)}=\mathbf{1}\,.

(172)

Substituting Eq. (
172
) into Eq. (
170
), we conclude that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))=-\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{W}}\langle\delta(\bm{\nu}-\bm{\nu}(t))\rangle\,,

(173)

which is Eq. (
49
) of the main text.

Appendix E
Derivation of Eq. (
112
)

We start from the definition of
{\bf T}_{\epsilon}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}
as the inverse of the noise-noise correlation matrix given by Eq. (
111
)

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(\tau)\rangle{\bf T}_{\epsilon}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}(\tau)=\mathbf{1}\delta(t)\,.

(174)

It is convenient to rewrite its expression using the matrix
\text{\bf M}\equiv\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{-1}
, so that the noise-noise correlations read

\langle\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t)\otimes\bm{\xi}_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{e}^{-\text{\bf M}(t-t^{\prime})/\epsilon^{2}}&\text{ if $t\geq t^{\prime}$}\\
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\text{e}^{-\text{\bf M}^{\mathrm{T}}(t^{\prime}-t)/\epsilon^{2}}&\text{ if $t<t^{\prime}$}\end{cases}

(175)

We then apply a Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}[{\bm{O}}](\omega)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\text{d}t{\bm{O}}(t)\text{e}^{-i\omega t}
to both sides of Eq. (
174
) and use the convolution theorem to obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}&[{\bf T}_{\epsilon}^{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}](\omega)=\left[\left[\text{\bf M}+i\epsilon^{2}\omega\mathbf{1}\right]^{-1}+\left[\text{\bf M}^{T}-i\epsilon^{2}\omega\mathbf{1}\right]^{-1}\right]^{-1}\\
&=[\text{\bf M}^{\mathrm{T}}-i\epsilon^{2}\omega\mathbf{1}](\text{\bf M}+\text{\bf M}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}[\text{\bf M}+i\epsilon^{2}\omega\mathbf{1}]\\
&=[\mathbf{1}-i\epsilon^{2}\omega(\text{\bf M}^{T})^{-1}](\text{\bf M}^{-1}+(\text{\bf M}^{T})^{-1})^{-1}\\
&\cdot[\mathbf{1}+i\epsilon^{2}\omega\text{\bf M}^{-1}]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}[\mathbf{1}-i\epsilon^{2}\omega\bm{\lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}](\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}[\mathbf{1}+i\epsilon^{2}\omega\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\\
&+i\epsilon^{2}\omega((\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}-\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1})\\
&+\epsilon^{4}\omega^{2}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S})^{-1}\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}\bigr]\,,\end{split}

(176)

where we made use of the matrix identity
[\text{\bf A}+\text{\bf B}]^{-1}=\text{\bf B}^{-1}[\text{\bf B}^{-1}+\text{\bf A}^{-1}]^{-1}\text{\bf A}^{-1}
, and we introduced the symmetrized matrix
\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}},S}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}+\bm{\lambda}_{{\bm{P}}{\bm{P}}}^{\mathrm{T}})
. Taking an inverse Fourier transform we obtain Eq. (
110
) of the main text.

Appendix F
Derivation of
\sigma_{\text{s,disk}}=0

In this Appendix we show that the symmetrized entropy production rate of the disk,
