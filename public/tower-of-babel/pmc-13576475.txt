Domestic fungal exposure and asthma morbidity in New York City children following Superstorm Sandy
Authors: Nina M. Flores, Maureen Little, Luis Acosta, Christopher D’Andrea, Adnan Divjan, Samuel J. Cochran, Edward Sobek, Nancy Clark, Angela R. Lemons, Karen C. Dannemiller, Brett J. Green, Matthew S. Perzanowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Following Superstorm Sandy (SS), domestic fungal growth and the associated respiratory health impacts concerned New York City (NYC) residents, especially those with asthma.OBJECTIVES:Test hypotheses that compared to non-damaged homes, SS-damaged homes would have different fungal profiles and children living in SS-damaged homes would have higher asthma morbidity.METHODS:We recruited 59 families with asthmatic children from SS-damaged NYC homes 6–18 months following the storm to compare with 68 asthmatic children from non-damaged homes in a concurrent study. Home visits included questionnaires, physical health assessments, and settled floor dust collection. We measured fungi in dust using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and next-generation DNA sequencing (NGS).RESULT:Compared with non-damaged homes, SS-damaged homes had lower fungal diversity (Shannon index) and higher total and allergenic fungal concentrations. NGS and qPCR revealed different genera compositions (e.g., elevated Epicoccum and Cladosporium in damaged homes). Repeat measures in damaged homes revealed that the persistence of these changes was variable (e.g. fungal diversity increased; sustained increase in Epicoccum over the following year). There were no statistically significant differences in asthma symptom frequency, atopy to fungi, lung function, or exhaled nitric oxide among children living in SS-damaged vs. non-damaged homes. Findings were consistent when among SS-damaged homes either with mold odors or visible mold.SIGNIFICANCE:qPCR and NGS revealed that SS-damaged homes experienced persistently higher total and allergenic fungal concentrations and lower diversity, compared to non-damaged homes. Such fungal profiles have previously been associated with asthma development and exacerbation. While we identified health-relevant differences in fungal profiles, we did not observe differences in health outcomes between SS-damaged and non-damages homes. We elaborate on potential reasons for the null health findings including: the study population’s relatively mild asthma, recruitment timing, statistical power, and ceiling effects.IMPACT:Despite the wide population impacted by Superstorm Sandy, and the popularization of terms like “Sandy cough,” there have been no studies, to our knowledge, characterizing changes in the fungal profile in Sandy-damaged homes and their asthma-related health impacts. Comparing Sandy-damaged homes to non-damaged homes, quantitative polymerase chain reaction and next-generation DNA sequencing methods revealed higher total and allergenic fungal concentrations and lower diversity in damaged homes— profiles that have previously been associated with asthma development and morbidity. However, these fungal differences were not consistently associated with asthma outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Domestic fungal contamination (mold) is widespread in New York City (NYC) with an estimated 9.5% of homes impacted citywide [1, 2]. Though fungal issues are common in urban homes in general, they disproportionately affect low-income families who have less control over conditions conducive to fungal growth, like inefficient building envelopes, leak-prone steam-boiler systems, decaying piping, and a lack of ventilation and dampness control [3–7]. The heightened prevalence and disparities in fungal exposure are major public health concerns and have direct health implications; fungi can release spores, fragments, irritants, allergens, and toxins into the air, which can lead to a range of adverse health responses, including irritation of the eyes, skin, nose, throat, and lungs. Moreover, fungi have been consistently associated with asthma development and are particularly dangerous for individuals with asthma, for whom fungal exposure can trigger acute asthma symptom exacerbation [8–10]. Issues surrounding fungal exposures, disparities, and adverse health responses may be exacerbated by extreme weather events, including extreme precipitation events, which result in flooding and domestic water intrusion that can spur mold growth [11, 12].

Rising greenhouse gas emissions have been linked to increased frequency and intensity of such events [11, 12]. Coastal urban cities are particularly vulnerable to substantial public health impacts from such natural disasters because of their low elevation above sea level and high population densities [13]. As such, Hurricanes Katrina and Rita (2005) resulted in extensive fungal growth in flood-damaged homes in New Orleans, Louisiana [14–17]. In October 2012, a strong Atlantic hurricane, named Sandy, tracked through Jamaica, Cuba, and the Bahamas before making landfall near Atlantic City, NJ as a superstorm, bringing with it extreme wind speeds and heavy rainfall that impacted much of the east coast. In NYC, storm surges reached levels never seen before, and more than 300,000 homes were in flood inundation zones [18–20]. In the months and years to follow, indoor fungal growth and contamination following Superstorm Sandy (SS) became a major public concern [21].

Studies following Hurricanes Katrina and Rita found that, beyond increased fungal growth, the domestic fungal profile significantly changed due to storm-driven water intrusion but there was limited evidence linking these exposures to respiratory health [14–17, 22]. Notably, due to the severity of storm damage from Hurricanes Rita and Katrina, many New Orleans residents evacuated during the storm and did not return to their homes until after remediation was complete [23]. Conversely, a substantial portion of NYC residents remained or quickly returned to water- or fungal-damaged homes; thus, NYC residents may have been more likely to demo nstrate adverse health effects from SS-related fungal growth. Following SS, as the term “Sandy cough” became popularized [24], adverse respiratory health impacts became a great public concern among NYC residents, including concern about how SS-related changes could affect asthmatic New Yorkers. However, to our knowledge, there are no studies characterizing changes in the fungal profile in SS-affected homes or characterizing how SS may have impacted clinically relevant asthma symptoms (e.g., nighttime waking, related urgent visits, wheeze) or impacted lung and airway health among this population.

Using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) for identifying fungal species in dust [25, 26], we conducted fungal profile analyses in NYC homes following SS from 6 months to 18 months following the storm. We linked the fungal exposures to allergic sensitization and health outcomes using questionnaires, serum samples, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) to assess airway inflammation. Specifically, we assessed (a) differences in mold profiles among SS-damaged and non-damaged homes, (b) the persistence of fungal profile differences among damaged homes, (c) the prevalence of allergic sensitization to fungi in both damaged and non-damaged homes, (d) the difference in health outcomes in asthma symptoms, lung function, and airway inflammation in SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes, and (e) the fungal profile associations with and lung function and airway inflammation among in SS-damaged homes.

METHODS

Study participants and study overview

Participants from SS-damaged homes were recruited through door-to-door flier distribution in affected areas (including schools) in the 6 months to 1.5 years following SS; geographically targeted mailings; community groups working on SS recovery; the NYC information line, 311 (callers asking the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene [DOHMH] about mold damage were asked if they would like to participate in a research study); and by contacting volunteers that had taken fungal contamination clean-up training funded by the DOHMH. We administered a brief screening questionnaire to establish that at least one child (7–17 years of age) living in the home had current asthma, that the home sustained damaged from SS (by participant report), and that the home was in NYC. Children were classified as asthmatic if parents reported that they had been diagnosed with asthma by a physician and had at least one of the following for the child in the prior 12 months: wheeze [27]; nighttime awakening with cough in the absence of a cold; wheeze with exercise; or report of medication use for asthma [28]. Children not meeting these criteria were excluded. Fifty-nine participants in SS-damaged homes met the inclusion criteria and thus were included in the study.

We utilized participants already enrolled in the concurrent NYC Neighborhood Asthma and Allergy Study (NAAS), to serve as control (no SS damage) participants from non-damaged homes [28]. The NAAS is a case-control study of asthmatic children living in NYC. Parents of 7- and 8- year-old-children were recruited through a middle-income health insurance plan (n = 350) and children were classified as asthma cases (n = 206) or non-asthma controls (n = 144) based on reported symptoms in the previous year [28]. We selected NAAS asthma cases that were (a) from homes in the same geographic regions as the SS-damaged homes (The Bronx and Queens counties), (b) between 7 and 14 years of age which is comparable to the damaged group, and (c) from homes either visited prior to SS (n = 28) or, if homes were visited following SS, were confirmed not have SS-related water damage by the study team during the home visit. This resulted in 68 participants from non-damaged homes in the study.

We conducted home visits for both the SS-damaged and non-damaged homes. The resulting study population was largely children who self-identified as Black race or Hispanic ethnicity, with only 16–18% of the children in each group neither Black nor Hispanic. Thus, this study consists of groups often underrepresented in research and who often face inequitable environmental exposures [29–33]. During home visits, we administered a comprehensive questionnaire regarding asthma (current and historical), family history of and risk factors for asthma, housing type (single family, multi-family, apartment), building materials (carpet vs. no carpet), sociodemographic characteristics, adverse health symptoms, and completed physical health assessments and fungal measurements. In SS-damaged home visits, study investigators documented which homes had mold odors and visible mold; we used this information to identify SS-damaged, with mold odors and SS-damaged, with visible mold home groupings. In all analyses, we compared non-damaged homes to SS-damaged homes overall, with mold odors, and with visible mold. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Columbia University (AAAC7688, AAAM0105).

Changes in total fungal concentrations related to SS

We collected dust using the Dustream® collector (Indoor Biotechnologies, Charlottesville, VA), operated for 3 min from a 2 m2 area in bedrooms of children recruited to the study. Dust was stored desiccated at −20°C until analysis. Three separate analyses were performed to obtain (a) sequencing summary metrics – performed by the Ohio State University and Research and Testing Laboratory (Lubbock, TX), (b) total fungal concentrations – performed by the Ohio State University, and (c) Environmentally Related Moldiness Index (ERMI) related information – performed by Assured Bio Labs LLC (Oak Ridge, TN).

The analysis steps have been described in detail previously and are summarized here [34]. The Ohio State University sieved dust aliquots intended for quantitative analysis using 250 μm sieves (Dual MFG Co., Franklin Park, Illinois). Masses of homogenized dust were aliquoted relative to the total dust mass of each sample, then extracted for DNA using QIAGEN DNEasy PowerLyzer PowerSoil kits (Qiagen; Hilden, Germany) with bead-beating and final elution modifications [35] and extracted samples were diluted 10-fold.

The Research and Testing Laboratory applied Next Generation Sequencing of the Internal Transcribed Spacer region to the extracted DNA. Bioinformatics analysis was applied to the resulting data in QIIME, ver. 1.9 to calculate diversity metrics including fungal richness (OTUs) and fungal richness (Shannon diversity index). We examined the resulting genera sequences against a list of fungal allergens to determine the relative proportion of a sample that consisted of allergenic genera [36, 37].

The non-damaged homes and a subset of the damaged homes (n = 28) were then evaluated for total fungal concentrations. Standards were prepared from known concentrations of Aspergillus fumigatus (AF293 strain). Thus, total fungal concentrations are reported as spore-equivalents. This represents the concentration of genetic material in experimental samples relative to the known number of spores in the standard. All samples were normalized by the mass of dust from which they were extracted to obtain spore-equivalents per milligram of dust.

Assured Bio Labs, LLC sieved and processed dust aliquots intended for fungal taxonomic analysis 300 μm mesh basket. Assured Bio LLC extracted the DNA using the High Pure PCR Template Kit [38] and used quantitative polymerase chain reaction [37] to identify and quantify the 36 fungal species species in the EPA-developed Environmentally Related Moldiness Index (ERMI) [26, 39–42]. Assured Bio Labs, LLC is trained and certified to perform this technique and operates under EPA license number 416–07 using an Eppendorf 5075 liquid handling device and a LightCycler 480.

Changes in fungal profiles related to SS

We used linear regression models to evaluate the association between five dependent variables including the (A) Shannon fungal diversity index—a type of alpha diversity that accounts for richness and evenness, (B) number of operational taxonomic units (OTUs), which approximates richness by grouping individuals with high sequence similarity, (C) the Environmental Relative Moldiness Index (ERMI) scale, (D) percentage of allergenic fungi, and (E) total fungi across SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes, while adjusting for housing type, presence of carpet in the bedroom, and season of collection. In addition to analyzing the overall ERMI scale, we assessed differences in detectability (≥ 10 spore equivalents/mg dust) for each of the 36 species in the ERMI among SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes using logistic regression. For a wider panel of analysis, we conducted a compositional data analysis among the 284 genera present in at least 20% of homes using a penalized regression implemented with the package coda4microbiome [43, 44].

The persistence of any identified fungal profile changes in SS-damaged homes

Twenty-six of the 58 SS-damaged homes were visited two more times, resulting in three visits within a year, with each visit occurring in a different season. During these visits, dust samples were once again collected, as described above. Our analysis for this objective was descriptive. To determine the persistence of identified fungal profile changes in SS-damaged homes, we displayed the distributions of the Shannon fungal diversity index, number of OTUs, and percentage of allergenic fungi as well as the average PCR concentration of each species, and both the top and bottom five genera most different genera between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes at each of the three visits.

The prevalence of allergic sensitization to fungi among SS-damaged and non-SS-damaged homes

As part of the NAAS study, we collected serum from homes willing to donate a sample (n = 50). We similarly collected serum from children in SS-damaged homes (n = 57). We assessed seroatopy of the children using serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels. Seroatopy is characterized by IgE production in response to allergen exposure [34]. We evaluated serum IgE levels for the participants. SS-damaged home samples were tested for IgE to Penicillium chrysogenum, Cladosporium herbarum, Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida albicans, Alternaria alternata, Helminthosporium halodes, Mucor racemosus, Aureobasidium pullulans, Epicoccum purpurascens, Trichoderma viride, A. niger, P. glabrum, and a fungal mix (Mx2, which includes P. chrysogenum, C. herbarum, A. fumigatus, C. albicans, A. alternata, Seto-sphaeria rostrata). Of these, samples from non-damaged home were tested against Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida albicans, Alternaria alternata, Helminthosporium halodes, Mucor racemosus, Aureobasidium pullulans, A. niger, and the fungal mix - the eight fungal IgE tests that identified the most positive children in SS-damaged homes (Figs. S4 and S5). We used logistic regression to compare the odds of measurable IgE to one or any of the eight fungi tested between SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes, adjusting for building type, presence of carpet in bedroom, child sex, child age, maternal asthma, and proxies of social disadvantage including maternal race, ethnicity, and education level [45]. 0.35 lU/ml has typically been used as a lower threshold for specific IgE based on the original lower detection limit of the assay, we have demonstrated a clinical relevance of IgE measured in the 0.1 – 0.35 lU/ml, specifically for the allergenic fungal genus, Alternaria [46]. For our primary analyses, we present the results with the >0.1 lU/ml standard and include sensitivity analyses using >0.35 lU/ml threshold in the Supplement (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Health effects

We collected asthma morbidity information in participant questionnaires, including the frequency of nighttime waking due to cough, breathlessness, or chest tightness; asthma-related urgent medical visits; and wheeze in the past 12 months. For all symptom and lung function models, we adjusted for an a priori selected group of hypothesized confounders and precision variables. The plausible confounders, including the presence of a smoker in the home, housing type, maternal race, ethnicity, and education level, were selected as proxies for social disadvantage and environmental conditions relevant to asthma morbidity, as they were expected to be associated with both Sandy damage and asthma outcomes. We also included the season of a visit as a confounder, given expected associations with both fungal exposure and asthma morbidity. We additionally adjusted for presence of carpet in the bedroom, maternal asthma status, child age, sex, and seroatopy which we theorized would be associated with asthma morbidity even if not directly related to our exposures of interest.

Asthma symptoms in the past year were categorized as frequent (≥ 4 episodes), infrequent (1–3 episodes) or absent (0 episodes). We used logistic regression models, adjusting for the aforementioned covariates to evaluate the association between living in SS-damaged vs. non-damaged homes and the frequency of symptoms.

To assess lung function, we conducted spirometry according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines as previously published for the NAAS cohort [47]. Tests, scored by a study physician, were considered acceptable if they met these criteria: a rapid upstroke; volume extrapolated <5% of forced vital capacity; premature termination <15%; and smooth exhalatory limb.

We assessed airway inflammation using FeNO, a non-invasive method. Exhaled breath was collected from three separate forced expirations of the children using the offline FeNO described in the ATS guidelines. Briefly, the child inspired and expired air through the offline collection kit made by Sievers (CBSK 01400–01) as described previously [48]. This device scrubs NO from the inspired air and has a restriction valve that forces the child to exhale at a rate of 83 mL/s (FeNO83). The samples were collected in three separate bags and returned to the laboratory for NO analysis within 12 h of sample collection. The FeNO offline collection device has been modified to include a more efficient NO scrubber to reduce contamination from ambient NO. In cut-point analyses, samples collected at an ambient NO < 100 ppb were not significantly contaminated but beyond 100 ppb, FeNO and ambient NO were significantly correlated [47]. Samples collected at >100 ppb ambient NO were excluded from analyses, which removed 13 observations from analysis.

We used linear regression to assess the association between each of the significantly different fungal measures, the most prevalent genera in SS-damaged homes, and lung function and airway inflammation, adjusting for the aforementioned covariates.

We used linear regression models, adjusting for covariates, to compare lung function, as measured by FEV1/FVC, and airway inflammation, as measured by lnFeNO83, among those living in SS-damaged vs non-damaged homes.

RESULTS

Study participants and home characteristics

We collected information on asthma (risk factors, family history, symptoms), housing characteristics, sociodemographic characteristics, fungal exposures, and other health-related variables, among 59 children with asthma living in SS-damaged homes—24 of which had mold odors and 42 of which had visible mold upon inspection—and 68 asthmatic children living in non-damaged homes (Table 1). Compared to participants in non-damaged homes, participants in SS-damaged homes were less likely to have a mother with a college degree and more likely to have a mother with asthma, a smoker in the home, live in an apartment and have carpeting in the bedroom. The season during which homes were visited differed between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes, but, overall, most visits occurred in the spring.

Among the SS-damaged homes, 39 (66%) of the families reported actively drying their homes following damage and 42 (71%) reported using cleaning products that prevent mold. SS-damaged homes also reported dampness in their homes for a median of 5 days following Sandy (IQR: 0, 15).

Differences in fungal profiles among SS-damaged and non-damaged homes

Among SS-damaged homes, we observed, on average, higher total and allergenic fungi concentrations, and lower fungal diversity (Shannon) compared to non-damaged homes, after adjusting for building type, season of measurement, and carpet in bedrooms (Fig. 1). We observed no differences in the ERMI score overall between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes. In logistic regression models comparing the odds of detectable fungi (≥ 10 spore equivalents/mg dust) between SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes for each of the 36 species measured by qPCR, we found that E. amsetlodami, C. herbarum, C. cladosporiodes, and A. penicilloides each had higher odds of being detected in SS-damaged homes than non-damaged homes (Appendix Fig. S2). The directions of effects were consistent when considering the subsets of SS-damaged homes with mold odors or visible mold upon inspection.

Fungal NGS revealed different fungal profiles among SS-damaged and non-damaged homes (Appendix Figs. S2-S4). 16 genera including Epicoccum, Neodevriesia, Arthrocladium, Pichia, and Sakaguchai were higher in SS-damaged homes whereas 10 genera including Knufia, Neophaeococcomyces, Synchytrium, Cladosporium, and Saccharomyces were lower (Appendix Fig. S2). In the subsets of SS-damaged homes with confirmed mold odors or visible mold growth, we identifed consistent genera differences including increased Arthrocladium and decreased Saccharomyces (Appendix Figs. S3-S4).

The persistence of fungal profile changes in SS-damaged homes

Fungal diversity in SS-damaged homes appeared to increase over time but by visit 3 was still below visit 1 of non-damaged homes (Fig. 2 a-c). Fungal richness decreased from visit 1 to 2 but was similar to visit 1 of non-damaged homes by visit 3. Allergenic fungi fluctuated over the visits with the highest percentage at visit 2 and the lowest at visit 3 (Fig. 2a-c). We also present the total concentrations over time of the four qPCR-measured species that were higher in SS homes (Fig. S5). C. herbarum concentrations were consistent across the three visits, while the other fungal species, particularly E. amsetlodami and A. penicilloides fluctuated substantially with time. For these four species, A. penicilloides was highest across all time points for homes with confirmed mold odors or visible mold.

For the NGS analyses, at visit 3 the relative abundance of genus Epicoccum and Arthrocladium remained elevated, and the relative abundance of each had doubled on average in the SS-damaged homes (Appendix Fig. S3). At visit 3, the relative abundance of genus Cladosporium remained suppressed in SS-damaged homes, with values at visit 3 less than half those at visit one. Genus Knufia, increased over time while genus Sakaguchia decreased over time. The other genera identified with the most significant differences between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes in the signature analysis appeared to fluctuate over time.

The prevalence of allergic sensitization to mold among SS-damaged and non-SS-damaged homes

Among asthmatic children living in SS-damaged homes, over half (50.9%) had fungal sensitization to at least one of the fungi tested, compared to 44% among children living in non-damaged homes (Fig. 3). Fungal sensitization to Alternaria alternata (36.8%), Candida albicans (22.8%), and Aureobasidium pullulans (19.3%) was the most common among asthmatic children living in SS-damaged homes. The eight fungal tests that had the most common response or represented sole fungal sensitizers among the SS children were tested in asthmatic children in non-SS-damaged homes. When comparing fungal sensitization (> 0.1 IU/ml) between asthmatic children living in SS-damaged vs. non-damaged homes (Appendix Fig. S6a, b), we did not find that any individual species sensitizations significantly differed. When using the higher cut point for positive (≥ 0.35 IU/ml), the prevalence of sensitization was again similar between the asthmatics in SS-damaged and non-damaged homes (Appendix Fig. S7a, b).

The effects of changed fungal profiles

We found higher total fungi concentrations and allergenic fungi concentrations, and lower fungal diversity (Shannon) in SS-damaged homes, compared to non-damaged homes (Fig. 1). We also identified the top 5 increased and decreased genera between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes. We compared each of these significantly different metrics against FEV1/FVC and lnFeNO in linear regression analyses. For example, genus Arthrocladium, which was elevated in SS-damaged homes, was positively associated with eNO and negatively associated with lung function (Appendix Figs. S7 and S8).

The reported frequencies of wheeze, nighttime waking because of cough, breathlessness, or chest tightness, and urgent medical visits for asthma were not significantly different when adjusting for key confounders (Fig. 4). Similarly, when comparing more severely SS-damaged homes (as measured by higher % allergenic genera, lower fungal diversity, more days with damage, and higher measured household humidity) there were no statistically significant differences in symptoms (Fig. S10). We found no significant differences in linear regression models, comparing lung function, as measured by FEV1/FVC, among those living in SS-damaged vs non-damaged homes (Table 2). However, there was evidence that airway inflammation, as measured by FeNO, was slightly lower among those living in SS-damaged vs non-damaged homes. In sensitivity analyses considering the time since SS by stratifying the SS-damaged participants into early and late groups, our main findings were consistent (Figs. S11, S12)

DISCUSSION

In this analysis, we investigated differences in fungal profiles, their persistence, and subsequent health impacts among NYC children with asthma residing in SS-damaged and non-damaged homes. We found that, compared to non-damaged homes, homes with SS-associated water damage had higher total fungi and relative allergenic fungi concentrations, and lower fungal diversity. We found no difference in IgE sensitivity between children living in SS-damaged and non-damaged homes, but found that overall, IgE sensitivity to fungi was high (> 40%) among this home damaged and non-damaged homes for this NYC population of children with mild asthma. We also did not see a significant difference in asthma symptoms. We describe potential reasons for the null health findings below.

We observed an increase in total fungi among SS-damaged homes compared to non-damaged homes. In a limited sample, we measured about an order of magnitude higher spore equivalents per milligram in SS-damaged compared to non-damaged homes. Given that water intrusion and flooding create damp conditions in which fungi can thrive, this is consistent with what we expected and what was observed following flooding events in other geographic locations (e.g., Louisiana, Colorado, and Puerto Rico). For instance, in a CDC investigation visually assessing mold growth in 112 homes following Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in New Orleans, Louisiana, 44% of homes had visible mold growth and, of these, 19% had heavy mold growth correlated with greater damage [17]. Similarly, an investigation following flood damage in Boulder, Colorado, resulting from the 2013 Colorado Front Range Flood found that fungal abundance was three times higher in flooded, relative to non-flooded, homes [49]. In our analysis, we identified higher fungal concentrations in SS-damaged homes as compared with non-damaged homes, and this difference included allergenic species. PCR approaches identified E. amsetlodami, C. herbarum, C. cladosporiodes, and A. penicilloides - which have documented allergenic properties [50–52] - each had higher odds of being detected in SS-damaged homes than non-damaged homes. These results are also consistent with findings following flooding events in other geographic locations. Following Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in Louisiana, several studies isolated Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Cladosporium, with Aspergillus and Penicillium often being the most common fungi [14, 17, 53–63]. Following Hurricane Maria, an analysis on indoor home profiles of fungi in San Juan, Puerto Rico, also found high proportions of Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Cladosporium [58]. Next generation sequencing provided further insight and resolution into the composition of the increased fungal abundance among SS-damaged homes. For example, we found that Epicoccum, a genus known to cause allergic reactions and irritate the respiratory tract [59] was much more common in SS-damaged homes compared to non-damaged homes. E. nigrum is also an indicator of present water damage and past water damage due to long spore survival times [60]. Knufia, which we found was less associated with damage, has previously been associated with lower moisture conditions [61].

It is important to consider allergenic fungi, particularly among atopic individuals, who may have an allergy specific to fungi. A previous study estimates that over half (56%) of pediatric asthma cases in the United States are atopic [62]. Notably, we observed that fungal sensitization, specifically, was common among the asthmatic children living in SS-damaged homes with more than half (51%) of the children with detectable IgE to at least one of the fungi we tested. We similarly observed a similarly high (44%) prevalence of fungal sensitization among the asthmatic children living in homes not damaged by SS. There was also high prevalence of fungal sensitization to Alternaría which belongs to the same order and has cross-reactivity with the genus Epicoccum —a genus that was elevated in damaged homes [63]. We identified no differences in fungal sensitization to the tested fungal species among children in SS-damaged and non-damaged homes, however, it is difficult to disentangle whether this null effect reflects true nullity or limitations like the IgE panel missing relevant exposures or that these assessments which were conducted in the period shortly after SS underestimate longer-term changes in sensitization patterns. It is also possible that high baseline fungal exposure in both group jointly produced a ceiling effect that masked storm-related differences. While it is important to consider allergenic fungi and atopic individuals, all fungi contain proteins and glycoproteins to which humans can have a respiratory response [50]. Therefore, the increase in fungi overall in damaged homes may have health implications outside of the allergic pathways; previous works highlights that non-atopic children may be more likely to have a response to high fungal concentrations [36].

Beyond the higher concentrations of fungi in SS-damaged homes, we identified other aspects of fungal profile changes due to Sandy which may warrant health concerns. We found that fungal diversity, as measured by the Shannon diversity index, was significantly lower in SS-damaged homes compared to non-damaged homes. This is consistent with lower richness and other measures of alpha-diversity in water-damaged and moldy indoor environments, especially in close proximity to the source [64, 65]. Previous work highlights the importance of fungal diversity in both asthma morbidity and asthma development. Highlighting the impact on morbidity, an analysis of 344 7–8 year old children from the NYC Neighborhood Asthma and Allergy Study found that Shannon diversity was inversely associated with the frequency of asthma symptoms in models that adjusted for sex, race, Hispanic ethnicity, seroatopy, and maternal asthma [66]. However, fungal diversity is a non-specific measure and more research is needed to understand the associations with health outcomes.

Despite higher levels of allergenic fungi, higher concentrations of some fungal species and lower fungal diversity—all of which are health-relevant fungal exposures— we did not observe a statistically significant difference in asthma symptoms among asthmatic children living in SS-damaged versus non-damaged homes. These null findings could be explained by a number of limitations including (1) lacking pre-SS measurements, (2) limited power to detect differences, (3) a limited selection of fungal extracts, (4) relatively mild asthma among the study population, (5) selection bias, (6) missing confounder information, and (7) seasonality impacts. While our study benefited from key strengths including the temporality of our measures and our sample size of nearly 60 homes in the SS-damaged and non-damaged groups, which is larger than previous studies, these outlined limitations must be considered to fully contextualize the results. First, the study lacked pre-SS measurements of exposure and health outcomes. If the control asthmatic children we used for this study had greater asthma morbidity than the children living in the SS-damaged homes before SS, this could have biased the study findings toward the null. Second, the sample size, while large for a disaster-related mold study of this type, still may have been underpowered to detect smaller differences in asthma symptoms between children living in SS-damaged vs. undamaged homes. A recently published study that used administrative insurance claims data to examine the impact of tropical cyclones from 2000–2018 in the Eastern United States found no overall association between tropical cyclones and asthma exacerbation, but did find that there was an increase in asthma exacerbations in the 12 months following SS (RR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.39) [67]. It is important to point out that we were statistically underpowered to detect an increased risk of this magnitude. Third, particularly for the atopy analysis, we had a limited selection of fungal extracts to examine atopy in this population. The panel may not have been reflective of the composition of fungal sources in the SS and non-SS environment. Fourth, the children enrolled in both our SS-damaged and non-damaged homes all had relatively mild asthma, with infrequent symptoms, so these findings should not be generalized to more severe asthmatics with more frequent symptoms. Fifth, there is possible selection bias for the damaged homes included in the study. It is possible that those who reported damage, especially those who were recruited through certain channels like those who had participated in fungal contamination cleanings, were more proactive with remedial measures than the general population would have been, which could have underestimated the associated between SS damage and health impacts. In our surveys among damaged homes a high percentage reported actively drying their homes (66%) or using cleaning products that prevent mold (71%). Sixth, though we controlled for key environmental variables including flooring materials and building type, there are some variables like the presence and use of air conditioning that may influence the fungal profiles of a home on which we did not have information. Seventh, not all the fungal measurements were taken at the same time although there is known seasonality in fungal growth. To account for this limitation, we incorporated the season of collection into all regression models. Eighth, there are other people beyond asthmatics that would be vulnerable to mold exposures (e.g., subjects that are immunocompromised or have COPD) that were not considered in this paper. In a similar vein, since our study population focused on asthmatic children and recruited through a middle-income health insurance plan, our results may not be generalizable to the NYC population. For example, low-income residents living in the NYC Housing Authority dealt with unremedied mold for months to years following the storms [24]. These types of exposures and scenarios were not evaluated in the present study.

Furthermore, there may be longer-term impacts on asthma development in the SS-damaged homes that would not have been experienced otherwise, resulting from SS-driven changes in fungal diversity. In a Californian birth cohort, where researchers identified 13 asthma cases and 28 controls, lower fungal diversity measured by the number of fungal operational taxonomic units in early life (12 months) was associated with asthma development by age 7 [68]. We also measured lower operational taxonomic units in the SS-damaged homes, though these were not significant decreases compared to the non-damaged homes. Another factor contributing to the longer-term impacts of SS damage is the persistence of changes in the fungal profile. It appeared that some of the fungal species associated with SS damage as measured via quantitative PCR such as E. amsetlodami, C. herbarum, C. cladosporiodes, and A. penicilloides persisted in the domestic environment, which may have health implications. For example, Cladosporium has been associated with asthma in other studies and ~10% of the children are sensitized [50]. We also found that the lower fungal diversity and richness we measured appeared stable across visits, which could have longer-term health implications. Studies following workers involved in mold remediation efforts found that these workers were initially exposed to high levels of fungi but that this increased drastically within the first year [12]. Our study found that at baseline 6–18 months away from Sandy, fungal composition, concentrations, and diversity metrics were markedly different between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes and that some of these changes persisted at least another year. Our repeat analyses also showed that total fungi were still high at the third visit, possibly suggesting that the previous home damage set the stage for certain fungi to thrive in the future. It is important to acknowledge that the changes in the fungal profile over time could have been due to increasing time since SS damage, use of disinfectants or other remediation measures, or natural seasonal variation, which was not something we could determine with this study design. For instance, a seasonal analysis of fungi in NYC found that spring is associated with increased total and allergenic fungal concentrations in house dust [69].

Additional fungal measures related to fungal function might provide additional insights in the future [70]. The Environmental Relative Moldiness Index (ERMI is a scale developed in 2007 with the purpose of describing mold burden in a home with a single value [40]. The tool has been evaluated for use to quantify mold and its health impacts [71]. However, the US EPA does not recommend the routine public use of ERMI in homes, schools, and other buildings and instead recommends inspection for water damage and mold [72]. Notably, direct measures of dampness and mold are more strongly associated with health outcomes [73]. In our analysis, the ERMI was not able to detect differences in fungal exposure between SS-damaged and non-damaged homes, evident in other metrics such as the Shannon diversity index. Thus, the ERMI was not able to identify mold burden due to water damage in our study. Comparing the ERMI with NGS data, we found that prevalent fungi in SS-damaged homes were not represented on the ERMI panel, highlighting one of the limitations of its use. Though our work moves beyond the ERMI by presenting total fungi, allergenic fungi, fungal diversity, and fungal richness, there is still opportunity to more fully capture fungal exposures through metrics like gene expression, which do not rely on identifying specific genus or species [70].

Environmental conditions, such as temperature, water availability, light quality/quantity, and humidity directly affect fungal growth and survival and some studies suggest these conditions have shifted in response to climate change [74]. Projections indicate that extreme weather events that lead to flooding and water damage, like SS, could become more frequent and intense [11, 12]. These events increase the risk of domestic fungal exposure, particularly in urban areas with large populations at low elevation and older or less resilient neighborhood and housing infrastructure [13–16, 30, 58, 75]. Our results add to growing literature demonstrating that these water intrusion events lead to significant changes in domestic fungal profiles [14–17]. We highlighted a high percentage of mold sensitivity among NYC asthmatics and demonstrated increased total and allergenic fungal load and decreased diversity and richness among SS-damaged homes, all of which are parameters that have been previously tied to adverse health conditions [39, 62]. Given the high prevalence of asthma and mold sensitivity in urban populations like NYC, fungal exposure following extreme weather events represents an ongoing public health concern [11, 12, 14–16, 63].

There is a growing interest in measuring total environment (exposome) [76]. Here we focused only on home exposures but did not consider whether the children went to schools damaged by Sandy. Expanding our studies to measure the total environment and addressing the limitations and other necessary next steps outlined herein represent future directions that can (a) improve our understanding of fungal exposures in a changing climate and (b) identify ways to reduce the health burden of indoor fungal exposures.

CONCLUSION

Using qPCR and NGS approaches, we found that SS-damaged homes experienced persistent increased total and allergenic fungal exposure and decreased diversity compared to non-damaged homes— changes that have previously been associated with both asthma morbidity and development. However, among a population of children with relatively mild asthma, these differences were not consistently associated with asthma outcomes in the years to follow the storm.
