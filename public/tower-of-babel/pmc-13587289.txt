Yeast surface display-based screening and preliminary characterization of nectin-4-targeting nanobodies for bladder cancer
Authors: Jianhua Wen, Liangyi Wei, Jun Huang, Liujuan Zhou, Jiayin Yu, Hualin Cao, Haiqi Liang, Min Qin, Zelin Cui, Hao Chen, Yujian Li, Jiawen Zhao, Fubo Wang, Siming Liao, Jiwen Cheng

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvanced bladder cancer (BCa) is associated with a poor prognosis, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies with improved efficacy and reduced toxicity. Nectin-4 (PVRL4, here after referred to as N4) is a clinically validated therapeutic target; however, existing antibody-based agents are limited by inadequate tumor penetration and off-target toxicities. Nanobodies (Nbs) possess favorable characteristics, including small molecular size, enhanced tissue penetration, and ease of sengineering. This study aimed to identify and preliminarily characterize N4-targeting Nb candidates for BCa using a yeast surface display-based screening strategy.ResultsN4 was highly expressed in BCa and significantly associated with an unfavorable prognosis. The YSD platform supported efficient library expansion, surface display, and iterative enrichment of N4-binding clones. Among the identified Nbs, Nb-80 exhibited apparent binding activity, with an EC₅₀ of 0.150 μg/mL. BLI analysis demonstrated rapid association–dissociation kinetics.ConclusionsAn N4-binding Nb candidate was identified and preliminarily characterized using a YSD-based screening strategy. These findings support the utility of the YSD platform for Nb discovery and provide a preliminary basis for further validation of N4-targeting Nb candidates in BCa.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01170-w.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is one of the most common malignancies of the urinary system, ranking as the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer in men and imposing a significant socioeconomic burden [1]. Despite advancements in surgical, radiotherapeutic and chemotherap euticinterventions, the high rates of recurrence and metastasis present substantial challenges in improving patient outcomes [2]. Targeted therapy has thus emerged as a crucial adjunct or alternative treatment strategy for advanced or metastatic BCa [3–5]. Therefore, the identification of tumor-associated targets that are highly expressed and functionally relevant in BCa is essential for overcoming current therapeutic limitations.

Nectin-4 (PVRL4, hereafter referred to as N4) is a transmembrane cell adhesion molecule that is markedly overexpressed in BCa and closely associated with tumor invasion, metastasis, immune regulation, and poor prognosis [6]. Previous studies have reported N4 positivity in approximately 87% of non–muscle-invasive urothelial carcinomas and 68% of muscle-invasive urothelial carcinomas [7]. Due to its tumor-selective expression, N4 has become an attractive target for antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) development. However, N4-targeted ADCs based on IgG frameworks are limited by their large molecular size, restricted tumor penetration, and dose-limiting toxicities, including cutaneous adverse effects [8]. Consequently, reducing the antibody size to improve intratumoral distribution has become a key focus in targeted drug development.

Nbs are single-domain antibodies derived from camelid heavy-chain antibodies and represent the smallest known functional antigen-binding fragments [9]. Their small size enables efficient tissue diffusion and homogeneous distribution [10]. In addition, Nbs exhibit high stability, low immunogenicity, and ease of genetic engineering [11–13]. These unique properties have enabled the successful clinical translation of Nb-based therapeutics, exemplified by the approval of caplacizumab [14]and the rapid expansion of Nb-derived platforms in oncology and immunotherapy [15]. In the context of solid tumors, nanobody–drug conjugates have demonstrated faster tumor accumulation and improved antitumor efficacy relative to traditional ADCs in preclinical studies [16–19]. Nevertheless, Nb-based targeting of N4 in BCa has not yet been reported. Compared with traditional immunized libraries, synthetic Nb libraries enable rapid construction and rational design, providing an efficient platform for screening N4-specific Nbs [20, 21].

Compared with traditional screening systems such as phage display, YSD offers an eukaryotic display environment that is more conducive to preserving the proper folding and functional conformation of Nbs. Furthermore, integration with magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) allows high-throughput, quantitative, and iterative screening of large libraries, with simultaneous assessment of clone specificity, binding characteristics, and display efficiency [22, 23]. Therefore, YSD represents an effective platform for the discovery of N4-specific Nbs and supports subsequent optimization through affinity maturation and engineering.

These findings offer a strong biological and clinical basis for exploring alternative N4-targeting approaches with enhanced tumor penetration, such as Nb-based binders. To this end, a YSD platform was employed to screen and characterize N4-specific Nbs. The apparent binding activity and preliminary kinetic properties of the selected Nbs were evaluated, providing an initial basis for the identification and preliminary characterization of N4-targeting Nb candidates in BCa.

Methods

Data acquisition

All datasets used in this study were obtained from publicly available databases. RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for 407 BCa samples and 19 adjacent normal tissues were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Microarray datasets (GSE13507 and GSE48075) and a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE135337) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Immunohistochemical staining images of BCa and normal bladder tissues were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database.

Data processing and bioinformatic Analysis

A systematic bioinformatic workflow was applied to investigate the biological significance and prognostic value of N4 in BCa. Differential expression analysis was performed using the TCGA-BLCA dataset, in which samples were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on the median N4 expression level. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the edgeR package with thresholds of |log₂ fold change| >1 and p < 0.05.

Survival analysis was conducted by integrating gene expression data with clinical information from the GSE13507 dataset. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the associations between N4 expression and overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Based on these results, a prognostic nomogram was subsequently constructed.Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of the GSE135337 dataset was performed using the Seurat package. After quality control, principal component analysis (PCA), uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering, and cell-type annotation using SingleR were conducted to characterize N4 expression at the single-cell level.

Recovery, expansion, and surface display assessment of the YSD library

The YSD library stored at − 80 °C was inoculated into Yglc4.5 −Trp medium and cultured overnight (approximately 16 h) for recovery. A small aliquot of the culture was serially diluted (10− 4 −10− 7) and plated onto YPD agar plates. After incubation for 48 h, colony-forming units were counted to estimate library diversity, and colony morphology was examined under a Nikon 80i microscope at 40× magnification.Recovered yeast cells were subsequently induced in −Trp medium supplemented with galactose to express Nbs. Surface-displayed Nbs were stained with an anti-HA antibody (Ab6, Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated HA antibody; catalog no. CL647-66006) and analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate display efficiency.

YSD-based screening of N4-binding Nbs

A YSD-based stepwise screening workflow was employed to enrich N4-binding clones through negative selection, antigen-guided enrichment, and iterative FACS.Magnetic beads were washed and activated using selection buffer prior to use. Yeast cells were first incubated with magnetic beads for negative selection to remove nonspecific binders. Subsequently, His-tagged N4 antigen was added for positive selection, and the antigen-binding yeast populations were collected as round 1 (R1). Following induction, the R1 population underwent a second round of enrichment (R2) using half the antigen concentration and bead amount. Four subsequent rounds of FACS were performed on the R2 population. During the sorting process, Fc-tagged and His-tagged N4 antigens were alternated, with progressively decreasing antigen concentrations (100, 50, 20, and 10 nM). This concentration gradient was designed to gradually increase selection stringency while maintaining candidate diversity during the early screening stage, thereby facilitating iterative enrichment of clones with relatively favorable binding capacity and reducing the risk of prematurely losing potentially valuable candidates. To isolate double-positive yeast populations, surface display was detected using Ab6 (Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated anti-HA; CL647-66006) or Ab9 (Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-HA; CL488-66006), in combination with Ab8 (IgG Fc antibody; ab19591) or Ab7 (6×His tag antibody; CL647-66005) for antigen binding detection.

Unstained samples and secondary antibody-only controls were included to establish gating strategies.

Monoclonal evaluation of N4-binding Nbs

Monoclonal yeast clones representing independent Nb sequences were subjected to specificity validation. Two experimental groups were established, in which yeast cells were incubated with either Fc-tagged or His-tagged N4 antigen. Following incubation with N4 antigen, surface expression and antigen binding were independently detected using Ab6 or Ab9 and Ab8 or Ab7, respectively, and double-positive populations were analyzed by flow cytometry. Double-positive populations were defined based on the gating boundaries established from unstained and secondary antibody-only controls, and subsequent clone evaluation focused on cells distributed within the Q3-2 gate.

Purification of N4-binding Nbs

Nb genes were cloned into the pPICZαA vector. A Pichia pastoris secretory expression system was selected as a practical strategy for soluble protein production and subsequent functional characterization. Linearized plasmids were electroporated into Pichia pastoris, and positive transformants were selected on zeocin-containing plates. Culture supernatants were collected, and Nbs were purified using nickel-affinity chromatography. The purity of the purified Nbs was assessed by 12% SDS–PAGE, and the protein concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The purified Nbs were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Evaluation of binding activity of N4-binding Nbs

Nb’s binding activity was evaluated using an indirect enzyme-linked ELISA. Fc-tagged N4 antigen was coated onto ELISA plates at a concentration of 1 µg/mL. Serial dilutions of Nbs (initial concentration range, 2.0–20 µg/mL) were added to the plates to assess binding sensitivity.A MonoRab™ Rabbit Anti-Camelid VHH Cocktail conjugated with horseradish peroxidase was used as the secondary antibody. Color development was achieved using TMB substrate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. ELISA data were obtained from three independent replicates and are presented as mean ± SEM. Apparent EC₅₀ values were estimated by nonlinear regression fitting of the dose–response curves. In addition, cross-reactivity of the selected Nbs was further evaluated by ELISA using recombinant Nectin-1(N1), Nectin-2(N2), and Nectin-3(N3) proteins. BSA and OX40 were included as control antigens to assess nonspecific binding under comparable assay conditions.

Binding kinetic analysis of N4-binding Nbs

Binding kinetics were analyzed using BLI on an Octet® R8 system (Sartorius). The anti-human Fc capture biosensors were hydrated and equilibrated prior to analysis. After establishing a stable baseline in kinetic buffer, the biosensors were loaded with 2 µg/mL of Fc-tagged N4 antigen to a loading threshold of 1.0 nm. Following equilibration, the biosensors were sequentially immersed in Nbs solutions at serial concentrations (0.617–10 µg/mL, two-fold dilutions) to monitor association, followed by transfer to buffer for dissociation monitoring for 180 s. The biosensors were regenerated using 10 mM glycine–HCl (pH 1.7) between runs. Raw data were processed to exclude sensorgrams with response changes below 0.01 nm, as signals near this level were considered close to baseline and unsuitable for reliable kinetic fitting. Under the analyte concentrations used in this study, such low responses were more likely to reflect weak binding with an insufficient signal-to-noise ratio than inadequate ligand loading on the sensor. Processed sensorgrams were globally fitted using a 1:1 binding model to calculate the association rate constant (Ka), dissociation rate constant (Kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Structural modeling and molecular docking

To investigate the interaction between Nb-80 and N4, computational modeling was performed. The amino acid sequence of VHH-80 was submitted to the SWISS-MODEL server to generate a three-dimensional structure of Nb-80. Molecular docking was conducted using the ClusPro 2.0 server with the extracellular domain of N4 (PDB ID: 4FRW).The top-ranked docking conformation was selected, and the binding Gibbs free energy (ΔG) at 25 °C was predicted using the PRODIGY web server. The final complex structure was visualized and analyzed using PyMOL software.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatic analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). For parametric data, comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance. For nonparametric data, comparisons between two groups were conducted using the chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

N4 is highly expressed in BCa

To investigate the expression pattern of N4 in BCa, transcriptomic data from 407 BCa tissues and adjacent normal tissues in the TCGA-BLCA cohort were analyzed. N4 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (p = 0.013; Fig. 1A). Further analysis showed that N4 expression was not significantly associated with patient age (p = 0.744; Fig. 1B). In contrast, N4 expression was correlated with several clinicopathological characteristics. Male patients with BCa exhibited higher N4 expression levels than female patients (p = 0.029; Fig. 1C). Additionally, N4 expression was significantly higher in patients with N2-stage disease than in those with N0-stage disease (p = 0.021; Fig. 1D). Consistent results were obtained from independent transcriptomic datasets. In the GSE13507 cohort, which included 188 BCa and 68 normal urothelial samples, N4 expression was markedly elevated in BCa tissues (p < 0.001; Fig. 1E). Moreover, N4 expression was significantly higher in muscle-invasive BCa (MIBC) than in non–muscle-invasive BCa (NMIBC) samples (p = 0.045; Fig. 1F). Similar expression patterns were observed in the GSE48075 dataset, where N4 expression was also higher in MIBC than in NMIBC samples (p = 0.002; Fig. 1G). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated stronger N4 staining intensity in BCa tissues (high expression) compared with normal urothelial tissues (medium expression) (Figs. 1H and I). High N4 expression is associated with poor prognosis in BCa

To further investigate the prognostic significance of N4 expression in BCa, Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed. The results showed that patients with high N4 expression exhibited significantly poorer OS, PFI, and RFS compared with those with low N4 expression (Figs. 2A–C). Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis indicated that age, T stage, N stage, M stage, muscle invasion status, and N4 expression level were all significant risk factors for OS in patients with BCa (Fig. 2D). Further multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that age, T stage, N stage, M stage, and N4 expression remained independent risk factors for OS (Fig. 2E). Based on these independent prognostic factors, including age, T stage, N stage, M stage, and N4 expression level, a nomogram was constructed to predict OS in patients with BCa (Fig. 2F). The calibration curve indicated good agreement between the predicted and observed 5-year survival probabilities, suggesting favorable stability and predictive performance of the nomogram (Fig. 2G). YSD supports efficient Nb screening

After confirming the expansion capacity and surface display efficiency of the yeast Nb library, the overall YSD-based screening strategy was summarized schematically (Fig. 4F). The workflow incorporated two antigen-detection formats for flow cytometric evaluation of Nb surface display and antigen binding, using Fc-tagged or His-tagged N4 with the corresponding detection antibodies. Starting from the naive yeast library, antigen-binding clones were subjected to iterative rounds of antigen incubation, MACS, FACS, and amplification, allowing progressive enrichment of candidate N4-binding Nbs. This schematic overview outlines the stepwise screening design that formed the basis for the subsequent enrichment and validation analyses.

YSD-Based enrichment of N4-binding Nbs

To isolate Nb clones capable of binding to N4, the yeast-displayed Nb library was subjected to a stepwise screening strategy. After two rounds of magnetic bead enrichment, the library size was markedly reduced. Colony counting on YPD agar plates indicated that approximately 10^5–10^6 viable yeast clones were recovered following selection (Fig. 5A). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a marked increase in the proportion of antigen-binding yeast cells after the second round of magnetic selection compared with the first round (Fig. 5B -C). Subsequently, four consecutive rounds of FACS were performed to further enrich N4-binding Nb clones. During the sorting process, there was a progressive increase in the proportion of N4-binding positive cells observed with successive enrichment rounds. Notably, the binding-positive population increased to 20.0% under the lowest antigen concentration tested (10 nM) (Fig. 5D–G). Identification and monoclonal validation of N4-binding Nbs

To identify N4–binding Nb sequences, a total of 136 monoclonal yeast clones were randomly selected for sequencing from the enriched screening population. Sequence alignment and redundancy analysis yielded 34 unique Nb sequences, consistent with convergence of the enriched population after multiple rounds of MACS and FACS. The monoclonal yeast clones corresponding to the 34 independent Nb sequences were induced for surface expression and subjected to flow cytometric validation using two dual-color staining strategies, including anti-hemagglutinin (HA) combined with anti–6×His and anti-HA combined with anti-Fc antibodies. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of the proportion of double-positive cells within the Q3-2 gate and the corresponding dot plot distribution patterns, a total of nine N4-binding positive clones were identified. Further flow cytometric analysis revealed that, among the positive clones, Nb6 and Nb80 were selected as representative clones for presentation (Fig. 6A). Although Nb6 showed a relatively high proportion of double-positive cells, Nb80 displayed a more concentrated antigen-binding positive population. Sequence analysis revealed that these positive Nb clones shared conserved framework regions, while their complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences showed substantial diversity (Fig. 6B). In addition, a phylogenetic tree constructed based on the 34 unique Nb sequences demonstrated clear divergence among the positive clones, indicating distinct evolutionary relationships at the sequence level (Fig. 6C). Nb-80 exhibits apparent in vitro binding activity after purification

Nine Nb clones with distinct CDR3 sequences were successfully expressed in Pichia pastoris using the pPICZαA vector, each carrying a C-terminal His tag, and their expression was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis. The recombinant expression constructs were subjected to restriction enzyme digestion before protein expression (Fig. 7A). Following induction, culture supernatants were collected and Nbs were purified by Ni–NTA affinity chromatography.SDS–PAGE analysis showed that all purified Nbs appeared as single bands with the expected molecular weight, indicating high purity (Fig. 7B). The binding activities of the purified Nbs were subsequently evaluated by ELISA. Among the tested candidates, four Nbs (Nb-8, Nb-31, Nb-39, and Nb-80) showed apparent binding activity toward N4, with minimal reactivity against the control antigens BSA and OX40 (Fig. 7C). Cross-reactivity with recombinant Nectin-1 (N1), Nectin-2 (N2), and Nectin-3 (N3) was further evaluated by ELISA to provide an initial assessment of selectivity within the Nectin family (Fig. 7D).Under the current assay conditions, no obvious cross-reactivity with N1, N2, or N3 was detected. Dose-dependent ELISA analysis showed that Nb-80 exhibited apparent binding activity, with a half-maximal effective concentration (EC₅₀) of approximately 0.153 μg/mL, corresponding to an estimated concentration of ~ 10 nM based on its molecular weight (~14 kDa) (Fig. 7E). Among the evaluated candidates, Nb-80 showed the strongest apparent ELISA reactivity towards N4. Binding characteristics of N4–binding Nbs

To further quantitatively characterize the binding kinetics of N4-binding Nbs, BLI was employed to analyze their interactions with the N4 antigen. Fc-tagged N4 antigen was immobilized onto the biosensor surface as the ligand, and purified Nbs were used as analytes for kinetic measurements. As shown in Fig. 8A–D, BLI sensorgrams demonstrated rapid association of Nbs with the N4 antigen, followed by a fast dissociation phase. The calculated equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) were in the micromolar range (approximately 5–25 μM), consistent with a fast association–fast dissociation kinetic profile. Given the relatively low response signals and rapid dissociation observed for some candidates, the kinetic parameters should be interpreted with caution. To further characterize the potential binding epitope between Nbs and N4, molecular docking analysis was performed using ClusPro 2.0. PRODIGY prediction estimated a Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) of −7.6 kcal/mol for the Nb–antigen complex, providing a preliminary computational model of the potential interaction. As shown in Fig. 8E–F, the docking model revealed that Nb-80 primarily interacts with N4 through hydrogen bonding. In addition, no prominent hydrophobic interactions or salt bridges were observed between Nb-80 and the FG loop or C′C″ loop regions of N4.

Discussion

Advanced metastatic urothelial carcinoma is associated with an extremely poor prognosis, with a reported 5-year OS rate of approximately 10% [24]. This poor outcome is mainly due to the limited effectiveness and low response rates of standard first-line chemotherapy [25, 26]. Targeted therapies directed against molecular biomarkers such as N4 have demonstrated improved objective response rates and survival benefits [4, 27]. Accumulating evidence indicates that N4 is highly expressed in BCa and is associated with disease progression and a poor prognosis [28, 29]. In the TCGA cohort, N4 expression was significantly higher in male patients than in female patients. Although this sex-related difference warrants further attention, the currently available evidence does not suggest that it substantially alters the overall potential applicability of N4-targeted strategies in BCa, as N4 remained generally overexpressed in BCa and was consistently associated with an unfavorable prognosis across the datasets analyzed in this study. Nevertheless, the basis and reproducibility of this sex-related difference remain unclear and should be further evaluated in independent cohorts with sex-stratified analyses. In addition to regulating tumor cell adhesion and migration, N4 is involved in immune-related processes, including antigen presentation and immune cell recruitment [30, 31]. Although currently available N4-targeted agents, such as enfortumab vedotin (EV), have shown clinical benefit, treatment responses remain variable and notable toxicities have been reported [19, 32, 33]. Nbs offer distinct advantages for targeted applications due to their small size, stability, low immunogenicity, and favorable production characteristics [16–18]. Recent studies have demonstrated that engineered N4-targeting Nbs can exhibit potent antitumor activity with reduced toxicity in gastric cancer models [34]. Thus, the identification of N4-binding Nbs using a practical and efficient screening platform may provide a useful starting point for subsequent optimization and validation.

In this study, a YSD platform was utilized for efficient screening of N4-binding Nbs. Compared with phage or bacterial display systems, YSD facilitates the presentation of properly folded and functionally relevant binders in a eukaryotic context, which is particularly advantageous for targeting structurally complex, membrane-associated antigens such as N4 [35]. Starting from a diverse library, successive rounds of magnetic enrichment followed by FACS under decreasing antigen concentrations enabled bulk enrichment and flow cytometric discrimination of candidate N4-binding clones.

ELISA analysis indicated apparent binding activity of Nb-80 under solid-phase endpoint assay conditions, whereas BLI showed a fast association–fast dissociation kinetic profile with KD values in the micromolar range. Although both parameters are related to binding characteristics, they arise from different assay contexts and therefore should not be interpreted as directly equivalent. In the present study, the ELISA-derived EC₅₀ is more appropriately regarded as an apparent binding readout, whereas the BLI-derived KD provides preliminary kinetic information. These results should therefore be interpreted cautiously and should be considered an initial indication of binding activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that the binding performance of Nbs can be improved through strategies such as multimerization, CDR engineering, and structure-guided design [36–38]. At the current stage, it is appropriate to view the identified Nb as a preliminarily characterized candidate rather than a fully validated targeting reagent.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The current assessment was mainly based on recombinant antigen-based assays and did not include cell-based verification of endogenous N4 recognition, which will be important for confirming the biological relevance and target specificity of the identified candidate. In addition, the micromolar-range affinity and rapid dissociation observed by BLI indicate that the current candidate may require further affinity optimization for applications requiring stronger binding stability or prolonged target retention. The molecular docking analysis should be interpreted only as a preliminary computational reference. Therefore, the identified Nb should be regarded as a preliminarily characterized N4-binding candidate, and further cell-based validation, affinity maturation, and functional evaluation are needed in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, expression and prognostic analyses further supported the relevance of N4 in BCa. Through a YSD platform, an N4-binding Nb candidate, Nb-80, was identified and preliminarily characterized, exhibiting apparent binding activity and preliminary kinetic characteristics.These findings support the utility of the YSD platform for Nb discovery and provide a preliminary basis for further validation of N4-targeting Nb candidates in BCa.
