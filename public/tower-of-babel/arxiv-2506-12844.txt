Choosing iteration maps for the parallel Pollard rho method
Finn Rudolph

Abstract
. Pollard’s rho method finds a prime factor p p of an integer N N by searching for a collision in a map of the form x ↦ x 2 ​ k + c x\mapsto x^{2k}+c modulo N N . This search can be parallelized to multiple machines, which may use distinct parameters k k and c c . In this paper, we give an asymptotic estimate for the expected running time of the parallel rho method depending on the choice of k k for each machine. We also prove that k = 1 k=1 is the best choice for one machine, if nothing about p p is known in advance.

1.
Introduction

In
[
CP05
, p. 255,5.24]
, Crandall and Pomerance pose the following question:
Assume
M
machines run the rho method independently on an integer

N
and the
i
-th machine uses
x\mapsto x^{2k_{i}}+c_{i}
as its
iteration map. How to assign parameters
k_{i}
to them in an optimal
way? This problem is the subject of this paper. Let

N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
be the number to be factored and
p
be the
smallest prime divisor of
N
not equal to 2. Statements about the
expected running time of the rho method to factor
N
are always
made in terms of
p
.

The choice of the
c_{i}
is not discussed here, since they do not seem
to have much influence on the
running time and are best chosen independently at random. (Except
that
0
and
-2
should be avoided, see
[
Pol75
, p. 333]
and

[
Cra99
, p. 6]
.)
In contrast, the
k_{i}
can affect
the running time significantly depending on their common factors with

p-1
. Let
d_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\gcd(p-1,2k_{i})
. The expected number
of steps the
i
-th machine requires is reduced
by a factor of
\sqrt{d_{i}-1}
(this will become clear from Theorem

1
, or see
[
BP81
]
). However, the
running time per iteration of the
i
-th machine is increased by a
factor of
\log_{2}2k_{i}
in comparison to
k_{i}=1
. Thus, it is not
immediately clear which choice of
k_{i}
yields the best expected
running time.

1.1.
The main result

As usual for the analysis of the rho method, we assume that
the rho iteration map behaves like a random map (see Heuristic

1
). Under this assumption, the expected time until some
machine finds
p
as a factor of
N
is asymptotic to

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\pi p/2}\,\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\gcd(p-1,2k_{i})-1}{\log_{2}^{2}2k_{i}}\right\rparen^{\!\!-1/2}

as
p\to\infty
. We say two functions
f,g:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
are
asymptotic
and write
f\sim g
, if
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
.

For
M=k_{1}=1
, (
1.1
) evaluates to

\sqrt{\pi p/2}
, which is the
classical estimate for rho method given
by
[
Pol75
]
. For
M=1
and arbitrary
k_{1}
, (
1.1
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sqrt{\pi p/2}\;\frac{\log_{2}2k_{1}}{\sqrt{\gcd(p-1,2k_{1})-1}}\,.

This also matches previous results, see e.g.
[
BP81
]
or

[
CP05
, p. 255,5.24]
.

1.2.
Formulation for random maps

We briefly recall aspects of the rho method relevant to our
analysis. For a complete description of the algorithm, see

[
Pol75
]
or
[
CP05
, p. 229]
. As mentioned in
§
1.1
, there is a commonly used heuristic for
analyzing the rho method. To state it, a few definitions are required. Let

\displaystyle f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},\quad f_{i}:x\mapsto x^{2k_{i}}+c_{i}

be the iteration map of the
i
-th machine, viewed modulo
p
, and

x_{i,0}\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
its initial value, also modulo
p
. The initial
value is chosen uniformly at random from
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
f:X\to X
be a function on a finite set
X
and denote by
f^{j}

the
j
-fold composition of
f
with
itself. For an element
x\in X
, we define the
rho
length
of
f
at
x
by

\displaystyle\rho(f,x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left\lvert\left\{f^{j}(x):j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\right\rvert.

We include 0 in the natural numbers, so
x
is also in the above set.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and assume that
d
divides
n
. Define

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{d}
as the set of all functions
f:\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

such that
|f^{-1}\{x\}|\in\{0,d\}
for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.
When picturing an element of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{d}
as a directed graph,
exactly
n/d
nodes have indegree
d
, and all other nodes have
indegree 0.

The main issue in the analysis of the rho method is to estimate the
number of iterations a machine requires to discover
p
. This number
is
\rho(f_{i},x_{i,0})
for the the
i
-th
machine, since it iterates
f_{i}
starting from
x_{i,0}
until a
value is encountered twice. However, there are currently no estimates
for the distribution or mean of
\rho(f_{i},\,\cdot\,)
known,
so we rely on the following heuristic.

Heuristic 1
.

The distribution of
\rho(f_{i},\,\cdot\,)
is close to the
distribution of the map
\mathcal{F}_{p-1}^{d_{i}}\times\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}
sending
(f,x)
to
\rho(f,x)
.

Close
is to be understood as
close enough to be
interchangeable for the analysis of the rho method.
An intuitive
justification for Heuristic
1
stems from the distribution
of indegrees of
f_{i}
. Let
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
be the group of units of

\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Since
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is cyclic, exactly
(p-1)/d_{i}
elements have
d_{i}
preimages under the map
x\mapsto x^{2k_{i}}
on
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. So the distribution of indegrees of

f_{i}
equals the distribution of indegrees of elements in
\mathcal{F}_{p-1}^{d_{i}}
, up to a shift by one due to the inclusion of zero.
Thus, heuristically assuming that
f_{i}
exhibits no further patterns, we
approximate the distribution of
\rho(f_{i},\,\cdot\,)
by the
rho length distribution of a random element of
\mathcal{F}_{p-1}^{d_{i}}
.
Crude as this heuristic may seem, it works remarkably well in
practice. Theoretical and numerical results regarding Heuristic

1
are summarized in §
7.3
.

We now formulate a rigorous result that can be used to derive
(
1.1
) under Heuristic
1
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
such that
d_{i}\mid n
for every
1\leq i\leq M
. We consider
M
machines simultaneously iterating a function on

\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Each machine stops when it encounters a repeated value. The
function used by the
i
-th machine is drawn uniformly at
random from
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{d_{i}}
and its initial value is drawn
uniformly from
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. The setup is represented by the space

\Omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{M}\left\lparen\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{F}_{n}^{d_{i}}\right\rparen
,
equipped with the uniform probability measure.

Theorem 1
.

For
1\leq i\leq M
, let
\lambda_{i}\in(0,\infty)
and define random
variables
X_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
by
X_{i}((x_{1},g_{1}),\dots,(x_{M},g_{M}))=\lambda_{i}\rho(g_{i},x_{i})
. Then

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lparen\min_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\right\rparen\sim\sqrt{\pi n/2}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{d_{i}-1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\right\rparen^{\!\!-1/2}

as
n\to\infty
.

One may interpret
\lambda_{i}
as the time per iteration of the
i
-th
machine, and
X_{i}
as the time required by the
i
-th machine to
terminate. Formula (
1.1
) follows from Theorem
1

by taking
n=p-1
,
d_{i}=\gcd(p-1,2k_{i})
and
\lambda_{i}=\log_{2}2k_{i}
. We choose
\lambda_{i}=\log_{2}2k_{i}
, because calculating
the power
x^{2k_{i}}
in each iteration requires
\log_{2}2k_{i}
steps
(see §
7.2
for more details).

1.3.
Without information about
p

To apply (
1.1
), we need to know
\gcd(p-1,2k_{i})
for
every
1\leq i\leq M
. But often such information is not available
when trying to factor a large number. This raises a natural question:
Which
k_{i}
should be chosen if nothing about
p
is known?

To address this question, we consider all possible congruences of
p

modulo
\ell\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lcm\{2k_{i}:1\leq i\leq M\}
, since we
already know the expected running time for a fixed congruence
class from (
1.1
). Since
p

is prime, the possible congruence classes are
exactly the units in
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
(we may assume
p\nmid\ell
,
since
p
is much larger than
\ell
in all cases of interest).
Dirichlet’s theorem implies that all these congruence classes occur
with equal frequency for sufficiently large
p
(see e.g.

[
IR90
, p. 251, Theorem 1]
). So we define

(1.3)

\displaystyle G(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\sum_{d\in\left\lparen\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}\right\rparen^{\times}}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\gcd(d-1,2k_{i})-1}{\log_{2}^{2}2k_{i}}\right\rparen^{\!\!-1/2}.

By the above discussion,
G(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})\sqrt{\pi p/2}
can be thought of as the expected time to find the
prime factor
p
, when no information about
p
is available. The
term
\sqrt{\pi p/2}
of (
1.1
) was omitted in
(
1.3
), because it is irrelevant for comparing different
parameterizations.

The choice of
k_{i}
that minimizes
G(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
could not be
identified for general
M
. Numerical computations for small
M
and

k_{i}
suggest that
k_{1}=\cdots=k_{M}=1
is optimal for every
M\geq 1
(see §
6
and
§
7.1
). For
M=1
, we prove this rigorously.

Theorem 2
.

G(1)<G(k)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.

Structure of the paper

introduces the notation and definitions,

1
. The proof of

1

2.
Preliminaries

As in §
1.2
, assume that
M,n
and

d_{i}
are nonzero natural numbers such that
d_{i}\mid n
for
i=1,\dots,M
. Define
\Omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{M}\left\lparen\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\times\mathcal{F}_{n}^{d_{i}}\right\rparen

with the uniform probability measure
P
. On
\Omega
, let
S_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{N}
be random variables given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle S_{i}:((x_{1},g_{1}),\dots,(x_{M},g_{M}))\mapsto\rho(g_{i},x_{i})\,.

Like in Theorem
1
, we also let
\lambda_{i}\in(0,\infty)
for

1\leq i\leq M
and define
X_{i}=\lambda_{i}S_{i}
.

Let
q_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}n/(d_{i}-1)
. Brent and Pollard showed
in
[
BP81
]
that

\displaystyle P(S_{i}=s\,|\,S_{i}\geq s)=s(d_{i}-1)/n=s/q_{i}\,.

Thus

(2.2)

\displaystyle P(S_{i}=s)=\begin{cases}\frac{s}{q_{i}}\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\left\lparen 1-\frac{j}{q_{i}}\right\rparen&s\leq q_{i}\\
0&s>q_{i}\\
\end{cases}\,,

because in the first
s-1
steps, no collision may occur, and in
the last step, a collision must occur.

Using the definitions in (
2.1
) and
(
2.2
), the left hand side of
(
1.2
) can be expressed as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lparen\min_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\right\rparen=\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{\infty}P(S_{1}=s_{1})\,\cdots\!\sum_{s_{M}=0}^{\infty}P(S_{M}=s_{M})\min_{i=1}^{M}(\lambda_{i}s_{i})\,.

The dots in the middle represent the sums over
s_{i}
for
i=2,\dots,M-1
.

As a motivation for the next definition, we approximate
1-j/q_{i}
by
e^{-j/q_{i}}
in (
2.2
). This yields

\displaystyle P(S_{i}=s)\approx\frac{s}{q_{i}}e^{-s(s-1)/(2q_{i})}\approx\frac{s}{q_{i}}e^{-s^{2}/(2q_{i})}\,.

The right expression is more manageable than the product in
(
2.2
) and allows us to find an asymptotic
estimate for (
2.3
).
Section §
4
is devoted to proving that this
approximation can be applied to (
2.3
) in a
rigorous way. For
q\in(0,\infty)
, define
Q_{q}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.4)

\displaystyle Q_{q}(s)=\frac{s}{q}e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}.

Note that
Q_{q}
is the density function of a
Rayleigh-distributed random variable with parameter
\sqrt{q}
.

3.
Asymptotic estimate of the Rayleigh-approximated version

The proof of Theorem
1
is structured as follows: To
estimate (
2.3
) asymptotically, we first
replace
P(S_{i}=s_{i})
by the Rayleigh densities

Q_{q_{i}}(s_{i})
, yielding

(3.1)

\displaystyle T_{d_{1},\dots,d_{M},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}}(n)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{1}}(s_{1})\,\cdots\!\sum_{s_{M}=0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{M}}(s_{M})\min_{i=1}^{M}(\lambda_{i}s_{i})\,.

When unambiguous in the context, we abbreviate
T_{d_{1},\dots,d_{M},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}}(n)
by
T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}(n)
. In this section, we derive an asymptotic estimate for

T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}
, which will be exactly the right hand side of
(
1.2
). In §
4
, we show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lparen\min_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}\right\rparen\sim T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}\,,

thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1
.

To evaluate
T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}
, we wish to replace the sums by
integrals. In Lemma
1
, the
Euler-Maclaurin formula is applied to show that this substitution
preserves the asymptotic behavior in the case
M=1
. We allow a
slightly more general
class of functions than necessary, which allows us to extend the
result to
M\geq 2
later. For
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, define
I(a,b)=[a,b]
if
b<\infty
and
I(a,b)=[a,b)
if
b=\infty
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
a\leq b
.
Let
g:I(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
be a piecewise linear function, which is
differentiable at all but finitely many points. Then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{s=a}^{b}g(s)Q_{q}(s)-\int_{a}^{b}g(s)Q_{q}(s)\,ds=O\left\lparen 1\right\rparen

as
q\to\infty
. For fixed
g
, this holds
uniformly with respect to
a
and
b
. That is, there exist two

O
-constants that work
for every pair
a\leq b
.

Proof.

We make two quick reductions. First, we may assume that
b<\infty
, because we will prove that
the asymptotic estimate holds uniformly with respect to
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus it also holds in the limit
b\to\infty
.

Second, we reduce to the case that
g
is linear. For this, let

u_{1}<\cdots<u_{l}
be the non-differentiable points of
g
in

(a,b)
. Define
u_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}a
and
u_{l+1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}b+1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{s=a}^{b}g(s)Q_{q}(s)-\int_{a}^{b}g(s)Q_{q}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{l}\left\lparen\sum_{s=\lceil u_{j}\rceil}^{\lceil{u_{j+1}}\rceil-1}\ g(s)Q_{q}(s)\,-\,\int_{\lceil u_{j}\rceil}^{\lceil{u_{j+1}}\rceil-1}g(s)Q_{q}(s)\,ds\right\rparen-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\!\!\!\int_{\ \ \,\lceil u_{j}\rceil-1}^{\lceil{u_{j}}\rceil}g(s)Q_{q}(s)\,ds\,.

Because
g
is linear on every interval
[\lceil u_{j}\rceil,\lceil{u_{j+1}}\rceil-1]
, the left sum in the
second row is bounded by
O(q^{-1/2})
if (
3.2
)
holds for linear functions.
The right sum is
O(q^{-1})
, because
Q_{q}(s)=O(q^{-1})
as
q\to\infty
for every
s\in[0,\infty)
.
Thus, it suffices to prove (
3.2
) in the
case that
g
is linear.

For the sake of shorter notation, let
h(s)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g(s)Q_{q}(s)
. By
applying the second derivative version of the Euler-Maclaurin formula, we get

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{s=a}^{b}h(s)=\int_{a}^{b}h(s)\,ds+\frac{h(a)+h(b)}{2}+\frac{B_{2}}{2}\left\lparen h^{\prime}(b)-h^{\prime}(a)\right\rparen+R_{2}\,,

where
B_{2}
is the second Bernoulli number and

R_{2}
the remainder term. The second summand of
(
3.3
) expands to

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{h(a)+h(b)}{2}=\frac{g(a)ae^{-a^{2}/(2q)}+g(b)be^{-b^{2}/(2q)}}{2q}\,.

Note that
s\mapsto(s^{2}/q)e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}
is bounded
for
s\in[0,\infty)
and its maximal value is independent of

q
. Thus (
3.4
) is bounded independently of

q,a
and
b
for
q\geq 1
. As for the third summand of
(
3.3
), the derivative of
h
is

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(s)=\left\lparen\frac{sg^{\prime}(s)+g(s)}{q}-\frac{s^{2}g(s)}{q^{2}}\right\rparen e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}\,.

Again using that
s\mapsto(s^{2}/q)e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}
is
bounded independently of
q
, we see that
h^{\prime}(a)
and
h^{\prime}(b)

are bounded independently of
q,a
and
b
. Thus the third
summand of (
3.3
) is
O\left\lparen 1\right\rparen
.

Lastly, the remainder term is

(3.5)

\displaystyle R_{2}=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{B_{2}(s-\lfloor s\rfloor)}{2}\,h^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds\,.

where
B_{2}
is the second Bernoulli polynomial. The second
derivative of
h
is

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\left\lparen\frac{2g^{\prime}(s)}{q}-\frac{s^{2}g^{\prime}(s)}{q^{3}}+\frac{s^{3}g(s)}{q^{3}}-\frac{3sg(s)}{q^{2}}\right\rparen e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}\,.

Inserting this into (
3.5
) and
applying the fact that
|B_{2}(s)|\leq 1
for every
s\in[0,1]
, we get

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left\lvert R_{2}\right\rvert\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left\lparen\left\lvert\frac{2g^{\prime}(s)}{q}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{s^{2}g^{\prime}(s)}{q^{3}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{s^{3}g(s)}{q^{3}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{3sg(s)}{q^{2}}\right\rvert\right\rparen e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}\,ds\,.

We may assume that
g(s)\geq 0
and
g^{\prime}(s)\geq 0
for every
s\geq 0
.
If this is not the case, we replace
g
by a different linear function
\widetilde{g}
such that
\widetilde{g}(s)\geq|g(s)|
for
s\geq 0
. Thus the absolute
value bars in (
3.6
) may be removed.
By integrating the four summands separately and using that

\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{l}e^{-s^{2}/(2q)}\,ds=O\left\lparen q^{(l+1)/2}\right\rparen
, it follows that
|R_{2}|=O\left\lparen q^{-1/2}\right\rparen
.
∎

Repeatedly applying Lemma
1
to the nested sum in
(
3.1
) allows us to replace each sum by an integral.
This yields the following estimate for
T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}
.

Lemma 2
.

As
n\to\infty
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle T_{d_{i},\lambda_{i}}(n)=\sqrt{\pi n/2}\,\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{d_{i}-1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\right\rparen^{\!\!-1/2}+O\left\lparen 1\right\rparen\,.

Proof.

Let
g:[0,\infty)^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function which is piecewise linear with
finitely many non-differentiable points when any
M-1

coordinates are fixed. We claim that for such
g

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{1}}(s_{1})\,&\cdots\!\sum_{s_{M}=0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{M}}(s_{M})\,g(s_{1},\dots s_{M})\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{1}}(s_{1})\,\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{q_{M}}(s_{M})\,g(s_{1},\dots s_{M})\,ds_{M}\cdots ds_{1}+O\left\lparen 1\right\rparen\,.\end{split}

The proof is by induction over
M
. For
M=1
, the claim follows
immediately from Lemma
1
. If
M\geq 2
, we apply Lemma
1
to the innermost sum.
This is possible because
s_{1},\dots s_{M-1}
are fixed
inside this sum, so
g
(as a function in
s_{M}
) satisfies the
hypothesis of Lemma
1
. Thus

Justification of the Rayleigh density approximation

For the proof of Theorem

is asymptotic to

Lemma 3
.

Let

be as in (

and

(a)

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{q_{i}}(s)-P(S_{i}=s)\right\rvert=O\left\lparen q_{i}^{4\alpha-3}\right\rparen

(b)

For every

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{q_{i}}(s)-P(S_{i}=s)\right\rvert\leq\frac{s}{q_{i}}e^{-(s-1)s/(2q_{i})}\,.

Proof.

For part

, let

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{q_{i}}(s)-P(S_{i}=s)\right\rvert=\frac{s}{q_{i}}\left\lvert e^{-s^{2}/(2q_{i})}-\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\left\lparen e^{-j/q_{i}}-R(-j/q_{i})\right\rparen\right\rvert\,.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\lvert Q_{q_{i}}(s)-P(S_{i}=s)\right\rvert\\

The left term in the second line of (

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{s}{q_{i}}\left\lvert e^{-s^{2}/(2q_{i})}-e^{-s(s-1)/(2q_{i})}\right\rvert\leq\frac{s}{q_{i}}\left\lvert e^{-s/(2q_{i})}-1\right\rvert\leq\frac{s^{2}}{q_{i}^{2}}\leq q_{i}^{2\alpha-2}\,.

The first inequality follows by factoring out

For the second inequality, we used that

We turn to the right term of (

\displaystyle r=\frac{s}{q_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\subset A\\

If

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{s}{q_{i}}\sum_{m=1}^{s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\subset A\\

The last inequality holds because

. Combining (

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{q_{i}}(s)-P(S_{i}=s)\right\rvert\leq q_{i}^{2\alpha-2}+q_{i}^{4\alpha-3}\frac{1}{1-q_{i}^{3\alpha-2}}\,.

By the assumption

.

For part

1

1

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We write
k\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}k_{1}
in this proof. Then
\ell=2k
. Substituting
M=1
into (
5.3
) yields

(5.6)

\displaystyle G(k)=\frac{\log_{2}2k}{\varphi(2k)}\sum_{g\mid 2k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g-1}}\prod_{p^{e}\mid 2k}\psi(p^{e},g)\,.

Since
G(1)=1
, our aim is to show that
G(k)>1
for all
k\geq 2
. To derive a lower bound, we only consider the summand where
g=2
. Let
e_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{e_{2}}\mid 2k
and
2^{e_{2}+1}\nmid 2k
(so
e_{2}\geq 1
). Then
\psi(2^{e_{2}},g)=\varphi(2^{e_{2}-1})
and
\psi(p^{e},g)=\varphi(p^{e})-p^{e-1}
for every prime
p

not equal to 2, by Lemma
4
. Thus

\displaystyle G(k)\geq\frac{\log_{2}2k}{\varphi(2k)}\prod_{p^{e}\mid 2k}\psi(p^{e},g)=\frac{\log_{2}2k}{\varphi(2k)}\,\varphi(2^{e_{2}-1})\prod_{p^{e}\mid 2k,p\neq 2}(\varphi(p^{e})-p^{e-1})\,.

Factoring out
\varphi(p^{e})
from the product on the right for every

p
and using that
\varphi(p^{e})=p^{e}-p^{e-1}
yields

(5.7)

\displaystyle G(k)\geq\frac{\log_{2}2k}{\varphi(2k)}\varphi(k)\prod_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{p-1}\right\rparen\geq\frac{\log_{2}2k}{2}\prod_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{p-1}\right\rparen\,.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{p-1}\right\rparen=\exp\left\lparen\sum_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\log\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{p-1}\right\rparen\right\rparen\,.

Since
\log\left\lparen 1+x\right\rparen\geq x-x^{2}/2+(5/9)x^{3}
for
x\in[-1/2,0]
,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\geq\exp\left\lparen-\sum_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\frac{1}{p-1}-\sum_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\frac{1/2}{\left\lparen p-1\right\rparen^{2}}-\sum_{p\mid k,p\neq 2}\frac{5/9}{\left\lparen p-1\right\rparen^{3}}\right\rparen.

Let
\omega(k)
be the number of distinct prime divisors of
k
.
Denote by
p_{i}
the
i
-th prime number (so
p_{1}=2,\,p_{2}=3,\,\dots
). Because
1/(p-1)
is decreasing in
p
, we may
replace the prime divisors of
k
by
p_{2},\dots,p_{\omega_{2}(k)+1}
in the sums in (
5.8
). If
2\mid k
, one
additional prime is included this way, since the sums in
(
5.8
) range only over primes not equal to 2.
To derive a lower bound, however, this is acceptable. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\geq\exp\left\lparen-\sum_{i=2}^{\omega(k)+1}\!\frac{1}{p_{i}-1}-\sum_{i=2}^{\omega(k)+1}\!\frac{1/2}{\left\lparen p_{i}-1\right\rparen^{2}}-\sum_{i=2}^{\omega(k)+1}\!\frac{5/9}{\left\lparen p_{i}-1\right\rparen^{3}}\right\rparen\,.

Since
p_{i}-1\geq p_{i-1}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\geq\exp\left\lparen-\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{p_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{2}}-\frac{5}{9}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{3}}\right\rparen\,.

Using that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1/p_{i}^{2}\approx 0.452
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1/p_{i}^{3}\approx 0.125
, we can bound this by

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}>\exp\left\lparen-\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{p_{i}}-\frac{3}{10}\right\rparen.

If
\omega(k)\leq 2
, then by (
5.9
)
\mathcal{P}
is bounded by
e^{-17/15}
from below, since
1/2+1/3+3/10=17/15
. Inserting this into (
5.7
) yields
G(k)>\left\lparen\log_{2}2k\right\rparen/(2e^{17/15})
, which shows that
G(k)>1

for
k\geq 38
.

Now to the case
\omega(k)\geq 3
. By Rosser’s theorem
[
Ros39
]
,
p_{i}>i\log i
. Thus

(5.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{p_{i}}<5/6+\sum_{i=3}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{i\log i}\leq 5/6+\int_{2}^{\omega(k)}\frac{1}{x\log x}\,dx<\log\log\omega(k)+6/5\,.

Plugging (
5.10
) into (
5.9
), we get

\mathcal{P}>e^{-\log\log\omega(k)-3/2}
. But
\omega(k)<\log k
by the assumption
\omega(k)\geq 3
, so
\mathcal{P}>e^{-3/2}/(\log\log k)
. Applying this bound to
(
5.7
), we obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle G(k)>\frac{\log_{2}2k}{2e^{3/2}\log\log k}\,.

If
k\geq 2.1\cdot 10^{7}
, the right side of
(
5.11
) is greater than 1. For all
2\leq k<2.1\cdot 10^{7}
, the inequality

G(k)>1
was verified numerically, with the code available at

https://github.com/finn-rudolph/rhok-formula
.
∎

7.2.
The time per iteration

As briefly noted in §
1.2
, we assume that the
duration of each iteration of the
i
-th machine is proportional to

\log_{2}2k_{i}
. This assumption was used by setting
\lambda_{i}=\log_{2}2k_{i}
in
the derivation of (
1.1
) from Theorem
1
. To
justify it, the optimization described by Brent
[
Bre80
]
must be
applied. Then, the
\gcd
computation happens in
batches, which only scales the total running time by a constant and
does not impact the per-iteration cost. The work per iteration thus
only consists of the computation of the power
x^{2k_{i}}
, a few
additions and one multiplication. The power requires around

\log_{2}2k_{i}
steps using binary exponentiation and the other
operations are negligible.

7.3.
The random map heuristic

Numerical results supporting Heuristic
1
are given in

[
BP81
]
. There are few rigorous results about the
apparently random behavior of
f_{i}
. Bridy and Garton remark in

[
BG18
]
that the cycle structure of maps of the form
x\mapsto x^{k}+a
cannot exactly match the cycle structure of random maps,
because the number of cycles of length
m
is inherently bounded by

m^{-1}\deg f_{i}^{m}
. However, they show that it is “as
random as possible” under this constraint. In
[
Bac91
]
, Bach
proves a rigorous estimate for the success probability of the rho
method after a certain number of steps.
