Neonatal inflammation predicts adolescent brain volume and neurologic outcomes in children born extremely prematurely
Authors: Carmen A. Marable, Kyle Roell, Lei Zhang, Ryan McNaughton, Timothy Heeren, Karl Kuban, Hadley J. Hartwell, T. Michael O’Shea, Rebecca C. Fry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Children born extremely preterm (< 28 weeks gestation) are at increased risk for neurodevelopmental disorders and reduced brain volumes. This study examined whether neonatal inflammation predicts adolescent brain volume and whether associations differ by sex or presence of major neurologic disorders. Data were drawn from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborns (ELGAN) Study. Inflammatory protein levels measured during the first two postnatal weeks were used to classify neonates into low, moderate, or high inflammation groups. At age 15, participants underwent 3T MRI, and total and regional brain volumes were quantified using FreeSurfer. Neurologic status was assessed at ages 2, 10, and 15. Higher neonatal inflammation was associated with reduced volumes in the cerebellum cortex, brainstem, and subcortical gray matter, including the ventral diencephalon, thalamus, and amygdala. Sex-specific effects were observed: females with moderate inflammation showed reduced volumes in total brain, cerebellum cortex and white matter, ventral diencephalon, and thalamus, while males with moderate or high inflammation had reductions in the cerebellum cortex, corpus callosum, and brainstem. Among adolescents with major neurologic disorders, higher inflammation was linked to smaller cerebellum cortex, cerebellum white matter, and brainstem volumes. These findings suggest lasting effects of neonatal inflammation on brain development in extremely preterm individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51066-0.

Introduction

Extremely low gestational age newborns (ELGANs), defined as neonates born prior to 28 weeks gestation, face an increased risk for neurodevelopment abnormalities, including cognitive impairment, cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and epilepsy.1–6 Advances in neonatal care have improved survival rates for these infants; however, many survivors develop long-term neurological and cognitive disorders. These outcomes are thought to result, in part, from disruptions in critical periods of brain development that would normally occur in utero during the third trimester but instead occur ex utero in infants born extremely preterm.

A growing body of research has demonstrated that children born preterm, as compared to full-term peers, exhibit structural differences in brain development, including reduced volumes in cerebral white matter, gray matter, the cerebellum, and subcortical structures.7–21 These volumetric differences have been associated with poorer cognitive, psychological, and educational outcomes during childhood.13,20,22 Most research on neurological outcomes among individuals born preterm has focused on brain development in childhood, leaving open the question of whether early-life differences persist or evolve during adolescence. Our team recently demonstrated that adolescents born extremely preterm and diagnosed with a major neurologic disorder (cognitive impairment, cerebral palsy, ASD, or epilepsy) exhibited lower brain volumes than their full-term peers.23 These findings underscore the importance of identifying biological factors in early life that may contribute to lasting alterations in brain structure and neurodevelopment among adolescents born preterm.

A likely contributor to altered brain development after preterm birth is systemic inflammation during the neonatal period, which has been linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in ELGANs. The ELGAN Study, a large, multi-center longitudinal cohort of infants born extremely preterm, was designed, in part, to investigate how early biological and environmental exposures impact long-term health and development after extremely preterm birth. Prior work from this cohort has shown that neonatal systemic inflammation is associated with an increased risk of cerebral palsy and cognitive impairment, as well as reduced brain volumes at age 10.24,25 However, it remains unclear whether associations between neonatal systemic inflammation and adolescent brain structural outcomes vary according to sex or major neurologic disorder status, two key factors known to influence neurodevelopment. While sex differences in brain development and susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disorders are well-documented in children born full-term,26–30 less is known about how these differences manifest in adolescents born preterm.

The current study aimed to fill these knowledge gaps by examining associations between early-life levels of circulating inflammatory proteins with total and regional brain volumes at age 15 in participants from the ELGAN Study. We also assessed whether these relationships were modified by sex or the presence of a major neurological disorder. It was hypothesized that higher levels of neonatal inflammation-related proteins would be associated with reduced total and regional brain volumes in adolescence, and that these associations would vary by sex, with stronger correlations in individuals with neurological disorders.

Results

ELGAN study participants

Characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. Of the 323 ELGAN participants, 156 (48.3%) were female and 167 (51.7%) were male. The majority, 216 (66.9%), self-identified as white, while (21.4%) identified as black, and 33 (10.2%) identified as another racial category. The mean gestational age at birth was 26.1 weeks, and the mean maternal age was 29.4 years. A total of 89 participants (27.6%) had a major neurological disorder (ND+), of whom 49 (55.1%) were male and 40 (44.9%) were female. Among the 198 participants without a major neurological disorder (ND-), 95 (47.9%) were male and 103 (52.0%) were female.

Inflammation status did not differ meaningfully by sex. Adolescent brain volume

Table 2 presents volumetric details for total brain and individual brain regions across the cohort (N = 323), as well as stratified by sex. The mean total brain volume was 1,041 cm³, with males averaging 1,091 cm³ and females averaging 987 cm³. Regional mean brain volumes ranged from the smallest structure, the accumbens (1.28 cm³), to the largest, the cerebral cortex (480 cm³). Overall, males had significantly larger total brain volumes than females (p ≤ 0.05), except for the corpus callosum and lateral ventricles.

When adjusting for total brain size, sex-based differences remained in specific regions, including the cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, cerebellum white matter, corpus callosum, caudate, and amygdala (Supplemental Table 1). Note that total brain volume included ventricular volumes. In this cohort, ventricular volumes were not significantly different between males and females, suggesting that inclusion of ventricular volume in total brain volume is unlikely to have substantially influenced sex-stratified comparisons using brain volumes adjusted for total brain volume. In addition to mean ± SD values, volumetric data are also summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) in Supplemental Tables 2 to provide distribution-robust estimates.

Neonatal inflammatory status is associated with brain volume at age 15

Participants with moderate neonatal inflammation had reduced brain volumes compared to those with low neonatal inflammation in the cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, and subcortical gray matter, including the ventral diencephalon, and thalamus (p ≤ 0.05, Table 3). ELGANs with high neonatal inflammation exhibited even greater reductions, specifically in the cerebellum cortex, brainstem, and subcortical gray matter, including the ventral diencephalon, thalamus, and amygdala (p ≤ 0.05, Table 3). Across the cohort (e.g., not sex stratified), both moderate and high neonatal inflammation were associated with reduced volumes in the subcortical gray matter, including subregions of the ventral diencephalon, and thalamus compared to low inflammation levels (Table 3; Fig. 1). Effect sizes, calculated using Cohen’s d, ranged from small to large, with values exceeding 0.2. The largest effect sizes were found in the high inflammation group for the cerebellum cortex, brainstem, and ventral diencephalon. However, no significant associations were found between neonatal inflammatory status and brain volumes in the cerebellum white matter, corpus callosum, lateral ventricles, putamen, hippocampus, caudate, pallidum, or accumbens.

Neonatal inflammatory status is associated with brain volume at age 15 in a sex-specific manner

In the ELGAN adolescent cohort, 118 females had low neonatal inflammation, 29 had moderate inflammation, and nine had high inflammation. Among males, 123 were classified with low neonatal inflammation, 33 with moderate inflammation, and 11 with high inflammation. The distribution of neonatal inflammation levels did not differ significantly between sexes (Table 1).

The relationship between neonatal inflammation and brain volume displayed sex specific patterns (Fig. 2). Specifically, among females, moderate neonatal inflammation was associated with significantly reduced total brain volume (Fig. 2A). Additionally, reductions were observed in several brain regions, including the cerebellum cortex, cerebellum white matter, ventral diencephalon, and thalamus (Fig. 2B, C and F, and 2G). In males, both moderate and high neonatal inflammation were associated with reduced volumes in the cerebellum cortex, corpus callosum, and brainstem, (Fig. 2B and D, and 2E). Complete brain volume data stratified by sex and inflammation category are provided in Supplemental Table 3.

When comparing brain volumes between sexes within each neonatal inflammation group, brain tissue volumes were normalized to each ELGAN participant’s total brain volume and expressed as percentages of total brain volume. Females exhibited lower percentages of total brain volume in the cerebral white matter in the low inflammation group, in the corpus callosum in the moderate inflammation group, and in the subcortical gray matter, hippocampus, caudate, and amygdala in the high inflammation group. In contrast, males demonstrated lower percentages of total brain volume in the cerebellum white matter in the low inflammation group, the corpus callosum in the high inflammation group, and the caudate in the moderate inflammation group (Supplemental Fig. 1; Supplemental Table 4).

Neonatal inflammatory status is associated with brain volume among individuals with a neurologic disorder

Among participants with a neurological disorder, neonatal inflammation was negatively associated with brain volume in four regions, including the cerebellum cortex, cerebellum white matter, brainstem, and ventral diencephalon (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table 5). These results demonstrated a pattern of decreasing brain volumes across increasing inflammatory status among participants in this group, while no similar trend was observed among participants without a neurological disorder.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between neonatal inflammation during the first two weeks of life and brain volume at age 15 in a sample of individuals born extremely preterm. Four key findings emerged: first, even after adjusting for total brain volume, males born extremely preterm had larger volumes in specific brain regions compared to females; second, adolescents with moderate or high neonatal inflammation exhibited reduced volumes in certain brain regions; third, the association between neonatal inflammation and brain volume displayed sex-specific patterns, with different regions affected in males and females; and fourth, the association between neonatal inflammation and brain volume was stronger among ELGANs with a major neurological disorder. These findings highlight the significant impact of neonatal inflammation on adolescent brain development in individuals born extremely preterm and underscore the moderating roles of sex and neurological disorders.

Sex-specific differences in brain volume have been well-documented in the ELGAN cohort and other studies.23,31–35 Previously, we reported that at age 10, males had larger white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid volumes than females.23 In this study, we extend those findings by examining region-specific volumes as a proportion of total brain volume. Our results highlight that at age 15, ELGAN males continue to exhibit larger deep gray matter nuclei, and other parcellated brain regions compared to females.23,31–35 While sex-based differences in brain volume have been widely reported, inconsistencies in adjusting for total brain volume across studies limit cross-study comparisons. This underscores the importance of presenting both unadjusted and adjusted analyses to ensure more standardized interpretations. Overall, the sex-specific findings in ELGAN adolescents provide valuable insight, representing one of the first studies to investigate brain volume in teenagers born extremely preterm.19,36,37.

We have previously reported an association between neonatal inflammation and brain volumes in ELGAN children at age 10, including gray matter and brain stem/cerebellum volumes.24 In the present study, we build on this work by demonstrating an association between increased neonatal inflammation and decreased regional brain volumes in ELGAN adolescents. Specifically, increased neonatal inflammation (both moderate and high) was associated with decreased brain volume within the subcortical gray matter, including the thalamus and the ventral diencephalon. To our knowledge, an association between neonatal inflammation and reduced regional brain volumes of extremely preterm adolescents has not previously been reported. No significant associations were observed between neonatal inflammatory status and volumes of the lateral ventricles, putamen, hippocampus, pallidum, and accumbens, suggesting lower vulnerability of these brain regions to early-life inflammation as compared to other regions. Notably, the association between neonatal inflammation and reduced total subcortical gray matter volume was largely attributable to reductions in the thalamus and ventral diencephalon, as volumes of other constituent subcortical structures were not significantly associated with neonatal inflammatory status.

The present analysis highlighted distinct relationships between neonatal inflammation and brain volume reductions between males and females. While the mechanisms underlying these sex-specific associations are likely complex, one potential explanation involves sexual epigenetic dimorphism of the placenta. Our team has identified differential DNA methylation, miRNA expression, and mRNA expression in the male or female-derived placenta.38,39 An example of a key pathway that could link neonatal inflammation to brain volume and underlie sex-differences is nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB). The NF-κB pathway is a key regulator of response to inflammatory and immunomodulatory factors and has been linked to perinatal exposures as well as ELGAN health outcomes assessed longitudinally.40 Sex-based differences in the expression of the NF-κB pathway and other inflammatory and immune-responsive pathways in the placenta could influence differential fetal responses to inflammatory-related insults. Similarly, sex differences in the expression of potential neuroprotective molecules, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, could contribute to attenuated effects of neonatal inflammation.41.

The relationship between neonatal inflammation and brain volume was found almost exclusively among individuals with a major neurologic disorder. This finding is consistent with a pathway linking neonatal inflammation to neurologic impairments, mediated by disrupted brain development, as reflected by decreased brain volumes. While there was a higher proportion of males than females with a major neurological disorder (34% versus 28%), this difference cannot fully explain the observed inflammation-brain volume relationship. The reduction in volume of specific brain regions for individuals with a major neurological disorder is likely a result of the strong association between brain volume and neurological disorders.7,21,37,42,43.

Strengths of this study include its large, multi-center cohort of adolescents born extremely preterm; the assessment of 15 brain regions, including several deep gray matter structures; and the availability of biomarker data to quantify neonatal systemic inflammation.44,45 Limitations include the exclusive focus on individuals born extremely preterm, which may limit generalizability to adolescents born at term. Additionally, potential selection bias may have arisen from the exclusion of participants with unusable MRI data. In our experience, those with major neurological disorders were more likely to exhibit excessive motion during scanning, which disproportionately contributed to unusable imaging data.46 Finally, despite the advantages of synthetic MP-RAGE processing, quantitative MRI maps were generated from dual-echo and single-echo TSE acquisitions with an original resolution of 0.5 × 0.5 × 2 mm³, and the synthetic MP-RAGE images used for segmentation were interpolated to 1 mm isotropic resolution, which may have introduced minor segmentation inaccuracies.

In summary, our findings highlight the importance of neonatal inflammation as a risk factor for brain structural and functional abnormalities among children born extremely preterm. Interventions to prevent or attenuate perinatal inflammation might improve neurodevelopmental outcomes of infants born extremely preterm.

Materials and methods

ELGAN study participants

The ELGAN Study is a multicenter observational study of the risk of neuroanatomical and neurofunctional disorders in extremely premature infants.47 From 2002 to 2004, 1249 mothers and 1506 infants who were born less than 28 weeks of gestation were enrolled at 14 hospitals in 5 states. At age 15, 1198 surviving adolescents were targeted for recruitment. Of these 1198 participants, 462 participants (39%) underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI images were acquired on Siemens Prisma 3T MRI scanner at six participating sites, Philips 3T MRI scanner at 3 sites, GE 3T MRI scanner at two sites, and a 1.5T MRI scanner at one site. Of 462 participants with MRI data, we excluded 139: 24 who were scanned on a 1.5T MRI, 85 with triple-TSE data mismatches (syn-MP-RAGE FreeSurfer segmentation failure due to motion, low image quality, and/or extreme anatomy), and 30 who lacked neonatal blood protein data. Therefore, a total of 323 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplemental Fig. 2).

This study was conducted under the approval of the institutional review boards of all participating institutions. All procedures were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines, regulatory requirements, and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Adolescent participants gave assent, and a parent, or when applicable their legally authorized guardians, provided written informed consent before contributing samples or data. The study team followed established practices to ensure participant privacy and confidentiality.

Neonatal blood protein measurements and inflammation status classification

As detailed previously, to assess neonatal inflammation in the first two weeks of life, whole blood was collected on filter paper (Schleicher & Schuell 903, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) on postnatal day 1 (range, 1–3 days), 7 (range, 5–8 days), and 14 (range, 12–15 days).24,48 Inflammatory-associated proteins were measured in the Laboratory of Genital Tract Biology, Brigham and Women’s Hospital.24 Six inflammation-related proteins previously reported to be associated with structural and functional neurologic outcomes in ELGAN Study were analyzed in the present study including interleukin 6 (IL-6); tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα); intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1); interleukin 8 (IL-8); serum amyloid A (SAA); and C-reactive protein (CRP).24,49–51 Published associations between these proteins and neurological outcomes in ELGAN participants are summarized in Supplemental Table 6. Protein abundance levels were categorized into four quartiles. Sustained inflammation was classified as a level in top quartile (> 75th percentile) for at least two of the three measurements made in the first two postnatal weeks. If sustained inflammation was present for 0–1 proteins out of the six, ELGANs were categorized into the low inflammation group. If sustained inflammation was present for 2–3 proteins out of the six, ELGANs were categorized into the moderate inflammation group. If sustained inflammation was present for 4 or more proteins out of the six, ELGANs were categorized into the high inflammation group (Supplemental Fig. 3). These inflammatory risk groups were used to study the association between neonatal inflammation and brain regional volumes at age 15.

MRI image acquisition and analysis at age 15

A dual-echo turbo spin echo (DE-TSE) and a single-echo turbo spin echo (SE-TSE) sequence were acquired and collectively referred to as a triple turbo spin echo (Tri-TSE) dataset. Typical imaging parameters were: field-of-view = 240 × 240 × 160 mm3, voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 2 mm3, TEeff1,2 = 12 ms and 102 ms, TRlong = 10 s, TRshort = 0.5 s, echo train length = 10 for SE-TSE, and 20 for DE-TSE, with a total scan time of 7.57 min.52.

Using an image processing pipeline constructed using MATLAB R2018b (MathWorks, Natick, MA) we generated qMRI maps of the proton density (PD), and the relaxation times (T1 and T2), as well as synthetic magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) images. Such synthetic MP-RAGE images have high image white-to-gray matter contrast and are therefore suitable for multi-region brain segmentation using the FreeSurfer package. Compared to directly acquired MP-RAGE images, synthetic MP-RAGEs are less vulnerable to degradation from magnetic field inhomogeneities caused by dental hardware (braces), which are common in adolescents. Synthetic MP-RAGE images were interpolated to 1 mm isotropic resolution and used for further segmentation with Freesurfer.

Quality assurance was conducted in two steps. First, participants whose images failed Freesurfer analysis were excluded from further analysis. Second, for images that successfully completed FreeSurfer processing, visual inspection was further performed to verify image quality and segmentation accuracy. Participants were excluded if their images exhibited excessive motion artifacts, pronounced local image inhomogeneity or low signal, or severe brain ventriculomegaly caused ill-segmentation.

For brain segmentation, FreeSurfer v7.1.0 under Linux RHEL-7 environment was used. Bi-hemisphere brain regional volumes including cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, cerebellum cortex, cerebellum white matter, corpus callosum, brain stem, lateral ventricles, subcortical gray matter, ventral diencephalon, thalamus, putamen, hippocampus, caudate, pallidum, amygdala, and accumbens were derived. Note that total brain volumes were derived as independent measurements and do not reflect the sum of individual brain regions. Likewise, subcortical grey matter volumes were derived as independent measurements and do not reflect the sum of individual brain regions.

Data harmonization

Data harmonization was performed for all original brain regional volume data extracted by FreeSurfer using neuroCombat.54 Each of the six participating sites in the present study and their associated scanners were treated as unwanted sources of non-biological variance and assigned with categorical batch ids. The Combat model was run using an empirical Bayes procedure and parametric priors for improved estimation of parameters. The output of the model is the harmonized data with the same dimension as the original data input.

Identification of neurological disorders

Cerebral palsy was identified using a standard neurological evaluation when participants were ~ 2 years of age.5 Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and epilepsy were identified when children were ~ 10 years of age using rigorous multi-step processes as previously described.23,55,56 Cognitive impairments were identified at 15 years of age using the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence – II (WASI-II).4,5,55,57–59 In the current analysis, children were classified as having a neurological disorder if they had one or more of the following: cerebral palsy at age 2,5 ASD at age 10,55 epilepsy at age 10,4 or a full-scale IQ < 85 at age 15.59

Statistical analysis

Multiple regression was used to assess associations between the neonatal inflammation category, as previously defined, and brain region volumes. Models were run with brain region volume as the dependent variable and inflammation category, as the predictor variable. Inflammation is modeled as a 3-level categorical variable. These models were adjusted for sex, socioeconomic status, birthweight z-score, and participation site. For the measure of socioeconomic status, we used a summative index of maternal social disadvantage, which increased by one point for each of the following: maternal education less than high school, lack of private health insurance, receipt of government-provided supplemental nutritional assistance, and single marital/unpartnered status. For comparisons of brain volume between sexes, two variations in analyses were performed. One method used average volumes that were unadjusted by participant brain volume. A second method normalized each brain region to the participants’ total brain volume resulting in a proportion. To test for moderation, sex-stratified regression analyses were conducted to characterize the relationship between neonatal inflammation status and brain region volumes within sex stratum. An additional moderation analysis was performed where the relationship between neonatal inflammation status and brain region volumes was assessed within neurologic status stratum. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences in brain volume. Mutiple comparisons were adjusted using Tukey’s test, and adjusted P-values (p_adj) were reported (alpha ≤ 0.05). All analyses were conducted in R v4.1.2.60 Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism v10.5.0.
