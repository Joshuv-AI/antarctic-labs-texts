The Solar Wind Electron (SWE) Instrument for the Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe Mission
Authors: R M Skoug, T B Schultz, D M Venhaus, C E Amaya, T Choedon, E R Christian, R D Crosier, A De Los Santos, P A Fernandes, J J Ford, M Gkioulidou, J J Hanley, D S Harvey, P J Hoeper, P W Kubeczka, Y Liu, J P Martinez, D J McComas, H D Mohr, C Nunez, T R Pacheco, S E Pope, H Rodriguez, B F Sandoval, C M Schiferl, N A Schwadron, L C Spalsbury, D N Seitz, R M Smith, J T Steinberg, S A Storms, W Toczynski, Y D Tyler, V J Vigil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Solar Wind Electron (SWE) instrument of the Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe (IMAP) mission is designed to measure the in situ solar wind thermal and suprathermal electrons at the spacecraft. SWE contributes to the IMAP science objective to understand particle injection and acceleration processes near the Sun and in the heliosphere and heliosheath, and provides context for the IMAP measurements of energetic neutral atoms from the outer heliosphere. SWE measures the solar wind thermal and suprathermal electron distribution from 1–5000 eV in 24 log-spaced steps with energy resolution ΔE/E of 14%. Electrons are measured at 7 polar angles relative to the spacecraft spin axis using 7 CEM detectors, and in 30 spin angle bins, giving coverage over ∼95% of phase space as the spacecraft spins. A subset of SWE measurements are transmitted in near real time as part of the IMAP I-ALiRT system, providing the first real time identification of counterstreaming solar wind electrons. We describe the SWE instrument design and performance, and SWE operations and data processing.

Introduction

The Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe (IMAP) mission (McComas et al. 2018, 2025) is designed to understand the acceleration of charged particles at the Sun and in the solar wind, and the interaction of those particles with the local interstellar medium. IMAP is a Sun-pointed spin stabilized spacecraft with a nominal τSC = 15 second spin period, in orbit about the L1 Lagrange point between the Sun and the Earth. The IMAP mission is centered around four science objectives, which are addressed using measurements from ten different instruments covering a full range of charged particles and energetic neutral atoms, as well as the interplanetary magnetic field, Ly-α light, and interstellar dust. The first three IMAP objectives are focused on understanding of the local interstellar medium and processes at the boundary between the solar wind and the interstellar medium. The Solar Wind Electron (SWE) instrument primarily contributes to the fourth IMAP science objective, to “identify and advance understanding of particle injection and acceleration processes near the Sun, in the heliosphere and heliosheath”.

SWE was designed to measure the three-dimensional distribution of thermal and suprathermal solar wind electrons over the 1–5000 eV energy range. SWE measurements provide crucial information about solar wind structures and processes across different types of solar wind flow including streamer belt and coronal hole solar wind as well as transient solar wind from coronal mass ejections (Cohen et al. 2026). Suprathermal electron measurements provide unique information about solar wind magnetic topology, including connection to the Sun, the Earth’s magnetosphere, and shocks in the solar wind. SWE measurements also provide context for the IMAP energetic neutral atom measurements, as the solar wind electrons contribute to processes occurring at the boundary of the heliosphere and the interstellar medium. A subset of SWE measurements is included in the IMAP Active Link for Real-Time (I-ALiRT) real time telemetry system (Lee et al. 2025), providing the first real time identification of counterstreaming solar wind electrons.

SWE draws extensive heritage from the solar wind electron instrument design flown as the NASA Ulysses/SWOOPS (Bame et al. 1983, 1992), ACE/SWEPAM (McComas et al. 1998), and Genesis/GEM (Barraclough et al. 2003) instruments. While the electro-optic design is unchanged from the heritage instruments, SWE has been updated using modern electronics and current design standards, and to extend the measurement range to higher energies. This paper documents the SWE instrument and operations. Section 2 reviews solar wind electron science and SWE science objectives. Section 3 provides details of the instrument design, and Sect. 4 covers SWE performance including calibration measurements and results from the SWE end-to-end performance model. SWE operations and data processing are described in Sect. 5.

Scientific and Measurement Objectives

Solar wind electrons have been measured by many previous solar wind missions over a range of distances from the Sun (see e.g. Feldman et al. 1975, 1978, 1982; Rosenbauer et al. 1977; Pilipp et al. 1987a, 1987b; Phillips et al. 1992; Hammond et al. 1996; Lin et al. 1997; Ogilvie et al. 1999, 2000; Skoug et al. 2000; Štverák et al. 2009; Lavraud et al. 2010; Berčič et al. 2020; Salem et al. 2023). SWE measurements are examined in the context of these previous measurements, allowing study of variations in the solar wind electron distribution as a function of solar cycle and solar wind conditions. SWE observations contribute to the global understanding of the Sun and the heliosphere obtained from the comprehensive IMAP instrument suite, and provide important context for the IMAP ENA measurements, by characterizing the electron populations that will later reach the termination shock and heliosheath and impact the ion populations that become the measured ENA distributions. SWE also contributes to space weather forecasting, with the magnetic field-aligned suprathermal electron beam measurements providing the first real time identification (Lee et al. 2025) of solar wind magnetic field connection to the Sun, as discussed below in this section. A full discussion of IMAP contributions to solar wind particle acceleration and transport is given by Cohen et al. (2026); we refer the reader to that paper for a comprehensive discussion and here present a brief overview of solar wind electron measurements.

To achieve the IMAP scientific objectives, SWE makes the required electron observations by measuring solar wind electrons from 20 eV to 2 keV in at least 5 energy bands per decade, with an angular resolution of at least 30°, and a cadence of 1 minute or shorter. The 1-minute time resolution provides measurements of solar wind structure which allow determination of in situ particle acceleration mechanisms. As discussed in Sect. 4, SWE measurements exceed these requirements in most areas.

Electrons in the solar wind include two components, a cooler thermal population and a hotter suprathermal population (Feldman et al. 1975). The thermal or “core” population convects outward from the Sun with the solar wind bulk flow. The core electrons are the dominant electrons (∼95%) and are observed below a spectral breakpoint energy of ∼70 eV in measurements at the IMAP location ∼1 AU from the Sun. The suprathermal population (∼5%) carries the majority of the heat flux in the solar wind (Feldman et al. 1975), and itself consists of two components: a diffuse, roughly isotropic “halo”, and an anisotropic beamed “strahl” (Feldman et al. 1975; Rosenbauer et al. 1977; Pilipp et al. 1987a,b). A higher energy “superhalo” population is observed at energies above ∼2 keV (Lin et al. 1997). The strahl electrons result from the unbound escape of electrons from the solar corona. These higher energy electrons are not collisionally bound to the thermal population and move out from the Sun along the interplanetary magnetic field (IMF), forming a field-aligned electron beam that interpenetrates the bulk solar wind. The direction of this strahl beam, either parallel or anti-parallel to the IMF, indicates the anti-sunward direction along an interplanetary field line.

At times, a strahl may be observed in both the parallel and anti-parallel directions, which can indicate a field line that is doubly connected to the corona, indicative of an interplanetary coronal mass ejection (e.g. Gosling et al. 1987). Figure 1 shows a 12-hour example of suprathermal electron pitch angle distributions at 272, 372, and 519 eV from the SWE heritage ACE/SWEPAM instrument. A unidirectional strahl at 0° pitch angle is observed at the beginning of the period, with counterstreaming strahls observed starting at 2140 UT on November 7. Counterstreaming electrons can also be produced by other magnetic field configurations, including magnetic field line connection to the Earth’s bow shock (Feldman et al. 1982) or to a distant shock or corotating interaction region (CIR) (Gosling et al. 1993; Steinberg et al. 2005). Counterstreaming can also result from a depletion of halo particles at 90° pitch angle (Gosling et al. 2001, 2002; Skoug et al. 2006), produced by magnetic field magnitude enhancements at heliospheric distances beyond the spacecraft. To the extent that field-aligned strahl electrons propagate outward without scattering, they serve as indicators for the global IMF topology. Counterstreaming electrons thus provide a valuable tool for studying macro-scale magnetic field structures in interplanetary space.

Solar wind suprathermal electron distributions result from the combination of varying conditions at the coronal source and processing by waves and IMF structures as the electrons travel outward from the Sun. Under pure adiabatic propagation, magnetic moment conservation would focus the suprathermal electrons into a very narrow (<1°) beam at 1 AU. The general absence of such narrow strahls indicates that the electrons are scattered, likely by wave-particle processes (e.g. Lemons and Feldman 1983; Gary and Saito 2007; Saito and Gary 2007; Owens et al. 2008; Colomban et al. 2024), with the observed strahl width resulting from the combination of magnetic focusing and scattering. Strahl width may vary with energy and may either broaden or narrow with energy (Anderson et al. 2012). The narrowest strahls are typically associated with fast coronal hole solar wind flow (e.g. Feldman et al. 1982; Pilipp et al. 1987a) or with counterstreaming electrons (Anderson et al. 2012), where the two strahls may have different widths. In addition, the strahl may be modified by macroscopic structures such as shocks, compression regions, and local field line enhancements. As a result, the locally measured strahl carries a wealth of information about the medium through which the strahl electrons have traveled, including macroscopic structures, plasma conditions along the travel path, and small-scale variability of the background magnetic field.

The isotropic halo is thought to be produced by processing of strahl electrons (e.g. Gosling et al. 2001; Maksimovic et al. 2005), perhaps from mirroring near the Sun of electrons that are scattered beyond a 1 AU spacecraft (Gosling et al. 2001). The observations of isotropic halo distributions (e.g. Feldman et al. 1975), and 90° pitch angle depletions (Gosling et al. 2001; Skoug et al. 2006), suggests that the halo electrons propagate relatively adiabatically between the Sun and 1 AU, in contrast to the significant processing implied by the typical finite strahl widths.

The electron distributions also retain an imprint of the coronal source conditions. Under minimal scattering conditions, the temperature of the halo/strahl may directly reflect the coronal temperature (Berčič et al. 2020; Steinberg et al. 2022). Coronal information carried by the suprathermal electrons observed at 1 AU is only a few hours old (<7 hours for >100 eV electrons), in contrast to the bulk solar wind which left the Sun several days earlier. Comparison of solar wind plasma with suprathermal electrons thus provides insight into changes occurring between the times when the bulk plasma and the suprathermal electrons left the Sun, for example by evolution of the solar source region or motion of field line foot points across the Sun through field line migration (e.g. Jokipii and Parker 1968) or interchange reconnection (e.g. Fisk 1996).

SWE Instrument Description

SWE measures the 3D distributions of 1–5000 eV electrons, including the thermal and suprathermal components of the solar wind electron population. While the electro-optic design is unchanged from the heritage Ulysses, ACE, and Genesis instruments SWE has been updated to extend these measurements to higher energies, and to meet the operational requirements of the IMAP spacecraft.

The SWE instrument includes a sensor head (SH) mounted on top of an electronics box (EBOX). The sensor head consists of a spherical section electrostatic analyzer (ESA) followed by seven channel electron multiplier (CEM) detectors. Front end electronics (FEE) are co-located within the Sensor Head. Electrons enter the instrument through an aperture oriented normal to the spacecraft spin axis. A positive high voltage on the inner ESA plate biases the analyzer such that only electrons within a narrow range of energies and angles pass through and are detected by the CEMs. Stepping this voltage provides coverage of the full energy range. The EBOX module contains a low voltage power supply (LVPS), high voltage power supply (HVPS), a backplane circuit board, and a command and data processing board (CDH).

The frame surrounding the aperture in the photos is the mounting interface for the spacecraft thermal blankets, designed to ensure that the aperture is not obstructed by blanket material. In flight, all of the SWE instrument except the region around the aperture is covered by blanket material (right panel of Fig. 4). Because SWE is designed to measure very low energy electrons, appropriate blanket resistivity is critical for instrument operation (McComas et al. 1998; Barraclough et al. 2003). To minimize spacecraft charging effects on low energy electron measurements, thermal blankets within 50 cm distance from the SWE aperture have surface resistivity of at most 5000 ohms per square, as compared to at most 108 ohms per square for other locations on the spacecraft. In addition, spacecraft magnetic fields within the SWE FOV are limited to <600 nT (Barraclough et al. 2003).

Sensor Head

The SWE Sensor Head includes the entrance aperture, electrostatic analyzer, and seven detectors for measurement of individual solar wind electrons. These elements are all located within a grounded cylindrical enclosure The front end electronics are located in a box attached to the sensor head enclosure, to provide initial amplification of the detector output pulses. Figure 6 shows a cross-section of the SWE sensor head through the central detector, illustrating the path of an electron through the instrument.

A cross-section of the SWE Sensor Head, showing the path of an electron (cyan) through the entrance aperture and the ESA to the detectors

Electrostatic Analyzer

SWE uses a 120° spherical section electrostatic analyzer to select electrons over the range of energy per charge. As electrons all have the same electric charge, the analyzer thus effectively allows selection by energy. As shown in Fig. 6, electrons enter the instrument through a small (4×10 mm) aperture (see also Fig. 4) and enter the gap between the electrostatic analyzer plates. The outer ESA plate is held at ground, while a positive high voltage is applied to the inner ESA plate, allowing only electrons within a small range of energy and azimuthal angle to pass through the ESA and reach the detectors. Electrons which enter the aperture at different polar angles are measured by different CEM detectors. The ESA plates are serrated and blackened with Ebonol-C to minimize scattering of ultraviolet light into the detectors. The gap between the ESA plates has a central radius of 41.91 mm, and a width of 3.5 mm between the tops of the serrations and 4.9 mm between the bottoms of the serrations. The ESA plate geometry defines the ESA analyzer constant k, which is the ratio of the electron energy E which transits the ESA to the voltage V across the ESA plates, k = E/V. Analytic calculation of the SWE analyzer constant is complicated by the serrated plates, with values of 6.0 and 4.5 obtained using the tops and bottoms of the serrations, respectively, as reported for the heritage instruments (Bame et al. 1992; McComas et al. 1998). For SWE, k is determined from laboratory measurements, to account for the as-built shape of the serrations (Sect. 4).

Channel Electron Multiplier Detectors

An exit aperture plate is located at the back of the ESA, between the ESA output and the CEM detectors. This stainless steel plate from Precision Eforming has seven aperture holes, for the seven CEMs, and is covered by a 70 line-per-inch nickel grid with 88% open area which is attached to the plate with Loctite Abelstik 2902 conductive silver epoxy. The plate and the grid are biased to +200 V to accelerate the electrons into the detectors. The detector response is uniform over an incoming electron energy range of 200 eV–5 keV, so this post acceleration provides a uniform response over the SWE energy range.

The seven channel electron multipliers are Sjuts model KBL-11RS, each with an 11 mm diameter circular funnel. Each CEM is mounted on an FR-4 printed circuit board (PCB), which also includes HV input, HV capacitors and resistors to bias the CEM front and rear, and a micro-miniature coaxial (MMCX) connector for signal output to the FEE board. CEMs were burned in by application of electrons to extract ∼0.1 C of charge (a small fraction of the total extractable charge) to allow identification of any defective CEMs and to allow selection of a matched set of CEMs with similar gains and electrical properties. Because all seven CEMs are biased via a single HVPS, CEM selection was primarily based on matching the required CEM bias voltage. In addition, the selected CEMs have similar, relatively high gains and narrow pulse height distributions. Table 3 gives impedance and gain measurements for each CEM detector.

The 7 CEM detectors are oriented at polar angles of +/−63°, +/−42°, +/21°, and 0° relative to the center of the field of view. These correspond to angles of 27°, 48°, 69°, 90°, 111°, 132°, and 153° relative to the spacecraft spin axis. The exit aperture sets the polar angle field of view for each detector to 21° FWHM, providing overlap between the detectors and giving full coverage of the polar angle field of view. The azimuthal angle FOV for a spherical section ESA is a function of polar angle, with the narrowest FOV at the center. Details of the FOV for each detector are discussed in Sect. 4.

Each CEM detector is mounted on a circuit board, which includes coupling capacitors and resistors to bias the CEM and to couple the signal output to the FEE. All components are located on one side of the CEM PCB, with a ground plane on the other side providing shielding to minimize cross-talk between channels. The CEM fronts are biased to the same +200 V as the ESA exit aperture. The CEM rear bias is nominally set to +2200 V at the beginning of the mission. The rear voltage can be adjusted to a maximum of +4200 V, to account for detector aging over the mission. The CEM front bias is provided using a front bias board, which generates +200 V via a resistor divider from the CEM back bias voltage. Signal transmission between the CEM and FEE boards uses semi-rigid coaxial cable, providing signal isolation and strain relief to accommodate tolerance stack-up.

Front End Electronics Board

The FEE board is mounted in a box co-located with the SH enclosure (Fig. 2, Fig. 6). The primary function of the FEE board is to receive outputs from the 7 CEM detectors and amplify these signals for transmission to the electronics box. The FEE board is powered by +3.3 V and +5 V inputs from the LVPS. Output from each CEM anode connects to an MMCX connector on the FEE. Each signal is then amplified and shaped using an Amptek A121 charge amplifier followed by a logic level translator to isolate digital and analog electronics and a low voltage differential signaling (LVDS) driver for transmission to the EBOX. Each analog signal chain is isolated from the others to minimize cross-talk. An analog threshold signal is obtained from the HVPS board, allowing pulses below this threshold to be removed from further processing. The same threshold signal is used for all 7 CEM signal chains. The HVPS board can also be used (via commands from the CDH board) to inject artificial pulses into the input of each FEE electronics channel, allowing end-to-end testing of the full electronics chain without input from the CEM detectors. The pulse width and repetition rate can be configured via commanding.

Electronics Box

The SWE electronics box (EBOX) provides communication with the spacecraft, as well as generating required voltages from spacecraft power and processing analog data signals into SWE telemetry products. Mechanical connection to the spacecraft is also achieved via the EBOX, with Cho-Seal between the flat EBOX bottom and the spacecraft deck to optimize thermal connectivity. SWE is one of four IMAP instruments using the IMAP Common Electronics (ICE) electronics box, which includes the mechanical structure, backplane board, low voltage power supply and command and data handling boards. The SWE high voltage power supply board is also located within the EBOX. The block diagram in Fig. 7 shows these elements, together with connections within SWE and to the spacecraft. The EBOX housing is machined from a single piece of aluminum, with the backplane board mounted to a removable rear panel and connections to the spacecraft and the sensor head via a removable front panel. Rails are used for horizontal mounting of the other circuit boards. The backplane is used to connect the LVPS, HVPS, and CDH boards. No active components are included on the backplane, and backplane connectors are staggered to ensure each connecting board can only be integrated in the proper location.

Low Voltage Power Supply

The LVPS board converts primary spacecraft power (nominal +31 V) to secondary voltages +3.3 V, +/−5 V, and +/−12 V for use by the SWE electronics boards. Three International Rectifier DC-to-DC converters provide the required voltages; the board also includes an electromagnetic interference (EMI) filter and a slow start circuit. Load resistors are required on the +5, −5, and −12 V outputs to ensure minimum load on each output (SWE does not use the ICE-provided −5 V output) and balanced loads on these dual-output converters. The LVPS board is located at the bottom of the EBOX chassis, maximizing thermal conduction to the spacecraft.

High Voltage Power Supply

The HVPS board provides positive polarity ESA (VESA) and CEM (VCEM) voltages. The supply uses a resonant fly-back topology running at 100 kHz for the two converters. The ESA stepper is based on a high-voltage optocoupler. HV output is provided on flying lead coaxial cables, with lug terminations at the sensor. The ESA supply is dual-range, to provide the required voltage resolution over the full VESA range, with the low range covering 0–24.5 V and the high range extending up to 1200 V. This voltage range allows measurement of electrons from 1–5000 eV, as will be discussed in Sect. 4. In normal operation, VESA is stepped every 83.333 ms to provide coverage of the full energy and angle ranges over a 60 s period. The stepping time can be adjusted in flight. This stepping time includes an HV settling time of 3.333 ms, followed by an 80 ms data acquisition period. The largest step in nominal operations is 536 V, well within the 1000 V maximum step that can be achieved in the 3.333 ms settling time.

The CEM supply provides output voltages from 0–4200 V. VCEM is applied to the rear of the CEM detectors, and is programmable to allow the CEM bias to be adjusted over time as the detectors age. The initial operating value is VCEM = +2200 V. The BOL power value in Table 1 assumes this initial VCEM level; the EOL power value in Table 1 is an estimate based on the maximum value of VCEM = +4200 V. Acceleration of electrons into the CEMs is provided by applying a voltage of approximately +200 V to the ESA exit aperture, with this same voltage applied to the CEM fronts. The +200 V is generated via resistor divider from VCEM, via a separate board located within the sensor head.

The HVPS board is powered by +3.3, +12, and −12 V inputs from the LVPS. The board includes a field programmable gate array (FPGA), which communicates with the CDH board, provides HVPS control and status, and provides test pulses. HVPS signals include an overall HVPS enable signal as well as enable signals for the ESA bulk supply, ESA low and high ranges, and the CEM supply, and digital to analog converters (DAC) to set the voltage levels for each supply. The board includes analog voltage monitors for the ESA bulk supply and high and low range outputs and for the CEM output, as well as a current monitor for the CEM supply. For safely testing in atmosphere, the output voltage can be limited to V/10 or fully disabled using an external hardware plug.

The HVPS board also provides test stimulation pulsers, which are used for testing the electronics chain separately from the CEM detectors. This mode is particularly useful for ground testing at atmospheric pressure. The test pulses from the HVPS board are injected into the front end electronics board. The pulse duration and repetition period are configurable, and the pulses can be sent to all CEMs or to either odd or even numbered CEMs.

Command and Data Handling Board

The Command and Data Handling (CDH) board is powered by +3.3 V, +5 V, and +/−12 V inputs from the LVPS. This board provides the spacecraft command and telemetry interface, controls the instrument low and high voltage power supplies, collects and processes science measurements from the sensor head, and handles collection and processing of analog housekeeping signals. The CDH board is part of the ICE electronics, with the specific configuration optimized for SWE requirements. The CDH board is processor based, with the AT697F SPARC V8 LEON2-FT microprocessor supported by external EDAC protected PROM, MRAM, and SDRAM, as well as an FPGA. The CDH board uses a 50 MHz clock source, which is used to generate a processor clock and a 25 MHz clock for the FPGA. The board generates secondary voltages of +1.5 V for the FPGA core, +1.8 V for the processor core, and +5 V ADC reference voltage. A 12-bit ADC is used to monitor HVPS and LVPS voltages and currents and temperature sensors. Interfaces include LVDS connection to the spacecraft, LVDS interfaces to the sensor head, LVDS interface to the HVPS, and bi-level input from the HV safing plugs.

FPGA functions are performed through a number of modules. The spacecraft interface module receives commands, which are stored in an EDAC-protected FIFO buffer. Data for transmission to the spacecraft are also stored in an EDAC-protected FIFO buffer and are transmitted upon receipt of the 1 pulse-per-second (1-PPS) signal from the spacecraft. The instrument power module controls low voltage power to the sensor head. The mission elapsed timer module includes coarse (1 sec resolution) and fine (1 μs resolution) counters sufficient for timing of HV steps. The ADC module collects analog voltage, current, and temperature telemetry. The HV interface module sends commands to the HVPS. The science acquisition module handles science data acquisition and tracks HVPS transitions. The CEM data module counts CEM pulses from the FEE during the data acquisition period and latches these counts at the end of the period. As discussed further below, SWE data acquisition is not synchronized to the spacecraft spin pulse but is instead time-based. This scheme means that the data acquisition time is the same for each HVPS step, greatly simplifying the analysis of SWE measurements.

SWE Flight Software and Operational Modes

The SWE flight software (FSW) runs on the Microchip AT697F SPARC V8 processor on the CDH board and is written in C. The software includes Boot flight software (Boot FSW), which is common across the four ICE instruments, and Application flight software (APP FSW), which is specific to the SWE instrument. The Boot FSW is stored in PROM and has limited command and telemetry capability for validating and booting the Application software. Memory dumps and memory loads can also be performed using the Boot software. Application FSW is started automatically from the Boot software and includes two redundant images stored in MRAM. The Application FSW controls sensor and HVPS operations; handles commanding from the spacecraft and through macros; produces telemetry; and handles fault detection and memory scrubbing. The code uses the 1-PPS signal from the spacecraft to schedule tasks. The App FSW Main task is run 10 times per second, receiving commands and sending telemetry, including execution of short commands. Longer memory operations are performed with the Memory Operations task, run when such a memory command is received. Memory scrubbing of the MRAM is run continuously in the absence of other tasks. V_ESA stepping in science mode is performed via an interrupt service routine, which sets the V_ESA DAC value and reads the latched CEM counter values from the CDH FPGA.

SWE has four operational modes, as well as two modes used for bootup and boot debugging, as shown in Fig. 8. When SWE is turned on, the SWE Boot FSW is used to initiate instrument operations and start the APP FSW which handles the bulk of SWE operations. Following Boot mode, SWE always transitions into low voltage engineering (LVENG) mode. In this mode, as well as in the other operational modes, low voltage monitor and engineering housekeeping data are collected and telemetered, and memory upload, dump, and diagnostic commands are allowed. Instrument safing from any mode will bring SWE back to LVENG mode. Low voltage science (LVSCI) mode is used to test end-to-end science data acquisition without needing to enable high voltages. Stimulation pulsers can provide a signal to the electronics in LVSCI mode. In high voltage engineering (HVENG) mode, high voltages can be commanded to fixed levels. This mode is used extensively for ground testing and is also used during commissioning to slowly ramp high voltages before the start of science operations. CEM counters data are transmitted once per second in HVENG mode. High voltage science (HVSCI) mode is used for science data collection during nominal operations. In this mode, VESA is stepped automatically by the FSW, with CEM counter data collected at each voltage step.

SWE Operating modes and mode transitions

SWE commands are sent from and telemetry is sent to the spacecraft in Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS) packets within an Interface Transfer Frame (ITF). The ITF includes a frame header and a checksum, with frame transmission synchronized to the 1-PPS signal from the spacecraft. An autonomy telemetry packet is sent every second for spacecraft instrument aliveness verification. Each SWE command and telemetry packet type is fixed size and is sent within a single frame. SWE uses the same set of commands for commanding from the spacecraft and for commanding via stored macros and can simultaneously process commands from both sources.

To simplify operations, the SWE FSW implements a macro system for common tasks. Each macro consists of a pre-defined sequence of up to 64 commands which can be run via a single command to the instrument. The use of macros thus reduces the number of commands sent to the instrument, streamlining operations and minimizing the possibility of a missed command. Up to 16 macros are stored in MRAM table space and are updated via memory load command. Macros are prioritized by identification number, such that a higher-priority macro will interrupt a lower priority macro. SWE uses macros for periodic in-flight calibration gain sweep sequences, to ramp down instrument high voltages in response to maneuvers or unusually high CEM counts, and to return the instrument to nominal science mode following shut down for a maneuver or other reason.

The Application FSW control is used to enable or disable and to set the output level of each HVPS output. The FSW also is used to power on the sensor head, to set the FEE analog threshold level, and to enable or disable stim pulsers to the FEE. APP FSW monitors housekeeping parameters stored in a reconfigurable MRAM table containing limits and persistence requirements and takes action if a parameter such as voltage or current is out of range. These actions range from simply reporting the violation for evaluation on the ground to safing the instrument and returning to LVENG mode.

As noted above, SWE measurements are time-based, rather than synced to the spacecraft spin. To ensure full azimuthal angle coverage in each SWE measurement, SWE data collection is based on a nominal 15-second period, referred to as a SWE quarter-cycle. The actual length of a quarter-cycle is set based on the observed spin period in flight to be slightly longer that a spacecraft spin period. A SWE quarter-cycle consists of 180 VESA steps, with the SWE ESA voltage nominally stepped every 83.333 ms, and a science data packet transmitted to the spacecraft at the end of every quarter-cycle. Figure 9 shows FSW tasks that take place within one second, and within a 15-second quarter-cycle. The length of an ESA step is configurable in flight and will be set based on the observed spacecraft spin period. The duration of a quarter-cycle may thus be slightly longer or shorter than the nominal 15 seconds shown in Fig. 9.

SWE FSW tasks within a nominal 1-second slice of time, and within a 15-second quarter-cycle

SWE real time data for I-ALiRT are a subset of the science data collected over a quarter-cycle. I-ALiRT data are sent to the spacecraft once per second for inclusion in the spacecraft I-ALiRT telemetry packet. I-ALiRT measurements from a given quarter-cycle are stored in RAM and sent to the spacecraft over the next quarter-cycle (Fig. 9). If the duration of a SWE quarter-cycle is not exactly 15 seconds, it may occasionally be necessary to either have no packet for one second or skip sending a packet to maintain synchronization of the I-ALiRT data set with the SWE science data; these minor adjustments will not affect the quality of SWE I-ALiRT data products.

SWE Performance

End-to-end SWE performance verification is achieved through a combination of measurements and numerical modeling. While laboratory measurements provide the most accurate description of instrument performance, they do not provide a full picture of the instrument. Because the parameter space covered by the instrument is very large in both energy and angle space, measurement of each combination of parameters is not feasible. In addition, determination of the instrument geometric factor is not easily obtained from the mono-energetic, relatively collimated electron beams available in the laboratory. To address these limitations, laboratory measurements are used to validate the SWE electro-optic model, which is then used to determine the instrument response over the full range of input particles.

Calibration Measurements

SWE laboratory calibrations were performed in three phases. Phase 1 consisted of characterization of the SWE flight model (FM) Sensor Head using the engineering model (EM) EBOX and laboratory high voltage power supplies. This phase allowed early verification of the sensor electro-optics and front end electronics prior to integration with the electronics box. Phase 2 and Phase 3 calibrations were performed using the fully integrated FM SWE instrument, with Phase 2 calibration prior to instrument environmental testing and Phase 3 calibration following instrument environmental testing, to verify that instrument performance was unchanged through the test campaign. Phase 1 calibration was performed at the Los Alamos calibration facility (Funsten et al. 2013) using both a 63Ni electron source and an ion beam, and Phase 2 and Phase 3 calibrations at the Southwest Research Institute electron calibration facility using a Kimball Physics EGG-3101C electron gun. The combination of electron source and electron gun measurements allowed verification of the SWE response to electrons over the full 1–5000 eV range. For all electron calibrations, Helmholtz coils were used to null the Earth’s magnetic field within the vacuum chamber, necessary for measurement of low energy electrons. An imaging microchannel plate (MCP) was used to optimize electron beam uniformity at the SWE aperture. Both chambers include rotational motion stages, which are used to scan over polar and azimuthal angles, and translation motion stages used to center the beam on the entrance aperture.

Electro-Optic Simulations

Although SWE is a high-heritage instrument, this version of the instrument represents the first full electro-optic simulation. The mechanical computer-aided design (CAD) model of the instrument was used to develop a fully three-dimensional model of SWE using the SIMION software package (Dahl 2000). The model includes the entrance aperture, the inner and outer ESA plates, the ESA exit aperture and all 7 CEM detectors. The model resolution of 20 grid units per mm is sufficient to resolve the serrated analyzer plates. In the simulations, as during normal operations, the entrance aperture and outer ESA plate were grounded, the ESA exit aperture and the CEM fronts were biased to +200 V, and the inner ESA plate voltage VESA was varied to allow transmission of particles in a selected energy range.

Figure 10 shows the SWE CAD model used to develop the SIMION model, and a 2D cut of the SIMION model with example particle trajectories. This figure indicates CEM numbering from 1 to 7, consistent with the flight software numbering of the detectors. The SIMION +Y axis corresponds to the spacecraft spin axis; thus CEM 7 looks most Sunward. The SIMION model is used with two different types of input. First, the particle input is designed to simulate the parallel monoenergetic beam produced in the laboratory from the electron gun or the ion beam source. This input provides validation of the model by allowing direct comparison with laboratory measurements. Secondly, the model is run with a random input over the full range of instrument energies and angles, allowing determination of the instrument geometric factors.

CEM Detector Gain

Measurements of the CEM gain as a function of bias voltage are used to determine the initial operating bias voltage. As the voltage across the detectors is increased, counts increase as the pulse magnitude out of the detector increases. At some voltage, the count rate becomes insensitive to further bias voltage increases; operation at voltages just above the onset of this plateau is optimal, providing consistent detector output while maximizing detector lifetime. Figure 12 shows CEM gain curves with 1 keV electrons from Phase 3 calibration. Results were unchanged through the calibration campaign. Based on these measurements, an initial operating voltage VCEM = +2200 V was selected. At this voltage, a threshold level of 3500 (4.27 V) removes noise counts and cross-talk between adjacent CEMs with minimal removal of any valid CEM pulses. The threshold value can be adjusted in flight to accommodate changes in the CEM gains.

SWE Voltage-gain sweeps from Phase 3 calibration

CEM gain decreases as the detectors age and charge is extracted over time. To account for this gain decrease, VCEM can be increased in flight. SWE operations include periodic in-flight gain tests, in which VCEM is varied about the nominal operating level while VESA is fixed (nominally 5.6 V for measurement of the relatively stable ∼25 eV electron population). Based on these in-flight calibration measurements, VCEM will be increased as needed to maintain operation on the gain plateau. As SWE includes a single CEM HVPS and threshold setting, values of these parameters will be selected to optimize operation across all 7 CEMs.

Energy and Angle Response

Calibration measurements were defined to cover the range of SWE energies and angles. Polar angle scans were performed using a mono-energetic electron beam at a fixed azimuth angle of 0°. Figure 13 shows a polar angle scan using 1 keV electrons, together with the corresponding simulated polar angle scan from SIMION. The measurements have been corrected for CEM gains, as given in Table 3. The measurements for CEMs 1 and 7 were affected by scattering and/or blocking of the electron beam and are not suitable for validation of the model. Other than CEMs 1 and 7, the agreement between measurement and simulation is excellent, and is used to validate the polar angle response aspects of the model. The polar angle response is also consistent with the results from the heritage Ulysses/SWOOPS-E (Bame et al. 1992) and ACE/SWEPAM-E (McComas et al. 1998) instruments.

Polar angle scan using 1 keV electrons at 0° azimuth angle from SWE Phase 3 calibration (dashed lines & stars) together with SIMION simulation of the same polar scan (solid lines & diamonds). Laboratory measurements have been corrected based on CEM gains

The coupled energy and azimuth angle response is measured at a fixed polar angle and beam energy by scanning over azimuth angle and VESA. High resolution scans used azimuth angle steps of 1° and VESA steps of ∼1%, with lower resolution scans used to more efficiently verify performance over a wider input range. Scans were performed using 1 keV electrons for all 7 CEM detectors. In addition, scans were performed for selected CEMs at lower (0.5 keV) and higher (2.5 keV and 5.2 keV) energies. As for all calibration measurements, results for CEMs 1 and 7 were less reliable. Measurements of CEMs 1 and 7 qualitatively demonstrate ESA and CEM performance, but are not suitable for quantitative validation of the electro-optic model. Similar scans over energy and angle were performed using the SIMION model. Comparison of the laboratory and model results for CEMs 2–6 was used for validation of the model, allowing the model to be used to determine parameters that were not feasible to measure, including the quantitative response for CEMs 1 and 7.

Figure 14 shows examples of energy-azimuth scans for SWE CEMs 4 and 6 using 1 keV electrons. In each case, the middle panel shows measured counts as a function of azimuth angle and VESA. These data have been corrected for variations in the electron beam flux over time. In the left and right panels, the measured data are collapsed over all angles or voltages, respectively, allowing determination of the peak response in VESA and azimuth. The analyzer constant is consistent across the 7 CEM detectors, with an average measured value of k = 4.72. As shown in the Fig. 14 examples, energy-angle scan measurements show an energy response width ΔE/E of ∼14%, and an azimuthal angle response width of ∼10° for the central CEMs (numbers 3–5), with a wider response for the outer CEMs.

Backgrounds

The SWE instrument response to UV light was measured using a McPherson 634 UV lamp, which outputs ∼0.2-Sun flux levels in the 115–165 nm wavelength range. These measurements show an expected light signal at full Sun UV levels of <5 counts per 80 ms measurement for all CEMs at all angles, and <1 count per measurement for most measurements. The higher signal was observed for CEMs 1 and 7 when the UV light was directed at angles of >60° from the center of the CEM response. Light from the angles where the highest counts were observed during UV testing are unlikely to be realized in flight on the Sun-pointed IMAP spacecraft.

The SWE noise background in the absence of particle input was measured to be <5 Hz. This represents <0.5 counts per 80 ms measurement. Measurements were made both during calibration and during environmental testing, with no significant background observed at any time.

Based on observations from the heritage ACE/SWEPAM and Ulysses/SWOOPS measurements, we anticipate that SWE will be sensitive to high energy particle backgrounds during solar energetic particle events. To protect the CEM detectors during such events, VCEM will be automatically reduced in response to very high counts.

Geometric Factors

The geometric factor G (cm2 sr eV/eV) relates the measured counts C to the intensity of particles entering the instrument per unit area, solid angle, and energy. The full geometric factor G includes the CEM efficiency ϵCEM and entrance grid transmission Tgrid as well as the ESA geometric factor GESA: G = ϵCEMTgridGESA. ESA geometric factors GESA are calculated in two ways, analytically (Gosling et al. 1984) and numerically using the validated SIMION model. In both cases, the geometric factor is calculated separately for each CEM, by integrating over the range of input locations, energies, and angles. Results from the two methods agree to within 10%. Figure 16 shows SWE geometric factors GESA (top panel) and G (bottom panel) for the 7 CEMs, including the ESA exit aperture grid transmission of 88%, CEM efficiency of 78%, and the CEM gain factors given in Table 3. Numerical values of these geometric factors are given in Table 5. Figure 16 also includes the geometric factors from the heritage ACE/SWEPAM-E instrument (McComas et al. 1998). The SWE geometric factors are slightly higher than the SWEPAM-E geometric factors and are also comparable to the heritage Ulysses/SWOOPS-E geometric factors (McComas et al. 1998).

SWE Operations

Normal Science Operations

SWE science operations take place in HVSCI mode. As discussed in Sect. 3, SWE data collection is time-based, with a nominal 1-minute full science data acquisition cycle. A full cycle consists of four quarter-cycles, with the duration of a quarter cycle designed to be slightly longer than a spacecraft spin period. Each quarter cycle consists of a set of ESA voltage levels, with the ESA level nominally updated every 83.333 ms, corresponding to ∼2° of spacecraft spin. The time for an ESA voltage step consists of two components, a nominal 3.333 ms HVPS settling time τSETTLE, and a nominal 80 ms data acquisition duration τACQ, as shown in the bottom right panel of Fig. 17. Both τSETTLE and τACQ can be adjusted in flight and are set such that a SWE quarter-cycle is comparable to but slightly longer than an IMAP spacecraft spin period, predicted to be between 14.6–15.4 seconds.

SWE HVSCI mode science data acquisition timing and flow

A full SWE cycle includes measurements at 24 energies in 30 12°-wide spin angle bins. As shown in the top panel of Fig. 17, measurements in a single quarter cycle cover half the energies in half the spin angle bins, providing coverage of the full energy-angle space with lower resolution within each spin. The bottom left panel of Fig. 17 shows VESA steps over a nominal one second measurement, including 6 energy steps in each of two adjacent 12° spin angle bins. The energy steps are selected using an on-board look up table. Multiple tables are stored in the SWE CDH MRAM, to allow for alternative data collection schemes, and the tables can be updated in flight if necessary. For each ESA step, counter values are accumulated for each of the 7 CEM detectors, giving measurements as a function of energy, spin angle, and polar angle. Every quarter cycle (nominally 15 seconds), the FSW transmits these counter values to the spacecraft for telemetry. Counter values are compressed from 16 to 8 bits for transmission to the ground. In addition to the science counters data, SWE housekeeping data, including voltages, currents, and temperatures, are routinely (every 7 seconds) telemetered.

I-ALiRT Real Time Space Weather Data

SWE is part of the IMAP Active Link for Real-Time (I-ALiRT) real time data system. SWE I-ALiRT telemetry products include electron counts from all 7 CEM detectors at all spin angles for a subset of 8 of the 24 energy channels, including VESA steps 12–19 in Fig. 17. These channels are selected to cover the suprathermal electron energy range, from 100–1000 eV. A primary data product is identification of the presence of unidirectional or counterstreaming strahl beams along the magnetic field direction and characterization of these electron beams. This processing algorithm is based on a similar algorithm run onboard by the Genesis/GEM instrument (Neugebauer et al. 2003). A second data product consists of normalized counts as a function of time at each energy step.

Processing of the SWE I-ALiRT measurements on the ground is done on a two quarter-cycle basis, as all energy steps are covered in each pair of quarter cycles. Processing includes normalization of counts based on the geometric factors for each CEM detector, followed by identification of peaks in the angular distribution (either spin angle or polar angle) at each of the 8 energy steps within the two quarter-cycles. Counterstreaming electrons are identified if two peaks are present in the angular distribution for at least 3 of the 8 I-ALiRT energy steps.

Data Processing

SWE measurements are continuously transmitted to the IMAP spacecraft, where they are stored for transmission ∼3 times per week to the Deep Space Network ground stations. The data are then transferred to the Mission Operations Center (MOC) at APL and then to the Science Operations Center (SOC) at University of Colorado Laboratory for Atmospheric and Space Physics (LASP). Within the SOC, the Payload Operations Center (POC) is responsible for instrument operations, and the Science Data Center (SDC) for data processing.

SWE science measurements, consisting of electron counts as a function of detector, spin sector, ESA voltage, and time, are collected only in HVSCI mode. Additional data products include diagnostic electron counter measurements in other operating modes, housekeeping data such as voltage, current, and temperatures monitors, and occasional diagnostic data such as flight software reports and memory dump data. Raw CCSDS packets from the spacecraft telemetry are defined as Level 0 data. Ground data processing then converts these raw data to physically meaningful quantities. Level 0 – Level 3 processing is performed by the IMAP Science Data Center at LASP.

Level 1 processing begins with decommutation of the CCSDS packets, and expansion of each parameter to the appropriate length in bytes. Conversion of housekeeping data products to engineering units is done as part of Level 1 processing. For science data, the SWE Level 1A product consists of raw counts as a function of voltage step and spin angle sector. Level 1A processing includes decompression of the electron counts from 8-bit to 16-bit values. Level 1A SWE data are provided in measurement order, with 180 measurements for each CEM in each quarter-cycle, as shown in Fig. 17.

In Level 1B processing, deadtime correction is performed and counts are converted to count rate based on the data acquisition period τACQ. The deadtime correction uses a non-paralyzable model to account for the 360 ns time required for processing of a count by the analog and digital electronics. In addition, Level 1B processing incorporates time-dependent calibration of the detector gains based on in-flight VCEM voltage scans. Application of these factors gives consistent detector calibration over time. Level 1B processing also combines data from four quarter-cycles into a full cycle data product, with data organized by CEM detector, VESA step, and spin angle sector number.

Level 2 data processing incorporates instrument ground calibration results (Sect. 3) to convert count rates to physically meaningful distributions suitable for scientific analysis. At this stage, the SWE analyzer constant determined from calibration is used to convert ESA voltages VESA to measured particle energies. The SWE geometric factors (Table 5) are used to convert count rates to units of phase space density (s3 cm−6) or intensity (cm2 s ster eV)−1. CEM number is converted to polar angle (e.g. Fig. 13). Spacecraft ephemeris information is used to convert spin angle sectors to spacecraft spin angles in a physically meaningful coordinate system. Because SWE measurements are time based rather than synced to the spacecraft spin, the spacecraft spin angle can be different for measurements within a given spin angle bin. To account for these variations, SWE Level 2 measurements are binned into 30 12-degree-wide bins in despun spacecraft coordinates. The primary SWE Level 2 data set is a (24 energy × 30 spin angle × 7 polar angle) matrix every full SWE cycle (nominally 1 minute) of electron phase space density as a function of energy, polar angle, and spin phase angle, and the same matrix with units of electron intensity.

Level 3 data products incorporate data from other IMAP instruments for calculation of the electron pitch angle distribution and electron moments. Calculation of the pitch angle distribution in the solar wind reference frame includes correction of energies based on the spacecraft potential determined from SWE measurements, transformation to the solar wind reference frame using the solar wind ion plasma velocity from the SWAPI instrument (Rankin et al. 2025), and combination with the magnetic field observations from the MAG instrument (Horbury et al. 2026) to calculate the pitch angle (particle travel direction relative to the magnetic field) for each individual SWE measurement. Data are then binned to obtain the 3D particle distribution in units of both phase space density and intensity as a function of energy and pitch angle. Electron moments, including density, velocity, temperature, and heat flux, are calculated by integration of the measured electron distribution. Moments are calculated separately for the thermal (“core”) and suprathermal (“halo”) components of the distribution. Fits to the distribution are used to fill in the lowest-energy end of the electron distribution, as SWE measurements necessarily do not extend to zero energy.

Summary

SWE is a high-heritage instrument designed to measure solar wind thermal and suprathermal electrons from 1–5000 eV. Calibration measurements have validated the first electro-optic simulation of the sensor head design, showing that SWE is prepared to contribute to the IMAP objectives to study the solar wind and its interaction with the local interstellar medium.
