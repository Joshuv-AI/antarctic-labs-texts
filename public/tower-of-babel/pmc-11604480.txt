Effect of remote ischemic preconditioning on cerebral circulation time in severe carotid artery stenosis: Results from the RIC-CCT trial
Authors: Quan-Ying Liu, Yu Cui, Wei Li, Jing Qiu, Thanh N Nguyen, Hui-Sheng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn patients with severe internal carotid artery stenosis (sICAS), cerebral circulation time (CCT) is associated with cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome. This study aims to investigate the effect of remote ischemic preconditioning (RIC) on CCT in patients with sICAS. Patients are randomly assigned to the RIC group (RIC twice daily, for 2–4 days before carotid artery stenting [CAS] as an adjunct to standard medical therapy) and the control group. The results show that RIC produces a significant decrease in CCT of the stenosis side (sCCT) from baseline to pre-CAS, and the occurrence of contrast staining on brain computed tomography (CT) is lower in RIC versus control group after CAS. In addition, significant changes in some serum biomarkers suggest that anti-neuroinflammation, anti-oxidative stress, protecting endothelial injury, and improving cerebral autoregulation may be associated with the effect of RIC. These findings provide supporting evidence that RIC can modulate cerebral circulation in patients with sICAS. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05451030).Keywords: remote ischemic preconditioning, cerebral circulation time, internal carotid artery stenosis, carotid artery stenting, cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome, TK, HSP70, 25-HC, STING, IL-6

Introduction

Carotid artery stenosis caused by atherosclerosis is a common cause of acute ischemic stroke.1 Carotid artery stenting (CAS) and carotid endarterectomy are common treatments for carotid artery stenosis.2 CAS-related complications include cerebral embolism, vascular dissection, and cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS), while patients with severe internal carotid artery stenosis (sICAS) are at a higher risk of CHS after CAS.3,4,5 Reducing the complications of CAS is an important issue in clinical practice.

Previous studies suggested the close relationship of cerebral circulation time (CCT) based on digital subtraction angiography (DSA) with cerebrovascular reserve.6,7 Prolonged CCT was found to be an independent prognostic factor for the occurrence of disabling ischemic cerebrovascular events in patients with symptomatic non-disabling middle cerebral artery stenosis,8 and associated with CHS after CAS.9 Furthermore, our recent studies suggested that CCT was associated with clinical outcome in patients with large vessel occlusion who underwent successful recanalization after endovascular treatment.10,11 Given that CCT is readily available and can provide alternative information about cerebral reperfusion and cerebrovascular reserve, CCT can be used as a surrogate indicator to investigate the effect of cerebroprotective treatments.

A series of basic and clinical studies have shown the cerebroprotective potential of remote ischemic preconditioning (RIC), which has been shown to improve cerebral blood flow autoregulation, reduce brain infarction, improve neurologic outcomes, and reduce the recurrence of stroke.12,13,14,15,16 Recently, two large clinical trials provided robust evidence for the cerebroprotective effect of RIC in ischemic stroke.17,18 However, to date, the underlying mechanisms of RIC are not well understood. In this context, we hypothesize that RIC may improve CCT and reduce CHS after CAS in patients with sICAS.

In this phase 2, randomized, blinded endpoint trial, we explored the effect of RIC on CCT in patients with sICAS.

Results

Between June 28, 2022 and March 6, 2023, a total of 122 patients were recruited and randomly assigned to the RIC group (61 patients) or the control group (61 patients). No patient was lost to follow-up (Figure 2). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups (Table S1).

For secondary outcomes, the occurrence of contrast staining at 1 h after CAS was lower in the RIC group (4.91%) than in the control group (14.75%) (odds ratio [OR] 3.18, 95% CI 0.81–12.40, p = 0.08), and similar results were found at 24 h after CAS (4.91% vs. 14.75%, OR 2.51, 95%CI 0.62–10.19, p = 0.20). CHS occurred in 1 patient (1.64%) in the RIC group and 3 (4.91%) in the control group within 24 h of CAS, respectively (OR 3.10, 95% CI 0.31–30.70, p = 0.33). No CHS events were observed in both groups from 24 h to 7 days after CAS. Compared with baseline, 2 patients (3.28%) had improvement in collateral scores in the RIC group, whereas no patients in the control group had improvement in collateral scores (Table 1).

The levels of all 24 serum biomarkers at baseline were not significantly different between 2 groups, and no change in them was found at the end of RIC treatment compared with the baseline in the control group (Table 2). In the RIC group, 8 serum biomarkers significantly decreased at the end of RIC treatment, including 25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC) (p < 0.001), interleukin-6 (IL-6) (p = 0.027), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) (p = 0.006), stimulator of interferon genes (STING) (p = 0.027), macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) (p = 0.027), neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (p = 0.002), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (p = 0.027), and IL-1(p = 0.027), with a significantly decreased trend of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) (p = 0.051) and histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) (p = 0.051) (Figure 4; Table 2). A significant increase in tissue kallikrein (TK) (p = 0.029) and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) (p = 0.027) at the end of RIC treatment was observed (Figure 4; Table 2).

Discussion

In the current study, we found that short-term RIC significantly improved prolonged sCCT in patients with sICAS, which is the most important finding in this trial (central illustration). In addition, short-term RIC was associated with numerically improved collateral circulation and a decreased occurrence of CHS and contrast staining on brain computed tomography (CT) after CAS, compared with the control group. Collectively, this prospective, randomized clinical trial provided evidence to support that RIC treatment can improve slow cerebral blood flow circulation, which could be associated with a decreased occurrence of CHS and contrast staining on brain CT after CAS.

Prior studies have suggested the benefit of RIC on cerebral circulation.19,20,21,22,23 The current study shows that RIC could improve brain circulation, which further supports the cerebroprotective action of RIC.18,24,25 We contend that the improvement of prolonged sCCT including collateral circulation could be attributed to the effect of RIC on cerebral autoregulation and cerebral vascular reserve.26 Previous animal studies have shown that RIC for 3 weeks induced cerebral vascular remodeling, angiogenesis, arteriogenesis, and new collateral formation,27 but these structural changes in the angioarchitecture were impossible to underlie the improvement in collateral scores and CCT, given the short-term RIC in this trial. In the current study, we found that 25-HC, IRF3, HDAC3, STING, MIF, IL-1, IL-6, MCP-1, NSE, and iNOS were significantly downregulated in the RIC group, while TK and HSP70 were upregulated. These results were consistent with prior studies,28 for example anti-inflammation,29,30,31,32,33,34 antioxidant stress,35,36 downregulating cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-STING pathway,37,38 and upregulating HSP 7039,40,41,42 and TK43 of RIC. Collectively, the current results suggest that anti-inflammation, anti-oxidative stress, protecting endothelial injury, and improving cerebral perfusion were involved in the protective effect of RIC, which may contribute to the improved collaterals and sCCT. In addition, good collateral circulation may help reduce CCT in some patients with severe carotid artery stenosis,44 which may be partially attributed to the fact that patients with good collaterals usually have lower resistance to venous return.45 Taken together, these results suggest that improvement in collateral circulation and cerebral blood flow circulation by RIC may be complementary, while CCT is more sensitive than collateral scores to observe the effect of RIC.

In the present study, CCT was used as the primary outcome to investigate the cerebroprotective effect of RIC, given that it is readily available and can provide alternative information about cerebral reperfusion and cerebrovascular reserve.6,7 Furthermore, we chose the occurrence of contrast staining on brain CT and CHS after CAS as secondary endpoints because they can reflect cerebral reperfusion damage from different perspectives, whereas changes in collateral circulation scores suggest improvements in cerebral blood flow. We found a numerically lower occurrence of CHS and contrast staining after CAS in the RIC group compared with the control group. These results were concordant with the results of CCT and collateral scores. Prior studies have found that slow CCT was associated with CHS after CAS.46 In addition, contrast staining on CT after CAS reflected the disruption of blood-brain barrier and ischemia-reperfusion injury.47,48,49,50 Thus, the improvement in sCCT and collaterals by RIC can explain the lower occurrence of CHS and contrast staining on CT after CAS, possibly related to the protective effect of RIC on the blood-brain barrier and ischemia-reperfusion injury.

The strength of this trial is that it provides supportive evidence that RIC can improve cerebral blood flow circulation across several clinical and neuroimaging parameters, including lower CHS and improved CCT and collaterals, which reinforces the cerebroprotective effect of RIC. The latter endpoints were conducted via personnel who were blinded to treatment allocation to reduce bias. Moreover, this trial further supports the clinical value of CCT as a surrogate marker of cerebral circulation given real-time CCT monitoring during DSA.

Limitations of the study

This trial has limitations. First, due to the projected low number of clinical endpoints with CAS, CCT was used as the primary outcome in this trial. Future clinical studies should be designed and conducted with clinical events such as CHS as primary endpoints. Second, CT perfusion was not used to screen patients, but bilateral CCT difference >0.8 s was selected as the inclusion criteria to reflect the cerebral hypoperfusion state. Third, given that the blood samples from only 10 patients were collected and only 24 biomarkers were measured in each group, our biomarker results do not fully reflect the changes in biomarkers after RIC, which need to be validated in clinical trials with larger samples. Finally, further confirmation of these findings in other centers or in non-Chinese populations would be needed, given the differences in body mass, co-morbid factors, and patterns of cerebrovascular disease compared with other populations.

Conclusion

Among patients with sICAS, short-term RIC can significantly improve sCCT, which may be associated with a lower occurrence of contrast staining on brain CT and CHS. Multiple mechanisms, including the anti-neuroinflammation, anti-oxidative stress, protecting endothelial injury, and improving cerebral autoregulation, may be involved in the effects of RIC.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study included human participants from the local area of Shenyang (CN; NCT05451030). Data were collected in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Signed informed consents were obtained from the patients or their legally authorized representative. The trial was approved by the ethics committee of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command. This study was supported by grants from the Science and Technology Project Plan of Liaoning Province (2022JH2/101500020). In RIC group, 53% are male, with a median age of 64 years (IQR 59–69.5) and in control group, 51% are male, with a median age of 66 (IQR 62–71). Participant characteristics are presented in Table S1. Eligible subjects were adults aged 18 years or older with severe carotid artery stenosis (70–99% stenosis confirmed by DSA) and prolonged CCT of the stenosis side (sCCT) compared with the CCT of the contralateral side (cCCT) on baseline DSA, who had been functioning independently in the community (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] scores 0 to 2), and were planned for CAS after baseline DSA. Key exclusion criteria were patients who were not qualified for CAS such as those with severe long-segment calcification of the carotid artery, severe aortic arch distortion, anatomical variation of the aortic arch, spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage within 12 months or myocardial infarction within 3 months, and had contraindications to remote ischemic preconditioning, such as those with more severe soft tissue injuries, fractures or vascular injuries in the upper extremity, or peripheral vascular lesions in the distal upper extremity.

Method details

Study design

The effect of Remote Ischemic Preconditioning on Cerebral Circulation Time in patients with severe carotid artery stenosis (RIC-CCT) is an investigator-initiated, prospective, randomized, open label, blinded endpoint (PROBE) clinical study. The trial was approved by the ethics committee of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command and conducted at the stroke center of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command from June 28, 2022 through March 6, 2023. The trial was registered at www.ClinicalTrial.gov (Unique identifier: NCT05451030).

Participants

Eligible subjects were adults aged 18 years or older with severe carotid artery stenosis (70–99% stenosis confirmed by DSA) and prolonged CCT of the stenosis side (sCCT) compared with the CCT of the contralateral side (cCCT) on baseline DSA, who had been functioning independently in the community (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] scores 0 to 2), and were planned for CAS after baseline DSA. Key exclusion criteria were patients who were not qualified for CAS such as those with severe long-segment calcification of the carotid artery, severe aortic arch distortion, anatomical variation of the aortic arch, spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage within 12 months or myocardial infarction within 3 months, and had contraindications to remote ischemic preconditioning, such as those with more severe soft tissue injuries, fractures or vascular injuries in the upper extremity, or peripheral vascular lesions in the distal upper extremity. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is available in the trial protocol (Data S1).

Randomization and masking

In this trial, eligible patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to either a RIC or control group using a random number table prepared by an independent statistician, which was collected by the study coordinator to ensure randomization. The indicators used to assess the outcomes such as CCT, brain CT, collateral scores and CHS were evaluated by two qualified personnel (W.L and J.Q) who were blinded to treatment allocation and participant identity according to a standardized procedure manual.

Procedures

In the RIC group, patients received RIC treatment by a pneumatic electronic auto-control device (Patent Number: ZL201410834305 · 2; Device Model: IPC-906; Beijing Renqiao Cardiocerebrovascular Disease Prevention and Treatment Research Jiangsu Co., Ltd) as an adjunct to standard medical therapy: 5 cycles of cuff inflation (200 mmHg for 5 min) and deflation (for 5 min), for a total procedure time of 50 min, once or twice daily from randomization day to CAS day (about 2–4 days). Patients in the control group only received standard medical therapy. Follow-up was performed at baseline, end of RIC treatment (on CAS day), 1 h, 24 h and 7 days after CAS, respectively. Blood samples and biomarkers measurement

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the consecutive 10 patients at baseline and at the end of RIC treatment (on CAS day). All samples were collected from a brachial vein into ice-chilled disposable polypropylene tubes containing aprotinin and EDTA. Plasma samples were immediately separated centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and stored at−80°C until batch evaluation. A total of 24 preset serum biomarkers including25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC), S-100B, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), stimulator of interferon genes (STING), macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), neuron specific enolase (NSE), interleukin-1 (IL-1), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3), Adenosine, cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), tumor necrosis factor-a (TNF-a), hypoxia inducible factor (HIF), NOD like receptor pyrin domain-3 (NLRP3), Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), tissue kallikrein (TK), and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was change in sCCT pre-CAS compared with baseline. In concordance with prior studies,10,11 CCT is defined as the time from the appearance of contrast at the siphon segment of the internal carotid to the end of the arterial phase during DSA on the anteroposterior and lateral image. The secondary outcomes were: (1) occurrence of contrast staining on brain computed tomography at 1 h after CAS; (2) occurrence of contrast staining on brain CT at 24 h after CAS; (3) change in collateral score based on digital subtraction angiography (DSA) from baseline to pre-CAS; (4) occurrence of CHS within 24 h after CAS; (5) occurrence of CHS within 7days after CAS. The detailed measurements of CCT, collateral score, and definition of CHS are reported in Appendix 3-5 of Supplement 1. Safety outcomes included RIC-related adverse events such as arm pain assessed by a visual scale, redness or swelling of arms, skin petechiae on arms, palpitations, intracerebral hemorrhage, and dizziness not present at the beginning of the study; procedure-related complications; stroke or other vascular events.

As an exploratory outcome, we also investigated the changes in plasma 25-HC, S-100B, IL-1β, IL-6, MCP-1, cGAS, CXCL1, STING, MIF, NSE, iNOS, eNOS, IL-1, IRF3, HDAC3, Adenosine, TNF-a, HIF, NLRP3, COX-2, VEGF, TGF-β, TK and HSP70 levels at the end of RIC treatment compared with the baseline.

Sample size

According to the results of our retrospective study (unpublished data), the mean baseline sCCT is 2.99s in patients with severe carotid artery stenosis. The change in CCT on the CAS day compared with baseline was assumed to be reduced by 0.3s in the RIC group compared with Control group. Using a power of 90% and α of 0.05 to carry out the two-tailed test, the calculated sample size was 110. In consideration of 10% of patients lost to follow-up, the total sample size would be 121. Therefore, this study included 122 subjects, with 61 patients in each group.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Primary analyses were performed based on an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis principle, which included all randomized patients. Descriptive statistics were used for the baseline characteristics of patients. Categorical variables are presented as absolute values and percentages, and continuous variables as mean with standard deviation if normally distributed or median with interquartile range if not normally distributed.

For the primary outcome, we performed linear regression to compare the changes in CCT from randomization to pre-CAS between the RIC and Control group and calculate the beta coefficient with its 95% confidence interval (CI). For the secondary outcomes, we performed binary logistic regression analysis to compare the differences in the occurrence of contrast staining on brain CT or CHS, and the change in collateral score between the two groups and calculate odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% CIs.

The analyses for the primary and secondary outcomes were unadjusted. Covariate-adjusted analyses were also performed for all outcomes as a sensitivity analysis. For the primary outcome, the number of RIC received, baseline collateral score, the presence of other large vessel stenosis, and catheter location were adjusted as prespecified confounding factors given their potential effect on CCT. For the secondary outcomes, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of stroke, and presence of emboli in the embolic protection device were adjusted as prespecified confounding factors given their potential effect on contrast staining or CHS. The selection criteria for the above covariates were based on previous relevant studies3,17 and factors that we believed may influence the outcomes.

As an exploratory outcome, changes in serum biomarkers were presented as mean and standard deviation, and compared by using Student’s t test.

A 2-sided p value < 0.05 was considered significant for all tests. Considering the potential for type I error due to multiple comparisons, findings for analyses of secondary and exploratory outcomes should be causally interpreted. All data were analyzed with SPSS software version 26 (IBM).
