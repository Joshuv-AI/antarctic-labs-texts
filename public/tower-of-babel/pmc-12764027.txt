Development and psychometric evaluation of the Osteoporosis Preventing Behaviors Questionnaire (OPBQ) based on pender’s health promotion model
Authors: Mahsa Askarian, Ali Baloochi, Fatemeh Vizeshfar, Fatemeh Mohammadizadeh, Mobin Mottahedi, Farzad Abaszadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis affects 38% of women and 25% of men aged ≥ 50 years in Iran, with fracture-related costs estimated at US$393 million annually. Prevention hinges on modifiable behaviors such as adequate calcium intake, weight-bearing exercise, and sun exposure. However, existing instruments like the Osteoporosis Health Belief Scale (OHBS) and Osteoporosis Knowledge Test (OKT) mainly assess beliefs or knowledge and lack cultural adaptation for Middle Eastern populations. Pender’s Health Promotion Model (HPM) offers a comprehensive framework for evaluating preventive behaviors via constructs including perceived benefits/barriers, self-efficacy, planning, substituting behaviors, commitment, and social support. This study aimed to develop and psychometrically test the Osteoporosis Preventing Behaviors Questionnaire (OPBQ) for Iranian women aged 30–60 years, considering socio-cultural factors such as modest clothing that limits vitamin D synthesis.MethodsFollowing COSMIN guidelines, this methodological study was conducted in two phases. In Phase 1, 37 items across six HPM-based subscales were generated from literature, WHO/NOF/IOF recommendations, and expert input. Content validity was assessed by 12 experts (Content Validity Ratio [CVR] ≥ 0.56, Scale-level Content Validity Index [S-CVI/Ave] ≥ 0.90), and face validity by 30 women. In Phase 2, 600 women (mean age 45.2 ± 8.7 years) from Shiraz health centers were recruited via convenience sampling. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (principal axis factoring, varimax rotation; KMO = 0.86, Bartlett’s p < 0.001). Reliability was determined by Cronbach’s alpha (≥ 0.70) and test–retest Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC > 0.75) in 30 participants.ResultsContent validity was strong (mean CVR = 0.82, S-CVI/Ave = 0.94). EFA supported a six-factor structure explaining 68.4% of variance, resulting in a 35-item OPBQ after removing two low-loading items. Communalities ranged from 0.32 to 0.78, with minimal cross-loadings. Internal consistency was high (α = 0.91 overall; subscales 0.76–0.88) and test–retest reliability robust (ICC = 0.89 overall; subscales 0.78–0.92).ConclusionThe OPBQ is a valid, reliable, and culturally sensitive HPM-based instrument for assessing osteoporosis-preventive behaviors in Iranian women. It can be applied in clinical screenings, educational programs, and public health policy. Broader validation and longitudinal studies are recommended to enhance generalizability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25697-3.

Introduction

Osteoporosis, the most common metabolic bone disorder, is a major contributor to fragility fractures, disability, and diminished quality of life, particularly among women [1–3]. Characterized by reduced bone mineral density and compromised microarchitecture, it substantially increases fracture risk, often with minimal or no trauma [4]. Its high global prevalence, silent progression, and severe consequences have made it an important public health priority [5]. Globally, one in three women and one in five men aged ≥ 50 years will sustain an osteoporotic fracture, with significant impacts on morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [6–8]. The burden is projected to rise sharply with population aging [9].

Within the Eastern Mediterranean Region, the prevalence of osteoporosis is estimated at 24.4% [10]. In Iran, data from the Iranian Multicenter Osteoporosis Study (IMOS) and subsequent meta-analyses reveal a high prevalence of bone loss, with pooled estimates indicating 38% of women and 25% of men aged ≥ 50 years affected [8]. Recent figures show rates of 62.7% in elderly women and 24.6% in elderly men [11]. The clinical and economic impact is substantial: in 2020, approximately 154,530 osteoporotic fractures occurred in Iran, including 14% hip, 15% vertebral, and 17% forearm fractures, resulting in 3,554 deaths and an estimated economic burden of US$ 393 million (US$ 2.165 billion purchasing power parity) [1].

Given its asymptomatic course until fracture, prevention is the most cost-effective strategy [12]. Evidence supports the role of adequate calcium and vitamin D intake [13], regular weight-bearing exercise [14], and avoidance of smoking [15] and limiting excessive alcohol consumption [16] in preserving bone health. However, long-term adherence to such behaviors is challenging and requires an understanding of the psychosocial and environmental determinants of health-related actions. Pender’s HPM offers a robust framework for this purpose, emphasizing the influence of individual characteristics, behavior-specific cognitions, and commitment to action, moderated by competing demands [17–19].

While several behavioral models, such as the Health Belief Model (HBM), exist, the HPM was deliberately selected for its distinctive and more comprehensive approach to health behavior. The HBM, though foundational, has been critiqued for its primary focus on disease avoidance and perceived threat [20], its relative neglect of social and emotional factors [21], and its static nature [21] which may not fully capture the dynamic process of long-term behavior adoption. In contrast, Pender’s HPM is fundamentally a model of positive motivation and health promotion [22], making it inherently more aligned with the proactive, lifelong commitment required for osteoporosis prevention, such as maintaining physical activity and a calcium-rich diet. Crucially, the HPM moves beyond individual cognitive perceptions to explicitly incorporate the influence of social support and places a strong emphasis on self-efficacy and commitment to a plan of action [23]. This holistic framework allows for capturing the multifaceted psychological and social determinants that influence the adoption and maintenance of osteoporosis-preventive behaviors.

Although HPM has been applied to domains such as diabetes management, physical activity, and nutrition [24–29], existing instruments are largely generic and lack specificity for osteoporosis prevention. Moreover, no culturally adapted, psychometrically validated HPM-based tool exists for Middle Eastern populations, where factors such as dietary patterns, gender-specific barriers to physical activity, and widespread vitamin D deficiency strongly influence preventive behaviors.

Recognizing this gap, we designed the Osteoporosis Preventing Behaviors Questionnaire (OPBQ), grounded in HPM constructs and culturally adapted for Iranian women aged 30–60 years. This paper reports on the systematic development and psychometric evaluation of the OPBQ, encompassing item generation, content and face validity, construct validation through exploratory factor analysis, and reliability testing.

Methods

Study design

This methodological study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate the OPBQ based on HPM. The HPM provides an evidence-based framework for understanding and promoting health behaviors by addressing individual perceptions, modifying factors, and behavioral outcomes [30]. The research was conducted in two sequential phases: (1) instrument development and (2) psychometric evaluation. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: CT-9376-7416). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor [31].

Phase 1: instrument development

Item generation

The initial step involved generating items through a systematic and comprehensive approach to ensure the questionnaire captured the multifaceted aspects of osteoporosis preventive behaviors within the HPM framework. An extensive literature review was conducted on osteoporosis prevention strategies, including dietary habits, physical activity, lifestyle modifications, and psychosocial factors influencing adherence. Key constructs from HPM were targeted: perceived benefits (anticipated positive outcomes of behaviors), perceived barriers (obstacles hindering action), perceived self-efficacy (confidence in performing behaviors), activity-related affect (emotional responses to activities), commitment to a plan of action (dedication to sustained behavior change), and interpersonal influences (social support from others) [32].

To gather evidence-based content, national and international scientific databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, SID (Scientific Information Database), and Magiran were systematically searched. Keywords included combinations like “osteoporosis prevention,” “health promotion model,” “preventive behaviors,” “self-efficacy in bone health,” “barriers to calcium intake,” and “social support for exercise,” with Boolean operators (AND/OR) and filters for English and Persian languages, publication dates from 2000 onward, and peer-reviewed articles. Additionally, clinical guidelines from authoritative bodies, including the World Health Organization (WHO) [33], the National Osteoporosis Foundation (NOF) [34], and the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) [35, 36], were consulted to incorporate evidence-based recommendations on risk factors, screening, and interventions.

This process yielded an initial pool of 37 items, strategically distributed across six subscales to reflect HPM’s core elements while addressing osteoporosis-specific behaviors (e.g., calcium-rich diet, weight-bearing exercise, avoidance of smoking, and adequate sun exposure for vitamin D synthesis). The initial scoring approach utilized a combination of 4-point Likert scales (for perceived benefits and barriers, with barriers being reverse-scored), 3-point Likert scales (for self-efficacy, planning, commitment, and social support), and binary scoring (for substituting behaviors) to capture the multidimensional nature of the constructs. The final item selection, factor structure, and definitive scoring algorithm for the OPBQ were established based on the subsequent psychometric evaluation (Phase 2), as detailed in the Results section.

In addition to the core items, a demographic and medical history section was incorporated to contextualize responses. This included variables such as age (in years), education level (e.g., primary, secondary, tertiary), marital status (single, married, divorced, widowed), parity (number of children), body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kg divided by height in m²), family history of osteoporosis (yes/no), presence of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, thyroid disorders), history of oophorectomy (yes/no), long-term corticosteroid use (yes/no), current calcium supplementation (yes/no/dosage), smoking status (never, former, current/packs per day), exercise habits (type, frequency, duration), and sun exposure (daily hours outdoors). These variables were selected based on established risk factors for osteoporosis to allow for subgroup analyses and enhance the questionnaire’s utility.

Face and content validity

Face validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively among 30 women aged 30–60 years from community health centers. Items with impact scores ≥ 1.5 were retained [37], leading to the retention or modification of items.

Content validity followed established psychometric guidelines, including those recommended by Polit and Beck [38, 39] and in accordance with the COSMIN framework for content validity assessment. Twelve experts were purposively selected based on expertise in osteoporosis prevention, instrument development, and health promotion—three in public health, three in nursing, two in health education, two in nutrition, and two in endocrinology. The selection criterion was a minimum of five years of academic or clinical experience in osteoporosis-related research or practice.

Experts rated each item’s relevance, clarity, and simplicity. Lawshe’s method was used to calculate the Content Validity Ratio (CVR), with acceptable values ≥ 0.56 for a 12-member panel [40, 41]. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was computed at both item (I-CVI ≥ 0.78) and scale levels (S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90) [42, 43]. Items not meeting thresholds were revised or removed accordingly.

The average time for participants to complete the OPBQ during pilot testing was approximately 12–15 min, indicating acceptable respondent burden for a self-report behavioral measure.

Phase 2: psychometric evaluation

Participants and sampling

A total of 600 women aged 30–60 years were recruited through convenience sampling from health centers affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The sample size followed methodological recommendations of 5–10 participants per item (yielding a minimum of 185–370 for 37 items) to ensure robust factor analysis [44, 45]. Similar sample size justifications have been supported in psychometric literature [46, 47]. Inclusion criteria included absence of diagnosed osteoporosis, Persian literacy, and voluntary participation with written consent. Exclusion criteria comprised cognitive impairment or withdrawal after consent.

Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation. According to the COSMIN taxonomy, this analysis falls under structural validity as it examines dimensionality within the construct framework. The orthogonal rotation method was chosen because it maximizes factor independence and interpretability, a widely recommended approach in behavioral and health questionnaire validation [48, 49].

Sampling adequacy was verified by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO >0.7) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.05). Factors with eigenvalues >1 were retained, and the scree plot was used for verification. Items with communalities < 0.3 or factor loadings < 0.4 were removed after theoretical review [50, 51].

Reliability

Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha (acceptable α ≥ 0.70) [52]. Test–retest reliability was evaluated among 30 randomly selected participants who completed the OPBQ twice, with a two-week interval ensuring temporal stability without recall bias. The test–retest was conducted in a stable environment (same location, time of day, and administration conditions) to minimize situational variability.

The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, in line with psychometric recommendations for test–retest reliability of health behavior instruments [53]. A sample of 30 participants is generally acceptable for reliability testing [54], as supported by empirical simulation studies showing stable ICC estimates for n ≥ 30 [55, 56].

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), a widely used software for psychometric evaluations. Descriptive statistics, including means (± standard deviations) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables, were computed to characterize participant demographics. For psychometric evaluation, CVR and CVI were derived as described; EFA included factor extraction and rotation; Cronbach’s alpha was calculated per subscale; and test–retest reliability was assessed using the two-way mixed-effects model ICC (ICC [1, 2]) with absolute agreement. Assumptions such as normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) were checked, and significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

In the psychometric evaluation phase, 600 women aged 30–60 years (mean 45.2 ± 8.7) from urban health centers in Shiraz participated. Most were married (72.3%) and had secondary or higher education (58.7%), with a mean BMI in the overweight range (25.4 ± 4.1 kg/m²). Risk factors included a family history of osteoporosis (28.0%), comorbidities such as diabetes or thyroid disorders (15.3%), and history of oophorectomy (9.7%). Regarding lifestyle, 42.0% engaged in regular exercise, 31.3% used calcium supplements, 12.7% smoked, and mean daily sun exposure was 1.2 ± 0.8 h. Mean parity was 2.1 ± 1.4, and 6.0% reported corticosteroid use for over three months. Full characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Phase 1: instrument development

Face and content validity

Qualitative face validity was assessed through a pre-test with 30 women from the target population, who reviewed the questionnaire items and provided feedback on clarity, cultural relevance, and ease of comprehension. Common suggestions included simplifying phrasing for dietary items to better reflect Iranian culinary practices (e.g., incorporating examples like yogurt or traditional calcium-rich foods). This led to minor revisions in 8 items, such as rephrasing ambiguous terms related to sunlight exposure, considering cultural norms of modest clothing.

Quantitative face validity demonstrated strong item relevance, with impact scores ranging from 1.6 to 4.2 (mean = 2.8). All 37 initial items exceeded the acceptability threshold of ≥ 1.5, confirming their importance and appropriateness for the target group.

Content validity was evaluated by a multidisciplinary panel of 12 experts (3 public health specialists, 3 nurses, 2 health educators, 2 nutritionists, and 2 endocrinologists). The CVR for individual items ranged from 0.58 to 1.00 (mean CVR = 0.82; all ≥ 0.56), indicating essentiality. The item-level Content Validity I-CVI ranged from 0.80 to 1.00 (mean I-CVI = 0.92), with relevance, simplicity, and clarity ratings all exceeding 0.78. The scale-level S-CVI/Ave was 0.94 (≥ 0.90), reflecting excellent overall content coverage. Based on expert feedback, three items were refined for precision, but none were eliminated at this stage.

Following these assessments, the preliminary OPBQ retained 37 items across six subscales, as outlined in the Methods.

Phase 2: psychometric evaluation

Construct validity: EFA

Prior to EFA, data suitability was confirmed: the KMO measure was 0.86 (acceptable > 0.7), indicating adequate sampling correlations, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was highly significant (χ² = 2845.67, df = 666, p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis of an identity matrix.

EFA using Principal Axis Factoring extraction with varimax rotation (orthogonal to assume independent factors) extracted six factors, aligning with the theoretical constructs of Pender’s HPM. These factors accounted for 68.4% of the total variance, exceeding the common threshold of 60% for behavioral instruments [57]. Eigenvalues for the retained factors were 8.5, 5.2, 3.1, 2.4, 1.8, and 1.2 (all >1.0), while subsequent eigenvalues dropped below 1.0 (e.g., 0.9 for Factor 7, 0.7 for Factor 8). The Scree Plot (Fig. 1) displayed a distinct “elbow” after the sixth factor, visually supporting the six-factor solution and confirming no over-extraction.

Scoring and missing data management

The final Osteoporosis Preventing Behaviors Questionnaire (OPBQ) comprises 35 items across six subscales. The scoring procedure was as follows: items in the Perceived Benefits subscale (items 1–5) were scored from 4 (Completely Agree) to 1 (Completely Disagree); Perceived Barriers items [6–10] were reverse-scored [1–4]; Perceived Self-Efficacy [11–17], Planning for Action [18–22], Commitment to a Plan of Action [27–30], and Social Support [31–35] items were scored from 3 (Often/Sure) to 1 (Never/Cannot at all); and Substituting Behaviors items [23–26] were dichotomously scored (1 for Correct behavior, 0 for Incorrect behavior). The total OPBQ score was calculated by summing the scores of all 35 items, yielding a theoretical range of 31 to 107, with higher scores indicating more positive osteoporosis-preventing behaviors. To handle missing data, participants with more than 20% missing responses (>7 items) were excluded from analysis. For participants with sporadic missing data (≤ 7 items), the mean score of the completed items within the corresponding subscale was used for imputation.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values indicating good to excellent homogeneity. Subscale alphas were: Perceived Benefits and Barriers (α = 0.82), Perceived Self-Efficacy (α = 0.88), Planning for Action (α = 0.79), Substituting Behaviors (α = 0.76), Commitment to a Plan of Action (α = 0.85), and Social Support (α = 0.81). The overall questionnaire alpha was 0.91, surpassing the ≥ 0.7 threshold for all components. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.42 to 0.76, supporting item retention.

Test-retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of 30 participants (demographically similar to the main sample) over a two-week interval. The ICC for the total score was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94), denoting excellent temporal stability (> 0.75). Subscale ICCs ranged from 0.78 (Planning for Action) to 0.92 (Perceived Self-Efficacy), with no significant differences between test occasions (p > 0.05 via paired t-tests). These results confirm the OPBQ’s consistency across time in the target population.

Descriptive statistics and measurement precision of the OPBQ

Descriptive statistics, internal consistency, and indices of measurement precision for the OPBQ total and subscale scores are summarized in Table 3. The total score demonstrated good variability, with the mean falling within the upper-middle range of the possible scale. Internal consistency was excellent for the total score (α = 0.91) and acceptable to good for all subscales (α = 0.76 to 0.88). The Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) for the total score was 2.10 points, indicating high measurement precision. The Minimal Detectable Change at the 95% confidence level (MDC₉₅) was 5.82 points, establishing that a change of more than approximately 6 points in an individual’s total score represents a true change beyond measurement error. These metrics support both the reliability and clinical utility of the OPBQ for detecting meaningful changes in osteoporosis-preventive behaviors.

Discussion

Need and HPM-based innovation

The high prevalence of osteoporosis in Iran (38% among women aged ≥ 50 years) [8] and the lack of culturally relevant behavioral tools, unlike belief-focused instruments like the Osteoporosis Health Belief Scale (OHBS) or Osteoporosis Knowledge Test (OKT), necessitated a tailored approach [58, 59]. Grounded in Pender’s HPM, the OPBQ measures key constructs—perceived benefits/barriers, self-efficacy, planning, substituting behaviors, commitment, and social support—for Iranian women aged 30–60 years. Self-efficacy (loadings 0.74–0.81) was a key driver, aligning with HPM’s emphasis on confidence [60]. Cultural adaptations, such as referencing yogurt and addressing modest clothing barriers to sun exposure, distinguished the OPBQ from Western tools, while removing two items (a generic benefit and a minor dietary barrier) due to low loadings (< 0.4) ensured specificity.

Summary of main results

The OPBQ showed strong content validity (CVR = 0.58–1.00, S-CVI/Ave = 0.94) and a six-factor EFA structure, explaining 68.4% of variance with robust loadings (0.62–0.81). Reliability was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.76–0.88, overall α = 0.91; ICC = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.82–0.94), meeting COSMIN standards. Item-total correlations (0.42–0.76) and minimal floor/ceiling effects (< 15%) confirm its ability to capture diverse behaviors. These findings support its robust psychometric foundation.

Comparison with existing instruments

The OPBQ addresses a critical gap in the existing arsenal of osteoporosis tools by moving beyond isolated beliefs or knowledge to measure modifiable behavioral constructs. Established instruments like the OHBS effectively assess perceptions but lack action-oriented components like planning and commitment [59, 61]. Similarly, knowledge-based tools such as the OKT and the Osteoporosis Knowledge Assessment Tool (OKAT) are valuable for assessing factual recall but do not capture the psychosocial drivers of behavioral enactment, such as self-efficacy or social support [58, 62, 63].

While instruments like the Bone-Specific Physical Activity Questionnaire (BPAQ) and the Osteoporosis Self-Efficacy Scale (OSES) focus on specific domains (physical activity history and confidence, respectively), their scope is narrow [64, 65]. The OPBQ integrates these dimensions into a single, holistic profile based on the HPM. Furthermore, its cultural adaptations (e.g., items on dietary habits and sun exposure barriers) make it particularly relevant for the Iranian context. This comprehensive, theory-driven, and culturally-tailored approach positions the OPBQ as a superior tool for designing and evaluating targeted behavioral interventions, which are essential for effective osteoporosis prevention.

Methodological considerations

The 600-participant sample (~ 17 per item) supported robust EFA (KMO = 0.86, Bartlett’s test p < 0.001). Items were developed using HPM, WHO/NOF/IOF guidelines, expert reviews (n = 12), and pretesting (n = 30), ensuring cultural relevance. Two items were removed for low loadings and overlap. Persian development eliminated translation needs, but measurement invariance across demographics was not tested.

Scientific and practical implications

The development of the OPBQ carries significant scientific and practical implications. Scientifically, it provides researchers with a robust, theory-driven tool to measure osteoporosis-preventive behaviors based on the HPM, enabling more precise etiological research and the evaluation of theory-based intervention studies. In clinical practice, the OPBQ can be used as a screening and needs-assessment tool. By identifying individuals with low total scores or low scores on specific subscales (e.g., low self-efficacy or high perceived barriers), healthcare providers can deliver targeted, personalized counseling to address these specific deficits. For public health policy, the OPBQ offers a valuable instrument for conducting large-scale screenings to identify populations at risk and for monitoring the effectiveness of community-based health promotion programs. Given the substantial economic burden of osteoporotic fractures in Iran, estimated at US$393 million annually, employing a validated tool like the OPBQ is a critical step toward implementing cost-effective, evidence-based prevention strategies to mitigate this growing public health challenge.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the use of a convenience sample from urban health centers in Shiraz limits the generalizability of our findings. The participants may not fully represent the broader Iranian population, particularly women from rural areas who often face distinct challenges such as limited access to healthcare facilities, nutritional differences, and varying cultural norms regarding physical activity. Furthermore, our urban sample may not capture the full spectrum of socioeconomic diversity, a key determinant of health behaviors. Second, the reliance on self-reported data is susceptible to social desirability and recall bias, although anonymous administration was used to mitigate this. Third, while EFA established a robust factor structure, the absence of Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), tests for convergent validity, and measurement invariance across subgroups (e.g., by age or menopausal status) limits the confirmation of the model and deeper structural insights. Future studies should employ stratified random sampling across diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings to validate the OPBQ and establish population norms. Longitudinal designs incorporating CFA, tests for measurement invariance, and assessments of predictive validity and responsiveness are strongly recommended. Finally, developing a short-form, establishing clinical cut-off scores, and pursuing cross-cultural validation in other Middle Eastern populations are important next steps for the instrument.

Conclusion

The OPBQ is a valid, reliable HPM-based tool that addresses a critical gap in measuring osteoporosis-preventive behaviors in Iranian women. By identifying behavioral determinants, it supports integration into Iran’s health system for screenings and education, promising to reduce disease burden through further validation.
