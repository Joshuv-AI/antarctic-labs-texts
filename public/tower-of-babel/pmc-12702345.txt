Lactate as a metabolic–epigenetic signal linking high-intensity interval training (HIIT) to miRNA-Centered remodeling of the skeletal muscle methylome and transcriptome
Authors: Lei Zhou, Soroosh Mozaffaritabar, Kumpei Tanisawa, Takuji Kawamura, Mitsuru Higuchi, Istvan Boldogh, Xueqing Ba, Sataro Goto, George Brooks, Yaodong Gu, Zsolt Radák
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLactate, a key exercise-derived metabolite and exerkine, is increasingly recognized as a metabolic–epigenetic signal, yet whether lactate and its transport directly shape skeletal-muscle epigenetic and transcriptional adaptations to exercise remains unclear.MethodsYoung male mice underwent six-week interventions: Control, lactate administration, high intensity interval training (HIIT), monocarboxylate transporter isoforms 1 and 2 (MCT1/2) inhibition, or HIIT plus inhibition. Gastrocnemius muscle was profiled by DNA methylation arrays, mRNA and miRNA-seq, together with analyses of signaling proteins, metabolites, and running performance.ResultsExogenous lactate and HIIT each elicited broad, site-specific remodeling of the skeletal muscle methylome and transcriptome with substantial overlap at promoter CpGs and differentially expressed genes. Promoter methylation changes showed weak coupling to steady-state mRNA, whereas integrative analyses revealed robust anti-directional miRNA–mRNA networks and included numerous chromatin and epigenetic regulators, identifying a lactate-driven, miRNA-centered axis. At the protein level, lactate increased TET2/DNMT3A and activated signaling involved in satellite-cell activation, angiogenesis, and AKT–S6 axis, accompanied by reciprocal miRNA–mRNA pairs. HIIT increased TET1/2 and DNMT3A, reduced DNMT3B, and uniquely enhanced mitochondrial/antioxidant signaling. Pharmacologic MCT1/2 blockade abrogated HIIT-induced methylome and miRNA remodeling and blunted transcriptomic and protein adaptations, demonstrating that intact lactate flux is required for exercise-induced epigenetic reprogramming. Despite molecular convergence, chronic lactate did not improve running performance, suggesting that lactate is necessary, but not sufficient for the full physiological benefits of HIIT.ConclusionsThese data support a lactate–miRNA–transcriptome/epigenome interplay that links metabolic perturbation to gene regulation in skeletal muscle. Using an integrated multi-omics approach, we propose a mechanistic framework for future studies targeting metabolic–epigenetic signaling in both physiology and pathology.Keywords: High-intensity interval training (HIIT), Lactate, microRNA, DNA methylation, Transcriptome, Skeletal muscle adaptation, Epigenetic regulation, MCT1, MCT2

Introduction

Lactate is more than an intermediate of anaerobic metabolism. It is an energy-rich carbon carrier that circulates between organs and serves as a myokine/exerkine that communicates physiological state across tissues [1,2]. From Warburg's observation that proliferating tumor cells produce lactate despite ample oxygen [3] to modern epigenomics, accumulating evidence shows that lactate, via lactylation, modulates DNA methylation, histone modifications, and miRNA networks [[4], [5], [6], [7]], thereby linking metabolism to epigenetic and transcriptional control. Elevated lactate can modulate TET activity [4], which catalyzes DNA demethylation and increases promoter accessibility for transcription-factor binding [8,9]. In addition, lactate-derived lactylation on histones and non-histone proteins has been shown in cancer models to couple glycolytic flux to miRNA programs and chromatin activation [7]. However, these lactate–epigenetic mechanisms have been characterized exclusively in pathological contexts, leaving their relevance in skeletal muscle largely unexplored.

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) markedly elevates lactate levels and is associated with broad adaptive responses, including enhanced protein synthesis [10], epigenetic remodeling [11] and transcriptional reprogramming [12]. Acute exercise-induced epigenetic modifications can occur extremely rapidly after exercise, and often precede mRNA alterations observed hours later [13]. Emerging evidence indicates the potential that exercise-evoked metabolic disturbances can reprogram miRNA networks and reshape DNA and histone methylation [14]. Among exercise-linked metabolites, lactate stands out as a potential regulator of epigenetic enzymes, forging a link between cellular metabolic, epigenetic, and transcriptional potential [15]. Several studies indicate that lactate itself contributes to skeletal muscle growth and remodeling [16,17]. Yet, despite the substantial lactate production during exercise, its direct role in driving epigenetic and transcriptomic adaptations in skeletal muscle has not been investigated.

To address these gaps, we compared chronic lactate administration with HIIT, with or without pharmacological inhibition of MCT1/2—the transporters that mediate lactate uptake across the sarcolemma and, via mitochondrial MCT1, delivery into the mitochondrial matrix [[18], [19], [20]]. Using an integrated multi-omics strategy, we profiled skeletal-muscle DNA methylation, mRNA and miRNA-seq, together with analyses of signaling proteins, metabolites, and running performance. We aimed to determine: (i) to what extent lactate recapitulates HIIT-induced methylome and transcriptome signatures; (ii) whether lactate flux is required for training-induced epigenetic and transcriptional remodeling; and (iii) how lactate-linked miRNA and DNA methylation axis provides a mechanistic bridge between metabolic disturbance and transcriptional remodeling in skeletal muscle. We further hypothesized that lactate is necessary for epigenetic remodeling but not sufficient to confer the full physiological benefits of HIIT, which require the integration of mechanical, neural, endocrine, and energetic inputs.

Methods

Ethical approval

All animal procedures were performed at the Research Institute of Molecular Exercise Science, Hungarian University of Sports Science (Budapest, Hungary). Experiments complied with the Guiding Principles for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and relevant Hungarian regulations, and were approved by the National Animal Experimentation Scientific Ethics Council (Hungary) (approval No: KA-4416).

Animals and animal procedures

Male BALB/c mice (∼2 months old) were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled room (12:12 h light–dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Mice were obtained from The National Institute of Oncology (Hungary). After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 6/group): Control, Lactate, HIIT, MCT1/2 inhibitor (MCTi), and HIIT plus MCTi (HIIT + MCTi). The Lactate group received sodium l-lactate (500 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, 71718) intraperitoneally (i.p.) five times per week for six weeks (0.2–0.3 mL per injection) in sterile PBS (Sigma, 806552). Control and HIIT groups received volume-matched PBS injections. For HIIT and HIIT + MCTi, mice underwent 3 days of treadmill familiarization (Columbus Instruments), followed by an incremental speed test on a treadmill set at 15 % incline. The test began with a 5-min warm-up at 6 m/min, after which speed was increased by 1 m/min every 2 min until exhaustion. Exhaustion was defined as failure to resume running within 5 s despite gentle stimulation. The average maximal speed attained by each mouse was used to prescribe training intensities. The HIIT protocol consisted of ten 3-min intervals at 85 % of mice's maximal speed, interspersed with 2-min bouts at 45 % of maximal speed, all at 15 % incline. Each session began with a 5-min warm-up at 50 % maximal speed. HIIT and HIIT + MCTi sessions were performed five days per week for six weeks. Notably, mice in the HIIT + MCTi group did not improve running capacity over the training period; therefore, the running speed was maintained at the initial level throughout the 6-week intervention across groups. MCT1/2 inhibition was achieved using AR-C155858 (MedChemExpress, MCE). The compound was dissolved in DMSO and diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 5 % DMSO in PBS. Mice in the MCTi and HIIT + MCTi groups received AR-C155858 (3 mg/kg [19]) 30 min before each training session throughout the 6-week period. To capture chronic rather than acute exercise effects, tissues were collected 48 h after the final training or injection session. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, gastrocnemius muscle was rapidly removed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Blood lactate measurement

Blood lactate concentrations were measured using a portable electrochemical lactate meter and compatible sensors (Nova Biomedical, 40828/40813) following a previously described protocol [21]. After a single HIIT session or lactate injection (500 mg/kg), blood was collected from the tail tip at multiple time points until lactate levels returned to baseline.

Treadmill fatigue test

Running performance was assessed by a treadmill fatigue test adapted from previously described protocol [22]. Testing was terminated when a mouse remained in the designated “fatigue zone” (defined as the area within approximately one body length) with stimulation for 5 continuous seconds without resuming running. All procedures were conducted under identical lighting and environmental conditions, and the treadmill was cleaned with 70 % ethanol before the trial to eliminate odor cues.

Voluntary wheel running test

To evaluate spontaneous locomotor activity and voluntary endurance capacity, mice were individually housed in cages equipped with a running wheel. After five weeks of intervention and a 24-h rest period, voluntary wheel running was recorded for 24 h. Wheel rotations were detected by a magnetic sensor and recorded continuously via an automated acquisition system. Total running distance over 24 h was calculated for each mouse. Cages and wheels were cleaned and disinfected prior to the test, which was conducted in a quiet, temperature-controlled room (22 ± 2 °C) without additional external stimuli.

DNA and RNA extraction

Genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from gastrocnemius muscle using the Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Tissue) (Geneaid) and the Quick-RNA™ Miniprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols. Concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). NanoDrop data are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA methylation profiling

Genomic DNA quality was assessed with the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent) and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher). For each sample, 200 ng of DNA was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research). Genome-wide methylation was measured using the Infinium Mouse Methylation BeadChip (>285,000 CpGs; Illumina) and scanned on the Illumina iScan system (n = 5–6). Raw IDAT files were imported into R (v4.3.1) and processed with the minfi package. Probes were annotated using the Illumina Mouse Methylation Manifest 12.v1 (mm10). Groups were abbreviated as Control (C), Lactate (L), HIIT (H), MCT inhibitor (M), and HIIT plus MCT inhibitor (HM). Probes were retained if detection p < 0.01 in ≥80 % of samples. Normalization was performed with preprocessNoob. β-values (proportion methylation) were used for visualization, and M-values (logit-transformed β-values) were used for statistical testing. Data quality was evaluated using β-value density plots and PCA on M-values. Batch correction was not applied because experimental groups were partially confounded with chip batches and no major technical bias was detected. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation. Importantly, previous work with this platform has demonstrated extremely high replicate concordance [23], with between-laboratory correlations of r = 0.9924 at the same plate/position, within-plate correlations of r = 0.9945 across positions, and within-laboratory between-plate correlations of r = 0.9981 at the same position, indicating minimal chip and position effects. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were identified using the limma package with empirical Bayes moderation applied to M-values. Linear models were fitted with contrasts specified as intervention vs Control, and significance was defined as Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 together with an absolute change in M-value (|ΔM|) > 0.3. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R (v4.3.1). Significant CpGs were annotated to genomic coordinates, CpG-island context, and UCSC features. Global and promoter (TSS200/TSS1500) specific methylation levels were summarized as mean β-values per sample, with group differences tested by one-way ANOVA and Tukey's post hoc test. Functional enrichment of hyper- and hypomethylated genes was performed with clusterProfiler, using org.Mm.eg.db for GO (Biological Process) and enrichKEGG (organism = “mmu”). Significant terms (FDR <0.05) were visualized as bubble plots. Results were exported as Excel tables (Supplementary Table S2–S6).

RNA sequencing

RNA libraries were generated using the QuantSeq 3′ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit FWD v2 for Illumina (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). Briefly, 100 ng of total RNA was used as input for first strand cDNA generation using oligodT primer followed by RNA removing. Thereafter, the second strand synthesis is initiated by random priming, and the products were purified with magnetic beads. Finally, the libraries were amplified and barcoded using PCR. All libraries were assessed on the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) to examine if adapter dimers formed during PCR. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (75 bp, single-end, n = 4–6). Sequencing data were processed on an Ubuntu-based server. Initial quality control was performed with FastQC and MultiQC. Adapter sequences, poly-X tails, and low-quality reads were trimmed with fastp. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs, 6 nt) and spacer sequences (4 nt) were processed using UMI-tools and cutadapt, and deduplicated reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR. BAM files were managed with samtools. Gene-level counts were generated with featureCounts in strand-specific forward mode. Raw counts were imported into R. Lowly expressed genes were removed using filterByExpr (edgeR). Differential expressions were performed with DESeq2 using the design ∼ Batch + Grouping, with the Control group (C) set as the reference level. Pairwise contrasts (L vs C, H vs C, M vs C, HM vs C) were tested with Wald tests, and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Genes with FDR (BH-adjusted p value) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 0.5 were considered differentially expressed. Variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied to normalized counts; ComBat (sva) was used only for batch correction of VST matrices for visualization (PCA and heatmaps), not for differential testing. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using clusterProfiler for GO Biological Process and KEGG pathways. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted with fgsea using MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (10,000 permutations; adjusted p ≤ 0.25). All analyses and figures were generated in R (v4.3.1), and results were exported to Excel (Supplementary Table S7–S9).

Integrated DNA methylation and RNA-seq analysis

To explore the relationship between promoter methylation and transcriptional activity, promoter-level ΔM values were derived by averaging logFC values of DMPs within annotated promoter regions (TSS200/TSS1500). These values were integrated with RNA-seq log2 fold changes from corresponding genes. Correlation analyses were performed at two levels: (i) across all promoter-associated CpGs and (ii) restricted to double-significant genes (meeting thresholds in both DMP and DEG analyses). Pearson and Spearman correlations were calculated, and linear regression slopes were reported. Genes were categorized into Hyper + Down, Hypo + Up, Hyper + Up, Hypo + Down quadrants. In addition, heatmaps of intersecting gene sets across comparisons were produced to highlight core genes with methylation–expression regulation.

miRNA differential expression and integrative analysis

Small RNA libraries were prepared from 100 ng total RNA using the NEXTFLEX Small RNA-Seq Kit v4 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Briefly, 3′ and 5′ adapters were ligated, ligation products were reverse-transcribed to cDNA, bead-based cleanup was performed, and libraries were amplified and barcoded with 17 PCR cycles. Library quality was assessed on an Agilent TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) to monitor adapter-dimer formation. Pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to yield ∼10 million single-end reads per sample (n = 5–6). FASTQ files were processed with miRge3.0 (mouse; miRBase v22.1), which performed 3′-adapter trimming (Illumina adapter TGGAATTCTCGGGTGCCAAGG), read collapsing, Bowtie-based alignment to miRNA references, and quantification; inserts were restricted to 16–30 nt. Mature-miRNA count matrix was imported into R and filtered to retain miRNAs with ≥5 counts in ≥20 % of samples. Differential expression of mature miRNAs was assessed using DESeq2 with the design formula ∼ Grouping, setting the Control group (C) as the reference. Wald tests were used for pairwise contrasts against Control, and p values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method; miRNAs with FDR <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 0.5 were considered differentially expressed. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R (v4.3.1). A study-specific miRNA–target compendium was assembled with multiMiR (organism = mouse), retaining all validated interactions and predicted interactions supported by ≥ 3 databases. Target gene symbols were mapped to Entrez IDs via org.Mm.eg.db; the union of all targets was used as the enrichment universe. For integrative analyses, significant miRNAs were combined with differentially expressed mRNAs from the matched RNA-seq contrasts (same thresholds: FDR <0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5) to construct anti-directional pairs (Up-miR ∩ Down-mRNA and Down-miR ∩ Up-mRNA). Target-based GO and KEGG enrichment used clusterProfiler with BH correction for multiple testing. To prioritize epigenetic regulation, we curated an epigenetic whitelist from MSigDB (mouse GO:BP and Reactome terms related to chromatin organization/remodeling and histone/DNA (de)methylation/(de)acetylation). Epigenetic genes present in the anti-direction sets of L vs C were summarized and visualized with lollipop plots. Results are provided in Supplementary Table S12–S14.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed as described previously [24] and we used the following primary antibodies: TET1 (PA5-49432), TET2 (PA5-85488), DNMT3A (PA5-85548), DNMT3B (PA1-884), MCT1 (PA5-72957), OGDH (PA5-28195), LDH (sc-33781), Visfatin/NAMPT (ab45890), PGC-1α (NBP1-04676), citrate synthase (CS, ab96600), phospho-AMPK (Thr172, 2535) and total AMPK (2532), phospho-PKA substrate (44–988A) and total PKA (PA5-17626), SIRT3 (2627), SOD2 (ab68155), PAX7(ab92317), acetylated lysine (MA1-2021), pan lactic acid-lysine (STJ11101522), VEGFA(ab46154), phospho-mTOR (Ser2448, 5536) and total mTOR (2983), phospho-AKT (Ser473, 9271) and total AKT (4691), phospho-S6 ribosomal protein (Ser235/236, 5364) and total S6 (2217), and FOXO1 (9454). Total protein intensity (Ponceau S) was quantified using ImageJ for normalization. For phosphorylated proteins, membranes were first probed for the phosphorylated form, then stripped and re-probed for total protein, with normalization performed on the same PVDF membrane. Each lane represents one animal (n = 4–6). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons among the five groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test versus the Control group. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in GraphPad Prism.

Lactate, pyruvate, NAD, and αKG quantification

Lactate and pyruvate concentrations in gastrocnemius muscle were measured using assay kits (Abcam; lactate kit ab65330, pyruvate kit ab65342, αKG kit ab83431, NAD/NADH kit ab176723). Approximately 20 ± 2 mg of homogenized tissue from the pre-mixed gastrocnemius of six mice per group was weighed and lysed on ice. Following deproteinization, samples were processed and analyzed according to the manufacturer's protocols. Metabolite levels are expressed as fold-change relative to Control (mean ± SD), and group differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's post hoc test versus the Control group (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in GraphPad Prism.

Results

Lactate concentrations and concentration changes (dynamics) and exercise performance

An overview of the experimental workflow is shown in Fig. 1A. Intraperitoneal lactate (500 mg/kg) elicited a rapid rise in blood lactate, peaking at ∼10–15 min. A single HIIT session similarly elevated blood lactate in both HIIT and HIIT + MCTi groups, with a smaller rise in HIIT + MCTi (Fig. 1B). Acute MCT1/2 inhibition increased running performance, whereas chronic inhibition did not sustain this effect (Fig. 1C). After the chronic interventions, HIIT increased running distance in fatigue test and voluntary wheel-running, whereas HIIT + MCTi abolished the increases in both treadmill and voluntary running performance (Fig. 1C). Global β-value means showed small, non-significant group differences. A mild reduction (non-significant) in global methylation was observed in the HIIT and lactate groups. Similar patterns were observed in promoter regions (TSS200/TSS1500), with a minor increase (non-significant) detected in the MCTi group (Fig. 1D). At the protein level, chronic HIIT increased TET1, TET2, and DNMT3A while reducing DNMT3B, whereas chronic lactate selectively increased TET2 and DNMT3A (Fig. 1E). These HIIT-induced changes were attenuated with MCT1/2 inhibition. Taken together, these findings indicate that HIIT, but not chronic lactate administration, improves running capacity, whereas pharmacological MCT1/2 inhibition prevents HIIT-induced gains in performance. Despite remodeling DNMT/TET proteins, global and promoter methylation changes remained modest.

Genome-wide DNA methylation remodeling

Across groups, β-value densities showed the expected bimodal distribution, and PCA of M-values did not reveal clear group separation (Fig. 2A and B). Compared with the Control group (limma; FDR <0.05; |ΔM| > 0.3), Lactate yielded 3155 DMPs (1527 hypermethylated; 1628 hypomethylated), HIIT yielded 2671 DMPs (1378 hyper; 1293 hypo), and the MCTi yielded 10,268 DMPs (6420 hyper; 3848 hypo), whereas HIIT + MCTi produced no significant DMPs at these thresholds (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Table S2). These counts provide the basis for downstream enrichment and overlap analyses (Fig. 2D).

GO (Biological Process) enrichment was performed separately for hyper- and hypomethylated genes in the HIIT and Lactate comparisons (Supplementary Table S3). The top 10 enriched terms for each set are shown in Fig. 2D. Overlap analysis identified 801 shared hypomethylated and 948 shared hypermethylated CpGs between Lactate and HIIT (Fig. 2E and F; Supplementary Table S4). Approximately 40 % of significant DMPs mapped to promoter regions (Fig. 2G). Within promoters, Lactate and HIIT shared 464 hypermethylated and 286 hypomethylated CpGs (Supplementary Table S5), with the shared hypermethylated promoter set enriched for pathways including MAPK, PI3K/AKT, ERBB, VEGF, insulin, BDNF, mRNA metabolism, and neural development (Supplementary Table S6).

Together, these findings show that both Lactate and HIIT drive extensive, site-specific DNA methylation remodeling with substantial overlap, whereas the absence of DMPs in HIIT + MCTi demonstrates that intact lactate transport is required for the methylation remodeling induced by HIIT.

Transcriptomic remodeling

PCA and heatmap analyses showed partial segregation by intervention (Fig. 3A and B). Using DESeq2 (FDR <0.05; |log2FC| > 0.5), Lactate vs Control yielded 857 DEGs (412 up; 445 down), HIIT yielded 474 (265 up; 209 down), MCTi yielded 40 (28 up; 12 down), and HIIT + MCTi yielded 414 (154 up; 260 down) (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table S7). Volcano plots summarize effect sizes and significance (Fig. 3D).

GO enrichment analyses of DEGs revealed distinct biological processes influenced by each intervention (Fig. 3E) (Supplementary Table S8). HIIT upregulated hormone, muscle-system, insulin, and metabolic pathways; Lactate upregulated angiogenesis, muscle-system and metabolic processes. HIIT downregulated BMP/structural pathways, whereas Lactate downregulated mitochondrial and metabolic genes. Notably, MCTi inhibition reduced, but did not abolish HIIT-evoked transcriptional changes. The remaining TOP 10 terms were enriched for TOR signaling, a pathway readily triggered by mechanical loading.

Hallmark GSEA revealed distinct pathway signatures for each intervention (Fig. 3F). HIIT enriched inflammatory, hypoxia-related, mTORC1, complement, and oxidative-phosphorylation programs, whereas Lactate enriched angiogenic, secretory, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and hormone-responsive pathways, accompanied by negative enrichment of MYC, p53, and DNA repair signatures.

Cross-contrast overlap analysis revealed a substantial set of genes commonly upregulated by both Lactate and HIIT (Fig. 3G), enriched for insulin/peptide hormone responses, skeletal muscle adaptation, purine/ATP-related metabolism, and KEGG pathways including insulin signaling, long-term potentiation, and mTOR signaling (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Table S9).

Together, these findings indicate that Lactate and HIIT each induce broad transcriptomic remodeling with shared core signatures, whereas MCT1/2 inhibition attenuates, but does not fully abolish, HIIT-evoked transcriptional changes, supporting a contributory role of intact lactate flux in the training adaptation.

Integrated analysis of promoter methylation and gene expression

At the genome-wide scale, promoter (TSS200/TSS1500) methylation correlated weakly with RNA expression in both Lactate and HIIT comparisons, indicating limited global coupling between promoter methylation and transcription (Fig. 4A and B). When analyses were restricted to genes significantly altered at both the methylation and expression levels, all four methylation–expression quadrants (Hyper + Down, Hypo + Up, Hyper + Up, Hypo + Down) were represented (Supplementary Table S10–S11). miRNA profiling and anti-directional miRNA–mRNA integration

PCA of normalized miRNA expression showed no clear group separation (Fig. 5A). Differential expression (DESeq2; FDR <0.05; |log2FC| > 0.5) identified 23 miRNAs in the Lactate comparison (15 up, 8 down), 10 in HIIT (5 up, 5 down), 68 in the MCTi comparison (45 up, 23 down), and 1 in HIIT + MCTi, all compared with Control (Fig. 5B and C; Supplementary Table S12).

Predicted targets of these miRNAs exhibited distinct contrast-specific themes (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S13): Lactate targets involved cell-cycle and stress responses, proteostasis/translation, and vascular–muscle remodeling; HIIT targets mapped to lipid and energy metabolism, cytoskeletal and angiogenic pathways, and insulin/MAPK/mTOR signaling; MCTi targets were enriched for autophagy–proteasome, metabolic, immune, and epigenetic pathways.

Integration of miRNAs with mRNAs via validated/predicted interactions revealed extensive anti-directional networks (Up-miR∩Down-mRNA; Down-miR∩Up-mRNA). Lactate administration produced the largest networks (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Table S14). In the Lactate comparison, Down-miR ∩ Up-mRNA targets were enriched for angiogenesis/vasculature, adhesion/migration, and intracellular signaling, consistent with pro-growth and pro-angiogenic activation. Conversely, Up-miR ∩ Down-mRNA targets mapped to proteostasis/autophagy, cell-cycle control, and stress/DNA-damage pathways, indicating a coordinated restraint on degradative and stress-responsive processes (Fig. 5F). Epigenetic regulators were also represented within these anti-directional pairs, aligning with the observed methylome remodeling.

Anti-directional miRNA–mRNA integration showed that Lactate produced markedly more pairs than HIIT, with a modest overlap between the two (Fig. 5G). GO analysis of the shared Down-miR ∩ Up-mRNA genes highlighted reduced extrinsic apoptosis, enhanced translational/post-transcriptional control, and increased migration/adhesion, together with activation of TOR–MAPK and related signaling programs (Fig. 5H).

Together, these findings suggest that lactate substantially contributes to miRNA-centered regulatory networks that interface with DNA methylation and modulate mRNA output in skeletal muscle.

Metabolic, mitochondrial, and growth-signaling adaptations

For metabolites (Fig. 6A), muscle lactate and NAD levels were unchanged across groups, whereas pyruvate showed a modest, non-significant increase in the Lactate and HIIT groups, accompanied by a trend toward a lower lactate/pyruvate ratio; αKG displayed a slight, non-significant rise with HIIT. Among metabolic enzymes (Fig. 6B), OGDH and NAMPT were significantly increased in the HIIT group, while LDH remained unchanged. The lactate transporter MCT1 was significantly elevated in the lactate group (Fig. 6C). Mitochondrial/metabolic biomarkers (Fig. 6D) showed higher PGC-1α, CS, SIRT3, AMPK p/t ratio, and SOD2 abundance in HIIT, whereas PKA p/t ratio was not altered. For growth-related signaling (Fig. 6E), VEGF, PAX7, and AKT and S6 p/t ratios were increased in the lactate group, while HIIT significantly raised mTOR and AKT p/t ratios; FOXO1 abundance was unchanged. Fig. 6F has shown that global protein Lactylation and Acetylation levels are prominent but did not differ across interventions. Taken together, these data indicate that HIIT uniquely enhances mitochondrial and oxidative-metabolic programs, whereas lactate preferentially activates growth-related signaling, highlighting a clear separation between lactate-driven signaling and the broader remodeling elicited by exercise.

Discussion

This study identifies a lactate–miRNA axis that links lactate signaling to epigenetic and transcriptomic remodeling in skeletal muscle. Chronic lactate administration reproduces a substantial subset of HIIT-induced DNA methylation and mRNA signatures but did not recapitulate the full adaptive phenotype: mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant reinforcement, and endurance adaptations. Notably, pharmacological MCT1/2 blockade attenuated HIIT-induced DNA methylation and miRNA remodeling and partly suppressed transcriptional and protein adaptations, indicating that intact lactate flux is required for exercise-induced muscle reprogramming. A key advance is the demonstration that lactate robustly reprograms miRNAs that inversely track their mRNA targets, whereas steady-state promoter methylation is only weakly coupled to gene expression. Overall, our findings suggest that, in skeletal muscle, miRNAs serve as proximate translators of the lactate signal into transcriptional output, while DNA methylation acts as a stabilizing layer rather than a simple on/off switch. More broadly, lactate appears capable of reshaping cellular programs in ways that may be relevant to cancer and metabolic disease.

At the global level, both HIIT and exogenous lactate produced only subtle, non-significant shifts in mean β-values across all CpGs and promoter regions, consistent with reports that exercise induces highly site-specific rather than uniform global methylation changes [25] and promoter hypomethylation after exercise is typically modest or transient and depends on sampling time and intensity [11,26]. Despite modest global differences, both interventions elicited extensive, site-specific DMPs, approximately 40 % of which mapped to promoters, with substantial overlap between lactate and HIIT. Functional enrichment of these loci highlighted canonical exercise-related pathways, including p38–MAPK, ERBB/insulin–PI3K–AKT, VEGF/BDNF signaling, cytoskeletal and vascular remodeling, post-transcriptional control, and neural development. Notably, enriched terms tended to arise from hypermethylated loci. We interpret this as repeated HIIT/lactate exposure tightening basal promoter activity at key signaling nodes to restrain chronic overactivation, while preserving acute inducibility via distal enhancers and post-transcriptional control. This pattern aligns with reports that initial post-exercise hypomethylation is followed by a rebalancing phase in which both hyper- and hypomethylated marks emerge [27]. It also accords with evidence that training modality and intensity influence the direction of methylation changes, with aerobic training commonly linked to hypermethylation and anaerobic or mixed regimens often associated with hypomethylation [28]. In this framework, DNA methylation acts as a multilayered regulator rather than a simple promoter on/off switch that prevents over-activation of signaling during chronic adaptation [11,29]. While prior studies hypothesized that metabolites couple energetic flux to demethylation machinery [14,30], direct in-muscle evidence that lactate alone reproduces exercise-like methylation patterns has been limited. Our overlap analyses fill this gap, showing that lactate is sufficient to induce a substantial fraction of the HIIT methylation signature and thereby acts as a metabolic–epigenetic signal in skeletal muscle. Consistent with recent exercise-epigenome work, we found a large fraction (∼60 %) of DMPs localized outside promoters. Non-promoter regions, particularly gene bodies and enhancers, appear to be especially responsive to exercise [11,31]. Intragenic methylation protects the gene body from spurious transcriptional activity, maintains the fidelity of transcriptional initiation, and could prevent alternative splicing [31].

Our data also clarify how lactate engages the DNA methylation machinery. At protein level, HIIT upregulated TET1/2 with increased DNMT3A and decreased DNMT3B, consistent with active remodeling of methylation system. Lactate selectively elevated TET2 and DNMT3A, aligning with evidence that lactate can enhance TET activity [4] and in line with the extensive methylome remodeling observed in both groups. Importantly, our miRNA–mRNA integration identified lactate-responsive miRNAs forming anti-directional pairs with mRNAs encoding DNA (de)methylation and chromatin regulators, supporting a feed-forward circuit whereby lactate reshapes the miRNA landscape to influence DNA methylation and chromatin complexes. This extends emerging cancer data on lactate–lactylation–miRNA crosstalk [7] to skeletal muscle adaptation.

Beyond epigenetic enzymes, lactate induced large anti-directional miRNA–mRNA networks across diverse functional domains, including angiogenesis and vascular remodeling, insulin/AKT–mTOR–S6 signaling, cytoskeletal organization, proteostasis/autophagy, and RNA processing. In contrast, promoter methylation and mRNA expression demonstrated only weak correlations, positioning miRNAs as the proximate regulators of the lactate-evoked transcriptome. This interpretation is consistent with prior training studies: after chronic training, ∼80 % of differentially expressed genes showed no correlation with methylation, and only ∼7 % exhibited the classical inverse relationship in skeletal muscle [32]. Other work similarly found no significant gene-specific associations between differential methylation and mRNA expression after chronic aerobic or resistance exercise [[33], [34], [35]]. These data support the notion that promoter methylation does not map one-to-one onto transcriptional outcomes and likely interacts with additional regulatory layers—promoter accessibility, gene-body methylation, distal enhancers, insulators, tissue-specific transcription-factor networks, and post-transcriptional control [12]. Nevertheless, a small subset of genes displayed coordinated promoter hypermethylation and altered expression in both Lactate and HIIT, clustering in metabolic, structural, and translational pathways relevant to muscle remodeling. For these genes, promoter methylation may help consolidate training-induced expression states.

A second insight from this work is the distinction between molecular mimicry and functional sufficiency. Pharmacological blockade of MCT1/2 abolished HIIT-induced miRNA remodeling and methylome reprogramming and dampened transcriptomic/protein adaptations, thereby linking exercise-derived lactate flux to coordinated miRNA reprogramming and DNA demethylation [14]. Data from previous studies can also be interpreted to mean that DNA methylation changes occur at medium-to-high exercise intensities, where lactate production is greater, whereas low-intensity exercise exerts minimal effects [36]. Yet, despite generating a large anti-directional miRNA–mRNA network, lactate alone did not reproduce mitochondrial or performance outcomes. This indicates that the full phenotype of endurance training depends on signal integration [11,13]. Lactate's metabolic message must be integrated with other exercise-evoked signals to produce the exercise adaptation. Practically, this clarifies why “exercise mimetics” based on single metabolites underperform: lactate can launch the gene-regulatory cascade but cannot substitute for the biomechanical and bioenergetic milieu that completes the adaptation. Interestingly, we observed that acute MCT1/2 inhibition transiently improved running performance, consistent with reports that skeletal-muscle MCT1 deficiency can enhance respiration and mitochondrial activity [18]. However, our data further demonstrates that chronic inhibition of MCTs did not reproduce these effects; instead, it abolished the HIIT-induced benefits. Taking together, these data are interpreted to indicate that transient perturbation of lactate transport may yield short-term performance effects, but intact lactate shuttling is indispensable for exercise adaptations.

Both HIIT and exogenous lactate reprogrammed the skeletal-muscle transcriptome with extensive shared signatures, in line with recent exercise-focused meta-analyses and multi-omic studies [12,25,37]. The shared upregulated HIIT–lactate gene set was enriched for insulin/IGF–ERBB–TOR signaling, muscle growth/adaptation and contraction, RNA metabolism/translation, purine/nucleotide and energy pathways, mitochondrial protein localization, and stress-responsive modules (hypoxia/glucocorticoid, JAK–STAT). By contrast, oxidative phosphorylation increased with HIIT but not with lactate alone, paralleling protein-level elevations in PGC-1α, CS, SIRT3, p/t AMPK, and SOD2 only after training—hallmark endurance adaptations [38]. Lactate preferentially increased PAX7 and the p/t ratios of AKT and S6, in line with prior reports that lactate can engage anabolic and regenerative pathways [16,17,39], consistent with our miRNA–mRNA integration. Notably, this pattern accords with in-vitro evidence in L6 skeletal muscle cells demonstrating that lactate alone can activate ROS-responsive transcriptional reprogramming [40]. HIIT, in contrast, coordinated AKT–mTOR with AMPK–PGC-1α, thereby coupling translational control and mitochondrial biogenesis. This distinction between HIIT and lactate is in line with our omics and running performance data: lactate reproduces translation/insulin-responsive modules, HIIT is required to drive mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense [13]. Accordingly, only HIIT—rather than lactate alone—activated endurance-related pathways that matched the improved running performance, while MCT1/2 inhibition abolished these remodeling, providing a direct molecular explanation for the divergent performance outcomes across groups. MCT1/2 inhibition produced few DEGs on its own but yielded a distinct transcriptomic response when combined with HIIT. The remaining top terms in HIIT plus MCTi were enriched for TOR signaling, a pathway readily triggered by mechanical loading, suggesting a shift toward mechanical adaptation when lactate transport is blocked [13]. Importantly, in cancer, lactate promotes angiogenesis by stimulating AKT signaling via an MCT1-dependent pathway [41]. Here, we observed the same lactate–MCT1–AKT axis in skeletal muscle, but in an adaptive rather than malignant context. Moreover, exercise has been reported to modify the methylation of cancer-related genes [42], which is in line with our findings. Therefore, lactate is not an inherently “good” or “bad” molecule; it is a context-based signaling that can support tumor vascularization and growth, yet in skeletal muscle couples to MCT1–AKT signaling and an epigenetic “memory” program that promotes training adaptation.

After chronic intervention, skeletal muscle αKG level showed a modest, non-significant increase only with HIIT, while OGDH and NAMPT were significantly elevated at the protein level, indicating an exercise-dependent reinforcement of TCA flux and NAD+ salvage that was not reproduced by lactate alone. Given that αKG can enhance TET activity [4], we initially hypothesized that higher αKG would potentiate epigenetic remodeling; however, the lack of a significant αKG increase at our sampling point suggests either a transient αKG surge that was missed, or an αKG-independent route whereby lactate reshapes the miRNA program and, secondarily, DNA methylation.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not directly validate the molecular targets identified by our multi-omics analyses. Although our integrated multi-omics approach provides a comprehensive, multilayered characterization, we did not perform in vivo or in vitro experiments to directly test the causal roles of specific candidate miRNAs or DNA-methylation regulators. Second, all analyses were performed in a single tissue (gastrocnemius muscle) and at one post-training time point, which may overlook important temporal dynamics and inter-organ crosstalk. Future work using time-resolved sampling, additional tissues, and targeted perturbations of candidate regulators will be essential to establish mechanistic causality. Such work is crucial to fully elucidate their contribution to skeletal muscle adaptation and lactate-related physiology and pathophysiology, including cancer [43].

In summary, we identify lactate as a key metabolic–epigenetic signal that contributes to skeletal-muscle remodeling during HIIT. Through integrative multi-omics, our data support a lactate → miRNA → transcriptome/epigenome cascade that connects metabolic stress to gene-regulatory architecture. Lactate reshapes the miRNA landscape, leading to inverse regulation of their mRNA targets, including genes encoding epigenetic enzymes, while simultaneously inducing site-specific DNA methylation changes at promoter and non-promoter regions. Together, these findings redefine lactate as more than a metabolic intermediate, positioning it as a core integrator of exercise signaling with relevance for both physiological adaptation and pathological remodeling.
