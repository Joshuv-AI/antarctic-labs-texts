Enhancing the longevity of lithium-manganese-rich layered oxides by promoting oxygen redox reversibility
Authors: Munsoo Song, Danwon Lee, Bonho Koo, Hyunjun Choi, Daesoo Kim, Juwon Kim, Chihyun Nam, Sungjae Seo, Jiseok Kim, Donghoon Lee, Yojin Kim, Jongwoo Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo enable widespread adoption of electric vehicles, positive-electrode materials must fulfill high energy, long-term stability, and earth-abundant composition within large-format architectures. Lithium-manganese-rich layered oxides show promise through high energy enabled by oxygen redox chemistry and abundant manganese content while minimizing cobalt content. However, oxygen redox irreversibility and gas evolution present challenges in its adoption to large-format cells. Here, we reveal how applied voltage window governs oxygen redox reversibility through integrated X-ray spectroscopy and microscopy, electron microscopy, and in-situ mass spectrometry. An optimized potential window of 4.3-2.0 V vs. graphite confines oxygen redox to reversible regimes while ensuring complete reduction, minimizing electron deficiency and suppressing gas evolution and structural degradation. Building on these insights, our formation and cycling protocol achieves specific energy of 663 Wh/kg based on the mass of the positive electrode active material with 92.2% retention after 883 cycles in 40 Ah cells using cobalt-lean lithium-manganese-rich layered oxides (0.27 wt% cobalt), demonstrating viability for scalable and affordable electric-vehicle batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

The widespread adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) demands the development of large-format cells employing positive-electrode materials that are composed of earth-abundant and low-cost elements, capable of delivering high energy density, and stable over long-term operation (refs. 1–3). For rapid commercial adaptation, such development also necessitates preparation processes of positive-electrode material, electrode, and cell to remain compatible with current manufacturing processes. Current high-energy EV batteries have been developed around the process chemistry of layered oxides, which deliver high energy density due to their high lithium-to-host ratio (refs. 4–6). However, layered oxides contain large amounts of nickel and cobalt, whose limited availability and geographic concentration create supply chain vulnerabilities and cost pressures7–9. This urges employment of layered oxides that replace nickel and cobalt with more earth-abundant elements. Lithium-manganese-rich layered oxide (LMR) is a candidate as it contains significant amounts of manganese—an element that is both geographically diverse and earth-abundant—and delivers high energy through oxygen redox (refs. 10–13), thereby directly addressing the aforementioned limitations.

Despite its promise, the adoption of LMR in large-format EV cells with tens of ampere-hours remains constrained by uncontrolled gas evolution stemming from the inherent irreversibility of oxygen redox14–22. Current research efforts have pursued various strategies to enhance oxygen redox reversibility23–27. Electrolyte optimization has significantly improved long-term stability in coin half-cells by mitigating surface phase transitions, but incompatibility with graphite negative electrodes hinders full-cell integration and scalability23. Surface engineering has shown promising long-term stability in 1.6 Ah pouch full cells, yet this approach requires high cobalt content, and its applicability to large-format cells is unexplored24. Large-format cells with limited headspace require stricter control of oxygen redox to suppress gas evolution, as even subtle changes in electrochemical environments can impair cell safety and reliability28–30. This underscores the need for rational design of operating protocols beyond material modification to effectively manage oxygen redox and ensure stable long-term performance at scale.

We achieved a specific energy of 663 Wh/kg based on the mass of the positive electrode active material with an energy retention of 92.2% after 883 cycles in large-format cells exceeding 40 Ah by rationally designing the voltage window for a Co-lean LMR material with 0.27 wt% cobalt. The complex redox chemistry of LMR renders large-format cells highly sensitive to operating conditions, posing a challenge in identifying voltage windows that simultaneously ensure long-term stability and high capacity. This challenge is further compounded by the inherent trade-off between capacity and cycle life in conventional voltage window regulation (refs. 31,32). Conventionally, narrowing the voltage window improves cycle stability at the expense of capacity, whereas expanding it increases capacity at the expense of cycle stability. However, our comprehensive understanding of its redox chemistry enables overcoming this limitation through the voltage design that enhances the stability and reversibility of oxygen redox. Specifically, we promote the complete reduction of oxidized oxygen by extending the discharge cutoff voltage. This deep discharge protocol simultaneously enhances both capacity and cycle life, unlike conventional layered oxides (Fig. 1). The optimal voltage window of 4.3–2.0 V effectively minimizes oxygen electron deficiency throughout cycling, thereby suppressing oxygen release and structural degradation. These results, supported by electron microscopy, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, scanning transmission X-ray microscopy, and online electrochemical mass spectrometry, demonstrate that rational voltage design enables the practical implementation of LMR, fulfilling both performance and sustainability goals for next-generation EV batteries.

Results

Cobalt-lean LMR used in this study has the chemical composition of Li1.14Ni0.312Mn0.544Co0.004O2, in which C2/m (Li2MnO3) and R3¯/m (LiTMO2, TM = Ni, Co, Mn) phase coexist in the pristine state. This composition was modified from conventional LMR by significantly reducing the cobalt content to 0.27 wt% and slightly adjusting Mn–Ni ratio to moderate oxygen redox participation (refs. 24,33–35). The crystal structure of LMR, characterized by high-resolution X-ray diffraction (HR-XRD), showed pronounced diffraction peaks corresponding to the (003) and (104) planes of the R3¯/m phase and the (020) plane of C2/m phase. Notably, the diffraction pattern does not exhibit additional peaks beside those expected for a layered structure, indicating the absence of other impurities (Fig. S2a). The field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) image of the cross-section electrode reveals spherical particles with an approximate size distribution of 5–8 μm (Fig. S2b), ideal for achieving high loading density.

Strategic voltage window regulation and its impact on electrochemical performance

To simultaneously enhance initial capacity and cycle life, we established an optimal charge cutoff potential for oxygen oxidation (activation) and a discharge cutoff potential for complete reduction of oxygen (restoration). First, we investigated oxygen redox behavior under different charge cutoff potentials through window-opening experiments spanning 2.0–4.6 V vs. Li/Li+ (Fig. 2a, b). When the charge cutoff potential exceeds 4.2 V vs. Li/Li+, a hysteric reduction process is observed below 3.6 V vs. Li/Li+ during discharge. Increasing the charge cutoff potential, where oxygen oxidation progressively increases, shifts a large portion of capacity into this low potential hysteric region and intensifies the kinetic limitation of the reduction process (refs. 36,37). The reduction capacity in this hysteretic region increases at slower discharge rates, demonstrating that this reduction capacity was accessible in principle yet kinetically hindered. This rate dependence becomes more pronounced at higher charge cutoff potential, where oxygen oxidation becomes intensive (Fig. S3). We attribute this behavior to the kinetically hindered reduction of highly oxidized oxygen species, rather than transition metal redox, which exhibits weaker rate dependence.

A higher charge cutoff potential increases the hysteresis during discharge, likely making the reduction of oxidized oxygen more difficult and thereby compromising its reversibility. On the other hand, discharge cutoff potential can also potentially influence oxygen redox reversibility, as the reduction of oxidized oxygen also occurs in hysteretic region at low potentials during discharge. To systematically investigate the impact of charge and discharge cutoff potential on oxygen redox reversibility, we compared three protocols: protocol 4620 (4.6–2.0 V vs. Li/Li+), which employs a high charge cutoff potential and thus exhibits pronounced hysteresis; protocol 4320 (4.3–2.0 V vs. Li/Li+), which uses a moderate charge cutoff and thus exhibits negligible hysteresis with sufficiently high capacity as shown in Fig. S4; and protocol 4330 (4.3–3.0 V vs. Li/Li+), which shares the same charge cutoff of 4.3 V vs. Li/Li+ but with a higher discharge cutoff of 3.0 V vs. Li/Li+.

Investigating how applied voltage influences oxygen redox reversibility requires directly probing changes in the oxidation state of oxygen throughout cycling. Scanning transmission X-ray microscopy (STXM) in transmission mode enables precise characterization of oxygen redox by capturing undistorted bulk spectral features. This contrasts with total electron yield (TEY-XAS) and fluorescence yield (FY-XAS) modes, which are less reliable for bulk redox analysis due to surface sensitivity and self-absorption effects, respectively (Figs. 3a and S5)16,22,38. The fraction of oxidized oxygen, which reflects oxygen’s oxidation state and redox activity, was analyzed via O K-edge STXM measurements.

Reference O K-edge spectra were collected from the pristine state through the first charge to capture the full evolution of oxygen redox. We analyzed the dataset comprising over 9000 pixel spectra using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), which identified three primary spectral components. Each pixel spectrum was expressed as a non-negative linear combination of these components, and the spectrum with the highest weighting for each component was selected as its representative end-member. These end-member spectra correspond to three distinct oxygen states: pristine lattice O2−, O2− coordinated to oxidized TM, and oxidized oxygen species (Figs. 3b and S6). The relative fraction of oxidized oxygen was quantified by linear combination fitting using these end-member spectra. In addition, the oxidized oxygen fraction can also be qualitatively inferred from the relative ratio of peaks at 529.5 and 530.9 eV in the O K-edge STXM spectra (refs. 16,22,38).

STXM results directly demonstrate the oxidized oxygen fraction at various states of charge for each protocol (Fig. 3c–e). For protocol 4620, the oxidized fraction increases from 0.277 to 0.483 (Δ = 0.206) during charge within 4.1–4.6 V, but only decreases to 0.360 (Δ = −0.123, 59% reversal) upon discharge in the same potential range. The remaining fraction is recovered upon further discharge to 2.0 V (0.360 to 0.196, Δ = −0.164). Overall, protocol 4620 recovers 86.1% of the oxidized oxygen, leaving a residual of 0.046 above the pristine value of 0.150. In contrast, protocol 4320 shows a smaller increase from 0.277 to 0.359 (Δ = 0.082) within 4.1–4.3 V during charge, and it decreases to 0.282 (Δ = −0.077, 93% reversal) upon discharge in the same potential. The oxidized fraction further decreases from 0.282 to 0.156 upon discharge to 2.0 V. Protocol 4320 recovers 97.1% of the oxidized oxygen, leaving a residual of only 0.006. These results demonstrate that limiting the charge cutoff potential substantially improves oxygen redox reversibility, consistent with reduced oxygen dimer formation and the associated kinetic barrier for its reduction.

The comparison between protocols 4320 and 4330, which share the same charge cutoff potential but differ in discharge cutoff, reveals the role of discharge depth in oxygen redox reversibility. The oxidized fraction continues to decrease from 0.186 to 0.156 (Δ = −0.030) in the 3.0–2.0 V region, indicating that oxygen reduction persists into the deep discharge region. In protocol 4330, this fraction remains unreduced due to its higher discharge cutoff of 3.0 V, resulting in greater oxygen redox irreversibility despite the identical charge conditions. Despite efforts in previous studies to regulate oxygen redox by optimizing the charge cutoff potential (refs. 39,40), the role of the discharge cutoff potential in governing oxygen redox reversibility has remained unexplored. Our results demonstrate that lowering the discharge cutoff potential from 3.0 to 2.0 V enables more complete reduction of oxidized oxygen, improving oxygen redox reversibility and coulombic efficiency by utilizing the hysteretic region otherwise inaccessible at higher discharge cutoff potentials. This approach contrasts with conventional NMC or LCO based positive electrodes, where extending the discharge cutoff potential provides negligible capacity gain and increases risks of material degradation (refs. 41–45). The redox chemistry of oxygen in LMR thus presents an opportunity to simultaneously enhance both capacity and cycle life through voltage window design (refs. 16,46).

Based on these findings, we designated protocol 4320 as the optimal voltage window for promoting reversible oxygen redox while maximizing capacity. To evaluate the impacts of voltage windows on oxygen redox activation/restoration and cycle life, we compared protocol 4320 against two alternatives—protocol 4620 (higher charge cutoff) and protocol 4330 (higher discharge cutoff)—in 1 Ah graphite | |LMR pouch full cells. The N/P ratio of the cells was identically set to 1.07, determined from a 0.1 C charge to 4.55 V vs. graphite at 45 °C during the initial cycle. All cells underwent an activation protocol spanning 2.00–4.60 V at 45 °C. This activation enhanced redox activity, delivering an initial discharge capacity of ~207.7 mAh/g within a voltage window of 2.00–4.30 V (Fig. S7).

To examine the actual operating potential ranges of both positive and negative electrodes in the full-cell configuration, three-electrode measurements were performed for all protocols (Fig. S8). Across all protocols, the graphite negative electrode operates predominantly within stage 2 and stage 3 graphite intercalation compounds, which are characterized by a narrow and stable potential range. The graphite negative electrode remains within approximately 0.08–0.20 V vs. Li/Li+, well above the lithium plating threshold (0 V vs. Li/Li) (refs. 47,48). Moreover, the minimal potential variation in graphite ensures that the applied full cell voltage windows directly translate to corresponding positive electrode potential ranges: protocol 4620 (4.51–2.16 V vs. Li/Li+), protocol 4330 (4.28–3.13 V vs. Li/Li+), and protocol 4320 (4.28–2.23 V vs. Li/Li+), closely matching the intended design. This enables direct assessment of how the difference in oxygen redox reversibility across voltage windows influence full-cell performance.

Long-term cycling performance was evaluated up to the 150th cycle (end-of-life, EoL) at a specific current of 66 mA/g (0.33 C) at 45 °C (Figs. 4a, b, and S9). For protocol 4620, we designated the 80th cycle as EoL due to safety concerns from significant gas accumulation. Protocol 4620 showed the lowest capacity retention (58.2%), with failure already apparent by the 50th cycle (mid-of-life, MoL). In contrast, protocols 4320 and 4330 stably operated with capacity retention of 90.3% and 87.2%, corresponding to 188.7 mAh/g and 160.6 mAh/g at the EoL stage, respectively. We note that Li | |LMR pouch half cells demonstrate the same performance trend observed in the pouch full cells (Fig. S10), substantiating that the observed performance differences originate primarily from intrinsic positive electrode degradation.

To investigate electrochemical fingerprints reflecting changes in redox activities and structural degradation of LMR electrodes, we extracted positive electrodes from degraded graphite | |LMR full cells and reassembled them into half-cells with lithium metal counter electrodes. These cells were cycled within 2.0–4.6 V vs. Li/Li+, a procedure referred to as the RHT. Capacity retention followed the order of protocols 4320, 4330, and 4620, consistent with results in graphite | |LMR full cells (Fig. 4c). Notably, protocol 4620 exhibited a significant decrease in average potential from 3.71 V (vs. Li/Li+, BoL) to 3.44 V (vs. Li/Li+, EoL), while protocols 4320 and 4330 maintained higher average potentials of 3.63 V and 3.60 V (vs. Li/Li⁺), respectively. These potential drops suggest structural degradation (refs. 49–51), which is further supported by dQ/dV analysis (Fig. 4d and S12).

dQ/dV analysis revealed that a decrease in high-potential Ni redox capacity follows the order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620 and that an increase in low-potential Mn redox capacity also follows the order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620. The decrease in Ni redox suggests structural disordering, including transition metal migration and oxygen-loss-assisted densification (refs. 17,52). The increased Mn redox also implies progressive formation of a spinel-like disordered phase upon oxygen loss, which reduces Mn and activates its redox subsequently (refs. 12,53). These results highlight that lowering the charge cutoff potential enhances structural stability, while extending the discharge cutoff potential for full reduction of oxidized oxygen is also essential for maintaining structural integrity.

Structural evolution across different voltage windows at end-of-life

To investigate the voltage-window-dependent structural changes inferred from electrochemical fingerprints, we performed high-resolution X-ray diffraction (HR-XRD) measurements on electrodes at both BoL and EoL stages for each protocol. All electrodes were extracted from degraded graphite | |LMR full cells, reassembled into Li | |LMR pouch half cells, and subjected to the (RHT, 4.6–2.0 V vs. Li/Li+). Diffraction patterns of all electrodes were collected at the fully discharged state (2.0 V vs. Li/Li+).

Ex situ HR-XRD analysis reveals lattice expansion and cation redistribution in EoL electrodes. The (003), (101), and (104) diffraction peaks clearly shift toward lower 2θ angles at the fully discharged state, indicating lattice expansion along both the a and c axis, as shown in (Fig. 5a–c). The magnitude of these shifts increases in the order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620. At the same time, Rietveld refinement shows that transition metal occupancy in the lithium layer increases in the identical order, reflecting a greater extent of structural disorder (Fig. 5d). These concurrent structural changes can be attributed to the formation of oxygen vacancies that alters the local electronic and coordination environment—including partial reduction of neighboring transition metal, elongation of TM–O bond length, decrease in electrostatic interaction between interlayers, and migration of transition metal into the neighboring lithium layer22,54. In situ XRD results further complement this observation by demonstrating the dynamic lattice evolution associated with oxygen vacancies (Fig. S13–16). The expansion of c axis is substantially suppressed in EoL electrodes compared to BoL electrodes, with the extent of suppression increasing in the identical order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620. This behavior is likely associated with weakened interlayer electrostatic repulsion due to oxygen vacancy formation and a pinning effect from transition metal migration defects, which collectively limit the lattice expansion upon lithium extraction (refs. 55,56). Accordingly, crystallographic evidence coherently suggests progressively increased oxygen vacancy formation from protocol 4320 to protocol 4620.

STEM analyses were performed to directly observe local structural changes in EoL electrodes. At the particle surface, the formation of a rock-salt-type reconstructed layer is observed in all EoL electrodes (Figs. S18 and S19). Line-profile analysis across these regions exhibits lattice spacings of ~2.82 Å, consistent with the {200} planes of rock-salt structures. This surface phase transformation is driven by concurrent oxygen release, lithium extraction, and transition metal migration, which collectively render the conversion from layered to rock-salt structure thermodynamically favorable. For the particle bulk, STEM images acquired along the [100] zone axis reveal expanded interlayer spacing along the c axis in all EoL electrodes relative to the BoL electrode (Fig. S20), with the extent of expansion increasing in the order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620. In addition, EoL electrodes cycled under protocols 4620 and 4330 exhibit locally disordered regions in which transition metals migrate into adjacent lithium layers. Collectively, these nanoscale features provide a local microscopic picture of the lattice expansion and cation mixing observed by ex situ HR-XRD and Rietveld refinement.

Moreover, nanovoids are clearly observed within the bulk regions of electrodes cycled under protocols 4330 and 4620, whereas they are absent in protocol 4320 (Fig. 5e–m). Line-profile analysis across these nanovoid regions reveals three features: reduced intensity of transition metal columns adjacent to voids, indicating local cation depletion; disrupted lattice periodicity accompanied by non-uniform interlayer spacings (4.96 and 5.33 Å in protocol 4620, compared with a uniform spacing of 4.95 Å in protocol 4320); and locally densified regions near voids associated with transition metal migration into neighboring lithium layers (blue arrows in Fig. 5g). These features provide direct evidence that nanovoid formation is coupled with cation disordering and local structural collapse. Accordingly, structural evolution proceeds through local intermixing of layered and rock-salt-like structural motifs driven by oxygen vacancy formation and cation disordering.

The observed nanovoids can be categorized into two types: closed voids enclosed within the particle and open voids connecting the particle interior to the surface (Figs. S22 and S23). Closed voids contain trapped molecular oxygen, which reduces and reincorporates into the lattice on discharge. This reincorporation of oxygen contributes to voltage fading through the formation of electronically and ionically insulating Li2O-like domains within the closed voids (ref. 20). Open voids expose particle’s interior to the electrolyte through fracturing of the particle, facilitating further oxygen loss at the newly formed cathode-electrolyte interface (CEI), as evidenced by the densified structure in these regions. The evolution and growth of these nanovoids limit available sites for lithium storage, disrupt the redox pair potentials, and accelerate capacity and voltage fade. Accordingly, the extents of nanovoid formation—most severe in protocol 4620, followed by 4330 and 4320—align with the observed electrochemical behavior.

This structural evolution can be rationalized by the difference in oxygen redox reversibility across varying voltage windows. Specifically, the use of high charge cutoff voltage induces excessive oxygen oxidation, promoting oxygen dimer formation, subsequent oxygen release, and cation disordering. The resulting vacancies tend to cluster and form nanovoids as shown in protocol 4620. Limiting the charge cutoff voltage to 4.3 V moderates oxygen oxidation and effectively suppresses nanovoid formation, as shown in protocols 4330 and 4320. More importantly, the distinct difference in nanovoid formation between protocols 4330 and 4320, despite sharing the same charge cutoff voltage, underscores the critical role of utilizing low voltage region (2.0–3.0 V) in governing oxygen redox reversibility and oxygen release. We suppose that the incomplete reduction of oxidized oxygen leads to increased electron deficiency of oxygen in subsequent cycles (ref. 30), progressively exacerbating oxygen redox reversibility and contributing to structural degradation. This will be discussed later in further detail.

Comprehensive end-of-life analyses after cycling under different voltage windows

To understand the degradation behavior across varying voltage windows, redox behavior of oxygen and transition metal was investigated at the EoL stage. We extracted positive electrodes from EoL cells that had been subjected to three different protocols (4620, 4330, and 4320) and reassembled them in half-cells. These samples were then cycled within a potential range of 2.0–4.6 V vs. Li/Li+ using the RHT protocol. We collected samples at both fully charged (4.6 V vs. Li/Li⁺) and fully discharged (2.0 V vs. Li/Li⁺) states to investigate oxygen redox behavior through O K-edge STXM analysis. As shown in Figs. S24 and S25, oxygen redox activity and its reversibility increased in the order of 4620 < 4330 < 4320. Protocol 4320 exhibited the highest degree of oxygen oxidation at full charge—comparable to the BoL electrode—and the lowest fraction of oxidized oxygen at full discharge. This indicates highly reversible oxygen redox. In contrast, protocols 4620 and 4330 showed both reduced oxygen oxidation at full charge and higher residual oxidized oxygen at full discharge, suggesting diminished reversibility. This trend correlates directly with the degree of structural degradation accumulated during cycling. Oxygen redox reactions are known to promote the formation of oxygen dimers and subsequent cation disordering. The release of oxygen dimers from the lattice creates oxygen vacancies, which further destabilize the local cation framework. This degradation lowers the density of accessible nonbonding oxygen states required for redox and impedes the reduction of oxidized oxygen species, thereby progressively diminishing the reversibility over cycling. Accordingly, the extensive structural degradation observed in protocols 4620 and 4330 appears to have significantly hindered reversible oxygen redox processes.

The change in redox behavior of transition metals also reflects structural degradation induced by oxygen redox irreversibility (Figs. S25–27). Ni redox capacity decreased in the order 4320 > 4330 > 4620, consistent with the observations in the dQ/dV plot in (Fig. 4d). This suggests that protocol 4320 suppressed the formation of redox-inactive Ni by mitigating transition metal migration and densification, which are promoted by O–O dimerization and subsequent oxygen loss that destabilize the local framework (ref. 52). On the other hand, the capacity corresponding to Mn redox increased in the order of protocols 4320 < 4330 < 4620, reflecting progressive activation of the Mn4+/Mn3+ redox couple intrinsically coupled to oxygen loss. Oxygen dimerization during excessive oxidation transforms the continuous O 2p band into discrete molecular orbitals with antibonding σ* states above the Mn 3 d bands. Manganese accepts electrons from these high-energy σ* states, reducing Mn4+ to Mn3+ and activating its redox participation (refs. 12,53). Thus, the observed increase in Mn redox activity correlates strongly with the rise in oxygen loss.

Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis at the Ni and Mn K-edges revealed a progressive decline in first-shell coordination intensity across protocols 4320 > 4330 > 4620 (Fig. S28). This coordination corresponds to TM-O bonding, where each transition metal is coordinated by six oxygen atoms in octahedral geometry. The diminished first-shell intensity reflects a reduction in TM-O bond density and orbital overlap, indicating disruption of the local symmetry. Such structural disruption is likely driven by transition metal migration and oxygen vacancy formation (ref. 22). In particular, oxygen vacancies may facilitate the migration of transition metals into adjacent lithium sites, further disrupting the local structure. Therefore, these results suggest that oxygen dimer formation and subsequent oxygen loss progressively increase in the corresponding order, inducing cation disordering and oxygen vacancy formation and ultimately contributing to the observed differences in structural degradation.

In addition to bulk structural and redox evolution, the release of lattice oxygen also alters the particle surface by accelerating interfacial reaction and cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) buildup. The released oxygen exists predominantly in a highly reactive singlet state (1Δg or 1O2), which can either quench to triplet O2 or directly attack carbonate solvents57. The chemical oxidation of carbonate solvents generates CO2 and organic decomposition products, which progressively accumulate as an organic-rich CEI58,59. In parallel, the decomposition of carbonate solvents can generate protic species that promote LiPF6 degradation to reactive intermediates (PF5/POF3) and HF60. These species can further react to form inorganic CEI components such as LixPFyOz and LiF. XPS analysis at O 1 s and F 1 s regions reveals increased accumulation of organic-rich CEI species (C–O, C = O, and R–O–Li) and fluorinated inorganic components (LixPFyOz and LiF) in the order of protocols 4620 > 4330 > 4320 (Figs. S29 and S30). Such increased CEI coverage, especially of fluorinated inorganic species, is expected to increase interfacial resistance and polarization, thereby contributing to larger overpotentials. Notably, this interfacial signature evolves in parallel with bulk degradation driven by oxygen-vacancy formation and cation disordering. These results indicate that oxygen redox irreversibility concurrently drives bulk degradation and CEI growth, providing a consistent interpretation of the stability and performance across voltage windows.

Impact of discharge cutoff voltage on oxygen redox reversibility

To elucidate the mechanism by which extending the discharge cutoff voltage enhances oxygen redox reversibility, we conducted real-time gas analysis using online electrochemical mass spectrometry (OEMS) coupled with oxygen oxidation state analysis via STXM during the 5th cycle. Positive electrodes extracted from BoL full cells were reassembled in lithium half-cells equipped with a customized OEMS setup (refs. 61,62). The cells underwent four complete cycles under protocols 4330 and 4320, and O2 and CO2 evolution were monitored during the 5th charging cycle. Protocol 4330 exhibited significantly higher O2 and CO2 evolution, indicating increased oxygen release from the lattice and more pronounced irreversible oxygen redox (Fig. 6a). Previous studies have shown that oxidized oxygen directly generates reactive singlet oxygen (1Δg or 1O2), which either quenches into inert triplet oxygen (3O2) or reacts with the carbonate-based electrolyte to form CO2 (refs. 57,58). Since gas evolution from direct electrolyte oxidation is negligible below 5 V and residual lithium decomposition occurs primarily during the initial cycles, the observed gas evolution is attributed to oxygen release from the lattice of LMR63.

STXM results revealed distinct differences in oxygen redox behavior and its reversibility between the two protocols, aligning with OEMS results. Protocol 4320, with its extended discharge cutoff voltage, showed reversible oxygen redox by facilitating the complete reduction of oxidized oxygen. This protocol exhibited nearly symmetric changes in the fraction of oxidized oxygen between charge and discharge processes, with its discharge endpoint value (L3 in Fig. 6c) returning to a level comparable to the initial state (L1 in Fig. 6c). In contrast, protocol 4330 exhibited asymmetric behavior between charge and discharge. The higher voltage limit of 3.0 V hindered the complete reduction of oxygen, resulting in a higher fraction of oxidized oxygen at the discharge endpoint (H3 in Fig. 6c) compared to the initial value (H1 in Fig. 6c). This incomplete reduction likely led to the gradual accumulation of electron-deficient oxygen over repeated cycles. Consequently, protocol 4330 began each cycle with a higher level of oxidized oxygen than protocol 4320 and maintained higher levels throughout the cycling, particularly showing a higher value at the charge endpoint (L2 and H2 in Fig. 6c).

Incomplete reduction of oxidized oxygen increases the average electron-deficiency of oxygen. Under increasingly electron-deficient conditions, oxygen tends to transition toward its most energetically favorable state as dimers. This triggers structural rearrangement by disrupting the coordination environment. O–O hybridization in dimers inherently reduces hybridization with adjacent transition metals and limits the anionic charge necessary to stabilize nearby transition metal sites. Consequently, TM-O coordination becomes destabilized, making cation sites adjacent to oxygen dimers energetically less favorable and driving transition metal ions to migrate away from dimer-rich regions. Additionally, oxygen dimers eventually transition into O2 molecules that escape from the lattice surface and propagate into the bulk, creating oxygen vacancies throughout the structure. Therefore, structural disordering becomes more prevalent when oxidized oxygen remains incompletely reduced during discharge. These sequential events—incomplete reduction, electron deficiency, dimerization, and structural disordering—collectively establish a mechanistic link between oxygen redox reversibility and discharge cutoff voltage in maintaining structural integrity.

Toward commercial applications: scale-up challenges and optimization of formation and cycling protocol in 40 Ah cells

Large-format cells are essential in EV applications to maximize energy density at the pack level while minimizing cell-to-cell interfaces, dead volume, and manufacturing complexity. We evaluated the commercial viability of voltage-window-regulated LMR in large-format cells exceeding 40 Ah. Conventionally, battery reliability has been assessed based on performance observed in small-format pouch cells. However, this approach insufficiently accounts for challenges unique to large-format cells. These cells feature increased electrode area and higher stacking numbers, reducing the relative proportion of inactive components such as tabs, current collectors, and packaging (Fig. 7a). This cell geometry restricts available headspace within the cell’s volume, while gas evolution occurs at a consistent rate per unit capacity regardless of cell size. Consequently, large-format cells inherently possess lower tolerance thresholds for internal pressure accumulation compared to small-format cells. This fundamental limitation renders large-format cells vulnerable to venting failure, requiring formation and cycle protocols that can effectively manage internal pressure for safe and reliable long-term operation.

Comparative evaluation of 1 Ah and 40 Ah cells confirmed the scaling disparity between small and large-format cells. While 1 Ah cells demonstrated stable cycling over 200 cycles under different formation conditions (Figs. 7b and S31), gas analysis revealed that the formation protocol with higher voltage and temperature (2.00–4.60 V at 45 °C) leads to greater gas accumulation than the moderate protocol (2.00–4.55 V at 35 °C), as shown in Fig. 7c. This difference remained manageable in small-format cells, where gas accumulation had minimal impact on long-term performance. However, the excessive gas accumulation is critical in 40 Ah cells, where internal pressure caused venting failure after only 112 cycles under the harsh formation protocol, as shown in Fig. 7d. These findings validated our rational approach that operating protocols must be optimized for large-format cells to balance electrochemical performance with gas management.

To identify an effective lever for gas management during formation, we decoupled the impacts of temperature and charge cutoff voltage by comparing gas evolution under three conditions: 2.00–4.60 V at 45 °C, 2.00–4.55 V at 45 °C, and 2.00–4.60 V at 35 °C (Fig. S32). Lowering the temperature from 45 °C to 35 °C while maintaining a charge cutoff voltage of 4.60 V suppresses gas evolution more effectively than lowering the charge cutoff voltage from 4.60 V to 4.55 V at 45 °C. This stronger temperature dependence arises because elevated temperature not only facilitates deeper lithium extraction and oxygen oxidation but also accelerates the kinetics of oxygen dimer formation and release by increasing the population of species that can overcome activation barriers (refs. 36,64). Thus, optimizing formation temperature proves critical for managing gas evolution in large-format cells.

We demonstrated that subtle optimization of formation protocols (2.00–4.55 V at 35 °C) could substantially improve performance in 40 Ah large-format cells. Using the optimized formation protocols, we tested protocols 4330, 4320, and 425200 (4.25–2.00 V), as shown in Fig. 7d, S33 and S34. All specific energy values are based on the mass of the positive electrode active material. Protocol 4330 delivered an initial specific energy of 641 Wh/kg, retaining 530 Wh/kg (82.7%) after 400 cycles. Protocol 4320 achieved a higher initial specific energy of 698 Wh/kg and retained 650 Wh/kg (93.1%) over the same period. The final optimized protocol 425200 demonstrated stable long-term operation, achieving an initial specific energy of 663 Wh/kg and retaining 635 Wh/kg (95.7%) after 400 cycles and 612 Wh/kg (92.2%) after 883 cycles. This performance shows that rational protocol design can establish LMR as a commercially viable positive electrode material by overcoming the fundamental limitations that previously hindered large-format implementation.

Discussion

This study presents voltage window regulation as a fundamental strategy for addressing oxygen redox irreversibility and resulting gas evolution in LMR, which has constrained its adoption in EV applications. We successfully rationalized how applied voltage activates and restores oxygen redox and its cumulative impact on long-term stability. Our findings reveal that while restricting the charge cutoff voltage moderates oxygen oxidation to the reversible threshold, extending the discharge cutoff voltage is equally critical for completely reducing oxidized oxygen and enhancing redox reversibility. Complete reduction alleviates oxygen electron deficiency, thereby suppressing dimer formation and subsequent oxygen release, which otherwise induces structural degradation, including cation disordering and oxygen vacancies. Our mechanistic understanding establishes clear relationships between oxygen redox reversibility and applied voltage that guide rational design of operating conditions.

The significance extends beyond the enhancement of oxygen redox reversibility to address fundamental scaling challenges. Large-format cells with tens of ampere hours require stricter gas management to ensure safe and reliable operation due to their limited headspace. Through systematic optimization of formation and cycling protocols specific to large-format cells (2.00–4.55 V vs. graphite and 4.25V–2.00 V vs. graphite, respectively), we achieved a specific energy of 663 Wh/kg based on the mass of the positive electrode active material with 92.2% retention after 883 cycles in 40 Ah pouch full cells using Co-lean LMR containing only 0.27 wt%. Successfully demonstrating reliable long-term performance in 40 Ah cells showcases the commercial viability of manganese-rich positive electrode materials that preserve high energy density while utilizing earth-abundant elements. This work establishes a pathway for practical battery systems that will accelerate widespread electric vehicle adoption through sustainable and accessible energy storage solutions.

Methods

Cell specifications and electrochemical operation

The graphite | |LMR pouch cells were fabricated by LG Energy Solution Ltd. using a proprietary commercial-scale manufacturing process. Manufacturing details, including the identities and contents of the binder and conductive additives, separator, electrolyte volume, and applied external stack pressure, cannot be disclosed for proprietary reasons. All parameters that can be disclosed are reported below.

Pouch-type full cells were supplied by LG Energy Solution Ltd. The pouch-type full-cells consisted of a LMR positive electrode (Li1.14Ni0.312Mn0.544Co0.004O2, LG Energy Solution Ltd.), a graphite negative electrode (LG Energy Solution Ltd.), a ceramic-coated polyethylene separator film, and electrolyte (1.2 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/ethyl methyl carbonate/diethyl carbonate = 20/70/10 by volume, with 0.5 wt% vinylene carbonate and 0.5 wt% propane-1,3-sultone, LG Energy Solution Ltd.). The N/P ratio of the graphite | |LMR pouch full cells was identically set to 1.07, determined from a 0.1 C charge to 4.55 V vs. graphite at 35 °C in a temperature-controlled chamber (35 ± 1 °C) during the initial cycle. Standard pouch stack cells with a nominal capacity of 1.5 Ah were cycled in a temperature-controlled environmental chamber maintained at 45 ± 1 °C in the voltage range specific to each protocol (Protocol 4620, Protocol 4330, and Protocol 4320). Charging was performed at 0.33 C ( ~ 1.15 mA/cm²) via constant-current mode, followed by constant-voltage mode at the cutoff voltage until the current dropped to 0.05 C. Large-format cells (290.5 mm × 159.0 mm × 11.5 mm) with a nominal capacity of 40.9 Ah were cycled in a temperature-controlled chamber at 25 ± 1 °C under similar conditions. CC charging was performed at 0.33 C ( ~ 11.7 A), followed by CV charging until the current reached 0.05 C, with CC discharging conducted at the same rate ( ~ 11.7 A).

Post-mortem characterization

Pouch-type full-cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glove box (with O2, H2O < 0.1 ppm) in the full discharge state at designated cycle numbers, and were transferred between the glove box and each instrument without exposure to ambient air. All harvesting and sample preparation were performed at 25 °C. Prior to post-mortem analysis, the harvested electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with excess dimethyl carbonate (anhydrous, ≥99%, Sigma Aldrich) to remove residual electrolyte. LMR positive electrodes were extracted and punched into 10 mm disk electrodes using a precision disk punch (Wellcos Corp.), which were then reassembled into CR2032 coin cells with Li metal (Wellcos Corp.) as the counter electrode and fresh electrolyte (1.2 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/ethyl methyl carbonate/diethyl carbonate = 20/70/10 by volume, with 0.5 wt% vinylene carbonate and 0.5 wt% propane-1,3-sultone, LG Energy Solution Ltd.). A layer of 2320 separator (polypropylene/polyethylene/polypropylene layered microporous membrane, Celgard LLC) was used: thickness 20 µm, porosity 39%, and average pore size ≈0.03 µm. The separator was punched to a diameter of 18 mm for the CR2032 coin-cell configuration. Unless otherwise noted in the main text, the coin cells were cycled between 2.0 and 4.6 V vs. Li/Li+ in a temperature-controlled chamber at 25 ± 1 °C after a 10-h resting period to ensure complete electrolyte wetting on electrodes with high areal capacity loading. For ex situ analysis, the final state of charge (SoC) was controlled by CC charging or discharging at 0.33 C, followed by CV charging at 4.6 V until the current dropped to 0.05 C. For ex situ measurements, the final State of Charge (SoCs) of the positive electrode was controlled by constant-current charging (or discharging) at 0.33 C (∼1.15 mA cm−2) and followed by constant-voltage charging at the cutoff voltage (4.6 V) until the current reached 0.05 C.

7High-resolution synchrotron X-ray diffraction (HR-XRD) measurements were conducted at the BL19A1 beamline of the Taiwan Photon Source (TPS) for powder samples and beamline 9B of Pohang Light Source (PLS-II) for electrode samples. Powder samples were loaded and packed into 0.3-mm-diameter quartz capillaries in an argon-filled glovebox and subsequently sealed with a UV-curable resin. Powder samples in capillaries were analyzed using Debye-Scherrer geometry (transmission mode) with a resolution of 0.03°, a step time of 60 s, and an energy of 20 keV. For electrode samples, EoL positive electrodes were extracted from degraded full cells, reassembled into Li half-cells, and cycled within 2.0–4.6 V vs. Li/Li+ in a temperature-controlled chamber at 25 ± 1 °C. The electrodes were harvested and rinsed with dimethyl carbonate to remove residual electrolyte, dried under an inert atmosphere, and sealed in aluminum-laminated pouches to prevent air exposure during storage and transport. HR-XRD measurements were performed at the fully discharged state, and lattice parameters and cation occupancies were quantified by Rietveld refinement. In situ XRD measurements were conducted using an in-house diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker) on both beginning-of-life (BoL) and EoL electrodes within their respective cycling potential windows. In addition, EoL electrodes were examined under identical reference conditions (4.6–2.0 V vs. Li/Li+) to distinguish accumulated structural damage from reversible voltage-induced effects.

Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images were obtained using JEOL JEM-ARM200F (JEOL Ltd, Japan) under 200 keV. The cross-sectional samples were prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB) using a Helios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at the National Center for Inter-University Research Facilities (NCIRF) of Seoul National University.

Scanning transmission soft X-ray microscopy (STXM) analysis at O K-edge was performed at beamline 10 A of PLS. Positive electrode materials extracted from the cycled electrode were dispersed onto carbon-coated Cu TEM grids for the measurements. The measurements were repeated over different X-ray energy ranges to obtain the image stacks. The image stacks were acquired in 0.2 eV steps with a 2 ms dwell time and were aligned using the aXis 2000 software package.

Ex situ X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements at the Ni and Mn K-edges were performed at the 7D and 10 C Wide-XAFS beamlines of the PLS. The electrodes were sealed in aluminum-laminated pouches to prevent air exposure during transport and measurement. Spectra were calibrated using Ni and Mn metal foils as references. Pre-edge and post-edge backgrounds were subtracted, and the normalized spectra were analyzed using Athena software.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted at the Research Institute of Advanced Materials, Seoul National University. All measurements were performed under an argon atmosphere without exposure to ambient air to prevent any alteration of the CEI during analysis. For sample preparation, EoL electrodes were extracted from degraded full cells at the fully discharged state, rinsed with dimethyl carbonate, and sealed in an argon-filled environment prior to measurement. XPS data were processed using the XPSPEAK41 software. After Shirley's background subtraction, peaks were fitted using a nonlinear least-squares with a mixed Gaussian–Lorentzian line shape (70% Gaussian and 30% Lorentzian).

Operando gas analysis

Operando gas analysis was carried out using an online differential electrochemical mass spectrometer (HPR-40, Hiden Analytical). Custom-built OEMS cells were assembled with LMR positive electrodes, Li metal negative electrodes, and fresh electrolyte. The OEMS cell was connected to a mass spectrometer operating under ultrahigh vacuum ( < 10−7 Torr). Gas evolution during electrochemical cycling was monitored continuously at a controlled flow rate. Electrochemical data were collected using a VSP-200 potentiostat, while gas signals were recorded via Hiden Analytical software. Background stabilization was achieved through a 1-h open-circuit voltage period prior to cycling. Quantitative gas analysis was performed using a 3D calibration surface, correlating signals with partial pressures of standard gas across various concentrations.
