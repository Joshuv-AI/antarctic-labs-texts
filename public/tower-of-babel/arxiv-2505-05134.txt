Abstract
Motivated by applications in reduced-order modeling (ROM) of parametric partial differential equations, we investigate the algebraic properties of Bochner matrices—matrices with entries in an abstract Hilbert space. Low-rank cross approximation is extended to Bochner matrices and its approximation guarantees are derived. The high-dimensional nature of the entries is shown to manifest itself in maximum-volume bounds, making them smaller than in the classical setting. An analogue of adaptive cross approximation is proposed and validated as a non-intrusive ROM method in numerical experiments with parametric nonlinear Stokes equations.

1.1
Reduced-order modeling of parametric PDEs

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open and bounded, and consider a Dirichlet boundary-value problem for the Poisson equation in
\Omega
with right-hand side
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and varying coefficient
\kappa\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\kappa\geq 1
almost everywhere:

-\mathrm{div}(\kappa\nabla u)=f~\text{ in }~\Omega,\qquad u=0~\text{ on }~\partial\Omega.

This problem admits a unique weak solution in the Sobolev space
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

[
7
, Ch. 9]
.
Suppose now that
f=f_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\kappa=\kappa_{\alpha,\beta}
depend on parameters
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
; then there is a well-defined
solution map

(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto u_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Upon introducing grids
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, we obtain an
m\times n
matrix with entries in
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

In reduced-order modeling (ROM), one often seeks to construct a data-driven surrogate model to approximate a quantity of interest (QoI) associated with the solution of a parametric PDE, allowing for rapid evaluation without querying the PDE solver.
This essentially reduces to approximating a function
[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
from samples, a problem with numerous established approaches.
When the QoI is discretized into a matrix,
cross approximation

[
20
,
4
]
provides a surrogate model by inspecting a small number of rows and columns and solving the PDE only a small number of times
[
1
]
.

Our central thesis is that cross approximation can be extended to matrices over a Hilbert space and used as a non-intrusive ROM method to approximate the solution map itself, rather than merely an associated scalar QoI.

1.3
Notation

We write vectors as
\mathsf{a,b}
and matrices as
\mathsf{A,B}
, denoting the
n\times n
identity matrix by
\mathsf{I}_{n}
.
The transpose is written as
\mathsf{A}^{\intercal}
, the complex conjugate as
\overline{\mathsf{A}}
, and the conjugate transpose as
\mathsf{A}^{\ast}
.
We let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
By convention, the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}
of a Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}
is linear in the second argument.

2
Bochner matrices

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}
over a field
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that an
n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}
array with entries in
\mathrm{H}
is a
Bochner tensor
of order
d
, which we refer to as a Bochner matrix when
d=2
and a Bochner vector when
d=1
.
Equivalently, a Bochner tensor is a map

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}:[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]\to\mathrm{H},\quad\mathfrak{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\mapsto\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})={\bm{a}}_{\mathfrak{i}}.

Tensors over the field
\mathbb{F}
will be referred to simply as “tensors,” or as “classical tensors” for additional contrast.

Under entrywise addition and scalar multiplication, the set
\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
of Bochner tensors is a vector space.
We endow it with the family of
\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})
norms:

\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})}}=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{i}\in[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]}\|{\bm{a}}_{\mathfrak{i}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{p}\right)^{1/p},&1\leq p<\infty,\\
\max_{\mathfrak{i}\in[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]}\|{\bm{a}}_{\mathfrak{i}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}},&p=\infty.\end{cases}

These norms are pairwise equivalent, satisfying the bounds

\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{q}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq(n_{1}\cdots n_{d})^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{q}(\mathrm{H})}},\quad p\leq q,

and satisfy Hölder’s inequality:

|\langle\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}|\leq\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})}}\|\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{p_{\ast}}(\mathrm{H})}},\quad p_{\ast}=\tfrac{p}{p-1},

where
\langle\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{i}}\langle{\bm{a}}_{\mathfrak{i}},{\bm{b}}_{\mathfrak{i}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}
is the inner product inducing the
\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})
norm.

A basic Cauchy-sequence argument shows that the vector space of Bochner tensors equipped with the
\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})
norm is complete, and so is itself a Hilbert space when
p=2
.
Let us remark in passing that Bochner tensors can be identified with functions from Bochner–Lebesgue spaces with respect to the discrete counting measure.

3
Bochner matrices as linear operators

An
m\times n
Bochner matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
can be multiplied from the right by classical vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
.
This induces a linear operator
\mathsf{x}\mapsto\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{x}
between Hilbert spaces
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and
\mathrm{H}^{m}
.
The operator norm of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is equivalent to the
\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})
norms, satisfying the bounds
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{1}(\mathrm{H})}}
, and thus the operator is bounded.
Furthermore, it has a finite rank not exceeding
n
, which we denote by
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
and that satisfies the following basic property.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
.
Then

\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{B})\leq\min\{\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B})\},\quad\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{B})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),&\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B})=n,\\
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B}),&\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=n.\end{cases}

Proof.

The inequality is standard.
The equalities follow directly from Sylvester’s rank inequality, which applies as the operator of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
has a finite-dimensional domain.

As a bounded operator,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
admits a bounded
adjoint

\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}:\mathrm{H}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
given by

\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,1),\bm{\mathsf{y}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}&\cdots&\langle\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,n),\bm{\mathsf{y}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.

We say that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
has
orthonormal columns
when its induced operator is a partial isometry, i.e.,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{I}_{n}
, where we adopt the convention that the adjoint
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}
can be applied to Bochner matrices columnwise.
Since the range of the operator induced by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is finite-dimensional (and hence closed), there exists a unique bounded
pseudoinverse

\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}:\mathrm{H}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
defined by the standard Moore–Penrose equations
[
6
, Ch. 9]
.

Since
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
corresponds to a finite-rank (and hence compact) operator, it admits a singular value decomposition (SVD) with
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
uniquely defined nonzero singular values
[
19
, Ch. 2]
.
We formulate this classical result in terms of Bochner matrices.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
be nonzero and
r=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
There exists a unique
\mathsf{\Sigma}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
with
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
, and there exist
\bm{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times r}
and
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times r}
with orthonormal columns such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
.
Furthermore, the pseudoinverse is given by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}=\mathsf{V}\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}^{\ast}
.

The optimality of the truncated SVD for low-rank approximation of compact operators is well-established in the context of symmetrically normed operator ideals
[
19
, Ch. 3, Lem. 6.1]
.
Finite-rank operators belong to all symmetrically normed operator ideals, and hence the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem holds for Bochner matrices.
A more direct proof of this result can be obtained via a reduction to classical matrices.
In the formulation of the theorem below, we set
\sigma_{j}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=0
for all
j>\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
.
Let
\phi
be a symmetric gauge function
1
1

1

To simplify submultiplicative norm bounds, we assume throughout the text our symmetric gauge functions to be normalized as
\phi(1,0,\ldots,0)=1
. For instance, such normalization is used in
[
19
]
.
inducing a unitarily invariant norm
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}=\phi(\sigma_{1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),\ldots,\sigma_{n}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}))
.
For every
1\leq k<\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, the best approximation of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
by a Bochner matrix of rank not exceeding
k
is achieved by the
k
-truncated SVD, denoted
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{A}}\rfloor_{k}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{k}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{k}\mathsf{V}_{k}^{\ast}
:

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{A}}\rfloor_{k}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}=\min\{\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{B}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}:\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})\leq k\}.

Clearly, the
\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})
norm and the operator
\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})
norms are unitarily invariant.
Let us also note that every such norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}
is equivalent to the
\ell_{p}(\mathrm{H})
norms, a property which we state without proof for brevity.

4
Bochner matrices as quasimatrices

A
quasimatrix

[
51
,
50
,
47
]
is a generalized matrix with columns that are functions in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,1)
.
Adopting a broader view, the columns can lie in an abstract Hilbert space, which allows us to treat a Bochner matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
as a quasimatrix with columns in
\mathrm{H}^{m}
.
Consequently,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
has exactly
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
linearly independent columns and admits an
interpolative decomposition
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
be nonzero and
r=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
There exists an index set
{J}\subseteq[n]
of cardinality
r
together with
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times n}
such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})\mathsf{B}
.

As the columns of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
belong to a Hilbert space, they can be orthogonalized via the Gram–Schmidt procedure, yielding a
thin
QR decomposition.
The QR decomposition also provides a practical way to compute the SVD of a Bochner matrix, since it reduces the original problem to computing the SVD of a classical triangular matrix; the
n\times n
Gram matrix of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
could also be used to approach the computation of the SVD.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
satisfy
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=n
.
Then there exists a unique
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
with orthonormal columns, and a unique upper triangular
\mathsf{R}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
with strictly positive diagonal entries, such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\mathsf{R}
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\neq 0
and
r=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})<n
.
There exist
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times r}
with orthonormal columns, an upper triangular
\mathsf{R}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times r}
with strictly positive diagonal entries,
\mathsf{R}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\times(n-r)}
, and an
n\times n
permutation matrix
\mathsf{\Pi}
such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{\Pi}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}[\mathsf{R}_{1}~\mathsf{R}_{2}]
.

Proof.

Apply
theorem

5
to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})
from
proposition

4
.

The columnwise structure of Bochner matrices permits their low-rank approximation via
approximate interpolative decompositions
as in
proposition

4
.
Given selected column indices
{J}
, the approximation can be obtained as
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\approx\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and
{J}\subseteq[n]
, and denote
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})
.
Then
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

Proof.

Follows from
lemma

1
and the fact that
\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}
is an orthogonal projection operator onto the column span of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
.

When the goal is to select a small number of columns, the
column subset selection
problem aims to identify the optimal ones and can be approached via rank-revealing QR decompositions or volume-based sampling
[
31
]
—relying only on the inner-product structure of the space of columns.

The
rows
of a quasimatrix remain undefined unless its columns possess additional structure, e.g., its column-functions having continuous representatives in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,1)

[
50
]
or lying in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
[
47
]
.
Bochner matrices exhibit a different enabling structure.

5
Bochner matrices as matrices

Finally, we acknowledge that Bochner matrices are two-dimensional arrays, and hence can be transposed.
However, the ranks of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
are, in general, distinct.
An example demonstrating this divergence is an
m\times n
Bochner matrix whose entries constitute an orthonormal system in
\mathrm{H}
: it has
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=n
and
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})=m
.
Therefore, we distinguish between the
column rank
and
row rank
of a Bochner matrix:

\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),\quad\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}).

All of the decompositions discussed so far apply equally well to transposed Bochner matrices, though it is important to keep in mind that the like decompositions of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
are not connected in any immediate way.
Another property of classical matrices that fails for Bochner matrices is the commutativity of transposition with taking the adjoint or pseudoinverse, because the latter are defined strictly as linear operators.

Since we now allow ourselves to access the rows, we can multiply Bochner matrices on the left by classical matrices.
While
lemma

1
describes the row rank of
\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
via transposition, its column rank needs to be treated separately.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times m}
.
If
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B})=m
then
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, otherwise
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

Proof.

Applying the rank-nullity theorem to the linear operator
\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathrm{H}^{k}
and representing its kernel as a direct sum
\ker(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\ker(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\oplus(\ker(\bm{\mathsf{A}})^{\perp}\cap\ker(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}))
, we obtain the following identity:

\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})-\dim(\ker(\bm{\mathsf{A}})^{\perp}\cap\ker(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})).

The rank inequality is its immediate consequence.
To prove the equality, it is sufficient to show that
\ker(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\ker(\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{z}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m}
satisfy
\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{z}}=0
.
For any
{\bm{h}}\in\mathrm{H}
, we have

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{1,j}{\bm{z}}_{j},{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\\
\vdots\\
\langle\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{k,j}{\bm{z}}_{j},{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\end{bmatrix}=\mathsf{B}\begin{bmatrix}\langle{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\\
\vdots\\
\langle{\bm{z}}_{m},{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\mathsf{B}
has full column rank by assumption,
\langle{\bm{z}}_{j},{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}=0
for each
j
.
But as
{\bm{h}}
is arbitrary, we conclude that
\bm{\mathsf{z}}=0
and the kernels coincide.

Furthermore, the access to rows provides us with a different way to construct the approximate interpolative decomposition, generalizing
proposition

7
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
,
{I}\subseteq[m]
, and
{J}\subseteq[n]
.
Denote
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})
,
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},:)
, and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},{J})
.
Then
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

Proof.

If
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
then
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
Conversely, we have
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
By
proposition

7
,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and thus
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
On the other hand,
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, so that
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
by
proposition

7
.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}(:,{K})\mathsf{B}
be an interpolative decomposition from
proposition

4
, and note that
\mathsf{B}
has full row rank.
We then have
\bm{\mathsf{G}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}(:,{K})\mathsf{B}
, and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}(:,{K})
has full column rank by
lemma

1
.
To finish the proof,

\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{B}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(:,{K})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(:,{K})\mathsf{B}\mathsf{B}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}(:,{K})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{B}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}(:,{K})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}.

The interpolative decomposition in
proposition

9
is written in exactly the same form as the skeleton decomposition of classical matrices
[
20
]
, i.e.,
\mathsf{A}(:,{J})\mathsf{A}({I},{J})^{\dagger}\mathsf{A}({I},:)
.
Despite this syntactic similarity, there are two crucial differences.
First, the classical skeleton decomposition is a product of three factors, whereas the decomposition in
proposition

9
fundamentally collapses into two factors because the pseudoinverse operator cannot stand alone as a purely algebraic middle factor.
Second, the skeleton decomposition of classical matrices is a CUR decomposition
[
23
]
; that is, every column is expressed as a linear combination of selected columns and every row is expressed as a linear combination of selected rows.
In contrast, the decomposition in
proposition

9
is interpolative only with respect to the columns and fails to leverage the row rank.

In fact, CUR decompositions in the sense described above are structurally impossible for Bochner matrices: they would contain two Bochner-matrix factors, yet those cannot be multiplied within our framework.
In this regard, consider a verbatim extension of the projection-based classical CUR decomposition
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{\dagger}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{R}^{\dagger}\mathsf{R}

[
33
]
, which we interpret not as a three-factor product with
\mathsf{C}^{\dagger}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{R}^{\dagger}
in the middle, but as the simultaneous application of column and row orthogonal projectors.
For Bochner matrices, we can write this down as
(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
or
\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
.
Consider a simple
2\times 2
example where the entries of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
form an orthonormal system in
\mathrm{H}
, and let
{I}={J}=\{1\}
.
Then the two projection-based CUR extensions evaluate to

(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(1,1)&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(1,2)\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(1,1)&0\\
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(2,1)&0\end{bmatrix}.

They do not agree, the column and row projections overriding each other, and neither is strictly a CUR decomposition.
Note also that the subspaces we selected to project on are dominant left singular subspaces of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
.

6
Bochner matrices as third-order tensors

As demonstrated, a CUR-style application of the one-sided interpolative decompositions from
propositions

7
and
9
does not yield a simultaneous reduction of both the column and row ranks.
Instead, we will extend classical
two-sided interpolative decompositions

[
54
]
into the Bochner setting.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
be nonzero with
\rho=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
,
\kappa=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
There exist index sets
{I}\subseteq[m]
of cardinality
\rho
and
{J}\subseteq[n]
of cardinality
\kappa
, together with
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times\rho}
and
\mathsf{C}\in\mathbb{F}^{\kappa\times n}
, such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},{J})\mathsf{C}
.

Proof.

Applying
proposition

4
to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
, we get
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},:)
.

The structure of the decomposition in
proposition

10
, having a Bochner matrix as its inner core and classical matrices as its outer factors, strictly mirrors the Tucker decomposition of classical tensors
[
2
]
.
Indeed, in the finite-dimensional special case of
\mathrm{H}=\mathbb{F}^{k}
this reduces to the classical Tucker2 decomposition; and in the general case, the space of Bochner matrices
\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
is isometrically isomorphic to the tensor-product Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{F}^{m}\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{F}^{n}

[
22
]
.
Motivated by these correspondences, we call every decomposition of the form
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\mathsf{D}
a
Tucker decomposition
and refer to it as a
cross decomposition
when its core
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
is a submatrix of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
Accordingly, we define the
Tucker rank
of a Bochner matrix as a tuple
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=(\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}))
.

The following theorem extends
proposition

9
to cross decompositions and connects them to the CUR-style application of one-sided interpolative decompositions.
This generalizes properties of classical matrices and tensors derived in
[
12
,
23
,
11
]
to Bochner matrices.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
,
{I}\subseteq[m]
, and
{J}\subseteq[n]
.
Denote
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,{J})
,
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},:)
, and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I},{J})
. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
and
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
;

2.

\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
;

3.

\bm{\mathsf{A}}=(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
;

4.

\bm{\mathsf{A}}=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}})
.

Proof.

\mathbf{(1\Rightarrow 2).}

proposition

7
gives
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, and thus
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
It follows that
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})
since
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
is a submatrix of
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
.
Similarly,
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})
, and there exists
{K}\subseteq[m]
together with
\mathsf{X}
of full column rank such that
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\mathsf{X}\bm{\mathsf{G}}({K},:)
.
By
lemma

8
, we get
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}({K},:))\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})
.
By analogy,
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

\mathbf{(2\Rightarrow 1).}

We have
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
as
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
is a submatrix of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
.
Similarly,
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

\mathbf{(1\Rightarrow 3).}

By
proposition

7
,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
.
Repeat the argument for
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
to get the second representation.

\mathbf{(3\Rightarrow 1).}

By
lemma

1
,
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, and similarly for
\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}
.

\mathbf{(2\Rightarrow 4).}

proposition

9
yields
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}=(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
, whence we obtain
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}
.

\mathbf{(4\Rightarrow 2).}

Note that
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}})=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}
by the definition of pseudoinverse.
Then
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
, and
proposition

9
gives
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
The same argument gives
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
when applied to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
.

The interpretation of Bochner matrices as third-order tensors paves the way for their low-rank approximation by means of
higher-order SVD
(HOSVD)
[
15
]
.
However, as the example at the end of
Section

5
shows, unlike the case of classical tensors, low-rank truncation for each unfolding (i.e.,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
) cannot be performed via column-space projection: the two projections do not commute and interfere with one another.
Instead, we must apply classical orthogonal projection matrices on the right, thereby projecting in the row space.
Such construction is perfectly aligned with the definition of mode unfoldings in abstract tensor-product Hilbert spaces
[
22
]
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
be nonzero, and let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\mathsf{\Lambda}\mathsf{Q}^{\ast}
be its SVDs.
For every pair of
1\leq\rho\leq\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
and
1\leq\kappa\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, denote by
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{A}}\rfloor_{\kappa}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}
and
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{\rho}\mathsf{\Lambda}_{\rho}\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}^{\ast}
the corresponding truncated SVDs.
Then

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\overline{\mathsf{Q}}_{\rho}\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\|\mathsf{\Sigma}-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}+\|\mathsf{\Lambda}-\mathsf{\Lambda}_{\rho}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\min\Big\{\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{B}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}:\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})\leq\rho,~\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})\leq\kappa\Big\}.

Proof.

The Pythagorean theorem gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\overline{\mathsf{Q}}_{\rho}\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|(\mathsf{I}-\overline{\mathsf{Q}}_{\rho}\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}^{\intercal})\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}\mathsf{Q}_{\rho}^{\ast})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathsf{\Sigma}-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}+\|\mathsf{\Lambda}-\mathsf{\Lambda}_{\rho}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}.

A standard corollary of the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem (
theorem

3
) is that the set of Bochner matrices with bounded Tucker rank is closed, and therefore the minimal distance is achieved at some
\bm{\mathsf{B}}_{\circ}
.
Invoking
theorem

3
again, we obtain

\|\mathsf{\Sigma}-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{B}}_{\circ}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2},\quad\|\mathsf{\Lambda}-\mathsf{\Lambda}_{\rho}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{B}}_{\circ}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}.

The proof is a direct adaptation of the HOSVD argument for classical tensors
[
15
]
, and it extends to tensors in abstract tensor-product Hilbert spaces because their mode unfoldings are Hilbert–Schmidt operators (even though
[
22
, Thm. 10.3]
is restricted to classical tensors).
A similar quasioptimality bound can be shown for the
sequentially truncated HOSVD
of Bochner matrices (see
[
53
]
and
[
22
, Thm. 10.5]
).

7
Cross approximation of Bochner matrices

In the preceding sections, we established that Bochner matrices can be approximated with low Tucker rank via the truncated HOSVD (
theorem

12
) and derived necessary and sufficient conditions for their cross decompositions to be exact (
theorem

11
).
However, our motivating application introduced in
section

1.1
relies on
approximate
cross decompositions, and therefore this section investigates the theoretical properties of
cross approximation
:

\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{B}},{I},{J})=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}),

where
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}(:,{J})
,
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}({I},{J})
,
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}({I},:)
are the selected submatrices.
We will drop the subscripts for the submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and use regular font for the submatrices of classical matrices.

7.1
Interpolation property

When the conditions of
theorem

11
are not met and cross approximation fails to recover the entire Bochner matrix, it still interpolates entries residing in the selected rows and columns.
The following theorem formally extends the interpolation property of classical cross approximation to Bochner matrices.
For instance, it shows that every cross approximant is a cross decomposition of itself.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and
\bm{\mathsf{X}}=\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})
.
Then

1.

\bm{\mathsf{X}}({I},{J})=\bm{\mathsf{G}}
and
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{X}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})
;

2.

\bm{\mathsf{X}}({I},:)=\bm{\mathsf{R}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})
;

3.

\bm{\mathsf{X}}(:,{J})=\bm{\mathsf{C}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})
;

4.

\bm{\mathsf{X}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

Proof.

\mathbf{1.}

By the defining Moore–Penrose equations of pseudoinverse:

\bm{\mathsf{X}}({I},{J})=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}})=((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}=(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}.

By
lemmas

1
and
8
,
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{X}})
is upper bounded by
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})
.
As
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
is a submatrix of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
, the reverse inequality holds as well.

\mathbf{2.}

By definition,
\bm{\mathsf{X}}({I},:)=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
.

proposition

7
guarantees that
\bm{\mathsf{X}}({I},:)=\bm{\mathsf{R}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}})
.

\mathbf{3.}

Apply the same argument to
\bm{\mathsf{X}}^{\intercal}
.

\mathbf{4.}
Follows from
theorem

11
.

7.2
Error bound for one-sided interpolation

As an intermediate step towards the error bound for Bochner cross approximation, we derive an error bound for the approximate one-sided interpolation (
proposition

9
).
The proof mirrors the argument used for cross approximation of classical perturbed low-rank matrices
[
24
]
.
We begin with a technical lemma.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
be nonzero and satisfy
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})
, and let
\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
be its SVD.
Then

\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}},\quad\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}=\|\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}.

Proof.

By definition,
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}({I},:)\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}({J},:)^{\ast}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}

\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
.
The rank assumption guarantees, via
lemma

1
, that
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}
has full column rank and
\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}
has full row rank.
Therefore,
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}
and thus

\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger},\quad\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}.

It remains to note that
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
and

.
To finish the proof, we need to bound the operator norms of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}
,
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
,
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}
,
\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
.
The first two bounds follow from
lemma

14
.
For the third bound, consider

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\big(1+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\big(1+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\big),

where we use
\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}
to simplify the overall bound.
Likewise,

\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}\leq\|\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}\big(1+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\big)+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}.

Combining all the intermediate bounds results in the final bound.

theorem

15
quantifies how well the approximate one-sided interpolation recovers an underlying Bochner matrix
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
with low column rank; an error bound for
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
itself follows by triangle inequality.
Notably, the low-rank assumption is not imposed explicitly; rather, we replace it with a rank-equality condition, essentially demanding that the selected index sets be sufficiently rich for the particular
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
.

The decomposition produced by
theorem

15
possesses an inner dimension
|{J}|
and satisfies a rank bound
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}})\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq|{J}|
.
For a fixed selection of indices, we can further reduce the column rank by truncating
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
to lower column rank prior to taking its pseudoinverse.
Recall that
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{k}
denotes the SVD of
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
truncated to
k
nonzero singular values.
Similarly, let us denote by
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}
the result of discarding all singular values below
\tau>0
; we distinguish between the two based on whether the subscript is an integer or a real number.
With
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}
used in place of
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
, the second-order error term can be controlled better, overcoming the stability issues due to the possible ill-conditioning of
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
—which is common when the indices are generously oversampled.
However, the interpolation properties break down once the pseudoinverse is regularized.

Corollary 16
.

Let
\tau>0
.
In the setting of
theorem

15
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{r}\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{r}\eta_{c}(2\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\tau}\Big(\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\big(\eta_{r}\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{r}\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\big)\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\Big).

Proof.

The proof of
theorem

15
extends almost verbatim.
The only step that requires special attention is
Equation

1
.
Expanding its right-hand side, we note that
\bm{\mathsf{G}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}=\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}^{\dagger}
and, subsequently, rely on
\bm{\mathsf{G}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}^{\dagger}
and
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\tau}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}
also being orthogonal projection operators due to the SVD truncation.

A similar corollary holds if we truncate the rank rather than apply thresholding to the singular values.
Below, we bound the norm of the pseudoinverse of the truncated
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
in terms of
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}
, separating their contributions.

Corollary 17
.

In the setting of
theorem

15
, let
1\leq k\leq\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}})
be such that
\gamma=\sigma_{k}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}})-\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}>0
.
Then

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{r}\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{r}\eta_{c}(2\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{k}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\big(\eta_{r}\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}+\eta_{r}\eta_{c}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\big)\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\phi}}\Big).

Proof.

By Weyl’s inequalities, which apply to Bochner matrices,
\sigma_{k}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\geq\gamma>0
, and thus the rank truncation
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{k}
is well-defined.
The proof remains the same, except for the bound
\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{k}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}=\sigma_{k}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})^{-1}\leq\gamma^{-1}
.

7.3
Well-conditioned submatrices

The approximation error bound in
theorem

15
is governed by the operator norms of the pseudoinverses of submatrices of singular factors.
The existence of well-conditioned submatrices of classical
n\times\kappa
matrices with orthonormal columns is a long-standing question.
It was conjectured in
[
20
]
that there always exists a
\kappa\times\kappa
submatrix for which the operator norm of its inverse is bounded by
\sqrt{n}
; see
[
42
,
45
]
for recent progress in this direction.

Maximum-volume
submatrices do not resolve this conjecture, but are nonetheless well-conditioned
[
20
,
14
,
37
,
43
]
.
The volume is defined as
\nu(\mathsf{A})=\sqrt{\det(\mathsf{A}^{\ast}\mathsf{A})}
, and if a
|{J}|\times\kappa
(
|{J}|\geq\kappa
) submatrix has the largest volume among all
|{J}|\times\kappa
submatrices then the operator norm of its pseudoinverse is upper bounded by

\sqrt{1+\frac{(n-|{J}|)\kappa}{|{J}|-\kappa+1}}.

(2)

We generalize this to Bochner matrices and define their volume as
\nu(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\sqrt{\det(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{A}})}
, stressing that
\nu(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal})\neq\nu(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
in general.
In the following statements, we also denote by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i}
the Bochner matrix with its
i
th row
\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i}^{\intercal}
removed.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{N\times\kappa}
have
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\kappa
.
Let
\mathsf{\Gamma}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}=(\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i}^{\intercal})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i}^{\intercal}
for every
1\leq i\leq N
.
Then

\frac{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})}{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})}=\det(\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}-\mathsf{S}_{i}),\quad\mathsf{S}_{i}=\mathsf{\Gamma}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}\mathsf{\Gamma}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}.

Let
\Delta_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\det(\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}-\mathsf{S}_{i})-(1-\mathrm{tr}(\mathsf{S}_{i}))
and
\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
Then

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})}{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})}=N-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}}).

Proof.

By assumption,
\mathsf{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{F}^{\kappa\times\kappa}
has full rank and
\nu(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\neq 0
; consequently, all
\mathsf{S}_{i}
are well-defined positive semidefinite matrices.
Furthermore,
\mathsf{\Gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{S}_{i}=\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}
.
We expand the definition of
\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})
to prove the first equality:

\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})=\det(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})=\det(\mathsf{\Gamma}-\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i})=\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\det(\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}-\mathsf{S}_{i}).

To obtain the second equality, observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-\mathrm{tr}(\mathsf{S}_{i}))=N-\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{S}_{i}\right)=N-\mathrm{tr}(\mathsf{I}_{\kappa})=N-\kappa.

We call
\Delta_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
the
intra-row rank excess
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
its
inter-row rank excess
.
Since the matrices
\mathsf{S}_{i}
are positive semidefinite and satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{S}_{i}=\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}
, their eigenvalues lie in
[0,1]
.
Thus, by the Weierstrass product inequality, we have
\Delta_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\geq 0
; moreover,
\Delta_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
vanishes if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i})\leq 1
.
Based on the SVD of
\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i}^{\intercal}
, we can establish a simple yet fundamental relationship:

\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i}^{\intercal}).

Therefore, the inter-row rank excess is always zero for classical matrices.

Lemma 19
.

In the setting of
lemma

18
, let
i_{\ast}
be such that
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}})=\kappa
, and denote
\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}
.
Then

\frac{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})}{\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}})}=\det\Big(\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}+\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i_{\ast}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\Big)\geq 1+\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}.

Proof.

The equality follows from the definition of volume.
To prove the inequality, consider vectors
\mathsf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{\kappa}
of unit
\ell_{2}
norm and let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\mathsf{\Lambda}\mathsf{Q}^{\ast}
be an SVD.
Then

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i_{\ast}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\Big\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathsf{x}}\Big|\mathsf{x}^{\ast}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i_{\ast}}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{x}\Big|=\max_{\mathsf{x}}\Big\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathsf{x}\Big\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}=\Big\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{\Gamma}_{-i_{\ast}}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\Big\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{-1}\Big\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}=\Big\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{\Lambda}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\ast}\Big\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}=\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2},

where we use the unitary invariance of operator norms.
The inequality follows since the remaining eigenvalues are nonnegative.

Combining
lemmas

18
and
19
, we can bound the volume of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
as

\Big(1+\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\Big)\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}})\leq\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\frac{N}{N-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}})}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i})\bigg).

Suppose that the squared volume of the submatrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}
is at least the average squared volume across all
(N-1)\times\kappa
submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
Then this volume bound yields

\|\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{i_{\ast}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{-i_{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa-\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}})}{N-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}})},

(3)

which extends the classical maximum-volume least-squares bound to Bochner matrices and relies on a relaxed above-average volume condition.
We are finally ready to derive an operator-norm bound for the pseudoinverse of a maximum-volume submatrix.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times\kappa}
have orthonormal columns.
Let indices
{I}\subseteq[m]
be such that
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}
has the largest volume among all
|{I}|\times\kappa
submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
.
If
\nu(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}})>0
,

\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\leq\sqrt{1+\sum_{i\not\in{I}}\frac{\kappa-\Delta(\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}_{i})}{|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta(\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}_{i})}},\quad\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Using the standard properties of the operator norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}=\|(\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})+\sum_{i\not\in{I}}(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})\bigg\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}+\sum_{i\not\in{I}}(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})\bigg\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{i\not\in{I}}\|(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i\not\in{I}}\|\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\mathrm{H}}}^{2},

since
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}
is an orthogonal projector.
We then bound each term in the sum using
eq.

3
; this is allowed because the positive volume of
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}
implies
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}})=\kappa
.

For classical matrices,
theorem

20
recovers the standard well-conditioning bound for maximum-volume submatrices.
Meanwhile, the presence of inter-row rank excesses in the bound shows that maximum-volume submatrices are
even better conditioned
in the Bochner setting; indeed, the right-hand side in
eq.

3
is a monotonically decreasing function of the inter-row rank excess.

To illustrate the conditioning gains, consider
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times\kappa}
whose entries are mutually orthonormal in
\mathrm{H}
and set
\bm{\mathsf{U}}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}/\sqrt{m}
.
Due to symmetry, all
|{I}|\times\kappa
submatrices have the same volume, and it holds for every row-augmentation in
theorem

20
that

\Delta(\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}_{i})=(|{I}|+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{|{I}|+1}\right)^{\kappa}-(|{I}|+1-\kappa),

which we can substitute into the bound, simplifying to

\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\leq\sqrt{1+(m-|{I}|)\Big[\Big(1+\frac{1}{|{I}|}\Big)^{\kappa}-1\Big]}<\sqrt{1+(m-|{I}|)\big(e^{\kappa/|{I}|}-1\big)}.

It is instructive to compare this bound with the bound for classical maximum-volume submatrices
eq.

2
; see
table

1
.
When a square submatrix is selected, classical matrices enjoy the bound
\sqrt{1+m\kappa}
, whereas the bound for
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
is
\sqrt{1+(e-1)m}
and does not grow with
\kappa
.
A standard way to regularize classical submatrices is to sample twice as many rows as there are columns, improving the bound to
\sqrt{1+m}
.
For the specific
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
, such oversampling leads to an even smaller bound
\sqrt{1+(e^{1/2}-1)m}
.
Unlike classical matrices, the rows of Bochner matrices can be
undersampled
, while still delivering well-conditioned submatrices.
For example, the selection of
\kappa/\log(\kappa)
rows results in a bound that is similar to the case of classical square submatrices.

Let us note that
theorem

holds for any
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}
that has the largest volume among all submatrices that disagree with it in exactly one row.
Relaxed further, it is sufficient for

.

7.4
Entrywise error bound for one-sided interpolation

Another important class of approximation guarantees for classical cross approximation are entrywise error bounds for when the submatrix at the intersection of the selected columns and rows itself exhibits maximum-volume properties.
Following
[
43
]
, we define the
projective volume
of a Bochner matrix as
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\prod_{s=1}^{\kappa}\sigma_{s}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
and truncate the rank of the pseudoinverse for regularization.
We also introduce a
projective inter-row rank excess

\Delta_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})=\sup_{\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa}}\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})
, where
\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa}
are (non-unique) truncated right singular factors of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
This quantity is well-defined when
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\geq\kappa
.

Theorem 21
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
have the maximum
\kappa
-projective volume among all
|{I}|\times|{J}|
submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
If
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})>0
,

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa-\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}{|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})

with
\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}=\min_{i\not\in{I}}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\Delta_{\kappa}\big(\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I}\cup\{i\},{J}\cup\{j\})\big)
.

Proof.

For fixed indices
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
, denote

{\bm{a}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j),\quad\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}(i,:),\quad\bm{\mathsf{r}}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}(:,j),\quad{\bm{e}}=\bm{\mathsf{a}}-\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}.

Our goal is to bound
\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}
depending on whether
i
and
j
fall into the index sets
{I}
and
{J}
, respectively.
We shall assume that
{\bm{e}}\neq 0
, for otherwise the bounds are trivial.

First, let
i\in{I}
and
j\in{J}
.
Then

\displaystyle\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}=\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})\leq\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}),

where we use standard properties of the pseudoinverse and singular values.

Second, let
i\not\in{I}
and
j\not\in{J}
.
Construct an augmented submatrix

\bm{\mathsf{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{G}}&\bm{\mathsf{r}}\\
\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}&\bm{\mathsf{a}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{H}^{(|{I}|+1)\times(|{J}|+1)}

and let
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}
be a truncated SVD.
Consider an auxiliary matrix

\mathsf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}&-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}^{(|{J}|+1)\times(\kappa+1)}

and multiply
\bm{\mathsf{M}}
with it on the right:

\bm{\mathsf{K}}=\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}&\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\\
\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}&{\bm{e}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{H}^{(|{I}|+1)\times(\kappa+1)},\quad\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}=\bm{\mathsf{r}}-\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\in\mathrm{H}^{|{I}|}.

By the definition of volume, we get

\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{K}})=\det(\bm{\mathsf{K}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{K}})=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2}+(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}&(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}{\bm{e}}\\
{\bm{e}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}&\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right).

Since
{\bm{e}}\neq 0
by our assumption, we can invoke Schur’s complement:

\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{K}})=(\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2})\det\bigg(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2}+(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-\frac{(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}}{\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}}\bigg).

The matrix

(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-\frac{(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}}{\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}}=(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})

is a Gram matrix and hence PSD; therefore,

(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-\frac{(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}}{\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}}\succcurlyeq(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}-\frac{(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa})^{\ast}{\bm{e}}{\bm{e}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}}{\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}}\succcurlyeq 0,

and the monotonicity of determinant for PSD matrices implies

\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{K}})\geq(\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}+\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2})\det(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2})\geq\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\det(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2}).

Let us relate the volumes of
\bm{\mathsf{K}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{M}}
.
Using Schur’s complement, we can show that the auxiliary matrix
\mathsf{T}
satisfies

\nu_{\kappa+1}^{2}(\mathsf{T})=\nu^{2}(\mathsf{T})=\det(\mathsf{T}^{\ast}\mathsf{T})=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}&-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\\
(-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}})^{\ast}&1+\|\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=1.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\mathsf{\Lambda}\mathsf{Q}^{\ast}
be an SVD, then by the log-majorization of singular values of a matrix product
[
26
, Thm. 3.3.14]
we obtain

\nu(\bm{\mathsf{K}})=\nu(\bm{\mathsf{P}}\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{T})=\nu(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{T})=\nu_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{T})\leq\nu_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}})\nu_{\kappa+1}(\mathsf{T})=\nu_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}),

and therefore

\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\leq\frac{\nu(\bm{\mathsf{K}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}\leq\frac{\nu_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}=\frac{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})\leq\frac{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}).

To bound the volume ratio, we shall expand
\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})
into a sum of squared volumes of submatrices with removed columns and rows based on the fundamental identities

\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})=\nu(\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}})=\nu(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})=\max_{\bm{\mathsf{\tilde{P}}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{\tilde{P}}}=\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}}\nu(\bm{\mathsf{\tilde{P}}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}})=\max_{\mathsf{\tilde{Q}}^{\ast}\mathsf{\tilde{Q}}=\mathsf{I}_{\kappa}}\nu(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{\tilde{Q}}).

Denote by
\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}
the submatrix with
s
th row removed, by
\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{:,-t}
the submatrix with
t
th column removed, and by
\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s,-t}
the submatrix with both
s
th row and
t
th column removed.
Applying
lemma

18
to
\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa}
, which is allowed since
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})>0
, we obtain

\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})=\Big(|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})\Big)\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}).

Next, we bound each term via
\nu(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})\leq\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})
.
If
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})=0
then we move on; otherwise, let
\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{s,\kappa}
be the truncated left singular factor of
\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}
.
Then we use the equality
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})=\nu(\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{s,\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})
and apply
lemma

18
to
(\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{s,\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})^{\intercal}
.
Because this is a classical matrix, its inter-row excess is zero, and hence

\sum_{t=1}^{|{J}|+1}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{s,\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s,-t})=\Big(|{J}|+1-\kappa\Big)\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}).

Combining the two sums yields

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\sum_{t=1}^{|{J}|+1}\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s,-t})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\sum_{t=1}^{|{J}|+1}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}_{s,\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s,-t})=\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\Big(|{J}|+1-\kappa\Big)\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})

\displaystyle\geq\Big(|{J}|+1-\kappa\Big)\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})

\displaystyle=\Big(|{J}|+1-\kappa\Big)\Big(|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})\Big)\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}),

and it remains to recall that
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s,-t})\leq\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})
and maximize over the SVDs of
\bm{\mathsf{M}}
.

Third, let
i\in{I}
and
j\not\in{J}
.
Then we augment as
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=[\bm{\mathsf{G}}~\bm{\mathsf{r}}]
and use the same auxiliary matrix
\mathsf{T}
to form
\bm{\mathsf{K}}
.
We can then readily show that

\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{K}})=\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}\det(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2})\geq\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\det(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2}),

since
{\bm{e}}
is now a component of
\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\perp}
, proceed to the same bound
\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\leq\tfrac{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, and skip the row-removal step in volume expansions to get

\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}).

Fourth, let
i\not\in{I}
and
j\in{J}
.
Then we augment as
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=[\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}~\bm{\mathsf{c}}]^{\intercal}\in\mathrm{H}^{(|{I}|+1)\times|{J}|}
and introduce an auxiliary matrix
\mathsf{T}=[\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}~\mathsf{e}_{\omega}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}]\in\mathbb{F}^{|{J}|\times(\kappa+1)}
, where
\omega
is the local position of
j
within
{J}
.
This modification of
\mathsf{T}
ensures that
\bm{\mathsf{K}}
has exactly the same block structure as in the second considered case.
An identical argument yields the same bound
\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}\leq\tfrac{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, where now

\det(\mathsf{T}^{\ast}\mathsf{T})=\|\mathsf{e}_{\omega}-\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\mathsf{e}_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}\leq 1.

Applying
lemma

18
we obtain

\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})\geq\sum_{s=1}^{|{I}|+1}\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})=\Big(|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta(\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{Q}_{\kappa})\Big)\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})

and can directly bound
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s})\leq\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})
since
\bm{\mathsf{M}}_{-s}
is of size
|{I}|\times|{J}|
.

A standard remark is in order that
Theorem

21
holds when the projective volume of
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
is maximum only among the submatrices that differ from it in at most one row and at most one column.

7.5
Error bounds for cross approximation

The preparatory work on the approximation guarantees for one-sided interpolation can now be streamlined to derive error bounds for cross approximation.
We begin with a bound in the
\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})
norm, the only unitarily invariant norm that is also invariant to transposition.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}+\bm{\mathsf{E}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
and suppose that

\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\epsilon,\quad\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\delta.

Then

\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\epsilon+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}(\delta+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}})

Proof.

We expand the error by triangle inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}-\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}-\big((\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\big)^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\big(\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\big)\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\big(\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\big)\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}.

The following theorem builds upon
theorem

15
, and we simplify its error bound for the sake of presentation.
The proof relies on the sequential processing of a Bochner matrix and its transpose, so a symmetric bound holds too.
The effects induced by the regularization of two pseudoinverses can be incorporated in the cross-approximation bound by means of
corollaries

16
and
17
, though we omit such extension for brevity.

Theorem 23
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{B}}+\bm{\mathsf{E}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
with
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\neq 0
, and let
\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\mathsf{\Lambda}\mathsf{Q}^{\ast}
be SVDs.
Denote

\displaystyle\eta_{r}=\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}},\quad\eta_{c}=\|\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{V}^{\ast}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}},\quad\xi_{r}=\|\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{Q}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}},\quad\xi_{c}=\|(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{\intercal}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}=\eta_{r}+\eta_{c}+3\eta_{r}\eta_{c},\quad\tilde{\xi}=\xi_{c}+\xi_{r}+3\xi_{c}\xi_{r}.

Let
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{\bm{\mathsf{B}}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})
, then

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\eta}(2+\tilde{\xi})\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle+(1+\tilde{\eta})(2+\tilde{\xi})\Big(\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}+\|(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}\Big)\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}.

Proof.

Coarsening of the bound in
theorem

15
gives

\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\tilde{\eta}\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}(1+\tilde{\eta})\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}.

By the same token, we bound

\|\bm{\mathsf{B}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\tilde{\xi}\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}(1+\tilde{\xi})\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}^{2}.

To finalize the proof via
lemma

22
, we need a bound on
\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}
, which we take from the end of the proof of
theorem

15
:

\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}\leq\eta_{c}+\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})\to\ell_{2}}}(1+\eta_{c})\|\bm{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}.

We substitute the bounds in
lemma

22
and massage the expression using
\eta_{c}\leq\tilde{\eta}
.

To extend the maximum-volume bound from
theorem

21
to cross approximation, we need to assume that the projective volumes of both
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}
are simultaneously maximal across the submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}
, respectively.
However, this is a stringent assumption, because submatrices maximizing both volumes at the same time do not exist for Bochner matrices in general.
Therefore, we rely instead on a
quasi-maximum volume
assumption and require that
\theta\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})
be larger than or equal to the maximum projective volume for
\theta\geq 1
.

Theorem 24
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
.
Let
\rho,\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta_{c},\theta_{r}\geq 1
.
Suppose
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
has the
\theta_{c}
-quasi-maximum
\kappa
-projective volume among all
|{I}|\times|{J}|
submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}
has the
\theta_{r}
-quasi-maximum
\rho
-projective volume among all
|{J}|\times|{I}|
submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}
.
Denote
\bm{\mathsf{X}}=(\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}}(\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}})
.
If
\nu_{\kappa}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})>0
and
\nu_{\rho}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})>0
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{c}\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa-\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}{|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})

\displaystyle+\theta_{r}\theta_{c}\sqrt{1+\frac{\rho}{|{I}|+1-\rho}}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\theta_{c}^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}\sigma_{\rho+1}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}).

with
\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}=\min_{i\not\in{I}}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\Delta_{\kappa}\big(\bm{\mathsf{A}}({I}\cup\{i\},{J}\cup\{j\})\big)
.

Proof.

By triangle inequality, we have

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}+\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}.

We bound the first term via a quasi-maximum-volume variant of
theorem

21
, the proof of which is a direct modification of the original proof:

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\theta_{c}\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa-\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}{|{I}|+1-\kappa+\Delta_{\kappa}^{\prime}}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}\sigma_{\kappa+1}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}).

Suppose that the maximum error in the second term is attained at the
(i,j)
th entry and denote
\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}(i,:)
and
\bm{\mathsf{r}}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}(:,j)
as previously:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{C}}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\|(\bm{\mathsf{C}}-(\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathsf{G}})\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{\infty}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\|(\bm{\mathsf{c}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}})^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{c}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}.

As in the proof of
theorem

21
, we consider different cases based on the position of
(i,j)
relative to
({I},{J})
.
When
i\in{I}
and
\bm{\mathsf{c}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\mathsf{e}_{\omega}
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{c}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle=\|(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})\mathsf{e}_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}-\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\sigma_{\rho+1}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})\leq\sigma_{\rho+1}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}).

When
i\not\in{I}
, we repeat the argument from the third part of the proof of
theorem

21
.
Namely, we form an augmentation
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=[\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}~\bm{\mathsf{c}}]
, take an auxiliary matrix
\mathsf{T}
, multiply
\bm{\mathsf{K}}=\bm{\mathsf{M}}\mathsf{T}
, obtain an equality

\nu(\bm{\mathsf{K}})=\nu_{\rho}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})\|\bm{\mathsf{c}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}},

and derive the bound

\|\bm{\mathsf{c}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}\rfloor_{\rho}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{c}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\frac{\nu(\bm{\mathsf{K}})}{\nu_{\rho}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})}\leq\frac{\nu_{\rho+1}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\rho}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal})}\leq\theta_{r}\sqrt{1+\frac{\rho}{|{I}|+1-\rho}}\sigma_{\rho+1}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}).

When
j\in{J}
and
\bm{\mathsf{r}}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\mathsf{e}_{\omega}
, we have

\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}=\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\mathsf{e}_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}\leq\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}}}\leq 1.

When
j\not\in{J}
, consider
\bm{\mathsf{M}}=[\bm{\mathsf{G}}~\bm{\mathsf{r}}]
, and let
\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}\mathsf{V}_{\kappa}^{\ast}
be an SVD.
Then, by the matrix determinant lemma,

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})\geq\nu^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{M}})

\displaystyle=\det\Big(\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{2}+(\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}})(\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}})^{\ast}\Big)

\displaystyle=\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})(1+\|\mathsf{\Sigma}_{\kappa}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{\kappa}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2})=\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})(1+\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}),

and consequently

\|\lfloor\bm{\mathsf{G}}\rfloor_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{M}})}{\nu_{\kappa}^{2}(\bm{\mathsf{G}})}-1}\leq\sqrt{\theta_{c}^{2}+\frac{\theta_{c}^{2}\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}-1}=\theta_{c}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\theta_{c}^{2}}+\frac{\kappa}{|{J}|+1-\kappa}}.

The bound in
theorem

24
is asymmetric, and a similar one holds with the role of rows and columns reversed.
In addition, the statement still holds when
\bm{\mathsf{G}}
optimizes the volume among submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
that differ from it in at most one row and at most one column, and
\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\intercal}
optimizes the volume among submatrices of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\intercal}
that differ from it in at most one column.

8
Numerical experiments

8.1
Adaptive Bochner cross approximation

For classical matrices,
adaptive cross approximation
(ACA,
[
4
,
5
]
) is a widely adopted approach to index selection for cross approximation, whereby columns and rows are picked one by one.
At its every iteration, the position of an entry of large absolute value is chosen in the current residual.
In full pivoting, the largest entry in the whole residual is selected, but then every entry needs to be inspected.
In practice,
rook pivoting
delivers a good balance between stability and efficiency and is de facto the standard way to select indices in ACA
[
17
,
46
]
.

Algorithm

1
is a direct extension of rook pivoting to Bochner matrices.

Algorithm 1

Rook pivoting for Bochner matrices

Input:

\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
, starting column index
j_{*}\in[n]
, number of rounds
n_{\mathrm{rook}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

1

i_{*}\leftarrow\mathrm{argmax}_{i\in[m]}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j_{*})\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}

2

for

s=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{rook}}

do

3

j_{*}\leftarrow\mathrm{argmax}_{j\in[n]}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{*},j)\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}

4

i_{*}\leftarrow\mathrm{argmax}_{i\in[m]}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j_{*})\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}

Output:

pivot indices
(i_{*},j_{*})

The ACA algorithm maintains a decomposition
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}+\mathsf{E}
, starting with
\mathsf{B}=0
and increasing its rank by one at every iteration by adding a rank-one cross approximation of
\mathsf{E}
to it.
A fundamental property of ACA is additivity; that is, if
\mathsf{B}=\mathrm{cross}(\mathsf{A},{I},{J})
with invertible submatrix
\mathsf{A}({I},{J})
and
\mathsf{E}(i,j)\neq 0
then

\mathsf{B}+\mathrm{cross}(\mathsf{E},i,j)=\mathrm{cross}(\mathsf{A},{I}\cup\{i\},{J}\cup\{j\}).

Therefore, ACA iteratively builds up cross approximation based on rank-one updates.

However, this property ceases to hold for Bochner matrices.
Recall the example from
section

5
of a
2\times 2
Bochner matrix whose entries are mutually orthonormal:

\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},1,1)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(1,1)&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathsf{B}}+\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{E}},2,2)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(1,1)&0\\
0&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(2,2)\end{bmatrix},

whereas
\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},\{1,2\},\{1,2\})=\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
Rank-one updates do not add up to a higher-rank cross approximation, requiring a proper recomputation.
We take this into account in our
adaptive Bochner cross
(ABC) approximation algorithm (
Algorithm

2
).

Algorithm 2

Adaptive Bochner cross approximation

Input:

\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
, number of iterations
n_{\mathrm{iter}}\in\mathbb{N}
, number of sub-iterations
n_{\mathrm{sub}}\in\mathbb{N}
, number of rook-pivoting rounds
n_{\mathrm{rook}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

1

{I}\leftarrow\varnothing,~{J}\leftarrow\varnothing,~\bm{\mathsf{B}}\leftarrow 0

2

for

k=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{iter}}

do

3

\bm{\mathsf{E}}\leftarrow\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{B}}

/*
actual residual */

4

for

s=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{sub}}

do

5

j_{*}\leftarrow\mathrm{random}([n])

6

(i_{*},j_{*})\leftarrow\textup{{rook}}(\bm{\mathsf{E}},j_{*},n_{\mathrm{rook}})

7

if

\bm{\mathsf{E}}(i_{*},j_{*})\neq 0

then

8

{I}\leftarrow{I}\cup\{i_{*}\},~{J}\leftarrow{J}\cup\{j_{*}\}

9

\bm{\mathsf{\Delta}}\leftarrow\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{E}},i_{*},j_{*})

/*
rank-one correction */

10

\bm{\mathsf{E}}\leftarrow\bm{\mathsf{E}}-\bm{\mathsf{\Delta}}

/*
approximate residual */

11

\bm{\mathsf{B}}\leftarrow\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})

Output:

indices
({I},{J})
, cross approximation
\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},{I},{J})

Note the presence of the inner loop, in which the residual undergoes fast approximate rank-one corrections.
When
n_{\mathrm{sub}}=1
, the ABC algorithm extends an ACA-like algorithm developed in
[
12
]
from classical third-order tensors to Bochner matrices (neglecting one mode).
With
n_{\mathrm{sub}}>1
, we attempt to garner more indices at reduced computational cost, making the algorithm more flexible.

Unlike classical ACA, the ABC algorithm can select the same indices multiple times.
This follows from the weaker interpolation properties of Bochner cross approximation (
Theorem

13
), rooted in the difference between column and row ranks.

8.2
Reduced-order modeling: Nonlinear Stokes equations

To validate the ABC algorithm as a non-intrusive ROM method for parametric PDEs, we consider the two-dimensional Stokes equations in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which describe the stationary flow of a fluid with dominating viscous forces.
Let
\mathsf{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the velocity and
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be the pressure.
When there are no external forces, the Stokes equations read

-\mathrm{div}(2\nu\epsilon(\mathsf{u})-p\mathsf{I}_{2})=0,~\mathrm{div}(\mathsf{u})=0~\text{ in }~\Omega,

(4)

where
\epsilon(\mathsf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\mathsf{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathsf{u})^{\intercal}
and
\nu
is the viscosity, which we model as

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathsf{u})=\nu_{0}+\alpha\|\nabla\mathsf{u}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{\beta},

making the equations nonlinear and parametric.
We partition the boundary as
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega_{D}\cup\partial\Omega_{N}
and impose the following boundary conditions:

\mathsf{u}=0~\text{ on }~\partial\Omega_{D},\qquad-(2\nu\epsilon(\mathsf{u})-p\mathsf{I}_{2})\mathsf{n}=p_{0}\mathsf{n}~\text{ on }~\partial\Omega_{N},

(5)

where
\mathsf{n}:\partial\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the outward normal and
p_{0}:\partial\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
.

This boundary-value problem is a standard example used to illustrate the FEniCS
3

https://fenicsproject.org/
library.
We take the domain, mesh, and boundary conditions from
[
44
]
to make the problem
eqs.

4
and
5
concrete and use FEniCS to solve it using Taylor–Hood finite elements with 6495 degrees of freedom.

\nu_{0}=0.01
and
\alpha=\beta=0
are shown in

9
Remarks

Unrestricted Hilbert space.

The developed framework of Bochner matrices and their cross approximation does not impose any constraints on the underlying Hilbert space, relying exclusively on its inner-product structure.
In contrast, the framework of quasimatrices
[
50
,
47
]
and quasitensors
[
32
,
25
]
requires a reproducing kernel Hilbert space or assumes that the entries are continuous functions.
Similarly, the infinite-dimensional extension of tubal tensors in
[
40
]
requires separability.

Structured sampling.

The formalism of Bochner matrices and tensors provides an abstraction for data models where the naturally accessible unit is a fiber or slice, rather than a single entry of a classical tensor.
Tensor completion with such structured sampling was studied in
[
49
]
and applied to tensorial parametric ROM of dynamical systems in
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
.

Cross approximation of Bochner tensors.

The present work establishes the theoretical foundation of cross approximation for Bochner matrices.
In a follow-up paper
[
9
]
, we extend this framework to the Tucker-cross approximation of Bochner tensors, focusing primarily on its applications to ROM of parametric PDEs.
