Linearly dispersing carriers in atomically thin NdTe3
Authors: Oleksandr Zheliuk, Yuliia Kreminska, Davide Pizzirani, Claudius S A Müller, Pim van den Berg, Sardar Hameed, Puhua Wan, Xiaoli Peng, Shiming Lei, Sergii Grytsiuk, Leslie M Schoop, Malte Rösner, Uli Zeitler, Steffen Wiedmann, Jianting Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for materials supporting ultrafast charge transport has long driven the development of nanoelectronics. Since the isolation of graphene, numerous efforts have focused on expanding the class of linearly dispersive layered materials while scaling their electronic properties to the unit-cell limit. Here, we demonstrate that atomically thin layers of antiferromagnetic NdTe3 can be isolated onto Au films, down to a single unit cell, through enhanced adhesion. Exfoliation using discontinuous Au films furthermore enables direct electrical transport measurements. Magnetotransport reveals the persistence of Shubnikov-de Haas oscillations in atomically thin samples. Despite suppressed residual resistivity ratio and magnetoresistance relative to bulk crystals, we observe no significant modifications of the electronic structure, with linear dispersive bands persisting across all thicknesses. Multiple spin-zero effects from distinct Fermi pockets further indicate an enhanced effective g-factor that may extend to other members of the RTe3 family.Subject terms: Nanoscale devices, Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

The isolation of graphene1 marked a major milestone in the study of linearly dispersive electronic systems. Their exceptional transport properties originate from the underlying energy-momentum relationship in reciprocal space, which governs the electronic behavior of crystalline solids2. Materials with linearly dispersive electronic bands are theoretically favorable for high-speed nanoelectronic applications3. In such systems, electrons behave as quasiparticles with high group velocity and vanishing or extremely low effective mass. These features facilitate ultrafast carrier response under applied fields and, when combined with symmetry-protected transport channels (e.g., pseudospin or chirality), can also suppress backscattering and enhance carrier mobility.

Linear dispersions are not unique to a single material class or specific to a certain dimensionality. In fact, linear dispersions can arise in several qualitative ways. Firstly, in materials where the dispersion only appears linear over a certain energy range, such as narrow-gap semiconductors (InAs, HgCdTe)4,5, or in heavy fermion systems where hybridization between localized f-electrons and conduction states results in light bands with near-linear character, alongside extremely heavy quasiparticles. Even quasi-one-dimensional systems can host linear subbands arising from geometrical confinement6. In these cases, quasiparticles remain largely Schrödinger fermions, albeit strongly renormalized. Secondly, systems where symmetry protection enforces linear crossings, including graphene, topological insulators, Dirac and Weyl semimetals (e.g., Cd3As2, TaAs)7,8. Here, quasiparticles near the crossing have massless dispersion and are protected against gap opening by time-reversal, inversion, or sublattice symmetries. However, practical transport performance is often limited by extrinsic factors, including disorder, environmental sensitivity, and substrate coupling. When realized in two-dimensional (2D) form, linear dispersions offer additional benefits allowing for reduced Coulomb screening, gate tunability, quantum confinement, and the potential for novel correlation effects. These advantages are exemplified by graphene, where the Dirac band structure leads to ultrahigh mobility and field-effect performance9,10. Motivated by these perspectives, significant attention has been devoted to identifying new 2D materials with Dirac- or Weyl-like linear dispersions.

Recently, these efforts have been extended to materials with magnetic or correlated ground states. Among promising candidates are rare-earth tri-tellurides RTe3 (R = rare earth elements), a family of layered compounds that host a rich variety of electronic phases. In their bulk form, RTe3 materials exhibit highly mobile charge carriers confined to Te planes11, antiferromagnetic ordering at low temperatures12,13, multiple charge–density wave (CDW) transitions14,15, and superconductivity under pressure16. These features make them attractive for exploring the interplay between collective order and low-dimensional electron dynamics. While these properties have been identified in bulk RTe3 crystals, it remains an open question whether the electronic structure can be preserved in the atomically thin limit. In reduced dimensions, electronic states become highly sensitive to interlayer coupling, surface effects, and disorder – factors that can significantly alter the electronic structure. However, isolating ultrathin RTe3 layers has remained a challenge due to their strong interlayer binding energy, surpassing that of typical van der Waals (vdW) materials17, as well as their sensitivity to ambient conditions11. Overcoming these challenges is essential for accessing the intrinsic low-dimensional electronic properties of this system.

Results and discussion

Au-enhanced exfoliation and layer-dependent transport in NdTe3

Dimensional reduction is a well-established strategy for engineering new physical phenomena, as exemplified by the emergence of massless Dirac fermions in graphene1,9,10, band structure reconstruction in layered semiconductors18–21, enhanced correlation effects, and the formation of moiré superlattices22–24. In many-body systems, reduced dimensionality can significantly influence emergent properties, such as the critical temperature and magnetic field of superconductors25–29, the ordering temperature in magnetic materials30–32, and the degree and dynamics of Coulomb screening33–35.

The realization of 2D materials has been largely enabled by mechanical exfoliation of layered vdW crystals1. This process relies on the weak interlayer interactions and the air stability of the parent materials. However, this advantage turns into a limitation when applied to metallic layered compounds, where strong interlayer interaction impedes thin film isolation, and the metallic nature often leads to rapid degradation in ambient conditions. For instance, exfoliated thin films from superconducting NbSe2, TaS2, and FeSe (refs. 25–27,29) exhibit reduced critical temperatures and fields, typically due to defect formation during exfoliation. Similarly, in semimetallic WTe2 (refs. 19,36), the quantum oscillation vanishes, and the material becomes insulating below a few layers, while in layered ferromagnets such as Fe3GeTe2 (refs. 31), the Curie temperature drops sharply below 10 layers, accompanied by a transition to more insulating behavior.

RTe3 compounds, a class of metallic layered materials, have proven difficult to exfoliate due to strong interlayer bonding. Conventional techniques can yield relatively thick flakes, but atomically thin layers suitable for electronic characterization have remained inaccessible17. In this work, we overcome this limitation using a modified top-down approach based on Au-assisted exfoliation to isolate atomically thin layers of NdTe3, a magnetic member of the RTe3 family. The strong adhesion between Au and chalcogen layers allows the preparation of these ultra-thin samples, enabling us to probe their quantum transport properties and trace the evolution of the band structure down to the bilayer limit.

To isolate strongly bonded RTe3, we employ Au-enhanced exfoliation37,38. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 1a, a conventional SiO2/Si++ substrate is first modified by depositing a Ti/Au layer. The subsequent fabrication steps are performed entirely in an argon-filled glove box: exfoliation of NdTe3 onto the Au surface, followed by encapsulation with thin h-BN. After encapsulation, the top h-BN layer is selectively etched using an O2/CF4 plasma to expose the contact regions. Finally, Ti/Au electrodes are defined by e-beam evaporation.

The NdTe3 is a layered crystal Fig. 1b, consisting of two Nd-Te layers intertwined with Te bilayers to form a unit cell. Figure 1c (top panel) shows an optical micrograph of an exfoliated NdTe3 sample obtained using Au-assisted exfoliation. Large-area terraces of few-layer NdTe3 can be easily resolved by optical color contrast. The subsequent atomic force microscopy (AFM) scan of the highlighted region in the optical image (Fig. 1c, middle panel) shows atomically flat terraces extending up to tens of microns. The height profile shows step edges corresponding to mono- to few-layer thicknesses (Fig. 1c, bottom panel), where the measured step height of ~1.3 nm agrees well with the unit-cell thickness13.

The key to achieving successful electrical characterization is optimizing the Au film thickness by balancing the competing factors of increasing the coverage for better adhesion and minimizing it to stay electrically insulating below a nominal thickness. As shown in Fig. 1d (left axis), the sheet resistance Rs of bare Au adhesive films increases sharply below 2.5 nm, indicating the formation of a discontinuous morphology.

Additionally, as shown on the right axis of Fig. 1d, the exfoliation yields high areal coverage. We quantify the fraction of area of a given layer number L as γ=AL/A, where AL is the area covered by that specific thickness, and A is the total area. Owing to the strong chalcogen-Au interfacial interaction, the cleavage preferentially yields regions that are half- to one- unit cell thick. For consistency across all measurements, we selected an Au film thickness of ~2.1–2.3 nm, which provides sufficient exfoliation yield while remaining below the discontinuity threshold.

For various thicknesses, the temperature dependences of normalized resistivity ρ/ρ200K of NdTe3 devices is plotted in Fig. 1e. Overall, metallic transport in NdTe3 persists down to one unit-cell samples, whereas the half-unit-cell ones are insulating. As L decreases from 48 to 2, the ρ/ρ200K curves show progressively weaker temperature dependence, leading to a smaller residual resistivity ratio (RRR) from 37 to 1.7 (Fig. 1e, inset). In contrast, bulk NdTe3 (1~10 µm thick) can reach an RRR of ~1000 (refs. 14), suggesting exfoliation-induced degradation in sample quality. A similar thickness dependence is observed in the magnetoresistance, defined as MR=100ρxxBρxx0−1%, as shown in Fig. 2a. The MR reaches ~550% at B=14 T in the L=48 sample, which is reduced compared to the bulk counterpart12, already exhibiting MR ~ 6000% at 9 T. Further reduction in MR is observed in thinner samples, down to ~1.4% when L=2.

Shubnikov-de Haas quantum oscillations in a layered metal

In our ultrathin NdTe3 devices, a prominent feature is the robust Shubnikov-de Haas (SdH) oscillations in magnetic fields up to 14 T. Starting from the SdH oscillations in bulk-like samples (thicker than 21 layers, Fig. 2a), the amplitude remains strong in samples with less than 10 layers and is still observable down to a bilayer sample. As shown in Fig. 2a, the overall diminishing SdH oscillations correlate with weaker MR and reduced RRR in thinner samples. Nevertheless, an exception is found in comparing L=4 and 7 samples. Both MR and RRR are more pronounced in the L=4 sample than in the 7-layer sample, which distinguishes the 4-layer sample from the other thicknesses. This anomaly favors the scenario that device fabrication details might matter, as thinner samples are more air unstable13. Consistently, despite being metallic, the NdTe3 bilayer shows a weak MR of ~1.4% and an overall RRR of 1.7 along with a reduced SdH signal (Fig. 2a).

A similar behavior is observed for higher oscillation frequencies (Fig. 2d), indicating that despite the presence of extrinsic scatterers generated during exfoliation, the integrity of the electronic structure of NdTe3 persists across all thicknesses.

The Fermi surface of NdTe3 primarily originates from the in-plane px and py orbitals of the Te bilayers, which host bands spanning over ~ 2eV above and below the Fermi level and dominate the transport. The Nd-derived bands are situated close in energy but remain comparatively localized, with their minor contribution to the observed oscillations (Supplementary Fig. 8). Such an orbital character underpins the multi-pocket structure and can be resolved further in our measurements via spectral analysis of SdH oscillations.

The Fast-Fourier Transform (FFT) spectra of the oscillating part of the longitudinal resistivity (Fig. 2d), reveal four distinct frequency branches, denoted as α, β, γ, and η. These frequencies correspond to quantum oscillations previously observed in de Haas-Van Alphen measurements of bulk single crystals12. Here, the β and γ branches are associated with pockets of diamond-shaped Fermi surfaces centered around the X point of the Brillouin zone (BZ)40–42. As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the Fermi surface reflects the lifting of layer degeneracy between adjacent tellurium planes, where the interlayer hopping t⊥ leads to a band splitting and emergence of dual Fermi pockets12. The α1 and α2 frequencies originate from hybridized shadow bands that form between the γ and δ pockets43. The δ pocket, centered at the Γ point, is not observed in the present samples. In contrast, the η frequency branch is clearly observed in samples with high RRR ratio >27, e.g., in relatively thick sample with L = 21. Here, the η pocket corresponds to an extra Fermi surface, arising from reconstructed shadow bands and original Fermi surfaces43.

The formation of CDW state introduces partial gapping at the Fermi level, leading to a distortion of the Brillouin zone and modifying the geometry of the Fermi surface. This is manifested as a slight mismatch in the cross-sectional area of the β1,2 and γ1,2 pockets, centered at X and Z points, respectively. The splitting of these branches is most evident in the FFT spectra of the 48-layer sample, where both β and γ peaks appear as doublets, whereas in thinner or more disordered samples, only single dominant peaks are observed.

Zeeman splitting of Landau Levels and spin-damping of SdH oscillations

In the following, we present an angular dependent measurement to extract the effective g*-factor. A typical example of the quantum oscillation of the 4-layer sample in a tilted magnetic field with respect to the sample normal is shown in Fig. 3a–b. Here, the frequency fi obtained for different pockets can be exactly scaled by the 1/cosθ factor, which highlights the two-dimensional character of the Fermi surfaces and a predominant contribution of the out-of-plane component of the applied B field (Fig. 4a). The fi/cosθ dependence is well maintained beyond 60 degrees away from the sample normal (α pockets, 4L and 10L samples) and magnetic fields up to 35 T (β and γ pockets, 7L sample) (Fig. 4b). The latter is in good agreement with the pronounced layered transport of RTe3, where the out-of-plane conductivity is much weaker compared to the in-plane transport14 and forms a nearly kz-independent Fermi surfaces.

A closer look at the angular dependence of the FFT spectra reveals the nodal points—tilting angles where the oscillation amplitude vanishes. These nodes are particularly prominent in 7 and 10–layer samples (Fig. 4b). To examine the origin of these nodal points, we extract the FFT amplitudes along the fi/cosθ lines for the α, β, γ pockets of our thin samples, as shown in Fig. 4c. In the 7–layer sample, amplitudes of the β and γ pockets vanish near ±24°, followed by a reappearance as the angle tilts further away from the out-of-plane direction. A similar trend is observed over an extended field range up to 33 T, although the dip near the nodal point becomes less pronounced (open symbols in Fig. 4c), and remains visible in the resistivity oscillations (Supplementary Fig. 6). In addition to the β and γ pockets, the 10–layer sample exhibits a nodal suppression of the α pocket at ±33°. In sharp contrast, no distinct off-axis node behavior is observed in the 4– and 36–layer samples.

The intensity of the β and γ peaks of the 4–layer sample decay with increasing angle, while the α peak of a 36–layer sample exhibits a slight enhancement before showing similar decay. This places NdTe3 among materials where linear dispersion and strong spin response are expected to play a central role in shaping electronic behavior, potentially enabling unconventional superconductivity and related quantum phases.

This is the so-called spin-zero effect39,42–46, manifested as vanishing oscillations across a broad magnetic field range (Supplementary Fig. 6). It is worth noting that the spin-zero effect is especially pronounced in our samples with reduced RRR (Fig. 4b). The condition for weak d(E) modulation requires a substantial broadening of Landau levels compared to their spacing ΔΓ≅ℏωc (Supplementary Fig. 7d), which can be readily satisfied in various metals. However, a slight reduction in ΔΓ can lead to incomplete amplitude cancellation (α pocket Fig. 4c), hence the disappearance of nodes (4-layer sample, Fig. 4c). The situation changes dramatically when the Landau level splitting exceeds ΔΓ (Supplementary Fig. 7c) and the spin-splitting becomes well resolved. A typical example is a coincidence experiment in a 2D electron gas of single-band semiconductors47,48. In this case, instead of vanishing oscillations, a doubling of the primary frequency is observed at θc (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

Thus, for a linear dispersion, the cyclotron mass is expected to scale as m*~f/vF, which appears as a straight line with a slope of 12 on a logarithmic plot. Here, the slope reflects the power-law exponent in m*f, and the vertical offset (in m*me axis) reflect the inverse Fermi velocity, ~1/vF. The dashed line in Fig. 5 indicates the scaling relation m*~f/vF, derived from a Dirac-like linear dispersion with Fermi velocity vF≈106 m/s, serving as a reference across diverse systems.

Most material families deviate from this scaling. For instance, heavy fermion compounds (top right corner) exhibit a strong many-body renormalization effect that greatly enhances the effective mass. Their Fermi surfaces emerge from hybridization between localized f-electrons and conduction bands, resulting in extremely heavy carriers and strong temperature dependences. In 3D semimetals, multiple Fermi surface pockets with distinct dispersions often prevent a simple power-law relation between m* and f. By contrast, the data for NdTe3 (colored circles in Fig. 5), converge in an area seemingly consistent with a single m*~f trend, yielding a Fermi velocity of vF = 5.8×105 m/s, slightly below the upper bound. The persistence of this scaling across a wide thickness range, covering all samples, indicates that the band structure remains linear and intact. This strongly suggests that quantum oscillations across samples arise from the same manifold derived from the p–orbitals of Te, and the electronic integrity of NdTe3 is preserved down to atomic thicknesses, extending to a bilayer unit cell (Supplementary Fig. 8).

This remarkably coherent behavior closely resembles that of a linearly dispersing single-band system. Graphene, located at the lower left corner, exemplifies the ideal Dirac system based on a hexagonal lattice, where simple tight-binding theory shows vF=32taℏ with t ≈ 2.8 eV and a = 1.42 Å2. While NdTe3 is based on the motif of square nets of tellurium atoms with dominating in-plane px and py orbitals, giving rise to highly dispersive bands. The CDW state and antiferromagnetic ground state form complicated modifications together with a Te-bilayer splitting and zone folding, producing multiple pockets. Nevertheless, these decorations to the electronic structure due to ordering do not alter fundamental properties held by the motif, well described by a simple tight-binding model on a 2D square lattice. Our result place NdTe3 among a rare group of materials that exhibit simple and consistent mass-frequency scaling, reflecting a clean linearly dispersive band with minimal renormalization, despite complexity in structure, ordering, and reduced dimensionality.

Methods

Crystal growth

Commercially available neodymium (>99.9%; Sigma-Aldrich) was mixed with purified tellurium in a well-above stoichiometric ratio of 3:97 to ensure the excess of Te flux. The materials were then heated in a sealed and evacuated quartz tube to 900 °C. After 12 h, the tube was slowly cooled down to 550 °C at 2 °C/h. Next, liquid Te was separated from the NdTe3 crystals by a centrifugation process. Crystals were further cooled down naturally to room temperature.

Sample fabrication and transport measurements

The NdTe3 were exfoliated utilizing the Au-enhanced adhesion technique37,38. At first, the Si++/SiO2 substrate was coated with 0.5/2.0 nm of Ti/Au by e-beam evaporation. Then, the wafer was transferred into an inert Argon environment of the glovebox without further exposure to the air. A freshly prepared surface of NdTe3 was then pressed against the substrate. The selected NdTe3 sample was then protected by h-BN and spin-coated with standard e-beam resist Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA). Next, electrode regions were patterned with e-beam lithography, and windows through h-BN were opened by the selective plasma etching process of the O2 and CF4 mixture in the reactive ion etching (RIE) chamber. Finally, 0.5/50 nm of Ti/Au was deposited on top by an e-beam evaporator. The Au modified substrates were measured to be electrically insulating (> 1GΩ), preventing any shunting paths. The device was inserted into the cryostat and gradually cooled down to the base temperature. The transport measurements were performed in a four-probe configuration using the standard AC lock-in technique (Stanford Research SR830 at 13 Hz) in a 4He environment.
