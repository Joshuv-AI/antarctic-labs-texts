Time-to-Event Modeling with Pseudo-Observations in Federated Settings
Hyojung Jang

Abstract
In multi-center clinical studies, concerns about patient privacy often prohibit pooling individual-level time-to-event data. We propose a non-iterative, one-shot federated framework using distributed pseudo-observations, derived from a sequentially updated Kaplan-Meier estimator and fitted with renewable generalized linear models. This framework enables the estimation of survival probabilities at specified landmark times and accommodates both time-invariant and time-varying covariate effects. To capture site-level heterogeneity, we introduce a soft-thresholding debiasing procedure that adaptively shrinks local estimates toward the global fit. Through extensive simulations across varying event rates and site-size distributions, our method demonstrates performance comparable to pooled Cox and the one-shot Optimal Distributed Aggregation (ODAC) models, with added flexibility to capture non-proportional hazards. Applied to pediatric obesity data from the Chicago Area Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network (CAPriCORN), which comprises four different sites and includes a total of 45,865 patients. The federated pseudo value regression model produced estimates of both time-constant and time-varying hazard ratios that closely aligned with those obtained from the pooled analysis, demonstrating its utility as a robust and privacy-preserving alternative for collaborative survival research. To further address potential heterogeneity across sites, we applied a covariate-wise debiasing algorithm, enabling site-level adjustments while preserving consistency with the global model.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and Motivation

In modern healthcare research, the widespread adoption of electronic health records (EHRs) and administrative claims data has substantially increased the availability of longitudinal patient-level data. These datasets provide valuable opportunities to investigate clinical outcomes that depend on the timing of events, such as disease progression, treatment efficacy, and adverse effects. urvival analysis methods play a critical role in this context, enabling researchers to evaluate the timing of events and to identify covariates associated with time-to-event outcomes.

Although longitudinal data are increasingly available at individual healthcare institutions, many clinical research questions pertain to rare outcomes or narrowly defined patient subgroups that are inadequately represented within a single site. For instance, investigations of rare diseases, such as specific cancers or uncommon genetic disorders, often require data aggregation across multiple centers to achieve sufficient statistical power. Likewise, the evaluation of infrequent adverse events associated with emerging treatments or vaccines, such as severe allergic reactions or rare thrombotic complications, benefits greatly from collaborative efforts spanning multiple institutions. Research targeting specialized populations, including pediatric patients or individuals with rare comorbidities, also demands combined datasets to ensure robust, accurate, and generalizable findings.

However, integrating data across institutions introduces substantial challenges. Stringent regulatory and policy constraints often prohibit the exchange of detailed patient-level information, posing significant barriers to traditional pooled analyses and raising concerns about patient privacy. To overcome these limitations, there is a critical need for federated analytic frameworks that facilitate cross-site collaboration while preserving data privacy by avoiding the direct sharing of sensitive individual-level records.

1.2
Existing Methods

Existing federated survival analysis approaches primarily utilize iterative procedures. For example, a web service for distributed Cox model learning (WebDISCO)
(
Lu et al. 2015
)
require multiple rounds of summary data exchange to fit Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox 1972
)
. Non-iterative approaches
(
Duan et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2021
)
have also recently emerged to simplify federated learning process without the need of iterative computations. Despite their practical benefits, these methods remain limited by assumptions such as proportional hazards (PH), reducing their flexibility for handling complex real-data survival scenarios where treatment effects vary over time. To handle the complexities of real-world data and support collaborative survival analysis across multiple sites, there is an urgent need for more flexible model for time-to-event data.

1.3
Objective

Therefore, in this paper, we introduce a novel federated survival modeling framework utilizing distributed pseudo-observations, specifically addressing survival probabilities. Our approach facilitates pseudo-observations obtained via sequentially updated distributed Kaplan-Meier estimations
(
Risk et al. 2025
)
and employs renewable generalized linear models
(
Luo & Song 2020
)
with the distributed pseudo observations as the outcome.

Our proposed methodology represents a significant advancement in federated survival analysis, primarily through two key contributions. First, we establish a novel federated pseudo-value regression framework designed to directly estimate absolute survival probabilities at specific time points, given covariates. This is achieved by leveraging distributed pseudo-observations within a renewable generalized linear modeling (GLM) approach. A notable advantage of this methodology is its flexibility to accommodate both proportional hazards and non-proportional hazards, as it does not impose the restrictive proportional hazards assumption. The reliability of our approach is further enhanced by integrating a sandwich-variance estimator directly into the renewable GLM, which ensures accurate and dependable standard errors for the estimated parameters even with repeated measurements and clustered observations.

Second, we propose a debiasing procedure that generates site‐specific coefficient estimates while leveraging the information from the global model. Following the estimation of the global distributed pseudo-value regression, each site performs a covariate-wise soft-thresholding adjustment to its local coefficient vector, effectively shrinking the estimates toward the global benchmark. This two-stage ”fit-and-shrink” framework is designed to accommodate sparse heterogeneity across centers, enabling sites with distinct patient populations to preserve local signal while maintaining statistical efficiency through shared information.

Together, these innovations enable a flexible, privacy‐preserving solution for multicenter time‐to‐event analyses. Unlike traditional federated Cox approaches, which are often limited by proportional hazards assumptions, our federated pseudo‐value framework naturally handles both time‐invariant and time‐dependent covariate effects, recovering valid survival probabilities under non‐proportional hazards. Through extensive simulation studies and a real-world application to pediatric obesity data from the Chicago Area Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network (CAPriCORN), comprising four participating sites, we demonstrate that our approach achieves inferential accuracy comparable to that of pooled analyses while effectively capturing complex survival dynamics and accounting for site-level heterogeneity.

2
Methods

2.1
Notation

We first introduce a description of key notations and functions in survival analysis. In survival analysis, each subject
i
is characterized by two latent times: the true event time
T_{i}
(for example, the time to death or the onset of disease) and the censoring time
C_{i}
, which marks the last point at which the individual remains under observation. Since neither
T_{i}
nor
C_{i}
is observed directly, we instead record the follow-up time
X_{i}=\min(T_{i},C_{i})
and the event indicator
\Delta_{i}=I(T_{i}<C_{i})
, so that
\Delta_{i}=1
indicates that the event occurred before censoring.

Let
N
denote the total number of subjects, with observed follow-up times
X=(X_{1},...,X_{N})
and the corresponding censoring indicators
\Delta=(\Delta_{1},...,\Delta_{N})
. The survival function
S(t)
, which represents the probability that an event has not occurred by time t, is defined as
S(t)=P(T_{i}>t)
. The Kaplan-Meier estimate
(
Kaplan & Meier 1958
)
for
S(t)
is defined as

\hat{S}_{N}(t,X,\Delta)=\prod_{j:t_{j}\leq t}\Bigl(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}=t_{j})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}>t_{j})}\Bigr)

(1)

where
{t_{j}:t_{j}\leq t}
are distinct event times up to t.

2.2
Methods in Non-distributed Settings: Pseudo-value Regression for Survival Probabilities

When all individual-level data
(X_{i},\Delta_{i},Z_{i})
are available in a single dataset, pseudo-value regression
(
Andersen & Pohar Perme 2010
)
provides a flexible and interpretable way to model survival probabilities directly. The process proceeds in two main steps. The first step is to calculate the pseudo-observations. Let
\hat{S}(t)
be the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the survival function at time t based on all N subjects, and let
\hat{S}^{-i}(t)
denote the Kaplan-Meier estimate computed with the i-th subject omitted. The jackknife pseudo-value for subject i at a prespecified time point
t_{j}
is then

Y_{ij}=N\hat{S}(t_{j})-(N-1)\hat{S}^{-i}(t_{j}),\quad i=1,...,N,\quad j=1,...,J

(2)

Each
Y_{ij}
can be interpreted as individual i’s pseudo-contribution to the overall estimate of
S(t_{j})
. The second step is to fit the generalized regression model with pseudo-observations as the outcome to quantify the effect of covariates on the survival probability at a given time
t_{j}
.

g(E(Y_{ij}))=\beta_{0}(t_{j})+Z_{i}^{T}\beta(t_{j})

(3)

where
g(\cdot)
is a suitable link (identity for absolute risk differences, logit for odds, or complementary log-log for hazard-type scale). In the equation,
\beta_{0}(t_{j})
is the time-specific intercept, and
\beta(t_{j})
measures the covariate effects on survival probability specific to
t_{j}
.

The model can be extended to accommodate both a joint proportional-hazards framework and the estimation of time-varying covariate effects on hazard ratio by employing complementary log-log link function. To extend the single time point pseudo-value regression into a joint proportional hazard-type framework, we stack all J sets of pseudo-values
(Y_{ij}:i=1,...,N;j=1,...,J)
into a long vector and fit a generalized estimating equation (GEE) of the form

g(E(Y_{ij}))=\beta_{0}(t_{j})+Z_{i}^{T}\beta

(4)

where
\beta
is now constant across time points. There are studies which have studied the sensitivity to the decision of the number of time points for the model
(
Andersen & Pohar Perme 2010
;
Anyaso-Samuel et al. 2023
)
. Their findings suggest to include at least five equally spaced evaluation times—selected within the observed range of the survival curve. By choosing the complementary log-log link and treating
\beta
as time-invariant, the resulting estimates closely mirror a Cox proportional-hazards model.

When the proportional-hazards assumption is suspected, we simply allow
\beta
to depend on
t_{j}
by including the interaction between time point indicators and covariate in the model. Under the formulation, the effective coefficient at time
t_{j}
becomes
\beta(t_{j})=\beta+\gamma_{j}
, where
\gamma_{j}
captures the additional, time-specific deviation from the baseline effect
\beta
. The specification enables the estimation of covariate effects varying across discrete time points within a single unified model, obviating the need to fit separate regressions for each time horizon. For both modeling, the robust sandwich variance estimators ensure valid standard errors despite the induced within-subject correlation of pseudo-values over time.

2.3
Our Approach in Distributed Settings: Federated Pseudo-value Regression for Survival Probabilities with Renewable Generalized Estimating Equation

In a federated environment, individual-level data cannot be pooled across sites. We assume data reside at K clinical centers, with the jth site holding
n_{j}
observations, so that the sample size is
N=\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}
.
To perform pseudo-value regression without centralizing patient records, we first construct a distributed Kaplan-Meier estimator
(
Risk et al. 2025
)
, which is essential for deriving the pseudo-observations of survival probability. The distributed Kaplan-Meier estimate is sequentially updated by aggregating the influence function contributions from each individual’s data. If
\hat{S}_{i}(t)
denotes the aggregated survival estimate after processing data from ith individuals, the
(i+1
)th update is computed as:

\hat{S}_{i+1}(t)=\hat{S}_{i}(t)+\frac{\hat{\psi}(X_{i+1},\Delta_{i+1})(t)}{i+1}

(5)

Here,
\hat{\psi}(X_{i+1},\Delta_{i+1})
is the empirical influence function of the Kaplan-Meier estimator at specified time
t
. The influence function
{\psi}(X_{i},\Delta_{i})
for a generic individual i is generally expressed as:

\begin{split}\psi(X_{i},\Delta_{i})(t)=-S(t)\left[\frac{\Delta_{i}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\leq t)}{Y(X_{i})}-\int_{0}^{x_{i}\land t}\frac{\lambda(u)}{Y(u)}du\right]\end{split}

(6)

where
Y(t)=P(X_{i}>t)
denotes the number of subjects at risk at time
t
and
\lambda(t)=\frac{-dS(t)}{dt}/S(t)
is the hazard function of our survival curve. In this federated setting, the pseudo-values
Y_{ij}
at time
t_{j}
are derived through their influence function representation based on the distributed Kaplan-Meier estimates,
Y_{ij}=N\hat{S}(t_{j})-(N-1)\hat{S}^{-i}(t_{j})
which can be finally approximated as belows.

\displaystyle Y_{ij}

\displaystyle=n\hat{S}(t_{j})-(n-1)\hat{S}^{-i}(t_{j})

(7)

\displaystyle=n\hat{S}(t_{j})-(n-1)[\hat{S}(t_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\psi_{i}(t)]

(8)

\displaystyle=\hat{S}(t_{j})+\frac{n-1}{n}\psi_{i}(t_{j})

(9)

\displaystyle\approx\hat{S}(t_{j})+\psi_{i}(t)

(10)

\displaystyle=\hat{S}(t_{j})-\hat{S}(t_{j})[\frac{\Delta_{i}\mathbb{I}(X_{i}\leq t_{j})}{Y(X_{i})}-\int_{0}^{x_{i}\land t_{j}}\frac{\lambda(u)}{Y(u)}du]

(11)

After constructing the federated pseudo‐observations
Y_{ij}
at each prespecified time point
t_{j}
, we estimate the corresponding survival probabilities by fitting a generalized linear model via a sequentially renewable procedure
(
Luo & Song 2020
)
. Starting from an initial MLE
\hat{\beta}^{(1)}
, calculated at Site 1, the renewable GLM updates the parameter estimate across sites without ever sharing individual-level data. Only the current parameter estimate and the accumulated information matrix are communicated between sites. If the pseudo-observations are generated at multiple time points for each patient to support a joint proportional-hazards or to capture time-varying covariate effect model, the cumulative meat matrix should be transmitted alongside the accumulated information matrix, in order to obtain a sandwich-based variance estimate. Concretely at site
k
, upon receiving
\hat{\beta}_{k-1}
(the current parameter estimate),
I_{k-1}
(the cumulative information matrix), and
M_{k-1}
(the cumulative meat matrix) from the previous site, we compute local score vector
U_{k}(D_{k};\hat{\beta}_{k-1})
and the local Hessian matrix
H_{k}(D_{k};\hat{\beta}_{k-1})
using the local data
D_{k}
. These local summary statistics are then incorporated into the process of solving equation (12), which is the incremental estimating equation to update the estimate
\hat{\beta}_{k}
. The equation arises from a first-order Taylor expansion of the cumulative score function up to site
k
.

H_{k-1}\bigl(\hat{\beta}_{k-1}\bigr)\bigl(\hat{\beta}_{k-1}-\hat{\beta}_{k}\bigr)+U_{k}\bigl(\hat{\beta}_{k}\bigr)=0

(12)

This framework updates the parameter estimates by applying the Newton-Raphson algorithm for non-canonical link (complementary log-log link) and Fisher scoring when the link is canonical (logit and identical link) using only current parameter estimates, and aggregated summary statistics. In doing so, it fully preserves patient confidentiality while delivering inferential precision comparable to a traditional pooled analysis. The detailed description of how our methods updates the parameter estimates
\hat{\beta}
and its variance
\hat{V}(\hat{\beta})
in the renewable estimating equation is provided in Algorithm 1. The renewable GEE, as outlined in Algorithm 1, involves the sequential updating of both the parameter estimate and its robust variance using only site-level summary statistics. The key innovation of our renewable GEE lies in the use of sandwich variance estimation to account for the multiple pseudo-observations across different time points from the same individual. We accumulate each cluster’s outer-product of its score contributions into a meat matrix
M
, and combine this with the final Fisher information
I
to form the sandwich variance estimator
\hat{V}(\hat{\beta})=I^{-1}MI^{-1}
, thereby ensuring valid standard errors that mirror those from a pooled analysis.

2.4
Our Approach in Distributed Settings: Debiasing Local Updates with Soft-thresholding Shrinkage

In federated-learning, it is common for one or more centers to exhibit true site-specific effects that deviate from the pooled pattern. To capture this heterogeneity across different sites, we first obtain the global coefficient vector
\hat{\beta}_{global}
via federated pseudo observations and the renewable GEE, then perform a
soft-thresholding debiasing

(
Donoho & Johnstone 1994
)
step at each site
k
. Let
\hat{\beta}_{local}^{(k)}
be the site’s local pseudo-value regression coefficients, and define coordinate-wise thresholds
\lambda_{j}^{(k)}=\gamma_{j}\sqrt{v_{j}^{(k)}}
, where
v_{j}^{(k)}
is the
j
th diagonal element of the site’s empirical covariance matrix and
\gamma_{j}
is a user-specified constant. Huang et al,. recommend to use
\gamma_{j}^{(k)}=c\sqrt{ln((N/n_{k})\wedge d)}
where
N
implies the number of subjects across all the sites,
n_{k}
implies the number of subjects in site
k
respectively and
d
refers to the dimension of covariates
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
.

For each coordinate j, the debiased estimate is

\hat{\beta}_{j}^{(k)}=\hat{\beta}_{global,j}+\mathrm{SoftThresholding}\bigl(\hat{\beta}_{local,j}^{(k)}-\hat{\beta}_{global,j}\;;\;\lambda_{j}^{(k)}\bigr)

(13)

where SoftThresholding(
x
,
\lambda
) = sign
(x)(|x|-\lambda)
. The main idea of the debiasing method here is to directly compare the local and global regression estimates and the threshold becomes the direct rule for admissible difference between the two estimates. Intuitively, if
|\hat{\beta}^{(k)}_{local,j}-\hat{\beta}_{global,j}|\leq\lambda_{j}^{(k)}
, the discrepancy is treated as sampling noise and shrunk entirely to the global estimate. If it exceeds the threshold, the local deviation is preserved allowing genuine heterogeneity while controlling for estimation error.
Under sparse heterogeneity assumption in site-specific parameters
\hat{\beta}^{(k)}_{local,j}
, most coordinates satisfy
|\hat{\beta}_{local,j}^{(k)}-\hat{\beta}_{global,j}|\leq\lambda_{j}^{(k)}
and are shrunk exactly to
\hat{\beta}_{global,j}
, while a small number of sites having truly divergent effects pass the threshold and incur only partial shrinkage.

This two-stage ”global then debias” strategy leverages the common structure to stabilize estimation while permitting local deviations where warranted. The detailed description of how we perform debiasing after achieving global estimates via renewable GEE is described in Algorithm 2.

3
Simulation Studies

We conducted simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the distributed pseudo-observation regression framework under two modeling scenarios. First, under the assumption of proportional hazards, we implemented a joint modeling approach by regression pseudo-values at multiple prespecified time points on covariates, constraining the regression coefficient to remain constant across time. Second, to allow for non-proportional hazards, we extended the model by including interaction terms between covariates and time-point indicators, enabling time-varying covariate effects.

We initially assessed the federated algorithm under the proportional hazards framework across four simulation settings, combining two overall event rates (
10\%
and
30\%
) with two site-size configuration, while holding the total sample size constant at 7,500. We compared the performance of our method against both the fully pooled Cox proportional hazards model and ODAC (One-shot Distributed Algorithm for Cox models), which approximates the partial likelihood through surrogate likelihood construction.

In each of 500 simulation replicates, we generated a continuous covariate
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and a binary treatment indicator
T\sim\text{Bernoulli}(expit(0.5X))
. Survival times were drawn from an exponential distribution with individual-specific hazards defined as
\lambda_{i}=h_{0}\,\text{exp}(\beta_{x}\,X_{i}+\beta_{T}\,T_{i})
, where the true log hazard ratios were
\beta_{x}=\text{log}(0.7)
and
\beta_{T}=\text{log}(1.15)
. Two baseline hazards,
h_{0}=1/20
and
h_{1}=1/50
, were used to yield approximate event rates of
30\%
and
10\%
, respectively. Figure 1 displays boxplots of the bias in the estimated treatment log hazard ratios across 500 replicates, stratified by event rate and site-size distribution. The results demonstrate that our federated pseudo-observation regression method achieves accuracy and precision comparable to both the pooled Cox model and ODAC under the proportional hazards assumption.

To demonstrate the capacity of pseudo-value regression to accurately recover genuinely time-varying covariate effects, we conducted a second simulation study based on a treatment-dependent Weibull survival model. Specifically, we simulated a cohort of
N=5,500
individuals distributed across
K=20
centers, with site-specific sample sizes ranging 50 to 3,000. A continuous covariate
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and a binary treatment indicator
T\in\{0,1\}
were generated for all individuals. To induce non-proportional hazards, we specified a Weibull model in which both the scale and shape parameters depended on treatment status. The shape parameter was defined as
\text{shape}_{i}=0.5+1.7\,T_{i}
, and the scale parameter as
\text{scale}_{i}=15\,\text{exp}\,(\text{ln}\,(0.7)\,X_{i}+\text{ln}\,(2)\,T_{i})
. This structure allows the treatment to influence both the magnitude and time dependency of the hazard, yielding non-proportional treatment effects over follow-up time.

The close agreement between the pseudo-value estimates and the true curve across all time points highlights the method’s ability to accurately capture nonlinear and evolving treatment effects without access to individual-level data.

In the simulation scenarios described above, the data were generated under homogeneous settings, where the true log hazard ratios for all covariates were identical across sites. However, real-world data networks often exhibit sparse heterogeneity, where a small number of sites deviate from the overall population effect. To evaluate the performance of the proposed debiasing procedure under such heterogeneity, we simulated data across
K=23
centers with varying sample sizes
\{3000,1000,500,100,50\}
, totaling
N=8,500
, then selected one ’target’ site whose sample. The designated site has its sample size varied among
\{500,100,50\}
. At this target site, we perturbed the true treatment effect by an amount
\delta\in\{0.05,0.15,0.3,0.5\}
, relative to a shared treatment log hazard ratio of log(1.5) used across the remaining sites. We focused on moderately sized target sites (50-500 subjects) because these small cohorts experience greater sampling variability and thus stand to benefit more from soft-thresholding debiasing.

We compared the following three estimators in terms of bias and precision: (i) the global federated pseudo-value model, (ii) the local Cox proportional hazards model fit at the target site, and (iii) the debiased local estimate obtained via the proposed covariate-wise soft-thresholding adjustment. Given that the data generation process adheres to the proportional hazards assumption, the local Cox model serves as a natural benchmark for assessing the local treatment effect estimate. These experiments highlight the utility of soft-thresholding debiasing, particularly for smaller sites with greater estimation variability.

5
Discussion & Conclusion

5.1
Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a federated pseudo‐observation framework for time‐to‐event analysis that preserves individual‐level privacy without maintaining inferential validity. Through comprehensive simulation studies, we demonstrated that the proposed distributed pseudo‐value regression performs comparably to the fully pooled Cox model and existing one-shot methods such as ODAC under proportional hazards assumptions, exhibiting similar bias and variance properties. Notably, in settings with time-varying treatment effects, such as our Weibull-based simulations, the pseudo‐value approach accurately captured the underlying nonlinear log-hazard trajectories across multiple landmark times. These findings highlight the methodological flexibility of the distributed pseudo‐value regression framework, which accommodates both proportional hazards and time-varying effects within a unified federated procedure.

When applying our method to the CAPriCORN pediatric obesity cohort, we observed close concordance between federated and pooled analyses. Site-specific Schoenfeld residual plots revealed mild violations of the proportional hazards assumption for baseline
age
and
BMI percentile
, prompting the inclusion of landmark-time interactions for these covariates. Both pooled and federated pseudo‐observation regressions produced nearly identical time‐varying coefficient trajectories. Furthermore, the estimated effects of constant covariates (e.g., sex, race/ethnicity, blood pressure, and insurance status) from the federated model closely matched those from the pooled analysis. In practical terms, this confirms that real‐world federated survival analyses can achieve essentially the same interpretability and statistical precision as pooled data studies.

Our debiasing procedure further enhances the federated framework by explicitly modeling site-level heterogeneity. In simulation study, particularly under small‐sample conditions (e.g., 50–100 patients per site), the debiased estimates closely approximated the true site-specific effects, demonstrating substantially lower variance than local-only Cox models and reduced bias compared to naïve global estimators. This two-stage approach—first constructing a stable global model by pooling information, then applying coordinate-wise soft-thresholding to adapt local coefficients—provides a principled strategy for borrowing strength across institutions while preserving meaningful heterogeneity. In practice, this enables individual hospitals to initialize with the federated model’s coefficients as a reliable starting point and subsequently tailor the estimates to their local patient population, even with limited data.

5.2
Conclusion

This manuscript introduces a novel federated survival modeling framework that addresses the critical challenge of conducting time-to-event analyses across multiple institutions while preserving patient privacy. By leveraging distributed pseudo-observations within renewable generalized linear models, our non-iterative approach provides a highly flexible solution capable of accommodating both time-invariant and complex time-varying covariate effects within a single unified model. This methodological advancement, coupled with robust sandwich variance estimation and a debiasing strategy to account for site heterogeneity significantly overcomes limitations of traditional centralized and existing federated survival methods. Through the real-world analysis with CAPriCORN network, we demonstrated that our federated algorithm consistently yields accuracy and precision comparable to pooled analyses. Ultimately, our framework offers a robust, privacy-preserving, and highly adaptable alternative for collaborative survival research, proving invaluable for studies on rare outcomes, specific patient subgroups, or new treatment evaluations where data sharing constraints are paramount.
