Electrochemical differentiation of native-denatured hemoglobin using a same-metal PCB-based three-electrode system
Authors: Churun Jauharoh Al Aryachiyah, Mohammad Nofil, Akmal Fathurrahman Zullkifli, Prince Nishchal Narayanaswamy Elumalai, Chethan C. Thimmarayappa, Sara Talebi, Mitsumasa Iwamoto, Vengadesh Periasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Denaturation statistically decreases a protein's structural stability and alters its reactivity, particularly in its redox reactions. One such protein is the redox-active hemoglobin (Hb) molecule, which is among the most extensively studied due to its importance in respiration. To investigate its redox activity, the standard electrochemical sensing method using a three-electrode platform has been widely employed. In this work, however, an unconventional approach was used whereby a same-metal (gold) three-electrode sensor was utilized. Interestingly, despite the absence of dissimilar electrode materials, the current PCB-based same-metal three-electrode (PCB-3T) sensor successfully produced characteristic Hb electrochemical profiles. In phosphate-buffered saline, closely spaced anodic and cathodic peak potentials were observed, indicating enhanced redox reversibility; an outcome that is difficult to achieve in in vitro studies of conventional systems without extensive surface modification. Additionally, a cathodic potential peak was detected when Hb was dissolved in ultrapure H2O, consistent with previous in vivo research. The miniaturized architecture of PCB-3T enables microliter-scale analysis with minimal activation cycles, making it suitable for characterizing unstable proteins, such as denatured Hb. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) results indicated that denatured Hb exhibited irregular peak potential shifts and higher peak currents than native Hb; consequently, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed differences between native and denatured Hb in Nyquist and Bode plots. p-values of 0.003 and 0.02 (α = 0.05, n = 5) were obtained for CV anodic potential differentiation, indicating improved resolution, and the reproducibility offers potential for further protein electrochemistry studies.

Introduction

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a metalloprotein with a molecular weight of 64 500 Daltons composed of two pairs of α and β chains, each bearing a heme group. As the dominant component of red blood cells, Hb plays a key role in oxygen transport, with the iron atom in each heme group being responsible for oxygen uptake and transport.1 This process depends on Hb's ability to undergo conformational changes, primarily in its tertiary and quaternary structure, thereby influencing the oxygen affinity during redox activity.2 Failure to maintain Hb's structure and functionality leads to systemic disturbances, such as anemia, nutrient deficiencies, inflammation, the generation of reactive oxygen species, organ dysfunction, and other secondary blood disorders (sickle cell disease, thalassemia, etc.).3–6 Globally, Hb abnormalities remain highly prevalent: from 1990 to 2021, anemia alone affected about one-quarter of the population, with iron deficiency occurring at roughly twice this rate, often underlying undiagnosed anemia.7,8

Unstable heme, when it is released from hemoproteins, causes toxicity and hemolysis. This may be generated, for instance, during and after Hb aggregation.9,10 Understanding Hb's susceptibility to aggregation requires insights into its denaturation property, which refers to the unfolding of a macromolecule's secondary and tertiary structure. This process alters molecular properties, leading to reduced solubility, increased aggregation, altered reactivity, and loss of biological function.11,12 However, research on this remains limited due to the lack of a standardized method.

Beyond the lack of a standardized, broadly applicable approach for identifying unstable conditions, denaturation, and aggregation in hemoproteins, several techniques are commonly employed to assess biomolecules, including high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), immunoassays, electrophoresis, and spectroscopy.10,11,13 These sophisticated methods are often challenged by time and cost inefficiency and complexity due to multiple reagents and sample labeling.13–15

Moreover, electrochemical profiling, such as redox behavior analysis, offers advantages including simplicity, low cost, ease of fabrication, and rapid response time.6,16 However, research on the electrochemical characterization of Hb remains limited due to the challenges in signal acquisition. The electrode system is often hindered by high impedance as Hb charged groups are shielded within the polypeptide structure.16 To address these challenges, over the decades, innovations in bulky three-electrode systems have focused on localizing biological molecules at the electrode, controlling the solvent environment, modifying electrode materials, and enhancing the electrode–electrolyte interface by increasing surface area, porosity, inertness, and electrical conductivity.17–20 Correspondingly, several important studies have explored the cyclic voltammetry (CV) profile of native Hb using three-electrode systems, yielding a diverse set of peaks, the most commonly reported are within the range of +0.4 V to −0.4 V.6,13,20–23

However, while electrode modifications are necessary to obtain distinct redox signals, particularly when working with limited samples such as body fluids and extremely valuable substances, they also increase the complexity of electrode fabrication and data analysis. Besides, due to the complexity, most studies reported a quasi-reversible redox profile, whereby analysis is meaningful only when focusing on either oxidation or reduction due to low resolution; reports on both oxidation and reduction analysis remain limited.6,17,18 Further, the previous study on denatured Hb, based on liquid–liquid interactions within a multiple electrolyte framework, focused on the ionic-transfer principle; thus, there remains limited data on denatured Hb based on the solid-state sensor.21

In recent decades, screen-printed electrodes (SPEs) have emerged as a miniaturized three-electrode system to overcome the challenges in bulky systems.22–24 Nevertheless, these SPEs generally have a short life due to electrode fouling.23,25 Hence, to address these limitations, a polishable triple-electrode (PTE) configuration has been introduced as a three-electrode system with conventional electrode materials (Pt, GCE, Ag/AgCl) embedded in epoxy resin, thereby creating a miniaturized interface. It provided the sensitivity that conventional systems did, but allowed up to 10 µl of sample to be tested. Despite its insight into proposing the robustness of a miniaturized system, however, the reported study using PTE was limited to organic material analysis, such as acetaminophen,22 which may differ from biological molecules, especially proteins.

These have therefore prompted the use of coplanar electrodes to facilitate liquid sample configurations that require only trace amounts of the sample solution for biological molecule characterization.26–28 Correspondingly, the studies employed printed circuit board (PCB) sensors based on the same-metal (gold) two-electrode system and successfully evaluated the electronic properties of proteins and vitamins via a label-free mechanism.28,29

In accordance, on the use of configurations in which all electrodes were fabricated from the same metal (gold), a same-metal three-electrode PCB-based sensor (PCB-3T) [Malaysian patent granted (PI2020001607 and PI2022006111) and US patent pending (18/816419 and 17/615055)] as a disposable miniaturized electrode system has been fabricated and characterized for its reproducibility to establish redox profiles of potassium ferricyanide in CV and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis. Further, it reported the detection of deionized ultrapure H2O, which demonstrated the PCB-3T's performance as an electrochemical sensor under non-ionic solvents/minimally mediated conditions.30 Moreover, its performance is highly comparable to that of the standard three-electrode systems (Pt, Au, Ag/AgCl), but with greater time efficiency, enabling only 2–4 activation cycles, compared with dozens to hundreds of cycles typically required in standard three-electrode configurations.30,31

Given the importance of understanding Hb's electrochemical properties, which reflect its structural state, this study focuses on characterizing lyophilized human Hb from a commercial research-grade preparation under a label-free environment. For the first time, a non-buffered, non-electrolyte solvent was introduced to analyze Hb while maintaining its structural integrity in a three-electrode system, recording its electrochemical properties with minimal interference and eliminating the need for mediating reagents. Thereby proposing a universal instrument for molecular biology detection. Each experiment was conducted under controlled conditions with optimized parameters using the PCB-3T sensors, enabling high-reproducibility investigation of the electrochemical profiles of native and denatured Hb.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

High-purity (research-grade) lyophilized human hemoglobin powder (Hb, ≥99%) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablets were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Deionized ultrapure H2O was used with a resistivity of 18.2 M Ω cm−1 at 25–28.5 °C. The two solvents, PBS (pH 7.4) and ultrapure H2O (pH 6.65 ± 0.2), were used at room temperature (24–28.5 °C) with relative humidity between 45 and 65%.

Sample preparation

Protein handling was performed under aseptic conditions, in which Hb solutions were prepared across a range of concentrations (0.1–50 mg mL−1), with 1.7 mg mL−1 (26.4 µmol l−1) optimized for comparative analysis in electrochemical analyses, microscopy, and spectroscopy-based replicates.

The microliter stock solutions of native Hb were prepared by dissolving lyophilized Hb powder directly into the respective solvent. The PBS molarity was optimized to 5 mM for most experimental setups, while for sensitivity and LOD analyses, it was diluted to 1 mM to avoid excessive current. Correspondingly, denatured Hb was obtained by heating aliquots of native protein solution in a water bath at the optimized temperature of 95 ± 1 °C (for Hb in ultrapure H2O) and 75 ± 2 °C (for Hb in PBS) for 5 min, followed by thermal quenching at ∼−20 °C for 3 min to prevent aggregation. Subsequently, native and denatured Hb solutions were aliquoted into sterile, sealed PCR vials (one vial per test) to minimize exposure to air and contamination. All analyses were conducted immediately after sample preparation to avoid molecular changes.

In an independent set of experiments for the comparative study of PCB-3T with the standard three-electrode method, an Hb stock solution (in milliliters) in 0.005 M PBS was prepared. Correspondingly, the denaturation parameter was optimized at 75 ± 2 °C for 10 min, followed by 5 min of thermal quenching.

Electrode preparation, PCB-3T, and conventional setup

The PCB-3T platform (Fig. 1a) was employed for all electrochemical characterization in this work. The sensors were designed in-house as printed circuit boards (PCBs) incorporating three interfacial electrodes within a circular sample-loading site, along with three terminal pads for connection to the potentiostat. All conductive components on the PCB-3T surface were fabricated from gold (Au). The gold thickness of ∼0.049 to 0.052 µm was electroplated on a nickel (Ni) base layer at a thickness of 4–5 µm.30,31 Each sensor dimension was 15 mm × 15 mm; the circular sample loading site had a diameter of ∼0.5 cm, giving an area of ∼0.19 cm2, and each of the three Au electrodes was ∼0.1 cm × ∼0.1 cm, providing the sample area of the working electrode at approximately ∼0.01 cm2. Further details on PCB design, surface contact angle analysis, and standardized surface cleaning procedures were reported in previous work.30

A standard three-electrode configuration of Ag/AgCl (RE), Au (WE), and Pt (CE) (Fig. 2) was employed for reference in the analysis of an upper range of Hb concentration (50 mg mL−1) and in the optimized concentration of 1.7 mg mL−1, which is widely controlled in the current study.

Experimental procedure for electrochemical analysis

The electrochemical measurements were conducted using a Potentiostat/Galvanostat system (Gamry Instrument Inc., USA). Before commencing the experiments, all accessible surfaces and the Gamry calibration cage were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (IPA). The Gamry software was then configured according to the electrochemical analytical parameters, and a PCB-3T sensor was placed within the calibration cage. The sensors' contact pads were connected to the potentiostat using the reference-working-counter (RWC) electrode configuration (Fig. 1c). Immediately prior to measurement, 15 µL of Hb solution was pipetted onto the PCB-3T sensor to ensure complete coverage of all three electrode surfaces (Fig. 1b). A 5-minute incubation time for the sample on the PCB-3T was optimized to improve repeatability and was applied across all measurements.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiments were performed within a potential window of +1.5 to −1.5 V for Hb dissolved in ultrapure H2O and +1.0 to −0.5 V for Hb dissolved in PBS. Three activation cycles were applied at a scan rate of 0.025 V s−1. The scan rates used for measurements were 0.025, 0.050, 0.075, and 0.100 V s−1, with the 0.100 V s−1 data primarily presented.

Main electrochemical measurements were performed using CV, and three technical replicates were performed for each of the five biological replicates. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using a non-parametric t-test (Welch's test) due to unequal standard deviation (SD) among the groups. Results are presented as mean values with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied.

In addition to CV analysis, Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted both before and after CV measurement, following a dual-stage experimental scheme distinct from the CV-only setup of the main analysis. The EIS frequency range spanned from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz, with an applied alternating current (AC) amplitude of 10 mV. Correspondingly, chronoamperometry was employed to assess linearity and sensitivity, including the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ).

Regarding the comparative benchmarking of the PCB-3T platform against a conventional Ag/AgCl reference electrode system, parallel experiments were conducted under identical electroanalytical parameters and a 5 min sample incubation period. The only difference in the protocol for the conventional approach was that the CV activation cycle was performed over 20 cycles at 0.025 V s−1.

Experimental procedure for surficial and structural analysis

The assessment of the sensor-biomolecule interface was conducted using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) at an acceleration voltage of 5.0–10 kV on a JEOL JSM-7600 F instrument after electrochemical testing (dry surface) of the PCB-3T electrode. Surface imaging was performed on the working electrodes of a bare (gold) electrode and electrodes with deposits after electrochemical assessment.

The protein structure was evaluated using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Nicolet™ Summit Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) recorded at a resolution of 2 cm−1 to obtain absorbance profiles, and CD spectroscopy was conducted using a J-815 Spectropolarimeter (Jasco, UK). CD measurements were acquired with high sensitivity over the wavelength range of 600–200 nm. Baseline correction was performed using the respective solvent.

Results and discussion

Hb profiles in conventional three-electrode setup

To establish a comparative benchmark, cyclic voltammetry (CV) of a 5 mL Hb solution was evaluated using a standard three-electrode configuration. Each volume of native Hb, denatured Hb, and the corresponding baseline of 0.005 M PBS (pH 7.4) was assessed. Against an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (RE), the cathodic peak potential of native Hb in 50 mg mL−1 was evident near +0.1 V. This potential lies within the approximated interval potential of the widely reported Hb profile13 and the overall trend between native and denatured Hb aligned closely with findings by Herzog et al. (2010).21 Furthermore, the identified peak value coincided with that obtained using the novel setup (PCB-3T) proposed in this study (Fig. 4a).

Given that the evaluation at an elevated Hb concentration of 50 mg mL−1 (Fig. 3a) revealed well-defined voltammetry profiles, with native and thermally denatured Hb states clearly distinguishable, the conventional measurement was initially attempted at 1.7 mg mL−1 (Fig. 3b), matching the optimized concentration used throughout the PCB-3T electrochemical analysis and the spectroscopic investigations. However, detection at this lower concentration in a milliliter-scale volume without surface modification proved insufficient. Although a minor peak current difference could be observed, the signal intensity was close to baseline and therefore could not be confidently interpreted as a resolved Hb peak. Consequently, while the distinction between native and denatured Hb was not observable at this low concentration with the standard setup, it was clearly distinguishable in the novel setup for both cathodic and anodic peaks.

Image shows the Hb profiles in a standard three-electrode configuration where (a) is the CV profile differentiation of 50 mg mL−1 and (b) 1.7 mg mL−1 of Hb solution.

On top of that, prior studies13,20 indicated that the establishment of a pair reduction–oxidation CV profile in a standard three-electrode system required surface and solution modifications. Without such modifications in the standard configuration used in the current work, the observed response was limited to the reduction peak.

Apart from improved detectability, the microliter-scale volume on the PCB-3T platform achieved higher resolution, yielding a current response approximately 102-fold higher than that of the milliliter-scale sample analyzed with the conventional system. Notably, the time saved during measurement was reflected in the requirement of only 3 activation cycles with the horizontal microdroplet PCB-3T sensor, whereas the conventional configuration required 20 cycles to obtain a reproducible CV response, as expected due to its milliliter-scale sample.

On the Au WE for both conventional (vs. Ag/AgCl) and PCB-3T (vs. pseudo-reference gold (Au) electrode), the Hb redox profiles were observed. The use of Au as the alternative material for the WE, aside from the carbon-based WE in many recent studies, is due to its inertness and remarkable chemical and physical stability.32 Further, a few studies, such as Solchenbach et al. (2016) reported the use of Au as a (pseudo) RE,33 thereby providing a stable system. In recent decades, the potential stability has not been solely dependent on the intrinsic properties of the RE material but has also been influenced by the potentiostat's circuit and element configuration. The electrode configuration allowed the potential difference between the RE and WE to be controlled with minimal interference from the ohmic drop (iR drop) and minimized current flow to the RE, thereby supporting the establishment of a stable RE.32,34 Nonetheless, the optimization of the RWC electrode configuration for PCB-3T use was reported in our previous work, which employed ferricyanide as the objective solution.30

In addition, a miniaturized system was previously proposed to enhance the electroactive area ratio, electron-transfer kinetics, and reversibility.35 Therefore, miniaturization may improve the resolution of signal acquisition. In the previous work on PCB-3T optimization,30 it was proposed that, along with higher resolution, the reproducibility across the disposable PCB-3T sensors may be attributed to the three fixed electrodes maintained at a constant distance, whereas the conventional approach with constant electrode spacing was not easily maintained.

Redox profiling on PCB-3T: native and denatured Hb in a buffer

The PCB-3T system was subsequently used to examine the redox behavior of native and thermally denatured Hb, with particular emphasis on whether structural changes were accompanied by measurable changes in the electrochemical profile. Native and denatured Hb samples were analyzed under the same experimental conditions, and solvent-only controls were included to distinguish Hb-associated responses from its background. PBS was selected as the buffered reference matrix as it is commonly used to maintain proteins under in vitro conditions.

Electrochemical profiling on the label-free PCB-3T system resolved clear anodic and cathodic faradaic peaks without the addition of a redox mediator or Au electrode surface modification. Further, a comparable CV profile was observed for Hb and the ferricyanide system investigated in our previous complementary work,30 although a slight displacement of the peak potential was evident. This correspondence provides a useful electrochemical reference and raises the possibility that the redox response observed for Hb is associated with its redox-active heme iron center. However, the difference in peak potential and the complex protein environment should be considered when interpreting this similarity. Thus, the peak may be tentatively attributed to an electrochemically active heme-associated redox process, while further evidence would be required to confirm its specific assignment to the Fe2+/Fe3+ couple, as these iron ions are generally proposed to play an important role in Hb redox.36

The anodic response of native Hb in PBS, located approximately between +0.2 and +0.4 V (Fig. 4a) is also consistent with that previously reported by Rafiq et al. using a modified standard three-electrode.37 More importantly for the present comparison, the transition from native to denatured Hb was statistically more pronounced for the anodic peak potential than for the cathodic peak potential. Fig. 4b and c demonstrates the significance analysis on the transition of native-denatured Hb's redox profile, where Welch's analysis gave high significance for the anodic peak potential (p = 0.003) and lower significance in the cathodic potential (p = 0.171). Thus, within the present dataset, the anodic peak potential provides stronger discrimination between two conformational states of Hb than the cathodic peak potential.

The same qualitative direction of change was subsequently observed in a non-electrolyte solvent, ultrapure H2O, indicating that the predominance of the anodic shift was not restricted to PBS. The smaller statistical response for the cathodic potential should nevertheless be retained as an important finding rather than interpreted as the absence of Hb's specific cathodic process. The limited cathodic differentiation may reflect the combined effects of conformational changes, solvent environment, and the electrochemical accessibility of the heme center, but the available measurements in the current study are insufficient to distinguish these contributions.

Thermal denaturation is also likely to produce a partially unfolded state rather than the degree of unfolding achievable with stronger chemical denaturants. This consideration is important when interpreting the relatively subtle electrochemical changes. It might correspond to Narayan et al.38 that reported thermally denatured states may be ∼50% lower than chemically denatured states in the degree of unfolding of the same protein. The accompanying spectroscopic measurements were therefore used to provide structural context for the electrochemical observations. Besides, the relative electrochemical changes of denatured Hb from its native state calculated across the five biological replicates for Hb dissolved in PBS were 27.53% and 22.19% for anodic and cathodic Hb, respectively. However, these percentages may be interpreted as descriptive values rather than direct measures of the degree of molecular unfolding.

The solvent matrix plays a fundamental role in dictating the microenvironment surrounding the protein structure. Theoretically, less stable proteins potentially exhibit abnormal functionality, and unfavorable kinetics may alter Hb's swiftness and reversibility of the redox reaction.39 Therefore, the buffer solution may better maintain Hb's structural stability than ultrapure H2O. The different environments provided by the solvents can be observed from the symmetrical difference in the voltammograms (Fig. 4 and 5) and the detailed CV parameters in Table 1.

Redox profiling on PCB-3T: native and denatured Hb in a non-electrolyte

The electrochemical response in ultrapure H2O provides a complementary assessment of the PCB-3T platform because the matrix contains substantially fewer ions than PBS. As shown in Fig. 5, the optimized potential window for Hb in ultrapure H2O differed from that in PBS. During optimization, PBS required a narrower potential window to obtain stable and reproducible voltammograms, whereas extending the window resulted in excessive current responses. This is consistent with the greater conductivity and lower resistance of PBS relative to ultrapure H2O. The ionic condition of the solvent may influence the variability of the optimized potential window for CV evaluation.6,20,21

The profile was stacked over three technical replicates of five biological replicates for the optimized sample parameter.

The ability of PCB-3T to acquire a measurable response from ultrapure H2O is consistent with the previous report using the platform.30 In the present study, this capability enabled the evaluation of Hb without a supporting electrolyte. Therefore, for the first time, ultrapure H2O was used as a solvent for the electrochemical analysis of protein in this work. However, it should be emphasized that the present study was not intended to elucidate the electrochemical mechanisms of hemoglobin under physiologically relevant conditions. Rather, it represents a preliminary investigation aimed at demonstrating the potential of the PCB-3T platform as a label-free electrochemical system for discriminating between proteins with different structural conformations, using native and denatured hemoglobin as model analytes.

The high resistance of ultrapure H2O could introduce an associated ohmic (iR) drop that affects the electrochemical response. The iR drop refers to the potential distortion when an electric current flows through a medium with significant electrical resistance.32 This may be represented by the CV profiles of irreversible redox and slow electron transfer, and the EIS results revealed uncompensated solution resistance. In relation to the slow electron-transfer process, the CV profiles of Hb in ultrapure H2O (Fig. 5) exhibited a broader area under the anodic peak than those of Hb in PBS (Fig. 4). A broader potential peak difference () and the area under the peak might indicate slower electron transfer between the electrode and the electroactive species. Detailed CV quantitative parameters related to these were summarized in Table 1. This slow process behavior might be attributed to several factors: (1) the heme group, which is buried deep within the globular protein structure, limits the charge transfer; (2) the high resistance of ultrapure H2O, whereby the absence of ions may lower the peak due to reduced diffusion, which is necessary to drive the molecule toward the electrode surface.

CV profiles of Hb dissolved in ultrapure H2O in this study exhibit peaks in the region of negative potential near −0.2 V, which is similar to those reported by Toh et al. using a modified standard electrode (vs. Ag/AgCl),6 who studied Hb dissolved in 0.05 M PBS (pH 5.5 and 3.5) in a realistic biological system (in vivo study). Toh et al.6 interpreted the reduction peaks at potential intervals of −0.3 V and −0.15 V as most likely involving hydrogen ions (H+) in the protonation mechanism. Regarding the current work, these mechanisms are more likely to be involved in the Hb redox reaction in ultrapure H2O than in PBS, due to the abundance of hydrogen in ultrapure H2O. However, the proton-related interpretation should consequently be regarded as a hypothesis for future investigation rather than a mechanistic assignment.

Moreover, the appearance of a small peak at high potentials (Fig. 5), beyond the 1 V potential window, may be associated with enhanced mass deposition on the electrode surface. The high potential might induce dynamics within the sample-electrode system, prompting additional chemical reactions.39 Nevertheless, the present measurements do not provide sufficient evidence to distinguish among these possibilities, and the feature was therefore not used as a principal analytical peak.

In addition, the native-denatured Hb differentiation across the attempted scan rates (Fig. 5b and c) showed identical responses for both, exhibiting the same pattern in terms of potential peak difference (the difference between anodic and cathodic peak/, ) across scan rates. For denatured samples, values at scan rates of 0.050, 0.075, and 0.100 V s−1 were 0.29, 0.36, and 0.42 V, respectively, while for native Hb, they were 0.25, 0.33, and 0.44 V.

Concentration-dependent evaluations confirmed that analyte abundance significantly modulates faradaic peak currents (Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 6c, native Hb exhibited a slightly lower calibration slope than denatured Hb. Moreover, higher protein concentrations may increase the spatial density of electroactive species near the electrode surface, enhancing peak currents under mass-transport and charge-transfer control. Nonetheless, the evaluation of whether mass transport (diffusion) or charge transfer governed the mechanism, as illustrated by the CV profile in this study, may be more clearly supported by the EIS analysis in the following section. Notably, at the lowest evaluated concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1, the denatured Hb peak current signal was below the baseline threshold, prompting a future study to include a detailed analysis of the sensor's sensitivity when Hb is dissolved in a non-electrolyte medium, ultrapure H2O.

The transition from native to denatured Hb induced a statistically significant shift in the anodic peak potential, whereas cathodic shifts exhibited greater variance (quantitative parameters as provided in Table 1). Furthermore, denatured Hb consistently generated higher peak currents than native Hb in both H2O (Fig. 8) and PBS matrices (corroborated by chronoamperometric data in Fig. 14).

In accordance with the previous work, the shift between native and denatured Hb was also observed in a subtle degree of change according to Herzog et al. (2010).21 Herzog et al. studied the CV profiles of denatured Hb induced by a chemical denaturant in a liquid–liquid interface electrochemical system. He found a subtle shift in peak potential when compared to native Hb, with a difference of less than 0.05 V. Moreover, within the positive potential range of +0.8 to +0.1 V, the peak current decreased, with denatured Hb showing a lower peak current than native Hb. Contrarily, in this study, denatured Hb exhibits a higher peak current than native Hb in most cases.

Regarding the peak current difference reported by Herzog et al.,21 it is reasonable given the differences in the systems' mechanisms. In a liquid–liquid interface system, the interface was a soft boundary between two immiscible electrolyte solutions. In such a configuration, the primary observed mechanism in the redox analysis may be ion transfer across phases, and the native structure of Hb is crucial for facilitating ion and electron transfer through its organized electrostatic and hydrophobic domains. Therefore, denaturation caused the hydrophobic region to unfold. It may disrupt the interfacial alignment and the local environment that supports anion transfer. Thus, it may significantly decrease the peak current.

In contrast, in a three-electrode system with solid electrodes (PCB-3T), the electron-transfer mechanism may be more clearly recorded (although the ion-transfer mechanism, included in the overall mass-transfer mechanism, also contributes to the CV profiles). In its native state, the heme is buried and less accessible, leading to slower electron transfer. Meanwhile, denatured Hb exposes more heme to the electrode surface, thereby increasing electron transfer. Notably, in-depth analysis of the mechanism is provided in EIS-based characterization.

Furthermore, the redox reversibility of Hb is reflected in one of its properties: the difference between cathodic and anodic peaks in both peak potential and/or peak current. In most of the previous studies, ΔEp typically ranges from 0.05 to 0.4 V depending on the solvent and environmental factors, though large separations of up to ∼1 V have been observed in a previous study of Pakapongpan et al..20 The potential peak difference and the other CV parameters yielded in the current work are provided in Table 1.

Furthermore, baseline results (blank solution) in the EIS analysis were difficult to obtain due to the very high resistance. In most studies, using ultrapure H2O as the solvent for Hb analysis is considered impractical. The dielectric constant of pure H2O at ∼20 °C is 80 (roughly 80 times higher than that of a vacuum).40 Meanwhile, despite the relatively high conductivity of PBS compared to ultrapure H2O, PBS was used at a very low molarity; therefore, a high resistance was observed in most EIS analyses for the blank solution. Consequently, the two matrices impose different electrical boundary conditions on the PCB-3T sensor.

Correspondingly, such environments, combined with protein dynamics, may not possess a Randles circuit under ideal conditions. The circuit approximation for the fitting analysis of the Bode and Nyquist plots is shown in Fig. 11 and 12. Modified versions of the Randles circuit were proposed for approximation. Fig. 11 shows the circuit fit for Hb dissolved in PBS, and Fig. 12 for Hb dissolved in ultrapure H2O. The circuit fit was provided for both EIS measurements, pre- and post-CV scanning, to further examine the electrochemical behavior and its transition. Each PBS and ultrapure H2O has a different pH and a set of characteristics, mirroring the differences in the approximated equivalent circuit fit.

The parameters from the approximated circuit fit may help elucidate the mechanism during CV scanning, including whether charge transfer and/or diffusion-controlled mass transfer occur in the Hb redox mechanism. Overall, as suggested by Zeng et al.,41 an electrochemical signal may not be automatically assigned to a single electronic event; rather, redox kinetics can depend on many factors, including the charge-transfer dynamics, interfacial interactions, and the local chemical environment.

Prior to in-depth analysis of the EIS profile, the supporting results from the CV assessment indicated a linear relationship (R2 ≥ 0.9) between the square root of the scan rate and the peak current across all matrix conditions and for both native and denatured Hb (Fig. 10). This can provide the first insight into the substantial diffusion mechanism, which was one of the mechanisms governing the CV. Further, as reflected in the different slopes and the reachable peak current for each condition (Fig. 10), and as indicated by the changes in the EIS profile (Fig. 9), these differences may reflect changes and dynamics in charge transfer and diffusion.42 Hence, the details of the EIS circuit fit may elucidate the contribution of each factor to the Hb electrochemical mechanism.

The non-ideality was suggested by the ideality factor (α) provided in Table 2, which was not close to α = 1. Overall, the absence of an additional supporting electrolyte was expected to add a diffusion mechanism, represented by the Warburg component in all circuit approximations of pre-CV EIS (Fig. 11 and 12). This EIS evaluation was proportional to the current response, as a function of the square root of the scan rate, indicating a diffusion mechanism.

Table 2 provides parameters from the approximated model of the Hb circuit and reveals significant differences in interfacial charge-transfer dynamics and diffusion behavior depending on the matrix buffer (0.005 M PBS, pH 7.4 vs. ultrapure H2O, pH 6.8) and protein conformation (native Hb vs. denatured Hb).

Following CV potential sweeps (post-CV EIS), the interfacial phenomenon changes substantially from the pre-CV EIS. Overall, this shows the suppression of mass-transport diffusion in favor of surface-confined charge transfer, which may highlight successful redox reactions during voltammetry scans. However, the system in ultrapure H2O remains diffusion-dominated post-CV, as indicated by large Warburg (W) values.

Furthermore, post-CV conditioning in PBS led to a notable reduction in primary charge-transfer resistance (R2), dropping from 19.60 to 7.57 kΩ for native Hb, accompanied by a decrease in the phase exponent (α2) from 0.93 to 0.64. The possibility is that the shift toward lower values might signify increased micro-roughness, inhomogeneity, or continuous molecular restructuring of the protein film on the sensor surface. Notably, future validation with electronic structure analysis is needed.

Overall, despite the high solvent resistance due to the absence of an additional electrolyte, a well-defined EIS profile of hemoglobin dissolved in the respective solvents was obtained. These findings confirm that CV conditioning activates interfacial redox channels and provides clear impedance signatures that differentiate the conformational states of native and denatured Hb. In particular, for ultrapure H2O, the very high resistance that may cause iR drop among the electrodes during the electrochemical process may contribute to a higher resistance than the capacitive characteristic displayed by the Nyquist plot. However, the miniaturized three-electrode system of the PCB-3T keeps the iR drop significantly smaller where it is nearly negligible when comparing to a conventional setup. Additionally, the configuration of the RWC electrodes may have effectively lowered the iR drop.32,34

FTIR is sensitive to hydrogen bonding between different secondary-structure moieties, whereas far-UV CD is more sensitive to the orientation and environment of side chains.

The introduction of a label-free approach in the current work, followed by sample incubation before electrochemical analysis, conditions the sample on the platform for 5 min prior to the experiment. Correspondingly, a planar electrode configuration allows spontaneous self-assembly of analytes on the electrode surface. Using linear regression IUPAC formula to calculate the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ), which are mainly based on the steady-state current occurring in a certain interval of time during a chronoamperometry experiment (Fig. 15), the values were obtained at 5.84 and 17.71 mg mL−1 (denatured Hb) and 5.35 and 16.20 mg mL−1 (native Hb), respectively. The slope parameters calculated from these values provided sensitivities of 1.234 × 10−6 A (mg mL−1)−1 for denatured Hb and 1.123 × 10−6 A (mg mL−1)−1 for native Hb. Maintaining such closely aligned metrics across different conformational states is highly significant. Protein denaturation typically alters surface charges and structures, which causes extreme signal variance or calibration shifts on traditional sensors. The nominal 9% variance in the quantification threshold proves that the platform's underlying sensing mechanism relies on fundamental, macro-abundance interfacial mass-transport or surface-adsorption properties that remain robust regardless of protein unfolding.

Based on standard clinical haematology guidelines established by the World Health Organization (WHO), the normal adult human Hb reference value ranges from about 12.1 to 17.2 g dL−1, which corresponds to 121–172 mg mL−1, and for mild anaemia of 110–119 mg mL−1 and extremely severe anaemia is below 80 mg mL−1.47 Thus, in the current work, achieving milligram-per-millilitre resolution through a label-free mechanism on a uniform metallurgy architecture demonstrates that the PCB-3T Sensor can capture faradaic signals within those specific concentration ranges. This dual-state monitoring approach provides an initial framework for investigating decentralized screening strategies and protein conformation dynamics. Furthermore, the capacity to resolve target concentrations across structurally diverse states highlights the physical stability of the planar electrode design under different protein configurations. This consistent response (Fig. 15a and b) indicates that the system is well-suited for further optimization in label-free biosensing environments where structural variations frequently complicate signal processing.

In addition, the analysis of the chronoamperometry profile shows that the late-time current response (the current response at each concentration at the end of the measurement window) demonstrates the concentration-dependent chronoamperometric response (Fig. 15).

Conclusions

The PCB-3T system successfully recorded the electrochemical profiles of Hb, which proposes high sensitivity in a label-free test, particularly in a non-electrolyte, ultrapure H2O. A pair of redox peaks was clearly demonstrated, consistent with Hb's strong conformational stability maintained in the PCB-3T horizontal configuration which is intrinsically redox-active. Despite the subtle redox transition between native and denatured Hb that may correspond to partial thermal denaturation, the instability of denatured Hb was indicated by the broader distribution (CI) of the peak current and potential compared to native Hb. A higher mean peak current of denatured Hb may be attributed to greater exposure of the unfolded electroactive heme site, consistent with the spectroscopy-based evaluation for the conformational change. These findings, we believe, provide valuable input to related studies, particularly regarding the scope of advanced characterization of biomolecules.

Although variations in Hb concentration were evaluated down to a baseline of 0.1 mg mL−1, showing a strong linear correspondence between the analyte concentration and the current decay (in chronoamperometric analysis), the calculated LOD and LOQ boundaries across both structural states remained highly congruent. This close mathematical alignment indicates that the PCB-3T Sensor exhibits excellent stability and provides a strong case for utilizing this platform to track conformational changes via chronoamperometry. Furthermore, the study offers insight into future analysis on solvent variability to specific controlled oxidation states of the physiological state of Hb.
