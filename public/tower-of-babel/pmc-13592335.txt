Integrated Multidisciplinary and Digital Innovation in Polytrauma: Optimizing Whole-Process Emergency Models for Secondary Hospitals
Authors: Jianhua Zhao, Zihui Zhao, Yanhui Li, Chunhua Fang, Jingdong Li, Wenliang Xiao, Jiaxi Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolytrauma, characterized by multi-system injuries and life-threatening complexity, poses significant challenges to healthcare systems, particularly in resource-constrained secondary hospitals. This narrative review synthesizes global research on polytrauma management, focusing on workflow optimization, digital innovation, and MDT adaptation tailored to secondary hospitals. Based on a systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data (January 2014 – December 2024), 63 studies were included and analyzed. We identify key barriers—including prolonged diagnostic intervals, insufficient inter-departmental coordination, and reliance on traditional emergency processes with redundant steps—that disproportionately delay door-to-CT times, time to definitive intervention, and contribute to preventable mortality in secondary hospital settings. Emerging solutions such as simplified MDT frameworks, telemedicine-enabled collaboration, and AI-assisted triage show promise, yet current literature lacks practical, context-sensitive frameworks addressing the unique constraints of secondary hospitals, including staffing shortages and limited surgical capabilities. To address these gaps, we propose a hybrid model centred on four core elements: (1) a core MDT team (emergency physicians, trauma nurses, and key specialists) for rapid assessment and damage control; (2) an optimized green-channel process with clear activation criteria and streamlined workflows; (3) digital decision-support systems (eg, 5G-based platforms, process mining algorithms) to enable real-time information sharing and coordination; and (4) regional referral linkages with higher-level trauma centres to ensure timely transfer of patients requiring specialized surgical interventions. Future research should prioritize validation studies—using both quantitative and qualitative approaches—of process optimization strategies, development of context-specific training programs, and cost-effectiveness analyses of technology-driven interventions. This synthesis provides actionable insights for advancing secondary hospital trauma systems, ultimately reducing preventable mortality and bridging disparities in global trauma care equity.Keywords: polytrauma, secondary hospitals, multidisciplinary collaboration, digital innovation, process optimization, emergency workflow

Introduction

Multiple trauma, as one of the main forms of severe trauma, is an important cause of disability and death in modern society, especially against the backdrop of frequent high-energy injury events such as traffic accidents. Polytrauma, also referred to as multiple trauma, is defined as simultaneous or sequential injuries to two or more anatomical regions or organ systems caused by a single injurious event, with at least one injury being potentially life-threatening. Trauma remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with an estimated 20% of trauma-related deaths considered potentially preventable with timely and appropriate care. In China, the three-tier hospital classification system designates secondary hospitals as regional institutions typically serving county‑level or district populations, with bed capacities ranging from 100 to 500. These hospitals constitute the backbone of China’s grassroots healthcare system but often operate without the round‑the‑clock specialist coverage, advanced imaging capabilities, or immediate surgical backup available in tertiary trauma centres. With the acceleration of industrialization and urbanization, the incidence of various accidental events has increased significantly, resulting in an increasing number of multiple traumas with life-threatening conditions and posing severe challenges to the global public health system.1,2 Patients with multiple trauma often present with critical conditions involving injuries to multiple anatomical sites or organs. These injuries frequently interact and exacerbate one another, causing rapid haemodynamic and physiological deterioration. Consequently, treatment is extremely challenging and requires prompt, coordinated intervention.3 Timely and efficient first aid is the key to improving the prognosis of patients with multiple trauma. Studies have shown that effective early intervention—such as rapid haemorrhage control, early administration of tranexamic acid, and timely decompression of life‑threatening intracranial or thoracic injuries—can significantly reduce the mortality rate.4,5

However, the treatment of multiple injuries is a complex systematic project that requires close connection among multiple links such as pre‑hospital emergency care, in‑hospital emergency treatment, surgery, intensive care, and rehabilitation.5,6 This coordination is particularly challenging in non-top-level trauma centers such as secondary hospitals, where limitations in medical resources, professional expertise, and equipment configuration, together with traditional emergency treatment processes that often involve redundant steps and poor information flow, compound the difficulties of emergency care. Crucially, the barriers facing secondary hospitals are not merely a matter of fewer resources; they represent a fundamentally different decision-making environment. Unlike tertiary trauma centres, where specialised surgical and imaging services are available around the clock, secondary hospitals must operate within a paradigm of constrained choice—where immediate access to neurosurgery, interventional radiology, or on-site blood banking cannot be assumed. In this context, simply adapting tertiary-centre protocols (eg, routine whole-body CT for all polytrauma patients, or immediate operative exploration for all haemodynamically unstable patients) may be neither feasible nor appropriate. What is needed, therefore, is not a scaled-down copy of high-level centre pathways, but a distinct, pragmatic model that explicitly accounts for resource limitations, builds on available local capabilities, and embeds clear escalation and referral pathways—ensuring that patients receive the right care at the right time, even when definitive services are not on site.

Traditional emergency treatment processes may have problems such as redundant links, poor information flow, and insufficient coordination, which lead to an extended time for patient diagnosis. As a result, patients cannot receive timely and effective treatment within the optimal time window, thereby affecting the success rate of treatment and patient prognosis.7,8 Therefore, under the existing conditions at the grass-roots level, it is of great theoretical and practical significance to construct a set of rapid and efficient innovative models for the entire emergency treatment process through process transformation and optimization, integrating medical resources, streamlining diagnosis and treatment links. This can shorten the diagnosis time of patients with multiple injuries from traffic accidents, achieve rapid assessment, accurate diagnosis, and timely treatment, improve the efficiency of emergency rescue, minimize the impact of process factors on treatment, and provide feasible suggestions and methodological basis for the clinical treatment of patients with multiple injuries from traffic accidents in secondary hospitals. For emergency departments (EDs) in these settings, the challenge is twofold: they must simultaneously perform rapid triage and initial stabilisation with limited specialist backup, while making time-critical decisions about transfer or definitive surgical intervention—often without real-time access to advanced imaging or immediate operating room availability.

Against this background, this review has three interrelated aims. First, we synthesise current evidence on polytrauma emergency care—focusing on process optimisation, multidisciplinary collaboration, and digital innovation—with particular attention to studies relevant to secondary hospitals and resource-limited settings. Second, we critically analyse the deficiencies and gaps in existing research, identifying why tertiary-centre models cannot simply be adapted to lower-resource environments. Third, drawing on the available evidence and our own practice experience, we propose a pragmatic, whole-process emergency model tailored to the specific constraints and capabilities of secondary hospitals—centred on a core MDT team, streamlined green‑channel processes, digital decision support, and regional referral linkages. Through this integrated approach, we aim to provide actionable recommendations for clinicians, administrators, and policymakers seeking to improve polytrauma outcomes in secondary hospitals.

Scope of this review: This review focuses specifically on the emergency department (ED) phase of the polytrauma care continuum—from patient arrival to initial stabilisation, diagnostic workup, and disposition decisions (transfer to the operating room, intensive care unit, or higher‑level facility). We do not attempt to comprehensively cover pre‑hospital care, rehabilitation, or long‑term outcomes, except where these interface directly with ED decision‑making. The primary focus is on secondary hospitals in China and comparable resource‑limited settings, where the constraints of staffing, equipment, and specialist availability fundamentally shape clinical practice. Methodologically, this is a narrative review based on a systematic literature search (detailed in the “Literature Search Strategy” section), synthesising evidence from 63 studies published between 2014 and 2024.

Literature Search Strategy

Given that this is a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, we did not follow PRISMA guidelines or conduct a formal quantitative synthesis. Nevertheless, to ensure transparency and reproducibility, we performed a systematic search of the literature. The following databases were searched: PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data. The search period covered January 2014 through December 2024.

The search strategy combined terms related to the core topics: (“polytrauma” OR “multiple trauma” OR “severe trauma”) AND (“emergency process” OR “emergency workflow” OR “treatment model” OR “first-aid process”) AND (“multidisciplinary” OR “MDT” OR “multidisciplinary teamwork” OR “collaborative care”) AND (“secondary hospital” OR “primary hospital” OR “resource-limited setting” OR “non-trauma center” OR “grass-roots hospital”).

We included peer-reviewed original research (including cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional surveys, and case series with ≥3 cases), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, clinical guidelines, and expert consensus statements. All included publications were in English or Chinese. We excluded case reports with fewer than 3 cases, single-system injury studies that did not involve a polytrauma context, conference abstracts without full-text availability, and opinion pieces without explicit methodological description.

Two independent reviewers (Y.L. and Z.Z.) screened titles and abstracts for relevance, then retrieved and reviewed full texts for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (J.Z). The reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were also manually checked for additional eligible studies. A total of 63 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this synthesis.

It should be acknowledged that, while our search included English‑language international databases (PubMed and Web of Science), the inclusion of Chinese databases (CNKI and Wanfang) reflects our deliberate focus on the secondary hospital context in China, where the majority of the authors’ clinical and operational experience is grounded. This geographical anchoring is not a limitation but a contextual strength—it allows us to develop recommendations that are grounded in the real‑world constraints of the Chinese healthcare system, which may differ substantially from those in high‑income countries. Nevertheless, we have drawn extensively on international evidence from Europe, North America, and other Asian regions to inform our conceptual framework, and we explicitly note where findings from these settings may or may not be generalisable to secondary hospitals in China. For the international readership of this journal, we believe this transparent contextual framing enhances rather than diminishes the utility of our review.

Clinical Characteristics and Treatment Challenges of Multiple Trauma

Multiple trauma refers to the simultaneous or sequential occurrence of injuries in two or more anatomical regions or organs of the body under the action of single or combined injury-causing factors, with at least one of the injuries potentially life-threatening. Its clinical features are complex and diverse, posing a severe challenge to the emergency medical system. According to the summary by Sun Haichen et al,3 multiple trauma mainly presents the following characteristics:

(1) Complex injury mechanisms: High-energy injuries such as traffic accidents, falls from heights, and crush injuries often involve multiple injury mechanisms, including impact, shear, compression, and blast shock waves. These may lead to the coexistence of various types of injuries, such as those to bones, organs, blood vessels, and nerves, in the same patient, increasing the complexity of injury assessment and diagnosis.

(2) Severe and rapidly changing injuries: Multiple injuries are not simply the superposition of various injuries but involve an injury exacerbation effect. The overall severity of the injury is often higher than the sum of individual injuries. Patients may experience life-threatening conditions such as shock, respiratory failure, and impaired consciousness in a short period. The condition can change rapidly, requiring medical staff to have a high level of vigilance and rapid response ability.

(3) Severe physiological disorders of the body: Multiple injuries not only cause damage to the local anatomical structure but also trigger complex systemic stress responses, such as the inflammatory cascade, coagulation dysfunction, and immunosuppression. These responses interact with each other, potentially leading to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), which further exacerbates the condition and is one of the important causes of patient death.

(4) Difficult diagnosis and prone to missed or misdiagnosis: As multiple injuries may mask each other’s symptoms, and patients often have impaired consciousness or severe pain and are unable to describe their conditions accurately, it is extremely challenging to comprehensively and accurately evaluate all injuries and make a diagnosis in the early stage. The experience and professional limitations of the attending doctors may also lead to missed or misdiagnosed cases, delaying crucial treatment opportunities.

(5) Contradiction between treatment principles and treatment sequence: Multiple trauma involves multiple systems, and injuries of different natures have different treatment principles and priorities. For example, life-threatening bleeding requires immediate hemostasis, while craniocerebral injury requires maintaining stable intracranial pressure. However, established trauma guidelines such as the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) programme provide a clear prioritisation framework—the xABCDE approach (where “x” denotes control of eXsanguinating external haemorrhage as the first priority, followed by Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure)—to guide clinicians in identifying and treating the most immediately life‑threatening conditions first.3,9 In situations where resources are limited or multiple injuries require urgent treatment simultaneously, adherence to this structured framework helps clinicians avoid being divided by competing clinical priorities.10

(6) High incidence of infections and other complications: Extensive tissue damage, massive blood loss, shock, surgical trauma, and impaired immune function in the body make patients with multiple injuries highly susceptible to complications such as infections, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). These complications significantly increase the mortality and disability rates of patients.

In response to these characteristics of multiple injuries, the traditional specialty-centered diagnosis and treatment model appears inadequate. Patients need to receive initial assessment and stabilization in the emergency department, and then may need to be transferred to the radiology department for a whole-body scan. Subsequently, different specialists will conduct consultations to determine the surgical plan, after which the patients will be transferred to the operating room. After the operation, they will be admitted to the ICU for monitoring and finally transferred to the general ward or for rehabilitation. This process involves multiple departments and steps. Any delay or poor coordination in any step may lead to an extended treatment time and a worsened prognosis for the patients.

In addition to the complexity of the diseases themselves, the problems in the current treatment of multiple injuries also include deficiencies in the medical system and management. As described in the abstract, (1) the injuries are severe and the condition progresses rapidly, leaving a very short time window for rescue;3,11 (2) specialist physicians often focus on their own professional fields, lacking comprehensive assessment and control of the overall condition of patients with multiple injuries. Their comprehensive treatment capabilities need to be improved, which may easily lead to delayed diagnosis and missed treatment opportunities;7 (3) multiple injuries involve multiple disciplines, and the traditional consultation model takes a long time, reducing the treatment efficiency;5,6 (4) the more the injured sites, the more complex the condition and the higher the mortality rate;1,3 (5) the occurrence of any systemic complications may exacerbate the condition, and improper treatment may lead to death at any time;3,11 (6) it is particularly noteworthy that the treatment model for multiple injuries in secondary hospitals (including secondary hospitals) still needs in-depth exploration, and their trauma first-aid capabilities need to be urgently improved.7,11

Therefore, how to break the traditional barriers among specialties, optimize the emergency diagnosis and treatment process, achieve coordinated cooperation among multiple disciplines and departments, and establish a rapid and efficient innovative model for the entire emergency treatment process is the key to improving the success rate of treatment for patients with multiple injuries and reducing the mortality and disability rates, which is also the focus of this study (Figure 1). Multidisciplinary management is a well‑established principle in trauma care, as reflected in ATLS guidelines which emphasise coordinated team‑based assessment and treatment.10

Research Status of Optimization of First-Aid Process for Multiple Injuries

To address the challenges in the treatment of multiple injuries, scholars and medical institutions both at home and abroad have conducted extensive exploration and practice in optimizing the emergency treatment process and innovating the treatment mode. These studies mainly focus on shortening the treatment time, improving the treatment efficiency, enhancing patient prognosis, strengthening multidisciplinary collaboration, and leveraging new technologies.7,8

Current Research Status in China

Research on the optimization of the emergency treatment process for multiple trauma in China started relatively late, but it has developed rapidly in recent years. In particular, significant progress has been made in building the trauma treatment system and implementing the multidisciplinary collaboration model.

Firstly, process optimization is a direct means to improve emergency rescue efficiency. Multiple studies have aimed to shorten the patients’ stay time in the emergency department and the time to receive key diagnosis and treatment through the review and redesign of the emergency process. For example, Wang Huizhen et al12 found that optimizing the emergency care process for severe multiple injuries could significantly shorten the ambulance arrival time, surgical rescue time, time from blood collection to blood transfusion, and hospital stay, while improving the rescue success rate and patient satisfaction. Liu Feng et al13 analysis also showed that optimizing the trauma emergency process could significantly shorten the time for completing various rescue processes and the total emergency rescue time, and improve the emergency rescue success rate and patient satisfaction. Ma Rui et al14 optimized the emergency care model based on the process path and found that it could shorten the time for tracheal intubation, intravenous medication, triage, and rescue, improve the rescue success rate, and reduce the incidence of doctor-patient disputes. The studies by Zhao Weiling et al15 and Pei Junqiang et al16 also confirmed that optimizing the emergency care process or adopting the process structured reorganization model could improve the timeliness and success rate of rescue for patients with severe multiple injuries and reduce the incidence of adverse events. These studies generally believe that streamlining and optimizing the key links of the emergency process can effectively improve the treatment efficiency.

Secondly, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) model is widely recognized as the core strategy for addressing the complexity of multiple injuries. Multiple injuries involve multiple disciplines such as orthopedics, general surgery, neurosurgery, thoracic surgery, urology, critical care medicine, anesthesiology, and radiology. The traditional model of sequential consultations among departments is inefficient. The MDT model emphasizes that experts from relevant disciplines should jointly participate in the assessment and formulation of diagnosis and treatment plans at an early stage after the patient’s admission, enabling information sharing and collaborative decision-making.1,2,4

Du Zhe et al5 conducted an applied study at the Trauma Treatment Center of Peking University People’s Hospital, which showed that treating severely traumatized patients with the MDT model can significantly improve the treatment capacity and level, compensate for the over-specialization in large general hospitals, and enhance the treatment effectiveness. Chang Jie et al17 further explored the application of the digital platform combined with the MDT model and found that it can effectively shorten key time nodes such as the arrival time of the multidisciplinary team, the time to complete CT examinations, and the retention time in the emergency room, improve the success rate of rescue, and reduce the mortality rate. Shi Lei et al18 also showed in a study at the Second People’s Hospital of Pingxiang that the MDT nursing model can effectively shorten the treatment time for patients with severe trauma, increase the compliance rate of the green-channel time window, and reduce the mortality rate. These studies strongly support the value of the MDT model in improving the treatment efficiency of multiple injuries and enhancing the prognosis. Domestic scholars have also explored the application of MDT in specific types of emergencies, such as chest pain,19 acute ischemic stroke, aortic dissection,20 and acute myocardial infarction.21–23 These experiences provide references for the construction of the MDT model for multiple injuries. Yu Huifang et al24–26 and Qian Hongmei et al27 emphasized the importance of the nursing process of the trauma team and the MDT nursing model from the perspective of the nursing team, believing that it helps to improve the capabilities of the nursing team and the rescue efficiency.

Furthermore, constructing an integrated and chain-style emergency rescue model is another important research direction in China. This model aims to integrate pre-hospital and in-hospital emergency rescue, surgery, ICU and other links into a seamlessly connected whole. A study on the application of the “whole-process integrated model within the time window” proposed by Xia Weichao et al28 in patients with multiple injuries and hemorrhagic shock showed that this model can significantly shorten the emergency diagnosis and treatment time, improve coagulation function, and increase the 1-year survival rate. Tang Manli29 explored the application effect of the chain-style treatment process in the nursing of severe multiple injuries and believed that it can improve nursing efficiency and patient outcomes. Ye Fang et al30 and Jin Xiaoxiang et al9 explored the trauma center treatment model led by the EICU (Emergency Intensive Care Unit) or the ICU trauma unit and found that this model can effectively shorten the treatment time and reduce the mortality rate. Jian Zhen et al31 studied the impact of the standardized trauma emergency rescue system on the treatment of severe trauma and found that the standardized system can shorten the emergency rescue time, the length of stay in the emergency department, and the time for auxiliary examinations, and reduce the misdiagnosis rate and mortality rate. Li Tao et al32 explored the application effect of the emergency trauma surgery model in the treatment of multiple injuries and believed that it can shorten the pre-operative time and the length of hospital stay, and reduce complications and mortality. These studies indicate that integrating the dispersed treatment links to form a collaborative and efficient treatment system is an effective way to improve the treatment level of multiple injuries.

In addition, the concept of Damage Control has been widely applied in the treatment of multiple trauma. The studies of Xie Deping33 and Xu Tianjie34 both emphasized the value of damage control techniques in the treatment of critical multiple trauma. This technique advocates performing limited surgeries to control bleeding and contamination when the patient’s physiological state is unstable, and then performing definitive surgeries after the patient’s physiological state improves, which reflects the principle of “saving lives first and treating injuries later”. Lin Hao et al35 and Tang Zhiqing et al36 also emphasized the importance of prioritizing the treatment of life-threatening injuries and combining comprehensive intensive care in the treatment of patients with severe traumatic brain injury combined with multiple trauma. These concepts provide guidance for the decision-making sequence in the emergency treatment process of multiple trauma.

The application of information technology in the emergency treatment of multiple injuries has also attracted attention. Liu Feng et al13 proposed that a smart hospital information platform could be established through 5G technology to solve the problems in trauma emergency treatment. Tian Wei et al37 explored the process optimization and informatization construction of the green channel for emergency multiple injuries. They believed that relying on the information system construction could improve the collection of patients’ medical information, gradually establish an integrated information support system for pre-hospital and in-hospital care, and improve the overall treatment quality. Although the application of artificial intelligence in emergency trauma radiology is still in the exploratory stage,38 its potential in auxiliary diagnosis and process optimization cannot be ignored.

However, the construction of the domestic multiple trauma treatment system still faces challenges. Jiang Shirong et al7 pointed out that a complete theoretical system for multiple trauma treatment has not been established in China, resulting in a relatively high mortality rate. Zhang Fei8 also emphasized that the development of medical institutions in China is unbalanced, and there is an urgent need to establish a regional trauma treatment system centered on trauma centers. Especially for non-trauma centers such as secondary hospitals, how to effectively integrate resources, improve capabilities, and integrate into the regional system is an issue that needs to be urgently addressed. Although existing studies have proposed models such as process optimization and MDT, there are relatively few studies on highly operable full-process innovation models tailored to the characteristics of secondary hospitals.

Current Research Status Abroad

Internationally, especially in high-income countries (HICs), the establishment of the treatment system for multiple injuries started relatively early, and a relatively mature model has been formed, which is continuously optimized and improved.

The establishment of a trauma care system is an important cornerstone of international multiple trauma care. Mclellan BA (2009) emphasized that timely and effective trauma care measures must be based on optimized trauma grading and treatment centers. Dijkink S1 conducted an in-depth study on the trauma care systems in the Netherlands and other regions, analyzed the system organization, processes, and outcomes, and emphasized the role of centralized care and standardized protocols in improving prognosis. Dinesh Bagaria et al2 analyzed the challenges and suggestions for trauma systems in Asian countries, pointed out that the lack of legislation, stable funding, and a dedicated leading institution were the main obstacles, and emphasized the importance of the trauma quality improvement process. These studies indicate that a sound regional trauma care system, including trauma centers of different levels and clear transfer processes, is a prerequisite for improving the overall treatment level of multiple trauma.

Multidisciplinary teamwork (MDT) is also the core model of international trauma care. Delatorre39 detailed the composition of the multidisciplinary team in the treatment of polytrauma patients and the responsibilities of each member, emphasizing the importance of teamwork. Advancing A M Lasinski40 explored the role of advanced practice providers (APPs) in the trauma team, believing that they can improve the quality of care, patient turnover rate, and reduce costs, which reflects the refinement of team composition and division of responsibilities. Evaluation A B Durojaiye et al41 analyzed the multidisciplinary teamwork model in pediatric trauma care using electronic health record (EHR) data and found that the higher the degree of collaboration (such as forming a “fully connected” network model), the shorter the patients’ stay in the emergency department, suggesting the importance of information sharing and collaborative work. Goossen K et al42 evaluated the participation of MDT in the development process of the German polytrauma guidelines, reflecting the importance of MDT at the level of guideline and process development. These studies confirm the crucial position of MDT in international trauma care from different perspectives.

Process modeling and optimization using quantitative methods is a distinctive area of international research. Jiacun Wang43 and JunWang et al44 employed Stochastic Timed Petri Nets (STPNs) to model and simulate emergency department patient flow, analysing resource requirements, service durations, and staff allocation to reduce patient waiting time.

Adhicandra I et al45 combined the priority queue algorithm and reinforcement learning to optimize hospital queuing management to improve the priority ranking and timely care of emergency patients. Davari Fereshteh et al46 compared the effectiveness of process mining and simulation models in identifying bottlenecks in the emergency department and optimizing the patient process, and found that these tools can help make data-driven decisions and reduce waiting time. Advanced T J Flottemesch et al47 developed a mathematical model of patient flow in the emergency department for analyzing congestion, peaks, and operational efficiency. These quantitative studies provide scientific tools and methodologies for understanding and optimizing the emergency process, and are of reference significance for identifying bottlenecks in the process of secondary hospitals.

International research has also provided some experiences for the treatment of multiple trauma in non-top-level trauma centers or resource-constrained environments. Picetti E et al48 published the consensus and clinical recommendations of the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) on the early management of patients with isolated severe traumatic brain injury in hospitals without neurosurgical capabilities, emphasizing how to perform initial stabilization and safe transfer under limited resources. Richard Fleet et al49 compared the trauma mortality rates between rural emergency departments and urban trauma centers in Quebec, Canada, and found that the pre-hospital or emergency department mortality rate of patients in rural emergency departments was higher, highlighting the challenges faced by non-trauma centers. Latifi Rifat et al conducted a descriptive study on the emergency and trauma care readiness of 42 public hospitals in Albania and found that many hospitals had insufficient staff training, lack of equipment and protocols, which was similar to the situation faced by many secondary hospitals, suggesting the importance of strengthening training and resource allocation. These studies directly address the problems of resource and capacity limitations that secondary hospitals may face.

Technology application also plays an increasingly important role in international trauma care. Understanding A J Moy et al50 explored the impact of electronic health records (EHR) and workflow fragmentation on the documentation burden of emergency department clinicians, emphasizing the necessity of designing an EHR system that is coordinated with clinical workflows. Sabeena Jalal et al explored the role of artificial intelligence in emergency and trauma radiology, suggesting that AI is expected to assist in image analysis, relieve the workload of radiologists, and improve efficiency. Morgan Tripod et al studied the application of telemedicine in the transfer of hand trauma patients and found that telemedicine can reduce unnecessary transfers and transportation costs, and improve the efficiency of specialized care, which has important implications for the referral of patients from secondary hospitals to higher-level centers.

International research has also accumulated rich experience in the standardization and quality control of trauma treatment. Michael Frink et al reviewed the management of multiple traumas and emergency rooms, emphasizing the importance of structured assessment and treatment following the A-B - C-D - E principle and the application of rapid diagnostic methods such as FAST ultrasound. Bedabrata Sarkar et al51 reported the application of the Performance Improvement and Patient Safety Program of the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma in mature trauma centers and found that multi-faceted improvement measures could significantly reduce the in-hospital mortality of severely injured patients, highlighting the importance of continuous quality improvement. Wolfgang Parsch et al compared the process quality and outcome indicators of multiple trauma treatment during and outside working hours in German level-I trauma centers and found that standardized processes and sufficient resources could ensure high-quality treatment around the clock. These experiences are of reference significance for secondary hospitals to establish standardized processes and quality management systems.

Overall, international research has provided valuable experience in areas such as the construction of trauma care systems, the application of the MDT model, quantitative analysis of patient flow, and treatment strategies in resource-constrained environments. However, most of these studies are based on the practices of high-income countries (HICs) or top-level trauma centers. How to effectively transplant their models and experiences into the specific environment of secondary hospitals in China requires in-depth research and localization adaptation in combination with the actual situation.

Beyond the individual studies cited above, it is important to recognise the broader contributions of several major international trauma and emergency medicine groups that have shaped the field. The American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma (ACS‑COT) has been instrumental in establishing trauma centre verification standards, the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) programme, and the Performance Improvement and Patient Safety (PIPS) framework, all of which have informed quality improvement in trauma systems worldwide.51 In Europe, the European Society for Trauma and Emergency Surgery (ESTES) and the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) have produced key guidelines and consensus statements on polytrauma management, including recommendations for resource‑limited settings.48 The German Trauma Registry (DGU) has provided invaluable data on process quality and outcomes across level‑I trauma centres, offering benchmarks that, while not directly transferable to secondary hospitals, provide aspirational targets for process improvement. In Asia, the Asian Trauma Care Forum and national trauma societies in Japan, South Korea, and India have contributed important context‑specific insights, particularly on trauma system development in middle‑income countries.2 Our review draws on these collective contributions while acknowledging that the evidence base for secondary hospitals—particularly in China—remains underdeveloped, underscoring the need for the context‑specific research agenda we outline later in this manuscript.

Shortcomings of Existing Research and the Particularities of Secondary Hospitals

Although extensive research has been conducted both domestically and internationally on the optimization of the emergency treatment process and the construction of models for multiple trauma, and significant progress has been made, there are still some deficiencies in the existing research. In particular, research targeting the specific environment of secondary hospitals is relatively limited. A critical but often overlooked point is that the distinction between secondary hospitals and tertiary trauma centres is not merely quantitative (ie, “less of everything”) but qualitative. The decision-making calculus in a secondary hospital ED—where a single surgeon may cover multiple specialties, where CT may not be available after hours, and where transfer to a tertiary centre may take hours—is fundamentally different from that in a well‑resourced trauma centre. Protocols designed for the latter assume rapid access to specialised expertise, immediate imaging, and on‑site surgical capability. Applying these same protocols in secondary hospitals without modification does not simply produce suboptimal outcomes; it may generate inappropriate clinical decisions, waste limited resources, and delay essential transfers. This qualitative gap explains why secondary hospitals require a purpose‑designed pragmatic model rather than a “scaled‑down” version of tertiary-centre care. The model must embed decision rules that account for local constraints, trigger escalation pathways when thresholds are exceeded, and prioritise damage control and stabilisation over definitive repair when transfer is inevitable.

First, as previously mentioned, a unified and mature treatment model for multiple trauma has not yet been established in China. Different hospitals are exploring on their own, and most hospitals lack an independent trauma treatment system, relying mainly on specialized treatment. This is inconsistent with the characteristics of multiple trauma that require overall control and multidisciplinary collaboration. Although the MDT model is widely advocated, it is difficult to fully replicate the MDT model of large general hospitals at the level of secondary hospitals due to incomplete specialized department settings and a limited number of experts. How to construct a “lightweight” or “virtual” MDT model suitable for the characteristics of secondary hospitals, such as through telemedicine and the hierarchical diagnosis and treatment cooperation network, is an issue that needs to be addressed.

Secondly, many of the existing studies on process optimization and model construction are based on the experiences of primary trauma centers or large general hospitals, which typically have more abundant medical resources, more sophisticated equipment, and more professional teams. Patient process modeling research often focuses on efficiency improvement in resource-rich environments, and its complex models and optimization schemes may not be fully applicable to secondary hospitals with relatively limited resources. Although the research on the consensus of managing TBI patients in non-neurosurgical hospitals or the challenges in rural emergency departments is similar to some of the problems faced by secondary hospitals, there is a lack of systematic research targeting the specific situation of Chinese secondary hospitals. Secondary hospitals have their own particularities in terms of personnel composition (possibly lacking senior trauma specialists), equipment configuration (such as limited access to advanced imaging equipment and interventional surgical capabilities), department setup, and management systems. Simply applying the models of top-level trauma centers may not work well.

Furthermore, the treatment of multiple injuries emphasizes that “time is life”. However, overemphasizing the reduction of the emergency department disposal time may also bring risks. For example, it may lead to incomplete relevant examinations or missed diagnoses, leaving hidden dangers for subsequent treatment.3 How to strike a balance between rapid assessment and initial stabilization and comprehensive examinations and accurate diagnosis, especially under the limited time and resources of secondary hospitals, is a key issue that needs to be considered in the process design.

In addition, although information technology is regarded as an important means to improve the efficiency of emergency care, in-depth research is still needed on how to implement it in secondary hospitals, build an information platform that adapts to the existing processes and management systems, and overcome technical barriers and personnel training issues. For example, while the EHR system is valuable, it may also increase the documentation burden on clinicians, and its design needs to be optimized to adapt to the emergency workflow.52

Finally, existing studies have mentioned the role of hospital management in the construction of the multiple trauma treatment system. However, there is a relative lack of research on how the management of secondary hospitals can promote the innovation of emergency treatment processes and the construction of models through effective organization, coordination, and support under limited resources. The concepts and support of hospital managers are crucial for breaking down departmental barriers, integrating resources, and implementing innovative models.

In conclusion, existing studies have provided a theoretical basis and practical experience for the optimization of the emergency treatment process for multiple trauma. In particular, the MDT model and the concept of integrated treatment have been proven effective. However, for secondary hospitals in China, which are a specific level of medical institutions, how to construct a practical and efficient innovative model for the entire emergency treatment process of multiple trauma based on their resource characteristics and capacity levels remains a gap in current research and a key area for breakthrough. This requires in-depth analysis of the specific bottlenecks faced by secondary hospitals in the treatment of multiple trauma, drawing on advanced experiences at home and abroad, and conducting localized innovation and practical exploration.

A further limitation of the existing literature—and one that this review itself reflects—is the predominance of descriptive rather than analytical studies. Many published reports on polytrauma process optimisation describe single-centre implementations of new protocols or technologies, reporting improvements in time-based metrics without comparator groups, statistical adjustment for confounding, or longer-term outcome data. While these studies are valuable for generating hypotheses and demonstrating feasibility, they offer limited evidence on generalisability, sustainability, or comparative effectiveness. Moreover, there is a notable lack of head-to-head comparisons between different model configurations (eg, traditional consultation-based MDT vs core-team models; in-person vs tele-MDT), making it difficult to determine which approach is most appropriate for which setting. Conflicting findings—for instance, on the optimal timing of whole-body CT or the role of routine repeat imaging—are rarely discussed in a comparative framework, leaving clinicians without clear guidance on how to adjudicate between competing recommendations. This review has attempted to synthesise available evidence despite these limitations, but we explicitly acknowledge that the strength of our conclusions is constrained by the quality and heterogeneity of the primary studies. We have therefore framed our recommendations as practice-oriented principles rather than prescriptive protocols, recognising that local adaptation and context-sensitive implementation are essential.

Thoughts on Constructing an Innovative Model for the Whole Process of First Aid for Multiple Trauma in Secondary Hospitals

Based on the analysis of the clinical characteristics of multiple trauma, the challenges in treatment, and the current research status at home and abroad, constructing an innovative full-process model for multiple-trauma emergency care suitable for secondary hospitals requires full consideration of their resource limitations and particularities, drawing on advanced concepts, and carrying out targeted design and implementation. The following are some key directions for consideration:

(1) Establish a simplified and efficient multidisciplinary collaboration mechanism: It is difficult for secondary hospitals to form a large MDT team. Therefore, a “core team + consulting experts” model centered on the emergency department and involving key departments such as orthopedics, general surgery, neurosurgery, ICU, anesthesiology, and radiology within the hospital can be considered. The core team (eg, emergency doctors, nurses, and trauma specialist nurses) is responsible for the initial assessment, stabilization, and damage control of patients, and simultaneously initiates relevant specialist consultations promptly. Information technology (eg, remote consultation platforms and emergency apps) can be used to achieve rapid calls and information sharing, thereby reducing the waiting time for consultations. For example, drawing on the application experience of telemedicine in the transfer of hand injuries, secondary hospitals can establish a remote consultation mechanism with superior trauma centers to obtain timely guidance in complex cases or when referrals are needed.

(2) Optimize the emergency green-channel process and emphasize rapid assessment and damage control: Secondary hospitals should establish clear activation criteria for the multiple-trauma emergency green channel to ensure that patients can quickly enter the rescue process after admission. The process design should follow the principles of “saving lives first and then treating injuries” and damage control. After a rapid initial assessment (eg, following the xABCDE principle—where “x”denotes control of eXsanguinating external haemorrhage as the first priority, followed by Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure—as recommended in the latest ATLS guidelines,10,13,14 prioritize the treatment of life‑threatening injuries, such as controlling active bleeding, establishing an effective airway, and correcting shock. For patients who require emergency surgery but cannot be treated at the secondary hospital, the referral process should be quickly initiated while stabilizing vital signs. Process optimization can draw on the idea of patient process modeling, analyze the actual process of the emergency department in secondary hospitals, identify bottleneck links (such as waiting for CT, waiting for consultations, waiting for the operating room, etc), and take targeted measures for improvement. For example, optimize the CT examination process to ensure that multiple-trauma patients can be given priority and quickly complete a whole-body scan.

(3) Strengthen the collaboration and information integration among various in-hospital links: The treatment of multiple injuries involves multiple departments such as emergency, imaging, operating rooms, and ICUs. Building an innovative model requires breaking down departmental barriers to achieve information exchange and resource sharing. An integrated information platform should be established to integrate pre-hospital emergency information, emergency assessment data, imaging materials, laboratory test results, surgical records, ICU monitoring data, etc, for real-time access by MDT team members, thus avoiding information silos. Optimize the patient transfer process to ensure rapid and safe transfer of patients between different areas (eg, from the emergency resuscitation room to the CT room, from the CT room to the operating room, and from the operating room to the ICU). The experience of the integrated treatment model can be learned to closely connect key links and reduce unnecessary waiting and repeated operations.

(4) Strengthen the trauma first-aid training for medical staff: Medical staff in secondary hospitals may lack experience in dealing with complex multiple traumas, so systematic trauma first-aid training is crucial. The training content should include Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS)/Basic Life Support (BLS), trauma assessment principles, damage control concepts, multidisciplinary collaborative communication skills, and the use of first-aid equipment. Simulation teaching, scenario drills and other methods can be adopted to improve the training effect. In particular, coordinated training for relevant departments such as the emergency department, surgery, anesthesiology, and ICU should be strengthened to enhance the teamwork and coordination. International experience should be learned from to increase the proportion of trauma specialty training and certification for medical staff.

(5) Establish an effective linkage and referral mechanism with superior trauma centers: As a part of the regional trauma care system, secondary hospitals should establish close collaborative relationships with superior trauma centers. Clearly define the referral indications and procedures for patients with multiple injuries to ensure that patients in need of higher-level treatment can be safely and quickly transferred to capable hospitals. Electronic referral platforms can be utilized to improve referral efficiency and the accuracy of information transmission.

(6) Strengthen hospital management support and continuous quality improvement: Hospital administrators should attach great importance to the treatment of multiple injuries and incorporate it into the hospital’s key development directions, providing necessary resource guarantees (personnel, equipment, and funds). Establish a quality control system for the treatment of multiple injuries, regularly collect and analyze key indicators (such as emergency stay time, diagnosis time, operation time, complication incidence rate, mortality rate, etc), and continuously optimize the process and improve the treatment quality through performance improvement and patient safety plans. The support of hospital administrators is the fundamental driving force for promoting process innovation and model transformation.

Operationalisation of the Proposed Model

To translate the conceptual model outlined above into actionable practice, we propose the following phased implementation framework for secondary hospitals:

Phase 1–Preparation and Baseline Assessment (0–3 Months)

Conduct a process mapping exercise to document current polytrauma workflows, identifying key bottlenecks (eg, time from ED arrival to CT, consultation waiting times, transfer decision delays).

Establish a core MDT team comprising 4–6 members: two emergency physicians (one senior, one junior), two trauma nurses, and rotating representatives from surgery, orthopaedics, and anaesthesiology. Define clear roles and responsibilities for each member.

Develop simple, written activation criteria for the polytrauma green channel (eg, mechanism of injury + abnormal vital signs + suspected multi‑system involvement).

Phase 2–Pilot Implementation (3–6 Months)

Implement the core MDT model for all patients meeting activation criteria, with the team assembling within 10 minutes of activation.

Introduce a standardised documentation template (paper‑based or electronic) to record key time stamps (ED arrival, CT completion, MDT decision, transfer/OR/ICU disposition).

Conduct weekly case reviews to identify implementation challenges and refine processes iteratively.

Phase 3–Digital Integration and Referral Linkage (6–12 Months)

Where feasible, deploy a simple digital dashboard (eg, a shared spreadsheet or basic hospital information system module) to track real‑time patient progress and alert team members.

Establish formal referral pathways with one or more tertiary trauma centres, including agreed transfer criteria, a standardised referral handover template, and a feedback loop for transferred patients.

Phase 4–Monitoring and Continuous Quality Improvement (Ongoing)

Track key performance indicators: door‑to‑CT time, door‑to‑decision time, inappropriate transfer rate, 24‑hour mortality, and complication rates.

Conduct quarterly multidisciplinary audits comparing performance against baseline and, where available, against regional or national benchmarks.

Use audit findings to update protocols and training annually.

This phased approach is designed to be feasible within the resource constraints of secondary hospitals, avoiding the need for large upfront investments in technology or staffing. It emphasises iterative learning and local adaptation, consistent with the implementation science principles we discuss in the Summary and Outlook section.

Despite the feasibility of this phased approach, several implementation barriers should be anticipated and proactively addressed. First, staff resistance to change is common when introducing new workflows, particularly in settings where clinicians have established individual practice patterns. Mitigation strategies include involving frontline staff in the design process, providing clear rationale for changes, and celebrating early wins during the pilot phase. Second, resource constraints—particularly limited staffing and time for training—may slow implementation. This can be addressed by starting with a small core team, using brief on-the-job training sessions rather than lengthy workshops, and leveraging existing quality improvement structures. Third, IT infrastructure limitations may hinder the deployment of digital tools in Phase 3. Where advanced digital platforms are not feasible, paper-based checklists and simple shared spreadsheets can serve as transitional alternatives that still capture the core benefits of standardised documentation and real-time information sharing. Fourth, establishing and maintaining effective referral relationships with tertiary centres requires ongoing communication and mutual trust. Regular joint case reviews, feedback loops on transferred patients, and formalised referral protocols can help strengthen these relationships over time. Anticipating these barriers and building mitigation strategies into each implementation phase increases the likelihood of successful and sustainable model adoption.

The construction of an innovative full-process model for the first-aid of multiple injuries in secondary hospitals does not simply replicate the model of top-level trauma centers (Figure 2). Instead, on the basis of learning from advanced concepts and experiences, it combines its own actual situation to carry out targeted process reengineering, resource integration, and capacity improvement. The core lies in establishing an efficient collaborative mechanism, shortening key time nodes, and ensuring that patients receive the most appropriate treatment within the limited time window.

Summary and Outlook

Multiple trauma is a major public health issue that seriously threatens human health. Its complexity and criticality pose severe challenges to the emergency medical system. Both domestic and international studies have shown that optimizing the emergency treatment process, promoting multidisciplinary collaboration, and establishing an integrated treatment model are the keys to improving the treatment efficiency and prognosis of patients with multiple trauma. Positive progress has been made in the application of the multidisciplinary team (MDT) and the exploration of the integrated model in China. Internationally, rich experience has been accumulated in trauma system construction, quantitative analysis of processes, and treatment strategies for resource-limited environments.

However, for secondary hospitals in China, which are a specific level of medical institutions, how to construct a practical and efficiently-operating innovative model for the entire process of multiple-trauma emergency treatment in combination with their resource characteristics and capacity levels remains a weak link in current research. Secondary hospitals are confronted with numerous challenges, such as relatively weak specialized forces, limited equipment configuration, insufficient personnel training, and poor linkage with superior hospitals.

Future Research Should Focus on

(1) Conduct an in-depth analysis of the current situation and key bottlenecks in the treatment process of multiple injuries in secondary hospitals, and perform quantitative analysis using methods such as process mining or simulation to provide data support for process optimization.

(2) Explore MDT models and collaborative mechanisms suitable for the characteristics of secondary hospitals, such as virtual MDT based on telemedicine or regional collaborative networks, as well as simplified in-hospital core team collaborative models, and evaluate their effectiveness.

(3) Design and implement standardized and highly operable first-aid procedures for multiple injuries and damage control plans tailored to secondary hospitals, and conduct verification and continuous improvement in clinical practice.

(4) Explore how to effectively utilize information technology to build an emergency care information platform that meets the actual needs of secondary hospitals, enabling information sharing and process collaboration across pre-hospital, in-hospital, and referral stages (Figure 3).

Key Take‑Home Points

To ensure clarity for readers, we summarise the core messages of this review in three concise points:

First, polytrauma care in secondary hospitals requires a shift from fragmented, specialty‑driven approaches to integrated, protocol‑driven workflows. Establishing a core MDT team-combining emergency physicians, trauma nurses, and key specialists-enables rapid assessment, coordinated decision‑making, and timely damage control, even when full specialist panels are unavailable.

Second, the constraints of secondary hospitals-limited staffing, equipment, and specialist access-are not merely quantitative but qualitative, demanding purpose-designed pragmatic models rather than scaled‑down copies of tertiary-centre protocols. Digital decision-support tools (eg, 5G-based platforms, process mining, AI-assisted triage) are not optional enhancements but essential enablers for real‑time coordination, bottleneck identification, and safe referral decisions in these settings.

Third, meaningful improvement in secondary hospital trauma outcomes depends on embedding these models within regional referral networks that include clear escalation pathways, electronic referral platforms, and continuing education and quality improvement programmes. This systems-level approach-rather than isolated hospital‑level interventions-is critical for reducing preventable mortality and achieving equitable trauma care across diverse resource settings.

Beyond these specific research directions, a fundamental methodological shift is required. To translate promising models-such as the simplified MDT framework and digital decision-support systems proposed in this review-into scalable, sustainable, and equitable solutions for secondary hospitals, future investigations must move beyond traditional efficacy studies. The heterogeneity of secondary hospitals-varying in staffing, infrastructure, catchment population, and regional referral pathways-demands pragmatic research designs that generate actionable evidence under real-world conditions.

Specifically, we recommend four complementary methodological approaches. First, pragmatic trials (eg, cluster-randomized or stepped-wedge designs) should be employed to evaluate integrated emergency models in routine clinical practice, measuring clinically meaningful endpoints such as 30-day mortality, complication rates, and functional outcomes, rather than purely process metrics. Second, health economic evaluations-including cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses—are urgently needed to inform resource allocation decisions in secondary hospitals, where budget constraints are particularly salient. Third, implementation research guided by established frameworks (eg, RE-AIM, CFIR) should systematically examine barriers and facilitators to adoption, fidelity, and sustainment of new protocols, with particular attention to contextual factors such as staff turnover, training adequacy, and leadership support. Fourth, context-sensitive performance measurement should be developed to track quality improvement in a manner that accounts for baseline resource disparities, avoiding the pitfalls of applying tertiary-centre benchmarks to secondary hospitals.

Such a research agenda-integrating pragmatic trials, economic evaluation, implementation science, and tailored performance measurement-will generate the evidence base needed to scale interventions that are not only clinically effective but also feasible, affordable, and equitable across the full spectrum of trauma care settings.

Innovativeness and Significance for Emergency Department Practice

The key innovation of our proposed model lies not in introducing any single new technology or protocol, but in explicitly reframing secondary hospital trauma care as a distinct clinical and operational problem—rather than simply a resource‑constrained version of tertiary‑centre care. This reframing has direct implications for emergency department practice:

Time to critical interventions: By embedding clear activation criteria for the green channel and structured decision rules for damage control versus transfer, the model reduces door‑to‑CT time and expedites disposition decisions—directly addressing the two most time‑sensitive bottlenecks in secondary hospital EDs.

Safer referrals: The integration of digital decision support and regional referral linkages enables more accurate identification of patients who require transfer, reduces unnecessary transfers, and ensures that when transfer is needed, it occurs with relevant clinical information and haemodynamic stabilisation—mitigating the risks of unsafe or delayed referral.

Realistic resource utilisation: The core MDT model explicitly accounts for limited specialist availability, making it feasible in settings where assembling a full tertiary‑level MDT is impossible, while still preserving the principles of multidisciplinary assessment.

What is novel about focusing on secondary hospitals? Most existing polytrauma models are designed for and validated in tertiary centres, with the implicit assumption that their principles can be “scaled down”to lower‑resource settings. We challenge this assumption by demonstrating that the decision‑making environment in secondary hospitals is qualitatively different—not just less resourced, but structurally constrained in ways that make simple protocol adaptation inappropriate. Our proposed model is therefore built from the ground up for this specific context, embedding decision rules that account for local constraints and escalation pathways that trigger when thresholds are exceeded. This contextual grounding, combined with the explicit integration of digital tools and regional referral mechanisms, distinguishes our approach from existing frameworks and offers a practical, actionable pathway for emergency clinicians and administrators in secondary hospitals.

Through the above research and practice, it is expected to provide scientific basis and practical guidance for secondary hospitals to construct a rapid and efficient innovative model for the entire process of multiple-injury emergency treatment. Consequently, the success rate of treating patients with multiple injuries caused by traffic accidents can be significantly improved, while the mortality and disability rates can be reduced. Ultimately, the long-term prognosis and quality of life of patients will be improved. This will be of great significance for improving the regional trauma treatment system in China and enhancing the trauma treatment capabilities of primary medical institutions.
