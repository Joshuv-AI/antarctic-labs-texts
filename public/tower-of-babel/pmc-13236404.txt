An analysis of educational needs to enhance the response competency of infectious disease control personnel to emerging infectious diseases
Authors: Sun Young Jeong, Sungae Choi, Jaeyeun Kim, Sojin Lee, Young-soon Park, Kyunghee Chun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to explore the training experiences and educational needs of personnel working at nationally designated infectious disease control institutions in South Korea. Specifically, it sought to identify the barriers encountered in infectious disease response, assess the perceived effectiveness of current training programs, and determine key competencies and tailored educational needs across occupational groups.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using focus group interviews with 34 participants, including healthcare and nonhealthcare personnel engaged in infectious disease control. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling. Data was collected over 5 weeks via online interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using qualitative content analysis. Investigator triangulation and member checking were used to enhance the analytical rigor.ResultsThe participants reported that the current training program contributed to improved confidence, role clarity, and interdepartmental collaboration. However, multiple barriers were identified, including a lack of standardized scenarios, insufficient hands-on training, unclear guidelines, and low accessibility across roles. Key educational needs included scenario-based simulations, job-specific content, advanced clinical skills, psychological support, and interorganizational collaboration. Training preferences varied by job category, underscoring the need for differentiated role-based training modules.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of shifting from theory-based to practice-oriented education on infectious disease responses. To build sustainable preparedness systems, training programs should be scenario-driven, repetitive, and psychologically supportive. Institutional policies must ensure flexibility, equity of access, and proper incentives for participation. Future efforts should prioritize the development of standardized competency-based curricula tailored to diverse occupational roles within infectious disease response systems.Keywords: Focus group interview, Infectious disease control, Needs assessment

Introduction

Over the past 3 decades, humanity has faced unprecedented challenges from infectious diseases, including severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), influenza A virus subtype H1N1, Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), Ebola virus disease, Zika virus disease, and, most notably, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). The global spread of COVID-19 in particular led to significant loss of life and economic disruption, underscoring the ongoing risk posed by such diseases. More recently, the monkeypox outbreak in 2022 demonstrated the increasingly unpredictable nature of global infectious threats. Over the past 3 decades, these major outbreaks have demonstrated that humanity remains persistently exposed to the risk of unpredictable and potentially devastating infectious diseases, highlighting the necessity for thorough preparedness and robust international collaboration [1].

In South Korea, one national infectious disease specialist hospital and five regional infectious disease hospitals, and 38 nationally designated isolation and treatment facilities have been established to respond to such crises [2,3]. Notably, the nationally designated isolation and treatment facilities, as key institutions for infectious disease control, are medical institutions equipped with specialized infrastructure and equipment within segregated areas to isolate and treat patients with emerging infectious diseases during public health emergencies, thereby preventing further transmission [4]. These facilities have been established and operated since 2006, and, since 2017, annual evaluations of their infrastructure, workforce, and operational systems have been conducted to strengthen their infectious disease emergency response capabilities [5]. In accordance with operational and management guidelines, all 38 institutions must provide at least one annual training session on infection control to their operational teams caring for infectious disease patients [3] and are mandated to offer regular education and training to healthcare personnel and public health workers in close contact with these patients [6].

The most effective approach to infectious disease response is to enhance the infection control competencies of the personnel involved in such efforts, making education and training essential components of preparedness [7]. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, the importance of infectious disease response has become increasingly prominent, highlighting the need for systematic and tailored training programs [8-10]. In medical and health professions education, infectious disease outbreaks and pandemics have reinforced the need for structured preparedness training, including infection prevention and control, risk communication, and teamwork competencies. Systematic and scoping reviews show that educational interventions can improve learners’ knowledge, skills and attitudes, but also highlight gaps in evaluation quality and consistency across contexts [11]. However, much of the published work has focused on students or single professional groups, while less is known about role-differentiated training needs across the multidisciplinary workforce in high-consequence setting such as designed infectious disease institutions [12]. Accordingly, a needs assessment grounded in frontline experience is necessary to inform competency-based, interprofessional training design and institutional support strategies.

Current training programs primarily target physicians and nurses, often neglecting other essential personnel such as transport staff, environmental services, and administrative teams. Additionally, these programs tend to focus on theoretical knowledge and basic infection protocols, with limited emphasis on hands-on experience, role-specific competencies, or real-world application. As a result, many frontline workers feel unprepared to respond effectively during outbreaks [13].

In response to this need, the National Medical Center conducted a series of surveys in 2023, including the “Survey on the educational and training needs for emerging infectious disease response personnel [14],” the “Survey on the current status of emerging infectious disease response personnel and education/training supply [15],” and the “Survey of training providers related to emerging infectious diseases [16].” The resulting reports analyzed the current status and structure of infectious disease response training systems in Korea, identified challenges, and proposed areas for improvement. Notably, the findings revealed that most existing education and training programs are primarily directed at specialized healthcare professionals, such as physicians and nurses. These surveys showed that existing guidelines are difficult to interpret and apply in practice, and that most training content does not reflect the actual responsibilities of diverse occupational groups involved in infectious disease control.

Given the gaps, this study aims to explore the real-world training experiences and educational needs of personnel working in Korea’s nationally designated infections disease institutions. By focusing on both healthcare and non-healthcare workers, the study seeks to understand common challenges, assess the effectiveness of existing training, and identify key competencies needed for field readiness. To gather in-depth insights, the research team employed qualitative focus group interviews (FGIs). This approach was chosen for its ability to capture detailed personal experiences and to foster discussion among individuals in similar roles. The study ultimately aims to generate practical recommendations for improving the structure and delivery of training programs, moving from generalized education toward differentiated, scenario-based learning that reflects the complexity of infectious disease response environments.

This study aimed to explore the following research questions: (1) What are the experiences of personnel at infectious disease control institutions (i.e., nationally designated isolation and treatment facilities) regarding education and training for infectious disease response? (2) What are the barriers faced by these personnel during infectious disease responses and in the process of participating in infectious disease training? (3) What are the perceived outcomes of infectious disease response training, and what are the key competencies required in the field? (4) What are the specific educational and training needs necessary to support effective field performance and strengthen response capabilities?

Methods

1. Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Konyang University (IRB no., KYU 2024-09-007). All participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, and data confidentiality, and provided written consent before participation.

2. Participants

A total of 34 individuals were recruited via snowball sampling, based on referrals from infection control professionals at nationally designated infectious disease institutions or related oversight agencies, who had participated in or supported infectious disease response and/or related training, were able to join an online FGI in Korean, and provided informed consent. Given the exploratory qualitative design, our aim was maximum variation rather than statistical representativeness; recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached within the major occupational groups. The sample included 16 healthcare workers (physicians and nurses), 15 administrative staff members (specializing in patient transport, environmental services, or facility management), and three government officials involved in institutional oversight. Government officials were included as key informants to complement the field perspectives, and findings were interpreted with caution to avoid overgeneralization to this subgroup.

3. Research instruments

To conduct the FGI, a semi-structured interview guide was developed in three steps [17]. First, the research team reviewed relevant literature and guidelines on infectious disease response and personal competencies [18-25]. Second, a panel of three experts including infectious disease and education specialists validated the preliminary questions for clarity and relevance. Third, a pilot interview tested the flow and clarity of questions. The finalized guide covered three main domains: (1) training experiences and challenges in practice, (2) required field competencies, and (3) educational needs by occupational group. Questions were tailored to each participant’s role, with flexibility during interviews to adapt to emerging themes.

4. Data collection

Data was collected over 5 weeks from September 19 to October 21, 2024, via Zoom interview (Zoom Video Communications Inc., San Jose, USA). Each session included a lead interviewer and two assistant moderators. Sessions averaged 2 hours, and interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached. All sessions were video recorded with consent. Researchers also took notes on nonverbal cues and contextual elements. After each interview, key discussion points were shared with the participants for verification, and each participant received a nominal honorarium.

5. Data analysis

Data was analyzed using qualitative content analysis methodology proposed by White and Marsh [26] in 2006 to systematically analyze the FGI data. In line with directed/deductive content analysis, we first developed an initial coding frame (categorization matrix) based on the study aims and the three domains of the semi-structured interview guide, which were informed by prior literature and guidelines on infectious disease response training and competencies [27,28]. The a priori frame covered: (1) training experiences and perceived outcomes, (2) barriers/challenges in training and response, and (3) role-based competencies and educational needs. Transcripts were analyzed as the unit of analysis, and meaning units were coded and condensed into categories and themes. Three researchers independently conducted open coding within the initial frame, and inductive codes were added when data did not fit pre-existing categories. Through regular analytic meetings, we refined code definitions, resolved discrepancies, and iteratively revised the coding frame until consensus was reached. Member checking was used to enhance credibility by sharing key interpretations with participants.

2. Experiences of education and training for infectious disease response

Using qualitative content analysis, we identified three categories with subcategories regarding education and training experiences: (1) training providers and participation pathways, (2) training content and duration, and (3) perceived educational outcomes.

Participants received training from their own healthcare institutions as well as external organizations, including the National Medical Center, major university hospitals, academic societies and associations, and public agencies. Access to these programs was facilitated through official announcements, work assignments, voluntary participation, and institutional recommendations. Instructional methods were primarily practice-oriented, featuring simulations, mock drills, case-based learning, and hands-on sessions, which participants found highly applicable to real-world scenarios. Training duration varied, categorized as short-term (1–4 hours), medium-term (4–8 hours), or long-term (8–16 hours). Core training content covered a comprehensive range of topics, including patient triage, infection control principles, proper use of personal protective equipment (PPE), environmental disinfection, crisis communication, and the management of vaccines and specimens.

The training programs produced meaningful impacts at both organizational and individual levels. Organizationally, the training contributed to the validation of infectious disease response protocols, strengthen interdepartmental collaboration, and the establishment of safer working environments. Individually, participants reported improved response competencies, enhanced professional knowledge, greater psychological stability, and the development of innovative perspectives on infectious disease management.

Perceived Effectiveness of Training and Required Competencies for Infectious Disease Responses

Training was perceived as effective at both the organizational and individual levels. Organizationally, it was seen as vital for establishing and evaluating cooperative response systems, enhancing understanding of national policies, and creating a safer healthcare environment. Individually, participants reported increased confidence in their response capabilities, improved work performance, and stronger collaboration.

Based on their experiences, participants identified several key competencies for effective response: problem-solving and adaptive capabilities, effective communication and leadership, and a strong sense of professional identity and self-efficacy. They particularly highlighted the ability to respond promptly to emerging cases, solve problems in crisis situations, provide appropriate care for critically ill patients, ensure self-protection and psychological stability, and maintain seamless communication across departments.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the training experiences and educational needs of diverse healthcare professionals involved infectious disease response in South Korea. Our findings reveal that educational experiences varied significantly based on the organizing institution, training format, and specific program objectives. From a medical education perspective, this study extends the preparedness-training literature by offering a multidisciplinary, role-sensitive map of barriers, required competencies, and educational needs in designated infectious disease institutions [29]. The categories identified here can be translated into curriculum elements for competency-based and interprofessional continuing professional development, guiding the alignment of learning objectives, instructional strategies, and organizational supports [30]. Importantly, our findings suggest that effective preparedness education should address not only clinical skills but also operational workflow, psychological safety, and cross-role coordination that are often under-specified in traditional clinically oriented training.

The shift from traditional lectures to practical training methods, such as simulations and hands-on drills, was a key theme. Participants affirmed that methods focused on PPE use, patient transport, and crisis communication were highly effective for strengthening real-world responses capabilities. This suggests that conventional, theory-heavy infection control education must be augmented with practice-oriented strategies to be truly effective [9].

However, significant barriers hinder the quality and sustainability of this training. We identified structural constraints on participation, inadequate infrastructure for practical training, challenges in interdisciplinary collaboration, and difficulties in applying guidelines to clinical settings. Obstacles such as conducting joint training across different occupational groups and developing realistic simulation scenarios align with previous research on implementation barriers in infection control education [31]. Furthermore, participants noted a disconnect between some training programs and real-world practice, citing limited time, staff shortages, and unclear guidelines as persistent challenges. These findings underscore the need for strong policy support and institutional frameworks to overcome such barriers.

Previous studies emphasized that outbreak-related training is most effective when it combines clear guidance with practice-based methods, while also noting wide heterogeneity and limitations in implementation and evaluation [29]. In our study, participants’ strong preference for scenario-based, hands-on training aligns with evidence that simulation-based infection prevention and control education can strengthen skills and attitudes, whereas lecture-based approaches mainly reinforce knowledge [30]. The structural barriers reported here, limited protected time, staffing constraints, and difficulties translating guidelines into practice, are also commonly described internationally during viral epidemics [29]. Importantly, our inclusion of non-clinical personnel echoes qualitative work showing that support staff face distinct safety concerns and training needs, underscoring the necessity of role-differentiated, team-based preparedness education [32].

In response to these challenges, participants universally emphasized the need for learner-centered programs. They expressed a strong preference for scenario-based simulation training grounded in realistic settings, opportunities for hands-on practice, and regular, repetitive sessions tailored to their specific roles and work environments.

The study also revealed considerable variability in training needs across occupational groups. Infection control professionals required advanced simulation-based training with standardized scenarios, while nurses prioritized practical training on clinical procedures for critically ill patients. Administrative staff sought training to improve their understanding of hospital systems and facilitate interdepartmental collaboration. This diversity highlights that a “one-size-fits-all” model is ineffective; training must be designed according to the distinct functions and responsibilities of each group [8].

Crucially, participants perceived infectious disease response competency as a multidimensional construct that extended beyond technical skill. They emphasized psychological resilience, collaborative ability, and a strong sense of professional identity as equally important. This indicates that response competency is an integrated concept, combining technical skills with emotional stability, leadership, communication, and professional pride. Consequently, competency models for infection control must incorporate these emotional and social domains alongside clinical skills [9,33]. Training must therefore evolve beyond knowledge instruction to include support for self-efficacy and psychological well-being, while simultaneously strengthening leadership and communication within healthcare teams.

Based on these findings, we propose several recommendations: First, develop job-specific, multidimensional training modules. Curricula should be differentiated for distinct occupational groups and cover a wide range of competencies, from technical knowledge to communication and emotional regulation. Second, expand practice-based and simulation-centered training, institutionalizing methods like tabletop exercises and simulation drills to ensure skills are internalized. Third, provide robust institutional and policy support. This includes guaranteeing protected training time, offering incentives, linking training to career advancement, and fostering inter-hospital collaboration through joint exercises and sharing best practices.

This study has limitations. It was conducted during a period of healthcare labor disputes, which precluded the participation of certain frontline professionals like medical residents, potentially leaving some field-specific needs unaddressed. The uneven distribution across occupational groups, particularly the small number of government officials, may limit transferability of findings for that subgroup. Additionally, its focus on nationally designated treatment institutions means the findings may not be generalized to all healthcare settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a meaningful foundation for developing structured, evidence-based training programs. Future research should involve a broader range of occupational groups and employ mixed method approaches with quantitative evaluation indicators to evaluate program effectiveness. Continued exploration into standardized training frameworks tailored to the national disaster response system is also warranted.
