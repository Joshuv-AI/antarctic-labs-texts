Electrographic flow mapping of persistent atrial fibrillation: intra- and inter-procedure reproducibility in the absence of ‘ground truth’
Authors: Vivek Y Reddy, Melissa H Kong, Jan Petru, Abhishek Maan, Moritoshi Funasako, Kentaro Minami, Peter Ruppersberg, Srinivas Dukkipati, Petr Neuzil
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsValidating mapping systems that identify atrial fibrillation (AF) sources (focal/rotational activity) is confounded by the absence of ground truth. A key concern of prior mapping technologies is spatiotemporal instability, manifesting as poor map reproducibility. Electrographic flow (EGF) employs a novel algorithm that visualizes atrial electrical wavefront propagation to identify putative AF sources. We analysed both intra- (3 min) and inter- (>3 months) procedure EGF map reproducibility.Methods and resultsIn 23 persistent AF patients, after pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), EGF maps were generated from 3 serial 1 min recordings using a 64-electrode basket mapping catheter (triplets) at right and left atrial locations. Source prevalence from map triplets was compared between recordings. Per protocol, 12 patients returned for 3-month remapping (1 non-inducible): index procedure post-PVI EGF maps were compared with initial EGF remapping at 3-month redo. Intra-procedure reproducibility: analysing 224 map triplets (111 right atrium, 113 left atrium) revealed a high degree of map consistency with minimal min-to-min shifts: 97 triplets (43%), exact match of leading sources on all 3 maps; 95 triplets (42%), leading source within 1 electrode space on 2 of 3 maps; and 32 triplets (14%), chaotic leading source pattern. Average deviation in source prevalence over 60 s was low (6.4%). Inter-procedure reproducibility: spatiotemporal stability of EGF mapping >3 months was seen in 16 of 18 (89%) sources mapped in 12 patients with (re)inducible AF.ConclusionElectrographic flow mapping generates reproducible intra- and inter-procedural maps, providing rationale for randomized clinical trials targeting these putative AF sources.

Introduction

Although radiofrequency (RF) catheter ablation to electrically isolate the pulmonary veins (PVs) has evolved as an effective treatment for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF), its outcomes for persistent and long-standing persistent AF remain suboptimal.1,2 Significant heterogeneity exists among the ablation strategies employed for non-paroxysmal AF—particularly with regard to adjunctive ablation targets outside the PVs. Recent data from pre-clinical and clinical studies support the role of extra-PV self-sustaining localized drivers and triggers in the pathophysiology of AF.3–5 Over the last decade, a variety of algorithm-based approaches for localizing extra-PV AF drivers/sources (focal or rotational activity) have been put forth as potentially promising tools to improve AF ablation guidance. However, these mapping technologies suffer from technical limitations including high sensitivity to signal quality, noise and artefacts; inability to show time-dependent electrical behaviours; lack of real-time or beat-by-beat mapping; and susceptibility to timing errors and morphology correlation.6 Furthermore, validating AF source mapping systems is confounded by the lack of ground truth—that is, no simple test exists to determine if a putative identified source is indeed ‘real’ (short of a time/resource-intensive randomized clinical trial). An elemental concern is the spatiotemporal instability of previous mapping technologies, manifesting as poor map reproducibility. Indeed, conflicting outcomes have been reported from ablation guided by these various mapping strategies.7–10 And given poor map reproducibility, it comes as little surprise that recent large multicentre randomized trials have failed to identify any improvement in efficacy of the most studied of these mapping strategies, focal impulse and rotor modulation (FIRM) during either de novo or redo procedures for persistent AF.11,12

Electrographic flow (EGF) mapping is an innovative technique to create near real-time visualization of atrial electrical wavefront propagation to identify putative AF sources, and detailed explanations of the algorithm as well as its theoretical underpinnings have been previously published.13 Electrographic flow does not involve phase analysis or other traditional signal processing methodologies but rather uses principles of optical flow and fluid dynamics to estimate cardiac action potential flow travelling through the atrial myocardium as a result of transmembrane voltage changes represented by unipolar electrograms (EGMs).14,15 It integrates the chaotic, often short-lived behaviours of AF sources over the time domain to display dominant patterns of wave propagation, even differentiating active sources from passive rotations. Short of optical mapping studies, it remains difficult for any of the mapping modalities to prove that identified drivers are true, realistic drivers; however, what differentiates EGF mapping is its ability to reproducibly show the time-dependent activity of EGF-identified AF sources that do appear to matter in terms of AF recurrence post-ablation.16 Additionally, a recently published pre-clinical study performed in an animal model of spontaneously persistent AF found that EGF mapping can visualize flow patterns and accurately identify focal sources of AF simulated by rapid atrial pacing during spontaneously persistent AF.17 The goal of this study is to demonstrate the spatiotemporal stability of EGF-identified AF sources/drivers by assessing both the intra-procedural reproducibility over 3 min and inter-procedural reproducibility over 3 months of EGF maps with respect to putative AF source detection, localization, and prevalence.

Methods

Study population

The recently completed randomized controlled FLOW-AF study (NCT 04473963) was designed to assess the safety and feasibility of EGF mapping in patients with non-paroxysmal AF of duration <36 months (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).18 The present analysis includes the first 23 consecutive patients enrolled at a single centre between September 2019 and October 2020 and a total of 18 de novo patients who had never previously undergone catheter ablation for AF. FLOW-AF was approved by the Homolka Hospital Ethical Committee and Czech Regulatory Authority. Clinical characteristics, ablation strategy, and outcomes are not relevant to the present analysis, which focuses on characterizing the spatiotemporal stability of EGF-identified AF sources and not whether these sources are true drivers in that targeting them improves AF ablation outcomes, which will be determined based on the data collected from FLOW-AF.

Consent

Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to study inclusion and enrolment. Internal data analysed as part of the risk assessment of the Ablamap Software did not indicate any increased or unforeseen patient or user safety issues or hazards from participation in the trial. After informed consent, all procedures were performed under sedation. As described in the FLOW-AF Rationale & Design publication, a subset of patients in the FLOW-AF protocol were de novo persistent AF patients, never having previously undergone pulmonary vein isolation (PVI).18

Study procedures

After PVI was performed (typically using a cryo- or visually guided laser balloon ablation catheter), EGF mapping was performed as described below—typically three consecutive 1 min recordings. After a 3-month period, a subset of these de novo patients returned for a second procedure. Accordingly, it was possible to compare map triplets recorded during AF immediately post-PVI in the index procedure with recordings of AF at the start of the second procedure, prior to any ablation—thus allowing an analysis of EGF map consistency over a 3-month period.

During the procedures, intravenous heparin was used to maintain an activated clotting time >300 s prior to insertion of the 64-electrode basket catheter (FIRMap, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL). Three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping was performed in both the right (RA) and left atria (LA) using either CARTO (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) or EnSite NavX (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) mapping systems. For each EGF map, the basket catheter spline locations were recorded on the electroanatomic mapping systems to allow spatial correlation of AF sources.

Electrographic flow mapping

Electrographic flow mapping was performed using Ablamap software (Ablacon, Wheat Ridge, CO) to create a complete, near real-time temporospatial visualization of organized electrographic flow during AF by estimating electrical wavefront propagation through atrial myocardium (Figure 1). Unipolar EGMs are recorded with the 64-electrode basket catheter connected to a proprietary, CE-marked recording system (EP Map, Herdecke, Germany) and processed using a proprietary algorithm that includes removal of the QRS complex, baseline fluctuations, common noise, and remaining far-field signals as well as a normalization of voltage values across channels.16,19 The details of EGF mapping have been published previously (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2),13 but in brief: (i) the electrical field is estimated by biharmonic spline interpolation based on Green’s function, (ii) a modified Horn–Schunck flow estimation of the Green’s interpolation frames is performed, and (iii) using a proprietary algorithm, a ‘film’ of atrial action potential propagation over time is calculated. Electrographic flow mapping displays dominant patterns of the excitation wave propagation film in flow vector maps and measures their consistency over time, allowing EGF visualization and statistical singularities (either focal triggers or rotational activity), called sources. As previously studied and described, active sources are characterized by divergent action potential waves emanating from a singularity of flow vectors.14

If the presenting rhythm was AF, then baseline EGF mapping of both atria was performed using the basket catheter in all standardized positions. If the presenting rhythm was not AF, prior to EGF mapping, the patient underwent AF induction using standard induction pacing protocols. To optimize LA basket coverage, two standardized mapping positions were obtained: (i) posterosuperior LA with basket dome pointing towards trigonum of left PVs and left atrial appendage (LAA) and (ii) lateral LA with basket dome pointing towards LAA and mitral valve. In the RA, three standardized basket positions were employed: (i) superior vena cava (SVC)/RA junction with basket position halfway into the SVC, (ii) central RA with basket dome pointing to SVC with vertical catheter shaft orientation and basket expanded to fill RA; and (iii) anterolateral RA with basket dome pointing to RA appendage and tricuspid valve and catheter deflected anterior and lateral from SVC. Depending on atrial size/shape, at physician’s discretion, additional basket positions were performed to achieve comprehensive atrial coverage. In all cases, a circular multielectrode catheter was used to interrogate PVs. For the de novo patients, who had not previously undergone PVI, EGF mapping was first performed pre-PVI and then PVI was performed. After a minimum 20 min wait following the last PVI ablation application, electrical isolation was checked. Once intact PVI was confirmed, EGF mapping was repeated in both atria. For the remainder of patients (redo population), who had previously undergone prior PVI as one of the inclusion criteria, all of the PVs were checked and if reconnections were found, PV touch-up was performed. After a minimum 20 min wait following the last PVI ablation application, once intact PVI was confirmed, EGF mapping was repeated in both atria.

With the 64-electrode basket catheter in a standardized position, unipolar EGMs were recorded for 1 min, generating an EGF summary map. The EGF summary map displays all active sources and passive rotational phenomena detected over the 1 min recording. Three serial 1 min recordings were obtained in each basket position, resulting in a total of 180 s of recordings per basket position. As acquisition of these map triplets was not a requisite part of the FLOW-AF study protocol, triplet maps were not obtained at every location and, in some locations, >3 maps were recorded in the same location. If >3 recordings were obtained in a single position, only the first 3 were included in this analysis. If the rhythm changed in between or during serial recordings, these were excluded from this analysis.

Source prevalence analysis

The EGF mapping algorithm detects sources as origins of excitation from a singularity of flow vectors, which emanate or diverge from this location. All detected active sources (low prevalence, blue; high, red) and passive rotations (low prevalence, grey, to high, white) are summarized with a radius of influence in the EGF summary map, where the radius depends on the velocity detected around the singularities.15 Corresponding prevalence histogram plots display cumulative data from each 2 s segment of the 1 min recording. Each 2 s segment yields 105 results of the source detection process. If at a certain position in each of the 105 frames, a source is detected, the prevalence or detection rate of the respective segment would be 100%. The segment containing the highest prevalence or rate of source detection is called the most source-prevalent segment, and the respective source is called the leading source. To determine source activity level (SAC), all segments with prevalence are counted to calculate the percentage of time during which a leading source was detected as active. The SAC of all segments is then normalized by the SAC of the most relevant segment to 100%, and the average of all normalized segment SAC yields the SAC value of the full minute as displayed on the EGF summary map.20 For example, if an unstable or switching source is detected on average in 59/105 frames over all 30 2 s segments recorded over 1 min but in 105/105 frames in the most relevant segment, then SAC in that segment is 100%, and total prevalence and SAC are both 56.1%. A detailed example of how EGF mapping captures the time-dependent activity of AF sources is shown in Figure 2. For the present analysis, we examined source prevalence calculated over the first 15, 30, and 60 s of recording. We then compared each of these calculated source prevalence from the first 15, 30, and 60 s to the result of the combined second and third minutes of recording and computed the mean absolute variation. The variation in calculated prevalence was also compared between the first full minute vs. second full minute of recording.

Visual electrographic flow map classification

To assess the consistency and reproducibility of EGF maps, we created a classification framework with which to evaluate map triplets (Figure 3). All maps were visually compared by two independent physician investigators (A.M. and M.H.K.) and adjudicated by two additional independent physician investigators (V.Y.R. and P.R.). This schema starts by examining overall EGF map patterns including detection and location of the leading source in each map compared with other maps obtained in the same basket position. If the leading source is visualized on all three maps within a ≤1 electrode space, then the map triplet is classified as an Exact Match. However, as some sources exhibit intermittent source activity such that they are not stably active over time and switch on and off—if the same leading source was detected and localized within a ≤1 electrode space in any two of three maps and the third map shows a distinctly separate leading source in a different location—these triplets were categorized as Switching Sources. Non–source-dependent AF was characterized by highly variable switching sources and no relevant source with SAC threshold ≥26.5%. Source activity level threshold for relevance was set at 26.5% for the FLOW-AF ablation protocol based on a retrospective analysis of clinical EGF cases that showed that outcome dependence of AF SAC peaked at 26% and the selection of a statistically derived threshold for SAC enabled the definition of clinical relevance.16 In cases where the leading source changed from minute to minute, but with none above threshold, triplets were classified as No Relevant Sources. Examples of each category are presented in Figure 4.

For comparison of inter-procedural EGF mapping, by definition, the basket catheter position was not identical from the first procedure to the next 3 months later. As such, the electroanatomic maps were visually evaluated with the basket electrode visualizations to identify the anatomic locations of any EGF-identified sources. Images of the basket catheter with the labelled electrodes were stored in multiple views to enable the authors to localize the electrode coordinates from the EGF maps to the basket catheter electrodes on the electroanatomic maps. Inter-procedural EGF map comparisons were judged by the same criteria as the intra-procedural evaluations. To determine spatiotemporal stability between procedures, we looked to see if the active sources above threshold seen on the final EGF maps recorded during the first procedure (de novo PVI only) were again seen in the same anatomic locations in the first EGF maps prior to any ablation in the second procedure (randomization procedure scheduled 3 months after de novo PVI procedure).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are shown in percentages. For paired samples, we performed a double-sided Student’s t-test. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (State Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

The mean LA diameter was 4.4 ± 0.6 mm. Mean CHA2DS2-VASc score was 2.4 ± 1.2.

Map source prevalence consistency

A total of 224 unique map triplets were obtained, of which 113 (50%) and 111 (50%) were obtained from the LA and RA, respectively. Comparing calculated leading source prevalence of the first 15 s with the last 120 s, the average deviation in calculated prevalence level was 12.0 ± 0.01%. Comparing the first 30 or first 60 s with the last 120 s, average deviations in source prevalence decreased to 8.8 ± 0.01% and only 5.9 ± 0.004%, respectively. The average deviation in computed prevalence of the first 60 s of recording compared with the second 60 s of recording was 6.4 ± 0.004% (Figure 5A). The average deviation in calculated source prevalence was statistically different comparing 15 or 30 s vs. the last 120 s (P < 0.05) and comparing the first 30 or 60 s vs. the last 120 s (P < 0.05); however, the difference between comparing the first 60 s with the last 120 s vs. the first 60 s with the second 60 s was not statistically significant (P = 0.16). Above an SAC of 30%, there was no significant difference in average deviation in source prevalence whether calculated from recordings of 60 vs. 120 s (Figure 5B).

(A) Average deviation in average source prevalence: shown is a comparison of the calculated leading source prevalence of the first 15, 30, and 60 s with the last 120 s as well as the first 60 vs. second 60 s. (B) Comparison of the deviation in source prevalence: above an SAC of 30%, there was no significant difference in average deviation in source prevalence whether calculated from recordings of 60 vs. 120 s.

Intra-procedural electrographic flow map visualization of leading sources over 3 min

Using the classification criteria outlined in Figure 3, there were 97 triplets (43.3%) designated as Exact Match. Of these Exact Matches, 24 (25%) revealed no relevant sources above threshold. There were 95 triplets (42.4%) that showed Switching Sources with an exact match in 2 of 3 maps and a third map showing a completely different leading source. All 95 maps categorized as Switching Sources were secondarily reviewed to determine whether they might represent map shifts; however, all of these triplets showed consistently stable non-dominant sources, passive rotations, and/or stable flow patterns from map to map. Thus, although leading SAC fluctuated over time, the maps of these intermittent, switching sources were consistent and did not appear to represent map shifts. Of this group, 57 (60.0%) revealed no relevant sources above threshold in any of the 3 maps, consistent with the fact that many of these sources themselves exhibited less stable activity.

Finally, there were 32 triplets (14.3%) with no relevant or stable sources. In other words, the SAC of the most active source was always below threshold and changed from minute to minute. These triplets were all secondarily reviewed, and the consistent detection and location of stable passive rotational phenomena and other less active singularities suggest that although no stable sources were detected, the maps were consistent. There were no triplets that revealed inter-map shifts.

Taken together, these data demonstrated a substantial percentage of intra-procedural consistency of active sources over repetitive 1 min EGF map acquisitions.

Inter-procedural electrographic flow map visualization of leading sources over 3 months

In total, 18 de novo persistent AF patients returned for a remap procedure 3 months after their de novo PVI procedure. There were 12 patients who were fully mapped and remapped, i.e. who underwent 2 EGF mapping procedures scheduled 3 months apart. On average, the remap procedures were performed at 99.7 ± 20.9 days post-PVI, after de novo PVI. Five patients were non-inducible for AF at the time of the second procedure and were excluded. One additional patient was excluded because post-PVI basket position was not stored with the EGF maps such that anatomic location of sources could not be verified. Accordingly, EGF maps were recorded from the 12 remaining patients at 3 months.

Among these 12 patients, there were 18 sources with SAC > 26%; 10 were detected in the RA and 8 in the LA after de novo PVI. Seven patients had sources in both atria. After reconciling the corresponding standardized basket positions of the EGF maps using the electroanatomical localization data, remap procedures revealed that 16 of the 18 (89.0%) sources were at the same anatomic location as in the initial procedure. The remaining two sources were detected in the SVC after the initial procedure post-PVI and were again seen in the SVC on remap but in a different aspect of the SVC. One patient did not have any sources detected post-PVI in the de novo procedure but was found to have an RA source after the PVs were reisolated during the remap procedure.

While there was no way to guarantee colocalization of the recording area, i.e. basket electrode locations, the basket positions were standardized in the FLOW-AF protocol and, thus, generally covered the same recording areas of interest but with inherently different individual electrode locations. As shown in the example in Figure 6, there was good correlation of not only the anatomic locations and flow patterns of the leading sources but also conservation of passive rotations across both 3 min and 3 months.

Discussion

We present a prospective experience using EGF mapping technology to repetitively detect, localize, and characterize putative AF sources. The major findings of this study are as follows:

Electrographic flow mapping based on 1 min unipolar EGM recordings from a 64-electrode basket catheter generates largely consistent source detection, localization, and prevalence statistics.

Together, these observations increase one’s confidence that EGF mapping identifies in vivo, time-dependent dynamic electrical behaviour of putative extra-PV AF sources.

In persistent AF, it is generally accepted that electrical remodelling can be a consequence of chronic structural remodelling with the creation of an abnormal substrate and the existence of extra-PV drivers and triggers of AF.21 Optical mapping studies in pre-clinical AF models have demonstrated focal enhanced automaticity or triggered activity and functional intramural re-entry circuits.22–25 These focal activations or active sources have also been found in proximity to passive rotational phenomena.10,19 A multitude of algorithms and technologies have been put forth trying to identify relevant extra-PV ablation targets.26 However, pre-clinical studies have demonstrated that mapping algorithms may not be able to detect the true drivers of fibrillatory conduction but rather detect epiphenomenal rotational and focal activation sites unrelated to AF induction or maintenance.27 Indeed, clinical studies using these various mapping strategies have yielded inconsistent clinical outcomes.9,10,28–30

The variable success of these targeted ablation strategies for discerning relevant AF sources may stem from the methodologies used and their inherent technical limitations.26,31 In particular, none of these technologies can reproducibly display the time-dependent activity of AF sources as shown with EGF mapping. Electrographic flow mapping does not rely upon any form of phase analysis or activation timing, and the use of the low-density electrode basket catheter enables the mapping of large regions of a cardiac chamber with high temporal resolution. Electrographic flow mapping uses this high temporal resolution to detect regions of coherent atrial electrical activation and can thus characterize more consistent flow patterns over time rather than trying to display complex conduction patterns that are highly variable. In contrast to EGF mapping, phase analysis not only suffers from the approximation of a periodic function resulting in the creation of artefacts and false positives, particularly when these often low-amplitude EGMs are mixed with signal noise, but is also unable to distinguish between active AF drivers and passive bystander rotational phenomena due to an inability to deduce excitation wave propagation vectors from estimated phase angles, and ultimately, phase maps remain limited in terms of detecting time-dependent relevance or stability over longer recordings.19,27,32

Likewise, other mapping methods that use sequential mapping techniques to stitch together short, multielectrode regional catheter recordings of repetitive morphological patterns of atrial activation, indexed by recordings from a fixed coronary sinus catheter, cannot see across the spectrum of time and may miss intermittent or switching sources.9 Still other signal processing techniques based on activation mapping remain limited by the inability to accurately assign local activation times during AF. While studies of activation mapping using shorter recording durations of 10 s have shown AF mapping to be unstable, more recent methods such as using an orbital plot to accumulate and analyse these data over at least 30 s have demonstrated evidence of spatiotemporal stability of activation wavefronts, complex fractionated atrial EGMs, and bipolar voltage.33 A study using non-contact charge density mapping to create a global activation map using an ultrasound basket catheter to generate a reconstruction of the atria and to collect EGM signals found that irregular activation patterns characterized by changing wavefront direction are both temporally and spatially more stable than focal activations or localized rotational activations; however, they also noted that stability appeared dependent on recording duration and only up to 30 s of recording was performed.34

Electrographic flow mapping attempts to address these limitations by organizing the chaotic endocardial EGMs during AF even at noise levels that cannot be interpreted by the human eye. Source detection with EGF mapping appears robust and uses an optical flow algorithm that triangulates between electrodes, provided that electrode distance is larger than excitation wavelength. It affords a spatial resolution beyond the grid constant of the multielectrode basket catheter used as its ‘input device’ for EGM acquisition. Panoramic mapping of the global atria also enables visualization of time-dependent electrical behaviours instead of a static map.

In this prospective experience, unipolar EGMs were recorded from a 64-electrode basket catheter over 1 min with a near real-time output of a summary map, EGF segment map, and 3D EGF segment map, along with source statistics and prevalence histogram. The speed with which an EGF map can be generated allows for efficient mapping of dynamic electrical behaviours occurring throughout the atria. Electrographic flow mapping demonstrates strong map reproducibility over serial measurements performed in the same basket position both during the same procedure and across separate procedures performed 3 months apart in the same patient. A clinical case report was recently published showing spatiotemporal stability of EGF mapping across ablation procedures performed 18 months apart in the same patient.35 Computed source prevalence exhibits only 6.4% average deviation when comparing any 60 s of recording, making source statistics reliable from map to map.

This study also supports a 1 min recording duration for EGF mapping although stability of SAC affects ideal recording time. As shown in Figure 5B, above an SAC of 30%, there was no significant difference in average deviation whether you recorded for 60 or for 120 s. At 60 s, we reach a point of diminishing marginal return on improved map reproducibility compared with longer recording durations. Thus, 60 s recordings appear to be the appropriate time trade-off from a clinical workflow standpoint.

It is important to recognize that EGF mapping did not always identify stable sources: in 14.3% of the map triplets, there were no relevant or stable AF sources. There are several potential reasons for this—the most obvious being that in those particular locations of the mapping basket catheter, there simply were no AF sources. It is also possible that because of potential ‘splaying’ of the splines of the basket catheter, there was reduced sensitivity in identifying putative AF sources. Or perhaps in these 14.3% of triplets, the resolution of EGF mapping in identifying AF sources was exceeded. Ultimately, because of the absence of ground truth in the identification of AF sources, the present mapping study can only speak to the specificity of AF source detection—the sensitivity of EGF mapping in source detection is completely unknown. Nonetheless, EGF mapping is differentiated by its ability to reproducibly show the time-dependent activity of AF sources. In the absence of ground truth for confirming AF sources, clinical data to support the clinical relevance of EGF-identified active sources are growing. A recently published single-centre, prospective clinical study of 70 persistent AF patients examined the ablation outcomes of 39 patients who underwent EGF-guided ablation compared with 16 patients who underwent PVI-only and 15 patients who received PVI + linear ablation and found that the PVI + EGF group had the lowest rate of AF recurrence at 12 months post-ablation (25.6% vs. 62.5% vs. 53.3%, P = 0.02).36 In addition, a retrospective study of 64 patients with persistent or long-standing persistent AF suggests that ablation of EGF-identified active sources improves outcomes post-ablation, while the persistence of EGF-identified sources post-ablation correlates with increased AF recurrence rates.16

Although reproducibility does not equal confidence in a mapping modality’s ability to faithfully reproduce the features of AF waves on a beat-by-beat basis, the question of the clinical utility of beat-by-beat mapping of AF also remains unclear as it may be unable to show more global, time-dependent electrical behaviours. With EGF mapping, we are proposing that the ability to zoom out and understand the time-dependent result of the beat-by-beat variations in AF waves may be more clinically relevant in terms of advancing our understanding of an individual patient’s disease. As such, the reproducibility and spatiotemporal stability of the statistically derived EGF summary maps becomes critically important.

Limitations

The most important limitation of this study is the absence of definitive proof that these reproducible sites were indeed sources that drive AF and not irrelevant epiphenomena. When published, the FLOW-AF clinical trial will address this question since these putative AF sources were ablated in a randomized fashion. Also, our study is limited by the relatively small patient numbers and map triplets included in this analysis—though it is unlikely that adding more patients would materially alter the conclusions of this analysis. For reasons of cost and practicality in a real-world clinical setting, in the FLOW-AF trial, the RA and LA were not mapped simultaneously and thus we cannot definitively rule out the absence of sources. Similarly, if it were practical in a real-world clinical setting, this analysis would have been strengthened by spacing the serial 1 min recordings by several minutes rather than seconds; however, the reproducibility of the inter-procedural EGF mapping reproducibility helps mitigate this concern. Reproducibility in two of three recordings may be considered too low for reliable mapping; however, it was unrealistic to record for 10 min per basket position in humans as this would have unacceptably prolonged the procedure.

Fourth, it is well-appreciated that the splines of the basket catheter used for atrial EGM acquisition have the potential to ‘splay’ apart to a variable extent. However: (i) by recording the location of each of the splines by electroanatomical mapping, we were able to maintain/reposition the basket in the same position for each recording, and (ii) because the fundamental strategy of the EGF algorithm is to identify EGM ‘flow’ away from AF sources, it is less sensitive to these variabilities in electrode spacing. Thus, while the sensitivity of the EGF algorithm may be affected by spline splaying, its specificity is unlikely to be affected. In terms of non-uniform endocardial contact with the basket electrodes and incomplete atrial coverage, the recommendation to perform EGF mapping in 2–3 different basket positions per atria provides some map overlap and improves the software’s ability to fully characterize the atrial source patterns. The EGF mapping algorithm also includes a near-field score that quantifies the near-field component of signals per electrode as an indication of the f-wave signal quality and displays the degree of contact in the map.

Lastly, due to the transient and shifting nature of non-relevant sources, we cannot absolutely exclude the possibility of small map shifts among the triplets classified as No Relevant Sources; however, this possibility was minimized by detailed map review to confirm the presence of other stable features that were consistent between maps. It should be noted that the accuracy of the multielectrode recording system for detecting local AF waves on a beat-by-beat basis remains unknown and Green’s interpolation does not completely mitigate the inherent limitation of an electrode capturing the potentials of near and far AF waves with unknown amplitudes; however, the goal of this study is to present data on the temporal stability of EGF mapping and its ability to show the time-dependent nature of AF conduction.

Conclusions

Electrographic flow mapping produces consistent and reproducible results for maps obtained over 3 min apart during the same procedure and for maps obtained over 3 months apart during different procedures. Longer recording durations improve map reproducibility for less stable sources but after 60 s of recording reach a plateau with minimal further incremental improvement in consistency. The higher the prevalence of a given source, the higher the map reproducibility across multiple recordings because the source itself is more stable.
