Engagement of general practice preceptors in teaching residents in outpatient settings in China: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Lingyan Wu, Jianjing Zhang, Weihua Wang, Jie Liu, Chuan Zou, Zhuozhuo Ren, Bing Li, Juan Wu, Xinyan Yu, Yingying Yu, Xinmei Zhou, Jiali Wang, Yening Zhou, Lingna Mao, Yuling Tong, Yi Guo, Zhenya Song, Zhijie Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutpatient training is crucial for the standardized residency training of General Practitioners (GPs) in China. However, there is concern that General Practice (GP) preceptors may lack the willingness and capacities to teach residents in outpatient settings. Understanding how GP preceptors practice routine outpatient training and their views is essential for designing strategies; however, this issue has not been well studied. Thus, our study aimed to investigate the extent of GP preceptors’ engagement in teaching residents in outpatient settings and to identify the factors associated with their practice of providing guidance in China.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we distributed a self-administered questionnaire to 417 GP preceptors. Participants were selected from 142 hospitals across 24 provinces and had participated in the National GP Preceptor Training Program in early May 2024 in Hangzhou, China. A descriptive analysis was used to assess the extent of GP preceptors teaching residents in outpatient settings and their perceptions of outpatient training. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify the associated factors of GP preceptors’ engagement in teaching residents in outpatient settings.ResultsOf the 297 participants included in the final analysis, 64.3% were female, with a mean age of 41.8 years (range: 29–56). A majority (63%) of GP preceptors expressed confidence in their ability to conduct outpatient training, and 68.7% indicated a willingness to increase their teaching efforts. While over 70% reported “always” or “often” providing helpful guidance to residents, only about a third consistently encouraged residents to take medical histories, perform physical examinations, and make preliminary clinical decisions. Logistic regression analysis shows that GP preceptors’ frequent outpatient training, more medical experience, longer preceptorship, and confidence and determination were positively correlated with offering helpful guidance to residents, whereas concerns about reduced consultation efficiency and income were negatively associated with this guidance.ConclusionAlthough most GP preceptors in China report a strong willingness to engage in outpatient training, their actual teaching practice falls short of expectations in some respects and is correlated with their prior experiences and perceptions of such training. Strategic interventions that offer incentives, support, and increased opportunities for engagement in outpatient training are essential to enhance teaching practices.Keywords: GP Preceptor, GP residents, Outpatient training, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Outpatient training is a component of postgraduate education in which residents gain practical experience in providing care in outpatient settings [1, 2]. Residents are supervised by experienced physicians known as preceptors who provide guidance, feedback, and support throughout the training process. Its primary goal is to develop skills necessary to manage outpatients, including diagnosis, decision-making, and treatment [3]. For general practice (GP) residents, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, the need for outpatient training is pronounced, necessitating the cultivation of a more holistic and patient-centered skill set attuned to the diverse and complex health needs of the community [4].

Given the vast population and the increasing demand for accessible healthcare services in China, the competencies of General Practitioners (GPs) in providing efficient and effective ambulatory care are crucial [4, 5]. As a core component of China’s ‘5 + 3’ GP training model—comprising a five-year undergraduate medical curriculum followed by a three-year residency program—the standardized training framework is designed to equip GP residents with a robust educational foundation. This system prepares GP residents to become competent practitioners capable of meeting the diverse healthcare needs of the Chinese population. China’s standardized GP residency training, as mandated by the Chinese Medical Doctor Association (CMDA) includes approximately four months in primary care, six months in specialty outpatient departments, and one month in general practice outpatient settings within a comprehensive hospital [6].

However, the current training system for GP residents in China is predominantly focused on inpatient care; not all departments have established outpatient training programs specifically for GP residents [6, 7]. Previous studies have pinpointed numerous challenges in delivering high-quality outpatient training for GP residents, which warrants further investigation and exploration for improvement [8]. Preceptors face considerable challenges, such as potential deficiencies in their capacities for clinical training [8–10]. Additionally, the demanding nature of clinical responsibilities may overshadow educational duties, leading to a diminished focus on mentorship and lack of time for teaching activities [10, 11]. Furthermore, GP preceptors may be hindered by concerns that teaching could lower their consultation efficiency or patient care quality. Studies suggested that teaching residents in outpatient settings could increase the length of patient consultations and, in some cases, may negatively influence patient satisfaction or the perceived quality of care [1, 12]. The absence of incentives for their educational efforts may exacerbate their reluctance to engage in the teaching process [13–15].

Despite an increasing number of studies targeting GP preceptors and exploring approaches to improve the quality of outpatient training, the current status of preceptors’ practices of teaching GPs in outpatient settings in China and its associated factors remain insufficiently understood. While some studies have investigated the varying perspectives and attitudes of GP preceptors towards outpatient training, there is a notable gap in research exploring the association between their perceptions and actual teaching practices [16–18]. This limitation may be further compounded by heterogeneity in preceptors and outpatient training environments across different regions and hospitals. Addressing this research gap is essential for developing targeted interventions that can enhance the educational experiences of GP residents and ensure the development of a standardized training system in China.

We hypothesized that GP preceptors with more extensive teaching experience and more positive attitudes significantly enhance their practice in delivering guidance. Our objective was to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the engagement of GP preceptors’ teaching residents in outpatient settings and to explore the factors associated with their practice of providing guidance in China. The insights derived from this study could inform the development of evidence-based strategies to enhance the overall quality of outpatient training programs.

Methods

Study design, population and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design, targeting a specific cohort of GP preceptors. The study period was from May 6 to May 12, 2024, when the 17th National GP Preceptor Training Program was conducted in Hangzhou, China. Our survey utilized a convenience sampling approach, specifically targeting GP preceptors who had participated in the program with experience teaching residents in outpatient settings. In total, 417 GP preceptors from 142 hospitals across 24 provinces were invited. The program was jointly organized by The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine and Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University.

The inclusion criteria required participants to be licensed GPs, hold the professional title of attending physician, and have obtained certification as GP preceptors. This requirement complies with CMDA regulations mandating that GP preceptors undergo training in clinical teaching methods every five years. Participants who either declined to provide informed consent or reported no prior experience of teaching GP residents in outpatient settings were excluded from the study.

The survey was distributed through an online questionnaire using the WeChat platform, a widely used social media application in China. Informed consent was obtained at the outset of the survey process, with each participant fully informed about the study’s objectives, content, rights, and obligations. All responses were collected anonymously to encourage candid feedback and protect the privacy of the respondents. Participants were given the option to withdraw from the survey at any point before submission with the assurance that their data would remain confidential.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the protection of participants’ rights and integrity of the research process [19].

Instrument

Educational activities within outpatient settings include observing residents’ consultation, providing explanations, demonstrations, offering them opportunities to engage in patient consultations, and giving constructive feedback and suggestions for improvement. The development of the questionnaire utilized in this study was anchored in standardized scales published by the CMDA, supplemented by relevant previous research, and refined through extensive discussions within the research team and consultations with subject matter experts [10, 18]. To enhance the validity and reliability of the questionnaire, a pilot test was conducted with a small sample of preceptors to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the items. Feedback from the pilot test was used to make the necessary revisions before the full-scale administration of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire comprised three main sections. The first section provides general information about GP preceptors, including sex, age, professional title, educational background, specialty, years of practice, scope of practice, and hospital type. Additionally, this section inquired about years of service as a preceptor and their experience with outpatient teaching, including the number of residents they typically supervised during each outpatient training session and the frequency of conducting outpatient training.

The second section examines preceptors’ practices regarding outpatient training for GP residents, encompassing 10 items. For example, respondents were asked about the frequency of encouraging residents to make preliminary decisions for patients or observing their performance when they were caring for patients. Responses to each item were on a four-point scale ranging from “Always” to “Never.”

The third section investigates preceptors’ perceptions of teaching GP residents in an outpatient setting and consists of 12 items. It includes both positive statements (e.g., “Patients have greater trust in me when the residents are in the clinic”) and negative statements (e.g., “Outpatient training reduces the efficiency of my patient consultations”). Respondents are asked to rate their agreement with each statement on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

Data collection

The questionnaire was crafted and uploaded to the Questionnaire Star platform, where it was edited and refined to ensure clarity and relevance. Questionnaire Star (https://www.wjx.cn/) is an online survey platform operated by Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd. Recognized for its ease of use and efficiency, Questionnaire Star facilitates the completion of surveys across various digital devices, including smartphones, tablets, and computers. The platform’s automated features streamline the process of disseminating the survey to a wide audience while also ensuring data integrity through real-time compilation and storage.

We used the Questionnaire Star platform to generate an online link for survey distribution. We employed WeChat to share the survey link with GP preceptors, enabling them to initiate the survey process conveniently. The survey was designed with built-in quality control measures to ensure the quality and completeness of responses. It was structured to require full engagement from respondents, thereby minimizing the likelihood of incomplete or missing data. To facilitate a thoughtful response to each question, the survey was programmed to prevent submission until at least 90 s had elapsed since the completion of the first item. Furthermore, stringent measures were implemented to avoid potential duplicate entries, limiting each unique IP address to a single submission. Upon submission, the data from each completed questionnaire were instantly collected and transferred to a centralized web-based database by our research team, who were vigilant in maintaining the accuracy and integrity of the collected information.

In addition to the initial distribution, intermittent reminders were distributed during the survey period to prompt participants to complete the survey if they had not yet done so. To encourage active participation from GP preceptors, a monetary award of 10 RMB was offered to each respondent who fully completed the questionnaire. Upon completion of the survey, data were downloaded from the Questionnaire Star platform. A thorough manual screening process was conducted on all responses to identify and exclude invalid questionnaires with clear inconsistencies or incomplete answers.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a descriptive analysis to characterize the categorical variables and GP preceptors’ practices and perceptions of teaching residents in outpatient settings. To explore the factors associated with GP preceptors’ teaching practices during outpatient training, we selected specific statements as the primary outcomes of interest. The statement, “Overall, I provide helpful guidance for the residents during the outpatient training,” was identified as a key indicator of the teaching practice in outpatient settings. The frequency of this statement’s responses, categorized as “always,” “often,” “sometimes,” and “never,” was subjected to logistic regression analysis. This analysis was conducted to evaluate both the unadjusted and adjusted associations between the main statement and various factors, including the respondents’ characteristics, teaching experience, and perceptions of outpatient training. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed α value of 0.05 for all analyses. SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform all analyses.

Results

GP preceptors’ characteristics and teaching experience

A total of 348 GP preceptors consented to participate in the survey (response rate: 83.5%) and 332 respondents completed the survey as required. After excluding 35 respondents who reported that they lacked experience of teaching GP residents in outpatient settings, the final analysis included 297 preceptors. Most preceptors (64.3%) were female. The average age of the respondents was 41.8 years (range: 29–56). Nearly half (42.7%) of the GP preceptors had a master’s or doctoral degree and over half (56.2%) had a professional title higher than that of attending physicians. Nearly half (49.2%) of participants were affiliated with primary care institutions.

The average duration of teaching experience reported by the preceptors was 4.9 years (range: 1–24 years). Nearly half (41.1%) of the participants supervised a single resident during outpatient training, and only 7.1% of the GP respondents supervised four or more residents. The frequency of outpatient training varied, with 38.7% of preceptors doing so less frequently than once a month and 32.0% performing such training once a week (Table 1).

GP preceptors’ practice of teaching residents in outpatient settings

The majority (71.0%) of respondents reported that they “always” or “often” provide helpful guidance to the residents during outpatient training (Fig. 1). Over half (50.5%) of respondents “always” or “often” engaged in thorough communication with residents before training begins, while 59.6% placed emphasis on acquiring a clear understanding of the residents’ general information and clinical competencies. Over 70% of these preceptors consistently tailored their guidance to residents’ demonstrated competencies. Furthermore, approximately 70‒80% of the participants routinely observed the residents’ clinical performance, praising their commendable efforts and around 50% provided constructive criticism to address areas for improvement. Nevertheless, only a third of the GP preceptors reported that they “always” or “often” encouraged residents to take medical histories, conduct physical examinations, and make preliminary clinical decisions, indicating a low level of engagement in these critical aspects of training.

GP preceptors’ perceptions of outpatient training

Approximately two-thirds of the respondents “strongly agreed” or “agreed” that they were fully competent to undertake such training and were willing to invest additional time and effort in this endeavor. The majority (76.1%) recognized the benefits of updating their professional knowledge and 70.7% recognized the benefits of helping them identify their own shortcomings. A total of 77.1% of the respondents perceived the presence of residents in the clinic as a factor that bolsters patient trust in medical professionals.

Despite these positive perceptions, a quarter of the respondents (24.9%) “strongly agreed” or “agreed” that outpatient training could reduce the efficiency of patient consultations, while 14.5% feared that it might adversely affect patient satisfaction. A small percentage (13.5%) of the participants felt that their abilities were not sufficient for outpatient training, and 9.8% reported little interest in guiding residents in the clinic. Additionally, 33.7% of the participants voiced apprehension regarding the potential interference of outpatient training with their routine patient care and its possible detrimental effects on their income (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with GP preceptors’ practice of outpatient training

The logistic regression analysis found that the years of practice (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.05; 95% CI, 1.02–1.09) and the years of being a preceptor (aOR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.07–1.77) are positively associated with providing helpful guidance (Table 2). Hospital type was found to be associated with the preceptors’ teaching practices, with those working in secondary or tertiary hospitals being more likely to provide helpful guidance during outpatient training (aOR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.01–2.54). The frequency of outpatient training is another critical factor. Preceptors who conducted training sessions more frequently, especially those who did so at least twice a week, exhibited a higher likelihood of providing beneficial instruction.

Perceptions of outpatient training also play a crucial role in teaching practices. GP preceptors who felt fully competent in undertaking outpatient training (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.17–1.92) and those willing to devote more time and energy to the training (aOR 1.78, 95% CI 1.14–2.79) were more likely to provide effective guidance. Conversely, preceptors who believed that outpatient training reduces the efficiency of their patient consultations (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.31–0.74) and those concerned about the potential negative impact on their income (aOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.29–0.87) were less likely to provide helpful guidance, suggesting that these perceptions may hinder the quality of teaching in outpatient settings.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a national survey to explore the perceptions and teaching practices of GP preceptors in outpatient settings. The findings revealed prevalent positive perceptions of outpatient training among most respondents. However, this enthusiasm seems to be at odds with the observed inadequacies in some of their teaching practices. Our study also revealed several factors that were significantly correlated with the teaching practices during outpatient training, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between perceptions and practice.

Overall, the respondents in our study had positive perceptions of outpatient training. Most preceptors considered themselves fully competent and willing to invest additional time and energy to undertake outpatient training and perceived professional growth and enhanced patient trust as benefits of outpatient training. This finding resonates with attitudes previously identified in various healthcare settings in China, as illustrated by earlier surveys [20, 21]. Furthermore, a recent study confirmed this trend, demonstrating that 78.96% of GP preceptors were inclined to participate in outpatient training [22]. A study conducted in Thuringia, Germany showed that 81.9% of GPs were highly motivated to train students in their own practice [23]. However, more than one-quarter of the respondents in our study voiced concerns regarding the potential reduction in the efficiency of patient consultations during training sessions, which is particularly salient given the rigorous demands of GPs’ clinical schedules in outpatient settings in China [12]. Survey of Thuringian GPs echoed these concerns, with 42.3% of GPs fearing a reduction in the number of patients they could treat, while 37.9% were concerned about the potential for training to disrupt the operational flow of their practices [23]. Reconciling teaching responsibilities in fast-paced clinical environments is important for preceptors. This failure often leads to a scarcity of time, which can compromise the depth and richness of teaching interactions, and may impede opportunities for profound exchanges [18].

Moreover, nearly one-third of GP preceptors voiced apprehension regarding the potential adverse financial implications of engaging in outpatient training. Although the empirical validation of these concerns remains unexplored, the expressed apprehension highlights the urgent need to reassess incentive mechanisms within the training system for residents. Previous research has indicated the pivotal role of performance evaluations in stimulating teaching engagement and found that incorporating teaching responsibilities into these evaluations and offering commensurate rewards could substantially enhance physician participation in training activities [10, 20]. Our findings align with these observations and underscore the need for institutional frameworks to balance the dual objectives of clinical service and medical education. Notably, Block et al. [24] quantified the additional financial costs associated with interprofessional outpatient training programs, highlighting the necessity of a careful financial assessment to sustain such educational models. Addressing these financial concerns through well-structured incentive systems may encourage greater participation from preceptors and enhance the overall quality of outpatient training.

We also investigated GP preceptors’ practices of teaching residents in outpatient settings. We found that the majority of preceptors observed residents’ clinical performances, adapted their teaching methodologies to align with the demonstrated competencies of residents, and provide commendations and constructive criticism. This approach is recognized as a fundamental component of effective medical education as underscored in the guidelines published by the CMDA [25]. These findings suggest that the professionalization and standardization of outpatient teaching are improving as a result of the Chinese government’s current emphasis on medical education of general practice [8]. Nevertheless, the observed low engagement of GP preceptors in encouraging residents to undertake essential clinical tasks such as medical history taking, physical examination, and preliminary decision-making is a matter of concern. Recent research indicates that 37.59% of GP preceptors consider the cultivation of clinical thinking and enhancement of clinical decision-making skills among GP residents to be the most challenging teaching objective [22]. This finding contrasts with previous studies that emphasized the critical role of these activities in building a foundation for clinical reasoning and decision-making [20, 26], which may indicate an imperative need for enhanced training or a shift in preceptors’ teaching methodologies to prioritize these foundational skills.

The findings on the practice of outpatient training offer valuable insights into the factors influencing the quality of GP preceptors’ teaching in outpatient settings. Experienced preceptors, defined by more years of practice and preceptorship as well as conducting outpatient training more frequently, are more likely to provide beneficial guidance to residents. This is consistent with previous research, indicating that accumulated clinical experience and pedagogical skills lead to enhanced educational competencies [27]. Additionally, the type of hospital in which preceptors work influences their teaching practices, with those in secondary or tertiary hospitals being more inclined to offer effective instruction, possibly because of the complexity of cases and available resources [15, 18].

GP preceptors’ perceptions during outpatient training are also pivotal factors. We found that those who felt competent and were willing to invest additional time and energy in training were more likely to provide helpful guidance. However, negative perceptions such as the belief that training reduces consultation efficiency or impacts income can hinder teaching quality. Our findings align with those of Pichlhöfer et al. [17], who reported that 51% of GP preceptors felt an increased demand for time to ensure the effectiveness of outpatient training. This underscores the challenge of reconciling the time constraints inherent in balancing clinical duties with teaching responsibilities. The potential impact on their performance-related income is an additional concern that may adversely affect the teaching activities of GP preceptors. In China, dissatisfaction among GP preceptors with current teaching performance incentives is prevalent [18, 28]. The prevailing sentiment is that remuneration for teaching efforts is inequitable, and the perceived lack of tangible benefits for career advancement further exacerbates this dissatisfaction [18]. These factors may contribute to the hesitancy of some GP preceptors to actively participate in outpatient teaching.

Our study has several implications for clinical practice and future research. First, the results underscore the importance of the ongoing support for and professional development of GP preceptors. Medical institutions need to foster environments that encourage mentorship and continuous learning, which involve the provision of regular training workshops, feedback mechanisms, and resources to facilitate effective teaching strategies [29]. Second, our study suggests the need to reassess the balance between clinical responsibilities and teaching duties. Medical institutions should consider implementing policies that protect teaching time and prioritize the development of essential clinical skills among GP residents. This may also involve the creation of incentives that recognize the efforts of preceptors in outpatient training. Future research should focus on the development of equitable incentive systems to understand how to align the interests of GP preceptors with institutions’ educational goals. Third, the factors influencing the teaching practices of GP preceptors can be further studied using other methodologies. For example, longitudinal studies can track changes in teaching practices over time, providing insights into the impact of different factors. In addition, qualitative research can explore the personal experiences and motivations of preceptors, offering a contextual understanding of the challenges and facilitators of outpatient training.

Although our study provides valuable insights into the practices and perceptions of GP preceptors in outpatient settings, it has some limitations. First, the total number of respondents, although substantial, was relatively limited. The nonrandom sampling method employed may have introduced a selection bias, potentially skewing the generalizability of the findings. However, it is noteworthy that the respondents represented a diverse sample of 142 hospitals across 24 provinces in China, and most met the eligibility criteria for the study, enhancing the external validity of the results. Another limitation is the potential for social desirability bias in the survey responses. Given that the survey questions pertained to professional practices and attitudes, the respondents may have been influenced by their desire to present themselves in a favorable light. This could have led to overreporting of positive practices and attitudes or underreporting of negative ones. For instance, preceptors may have reported more frequent engagement in teaching activities or more positive perceptions of the teaching process than actually occur. In addition to social desirability bias, self-assessment bias is a critical factor that could distort the correlation between perceived and actual teaching practices. To mitigate these biases, we carefully selected GP preceptors with outpatient teaching experience to participate in the survey, ensuring that their responses were grounded in practical expertise. Furthermore, we implemented an anonymous survey process to encourage candid responses and minimize the influence of social desirability and self-assessment biases. These measures allow us to gather more accurate and unbiased insights into the teaching practices of GP preceptors.

Conclusions

Our study provides a comprehensive overview of the engagement of GP preceptors in teaching residents in outpatient settings in China, uncovering a strong willingness to teach but also significant challenges that hinder providing effective guidance. The findings reveal a gap between preceptors’ teaching intentions and the actual engagement of teaching practices, driven by factors such as the type of affiliated hospital, clinical experience, preceptorship duration, prior outpatient training experience, and perceptions of outpatient training. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions is critical to aligning teaching practices with preceptors’ intentions. By refining training systems to better support preceptors, these efforts can enhance the overall quality of GP resident training and ensure that educational goals and standards are effectively met.
