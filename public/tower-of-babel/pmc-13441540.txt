Deletion of Ybx1 results in germ cell hypoplasia in mouse embryos
Authors: Shinya IKEDA, Yayoi OBATA, Yusuke SOTOMARU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral evolutionarily conserved genes have been reported to be involved in gametogenesis. Y-box binding proteins, which have a conserved nucleotide-binding cold-shock domain, have been reported in both vertebrates and invertebrates. Although homologues of Ybx1 in Drosophila and zebrafish play important roles in oogenesis, the involvement of mouse Ybx1 in gametogenesis or gonadal development remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the female and male fetal gonadal phenotypes in Ybx1 knockout mice. Embryos of Ybx1 knockout mice at E13.5 showed slender gonads compared to those of the controls. Whole-mount gonadal immunostaining revealed a significant decrease in the number of female embryonic germ cells. Immunocytochemistry revealed that YB-1, a protein encoded by Ybx1, is expressed in both germ and gonadal somatic cells. In contrast, transcriptome analysis of germ cells and gonadal somatic cells at E13.5 revealed that deletion of Ybx1 did not cause global changes in gene expression. However, Anapc5 and Rpn1, which have been implicated in germ cell survival, were significantly downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female germ cells. Furthermore, Acta2 and Mmp2, known Ybx1-regulated genes, were also significantly downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female gonadal somatic cells, potentially contributing to germ cell hypoplasia. Collectively, our results suggest that YB-1 may have a role in gonadal development by promoting germ cell survival and gonadal development.Keywords: Fetal germ cells, Germ cell hypoplasia, Oogenesis

Gametogenesis produces mature oocytes or sperm with developmental potential for the next generation of sexually reproductive vertebrates and invertebrates. In mice, gametogenesis begins with primordial germ cells (PGCs), which arise as a small cluster of cells at the base of the allantois of an embryo at the embryonic day 7.25 (E7.25) [1]. Subsequently, PGCs colonize the genital ridges at approximately E10.5, increasing their number via mitosis. PGCs then complete sexual differentiation according to the surrounding somatic cells by E13.5 [2]. Fetal germ cells, which are segregated into oogenic and spermatogenic fates by sexual differentiation, proceed to the first wave of gametogenesis over a period of approximately 4 weeks, eventually becoming mature oocytes or sperms [3, 4]. Many studies have reported that diverse transcriptional factors and post-transcriptional regulators participate in these processes [5,6,7]. Of these, highly conserved proteins have been identified among species [8, 9].

Y-box binding proteins have a Cold Shock Domain (CSD) that is conserved among prokaryotes, invertebrates, and vertebrates [10]. Y-box binding proteins consist of a nucleotide-binding CSD and relatively low-conservation N- and C-terminal domains, which enable them to serve protein- and context-specific roles. In mice, three genes encode the Y-box binding proteins. Ybx2 is a germline-specific gene expressed in spermatocytes and spermatids during spermatogenesis [11]. In addition, Ybx2 is expressed in growing oocytes, and its homozygous deletion causes infertility in females and males [12, 13]. Ybx3 is expressed in the tissues of E9.5 and later-stage embryos, whereas its expression is limited to the testes of adult mice [14]. Although homozygous deletion of Ybx3 does not affect birth weight or growth, it causes infertility in a subpopulation of male mice [14]. Furthermore, it has been reported that male mice harboring compound heterozygous deletions of Ybx2 and Ybx3 are infertile, suggesting a potential functional redundancy between these two genes during spermatogenesis [15]. In addition to Ybx3, Ybx1 is ubiquitously expressed in mouse embryos [16].

Homologues of Ybx1 in Drosophila and zebrafish have been reported to play important roles as RNA-binding proteins in the female germline [17, 18]. A previous report showed that overexpression of human YBX1 was able to mitigate developmental defects in germline stem cells of Drosophila caused by the deletion of YPS, a Ybx1 homologue in Drosophila, suggesting the importance of Ybx1 function and its preservation between species [17]. In mice, YB-1, encoded by Ybx1, is a multifunctional protein that regulates transcription and post-transcriptional processes in a context-dependent manner [19]. Although several reports have shown that homozygous deletion of Ybx1 causes embryonic lethality with complete penetrance from E14.5, entailing exencephaly, liver hypoplasia, and anemia, the role of Ybx1 in the mouse germline remains unclear [16, 20]. A recent study revealed that conditional deletion of Ybx1 during spermatogenesis causes subfertility in male mice [21]. However, the expression of YB-1 in mouse fetal gonads and whether deletion of Ybx1 causes reproductive abnormalities in mouse embryos remains unknown. Identifying the involvement of mouse Ybx1 in fetal gonadal development will provide important insights into mammalian gametogenesis. In this study, we investigated the gonadal phenotypes of mouse embryos lacking Ybx1.

Materials and Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J and ICR mice were purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan) and used to establish and maintain Ybx1 knockout mice. The mice were maintained on a 12:12 LD cycle with ad libitum access to food. All experiments were approved by the Committee of Animal Experimentation at Hiroshima University (#A25-12).

Establishment of the Ybx1-deleted mice

Ybx1 knockout mice were established using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Briefly, two sets of crRNAs and tracrRNAs (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA) were annealed to form crRNA/tracrRNA complexes. The complexes flanking exon 3 of Ybx1 were incubated with Cas9 protein (Takara, Shiga, Japan) to prepare ctRNPs [22]. The prepared ctRNPs were injected into the pronuclei of C57BL/6J zygotes. Injected zygotes were cultured for 1 d in KSOMaa medium, and 2-cell embryos were transferred to the oviducts of pseudopregnant ICR mice as recipients. The founder knockout mice were crossed with C57BL/6J mice, and the descendants were maintained as heterozygous knockout mice. The target sequences of the crRNAs and oligonucleotides used for PCR genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Embryonic heads from E13.5 fetal mice were lysed in 2× sample buffer (380 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 4% SDS, and 20% glycerol) with glass beads. The protein concentration of each sample was measured using a TaKaRa BCA Protein Assay Kit (Takara) on a TriStar LB941 multimode plate reader (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany). The protein concentration of each sample was equalized, and the samples were diluted with 4× loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 8% SDS, 40% glycerol, 10% beta-mercaptoethanol, and 0.05% bromophenol blue). Subsequently, the proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and membrane transfer, followed by blocking with TBST containing 5% skim milk and immunoreaction. Antibodies and their dilutions are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunocytochemistry of gonadal cells

Embryonic gonads were dissected from E13.5 embryos, and the mesonephros was removed. The gonads were then subjected to enzymatic digestion using CTK (collagenase type IV, trypsin and knockout serum replacement) and Accutase. After enzymatic digestion, the gonadal cells were resuspended in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (−), settled on cover glasses, and fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Subsequently, blocking was performed using a blocking solution consisting of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.1% Triton-X 100 in 1× PBS (−) for 1 h. Thereafter, primary antibody treatment was performed at 4°C overnight, followed by secondary antibody treatment at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, cover glasses were mounted on VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Confocal images were acquired using the Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Antibodies used and their dilution rates are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Whole-mount immunostaining of embryonic gonads

Embryonic gonads were removed and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Blocking was performed in the blocking solution described above with agitation for 1 day, and the gonads were gently agitated in a primary antibody solution for 7 days. After subsequent agitation in a secondary antibody solution with 1 µg/ml DAPI, the gonads were mounted on VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI. Optical sections were captured using a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope at a distance of 5 µm between the planes. Numbers of germ cells per gonad were manually counted by examining each image stack using Fiji software [23]. The top three and the bottom three slices of the image stacks of the gonad were excluded to generate maximum intensity projections, thereby removing non-specific fluorescence on the surface of the gonad (Supplementary Fig. 1). The antibodies used and their dilution rates are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Purifying germ cells and gonadal somatic cells using fluorescence-activated cell sorting

Embryonic gonads dissected from E13.5 embryos, which had been removed from the mesonephros, were subjected to enzymatic digestion using CTK solution and Accutase. Gonadal germ cells were labelled with BV421 Mouse Anti-SSEA-1 antibody (1:100; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; catalog no. 562705) by incubating with the antibody on ice for 1 h. Subsequently, the labelled germ cells and non-labelled somatic cells were purified using a BD FACSAria Fusion Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). Purity of the sorted germ cells was preliminarily assessed by immunofluorescence using anti-MVH and anti-GENA antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 2).

mRNA-sequencing analysis

Germ and gonadal somatic cells from female and male fetuses were sorted using the CDS Sorting Solution of a SMART-Seq HT Plus Kit (Takara). Three biological replicates of each sample were collected for the mRNA-seq library construction. The sorted samples were then subjected to cDNA synthesis and library preparation using a kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Indexed mRNA-seq libraries were sequenced using the NextSeq 500 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) under a 76 base-pair single-end format. Sequencing data are available from the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under the accession number PRJDB38721.

Data analyses of mRNA-seq

The UCSC mm10 mouse genome and gene annotations were downloaded from Illumina iGenomes (http://jp.support.illumina.com/sequencing/sequencing_software/igenome.html). An annotation file of ribosomal RNA was obtained from the RepeatMasker track on the UCSC genome browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/).

mRNA-seq reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) and aligned to the genome using Hisat2 (version 2.1.0) under default settings [24, 25]. Ribosomal reads were removed from the aligned data using Samtools (version 1.10) and bedtools (version 2.27.1) [26, 27]. To count reads aligned to gene features, featureCounts (version 1.6.4) was used with the following options: -M -O -fraction [28]. The resultant read count data were subjected to differential expression analyses using the edgeR package (version 3.40.2) with RPM 1 as the cutoff [29]. To visualize mRNA-seq read coverage on the IGV genome browser, aligned data of biological replicates were merged and converted to a bigwig file using deepTools (version 3.5.4) with count per million (CPM) normalization [30, 31]. Transcript per million (TPM) values were calculated using R (version 4.2.2) and RStudio (version 2022.12.0.353) software [32, 33].

Statistical analysis

A Student’s t-test was performed to compare fetal germ cell numbers between Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) gonads using the R software.

Results

Establishment of Ybx1-knockout mice

To investigate the involvement of YB-1 in fetal gonadal development, we established whole-body Ybx1-knockout mice. The knockout mice were devoid of exon 3 of Ybx1, which encodes a partial region of the CSD (Fig. 1A). Exon deletion was expected to cause a frameshift in the open reading frame and ablation of the stop codon. We crossed heterozygous Ybx1 knockout mice to obtain homozygous knockout embryos and recovered embryos at E13.5, which were not anticipated to cause a lethal phenotype. In accordance with previous reports, exencephaly, small livers, and pale bodies were observed in Ybx1(–/–) embryos, but not in control Ybx1(+/–) fetuses (Fig. 1B). Next, embryonic heads of E13.5 embryos were subjected to western blotting, and the expression of YB-1 was assessed. YB-1 expression was abrogated in Ybx1(–/–) embryos (Fig. 1C). These results confirmed that our Ybx1 knockout mice lacked YB-1 expression and showed developmental defects, as previously described [16, 20].

Dark blue boxes represent exons, and the shaded regions indicate untranslated regions. Scissor marks indicate the target sites of the gRNAs. (B) Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) embryos at E13.5. The scale bars indicate 1 mm. (C) Western blotting images of embryonic heads from Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) embryos at E13.5. ACTB (beta-actin) was used as the loading control.

Number of germ cells declined in gonads of Ybx1(–/–) embryos

Next, we dissected the gonads of Ybx1-knockout embryos at E13.5. Compared to the gonads of Ybx1(+/–) fetuses, those of Ybx1(–/–) fetuses showed slender gonads (Fig. 2A). These samples were subjected to gonadal whole-mount immunostaining using an anti-MVH antibody to visualize the germ cells, and their absolute numbers were counted. Confocal images revealed a reduced number of germ cells and gonadal hypoplasia in female and male Ybx1(–/–) gonads (Fig. 2B). The average numbers of female and male germ cells in Ybx1(+/–) fetuses were 4798 ± 1696 (SD) and 4043 ± 1664 (SD) per gonad, respectively. Compared to female Ybx1(+/–) gonads, germ cell numbers in female Ybx1(–/–) embryos were significantly reduced, with an average of 1148 ± 640 (SD) (Fig. 2C). Although comparing the number of germ cells between male Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) fetuses did not show significance, the germ cell numbers in Ybx1(–/–) gonads showed a decreasing tendency, with an average of 2315 ± 935 (SD) (Fig. 2D). Previously, depletion of Ybx1 in mouse fetal liver-resident hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells has been shown to decrease the proportion of cells expressing Ki67, a marker of mitotic cycling [34]. Therefore, to investigate whether the reduction in germ cells was caused by mitotic arrest, female gonads at E12.5, when germ cells are expected to be in the mitotic cycling phase [35], were subjected to immunostaining for Ki67. However, confocal images showed that almost all germ cells of heterozygous and homozygous mutants expressed Ki67, suggesting that they were not in G0 mitotic arrest and that mitotic arrest is not a cause of germ cell hypoplasia in the Ybx1 deleted female gonads (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these results indicate that the deletion of Ybx1 causes a decrease in germ cells in embryos at the mid-gestation stage.

YB-1 was expressed in both germ and gonadal somatic cells

Although deletion of Ybx1 causes germ cell hypoplasia, it remains unclear whether YB-1 is expressed in germ cells, somatic cells, or both. However, YB-1 may be ubiquitously expressed in the gonads, which could make it difficult to identify the expression pattern of YB-1 by immunohistochemistry. Therefore, we used immunocytochemistry to detect YB-1. Embryonic gonads of Ybx1-deleted fetuses at E13.5 were subjected to enzymatic digestion and dispersed into single cells, and then YB-1 was immunolabeled. In both female and male gonadal cells, YB-1 signals were specifically detected in Ybx1(+/–) cells compared to those in Ybx1(–/–) cells (Figs. 3A and 3B). Female and male Ybx1(+/–) gonadal somatic cells exhibited relatively strong cytoplasmic YB-1 signals. Although the expression levels varied among cells, Ybx1(+/–) germ cells of both sexes labeled with the anti-GENA antibody presented relatively weak but significant signals for YB-1 in the cytoplasm. These results clarify the ubiquitous expression of YB-1 in mouse fetal gonads.

Expression analysis of YB-1 in fetal gonads. (A, B) Confocal images of immunostained gonadal cells from Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) female (A) and male (B) fetuses at the E13.5 stage. The nuclei of germ cells were probed with anti-GENA antibody (green), and the nuclei (blue) were counterstained with DAPI. YB-1 (red) was specifically expressed in both germ cells and gonadal somatic cells from Ybx1(+/–) fetuses. Scale bars indicate 10 µm.

Deletion of Ybx1 conferred limited effects on gonadal transcriptome

The ubiquitous expression of YB-1 in gonads motivated us to investigate the influence of its deletion on gonadal cells. SSEA-1-positive germ cells and -negative gonadal somatic cells of both sexes of Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) at E13.5 were purified by fluorescence-activated cell sorting and subjected to mRNA-seq (Supplementary Figs. 3A and 3B). First, Ybx1 mRNA expression was confirmed. The genome viewer showed that Ybx1 was expressed in all exons only in Ybx1(+/–) samples (Supplementary Fig. 3C). In contrast, all samples, including Ybx1(–/–) cells, showed RNA expression in the last two exons of Ybx1. The transcript is thought to be transcribed by the promoter activity of the introns. It is unlikely that this intron-derived Ybx1 short isoform compensates for the function of YB-1, because it does not have any open reading frame of Ybx1.

We conducted differential expression analyses of the four groups (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 3). Differential expression analyses of germ cells between Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) mice revealed a small number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; FDR < 0.05, TPM ≥ 1) in both sexes (Figs. 4A and 4B). The same tendency was observed in the differential expression analyses of gonadal somatic cells (Figs. 4C and 4D). None of the DEG groups showed significant enrichment after GO term enrichment or KEGG pathway analyses. Therefore, the influence of the Ybx1 deletion on the gonadal transcriptome of fetuses is limited. ​

Deletion of YB-1 caused the misregulation of genes involved in germ cell survival or gonadal development

The extraction of a few DEGs from the mRNA-seq data led us to investigate each gene. We first evaluated the expression patterns of several marker genes (Fig. 5A). Early germ cell markers and piRNA-related genes were specifically expressed in germ cells. In addition, gonadal somatic cell marker genes and ovarian development-related genes showed expected expression patterns. Despite the germ cell hypoplasia observed above, the expression levels of apoptotic genes did not show a prominent increase in Ybx1(–/–) cells. Furthermore, meiosis-related genes were preferentially expressed in Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) female germ cells at almost equal levels, indicating that meiotic entry was not compromised by Ybx1 deletion. Next, we investigated the expression of the Y-box genes. Ybx2 was not expressed in gonadal somatic cells, but was expressed at low levels in female germ cells. In contrast, Ybx3 was ubiquitously expressed at lower levels than Ybx1 in these specimens. Expression levels of Ybx2 and Ybx3 were unaffected in the absence of Ybx1.

(B) Expression of genes downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female germ cells with high significance. (C) Expression levels of Lincenc1, a gene significantly upregulated in Ybx1(–/–) germ cells of both sexes. (D) Expression of Rarres1, which is significantly upregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female gonadal somatic cells. Individual DEGs were also investigated (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4A). As germ cell reduction was prominent in female Ybx1(–/–) gonads, we focused mainly on highly significant female DEGs. Rpn1 (ribophorin I) and Anapc5 (anaphase-promoting complex subunit 5), which have been suggested to be important for germ cell survival, were significantly downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female germ cells compared to those in the control (Figs. 4A and 5 B) [36, 37]. Although statistical significance was not observed, these genes tended to decrease in Ybx1(–/–) male germ cells compared to the control. Lincenc1, a long non-coding RNA indispensable for naive pluripotency of mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), was significantly upregulated in both female and male Ybx1(–/–) germ cells with a low expression value (Figs. 4A, 4 B, and 5C) [38]. Lincenc1 was the only DEG shared among the comparison groups (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Rarres1 (retinoic acid receptor responder 1) was highly upregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female gonadal somatic cells, although retinoic acid synthesis genes were not differentially expressed (Figs. 4C, 5A, and 5D). In contrast, Acta2 (actin alpha 2; ɑSMA) and Mmp2 (matrix metallopeptidase 2), which had been shown to be transcriptionally regulated by YB-1 [39, 40], were significantly downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female gonadal somatic cells and showed a downregulation tendency in Ybx1(–/–) male gonadal somatic cells (Figs. 4C and 5E). Collectively, Ybx1 deletion resulted in the dysregulation of several genes, mainly in female gonadal cells. As these genes are potentially important for germ cell survival and gonadal development, they may cause germ cell hypoplasia in Ybx1(–/–) embryonic gonads.

​Discussion

​Although previous reports have clarified that deletion of Ybx1 in mice causes embryonic lethality, the gonadal phenotypes in Ybx1-deleted fetuses remain unknown. In this study, we investigated the influence of Ybx1 deletion on embryonic gonads. We focused on Ybx1 deleted fetuses at the E13.5 stage, in which embryonic lethality had not yet occurred, and gonadal phenotypes were assessed using whole-mount immunostaining and transcriptome analysis.

The established Ybx1(–/–) embryos completely lacked YB-1 expression, accompanied by the previously reported exencephaly, small livers, pale bodies, and developmental delay (Fig. 1). Although developmental defects were observed, the gonads were dissected from E13.5 Ybx1(–/–) embryos. Therefore, we subjected the gonads to whole-mount immunostaining and measured the absolute number of germ cells per gonad. A previous report has shown that the wild-type ovaries of E13.5 CD-1 fetuses had a mean number of 6070 ± 1120 (SD) germ cells per ovary, which is comparable to the mean number of germ cells in Ybx1(+/–) gonads at E13.5 observed in this study [41]. Compared with female Ybx1(+/–) mice, the number of E13.5 germ cells in Ybx1(–/–) female gonads was significantly reduced (Fig. 2C). In contrast, germ cell reduction in male Ybx1(–/–) embryos compared to male Ybx1(+/–) embryos was relatively moderate (Fig. 2D). These differences in germ cell reduction may be caused by sexual dimorphism in meiotic initiation timing [35, 42]. Additionally, as wild-type gonads from littermates of Ybx1 knockout embryos were not analyzed in the current study, the possibility remains that comparison of germ cell numbers between wild-type and Ybx1(–/–) embryos could result in a larger difference than described above due to a potential dosage-dependent role of Ybx1.

After determining the ubiquitous expression of YB-1 in fetal gonads, we conducted mRNA-seq experiments to investigate the effects on the gonadal transcriptome caused by Ybx1 deletion. However, knockout of Ybx1 in E13.5 embryos did not greatly impact either the germ or somatic cell transcriptomes, suggesting that germ cell hypoplasia and the slender gonads observed in Ybx1(–/–) embryos were not attributed to a delay in the developmental program, at least at the transcriptomic level (Fig. 4). A previous report showed that Ybx1 deletion did not cause global changes in the transcriptome of mouse embryonic fibroblasts [20]. In mice, it has been suggested that the roles of Y-box binding proteins during embryogenesis and spermatogenesis may overlap [14, 15]. Because both Ybx1 and Ybx3 were expressed in germ and gonadal somatic cells at E13.5 (Fig. 5A), Ybx3 may have compensated for the roles of YB-1 in Ybx1(–/–) gonads, which could have restrained the dynamic transcriptome changes.

Despite its limited effect on the transcriptome, Ybx1 deletion in female germ cells at E13.5 provoked the misregulation of a few genes that are interesting in the context of germ cell development (Figs. 5B and 5C). Anapc5, which encodes a subunit of the anaphase-promoting complex, was downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) female germ cells with high significance. Anapc5 is downregulated in mitotic-germ-cell-containing female embryonic gonads of CAST/EiJ mice compared to C57BL/6J mice, which could be the cause of the high penetrance of the germ cell loss phenotype in the Kif18a-mutated CAST/EiJ mice [37]. Rpn1 was also downregulated in Ybx1-deleted female germ cells, with a degree and significance equivalent to that of Anapc5 (Fig. 4A). Rpn1 is important for post-translational modifications in the endoplasmic reticulum, and it has recently been shown that conditional deletion of Rpn1 in spermatogenic cells causes a reduction in Stra8-positive germ cells and spermatocytes in the meiotic prophase, resulting in sterility [36]. Therefore, it is plausible that downregulation of these two genes is a potential cause of germ cell hypoplasia in Ybx1(–/–) female gonads. Further investigation using a conditional deletion approach is required to determine the relationship between germ cell hypoplasia and these genes.

In contrast, a comparison of the Ybx1(+/–) and Ybx1(–/–) female gonadal somatic cell transcriptomes revealed that Acta2 and Mmp2, which have been suggested to be transcriptionally regulated by YB-1, were significantly downregulated in Ybx1(–/–) gonadal somatic cells (Figs. 4C and 5E). Acta2, also known as aSMA, has been suggested to be transcriptionally suppressed in a mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line through the co-binding of YB-1, Purɑ, and Purb to the enhancer of Acta2 [39]. Mmp2 (matrix metallopeptidase 2; gelatinase A) contains an enhancer element called RE-1 in its upstream region, which is conserved among mice, rats, and humans. In luciferase assays using mouse, rat, and human cell lines, YB-1 preferentially binds to the single-stranded RE-1 sequence and increases the expression of downstream genes [40, 43, 44]. Since Mmp2 expression in smooth muscle cells is critical for extracellular matrix remodeling [45], its downregulation may affect gonadal development. However, it remains unknown whether the downregulation of these genes is attributable to the ablation of the transcriptional activities of YB-1. While our immunocytochemistry with an antibody targeting the C-terminus of YB-1 did not detect nuclear YB-1, we cannot exclude the possibility that the antibody failed to detect signals because of epitope accessibility changes caused by protein localization. Therefore, to investigate the detailed localization pattern of YB-1 in the gonadal cells, a mouse model with endogenous or exogenously tagged YB-1 is needed. Whereas downregulation of these genes, Rarres1 was upregulated in Ybx1(–/–) gonadal somatic cells (Figs. 4C and 5D). Although the role of Rarres1 in mouse ovaries remains unknown, this gene has previously been shown to be upregulated in the ovaries of double estrogen-knockout mice, which show transdifferentiation of granulosa cells into Sertoli-like cells [46]. Therefore, whether upregulation of Rarres1 affects ovarian function warrants further investigation.

In contrast to the downregulation of potential key genes, Lincenc1 was significantly upregulated in both female and male Ybx1(–/–) germ cells compared to their counterparts (Fig. 4A and 4 B). Lincenc1 is a non-coding RNA that maintains the naïve pluripotency of mouse embryonic stem cells by regulating glycolysis [38]. Although no evidence has been reported that YB-1 directly regulates Lincenc1 expression, YB-1 has been shown to positively regulate the expression of pluripotency-related genes in mouse embryonic stem cells. [47]. Given that the effects of excess Lincenc1 on germ cell development remain unclear, it is tempting to investigate the involvement of YB-1 in Lincenc1 regulation during germ cell development.

Collectively, we revealed that the deletion of Ybx1 causes germ cell hypoplasia, which is prominent in female gonads. Although this was not accompanied by global changes in the gonadal transcriptome, genes expected to be involved in germ cell survival or gonadal development were misregulated in Ybx1-deleted female gonadal germ and somatic cells. Therefore, the downregulation of these genes may be a potential cause of the germ cell hypoplasia observed in this study. Further studies using a conditional knockout mouse model are needed to determine whether these results are due to the direct or indirect effects of Ybx1 deletion. Our results suggest that Ybx1 plays an important role in mouse gonadal development, which raises the possible importance of the Ybx1 homologue in the reproduction of other mammals.
