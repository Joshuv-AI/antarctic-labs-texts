Persistent sciatic artery identified during prostatic artery embolization
Authors: Brayden Bagla, Amiya Bagla, Sandeep Bagla, Michael Hoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA persistent sciatic artery (PSA) is a rare congenital vascular anomaly that may complicate endovascular pelvic procedures. We report a case of PSA incidentally identified during prostatic artery embolization in a patient with symptomatic benign prostatic hyperplasia. Angiography revealed a dominant PSA continuing from the common iliac artery through the sciatic notch, supplying the gluteal branches and internal pudendal artery, with hypoplasia of the superficial femoral artery. The ipsilateral prostatic artery originated from the internal pudendal branch of the PSA. Awareness of PSA is critical during pelvic embolization because of its atypical anatomy, potential aneurysmal degeneration, and implications for safe catheter navigation. This case highlights the importance of recognizing PSA to avoid procedural complications and to ensure accurate identification of the prostatic arterial supply.Keywords: Persistent sciatic artery, Prostatic artery embolization, Pelvic angiography, Vascular anatomic variant, Internal iliac artery, Endovascular procedures

Introduction

A persistent sciatic artery (PSA) is a rare embryologic remnant in which the sciatic artery remains the dominant arterial supply to the lower extremity. Its estimated incidence is less than 0.05%, though it may be underdiagnosed. PSA has important clinical implications, including aneurysm formation, limb ischemia, and challenges during endovascular procedures.

Prostatic artery embolization (PAE) relies on precise angiographic identification of the prostatic arterial supply. Variant pelvic anatomy—including a PSA supplying internal iliac branches—can significantly alter catheterization strategy. Incidental detection of a PSA during PAE is uncommon but critical to recognize.

Case report

A 67‑year‑old male with lower urinary tract symptoms secondary to benign prostatic hyperplasia (80‑g prostate) was referred for outpatient PAE.

Initial left‑sided pelvic angiography demonstrated an aberrant vascular pattern. A large PSA originated from the common iliac artery and coursed posteriorly through the sciatic notch. The artery continued distally as the dominant inflow to the lower extremity, with a hypoplastic superficial femoral artery. Aneurysmal dilation was noted in the gluteal segment.

The PSA gave rise to typical internal iliac branches, including the superior and inferior gluteal, obturator, and internal pudendal arteries. Selective catheterization revealed that the left prostatic artery originated from the terminal internal pudendal branch of the PSA.

The contralateral pelvic vasculature demonstrated normal internal iliac bifurcation anatomy.

The patient underwent successful embolization without complication. No access‑site issues, pelvic ischemia, buttock claudication, or limb symptoms occurred.

Discussion

A PSA represents a failure of normal embryologic regression of the sciatic axial artery. When present, it often becomes the dominant arterial supply to the lower extremity, replacing or coexisting with a hypoplastic superficial femoral artery. Up to 60% of PSAs may develop aneurysmal degeneration, particularly in the gluteal segment, requiring close attention during pelvic angiography.

For interventional radiologists performing pelvic procedures such as PAE, PSA is most relevant for:

1.Altered vascular roadmap—standard internal iliac anatomy may be replaced by PSA‑derived branches.

Conclusion

A PSA is a rare but clinically significant vascular variant that may complicate PAE. Recognizing PSA anatomy allows accurate identification of the prostatic arterial supply and reduces procedural risk. This case highlights the importance of thorough pelvic angiography and vigilance for unusual arterial configurations during PAE (Fig. 1).

A 67-year-old male with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) due to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and a prostate volume of 80 grams was referred for outpatient prostatic artery embolization (PAE). Initial digital subtraction angiography (DSA) of the left pelvic vasculature revealed an aberrant course of the internal iliac system. (A) Selective angiography demonstrated a persistent sciatic artery (white arrow) arising from the common iliac artery, continuing through the sciatic notch, and the profunda femoral artery continuing anteriorly. This artery gave rise to all standard internal iliac branches, including the superior and inferior gluteal, internal pudendal, and obturator arteries (black arrow). (B) The sciatic artery continued distally into the thigh, acting as the dominant arterial inflow to the lower extremity in place of a hypoplastic superficial femoral artery with secondary aneurysmal degeneration in the gluteal segment. (C) Selective catheterization demonstrated that the prostatic artery on this side arose from the terminal segment of the internal pudendal artery, a branch of the PSA (white arrow). (D) Right internal iliac angiography demonstrated a normal internal iliac artery bifurcation without evidence of persistent sciatic artery, serving as a reference for comparison.
