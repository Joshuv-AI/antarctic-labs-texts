Retained central line guidewire traversing the heart: An unusual case of iatrogenic pericarditis-case report
Authors: Meghana Sharma, Humzah Qazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA man in his early 40s with peripheral arterial disease and prior ultrasound-assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis using the EKOS™ system (Boston Scientific) for arterial thrombosis presented with severe pleuritic chest pain. Initial evaluation demonstrated an acute right-sided segmental pulmonary embolism and a small-to-moderate pericardial effusion. His D-dimer was elevated at 2.8 µg/mL FEU, and his Wells’ score was calculated at 6, indicating moderate-to-high clinical probability. He was treated with intravenous heparin and colchicine for pulmonary embolism with suspected pericarditis. Persistent severe pain prompted repeat imaging, which revealed a retained intravascular guidewire extending from the inferior vena cava into the right atrium, associated with fibrin sheath formation and interval enlargement of the pericardial effusion. Endovascular retrieval achieved partial removal, but a residual intracardiac fragment remained. Two weeks later, the patient re-presented with recurrent symptoms, and an additional 10 cm retained guidewire fragment was successfully retrieved. This rare case of a retained intravascular guidewire causing iatrogenic pericarditis highlights the importance of careful post-procedural imaging review, multidisciplinary management, and confirmation of complete foreign body removal.Keywords: pericarditis, iatrogenic injury, vascular surgery, internal medicine, cardiology

Background

Retained central venous catheter guidewires are rare but potentially life-threatening complications of vascular access procedures and are classified as preventable “never events.” Complications described in the literature include thrombosis, arrhythmias, embolization, cardiac perforation, hemopericardium, and cardiac tamponade. Delayed presentation may obscure the causal relationship to prior interventions. We present an unusual case of a retained intravascular guidewire traversing the inferior vena cava (IVC) and right atrium, resulting in inflammatory pericarditis and recurrent chest pain months after vascular intervention.

Case presentation

A man in his early 40s with a history of peripheral arterial disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder presented with 2 days of progressive pleuritic chest pain.

Several months prior, he had undergone percutaneous transluminal thrombectomy of the left popliteal artery (arterial intervention) for acute limb ischemia, followed by ultrasound-assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis using the EKOS™ system. This procedure involved arterial access rather than venous intervention. Operative records confirmed arterial access during that procedure. Several years earlier, he had undergone open arterial thrombectomy of the same extremity. There was no documented prior history of deep venous thrombosis. He had been prescribed apixaban 5 mg twice daily, but admitted to intermittent noncompliance due to forgetfulness. He had smoked one pack per day for approximately 20 years and denied alcohol or illicit drug use.

His chest pain was sharp and pleuritic, worsened with coughing and positional movement, and transiently improved at rest. On presentation, his heart rate was 123 beats per min, blood pressure was 127/109 mmHg, respiratory rate was 24 breaths per min, and he was afebrile. Physical examination revealed moderate distress, but normal heart sounds without a clearly audible friction rub and clear lungs. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated leukocytosis of 15.4 K/μL, C-reactive protein of 195 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 49 mm/h, and an elevated D-dimer of 2.8 µg/mL FEU. His Wells’ score was 6, reflecting tachycardia, recent vascular intervention, and pulmonary embolism being more likely than alternative diagnoses. The lower extremities were examined clinically, and duplex ultrasonography did not demonstrate acute deep venous thrombosis.

Investigations

Initial chest radiograph demonstrated a mildly enlarged cardiac silhouette and subtle prominence of the right hilar vasculature without focal consolidation or pneumothorax. No radiopaque foreign body was identified on the initial interpretation. The electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia with diffuse PR depression and subtle diffuse ST-segment elevation consistent with pericarditis, without ischemic ST-segment changes.

Initial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (pulmonary embolism protocol) demonstrated an acute right segmental pulmonary embolism and a small-to-moderate circumferential pericardial effusion. Retrospective review later revealed a linear hyperdense structure within the IVC extending toward the right atrium that had not been recognized on the initial read.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a trace-to-small anterior pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology and identified a linear echogenic structure near the IVC–right atrial junction. Transesophageal echocardiography was not performed because computed tomography provided sufficient anatomical detail for procedural planning, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable.

Repeat contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed a retained metallic guidewire extending from the infra-hepatic IVC to the superior cavo-atrial junction and into the right atrium. A fibrin sheath appeared adherent along the intra-atrial septal region, and the pericardial effusion had increased compared to prior imaging. Review confirmed that the guidewire had been present on the initial study, but was not recognized at that time. No central venous catheter placement had occurred between the two imaging studies.

Differential diagnosis

Initially, pulmonary embolism was a leading consideration given the patient’s acute pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, and elevated D-dimer, along with intermittent noncompliance with prescribed anticoagulation. The prior vascular intervention had occurred several months before presentation. While this intervention was arterial and not directly predisposing to venous thromboembolism, it contributed to the overall clinical concern.

Pericarditis was also strongly suspected due to the pleuritic nature of the pain, its exacerbation with positional movement and coughing, transient improvement at rest, and the presence of pericardial effusion on imaging. The patient also demonstrated markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR, leukocytosis), further supporting this diagnosis. Both infectious and iatrogenic causes were considered, given the patient’s recent procedures. The eventual identification of a retained intravascular guidewire provided a direct source of pericardial irritation and explained the persistence of symptoms despite standard therapy with anticoagulation and colchicine.

Acute coronary syndrome was also considered, given the patient’s cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and peripheral arterial disease, although the pleuritic nature of pain made this less likely.

Ultimately, while pulmonary embolism and pericarditis were the most likely initial diagnoses, the discovery of the retained guidewire reframed the clinical picture, providing a unifying explanation for the patient’s recurrent chest pain, inflammatory findings, and fibrin sheath formation consistent with iatrogenic pericarditis.

Treatments

After identification of the retained guidewire, the patient was transferred for vascular and cardiothoracic evaluation. Due to interval enlargement of the pericardial effusion and concern for evolving tamponade physiology, pericardiocentesis with drain placement was performed prior to definitive foreign body retrieval.

For the first retrieval attempt, ultrasound-guided right common femoral venous access was obtained, and a 12-French sheath was placed. Venography confirmed that the wire extended from the level of the renal veins into the cardiac silhouette. A gooseneck endovascular snare was used for retrieval. Although a jugular approach was considered, femoral access was selected because of favorable alignment with the inferiorly oriented wire and operator familiarity. Partial extraction was achieved; however, significant resistance was encountered, likely due to fibrin sheath formation and partial endothelialization. A proximal portion of the wire was removed, but residual intracardiac material remained. Post-procedural imaging demonstrated persistent intra-atrial echogenic material. The patient recovered without immediate complication.

Two weeks later, he returned with recurrent pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea. Repeat computed tomography demonstrated a 2.3 × 2.7 × 3 cm curvilinear metallic-density structure within the superior right atrium consistent with a retained fragment surrounded by a fibrin sheath. A second endovascular intervention was performed using bilateral femoral venous access. Bilateral access was obtained to facilitate improved catheter maneuverability and stabilization for snare engagement of the retained fragment within the right atrium. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a snare catheter was advanced into the right atrium, and the approximately 10 cm retained fragment was successfully captured and completely removed. Fluoroscopy confirmed no residual intracardiac metallic fragments.

Following foreign body removal, pulmonary angiography was performed, and mechanical thrombectomy of the right and left pulmonary arteries was undertaken to address the bilateral embolic burden. Completion angiography demonstrated improved perfusion without significant residual obstruction. The patient was extubated and transferred to recovery in stable condition.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient recovered without immediate procedural complications and was discharged in stable condition. In the months following discharge, he has been unable to follow up consistently, and his current clinical status is unknown.

Discussion

Retained intravascular guidewires are uncommon but potentially life-threatening complications of vascular access and interventional procedures. The clinical presentation varies depending on location, duration of retention, and associated mechanical or inflammatory sequelae. Prior reports demonstrate that presentations range from acute hemodynamic collapse to delayed thrombotic or inflammatory complications.

Teis-Soley et al. 1 evaluated coronary artery perforations caused by intracoronary guidewires during percutaneous coronary intervention. Patients typically presented acutely with chest pain, hypotension, and angiographic evidence of vessel perforation. Detection occurred intra-procedurally through fluoroscopy and contrast extravasation. Management included prolonged balloon inflation, covered stent placement, urgent pericardiocentesis for tamponade, and, in severe cases, emergent surgical repair. Similarly, Park et al. 2 described retrograde guidewire fracture during coronary intervention resulting in acute pericardial tamponade. The diagnosis was made immediately via fluoroscopy when wire fracture and perforation were visualized, and treatment required urgent pericardiocentesis followed by retrieval of the retained fragment.

In contrast to these acute presentations, Hsu et al. 3 reported delayed hemopericardium caused by a retained epicardial pacing wire following congenital heart surgery. That patient presented with chest pain and hemodynamic compromise days after surgery. Transthoracic echocardiography identified a large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology, and computed tomography confirmed the retained wire. Surgical removal was required. Additional case reports have described delayed detection of retained central venous catheter guidewires months to years after placement, presenting with dyspnea, chest pain, or right atrial thrombus formation. In such cases, diagnosis was frequently established by chest radiography, echocardiography, or computed tomography, and management ranged from endovascular snare retrieval to open cardiac surgery when thrombus or endothelialization was present.

The present case differs in that the patient developed delayed inflammatory pericarditis rather than acute perforation or tamponade. The retained guidewire traversed the IVC and right atrium, and chronic mechanical irritation with fibrin sheath formation likely triggered the inflammatory response. Computed tomography was essential for defining the wire’s trajectory and associated effusion. Initial endovascular snare retrieval was incomplete, likely due to partial endothelialization, necessitating repeat intervention.

Conclusion

Retained intravascular guidewires represent a rare but potentially serious complication of vascular access and endovascular procedures. As demonstrated in this case, retained hardware can lead to a spectrum of adverse outcomes, including thrombosis, arrhythmias, infection, cardiac perforation, and inflammatory pericarditis. The trajectory of the guidewire through the right atrium in this patient likely resulted in chronic mechanical irritation and fibrin sheath formation, ultimately provoking the inflammatory response responsible for his recurrent pleuritic chest pain and pericardial effusion.

This case also underscores the risk of diagnostic anchoring in patients with complex cardiopulmonary presentations. The confirmed diagnosis of pulmonary embolism initially provided a sufficient explanation for the patient’s symptoms, which contributed to the delayed recognition of the retained guidewire. Persistent or evolving symptoms despite appropriate therapy should prompt reconsideration of alternative or concurrent etiologies, particularly in individuals with a history of recent vascular instrumentation. Careful and systematic review of imaging studies is essential in such scenarios to avoid missed findings.

Finally, retained guidewires remain a preventable event. This case highlights the importance of strict adherence to procedural safety protocols, including standardized line insertion checklists, formal wire accounting practices like surgical instrument counts, and appropriate supervision during trainee-performed procedures. Reinforcing these systems-based safeguards is critical to reducing the risk of recurrence and improving patient safety outcomes.
