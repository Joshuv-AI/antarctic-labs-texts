Single-cell RNA sequencing of appendiceal adenocarcinoma reveals a low proportion of epithelial cells and a fibroblast enriched tumor microenvironment
Authors: B.B. Gunes, N.J. Hornstein, M. Wang, M. Yousef, M.M. Fanaeian, A. Yousef, S. Chowdhury, M.A. Zeineddine, C. Haymaker, B. Helmink, K. Fournier, J.P. Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAppendiceal adenocarcinoma (AA) is an understudied gastrointestinal malignancy. Treatment is guided by its proximal counterpart, colorectal cancer (CRC), despite recent studies demonstrating AA’s unique mutational landscape and poor response to CRC chemotherapy. In this study, we describe AA on a single-cell level and uncover features highlighting the contrast between AA and CRC; we believe these findings will support AA as a unique disease entity and encourage further disease-specific focus.Materials and methodsThree patients with peritoneal metastases from AA and one from CRC profiled with 5′ single-cell RNA sequencing.ResultsTraditional k-means clustering analysis of >30 000 cells revealed three canonical compartments and 11 major cell types. AA samples were mostly comprised of stromal cells (56%), while healthy appendix samples had significantly more immune and epithelial cells. Strikingly, fibroblasts were the most abundant cell type in AA with cancer-associated fibroblasts from the mucinous AA tumors showing a distinct profile from goblet cell AA or CRC. Pseudobulk analysis comparing tumor cells from AA with normal appendiceal epithelial cells demonstrated up-regulation of a diverse range of oncogenic pathways including inflammatory, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and angiogenesis.ConclusionsAs the first application of single-cell technology to AA these data provide insight into the intratumor heterogeneity of AA and highlight the important contribution of the tumor microenvironment in this orphan disease. These results also reinforce multiple observations that AA is a unique disease entity from CRC and targeting the tumor microenvironment should be considered as a therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Appendiceal adenocarcinoma (AA) is a relatively rare malignancy with an incidence of ∼1.2 per 100 000 people every year; however, its incidence has risen dramatically in recent years.1,2 This rising disease burden has been especially striking among young-onset patients, defined as those under the age of 45-50 years.3 As an orphan disease, there are no drugs specifically approved for use in AA by either the US or European regulatory agencies. Patients with AA who are not candidates for surgical resection or cytoreductive surgery (CRS) and hyperthermal intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) are most often treated with chemotherapy regimens designed for colorectal cancer (CRC). This practice has been called into question by recent randomized controlled trials which have demonstrated their limited efficacy.4 Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel systemic therapies for AA. Given its rarity and lack of systematic investigation, the molecular characteristics of AA have been poorly defined to-date relative to more common tumor types. Accordingly, a better understanding of the disease state is required to generate novel hypotheses which may eventually lead to AA-directed therapies. Here, we report findings from the first systematic characterization of AA at single-cell resolution that highlight unique features in the AA tumor microenvironment (TME).

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

After obtaining informed consent (IRB Protocol Number: LAB10-0982; PI: Kopetz), we obtained specimens from a cohort of three patients with AA and one with CRC metastatic to the peritoneum. Specimens were collected during standard-of-care CRS carried out at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC). Histologic subtype and grade were confirmed by a board-certified pathologist with specialist expertise in appendix cancer. Additional single-cell data from healthy appendix and colon specimens was obtained from deceased transplant organ donors following ethical approval and donor families’ consent.5 The dataset is accessible from The Gut Cell Atlas (gutcellatlas.org, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094).

Tumor dissociation

Following surgical resection, tumor tissue samples were transported in sterile cold RPMI medium. After rinsing with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fresh specimens of 0.2-1.0 g of tumor tissue were minced into 5-10 mm tissue fragments and placed in a 15-ml conical tube containing 5 ml of RPMI media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gemini 100-106) and 1× collagenase A (Sigma 11088793001). The tube was rotated on a MACSmix Tube Rotator (Miltenyi 130-090-753, Bergisch Gladbach, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) inside a CO2 incubator at 37°C for 1 h. Dissociated cells were centrifuged at 450g for 5 min and resuspended in RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS, then filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer (Fisher 22-363-547, Waltham, MA). Cells were stained with 0.2% Trypan Blue solution and manually counted using a hemocytometer, then resuspended at a final concentration of 5 × 106 cells/ml in freezing media [90% FBS and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)] and cryopreserved in cryogenic vials before being stored at −80°C overnight. Cryopreserved cells were transferred to the vapor phase of a liquid nitrogen freezer the next day and stored until used for single-cell RNA sequencing.

Thawing of dissociated cells, viability sorting, library preparation, and sequencing

Frozen cell aliquots were transferred to the MDACC CPRIT Single Cell Genomics Core for library preparation and sequencing. Cryopreserved cells were thawed rapidly in a 37°C water bath and transferred into a 15-ml conical tube containing 10 ml of pre-warmed RPMI media supplemented with 20% FBS. Thawed cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 10 ml of RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS, DNase I (Thermo Scientific 89835, Waltham, MA) and 1 mM MgSO4 (Sigma-Aldrich 83266-100ML-F, Saint Louis, MO) followed by incubation in a 37°C water bath for 10 min. Cells were washed twice with 5 ml of cold 1× PBS without calcium and magnesium (Corning 21031CV, Corning, NY) supplemented with 0.04% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich A9418). For viable cell sorting, thawed cells were stained with a Zombie Green Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend 423111, San Diego, CA) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Stained cells were washed with 2 ml of cold PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA and resuspended in 1 ml of cold PBS without calcium and magnesium. Stained cells were sorted using a BD FACSJazz Cell Sorter flow cytometer equipped with a chiller unit and acquired using BD FACS software v1.2.0.142. For a post-sort purity check, sorted live cells were stained using the Live/Dead Fixable Yellow Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Scientific L34968) and evaluated using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer. Chromium single-cell sequencing technology from 10× Genomics was used to perform single-cell capture, barcoding, and library preparation by following the 10× Genomics Single-Cell Chromium 5′(PN-120237) protocol using V3 chemistry reagents (10× Genomics). The barcoded libraries were sequenced for 100 cycles on an S2 flow cell on the Novoseq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequence reads were converted to FASTQ files and UMI read counts via CellRanger software (10× Genomics).

Single-cell pre-processing and cell type annotation

Gene count matrices were converted to standard AnnData objects via Python (version 3.10.8) and the Scanpy6 module (version 1.9.3). Preliminary quality control was carried out by removing genes detected in fewer than three cells, and cells detected with <500 reads, <200 genes, or containing >50% mitochondrial transcripts. Following preliminary quality control, doublets were removed via application of Scrublet7 and empty droplets were excluded. Cells were batch corrected, normalized, and annotated with SCimilarity.8 Annotated labels were filtered for the cell types that have been represented at least in 20 cells. SCimilarity-derived annotation was orthogonally validated with a traditional k-means clustering approach (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). Cells were divided into three major compartments: immune, and epithelial through manual segmentation based on k-means clustering and orthogonally validated with automated cell annotation (SCimilarity). B cells (CD79B, MS4A1), plasma cells (MZB1, JCHAIN, IGHA1), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G), natural killer cells (GNLY, GZMB, NKG7), macrophage and monocytes (CD68, CSF1R, CD83, CD14), dendritic cells (HLA-DRA, CLEC4C), mast cells (KIT, TPSAB1), epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18), endothelial cells (VWF, PLVAP, CDH5), smooth muscle cells and fibroblast (DCN, COL1A1) subpopulations were manually annotated with respected marker genes.5,9 Previously published data derived from healthy appendix (n = 6) and colon samples (n = 31) were downloaded, and similarly processed (GCA cohort). From 31 healthy colon samples, two (HC 24, HC 28) were excluded due to the low number of total counts and genes detected (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094).

Subcluster and trajectory inference analysis

Raw counts of cells within epithelial and fibroblast compartments was extracted from healthy and tumoral cohorts independently, variance arising from total counts, number of genes detected, and mitochondrial genes were regressed out. Data were integrated using 7563 and 8402 highly variable genes for epithelial cells and fibroblasts, respectively, with Scanorama.10 Clusters were defined by performing Leiden clustering. Known marker genes were used to identify putative malignant cells within samples known to pathologically harbor adenocarcinoma. Further subtype identification was achieved with the use of known marker genes.5 Trajectory inference analysis was carried out with partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA).11

Differential abundance, gene expression, and pathway analysis

A two-sided t-test was carried out to compare differential abundance between different conditions, and significance was accepted as P value <0.05. To minimize the drop-off effect seen in single-cell sequencing, we summed up the raw counts of each sample to create a pseudobulk profile. Differentially expressed genes in single sequencing were identified by pseudobulk analysis using PyDESeq2.12 Genes that were not present in at least three cells and did not have at least 1000 counts were excluded from the analysis. P values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with P-adjusted value <0.05 were ranked by pi value [log10(P-adjusted value) × (log2-fold change)]. Gene ontology analysis was carried out using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) software v4.3.2 ‘GSEAPreranked’ exploring human hallmark gene set.13 The threshold for significance was a false discovery rate <0.25.

Results

Clinical features of the appendix cancer cohort

The natural history of appendiceal cancer is unique amongst gastrointestinal malignancies in that tumors generally have metastatic spread limited to the peritoneal cavity. Given that it is the peritoneal metastases, rather than the primary appendiceal tumor that is ultimately lethal to the patient, we chose to profile peritoneal metastatic tumors from three patients with AA and as a control, one patient with peritoneal metastatic CRC (Figure 1A). The first AA tumor came from a patient with well differentiated mucinous adenocarcinoma showing infiltrative invasion; this differentiates the lesion from a low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm14 (referred to hereafter as low-grade AA, Figure 1B). He initially presented with diffuse peritoneal metastases and was treated with 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin , oxaliplatin, bevacizumab (FOLFOX/Bev) and then 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, bevacizumab (FOLFIRI/Bev) with minimal response based on radiographic imaging, consistent with prior reports of lack of efficacy of these CRC chemotherapy regimens in low-grade AA.4,14 Following the ineffective chemotherapy, he was monitored without treatment for nearly a year before undergoing a palliative debulking surgery to a completeness of cytoreduction score (CCR) of 2 with a peritoneal cancer index (PCI) of 35. After the palliative surgery in which the sample was also collected, he has continued on surveillance for nearly 2 years (Figure 1C). The second AA tumor came from a patient who initially presented with peritoneal metastatic disease but had a complete CRS/HIPEC (CCR = 0, PCI = 11) at an outside facility and was disease free for 18 months before being found to relapse with diffuse tumor implants in the peritoneal cavity (referred to hereafter as high-grade AA). Pathology from the initial resection was moderately to poorly differentiated mucinous adenocarcinoma, with focal areas of tumor cells with signet ring morphology. Mutational profiling revealed KRAS and GNAS mutations. She was treated with FOLFIRI/Bev at our center with initial partial response on computational tomography (CT) imaging and normalization of CA19-9. After completing eight cycles, however, she clinically progressed with gastric outlet and small bowel obstruction accompanied by increased CA19-9 level. Due to obstruction, she was taken for a palliative debulking and bypass surgery where the sample for scRNAseq was taken. The pathology from this surgery showed marked heterogeneity with areas of well, moderately, and poorly differentiated tumor with a PCI score of 39 and retroperitoneal metastasis. Unfortunately, this patient experienced significant post-operative disease progression and subsequently transitioned to hospice care. The third AA tumor came from a patient with a metastatic, poorly differentiated goblet cell AA with EWSR1, KDM6A, POLD1, and PRKCI genomic mutations. She was initially treated with a right hemicolectomy at an outside facility before coming to MDACC and starting on FOLFOX/Bev before CRS/HIPEC. A complete resection (CCR = 0, PCI = 13) was achieved and she remains disease free. To provide contrast, one CRC tumor derived from peritoneal metastasis with FLT3, NOTCH2, TP53, and PIK3CA mutations was included. This patient presented with a T3N0M0 (stage 2) tumor and was initially treated with right hemicolectomy. On her first post-operative scan, however, peritoneal metastases were detected and the patient was started on systemic FOLFIRI/cetuximab. There was shrinkage of the lesions in radiographic imaging consistent with a partial response, so the patient was taken to CRS/HIPEC; pathology from that surgery showed complete pathologic response.Figure 1Clinical characteristics. Major compartments and cell types in single-cell RNA sequencing

scRNA-seq profiling of the combined MDACC and Gut Cell Atlas cohorts yielded a total of 32 471 and 43 730 single cells following quality control, respectively (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). Using Leiden clustering, we identified 26 clusters in the MDACC cohort (Figure 2A) and assigned the clusters to major TME compartments (Figure 2B) and cell types (Figure 2C and D). The first major finding was the paucity of epithelial cells in the AA tumors; epithelial cells represented only 2% of the total cells (Figure 2E). This low percentage of tumor cells was expected, especially for the mucinous AA tumors where as many as one-third of tumors sent for clinical molecular profiling fail quality control due to low tumor content.15 The chemotherapy preceding sample collection also likely reduced the number of tumor cells to some degree, and in the case of the high-grade AA sample, spatial heterogeneity may have also contributed. The low epithelial content was in contrast to healthy appendiceal tissue from patients without cancer where epithelial cells represented 29% of the total cells (Supplementary Figure S2E, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). The AA tumors also had a marked increase in stromal cells (56%) relative to the healthy appendix (26%). Evaluating specific cell types, fibroblasts were the most abundant cell type in AA (55%); nearly all of the stromal cells in the AA samples were fibroblasts (Figure 2F). This was in contrast to healthy AA where fibroblasts were only 15% of all cells and 58% of stromal cells (Supplementary Figure S2F, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). Although the overall fraction of immune cells was similar between AA and healthy appendix, there were major differences in the specific immune cell types. Macrophages/monocytes were the most prominent immune cells in AA tumors (25% of all cells) followed by T cells (10% of all cells). In contrast, in healthy appendix, T cells were most common (19%) followed by plasma cells (12%) and B cells (11%). Likewise, the compartment distribution, CRC and healthy colon TME were enriched by T cells, and epithelial cells, respectively (Supplementary Figure S2E and F, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094).

Epithelial cells in appendiceal tumors

From a total of 961 epithelial cells in the combined cohort, 376 cells were collected from AA tumors, and most of these were from the goblet cell AA specimen (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094, Figure 3A). Pseudobulk differential gene expression analysis identified 657 differentially expressed genes (P adjusted <0.05); interestingly the vast majority, 608, were up-regulated with expression of only 49 reduced in AA relative to normal appendix epithelium (Figure 3B). Clustering using these differentially expressed genes showed that the three AA samples clearly split from the healthy appendix samples (Figure 3C), and low-grade AA from the other two tumors. GSEA identified that the top up-regulated pathways in AA were related to inflammation [tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) signaling via nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), interferon-γ response, complement, inflammatory response] and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Figure 3D). To better understand the transcriptional changes that occur in AA tumorigenesis, we annotated 13 Leiden clusters into five subtypes including transit amplifying cells, colonocytes, goblet cells, and two putative malignant cell clusters (Supplementary Figure S3A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). Trajectory inference analysis using PAGA revealed that putative malignant cell cluster 1 was connected both with colonocytes and goblet cells, whereas putative malignant cell cluster 2 was more connected to putative malignant cell cluster 1 than colonocytes (Supplementary Figure S3C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094). Strikingly, putative malignant cells cluster 2 was mostly comprised of low-grade AA proportionally. Cluster 1, however, included tumor cells derived from goblet cell AA and high-grade AA, and clustered more closely to the goblet cells. Differentially expressed genes across epithelial subtypes showed enrichment of EMT-related genes in putative malignant cell cluster 2, whereas putative malignant cell cluster 1 was enriched in genes associated with cellular differentiation (Supplementary Figure S3D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100094).

Cancer-associated fibroblasts in appendix cancer

Fibroblasts were the most abundant cell type in the appendix cancer samples (55% of all cells). The fraction of fibroblasts was highest in the goblet cell tumor (63%) followed by the high-grade AA (34%) and low-grade AA sample with the smallest percentage (6%). A uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) representation integrating fibroblasts from healthy tissue with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) revealed that CAFs from the two mucinous AA samples clustered together but were distinct from CAFs from the goblet cell AA and the peritoneal metastatic CRC tumor (Figure 4A). Additionally, while the fibroblasts from the healthy appendix and healthy colon clustered together, all of the CAF clusters were distinct from this cluster of healthy fibroblasts. Both FAP and THY1, previously reported markers of CAF derived from a pan-cancer analysis,9 were expressed in the CAFs from the mucinous AA and CRC sample, but interestingly not the CAFs from the goblet cell tumor AA (Figure 4B). The clustering results seen in the UMAP were further delineated with differential gene expression analysis which again highlighted that the CAFs from the goblet cell AA had a unique transcriptional profile compared with those from mucinous AA (Figure 4C). Also similar to the UMAP fibroblasts from healthy colon and appendix samples shared similar transcriptional profiles. The CAFs from the peritoneal metastatic CRC were again distinct from other CAFs but were more similar to mucinous appendiceal cancer than to the goblet cell tumor. Discussion

Although it is well known to pathologists that AA tumors often have a small proportion of tumor cells, a detailed study of the TME of appendix cancer at single-cell resolution has not been previously reported. Here we find that across three samples, tumor cells contribute only 2% of the total cells. The sparsity of malignant epithelial cells has important implications as it suggests that prior bulk transcriptomic results are capturing TME as opposed to tumor intrinsic transcription.28 Given the low percentage of tumor cells, which is consistent with prior pathologic studies,15 investigation of the tumor intrinsic transcriptome of AA will require either single-cell or spatially resolved experimental methods; these are currently underway.29 In the future, deconvolution methods may enable extraction of the tumor intrinsic transcriptome from bulk RNAseq, but this will require sufficient training data with paired bulk and scRNAseq.30

The genomic mutational profile of AA is known to be distinct from CRC.31 Mutations in APC, a hallmark feature of CRC, are uncommon in all subtypes of AA. In contrast, AA commonly harbors GNAS mutation, particularly in mucinous tumors (consistent with the high-grade AA sample in our dataset).32,33 The transcriptional profile of the goblet cell AA sample, as demonstrated in trajectory analysis, further supports the uniqueness of this tumor subtype compared with both CRC and other AA subtypes in addition to identified uncommon genomic mutations such as EWSR1, KDM6A, POLD1, and PRKCI.34

With respect to the TME, the predominance of fibroblasts and macrophages/monocytes in AA was quite distinct from both CRC as well as healthy appendix tissue. This finding has important clinical implications as there is growing recognition that the TME influences both potential for metastasis as well as response to systemic therapy.35, 36, 37 Recent successful clinical strategies harnessing immunotherapy in AA highlight the potential of targeting the unique TME in AA.38

This study comes with significant intrinsic limitations, most prominently, the small sample size and lack of biologic replicates for each subtype of AA. Furthermore, given all samples were collected at an academic medical center, the cohort may not be reflective of all patients with AA. Finally, inclusion of normal tissue samples from prior atlas studies forces us to take a conservative analytic approach to guard against batch effects. Based on our analysis, however, many of the findings we report are reinforced by prior bulk sequencing approaches and orthogonal studies.

In conclusion, although this study has inherent limitations, a single-cell approach has allowed for the demonstration of several key aspects of AA biology including the sparsity of malignant epithelial cells, distinct profiles of AA and its subtypes, and provides hints as to the underlying molecular characteristics and potential targets of AA. This effort will hopefully engender future avenues of future research in this rare, orphan disease.
