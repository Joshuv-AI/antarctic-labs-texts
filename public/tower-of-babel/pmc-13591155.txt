Sex-Specific Differences in Dyspeptic Symptoms in Children Are Attenuated by Helicobacter pylori Seropositivity
Authors: Dragana Ilic, Nikola Jovanovic, Bojko Bjelakovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAge- and sex-related differences are important but often underestimated factors in pediatric gastrointestinal disorders. When consistently observed and statistically confirmed, these differences may reflect underlying differences in disease pathophysiology and warrant further clinical attention. This study aimed to investigate age- and sex-related differences in the presentation of dyspeptic symptoms among children with and without serological evidence of Helicobacter pylori infection.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 213 pediatric patients aged 6–18 years who presented with dyspeptic symptoms. A subgroup of 65 patients tested positive for H. pylori. Symptom patterns were compared by sex and infection status.Results H. pylori infection was significantly more common among male patients (p<0.05). In the overall cohort, abdominal pain was reported more frequently by males, whereas nausea was more common among females (p<0.01). However, symptom distribution did not differ significantly by sex in the H. pylori-seropositive subgroup.ConclusionSex-specific differences in the reporting of nausea and abdominal pain in pediatric dyspepsia were present among H. pylori-seronegative children but absent among seropositive children, suggesting that H. pylori-associated gastric inflammation overrides sex-related differences in symptom perception.

INTRODUCTION

Age- and sex-related differences in the clinical presentation of common pediatric disorders are well documented [123456]. These variations may reflect underlying pathophysiological differences and highlight the importance of considering effect modification by factors such as sex in clinical research.

In adults, sex-related differences in pain presentation have been reported, including differences in symptoms at presentation for myocardial infarction. Women often present with atypical symptoms, which can lead to delayed recognition and poorer outcomes compared with men [4]. In children with migraine, age- and sex-related differences have also been observed. Among children younger than 12 years, prevalence is approximately equal in males and females (1:1), whereas during adolescence, prevalence shifts significantly toward females, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1 [6]. Cardiomyopathy is another condition significantly influenced by both age and sex. Pediatric patients may carry specific genetic variants, with expression patterns and clinical severity often differing not only between males and females but also between children and adults [5]. Furthermore, anxiety and aggression scores have been shown to vary by sex, reflecting broader emotional and behavioral differences [7]. Emotional, social, and cultural factors also influence pain perception; however, these factors are difficult to quantify [38].

Functional dyspepsia, characterized by early satiety, epigastric pain, bloating, and nausea in the absence of structural abnormalities, is common in both adults and children. It has a multifactorial etiology that includes alterations in gut-brain signaling and microbial dysbiosis [9]. Although direct evidence is limited, Helicobacter pylori-induced gastric inflammation, mediated by cytokines, low-grade mucosal changes, and altered gut-brain signaling, could produce a more uniform nociceptive stimulus that masks subtle sex-related differences in visceral hypersensitivity or pain modulation observed in functional disorders [10].

Diagnosis in children is complicated by symptom overlap with other conditions and variability in symptom reporting, which may be influenced by sex [123]. In functional dyspepsia (FD), minor symptoms such as nausea and bloating may be attributed to anxiety or dismissed as less concerning, whereas more severe symptoms such as abdominal pain may lead to unnecessary and aggressive investigations [4], as also observed in our clinical experience. Differences in communication styles and healthcare-seeking behaviors may contribute to these patterns [28].

In resource-limited regions of southern and eastern Serbia, distinguishing between organic and functional causes of dyspepsia in children remains a diagnostic challenge [1112]. H. pylori infection can mimic or exacerbate symptoms of FD, leading to potential misclassification [13].

To date, few studies have examined how sex influences dyspeptic symptoms in pediatric populations, particularly in regions such as southern and eastern Serbia, where functional and infectious gastrointestinal conditions remain prevalent. This study aimed to evaluate sex-based differences in the presentation of dyspeptic symptoms, specifically abdominal pain and nausea, among children with and without H. pylori infection, with the goal of improving diagnostic accuracy and supporting individualized care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective observational study used the medical database of the Pediatric Clinic and included patient records spanning 11 years from March 2013 to November 2024. Clinical characteristics, including detailed descriptions of the presenting symptoms of FD, were analyzed in 213 pediatric patients aged 6–18 years who were diagnosed with FD and in a subgroup of 65 children who tested positive for H. pylori infection.

All patients were referred to our institution for further clinical evaluation. The children included in the study presented for the first time with dyspeptic symptoms, had not received previous treatment, and met the ROME IV diagnostic criteria for FD. The following FD symptoms were analyzed: abdominal pain, nausea, and bloating.

Serological testing for H. pylori was performed at the Central Laboratory of the Clinical Center, University of Nis, using a commercial immunoglobulin G (IgG) ELISA kit (Pyloristat; Wampole Laboratories). Serum IgG antibodies against H. pylori were measured using the Immulite 2000 system. Because of resource constraints, active infection was not confirmed in all patients using noninvasive methods (urea breath testing or stool antigen testing) or invasive methods.

Exclusion criteria were previous treatment for dyspepsia or H. pylori infection and difficulty communicating or expressing symptoms adequately. Based on these criteria, 16 patients were excluded before study inclusion. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Clinical Center Niš, Serbia (Approval No. 6679, issued on March 5, 2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Serological testing for H. pylori infection was performed in all patients using the Immulite 2000 system. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy) was performed in a selected group of 21 patients who had a poor response to initial conservative management.

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and are presented as the mean±standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The Student’s t-test and Chi-square test were used to assess differences in patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Co.).

Logistic regression models were fitted for abdominal pain and nausea and included sex, H. pylori status, and their interaction term to formally evaluate whether the association between sex and dyspeptic symptoms differed by H. pylori status. The interaction p-value was used to assess effect modification. Stratified ORs were reported when significant interactions were present.

RESULTS

A total of 213 pediatric patients with dyspeptic symptoms were included in the study: 125 males (58.7%) and 88 females (41.3%). The mean age was 10.4±3.34 years, with no statistically significant difference between the sexes (p=0.926).

Most patients (n=155, 72.8%) were younger than 12 years.

Serological evidence of H. pylori infection was found in 65 patients (30.5%), with H. pylori seropositivity more common among males (60.0%) than females (40.0%).

In the entire cohort (n=213), nausea was significantly more common among females than males (67.0% vs. 49.6%; p=0.011), whereas abdominal pain was reported more frequently by males than females (68.2% vs. 51.2%; p=0.010). Among H. pylori-seronegative children (n=148), these sex differences persisted: nausea was reported by 64.5% of females versus 45.3% of males (p=0.021), and abdominal pain was reported by 54.7% of males versus 32.3% of females (p=0.007).

Among H. pylori-seropositive children (n=65), no significant sex differences were observed in nausea (73.1% of females vs. 59.0% of males; p=0.244) or abdominal pain (30.8% of females vs. 38.5% of males; p=0.525).

Additionally, logistic regression analyses including sex, H. pylori status, and their interaction showed a significant sex× H. pylori interaction for abdominal pain (p=0.041), indicating that the association between sex and abdominal pain differed by infection status. Stratified analyses showed that male sex was associated with higher odds of abdominal pain among H. pylori-negative patients (OR≈2.5), whereas no significant association was observed among H. pylori-positive patients. A similar pattern was observed for nausea, supporting effect modification by H. pylori status. The study results are presented in Tables 3 and 4.

DISCUSSION

FD remains a poorly understood clinical entity and often presents with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, bloating, and early satiety [9]. In addition to these core features, children with FD may exhibit non-gastrointestinal symptoms, including headaches, fatigue, dizziness, and sleep disturbances, which can further obscure the clinical picture and complicate diagnosis [14].

In this retrospective study of pediatric patients in southeastern Serbia, we examined the most common dyspeptic symptoms in male and female patients and whether these symptoms differed significantly by sex. Abdominal pain was reported more commonly by male patients, whereas nausea was reported more frequently by female patients. These sex-specific differences in symptom presentation were statistically significant in the overall cohort but not in the subgroup of children with serological evidence of H. pylori infection. No sex-specific patterns were observed for other dyspeptic symptoms, including bloating, belching, heartburn, and early satiety.

The absence of differences in symptom presentation among H. pylori-seropositive children may suggest that the effects of infection on the gastric mucosa produce a more uniform clinical picture, resulting in similar perceptions and expressions of pain and nausea across sexes and a more uniform clinical phenotype.

Interestingly, the observed pattern, in which males reported more abdominal pain than females, contrasts with findings in adults with FD, among whom females typically predominate in pain reporting [4].

This discrepancy may reflect differences in symptom perception and expression or sociocultural norms regarding the communication of discomfort rather than underlying physiological mechanisms [3].

We also note that, in high-prevalence settings, H. pylori seropositivity may mask typical sex differences in dyspeptic symptom reporting. Furthermore, our findings highlight the need for clinicians to consider sex-related differences when evaluating dyspeptic symptoms in children, particularly in settings where H. pylori infection is endemic and may influence the clinical picture. A better understanding of symptom expression patterns could help prevent unnecessary diagnostic procedures and guide individualized management strategies.

The pathophysiology of visceral abdominal pain is complex and involves key mechanisms, including gut-brain signaling, visceral hypersensitivity, and inflammatory processes (Fig. 1). Low-grade inflammation and widespread antibiotic use have been hypothesized to contribute to functional abdominal pain by promoting microbiota imbalance [1516].

Another important consideration in FD is the substantial overall health burden and healthcare costs, which may be even higher than those associated with conditions expected to incur greater costs (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease) [17].

Treatment of FD is largely empirical and includes various approaches, such as medications to improve fundic accommodation, cognitive behavioral therapy targeting the brain-gut axis, and even virtual reality [18]. Our patients received dietary modifications, nutritional counseling, and symptomatic pharmacological therapy when indicated.

Chronic abdominal pain in children is difficult to assess and diagnose, contributing to underreporting and underdiagnosis. Numerous pain scales are available to assess pain and its severity, but their lack of standardization may cause confusion [19]. To enhance the effectiveness of these tools, we propose integrating social, sex-related, and geographic variables into their design and interpretation.

Our study had several limitations. The retrospective design and reliance on medical record documentation may introduce bias because of variability in symptom reporting and interpretation. Furthermore, IgG serology alone has limited positive predictive value in pediatric populations and cannot reliably distinguish past exposure from active infection, potentially overestimating clinically relevant cases, particularly in regions such as Serbia, where seroprevalence among asymptomatic children has historically been higher (approximately 35–40%). Unfortunately, additional data from endoscopy or stool antigen testing were limited in the seropositive subgroup; only 21 patients underwent endoscopy. If active infection testing had been available, it might have refined the subgroup differences. This finding supports a test-and-treat approach to resolve infection-driven symptoms in seropositive children. These limitations also highlight the need for future prospective studies with larger sample sizes, standardized symptom scoring, and more precise diagnostic methods (e.g., stool antigen testing or endoscopic confirmation) to determine whether eradication alters symptom perception differently by sex and whether infection is the dominant driver of the homogenized presentation.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that sex-based differences may influence the expression of dyspeptic symptoms in children without H. pylori infection, whereas infection may homogenize the clinical presentation. Greater awareness of these patterns may help clinicians make more accurate and individualized assessments and avoid unnecessary interventions in pediatric patients with dyspeptic complaints.
