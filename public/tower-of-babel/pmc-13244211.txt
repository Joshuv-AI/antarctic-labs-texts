To the Editor:

Hairy cell leukaemia (HCL) disorders, including classical HCL and HCL variant (HCL-V), are a heterogeneous group of mature B-cell lymphoid malignancies characterised by the presence of “hairy” cells, specific genetic abnormalities, and distinct clinical behaviours. Classical HCL is universally driven by the BRAFV600E mutation in more than 95% of cases [1], leading to constitutive activation of the MEK–ERK pathway. In contrast, HCL-V is a distinct entity associated with a poorer prognosis and defined by cells with absence of both CD25 expression and BRAFV600E mutation. Instead, MAP2K1 mutations, affecting the kinase directly downstream of BRAF, are frequent in HCL-V [2]. This genomic landscape is shared with Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) [3, 4] and Erdheim–Chester disease [5], two myeloid disorders affecting multiple organs, including the lungs. Notably, pulmonary involvement is particularly frequent in LCH. Pulmonary LCH (PLCH) primarily affects young smokers of both sexes. Genetically, the BRAFV600E mutation is found in 57% of LCH cases [3], while MAP2K1 mutations are present in 42% of patients [4]. High-resolution chest computed tomography (HRCT), crucial for diagnosis, typically reveals small nodules, cavitated nodules, and thick- and thin-walled cysts predominantly in the upper and middle lung fields, while the lung bases remain relatively spared. These lesions appear focal, interspersed with seemingly normal parenchyma, and involve both peripheral and central regions of the lungs [6, 7]. Considering the shared genetic background, we asked whether the BRAFV600E and MAP2K1 mutations might be associated with lung cysts detected on HRCT in patients with HCL disorders.

This multicentric observational study was based on data from the French HCL registry and included patients currently diagnosed with classical HCL or HCL-V. Inclusion criteria required HRCT performed at diagnosis and/or during follow-up, with no evidence of infection, left heart disease, or lung cancer. The diagnosis was established after a central review of the peripheral blood smears, the examination of the local immunophenotypic data, and the mutational landscape as previously described [8]. Differential diagnoses, including Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome and lymphangioleiomyomatosis, were systematically excluded through clinical, dermatologic, and radiologic evaluation, targeted mutation screening (including FLCN), and plasma VEGF-D measurement. No patient showed extra-thoracic manifestation of Erdheim–Chester disease or LCH. Chest computed tomography (CT) analysis was performed double blind by a pulmonologist (E. Bergot) and a radiologist (J. Domagne). We performed a Fischer test given the samples’ size. Bonferroni correction was applied for comparison of three or more subgroups. All tests were performed using a bilateral alpha risk of 0.05 with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0. (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The study was conducted after approval by the local ethics committee (Caen, under number 6945 - 2022).

50 patients were included. The mean age was 67.5±13.1 years, and 70% were male; 26% were former smokers. Among the 35 patients (70%) with classical HCL, 71.4% were male and 22.8% had a history of tobacco exposure. The BRAFV600E mutation was found in 90% of this group. 15 patients (30%) were diagnosed with HCL-V, 23.1% (i.e. three patients) of whom carried MAP2K1 mutations. Sex ratio and smoking status were similar between groups. Most patients were treated with cladribine or vemurafenib. 12 patients showed at least one cyst on CT scan, of these 70% of patients presented with a single cyst, 10% had between two and four cysts, and 20% had more than five. One patient showed diffuse interstitial pneumonia with reticulations and honeycombing. No specific spatial distribution (upper/lower, central/peripheral) was observed. The imaging pattern differed from that observed in LCH, as there were no nodules or cavitated lesions, and all cysts were thin-walled (figure 1). One patient with a single cyst in the HCL-V group died from haematologic complications.

We compared the prevalence of cysts in our population with published data from the population-based, asymptomatic cohort of the third generation Framingham Heart Study, including 2653 participants [8]. The prevalence of cysts was 3.8 times higher in patients with HCL compared to controls (28.6% versus 7.6%, p<0.001) [8]. While our population differed from the control population in terms of age, sex, and tobacco exposure [9], it is very unlikely that these factors significantly influenced our results, considering that the prevalence of pulmonary cysts remains stable between 50 and 69 years of age [9]. Moreover, tobacco exposure was lower in our cohort compared to the control cohort or to historical LCH cohort. Indeed, 90% of patients with active LCH are known to be active or former smokers. Smoking may play a key role in PLCH pathophysiology by increasing the risk of BRAFV600E mutations [3]. In our cohort, there is no relationship between tobacco exposure level and the number of cysts as observed in PLCH [5]. These results and observations suggest that, in opposition to LCH, smoking is not the main driver of the formation of cysts in HCL population.

Considering the shared genotype in both population, this led us to hypothesise that BRAFV600E mutation would be involved in the development of the cyst. Indeed, similar type and proportion of mutations are observed in HCL [1] and in PLCH [4]. PLCH is an inflammatory myeloid neoplasia characterised by the accumulation of monocytes/macrophages associated with MAPK-pathway mutations (86% i.e. 36% BRAFV600E mutations, 28% BRAF deletions, 15% MAP2K1 mutations and 4% NRAS mutations), and by PLCH cells harbouring CD1a+ and CD207+ expression but also S100, a monocyte marker [6]. The accumulation of CD1a cells into the distal bronchiolar mucosa forms granulomas progressively destroying the bronchiolar wall, leading to the formation of lung cysts [6]. In PLCH, lung biopsies generally objectify BRAF mutations within cysts [6]. Unfortunately, none of our patients underwent lung biopsy. While the role of these mutations in HCL remains still unclear, several data pointed to these BRAFV600E mutations as a driver of the LCH disease. Patients with high-risk LCH involving the bone marrow, liver, or spleen are associated with BRAFV600E mutations in mono-macrophagic progenitors, implicating MAPK pathway activation in the pathogenesis of haematopoietic dendritic cell progenitors [10]. In mice, BRAFV600E mutation in dendritic progenitor cells is associated to pulmonary LCH-like lesions [11]. Moreover, the BRAFV600E mutation in haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells drives a bias toward myeloid lineage differentiation and the development of LCH-like lung lesions containing CD207+ and CD1a+ lung cells [11]. The fact that the same mutation in HSCs gives rise to two distinct diseases (LCH and HCL) suggests that mechanisms beyond BRAF are involved, although these remain unknown at this stage [12, 13].

A prospective multicentre study would be necessary to confirm this observation. HCL may represent a rare cause of cystic lung disease, and it could be appropriate to consider CT screening at diagnosis, followed by longitudinal monitoring, including pulmonary function testing, to evaluate potential changes in lung function over time.
