Triggering mechanics and early warning for snowmelt-rainfall-induced loess landslide
Authors: Kaidierding Wulamu, Zizhao Zhang, Qianli Lv, Guangming Shi, Yanyang Zhang, Shichuan Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLoess landslides represent a significant natural hazard, especially in arid and semi-arid regions where slopes are exposed to prolonged snowmelt and rainfall infiltration. To analyze landslide stability and conduct early warning evaluations effectively, a robust monitoring system and appropriate equipment are crucial. This study utilizes historical data from the Kalahaisu landslide in Xinyuan County, Ili region, which was monitored using various techniques. The research assesses the time resolution, spatial resolution, and data accuracy of these monitoring methods. Additionally, the study explores the landslide disaster patterns and early warning indicators for the Kalahaisu landslide. It suggested that the deformation of the rainfall- and snowmelt- landslide is not only affected by the seasonal climates but also closely related to the internal structures of the loess. The other observation is that the respective time and spatial resolution of monitoring instrument would significantly affect the the interpretation time of the landslide deformation. Instruments with higher spatial resolution can more effectively identify unstable areas within a landslide, while instruments with higher temporal resolution can provide detailed time-series deformation data. Therefore, real-time and comprehensive monitoring of landslides using a suitable combination of monitoring equipment can provide strong data support for landslide stability evaluation and deformation trend prediction. Full-area monitoring techniques, such as Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and equipment that measures changes in internal properties like deep water content, deep displacement, and pore pressure, offer a more comprehensive and effective approach to monitoring and interpreting landslide deformation. The insights gained from this research may enhance the prediction and prevention of loess landslides in the Ili River valley.Keywords: Loess landslide, Rainfall, Freeze–thaw cycle, GNSSSubject terms: Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Loess landslides featured with the high frequency and severe damage are one of the most significant global geological disaster generally occurred in the arid and semi-arid regions, particularly, when they are subjected to the seasonal rainfall and snowmelt1,2. The progressive formation process of the loess landslides starts from the initial deformation to the eventual instability, associated with the development and penetration of the sliding surface, exhibiting a distinct geological phenomena.

The earliest research about the landslide monitoring can be back to the 1860s3 and from which the landslide monitoring is becoming critical for the early identification and warning of the disasters4,5. The main aims of developing various monitoring techniques is to obtain the characteristic information (i.e., the physical and chemical parameters) of the surface and the interior of the slope timely. It has been well noted that these information are essential for the accurate analysis and the management of landslides6. Despite extensive studies have been carried out, research the formation mechanisms and disaster patterns of the loess landslide are still requested to meet the precise prevention needs for geological disasters7,8. Early monitoring techniques generally involve the simple macroscopic geological observation methods and the information (i.e., the ground cracks, slope surface deformation, groundwater changes as well as the unusual surface vegetation) are usually selected for reference9. Different from the GNSS provided a real-time and three-dimensional surface deformation10, other monitoring instruments are easily affected by atmospheric conditions, terrain variations, vegetation cover, and human factors when the surface deformation is evaluated. Both the low temporal resolution and long data processing times attributed to the complexity of interpretation algorithms bring out the side-effect on the early warning of the landslide4,11,12. In recent years, the early identification, monitoring, and warning of the landslide attracts much attention with the application of the modern sensing, the satellite positioning, the remote sensing, and the wireless communication techniques13.

Monitoring the kinematic and hydrologica is crucial for slope stability analysis and the early warning14–17, for which the movement patterns of the landslide subjected to various climatic conditions should be well understood18. As previously demonstrated, the rainfall, temperature, moisture content and relative humidity of the soil, will trigger the landslide19,20. Various technical approaches and monitoring equipment are developed to customize the landslide monitoring schemes. No single device or method, however, can fully explain the landslide deformation mechanisms and a comprehensive monitoring is thus necessary21,22. The performance of monitoring technologies and equipment is evaluated based on time and spatial resolution, data processing accuracy, sensitivity, and monitoring stability23. The deformation characteristics of rainfall-triggered landslides have been explored through correlation analysis of monitoring data from various instruments, such as GNSS, crack gauges, and inclinometers, in previous research24–27. For instance, Malet et al.24 identified a strong correlation between surface displacement and deep displacement data by comparing results from several common deformation monitoring devices.

Although extensive research were carried out to explore the triggering mechanics of the loess landslides from various perspectives (Peng et al.28), its is still debate attributed the large amount of factors inducing the loess landslide failures. For example, some scholars believed that the rainfall is the dominant factor, while others argued that the freeze–thaw cycles are the main cause of instability. Additionally, some researchers suggested that the combined effect of freeze-thaw cycles and rainfall is the primary factor resulting the loess landslide instability and failure29–33. In the present research, the case study in the Kalahaisu landslide in Xinyuan County was selected to reveal the mechanism and disaster initiation patterns of the loess landslide in the Ili river valley. The main factors triggering the loess landslide were comprehensively evaluated. Furthermore, the effectiveness of different landslide monitoring techniques was evaluated.

Study area

The professional monitoring point for the Kalahaisu landslide is situated on a northwest-facing slope in eastern Xinyuan County, Ili Prefecture, Xinjiang. The landslide body is bounded by gullies on both sides, with a narrow top and wide bottom. Its longitudinal length is 500–580 m, and the transverse width of which is 100–400 m. The slope morphology is steep at both ends and gentle in the middle, forming a trapezoidal shape with an average slope of 26°. The foot of the landslide is gentler, while the middle part slightly tilts northward with minor undulations. From the middle to the rear edge, the slope steepens gradually, with multiple arc-shaped tensile cracks oriented east-west. These crown cracks appeared 10 years ago and still continued to deform to varying degrees during each rainy and snowmelt season.

Based on borehole data and special survey information, the upper part of the landslide body is covered by a thick Quaternary Upper Pleistocene aeolian silt layer, the average thickness of which is about 35 m, underlain by late Hercynian granite. The interface between the silt layer and the underlying granite is relatively flat and the silt layer thickness increased from the crown to foot.

The large scale of the Kalahaisu landslide poses a threat to the village of Areletuobie on the west side (The direction pointed to by the arrow in Fig. 1a cannot be shown in the landslide display map due to the distance), endangering many people and significant property. Consequently, this landslide was designated as a professional monitoring point at the end of 2020 and officially monitored from the September of 2021. In the present research, the monitoring data recorded from 2021 to 2023 was adopted to analyze the deformation and sliding conditions of the landslide body. Note that the interpretation on the deformation signs and observation on failure patterns were also during this monitoring period.

Distribution of monitoring devices

The Kalahaisu landslide is equipped with a series of professional monitoring devices (Table 1), providing reliable data for studying early-stage damage characteristics, damage mechanisms, and critical thresholds for forecasting and early warning. Apart from fifteen GNSS surface displacement monitoring points cover the middle and upper parts of the landslide body, six crack meters at the crown monitor crack deformation. Moreover, six soil moisture monitoring stations are positioned longitudinally from the middle to crown. Additionally, one deep displacement monitoring device is installed in both the rear and middle areas. These devices vary in temporal and spatial resolutions, monitoring ranges, sensitivities, and data processing accuracy. Inclinometer rope

The inclinometer ropes are installed in three boreholes to monitor minute deep-seated tilting deformation of the landslide body. Each inclinometer rope consists of a rigid lever integrated with an inclinometer sensor and azimuth sensor. This setup determines the coordinates of each node before and after changes. The inclinometer has an accuracy of 0.01°/0.2 mm and uses a bus structure that allows for multiple cascading connections and adjustable lengths as needed. The structure of the inclinometer rope is shown in Fig. 2. The tilt ropes were arranged with the interior of the borehole at a depth of 50 m, with nodes for deformation measurements every 2 m from top to bottom from the ground.

Schematic of deep displacement monitoring (inclinometer rope).

Three inclinometer ropes are connected to a single data acquisition station, which automatically records displacement changes with high temporal resolution. These ropes are placed only in areas where deformation is more pronounced and representative, so the monitoring data represents a small area around each point, resulting in lower spatial resolution35.

GNSS deformation monitoring station

The GNSS displacement monitoring stations are installed on levers with flanges at the top, totaling 15 locations. Nine are distributed along three parallel lines in the middle and upper parts of the landslide body, with two each at the rear and front edges and one on each wing. The monitoring stations use multi-frequency RTK satellite positioning modules with a spatial resolution of 0.2°/1m and a temporal resolution of 20 ns. They can monitor and record the deformation and movement trajectories of various points on the landslide body in real time, providing time series data on landslide deformation and failure.

This method provides suitable landslide deformation data at both local (around the monitoring points) and global (entire landslide area) scales. It enables large-scale, long-period, continuous measurements of the entire landform, capturing information on the direction and scale of surface movement. It features long-range, non-contact, and multi-dimensional macro monitoring, allowing for the mapping of deformation areas across the entire landslide body during the monitoring process36–39.

Crack meter

The crack meters are installed on custom-made cement piers and consist of a crack data acquisition instrument, a pull-wire guide, and a solar panel. They are placed at the crown cracks of the landslide, from the south end to the north end, with a total of six locations. The structure of the crack meter is shown in Fig. 3. The devices feature the BeiDou and the GPS dual positioning and an inclination sensor. Data is calculated using a single-turn encoder, with a deformation monitoring accuracy of 0.5 mm and a range of 0–5 m.

Installation diagram of crack deformation monitor (crack meter).

The inclinometers provide high spatial resolution within a small area around the monitoring point but have relatively lower overall spatial resolution. Therefore, crack monitoring data is only useful for interpreting deformation in the crack distribution area at the rear edge of the landslide body.

Soil moisture monitoring station

The water content sensors are fixed inside the levers and installed in pre-set boreholes with a total depth of 50 m, with sensors installed every 10 m from the surface downwards, and at different depths, with measurement depths of 0–5 m. The soil moisture monitoring stations are arranged along two lines, one longitudinal and one transverse, on the landslide body, with six locations longitudinally and four transversely, totaling ten locations. As soil moisture increases, its dielectric constant also increases. The soil moisture content can be measured based on this relationship.The structure of the soil moisture monitoring station is shown in (Fig. 4).

Installation diagram of soil moisture monitor.

Meteorological station

The meteorological station is located in the southern part of the landslide body and integrates precipitation, temperature, wind speed, and wind direction monitoring into a single system. Data is collected once every hour.

Landslide deformation analysis

To demonstrate the capability of different monitoring devices in identifying slope deformation within the same unit, the spatial resolution of each instrument is classified as either local or global. Devices such as the inclinometers and crack meters, while extremely sensitive to minor deformations, can only interpret stratigraphic activity within a small area around the monitoring point. However, these devices provide high temporal resolution data, clearly showing the correlation between movement events and their specific environmental conditions. Conversely, the GNSS surface displacement monitors, despite their lower deformation measurement accuracy, offer higher global spatial resolution. This enables them to interpret the overall movement of the landslide body on a global scale and provide robust data support for identifying unstable slope areas, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Analysis of more than two years of monitoring data

Over the 2-year period since September 2021, the Kalahaisu landslide has been monitored for changes in rainfall, temperature, GNSS surface displacement, and surface soil moisture content, as shown in Fig. 6. Representative monitoring points for GNSS surface displacement and soil moisture were selected from the crown, foot, and left wing of the landslide, each with different movement characteristics.

Changes in rainfall, temperature, surface displacement, and soil moisture content during the monitoring period.

It is in 2021 that the landslide area experienced intermittent heavy rainfall characterized by low frequency, short duration, and high intensity. During this period, soil moisture content varied slightly, indicating that most rainfall was discharged as surface runoff and water vapour evaporation, with only a small portion infiltrating the soil. According to the data provided by Xinjiang Geological and Environmental Monitoring Institute, the average annual evaporation in the study area is 1530.4 mm, of which the highest evaporation is in summer: 731.1 mm, 444.1 mm in spring, 292.1 mm in autumn and 63.2 mm in winter.The low permeability of the upper soil layer explains this insensitivity to heavy rainfall events40. Conversely, 2022 was a relatively dry year with infrequent and low-intensity rainfall events, leading to a continuous decline in soil moisture content. In 2023, frequent and prolonged rainfall events occurred. Compared to 2021, the soil moisture monitoring curve showed greater variability, indicating that surface soil moisture is more sensitive to continuous, low-intensity rainfall than to intermittent heavy rainfall.(In 2023, an anomalous segment of temperature data was recorded in the region. According to information from the local meteorological office and the Department of Natural Resources, these anomalies were attributed to issues with the monitoring equipment).

In the early monitoring phase, the moisture content and its variation pattern were consistent across different regions of the landslide body. However, as deformation continued, the crown’s surface soil moisture content became significantly higher than that of other regions in later stages, with greater variability. This suggests that the crown is more sensitive to rainfall events. Continuous deformation caused the crown cracks to expand, allowing more rainwater infiltration and easier evaporation under high temperatures. In contrast, the soil moisture content in other regions remained lower and relatively consistent, with significant variations during snowmelt and cool, humid seasons and minor changes at other times. This indicates that surface runoff dominates rainfall events in these areas.(There is a significant gap in the moisture content data for 2022. According to information from the Geological and Environmental Monitoring Institute (GEMI) and the local Department of Natural Resources (DNR), this gap resulted from damage to the monitoring equipment caused by local grazing activities.)

Over the two and a half years from September 2021 to the end of 2023, surface displacement monitoring data showed two significant deformation stages. The first stage occurred during the snowmelt period in early 2022, with a total displacement of 26.3 mm. This deformation resulted from snowmelt infiltration into the soil during spring warming, following several heavy snow events in winter, altering the hydrodynamic conditions and physical and mechanical properties of the upper loess. From April 2022, the landslide body entered a prolonged slow deformation phase lasting over a year, during which the area experienced a dry spell with low rainfall, high evaporation, and minimal water absorption by the soil, explaining the decreasing trend in soil moisture content. By August 2023, the landslide body entered a second accelerated deformation stage, with a cumulative displacement of 45 mm. During this period, temperatures gradually decreased while rainfall remained high, reducing evaporation and increasing water infiltration into the soil, explaining the gradual increase in soil moisture content.

In summary, the GNSS monitoring data indicates two phases of accelerated movement over the monitoring period. Further analysis will focus on high temporal and local spatial resolution data during the accelerated movement periods in spring 2021 and late summer 2022.

Phase 1–the spring of 2021

Figures 7 and 8; Table 2 show various monitoring data for the first accelerated deformation phase of the landslide over the two-year monitoring period, including data from monitors with high local spatial resolution such as crack gauges and tilt ropes.This data includes information from crack meters and the inclinometers, which have high local spatial resolution. Before the first phase of accelerated movement, the landslide experienced a wet winter with a cumulative snowfall of 376.7 mm. As temperatures warmed in the spring, surface soil moisture content rapidly increased. Several factors contributed to this accelerated movement: (1) Spring snowmelt increased soil moisture content and pore pressure; (2) Day-night temperature differences during the snowmelt process caused multiple freeze-thaw cycles, damaging the soil’s internal structure; (3) Runoff from snowmelt eroded the landslide surface.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, on February 19, the temperature in the landslide area rose to −5.4 °C, and soil moisture content experienced its first surge, indicating the onset of the snowmelt period. Temperatures fluctuated until March 2, when they sharply rose to 3.1 °C, causing a second rapid increase in surface soil moisture content and indicating extensive snowmelt on the upper surface.

GNSS monitoring data shows that landslide movement was very slow or negligible in the early monitoring period until March 4, when surface displacement data showed significant changes, marking the start of the accelerated deformation stage. During this period, the landslide body underwent freeze-thaw cycles, with daytime warming causing snowmelt infiltration and nighttime cooling causing freezing. These constant phase changes typically result in thaw settlement and frost heave, damaging the soil’s microstructure and altering the stability of soil aggregates, shear strength, moisture content, and bulk density. This enhances soil erodibility during infiltration and promotes the landslide’s instability and failure progression41.

After nearly 25 days of accelerated deformation, the phase transitioned into a dry period marked by rapid moisture loss. During this time, temperatures rose quickly, evaporation increased, pore water content gradually decreased, pore pressure dropped, and the landslide movement rate slowed, entering a stable phase. Notably, GNSS monitoring data showed that the front edge of the landslide began to accelerate almost 10 days later than other parts, with smaller movement and a shorter duration of accelerated deformation. Crack meter data revealed significant variations in movement across different parts of the crown of the landslide. The central axis showed the most noticeable changes in crack width, with the rate of change being more sensitive to variations in moisture content. The left-wing (the northern end of the cracks) had the smallest changes, with smoother monitoring curves and no significant correlation with changes in surface soil moisture content. The onset of accelerated deformation in the three monitoring curves lagged 3–5 days behind the snowmelt period, indicating that changes in crack width were not sensitive to changes in moisture content. Three inclinometers were installed at different locations from the rear to the front edge of the landslide. Deep displacement monitoring data, shown in Fig. 8, indicated different characteristics of deep displacement in these regions. As shown in Fig. 8a, deep deformation at the crown was distributed regularly, with greater depth corresponding to smaller deformations. Significant deformation was first observed on March 10th at depths of 18–20 m and above. Subsequent deformation continued, reaching a maximum depth of 28 m, with the greatest deformation occurring at the front surface. This suggests that shallow surface deformation at the crown pulls deeper soil layers, indicating a slip surface at about 28 m depth. Monitoring data from the inclinometer placed in the middle of the landslide (Fig. 8b) indicated smaller displacement and an irregular displacement-depth curve. Deformation started at depths of 26–28 m under snowmelt conditions, with the acceleration onset time consistent with the crown. Figure 8c highlights that deformation at the front edge only occurred above 12 m depth. The deformation was more consistent and smaller in magnitude, and the deformation monitoring curve had a regular depth distribution.

According to the monitoring data during the snowmelt period, the spring snowmelt infiltration has a strong relationship with the deformation of the landslide body, and the deformation amount during the snowmelt period is not large, but the acceleration of deformation is large, which shows that snowmelt infiltration has a large impact on the stability of loess landslides. This conclusion is consistent with the concepts of Flageollet et al. and Saez et al.42,43.

Phase 2–the late summer to the autumn of 2023

The second landslide activation phase occurred from early August to late November 2023, lasting 95 days. Before this phase, the landslide experienced a wet summer, leading to significant moisture accumulation in the soil layers and maintaining high moisture levels. Continuous rainfall starting on August 3, heavy rain on August 8, and continuous cooling since August 1 resulted in a rapid increase in soil moisture content, triggering the landslide’s accelerated deformation stage on August 8. Several heavy rainfalls within the following five days provided continuous moisture input to the landslide. During heavy rainfall events on August 8, 12, September 13, and 30, changes in soil moisture content were observed. The crown region’s soil moisture content was more sensitive to rainfall, while the middle and foot regions were less sensitive, consistent with Phase 1 observations. Temperature changes on October 18 and November 11 were compared with soil moisture content changes, revealing that the middle and front edge regions were more sensitive to temperature fluctuations, indicating significant influence from evaporation. In contrast, the crown region was less affected by temperature changes, with rainfall being the dominant factor. This is due to multiple tensile cracks at the crown, which provide advantageous pathways for rainwater infiltration, significantly improving moisture infiltration efficiency.

From the time series of landslide movement shown in Figs. 8 and 9, it is clear that the crown of the landslide entered the accelerated movement phase 29 days before the middle section and 34 days before the front edge. Activation of the crown began on August 8. During this phase, the GNSS surface displacement monitoring curve was characterized by multiple significant deformation events. Each event coincided with a substantial increase in soil moisture content at the crown, resulting in a prolonged period of accelerated deformation and significant cumulative displacement. This is also evident from the deep displacement distribution shown in Fig. 9a. Compared to the first acceleration event, the stratification phenomenon in the deep displacement curve during Phase 2 is more pronounced, indicating a slip surface at a depth of 26 m at the crown. Crack monitoring data shows that the cracks in the middle and right-wing exhibited larger and faster deformation, composed of multiple short-term, rapid, and significant deformation events. In contrast, the left end of the cracks experienced prolonged slow deformation. The middle region of the landslide maintained a slow creep deformation throughout the activation period, with a smaller slope in the GNSS monitoring curve. Only an extreme cooling event at the end of this phase triggered the sole rapid deformation event. The displacement-depth distribution curve was more regular than in Phase 1, with the slip surface at a consistent depth of 28 m, as inferred in Phase 1. The deformation characteristics of the front edge remained consistent with Phase 1, except that during Phase 2, the front edge experienced two short-term rapid deformation events, resulting in a double “S” shape in the displacement-time curve, with an intermediate plateau period.

According to the analysis of the above data, it can be seen that rainfall events play an important role in promoting the deformation of loess landslides, although the deformation rate is slow, but the deformation amplitude is large and can cause the landslides to continue to deform for a long time.

Landslide movement patterns and the applicability of monitoring technologies

This paper comprehensively analyzes meteorological and monitoring data from the Kalahaisu landslide in Xinyuan County. Empirical data from monitoring systems are used to discuss the deformation characteristics and failure mechanisms of various areas of the Kalahaisu landslide. Previous research typically suggests that rainfall is the primary factor inducing landslide disasters in the Ili region and other parts of the country. However, this study finds that, in addition to the significant influence of rainfall, spring snowmelt and freeze-thaw cycles caused by day-night temperature differences have a crucial deteriorating effect on the internal structural stability of surface loess, significantly accelerating landslide instability and failure.

Landslide deformation and failure mechanisms

Using data from various deformation monitoring instruments, combined with meteorological and surface soil moisture data, this study summarizes the movement patterns and deformation mechanisms of the Kalahaisu landslide. Figure 11 illustrates the landslide’s geomorphological characteristics and shows the failure patterns and movement mechanisms under specific meteorological conditions.

Damage pattern of the Kalahaisu loess landslide.

The deformation-time monitoring curves of the Kalahaisu loess landslide reveal that, besides the impact of rainfall, the spring freeze-thaw cycles also play a crucial role in promoting landslide instability, which is confirmed by the relationship between the deformation data and meteorological events in Figs. 6 and 7, and 9 of this paper, which confirms the conclusions obtained by domestic and foreign scholars on the studies related to the triggering factors of loess landslides42–44. In this paper, on the basis of analysing the monitoring data of the landslide, a meteorological-hydraulic conceptual model containing the driving process of slope deformation succession development and intermittent recovery initiation is proposed, as shown in Fig. 10. The landslide belongs to progressive landslide in terms of its deformation characteristics, and its conception and evolution generally need to go through a relatively long stage of development of structural surface and accumulation of strain energy, while its sliding surface is also gradually formed in a long period of time during the process of conception and catastrophe, and the overall deformation and change process includes four stages.

The first stage is the accumulation of water and the destruction of soil microstructure, including the pre-snowmelt infiltration and freeze-thaw cycle in spring and the infiltration of rainwater during the rainfall event led to the accumulation of a large amount of water in the topsoil layer of the landslide, as shown in Fig. 10a, b. In the process of seepage, the loess skeleton is destroyed, and under the action of seepage, the fine particles that can be migrated move downward and gather at a certain depth to form a water film layer with good lubricity, which then evolves into a surface of rupture in the slow deformation process45–48.

The second stage is the initial development of cracks, as shown in Fig. 10c. With the destruction of the microstructure of loess, seepage erosion and wet subsidence continue to strengthen, the water film layer formed in the first stage gradually liquefies, the shear strength is weakened, and it gradually evolves into a surface of rupture and produces wet subsidence cracks49. Usually, professional monitoring starts after a certain amount of deformation of the landslide body, so the deformation data of the initial stage of the landslide cannot be obtained50.

The third stage is the constant-rate deformation stage, shown in Fig. 11d. During this phase, the landslide deformation rate is slow. Rainfall is characterized by drought or short-term heavy rainfall, leading to minimal infiltration and low soil saturation. Pore water pressure is low, and the landslide is less affected by rainfall events. As a result, the deformation-time curve is gentle, indicating a slow deformation rate. As the landslide deforms slowly, tensile cracks formed during the initial stage gradually extend inward, forming a slip surface in the upper silt layer. Repeated infiltration gradually alters the soil’s microstructure. Deep displacement data from the inclinometers indicate that the surface of rupture depth of the Kalahaisu landslide ranges from 18 to 28 m, connecting cracks at the crown to the middle and front parts of the landslide.

The fourth stage is the accelerated deformation stage, which is represented in Fig. 11e. During this phase, the landslide body experiences a rapid moisture increase due to continuous rainfall or snowmelt infiltration. The cracks formed in the first and second stages provide pathways for rainwater infiltration, accelerating the infiltration process. The rapid increase in moisture content raises pore water pressure and saturation, increasing the weight of the landslide body. Under the combined effect of pore water pressure and self-weight stress, the landslide enters the accelerated deformation stage. Cracks at the crown deepen and widen, further expanding the infiltration pathways. The surface of rupture and cracks at the crown fully connect, forming a landslide block. During the accelerated deformation stage, the landslide may not necessarily develop into a full disaster but could revert to a constant-rate deformation stage after a prolonged acceleration phase, similar to the first and second accelerated deformation stages of the Kalahaisu landslide shown in Fig. 6.

Applicability of landslide monitoring technology

Rainfall-induced increase in water content and pore water pressure are the main factors for the destabilisation and damage of most loess landslides in the Ili region of Xinjiang and the world. Therefore, continuous monitoring of rainfall and soil water content with high temporal resolution provides an important support for technical methods to identify and predict landslide instability. The slip resistance of landslides is largely determined by the slip resistance of the slip surface, so monitoring the water content and pore water pressure at the depth of the presumed slip surface of a landslide plays a crucial role in evaluating the stability of slopes. And before the formation of the slip surface, the signs of deformation of the slope are not obvious, so the monitoring of the pre-deformation events of the landslide body can be carried out by remote sensing means such as SAR. The amount of deformation at each location in the deep part of the landslide body provided by the tilt-measuring rope can provide an effective basis for judging the depth of the distribution of the slip surface. The water content and pore water pressure sensors are deployed at the inferred location of the slip surface, and the monitoring data from the inclined rope can be used to more accurately understand the stability change of the slip surface. If the deep water content and pore water pressure monitoring equipment with high time resolution is installed, combined with continuous rainfall data, the potential destabilisation of the slope can be more accurately identified and predicted.

During the monitoring period, the use of a full-coverage GNSS surface displacement monitoring array allowed the vectorisation of the surface movement at each monitoring location on the landslide, leading to landslide stability zoning.This region-wide displacement monitoring is achieved by superimposing multiple GNSS monitoring stations on a certain range of nearby monitoring. To accurately monitor the surface displacement over the whole area of the landslide, the use of remote sensing means such as SAR is the most appropriate, especially in the early stage of landslide deformation.

Although the temporal and spatial resolution of GNSS monitoring stations is sufficient to determine the relatively stable areas of the landslide and the distribution boundaries of the landslide body throughout its entire range and movement cycle, higher resolution and a greater variety of monitoring indicators are particularly important for most landslides with rich threatened objects51. GNSS monitoring stations are highly sensitive to surface movement, while deep displacement monitoring data can provide more robust and critical support for evaluating landslide stability. Additionally, inclinometer data can distinguish different sliding modes between the middle-rear and front parts of the landslide.

This paper does not consider the various types of monitoring techniques and equipment as well as the costs required for monitoring, which are important constraints faced in most landslide monitoring projects. Although all kinds of monitoring instruments can provide us with all kinds of abnormal traces of landslides through real-time monitoring of the trend of each parameter, but the data provided by a single monitoring instrument can provide limited evidence for the deformation characteristics of landslides, therefore, through the synthesis of a number of monitoring methods in order to provide a more complete basis for the determination of landslide deformation and early warning. A set of monitoring programmes applicable to landslides is proposed on the basis of the research in this paper (Fig. 12), which demonstrates the role of the data obtained from various types of monitoring equipment in the process of analysing landslide movement patterns and evaluating landslide stability. It is shown that a better and more detailed understanding of the mechanisms and deformation behaviour that trigger landslide destabilisation and damage can be achieved by having monitoring data from monitoring equipment that covers all spatial and temporal resolutions.

Discussion

Monitoring data reveal that during wet and rainy seasons, especially during snowmelt periods, soil moisture content in the landslide body increases rapidly, approaching saturation. This leads to sudden changes in pore water pressure, disrupting the original stress equilibrium within the soil. However, this moisture increase is predominantly triggered by prolonged and intense rainfall, with short, heavy rain events having a lesser impact. This observation is consistent with several heavy rainfall events in 2021 and aligns with the findings of Zhang Lei et al. regarding rainfall-induced loess landslides33.

In the spring, large diurnal temperature variations cause snow to melt during the day and infiltrate the soil, only to refreeze at night due to lower temperatures. This leads to extended freeze-thaw cycles, which compromise the internal structure of the upper soil layers. The severity of these cycles is influenced by prior precipitation: higher initial soil moisture from earlier rainfall intensifies the impact of snowmelt. This effect on soil stability is evident when comparing monitoring data from the springs of 2021 and 2022, further supporting the conclusions of Zhu Sainan et al.32,39.

The original purpose of setting up specialized monitoring points for geologic hazards is to facilitate local workers to carry out early warning, forecasting and prevention of geologic hazards. Although the Geological Environment Supervision Institute will set a certain warning threshold in the system when setting up the professional monitoring points, but for the slow deformation of creep-slip type landslides, in addition to setting a fixed warning threshold, it is also necessary for the local staff to observe and analyze the trend of the monitoring curves from time to time to judge the deformation stage of the landslides. The significance of early warning in this paper is to provide a method for local staff to evaluate the stability of landslides and to judge the stage of landslide deformation through monitoring curves.

Aiming to explore the triggering mechanics of the loess landslides affected by the snowmelt and rainfall, various monitoring data, remote sensing images, field surveys, and specialized investigation data of the Karahaisu loess landslide were comprehensively investigated. The deformation characteristics of the Karahaisu landslide was affected by the meteorological conditions, which generally require a long period of structural development and strain energy accumulation.
