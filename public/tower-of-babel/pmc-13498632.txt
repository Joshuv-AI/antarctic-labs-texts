Acute myeloid leukemia with unusual mature lymphoid-like morphology in a subset of blasts
Authors: Christopher Vossen, Clarissa E. Jordan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia with atypical blast morphology. Peripheral blood smear showed an atypical, dimorphic population of myeloblasts, including a subset with typical morphology and a separate subset with unusual mature lymphoid-like morphology. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping was crucial in characterizing the dimorphic population as a single myeloid blast population.

An 82-year-old female with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath and fatigue. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed anemia (hemoglobin 4.1 g/dL) and marked leukocytosis (355.2 × 109/L). Peripheral blood smear showed an atypical, dimorphic cell population (98% of leukocytes). One subpopulation consisted of intermediate- to large-sized cells with ovoid nuclear contours, variably prominent nucleoli, fine chromatin, and a moderate amount of pale cytoplasm with variable granulation (Fig. 1A, blue arrows, Wright-Giemsa, 100 × objective). The second subpopulation consisted of small- to intermediate-sized cells with scant pale blue cytoplasm, clumped chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli (Fig. 1A, black arrows, Wright-Giemsa, 100 × objective). Co-occurrence of a myeloid neoplasm and a B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder was considered; however, flow cytometric immunophenotyping performed on the bone marrow aspirate characterized this dimorphic population as a single myeloid blast population (97% of total analyzed events), which was positive for CD34, CD13, CD33 (partial), CD117, HLA-DR, CD38 (dim), CD7, and nTdT (partial), and negative for cCD3, CD19, cCD79a, and cCD22 (Fig. 2A-H). No monotypic B-cell population was detected. This atypical lymphoid-like morphology has rarely been reported in the literature, and, to our knowledge, has not been previously described in only a subset of myeloid blasts [1–5]. This case is an excellent and unusual example of the morphologic heterogeneity that can be seen in AML, and underscores the critical role of flow cytometry (in conjunction with other ancillary testing) in establishing an accurate diagnosis.
