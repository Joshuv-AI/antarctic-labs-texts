Optimization of Hexavalent Chromium Removal from Wastewater Using Activated Carbon Derived from Liming Pelt Trimming and Tannin: A Response Surface Methodology Approach
Authors: Md. Faiaz Hossain, Md. Abdul Mottalib, Md. Abdul Goni, Trisha Paul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In leather industries, chromium compound is used in tanning and around 60–70% of the applied chromium is consumed by the leather fiber and the rest of them are being discharged as a mixed toxic effluent. This effluent is a major source of chromium contamination and generates significant amounts of toxic hexavalent chromium (Cr­(VI)) in wastewater. In this study, activated carbon derived from liming pelt trimmings, a byproduct of the leather industry, was utilized for the removal of Cr­(VI) from aqueous solutions. The process was optimized using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with Central Composite Design (CCD), achieving a remarkable 99% Cr­(VI) removal efficiency under optimal conditions (initial concentration, 118.26 ppm; pH, 2.11; adsorbent dosage, 14.39 g/L; and time, 5.44 h). Adsorption kinetics followed a pseudo-second-order model with an R 2 of 0.9889. Assessment and evaluation of different thermodynamic parameters such as ΔG o (−5.18 kJ/mol), ΔH o (−90.22 kJ/mol), and ΔS o (−0.29 kJ/mol) revealed that the adsorption of chromium species on activated carbon was exothermic and spontaneous in nature. The adsorption capacity of the activated carbon composite material from leather trimming wastes was recorded as 79.58 mg/g, exceeding that of other natural adsorbents. The results demonstrated that the synthesized activated carbon was an effective and sustainable adsorbent, offering a promising solution for the removal of Cr­(VI) from industrial wastewater. This novel approach not only helps mitigate the environmental impact of leather industry effluents but also turns waste materials into a valuable resource for pollution control.

Introduction

Emission of toxic mixed wastewater from industries into natural water bodies and lands without any treatment or with a partial treatment has become a major threat to terrestrial environment in many developing countries. A wide variety of industrial operations, such as leather processing, textile dyeing, electroplating, battery manufacturing, and metal polishing, commonly discharged toxic metallic species, can pollute both marine and freshwater ecosystems. − Basic chromium sulfate is the key agent in leather processing and only about 60–70% of the applied chromium compound reacts with the collagen fiber of leather and the rest of it remains unreacted or unused which is being discharged as mixed toxic effluents from industries without any chemical treatment. Untreated or partially treated chromium-containing wastewater causes severe pollution in soil, groundwater, as well as surface water bodies. Heavy metal contamination in water, soil, and plants has received considerable attention to scientific communities in recent years due to their adverse impact on human health through the food chain. Being a member of the transition metal family, chromium can form different compounds with variable oxidation states. Trivalent and hexavalent forms of chromium compounds are more stable than other respective chromium species. A smaller amount of trivalent chromium may be essential for the human body for biochemical reactions, whereas a very trace amount of the hexavalent form of chromium is turned out to be highly toxic for human. Trivalent chromium is 100 times less toxic than hexavalent chromium species. However, the discharge of very large volumes of Cr­(III) species from different leather industries increases the abundance of chromium in the tannery effluent, which ultimately causes severe environmental pollution worldwide. On the other hand, in many ways, trivalent chromium can be oxidized to toxic hexavalent species. The toxicity limit of Cr­(III) species is 28–80 ppm for fish and 0.05 mg/dm3 for drinking water, whereas the permissible limit of Cr­(VI) in drinking water is 50 ppb according to World Health Organization guidelines. The maximum discharge limit of Cr­(VI) and Cr­(III) is 0.05–2 and 5 mg/dm3, respectively, to the aquatic environment according to the regulations of the European Union. , Hexavalent chromium is known to be highly carcinogenic even at very low concentrations, and a higher Cr­(VI) concentration may cause liver, renal, and kidney damages, stomach cancer, chronic bronchitis, and skin irritation. , The presence of Cr­(VI) within aquatic habitats in greater quantities is particularly dangerous and quite alarming due to its severe effects on many aquatic species. Acute tubular necrosis in the kidney, as well as diarrhea, heartburn, blindness, allergic reactions, respiratory tract infections, and dermatitis, can all result from long-term exposure to some chromium compounds in humans. , Cr­(VI) species is highly water soluble and mobile in aquatic systems, which can easily be penetrated into underground water systems and make them poisonous to aquatic organisms as well as humans. Therefore, it is of utmost importance to remove any trace level of toxic and hazardous chromium­(VI) pollutants from industrial wastewater streams using the most suitable and sustainable method to protect aquatic ecosystems and save the lives of aquatic organisms as well as humans. Many methods are available and reported in the literature to extract and separate heavy metals from industrial waste streams. However, among them, the most available and commonly used method to eliminate heavy metals from wastewater streams is chemical precipitation, which has been used in many countries. , However, these methods have several limitations such as relatively higher amount of chemical usages, concentrated sludge accumulation with evident disposal problems, and expensive plant requirements or operational expenses. There are other processes to remove Cr­(VI) from wastewater bodies, which include oxidation, filtration, reverse osmosis, applications of ion exchanges materials, − electrocoagulation, and utilization of zerovalent iron-supported anion exchange resin composites, which are relatively expensive with high operational costs and energy requirements. However, despite their higher effectiveness in removing Cr­(VI) from wastewater streams, these methods and respective materials showed difficulties in recycling and they are very expensive with higher operational costs and energy requirements, which may lead to secondary pollution problems. Therefore, the prime goal of this study was to design and develop new adsorbents or bioadsorbents, which will be ideally suitable, relatively cheaper, and sustainable to use in metal separation technology from wastewater as well as eliminate secondary contamination and pollution in a terrestrial environment.

Due to the efficacy and availability, the utilization of natural, domestic, and agricultural wastes has received considerable interest in recent years to remove chromium­(VI) and other heavy metals from wastewater by adsorption processes. − The polyamine-modified carbon nanotubes (PA-CNTs) have been applied to remove Cr­(VI) from aqueous solutions. , Graphene oxide–CaO and graphene oxide chitosan α–FeO­(OH) hybrid nanocomposites have been developed and applied to remove Cr­(VI) from wastewater. − Recently, surface-modified water hyacinth, coconut tree fibers, and poly aniline-coated sugar cane bagasse were employed as adsorbents to remove Cr­(VI) from wastewater streams.

Activated carbon has been widely recognized and found to be effective to use as an excellent adsorbent for the removal of various contaminants from different aqueous solutions and wastewater bodies due to its high surface area, porosity, and higher adsorptive capacity. − A potential and cost-effective replacement adsorbent for the successful removal of Cr­(VI) from wastewater is amide-functionalized biochar. In recent years, many researchers have explored the potential and effectiveness of utilizing activated carbon derived from unconventional and renewable sources to address and solve different environmental and economic issues. − One such unconventional source is liming pelt trimming solid waste, which is generated from the leather industries. Liming pelt tannery solid waste consists of protein fiber and mineral residues, which is endowed with various organic functional groups (such as–OH, –CO, NH2, and –COOH) that can bind well with chromium species. Therefore, it could be a potentially suitable precursor to produce activated carbon. Furthermore, the addition of mimosa (tanning agent), a type of acacia tree which contains many organic functionalities, as a carbonization and activation agent can enhance the adsorptive properties of the resulting activated carbon. Though many studies have been carried out before on the removal of Cr­(VI) from wastewater and their significant results have been reported in literature, to the best of our knowledge, no study was conducted before and no report has been published on the removal of Cr­(VI) by the activated carbon generated from tannery liming pelt waste and tannin.

These tannery liming pelt solid wastes are available in the tanning industries. The collection of them involves zero cost as they cause severe pollution in the terrestrial environment and are a burden for the tanneries. With a significant focus on the conversion of waste into valuable byproducts and minimizing the solid wastes released from leather processing systems, the study was aimed to investigate the feasibility of utilizing activated carbon generated from tannery liming pelt to synthesize a potential composite material by chemical combination with mimosa and use the resulting new material in the chromium removal technology from wastewater streams by using adsorption technology. Potential removal of toxic and hazardous Cr­(VI) from industrial wastewater streams by suitable treatment with suitable adsorbents produced from tannery solid wastes will have a significant contribution to the reduction of environmental pollution caused by different leather industries in many developing countries. The present investigation also concentrated on the characterization of activated carbon with the evaluation of surface morphology, porosity, functional group moieties, chromium­(VI) removal performance, as well as kinetic and isothermal studies.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The liming pelt trimmings were collected from the leather manufacturing workshop at the Institute of Leather Engineering and Technology, University of Dhaka. Vegetable tannin agent, mimosa, was purchased from Mimosa Extract Company Ltd., South Africa. Potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and 1, 5-diphenylcarbazide were of analytical grade and purchased from Merck, Germany. All of the chemicals were used as purchased without any further purifications.

Preparation of Charcoal and Activated Carbon from Liming Pelt Trimming with Mimosa

Collected liming pelt trimmings were washed and cleaned with tap water and dried under sunlight. The trimmings were further dried in an oven at 100 °C. The dried trimmings were then ground using a grinding machine. Vegetable tannin mimosa was dried at 100 °C in the oven. Then, the pelt crush and mimosa were mixed at a ratio of 1:1 in the crucible and the resulting mixture was placed in the muffle furnace at 600 °C for 30 min, which yielded charcoal. 5 g of charcoal was mixed with 250 mL 1 M sulfuric acid solution in a conical flask and the mixer was stirred for 12 h at room temperature. The resulting mixture was decanted and washed with distilled water until the pH of the mixture reached to a neutral value. Then, the mixture was filtered to separate the activated carbon, which was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h.

Preparation of the Stock Solution

For preparing 500 ppm of Cr­(VI) stock solution, 1.4135 g of anhydrous potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was transferred in a 1000 mL volumetric flask. Deionized water was added to the flask, and the mixture was shaken properly to obtain a homogeneous solution. The solution was diluted up to the mark with deionized water, and it was mixed properly. A series of diluted solutions with known concentrations were prepared from the stock solution, which were used to construct the standard calibration curve. Depending on the alkalinity or acidity of the experimental sample solutions, a 0.1 M HCl or NaOH solution was used to alter the pH of the solutions. The adsorbent and adsorbate were mixed together in 100 mL volumetric flasks to carry out the series of adsorption batch experiments under specific experimental conditions.

Experimental Design and Statistical Analysis

As a Response Surface Methodology optimization method, central composite design (CCD) was used to investigate how the independent variables affected the adsorption process. Four process independent variables were examined in order to identify the ideal conditions: initial Cr­(VI) concentration (A), pH (B), dose (C), and time (D). The independent variables for optimization were selected based on their well-established influence on heavy metal adsorption processes. Solution pH affects both the surface charge of the adsorbent and the aqueous speciation of Cr­(VI), thereby governing electrostatic interactions and adsorption efficiency. Initial metal concentration provides the mass transfer driving force, adsorbent dose determines the availability of active binding sites, and contact time controls adsorption kinetics and equilibrium attainment. These parameters are widely recognized as the most critical factors influencing adsorption performance and were therefore chosen for optimization using RSM–CCD. The limit of the variables was set after several one-factor-at-a-time screenings. Their effects on the Cr­(VI) absorption capacity and removal % were also examined.

2 n factorial runs, 2n axial runs, and n c center runs are the components of the CCD. The experimental inaccuracy and reproducibility of the results were determined by the center points. Every point equally spaced from the center of the design has a constant model prediction variance, and the axial points are chosen to guarantee ratability.

The following equation calculates the total number of experimental runs: 1 N=2n+2n+nc N=24+2(4)+6=16+8+6=30 where N is the total number of experimental runs, nc is the number of center points, and n is the number of independent variables (factors). This showed that the modeling and optimization procedure required 30 experimental runs, which included 16 factorial runs, 8 axial runs, and 6 center runs. Design-Expert 13.0.0 software was used to analyze the experimental data that were collected. Batch studies with activated carbon prepared from liming pelt trimming with tannin were carried out in 500 mL Erlenmeyer conical flasks, each holding 200 mL of Cr­(VI) solution at the appropriate pH. The flasks were capped and stirred at a steady rate of 120 rpm. The experiments were conducted (each run experimented 3 times for minimizing error) according to the experimental conditions obtained from the central composite design (Table S1).

Characterization of Adsorbents

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Alpha 11, Bruker, Germany) was used to identify different organic functionalities within the structures of the adsorbent materials before and after the adsorption experiments. The structural morphology of a new adsorbent material was analyzed by the application of scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4000, Japan) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, XFlash Detector 630M, Germany), respectively. The zeta potential (pHpzc) analysis of the adsorbent was measured by using a particle size analyzer (Litesizer 500, Anton Paar, USA). A Varian Cary 50 UV–vis spectrophotometer (USA) was used to determine the concentrations of any remaining Cr­(VI) in the respective solutions obtained after the adsorption experiments. The maximum resonance peak found in the adsorption spectrum obtained from the highest concentrations of the Cr­(VI) ion was used to calculate the respective absorbances in the solutions before and after the biosorption experiments.

Regeneration and Reuse of the Adsorbent

The regeneration of activated carbon to reuse for further removal of Cr­(VI) ions was investigated by conducting four repeated cycles of adsorption–desorption tests. In this study, a conical flask containing 20 mL of a Cr­(VI) ion solution with a concentration of 120 ppm was transferred to a conical flask. 0.3 g (15 g/L) of the adsorbent was added into the flask. The resulting mixture was stirred for 4 h at room temperature and filtered to separate the adsorbent. The resulting filtrate was analyzed for the leftover chromium, and the result showed that 99% of the chromium­(VI) had been removed from the initial chromium solution. The used activated carbon adsorbent was regenerated by adding 1 M H2SO4 solution, and the resulting mixture was agitated for 12 h. The mixture was filtered, and the adsorbent on the filter paper was extensively washed with distilled and deionized water. Finally, it was dried at 60 °C for 4 h. These procedures were repeated four times. The regenerated activated carbon was further applied to remove chromium species from the respective aqueous solutions following similar procedures.

Results and Discussion

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) Analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was used to analyze the chemical composition and the changes in characteristic functionalities within the structures of different experimental materials such as liming pelt crush, mimosa, carbon, and activated carbon. The FT-IR spectra of liming pelt leather waste, mimosa, charcoal, and activated carbon are shown in Figure .

FTIR spectrum of liming pelt crush (leather waste), mimosa, charcoal, and activated carbon.

The appearances of absorption resonances within the region of 3200 to 3600 cm–1 indicate the presence of the O–H and N–H stretching vibration, which were overlapped with each other in the spectrum of leather waste sample and mimosa (Figure ). The O–H functionality in the structure of liming waste and mimosa samples separately showed strong resonances (Figure ), whereas these characteristic peaks turned out to be weaker and broader in the corresponding IR spectrum of charcoal and activated carbon. The weak absorption peak at 2950 cm–1 might have originated from C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic and aromatic organic compounds. In the leather waste sample, five strong bands had appeared at 1671, 1550, 1430, 1350, and 1036 cm–1, suggesting the presence of Amide I, Amide II, –CH2, C–O, and C–OH functionalities within the chemical structure of the sample. IR spectra of leather waste samples and mimosa were found to be very identical. The adsorption bands shown at 1671, 1550, and 1430 cm–1 apparently indicated the presence of an aromatic structures in leather waste and mimosa. In the case of charcoal, strong and weak resonance bands were observed at 1460 and 1625 cm–1, respectively. Interestingly, a strong resonance band was found at 1625 cm–1 in the spectrum of activated carbon, which strongly indicated the occurrence of structural modifications in the activated carbon material. The characteristic peak at 1625 cm–1 might be originated from the CC and CO vibrations, which occurred from the respective functional groups present in the structure of activated carbon material. Appearances of different characteristic FT-IR resonances peaks in the spectra strongly indicated the existence of various functional groups on the surface of the adsorbent material synthesized in the present study, which potentially can interact with different adsorbate species present in the respective solutions.

Zeta Potential (pHpzc)

Assessment of the zeta potential is a significant tool to define the value of pH at which the adsorbent surface gains a state of net electrical neutrality. In this study, the synthesized activated carbon was mixed into deionized water to find the zeta potential. The corresponding solutions were prepared at different pHs at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 by using 0.5 M H2SO4 and 0.5 M NaOH solution and these solutions at different pH were utilized to find the magnitude of the zeta potential (pHpzc). The zeta potentials were found to be 2.1886, −0.4231, −0.8856, −2.635, −3.59767, −13.3957, and −17.763, respectively, for the corresponding pH of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7, respectively. By plotting the pH against their respective zeta potentials, the point was found where the curve intersects the X axis and it was approximately 1.8, which has been shown in Figure . This point indicated the isoelectric point of the adsorbent. At this pH, the net charge of the adsorbent’s surface was zero, which provides the best adsorption condition for the adsorbent.

Adsorption Isotherm

Adsorption isotherm is a relationship that describes the equilibrium between the concentration of adsorbate molecules on the surface of the adsorbent material and their respective abundances in the surrounding gas or liquid phase at a constant temperature. Different adsorption isotherms are used in adsorption kinetic studies. However, the most utilized isotherms are Langmuir isotherm, Freundlich isotherm, Temkin isotherm, Dubinin–Radushkevich isotherm, and Sips isotherm, which can be applied to analyze and interpret the experimental data. Equilibrium isotherm data are typically assessed by fitting them into various isotherm models and determining the appropriate model in which the experimental data can be best-fit to explain the adsorption characteristics within the respective system.

Langmuir Isotherm

The Langmuir isotherm assumes that adsorption occurs on a homogeneous surface with a finite number of identical sites. It suggests that the adsorption proceeds with monolayer coverage and there is no apparent interaction occurring between adsorbed molecules. The graph was plotted with 1/q e vs 1/C f in the X and Y axes using the Langmuir isotherm equation and the results are shown in Figure a. Freundlich Isotherm

The Freundlich isotherm is an empirical model that describes heterogeneous adsorption on solid surfaces with a nonuniform distribution of active sites. It assumes a multilayer adsorption process with an interaction between adsorbed molecules. The Freundlich isotherm equation is given by 7 ln⁡qe=ln⁡Kf+1nln⁡Cf where q e is the amount of adsorbate species adsorbed per unit mass of the adsorbent, K f is the adsorption capacity constant, C f is the equilibrium concentration of the adsorbate, and n is an empirical constant related to the adsorption intensity. The Freundlich isotherm model effectively explains the heterogeneous surface adsorption and offers significant details regarding the correlation between adsorbent concentrations and adsorption capacity of the respective material. The magnitudes of the slope and intercept were calculated from the straight-line equation obtained from the plot of log q e vs log C f , which is shown in Figure b. The results of adsorption equilibrium showed that binding of chromium­(VI) ions on the surfaces of activated carbon was more fitted with the Freundlich isotherm model where the magnitude of coefficient (R 2) was 0.9827 (Table ).

Temkin Isotherm

The Temkin adsorption isotherm is a model that describes the adsorption of molecules on a solid surface. It was proposed by Temkin in 1940 as an alternative to the Langmuir adsorption isotherm. The Temkin isotherm takes into account the indirect interactions between adsorbate molecules and the surfaces of a solid, whereas the Langmuir isotherm did not consider this type of interaction. The following equation effectively represents the Temkin isotherm model: 8 qe=RTbln⁡Ce

This equation can be transformed into a linear form as shown below: 9 qe=RTbln⁡AT+RTbln⁡Ce where q e is the amount of adsorbate species adsorbed per unit mass of the adsorbent (mg/g). R is the ideal gas constant, T is the temperature in Kelvin, and b is the heat of adsorption (J/mol), which explains the strength of the adsorbate–adsorbent interaction, C e is the equilibrium concentration, and A T is the Temkin isotherm binding constant at the equilibrium state. The Temkin isotherm graph is shown in Figure c and the magnitudes of different parameters obtained from this equations for the adsorption of Cr­(VI) on the surfaces of activated carbon have been presented in Table . The linear relationship between the heat of adsorption and solid surface coverage is represented by the term RT/b in the equation. It is apparent that the Temkin isotherm is considered an empirical model, which may not accurately explain all adsorption systems. However, this model has been found to provide a reasonable approximation for many adsorption processes. The experimental data obtained from the adsorption of chromium on activated carbon were fitted into the Temkin isotherm equation, and the findings indicated that the adsorption process was more or less suited to the Temkin isotherm model where the magnitude coefficient (R 2) was found as 0.9429.

Proposed Mechanism of Cr (VI) with Activated Carbon

When Cr­(VI) species get in close contact with activated carbon, several pathways can be followed to effectively remove chromium from aqueous solutions by an adsorption process. Activated carbon is a highly porous material with larger surface area, which provides numerous active adsorption sites which can potentially bind with various contaminants including Cr­(VI) species. The plausible mechanism in which activated carbon could potentially interact with Cr­(VI) species by different pathways is shown in Figure .

Schematic presentation on the mechanism of Cr­(VI) adsorption onto the surfaces of the activated carbon composite synthesized from leather wastes.

Activated carbon can adsorb Cr­(VI) species onto its surface through both chemical and physical adsorption. The larger surface area and porous structure of activated carbon can facilitate the adsorption process. Cr­(VI) ions can interact with the functional active sites on the surfaces of activated carbon composite through different weak intermolecular forces such as dipole–dipole interaction and London forces. Although Cr­(VI) is commonly present in an aqueous solution as anionic species (HCrO4 –, CrO4 2–, or Cr2O7 2– depending on pH), the appearance of cationic chromium at the surface is explained by the reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(III) during the adsorption process. Carbon materials possess surface functional groups such as phenolic, hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxylic groups, which can act as electron donors. These groups facilitate the partial or complete reduction of Cr­(VI) to Cr­(III), particularly under acidic conditions where Cr2O7 2– predominates and the redox potential of the Cr­(VI)/Cr­(III) couple is high. Since the surface of activated carbon adsorbent is endowed with positive charge, therefore negative ions like Cr2O7 2– will be strongly attracted by surface functionality of adsorbent due to the electrostatic force of interaction. Activated carbon may also act as a reducing agent for Cr­(VI). The surfaces of activated carbon can provide electrons to reduce Cr­(VI) to Cr­(III) which is less toxic and less mobile in the terrestrial environment. The presence of different functional groups on the surfaces of activated carbon such as –OH or CO groups can facilitate electron transfer and promote the reduction process. These functional groups can act as ligands to bind with Cr6+ for forming surface complexes. Activated carbon may also exhibit ion-exchange properties where Cr­(VI) ions can be exchanged with other ions present on the surfaces of activated carbon. Therefore, the coexistence of anionic and cationic chromium species on the carbon surface arises from a coupled adsorption–reduction mechanism: initial adsorption of anionic Cr­(VI), its subsequent reduction to Cr­(III) by surface functional groups, and the stabilization of the resulting Cr­(III) cations on the carbon surface. In this mechanism, the activated carbon matrix releases other ions into the solution while capturing Cr­(VI) ions from aqueous solutions.

Regeneration and Reuse of the Adsorbent

The regeneration results showed the Cr­(VI) removal efficiencies of activated carbon composite from cycles 1 through 4, which were observed to be 91%, 80%, 65%, and 53%, respectively, following a couple of adsorption–desorption processes (Figure ). Ethanol has both a hydrophobic ethyl group and a polar OH– group that react with the adsorbed ions and release them from the surface of the adsorbent. Thus, it causes the desorption of the adsorbent and makes it available for reuse. A similar experiment found 45.55% adsorption efficiency after five cycles of regeneration. These findings revealed the remarkable Cr­(VI) removal efficiency of the activated carbon composite synthesized from leather wastes, which can be regenerated and reused several times. Thus, this activated carbon composite is very cost-effective to be used as a potential adsorbent to remove metallic contaminants from different aqueous solutions and industrial waste streams. Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the potential of activated carbon derived from liming pelt trimmings waste as an effective biosorbent for the removal of toxic hexavalent chromium (Cr­(VI)) from wastewater. A series of batch experiments were carried out, and the results revealed the higher affinity of the activated carbon composite for Cr­(VI) ions due to its larger surface area, porous structure, and strong adsorption properties. The optimized adsorption process achieved a high Cr­(VI) removal efficiency of 99% under the optimal conditions, highlighting the potential of this sustainable adsorbent material. This is quite an interesting and truly impressive finding that demonstrates the excellent chromium removal efficiency of the activated carbon composite. Increasing the experimental temperatures enhanced the chromium adsorption kinetics, whereas it reduced the overall adsorption capacity of the adsorbent. Different adsorption isotherms models were utilized to interpret the results of chromium adsorption on activated carbon, and the findings showed the magnitudes of coefficient (R 2) in the Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, Dubinin–Radushkevich, and Sips isotherm models, which were 0.9728, 0.9827, 0.9429, 0.9591, and 0.9277, respectively. Among the five types of adsorption isotherm models, the data from the chromium removal study were best fit to the Freundlich isotherm model (R 2 = 0.9827). The adsorption kinetics study indicated that the adsorption of Cr­(VI) on the surfaces of activated carbon followed the pseudo-second-order model with an R 2 value of 0.9889, whereas the thermodynamic analysis data revealed that the chromium adsorption process was spontaneous and exothermic. This novel activated carbon composite material derived from liming pelt crushed with mimosa can be utilized as an attractive potential adsorbent that can offer a promising solution for the treatment of Cr­(VI)-containing wastewater released from different industries and thus can reduce the chromium toxicity in the terrestrial environment as well as human health. The current research underscores the importance of utilizing industrial wastes as valuable resources, presenting a viable and cost-effective approach for environmental pollution control. The developed adsorbent has the potential for practical application in wastewater treatment systems, offering a promising alternative to traditional methods that often involve high costs and complex processes.
