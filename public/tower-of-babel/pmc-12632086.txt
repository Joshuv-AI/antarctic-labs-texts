Non-adherence to oral antidiabetic medications among patients with type 2 diabetes: evidence from a nationwide multicentre study in a lower-middle-income country
Authors: Muhammad Arshed, Mehwish Kiran, Waseela Ashraf, Muhammad Kashif Shahzad Virk, Shafqat Qamer, Muhammad Farooq Umer, Ahmad Umar, Javeria Saleem, Ali Hassan Gillani, Asif Hanif, Muhammad Naeem, Muhammad Maaz Arif, Syed Hanzila Azhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-adherence to oral antidiabetic medications is a critical, multifactorial challenge that undermines disease management nationwide. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and predictors of non-adherence among patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) across Pakistan.We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study between December 2023 and March 2025 in 225 healthcare facilities across all provinces of Pakistan. A total of 41,095 participants were selected using a multistage random sampling approach.Medication adherence was evaluated with self-reported pill counts and the Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Use Scale (SEAMS). Multivariable logistic regression using a generalized linear model (GLM) logit approach was employed integrated with Interaction analysis to discover predictors of non-adherence.ResultNon-adherence was present in 59.9% (n=24,616) of cases (n=24,616). Factors associated with reduced adherence included being female (AOR = 0.229, 95% CI: 0.067–0.779, P = 0.02), having only primary or secondary education (AOR = 0.186, 95% CI: 0.166–0.208, P < 0.001), absence of hospitalization, taking fewer medications per day, less frequent dosing schedules, and uncontrolled HbA1c levels. In contrast, factors linked to increased adherence were being married (AOR = 4.118, 95% CI: 3.611–4.695, P < 0.001), being a non-smoker (AOR = 12.98, 95% CI: 11.53–14.62, P < 0.001), having no family history of diabetes (AOR = 1.074, 95% CI: 1.021–1.129, P = 0.005), and having more than one comorbid condition. ConclusionNon-adherence to oral antidiabetic medication is prevalent and influenced by diverse personal, socioeconomic, and health-related factors. Targeted interventions addressing modifiable risk factors could improve adherence, ultimately reduce the healthcare burden and enhancing outcomes for patients with diabetes in Pakistan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25031-x.Keywords: Medication adherence, Risk factors, Cross-sectional, Odds ratios, Antidiabetic, Pakistan, Oral antidiabetic medications, Nationwide study

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is among the most common public health challenges globally [1]. Of the estimated 463 million individuals living with diabetes, approximately 90% are diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2]. In Pakistan, the escalating prevalence of T2DM has become a serious public health issue [3].

A combination of genetic predisposition, rising life expectancy, rapid urbanization, and shifts in dietary and lifestyle patterns contribute to this trend [4]. The situation is further aggravated by undiagnosed, untreated, and poorly managed diabetes, which exerts a substantial toll on individuals, communities, and the national healthcare infrastructure [5]. Pakistan’s healthcare infrastructure presents a challenging context for diabetes management.

The healthcare infrastructure is a fragmented mix of public and private services, shaped by limited resources and uneven access. With only 1.1 physicians per 1,000 people and health insurance coverage reaching just 3% of the population, patients often face significant barriers to care. Out-of-pocket expenses account for nearly 58% of total health expenditure, while public health spending remains critically low at 1.2% of GDP. Insurance programs like the Sehat Sahulat Card aim to improve financial protection but their reach and implementation remain uneven. These constraints push many individuals toward unregulated private providers, resulting in inconsistent quality care and limited chronic disease management especially in rural and underserved areas [6]. Diabetes contributes notably to the burden of avoidable illnesses, resulting in negative financial impacts through increased healthcare costs and diminished productivity [7]. However, compelling evidence suggests that diabetes and its complications can be mitigated through reasonably priced, population-level, and high-risk-targeted interventions [8].

A key determinant of effective diabetes management is medication adherence. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), adherence refers to the extent to which a person’s behavior—such as taking medications, following a diet, and implementing lifestyle changes—aligns with healthcare provider recommendations [9]. A patient’s actions align with the agreed-upon treatment plan usually developed in collaboration with healthcare professionals [10], whereas non-adherence denotes deviation from the prescribed regimen [11].

In the context of diabetes, adherence to oral antidiabetic medication is essential for maintaining glycemic control and preventing complications. Despite advances in therapeutic options, nearly half of patients tend to discontinue their medication during the first year after starting therapy [12]. Medications are ineffective when not taken as prescribed [13], and early discontinuation is a notable contributor to poor disease outcomes [14]. Patients’ beliefs about medications and their understanding of diabetes can significantly influence adherence behaviors [15, 16]. Additionally, treatment complexity, high costs, adverse effects, polypharmacy, and complicated dosing schedules can impede adherence [15, 17]. Healthcare system-related factors, including fragmented care and clinical inertia, also undermine adherence efforts [15]. Socioeconomic determinants such as limited education, low income, and the use of alternative medicines may further complicate adherence [15, 16, 18]. Effective patient-provider communication remains vital in fostering adherence [19]. Addressing these multifaceted determinants through targeted interventions is essential to increasing adherence and improving health outcomes for diabetics [20]. Globally, non-adherence to prescribed antidiabetic regimens ranges from 9% to over 80% [21]. Poor adherence is associated with inadequate glucose control and heightened risks of complications, including cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, and retinopathy—conditions that contribute to increased morbidity and premature mortality [22].

Unfortunately, medication non-adherence remains a persistent challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Pakistan. While high-income countries have conducted extensive research on adherence patterns, evidence from Pakistan remains limited and is often confined to single-center studies. This represents a significant evidence gap, especially given that managing diabetes is a lifelong endeavor, with patients responsible for over 95% of daily self-management activities [23]. Although healthcare reforms have improved access to medications, unregulated dispensing practices may inadvertently promote non-adherence [24]. Non-adherence is consistently associated with poorer health outcomes [25], escalating both individual and societal healthcare costs [26, 27]. Existing literature advocates for moving beyond small-scale, institutional, and retrospective cost studies, calling instead for national-level research incorporating larger sample sizes and accounting for indirect costs [28, 29].

In response to this critical evidence gap, our study presents a pioneering national-level investigation of medication adherence overcoming the limitations of single-center designs. With its large sample size, coverage of diverse regions, and assessment of various risk factors, it captures the complexity of Pakistan’s healthcare and population landscape more effectively than previous localized studies.

Medication non-adherence is a complex and multidimensional issue [13, 30], necessitating a thorough understanding of predictors that impact adherence behaviours. To this end, the present study conducted a cross-sectional national survey to assess the prevalence and determinants of non-adherence to oral antidiabetic medications in Pakistan. The findings aim to inform the design of comprehensive intervention strategies and guide policymakers in crafting effective responses to improve medication adherence, enhance glycaemic control, and reduce the escalating health and economic burden of diabetes.

Methods

Methodology

Participants and study design

A nationwide cross-sectional study was carried out in Pakistan between December 2023 and March 2025. The population-based study targeted T2DM individuals who were administered oral antidiabetic medications. A total of 225 healthcare facilities across Pakistan participated in the study, encompassing both primary care settings—such as Basic Health Units (BHUs) and Rural Health Centres (RHCs)—and secondary care facilities, including Tehsil Headquarters (THQs) and District Headquarters (DHQs).

The study covered all major provinces—Punjab, Sindh, Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), and Islamabad Capital Territory—as well as the independent area of Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJ&K), which are collectively referred to here as provinces. Pakistan’s administrative hierarchy further divides these provinces into divisions, districts, tehsils, and union councils. Healthcare facilities were selected division-wise, including Quetta (Balochistan); Peshawar, Bannu, Kohat, and Dera Ismail Khan (KP); Lahore, Multan, Rawalpindi, Sahiwal, Sargodha, Gujranwala, Faisalabad, Gujrat, Mianwali, and Dera Ghazi Khan (Punjab); Karachi, Hyderabad, Larkana, Sukkur, and Mirpur Khas (Sindh); along with Islamabad and AJ&K. Gilgit Baltistan was not included due to lack of accessible facility data and logistical limitations, and this exclusion is acknowledged as a limitation for nationwide generalizability.

Due to security concerns, regions within the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) and parts of Balochistan and KP were not included. Although these exclusions were unavoidable, they remain important limitations full nationwide generalizability, as these areas may possess unique sociocultural and healthcare dynamics influencing adherence patterns.

Sampling procedure

A multistage random sampling approach was utilized. A comprehensive list of THQs, BHUs, and RHCs in each division was obtained. From each division, a minimum of 10 hospitals were randomly selected, except in Islamabad, where only 5 hospitals met the inclusion criteria due to its smaller population and healthcare infrastructure. At least 100 diabetic T2DM patients were recruited from the Outpatient Departments (OPDs) of each selected facility.

Training, calibration, and quality control

Medical Officers (MOs)—licensed MBBS-qualified physicians—served as master trainers for data collection. Fourth-year MBBS students were recruited as Research Assistants (RAs) and received stipends for their involvement. Two RAs were assigned to each of the twenty data collection sites. MOs supervised and mentored RAs throughout the process.

All MOs and RAs participated in standardized training sessions delivered via Zoom by a certified epidemiologist using a pre-validated training package. These sessions covered the principles of medication adherence, particularly regarding antidiabetic medications. Additionally, on-site two-day refresher trainings were conducted to ensure consistency. The training curriculum included protocols for counseling participants and guiding them through the medication adherence questionnaire.

Eligibility criteria

Participants had to be at least eighteen years old, with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least one month prior to enrollment, and be taking oral antidiabetic drugs in a primary or secondary care facility to be eligible. Individuals were excluded if they had self-reported psychiatric disorders, were pregnant or within three months postpartum, had a history of cancer, participants with severe disability such as unable to comprehend study procedures or provide informed consent due to physical, cognitive, or mental impairment, were excluded from participation [31], or were unable to communicate in Urdu.

Study outcome

The primary study outcome was adherence to oral antidiabetic medications. Adherence was assessed using two validated self-reported measures: the Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS) and pill count methodology.

SEAMS is a widely recognized, valid, and reliable instrument for evaluating medication adherence in chronic illness populations [32]. It has been used in a number of earlier studies [33–35], including its validation studies [32, 36]. The Urdu version of the SEAMS (SEAMS-U) was used in this study. It demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.897), split-half reliability (Part 1 = 0.838, Part 2 = 0.789), test–retest reliability (Spearman’s ρ = 0.686, p < 0.001), and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.814. All procedures related to validity and reliability were carefully followed. The SEAMS-U is currently under publication. SEAMS consists of 13 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale: “not confident” (1), “somewhat confident” (2), and “very confident” (3) [32, 36].

Pill-count adherence was also self-reported, where participants provided information on the number of prescribed pills, doses missed, and doses taken over a specified period [13]. To minimize recall bias, patients were asked to show their medication strips during the survey visit, for pill count validation; however, in cases where strips were unavailable, self-reporting was relied upon, an approach commonly adopted in adherence research [34, 35, 37, 38].

Adherence was calculated from this information based on intake over the preceding 15 days. Adherence was calculated as the ratio of pills taken to pills prescribed. Participants with adherence rates ≥ 80% were classified as adherent. The SEAMS tool, however, does not have a validated cut-off for adherence classification; instead, it was analyzed as a continuous scale yielding an overall score ranging from 13 to 39. More scores indicate better medication adherence [32, 35, 36, 39]. A comprehensive dataset was collected, including sociodemographic, patient-specific, and disease-related variables [35].

Physical activity: Physical activity levels were self-reported by patients using a structured questionnaire administered during data collection. Participants were categorized as (1) Inactive (< 30 min/day, (2) Minimally Active (30–60 min/day), and (3) Vigorously Active (≥ 4 h/day), in line with WHO recommendations for physical activity surveillance [40].

Smoking status: We categorized participants as (1) Current Smokers (currently smoking any tobacco product), (2) Ex-smokers (previously smoked but abstinent at the time of survey), and (3) Non-smokers (never smoked). This classification follows standard definition for smoking.

Obesity Obesity was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criterion of body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m². Height and weight were measured using standardized protocols during data collection, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height² (m²) [41].

HbA1c: HbA1c values were obtained from participants’ most recent laboratory test reports at the time of survey. To ensure validity, only reports conducted within the last three months were included. In line with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommendations, glycemic control was classified as controlled (HbA1c < 7.0%) or uncontrolled (HbA1c ≥ 7.0%) [42, 43].

Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables, and averages (± standard deviation) were used to report continuous variables. Univariate analyses with a p-value threshold of 0.25 was applied for variable selection into the multivariate logistic regression. (Supplementary File 1).

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation Factor (VIF) statistics. All tolerance values ranged from 0.826 to 0.999, well above the commonly accepted cut-off value of 0.1, indicating that the variance of each predictor was largely independent of the others. Similarly, all VIF values were between 1.001 and 1.211, far below the conservative threshold of 5 and well under the widely cited critical value of 10. These findings suggest that multicollinearity was not present in our data, and therefore, the estimated regression coefficients in our multiple logistic regression model are unlikely to be adversely affected by collinearity among predictors (Supplementary File 1) [44].

A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to find the variables linked to adherence. Furthermore, an interaction analysis of gender × education × income × regions was done. Variables showing statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the Pearson product-moment correlation analysis were retained in the regression model using the enter method. All analyses were performed at a 95% confidence interval and 5% significance level. IBM SPSS version 26 and R Studio were used for data analysis.

Informed consent

The current study was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki's ethical criteria [34]. All participants provided written informed consent. Full confidentiality and privacy were maintained throughout the study [28]. Participants’ data were handled in compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines [45, 46].

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

Among the 41,095 participants, the majority were male (70.3%), nearly half aged above 50 years (49.3%). Ethnic distribution revealed Punjabis as the largest group followed by Seraiki (20%), Sindhi (16.8%). The majority were married (85.5%) and over one-third had attained at least a graduate degree (37.5%). Employment was reported by 53.8% of the participants, while household income showed that nearly 80% earned above PKR 50,000 monthly. More than two-thirds (70.8%) reported health insurance coverage (Table 1).

Lifestyle and health behavior analysis, most were non-smokers (89.6%). However, physical inactivity was common (49.8%). Almost half had a family history of diabetes and most (70.3%) were diagnosed within the past five years. Hypertension and cardiovascular disease emerged as the most common comorbidities. Notably, over half (53.4%) had uncontrolled HbA1c levels (Table 2).

Factors affecting adherence to antidiabetic medication

The multivariate logistic regression has identifed significant predictors of medication adherence. Gender emerged as a strong predictor, with females significantly less likely to be non-adherent compared to males (AOR = 0.229, 95% CI: 0.067–0.779, P = 0.02).

Ethnic background also played a critical role. Urdu speakers exhibited higher non-adherence (AOR = 1.826, 95% CI: 1.067–3.126, P = 0.028), while Pashtuns showed low non-adherence outcomes (AOR = 0.521, 95% CI: 0.306–0.887, P = 0.016). Marital status was associated with non-adherence, with married individuals more likely to be non-adherent (AOR = 4.118, 95% CI: 3.611–4.695, P < 0.001).

Socioeconomic status further influenced adherence behaviors. Participants with only primary or secondary education showed low non-adherence than those with postgraduate qualifications (AOR = 0.186, 95% CI: 0.166–0.208, P < 0.001). Fewer comorbid conditions and absence of hospitalization were linked to lower odds of non adherence. Similarly, patients taking fewer medications per day and on less frequent dosing schedules demonstrated lower odds of non-adherence. Interestingly, uncontrolled HbA1C levels were associated with higher reduced non-adherence (AOR = 0.817, 95% CI: 0.776–0.860, P < 0.001), suggesting potential discrepancies in self-perception of disease control or limitations in glycaemic monitoring.

Patient perceptions, including fear of medication dependence and concerns about side effects, were not significantly associated with adherence outcomes. Similarly, satisfaction with healthcare providers did not show a meaningful correlation with adherence status.

Interaction effects revealed that male participants with lower educational had significantly higher odds of non-adherence compared to higher educated participants. Furthermore, the protective influence of higher income varied across divisions while low-income groups were disproportionately more likely to report non-adherence.

Discussion

This study highlights the persistent challenge of diabetes patients who do not take their medications as prescribed in Pakistan, with 59.9% of respondents do not follow their recommended oral antidiabetic regimens. This figure aligns with findings from a meta-analysis and systematic review carried out in low- and middle-income nations (LMICs), which stated non-adherence rates of 43.4%, 29.5%, and 29.1%, depending on the adherence scale used (MMAS-8, MMAS-4, or an 80–90% threshold) [47]. Non-adherence to medication is a critical public health issue, as it increases the chance of serious diabetes-related difficulties like cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, and oral health problems, all of which compromise quality of life and escalate healthcare costs. Poor glycaemic control exacerbates these outcomes, resulting in higher rates of preventable diseases, economic losses, and reduced productivity.

The burden of diabetes in Pakistan has grown substantially, with prevalence increasing from 7.9% in 2011 to 30.8% in 2021 [48]. Non-adherence remains one of the contributing factors to this alarming trend. Diabetes also represents a major risk factor for coronary artery disease (CAD) in the Pakistani nation. Data taken from facilities show that 19.8% of diabetic patients suffer from CAD, 6.2% experience cerebrovascular events, 2.1% develop diabetic foot ulcers, and 25% present with retinal complications [49]. These complications tend to increase with age, disease duration, and poor glycemic control, placing significant strain on the healthcare system. However, recent studies suggest that many of these macrovascular complications are linked to modifiable risk factors [50], underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive, patient-centered interventions to promote treatment adherence and mitigate the growing burden of diabetes in Pakistan.

Sociodemographic, cultural, and behavioral influences on adherence

In our cross-sectional analysis a range of socio-demographic and patient-centered factors like gender, ethnicity, and marital status were associated with differences in medication adherence. Females were significantly having fewer possibilities to exhibit non-adherence than males, a finding consistent with a Nigerian study that similarly observed higher odds of non-adherence among males [51]. However, this contrasts with findings from China, where male patients demonstrated better adherence [52]. In the Pakistani context, this disparity may reflect gender-specific roles and responsibilities, although causality cannot be inferred from our data; many women, particularly homemakers, may benefit from more structured daily routines conducive to regular medication use. In contrast, men—especially those engaged in labor-intensive jobs—may face challenges due to irregular schedules, work-related stress, or social stigma associated with taking medication in public.

Although our multistage random sampling aimed to minimize selection bias, the final sample comprised a lower proportion of female participants. This likely reflects underlying gender disparities in healthcare access, clinic attendance, or willingness to participate in research, especially in rural and semi-urban settings. As a result, the observed higher adherence among females may be partially attributable to selection effects, in which participating women represent a more health-conscious or stable subgroup than the general population [53, 54].

Married individuals were also more likely to demonstrate non-adherence, a result echoed in a Brazilian study [55]. The demands of family life, caregiving responsibilities, and competing priorities may be associated with lower attention to personal healthcare in married populations. Conversely, single individuals may have more autonomy and time to dedicate to their health needs. While international studies often report marriage as protective due to increased social support, our findings may be associated with sociocultural realities in Pakistan. Similar findings from LMICs suggest that financial strain, household responsibilities, and gendered caregiving roles can compete with chronic disease management, diminishing adherence [53, 56].

Ethnic disparities in adherence were evident. Urdu speakers showed higher odds of non-adherence whereas Pashtuns demonstrated significantly lower non-adherence. While cultural and family support structures, rural residency, or healthcare access differences, along with dietary habits, and health beliefs, may contribute to these associations, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Ethnic disparities in adherence have been reported elsewhere in Asia, highlighting the complexity of disentangling ethnicity from socioeconomic and contextual determinants [20]. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Socioeconomic and educational factors

Socioeconomic status was associated with adherence outcomes. Surprisingly, individuals with higher educational attainment were more likely to report adherence, a finding also observed in research from Cameroon [57]. This counterintuitive pattern could potentially reflect overconfidence among educated individuals, who might question or modify treatment recommendations based on their own interpretations. In contrast, individuals with lower educational levels may place greater trust in healthcare providers and adhere more strictly to instructions. While this contrasts with much of the global literature, similar findings have been reported in LMICs such as Cameroon [4]. Potential explanations include greater exposure to conflicting health information, occupational stressors, and mistrust in healthcare systems [20].

Unemployment and low-income status were also associated with increased rates of non-adherence, consistent with findings from similar LMIC settings [51]. Financial constraints appear to be a major barrier to consistent medication use, particularly where newer or brand-name drugs are unaffordable or unavailable. Inaccessibility of nearby pharmacies and the cost of transportation can contribute to these challenges. To address these challenges, prescribers should prioritize affordable, locally available medication options and consider integrating prescription subsidies into public health programs [55].

The complexity of these socioeconomic factors is further highlighted by our interaction analyses, emphasize the multifactorial and context-specific determinants of medication adherence. The significant interaction between gender and education indicates that lower educational attainment may compound existing gender-based disparities in adherence, particularly among men. Likewise, the observed variations in the income–region interaction suggest that economic constraints influence adherence differently across geographical areas, with some divisions showing particularly vulnerable low-income populations. These results highlight the importance of designing interventions that are not only gender- and education-sensitive but also focus on regional socioeconomic disparities, to reduce non-adherence.

Lifestyle and health history influences

Our findings also suggest the influence of lifestyle behaviours and personal health history on adherence.

Non-smokers were significantly non-adherent than smokers, which may be attributed to the psychological toll of smoking—known to elevate stress, depression, and anxiety levels [56, 58]. One possible explanation is that smokers, aware of their increased health risks, may perceive a more need to stick with their treatment, whereas non-smokers might underestimate the severity of their condition. It is also important to note that the majority of our sample were non-smokers (89.6%), compared to smokers (10.4%). This imbalance may have influenced the observed association, and more susceptible to residual confounding. Therefore, this result should be interpreted with caution and warrants further investigation in studies with larger smoker populations.

Participants without a diabetes in the family were reported a lower adherence than those with such a history. This observation aligns with a study from Ghana, which found that individuals with first-hand exposure to diabetes complications within their families were more inclined to adhere to prescribed treatments [59]. The absence of personal connection to the disease’s severity may contribute to underestimation of its risks and reduced motivation for self-care. Furthermore, while participants with comorbid conditions often reported lower adherence—potentially due to treatment complexity—those with only one comorbid condition showed better adherence. Patients without a recent hospitalization were also more likely to report adherence, possibly due to greater stability in their daily routines and health status.

Medication regimen complexity and treatment perceptions

Simplicity of treatment regimen was positively associated with adherence. Participants taking fewer medications or following less frequent dosing schedules demonstrated higher adherence, consistent with existing evidence [59]. Approaches such as digital methods like mobile reminders and medication tracking applications could be explored to address forgetfulness and enhance adherence. An interesting finding was that patients with uncontrolled HbA1c reported higher adherence compared to those with controlled levels. Several possible explanations for this association exist. HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over the period of 2–3 months and is influenced by factors beyond medication adherence, including dietary practices, physical activity, comorbidities, and disease progression. In some cases, patients may remain hyperglycemic despite adherence due to advanced disease or poor lifestyle management or patients may aware of poor glycemic control and become more motivated to adhere closely to their prescribed regimen, while those with well-controlled HbA1c might demonstrate relatively lower adherence. Finally, the reliance on self-reported adherence introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, which could contribute to the observed discrepancy. Similar findings have been noted in prior research, highlighting that medication adherence does not always guarantee optimal glycemic outcomes [60, 61].

Notably, patients’ perceptions regarding side effects, fear of medication dependence, and satisfaction with physicians were not significantly associated with adherence in this population. These findings suggest that structural and behavioral factors may play a more prominent role in influencing adherence than subjective perceptions in the Pakistani context.

Although satisfaction with healthcare providers has been associated with adherence in other contexts [19], it was not a significant factor in our analysis. This discrepancy may reflect limitations in measurement (single-item binary variable rather than validated scale) or the influence of broader systemic barriers such as overcrowded clinics, short consultation times, and fragmented follow-up care [15].

Comparative and regional perspectives

Our results are consistent with findings from other South Asian countries, where socioeconomic disparities, cultural practices, and systemic gaps hinder effective chronic disease management [39, 62]. Successful community-based interventions in similar settings—such as peer counseling and family engagement—could serve as models for Pakistan. However, their implementation must be tailored to the country's sociocultural context to ensure acceptability and effectiveness.

Mobile health (mHealth) interventions, including SMS reminders and teleconsultations, represent promising, scalable solutions for enhancing adherence [34, 63, 64]. Previous studies from LMICs have reported a 20–30% reduction in non-adherence following mHealth implementation [34, 39]. However, successful integration in Pakistan requires careful consideration of barriers such as variable digital literacy, inconsistent internet coverage, and affordability, especially in rural areas. Given the widespread penetration of mobile phones in Pakistan, integrating mHealth into national chronic disease programs could revolutionize diabetes management.

Our interaction analyses underscore the multifactorial and context-specific determinants of adherence. The gender–education interaction suggests that limited education exacerbates adherence disparities, particularly among male. Similarly, income–region interaction shows that economic barriers, with certain divisions showing especially vulnerable low-income groups.

Implications for research and clinical practice

The results of this research have direct implications for tailoring adherence interventions in Pakistan. For instance, the lower adherence observed among Urdu-speaking patients and those with higher education suggests that conventional education-focused strategies may not suffice, and that interventions should address attitudes, trust in healthcare providers, and culturally specific beliefs about disease and treatment. Although resource constraints limit access to specialized counseling services, physicians can enhance adherence by guiding patients toward reliable sources of information, such as community pharmacies, health education programs, and credible digital platforms [33, 34, 65].

Digital health tools offer potential but should be piloted cautiously, with attention to barriers such as limited digital literacy, inconsistent internet coverage, and affordability in rural areas. As non-adherence contributes to complications, premature mortality, and healthcare costs [66, 67] customizing interventions to individual patient profiles is critical [34, 62, 68]. Future research should adopt more robust and complex study designs integrated with artificial intelligence [69] to investigate causal relationships and evaluate targeted strategies for improving adherence.

Limitations

There are various limitations of this study. First our reliance on self-reported adherence using the SEAMS tool could be subject to recall and social desirability bias, possibly leading to overestimation of adherence. This limitation may have introduced some misclassification and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Nonetheless, the SEAMS scale was chosen due to its validated use among populations with low literacy and was complemented by self-reported pill-count data to enhance reliability. Furthermore, the apparent discrepancy between SEAMS (73.9% reporting high confidence) and pill counts (59.9% classified as non-adherent) reflects a recognized ‘confidence–behavior gap.’ SEAMS measures perceived self-efficacy, while pill counts approximate actual adherence [13, 15, 64].

Another important limitation is the exclusion of Gilgit Baltistan. Due to lack of accessible healthcare facility data and logistical constraints, this region could not be covered, and thus our findings cannot be generalized to the entire federation. While, more objective measures such as pharmacy refill data or electronic monitoring could improve accuracy, they were not feasible given the study's national scale and resource limitations.

Second limitation is that we did not weight the sample distribution against underlying population sizes. This may have biased national estimates toward the characteristics of overrepresented provinces such as Punjab. Future studies should consider using weighted analyses based on national census data to enhance representativeness.

Another limitation of our study is the exclusion of patients with a history of cancer and severe disability. This decision was made to reduce confounding, as cancer diagnosis and treatment may alter adherence behaviors and health priorities. Similarly, severe disabilities can add confounding that make it challenging to determine the actual impact of non-adherence on treatment results. However, this exclusion may restrict the generalizability of our findings to the wider population of patients with T2DM who also have major comorbid conditions and malignancies. The study sample included a smaller proportion of female participants, which may limit the generalizability of the observed gender differences in medication adherence. Future research with more balanced gender representation is needed to confirm these findings. While we controlled for potential confounders using multivariate logistic regression and explored effect modification through interaction analyses. Nonetheless, residual confounding may persist, further studies are needed to explore these associations Also future research should closely examine these relationships and other potential influences on adherence, such as beliefs about the severity of the disease, and the quality of provider-patient communication. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study offers solid, nationally representative insights into the multifactorial dimensions of medication adherence among patients with diabetes in Pakistan.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study the rate of non-adherence was alarmingly high (60%) and is shaped by several distinct factors related to demographic, socioeconomic, behavioural, and clinical characteristics. Adherence patterns varied by gender, ethnicity, marital status, education, comorbidities, and hospitalization history, underscoring the importance of patient centred and culturally sensitive strategies. Notably, those with uncontrolled HbA1c individuals reported higher adherence highlights the need to explore patient perceptions of disease severity and motivation, suggesting that behavioural and psychological factors warrant closer investigation.

From a policy and practice perspective, interventions must balance ambition with feasibility. While multidimensional strategies are ultimately needed, an immediate priority should be strengthening physician and pharmacist counseling and providing structured, credible health education during routine care. Low-cost, low-technology solutions such as SMS reminders could be piloted in urban areas before broader rollout, whereas resource-intensive approaches (e.g., large-scale digital platforms or peer-support networks) may be longer-term goals once foundational systems are in place. By aligning interventions with identified predictors of non-adherence and the realities of Pakistan’s health system, these findings can inform pragmatic steps to improve diabetes outcomes while laying the groundwork for more comprehensive strategies in the future.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, immediate efforts should focus on strengthening patient-centered counseling during routine clinical encounters, with physicians and pharmacists prioritizing groups at higher risk of non-adherence, including married patients, Urdu speakers, and those with higher education. Given the high non-adherence rate observed, immediate steps should include: routine adherence screening during OPDs using validated short tools (e.g., SEAMS, MMAS-8); training frontline healthcare staff for targeted counseling; strengthening medicine supply chains; and establishing peer support groups in primary healthcare facilities. Impact should be monitored through DHIS2 integration, facility audits of refill rates, and HbA1c outcomes, alongside pilot implementation projects. Integrating brief lifestyle education on diet and exercise into every visit can address modifiable behavioral factors, while simplification of medication regimens, clear dosing instructions, and visual aids can reduce treatment complexity, particularly for patients with comorbidities or multiple medications. In urban settings, low-cost digital solutions such as SMS reminders or basic mobile notifications can support adherence by addressing forgetfulness and reinforcing daily dosing.

Solution: The use of mobile health (mHealth) technologies presents a scalable and promising solution to improve adherence. Subgroup-specific tailoring is essential: SMS reminders could support married individuals facing competing family responsibilities; smartphone applications could be used to counter misinformation among educated but non-adherent groups; and voice-based reminders in local languages could be deployed for rural and semi-literate populations. Such targeted approaches, informed by our findings, would maximize effectiveness and cultural acceptability of mHealth interventions.

Community and governmental roles are critical for extending support to socioeconomically disadvantaged populations through subsidized medication programs, community-based counseling, and targeted follow-up for patients with uncontrolled HbA1c or recent hospitalization, ensuring that vulnerable groups receive focused interventions. Over the longer term, scalable mHealth platforms and teleconsultations can be piloted once foundational strategies are established, while government-led initiatives using patient-level adherence data can inform national programs, prioritize resource allocation, and develop policies that align with the social, cultural, and economic realities influencing adherence. Given resource constraints, prioritization is critical. Based on available evidence, peer counseling and community-based support groups should be prioritized as the most feasible and culturally acceptable first-line interventions. School-based health programs are essential for long-term preventive impact, while mHealth should be introduced incrementally with low-tech solutions (e.g., SMS reminders) before transitioning to complex digital platforms.

By coordinating the responsibilities of healthcare providers, patients, communities, and policymakers, these tiered strategies offer a pragmatic, stepwise approach to improving medication adherence and ultimately enhancing diabetes outcomes across Pakistan.
