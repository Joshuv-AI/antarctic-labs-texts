Iron–aluminum co-regulation of elemental partitioning and organic matrix remodeling shapes leaf chemical composition and quality traits in tea
Authors: Huan Li, Zhenmin Hu, Zeyi Ai, Yiyang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea-leaf quality depends strongly on its chemical composition, which is influenced by both environmental metal availability and internal elemental redistribution. Under acidic growing conditions, iron (Fe) and aluminum (Al) frequently coexist at elevated levels and may jointly affect leaf chemical architecture and quality-related traits. In a factorial Fe × Al hydroponic experiment, we quantified cross-organ partitioning using elemental concentrations, translocation factors, and whole-plant partitioning metrics, and characterized leaf organic matrix composition by FTIR-derived indices for proteinaceous, aromatic, lipid, and polysaccharide domains. Multivariate and path analyses indicated that Fe—Al interactions altered root ionic competition and long-distance metal mobility, consistently coinciding with leaf matrix shifts. Protein-to-polysaccharide, aromatic-to-polysaccharide, and oxidation indices strongly tracked Fe- and Al-related metrics and were more closely associated with polyphenols, free amino acids, and chlorophyll index than individual elemental concentrations. This study demonstrates the utility of FTIR-based chemical profiling combined with chemometric analysis for linking metal-driven elemental redistribution to quality formation in tea leaves.Keywords: Tea, FTIR spectroscopy, Elemental partitioning, Quality traits

Introduction

Tea (Camellia sinensis) is one of the most widely consumed plant-derived beverages worldwide, valued for its distinctive chemical composition that underpins sensory attributes, nutritional quality, and potential health benefits (Kochman et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023). Key quality-related constituents of tea leaves include polyphenols, free amino acids, pigments, and structurally diverse organic matrices composed of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and phenolic polymers. In tea-quality evaluation, polyphenols are broadly related to astringency and body, free amino acids to freshness and umami-like taste, and chlorophyll status to leaf greenness and the physiological background that can influence processing suitability. The relative abundance and organization of these components largely determine tea quality as a food raw material and are known to vary with growing conditions and chemical background factors.

In acidic agroecosystems where tea is predominantly cultivated, metal elements constitute an important chemical background influencing leaf composition. Iron (Fe) is an essential redox-active element involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis and primary metabolism (Kobayashi & Nishizawa, 2012; Rodríguez-Celma & Schmidt, 2013), whereas aluminum (Al), although not essential, is frequently abundant and chemically reactive under acidic conditions (de Silva et al., 2016; Fang et al., 2014; Ruan et al., 2006). Tea plants are notable for their tolerance to Al and capacity to accumulate it without severe toxicity, making tea an informative system for investigating how metal-rich environments influence food raw-material chemistry (Dong et al., 2001; Karak et al., 2015; Morita et al., 2008). Accordingly, the Fe—Al interactions described here should be interpreted in the physiological background of an Al-tolerant accumulator species and may not fully mirror the response patterns of non-accumulator crops.

Previous studies have shown that Fe or Al availability can affect individual chemical traits of tea leaves, including nitrogen-associated metabolites and phenolic compounds (Fu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2017). However, these effects have typically been evaluated independently, with limited attention to how Fe and Al jointly shape elemental redistribution and organic chemical architecture. From a food chemistry perspective, elemental availability does not act solely through changes in bulk concentrations but also through its influence on chemical partitioning and matrix-level organization. Metal ions compete for binding sites, participate in coordination with oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups, and modify oxidative environments, thereby affecting the relative abundance and conformation of organic domains (Fu et al., 2020; Morita et al., 2008). Consequently, understanding tea quality formation requires an integrative framework linking elemental redistribution with changes in organic matrix composition, rather than focusing on single-element effects alone.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy provides a rapid and reagent-free approach for characterizing organic functional groups in complex food matrices. When combined with chemometric analysis, FTIR-derived indices capture coordinated variation in proteinaceous, aromatic, lipid, and polysaccharide domains and have been successfully applied to the chemical profiling of tea and other plant-based foods (Cai et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2022). Despite this potential, FTIR-based approaches have rarely been used to examine how metal-driven elemental redistribution translates into organic matrix remodeling and quality-relevant chemical signatures.

In this study, we employed a controlled hydroponic system with factorial Fe × Al supply to examine how coordinated metal availability influences elemental partitioning across roots, stems, and leaves, and how these changes are reflected in the organic chemical architecture of tea leaves. By integrating elemental concentrations, translocation factors, whole-plant partitioning metrics, FTIR-based functional-group indices, multivariate analyses, and path analysis, we aimed to (i) characterize metal-induced redistribution patterns, (ii) identify matrix-level chemical responses in leaves, and (iii) link these responses to key quality-related traits. This work provides a chemically interpretable framework for understanding how metal-rich environments shape the composition and quality of tea as a food raw material.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and growth conditions

Two-year-old clonal tea seedlings (Camellia sinensis cv. Longjing Changye) propagated by stem cuttings were obtained from a commercial nursery in Nanjing, China. Immediately after arrival, seedlings were rinsed thoroughly with tap water to remove surface debris, followed by three washes with deionized water. Uniform seedlings (similar height and leaf number) were selected for hydroponic culture. Seedlings were transplanted into plastic turnover boxes (33 × 23 × 10 cm) with lids containing 15 holes, each hole supporting one plant. Each Fe—Al treatment included three biological replicates, with 15 plants per replicate box (n = 45 plants per treatment) (Huang et al., 2021; Ruan et al., 1999). The hydroponic design was selected to minimize soil-derived heterogeneity and secondary mineral interactions, thereby enabling direct control of soluble Fe and Al supply and clearer chemical interpretation of metal-regulated changes in elemental partitioning and leaf chemical composition relevant to tea as a food raw material.

To facilitate acclimation to hydroponic conditions and minimize transplant shock, seedlings were first grown in deionized water for 3 days, with solution replacement every 12 h. Following this, nutrient strength was increased stepwise: 1/8-strength nutrient solution for 7 days, 1/4-strength nutrient solution for 7 days, 1/2-strength nutrient solution for 7 days, and then full-strength nutrient solution until abundant new white roots had developed. Preliminary tests confirmed no visible precipitation under the experimental conditions. All nutrient solutions were continuously aerated using miniature air pumps. The pH of the nutrient solutions was adjusted daily to 4.5–5.5 using 1 mmol L−1 HCl or NaOH, mimicking acidic soil conditions typical of global tea-growing regions. The baseline full-strength nutrient composition was: N 2.0 mmol L−1, P 0.1 mmol L−1, Mg 0.4 mmol L−1, Ca 0.8 mmol L−1, K 1.0 mmol L−1, Al 0.25 mmol L−1, B 10.0 μmol L−1, Mn 1.5 μmol L−1, Zn 1.0 μmol L−1, Cu 0.2 μmol L−1, Mo 0.5 μmol L−1, Fe 6.3 μmol L−1. Once new root systems developed, plants were transferred to Fe—Al treatment solutions.

A full factorial design was implemented with five Fe levels and five Al levels. Fe was supplied as EDTA–Fe (6.3 μmol L−1 in the full-strength solution), and Al as AlCl₃ (0.25 mmol L−1 in the full-strength solution) (Huang et al., 2021). Relative treatment labels (0×, 0.5×, 1×, 1.5×, and 2×) are used throughout the manuscript for readability.

Iron treatments (relative to full Fe): Fe0 = 0× Fe, Fe0.5 = 0.5× Fe, Fe = 1× Fe = 6.3 μmol L−1, Fe1.5 = 1.5× Fe, Fe2 = 2× Fe.

Aluminum treatments (relative to full Al): Al0 = 0× Al, Al0.5 = 0.5× Al, Al = 1× Al = 0.25 mmol L−1, Al1.5 = 1.5× Al, Al2 = 2× Al.

Each Fe—Al combination was assigned three replicate hydroponic boxes. Nutrient solutions were renewed every 5–7 days to prevent Fe oxidation and Al precipitation.

All plants were grown under identical environmental conditions: daylight fluorescent tubes, 1500 lx illuminance, 25 ± 1 °C, 60–65% relative humidity and continuous micro-pump supply. These conditions mimic typical low-elevation controlled environments used in tea physiology studies (Ruan et al., 1999).

Biomass measurements

After 5 months of exposure to Fe—Al treatments, plants were harvested and separated into roots, stems, and leaves. Samples were oven-dried at 65 °C to constant mass and weighed. Dry biomass values were used both for reporting tissue mass and for calculating whole-plant elemental pool partitioning (Sun et al., 2023).

Elemental analysis and stoichiometric indices

Dried tissues were ground to <75 μm using a stainless-steel ball mill (Retsch MM400). Total carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) were quantified using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, Elementar). The C/N ratio was calculated on a mass basis.

For mineral nutrient analysis, ∼0.20 g of powdered tissue was digested using a microwave digestion system (CEM Mars 6) with HNO₃–H₂O₂ (5:1, v/v). Digest solutions were diluted and analyzed with inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP–OES; Agilent 5110). The elements quantified included Al, Ca, Mg, K, Fe, Zn, Cu, and Mn, following established methods for mineral nutrition in Camellia spp. (Dong et al., 2001; Karak et al., 2015).

Translocation factors (TF) were calculated as TFleaf/root and TFstem/root for each element, using concentration ratios. Whole-plant pool partitioning (%) was estimated as (tissue concentration × tissue dry biomass) normalized by the sum across organs, providing an operational approximation of organ pool allocation. Ion-ratio metrics capturing competition/buffering were computed by organ (Al/Ca, Al/Mg, (Ca + Mg + K)/Al, Fe/Al, Al/Fe).

Determination of leaf chemical quality traits

Tea polyphenols (TP%) content was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method (ISO 14502-1), with minor modifications for tea matrices. Leaf powder (40 mg) was extracted with 70% methanol, reacted with Folin reagent, neutralized with saturated Na₂CO₃, and read at 765 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2600). Gallic acid was used as the calibration standard.

Total free amino acids (AA%) were measured following OPA (ortho-phthalaldehyde) derivatization. Leaf extracts were reacted with OPA reagent and measured at 340 nm. L-theanine was used for standard calibration, as it is the dominant amino acid in tea leaves (Mao et al., 2022).

Chlorophyll content (SPAD) was estimated non-destructively using a SPAD-502 Plus meter (Konica Minolta). Measurements were taken from three positions along mature leaves and averaged per plant. SPAD has been validated as a reliable proxy for N assimilation and photosynthetic capacity in tea (Sun et al., 2023).

FTIR spectroscopy and functional-group indices

Dried samples were homogenized and mixed with spectroscopic-grade KBr (1:100, w/w). FTIR spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in transmission mode across 4000–500 cm−1, with 32 scans at 4 cm−1 resolution. Spectra were processed using OMNIC 9.0 for baseline correction and vector normalization (Cai et al., 2015; Schulz et al., 1999).

Based on literature-established FTIR biochemical regions, semi-quantitative indices were computed via numerical integration.

Matrix–element coupling was evaluated by regressing FTIR indices against elemental concentrations and selected ratios (e.g., Arom/Poly vs Al; oxidation index vs Al/Fe), with organ-specific fits where appropriate.

FTIR-derived band areas and ratio indices were calculated from the baseline-corrected and vector-normalized spectra. Because vector normalization standardizes overall spectral intensity, comparisons among organs primarily reflect relative differences in biochemical composition rather than absolute absorbance magnitude.

Chemometric and statistical analyses

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on normalized FTIR spectra using singular-value decomposition. Spectra comprised 7209 wavenumbers × 21 leaf samples. PCA scores and loadings were used to identify treatment-driven biochemical shifts, particularly within amide, aromatic, lipid, and polysaccharide regions (Cai, Wang, Xi, Li and Wei, 2015, Esteki et al., 2023).

Partial least squares regression (PLSR) models were developed to evaluate the predictive capacity of FTIR spectra for TP, AA, and SPAD. Five latent variables were selected to maximize cross-validated performance (fivefold CV). Model evaluation metrics included training R2, CV-R2, and RMSECV. Regression coefficient spectra were interpreted to identify functionally meaningful vibrational modes (e.g., 1650 cm−1 for proteins, 1540 cm−1 for amide II, 1515 cm−1 for aromatic rings) (Schulz et al., 1999).

For TF data, a two-way ANOVA (Fe × Al) was conducted, followed by Tukey's HSD for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). Grouping letters were assigned to Fe—Al combinations within each TF response variable. For FTIR-derived indices (Prot/Poly, Arom/Poly, Lipid/Poly, and Oxidation Index), data were analyzed separately for leaf, stem, and root using one-way ANOVA across Fe—Al treatment combinations, followed by Tukey's HSD test (p < 0.05). Results are presented as mean ± SE, and lowercase letters were used to indicate significant differences among treatments within each organ and index. Partitioning and ion-ratio indices were summarized as mean ± SE by treatment to support cross-organ chemical interpretation.

Path analysis was used as a linear covariance-based framework to integrate relationships among metal supply, ion-balance variables, transport indices, matrix descriptors, and quality-related traits. The model was interpreted under the assumptions of linear relationships, additive effects, and independent residual errors, and was used to summarize covariance structure rather than to infer direct causality.

All analyses were performed in R, and final figures were assembled in Origin/PowerPoint following journal-quality plotting conventions. Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using training R2, cross-validated R2 (CV-R2), and RMSECV. Unless otherwise stated, statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Fe—Al co-supply reorganizes cross-organ metal allocation and ionic competition

Whole-plant pool partitioning highlights organ-resolved allocation behavior for Fe and Al

Fig. 1a–b show that Fe and Al pools were consistently root-biased across treatments, but the fraction allocated aboveground varied with supply regime. Under the Al = 1× series, increasing Fe supply altered Fe partitioning such that the leaf fraction increased in selected treatments, while a corresponding reduction in the root fraction was evident in the stacked composition (Fig. 1a). In contrast, under the Fe = 1× series, increasing Al supply elevated the leaf fraction of the Al pool and reduced the relative root retention in the same stacked representation (Fig. 1b), indicating that Al availability can increase its aboveground allocation in tea seedlings under these conditions.

Whole-plant partitioning (%) of elemental pools among root, stem, and leaf tissues in tea seedlings under Fe × Al treatments.

Base cation pools remained leaf-dominant but were not invariant to Fe—Al regime. Fig. 1c–e show that Ca, Mg, and K exhibited high leaf fractions across all treatments, whereas the stem fraction constituted a smaller but responsive component. Notably, several treatments shifted the stem fraction for Mg and K, suggesting that base-cation redistribution accompanies changes in Fe—Al status (Fig. 1d–e). Together, the pool-based view indicates that Fe and Al are primarily handled as root-retained metals, while Ca/Mg/K are delivered to leaves as structural/physiological cations, yet both groups are plastically redistributed when Fe—Al chemistry changes.

Ion-ratio “homeostasis” metrics localize Fe—Al effects to the root interface

Ion ratios extracted from each organ underscored that the strongest Fe—Al leverage occurs in roots. (Fig. 2c) shows that root Al/Ca displayed the largest magnitude and widest separation among treatments, whereas stem and leaf Al/Ca remained comparatively low. A parallel pattern was observed for Al/Mg, with root values dominating the organ comparison (Fig. 2d). These results indicate that Al supply reshapes competitive balance most strongly at the root interface, consistent with roots acting as the primary chemical buffering compartment in this system.

Note: (a) C/N, (b) (Ca + Mg + K)/Al as a composite base-cation buffering index, (c) Al/Ca, (d) Al/Mg, and (e) Al/Fe. Values are presented as mean ± SE for each organ under the different Fe × Al treatment combinations. These ion-ratio metrics are used to illustrate how coordinated Fe and Al supply reshapes ionic competition and buffering status across organs, with the strongest treatment sensitivity generally observed in roots, indicating that the root compartment functions as the major chemical buffering interface in this system.

A complementary buffering index, (Ca + Mg + K)/Al, further emphasized organ differences (Fig. 2b). Root values were higher but also more variable among treatments, while leaf values were lower in magnitude and shifted more modestly, consistent with partial buffering/retention of Al relative to base cations in roots. Finally, Fe/Al (or Al/Fe) ratios captured metal–metal balance. (Fig. 2e) shows that Al/Fe differed markedly among treatments and the divergence was most evident in roots, implying that the effective Fe—Al balance experienced by the plant is set largely by root-level chemistry rather than by leaf composition alone.

TFs quantify mobility differences and demonstrate non-additive Fe × Al control

Translocation factors provided a direct measure of cross-organ mobility and their sensitivity to Fe—Al co-supply. Table 1 reports TFleaf/root and TFstem/root for Fe, Al, and major base cations (Ca, Mg, K), including two-way ANOVA/Tukey groupings. Fe TFleaf/root varied widely across Fe—Al combinations and, in several cases, exceeded unity, indicating substantial leaf delivery relative to root concentration under those regimes. Al TFleaf/root was consistently <1 but shifted significantly among combinations, showing that Al mobility is constrained yet responsive to co-supply context. In parallel, Ca, Mg, and K TFs were generally >1 and exhibited significant groupings, consistent with their role as highly mobile nutritional cations.

Chemical storyline II: Leaf organic-matrix remodeling couples metal handling to quality traits

Leaf multivariate patterns connect mineral status with quality endpoints

The correlation matrix and PCA biplot summarize how mineral and quality traits covary at the leaf level. In the correlation map (Fig. 3a), Fe, Ca, and Zn tended to align positively with each other, whereas K showed strong negative association with Mn and several trace-metal patterns; C/N aligned inversely with N, as expected. Importantly, quality traits did not track a single mineral uniformly: SPAD aligned more closely with N-related and pigment-associated variation, AA clustered near variables reflecting N balance, while TP exhibited a distinct pattern consistent with polyphenol formation being influenced by both nutritional status and broader biochemical allocation.

(b) PCA biplot showing treatment-driven separation among leaf samples based on mineral and quality variables. Colors represent Al supply levels and shapes represent Fe supply levels. The figure illustrates that leaf chemical variation reflects a coordinated mineral regime rather than the effect of any single element alone.

The PCA biplot (Fig. 3b) further separated treatments along PC1 (33.2%) and PC2 (19.3%). Vectors for Al and Ca projected strongly along PC1, while Fe and Zn occupied a neighboring region, suggesting that the major axis of leaf variation captured a combined mineral regime rather than isolated Fe or Al effects. Quality vectors (SPAD, AA, TP) occupied different orientations relative to mineral vectors, reinforcing that mineral supply reshapes leaf chemistry in a multidimensional manner rather than as a single “more metal–more quality” relationship.

Multi-organ FTIR indices reveal organ-specific biochemical domains, with modest within-organ treatment variation

Representative normalized FTIR spectra across organs (Fig. 4a) showed clear organ-level differences, with prominent variation in the polysaccharide C—O region and amide/aromatic bands among leaf, stem, and root. Semi-quantitative FTIR indices further illustrated distinct baseline differences among organs. Within individual organs, numerical variation among Fe—Al treatments was observed for Prot/Poly, Arom/Poly, Lipid/Poly, and Oxidation Index; however, these differences were not statistically significant at p < 0.05 (Fig. 4b–4m). Therefore, these within-organ patterns are interpreted here as descriptive or exploratory rather than as statistically supported treatment effects.

Chemometric decomposition of leaf spectra supported these index-level patterns. The leaf PCA (Fig. 4n) separated samples by treatment and was accompanied by loadings (Fig. 4o) indicating that amide and aromatic/polysaccharide regions contributed strongly to the separation. PLSR coefficient profiles (Fig. 4p) further showed that spectral regions associated with proteins/amide and aromatic/polysaccharide vibrations contributed to prediction of AA and SPAD more strongly than to TP (as reflected in the displayed model fit metrics), consistent with AA and SPAD being more directly tied to N-rich biochemical domains.

Matrix–element coupling resolves selective associations between metals/ratios and organic domains

To test whether metals couple preferentially to specific biochemical domains, we regressed FTIR indices against elemental metrics. Fig. 5 (left panel) shows that Arom/Poly vs Al exhibited organ-stratified behavior: the root fit showed a negative slope with Al, while leaf and stem relationships were weaker or of different sign, indicating that Al associations with aromatic/polysaccharide balance differ between root and aboveground matrices. Fig. 5 (right panel) shows that oxidation index vs Al/Fe increased strongly in roots but remained comparatively flat in leaves, suggesting that the Al—Fe balance is more tightly linked to oxidation-related matrix features in the root compartment than in the leaf matrix.

Organ-specific matrix–element coupling between FTIR-derived indices and elemental metrics in tea tissues. Note: The left panel shows the relationship between Arom/Poly and Al, and the right panel shows the relationship between Oxidation Index and Al/Fe. Each point represents an organ-specific treatment value, with colors indicating root, stem, and leaf tissues. Solid lines indicate linear regression fits for each organ. The regression equation and r2 shown in each panel describe the direction and strength of the corresponding organ-specific fit. These plots illustrate that the association between metal status and organic-matrix descriptors differs among organs, particularly between roots and aboveground tissues.

These organ-specific couplings argue against treating “metal concentration” as a uniform predictor of organic chemistry across tissues. Instead, they support a view in which Fe-related metrics are more informative for protein-associated domains (especially in leaves), whereas Al metrics and metal ratios are more informative for aromatic balance and oxidation-related behavior, particularly in roots and stems.

SEM integrates cross-organ handling, leaf matrix remodeling, and quality endpoints

The structural equation model integrated these relationships into a coherent framework (Fig. 6). On the Al axis, Al supply was associated with higher root Al/Ca and root Al/Fe, and these root competition metrics jointly explained variation in TFleaf/root of Al (Fig. 6). TFleaf/root of Al then related negatively to stem Al partitioning, indicating that regimes promoting Al delivery relative to root retention were accompanied by altered aboveground allocation behavior. Stem Al partitioning was in turn linked to the leaf oxidation index, providing an interpretive statistical link from upstream Al handling to an oxidation-relevant matrix feature. On the Fe axis, Fe supply covaried with the root buffering metric (root (Ca + Mg + K)/Al), which explained TFstem/root of Fe, and TFstem/root of Fe positively explained stem Fe partitioning (Fig. 6). Stem Fe partitioning connected to leaf Prot/Poly, consistent with the idea that Fe delivery/redistribution is coupled to protein-associated domains in leaves.

Structural equation/path model summarizing the relationships among Fe and Al supply, root-level ion-balance metrics, cross-organ metal translocation, stem elemental partitioning, leaf FTIR-derived matrix descriptors, and final leaf quality-related traits in tea seedlings. Note: Boxes represent observed variables included in the final model. Single-headed arrows indicate retained directional paths, and the numbers adjacent to the arrows are standardized path coefficients. Positive coefficients indicate positive associations, whereas negative coefficients indicate inverse associations between connected variables. The model highlights two linked interpretive pathways: an Al-related pathway associated with root ionic competition, Al translocation, stem Al partitioning, and leaf oxidation-related matrix features, and a Fe-related pathway associated with buffering status, Fe translocation, stem Fe partitioning, and leaf protein-related matrix features. The SEM is presented as an integrative statistical summary of the observed covariance structure and should be interpreted as supportive, not as direct proof of causality.

Finally, leaf quality endpoints were connected predominantly through FTIR-defined matrix nodes: SPAD was positively linked with Prot/Poly, while AA and TP aligned with matrix balance terms (including Arom/Poly and oxidation index) with opposite signs, consistent with tradeoffs between N-rich domains and phenolic/aromatic allocation under different co-supply regimes. Collectively, the SEM supports a chemically interpretable chain where root-level competition/buffering constrains transport, stem partitioning captures redistribution, and leaf functional-group remodeling mediates the relationship to quality traits.

Taken together, these results support a chemically interpretable chain in which Fe—Al co-supply restructures elemental partitioning, reshapes leaf organic matrix composition, and thereby modulates quality-related chemical traits.

Discussion

To address the questions raised in the Introduction, the Discussion is organized around three linked aspects of the present dataset: (i) Fe—Al induced redistribution of mineral elements across root, stem, and leaf; (ii) organ- and matrix-level chemical responses reflected by FTIR-derived descriptors; and (iii) the relationship between these matrix characteristics and tea quality-related traits. This structure allows the findings to be interpreted in direct connection with the observed results and existing knowledge on tea mineral nutrition and food-matrix chemistry.

Fe—Al co-supply primarily reshapes elemental redistribution at the root–shoot interface

A central finding of this study is that coordinated Fe and Al supply reshaped the redistribution of mineral elements across roots, stems, and leaves, with the strongest treatment sensitivity located at the root-shoot interface. The partitioning profiles and ion-ratio patterns showed that Al remained predominantly root-retained, whereas Fe displayed greater aboveground mobility under specific Fe—Al combinations. The pronounced variation in root Al/Ca, Al/Mg, and Al/Fe further indicates that the root compartment acts as the major site of ionic competition and buffering under combined Fe—Al supply. These observations are consistent with previous studies showing strong root-level control of Al handling in tea and the importance of Fe transport regulation for aboveground metabolism (Ruan et al., 2006; Kochman et al., 2021; Rodríguez-Celma and Schmidt, 2013). Thus, the present results extend earlier work that examined Fe or Al separately by showing that their combined supply modifies not only element abundance but also cross-organ redistribution behavior in tea seedlings.

Importantly, these redistribution patterns were non-additive, highlighting the interactive nature of Fe—Al chemistry. Rather than acting independently, Fe and Al availability jointly constrained the internal metal background experienced by downstream tissues. From a chemical standpoint, this suggests that leaf metal composition reflects upstream partitioning processes that modulate both elemental pools and relative metal balance (de Silva et al., 2016; Fang et al., 2014; Ruan et al., 2006).

FTIR data mainly reveal organ-specific matrix characteristics, with modest treatment-related variation within organs

FTIR-based analyses revealed clear organ-specific differences in chemical matrix characteristics among leaf, stem, and root, whereas treatment-related differences within individual organs were comparatively modest in the present dataset. In particular, the FTIR index panels (Fig. 4b-4m) showed numerical variation among Fe—Al treatment combinations, but these differences were not statistically significant at p < 0.05. Therefore, the main contribution of the FTIR indices in this study is not to demonstrate strong treatment separation within each organ, but to describe organ-level differences in proteinaceous, aromatic, lipid, polysaccharide, and oxidation-related domains. Therefore, the FTIR indices are discussed primarily as descriptors of organ-specific matrix characteristics and exploratory compositional tendencies rather than definitive treatment-separation markers. At the same time, multivariate FTIR analysis, together with matrix-element coupling results, suggests that these matrix descriptors remain chemically informative when interpreted in relation to elemental redistribution patterns rather than as standalone treatment-response variables (Fu et al., 2020; Morita et al., 2008).

Notably, similar FTIR-derived indices measured in roots and stems exhibited distinct response patterns, underscoring the organ-specific nature of metal–matrix interactions. While roots primarily reflected metal retention and ionic competition, leaf matrices appeared more sensitive to the integrated outcome of redistribution and redox-related constraints. This organ-level differentiation supports the interpretation that chemical signatures observed in leaves emerge from cumulative upstream processes rather than from local elemental concentrations alone.

Matrix-level descriptors help explain variation in tea quality-related traits

The third question posed in the Introduction was whether matrix-level chemical descriptors are meaningfully linked to tea quality-related traits. The present results support this interpretation: in the multivariate analyses and SEM framework, quality-related variables such as SPAD, free amino acids, and polyphenols were more closely aligned with FTIR-derived matrix descriptors than with any single elemental concentration alone. In particular, protein-associated spectral domains were linked to SPAD and amino-acid-related variation, whereas aromatic and oxidation-related descriptors were associated with polyphenol-related patterns (Cai et al., 2015; Mao et al., 2022).

This interpretation is consistent with previous spectroscopic studies showing that tea quality cannot be adequately captured by individual chemical constituents alone, but instead reflects coordinated variation in multiple biochemical domains. Our results extend this idea by placing those matrix descriptors within a metal-regulated redistribution context.

From a food chemistry perspective, these results emphasize that quality formation in tea leaves is better represented by coordinated changes in organic matrix composition than by single chemical constituents. FTIR-based profiling therefore offers a practical and non-destructive approach for linking complex chemical backgrounds to quality-relevant outcomes (Karak et al., 2015; Ruan et al., 1999).

An integrative interpretation of the transport–matrix–quality linkage

The SEM does not provide direct mechanistic proof, but it offers an integrative statistical summary of the observed relationships among metal supply, root-level ionic competition, element translocation, matrix descriptors, and quality-related traits. In this framework, root ion-balance variables captured the upstream effects of coordinated Fe—Al supply, stem partitioning represented an intermediate redistribution step, and FTIR-derived matrix variables provided the closest link to leaf quality-related endpoints. This interpretation is consistent with the overall structure of the present dataset and supports the view that tea quality formation under variable Fe—Al supply is better understood as a transport-matrix-quality chain than as a direct response to single-element concentrations alone. Accordingly, the SEM is discussed here as an interpretive model of the observed covariance structure rather than as evidence of direct causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that coordinated Fe—Al supply reshapes tea leaf chemical composition primarily through its influence on elemental partitioning and organic matrix remodeling, rather than through single-element effects. By integrating elemental transport metrics with FTIR-derived descriptors, we show that matrix-level chemical variation is associated with Fe—Al redistribution patterns and with key quality-related traits.

Importantly, the results suggest that matrix-level chemical descriptors provide a more informative representation of leaf quality formation than elemental concentrations alone. The combined use of FTIR profiling and chemometric analyses therefore offers a practical and non-destructive approach for assessing chemical quality attributes of tea leaves under variable metal backgrounds. This framework may be extended to other food raw materials grown in metal-rich or acidic environments, supporting quality evaluation and monitoring from a chemical composition perspective.
