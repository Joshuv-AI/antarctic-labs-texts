Strategic locations of white matter hyperintensities associated with cognitive impairment across the cognitive spectrum among rural older adults: a population-based study
Authors: Chunyan Li, Jiafeng Wang, Ziwei Chen, Xiaodong Han, Cuicui Liu, Huisi Zhang, Jie Lu, Yifei Ren, Tao Gong, Yan Wang, Xiaoyu Wang, Qianqian Xie, Tingting Hou, Yongxiang Wang, Lin Cong, Grégoria Kalpouzos, Lin Song, Yifeng Du, Chengxuan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe location of white matter hyperintensities (WMH) is crucial for cognitive impairment. We sought to identify tract-based strategic locations of WMH that are associated with cognitive impairment across the cognitive spectrum from low cognitive function and subjective cognitive decline (SCD) through mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia among rural-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis population-based study included 1253 participants (age ≥ 60 years; 59.06% women) in MIND-China who underwent structural brain MRI in 2018–2020. We used a neuropsychological test battery to assess cognitive function and defined SCD, MCI, and dementia following international criteria. We used the Lesion Prediction Algorithm to segment WMH volumes and the Johns Hopkins University International Consortium for Brain Mapping probabilistic fiber tract atlas to further segment WMH volumes within 11 white matter tracts. Data were analyzed using logistic and linear regression models.ResultsGreater WMH volumes in anterior thalamic radiation (ATR), corticospinal tract (CST), forceps major (FMa), forceps minor (FMi), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), and superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) were significantly associated with dementia (false discovery rate [FDR]-corrected P < 0.05). The receiver operating characteristic curves showed outperformance of WMH volumes in ATR, CST, and FMi than total WMH volume in differentiating dementia from normal cognition. The tract-specific WMH were not significantly associated with SCD and MCI after FDR correction for multiple comparisons. In dementia-free participants, higher WMH volumes in ATR and FMa were significantly associated with a lower memory z-score; and higher WMH volumes in CST, FMa, IFOF, and SLF with a lower z-score of verbal fluency (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsATR, CST, and FMi represent strategic locations where greater WMH volumes were independently associated with poorer cognitive function across the cognitive spectrum in older adults, emphasizing the importance of tract-specific WMH in cognitive phenotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02023-6.Keywords: White matter hyperintensities, Strategic location, Cognitive spectrum, Mild cognitive impairment, Dementia, Population-based study

Introduction

The cognitive impairment in old age presents as a spectrum ranging from normal cognitive aging through subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to a full stage of clinical dementia [1]. Conceptually, SCD is an entity characterized by self-perceived persistent decline in cognitive capacity, despite the absence of evidence for objective cognitive impairment from neuropsychological assessments [2]. MCI represents an intermediate state between normal cognitive aging and dementia, and amnestic MCI (aMCI) and non-amnestic MCI (naMCI) are two common subtypes of MCI, defined depending on whether memory function is impaired or not [3]. Dementia is defined as a clinical syndrome characterized by a range of cognitive and behavioral symptoms (e.g., memory loss and disturbances of language, praxis, and executive function) that are severe enough to interfere with daily life. Thus, dementia is the leading cause of functional disability in older people, which contributes to the tremendous burden of health care system and the aging society [4].

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are known to contribute to various clinical sequelae, such as slow gait speed, mood and neuropsychiatric disorders, and dementia [5]. A systematic review concluded that WMH alone or in combination with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology could independently or additively affect dementia risk [6]. Indeed, a global WMH burden has been well linked with cognitive impairment and dementia [7]. However, a discrepancy between neuroradiological findings and cognitive phenotypes is often observed, such that some individuals with a high WMH burden remain cognitively intact, while others with a relatively low WMH burden exhibit significant cognitive deficits [8]. While cognitive reserve factors (e.g., educational attainment and social engagement) may partly contribute to the discrepancy [9, 10], the locations or topographic patterns of WMH may also play a crucial role [11]. Indeed, a multicenter memory clinic-based study revealed that WMH in strategic white matter tracts were associated with poor function of specific cognitive domains independent of total WMH volume [12]. In addition, previous studies of community-dwelling older adults from Austria and China revealed that high WMH volumes in anterior thalamic radiation (ATR) and forceps minor (FMi) were associated with poor processing speed and low global cognitive function [13, 14]. In line with these reports, data from the multicenter Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) showed that high tract-specific WMH could disrupt neural network function and reduce functional connectivity within default mode network areas [15]. Moreover, a population-based study of adults aged 20–90 years from Germany suggested that tract-specific WMH could accelerate the brain aging process throughout adulthood [16]. These studies suggest that disruption of strategic white matter tracts is a key mechanism through which WMH are linked with cognitive impairment. However, the relationship of tract-specific WMH with cognitive impairment across the cognitive spectrum has not yet been investigated in the general population settings of older adults. This is relevant because the generalizability of findings from clinical-based studies is limited only to the selective patient populations, whereas identifying the strategic locations of WMH for a range of cognitive phenotypes among community-dwelling older people would provide additional insights into the cognitive impairment associated with WMH.

Therefore, in this population-based study of rural-dwelling older Chinese adults, we first assessed the tract-specific WMH volumes based on the Johns Hopkins University International Consortium for Brain Mapping probabilistic fiber tract atlas (JHU-ICBM-tracts) [17]. Then, we sought to identify strategic locations of WMH that were associated with cognitive impairment across the cognitive spectrum. Specifically, we aimed to examine the associations of tract-specific WMH with (1) the cognitive spectrum from normal cognition through SCD and MCI to dementia, (2) subtypes of MCI, and (3) function of various cognitive domains. We hypothesized that WMH in older people were differentially associated with cognitive phenotypes across the spectrum depending on the white matter tracts.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a population-based cross-sectional study and the study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [18]. The study participants were derived from the Multimodal Interventions to Delay Dementia and Disability in rural China (MIND-China) project, as previously reported [19]. Briefly, the MIND-China project targeted people who were aged ≥ 60 years by the end of 2017 and living in 52 villages of Yanlou Town, Yanggu County, western Shandong province in northern China. From March to September 2018, 5765 participants (74.9% of all eligible persons) were recruited for MIND-China. Of these, 1304 participants from 26 villages who were randomly selected from all the 52 villages undertook structural brain MRI scans from August 2018 to November 2020 (i.e., MIND-China MRI substudy). Out of these, 51 participants were excluded due to having insufficient data (e.g., incomplete neuropsychological test data or missing data on AD8 test) necessary for making the diagnosis of their cognitive status (i.e., normal cognition, SCD, or MCI) (n = 21) or suboptimal image quality (n = 25) or failure of extracting WMH volume (n = 5), leaving 1253 participants for analyzing the relationships of tract-specific WMH volumes and cognitive phenotypes across the spectrum (SCD, MCI, and dementia) in comparison with normal cognition. Of these, we further excluded 28 participants with dementia, leaving 1225 dementia-free participants for analyzing tract-specific WMH volumes in relation to function of various cognitive domains. Finally, out of the 1225 participants, we further excluded 270 participants with SCD, leaving 955 participants with normal cognition or MCI for exploring the associations of tract-specific WMH volumes with subtypes of MCI in comparison with normal cognition. Data collection and assessments

The trained medical staff (e.g., master’s and doctoral students in the major of clinical medicine and physicians specialized in neurology) collected data via face-to-face interviews, clinical examinations, cognitive testing, and laboratory tests following a structured questionnaire [19]. The data included demographics (e.g., age, sex, and education), lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity), medical history (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke), use of medications, and APOE genotype. Definitions and assessments of these covariates have been described in detail elsewhere [20, 21].

MRI acquisition and processing protocol

Structural brain MR images were acquired using either the Philips Ingenia 3.0T MR System in Southwestern Lu Hospital or the Philips Achieva 3.0T MR System in Liaocheng People’s Hospital. The main MRI sequences included sagittal 3D sT1-weighted, axial T2-weighted, and sagittal 3D fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. The detailed parameters of these MRI sequences have been reported previously [19], and are provided in Table S1 as well.

The WMH volumes were automatically segmented from 3D FLAIR images in native space using the Lesion Prediction Algorithm (LPA) as implemented in the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST) version 3.0.0 (https://www.applied-statistics.de/lst.html) for the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM). LPA employed a logistic-regression classifier to generate voxel-wise lesion probability maps, which were binarized at a default threshold of 0.5 to obtain WMH masks, as previously reported [22, 23]. All probability maps and masks were visually inspected, with minor manual corrections being made when necessary to ensure sufficient precision for the segmentation and quantification of WMH volumes.

Then, individual WMH maps were realigned to the 3D T1w images with a linear registration, followed by spatial normalization using the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration through Exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) method to align the images to Montreal Neurological Institute 152 (MNI152) standard space (1 mm). The procedure was performed with the SPM 12 software (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Visual checks for the results of the registration process were performed for all participants, and 5 participants were excluded due to failure of registrations.

To determine the tract-specific WMH volumes, the spatially normalized WMH maps were superimposed onto the JHU-ICBM-tracts in the MNI152 standard space. This atlas includes probability maps for 20 fiber tracts, of which, 18 bilateral white matter tracts were integrated into nine regions of interest (ROIs). In addition to forceps major (FMa) and FMi, a total of 11 ROIs were ultimately generated for the analysis. For each specific fiber tract, the WMH map was overlaid on its corresponding probability map, which served as a mask. The probabilities of the voxels within this mask were then summed up across all voxels affected by WMH. When describing the distribution of tract-specific and strategic WMH volumes across the cognitive spectrum, we used the normalized tract-specific WMH volumes calculated as tract-specific WMH volume being divided by the volume of the tract, which was the sum of voxel-wise fiber tract probabilities from the JHU-ICBM-tracts, as previously described [15, 24]. We used the raw tract-specific WMH volumes when investigating the associations of tract-specific WMH volumes with cognitive phenotypes across the spectrum and function of various cognitive domains.

Assessments of cognitive function

We used a neuropsychological test battery to assess cognitive function [3], and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale [25] to assess cognitive changes. We used the Chinese version of Activities of Daily Living (C-ADLs) scale to assess the self-care and instrumental activities of daily living, as previously reported [26]. In brief, the C-ADLs scale included 20 items: 8 items for assessing basic ADLs (bathing, dressing, toileting, feeding, transferring, continence, and brushing teeth) and 12 items for assessing instrumental ADLs (taking a bus, preparing meals, taking medicine, light housework, washing own clothes, cutting toenails, shopping, using the telephone, and managing personal money). Each of the items was rated on a four-point scale: 1 = independent, 2 = performs with difficulty, 3 = requires assistance, and 4 = unable to perform. The total C-ADL score ranges from 20 to 80, with a higher score indicating greater functional dependence. The following four cognitive domains were assessed using different cognitive tests [3, 27]: memory was assessed using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) immediate recall, long-delayed free recall, and long-delayed recognition; verbal fluency was assessed using the Verbal Fluency Test, including animal, fruit, and vegetable categories; attention was assessed with the Trail Making Test (TMT)-Part A and Digit Span Forward test; and executive function was assessed using the TMT-Part B and Digit Span Backward test. In order to better reflect the individual differences, the TMT-Part A or Part B score was calculated as the number of correct displacements divided by the time in seconds spent to complete the test, as used in previous studies [3, 28]. Because a considerable proportion of participants in our study had no formal schooling education (35.35%), some of these persons were unable to complete the TMT-Part A or Part B test within the maximum time (150 s for Part A test and 300 s for Part A test). For these participants, the TMT-Part A or Part B score was calculated as the number of correct displacements divided by the maximum time. These raw test scores were standardized into z-scores using the age- and education-specific means and standard deviations (SDs) derived from all dementia-free participants at baseline in MIND-China, and the composite z-score for a specific cognitive domain was calculated by averaging the z-scores of tests for that domain [27]. Objective cognitive impairment was defined as a z-score ≥ 1.0 SD below the age- and education-specific mean z-score in any of the four cognitive domains [27].

Assessments and diagnosis of SCD, MCI, and dementia

We defined SCD following the principles outlined in the SCD-I recommendations [2]: (1) a consistent self-experienced decline in cognitive ability compared to a previous normal cognitive status; and (2) normal age- and education-adjusted performance on cognitive tests after standardization. The Ascertain Dementia 8-item Questionnaire (AD8) was administered to identify SCD among participants who were free of MCI and dementia, as previously reported [29]. In brief, the self-rated AD8 test consists of eight items, in which subjects were asked whether they had any change in self-perception of cognitive problems in the past year compared with their previous normal state. The self-rated AD8 score was obtained by summing the number of items with the answer “Yes, a change”, with the total score ranging from 0 to 8. A higher AD8 score indicates poorer subjective cognitive ability, and participants with the self-rated AD8 score ≥ 2 but without objective cognitive impairment were classified as having SCD, as previously described [29].

As previously reported [3], we defined MCI and subtypes of MCI following the Petersen criteria that were operationalized in the same way as used in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging: [30] (1) cognitive concern by the subject (according to responses to the three questions of memory problems that the participant experienced in the past year, including difficulty remembering, forgetting what had been planned, and worrying about memory decline), informant or physician (CDR score ≥ 0.5); (2) objective cognitive impairment evidenced in at least one of the four cognitive domains; (3) essentially preserved function of daily activities (from C-ADLs); and (4) absence of dementia, clinically defined according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition (DSM-IV) criteria. However, the final judgment about MCI was based on both neuropsychological test scores and a consensus agreement among neurologists. We classified MCI as amnestic MCI (aMCI) if the memory domain was impaired or non-amnestic MCI (naMCI) otherwise [3].

Dementia was clinically diagnosed according to the DSM-IV criteria following a three-step diagnostic procedure, as described in previous studies [31]. Briefly, trained clinicians and interviewers conducted interviews, routine clinical examinations, and testing to collect data on medical history, global cognitive function, and ADLs and recorded all the information into the structured questionnaires. Then, neurologists who specialized in dementia diagnosis and care reviewed all the data recorded in the first step and gave a preliminary diagnosis for participants who were suspected of having dementia. Finally, the neurologists conducted further face-to-face interviews with participants who were under suspicion of having dementia or who had insufficient data for making a diagnosis of dementia status or their informants, and reassessed their medical history, cognitive status, C-ADLs, and whenever available, neuroimaging data, and a diagnosis of dementia status was made. If there was any uncertainty, a senior neurologist was consulted and a consensus diagnosis of dementia was reached.

Statistical analysis

We presented frequencies (%) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables with skewed distribution. We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess whether the continuous variables of the study sample were from a normally distributed population. The characteristics of study participants by cognitive status were compared using chi-square test for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the correlation between WMH volumes when the data did not meet the assumption of normal distribution. Multinomial logistic regression models were used to examine the associations of WMH volumes with cognitive phenotypes across the spectrum from normal cognition (reference) through SCD and MCI to dementia as well as with the subtypes of MCI. We used linear regression models to examine the relationship of WMH volumes with cognitive z-score. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity between independent variables, with the VIF value ≥ 5 indicating the presence of potential multicollinearity [32]. Because additional adjustment of hypertensions and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol intake) did not materially alter the results, we presented the main results from the models that were only controlled for demographic factors (age, sex, and education). We used the false discovery rate (FDR) method proposed by Benjamini and Hochberg to adjust for multiple comparisons, with the significant FDR P-value control being at < 0.05 [33]. Briefly, our analysis for each of the cognitive outcomes involved tests of 12 hypotheses (i.e., 11 white matter tracts plus total WMH volume). The 12 raw P-values were ranked in descending order; then, we assigned a rank (denoted as “i”) to each raw P-value based on its position in the sorted list, with the smallest P-value getting the rank of 1. We calculated the FDR-corrected P-value (i.e., PFDR) using the formula: PFDR(i) = P-value(i) × (12/i). In addition, we used receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis to assess the ability of tract-specific WMH volumes in differentiating between cognitive states. Delong’s tests were used to compare the areas under the ROC curve (AUC) between two ROC curves.

We used IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and Stata Statistical Software: Release 15 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC) for all analyses. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

Of the 1253 participants, the median age was 69 (IQR: 66–72) years, 59.06% were women, and the median years of formal schooling were 3.00 (IQR: 0–6) years. Of these, 270 (21.55%) were defined with SCD, 286 (22.83%) with MCI, and 28 (2.23%) with dementia. Among the 286 participants with MCI, 243 (84.97%) were classified as having aMCI and 43 (15.03%) as having naMCI. Compared to participants with normal cognition, those with SCD, MCI or dementia were older, less educated, more likely to be women, and less likely to smoke and drink alcohol. Additionally, individuals with SCD, MCI or dementia had higher total WMH volume, a higher CDR score, and a higher C-ADL score. Participants with SCD and MCI had lower cognitive z-scores in memory, verbal fluency, attention, and executive function than those with normal cognition (all P < 0.05, Table 1). Participants across the cognitive spectrum did not differ significantly in median BMI and in the distribution of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and APOE ε4 allele (all P > 0.05, Table 1).

Figure 2 A displays the topographic distribution of WMH by lesion probability maps. WMH were detected in all participants. The overall distribution pattern of WMH was highly symmetrical between the left and right hemispheres, with a high prevalence being in the periventricular and deep white matter areas. As for the tract-specific WMH, the highest normalized WMH volume was detected in ATR region (median = 2.30; IQR = 1.32–3.73), followed by FMa region (1.92; 1.03–3.51) (Fig. 2B). In addition, WMH volumes in the 11 white matter tracts and the total WMH volume were significantly correlated (Fig. 2C).

Associations of tract-specific WMH volumes with cognitive phenotypes across the cognitive spectrum

Out of the 11 white matter tracts, higher WMH volumes in ATR, corticospinal tracts (CST), FMa, FMi, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), and SLF temporal part (tSLF) were significantly related to the multivariable-adjusted OR (95% CI) of 1.09 (1.03–1.15), 1.13 (1.05–1.21), 1.12 (1.02–1.24), 1.36 (1.13–1.64), 1.09 (1.02–1.17), 1.03 (1.01–1.06), and 2.14 (1.06–4.34), respectively, for dementia. In addition, higher total WMH volume was significantly related to multivariable-adjusted OR of 1.008 (95% CI 1.00-1.01) for dementia. After the FDR correction, all the associations, except tSLF, remained statistically significant (FDR-corrected P < 0.05, Table 2).

We further evaluated the performance of the total and tract-specific WMH volumes to differentiate dementia from normal cognition. The AUC values were significantly or marginally improved when adding WMH volume in CST, FMa, FMi, or ATR to the basic model that included age, sex, and education (P ≤ 0.05). The WMH volumes in three of the four tracts (i.e., ATR, CST, and FMi) showed significantly or marginally higher AUC values for differentiating dementia from normal cognition than the total WMH volume (Table S2).

The total and tract-specific WMH volumes were not significantly associated with SCD and MCI, and the uncorrected and FDR-corrected P values were provided in Table 2. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in differentiating MCI from normal cognition between the tract-specific WMH volume and the total WMH volume (Table S2).

In addition, the WMH volumes in the five identified strategic white matter tracts (i.e., ATR, CST, FMi, IFOF, and SLF) increased with the advanced cognitive impairment across the spectrum (P for trend < 0.05). Participants with SCD, MCI or dementia (vs. normal cognition) had higher WMH volumes in the five white matter tracts, except that people with SCD had no significantly increased WMH volume in FMi. Finally, participants with dementia had higher WMH volumes in four of the five white matter tracts, except SLF, than those with SCD, and higher WMH volumes in FMi than those with MCI (Fig. 3).

Additional analysis

We conducted additional analysis by further controlling for hypertension, smoking, and alcohol intake, which did not materially affect the main results regarding the associations of the total and tract-specific WMH volumes with cognitive outcomes across the clinical spectrum (Tables S3-S4) and with cognitive function (Tables S5).

Discussion

In this population-based study of rural Chinese older adults, we found that (1) WMH in the periventricular and deep white matter regions were highly prevalent, with ATR showing the highest WMH volume; (2) the associations of WMH with cognitive outcomes across the spectrum depended on anatomical locations, such that higher WMH volumes in ATR, CST, FMa, FMi, IFOF, and SLF and the total WMH volume were cross-sectionally associated with dementia, and that the WMH volumes in ATR, CST, and FMi outperformed in differentiating dementia from normal cognition than the total WMH volume; and (3) among dementia-free older adults, higher WMH volumes in ATR and FMa were associated with lower memory function, and higher WMH volumes in CST, FMa, IFOF, and SLF were associated with poorer verbal fluency. Taken together, our study revealed that ATR, CST, and FMi were the potential strategic locations where high WMH volumes appeared to be associated with poor function across the cognitive spectrum in older adults.

Most studies in the current literature that investigate the associations of regional WMH with cognitive outcomes have quantified WMH volume using relatively coarse anatomical parcellations (e.g., periventricular or deep or subcortical WMH, or regional WMH by cerebral lobes) [34, 35]. These classifications may overlook the complexity of white matter anatomy that is structured by fiber tracts, leading to a loss of sensitivity of detecting the location- or tract-specific WMH in association with cognitive phenotypes. Therefore, quantifying tract-specific WMH volumes could provide further insights into distinct cognitive phenotypes associated with tomographic patterns of WMH. Our study revealed that greater WMH volumes in ATR and FMi were associated with poorer memory function and higher likelihood of dementia in older people. This is largely in line with the previous reports from the memory clinic-based studies and the community-based studies of older people, which showed that higher WMH volumes in ATR and FMi were associated with poorer function in various cognitive domains including memory [12, 13, 36–38]. The fibers in ATR connect anterior and dorsomedial thalamic nuclei with the frontal and cingulate cortices, thus, participating in prefrontal-subcortical circuits [39]. Anterior thalamic nuclei bidirectionally connect with fornix, frontal, and cingulate cortices, forming part of the Papez circuit for episodic memory. The dorsomedial nucleus receives input from basal ganglia, amygdala, and hypothalamus, and projects to the prefrontal cortex. Thus, lesions involving in these nuclei could be linked with deficits in memory, verbal fluency, and executive function [12]. The FMi, a commissural tract that participates in frontal-subcortical circuits, primarily integrates higher-order cognitive information from both hemispheres, thus supporting the processing of higher cognition anteriorly [40]. Additionally, given that WMH volumes in ATR and FMi have been strongly correlated with brain atrophy, neurodegeneration secondary to damaged fiber tracts may also underlie the association of WMH volumes in ATR and FMi with cognitive impairment [36, 41].

Our population-based study further revealed that higher WMH volume in CST was associated with dementia and poorer function of memory and verbal fluency, which was partially in line with prior community-based studies of older adults that related higher WMH volume in CST to poorer processing speed and memory function [37, 42]. Moreover, diffusion tensor imaging studies showed that reduced white matter microstructural integrity in CST was associated with various poorer cognitive functions [43, 44]. Given that CST is known to be involved in regulating sensorimotor function, this finding is plausible due to the fact that motor component is a part of the applied neuropsychological tests [11]. In addition, a study within ADNI showed that the reduced white matter integrity in CST was correlated with lower hippocampal volume, which may partly explain the association of WMH volume in CST with poor function of memory [45].

We found that higher WMH volume in FMa was associated with dementia and poorer cognitive functions. The AD-associated pathologies may potentially underlie the observed associations because posterior white matter alterations (e.g., splenium of the corpus callosum) were associated with both neurodegeneration and Aβ burden [46, 47]. In addition, our study indicated that higher WMH volumes in IFOF and SLF were associated with dementia and poorer function of verbal fluency. The IFOF is crucial for the ventral intra-hemispheric transfer of information between frontal cortex and occipital, temporal, and parietal cortices [48], and previous research linked greater WMH volume in the tract with reduced functional connectivity in IFOF-connected default mode network in AD [15]. SLF tract connects frontal, parietal, and temporal association areas, where WMH could reasonably contribute to cognitive impairment [42]. Notably, the effect sizes of the WMH volumes in the IFOF and SLF were relatively small, and the sample size may not be large enough to capture such effects.

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale population-based MRI study to explore the tract-based strategic WMH associated with a range of cognitive phenotypes across the cognitive spectrum in rural-dwelling Chinese older adults, a socioeconomically disadvantaged group that has been substantially underrepresented in research of cognitive aging and dementia [49]. Thus, findings from our study could partly address the knowledge gap between rural and urban populations. However, several limitations in our study deserve a brief discussion. First, the cross-sectional nature of this study does not allow us to infer causality for any of the observed associations. Second, despite the relatively large sample size of the study, WMH were rare in certain functionally relevant tracts (e.g., cingulum hippocampus), which may have limited power to detect their association with cognitive outcomes. Third, due to the fact that MCI was a heterogeneous entity and that there were no reliable biomarkers in our study to ascertain the underlying etiology of MCI, caution was needed when interpreting the lack of associations between WMH and MCI and its subtypes. Finally, the study sample was recruited only from one rural area in northern China, which should be kept in mind when generalizing our findings to broader populations.

In conclusion, our population-based study of rural older adults in China provided evidence that the associations of the cognitive spectrum with WMH volume might vary partially depending on tract-based strategic locations: CST, ATR, and FMi were identified as potential strategic locations where higher WMH volumes were associated with dementia and poorer function of various cognitive domains, emphasizing the importance of specifying topographical locations when exploring cognitive phenotypes associated with WMH. These results implied that WMH in strategic locations may be a potential biomarker for cognitive impairment in older people. From a clinical perspective, exploring topographical aspects of WMH may facilitate the deeper understanding of the cognitive phenotypes associated with global WMH burden, which may further give new insights into preventive or therapeutic interventions to prevent or delay further cognitive deterioration and other clinical complications. Moreover, future prospective cohort studies that integrate conventional structural MRI data with highly sensitive diffusion tensor imaging data that could fully capture white matter microstructural alterations are warranted to further characterize white matter injury in strategic tracts or tomographic locations associated with clinical phenotypes across the cognitive spectrum, and to determine the ability of the tract-specific WMH for predicting cognitive deterioration and the conversion across the spectrum.
