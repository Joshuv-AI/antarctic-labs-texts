Removal of a Fishhook‐Shaped Foreign Body in the Bronchus and Literature Review
Authors: Wan Hu, Fanrui Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTForeign body aspiration, particularly involving complex metallic objects with sharp projections like fishhooks, presents a formidable therapeutic challenge, often leading to complications such as mucosal impaction and obstructive pneumonia. We report a challenging case of a metallic fishhook‐shaped foreign body deeply embedded within the distal bronchial wall, where initial attempts at retrieval using standard biopsy and foreign body forceps via flexible bronchoscopy were unsuccessful due to significant mucosal oedema and impaction. The successful management involved a novel, multi‐modal endoscopic strategy that sequentially integrated the stability of a rigid bronchoscope, the precise navigation of an ultrathin bronchoscope for direct visualisation of the embedded tip and the controlled dissection using a high‐frequency electrocautery knife to safely release the object, followed by its retrieval. This case demonstrates a reproducible, stepwise surgical strategy for the safe extraction of similarly complex, impacted sharp foreign bodies, thereby minimising the risk of iatrogenic injury.Keywords: foreign body aspiration, high‐frequency electrocautery knife, multi‐modal endoscopic, rigid bronchoscope

Introduction

Foreign body aspiration (FBA) represents a significant clinical entity, with a well‐described bimodal age distribution primarily affecting children and the elderly [1]. While the majority of aspirated objects are organic materials such as food particles, inorganic and structurally complex foreign bodies (FBs), particularly metallic ones with sharp projections like fishhooks, are exceptionally rare and pose formidable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [2].

The management of bronchial FBs typically involves endoscopic retrieval, most commonly using flexible or rigid bronchoscopy with an array of forceps, baskets or balloons [3]. Objects with sharp ends or hooks can become deeply impacted in the bronchial mucosa or wall, rendering standard grasping instruments ineffective and increasing the risk of complications such as mucosal laceration, haemorrhage or perforation during extraction attempts [4]. Repeated unsuccessful manipulations can induce significant local mucosal oedema and inflammation, further narrowing the airway lumen and obscuring visualisation, thereby escalating the procedural complexity and risk [5]. This creates a critical clinical dilemma where the foreign body must be removed to prevent long‐term sequelae like bronchiectasis or recurrent infection, yet the extraction process itself carries substantial hazard [6].

This case report describes the successful management of an extraordinarily challenging scenario: a metallic fishhook‐shaped foreign body embedded in the distal bronchial wall. The case illustrates the limitations of standard biopsy and foreign body forceps when confronted with an impacted, sharp object [4]. More importantly, it details an innovative, multi‐modal endoscopic strategy that sequentially integrated the stability and working channel of a rigid bronchoscope, the precise navigation and visualisation capabilities of an ultrathin bronchoscope and the controlled tissue dissection provided by an electrocautery knife to safely disimpact and retrieve the complex foreign body. This report provides a meticulously documented, step‐by‐step technical strategy for handling similar high‐complexity bronchial FBs.

Case Report

A patient, senior male, no significant past medical history, experienced choking while eating fish, reported coughing up fish flesh and a fish bone and subsequently developed a persistent cough that lasted for 1 month. Then he was admitted to the hospital due to a chief complaint of ‘cough for over one month’. An outpatient chest x‐ray revealed a fishhook‐shaped metallic foreign body shadow within the bronchus. Upon admission, a chest CT scan was performed, which confirmed the presence of a fishhook‐shaped high‐density shadow, approximately 20 mm in length, located within the right lower lobe bronchus, consistent with a foreign body (Figure 1A–C). Following completion of preoperative evaluations, the patient underwent an interventional procedure under general anaesthesia utilising a combined rigid and flexible bronchoscopy approach. Initial examination with the flexible bronchoscope under rigid bronchoscopic guidance visualised the metallic fishhook foreign body at the orifice of the basal segments of the right lower lobe (Figure 2A). Attempts to grasp and extract the object using standard biopsy and foreign body forceps were unsuccessful. The distal end of the fishhook was found to be embedded within the bronchial wall and its exact position could not be definitively ascertained (Figure 2B). Repeated manipulation resulted in displacement of the foreign body and oedema of the surrounding bronchial mucosa, leading to luminal stenosis. The procedural strategy was then modified (Figure 2C,D). A rigid bronchoscope sheath (STORZ) with a length of 33 cm and an outer diameter of 14 mm was placed and connected to high‐frequency jet ventilation, with its distal end positioned at the orifice of the right main bronchus. Under direct visualisation and guidance of an ultrathin bronchoscope (outer diameter, 3 mm; FUJIFILM), the foreign body was accurately localised. A rigid foreign body forceps was inserted through the rigid bronchoscope sheath to clamp and fix the proximal end of the foreign body (Figure 2E) and the forceps was stabilised by an assistant to maintain relative immobilisation of the foreign body. A 4.2 mm outer‐diameter bronchoscope (Olympus) was advanced along the fishhook from the proximal to the distal end for exploratory examination, which precisely identified the impaction site of the fishhook tip. The distal end of the fishhook was embedded within the medial mucosa of the lateral basal segment of the right lower lobe; direct forceps extraction would inevitably cause mucosal laceration of the entire basal segment. Subsequently, a high‐frequency electrosurgical device (modified tip of Olympus electrosurgical snare; power: 30 W, cut mode, 1 s per activation) was applied to perform gradual and meticulous dissection and release of the mucosal tissue overlying the distal fishhook (Figure 2F). Complete exposure of the entire fishhook was achieved and the electrosurgical dissection procedure lasted approximately 40 min. Under direct visualisation via the ultrathin bronchoscope, the rigid foreign body forceps firmly grasped and completely removed the intact foreign body (Figure 2G). Postoperative confirmation verified the retrieved foreign body as a size 4 small fishhook (Figure 3). The total duration of the rigid bronchoscopic procedure was approximately 3.5 h due to procedural complexity. Transient intraoperative tachycardia occurred and was stabilised after pharmacological intervention. Shivering was observed during anaesthesia recovery, which was relieved following intravenous administration of 10 mg tramadol. The patient developed postoperative sore throat, which was effectively alleviated with symptomatic analgesic treatment combined with budesonide nebulization.

Discussion

The management of bronchial FBs is a well‐established domain in respiratory intervention, yet complex cases involving impacted, sharp or hooked objects present significant challenges that deviate from standard protocols. Few reports in the literature describe sharp FBs impacted within the bronchial wall. This anatomical entrapment renders standard grasping and pulling techniques ineffective and potentially hazardous, as repeated attempts can exacerbate mucosal oedema and further obscure the operative field, a pitfall well illustrated in our case.

The technical innovation in this report lies in the sequential, multi‐modal endoscopic strategy, which distinguishes it from most documented approaches. However, the synergistic combination of a rigid bronchoscope for a stable conduit and forceful fixation with a rigid forceps, an ultrathin bronchoscope for precise navigation within an oedematous, narrowed lumen to visualise the embedded hook and the controlled application of a high‐frequency electrocautery knife to meticulously dissect the embedded tip from the bronchial mucosa represents a significant procedural advancement. This stepwise, instrument‐specific approach directly addresses the core limitation of conventional methods when faced with a mechanically fixed foreign body, offering a reproducible template for managing similarly complex, impacted sharp objects in the airway [7].

This case highlights two key points. First, a chest x‐ray may suggest the presence of a metallic foreign body; it often fails to delineate its precise three‐dimensional relationship with the bronchial wall [8]. Computed tomography is indispensable for pre‐procedural planning, as it can reveal impaction and guide the selection of appropriate endoscopic tools and strategies [5]. Second, repeated attempts may lead to foreign body displacement, airway mucosal oedema and luminal stenosis [3].

The management strategy of this case involves several key steps and each step poses a challenge to the operator's skills. First, the rigid bronchoscope establishes a secure, stable working channel and allows for the application of sustained, firm traction on the FB's proximal end via a rigid forceps, a fundamental prerequisite for subsequent manoeuvres [9]. Second, the ultrathin bronchoscope, with its superior manoeuvrability in narrowed spaces, is critical for navigating past the oedematous mucosa to achieve direct visualisation of the exact point of impaction, which is often invisible to standard scopes [4]. Third, the use of a high‐frequency electrocautery probe enables a controlled, precise release of the embedded tip with minimal thermal spread to surrounding tissues [10]. Finally, the coordinated teamwork highlights the importance of meticulous intra‐procedural communication for success in such constrained anatomical settings [11].

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that the successful management of a deeply impacted, sharp bronchial foreign body hinges on a comprehensive preoperative assessment, the strategic availability of specialised instrumentation and the execution of a flexible, multi‐stage procedural plan.

As a single‐centre case report, the generalizability of this approach requires validation through larger, multi‐centre experiences. Future comparative studies could explore the relative efficacy and safety of different energy‐based dissection platforms, such as laser, in similar contexts. Nevertheless, this detailed technical account provides a valuable procedural framework and highlights the critical need for tailored strategies when confronting the unique challenges posed by complex, impacted bronchial FBs.
