Exposure to TBBPA and polystyrene nanoplastics induces cardiac fibrosis via the activation of the ESR/H19/Wnt pathway
Authors: Xiang-Yu Pei, Xi-Ran Wang, Shao-Yan Liu, Meng-Die Cheng, Chang-Lei Li, Shuo Li, Hai-Ming Xu, Yin-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac fibrosis is a key pathological driver of heart failure and cardiovascular mortality, with environmental pollutants being increasingly implicated. Tetrabromobisphenol A (TBBPA), a dominant brominated flame retardant (BFR), is environmentally pervasive and poses potential cardiotoxic risks. However, direct evidence for TBBPA-induced cardiac fibrosis in mammals is lacking. Additionally, its ability to adsorb onto nanoplastics, such as polystyrene (PS-NPs), raises concerns about combined toxicity, especially cardiotoxicity. To addressed this, we systematically evaluated the cardiotoxicity of TBBPA and PS-NPs in vivo and in vitro. In mice, environmentally relevant TBBPA exposure impaired cardiac function and induced inflammation and fibrosis, leading to cardiac remodeling, whereas co-exposure with PS-NPs only induced fibrosis. In H9C2 cells, TBBPA and PS-NPs individually promoted the expression of inflammatory, fibrotic, and hypertrophic markers, with co-exposure resulting in a synergistic exacerbation and a remodeling phenotype. Furthermore, transcriptomic and mechanistic data showed that ESR inhibitors blocked the H19/Wnt pathway activation induced by TBBPA or PS-NPs. Through gain- and loss-of-function experiments, we further confirmed that H19 mediates pollutant-induced Wnt/β-catenin activation and fibrotic responses, positioning H19 as a critical downstream mediator of the ESR/H19/Wnt axis. Overall, for the first time, this study elucidates the cardiotoxic profiles of TBBPA and PS-NPs and identifies the ESR/H19/Wnt axis as a key pro-fibrotic mechanism, highlighting a cardiovascular risk and a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: TBBPA, Nanoplastics, Cardiac fibrosis, Estrogen receptor, lncRNA

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) contribute substantially to high morbidity and mortality rates worldwide [1]. Heart failure frequently represents the terminal stage resulting from various CVDs, such as dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), heart valve diseases, and myocardial infarction [[2], [3], [4]]. The key pathophysiological process underlying heart failure is cardiac remodeling, which primarily includes cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis and inflammation [5]. As a precursor to clinical heart failure, cardiac remodeling can progressively deteriorate heart function and ultimately lead to heart failure without timely intervention [6,7]. Cardiac fibrosis, in particular, plays a pivotal role in the transition from compensated remodeling to heart failure [5]. This process is regulated by a complex network of systemic pathways, including the renin–angiotensin system, the sympathetic nervous system, and classic molecular signaling pathways such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and PI3K/Akt pathway [8].

Furthermore, besides genetic predispositions, environmental factors, such as PM2.5, heavy metals and bisphenols (BPs), are recognized as key drivers to the development and progression of cardiac remodeling [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. A key mechanistic link between these pollutants and cardiac pathology may involve nuclear receptors (NRs) [13], such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) and estrogen receptors (ESRs), which have been implicated in this process [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of cardiac remodeling induced by environmental pollutants is imperative for advancing prevention and control strategies against CVDs. Tetrabromobisphenol A (TBBPA), a typical brominated flame retardant (BFR), dominates the BFRs market with a share of approximately 60% [18]. The total production of TBBPA in three major regions, including China, the USA and the Middle East, was approximately 241,352 tons in 2016 [19]. It has been frequently found in the environment, such as soil, rivers, building materials and electronics [20,21]. Related research has shown that TBBPA concentrations in soil varied from 1.64 to 7758 ng/g dry weight [22]. Hence, humans are inevitably exposed to TBBPA through dietary ingestion, respiratory, and skin contact [23]. The concentrations of TBBPA in human plasma range from 1.8 × 10−6 to 1.07 × 10−2 μmol/L, indicating that TBBPA is a potential threat to public health and thus it has attracted much attention from researchers [24]. While TBBPA is known to exert neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, immunotoxicity, endocrine and reproductive toxicity in mice, its observed accumulation in the heart suggests that the heart may also be a potential target for TBBPA toxicity [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Nevertheless, whether TBBPA indeed exhibits cardiotoxicity and induces cardiac fibrosis or remodeling in mammals remains to be elucidated.

Remarkably, exposure to pollutants in the environment does not exist in a single form, and the coexistence of multiple pollutants is more prevalent [30]. Among these pollutants, micro- and nanoplastics (M/NPs) are ubiquitously present in the environment and are considered emergent threats to ecosystems [31]. NPs, defined as plastic particles with diameters less than 1 μm, possess a tiny particle size and unique colloidal properties, enabling them to readily cross biological barriers and translocate to various human organs [32]. Although previous studies have shown that polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) can impair hESC cardiac differentiation, induce myocardial injury and apoptosis, their effects on cardiac fibrosis or remodeling remain unclear [[33], [34], [35]]. In addition, the combined effects of NPs and other pollutants have attracted widespread interest [[36], [37], [38]]. Due to their adsorption capacity, NPs are carriers of organic pollutants, heavy metals and pharmaceuticals [[39], [40], [41]]. NPs can adsorb TBBPA and increase its bioaccumulation and toxicity in lower organisms such as algae and Caenorhabditis elegans, raising a pivotal question for mammalian health: does such co-exposure also lead to cardiotoxicity? Furthermore, although TBBPA is known to bind and activate various NRs in multiple species, the ability of PS-NPs to similarly engage NRs, either alone or upon co-exposure, remains unknown [[42], [43], [44]].

Thus, in this study, we used C57BL/6 mice as an in vivo model and the H9C2 cell line as an in vitro model to assess the effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs on cardiac remodeling. Moreover, RNA sequencing and pharmacological inhibition assays were performed with the dual objectives of profiling the transcriptomic landscape and exploring the molecular mechanisms. Using this integrated approach, our investigation focused on ESRs and their role in activating the downstream Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cardiac remodeling. To our knowledge, this integrative study is the first to report TBBPA-induced cardiac remodeling in mammals and to delineate the transcriptomic landscape of cardiotoxicity caused by TBBPA alone or in combination with PS-NPs in vivo. We are also the first to identify the ESR/H19/Wnt axis as a critical mediator of cardiac fibrosis induced by TBBPA and PS-NPs.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

TBBPA (purity 97%) was obtained from Alfa Aesar (Tianjin, China). A stock solution of TBBPA was prepared by dissolving it in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Solarbio, China) to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL and storing it at −20 °C. The 100-nm diameter polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) suspensions (2.5% w/v, 10 ml) were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China) and stored at 4 °C. The morphology and size of the PS-NPs were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Olympus veleta-2kx2k, Japan), while the zeta potential was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, US). Fulvestrant (ICI 182,780) was obtained from Macklin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Animals and experimental design

Male C57BL/6 mice (5 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g; n = 48) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle for one week to adapt to the environment, with free access to food and water. Approved by the Committee for Animal Experimentation of Qingdao University (No. QDU-AEC-2022366), the experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for animal experimentation.

The mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 12 mice per group): control, TBBPA, PS-NPs and co-exposure (TBBPA + PS-NPs) groups. The body weights of the mice in the different treatment groups measured over the 8-week period are shown in Fig. S1. The dosage of TBBPA was determined with reference to its tolerable daily intake (TDI, 1000 μg/kg/d) recommended by the EU and was set at 1 mg/kg·bw every two days [45]. In addition, the estimated daily intake of microplastics ranges from 14 to 710 mg/kg for adults. Based on the body surface area normalization (BSA) conversion principle, this corresponds to an equivalent dose of 2.87 to 145.9 mg/kg in mice [46,47]. Therefore, we selected a dose of PS-NPs of 50 mg/kg·bw around the median concentration administered every two days. Throughout the experiment, the dose administered via oral gavage was adjusted weekly based on the recorded body weight of the mice. After 2 months, the mice were sedated and euthanized. Cardiac tissue and serum samples were collected for further analysis.

Transthoracic echocardiography

To assess the cardiac function in mice after TBBPA, PS-NPs and TBBPA + PS-NPs exposure, we performed transthoracic echocardiography using a high-resolution Vevo® 2100 imaging system (FUJIFILM Visual Sonics Inc., Toronto, Canada). The cardiac function parameters that were measured included the ejection fraction (EF%), fractional shortening (FS%), left ventricular systolic internal dimensions (LVIDs), interventricular septum thickness in systole (IVSs), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness in systole (LVPWs). The detailed procedure has been described previously [48].

Biochemical analysis

Blood was collected from the cardiac apex and allowed to clot for 1 h at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 15 min. Serum was then collected, and the levels of creatine kinase (CK) and creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB) were measured using a biochemical autoanalyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were quantified using ELISA kits (Reed Biotech, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines were determined by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) and calculating them from a standard curve established using known standards.

Histopathological analysis

Cardiac tissues were washed with saline, preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5–16 μm thick) were then prepared and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, and Sirius Red for microscopic assessment of myocardial morphology and fibrotic changes. For each staining method, five to six images per sample were acquired and analyzed using ImageJ 1.5.1.

Cell culture and cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

H9C2 rat cardiomyoblast cells (Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, China) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (HyClone, UT, USA) containing 10% FBS (Life-iLab, Shanghai, China) in humidified incubators with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The medium was replaced once a day, and the cells were subcultured upon reaching 80–90% confluence. In preliminary experiments, H9C2 cells were treated with TBBPA, PS-NPs and co-exposure to assess the effects of the individual and combination treatments. We treated H9C2 cells with the ESR inhibitor ICI 182,780 at a concentration of 0.1 μM to verify the involvement of the ESR in TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced cardiac fibrosis.

To evaluate proliferative toxicity, cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After adhesion, the cells were incubated with TBBPA (0, 5, and 50 μg/mL) or PS-NPs (0, 5, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 24 h or 48 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Life-iLab, Shanghai, China) was dispensed into each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h prior to measuring absorbance at 450 nm with a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA).

Measurement of the cell surface area

After 24 h of culture in DMEM, the cells were cultured for another 24 h with the following four treatments: control, TBBPA (5 μg/mL), PS-NPs (5 μg/mL), and co-exposure (5 μg/mL TBBPA + 5 μg/mL PS-NPs). We selected a nontoxic dose based on the results of the CCK-8 assay and the concentration range established in previous research [[49], [50], [51]]. Then we selected 5 μg/mL as the working concentration for both TBBPA and PS-NPs, which did not cause a significant reduction in cell viability. This dose was therefore adopted for subsequent experiments to evaluate cardiomyocyte hypertrophic and fibrotic responses, and to explore the underlying signaling mechanisms. After treatment, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 10 min. The cytoskeleton was labeled by an incubation with 100 nM phalloidin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium (Meilunbio, Dalian, China). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon A1 MP, Japan). The surface area of the cells was measured using ImageJ software. Cell size data were normalized to control values.

Transfection

H9C2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight and then were transfected with pcDNA-NC, pcDNA-H19, shRNA-NC or shRNA-H19 using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in the presence of P3000™ reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol. All plasmids were obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China).

RNA extraction and RT‒qPCR

As described previously [49], total RNA was extracted from tissue and cell samples using TRIzol reagent (Life-iLab, Shanghai, China). and the quality of the extracted RNA was verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel containing nucleic acid dye and an A260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 (NanoDrop ND1000, Wilmington, DE, USA). Then, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix with gDNA wiper (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. RT‒qPCR was performed using SYBR quantitative PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (ABI, USA). Primer sequences for the target genes were designed according to NCBI Gene Bank and are listed in Table S1. In addition, GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene to ensure that all the data were normalized using the 2−ΔΔCt method [52].

Immunofluorescence staining

Cardiac tissues from mice were embedded in OCT compound to prepare frozen sections. Sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS for 5 min, and blocked with 5% donkey serum for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-α-SMA (SY02-64, 1:2000), anti-Col1a1 (PSH06-20, 1:200) and anti-β-catenin (SA30-04, 1:100) antibodies. After three washes with PBS each for 5 min, sections were incubated with fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies (HA1121, 1:500) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark and then washed with PBS three more times for 5 min each. All primary antibodies used were rabbit-derived and were purchased from HUABIO (Hangzhou, China). Finally, anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI was added, and the sections were observed immediately and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

In vitro, H9C2 cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked as described above. Immunostaining was performed using the same antibodies and imaging procedures.

Western blot

Protein extraction and BCA quantification of H9C2 cell lysates were carried out following previously established procedures [53]. Protein samples (20–30 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE (Epizyme, China) and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h, and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies of anti-β-catenin (1:2000), anti-Myc (1:10000), anti-Axin (1:5000), anti-Col1a1 (1:1000), anti-Col3a1 (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:100000, as internal reference). The membranes were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary goat anti-mouse antibody (1:10000) or goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:40000). Anti-Myc was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), and anti-Axin was sourced from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). And all other antibodies were supplied by HUABIO (Hangzhou, China). Finally, protein bands were detected using a ECL detection reagent kit from YEASEN (Shanghai, China), and gray values were analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Cardiac tissue samples (30 mg) from five mice in each group were submitted to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), for RNA sequencing. RNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform, followed by transcriptome assembly and annotation, which were performed according to the company's standard protocols. In brief, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and mRNA was enriched with Dynabeads Oligo (dT). The purified mRNA was then fragmented and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries with an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system in a 2×150 bp paired-end (PE150) configuration. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with |fold change| ≥ 2 and p < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) employed were performed with the OmicStudio platform (accessible at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). The associations between DEGs and cardiovascular diseases were predicted by calculating inference scores using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database).

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality was tested using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed with Student's t-test, while multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1). Significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. The symbol “#” was used to denote significant differences between the two treatment groups.

Results

Characterization of PS-NPs incubated with or without TBBPA

To characterize the physicochemical properties of the PS-NPs, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and zeta potential analysis. TEM revealed that the PS-NPs were uniform spheres with an average diameter of 100 nm (Fig. 1A and B). The incubation with TBBPA did not significantly alter the particle size of the PS-NPs (Fig. 1C).

In addition to particle size, we measured the zeta potential of the PS-NPs and TBBPA + PS-NPs solutions, and their values were −33.77 mV and −3.51 mV, respectively (Fig. 1D). The zeta potential, an indicator of particle aggregation and dispersion stability, is considered unstable between −30 mV and +30 mV [51]. The substantial decrease in the absolute zeta potential following the incubation with TBBPA suggested that the stability of the solution system was decreased. Besides, a study by Shen et al. demonstrated that TBBPA can adsorb onto PS-NPs, which reduces the bioaccumulation of TBBPA [50]. Based on these findings, we speculate that the change in zeta potential may result from the adsorption of TBBPA onto PS-NPs, potentially mediated by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and van der Waals forces [54]. The adsorption of TBBPA onto the PS-NPs neutralized the negative charges on the PS-NPs surface, leading to a decrease in the absolute zeta potential and reduced stability.

Co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs triggered cardiac fibrosis in vivo, while TBBPA further induced cardiac remodeling

To determine whether TBBPA and/or PS-NPs exposure could affect cardiac function, transthoracic echocardiography was performed on treated mice. Compared with the control group, the TBBPA group showed significant decreases in EF% and FS% by approximately 27.55% and 37.87%, respectively (Fig. 1E–G), while these reductions were not significant in the PS-NPs group or co-exposure group. Furthermore, in the TBBPA group, the LVIDs were increased by approximately 39.27%, while the LVPWs and IVSs were decreased by approximately 27.88% and 30.85%, respectively, indicating an increase in the left ventricular internal dimension and thinning of the ventricular wall during systole (Fig. 1H–J). These morphological and functional changes suggest a DCM phenotype [55]. However, the PS-NPs group and co-exposure group did not show significant functional deficits, and the heart rate remained comparable across all groups (Fig. 1K).

H&E staining was used to assess the histopathological alterations in mice, and the results revealed inflammatory cell infiltration in the hearts of the TBBPA group; in addition, cellular edema was evident in both the PS-NPs and co-exposure groups (Fig. 2A). The severity of cardiac fibrosis was assessed using Masson's trichrome and Sirius Red staining. Significant myocardial interstitial and perivascular fibrosis were detected in the TBBPA group and co-exposure group but not in the PS-NPs group (Fig. 2A–C). α-SMA and Col1a1 are key markers of cardiac fibrosis [56]. Immunofluorescence staining further showed that TBBPA exposure increased the α-SMA- and Col1a1-positive areas in cardiac tissue by 2.08-fold and 3.04-fold, respectively (Fig. 3A–D). Additionally, we measured the serum levels of inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) and major myocardial injury markers (CK and CK-MB). The TBBPA group and co-exposure group exhibited increased serum levels of inflammatory factors. (Fig. 2D–E, Fig. S2).

Taken together, these findings indicate that TBBPA, both alone and in combination with PS-NPs, promotes cardiac fibrosis and inflammation. Importantly, TBBPA was identified as the primary contributor to these adverse effects, ultimately leading to cardiac remodeling.

Co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs induced cardiac remodeling in vitro

To further investigate the effects of TBBPA and/or PS-NPs exposure at the cellular level, we measured the expression of markers of cardiac fibrosis, hypertrophy, and inflammation in the H9C2 cell line. First, we used the CCK-8 assay to evaluate the potential toxicity of TBBPA (0, 5, and 50 μg/mL), PS-NPs (0, 5, 50, and 100 μg/mL) and their co-exposure on H9C2 cells. No significant reduction in cell viability was observed in any of these treatment groups (Fig. S3). Thus, based on its non-cytotoxic concentration, which falls within the range established in previous studies [[57], [58], [59], [60]], 5 μg/mL was selected as the concentration of both TBBPA and PS-NPs for use in all subsequent cellular experiments. After 24 h exposure to TBBPA or PS-NPs alone, immunofluorescence staining and RT‒qPCR revealed that the protein intensity of the α-SMA and the mRNA levels of the α-SMA, Col1a1 and Col3a1 increased to different degrees (Fig. 4A–D, H). Notably, the greatest increase in the expression of these markers was observed in the co-exposure group, with their levels exceeding those in any single-exposure group. A similar synergistic effect was observed for the inflammatory cytokine IL-6, whose mRNA expression was highest in the co-exposure group (Fig. 4I).

We further investigated cardiac hypertrophic responses by measuring the expression of the markers ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) and BNP (brain natriuretic peptide), as well as cardiomyocyte surface area. The co-exposure group showed the most pronounced changes, with ANP and BNP expression increasing by approximately 170% and 210%, respectively, and the cell surface area increasing by 136%. This group was followed by the TBBPA group, which displayed increases of approximately 150% (ANP), 160% (BNP), and 127% (surface area). The changes in the PS-NPs group were minimal, with increases of 130% (BNP) and 119% (surface area) (Fig. 4E–G). In summary, TBBPA, PS-NPs, and co-exposure all induced cardiac remodeling in vitro, albeit to varying degrees.

Identification of the estrogen signaling pathway and H19 as key mediators in co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs induced cardiac fibrosis

To elucidate the potential regulatory mechanisms of TBBPA- and/or PS-NPs-induced cardiac fibrosis in mice, we performed RNA sequencing to identify significant alterations in gene expression. Bar plots and volcano plots illustrated the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and their changes. Compared to the control group, 394 DEGs were identified in the TBBPA group (239 up and 155 down), 349 in the PS-NPs group (214 up and 135 down), and 435 in the co-exposure group (327 up and 108 down) (Fig. 5A, D–F). The intersections of the DEGs in each group were shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. 5B). Next, we generated a heatmap to visualize the DEGs, showing the consistency among the five biological replicates and the expression patterns of the top 50 genes with the smallest p-values, ranked according to log2 expression levels (Fig. 5C). To determine the biological functions of the DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were conducted. GO terms such as “extracellular region” and “small molecule binding” were prominently enriched in all three groups (Fig. 5G–I). The KEGG analysis revealed the top 20 enriched pathways (20 pathways with the lowest p value) (Fig. 5J–L). We noted the enrichment of redox-related pathways in the KEGG results. In the TBBPA group, cytochrome P450-mediated xenobiotic and Drug metabolism-cytochrome P450 were enriched, both closely linked to ROS generation. In the PS-NPs group, enriched pathways included calcium signaling, necroptosis, and cardiac muscle contraction. In the co-exposure group, pathways enriched included xenobiotic metabolism, estrogen receptor signaling, and chemical carcinogenesis–DNA adducts. Collectively, these enrichments indicate that oxidative stress and related cellular responses may contribute to the cardiotoxic effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs. Notably, the estrogen signaling pathway was identified in the co-exposure group (Fig. 5L). Given that TBBPA and PS-NPs are considered xenoestrogens, they can interact with estrogen receptors (ESR1 and ESR2) and exert agonistic or antagonistic effects. Herein, our results suggested that ESRs may be involved in cardiac fibrosis following exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs.

Furthermore, to identify key genes linking to CVDs, we analyzed the association between DEGs and diseases using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). We found that 24 DEGs in the TBBPA group, 7 DEGs in the PS-NPs group, and 23 DEGs in the co-exposure group were strongly associated with CVDs (Fig. 6A–C). From this analysis, the long non-coding RNA H19 emerged as a key candidate. Previous studies have reported that H19 is a therapeutic target for combating pathological cardiac fibrosis [8,[61], [62], [63]]. Moreover, it is a highly estrogen-responsive gene located downstream of ESRs. It was significantly downregulated in both the TBBPA (log2FC = −1.1) and co-exposure (log2FC = −1.01) groups and was strongly associated with heart failure, cardiomyopathies, and fibrosis in the CTD. We then performed tissue-level RT‒qPCR validation of H19. Consistent with the RNA sequencing data, H19 expression was significantly downregulated by 42.93% and 14.09% in the TBBPA and co-exposure groups, respectively (Fig. 6D and E). Therefore, the combined evidence from established knowledge and CTD mining strongly supported the selection of ESRs and H19 for further mechanistic investigation. In addition, our GSEA revealed the consistent enrichment of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway across all treatment groups (Fig. 6G–I), a finding of particular interest given its established role in cardiac fibrosis [[64], [65], [66]]. Based on these findings and previous evidence that H19 acts as an upstream regulator of Wnt/β-catenin [67,68], we postulated that this pathway mediates pollutant-induced cardiac fibrosis phenotypes. Immunofluorescence staining of heart tissue sections validated the protein expression and nuclear translocation of β-catenin, the central effector of the Wnt pathway, following exposure to TBBPA, PS-NPs and co-exposure (Fig. 6J). Therefore, these results suggest that ESRs and H19 likely function as key upstream molecules, activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and thereby collectively contributing to the potential mechanism of TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced cardiotoxicity. A summary diagram of this proposed signaling pathway is presented in Fig. 6F.

ESRs mediate TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced fibrotic responses in H9C2 cells

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism in vitro, we investigated the effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs on the ESR/H19 axis in H9C2 cells. Specifically, the role of ESR activation was assessed by administering its specific antagonist, ICI 182,780, in the following groups: the Control, TBBPA, PS-NPs, TBBPA + PS-NPs, ICI, TBBPA + ICI, PS-NPs + ICI, and TBBPA + PS-NPs + ICI. Exposure to TBBPA and co-exposure significantly upregulated the expression of both ESR1 and ESR2, an effect that was effectively abolished by ICI co-treatment, confirming the activation of ESRs (Fig. 7A and B). Consistent with the results in vivo, H19 expression was downregulated by the pollutants to different extents, and crucially, this downregulation was reversed by ICI in the TBBPA and co-exposure group, indicating that H19 is a downstream effector of ESR activation (Fig. 7C).

Having established the upstream ESR/H19 relationship, we next examined its downstream consequences. RT‒qPCR confirmed that TBBPA and PS-NPs promoted the upregulation of critical genes in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Ctnnb1, Gsk3β, Myc, and Axin) to varying degrees. Furthermore, the addition of ICI largely attenuated these effects, restoring the expression of these genes to near-control levels (Fig. 7D–G). This transcriptional activation was further substantiated at the protein level. Immunofluorescence staining showed that both TBBPA and PS-NPs promoted β-catenin nuclear translocation in H9C2 cells, as indicated by the increased fluorescence intensity in the nucleus. This effect was notably reversed by ICI co-treatment (Fig. 7J). Finally, we measured the expression of key fibrotic markers (Col1a1 and Col3a1) as representative indicators of the fibrosis process to directly link this signaling axis to cardiac fibrosis in vitro. The data revealed that the upregulation of these genes induced by TBBPA, PS-NPs and co-exposure was effectively mitigated by ICI co-treatment (Fig. 7H and I).

Briefly, these results establish that ESRs mediate the pro-fibrotic effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs through H19/Wnt activation, and that the pharmacological inhibition of ESRs blocks this cascade and subsequent cardiomyocyte fibrosis.

H19 acts as a critical downstream mediator of TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced cardiomyocyte fibrosis

To elucidate the role of H19 in mediating TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced fibrotic responses, we first examined the effects of TBBPA, PS-NPs, and their co-exposure on Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation and fibrotic marker expression in H9C2 cells. As shown in Fig. 8A–C and Fig. 7D–I (as described above), exposure to TBBPA, PS-NPs, or co-exposure significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein levels of Wnt pathway components (Ctnnb1, Myc, Axin) and fibrotic markers (Col1a1 and Col3a1) to varying degrees, with co-exposure showing the most pronounced effects. We then knocked down H19 using shRNA-H19 to determine whether H19 deficiency alone could recapitulate these TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced effects. The shRNA with the strongest inhibitory effect was selected (Fig. S4) and significantly reduced H19 expression compared with the control and NC groups (Fig. 8D). Meanwhile, the expression of Wnt pathway-related genes was upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels, and the mRNA and protein levels of Col1a1 and Col3a1 were also markedly increased (Fig. 8E–L). These findings indicate that H19 deficiency activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and induces a fibrotic phenotype in H9C2 cells.

Collectively, these findings establish a causal role for H19 in pollutant-induced fibrotic responses. We therefore identified H19 as a critical downstream mediator of the ESR/H19/Wnt axis in TBBPA- and PS-NPs-induced cardiomyocyte fibrosis.

Discussion

Recently, the cardiotoxicity induced by environmental pollutants has emerged as a major focus of toxicological research [[69], [70], [71]]. TBBPA is among the most widely produced and applied BFRs, yet its high potential for bioaccumulation and toxicity has raised considerable concern [72,73]. However, direct evidence of its cardiotoxic effects in mammals remains unknown. Furthermore, the cardiotoxic consequences of co-exposure to pervasive pollutants such as PS-NPs are uncharacterized. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the cardiotoxicity of TBBPA, PS-NPs, and their co-exposure. Overall, our findings revealed for the first time that TBBPA exposure induced cardiac remodeling in mice, and further reveal that, both TBBPA and PS-NPs promoted cardiac fibrosis via the ESR/H19/Wnt pathway in vitro.

In this study, we report the cardiotoxic effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs, alone and in combination, at environmentally relevant concentrations in mice. We found that TBBPA induced cardiac remodeling phenotypes both in vivo and in vitro. Previous studies have reported distinct effects of TBBPA on cardiac development: while 1–100 nM TBBPA showed no significant adverse effects on cardiac differentiation in human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) [74], 10 μg/mL TBBPA caused developmental cardiotoxicity in zebrafish, significantly downregulating key genes (Amhc, Vmhc, Nkx2.5, and Gata4) while leaving cardiac function intact [75]. Shifting the focus to postnatal life, we first demonstrate that exposure to an environmentally relevant dose of TBBPA (1 mg/kg·bw every two days) triggers acquired cardiac remodeling and functional impairment in adolescent mice, establishing a model relevant to exposure in developed individuals. We followed the strategy of Zhang et al., who used a daily dose of 5 mg/kg to achieve serum TBBPA levels in mice comparable to those found in human volunteers (0.34 ng/mL). Moreover, although our dose is higher than the estimated daily intake of TBBPA for the general population (2.52 ng/kg bw/day), it complies with the EU tolerable daily intake (1000 μg/kg/day) [28,76,77]. However, the effects of PS-NPs were distinct: they upregulated the expression of remodeling markers in vitro but did not induce significant cardiotoxicity in vivo. This absence of in vivo toxicity of PS-NPs contrasts with a previous report [78], a discrepancy potentially attributable to differences in the dosage and administration frequency. To better approximate real-world exposure scenarios, a dose of 50 mg/kg·bw was administered once every two days. According to Senathirajah et al., humans ingest approximately 5 g of small plastic particles per week, and the PS-NPs dose used in our study falls within this range [47]. This dose regimen was lower than the level reported to induce cardiotoxicity (2.5 mg/day), and the extended interval between administrations may have facilitated compensatory clearance mechanisms. Furthermore, achieving a blood concentration of PS-NPs in mice (∼3.37 μg/mL) comparable to the levels detected in human blood (∼1.6 μg/mL) reportedly requires an exposure dose as high as 250 mg kg−1 d−1 [79,80]. This evidence points to the low bioavailability of PS-NPs in vivo [81], which likely accounts for the absence of significant cardiotoxicity in our study. In contrast, the absence of complex biological barriers in cell culture systems may contribute to a higher effective cellular concentration of PS-NPs, which could result in the toxicity observed in H9C2 cells.

Based on a comprehensive assessment of functional impairments (reduced EF and FS) (Fig. 1E–G), structural remodeling (ventricular wall thinning and chamber dilation) (Fig. 1H–J), and histopathological alterations (Fig. 2A–C), TBBPA exposure induced a phenotype consistent with DCM in mice. Systolic function was markedly compromised in the TBBPA group, whose EF decreased significantly by 27.55% compared with that in the control group. Notably, 50% of the exposed individuals exhibited EF values between 40% and 49%, which aligns with the clinical spectrum of DCM with mid-range EF (mrEF DCM) and suggests a subclinical or early disease stage [82]. Despite being a leading cause of heart failure, the environmental triggers of DCM remain poorly understood [83], our study highlights TBBPA as a potential environmental risk factor for a DCM-like cardiac impairment.

Interestingly, in contrast to some reports suggesting that co-exposure to PS-NPs and pollutants exacerbates toxicity [36,[84], [85], [86]], we found that although co-exposure induced cardiac fibrosis, it did not trigger the full spectrum of cardiac remodeling and was less cardiotoxic than TBBPA exposure alone. Given that PS-NPs are known to adsorb organic pollutants such as TBBPA, thereby reducing their bioavailability in a Rhodococcus strain [50], we speculate that the adsorption of TBBPA onto PS-NPs led to a decrease in its effective internal concentration, thereby attenuating its cardiotoxic effects on mice. Previous studies have shown that micro- and nanoparticles with a diameter of less than 2.5 μm can be taken up by microfold (M) cells in the Peyer's patches and transported via the lymphatic system [87,88]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that after TBBPA is adsorbed onto PS-NPs, its uptake pathway in the body is altered. This not only prolongs the absorption half-life of TBBPA and reduces its peak blood concentration but also leads to the formation of a reservoir of the complex within immune cells, where it slowly degrades. Consequently, the acute exposure intensity of TBBPA in target organs such as the heart is further diminished. In addition, PS-NPs exposure may also enhance the fecal excretion of TBBPA through adsorption [89]. Our physicochemical data support this hypothesis: the adsorption of TBBPA significantly increased the zeta potential of the PS-NPs (Fig. 1D), which may indicate a substantial interaction that likely alters the environmental behavior and disposition of TBBPA. In vitro, however, the opposite pattern was observed. When the effects on H9C2 cells were evaluated, exposure to 5 μg/mL TBBPA or PS-NPs alone upregulated the expression of markers of fibrosis, hypertrophy, and inflammation to varying degrees, with the co-exposure group exhibiting the most pronounced effects. This increased toxicity may be attributed to the acute exposure setting, which lacks the systemic metabolic and compensatory mechanisms present in vivo, thereby resulting in the “Trojan horse effect” [90,91]. In this mechanism, PS-NPs act as carriers for TBBPA, facilitating its cellular uptake and increasing its local intracellular concentration, which amplifies the combined toxic response.

The long non-coding RNA H19, which has been extensively studied in CVDs, is considered as a highly promising therapeutic target for counteracting pathological cardiac remodeling [92,93]. H19 appears to play complex and sometimes opposing roles, including counteracting cardiac hypertrophy while also promoting fibrosis, likely reflecting its distinct functions in different pathological contexts of cardiac remodeling [64,94,95]. For instance, H19 is reported to be upregulated and to exacerbate cardiac dilation and fibrosis in models of isoproterenol (ISO)/TGF-β-induced fibrosis and transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced hypertrophy [96,97], whereas it is downregulated and capable of ameliorating fibrosis upon overexpression in the failing hearts of mice, pigs, and humans [98]. In our study, RNA sequencing revealed the significant downregulation of H19 in both the TBBPA and co-exposure groups, with a more pronounced reduction in the TBBPA group, thereby supporting the establishment of our pollutant-induced cardiac fibrosis model (Fig. 6D and E). To explore upstream regulatory pathways, we focused on NRs–related pathways that were enriched in the co-exposure group, including the estrogen signaling pathway and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 5L). Given that H19 is a highly estrogen-responsive gene directly regulated by ESRs in various pathophysiological processes [[99], [100], [101]], together with the reported estrogenic activities of TBBPA and PS-NPs [102,103], we focused on ESRs. Our mechanistic studies confirmed this link: the ESR antagonist ICI 182,780 not only suppressed pollutant-induced ESR activation but also reversed the downregulation of H19 (Fig. 7A–C). To further establish the causal role of H19, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in H9C2 cells. These manipulations confirmed that H19 acts downstream of ESRs to mediate Wnt/β-catenin activation and fibrotic responses induced by TBBPA and PS-NPs. Therefore, we deduce that the ESR/H19/Wnt axis is a critical signaling pathway that mediates the cardiotoxic effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs by integrating xenoestrogenic signals from the environment into cardiac fibrosis.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a well-established mechanism of cardiac fibrosis and remodeling [65,66]. Its activation promotes β-catenin stabilization and nuclear translocation. Within the nucleus, β-catenin associates with TCF/LEF transcription factors to promote target gene expression [104]. Previous studies show that PS-MPs induce cardiac and ovarian fibrosis in rats and lung fibrosis in mice via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [[105], [106], [107]]. Based on the established literature positioning that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is downstream of H19 [67,108,109] and support from our GSEA, which confirmed its significant enrichment (Fig. 6G–I), we hypothesized that TBBPA- and PS-NP-induced cardiotoxicity is mediated by the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Our research demonstrated that exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs activated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, leading to increased expression of the key fibrotic markers Col1a1 and Col3a1. Crucially, ICI treatment not only suppressed the activation of this pathway at both the gene and protein levels but also mitigated fibrosis in vitro (Fig. 7D–J). These findings indicate that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway functions downstream of ESR activation. Therefore, our findings establish a signaling cascade whereby TBBPA and PS-NPs activate ESRs to downregulate H19, which in turn activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promotes fibrosis. This conclusion is further supported by our gain- and loss-of-function data, positioning H19 as a critical downstream mediator of this cascade. Future studies are warranted to confirm this axis in vivo.

Furthermore, it is important to recognize that this study is subject to several limitations. Our investigation into the estrogen-like effects of TBBPA and PS-NPs was conducted in vitro using H9C2 cells. Future studies using primary cardiomyocytes or hiPSC-CMs, as well as transgenic animal models for in vivo ESR validation, are warranted to confirm our findings. In addition, further investigations are needed to dissect the potential differential roles of ESR1 and ESR2 subtypes. Moreover, future studies incorporating female mice are needed to determine whether the observed effects are sex-dependent. Notably, the interaction patterns in TBBPA and PS-NPs co-exposure are more complex. Whether the effect of co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs tends toward “adsorption-mediated detoxification” or “Trojan horse-mediated toxification” depends on intricate in vivo and in vitro environmental factors, which represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a comprehensive evaluation of the cardiotoxicity induced by orally administered TBBPA and PS-NPs for the first time. We demonstrated that co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs can cause cardiac fibrosis, while TBBPA further leads to cardiac remodeling. The findings from in vitro experiments revealed distinct outcomes: exposure to TBBPA or PS-NPs alone promoted the expression of cardiac remodeling markers to varying degrees, and co-exposure exacerbated these effects. Through RNA sequencing analysis and subsequent experiments, we propose that exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs may promote cardiomyocyte fibrosis by regulating the ESR/H19/Wnt pathway. Our findings offer new insights into the differential toxicity of co-exposure to TBBPA and PS-NPs in vivo and in vitro, and provide novel perspectives on the mechanism of cardiotoxicity induced by TBBPA and PS-NPs.
