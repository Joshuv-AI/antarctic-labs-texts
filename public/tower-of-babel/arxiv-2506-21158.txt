Diverse Mini-Batch Selection in Reinforcement Learning for Efficient Chemical Exploration in de novo Drug Design
Hampus Gummesson Svensson, Ola Engkvist, Jon Paul Janet, Christian Tyrchan, Morteza Haghir Chehreghani

Abstract
In many real-world applications, evaluating the goodness of instances is often costly and time-consuming, e.g., human feedback and physics simulations, in contrast to proposing new instances. In particular, this is even more critical in reinforcement learning, as new interactions with the environment (i.e., new instances) need to be evaluated to provide a reward signal to learn from. As sufficient exploration is crucial, learning from a diverse mini-batch can have a large impact and help mitigate mode collapse. In this paper, we introduce diverse mini-batch selection for reinforcement learning and propose to use determinantal point processes for this task. We study this framework in the context of a real-world problem, namely drug discovery. We experimentally study how our proposed framework can improve the effectiveness of chemical exploration in de novo drug design, where finding diverse and high-quality solutions is essential. We conduct a comprehensive evaluation with three well-established molecular generation oracles over numerous generative steps. Our experiments conclude that our diverse mini-batch selection framework can substantially improve the diversity of the solutions, while still obtaining solutions of high quality. In drug discovery, such outcome can potentially lead to fulfilling unmet medication needs faster.



1
Introduction

Many practical machine learning applications require quality assessment of data and experiences, e.g., via human expert evaluation, computer simulations, field testing, and laboratory experimentation. These assessment methods are frequently resource-intensive, demanding significant time and financial investment. Consequently, the volume of data that can undergo thorough evaluation is often constrained by budgetary limitations.

In this paper, we consider this problem in online reinforcement learning, where the training instances are provided solely from the agent’s interaction with the environment.
Reinforcement learning (RL) relies on learning from interactions with the environment. Many successful algorithms in environments, e.g.,
(
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Mnih et al. 2016
)
, run many copies of the environment in parallel to synchronously or asynchronously learn from numerous interactions. For synchronous on-policy algorithms, the experiences are accumulated to compute an average loss to update the agent’s policy. This is also the case in many applications of RL, e.g.,
de novo
drug design
(
Olivecrona et al. 2017
)
and text-to-image generation
(
Fan et al. 2023
)
. However, in real-world applications, this is necessarily not possible since not all interactions with the environment can be assessed to provide a reward signal. For instance, in recent years, reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of pre-trained models has shown great success in various applications
(
Zhai et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Olivecrona et al. 2017
)
. In such applications, the goal is often to fine-tune the pre-trained model from human preferences or experiments, while minimizing the number of interactions to be evaluated.

To efficiently compute a sufficient solution or find different modes, exploration mechanisms play a vital role in agent performance, especially in tasks with sparse reward or for a reward landscape with a vast number of local optima to explore. One popular approach to enhance exploration in RL is the addition of an exploration bonus to the reward function, known as
intrinsic reward

(
Burda et al. 2018
;
Badia et al. 2020
;
Seo et al. 2021
;
Tang et al. 2017
)
. Another common approach is maximum entropy RL, where the agent tries to maximize both the reward and entropy simultaneously, i.e., succeeding at a task while still acting as randomly as possible
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
. Our work provides a consistent perspective where, while acting diverse, it is important to keep high quality among the interactions used to update the policy. This is accomplished by considering mini-batch diversification in reinforcement learning, where a large number of interactions obtained from running copies of the environment in parallel are summarized in a smaller, diverse set of interactions used for updating the policy. This can provide an effective way to impose additional exploration in the learning process when there is a limited evaluation budget.

We argue that providing a diverse mini-batch of interactions makes the agent’s exploration more effective and increases the diversity of the forthcoming interactions. Therefore, we introduce a diverse mini-batch selection framework for reinforcement learning and propose to use determinantal point processes (DPP) for this task. DPPs provide an effective framework to sample a diverse set based on specified similarity information. Previous work has proposed a mini-batch diversification scheme based on DPPs for stochastic gradient descent and shown its effectiveness
(
Zhang, Mandt, and Kjellström 2017
;
Huang, Da Xu, and Oppermann 2019
)
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first effort to study the effects of diverse mini-batch selection in (online) reinforcement learning.

Particularly, we study the effects of diverse mini-batch selection in a real-world problem with large social impact, namely the
de novo
drug design problem. This problem can be formulated as an episodic RL problem, and previous work has shown that this approach provides promising solutions
(
Thomas et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2022
)
. The reward function is usually complex and has many high-rewarding modes that should be found and subsequently exploited to obtain a diverse set of solutions. Hence, chemical exploration and diversification are of integral importance in drug design. In real-world deployment of this problem, it is often costly and time-consuming to evaluate an instance (i.e., a state-action episode) to obtain a reward. On the other hand, it is usually inexpensive to generate an instance. This makes it a suitable problem to employ diverse mini-batch selection and study its effectiveness. On the other hand, we believe that this methodology can improve RL in general since exploration is a key challenge in RL, and domain-specific information can easily be incorporated.

Background

2.1
De novo
Drug Design

De novo
drug design is the challenge of designing novel drug molecules given a set of predefined constraints. This is a problem where diversity is important since a diverse molecular library increases the likelihood of identifying candidates with unique and favorable pharmacological profiles, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency and success rate of drug development pipelines. Also, it is a problem where real-world applications often use expensive computational simulations and/or human-in-the-loop feedback
(
Pitt et al. 2025
;
Sundin et al. 2022
)
.

A popular approach for
de novo
drug design is to use chemical language models to generate string-based representations of molecules
(
Arús-Pous et al. 2019
;
Segler et al. 2018
)
. To steer the chemical language model to promising areas of the chemical space, reinforcement learning can be leveraged
(
Olivecrona et al. 2017
)
.
An action
a
in this RL problem corresponds to adding one token to the string representation of the molecule where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of possible tokens that can be added, including a start token
a^{\text{start}}
and a stop token
a^{\text{stop}}
. The reward function asses the goodness of the molecule represented by the string. The
de novo
drug design problem can be modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP), e.g.,
(
Gummesson Svensson et al. 2024
)
.

One popular string-based representation of chemical entities is Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System
(
Weininger 1988
)
, abbreviated SMILES. Evaluations by both
Gao et al. 2022
and
Thomas et al. 2022
have concluded good performance of SMILES-based REINVENT
(
Segler et al. 2018
;
Olivecrona et al. 2017
;
Blaschke et al. 2020a
;
Loeffler et al. 2024
)
compared to both other RL-based and non-RL-based approaches for
de novo
drug design. REINVENT consists of a long short-term memory (LSTM) network
(
Hochreiter 1997
)
using SMILES to represent molecules as text strings. REINVENT utilizes an on-policy RL algorithm optimizing the policy
\pi_{\theta}
to generate higher rewarding molecules. Previous work has shown that minimizing their loss function is equivalent to maximizing the expected return, as for policy gradient algorithms
(
Guo and Schwaller 2024
)
. Our work builds upon the success of REINVENT and tries to improve its chemical exploration.

2.2
Diversity in RL-based
de novo
drug design

The drug-like chemical space is estimated to consist of
10^{33}
synthesisable molecules
(
Polishchuk, Madzhidov, and Varnek 2013
)
. To explore this space, several studies aim to improve the chemical exploration carried out by the RL agent. To improve the diversity in RL-based
de novo
drug design,
Blaschke et al. 2020b
introduced a count-based method that reduces the reward, i.e., reward shaping
(
Laud 2004
)
, for similar molecules based on their structure. The work of
Park et al. 2024
and
Wang and Zhu 2024
employs memory and prediction-based intrinsic motivation to improve the reward of the generated molecules. Moreover,
Svensson et al. 2024
show that structure- and learning-based intrinsic rewards in combination with domain-specific reward shaping can improve the overall diversity. Our work takes on an additional perspective to enhance diversity. Rather than just encouraging diverse and explorative behavior via the reward signal, our work studies the effect of maximizing the diversity of the molecules that we evaluate and the agent learn from.

To measure the diversity among a given set of molecules, several existing metrics have been proposed.
Hu et al. 2024
divides these metrics into two main categories: reference-based and distance-based. A reference-based metric compares a molecular set with a reference set to find the intersection. Distance-based metrics use pairwise distances among the molecular set to determine the diversity.

In this work, both metrics are applied. As the representative reference-based metric, the number of molecular scaffolds, also known as Bemis-Murcko scaffolds
(
Bemis and Murcko 1996
)
, is used. As a distance-based metric serves the number of

This work proposes a framework to tackle diverse mini-batch selection for reinforcement learning. We assume delayed rewards and that acquiring a sequence of states and actions is inexpensive compared to the evaluation. This is often true for real-world problems. Given a large set of online interactions, we wish to select a diverse mini-batch to evaluate and use these new experiences to update the parameters of our policy. We hypothesize that this affects the agent’s exploration of the solution space. This technique can provide additional domain-specific exploration in combination with other popular exploration techniques, e.g., intrinsic motivation and entropy-maximization.
The intuition is that learning from diverse experiences helps the agent to explore more effectively, given a fixed budget for evaluations to obtain rewards.

We suggest enforcing diversity among the selected interactions to improve the efficiency of the exploration. For this purpose, we propose a diverse mini-batch selection framework for reinforcement learning, see
algorithm

1
. Here, the focus is on trajectories of actions, i.e., an interaction corresponds to a trajectory. However, the framework can easily be extended beyond trajectories.

Over
G
training/generative steps, using the agent’s current policy
\pi_{\theta}
, a batch
\mathcal{B}
of
B
trajectories is sampled in parallel over copies of the same environment. Each trajectory has a maximum horizon of
T
steps, where the true length of each trajectory can depend on some stopping criteria or when the terminal state is reached. If
B
is chosen such that
B\gg k
and the agent’s policy is stochastic, this set will contain primarily unique items. Here, the RL agent can in each copy of the environment focus on maximizing the expected return of each trajectory with respect to the reward function, i.e., maximizing the return generated by the agent’s policy
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(\tau)],

where
\tau
is a state-action trajectory
S_{0},A_{0},S_{1}\dots,S_{T-1},A_{T-1},S_{T}
,
R(\tau)
is the return of following
\tau
and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\cdot]
denotes the expected value of a random variable given that the agent follows policy
\pi_{\theta}
. This generates
\mathcal{B}
under the belief that the agent tries to maximize each return, without explicitly considering the diversity among individual trajectories. This can be particularly important when the agent has to regard safety concerns, is sensitive to noise, or many trajectories are not practically viable. For instance, a SMILES string is not necessarily chemically feasible and small changes can easily break its validity. Therefore, it is important that the agent primarily focus on generating chemically valid SMILES strings of high quality. However, exploration can still be incorporated as a part of the reward, e.g., via intrinsic motivation
(
Burda et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2017
)
.

Given a large batch of trajectories
\mathcal{B}
, to stay within the given budget of evaluations, the next step is to obtain a smaller, diverse mini-batch
Y
that summarizes
\mathcal{B}
. We propose to use determinantal point processes
(
Kulesza, Taskar et al. 2012
)
for this purpose. A point process
\mathcal{P}
is a probability measure over finite subsets of a set
\mathcal{B}
. We consider the discrete case where
\mathcal{B}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, where
N
is the number of unique trajectories in
\mathcal{B}
. In this case, a point process is a probability measure on the power set
2^{\mathcal{P}}
, the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{Y}
. Determinantal point processes (DPPs) is a family of point processes characterized by the
repulsion
of items such that similar items are less likely to co-occur in the same sample. Given a kernel, providing a similarity measure between pairs of items, DPP places a high probability on subsets that are diverse with respect to the kernel. We consider a class of DPPs known as L-ensembles
(
Borodin and Rains 2005
)
, which is defined via a real, symmetric matrix
L
over the entire (finite) domain of
\mathcal{B}
. This matrix is often denoted as the
kernel matrix
. The probability of subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{L}(Y)\propto\det(L_{Y}),

(2)

where
L_{Y}=[L_{ij}]_{i,j\in Y}
denotes the restriction of
L
to the entries indexed by items of
Y
. Hence, the probability of sampling the set
Y\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is proportional to the determinant of
L_{Y}
restricted to
Y
. The normalization constant is available in closed form due to the fact
\sum_{Y\subseteq\mathcal{B}}\det(L_{Y})=\det(L+I)
, where
I
is the
N\times N
identity matrix.

Given the larger set
\mathcal{B}
, we want the smaller set
Y
to contain a pre-defined number of items from
\mathcal{B}
. Hence, we are interested in sampling a subset
Y
with a fixed cardinality
|Y|=k
to sample a mini-batch with a fixed size.
k
-DPPs
(
Kulesza and Taskar 2011
)
concern DPPs conditioned on the cardinality of the random subset. Formally, the probability of a
k
-DPP to sample a subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{L}^{k}(Y)=\frac{\det(L_{Y})}{\sum_{Y^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}:|Y^{\prime}|=k}\det(L_{Y^{\prime}})},

(3)

where
|Y|=k
. The
k
-DPP’s inherent ability to promote diversity makes it an excellent choice for selecting diverse and representative mini-batches in reinforcement learning. In this way,
k
-DPP provides a smaller and diverse set of items from a larger set of items.
Performing exact sampling from the
k
-DPP generally requires an eigendecomposition of its kernel
(
Kulesza and Taskar 2011
)
, typically requiring
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
time. Given a decomposition, drawing a sample typically takes
\mathcal{O}(NK^{3})
time overall
(
Kulesza, Taskar et al. 2012
)
. For more details, we refer to
(
Derezinski, Calandriello, and Valko 2019
;
Calandriello, Derezinski, and Valko 2020
)
for more efficient exact sampling procedures,
(
Li, Jegelka, and Sra 2016
;
Grosse et al. 2024
)
for approximative methods and
(
Anari, Gharan, and Rezaei 2016
;
Rezaei and Gharan 2019
)
for Markov-Chain-Monte-Carlo (MCMC) procedures.

How
k
-DPP will summarize the larger set is determined by the kernel matrix
L
. Constructing the kernel matrix entails using domain knowledge, but other information can also be used.
Let
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a quality term and
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
||\phi_{i}||=1
, a vector of normalized diversity features of the
i
th item in
\mathcal{B}
, e.g., the
i
th generated SMILES string. As proposed by
Kulesza, Taskar et al. 2012
, the entries of the kernel matrix can then be written as

L_{ij}=q_{i}\phi_{i}^{T}\phi_{j}q_{j},

(4)

where
q_{i}
is a quality term measuring the intrinsic “goodness” of the
i
th item, and
\phi_{i}^{T}phi_{j}\in[-1,1]
is a signed measure of similarity between
i
th and
j
th item.
Therefore, utilizing
k
-DPPs allows for a flexible sampling procedure that behaves differently depending on the information incorporated in the kernel matrix
L
. It does not directly optimize the determinant of
L
, but instead includes randomness to encourage additional exploration.

Next, each trajectory in
Y
is evaluated to obtain the corresponding returns and/or state-action rewards. Using the returns and rewards, the policy parameters are updated by employing an arbitrary RL. The discussed framework is agnostic to the RL algorithm used to update the policy parameters, and yields both the policy parameters
\theta
and a diverse set of trajectory-return pairs
\{\tau,R(\tau)\}
.

4
Experimental Evaluation

We evaluate our framework on
de novo
drug design. We run experiments on three reward functions based on well-established molecule binary bioactivity label optimization tasks: the Dopamine Receptor D2 (DRD2), c-Jun N-terminal Kinases-3 (JNK3), and Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (GSK3
\beta
) predictive activity models provided by Therapeutics Data Commons
(
Velez-Arce et al. 2024
)
. The final reward also includes the quantitative estimation of drug-likeness (QED)
(
Bickerton et al. 2012
)
, molecular weight, number of hydrogen bond donors, and structural constraints. Full specifications on the reward functions are available in the supplementary material.

To update the policy and generate SMILES, we use the REINVENT framework
(
Loeffler et al. 2024
)
together with the provided pre-trained policy as the prior policy. It is pre-trained on the ChEMBL database
(
Gaulton et al. 2017
)
to generate drug-like bioactive molecules. The action space
\mathcal{A}
consists of 34 tokens, including start and stop tokens. For a desired mini-batch of
k=64
molecules, we generate
B=k\times 10
SMILES. We evaluate the diversity of the generated set
\mathcal{M}
by the number of molecular scaffolds and the number of diverse actives (see
equation

1
), where the number of diverse actives is computed for every 250th generative step. For the diverse actives, we use the Tanimoto (dis-)similarity to measure the distance between 2048-bit Morgan fingerprints with radius 2 and distance threshold
D=0.7
as proposed by
(
Renz, Luukkonen, and Klambauer 2024
)
. When computing the diversity in terms of both scaffolds and diverse actives, we only regard active molecules, defined as molecules with both QED and predicted activity larger than 0.5.

We compare the use of DPP in combination with different techniques of reward shaping for
de novo
drug design: (1) no reward shaping technique, i.e., the reward function observed by the agent is stationary. This is included as a baseline to investigate if diverse mini-batch selection can act as an alternative approach to the commonly used reward shaping techniques to avoid mode collapse; (2) the effects of the DPP kernel matrices
L
when using the commonly used identical molecular scaffold (IMS) penalty
(
Blaschke et al. 2020b
)
, which sets the reward to 0 when
M
molecules with the same molecular scaffold have been generated; (3) the use of DPP when utilizing the reward shaping technique TanhRND proposed by
Svensson et al. 2024
, which shows promising empirical results in terms of diversity. We denote the raw reward without any reward shaping as the
extrinsic reward
. By
standalone
we refer to the configuration without DPP and reward shaping.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we present an easily applicable framework for enhancing mini-batch diversity in reinforcement learning algorithms. We propose to use determinantal point processes (DPPs) for the diversification process, since their kernel matrices provide a natural way to incorporate similarity between items. In this way, we summarize a larger batch of interactions by selecting a mini-batch of diverse interactions, which requires fewer evaluations. We argue that this enhances the exploration by only focusing on promising, diverse instances, while keeping the rewards high.

We experimentally evaluate the framework by focusing on a real-world problem, namely
de novo
drug design, where we observe that the proposed framework can substantially improve the diversity, especially when combined with a domain-specific reward shaping technique such as TanhRND
(
Svensson et al. 2024
)
. As expected, if the agent can provide sufficient solutions, our framework can substantially enhance the diversity of the generated molecules. We also explore different ways to construct the kernel matrix of DPP, where we observe that the additive combination of two kernels provides the best balance in terms of chemical exploration. Our experimental evaluation establishes a foundation for the efficacy of diverse mini-batch selection in reinforcement learning.

Following the formulation of

, the

a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
is the action taken at state
s_{t}
. We can define the current state as the sequence of performed actions up to round
t

s_{t}\coloneq a_{0:t-1}=a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{t-1},

(5)

where the initial action is always the start token
a_{0}=a^{\text{start}}
. This means that the distribution of the initial state

is deterministic

P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})=\delta_{s_{t}\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu+}a_{t}},

(6)

where
\mathbin{+\mkern-5.0mu+}

(7)

0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
