Can we better prevent cardiovascular disease? 'Stayin' healthy to stay alive'
Authors: Duncan J. Campbell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major contributor to premature morbidity and mortality. We have the tools to prevent CVD, but they are not being applied as effectively as they might. Key contributors to sub-optimal CVD risk factor control are the community's lack of awareness of CVD risk and individuals' lack of awareness of their own CVD risk and 'optimal' targets for risk factors. Additional contributors to sub-optimal CVD risk factor control include failure to treat, lack of adherence with therapy, and failure to achieve therapeutic targets. The Australian SunSmart program for preventing skin cancer by reducing sun exposure provides a potential template for a community-based education program, with support from nurses, pharmacists and allied health, that educates and empowers children and all members of the community to improve CVD risk management by increasing awareness and treatment of CVD risk, improving adherence with risk factor control strategies, and improving success in achievement of target outcomes for risk factors, thereby achieving life-long CVD risk factor control and CVD prevention.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are major contributors to premature morbidity and mortality [1,2]. A key contributor to CVD is atherosclerosis, which starts early in infancy and progresses throughout life [[3], [4], [5]]. Whereas atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD) may impair coronary artery blood flow by creating stenoses, it's impact on coronary microvasculature may also contribute to adverse cardiac events such as myocardial infarction, heart failure and sudden cardiac death [6,7]. Key to the development of atherosclerosis is the entry of plasma low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) into the subintima of arteries, a process accelerated by elevated blood pressure (BP) [8], and both elevated BP and plasma LDL-C levels are major risk factors for CAD [9,10].

CVD can be prevented by management of CVD risk factors [11]. Compared with individuals with two or more CVD risk factors, those with optimal risk factors live an additional 10–15 years free of CVD from age 45–50 years [[12], [13], [14]]. Yet, despite our expertise in CVD prevention we have failed to apply this expertise as effectively as we might [15].

Key contributors to sub-optimal CVD risk factor control are the community's lack of awareness of CVD risk, and individuals' lack of awareness of their own CVD risk and 'optimal' targets for risk factors [[16], [17], [18]]. Additional contributors to sub-optimal CVD risk factor control include failure to treat, lack of adherence with therapy, and failure to achieve therapeutic targets [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Guideline-directed management of CVD risk offers little to an individual who is unaware of CVD and their own CVD risk, fails to adhere to risk reduction strategies, and is unaware of, and fails to achieve, target outcomes for their CVD risk factors.

The focus of this Review is not the details of guideline-directed management of CVD risk, but rather how guideline implementation might be improved through improved awareness of CVD risk in the community, with particular reference to the two main modifiable risk factors: BP and cholesterol [9,10]. The Australian SunSmart program for preventing skin cancer by reducing sun exposure provides a potential template for a community-based program, with support from nurses, pharmacists and allied health, that educates and empowers children and all members of the community to achieve life-long CVD risk control and prevention [23,24]. Such a childhood-targeted approach may improve CVD risk management not only in children but in all members of the community through increased awareness of CVD risk, improved compliance with risk control strategies, and more successful achievement of target outcomes for CVD risk factors.

Impact of CVD

CAD was the underlying cause of 16,922 deaths (9.2 % of all deaths) and was a contributing cause to 16,685 deaths attributed to dementia in Australia in 2023 [25]. In addition, CAD was recorded as the principal diagnosis for 148,000 hospitalisations in 2021–2022 among Australians aged ≥25 years [9]. Australians aged 40 years had an estimated lifetime risk for myocardial infarction of 24.4 % for males and 13.2 % for females in 2018 [26]. Analysis of the Global Burden of Disease 2019 study predicted a 90 % increase in CVD prevalence between 2025 and 2050 [27,28].

Concern for cardiovascular health cannot be postponed to adulthood. The trajectories in cardiovascular health scores from early childhood through late adolescence in the United States (US) showed a decline in cardiovascular health at approximately 10 years of age among both males and females [29]. Underscoring the importance of early-life cardiovascular health, higher BP at age 7 years was associated with greater risk of premature CVD mortality during follow-up into the sixth decade life [30], childhood CVD risk factors at the ages of 3–19 years (including smoking, body mass index, BP, and triglyceride and cholesterol levels) predicted cardiovascular events in midlife [31], higher BP levels in adolescence were associated with a dose-dependent higher risk for atherosclerosis in middle age, particularly for severe coronary atherosclerosis [32], and exposure to atherosclerosis risk factors early in life (age 18–30 years) was associated with lifelong increased risk in mortality [33,34]. Moreover, adults younger than 40 years represented an increasing proportion of myocardial infarctions in the US where, despite declines in heart disease mortality, autopsy studies of decedents who died nonnatural deaths in 2007–2012 (mean age 41 years) revealed >30 % had >25 % area stenosis, and >7 % had >75 % area stenosis of at least one coronary artery [35], and the degree of CAD had not decreased, and may have increased, since 1994 [36]. There is, therefore, an urgent need to improve CVD risk factor control, particularly in the young [35,37].

CVD risk factors

The American Heart Association described 8 components of cardiovascular health: healthy diet, participation in physical activity, avoidance of nicotine, healthy sleep, healthy weight, and healthy levels of blood lipids, blood glucose, and BP [38]. To a large extent, CVD risk factors defined by the National Heart Foundation of Australia are the converse of these components of cardiovascular health: unhealthy diet, alcohol, physical inactivity, smoking, overweight or obesity, and elevated BP and cholesterol levels [39].

Hypertension is the most prevalent modifiable CVD risk factor and is the leading cause of death and disability in Australia and worldwide, with an increasing burden over the last several decades [19,40,41]. Raised BP was responsible for 42 % of coronary heart disease, 39 % of stroke, 37 % of chronic kidney disease, and 31 % of atrial fibrillation and flutter in Australia in 2018 [42]. Epidemiologic studies showed CVD mortality and morbidity were strongly and directly related to BP, without evidence of a threshold down to a systolic BP (SBP) at least 115 mmHg [27,43,44]. Intervention studies showed an SBP treatment target <120 mmHg reduced CVD events in people ≥21 years of age [[45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. Moreover, a simple stepped-care protocol to achieve an SBP goal of <130 mmHg and a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) goal <80 mmHg reduced dementia risk [51]. American and Australian guidelines describe a healthy or ‘normal’ SBP as <120 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg [19,52].

There are several reports of a U-shaped relationship between serum LDL-C levels and cardiovascular mortality, with optimal LDL-C levels reported to be in the range of 2.3–3.9 mmol/L [[53], [54], [55], [56], [57]]. Johannesen et al. reported an optimal LDL-C level of 2.3 mmol/L in individuals receiving lipid lowering treatment [56], and Chen et al. reported an optimal LDL-C level of 1.44 mmol/L in individuals with established CVD [53]. Moreover, the Global Burden of Disease Study defined a cholesterol level of 2.78–3.38 mmol/L as representing minimal risk [58]. The Heart Research Institute (Australia) recommends a total cholesterol <5.5 mmol/L and an LDL-C <3.5 mmol/L [59], whereas the Heart Foundation (Australia) and the World Heart Federation recommend an LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L for individuals at high cardiovascular risk [20]. Lipid-lowering therapies that reduced LDL-C to <2 mmol/L caused regression of coronary atherosclerosis in persons with CAD [60,61], and reduction in the risk of major vascular events with LDL-C lowering therapy was greater for younger individuals [62]. Additionally, LDL-C levels <1.8 mmol/L were associated with reduced dementia risk [63].

Current CVD risk reduction strategies in Australia

Similar to US and European guidelines [[64], [65], [66], [67], [68]], current Australian guidelines for assessing and managing CVD risk recommend use of a CVD risk calculator to assess absolute cardiovascular risk for individuals aged >45 years (>35 years for people with diabetes, >30 years for First Nations peoples), with BP- and cholesterol-lowering pharmacotherapy recommended for individuals with calculated 5-year risk ≥10 %, and consideration of these therapies for individuals with 5 %-<10 % 5-year risk [69,70]. For individuals with estimated 5-year risk <5 %, pharmacotherapy was not recommended, other than for individuals with BP > 160/100 mmHg or total cholesterol >7.5 mmol/L. Individuals with moderate-severe chronic kidney disease or familial hypercholesterolaemia are deemed to be at high risk [70]. However, absolute cardiovascular risk assessment is underutilised in Australian General Practice, particularly in younger people, having been used in only 11.8 % of instances where it was plausibly indicated [71]. Moreover, the age thresholds for use of the CVD risk calculator disadvantage younger individuals for whom CVD preventive therapy has most to offer, and a lower threshold 5-year risk for intervention in younger adults would prevent substantially more CVD events at a similar number needed to treat to prevent one event [72].

Failure of CVD risk factor control: hypertension and elevated cholesterol

Hypertension: The lifetime risk for developing hypertension (≥130/80 mmHg or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication) in the US ranges from 70 % to 90 %, whereas the prevalence of hypertension among individuals aged 20–30 years was 10 %–30 % [73]. Moreover, among US adults aged 18–39 years with hypertension, <40 % were aware of their condition, <20 % were receiving antihypertensive treatment, and <10 % had controlled hypertension (SBP <130 mmHg or DBP <80 mmHg) [2,74]. A similar failure to manage hypertension was observed in Australia, where a community-based survey of 10,046 adults aged ≥18 years, mean age 43 years, in 2017–2019 revealed approximately one in 3 had hypertension, but only half were aware of it [75]; of those with hypertension, 23.6 % were controlled to BP < 140/90 mmHg [75]. Further evidence for failure to achieve target outcomes for BP in an Australian cohort was provided by the Screening Evaluation of the Evolution of New Heart Failure (SCREEN-HF) study [21,22], a community-based study of 4054 adults aged ≥60 years, mean age 69 years, with known CVD risk factors, who were a motivated cohort of volunteers interested in their cardiovascular health and all had a primary care provider. Despite 99 % of 3294 SCREEN-HF participants with hypertension receiving antihypertensive medication, half had BP > 140/90 mmHg [21]. A similar prevalence of hypertension was observed in New Zealand, where one in 3 adults over 30 years of age had elevated BP, of which only a quarter had it under control [76]. More than 1 million New Zealanders are living with high blood pressure; of those, around 35 % were unaware they had high BP [77].

Elevated cholesterol: Among US adults 20–39 years of age, approximately 20 % had an elevated LDL-C (>3.4 mmol/L), and 23.3 % of those with elevated LDL-C were aware of having high cholesterol [2,78]. Cholesterol treatment was sub-optimal among US adults; of those with LDL-C >3.4 mmol/L, >90 % were not receiving cholesterol treatment, of those with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) 10-year risk ≥20 %, approximately 60 % were not receiving cholesterol treatment, and of those with established ASCVD, >40 % were not receiving cholesterol treatment [79]. Moreover, of 1209 SCREEN-HF participants with known CVD, 34 % of women and 20 % of men were not receiving statin therapy [21], and 71 % of women and 58 % of men with calculated 5-year CVD risk >15 % or known CVD and receiving statin therapy had LDL-C levels ≥2 mmol/L [21].

Can risk factor control be improved: The SunSmart program

Given the evolution of CVD over a lifetime, and the association of childhood risk factors with CVD events later in life [30,31], the challenge is to instil a mindset for cardiovascular health early in life that continues throughout life [80]. There is need for a childhood-targeted education program that increases peoples' knowledge of CVD and CVD risk, risk reduction strategies, and the need for adherence to risk reduction strategies to achieve target outcomes for risk factors.

Part of the success of the SunSmart program was its education and empowerment of children, with advice from "Sid the Seagull" [24], to instruct their parents to practice sun protection, and the Victorian SunSmart program was estimated to have prevented 43,000 skin cancers between 1988 and 2011 [81]. It is important to note that the benefits of the SunSmart program were due not to medication (apart from sunscreen) but from behaviour change. Such behaviour change regarding sun protection may extend over generations, as parents who experienced the SunSmart program as children may be more likely to instruct their children in sun protection than parents who did not have experience of the SunSmart program. The SunSmart program for preventing skin cancer by reducing sun exposure provides a template for community-based childhood-targeted education about cardiovascular health that could empower children to instruct their parents about CVD risk management.

Childhood-focussed CVD risk education

Health, sexuality and physical education are part of the Victorian School Curriculum (Australia). This health, sexuality and physical education provides a foundation for a "Stayin' healthy to stay alive" instruction that educates students about optimal lifestyle for cardiovascular health and provides a foundation for lifelong CVD risk control and prevention. People prefer to address single risk factors rather than calculated composite risk [82]. With guidance from a community nurse, students could learn how to measure BP, and to measure cholesterol levels with a 'point-of-care' instrument. Students could measure their own and their classmates' BP and take the BP monitor home to measure the BP of family members (with attention to cuff size) and encourage family members to obtain measurement of their cholesterol level. As part of their "Stayin' healthy to stay alive" instruction children could carry out an assignment whereby they tabulate the CVD risk factors of family members and discuss how their CVD risk factors might be improved. Every child at the end of secondary school should know their BP and cholesterol level, and they should encourage family members to know their BP and cholesterol level and bring them into the healthy range if they are elevated. Educating and empowering children to implement strategies that address CVD risk including knowledge and control of their BP and cholesterol levels, physical activity, a healthy diet, avoidance of overweight, moderation of alcohol intake, and never smoking, has potential to impact not only the lifetime CVD risk of the child but also the CVD risk of their families and the community.

Just as everyone should practice sun protection (without need for a prescription from a medical practitioner), everyone should know their BP and cholesterol level, and whether these levels place them at increased CVD risk. The Heart Research Institute recommends individuals >18 years of age have their BP checked at least every two years, and annually for individuals >45 years of age [83]. Additionally, the Heart Research Institute recommends individuals aged ≥45 years (≥35 years for Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander peoples) have their lipids measured every 5 years, or every 12 months if they have high BP, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, or kidney disease [59]. By contrast, the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend universal lipid screening for all children aged 9–11 years [84]. A pilot study in Western Australia showed screening of children aged 1–2 years for familial hypercholesterolaemia, undertaken at the time of immunization, was a feasible and effective approach to detect children, parents and other blood relatives with familial hypercholesterolaemia [85]. Universal adoption of this practice would potentially play an important role in reducing premature CVD events and CVD death.

Community-empowerment

Whereas behaviour modification contributes to management of diet, physical inactivity, smoking, and overweight, elevated BP and plasma cholesterol levels are major CVD risk factors currently managed predominantly by medical practitioners, and control of these two risk factors is less than optimal. The high prevalence of elevated BP and cholesterol levels in the community, including those receiving BP- and cholesterol-lowering therapies, provides motivation to explore alternative strategies for their control. Both American and European guidelines draw attention to a collaborative team approach to CVD screening and risk factor management [19,67]. Additionally, European guidelines provide a Class I recommendation for multidisciplinary team approaches in the management of patients with elevated BP and hypertension, including appropriate and safe task-shifting away from physicians [67]. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials of interventions targeting barriers to hypertension control reported pharmacists and community health workers were most effective at leading BP intervention implementation [86], and non-physician community health-care provider-led intensive BP intervention was effective in reducing CVD and death [87]. Individuals could be encouraged to measure their BP at home with an automatic BP monitor, or their pharmacist could provide this service. Another possible approach is the provision of self-service kiosks for BP measurement at retail stores [88]. The pharmacist could also provide cholesterol measurement with a point of care device and provide ambulatory BP monitoring when required. BP- and cholesterol-lowering therapies could be initiated by a medical practitioner, with supervision and continuation of prescription medication by a pharmacist. Such supervision could include monitoring of BP and cholesterol levels and adjustment of therapy by the pharmacist. Alternatively, pharmacists could initiate BP- and cholesterol-lowering therapies. Pharmacists could also provide advice about side effects of medication and encourage clinician review when indicated. Pharmacist co-management was shown to substantially improve lipid management in individuals with an indication for high-intensity or moderate-intensity statins [89]. An example of allied health professionals assisting individuals to pursue guideline-directed therapies is the Coaching patients On Achieving Cardiovascular Health (COACH) study [90]. Telemedicine and mobile-health technologies, operating synergistically with public-health education models, may similarly assist individuals in achieving CVD risk factor control and CVD prevention [91,92]. A key advantage of empowering people to manage their BP and cholesterol levels, with support from pharmacists and allied health professionals, is reduction in the burden of prevention on primary care [93].

Challenges to implementation

A successful CVD prevention strategy, like that which produced the SunSmart program, would require the skills of a creative marketing agency. The SunSmart program was delivered to the community primarily through television advertisements, and the cost of television advertisements represents a major challenge to implementation, although telemedicine and social media may offer alternative means of delivery in some healthcare systems.

For cardiovascular health to become part of the school curriculum, teachers would need to be provided with the skills to implement the program, although teachers may be keen to implement the program because they may gain from the program with respect to their personal CVD prevention. Additionally, participation of a community nurse in school-based education would require funding. To address privacy considerations, students could anonymise information about family members in their school assignment.

Self-management of pharmacotherapy is well established, as illustrated by individuals with diabetes who adjust their insulin dose. Pharmacist support for self-management of BP and cholesterol levels would be an extension of the role pharmacists already play in health promotion. In Australia, pharmacists provide vaccinations, and are authorised to resupply oral contraception, and to supply an antibiotic for a urinary tract infection if appropriate. The role played by pharmacists and allied health in other healthcare systems would depend on the regulations that apply in those healthcare systems.

Need for more research

The success of the Victorian SunSmart program in preventing skin cancer is reason for optimism that a similar strategy may improve CVD risk reduction. However, this is an evidence-free area. Many different community-based childhood-targeted approaches to CVD prevention are possible. Strategies implementing television advertisements, school-based education, telemedicine, social media, pharmacists, allied health and other strategies would need to be optimised for different healthcare systems. Future studies are required to establish which strategies are feasible, achieve optimal CVD risk factor control, and impact CVD events in later life. The prediction of CVD events by childhood CVD risk factors provides strong support for a childhood-targeted approach to CVD prevention.

Conclusion

Despite considerable understanding of CVD risk factors and how their control can prevent CVD, current CVD risk reduction strategies do not fulfil their potential. Current guidelines emphasise the need for innovative strategies to address gaps in awareness and treatment of CVD risk [65]. A childhood-targeted education program that instils a mindset for cardiovascular health early in life that continues throughout life, analogous to the Australian SunSmart program for prevention of skin cancer, offers considerable potential to improve CVD risk factor control and to better prevent CVD.
