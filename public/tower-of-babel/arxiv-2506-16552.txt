: Dense Retriever Learning via Language Modeling
Fengyu Cai, Tong Chen, Xinran Zhao, Sihao Chen, Hongming Zhang, [8pt] Sherry Tongshuang Wu, Iryna Gurevych, Heinz Koeppl, [8pt] Technical University of Darmstadt, [8pt] Carnegie Mellon University

Abstract
Dense retrievers play a vital role in accessing external and specialized knowledge to augment language models (LMs). Training dense retrievers typically requires annotated query-document pairs, which are costly and hard to obtain in special domains, like code—motivating growing interest in self-supervised retriever learning. Since LMs are trained to capture token-level dependencies through a self-supervised learning objective (i.e., next token prediction), we can analogously cast retrieval as learning dependencies among chunks of tokens. This analogy naturally leads to the question: How can we adapt self - supervised learning objectives in the spirit of language modeling to train retrievers? To answer this question, we introduce , a unified and scalable training framework for self-supervised retriever learning via language modeling. models semantic dependencies among documents by conditioning next token prediction on both local and cross-document context through an in-batch attention mechanism. This attention is weighted by retriever-computed similarity scores, enabling the retriever to be optimized as part of language modeling. We evaluate on both general-domain (BEIR) and domain-specific (CoIR) benchmarks across various retriever backbones. At a comparable parameter scale, outperforms the previous best method with absolute improvements of 5.2% (18.3% relatively) and 5.6% (14.4% relatively) on NDCG@10, respectively, underscoring its effectiveness. Performance increases with model size, highlighting both the scalability of our approach and its promise for self - supervised retriever learning.

Introduction

Central to information retrieval are dense retrievers
(
reimers2019sentence
;
karpukhin-etal-2020-dense
;
ma2024fine
)
, which map queries and documents into high-dimensional vector spaces and determine relevance through similarity calculations.
Typically, these models rely on carefully annotated query-document pairs and hard negatives for training.
However, creating such high-quality training data requires substantial human annotation, which is labor-intensive and difficult to scale in real-world, domain-specific scenarios such as law
feng-etal-2024-legal
and programming
jimenez2024swebench
.
This limitation stimulates the interest within the community to explore self-supervised approaches for training retrievers directly from unannotated raw texts, i.e., self-supervised retriever learning
DBLP:journals/tmlr/IzacardCHRBJG22
.

Modern LMs
(
grattafiori2024llama
)
, a successful case of self-supervised learning, are typically pretrained with the next-token prediction (NTP) paradigm, modeling dependencies among tokens within a single sequence.
Analogously, retriever aims to model relationships among larger units—chunks of tokens—capturing more macroscopic dependencies.
This motivates us to raise a key question:
How can we learn a retriever within a self-supervised learning framework of language modeling?

While NTP implicitly identifies the most relevant parts in the context during generation, conditioning language modeling of one sequence on others can serve as an effective proxy for modeling inter-sequence relationships.
This can offer a novel and principled approach to self-supervised retriever learning.

In this work, we introduce a self-supervised retriever learning paradigm -- Dense Retriever Learning via Language Modeling, abbreviated as .

Related Works

Self-supervised Retriever Learning

Dense retrievers are typically trained with query-document pairs, requiring extensive human annotation. Given the abundance of unlabeled corpora, a key challenge in the community is:
How can we train a dense retriever in a self-supervised manner
?

One line of works leverages weak supervision within documents.
Contriever
(
DBLP:journals/tmlr/IzacardCHRBJG22
)
employs contrastive learning, treating passages from the same document as positives and using in-batch negatives, while Co
-
Condenser
(
gao-callan-2022-unsupervised
)
warms up Condenser
gao-callan-2021-condenser
with Wikipedia hyperlinks as pseudo labels.
However, these methods are limited by overly simplistic assumptions that structure-associated passages are labeled as pseudo-positives, failing to capture complex relational patterns.
Another common strategy is to distill from other retrievers, exemplified by LaPraDoR
xu-etal-2022-laprador
, which combines dense retrieval with BM25
robertson2009probabilistic

through Lexicon
-
Enhanced Dense Retrieval.
Last but not least, autoencoding methods, such as TSDAE
(
wang-etal-2021-tsdae-using
)
and RetroMAE
(
xiao-etal-2022-retromae
)
, learn sentence embeddings by reconstructing sentences from their neural representations.
Autoencoding often lacks pairwise or contrastive supervision, which can result in overfitting to low-level details
(
steck2020autoencoders
)
.
Inspired by the aforementioned approaches, our method trains retrievers by modeling dependencies within a document, generating each sequence conditioned on others within the same batch.

LM-guided Retriever Learning

LMs (e.g., GPT
(
NEURIPS2020_1457c0d6
)
, LLaMA
(
touvron2023llama
)
) are trained on trillions of tokens with billions of parameters, enabling strong generalization and improving retriever learning through richer semantics and training signals.

LMs can facilitate query and document expansion, as demonstrated by approaches like Query2Doc
(
wang-etal-2023-query2doc
)
and Doc2Query
(
gospodinov2023doc2query
)
.
However, leveraging such capabilities often requires advanced models, leading to high computational costs.
Augmenting LMs with relevant information from external knowledge stores not only improves performance in various NLP tasks but also enhances retriever learning.
Atlas
(
izacard2023atlas
)
utilizes cross-attention scores between retrieved documents and the generated output as signal to train the retriever.
However, it is based on an encoder-decoder architecture, which diverges from the prevailing trend of decoder-only LMs
(
touvron2023llama
;
achiam2023gpt
)
.
Additionally, it requires periodic index updates (reindexing)
(
guu2020retrieval
)
, leading to significant computational costs.
In contrast, our work focuses on leveraging the widely adopted decoder-only architecture while eliminating the need for reindexing by modeling relationships between chunks within the same document.

With the rise of decoder-only LMs, REPLUG
(
shi-etal-2024-replug
)
enhances retrieval by prepending retrieved documents to the queries, training retrievers to produce query-document similarity aligned with the LM’s perplexity.
However, the perplexity of
frozen
LMs is often poorly calibrated
(
geng-etal-2024-survey
)
, resulting in suboptimal retriever learning.
This issue can be optimized in where retrievers and LMs are updated jointly during language modeling.

Domain-specific Retrieval

In pre-training corpora, domain-specific knowledge is both scarce and rapidly evolving
(
grossmann2023ai
;
wen2025synthetic
)
, making effective retrieval in specialized domains critically challenging.
To enhance the adaptability of dense retrievers across domains, researchers have explored continual learning
(
sachan-etal-2021-end
;
oguz-etal-2022-domain
)
and task-aware training
(
cheng-etal-2023-task
)
.
However, these approaches still rely on query-document pairs from domain-specific datasets.
Another approach seeks to simplify domain-specific retrieval for general-purpose dense retrievers.
Cai et al.
cai-etal-2024-mixgr
propose a divide-and-conquer strategy through a mixed-granularity retrieval framework, significantly enhancing dense retriever performance in scientific domains.
Our work demonstrates ’s domain adaptation capability through language modeling on domain-specific raw texts.

3.1
Training Objectives

LM training typically includes the maximization of NTP for token sequences.
Given a batch of documents
\{D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{B}\}
and an LM parameterized by
\Phi
, classical NTP on the token
x_{l}^{i}
in
D_{i}=\{x_{1}^{i},\dots,x_{L}^{i}\}
, can be calculated as, where
x_{<l}^{i}
denotes the tokens preceding
x_{l}^{i}
in
D_{i}

\displaystyle P(x_{l}^{i})=P_{\Phi}(x_{l}^{i}\mid x_{<l}^{i}).

(1)

’s architecture. With a specific attention map, the embeddings of in-batch attention
\{\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
can attend to the self-attention
\{\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
.

As show in Fig.
2
, in , the NTP for sequence
i
is conditioned not only on its own preceding context
x_{<l}^{i}
but also on every other document in the batch,
\{D_{j}\}_{j\neq i}
.
Specifically, we introduce a new attention mechanism in the transformer blocks, i.e., in-batch attention (Sec.
3.2
), which is supported by a specific mask design (Sec.
3.4
).
The attentions are weighted by the inter-sequence similarity computed by the retriever parameterized by
\Theta
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})
(Sec.
3.3
), where
\sum_{j\neq i}^{B}\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})=1
.
The retriever is dynamically optimized as the similarity is updated jointly with the NTP objective

\displaystyle P_{R}(x_{l}^{i})=P_{\Phi,\Theta}\!\bigl(x_{l}^{i}\mid x_{<l}^{i},\{D_{j}\}_{j\neq i}\bigr)

(2)

3.2
In-batch Attention

augments the transformer block with an
in-batch attention
mechanism to incorporate context from other sequences.
We denote the output of
D_{i}
at the
l
-th layer as
[\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l};\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l}]
, where
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l},\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
represent the outputs from self-attention and in-batch attention, respectively.
For simplicity, we omit layer normalization and feed-forward layers, so the input to the two attention modules in the
l
-th layer is
[\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l-1};\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l-1}]
.

Standard Self-Attention

In the
l
-th layer of the blocks, the self-attention mechanism computes

Q_{i}^{e}=\mathrm{e}^{l-1}_{i}W^{Q},\quad K_{i}^{e}=\mathrm{e}^{l-1}_{i}W^{K},\quad V_{i}^{e}=\mathrm{e}^{l-1}_{i}W^{V},

(3)

where
W^{Q},W^{K},W^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are learnable projection matrices. For multi-head attention with
H
heads (each of dimension
d_{H}=d/H
), the standard
causal
attention is computed as

\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l}=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i}^{e}K_{i}^{e\top}}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\right)V_{i}^{e}.

(4)

In-batch attention

combines standard self-attention with cross-document attention.
The embeddings of
k
-th token in
D_{i}
is obtained by (1) the prefix of
D_{i}
, and (2) the other documents
\{D_{j}\}_{j\neq i}
based on their similarity to
D_{i}
.
This encourages
D_{i}
to selectively attend to more relevant documents based on learned retrieval signals.
For the contribution from the prefix of
D_{i}
, the self-attention output
\mathrm{s}_{i}^{l}
in the in-batch attention uses the same projection matrices as above

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{h}=\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l-1}W^{Q},\quad K_{i}^{h}=\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l-1}W^{K},\quad V_{i}^{h}=\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l-1}W^{V},\quad\mathrm{s}_{i}^{l}=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i}^{h}K_{i}^{h\top}}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\right)V_{i}^{h}.

Cross-document attention

enables
D_{i}
to attend to other documents
D_{j}
using cached keys
K_{j}^{e}
and values
V_{j}^{e}
. With a
full
attention mask, the output
\mathrm{b}^{l}_{ij}
is computed as

\mathrm{b}^{l}_{ij}=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i}^{h}K_{j}^{e\top}}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\right)V_{j}^{e}.

(5)

Normalization by value norms
prevents any single token from dominating the attention by scaling the output
\mathrm{b}^{l}_{ij}
with the norm of
V_{j}^{e}
, thereby encouraging the cross-document attention to focus on sequence-level semantics
izacard2023atlas
.
This operation, referred to as
V-normalization
, is computed as

\tilde{\mathrm{b}}^{l}_{ij}=\frac{\mathrm{b}^{l}_{ij}}{N_{ij}+\epsilon},\quad\text{where }N_{ij}=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i}^{h}K_{j}^{e\top}}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\right)\|V_{j}^{e}\|_{2}.

(6)

where
\epsilon
is a small constant for numerical stability.

Weighting by cross-document similarity
aggregates the normalized attention outputs
\tilde{\mathrm{b}}^{l}_{ij}
using the similarity scores
\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})
computed by the retriever

\mathrm{b}^{l}_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{B}\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})\,\tilde{\mathrm{b}}^{l}_{ij}.

(7)

Combined output
integrates the results of self-attention and cross-document attention to form the final output of the in-batch attention

\mathrm{h}^{l}_{i}=\mathrm{s}^{l}_{i}+\mathrm{b}^{l}_{i}

(8)

3.3
Similarity Computation

Given a batch of
B
documents
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
, the retrieval mechanism proceeds in three steps. First, each document
D_{i}
is encoded into an embedding
\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{E}}
using an encoder
E_{\Theta}
, such that
\mathbf{h}_{i}=E_{\Theta}(D_{i})
. Second, the embeddings are normalized and pairwise cosine similarities are computed:
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}=\mathbf{h}_{i}/\|\mathbf{h}_{i}\|_{2}
, and the similarity score between documents
i
and
j
is given by
S_{ij}=\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j}
. Third, temperature-scaled softmax with temperature
\tau
is applied to obtain probabilities across documents

\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})=\frac{\exp(S_{ij}/\tau)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp(S_{ik}/\tau)}.

The resulting weights
\mathrm{Sim}(D_{i},D_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times B}
capture cross-document similarities and are used to condition language modeling on relevant in-batch documents as presented above.

3.4
Implementation Details

As described earlier, adapts the classical transformers by additionally including in-batch attention, which builds upon standard self-attention, as shown in Eq.
5
.
For the minimum modifications to the existing transformer’s implementation, we take the computation of
\mathrm{e}
and
\mathrm{h}
as duplicating documents and adjusting the attention mask, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.
In this way, the embeddings
\{\mathrm{h}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
produced by in-batch attention can be obtained by applying full attention over the self-attention outputs
\{\mathrm{e}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
and aggregating them.

Experimental Details

For both retrievers and LMs, we apply LoRA with a rank of 256. Training uses the temperature
\tau

1\mathrm{e}{-4}
, the small constant
\epsilon

1\mathrm{e}{-6}
, a learning rate of
1\mathrm{e}{-4}
, and 100 warmup steps, following the WarmupDecayLR schedule.
We train on 4× A100 80GB GPUs with a gradient accumulation step size of 8.
Passages are truncated to 160 tokens, and bf16 mixed precision is enabled.
We finetune the models for one epoch, therefore, there are 10,000 steps on Wikipedia and around 11,000 steps on code-related texts.

Specifically, when calculating the similarity between chunks derived from Wikipedia, we follow REPLUG—using only the first half of the chunk to compute similarity with other chunks.
This design is motivated by the inferential semantics inherent in natural language retrieval tasks.
In contrast, for code retrieval tasks, we retain the full chunk when computing similarity, as these tasks rely more heavily on precise semantic matching compared to natural language.

6
Discussion

Future Directions

This work shows how to train a retriever via self-supervised language modeling on raw text.
We outline several future directions to suggest follow-up research.
(1)
Iterative Indexing
:
Sec.
5.2
emphasizes the importance of semantically related passages in ’s batch construction.
While uses document chunking, a more general approach would iteratively index documents and group chunks by on-the-fly representations.
Though explored in prior work
(
izacard2023atlas
;
shi-etal-2024-replug
)
, the high computational cost of such methods remains a key challenge for future work.
(2)
Scaling up
:
We envision several directions to scale up , including expanding data coverage, as discussed in Sec.
5.2
, and increasing model sizes, as analyzed in Sec.
5.1
.
Additionally, incorporating advanced attention mechanisms
(
yuan2025native
)
may further enhance retriever learning and accelerate the training process.
(3)
Potential Influence on LM Training
:
focuses on retriever learning, treating the LM as auxiliary.
LM performance is not analyzed, and it remains unclear whether in-batch attention affects it.
Batch construction significantly impacts convergence and model quality
(
wang2024greats
;
hong2024diversified
)
.
Future work could explore using retrievers to guide batch construction for LM training.

Limitations

We conclude the limitations of the works from the following perspectives.
(1) Evaluation: We only evaluate our methods on an English retrieval benchmark (BEIR) and code retrieval (CoIR).
More experiments on multilingual retrieval tasks can make the work more comprehensive.
(2) Model scales: As mentioned in Sec.
6
, the experiments on larger models (both retrievers and LMs) can better verify the generalization of .
(3) Dataset construction: As shown in Sec.
5.2
, depends on the intrinsic structure within natural language.
How to better leverage the structure for retriever learning is an open question, which has not been covered in this work.

7
Conclusion

Efficiently building information-seeking systems is crucial due to the swiftly evolving world and the wide existence of specific domains.
In this work, we introduce , a self
-
supervised framework that couples dense retrieval with language modeling through a novel in
-
batch attention mechanism, where a token attends to both local context and other sequences in the batch during NTP.
By letting the retriever’s relevance scores weight cross
-
sequence attention, transforms NTP into a retrieval signal, making use of high-quality and diverse raw text and eliminating the need for manual relevance labels.
Our experiments on both general and domain-specific benchmarks demonstrate significant performance gains over existing self-supervised methods, with improvements scaling with retriever size.
Further analysis on training data composition and LM size highlights the potential of as a strong and scalable alternative to traditional self-supervised retriever training paradigms, paving the way for more general and efficient retriever learning.

A.3
REPLUG

Self
-
supervised batch loss.

In our setup, as there are no external queries, each document plays the role of both query and context.
For a batch
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{B}\}
, we embed every document with the retriever
E_{\Theta}
:

s_{ij}\;=\;\frac{E_{\Theta}(D_{i})^{\!\top}E_{\Theta}(D_{j})}{\tau_{r}},\qquad P_{\Theta}(j\mid i,\mathcal{D})=\frac{\exp(s_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{B}\exp(s_{ik})}.

A frozen LM
g_{\Phi}
measures how well
D_{j}
explains
D_{i}
:

\ell_{ij}\;=\;-\!\log g_{\Phi}(D_{i}\mid D_{j}),\qquad P_{\Phi}(j\mid i,\mathcal{D})=\frac{\exp(-\ell_{ij}/\tau_{\!lm})}{\sum_{k=1}^{B}\exp(-\ell_{ik}/\tau_{\!lm})}.

We train the retriever by aligning these two distributions for every anchor document:

\mathcal{L}(\Theta)\;=\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(P_{\Phi}(\cdot\mid i,\mathcal{D})\;\|\;P_{\Theta}(\cdot\mid i,\mathcal{D})\bigr).

LM parameters
\Phi
remain fixed; only
\Theta
is optimized.

Experimental setups

Both of temperatures,
\tau_{\!lm}
and
\tau_{r}
, are set as 0.001.
REPLUG is training on the same datasets with , with the learning rate
5e^{-4}
and the training steps 4500.
All other experimental setups are identical to .

A.5
Implementation of Reciprocal Rank Fusion

To combine the strengths of BM25 and , we employ Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)
[
42
]
.
Given their ranked lists
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
for the same query, respectively, RRF assigns a fused score to every document
d
according to

\text{score}(d)\;=\;\frac{1}{k+\mathrm{rank}_{R_{1}}(d)}+\frac{1}{k+\mathrm{rank}_{R_{2}}(d)},

where
\mathrm{rank}_{R_{i}}(d)
is the
1
-based position of
d
in run
R_{i}
(undefined documents are ignored) and
k
is a constant that controls how quickly the contribution of lower-ranked documents decays; we follow common practice and set
k=60
.
After summing the two reciprocal ranks, the documents are re-sorted by this fused score, and the top 200 are retained as the final ranking delivered to RRF.
