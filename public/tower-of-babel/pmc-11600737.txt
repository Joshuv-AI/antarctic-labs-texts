Complete mole coexisting with a normal fetus in a dichorionic diamniotic pregnancy: a case report
Authors: Maryanne Mwangi, Mjahid Hassan, Justus Wambugu Muthoni, Sikolia Wanyonyi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA molar pregnancy coexisting with a normal fetus is a very rare occurrence. It can present as a complete mole with a normal fetus or a partial mole with a normal fetus. There is paucity of data on optimal management of such patients who have this presentation, which mostly ends with a poor prognosis.Case presentationWe present a case of 26-year-old woman of African descent, a para 1 + 0 gravida 2, who came with a compete molar pregnancy coexisting with a normal fetus in the second trimester and the complexity of her management. Patient presented in second trimester for an anomaly scan, in which the complete mole coexisting with a normal fetus was diagnosed. The molar pregnancy was also covering the cervical os. The patient was advised to terminate the pregnancy due to the risk of massive hemorrhage, but she was keen on continuing with the pregnancy. She was admitted two times with bleeding and in the last admission she also had chorioamnionitis, which warranted a hysterotomy at 25 weeks 1 day. Patient made a good recovery, unfortunately the neonate had an early neonatal death at a different facility due to respiratory complications.ConclusionIn this case a patient with a normal fetus coexisting with a complete mole and complete previa was closely monitored, but a hysterotomy was done at 25 weeks 1 day gestation due to chorioamnionitis. The diagnosis was made by ultrasound, in which there was a snowstorm appearance of one of the gestational sacs while the other sac had a normal fetus. This is a rare entity that puts the doctor in a dilemma due to potential maternal and fetal complications. The take-home message is that a patient who presents with a complete mole and a coexisting normal fetus can be supported to a viability stage. Chorioamnionitis interfered with the continuation of this pregnancy.Keywords: Complete mole, Complete coexisting with normal fetus, Complete placenta previa, Chorioamnionitis

Introduction

Complete hydatidiform mole with a coexisting normal pregnancy is an exceptionally rare occurrence, with a reported incidence of 1 in 22,000–100,000 pregnancies [1, 2]. It is also called sad fetus syndrome. The coexistence of a normal fetus with a molar pregnancy makes management controversial due to fetal and maternal complications [3]. Only 54 cases in the literature have had live births after a twin gestation with a molar pregnancy. Most cases end up as miscarriages. Maternal complications are also common, occurring in up to 50% of pregnancies, with a very high incidence of preterm delivery [4]. There is therefore paucity of data on optimal management options. This presents a clinical dilemma in the management of these patients[1, 5]. We present a case of a complete mole coexisting with a normal pregnancy complicated by chorioamnionitis, which necessitated an early delivery.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old African woman, para 1 + 0 gravida 2 at 19 + 1, presented to the fetal–maternal medicine unit for an anomaly scan. She had a history of preeclampsia in her preceding pregnancy and later experienced placenta abruption, necessitating an emergency cesarean section.

The ultrasound revealed a twin pregnancy with a single live fetus in one sac and a second sac with multiple cystic lesions separated by a membrane (Fig. 1). The ultrasound also revealed the typical snowstorm appearance that is consistent with a complete molar pregnancy (Fig. 2). The placenta of the molar pregnancy was also covering the internal cervical os (Fig. 3). There was no evidence of placenta accreta spectrum. The live fetus had normal anatomy and growth parameters, with an estimated age of 19 + 5 on ultrasound.

Initial investigations revealed a Hb of 12 g/dl, alpha fetal protein of 286, and Beta hCG of 165,780.40 mIU/ml. The patient had no invasive tests for karyotype or cytogenetic studies. The patient was counseled on the poor prognosis of her pregnancy as well as the risk of massive hemorrhage and advised to consider termination. However, she was keen on proceeding with the pregnancy, thus we advised her of the need for fortnight obstetric scans and close follow-up.

She presented 4 days later with per vaginal bleeding. On examination, she had normal vitals and speculum exam demonstrated a partially open cervical os with no active bleeding, vesicles, or products of conception. Fetal auscultation was normal. She was discharged to continue home monitoring.

Then, 4 days later she presented with bleeding, lower abdominal and back pain, and headache, as well as mild dizziness. Full hemogram revealed a drop in her Hb to 10.5 g/dl. Ultrasound findings were a viable fetus at 20 + 3 and the molar pregnancy. The patient was admitted for observation and consent was obtained for possible emergency hysterotomy in the event of massive hemorrhage. The patient still declined termination and was keen on proceeding with the pregnancy. Intermittent fetal auscultation was done daily. She remained stable and was discharged 2 days later after resolution of her symptoms with clear instructions on danger signs and a review scheduled in 1 week.

She presented at 25 weeks 1 day, 2 weeks after a second review post admission, with copious bleeding. She was also experiencing chills accompanied by lower abdominal pain radiating to her back. She could perceive fetal movement. Her blood pressure was 132/64 mmHg with a tachycardia of 104. Speculum exam revealed obvious molar vesicles in the cervix with no active bleeding or drainage of amniotic fluid. The fetal heart rate was was normal. A full hemogram noted a neutrophilia of 90.8% with a total white blood cell count (WBC) of 12.37 and absolute neutrophil count of 11.24. C-reactive protein was elevated at 92. Ultrasound revealed a single live fetus with normal morphology in variable lie at an estimated fetal weight of 782 g. Placenta was observed to be posterior and low lying. The fetal heart rate was 168. The complete molar pregnancy was not observed.

Post-admission, the patient became febrile with a temperature of 38.5 °C, tachycardia of 140 and blood pressure of 105/78 mmHg. Samples were taken for blood, High vaginal swab (HVS), and urine cultures. Amniocentesis was performed to rule out intraamniotic infection for the viable pregnancy. An antibiotic regimen was initiated with ceftriaxone, clarithromycin, and metronidazole as per departmental policy, as well as betamethasone, tocolysis, intravenous fluids, and magnesium sulphate was continous while the paracetamol was 6 hourly. The neonatology team was informed, and monitoring of vitals instituted every 4 hours.

Despite interventions, her vitals did not improve, and 6 hours later, she had a temperature of 40.2 °C, tachycardia of 134, tachypnea of 22, and blood pressure of 101/51 mmHg. Fetal auscultation demonstrated a tachycardia above 160. A decision was made to undertake an emergency hysterotomy in view of deteriorating maternal status, placenta previa, and breech presentation.

A live male infant weighing 770 g with an APGAR score of 1154 106 was delivered in complete breech. Resuscitation was required and intubation was performed, and baby was admitted in the neonatal intensive care unit. The placenta had multiple vesicles (Fig. 4). There were no operative complications and estimated blood loss was 600 ml.

Gross appearance of whole placenta post-delivery with vesicles around the margin

The postoperative recovery was unremarkable. Blood and HVS cultures grew Streptococcus agalactiae sensitive to ampicillin. Both urine culture as well as amniotic fluid microscopy, culture, and sensitivity were normal. The patient was discharged on day 3 post-delivery. The patient was lost to follow-up, therefore beta Human Chorionic Gonadotrophin levels were not monitored. The neonate was transferred to a different hospital on day 4 of life, where he succumbed a few days later due to respiratory complications.

Histopathology of the placenta showed features consistent with acute chorioamnionitis and peripheral molar pregnancy features (Fig. 5). The subsequent postoperative period was uneventful.

Discussion

Complete hydatidiform mole with a coexisting fetus (CHCF) is a rare occurrence. Previous literature has described CHCF and partial moles coexisting with a normal fetus. In the setting of a hydatidiform mole coexisting with a normal fetus, there are two pathophysiology mechanisms. In a partial mole, a normal ovum is fertilized by one sperm that divides or by two sperms to form a triploid or tetraploid cell. In a complete mole, an enucleated ovum gets fertilized by two sperms or by a sperm that divides to form, in most cases, a diploid cell [5]. A complete mole has no fetal parts. The genetic makeup of a complete mole is paternal. A complete mole coexisting with a normal fetus results from a dizygotic fertilization whereby a normal ovum is fertilized by normal sperm to create a normal fetus. The second ovum is enucleated and gets fertilized by a sperm that divides or by two sperm [6]. Vimercati et al.’s two case reports of CHCF where the karyotype was 46 XY and the other was 46 XX diagnosis was done by invasive second trimester testing. Hemida et al. did a case series of 12 participants with molar pregnancies coexisting with normal fetuses; 6/12 were CHCF while 4/10 were partial moles coexisting with normal fetuses, the other two did not do karyotyping [4]. Only 2 fetuses out of the 12 pregnancies survived.

The diagnosis is made by ultrasound and confirmed by histology of the placenta or an amniocentesis to analyze the karyotype of the existing fetus [7]. The management of this rare disease entity is determined on a case-to-case basis. CHCF commonly presents with bleeding, preeclampsia, thyrotoxicosis, preterm birth, spontaneous abortions, and poor fetal outcomes. Bleeding is a common presentation that is seen in 59% of cases with CHCF [8]. Persistent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia is also more common in CHCF as compared with other molar pregnancies. Literature shows that the risk of malignancy transformation is 20% in a complete mole as compared with 1.5% in a partial mole [9]. There is no consensus on the risk of GTN following CHCF with conservative management versus intervention. Studies have shown that incidence is higher in patients who choose to terminate the pregnancy, but the stage of disease is lower than those who carry on with the pregnancy [8]. Other studies have shown that there is no risk association between termination and incidence of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, thus the reason the pregnancy can be allowed to continue [10].

If the coexisting fetus is normal, conservative management can be adopted, and the pregnancy can be allowed to continue but with close follow-up [1]. Investigations that help in the diagnosis of CHCF include an ultrasound, maternal alpha fetal protein, and beta HCG levels. Cytogenetic studies by chorionic villus sampling of the affected fetus can help in the definitive diagnosis of a complete mole in contrast to a partial mole, in which case androgenic chromosomes will be seen in a complete mole as compared with a partial mole, which has both maternal and paternal chromosomes [11]. The levels of beta hCG in this case report (165,780 mIU/ml) were comparable with levels in previous case reports where an average beta HCG level of 150,000 mIU/ml was reported [8].

Differential diagnosis that needs to be considered in cases of CHCF is placental mesenchymal dysplasia; a benign vascular anomaly of the placenta whose appearance on imaging closely resembles molar pregnancy. However, in placental mesenchymal dysplasia, bHCG levels are usually within normal ranges, with a distinction between the two conditions made through placental histopathology or cytogenetic studies [12].

This case in unique in that the patient had CHCF, with the molar sac having placenta previa and features of chorioamnionitis that were unexplained. To our knowledge, only one similar case has been reported, with a live birth at 28 weeks with eventual survival [7].

The diagnosis of chorioamniotinis is challenging, but can be made on the basis of clinical findings, positive cultures from amniotic fluid, or histopathological assessment of the placenta. This poses a dilemma in management of patients with suspected chorioamnionitis, as amniotic fluid culture, despite being the most reliable test, requires time (up to 72 hours) while histopathological diagnosis is usually post-delivery [13]. Clinical diagnosis is highly subjective due to heterogeneous presentation of patients with a need to rule out other foci of infection. However, up to 38.1% of clinically diagnosed cases of chorioamnionitis may not be corroborated by histological findings (14). Typical clinical presentation of chorioamnionitis includes maternal fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, uterine tenderness, and fetal tachycardia. None of these are specific to chorioamnionitis. Our patient did not present with uterine tenderness and collected amniotic fluid on amniocentesis was nonpurulent and non-foul-smelling during delivery. However, there was a possible focus of infection from the open cervix and aborted complete hydatidiform mole. The initial plan was to initiate antibiotics and establish the focus of infection to avoid possible unwarranted premature delivery. However, due to deteriorating maternal status despite intervention—antibiotics, intravenous fluids, and antipyretics—a decision was made to proceed with delivery for maternal safety.

The uniqueness of this case is that the sac with the normal fetus remained sterile as confirmed by amniotic and blood cultures from the neonate. The patient also had a live birth, which is rare. We opine that the sac with the complete mole underwent degeneration, hence the Per vaginal (PV) bleeding. Moreover, the complete mole was the presenting sac, and during examination on her last admission, the cervix was open, and vesicles were observed on speculum. Degeneration may have led to the bleeding and infection of the complete mole, and this may explain why the patient was pyretic, tachycardic, and tachypneic.

The ultrasound features of snowstorm appearance, together with the delivery of a normal fetus and the placental histology, were confirmatory of a complete molar pregnancy coexisting with a normal fetus.

Conclusion

A hydatidiform mole coexisting with a normal fetus is an extremely rare occurrence and presents a challenge in management due to fetal and maternal complications.
