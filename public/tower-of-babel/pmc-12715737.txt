Hydrogels for Analyte Sensing
Authors: Katia Cherifi, Simon Matoori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydrogels have emerged as a versatile platform technology for analyte sensing, offering unique advantages in tunable chemistry, for loading with sensors across multiple length scales, and biocompatibility. These smart materials undergo predictable changes in optical properties, conductivity, swelling, and porosity upon analyte interaction, enabling their function as biosensors. While hydrogels can respond to a variety of stimuli, their responses are most effectively quantified through optical and electrical readouts, which enable direct, real-time, and quantitative sensing in complex biological fluids. Optical approaches leverage fluorescence, chemiluminescence, and colorimetry, whereas electrical approaches leverage conductive fillers or redox-active groups. Hybrid platforms integrate multiple readout mechanisms, enhancing sensitivity, robustness, and multiplexing capabilities. Many of these systems were validated in various biological matrices, such as interstitial fluid, sweat, and wound exudates. Beyond technical advances, we discuss translational challenges including selectivity, stability, nonreversibility, signal standardization, device portability, and regulatory approval, as well as emerging opportunities in coupling hydrogel sensors with artificial intelligence for improved data interpretation and clinical integration. Together, these developments position hydrogel-based diagnostics as promising candidates for next-generation, real-time, point-of-care biosensing.

Introduction

Hydrogels are materials formed by a network of cross-linked hydrophilic polymers. − Thanks to their biocompatibility and hydrophilicity, hydrogels have been widely used as vehicles for drug delivery or for tissue engineering with uses as wound dressings, − vaccines, − or structural scaffolds. − Historically, hydrogels were first discovered in the 1960s by Wichterle and Lim and were initially applied as contact lenses, highlighting their ability to retain large volumes of water while maintaining mechanical integrity. The following decades saw hydrogels being widely adopted for controlled drug delivery, tissue engineering, and other biomedical applications. −

In parallel with these therapeutic uses, hydrogels began to emerge as attractive materials for chemical and biological sensing due to their capacity to take up analyte sensing materials, high water content, tunable porosity, and biocompatibility. A seminal study in Nature in 1997 by Holtz and Asher demonstrated the use of polymerized colloidal-crystal-based hydrogels for analyte sensing of glucose and ions. The diffraction color of the microparticle-loaded hydrogel shifts in response to hydrogel swelling after analyte binding (ions) or a biochemical reaction (glucose). This pioneering work established the concept of using hydrogel swelling for analyte sensing. Building on this foundation, subsequent advances integrated hydrogel matrices into more complex systems that interact with a variety of analytes and generate optical, electrical and combined signals, where they function as scaffolds enabling the incorporation of sensing elements.

Recent advances in stimuli-responsive hydrogels, which allow the materials to undergo predictable changes in swelling, porosity, optical properties, or conductivity in response to specific biological or chemical triggers, have expanded the utility of hydrogels into the diagnostic space. − ,− ,,− As a sensing matrix, the hydrogel provides a tunable environment that translates analyte interactions (either via intrinsic polymer changes or via embedded probes) into measurable signals. Depending on the nature of the trigger and the design of the sensing system, these responses can be modulated by a variety of physical and chemical stimuli, including mechanical (transducing force or pressure); capacitive and inductive schemes (converting changes in dielectric properties or coil resonance); power-transfer approaches (delivering energy wirelessly to sensing elements); and (bio)­chemical interactions such as (de)­protonation, antibody recognition, enzymatic activity, or aptamer binding, with all responses ultimately quantified through optical or electrical signals. −

For quantitative analyte sensing in complex biological fluids, optical and electrical modalities remain the predominant sensing mechanisms, because they represent the most direct routes to achieve quantitative, real-time sensing in physiological environments. Such responsiveness makes hydrogels uniquely suited as sensing matrices that convert molecular recognition events into measurable analytical signals. When coupled with miniaturized or portable devices, they offer potential for point-of-care diagnostics. Together, the hydrogel matrix and transducing elements constitute a complete sensor capable of quantitative real-time analyte detection.

In this Perspective, we highlight emerging hydrogel-based analyte-sensing systems that enable quantitative and multimodal biosensing and discuss translational challenges and opportunities that will shape their integration into diagnostic workflow.

Optical Readouts

Optical readouts remain among the most versatile and widely explored detection strategies for hydrogel-based diagnostics. In these systems, the hydrogel functions as the sensing material, undergoing physical or chemical changes upon exposure to an analyte (stimulus) that lead to a change in spectrophotometric properties. The optical detection system (fluorometer, camera, or spectrometer) contains the transducer, converting these optical changes into measurable electrical signals using photodiodes, photomultiplier tubes, or charge-coupled device (CCD) sensors. The combination of the hydrogel and the detector that includes electrical transducers constitutes a complete sensor capable of quantitative readouts. Common optical hydrogel sensors exploit fluorescence, absorbance, and chemiluminescence.

Light-Emitting Hydrogels (Fluorescence and Chemiluminescence)

Fluorescence is the emission of light by a substance after it absorbs energy, typically ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared light. When loaded with fluorophores, hydrogels can leverage the high sensitivity of fluorescence to sense biomarkers.

Fluorescent peptide-linked hydrogels were engineered to recognize specific DNA sequences associated with cancer mutations, where hybridization events induce conformational changes in the peptide-fluorophore conjugates, leading to an increase in fluorescence intensity. In this design, short, charged peptides engineered to gel in aqueous solution were covalently conjugated to azide-functionalized fluorophores. This capturing probe, attached to PEG cores, binds to mutant DNA, forming a gel that allows for the detection and quantification of the number of DNA mutations based on the size exclusion of the hydrogel-DNA complex (Figure A). The integration of cross-linking-peptides in the recognition probes enhanced the hydrogel’s fluorescence response, correlating proportionally with the number of EGFR mutations detected in vitro. The system achieved a linear response across the tested range, with a total assay time of approximately 1 h (Table ), outperforming conventional probes.

Overview of Hydrogel-Based Diagnostic Sensors and Their Signal Readouts

Similarly, pH-sensitive fluorescent hydrogels have been developed for the real-time monitoring of wound microenvironments. Embedded pH-responsive dyes incorporated in hydrogels undergo protonation or deprotonation, depending on local acidity, producing measurable changes in fluorescence intensity. In one example from our lab, an anionic pH-sensitive dye was adsorbed onto cationic microparticles and then incorporated into an alginate hydrogel, which minimized dye leakage and provided stable optical response within minutes in full-thickness excisional mouse wounds ex vivo (Figure B). The sensor demonstrated high linearity across physiologically relevant pH values (Table ), confirming its potential for wound diagnostics.

Chemiluminescent hydrogels provide light-emission through enzymatic- or catalysis-driven reactions within the hydrogel without the need for external excitation. In one example, an alginate hydrogel incorporating luminol, horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and cholesterol oxidase (COD) produced a chemiluminescent signal through enzymatic cascade reactions that correlated linearly with cholesterol concentration in human serum in vitro (Table ), enabling smartphone-based quantification (Figure C). Similarly, luminol was embedded in a chitosan hydrogel doped with Co2+ ions, which catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen peroxide via a slow-diffusion-controlled heterogeneous mechanism. This hydrogel emitted a sustained chemiluminescent signal in response to H2O2 in vivo, detectable for up to 60 min (Table ).

Light-Absorbing Hydrogels (Colorimetric)

Colorimetric hydrogels change the absorbance in the visible part of the spectrum in response to specific analytes, producing shifts detectable with the naked eye or simple optical devices. The simplicity of the detection makes them especially appealing for point-of-care diagnostics.

Building on our previously described fluorescent microparticle-loaded alginate hydrogel (Section ), we increased the loading of the microparticles with the pH-sensitive dye to enable colorimetric readouts. In this second design, as the wound environment becomes more alkaline, the dye-loaded microparticles shift from a nearly white color to bright yellow, with increasing color intensity translating into decreasing blue channel values in the RGB analysis. By shifting from fluorescence-based detection to visible color changes, the hydrogel can now be analyzed using smartphones for RGB quantification, and we further developed a home-built Arduino-based RGB detector to mitigate differences in smartphone camera models that can impact RGB analysis. Ex vivo testing in full-thickness excisional mice wounds demonstrated rapid pH sensing, translated into a drastic decrease in blue channel values when pH was increased (Table ), providing both qualitative and quantitative colorimetric readouts suitable for point-of-care pH monitoring (Figure B).

A carbon-dot-doped hydrogel sensor array was developed for simultaneous colorimetric detection of five wound biomarkers and/or wound condition indicators (pH, glucose, urea, uric acid, and total protein). In this system, agarose hydrogels serve as the supporting matrix, while embedded carbon dots interact with target analytes, inducing their aggregation and producing a visible color change that can be quantified through smartphone-based RGB analysis. The hydrogel also incorporates chromogenic substrates and organic dyes, with the carbon dots facilitating electron transfer to catalyze color-generating reactions, while the agarose network stabilizes and compartmentalizes the encapsulated multienzyme cascade. The five sensors exhibited consistent linear detection over clinically relevant concentration ranges of all wound biomarkers tested (Table ), and the platform was validated ex vivo using wound fluid collected from both healthy and perturbed rat wound models, demonstrating that color intensity accurately reflected analyte concentration (Figure D).

In holographic hydrogels, analyte-induced swelling or shrinkage of the hydrogel mechanically modulates its internal photonic structure, producing a diagnostic signal without the need for added dyes or enzymes. The holographic Ca2+ ion sensor consists of a carboxylate-rich poly­(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate/methacrylic acid) (poly­(HEMA/MAA)) responsive matrix with imprinted interference layers forming a holographic grating, fabricated on a flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate. Swelling or shrinking of the hydrogel in response to Ca2+ modulates the grating, producing a color change from dark red to red within 7 min, visible to the naked eye and quantifiable by smartphone. This sensor demonstrated linear detection across the tested Ca2+ range, enabling a quantitative wound assessment (Table ). This label-free detection method was validated ex vivo in simulated wound fluids, demonstrating that clinically relevant Ca2+ concentrations indicative of wound infection could be accurately measured (Figure E).

Electrical Readouts

Electrical hydrogels leverage their high water content, ionic conductivity, and tunable permeability to facilitate charge transfer at embedded electrodes. In these systems, the hydrogel functions as the sensing material, where analyte interactions alter local ion concentrations or redox states, which constitute the initial stimulus. These physicochemical changes are then converted to a transducer. Together, the hydrogel and the detector form a complete sensor capable of quantitative analyte detection. While hydrogels can respond to diverse stimuli (e.g., mechanical deformation, ionic exchanges, or biochemical reactions), the configuration of the reporter probe and transducer dictates whether the output is electron- or ion-mediated. Hydrogel matrices can be engineered to enhance signal transduction through the incorporation of conductive fillers, modulation of dielectric properties, or chemical functionalization with redox-active groups. −

Conductive, Inductive and Capacitive Hydrogels

Hydrogels doped with conductive fillers, including carbon nanomaterials, metal nanoparticles (NPs), or intrinsically conductive polymers, enhance electron transfer to the transducer, producing measurable and sensitive current changes in response to target analytes. Simpler filler-based hydrogels employ metal NPs such as gold, silver, and platinum embedded in the hydrogel pores, where these particles serve as the primary sensing elements. Carbon-based nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes or graphene oxide, have also been used to facilitate electron transport, providing high sensitivity in electrical-based biosensing. − ,

More complex composite conductive hydrogels combine filler-based and polymer-enhanced strategies to synergistically improve signal transduction. A representative example integrated a highly water dispersible conductive polymer, poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), into a dopamine-hyaluronic acid hydrogel loaded with platinum and silver nanoparticles (Figure A). In this system, the integration of PEDOT:PSS polymer enhances the hydrogel’s bulk conductivity, ensuring rapid and uniform electron transport, while the metal NPs are the fillers and sensing elements that nonenzymatically oxidize glucose and measure the resultant current. This microneedle-based platform was further validated in dermal interstitial fluid, achieving a high linearity (Table ), covering the hypoglycemic range that is challenging for most current continuous glucose monitoring devices and was successfully applied in vivo in a rat model of type 1 diabetes.

Other hydrogel systems exploit inductive sensing mechanisms, in which changes in the hydrogel’s dielectric or swelling properties modulate the resonance characteristics of an inductor-capacitor (LC) circuit, a coil-capacitor system that resonates at a characteristic frequency. A recent example is the development of a wireless, battery-free glucose sensor in which a glucose-responsive ferroparticle-loaded hydrogel is integrated with an LC resonator and a low-power application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC). In this system, glucose-mediated swelling or dielectric changes perturb the coil’s inductance and resonant frequency, which are detected wirelessly, converting biochemical interactions into measurable frequency shifts (Figure B). , This prototype sensor achieved sensing across the pathophysiological glucose range in glucose standard solutions (Table ). In this configuration, the hydrogel acts as a tunable dielectric element that modulates the coil’s resonance, enabling wireless transduction of biochemical events.

Capacitive hydrogel sensors have been engineered to operate as dielectric and charge-storage interfaces where analyte-induced conformational and volumetric changes within the hydrogel matrix modulate the capacitance at the electrode-hydrogel interface. For instance, a flexible single layer hydrogel composed of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) conjugated with poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (pNIPAm) microgels, sandwiched between thin gold electrodes to form a wearable capacitive sensor. In this system, the embedded AuNPs enhance the dielectric constant and polarization of the hydrogel matrix, while the thermoresponsive pNIPAm network provides a deformable and analyte-responsive structure. Exposure to external stimuli, such as temperature, mechanical deformation, bacterial adhesion, or metabolite interaction, induces microgel contraction or dielectric modulation, producing measurable capacitance changes proportional to analyte concentration (Figure C). The platform demonstrated multimodal sensing capabilities, enabling real-time detection of physiological signals on human skin, such as respiration and pulse, identification of Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis in sweat samples, and quantification of uric acid through ferrocene- and uricase-functionalized microgels in sweat samples (biomarkers of metabolic balance and oxidative stress) (Table ). This capacitive transduction mechanism differs from inductive systems by relying on direct modulation of dielectric and interfacial parameters rather than resonance frequency shifts, offering a compact and low-power modality for wearable biosensing.

Ionic and Electrochemical Hydrogels

Hydrogels functionalized with ionic or redox-active groups can transduce chemical reactions into electrochemical signals. A dual-function wearable microneedle sensor was developed for continuous, minimally invasive monitoring of levodopa (L-DOPA) in interstitial fluid from Parkinson’s patients, combining enzymatic chronoamperometry with nonenzymatic square-wave voltammetry on separate microneedles within the same patch. This microneedle patch was designed with alternating microneedle compositions: unmodified carbon paste electrodes (nonenzymatic sensing) directly oxidize the catechol group of L-DOPA to dopaquinone, while tyrosinase-modified electrodes (enzymatic sensing) that catalyze its conversion to dopaquinone via molecular oxygen; in both cases, electron transfer signals are transmitted through the hydrogel to the underlying electrode (Figure D). , Continuous ex vivo measurements demonstrated high sensitivity for both enzymatic and nonenzymatic sensing, enabling real-time monitoring of therapeutic levels (Table ).

A thin hydrogel micropatch incorporating redox-active moieties was developed for caffeine and lactate sensing in sweat. Accessing sweat for continuous biomarker monitoring has been challenging due to low sample volumes, variable secretion rates, and the need for minimally invasive collection, which often requires active stimulation that can introduce analyte variability. This agarose hydrogel overcomes these challenges by passively wicking fingertip sweat, concentrating analytes within its hydrated porous network, and providing a stable microenvironment for enzymatic activity and redox reactions (Figure E). For lactate detection, lactate oxidase embedded in the electrode catalyzes the conversion of lactate to pyruvate and hydrogen peroxide, with the electroactive H2O2 subsequently oxidized at the electrode and detected by amperometry, enabling reliable and sensitive quantification in 50 s (Table ). In parallel, caffeine is directly oxidized at the electrode surface by square-wave voltammetry, with analyte collection from the hydrogel being nearly three times higher than a dry pad and quantified by LC-MS/MS. In both cases, the hydrogel’s ionic network supports rapid electron transfer to the underlying electrodes, converting biochemical reactions into stable electrical readouts. Testing in human volunteers demonstrated strong correlations between analyte concentrations and electrochemical signals, with lactate measurements stabilizing under a minute. This approach also grants access to naturally secreted fingertip sweat, which is typically inaccessible with conventional collection methods. This feature allows coupling analytical sensing with fingerprint-based biometric verification.

Hybrid Readouts

Hybrid hydrogel platforms integrate multiple measurement modalities within a single material, providing complementary signals that can enhance sensitivity, selectivity, robustness, and accessibility. By combining multiple optical and/or electrical signals for the same analyte, these systems allow cross-validation of measurements to reduce false positives and negatives. They can also allow for multiplexing if a set of analytes cannot all be detected by the same measurement modality.

Fluorescent and Electrochemical Readouts

Hybrid hydrogels integrating both fluorescence and electrochemical signals allow complementary readouts, enabling the real-time cross-validation of optical and electrochemical outputs. This addresses the key challenges of more common monitoring systems, including photobleaching and physiological variability.

A fluorescent nanodiamond-based microneedle device was developed for continuous glucose monitoring. The hydrogel, comprised of phenylboronic acid-functionalized polyacrylamide containing nanodiamonds, is covalently bound to the porous barrel of the microneedle. The excitation and emission signals are transmitted through the microneedle via optical fibers, while the hydrogel’s ionic network transduces the glucose-induced redox change to an electrode (Figure A). In vivo testing in healthy mice and pigs, which received intraperitoneal injections of either glucose alone or glucose-followed insulin, demonstrated that the device could successfully monitor blood glucose levels in real time. The hydrogel exhibited a nearly linear fluorescence response to glucose levels in interstitial fluid (Table ), allowing real-time in vivo monitoring and demonstrating improved stability and reliability when compared to standard commercial systems.

Colorimetric and Electrochemical Readouts

Similarly to fluorescent and electrochemical combinations, colorimetric and electrochemical systems have been reported to achieve the visible optical signal of colorimetry while providing the quantitative, rapid, and sensitive signal of electrochemistry. The development of a hydrogel-based microfluidic biosensor composed of a cross-linked DNA network functionalized with aptamers, tested in vitro for organophosphorus pesticide detection exploits this dual-readout: the recognition of malathion by aptamers induces hydrogel collapse, producing a visible color change for rapid, on-site sensing, while simultaneously, the hydrogel’s interaction with embedded electrodes generates a robust electrochemical response, supporting reliable quantitative detection (Table , Figure B).

Conclusions and Outlook

Hydrogels offer unique opportunities for analyte sensing and monitoring with various measurement modalities. Their chemical versatility, biocompatibility, and encapsulation capacity enable biosensing in wound fluid, sweat, interstitial fluid, and other low-volume fluids. This provides new avenues for point-of-care diagnostics using optical, electrochemical, or hybrid readouts. −

As there is a growing interest in hydrogel-based diagnostics for point-of-care use, the development of small and portable detectors will become increasingly important. Key issues are the standardization, robustness, and reproducibility of signal detection, data analysis, and signal interpretation. For instance, smartphones have emerged as convenient readout tools due to their ubiquity and integrated imaging capabilities, yet variability in camera specifications between brands and models, lighting conditions, and user handling all introduce significant challenges for reliable quantification. , To date, no smartphone-based colorimetric diagnostics have received FDA approval, likely due to these limitations. , This highlights a broader need for analytical solutions that balance robust and reproducible measurements while maintaining accessibility and portability for end-users.

Several challenges for analyte-sensing hydrogels have been identified. Among these are cross-sensitivity and lack of specificity, where the hydrogel’s response to one analyte is influenced by secondary environmental stimulus, such as pH, ionic strength, or temperature. To address this issue, highly specific biomolecular recognition elements (enzymes, aptamers, small molecules) have to be developed; using hybrid readouts for the same analyte may provide a cross-validation. Furthermore, it is important for hydrogels to avoid signal drifts and to rapidly return to their baseline state to enable reversible sensing and biomarker monitoring over time. Nonsymmetric reversibility describes an inability of the system to return back to the baseline state, for instance due to irreversible chemical reaction, and hysteresis is the time lag between analyte binding and the hydrogel’s relaxation back to its baseline state. − ,, To minimize signal drift, hydrogel systems that are highly stable in biological fluids need to be developed. Rapid reversibility can be achieved by developing analyte sensing reactions with rapid (bio)­chemical and physical equilibria. Moreover, quantitative metrics such as limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), accuracy, precision, linear range, and response kinetics are essential for benchmarking hydrogel-based devices against established reference tests.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning approaches offer powerful tools to bridge these gaps by enabling a more consistent and automated interpretation of the measurement outputs of hydrogel sensors. In data analysis and interpretation, AI can compensate for environmental variability (e.g., light conditions) automatically. In material development, AI-driven models can accelerate formulation design by predicting hydrogel properties based on polymer composition and environmental stimuli. Furthermore, AI can support multiplexed hydrogel platforms by distinguishing overlapping signals from multiple sensing sites, potentially enabling real-time, multianalyte sensing with minimal user intervention. However, the integration of AI requires high-quality training data sets and rigorous validation in clinically relevant scenarios to ensure data privacy, unbiased analysis, and regulatory acceptance.

Translating these platforms into clinical practice requires navigating regulatory frameworks. Hydrogel-based diagnostics are considered medical devices, and depending on similarity to existing devices, may pursue FDA 510­(k) clearance for substantial equivalence or the De Novo pathway for first-in-class systems. Hydrogel sensors with optical probes (chromophores, fluorophores) must address biocompatibility, sterilization, and the safety and release kinetics of probes embedded in the hydrogel matrix. , To date, no hydrogel-based diagnostics have received FDA approval, underscoring the challenges in bridging technological innovation with regulatory compliance. , Early engagement with regulatory strategies is critical for guiding design and accelerating translation.

In summary, hydrogel-based platforms offer a unique combination of material versatility, biocompatibility, and direct-contact sensing and monitoring capabilities, complementing or replacing existing diagnostic technologies. Overcoming challenges in specificity, stability, robustness, and regulatory compliance, while leveraging AI, multiplexing, and innovative sensing strategies, will define the next generation of hydrogel diagnostics. With continued progress, these systems hold the potential to provide real-time, point-of-care and continuous monitoring solutions that detect diseases and complications early, and that guide personalized treatment decisions. ,,
