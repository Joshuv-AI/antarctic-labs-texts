Nutrient balance regulates soil microbial health under long-term fertilization
Authors: Da Lin, Qing-Xu Ma, Yu-Qiu Ye, Wolfgang Wanek, Andrew S. Gregory, Davey L. Jones, David W. Graham, Dong Zhu, Josep Penuelas, Yong-Guan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFertilizer application in intensive agriculture critically influences microbial communities. It is still unclear how long-term input of different nutrients shapes microbial eco-evolutionary strategies and ecological functions. Through 180-year-old field fertilization experiment, alongside microbial culturing, pot experiments, and comprehensive metagenomic data analysis, we show that exclusive fertilization with inorganic chemicals causes carbon-nitrogen imbalances that increase microbial resource competition and antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) levels. Viruses further amplify this expansion through “piggyback the winner” strategy. The imbalanced use of nitrogen in chemical fertilizers disrupt ecological niche connections, leading to an increase in virulent viruses and reducing microbial nutrient cycling capacity. In contrast, more balanced nutrient supplies from organic fertilization reduced microbial competition and promoted microbial growth. However, responsible antibiotic use in livestock is essential to maximizing these benefits. Our research provides insights into enhancing agricultural sustainability through the management of soil nutrient conditions.

Introduction

Soils provide a range of ecosystem services essential for sustaining life and serve as the foundation of agriculture.1 The ability to sustainably support plant productivity and other ecosystem functions is known as soil health.2,3 Soil microbial communities play an important role in the decomposition and storage of organic matter, nutrient cycling, and plant growth.4,5 Therefore, soil microbial health is central to overall soil health, which is vital for maintaining ecological balances and supporting sustainable agriculture.6 Current understandings of soil microbial health typically encompass the diversity and functional redundancy of microbial communities (e.g., driver of resistance and resilience against disturbance),7 growth rates (e.g., resource absorption efficiency),8 life history traits (e.g., “competitor,” “stress tolerant,” and “ruderal” [CSR] selection strategies),9 nutrient cycling,10 and health risks (e.g., ARGs and pathogenicity).11 However, our understanding of how soil microbial eco-evolutionary strategies respond to resource types and balances, and their consequences for soil microbial health, remains quite limited. Clarifying these knowledge gaps is important for defining agricultural best practices and promoting sustainable food production.

Fertilization is a key agricultural practice that greatly impacts soil microbial life by supplying available resources and altering the physicochemical properties of soils.12 Modern agriculture has come to depend upon the intensive use of chemical fertilizers, with nitrogen input being a common method for maintaining soil fertility and boosting crop yields.13 Although chemical fertilization provides enough nutrients to meet the demands of crop growth, it alters the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio in the soils, which increases the carbon demand of microbial communities.14 This has been shown to cause competition among bacterial communities and enhance the presence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs).15 However, the impact of nitrogen availability regulating soil microbial health still requires further exploration. In contrast, organic fertilization not only supplies adequate nutrients but also introduces diverse bioavailable carbon sources. This approach is considered more effective for achieving sustainable agriculture, as it encourages the reuse of organic waste and improves the bioavailability of soil organic matter. However, since many organic fertilizers, such as manure, are contaminated with ARGs and antibiotics due to the use of antibiotics and biocides in animal rearing,16 a significant threat to the health and sustainability of global soils is presented.17,18 As such, the use of animal manure for fertilization is controversial, despite its apparent superiority in maintaining better soil health.

Viruses are the most abundant and widely distributed biological regulatory factors in soils and were reported to respond more sensitively to fertilization than bacteria.19,20,21 Fertilizer management practices can alter the composition of viruses,22 reshape their interactions with hosts,23 and potentially change their contributions to nutrient cycling processes, including carbon24 and nitrogen25 cycles, as well as the spread of ARGs.26 As a result, viruses may play a crucial role in regulating microbial adaptation and ecosystem functions in response to nutrient variations caused by different fertilization methods. Viruses can be temperate or virulent, depending on their infection strategies.27 Temperate viruses integrate their DNA into the host chromosome and can regulate the metabolism of hosts through AMGs to support viral replication. When the fitness of both the host and the virus are aligned, the host may benefit from these AMGs. Researches have shown that viruses can carry AMGs related to carbon metabolism, such as GT75, and phosphorus-cycling gene, like phoB, in nutrient-poor soils, which enhance host competitiveness and environmental adaptability.15,19 Virulent phages influence microbial survival by lysing host populations. They facilitate the direct return of related nutrients to the food chain by breaking down host cells, thereby creating a unique “viral shunt” in the micro-food web and accelerating the carbon cycling processes.28 However, the interactions between viruses and their hosts, and their potential impact on soil microbial health under different fertilization regimes, remain largely unexplored.

Microbial communities appear to alter their ecological strategies to adapt to different agricultural nutrient management patterns. Although the impact of such microbial adaptations is increasingly recognized,29 the ecological and evolutionary consequences associated with different adaptation strategies and the role of viruses in these processes remain enigmatic and require further exploration to be understood. We hypothesize that (1) the varying nutrient conditions resulting from fertilization drive different microbial adaptation strategies, thereby affecting soil microbial health and that (2) balanced nutrient fertilization approach promotes better soil microbial health by ensuring the appropriate proportions of bioavailable nutrients and labile carbon and stimulating beneficial microbial interactions. Additionally, long-term experimental results can enhance the accuracy of detecting such microbial adaptation patterns. This is because microorganisms need a long time to stabilize their response to continuous fertilization.30,31 Long-term research can provide the temporal resolution required to understand the causes, consequences, and permanence of microbial adaptation. Additionally, considering long-term effects is crucial for achieving sustainable development.32 However, to our knowledge, only a handful of studies have explored fertilization effects over 60 years30,33 and only one exceeding 100 years.34

Here, we used a long-term (180 years) experimental field trial, combined with microbial culturing and pot experiments, and collated extensive metagenomic data to examine the impact of nutrient variation resulting from different fertilization strategies on soil microbial health in agricultural ecosystems. The experimental site, established since 1843 at Rothamsted Research,34 involved different fertilization regimes: no fertilization, chemical fertilization with three nitrogen levels (nitrogen-deficiency [PK], normal nitrogen [NPK], and high-nitrogen [HNPK]), and organic fertilization. The main objectives were to (1) characterize the impact of long-term fertilization with different fertilizers on soil microbial health by examining changes in microbial communities (including diversity, interactions, and growth) and functional traits (e.g., nutrient cycling ability, ARGs, and pathogenicity), (2) elucidate the ecological adaptation patterns of soil microbial communities driving these changes, and (3) identify the key factors influencing soil microbial health in organic fertilization to help clarify the ongoing debate surrounding organic farming and fertilization practices (Figure 1).Figure 1Schematic diagram of study(A) Our study combined 180 years of long-term field experiments with extensive metagenomic datasets to examine the impact of long-term fertilization on soil properties, microbial community features, and microbial functional traits.(B) Ecological adaptation patterns of the soil microbial communities driving the above changes were elucidated and validated through microdiversity analysis and microbial culture experiments.(C) Identify the key factors driving soil microbial health in organic agriculture through two consecutive years of wheat pot experiments.

Results

Effect of long-term fertilization on soil microbial health

The soil pH and microbial biomass nitrogen (MBN) were significantly lower, and the microbial biomass carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N) was significantly greater with an increasing nitrogen gradient after 180 years of continuous chemical fertilization (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.05; Table S1). Conversely, organic fertilization resulted in higher levels of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus contents in the soils (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.05; Table S1), whereas the pH level and microbial biomass C:N ratio did not significantly differ from the control plots (one-way ANOVA, p > 0.05; Table S1).

Long-term fertilization caused significant differences in the composition of soil microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and viruses (p < 0.01; Figure S1). The changes in fungi (R2 = 0.76), protozoa (R2 = 0.65), and viruses (R2 = 0.43) were significantly linked to shifts in bacterial communities (p < 0.01; Figure 2A), indicating a cross-community microbial response to differences in fertilization practice. Compared to no fertilization (CK), normal chemical fertilization (NPK) resulted in higher relative abundances of animal and zoonotic pathogens (p < 0.05; Figure S2A). Normal chemical fertilization resulted in soil functional homogenization, which resulted in lower functional diversity (p < 0.05; Figure S2D), and greater relative abundances of total and viral carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) genes, ARGs, and virulence factor genes (VFGs) (p < 0.05; Figure 2E). Further, nitrogen-deficient and high-nitrogen inorganic fertilization resulted in lower mineralization rates of soil nitrogen and carbon (p < 0.05; Figure 2F). Conversely, long-term organic fertilization resulted in the greatest functional diversity and mineralization rates for both soil carbon and nitrogen among regimes (p < 0.05; Figures 2F and S2D).

Ecological adaptation patterns of soil microbial communities

To understand how different fertilization practices affect microbial adaptation strategies that drive observed changes, multi-table co-inertia analysis (MCOA) was used to integrate information from four functional databases (life history traits, eggNOG, KEGG, and CAZy). The life history traits database was constructed based on genomic traits associated with life history strategies.9 MCOA1 and MCOA2 accounted for 31.0% and 24.2% of the bacterial functional variation, respectively (Figure 3C). The development along MCOA1 was associated with a tendency toward resource acquisition strategies (Figure 3C). The bacterial communities under normal chemical fertilization were positioned at the upper end of MCOA1, characterized by expanded metabolic capacities (Figure 3C). This included increased genetic potential for the degradation of oligosaccharides and complex polysaccharides in fungi, animals, and plant lignin; siderophore production; transporter systems; pathogenic interactions with humans; and ARGs (p < 0.05; Figures 3C and S4A). As bacteria reduce their metabolic capacity along the negative direction of the first principal component (MCOA1), MCOA2 separated communities based on genomic traits related to “stress tolerance” and “growth yield,” forming a triangle (Figure 3C). The bacterial communities associated with organic fertilization was positioned at the upper end of MCOA2. These communities reflected faster growth rates and an apparent enrichment of genes related to key nutrient cycles, including carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, while exhibiting the lower relative abundances of ARGs and VFGs (p < 0.05; Figures 3C and S4B). Bacterial communities resulting from no fertilization, nitrogen-deficient conditions, and high-nitrogen chemical fertilization were positioned at the lower end of MCOA2 (Figure 3C). This was manifested by an increased presence of genes involved in foraging of beneficial conditions (motility and chemotaxis) and in the building and repair of cell membranes (p < 0.05; Figures 3C and S4B).

Analysis of the collated metagenomic data indicated that compared to no fertilization, both chemical and organic fertilization resulted in greater prevalence of temperate viruses (p < 0.05) and relative abundances of AMGs (Figure S5A). Further analysis revealed that chemical fertilization led to an increase in putative AMGs associated with carbon utilization, whereas organic fertilization enhanced putative AMGs related to amino acid and nucleotide synthesis (i.e., promoting bacterial growth) (p < 0.05; Figure S5B). Medium-quality metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were extracted from all samples, and their viral-host associations were predicted. The Pyromonadaceae family, characterized by a large genome, carried a significant proportion of CAZy genes (47.5% of the total), ARGs (38.5% of the total), and VFGs (34.0% of the total) (Figure 4A). Changes in microbial microdiversity and gene expression

Evolutionary forces shaping the function of soil bacterial communities under different fertilizations were explored. Compared to other fertilization, the nucleotide diversity of bacterial and viral communities was notably higher under organic fertilization (p < 0.05; Figure 4B). Additionally, the proportion of non-synonymous to synonymous mutations (i.e., pN/pS) in genes and the percentage of genes under positive selection (i.e., pN/pS > 1) increased with organic fertilization (p < 0.05; Figures 4C and S6A). However, normal chemical fertilization led to an increase in total single-nucleotide variations (SNVs) in bacterial genomes (p < 0.05; Figure 4D). It also boosted the SNVs and pN/pS in ARGs, VFGs, and CAZy genes, thereby enhancing the nucleotide diversity of these functions (p < 0.05; Figures S6B and S6C).

To confirm the varying response of bacterial strain to nutrient changes under different fertilization conditions, pure culture experiments were conducted with Pseudomonas aeruginosa—a common human bacterial pathogen and a major host of ARGs frequently found in soils36—under three nutritional conditions: simulated no fertilization (oligotrophic medium only: Reasoner’s 2nd Agar [R2A] medium37), simulated chemical fertilization (oligotrophic medium with added nitrogen in the form of ammonium nitrate), and simulated organic fertilization (oligotrophic medium with nitrogen and carbon from glucose). Overall, the growth rates and gene expression patterns in the strain tested varied significantly across different treatments (p < 0.05; Figures S7A and S7B). In the chemical fertilization simulations, the cultivation of a strain resulted in the lowest growth rates but highest expression of ARGs and CAZy genes (p < 0.05; Figures S7A–S7C). Compared to the no fertilization and organic fertilization conditions, the cultivation of a strain under simulated chemical fertilization conditions upregulated both ARGs (e.g., mexV, APH(3′)-IIb, mexE, and catB7) and CAZy genes (e.g., GT1, AA1, GH102, GT51, and GH23) (p < 0.05; Figure S7D). Conversely, the cultivation of a strain in simulated organic fertilization media displayed the highest growths and reduced expression of ARGs and CAZy genes (p < 0.05; Figures S7C and S7D).

Identifying key factors influencing soil microbial health in organic fertilization

The meta-analysis revealed that both chemical and organic fertilization significantly increased the relative abundances of ARGs and VFGs across most categories in the soils compared to no fertilization (p < 0.05; Figure S8). This prompted us to more deeply investigate the effects of organic fertilizers on ARGs observed in the metagenomes versus our long-term field experiments. Since these long-term field experiments began before the discovery and mass use of antibiotics and sources of organic fertilizers (i.e., manure) may have changed with time, the effects of antibiotic use may be inconsistent with time, which has been observed previously.30 Therefore, we aim to clarify whether the spread of soil ARGs caused by organic fertilization is primarily due to human activities, such as feeding antibiotics to animals, or if organic fertilization itself (without antibiotic use) is more beneficial for overall soil microbial health. To assess this, wheat pot experiments were performed over two consecutive years employing four fertilization regimes: no fertilizers, chemical fertilization, organic fertilization using manure from animals reared with antibiotics, and organic fertilization from animals without exposure to antibiotics (Figure S9). The two organic fertilizers were manure from pigs fed either antibiotic-containing or antibiotic-free fodder, respectively. The relative abundances of ARGs were significantly higher in the organic fertilizer from animals exposed to antibiotics compared to those without antibiotic exposure (p < 0.05; Figure S10A). Organic fertilization resulted in higher soil relative abundances of bacteria compared to chemical fertilization, while also reducing the relative abundances of soil ARGs in the absence of manure with antibiotic exposure, similar to the long-term field experiment (p < 0.05; Figures 5A and 5B). In addition, over the 16 growth stages of wheat cultivation across 2 years, regardless of antibiotic contamination, the organic fertilization resulted in higher soil ammonia oxidation rates (antibiotic-exposed: 12.31–25.28 μg N kg−1 h−1; no antibiotic exposure: 6.48–29.43 μg N kg−1 h−1) and soil denitrification rates (antibiotic-exposed: 10.89–21.93; no exposure: 9.12–19.94 μg N kg−1 h−1) compared to chemical fertilization (p < 0.05; Figures 5C, 5D, and S10B). Discussion

The consequences of different long-term fertilization regimes on agricultural soils were examined and showed that organic fertilizers, such as manure, were more beneficial to soil microbial health than chemical fertilizer options. This was assessed by analyzing changes in microbial communities, including diversity, interactions, and growth, and functional traits such as nutrient cycling ability, ARGs, and pathogenicity. Specifically, extended chemical fertilization intrinsically produces C:N imbalances in soils, triggering greater bacterial investment in extracellular enzymes (e.g., CAZys) needed for carbon scavenging. Under chemical fertilization, bacteria must invest more energy to produce diverse extracellular enzymes to depolymerize organic compounds for energy and nutrient acquisition38 and develop heightened defense mechanisms, e.g., antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity.39,40 Conversely, the use of organic fertilizers provides carbon and other nutrients in a more balanced manner, reducing apparent bacterial stress and the need for and levels of cell defense systems, including ARGs and VFGs in resident bacteria.

In a nutshell, results here suggest our global use of chemical fertilizers in agriculture is driving an unrealized increase in intrinsic antibiotic resistance in soils, which has implications for soils and wider health.5 Chemical fertilizers not only enhance resource acquisition but also provide a competitive advantage for some bacterial traits in soils. In particular, microdiversity analysis suggests that bacteria appear to enhance the fixed probability of specific beneficial traits, such as the acquisition and production of CAZy genes, ARGs, and VFGs, which improve competitiveness for limited carbon resources.15 Furthermore, the dependence on specific and more limited carbon resources results in soil microbial functional homogenization, which reduces functional diversity and buffering capacity,41 and the selection of antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity traits, offering survival advantages to selected microorganisms.39,42 Additionally, the results indicate that chemical fertilization with excessively low or high levels of nitrogen can impair resource acquisition and ability of resident bacterial communities. Finally, pressures exerted by virulent viruses and eukaryotes under chemical fertilization forces bacterial communities to adopt stress tolerance strategies,10 which reduces the nutrient cycling capacity of soil microbial communities.9

In contrast, organic fertilization provides both inorganic nutrients and labile organic carbon, simplifying the resource acquisition capacity of bacteria, redirecting energy toward growth,43 and stimulating communication and cooperation among fungi, protists, viruses, and bacteria, ultimately enhancing microbial nutrient cycling.44,45 The overall microbial communities after extended organic fertilization exhibits the higher species, functional and nucleotide diversity, and enhanced nutrient cycling capacity, while simultaneously reducing the need for and selection of ARGs and VFGs. These results are strongly supported by the field experiment data and plant pot.

Results here also underscore the potential role of viruses in influencing ecosystem processes and biogeochemical cycles in agricultural systems. With chemical fertilization, the apparent temperate prevalence, as well as putative AMGs related to carbon utilization, CAZy genes, ARGs, and VFGs in viruses, all increased. Bacteria with larger genomes, due to their enrichment of CAZy genes, ARGs, and VFGs, are expected to exhibit stronger performance under resource-poor and competitive pressures.46,47 The stronger performance of these bacteria leads to higher abundance compared to other bacteria in the community, making them more likely to be hosted by phages. This allows both phages and their hosts to collaboratively compete for carbon resources and adapt to environmental pressure while occupying a broader ecological niche.48 This phenomenon is known as the “piggyback the winner” effect.21

The insufficient or excessive use of nitrogen fertilizers in chemical fertilization increased the abundances of virulent viruses. As previously reported,49 virulent viruses may accelerate carbon cycling by breaking down and releasing intracellular substances through the “viral shuttle” effect, thereby alleviating soil nutrient limitations and supporting microbial survival. However, this effect appears to result in lower growth yields. On the other hand, in soils enriched with carbon after organic fertilization, viruses could carry increased AMGs that modify the amino acid and nucleotide synthesis in the host to enhance viral replication. These modifications may be associated with increased metabolic activity in the infected hosts,50 thereby boosting microbial growth and activity. These virus-host interactions highlight how microbial relationships respond to changes in nutrient availability, with viruses acting as mediators of ecological adaptation and soil microbial health. Viruses often form relationships with their hosts, and their regulatory role in agricultural processes warrants greater consideration in the future.

Our research presents a conceptual framework explaining ecological adaptation patterns of microbial communities and their impact on soil microbial health under different fertilization strategies (Figure 6). It highlights the trade-off between growth yield (which results from organic fertilizer application, promoting the productivity of soil ecosystems; beneficial for soil microbial health) and stress tolerance (which arises from excessive or insufficient use of nitrogen fertilization, leading to increased prevalence of virulent viruses and lower bacterial productivity; detrimental to soil microbial health) (Figure 6). Additionally, it also emphasizes the trade-off between growth yield and resource acquisition (which is caused by normal chemical fertilization, resulting in reduced functional buffering capacity and increased ARGs and VFGs, with viruses amplifying this impact) (Figure 6). This framework helps predict microbial performance under varying nutrient conditions and provides a foundation for exploring methods to enhance agricultural sustainability through the management of soil microbial communities.Figure 6Conceptual frameworkA conceptual framework illustrating the ecological adaptation patterns of microbial communities and their impact on soil microbial health under different fertilization strategies. Furthermore, the pot experiments independently corroborate the negative impact of chemical fertilization on soil microbial health. Clearly, organic fertilizers (i.e., manure) are better for soil microbial health, but how the manure itself contributes to ARGs in the treated soils depends on the antibiotic used in the animal rearing. The introduction of manure from animals treated with antibiotics can significantly increase soil ARG levels, as shown in numerous studies.51,52,53 In contrast, manure from animals not treated with antibiotics does not pose this risk. Given the superiority of organic fertilization over chemical fertilization for soil health and the reduction of secondary negative effects (e.g., increased ARGs and VFGs), the importance of regulating antibiotic use in animal husbandry and promoting the responsible application of organic fertilizers is critical. Currently, the advantages of chemical fertilization over organic fertilization in agricultural consumption remain apparent.15 However, we show here that soils that have relied solely on chemical fertilizers for extended periods may have lower microbial productivity, and the food produced could be exposed to higher levels of ARGs. Studies have shown that this can pose potential health risks to humans, as demonstrated through models of the soil-plant-stomach-small intestine transmission chain.54 We advocate for a broader understanding of the importance of soil microbial health as a key component of overall soil health, particularly within the “One Health” framework, which recognizes the close links between environmental, animal, and human health.55 Given the current global threat of antibiotic resistance to public health,56 priority should be given to agricultural practices that minimize the impact on antibiotic resistance, particularly the use of antibiotic-free organic fertilizers.

Overall, our findings suggest that carbon-nitrogen imbalance caused by chemical fertilization negatively impacts soil microbial health, primarily by reducing microbial diversity and exacerbating the selection of antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity within soil ecosystems as an adaptation to carbon limitations. Further, the insufficient or excessive use of nitrogen fertilizers can increase prevalence of virulent viruses and reduce nutrient cycling capacity. In contrast, organic fertilization, characterized by optimized nutrient coordination, greatly enhances soil microbial health by promoting rapid growth and diversity of microbes and improving nutrient cycling. This study enhances our understanding of the trade-offs in soil microbial survival strategies and their impact on soil microbial health associated with fertilization management. Consider the current dominance of chemical fertilization in food production and its cost to soil health, promoting organic farming holds significant value if antibiotic contamination in organic fertilizers can be effectively controlled. This provides valuable insights for optimizing sustainable agricultural solutions.

Limitations of the study

While this research has conducted an in-depth analysis of the impact of nutrient variations on soil microbial health, it is important to acknowledge the potential caveats regarding the research design and genomic analysis that need to be addressed in future studies. First, given the inherent limitations of metagenomic analysis, such as the challenge of distinguishing between active and inactive microbes, its ability to directly quantify soil microbial health is limited. Further studies are needed to fully understand and address these issues, incorporating microbial isolation and characterization, and active microbial technologies (e.g., analyzing transcriptional activity in active microbes). Second, since natural community samples often yield limited input DNA, MDA is commonly employed prior to sequencing library preparation in soil virome studies.57 However, it is important to note that MDA can introduce bias by preferentially selecting small circular single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) templates and unevenly amplifying linear genome fragments,57 despite our study focusing on relative comparisons among treatments rather than absolute counts to minimize this bias. Third, the short-term physiological response observed in our microbial culture experiment offers a brief mechanism explanation of how resource environments associated with fertilizers can induce coordinated transcriptional responses within a single bacterial genome. However, it lacks the ecological complexity and evolutionary timescale needed to replicate community-level interactions or long-term adaptation processes observed in soil environments, highlighting the need for more complex community systems and longer-term cultivation to address these issues.

Experimental models and study participant details

Soils

The soils for field experiment is a Chromic Luvisol, with the soil texture ranging from clay loam to silty clay loam, overlying clay with flints.31 The soils for microcosm experiment were collected from the top 0–20 cm of sandy loam used for wheat cultivation in Ningbo, China (29°47′ N, 121°21′ E) in March 2021. After mixing, air-drying, and sieving through a 2 mm mesh, the soils were prepared for the pot setup.

Bacterial strains

Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated from field soils. Briefly, 100 μL of soil suspension in PBS was spread onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates. Following incubation at 37°C for 16 h, colonies with distinct morphologies (e.g., margin and color) were selected and purified by streaking to obtain pure strains. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 27F and 1492R for bacterial identification. The confirmed strains were then cultured in LB medium in shaker tubes at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 1.0, after which they were mixed with an equal volume of 50% glycerol and stored at −80°C.

Method details

Field experiment

The experimental site, Broadbalk Field, is situated at Rothamsted Research in Harpenden, Hertfordshire, United Kingdom (51°48′34.1″ N, 0°22′22.8″ W). The Broadbalk Winter Wheat ‘Classical’ experiment is the longest-running continuous agronomic experiment in the world, established in 1843. In general, the experiment has maintained a continuous design, except for changes in fertilizer amount or type, which have been adjusted several times according to soil conditions or crop yield. The experiment began in 1843; however, the treatments varied slightly in their initial years before becoming fully established in 1852. We selected the following treatments: (1) no fertilization; (2) nitrogen-deficient chemical fertilization, with 35 kg ha−1 year−1 of P and 90 kg ha−1 year−1 of K; (3) normal chemical fertilization, with 144 kg ha−1 year−1 of N, 35 kg ha−1 year−1 of P, and 90 kg ha−1 year−1 of K; (4) high-nitrogen chemical fertilization, with 192 kg ha−1 year−1 of N, 35 kg ha−1 year−1 of P, and 90 kg ha−1 year−1 of K; and (5) organic fertilization, with 35 t ha−1 year−1 of farmyard manure from cattle. The nitrogen application rate in the organic fertilization treatment is approximately 144 kg ha−1 year−1, equivalent to that of the normal chemical fertilization treatment. Detailed information on this experiment is publicly available online (https://www.era.rothamsted.ac.uk/experiment/rbk1). All treatments were implemented on plots continuously cultivated with winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.); cultivar details are provided in Table S2.

The application of modern statistics to agricultural experimental designs began only in 1908. As a result, early stages of this experiment did not incorporate modern experimental design principles such as replication and randomization. To ensure the rigor and scientific validity of the work and subsequent statistical analyses, we selected Section 1 of the experiment, which was continuously planted with wheat, and divided each treatment plot into four sub-plots. We collected approximately 20 soil cores from a 0–23 cm depth (plow layer) in each sub-plot using a 2.5-cm diameter gouge auger on May 19, 2022. The soil samples were then mixed to create a composite sample for each sub-plot. In total, 20 soil samples were thus obtained. Soil samples were collected approximately eight months after fertilization to minimize the immediate effects and focus on the long-term impacts of each fertilization regime, which led to contrasting nutrient profiles after 180 years.

Soil samples were transported to the laboratory within three hours in insulated containers on ice. Upon arrival, visible roots and stones were removed, and the soils were ground with liquid nitrogen before being passed through a 2 mm mesh sieve. All handling was carried out using sterile tools to minimize contamination. After pre-processing, each soil sample was divided into two parts: one was stored at 4°C for soil property analysis, and the other stored at −80°C for DNA extraction and virome preparations.

Analysis of soil properties

Soil pH and electrical conductivity were analyzed in soil-water suspensions (1:2.5 w/v). Gravimetric soil moisture was measured after oven-drying the samples at 105°C for 24 h. Soil organic matter (OM) content was determined by heating the soil in a muffle furnace at 450°C for 16 h. The available carbon (C) content in the soil was assessed after extraction with 0.5 M K2SO4 (soil: K2SO4 solution ratio of 1:5 w/v). The dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was determined using a Multi N/C 2100S TOC-TN analyzer (Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany). The NH4+ and NO3− contents in the soil extracts were determined colorimetrically at 667 and 540 nm, respectively, using a PowerWave HT microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). Soil microbial biomass carbon (MBC) and nitrogen (MBN) contents were analyzed using the chloroform fumigation extraction method. DOC and dissolved organic nitrogen (DON) in both fumigated and non-fumigated soils were measured using the multi N/C 2100S TOC-TN analyser. MBC and MBN were estimated using a conversion factor of 0.45 and 0.38, respectively.79 The soil carbon mineralization rate was measured by placing 30 g of field-moist soils in a 200 mL container and monitoring CO2 production daily using an EGM-5 portable infrared gas analyzer (PP Systems International Inc., Amesbury, MA). The 15NH4+ and 15NO3− pool dilution methods were used to measure the gross production and consumption of NH4+ and NO3−, respectively. Briefly, 2 mL of 0.1 mM 20% atom 15NH4+ or 15NO3− was added to 20 g of fresh soils. Extraction was performed using 0.5 M K2SO4 after incubating the soils at 25°C for 3 and 24 h. The NH4+ and NO3− concentrations, as well as the 15N isotopic ratios of 15NH4+ and 15NO3− in the soil extracts, were determined using microdiffusion and isotope ratio mass spectrometry.80 Gross rates of NH4+ and NO3− production (GP) and consumption (GC) were calculated using the following equations.GP=(Q1-Q2)ln(A1/A2)(t1-t2)ln(Q1/Q2)GC=GP−(Q2-Q1)(t2-t1)

Among these variables, Q1 and Q2 represent the NH4+ and NO3− concentrations, respectively, while A1 and A2 represent the 15N atom% in NH4+ and NO3− at times t1 (3 h) and t2 (24 h), respectively, after the NH4+ and NO3− pool was labeled.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, DNA was extracted from 0.5 g soil per sample using the Fast DNA Spin Kit For Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA). Each 100 ng soil DNA sample was then subjected to amplicon sequencing. The amplicons for 16S, ITS, and 18S rRNA were generated using the Illumina MiSeq PE 300 platform with the primers 515F/806R, ITS1F/ITS2R, and Euk1391f/EukBr, respectively.81 The sequencing was performed at Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). The primer sequences used for amplicon sequencing are listed in Table S3. 16S, ITS and 18S sequence analysis was performed using Qiime2 v2023.06.82 Raw paired-end reads were filtered, and chimeras were removed with the DADA2 plugin. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were clustered at 100% similarity according to the representative sequences. The representative sequences of 16S rRNA were aligned using the Multiple Bacterial Pathogen Detection (MBPD) pipeline58 to identify pathogens responsible for diseases in animals, humans, and plants. The MBPD database58 containing full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences of 1,986 reported bacterial pathogen species covering 72,685 sequences.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing of each 1 μg DNA sample was performed using the Illumina HiSeq platform, operated by Majorbio Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw reads from the long-term field experiments were trimmed using Fastp v0.22.083 to remove low-quality sequences. The clean reads were analyzed using local ARG-OAP (version 3.2) against the structured ARG reference database (SARG)84 and the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria core database (VFDB)85 to annotate potential ARGs and VFGs, respectively. The annotations were based on the following criteria: identity ≥80%, hit length ≥75%, and E-value ≤10−7. ARG-OAP (version 3.2) can simultaneously estimate the copy of 16S rRNA genes by referring to the Greengenes database86 and estimate cell numbers by referring to bacterial essential single copy marker genes.59 The relative abundances of ARGs and VFGs were normalized by adjusting the read abundance to the length of each gene and the gene copy number to the total cell count in each sample (i.e., copies/cell).

The clean reads from all samples were independently assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT v1.2.960 with the ‘--min-contig-len 500’ parameter. We then used EukRep v0.6.761 to identify and remove potential eukaryotic contigs, ensuring that subsequent analysis focused on bacterial sequences. The open reading frames (ORFs) of the contigs were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3.62 Then, all ORFs were clustered into a set of unique genes using CD-HIT v4.8.1,87 with a sequence identity threshold of 95% and an alignment coverage of 90%. The set of unique genes was queried against the KEGG databases using BLASTX in DIAMOND v2.0.14.15263 to identify the corresponding proteins and retrieve their functional annotations, with an e-value cutoff of 1e−5. The specific functions and pathways for each sample were determined based on the KO results, utilizing the pathways mapped by the annotated genes in the KEGG Pathway Database. The predicted genes were converted into amino acid sequences and compared with the Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZy) database and the eggNOG database using BLASTX in DIAMOND v2.0.14.152,63 with an e-value cutoff of 1e−5. Gene abundance in each sample was calculated by mapping raw reads to the non-redundant gene catalog, taking into account gene length and read counts (i.e., Transcripts Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads, TPM), using Bowtie2 v2.5.0.88 To understand how different fertilization practices affect soil bacterial ecological adaptation traits, multitable co-inertia analysis (MCOA) was used to integrate information from four databases: life history traits, eggNOG, KEGG, and CAZy. The life history traits database was constructed based on genomic traits associated with life history strategies.9,39,40,64 Details are shown in Table S4. Life history traits and other functional genes used in this study, Table S5. Function of KO used in this study, Table S6. Function of COG used in this study, Table S7. Function of CAZy enzymes used in this study.

Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were recovered from these metagenomes using VAMB v2.1.1.65 The quality of the obtained MAGs was assessed with CheckM v2.1.1,67 and only medium- and high-quality MAGs with a completeness >50% and contamination <10% were retained for further analysis. The relative abundances of MAGs were calculated as TPM through a three-step process. First, we the number of reads mapped to each MAG was normalized by the total length of its genome to eliminate the influence of differences in genome size among the MAGs. Next, the normalized read counts of all MAGs within a single sample were summed to correct for differences in sequencing depth. Finally, the proportion of each normalized read count of the MAGs relative to the total normalized read counts was converted into a million-scale value, resulting in the TPM value for each MAG in the sample. Taxonomic and phylogenetic analyses were performed using GTDB-Tk v1.7.035 and visualized in iTOL (https://itol.embl.de). Protein-coding sequences of the MAGs were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3.62 The sequences were then aligned against the CAZy, SARG, and VFDB databases using BLASTX with DIAMOND v2.0.14.152,63 applying the following criteria: identity ≥60%, coverage ≥70%, and E-value ≤10−5.

Population microdiversity metrics, such as nucleotide diversity, single nucleotide variations (SNVs), and their selection measures (pN/pS), were calculated using InStrain v1.0.0.66 The maximal growth rate of these MAGs and contigs was estimated by gRodon v.1.89

Virome sequencing and analysis

Virus-like particles (VLPs) from the 10 g soil samples were initially purified and concentrated using filtration and centrifugation, following a previously established protocol.90 Before viral DNA extraction, the virus concentrates were incubated with DNase I at 37°C for 1 h to remove exogenous DNA. Subsequently, the VLP suspension was used for viral DNA extraction with the Takara MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit Ver. 5.0. The DNA extracted from the viral fraction was then amplified using multiple displacement amplification (MDA) to generate shotgun metagenomic libraries. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the HiSeq X platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

After removing or trimming low-quality reads with Trimmomatic v0.3968 using default parameters, the remaining high-quality reads were used for sequence assembly with MEGAHIT v1.2.9.60 Viral sequences were identified from the assembled contigs (≥2,000 bp) using a combination of Virsorter2 (v2.0),69 DeepVirfinder (v1.1),70 and VIBRANT (v1.2.1).71 All contigs meeting the criteria of 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) and 85% alignment fraction (AF) were clustered into viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) using MUMmer v4.0.0.72 The completeness and contamination of these vOTUs were then estimated using CheckV v1.0.1 (database v1.2).91 CheckV was also employed to remove vOTUs identified as the provirus before downstream analyses. After aligning the clean reads from each sample to all vOTUs using Bowtie2 v2.5.0,88 read counts were normalized to Transcripts Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (TPM) enabling the relative abundances of vOTUs to be compared across samples. The protein-coding sequences of viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3.62 Viral putative AMGs were identified using DRAM-v (v1.2.0)73 with default parameters. To reduce the risk of false positives from host contamination, only putative AMGs situated between two viral genes or adjacent to viral genes were considered as viral putative AMGs for further analysis. The relative abundances of viral putative AMGs were also calculated using TPM with Bowtie2 v2.5.0.88 Sequence alignment against the CAZy, SARG, and VFDB databases was performed using BLASTX of DIAMOND v2.0.14.152.63 A sequence was classified as ARG or VFG fragment if it met the following criteria: identity ≥40%, hit length ≥75%, and E-value ≤10−7. A sequence was classified as CAZy fragment if it met the criteria: E-value ≤10−5 and bit score ≥50. The abundances of viral ARGs, VFGs, and CAZy were also calculated using TPM with Bowtie2 v2.5.0.88

The lifestyle of vOTUs was predicted using two pipelines: (1) Deephage v1.074 was employed to classify sequences as lytic or lysogenic with a cutoff of 0.6; (2) Functional annotation was performed for protein-coding sequences of vOTUs against Pfam v35.0 using ‘hmmscan’ in HMMER v3.1b2,75 with an E-value threshold of <10−5. Sequences containing lysogenic marker proteins (e.g., transposase, integrase, kinase, lyase, and recombinase) were selected for identification as lysogenic viruses. The vOTUs identified by these pipelines were classified as either lytic or lysogenic, while others were considered unknown.

In this study, three computational strategies based on nucleotide sequence similarity were employed for host prediction: (i) CRISPR spacer bar matching. CRISPR spacer sequences from the bacterial genome were predicted using the CRISPR recognition tool (CRT, v2.1).76 Detected spacer sequences were queried against the viral genome using the BLASTn-short77 function with the following parameters: at least 95% identity over the entire spacer length, allowing 1–2 SNPs at the end of the sequence, and E-value ≤10−10; (ii) tRNA matching. tRNA genes of vOUTs were annotated from using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.9)92 and then compared to prokaryotic genomes using BLASTn,77 with a threshold of >95% sequence identity; (iii) Nucleotide sequence homology. Prokaryotic genomes were aligned with viral genomes based on shared genomic regions using BLASTn.77 Matches that met the following criteria were retained: bit score ≥50, nucleotide identity ≥70%, match length ≥2500 bp, and E-value ≤10−3.

Analysis of microbial interactions and networks

The β-diversity of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and vOTUs among samples was calculated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, utilizing the vegan package.78 Linear regression analyses were conducted to investigate the relationships between β-diversity in bacterial communities and other microbial communities, using a two-sided test for statistical analysis. The ASVs of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and vOTUs were selected for co-occurrence network analysis to assess cross-community microbial associations. To minimize the impact of rare taxa, those with a detection rate below 10% across all bacterial, fungal, protozoan, and viral sequences were excluded. Spearman correlation matrix for each taxa using the WGCNA package.93 The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to adjust p-values, thereby reducing the likelihood of false-positive results.81 The co-occurrence network was constructed, consisting of nodes and edges. Each node represents a taxa, while the edges indicate their significant positive or negative correlations (spearman’s |r| ≥ 0.70, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05). Sub-networks for each soil sample from the meta-community networks were created by preserving the taxa present in each sample using the subgraph function from the igraph package.94 Network-level topological features, including network connectance (C = E/N2, where E is the number of edges and N is the number of nodes) and the rate of positive correlation between bacterial and other microbial communities, provided by igraph94 then calculated for each sub-network.

Metagenomic dataset collection

In September 2024, a literature search was conducted on Web of Science, Google Scholar, and PubMed using the keywords ‘organic fertilizer’, chemical fertilizer’, and ‘metagenome’ to collect metagenomic data and better understand general patterns in soil microbial traits in response to various fertilization practices. To minimize potential confusion during analysis, we carefully filtered the metagenomic samples from these studies based on the following criteria: (1) Chemical fertilization needed to include nitrogen fertilization to better align with our field experiments; (2) Samples without geographic coordinates were excluded, as they would not enable the identification of biogeographic patterns; (3) Only soil samples from field experiments were included; (4) Only paired-end sequencing reads in FASTQ format were included; (5) The treatments needed to include paired plots within the same experimental sites to enable paired comparisons (e.g., chemical fertilization vs. organic fertilization, chemical fertilization vs. no fertilization, organic vs. no fertilization). (6) unrealistically high fertilizer application amounts were excluded. After careful screening, we totally collected 184 metagenomic datasets from 20 field experiments, including 39 with no fertilization, 78 with chemical fertilization, and 67 with organic fertilization. All metagenomic datasets were downloaded and re-analyzed using methods and conditions for annotating microbial traits that were consistent with those employed in our experiment. Detailed information about the metagenomes is summarized in Table S8. Furthermore, soil properties reported in these studied were collected, including pH, carbon-related nutrient indices (e.g., total carbon (TC), soil organic carbon (SOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), microbial biomass carbon (MBC), β-glucosidase, particulate organic carbon (POC), and potential carbon mineralization (PCM)), nitrogen-related nutrient indices (e.g., total nitrogen (TN), ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N), nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N), and urease). The effect of organic fertilization on pH, carbon-related and nitrogen-related nutrient levels compared to chemical fertilization were characterized by averaging the fold-change of their corresponding indicators.

Meta-analysis: Compare the effects of different fertilization on soil microbial traits

Meta-analysis method described by Yang et al.95 was employed to compare the soil microbial trait indicators between no fertilization and chemical fertilization, no fertilization and organic fertilization, and chemical fertilization and organic fertilization (referring to the control and treatment in meta-analysis terminology). The dataset for this analysis was integrated from our long-term field experiments and samples collected from various other field studies. Response ratio (RR) was used to measure the effect sizes between different fertilization regimes on these indicators. The metafor package in R software was used to compute the weighted RR and the bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence interval. If the 95% confidence interval did not overlap with zero, the effects of different fertilization regimes on the related variables were considered significant (p < 0.05). Potential publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression via the ‘funnel’ and ‘regtest’ functions. The absence of publication bias was indicated by a symmetric distribution of studies in the funnel plots. Additionally, potential asymmetry of the funnel plots was evaluated using Egger’s regression. It was determined that there is no publication bias in the effects of different fertilization treatments on the related variables in our meta-analyses.

Microbial culturing experiment

A single colony of Pseudomonas aeruginosa was incubated in LB broth for 12 h at 37°C to achieve cell concentrations from 108 to 109 CFU/mL. The cells were then collected by centrifugation at 5500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the collected cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, resuspended to an OD600 of 0.1, and incubated under three different cultivation conditions: (1) oligotrophic medium: Reasoner’s 2nd Agar (R2A) medium,37 which contains the following components: tryptone: 0.25 g/L; casein acid hydrolysate: 0.5 g/L; yeast extract powder: 0.5 g/L; soluble starch: 0.5 g/L; K2HPO4: 0.3 g/L; MgSO4: 0.1 g/L; sodium pyruvate: 0.3 g/L; peptone: 0.25 g/L; glucose: 0.5 g/L, simulating no fertilization; (2) R2A medium +2 g/L ammonium nitrate, simulating chemical fertilization; and (3) R2A medium +2 g/L ammonium nitrate +10 g/L glucose, simulating organic fertilization, for 12 h. The total bacterial biomass was assessed by measuring OD600 using a SpectraMaxi3 device (Molecular Devices, USA).

Metatranscriptomic sequencing were conducted to investigate the gene abundances and expression of the bacterial strain. Total bacterial RNA was extracted using a TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, CA, U.S.A) following manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries and 150 bp paired-end sequencing were performed on the Illumina Hiseq X sequencing platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharml Technology Co. Ltd. Shanghai, China). SeqPrep gram (https://github.com/jstjohn/SeqPrep) and Sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) were used to filter the raw reads. The clean data was then mapped to the reference genome of E. coli (GCF_000005845.2) using Bowtie2 v2.5.0.96 The mRNA abundance was determined by the number of reads individually mapped to each transcript per million reads.97 RSEM was used to quantify transcript expression,97 and DESeq2 was employed to identify and analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among samples (log2FC ≥ 1 and p < 0.05).98 Sequence alignment against the CAZy, SARG, and VFDB databases was performed using BLASTX of DIAMOND v2.0.14.152.63 A sequence was classified as ARG or VFG fragment if it met the following criteria: identity ≥80%, hit length ≥75%, and E-value ≤10−7. A sequence was classified as CAZy fragment if it met the criteria: E-value ≤10−5 and bit score ≥50.

Soil microcosm experiment

To investigate the controversy surrounding the potential risks of organic fertilization on soil microbial health, e.g., ARGs, we conducted a pot experiment to determine whether these risks are primarily due to antibiotic exposures in animal rearing. The soils in the pots (pot height: 23 cm, diameter: 15 cm, with 3 kg of dry soil per pot) underwent a seven-day pre-cultivation period for stabilization, during which the soil moisture was adjusted to 60% of the maximum moisture content. The basic characteristics of the soils were as follows: pH = 4.75, clay content = 7.35%, total C = 32.4 g/kg, total N = 3.77 g/kg, and CEC = 13.76 cmol/kg. Wheat (cv. Yangmai 20, Ningbo Academy of Agricultural Sciences) seeds were sterilized using 10% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min and stored at 4°C for a week to ensure consistent germination prior to sowing. Three wheat seedlings were evenly transplanted into each pot, and the experiment was conducted in a greenhouse under natural light and humidity, with the temperature maintained between 25°C and 30°C. Four fertilization regimes were applied in four replicates: (1) no fertilization; (2) chemical fertilization; (3) organic fertilization with antibiotic contamination; and (4) organic fertilization without antibiotic contamination. Urea (CON2H4), superphosphate (Ca(H2PO4)2), and potassium chloride (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used as chemical fertilizers.

Organic fertilizers, i.e., manures from animals with and without previous antibiotic exposure through their feed, were sourced from the same local farms. The organic fertilizers with antibiotic exposure were from pigs that had been fed fodder with ofloxacin (100 μg/kg), whereas the other manure came from pigs fed antibiotic-free fodder. The concentration of antibiotics, such as ofloxacin, in organic fertilizers with antibiotics contamination (21 μg/kg) was seven times higher than in those without antibiotic contamination (3 μg/kg). The amount of fertilizer added in the pot experiment corresponded to typical regional agricultural management. Detailed fertilization information is provided in Table S9.

The pot experiments were irrigated with deionized water every three days to maintain consistent soil moisture (60%–70% of the maximum moisture content) throughout the experimental period. During the two-year experiment, a total of 16 soil collections were conducted, corresponding to 8 growth stages of crops across 2 cycles (Figure S9). In total, 256 samples were destructively collected from all treatments (all soils in each sample were obtained) to avoid disrupting the soil and microbiome structure in contrast to repeated sampling. Detailed information about the sample collection can be found in Figure S9. After homogenizing the soils, each soil sample was divided into two parts: one was stored at 4°C for measuring soil ammonia oxidation and denitrification rates within one week, and the other stored at −80°C for DNA extraction.

The rates of key N processes (i.e., ammonia oxidation and denitrification) in soils measured using 15N isotope pairing technique. Briefly, 3 g homogenized fresh soils were placed in a 12 mL gas-tight Labco bottle (Labco Exetainer, UK) and mixed with 3 mL of sterilized purified water. High-purity helium (He) gas (99.999%) was used to purge the cylinder through three cycles to replace the headspace air. The samples were then pre-incubated at 25°C for 48 h to remove residual nitrate. Subsequently, 100 μL of helium-purged stock solutions were injected into each isotope treatment as follows: (i) negative control with 15NH4Cl (98 atom% 15N), (ii) positive control with 15NH4Cl + K14NO3, and (iii) 14NH4Cl + K15NO3 (99.99 atom% 15N), resulting in a final nitrogen concentration of 100 μmol per vial. Negative controls were used to ensure that the background levels of NO3− and NO2− were consumed during the pre-incubation period, while positive controls confirmed the presence of ammonia oxidation. The addition of 14NH4+ and 15NO3− allowed for the measurement of ammonia oxidation and denitrification rates. The incubations were terminated at specified intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h) by adding 200 μL of 7 M ZnCl2 solution. The concentrations of 28N2, 29N2 and 30N2 produced during the incubation were determined using GasBench-II isotope ratio mass spectrometry (Thermo Delta V Advantage, Germany). Ammonia oxidation and denitrification rates were calculated as outlined in previous studies.99

Soil DNA was extracted using the method described above. High-throughput qPCR was then used to determine the abundances of nitrogen cycling functional genes, ARGs, and 16S rRNA genes. High-throughput quantitative PCR (HT-qPCR) was performed using the SmartChip Real-Time PCR System (Wafergen Inc., USA). A total of 300 primer sets were used to detect 14 nitrogen cycling functional genes, 285 ARGs across 11 major types, and 16S rRNA genes. All primer information is provided in Table S10. A 100 nL reaction mixture, consisting of DNA template from the samples, primers for qPCR amplification, sterile water, bovine serum albumin, and LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master, was prepared for qPCR. The thermocycling conditions were set to 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 30 s and 60°C for 30 s. Data were obtained using Wafergen software and analyzed with SmartChip Software. Data showing multiple melting peaks, low amplification efficiencies (<1.8), or weak amplification detection limits (threshold cycle, CT > 31) were excluded. The copy number and relative abundance of genes were calculated using the following formulas:Copy number of gene = 10((31-CT(gene))/(10/3))Relative abundance = copy number of gene/copy number of 16S rRNA gene

The relative copy numbers of nitrogen cycling functional genes obtained from the HT-qPCR were converted into absolute copy numbers by normalizing to the absolute 16S rRNA gene copy number. The absolute 16S rRNA copy numbers were determined using the standard curve (SC) method of quantification with the Roche 480 system. Each 20 μL qPCR reaction consisted of 10 μL of 2 × LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche Applied Sciences), 1 μg/μL bovine serum albumin, 1 μM of each primer, 1 ng/μL DNA template, and 6 μL of nuclease-free PCR-grade water. The thermal cycling protocol involved a 10-min initial enzyme activation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 15 s. A plasmid control containing a cloned and sequenced 16S rRNA gene fragment (1.39 × 1010 copies per liter) was used to generate eight-point calibration curves from 10-fold dilutions for standard calculation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in biological quadruplicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V26 software (IBM Inc., Chicago, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the LSD test was used to evaluate the statistical significance among three or more groups, while the Mann-Whitney U test was employed to assess the significance between two groups. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
