Posterior Atlanto-Occipital Membrane Bulging Preceding Hindbrain Herniation in Open Spinal Dysraphism: A Case Report
Authors: Ivonne Bedei, Gregor Kasprian, Siegmund Köhler, Benjamin Sass, Roland Axt-Fliedner, Maximilian Schulze, Corinna Keil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHindbrain herniation (HBH) is a feature of Chiari II malformation in fetuses with open spinal dysraphism (OSD) and constitutes an eligibility criterion for prenatal repair in many fetal surgery protocols. Bulging of the posterior atlanto-occipital membrane (PAOM) is frequently observed in association with vermian descent; however, its significance in the absence of HBH remains unclear.Case PresentationWe report two fetuses with OSD who initially showed no HBH on fetal MRI but presented with isolated CSF-filled PAOM bulging. In both cases, repeat MRI within 2 weeks demonstrated interval development of HBH to C1, thereby fulfilling criteria for prenatal repair. Case 1 presented at 22.3 weeks with L4 myelomeningocele, mild ventriculomegaly and preserved motor function. Prenatal repair was performed at 26.0 weeks. Case 2 presented at 20.3 weeks with L5 myelomeningocele, normal ventricles, and intact motor function. Prenatal repair was performed at 25.3 weeks. In both fetuses, postoperative MRI demonstrated reversal of HBH, whereas PAOM bulging persisted.ConclusionPAOM bulging may be associated with the subsequent development of HBH, but this observation requires confirmation in larger longitudinal cohorts. In fetuses with OSD and absent HBH at initial assessment, the presence of PAOM bulging may warrant consideration of short-interval follow-up MRI while prenatal repair remains an option.Keywords: Posterior atlanto-occipital membrane, Open spinal dysraphism, Prenatal surgery, Hindbrain herniation, Chiari II malformation

Introduction

The posterior atlanto-occipital membrane (PAOM) is an anatomical interface between the posterior fossa and the spinal canal. In fetuses with open spinal dysraphism (OSD), this region is exposed to altered cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and abnormal spatial relationships of the hindbrain, upper cervical canal, and posterior fossa. While hindbrain herniation (HBH) is an integral part of Chiari II malformation and a prerequisite for fetal repair in most centers, the temporal relationship between PAOM bulging and the development of HBH has received limited attention [1–3]. Bulging of the PAOM has been described in association with Chiari II malformation and other skull-base changes, including posterior fossa maldevelopment and central skull-base remodeling [1, 4]. Its prognostic relevance, however, remains uncertain. Freiling et al. [1] found greater membrane convexity in postnatally repaired patients requiring hydrocephalus treatment. Still, this association was not clearly confirmed in prenatally repaired cases. Similarly, fetal MRI data suggest that the PAOM protrusion may occur together with more severe supratentorial and posterior fossa abnormalities, including brain edema, deeper vermian displacement, and hydrocephalus [2]. These observations support the concept that the membrane may reflect relevant craniocervical remodelling but do not establish whether it is merely a secondary sign or an early indicator of impending hindbrain descent.

A small subgroup of fetuses with OSD presents without HBH at initial second-trimester assessment [5–7]. These fetuses are usually excluded from prenatal repair, although their natural history is heterogeneous. Recent cohorts have shown that some fetuses remain without HBH, whereas others develop herniation later in pregnancy or after birth [5, 6]. In the multicentre series, 4% of fetuses with OSD had no HBH at 19–27 weeks; postnatally, HBH was present in 50%, while the shunt rate remained low [5]. Flanders et al. [6] likewise showed that a subset of fetuses without HBH at initial consultation later developed prenatal or postnatal herniation.

Imaging findings associated with the subsequent development of HBH in fetuses without herniation at initial assessment have not been established. In this report, we describe two fetuses in whom cerebrospinal fluid-filled PAOM bulging was observed before overt HBH became apparent on follow-up MRI. We discuss the possible relevance of this temporal association for prenatal surveillance and surgical assessment, while recognizing that its reproducibility and predictive value remain unknown.

Case Presentation

The cases have been evaluated at a tertiary fetal surgery center offering prenatal repair for OSD. Candidates for fetal surgery were assessed according to the MOMS selection criteria [3]. In our institutional setting, prenatal repair is not offered in the absence of HBH, confirmed on fetal MRI. Neurosonography was performed in accordance with ISUOG guidelines [8, 9].

Clinical data were extracted retrospectively from the institutional fetal surgery database. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the University Hospitals of Giessen and Marburg, Germany: Giessen, IRB approval AZ 161/20; Marburg, IRB approval AZ 23–280 BO. Both patients had consented to the anonymized use of their clinical and imaging data for scientific purposes. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines, and the completed CARE checklist is provided as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/fdt/acuag011).

Prenatal ultrasound was performed transabdominally and transvaginally using a GE Voluson E10 ultrasound system. The assessment included detailed fetal neurosonography, lesion characterization, measurement of ventricular width, evaluation of the corpus callosum, posterior fossa assessment, determination of the anatomical and functional lesion levels, and documentation of associated anomalies. The institutional approach to prenatal evaluation and fetal surgery assessment has been described previously [10].

Fetal MRI was performed on 1.5-Tesla MRI systems (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Fast T2-weighted sequences were acquired in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes to assess the posterior fossa, brainstem, cerebellar position, fourth ventricle, cisterna magna, supratentorial structures, and spinal lesion morphology. HBH was evaluated in relation to the foramen magnum (FM), and the ectopia of cerebellar parts below FM was the criterion for fetal surgery.

PAOM bulging was defined as a posterior protrusion at the occipito-cervical junction, extending from the posterior fossa to the posterior arch of C1 and creating a dural-lined cavity identifiable on midsagittal imaging. The interval to follow-up MRI was not predefined. It was determined by gestational age at presentation and logistical considerations, to complete reassessment within the treatment window and allow sufficient time to organize prenatal repair if HBH subsequently developed. Postnatal evaluation was performed by a multidisciplinary OSD team and included clinical neurological examination, assessment of motor function, cranial ultrasound, and postnatal MRI, as previously reported [10].

Case 1

A pregnant patient was referred at 22.3 weeks’ gestation for evaluation of fetal myelomeningocele (MMC). Detailed neurosonography demonstrated mild ventriculomegaly, with a ventricular width of 10.6 mm. The corpus callosum appeared normal. The anatomical lesion level was assessed as L4, and the functional level was estimated as S1.

At initial assessment, fetal MRI showed no HBH. The fourth ventricle and cisterna magna remained visible. However, the PAOM was visibly protruding and appeared filled with cerebrospinal fluid.

As the fetus remained within the gestational window for potential fetal surgery, short-interval follow-up imaging was performed. Repeat MRI at 24.1 weeks demonstrated interval development of HBH below the FM, with effacement of the cisterna magna and fourth ventricle (shown in Fig. 1).

Case 1 showing CSF-filled bulging of the posterior atlanto-occipital membrane without HBH at 22.3 weeks (a), followed by development of HBH at 24.1 weeks (b). The line indicates the level of the FM, the arrowhead marks the PAOM bulging, and the arrow indicates the HBH.

Following MRI confirmation of HBH, the fetus fulfilled institutional eligibility criteria for prenatal repair. Successful fetal surgery was performed at 26 weeks. Postoperative imaging showed regression of HBH and persisting POAM bulging. At 1 year of age, no cerebrospinal fluid shunt had been required, and postnatal motor function corresponded to L5. Postnatal MRI confirmed persistence of mild PAOM bulging.

Case 2

A second pregnant patient was referred at 20.3 weeks’ gestation with fetal MMC. Ventricular width on ultrasound was 5.4 mm. The corpus callosum was normal. The anatomical lesion level was assessed as L5, and the functional level was estimated as S1.

Initial fetal MRI showed no HBH. As in case 1, however, a cerebrospinal fluid-filled bulging of the PAOM was present at first evaluation. In accordance with institutional criteria, fetal surgery was not offered at this stage because HBH was absent.

Follow-up MRI at 25.0 weeks’ gestation demonstrated new-onset HBH below the FM. Prenatal repair was subsequently performed at 25.3 weeks’ gestation. Postoperative imaging demonstrated regression of HBH and persisting PAOM bulging. At 5-month follow-up, the child has not required shunt placement. The persistence of PAOM bulging could not be assessed postnatally, as the infant is receiving follow-up care at a local university hospital and the scheduled MRI at 6 months of age has not yet been performed. Comparison with Fetuses without Subsequent HBH

To contextualize these observations, we compared the two cases with a previously published cohort of fetuses with OSD without HBH at prenatal assessment [5]. Cases managed at our center were re-analyzed with specific attention to the presence or absence of PAOM bulging. Of the 12 fetuses with OSD without HBH at initial assessment, six cases originated from our own center. They were independently re-evaluated by two investigators with particular attention to PAOM morphology. Two pregnancies were terminated, precluding longitudinal assessment. In the remaining four cases, imaging from the third trimester and/or the postnatal period was available in addition to the initial examination. PAOM bulging was present at the initial assessment in two fetuses and absent in the other two. In all four cases, the initial PAOM finding corresponded to the subsequent imaging appearance in the third trimester and/or after birth: the two fetuses with initial PAOM bulging subsequently developed HBH, whereas the two fetuses without PAOM bulging remained without HBH. However, short-interval follow-up MRI within the gestational window for fetal surgery had not been performed in these cases. Because imaging intervals were not standardized and the number of cases was small, this retrospective comparison does not permit assessment of the sensitivity, specificity, or predictive value of PAOM bulging.

Imaging findings associated, we additionally present a recently managed fetus presenting at 20.6 weeks GA with confirmed MMC, in whom neither PAOM bulging nor HBH was observed on serial prenatal MRI or postnatal imaging. The anatomic and motor level was S1. In accordance with institutional criteria, fetal surgery was not performed. After birth, the diagnosis of OSD was confirmed, while both HBH and PAOM bulging remained absent (Fig. 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing bulging of the PAOM preceding the appearance of HBH in a fetus with OSD. In established HBH, the space delineated by the membrane is frequently occupied by herniated cerebellar tissue, whereas after successful prenatal repair and regression of HBH, the membrane often remains bulged and is typically filled with cerebrospinal fluid. The observation that PAOM bulging was present before the development of HBH in our case suggests that this finding may represent an early manifestation of the Chiari II spectrum rather than merely a secondary consequence of hindbrain displacement. Taken together, these observations indicate a temporal association between PAOM bulging and the subsequent development of HBH in the two reported cases. Whether PAOM bulging differentiates fetuses who will develop HBH from those who will remain without hindbrain descent cannot be determined from the present data and needs to be assessed in larger studies.

The underlying mechanism remains speculative. Experimental data indicate that reduced tissue compliance at the craniocervical junction can alter cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and increase ventricular pressure [11]. Conversely, in fetuses with OSD, the PAOM may act as a relatively compliant structure that becomes distended in response to evolving pressure and volume changes. Such deformation could become visible before overt hindbrain descent is detectable on imaging. Corroenne et al. [4] recently showed that structural abnormalities at the craniocervical junction may persist despite successful prenatal repair. In their longitudinal cohort of 112 fetuses, occipital outpouching was frequent and remained essentially unchanged after surgery, irrespective of complete or partial HBH reversal and independently of severe ventriculomegaly. These findings support the concept that neural and non-neural components of Chiari II deformation may respond differently to restored CSF dynamics. Persistent CSF-filled PAOM bulging after cerebellar ascent could therefore represent residual deformation of a previously distended structure, although this interpretation remains hypothetical.

We therefore hypothesize that PAOM bulging may be associated with the subsequent development of HBH. Confirmation will require larger cohorts undergoing standardized longitudinal imaging. Pending such evidence, the observation of PAOM bulging may provide a rationale for considering short-interval follow-up MRI, particularly in fetuses who remain within the gestational window for prenatal repair.

HBH is a cardinal feature of Chiari II malformation and is present in most fetuses with OSD [12]. Consequently, its presence has been incorporated into most fetal surgery protocols derived from the MOMS criteria [3]. Historically, the absence of HBH was considered suggestive of a closed spinal lesion. However, several studies have shown that HBH may also be absent in fetuses with confirmed OSD [5–7, 13]. Furthermore, previous works by our group and Flanders et al. [6] indicate that HBH may develop later during gestation in a subset of these fetuses. Flanders et al. [6] reported that approximately 12% of fetuses without HBH at initial assessment subsequently developed HBH during the gestational window, in which fetal surgery is generally considered. Although the benefit of prenatal repair may be less pronounced than in fetuses with HBH already present at the initial evaluation, prenatal repair was nevertheless associated with benefit in this subgroup. Characterizing the longitudinal imaging evolution of fetuses without HBH at the initial assessment is therefore clinically relevant, particularly while prenatal repair remains a potential treatment option. In this setting, imaging features that precede overt HBH may help identify fetuses in whom short-interval reassessment should be considered. Consistent with our previous observations, the absence of HBH was identified only in fetuses with myelomeningocele and not in those with myeloschisis [5]. This observation is consistent with previous studies indicating that myelomeningocele and myeloschisis differ in their anatomical and clinical characteristics. These differences may reflect lesion-specific CSF dynamics. In myelomeningocele, the presence of a CSF-filled sac may partially buffer CSF loss and reduce downward displacement of the hindbrain, whereas the exposed placode in myeloschisis may permit more direct CSF leakage and be associated with more pronounced Chiari II changes [5, 14–16]. The absence of HBH in some fetuses with OSD may also be related to specific lesion characteristics. Previous studies have reported an inverse relationship between lesion size and the presence of HBH, with larger cystic lesions appearing less likely to be associated with HBH [6, 14, 15]. Partial skin coverage has likewise been proposed to reduce cerebrospinal fluid leakage and thereby attenuate downward displacement of the hindbrain [6]. These observations suggest that the extent of cerebrospinal fluid loss may vary among fetuses with OSD and may contribute to the heterogeneous presentation of Chiari II features, including absent, delayed, or less pronounced HBH [5, 6].

The principal contribution of this report is the description of PAOM bulging before the development of overt HBH in two fetuses with OSD. However, the small number of cases, retrospective nature of comparison, non-standardized imaging intervals, and the absence of a systematically assessed control cohort preclude conclusions regarding causality or predictive performance. Sensitivity, specificity, and positive or negative predictive values cannot be estimated. Larger prospective studies using standardized longitudinal imaging are required to determine whether PAOM bulging is reproducibly associated with subsequent HBH and whether it has clinical value for surveillance or treatment selection.

Conclusion

These two cases indicate that PAOM bulging may precede the development of overt HBH in fetuses with OSD. PAOM bulging may therefore be temporally associated with subsequent HBH, although this observation requires confirmation in larger longitudinal cohorts. It should not be regarded as a validated predictive marker or as an eligibility criterion for fetal surgery. At present, its potential clinical relevance is limited to supporting consideration of short-interval follow-up MRI in fetuses who remain within the gestational window for prenatal repair.
