Hospital-Based Reference Values for Hertel Exophthalmometry in Nepal
Authors: Anup Subedi, Roshni Malla, Mario Cantó-Cerdán, Santosh Chhetri, Samriddhi Lamsal, Ravin Kumar Mishra, Umesh Belbase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation-specific reference values for Hertel exophthalmometry are limited in South Asian populations, including Nepal. This limits clinical interpretation of ocular protrusion in routine practice. This study aimed to establish hospital-based reference values for exophthalmometry, evaluate intra-session measurement repeatability, and examine demographic and anthropometric associations.MethodsParticipants aged 6–85 years attending the outpatient department were enrolled using systematic sampling, with every fifth eligible attendee selected. Because recruitment was conducted within a tertiary eye hospital rather than through population-based sampling, the study was designed to provide hospital-based reference data rather than nationally representative estimates. Hertel exophthalmometry was performed by a single examiner with three repeated measurements per eye. Intra-session repeatability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and coefficient of variation. Group comparisons were performed using non-parametric tests, and multivariable linear regression identified independent correlates of right eye exophthalmometry value (EV).ResultsThis study provides the first hospital-based reference data for exophthalmometry in a Nepali clinical population with excellent intra-session repeatability. Provisional upper reference limits are 17.42 mm (95th percentile) and 18.31 mm (mean plus 2SD). Inter-eye difference (IED) values exceeding 1.50 mm were uncommon in this sample (one participant, 0.2%). These findings are descriptive only; diagnostic thresholds for clinically significant asymmetry cannot be established from this study and require population-based investigations. Hertel base value (BV), age, ethnolinguistic ancestry, and geographical region were the independent associated with EV.ConclusionThis study provides hospital-based reference values for Hertel exophthalmometry in a Nepali population. Mean EV was approximately 13.5 mm, with a 95th percentile upper limit of 17.42 mm. Measurement repeatability was excellent. EV was independently associated with age, Hertel base value, and demographic grouping variables, although clinical interpretation should consider the methodological limitations of hospital-based sampling. These findings may assist clinicians in interpreting exophthalmometry in Nepal; however, population-based studies are needed to establish definitive normative ranges.

Background

Ocular proptosis is a cardinal sign of orbital disease arising from thyroid-associated orbitopathy, orbital tumours, inflammation, and craniofacial abnormalities.1 Prompt recognition of abnormal ocular prominence depends on reliable reference data; without population-specific values, clinicians risk misclassifying physiological variation as pathological or, conversely, missing early disease. Accurate population-specific reference values are therefore useful for distinguishing physiological ocular prominence from suspected orbital pathology and for monitoring change over time.2 Exophthalmometry quantifies protrusion as the perpendicular distance from the lateral orbital rim to the anterior corneal apex. Hertel’s exophthalmometer, introduced in 1905, remains the standard clinical instrument owing to its simplicity and portability.3,4 The Hertel base value (BV), the inter-orbital distance at which the footplates rest, must be recorded alongside every measurement; without it, serial comparisons are unreliable even within the same patient.

Normative EV varies considerably across populations. Published mean EVs vary considerably across populations, ranging from 13.44 ± 2.60 mm in a Turkish population5 to approximately 18 mm in Black American males,2 Values reported in North Indian (14.94 ± 2.43 mm)6 and Chinese Han values6 (approximately 13.5–14.4 mm) fall between these extremes. Differences in exophthalmometry values between populations have been reported previously and may be related to variation in craniofacial and orbital anatomy, ocular biometry, and anthropometric characteristics, together with age and sex. However, the relative contribution of these factors varies between populations and study designs.7 Differences in craniofacial and orbital morphology have also been proposed as possible contributors to variation in exophthalmometry values between populations. Age has also been associated with variation in EV, with some studies reporting higher values in young adulthood followed by lower values at older ages, supporting consideration of age when interpreting reference data.8 In the absence of population-specific data, clinicians in Nepal may rely on reference values derived from other populations.

Nepal’s population is ethnically and geographically diverse, encompassing Tibeto-Burman highland communities and Indo-Aryan groups in the Terai lowlands, distributed across mountain, hill, and Terai zones with considerable differences in altitude, lifestyle, and population history.9 Ethnolinguistic ancestry and geographic origin may be associated with variation in craniofacial and orbital dimensions across populations.6 To our knowledge, no published hospital-based exophthalmometric reference dataset currently exists for Nepal. The present study addresses this gap by providing hospital-based reference values for EV and BV together with formal intra-session repeatability assessment and multivariable analysis of associated demographic and anthropometric factors.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Himalaya Eye Hospital, Pokhara, Nepal, between April and September 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee, Pokhara University. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or guardians for participants under 18 years.

Participants

Participants aged 6–85 years attending the outpatient department were enrolled by systematic random sampling, with every 5th individual selected; the next eligible person was enrolled when the selected individual met an exclusion criterion. Participants were recruited from an ophthalmic outpatient department and may have attended for routine eye examinations, refractive care, cataract evaluation, or other non-orbital ophthalmic conditions. Individuals with disorders known to influence globe position or orbital anatomy were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Inclusion required willingness to participate and a spherical equivalent between −3.00 D and +2.00 D. Participants aged ≥6 years were included because children at this age were considered able to maintain reliable fixation and cooperate with standardized Hertel exophthalmometry. Axial length measurements were unavailable; therefore, participants with spherical equivalent refractive error outside −3.00 D to +2.00 D were excluded to restrict the sample to a relatively moderate refractive-error range. This criterion was adopted to reduce the representation of marked ametropia, which may be associated with differences in globe position, rather than as a direct surrogate for axial length. The refractive-error criterion should therefore not be interpreted as controlling for axial length.

Previous studies have reported associations between ocular biometry, particularly axial length, and exophthalmometric measurements.7,10 The selected refractive error range was intended to reduce the influence of marked ametropia on globe position while retaining a broad representation of routine clinical attendees. Participants with a pre-existing clinical diagnosis of proptosis or enophthalmos, thyroid-associated orbitopathy, orbital tumour or other orbital disease, endocrine disease, and strabismus or nystagmus precluding reliable central fixation were excluded. No Hertel exophthalmometry cutoff was used to determine eligibility.

Sample Size

As no prior Nepali exophthalmometry data were available, formal sample size calculation based on local variance estimates was not feasible. A target sample of at least 400 participants was selected to provide stable estimates of reference values, percentile distributions, and subgroup comparisons, consistent with previous large exophthalmometry reference-value studies. The final sample of 450 participants was considered adequate for the study objectives.

Procedure and Measurements

Demographic data, including age, sex, ethnolinguistic ancestry, and geographical region of origin, were collected through structured interview. Ethnolinguistic ancestry was self-reported and classified as Indo-Aryan or Tibeto-Burman according to the broad groupings used in Nepal’s national census.9 Classification was based on participants’ self-reported ethnolinguistic affiliation and subsequently grouped into the two major census-based categories for analysis. Geographical region of origin was classified as Mountain, Hill, or Terai.

Visual acuity, objective refraction, and slit-lamp biomicroscopy were performed to exclude ocular and orbital pathology. Height (nearest cm) and weight (nearest kg) were measured using calibrated instruments, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight/height2 (kg/m2).

Hertel exophthalmometry was performed by a single trained examiner throughout the study. Participants sat upright in a well-illuminated room with eyes in primary gaze.4 The footplates of the Hertel exophthalmometer were positioned on the lateral orbital rims bilaterally, and the BV was recorded. The fellow eye was occluded to minimise parallax error. Three consecutive measurements were obtained for each eye at the same BV and averaged for analysis.4 Individual readings were retained for intra-session repeatability assessment.4 After each reading, the Hertel exophthalmometer was removed completely from the participant and repositioned on the lateral orbital rims before the next measurement. Each measurement was obtained independently at the same BV to minimise recall and positioning bias.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered in EpiData v4.7 and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (SciPy, Pandas). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; EV was not normally distributed, so non-parametric methods were used for all univariate analyses.

Intra-session repeatability was assessed using the one-way random intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC(1.1)] and mean coefficient of variation (CV%). The Mann–Whitney U-test compared EV and BV between two independent groups, with rank-biserial correlation reported as the effect size. The Kruskal–Wallis H-test evaluated differences across three or more groups, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed relationships between EV or BV and continuous variables.

Multiple linear regression with dummy-coded categorical predictors (reference categories: female, Tibeto-Burman, and Mountain) was used to identify independent correlates of right-eye EV. Variance inflation factors were used to assess multicollinearity, and influential observations were evaluated using Cook’s distance. Model residuals were assessed for normality and homoscedasticity. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Association of EV and BV with Age Group

Table 5 presents EV and BV by age group. EV demonstrated age-related variation, with the highest values observed in young adults and the lowest values in the elderly: it increased from children (OD: 13.63±1.83 mm) through adolescents (14.08±1.67 mm) to a peak in young adults (14.30±2.21 mm), then declined in adults (13.34±2.43 mm) and reached its lowest level in the elderly (11.27±2.39 mm. The Kruskal–Wallis H-test confirmed a significant age-group effect on EV (H=75.312 OD, H=71.690 OS; p<0.001) (Figure 2). Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected) showed that the elderly group differed significantly from all other age groups (all p<0.001); young adults differed significantly from adults (p=0.020); no significant differences were found among children, adolescents, and young adults. BV varied significantly by age group (H=57.929, p<0.001), rising from children (97.82±4.46 mm) to young adults (102.33±3.99 mm) and declining in the elderly (99.37±4.07 mm). Association of EV and BV with Geographical Region

A significant geographical gradient in EV was observed (Table 6 and Figure 3). Terai-origin participants had the highest OD EV (14.65±2.50 mm), followed by Hill (13.41±2.29 mm) and Mountain (12.77±2.56 mm) participants (KW H=17.354, p<0.001). Dunn’s post-hoc confirmed that Terai differed significantly from both Mountain (p<0.001) and Hill (p<0.001), while Mountain and Hill did not differ significantly (p=0.383). BMI was considered as a potential anthropometric predictor but was not retained in the final model because its univariate association with OD EV was negligible (Spearman r=−0.020, p=0.678) and its inclusion did not materially improve model fit (increase in adjusted R2 <0.7%). Height (p=0.991) and sex (p=0.390) were not independently associated with OD EV after adjustment for other covariates.

All variance inflation factors were below 3.0, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Residual diagnostics demonstrated approximate normality and no major evidence of heteroscedasticity (unequal residual variance across fitted values); the maximum Cook’s distance was 0.044. The final model explained 40.3% of the variance in OD EV (R2=0.403; adjusted R2=0.392).

Discussion

This study provides hospital-based reference values for Hertel exophthalmometry in a large Nepali sample and is one of the first datasets from the region to include formal assessment of measurement repeatability and multivariable analysis of associated factors. The mean right eye EV (13.52 ± 2.39 mm) is comparable to values reported in Turkish populations and lower than those reported in North Indian, Chinese, and African populations, consistent with previously reported differences in exophthalmometry values between populations.6,7,11–13 However, differences in study design, instrumentation, and sampling methods across published studies limit direct comparison.

The 95th percentile upper limit (17.42 mm) provides a provisional reference for clinical interpretation in this sample. While values up to 20 mm were among participants meeting the study eligibility criteria, these should not be interpreted as clinical thresholds. Inter-eye difference was small overall (0.34 ± 0.45 mm), and values above 1.5 mm were uncommon. However, the dataset was not powered to define diagnostic cutoffs for asymmetry, and these observations should be considered descriptive.

Measurement repeatability was excellent, with ICC values exceeding 0.998 for both eyes and very low coefficients of variation. The exceptionally high ICC values likely reflect the use of a single trained examiner and repeated measurements obtained under controlled conditions, including complete repositioning of the instrument between consecutive measurements. These estimates therefore represent intra-session intra-observer repeatability and should not be interpreted as inter-observer reproducibility. Inter-observer variability was not assessed and may provide additional information about reproducibility in routine clinical practice.2

EV showed a clear age-related pattern, with higher values in younger adults and lower values in older participants. While this trend is consistent with previous studies,6,11 the cross-sectional design does not allow inference about longitudinal change. Therefore, the observed age-group differences may reflect both biological variation and cohort effects.

Sex differences in EV were small and not clinically meaningful. Although one comparison reached statistical significance, sex was not an independent predictor in multivariable analysis. These findings do not support sex-specific reference values in this population.

Height and weight showed weak associations with EV, but these did not remain significant in multivariable analysis. BMI was not associated with EV, suggesting that general body habitus has limited influence on ocular protrusion in this dataset. In contrast, BV showed a consistent and strong association with EV across analyses,6,10,14 reinforcing the importance of documenting BV in all clinical measurements. Mean binocular EV was considered as an alternative analytical approach. However, the primary objective was to identify independent correlates while avoiding statistical dependence between paired eyes. Because bilateral symmetry was very high (Spearman r=0.968, p<0.001), with a mean absolute inter-eye difference of 0.34 mm, the right eye was selected as the representative eye for multivariable analysis. This approach avoids treating correlated bilateral measurements as independent observations while retaining a single clinically interpretable outcome. Several limitations should be acknowledged.

The hospital-based single-centre design may limit generalisability to the broader Nepali population, and participants were not evenly distributed across geographical regions (Mountain 9.8%) or ethnolinguistic groups (Indo-Aryan 70.4%); therefore, subgroup estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Participants were recruited from an ophthalmic outpatient department rather than through community-based sampling and may have presented for routine examinations, refractive services, cataract assessment, or other non-orbital ophthalmic conditions. Although disorders known to affect globe position were excluded, this recruitment strategy may have introduced selection bias, and the study population may not fully represent the general Nepali population. Participants with spherical equivalent outside −3.00 D to +2.00 D were excluded because axial length measurements were unavailable; however, the proportion excluded was small (12/480, 2.5%), and the remaining sample spanned a wide EV range (7–20 mm). Age-group sample sizes were unequal (children n=54, young adults n=167), which may affect percentile estimates in smaller groups, although the primary age analysis used continuous regression. Axial length measurements and orbital imaging were unavailable, limiting anatomical interpretation of the observed ethnolinguistic and geographical differences. In addition, the study was not designed to establish diagnostic thresholds for asymmetry; inter-eye difference observations are descriptive only, as the single participant with IED >1.50 mm is insufficient to define a clinically meaningful cutoff. All measurements were performed by a single examiner, and therefore ICC values reflect intra-observer reliability only; inter-observer reliability was not assessed. Future community-based multicentre studies incorporating proportional stratified sampling, inter-observer reliability assessment, axial length measurements, and orbital imaging would strengthen both hospital-based and population-based reference data for Nepal.

Conclusion

This study provides the first hospital-based exophthalmometry reference dataset from Nepal. Mean EV is approximately 13.5 mm; provisional upper reference limits are 17.42 mm (95th percentile) and 18.31 mm (mean plus 2SD). IED values exceeding 1.50 mm were uncommon in this sample. Further population-based studies are required before defining clinically applicable asymmetry thresholds for the Nepali population. Intra-session repeatability was excellent (ICC above 0.999). BV, age, ancestry group, and geographical region were independent correlates of EV in multivariable analysis. Recording BV at every exophthalmometric assessment is essential for accurate clinical interpretation.
