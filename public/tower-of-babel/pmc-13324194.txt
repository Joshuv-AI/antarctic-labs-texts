Advancing sustainable construction through waste valorization using plastic aggregate and industrial slag
Authors: Kavita Rani, Kasilingam Senthil, Amardeep Boora, Aditya Kumar Tiwary, Hussin Ahmad Hasrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To lower overall carbon emissions, this study investigates the viability of replacing cement and river sand with steel slag from electric arc furnaces and replacing coarse aggregate in concrete with plastic aggregate (PA). According to optimisation, it was best to replace 30% of the cement with slag (SAC), 30% of the coarse aggregate with PA, and 50% of the coarse aggregate with fine slag aggregate (SAFA), as this combination showed compressive strength values of 30, 29.14, and 41.16 MPa. The mixed mix that included 30% PA, 50% fine aggregate replacement, and 30% cement replacement was examined. The compressive strength of the mix, including cementitious slag and PA, was noticeably low (i.e., 36%). Other combined mixes showed a reduction in compressive strength value of up to 15.6%. These results underscore the importance of selecting suitable combinations in concrete mixes to ensure adequate tensile and compressive strengths. It is to note that the tensile strength of these mixed mixes was determined to be insignificant. This can be the result of inadequate cementitious slag-plastic bonding. Overall, the flexural strength of the mix with SAC and PA showed only marginal contributions, while SAFA showed upto 98% increase in strength value in comparison to the normal mix. While the mix with slag as cement and fine aggregate was showing an increase in flexural strength of up to 58%. Additionally, microstructural analysis identified a significant presence of CSH, ettringite, and portlandite. The CSH component enhances strength and rheological properties, ettringite aids in early setting time and strength development, and portlandite acts as a bonding agent.

Introduction

It is worth noting that cement is essential to a nation’s infrastructure development, while plastic has become a necessity in daily life1, commonly used for carrier bags, packaging, and food storage. These materials play a crucial role in national development and make our lives more convenient. However, their use also presents several risks. Fine particles of cement dust can enter the body through the air and negatively impact human health, while plastic nanoparticles can contaminate drinking water, posing further health risks2. According to a previous study3, approximately 60% of CO2 emissions occur during cement manufacturing. Importantly, plastic production involves approximately 8000 different chemicals, many of which are more hazardous to the atmosphere and human health than CO2 4. Therefore, rules governing the use of these industrial by-products in various building methods that may result in a sustainable environment must be proposed to preserve natural resources and produce an eco-friendly environment5. Some recent studies have also confirmed the use of other additives, such as recycled alkali-activated mortar incorporating waste concrete fines as a replacement for fine aggregate and as a binder6, the utilisation of recycled brick powder to make geopolymer concrete7, recycled coarse aggregate to form sustainable recycled sand8, and recycled concrete powder9 in sustainable construction practices without compromising the mechanical properties of the concrete mix.

In addition to generating raw materials, the steel industry has grown to be one of the most significant sectors in the world, creating waste materials such slag10. Large-scale slag production means that the construction industry has many chances to use slag in environmentally friendly building methods and contribute to lowering pollution levels11. Cement production in India is increasing by 10% a year, according to the Indian Bureau of Mines’ 2015 report. According to Mehraj et al.12, an increase in the amount of dispersed particles of cement powder in an exposed atmosphere causes a rise in the number of ailments, including heart disease and skin conditions, among others, which can have an impact on one’s quality of life. Several incurable illnesses, such as asthma, chronic respiratory conditions, and persistent obstructive lung disease, account for 10.7% of the 82% of deaths that occur as a result of these dispersed particles entering the human body13,3. The limited availability of open landfills, which contaminate soil mass and lower land fertility, is another significant reason to concentrate on the application of industrial leftovers, according to Vishwakarma and Uthaman14. This concerning circumstance necessitates the use of various waste materials in various construction techniques, which could aid in the preservation of natural resources and the creation of a sustainable ecosystem.

As a result, steel slag might be used in concrete in place of coarse or fine material. Teng et al.15 carried out an experimental investigation that examined the performance of slag as a replacement for fine aggregate under static mechanical tests. Based on the results, it was determined that slag is a useful product compared to sand because it accelerates the hydration process and increases the early strength. Prior research by Qasrawi et al.16 showed that using slag in place of either coarse or fine aggregate results in decreased concrete mix’s workability. However, it was discovered that the rate at which workability decreased was on the lower end of the spectrum.

Wang and Praetorius17 identified that over 8,000 chemicals are used as additives in plastic production, many of which are more harmful as greenhouse gases than carbon dioxide and pose risks to human health as well. Dang et al.18 examined the impact of nanoplastics on terrestrial plants and found that the adverse effects depend on the size of the nanoplastics, exposure time, and the medium. Numerous other recent studies around the world have investigated the negative impact of nanoplastics on human health, soil characteristics, and water bodies19–22. Though when PA was used in place of coarse aggregate, it was discovered that the workability rose because of PA’s reduced capacity to absorb water. Earlier23, a PET bottle was utilised as coarse aggregates, found that a significant enhancement in the workability of the concrete mix. The primary cause was the excess water’s availability, specifically the extremely low water absorption capacity of the plastic aggregate. Another study witnessed that the enhancement in the flexure and compressive strength due to the 10% PET lightweight aggregates as the replacement of natural aggregate having a variation in size, i.e., from 2 to 4.9 mm and fine aggregate sizes ranging from 0.05 to 2 mm24.

Recently25, it was identified that use of PA in concrete mix increases the durability properties of the mix like abrasion resistance and thermal insulation in comparison to normal concrete mix though the compressive strength was found to be decreased due to poor bonding between PA and concrete. However, treatment of plastic with different methods like acid etching, silica fume coating, and alkaline washes before utilizing in concrete will help in improving the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between plastic and cement paste which further improve the mechanical properties of mix26. Besides these, other studies also reported the improvement in the durability and thermal insulation properties of concrete mix due to the utilization of PA as the replacement of coarse aggregate27,28.

Devi and Gnanavel29 investigated the workability and flowability properties of the concrete mix with steel slag. They observed that the workability decreased by 20% when 30% of the steel slag was used in place of natural 20 mm aggregates. According to Monosi et al.30, the reduction in workability could be lessened by adding a plasticizer. They advised that electric arc furnace slag (EAFS) should be properly crushed for effective grading. Their findings indicated that EAFS as a coarse aggregate could yield greater strength compared to mixes with only natural coarse aggregates. Other studies, including Rondi et al.31, also suggested using steel slag to replace aggregates due to its benefits on tensile strength and shrinkage properties. Santamaría et al.32 recommended EAFS for its potential to minimize shrinkage issues and control volume expansion. Additionally, it was noted that incorporating slag could reduce the amount of cement needed for achieving desired strength. Due to the non-expansive nature of processed slag, strength was found to be increased in comparison to the untreated slag33.

This strength improvement was linked to the improved cementitious properties of the slag following reprocessing, as noted by Muhmood et al.34. Wu et al.35 recommended using copper slag as up to 40% of fine aggregate, as exceeding this amount could lead to increased voids and potential failure. Earlier, it was reported that EAFS may be fully replaced in the place of natural coarse aggregates since these aggregates provide both economic and environmental benefits36. To prevent the adverse nature of the slag on the concrete mixes under mechanical qualities, Pellegrino et al.37 suggested that the amount of steel slag used to replace coarse aggregates should not exceed 50%. A detailed review of the literature revealed that many investigations focused on slag as a partial replacement to natural river sand and 20 mm stone aggregates. However, there were few studies which are dealt with the slag as a partial substitute for cement and fly ash. Additionally, the performance of aggregates by plastic in conjunction with slag in the steel industry has been explored to a limited extent. Consequently, this study aims to promote environmental sustainability by reducing the exhaustion of natural river sand and stone aggregate, as well as lowering CO2 emissions through decreased cement usage in concrete production. In a recent study38, concluded that the use of EAFS as a replacement for natural aggregate helps in improving the stiffness and the compressive strength of the mix and also reduces the release of hazardous elements from slag (i.e. Zn, sulfates, chlorides). Salas et al.39 also found that the increase in compressive strength value up to 42% with the use of EAFS as a replacement for natural aggregates in building construction. In another study40, it was revealed that the iron oxide particulates present in EAF slag help in the formation of conductive networks, which increase the electrical conductivity and can be used in functional (sensing or conductive) concrete. Recently, Rani et al.41 stated that the use of 50–70% of EAFS improves the overall performance by enhancing microstructural densification of concrete mix. Besides this, usage of EAFS in varying proportions and sizes can be used in the formation of radiation shielding concrete, as EAFS helps in reducing the gamma radiation impact42.

To achieve this, the mix design was optimized by replacing up to 50% of cement with cementitious slag (in increments of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50%) and up to 70% of river sand with fine slag aggregate (in increments of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 70%). The design was further optimized by replacing up to 50% of coarse aggregate with plastic aggregate (in increments of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50%). The experiments on both fresh and hardened concrete served as the basis for the suggested optimal mix. In the optimization, plastic aggregate was used in place of coarse aggregate, while steel slag was used to replace both cement and fine aggregate. This mix was then evaluated through compression, flexural, and tensile tests. It is to note that concrete’s element types and quantities of elements were also examined using EDS and SEM, and the material’s crystallographic structure was examined using X-ray diffraction analysis.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, two distinct materials are examined namely Electric Arc Furnace Steel Slag (EAFS) and plastic aggregates. EAFS which is a byproduct of the steel manufacturing process (i.e., specifically from the electric arc furnace method) was obtained from Jindal Stainless (Hisar) Limited in Haryana, India, as shown in Fig. 1. Its composition primarily consists of silicates, with calcium silicate being a key component that enhances binding properties. EAFS also contains various oxides, including calcium oxide, which improves hydraulic properties, and magnesium oxide, which contributes to the durability of structures. Additionally, aluminum oxide affects reactivity and strength, while iron oxides influence color and mechanical properties. Trace amounts of phosphorus pentoxide and other minor elements vary depending on the raw materials used. The plastic aggregates used in this study were collected from recycled plastic waste, including polyethylene, polypropylene, polyvinyl chloride, and polystyrene. Each type of plastic imparts unique characteristics, such as lightweight properties and chemical resistance and hence affect the overall performance of the concrete. Furthermore, plastic aggregates help lower the overall density of the concrete and can be proved beneficial where weight is a critical factor. Other materials used to prepare the conventional concrete, were obtained from local suppliers. It is to note that different techniques like X-ray Diffraction (XRD) (i.e. to identify the crystalline phases and assess the chemical reactivity of the constituents), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) to show the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) which helps in revealing the texture and distribution of particles, and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS/EDX) were used for the identification and measurement of elements found in certain areas of the samples. All these test were carried out at Dr. B R Ambedkar NIT Jalandhar campus by collecting the samples in powder form. Additionally, cement with and without slag underwent physical testing to check the impact of additives on its physical properties. To be used as an alternative to cement, the EAFS was sieved using a 75-micron sieve.

Optimization

Slump tests on fresh concrete

A total of eighteen mixes were made, seventeen of which substituted plastic and slag aggregate for cement, river sand and natural aggregate. Figure 3 compares the slump value of different mixtures, as determined by the slump, to that of the conventional mix. The conventional concrete workability in terms of slump was found to be 53 mm, whereas the mixes containing 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50% cementitious slag were found to be 48, 41, 38, 33 and 30 mm, respectively. It was observed that the increase of cementitious slag led to continues decrease in the flowability. The flowability of concrete with 50% cementitious slag was found to be much lower than that of normal plain concrete, at 43%. The slump found to be 46, 39, 36, 31 and 28 mm, corresponding to mixes with the slag fine aggregate found to be 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50%, respectively, whereas the conventional concrete, i.e., 53 mm. The influence of fine slag aggregate and cementitious slag in the conventional concrete was found to be similar, which reduces the workability of the concrete. On the other hand, the concrete became more workable as the amount of plastic aggregate rose to higher and higher. Concrete mixes’ slump values were found to be 55, 58, 59, 61, and 65, containing 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50% plastic aggregate, respectively. In comparison to ordinary concrete, the 50% plastic aggregate mix had a 22.5% higher workability, which is in good agreement with the findings of Qasrawi et al.16.

Testing of hardened concrete

Concrete mix density

Figures 4(a)-(c) demonstrate the comparison of the density by different additives to that of the control mix after 28 days. The densities for concrete containing 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50% cementitious slag were 2379, 2385, 2379, 2311, and 2293 kg/m³, respectively, however regular concrete has a density of 2355 kg/m³. Among the investigated mixes, the mix containing 20% cementitious slag had the maximum density and found to exhibit a decrease in the density value with the increase in the slag %age as shown in Fig. 3(a). The slag’s inadequate packing properties might be the cause of this decrease. The normal concrete’s density was found to be 2355 kg/m³, whereas the same found to be increase initially and decreases beyond the addition of the 30% slag as replacement of river sand, (Fig. 4b). As shown in Fig. 4(c), when 50% plastic aggregate was utilized instead of regular concrete, the density dropped by 17%. However, the density significantly decreased the aggregate by plastic nature surpassed 40%, while a modest decline was observed with plastic aggregate levels up to 50%. It was found that the main factor lowering the density of the adjusted concrete mixes was plastic aggregate, but the total density increased as slag was substituted for cement and fine aggregate. The introduction of lighter materials led to the 17% reduction in density observed with 50% plastic aggregate. This characteristic could be advantageous for applications requiring in lightweight concrete, such as precast elements or structures where reduced load is essential.

Impact of cement substitute to EAFS’s

For varying curing intervals (one, two, and four weeks), a total of 54 samples were made with varying %ages of EAFS, ranging from 0% to 50%, as a cement substitute. Following IS: 516 (Part 1/Sect. 1)56 recommendations, the compressive strength values were examined. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the mix’s compressive strength value for normal concrete (NC) was calibrated at approximately 41.3 MPa. In mixtures incorporating EAFS (SAC1-SAC3) with 10%, 20%, and 30% cement replacement, the compressive strength was approximately 30 MPa. However, for mixtures having slag as the replacement f slag up to 40 and 50%, the strength value was found to be decreased, 15 and 9.7 MPa, respectively. Notably, SAC5 demonstrated a 75% reduction in strength compared to normal concrete, while SAC1-SAC3 showed a 27% decrease over normal concrete (SAC0). The compressive strengths of normal concrete were 16, 29 and 41 MPa at a week, two weeks and four weeks, respectively. Increase in the strength value for SAC0 was observed around 40% after 7 days and 70% after 14 days. However, the strength gain for mixes SAC4 and SAC5 was minimal after 14 days. It can be concluded that a 30% replacement of slag for cement resulted in a 25% reduction in compressive strength compared to normal concrete, while replacing more than 30% led to a 75% reduction. This suggests that slag may not offer the same binding properties or strength development as traditional cement.

Impact of fine aggregate substitute to EAFS

There are 72 samples were made using different %age of EAFS upto 70% as a substitute of river sand (SAF) for different curing. When compared to the mix known as SAF0, the compressive strength decreased by 19 and 15%, respectively, when slag of 10 and 20% was added as fine aggregate. Errors in the sample preparation and mixing could be the cause of this decrease. However, for mixes with 30, 40, and 50% EAFS (SAF3-SAF5), the compressive strength remained comparable to SAF0 at nearly 41 MPa. This consistency might be the result of EAFS’s greater SiO2 and CaO content, which helps to produce more CSH gel and hence increases in the strength value. Conversely, mixes SAF6 and SAF7 showed notable decreases in compressive strength i.e., 25 and 30% drop respectively in comparison of SAF0 as shown in Fig. 6.

Based on observations, it is stated that using 50% steel slag in place of river sand could result in strength values that are comparable to those seen in a typical mix. This finding is significant as it underscores the potential for incorporating industrial by-products in construction, promoting resource efficiency and waste reduction. A recent study57 found that steel slag has high surface energy and strong physicochemical properties, which help it bond better with the binding material. This stronger bonding allows stresses to be transferred more effectively across the interface, leading to better overall strength and longer-lasting performance of the material. However, in the present study, replacing more than 50% but less than 70% with steel slag resulted in a 30% decrease in compressive strength. This decline may be due to factors such as the angular shape and varied particle size distribution of the slag fine aggregate, which could affect workability and compaction. Additionally, the hydration reactions between cement and steel slag fine aggregate may differ from those involving natural river sand, potentially leading to weaker bonding within the concrete matrix.

Impact of plastic aggregate substitute to coarse aggregate

There are forty-five samples were made using different %ages of plastic aggregate upto 50% as a substitute for coarse aggregate (PACA) for a week, two weeks and four weeks of curing. All the modified concrete mixes having varying proportion of PA such as 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50% were showing a compressive strength of 26, 28, 29, 23, and 15.5 MPa, respectively as shown in see Fig. 7. It was observed that the concrete mix containing 30% plastic aggregate had a 30% lower compressive strength value than regular concrete, while the concrete containing 40% plastic aggregate had a 43% decrease.

This suggests that incorporating up to 30% plastic aggregate does not significantly compromise structural integrity, likely due to effective bonding between the plastic and cementitious materials. However, the substantial decline in strength at 40% indicates that higher proportions of plastic aggregate may disrupt the concrete matrix, leading to weaker bonding and lower density. These findings have significant implications for material selection, particularly in applications requiring high compressive strength; it is advisable to limit plastic aggregate to 30%. Furthermore, the use of plastic aggregates contributes to sustainability by recycling waste materials, although the trade-off in strength must be carefully evaluated. On the basis of the findings, it was noticed that using plastic aggregates beyond 40% is unsuitable for reinforced concrete applications, while up to 30% of can be utilized in the construction of rural roads.

Mix optimization

Experimental findings of the present study suggest that slag may be utilized up to 30% as a substitute for cement (SAC) and up to 50% replacement of fine aggregate (SAFA), which is consistent with the findings of Devi and Gnanavel’s24. Despite having a lower compressive strength than regular concrete16, the suggested mixes are appropriate for rural roads with low traffic volumes in accordance with IRC: SP 62-201458 criteria. Earlier, it was reported that the usage of 20% EAFS as a cement replacement results in a reduction in the compressive strength value as compared to normal concrete59. Notably, mixes including 50% EAFS in place of fine were showing a slight decrease in strength (i.e., 1%), compared to regular concrete. This is probably because the finer slag aggregate has a higher packing density. As a result, it is suggested to construct the National and State highways by incorporating 50% of EAFS as a substitute for fine aggregates. Furthermore, it was found that the usage of plastic aggregates in the construction of low-volume roads as a substitute for natural aggregates (up to 30%) yielded improved results. Impact of blended concrete on mechanical properties of Mix

Impact on workability

The control mix was used to compare the workability of different modified mixes, measured in terms of slump as shown in Fig. 8(a). The values for slump were as follows: SCFA at 33 mm, SFPA at 43 mm, SCPA at 38 mm, and SCFPA at 29 mm, while the normal concrete achieved a slump of 53 mm. Based on experimental study, the SCFA mix’s workability was shown to be 38% less than that of regular concrete. Conversely, mixes containing plastic aggregate demonstrated significant improvements in workability, with the SCPA mix showing a reduction of only 19% in comparison to the normal mix. The SCFPA mix, which contained plastic aggregate, fine slag aggregate, and cementitious slag, showed a significant drop in workability, measuring 45% less than regular concrete.

Impact on density of mix

The concrete density by a modified mixes were compared to the conventional mix after four weeks, see Fig. 8(b). The densities recorded were 2372 kg/m³ for SCFA, 2385 kg/m³ for SFPA, 2324 kg/m³ for SCPA, and 2295 kg/m³ for SCFPA, while the conventional concrete density of was 2355 kg/m³. The SCFPA mix exhibited the greatest reduction in density, showing a 2.5% decrease compared to normal concrete, while the SCFA mix had the highest density increase at 0.75% above that of the normal concrete. Thus, the SCFPA mix had the maximum density reduction among the combinations tested.

Impact on strength value

The strength value of mixes having 30% SAC and 50% SAFA was evaluated through compression tests, as well as the influence of 30% PACA on strength value was examined. Different mixes were exhibiting different strength values after 28-day curing time period i.e., in case of NC a strength value of 41.3 MPa was reported. While 30 MPa, 41 MPa, and 29 MPa were reported in case of SAC3, SAFA5, and PACA3 respectively as shown in Fig. 9(a). The SAC3 mix showed a 25% decrease in strength value as compared to NC, however the SAFA5 mix reached the NC mix’s desired strength. Additionally, the PACA3 mix showed a 30% decrease in compressive strength relative to NC.

Furthermore, the blended mixes’ compressive strength values were tested and compared with the conventional concrete mix. In comparison to regular concrete, the SCPA mix showed a 36% decrease in strength value after four weeks of curing, along with standard deviation as shown in Fig. 9(b). The SCPA mix obtained a strength value of 26.5 MPa at 28 days, whereas it exhibited 9.4 MPa and 13.5 MPa strength values at one week and two weeks, respectively. The primary reason for decreasing the strength value could be the presence of inactive cementitious slag particles, and the same findings were reported by Barnett60. Thus, the SCPA mix did not achieve early strength, falling below 30% of the target.

Fig. 9Compressive strength of concrete mix replacement using cementitious slag, fine aggregate slag, and plastic aggregate in (a) individual and (b) combination forms.

Compressive strength of concrete mix replacement using cementitious slag, fine aggregate slag, and plastic aggregate in (a) individual and (b) combination forms.

In contrast, the SCFA mix demonstrated significant early strength, achieving 24.9 MPa and 29.5 MPa at one week and two weeks, respectively, with a highest strength of 36.1 MPa at four weeks, over 70% of the target strength. After a week, two weeks and four weeks curing, the strength performance pattern of the SFPA mix was found to be comparable to that of the regular concrete. The strength of the SCFPA mix was reported as 22.1 MPa after 7 days. On the other hand, the PACA3 mix achieved only 10.5 MPa, indicating a substantial reduction in early strength for PACA3 compared to SCFPA because of the presence of slag as a substitute for cement and fine aggregate, which are responsible for triggering the early hydration process. According to the experimental findings, the main sources of the modified mixes’ lower compressive strength in comparison to the standard mix are plastic aggregates and cementitious slag. Notably, the smooth surface of the plastic aggregates may lead to weak bonding with the mortar, and slag used as a cement substitute may become inactive due to insufficient water, activating only after 14 days. When compared with other modified mixes, the modified mixes with slag as fine aggregates showed a higher strength after 28 days. This is probably because the slag has a better packing density than the native aggregate. The current study’s findings showed that using plastic aggregate in conjunction with cementitious slag and fine slag aggregate yields results that are comparable to those of ordinary concrete, with a maximum 15% decrease in strength value.

Impact on flexural strength values

The flexural strength of regular concrete (NC) was contrasted with that of the mixes SAC3, SAFA5, and PACA3 as per the guidelines of IS 516 (Part 1/Sect. 1)-202156. After 28 days of curing, the flexural strengths were recorded as follows: NC at 4.6 MPa, SAC3 at 5.25 MPa, SAFA5 at 9.08 MPa, and PACA3 at 5.17 MPa (Fig. 10a). While the Standard deviation bars were also used to show the variation in experimental data around the mean value. The SAFA5 mix demonstrated the highest flexural strength, achieving 98% of the strength of NC, while the SAC3 mix showed a 15% increase compared to NC. The PACA3 mix also displayed a 12% increase in compressive strength over NC. At 14 days, the flexural strengths of SAC3, SAFA5, and PACA3 increased by 8%, 93%, and 24%, respectively, compared to NC. Similarly, at 7 days, the flexural strengths of SAC3, SAFA5, and PACA3 were 25, 70, and 68% of NC’s strength. Additionally, the SCFA mix exhibited increases of 26, 35, and 58% in comparison to the normal mix at a week, two weeks and four weeks, respectively (Fig. 10b). Overall, flexural strength was greatly increased at all curing ages by using EAFS in place of cement and fine aggregates in addition to plastic aggregates. It was found that EAFS improved the microstructure of the concrete, encouraging denser packing and strengthening the link between the cement matrix and aggregates. As a result, it is determined that the substitution of EAFS in place of cement, as well as FA and PA in place of coarse aggregate, greatly increases flexural strength, which is crucial for concrete pavement.

Fig. 10Concrete mix flexural strength when cementitious slag, fine aggregate slag, and plastic aggregate are substituted in (a) individual and (b) combination forms.

Concrete mix flexural strength when cementitious slag, fine aggregate slag, and plastic aggregate are substituted in (a) individual and (b) combination forms.

Additionally, measurements were made and compared to regular concrete to determine the flexural strengths of the SFPA, SCFA, SCFPA, and SCPA mixtures. After 28 days, the flexural strengths of SCPA and SFPA were found similar to the NC as demonstrated in Fig. 10(b). The reduced flexural strength in these mixes can be attributed to the use of plastic aggregates instead of natural coarse aggregates, which provide superior mechanical strength and bonding capabilities crucial for the structural integrity of concrete. In contrast, plastic aggregates have lower density and hinder strong bonding with the cement matrix, further compromised by their surface texture. Among the investigated mixtures, the SCFA mix notably had the highest flexural strength, supporting the results of Qasrawi et al.16. The unique properties of steel slag, such as its angular particle shape, high density, and mineral composition, contribute to enhanced bonding and mechanical performance in concrete. For the SCFPA mix, the flexural strength at 28 days was 5.5 MPa, while PACA3 recorded 5.17 MPa, indicating that the cementitious slag and fine slag aggregate did not effectively enhance resistance to flexural loads. The flexural strength of SCFPA and SCFA combinations was 20 and 60% higher than that of ordinary concrete, respectively.

Impact on tensile strength values

A comparison was made between the tensile strength of the mixes SAC3, PACA3 and SAFA5 and that of regular concrete according to the guidelines of IS 5816 − 199961(i.e. by casting cylinders with dimensions of 300 mm in length and 150 mm in diameter). A total of 36 samples were casted to check the impact of the additives on the split tensile strength value of modified mix. After 28 days, the tensile strengths were recorded as 5.17 MPa for NC, 5.31 MPa for SAC3, 5.57 MPa for SAFA5, and 5.23 MPa for PACA3, as shown in Fig. 11(a). At this point, SAFA5 exhibited the highest flexural strength, exceeding NC by 7.7%, while SAC3 and PACA3 showed strengths nearly equivalent to NC. The split tensile strength did not significantly change with the incorporation of 3% slag as a cement replacement and 7.7% as fine aggregates when compared to normal concrete. These findings suggest that slag can be effectively used as a supplementary material in concrete mixes without compromising strength, which is encouraging for sustainable construction practices. Notably, replacing 40% of river sand with slag improved strength values due to the formation of denser concrete. However, increasing the slag proportion further resulted in decreased strength due to the high water absorption characteristics and porous structure of steel slag, as noted by Fronek33. Furthermore, the SCFA, SCPA, SCFPA and SFPA mixes’ tensile strengths were compared to those of regular concrete. After 28 days curing time period, the tensile strengths of SFPA and SCPA were comparable to that of normal concrete, as illustrated in Fig. 11(b). The slag’s high water absorption and porous structure are probably the reasons why the split tensile strength did not significantly change, which is consistent with Yu et al.‘s62 findings. In case of SAC mix, strength value at early age was found to be reduced due to inactive slag particles, while the PACA mix was showing lower strength value due to poor bonding of plastic aggregates with the mortar. While slag may enhance long-term durability and sustainability, it generally reacts more slowly than traditional Portland cement, leading to delayed strength gain in the initial curing period. Likewise, plastic aggregates may not bond effectively with the cement paste, resulting in lower density and strength at early ages. Thus, optimizing mix design, including improved curing conditions or chemical admixtures, may be necessary to enhance early strength development. In conclusion, EAFS and plastic aggregates can replace cement, fine aggregates, and coarse aggregates without compromising the tensile strength of concrete mixes. Furthermore, EAFS as a cement replacement could be advantageous in rural road construction, achieving tensile strength comparable to normal mixes, meeting the minimum requirements for such roads.

Microstructure investigation

X-ray diffraction analysis

Following a 28-day curing time, the crystalline phases found in the modified concrete produced because of the addition of different additives and the cementitious materials were identified using the X-ray diffraction (XRD) method, which is based on Bragg’s law. For every test specimen, a step size of 0.0170 was maintained. As shown in Fig. 12, after crushing the specimen, a sieve of 900 μm size used and the resultant powder was then examined to identify patterns of relative intensity and peak locations. XRD can identify several crystalline phases, such as dicalcium silicate, tricalcium silicate, and ferrite minerals, which are essential for understanding the reactivity of slag and its potential as a construction material. Additionally, XRD allows for the identification of important phases like alite, belite, and calcium sulfate that significantly affect the strength and setting behavior of cement. Overall, X-ray diffraction is a valuable analytical method for investigating the crystallographic structure of materials, particularly in complex composites that include electric arc furnace steel slag, cement, plastic aggregates, river sand, and coarse aggregates.

Fig. 12XRD analysis on (a) SCPA (b) SFPA (c) SCFA and (d) SAC mixes.

XRD analysis on (a) SCPA (b) SFPA (c) SCFA and (d) SAC mixes.

In order to examine the material’s crystallographic structure, experiments were carried out at room temperature using CuKα radiation. Scans were carried out throughout a 2θ range of 5 to 80°. The important components, such as portlandite, calcium silicate hydrate (CSH), quartz, and ettringite, were found to be responsible for the concrete mix’s increased strength. It is noteworthy that calcium silicate hydrate and calcium hydroxide, two important silicate components, are generated during cement hydration. It turned out that the calcium silicate hydrate resembled tobermorite, a naturally occurring mineral that is essential for determining the rheological characteristics and strength of cement concrete. The setting time, strength development, and shrinkage of the concrete mix depend on ettringite (3CaO•Al2O3•3CaSO4•32H2O), a by-product of hydrated Portland cement that is distinguished by needle-like crystals. In addition to being a key bonding agent, Portlandite’s cubic smooth structure helps keep the pH of concrete at 12.6 and is a performance indication of the material’s resilience to carbonation. Additionally, calcium silicate and calcium aluminate are vital for binding properties and early strength, respectively. The mechanical qualities of the concrete mix were found to be improved by the addition of xonotlite, which is distinguished by needle-like structures and is compact in nature. Furthermore, Gyrolite in the mixture also helps ordinary Portland cement (OPC) hydrate in the initial phases.
