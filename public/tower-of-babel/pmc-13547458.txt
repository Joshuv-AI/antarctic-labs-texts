Acute Effects of Exercise Snacks on Glucose and Lipid Metabolic Outcomes in Individuals with Metabolic Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Yuxin Peng, Haijun Kong, Qiang Wang, Qingyun Sun, Changxu Qu, Zhengqing Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo quantify the acute effects of exercise snacks on glucose and lipid metabolism in individuals with metabolic disorders (including those with type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and overweight or obesity) compared with sedentary behavior and continuous exercise, and to examine their effects across different exercise modalities.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, four databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus) were searched up to February 4, 2026, to identify randomized crossover trials that examined the acute effects of exercise snacks on glucose and lipid metabolism in individuals with metabolic disorders. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, and the quality of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach. The “meta” and “metafor” packages in R (version 4.5.2) were used to calculate pooled effect estimates (using Hedges’ g as the effect size) and to conduct publication bias tests, subgroup analyses, and regression analyses.ResultsThe analysis included 20 randomized crossover trials (21 studies) involving 392 participants. Compared with prolonged sedentary behavior, exercise snacks significantly reduced postprandial glucose AUC (Hedges’ g = −0.37, 95% CI −0.53 to −0.22, P < 0.01, I2 = 6.6%, moderate certainty of evidence) and insulin AUC (Hedges’ g = −0.49, 95% CI −0.68 to −0.30, P < 0.01, I2 = 15.7%, moderate certainty of evidence), whereas no significant improvement was observed for triglycerides (Hedges’ g = −0.014, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.15, P = 0.87, I2 = 0.0%, low certainty of evidence). Compared with a single session of continuous exercise, exercise snacks showed no statistically significant differences in postprandial glucose (Hedges’ g = −0.14, 95% CI −0.73 to 0.45, P = 0.64, I2 = 57.6%, low certainty of evidence), insulin, or triglycerides. The included studies did not provide separate data for individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of MetS; therefore, the subgroup analysis was limited to overweight or obese individuals and those with T2DM. When stratified by study population, exercise snacks significantly reduced the postprandial glucose AUC in overweight or obese individuals (Hedges’ g = −0.36, 95% CI: −0.52 to −0.20, P < 0.001), whereas the effect estimate in the T2DM group did not reach statistical significance (Hedges’ g = −0.45, 95% CI: −1.47 to 0.56, P = 0.385), and the difference between the two subgroups was not significant (P between subgroups = 0.86). Insulin AUC was significantly reduced in both the overweight or obese group (Hedges’ g = −0.42, 95% CI: −0.61 to −0.24, P < 0.001) and the T2DM group (Hedges’ g = −1.15, 95% CI: −1.76 to −0.54, P < 0.001), and the difference between subgroups was significant (P = 0.03 between subgroups); however, the estimate for the T2DM subgroup was based on only one study. Regression analysis indicated that older participants experienced a greater reduction in insulin levels (β = −0.01, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.00, P = 0.02).ConclusionIn individuals with metabolic disorders, repeatedly interrupting sedentary behavior with short bouts of mild- to moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (2–5 minutes of activity every ≤30 minutes) can acutely reduce postprandial glucose and insulin responses. A meta-regression analysis suggests that studies involving participants with a higher average age showed a greater reduction in postprandial insulin AUC. No statistically significant differences in short-term glycemic control were observed between exercise “snacks” and a single continuous exercise session; however, this comparison was based on only four studies with small sample sizes and is therefore insufficient to demonstrate the non-inferiority or equivalence of the two exercise patterns. Exercise “snacks” can serve as a low-barrier complementary strategy to regular exercise and other lifestyle management approaches.Keywords: exercise snacks, glycemic control, metabolic syndrome, obesity, sedentary behavior, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome have become global public health challenges, with insulin resistance and impaired glucose regulation as their core pathophysiological mechanisms.1 Sedentary behavior is defined as sitting or lying down while awake and characterized by an energy expenditure ≤1.5 METs2–5 and has emerged as a major independent risk factor for the onset and progression of metabolic diseases.6–8 Prolonged sitting is associated with postprandial glucose metabolism abnormalities, insulin resistance, and an increased risk of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.9–12 Although guidelines recommend that adults engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week,13 many people with type 2 diabetes do not meet the recommended physical activity levels; lack of time and difficulty maintaining consistency are the main barriers.14 Therefore, it is imperative to explore alternative exercise strategies that are more time-efficient and easier to integrate into daily life.

Exercise Snacks are a form of exercise that uses short intervals of time to interrupt prolonged sitting,15 they refer to short bursts of physical activity—such as brisk walking, climbing stairs, or bodyweight exercises—repeated at predetermined intervals during long periods of sitting (typically lasting ≤10 minutes per session).2,16 In addition to exercise type, frequency, and duration, energy expenditure is also a key dimension characterizing the dose of exercise snacking; its magnitude is jointly influenced by factors such as activity intensity, session duration, frequency of interruptions, and individual body weight, and may affect postprandial metabolic responses.17–19 Frequent interruptions of prolonged sitting trigger brief skeletal muscle contractions that may activate AMPK/TBC1D4-related signaling and GLUT4-mediated glucose transport,20,21 and may promote triglyceride hydrolysis by maintaining lipoprotein lipase activity in skeletal muscle.22 Randomized crossover trials and systematic reviews suggest that, compared with continuous sedentary behavior, such interruptions acutely improve postprandial glucose and insulin responses;23–25 high-frequency interruptions (at intervals of ≤30 minutes) may be more beneficial for glycemic control, although the certainty of the evidence remains low.26

Existing systematic reviews of this strategy have varied in their scope. Zhang et al compared cumulative physical activity with a single bout of continuous exercise;24 Loh et al included a mixed population of metabolically healthy and metabolically impaired individuals and compared activity interruptions with sustained sedentary behavior or continuous exercise;25 Yin et al focused on different frequencies of interruptions;26 an earlier review combined observational and experimental evidence;27 Chang et al evaluated postprandial glucose and insulin responses in obese adults.28 Related studies have also addressed comparisons with structured continuous exercise, different interruption frequencies and exercise modalities, as well as long-term or non-experimental implementation settings,29–34 further indicating that previous evidence lacks consistency in terms of study populations, intervention definitions, control groups, and outcome measures. Building on this foundation, this review includes studies published through 2025, limits the focus to adults with metabolic disorders or at high risk for such conditions, and, within a unified acute analysis framework, separately evaluates the effects of exercise “snacks” on postprandial glucose, insulin, and triglycerides compared to sustained sedentary behavior and a single bout of continuous exercise, while also exploring potential effect moderators and assessing the certainty of the evidence.

Given the above background, the primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to quantify the acute effects of exercise snacks vs prolonged sitting on the area under the curve (AUC) of postprandial glucose levels in individuals with metabolic disorders or those at high risk for such conditions. Secondary objectives include: (1) evaluating the effects of exercise snacks vs prolonged sitting on the AUC of postprandial insulin and triglycerides; (2) where data are available, to compare the effects of exercise snacks vs a single bout of continuous exercise on postprandial metabolic outcomes; (3) to explore the potential moderating effects of participant and intervention characteristics through subgroup analyses and meta-regression. These analyses aim to provide preliminary evidence for optimizing exercise snack protocols for individuals with metabolic disorders and for developing more targeted strategies to interrupt prolonged sitting.

Methods

Study Registration and Reporting Standards

This study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.35 The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD420261298381).

Literature Search Strategy

Two researchers independently conducted a systematic electronic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception to February 4, 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) with free-text terms. English search terms included the following: “exercise snacks”, “activity snacks”, “breaks in sitting”, “intermittent walking”, “short bouts of exercise”, “stair climbing”, “type 2 diabetes mellitus”, “metabolic syndrome”, “hyperglycemia”, “glucose metabolism”, and “insulin resistance”. The search was limited to English-language publications. Additionally, to minimize potential omissions, we manually searched the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews to identify additional studies that met the inclusion criteria.

The disease status for type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome was based on the clinical diagnoses reported in the original studies and the diagnostic criteria cited therein; this study did not reclassify participants’ disease status based on the aggregated data.

Exclusion Criteria

(1) non-randomized studies (eg, single-arm trials, observational studies); (2) animal or in vitro studies; (3) reviews, conference abstracts, letters, or editorials; (4) studies for which data could not be extracted or obtained after contacting the authors; and (5) studies involving pregnant women or individuals with severe mobility impairments. The homogeneity of dietary regimens across different studies was not established as a criterion for study inclusion or exclusion.

Literature Screening

One researcher independently used Zotero to remove duplicate records from the retrieved records. Two other researchers then screened the remaining records according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria by reviewing titles and abstracts. Studies that could not be excluded based on titles and abstracts were reviewed in full text for eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Data Extraction and Transformation

Two researchers independently extracted data using a predesigned standardized form. The extracted information included: (1) general information: first author, year of publication, country/region, and study design type; (2) participant characteristics: sample size (N), age, sex distribution, BMI, and baseline health status (eg, T2DM, obesity); (3) intervention details: Type of exercise (walking, stair climbing, or resistance training), frequency, intensity, duration per session, total duration of the intervention, and whether the original study quantified energy expenditure during activity, the measurement or estimation methods used, and the specific reported values; and (4) outcome data: mean and standard deviation (SD) for each group after the intervention. In addition, we extracted data on dietary restriction prior to the experiment, the energy and macronutrient composition of the test meals, meal times, and the standardization or matching of meals between the intervention and control groups. A third researcher verified the extracted data. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a fourth researcher.

For area under the curve (AUC) data, iAUC was prioritized; if not reported, tAUC was used. If only graphical data were available, the authors were contacted to request the original data. If contact was unsuccessful but figures were available, WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.1) was used for digital extraction. If the original study reported only standard error (SE) or 95% confidence intervals (CI), these were converted to SD36 according to the formulas recommended in the Cochrane Handbook (Version 6.3), with results rounded to two decimal places. Assessment of Risk of Bias

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias of the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2).37 Since all studies included in this review were randomized crossover trials, the RoB 2 tool adapted for crossover trials was applied. When discrepancies arose between the two researchers, consensus was reached through discussion or consultation with a third researcher. For studies reporting multiple independent subgroups of participants (eg, Gale 2023 reported separate data for overweight and obese participants), these were included as separate entries in the meta-analysis to obtain precise effect estimates; however, in the risk of bias assessment (RoB 2), the evaluation was conducted at the study level to avoid artificially inflating the weight of individual studies.

Level of Evidence

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to assess the certainty of evidence,38 which classifies evidence as “high”, “moderate”, “low”, or “very low”. The GRADE assessment was conducted independently by two researchers.

(1)Effect Size Synthesis: Given differences in measurement units and assessment tools across the included studies, continuous variables were analyzed using standardized mean differences (SMDs)40 with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To correct for small-sample bias, SMDs were calculated and reported as Hedges’ g, with thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively.41

Results

Literature Screening Process

This study searched four databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search retrieved 385 articles from PubMed, 1667 from Embase, 682 from Web of Science, and 1770 from Scopus. Additionally, 20 articles were identified through a review of relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses, resulting in a total of 4524 articles. After deduplication using Zotero software supplemented by manual review, 1436 duplicate articles were removed, along with one retracted article. Ultimately, 3088 articles remained for the title and abstract screening stage. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 20 articles17–19,45–61 were included in the meta-analysis. Characteristics of Included Studies

The 20 included studies were published between 2012 and 2025 and involved a total of 392 participants. Of these, 202 were men and 166 were women; gender was not reported for the 24 participants in one study. The mean or median age of participants across the studies ranged from 21.0 to 70.0 years. The study populations included individuals with diagnosed type 2 diabetes as well as those who were overweight or obese; however, no study specifically recruited participants with a formal diagnosis of metabolic syndrome. Among the included studies on type 2 diabetes, Dempsey et al explicitly confirmed type 2 diabetes based on the American Diabetes Association criteria,48 while Homer et al reported inclusion criteria such as a prior diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, disease duration, HbA1c levels, and the use of antidiabetic medications.60 The mean or median BMI of participants across the studies ranged from 26.4 to 36.6 kg/m2; the study populations were primarily classified as overweight or obese. Some studies used BMI cut-offs specific to Asian populations or waist circumference-based criteria for central obesity. Most studies measured postprandial blood glucose through repeated venous blood draws, while a few used capillary blood glucose testing or continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). A total of three studies employed CGM,17,45,53 one of which also measured venous plasma glucose.17 The BMI categories, diagnostic or inclusion criteria, blood glucose measurement methods, and sampling time points for each study are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

The exercise “snacks” took various forms, including walking, chair squats, and resistance training, with each exercise session lasting 0.4–5 minutes. The frequency of exercise varied across studies; the exercise “snack” protocols included in the meta-analysis involved 8–21 exercise sessions per study day or during a specified study period, with a preset interval of 20–60 minutes between consecutive sessions. All included studies used protocol-specified test meals. Nineteen studies explicitly reported that the main test meals were standardized or matched between the exercise intervention and control conditions; one study, although it provided protocol-specified test meals, did not explicitly state whether meals and fluid intake were fully replicated across the different conditions. The dietary protocols across studies were not entirely homogeneous, with variations in meal format, energy intake, macronutrient composition, number of meals, and meal timing. Outcome measures included postprandial blood glucose, insulin, and triglycerides. The detailed characteristics are summarized (Table 1).

Primary Outcomes

Compared with prolonged sedentary behavior, the exercise snacks intervention group significantly reduced the area under the postprandial glucose curve (AUC) (Overall Hedges’ g = −0.37, 95% CI [−0.53, −0.22], P < 0.01) (Figure 2). To determine whether exercise snacks are a sufficient alternative to the continuous exercise recommended by traditional guidelines, we extracted studies that included both intervention modes for a direct comparison, which included four studies. No statistically significant difference was observed between the exercise snacks and a single bout of continuous exercise in controlling postprandial glucose levels (Overall Hedges’ g = −0.14, 95% CI [−0.73, 0.45], P = 0.64) (Figure 3). This suggests that distributing an equivalent (or lesser) amount of exercise throughout the day may result in postprandial glucose control trends comparable to those observed with traditional single, extended-duration exercise sessions; however, current evidence is insufficient to confirm the equivalence of the two approaches.

Secondary Outcomes

Compared with continuous sedentary behavior, exercise-based snacking significantly reduced postprandial insulin AUC (Overall Hedges’ g = −0.49, 95% CI [−0.68, −0.3], P < 0.01) (Figure 4); however, the exercise snack intervention used in this study did not lead to a significant reduction in postprandial triglyceride concentrations (Overall Hedges’ g = −0.014, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.15], P = 0.87) (Figure 5).

Subgroup Analysis

To further explore potential effect modifiers, pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed based on participant characteristics (health status) and intervention characteristics (type of exercise, duration per session, and frequency of sessions). Given their clinical relevance, the detailed results of the subgroup analyses for postprandial glucose, insulin, and triglycerides are provided (Tables 2–4).

Subgroup Analyses for Triglycerides AUC Outcomes

Study Population

For postprandial glucose levels, a significant reduction was observed in overweight or obese individuals without T2DM (SMD = −0.36, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.20, P < 0.001), whereas no significant reduction was observed in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) (SMD = −0.45, 95% CI −1.47 to 0.56, P = 0.385). There was no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.86).

For insulin, significant reductions were observed in both T2DM patients (SMD = −1.15, 95% CI −1.76 to −0.54, P < 0.001) and overweight or obese individuals (SMD = −0.42, 95% CI −0.61 to −0.24, P < 0.001), with a significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.03).

No significant reduction in triglycerides was observed in either the non-T2DM or T2DM groups, and there was no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.36).

Type of Exercise

For postprandial glucose, aerobic exercise significantly reduced postprandial glucose (SMD = −0.41, 95% CI −0.60 to −0.22, P < 0.001), whereas the reduction observed with resistance exercise was not statistically significant (SMD = −0.24, 95% CI −0.59 to 0.11, P = 0.179). There was no significant difference between the two subgroups (P = 0.40).

For insulin, aerobic exercise also showed a significant reduction (SMD = −0.49, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.28, P < 0.001), while resistance exercise had no significant effect (SMD = −0.44, P = 0.14), and there was no significant difference between the subgroups (P = 0.88).

Neither exercise type had a significant effect on triglycerides (between-group interaction P = 0.81).

Duration of a Single Session

A single exercise session lasting ≤3 minutes (SMD = −0.41, 95% CI −0.62 to −0.20, P < 0.001) or >3 minutes (SMD = −0.32, 95% CI −0.59 to −0.05, P = 0.02) both significantly reduced postprandial glucose levels, with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.64).

For insulin, durations of ≤3 minutes (SMD = −0.51, 95% CI −0.79 to −0.24, P < 0.001) and >3 minutes (SMD = −0.45, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.16, P = 0.002) both showed significant reductions, with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.74).

No significant changes in triglycerides were observed across different exercise duration groups (between-group interaction P = 0.70).

Frequency of Interruptions

High-frequency interruptions (≤30 minutes) significantly reduced postprandial glucose (SMD = −0.40, 95% CI −0.58 to −0.22, P < 0.001) and insulin (SMD = −0.50, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.29, P < 0.001), whereas interruption frequencies >30 minutes showed no significant changes in postprandial glucose (SMD = −0.24, P = 0.197) or insulin (SMD = −0.35, P = 0.179). Interruption frequency had no significant effect on triglycerides (between-group interaction P = 0.63).

Total Duration of Exercise

For postprandial glucose levels, cumulative daily exercise durations of ≤30 minutes (SMD = −0.43, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.15, P < 0.01) and >30 minutes (SMD = −0.34, 95% CI −0.54 to −0.15, P < 0.01) both resulted in significant reductions, with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.62).

For insulin, significant reductions were observed in both the ≤30-minute group (SMD = −0.49, 95% CI −0.80 to −0.18, P < 0.01) and the >30-minute group (SMD = −0.49, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.23, P < 0.01), with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.97).

Triglyceride levels did not differ significantly across total exercise duration groups (between-group interaction P = 0.54).

Exercise Intensity

For postprandial glucose, low-intensity exercise significantly reduced postprandial glucose (SMD = −0.40, 95% CI −0.58 to −0.23, P < 0.01), whereas moderate-intensity exercise did not reach statistical significance (SMD = −0.39, 95% CI −0.83 to 0.05, P = 0.08), with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.96).

Both low-intensity (SMD = −0.48, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.23, P < 0.01) and moderate-intensity exercise (SMD = −0.50, 95% CI −0.84 to −0.15, P < 0.01) significantly reduced insulin levels, with no significant difference between subgroups (P = 0.93).

Neither exercise intensity had a significant effect on triglycerides (between-group interaction P = 0.11).

Meta-Regression and Sensitivity Analysis

To further explore the potential influence of continuous covariates on the effects of exercise snack interventions and to identify possible sources of heterogeneity across studies, univariate meta-regression analyses were conducted for three outcome measures—postprandial glucose, insulin, and triglycerides—using participants’ mean age, baseline body mass index, and duration of a single exercise session as moderator variables.

Postprandial Glucose

Mean age did not have a significant effect on the effect size for postprandial glucose (β = −0.00, P = 0.39). Baseline BMI was also not a significant source of heterogeneity (β = −0.03, P = 0.43). Furthermore, no significant linear relationship was observed between single-session exercise duration and the glycemic-lowering effect (β = −0.01, P = 0.90) (Figure 6). These findings suggest that the glycemic-lowering benefits of exercise snacks are consistent across different age groups, levels of obesity, and intervention durations.

Meta-regression of postprandial glucose effect sizes by age, body mass index, and exercise-snack duration.

Insulin

Meta-regression analysis revealed that mean age was a significant moderator of intervention effects. As mean age increased, the effect size of exercise snacks on reducing postprandial insulin showed a significant increase (β = −0.01, P = 0.02) (Figure 7). However, neither baseline BMI (β = −0.04, P = 0.34) nor single-session exercise duration (β = 0.02, P = 0.80) had a significant moderating effect. These findings suggest that exercise snacks may confer greater benefits for older individuals in improving postprandial insulin levels, and that these effects are relatively independent of obesity status and intervention duration.

Meta-regression of postprandial insulin effect sizes by age, body mass index, and exercise-snack duration.

Triglycerides

Triglycerides, a marker of lipid metabolism, showed no significant association with mean age (β = 0.00, P = 0.74) or baseline BMI (β = 0.01, P = 0.73). Furthermore, single-session exercise duration did not significantly explain heterogeneity across studies (β = 0.00, P = 0.97) (Figure 8).

Meta-regression of postprandial triglyceride effect sizes by age, body mass index, and exercise-snack duration.

Sensitivity Analysis

A stepwise exclusion method was used to perform sensitivity analysis. Sequential removal of individual studies did not substantially alter the pooled effect estimates or their 95% confidence intervals, indicating that the results were robust and not driven by any single study.

Assessment of Risk of Bias and Publication Bias

The risk of bias of the 21 included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) (Figure 9). The included trials showed low risk of bias in domains D2, D3, and D4, indicating generally low risk across these domains. Given the nature of exercise interventions, participants and intervention providers are typically unable to be blinded; therefore, assessments in the D2 domain are based primarily on whether knowledge of the intervention conditions led to deviations from the planned intervention, rather than solely on whether blinding was implemented. However, some studies in the DS domain have methodological limitations, resulting in some studies being rated as having some concerns. The primary reasons were inadequate reporting of washout periods and the lack of assessment of potential carryover effects in crossover designs. A few studies also raised concerns in other domains, including insufficient reporting of random sequence generation, allocation concealment procedures, and the absence of publicly available trial registration protocols. Overall, the risk of bias in the included studies was low to moderate, and the study results are reasonably reliable.

Discussion

Key Findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that interrupting prolonged sedentary behavior with brief bouts of physical activity can significantly improve blood glucose and insulin responses in individuals with metabolic disorders. Compared with continuous sedentary behavior, exercise snacks produced small-to-moderate improvements in postprandial blood glucose (SMD = −0.37) and near-moderate improvements in insulin (SMD = −0.49), consistent with the findings of Loh et al;25,28 however, no significant improvements in triglycerides were observed. Subgroup analyses revealed effects of a similar direction across several populations and intervention categories. In a direct comparison with a single bout of continuous exercise, the pooled results from four studies showed no statistically significant difference in postprandial glucose AUC (P = 0.64). However, due to the small number of included studies and wide 95% confidence intervals, the available results are insufficient to demonstrate non-inferiority, equivalence, or interchangeability between the two exercise modalities.

Furthermore, these four studies were inconsistent in terms of matching and reporting cumulative exercise duration, prescribed intensity, and energy expenditure. Gao et al explicitly set the average intensity and energy expenditure of the two walking protocols to be the same,53 while Holmstrup et al matched the cumulative exercise duration and specified relative intensity;57 although Wongpipit et al matched the cumulative duration, specified intensity, and walking speed, they did not report condition-specific total energy expenditure;56 Bhammar et al reported energy expenditure estimates that were relatively close, but the authors explicitly noted that they did not perform energy matching.45 Therefore, while the existing direct comparisons generally exhibit a certain degree of comparability in terms of exercise volume, this is insufficient to conclude that all studies achieved strict energy equivalence, and the pooled results cannot be entirely attributed to the exercise mode itself.

Meta-regression further indicated that the mean age of participants was a significant moderator of the postprandial insulin effect (P = 0.02), with studies involving participants of higher mean age showing a greater reduction in postprandial insulin AUC. One possible explanation is that age-related declines in skeletal muscle mass and function, as well as impaired insulin signaling, may allow non-insulin-dependent glucose uptake pathways activated by repeated muscle contractions (such as AMPK or Ca2 + -related pathways) to play a more prominent relative role. However, since this meta-regression is based on the mean age at the study level, this association should still be interpreted with caution and does not directly prove a causal relationship between individual age and intervention effects.

Physiological Mechanisms Underlying the Effects of Exercise Snacks on Metabolic Health

AMPK Activation and Insulin-Independent Glucose Uptake in Skeletal Muscle

From a physiological perspective, prolonged sedentary behavior leads to reduced skeletal muscle contraction, which in turn limits muscle utilization of glucose. Previous experimental studies suggest that repeated, short-duration muscle contractions may activate AMPK-related signaling and promote the translocation of GLUT4 to the cell membrane, thereby enhancing skeletal muscle glucose uptake through a pathway that is relatively independent of insulin signaling.20,21 Exercise-induced myokines (such as IL-6 and irisin) may also be involved in metabolic regulation among skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue.62 These interrelated physiological responses may collectively explain the simultaneous decrease in postprandial blood glucose and insulin AUC following an exercise snack.

Biological sex may also modulate the aforementioned metabolic responses. Previous studies suggest that women may have higher insulin sensitivity and experience greater postprandial reductions in blood glucose and insulin levels following low-intensity physical activity that interrupts sedentary behavior; however, exercise studies in individuals with T2DM have not yet demonstrated consistent sex differences. Since the included studies lacked gender-stratified outcomes, and this study assessed postprandial insulin AUC rather than directly measured insulin sensitivity, it is not yet possible to test for a moderating effect of gender. Future trials should incorporate gender interaction analyses and report stratified results in a standardized manner.

This meta-analysis did not observe any significant differences between exercise snacks and a single session of continuous exercise in terms of short-term improvements in postprandial blood glucose. However, this comparison included only four studies, and the confidence intervals for the effect estimates were wide; therefore, the equivalence of the two exercise modalities cannot be concluded based on this alone. Previous meta-analyses comparing high-intensity interval training with moderate-intensity continuous exercise suggest that the temporal distribution and interval structure of exercise may influence glucose regulation;63 however, such interventions are not entirely consistent with exercise snacking in terms of exercise intensity, duration, and physiological load, and thus can only serve as an indirect reference. Whether exercise snacks can achieve metabolic efficiency equivalent to or higher than that of continuous exercise while maintaining equivalent total exercise volume or energy expenditure remains to be determined by further rigorously designed direct comparative studies.

Delayed LPL-Mediated Lipid Metabolism Limits Acute Triglyceride Reduction

In contrast to the improvements in postprandial blood glucose and insulin levels, this study did not observe a significant effect of exercise snacks on the acute postprandial triglyceride AUC. This result is inconsistent with the reductions in triglycerides reported in the meta-analysis by Loh et al (SMD = −0.26, 95% CI: −0.44 to −0.09) and the recent meta-analysis by Peng et al (SMD = −0.42, 95% CI: −0.81 to −0.02);25,64 however, the former suggests that publication bias may exist regarding triglyceride outcomes, while the latter’s pooled results exhibit high heterogeneity (I2 = 71.4%). Recent evidence itself is also not entirely consistent: neither Chen et al nor Wan et al observed significant improvements in triglycerides;65,66 although Chen et al found that exercise snacks were beneficial for other metabolic syndrome-related indicators such as waist circumference, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and HDL, Wan et al reported improvements in total cholesterol and LDL. Differences in study conclusions may be related to the study populations, the definition of exercise snacks, the duration of intervention, and the outcome measure. This study focused on the postprandial triglyceride AUC within a single experimental day in overweight, obese, or T2DM individuals, whereas other reviews included broader populations, longer intervention periods, and fasting or routine cardiometabolic indicators. Therefore, the non-significant results of this study should be interpreted strictly as indicating that no immediate postprandial triglyceride improvement has yet been observed; however, this does not rule out the potential benefits of repeated or longer-term exercise-based snacks on triglycerides and other components of metabolic syndrome.

From a physiological perspective, the beneficial effect of exercise on triglycerides primarily depends on the upregulation of lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity; however, LPL gene expression and protein synthesis exhibit a marked lag effect, typically peaking 12–24 hours after exercise. Since the studies included in this review were predominantly single-day acute experiments, they may not have captured later-stage changes in lipid metabolism. Previous meta-analyses of continuous exercise have indicated that, to induce a clinically significant reduction in postprandial triglycerides, energy expenditure from a single exercise session typically needs to reach approximately 1.5–2.0 MJ.67,68

However, only some of the studies included in this review measured or estimated energy expenditure during exercise, and the reported metrics ranged from energy cost per unit time of activity to total energy expenditure over the entire experimental period, changes in resting energy expenditure, and cumulative estimates based on predictive equations. Consequently, it was not possible to perform a uniform quantitative meta-analysis, nor could these values be directly compared with the 1.5–2.0 MJ threshold proposed in studies on continuous exercise. The current findings suggest that the increase in energy expenditure resulting from some short-term exercise interruption protocols is relatively limited; this may be one of the reasons why no acute postprandial triglyceride improvement was observed in this study. However, it is not yet possible to determine the energy threshold required for exercise-based snacking to improve lipid metabolism based on these findings.

Clinical and Policy Implications

In terms of effect magnitude, the reductions in postprandial glucose AUC (Hedges’ g = −0.37) and insulin AUC (Hedges’ g = −0.49) in this study were small to moderate and close to moderate, respectively; both outcomes were assigned a moderate level of evidence, indicating that brief, repeated interruptions of sedentary behavior can produce relatively consistent acute postprandial metabolic benefits. Although there is currently no absolute effect threshold available to determine clinical significance, if these acute improvements can accumulate over time in daily life, they may help reduce repeated postprandial glucose and insulin exposure. Therefore, the present findings support the use of exercise snacks as an adjunctive intervention strategy with potential for clinical application, while long-term benefits regarding HbA1c, body weight, and cardiovascular metabolism remain to be further clarified.

Clinically, this study supports the use of “exercise snacks” as a low-threshold adjunctive strategy to reduce the postprandial metabolic burden associated with prolonged sitting, particularly for overweight, obese, or type 2 diabetic individuals who sit for extended periods or find it difficult to engage in prolonged exercise sessions at once. In practice, short walks or simple resistance exercises can be interspersed during prolonged periods of sitting. In line with current recommendations, interrupting sedentary behavior approximately every 30 minutes can serve as a practical starting point rather than a fixed prescription; specific protocols should be tailored to individual fitness levels, mobility, diabetes complications, and the use of glucose-lowering medications. Exercise snacks should complement—rather than replace—regular aerobic and resistance exercise, weight management, and medication therapy. At the policy level, workplaces, communities, and primary care settings can promote implementation by providing short activity breaks, digital reminders, and safe, accessible spaces for physical activity. Future long-term, real-world, and cost-effectiveness studies to further validate sustained benefits will help develop more personalized and scalable exercise prescriptions; until then, it is not appropriate to establish a single interruption frequency or activity protocol as a uniform, mandatory standard.

Advantages and Limitations

The strengths of this study include both comparisons with sedentary behavior and direct comparisons with continuous exercise, as well as strict adherence to the PRISMA guidelines. The inclusion of 20 studies enhanced statistical power, and the analysis covered three core metabolic outcomes, with rigorous subgroup analyses and multivariable meta-regression conducted to systematically explore sources of heterogeneity.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, studies with long-term interventions (>3 months) were not included, and the current evidence is based on short-term metabolic trials. Second, the number of studies directly comparing exercise snacks with continuous exercise was small (n = 4), with wide confidence intervals (−0.73 to 0.45). Due to limited sample size and statistical power, the results suggest no statistically significant difference between the two approaches. At present, there is insufficient evidence to confirm the non-inferiority or equivalence of exercise snacks compared with continuous exercise. Third, the number of studies specifically targeting patients with diagnosed type 2 diabetes remains limited, resulting in imbalances in subgroup sample sizes and reduced statistical power to detect potential differences between T2DM patients and overweight or obese individuals. Fourth, due to inconsistent reporting across studies, iAUC and tAUC were combined into a single AUC metric. Although standardized using SMD, physiological differences between these measures may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity.

Although most of the included studies standardized or matched the main test meal across different experimental conditions within the same study, thereby reducing dietary confounding within studies, there was inconsistency across studies regarding meal composition, energy intake, and meal timing. These differences may influence the magnitude of postprandial metabolic responses and constitute a potential source of clinical heterogeneity in this meta-analysis. Furthermore, due to the nature of exercise interventions, participants and intervention administrators are typically unable to be blinded, which may introduce implementation bias; although randomized crossover designs and objective metabolic outcomes can mitigate this effect, they cannot completely eliminate it. Finally, Egger’s test indicated potential small-sample (publication) bias, possibly reflecting the tendency for early, small-scale studies with positive findings to be published. Future large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are needed to validate and refine these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, compared with prolonged sedentary behavior, exercise-based snacking acutely reduces postprandial blood glucose and insulin AUC in individuals with metabolic disorders, but no significant improvement in triglyceride levels was observed. The direct comparison between exercise snacks and a single session of continuous exercise included only four studies with small sample sizes, and the 95% confidence intervals for the estimated postprandial blood glucose effects were wide. Therefore, the current results only indicate that no statistically significant differences were observed between the two exercise modes in terms of short-term glycemic control; they do not yet prove that exercise snacks are non-inferior, equivalent, or a substitute for continuous exercise. Exercise snacks can serve as a low-barrier supplementary strategy to interrupt sedentary behavior and alleviate the acute postprandial metabolic burden. Future large-scale, long-term randomized trials with strict matching of total exercise volume or energy expenditure are needed to further clarify their relative efficacy, optimal implementation strategies, and long-term clinical benefits.
