Effect of positive end-expiratory pressure on risk of microaspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia in mechanically ventilated ICU patients: a prospective study
Authors: Ji Chen, Min Zhang, Yan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients receiving mechanical ventilation. Microaspiration of subglottic secretions has a pivotal role in VAP pathogenesis, yet the optimal positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) for preventing microaspiration has not been extablished.MethodsThis prospective, single-center study determined the impact of different PEEP levels on microaspiration in 90 mechanically ventilated patients. Participants were stratified into three groups based on PEEP levels (0–3 cmH₂O, 4–6 cmH₂O, and 7–10 cmH₂O). Primary outcomes included biomarkers, such as pepsin concentration in airway secretions, and the incidence of microaspiration. Secondary outcomes assessed subglottic secretion volume, mechanical ventilation duration, and ICU length of stay.ResultsHigher PEEP levels (4–6 cmH₂O and 7–10 cmH₂O) were associated with significantly lower microaspiration rates and pepsin concentrations in airway secretions compared to the lowest PEEP group (0–3 cmH₂O; P < 0.05). Patients in the higher PEEP groups exhibited reduced total subglottic secretion volumes over 7 d and a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation. Group C (7–10 cmH₂O) demonstrated the most pronounced benefits with respect to microaspiration. No significant differences were observed in the duration of ICU stay among the groups.ConclusionElevated PEEP levels, especially within the 7–10 cmH₂O range, effectively reduce microaspiration, minimize subglottic secretion leakage, and shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation. These findings highlight the clinical importance of higher PEEP settings in reducing VAP risk and improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a prevalent pulmonary infection that occurs in patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation for >48 h or within 48 h after extubation [1]. The incidence of VAP among mechanically ventilated patients in China ranges from 9.7% to 48.4% [2]. Higher rates were noted in patients with severe underlying conditions requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. The management of VAP poses considerable challenges, frequently leading to extubation delays and complicating ventilator weaning, which may worsen patient prognosis [3, 4].

Microaspiration, characterized by the inadvertent entry of subglottic secretions into the lower respiratory tract, significantly contributes to the development of VAP [5]. Studies have shown that >60% of patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation experience microaspiration [6, 7]. The increased risk of VAP following microaspiration is associated with impaired swallowing function and the presence of an open glottis after intubation [8]. Microaspiration facilitates colonization of pathogenic bacteria from oral, pharyngeal, or gastric secretions in the cuff of the endotracheal tube [9]. Subsequent infiltration of these secretions into the respiratory system through suboptimal cuff inflation or leakage around the cuff can lead to pulmonary inflammation and the onset of VAP [10]. Therefore, prevention of microaspiration in mechanically ventilated patients is essential for reducing VAP rates and improving patient outcomes [11].

To identify and monitor microaspiration, clinicians commonly evaluate microaspiration by quantifying biomarkers, such as pepsin (>200 ng/mL) or α-amylase (>1685 UI/L), in airway secretions, particularly tracheal secretions [12]. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is a standard ventilatory strategy used in mechanical ventilation to enhance alveolar recruitment, prevent lung collapse, and improve oxygenation [13]. PEEP is vital in preventing microaspiration by sustaining airway pressure and impeding secretions from refluxing into the lower respiratory tract [8, 14]. However, the effectiveness of PEEP in averting microaspiration primarily rely on in vitro models, revealing a knowledge gap regarding the optimal PEEP level for reducing microaspiration and lowering the risk of VAP in critically ill patients.

The current study examined the influence of various PEEP levels on microaspiration in ICU patients undergoing mechanical ventilation utilizing pepsin as a biomarker. The findings offer valuable insights for enhancing the treatment of mechanically ventilated patients, thereby decreasing the incidence of VAP and enhancing overall healthcare provision.

Materials and methods

Patient information

The current study received approval from the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval no.: 2021-R139 [approved on 8 February 2022]) and included 90 cases of mechanically ventilated patients treated in the ICU department of a tertiary hospital in Ningbo between October 2022 and June 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from the family members of each patient.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) ≥ 18 years of age; 2) the duration of wearing invasive mechanical ventilation between 3 days and 14 days; 3) enteral nutrition through nasogastric feeding using a Flocare Infinity infusion pump (NUTRICIA China, N.V.NUTRICIA, Shanghai, China); 4) Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) score < 24; 5) stable vital signs during treatment with no contraindications for repositioning or body adjustments; and 6) intubation with a Covidien 18875 circular cuff tracheal tube (COVIDIEN Shanghai, Shanghai, China). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of VAP diagnosis in previous treatments according to medical history; (2) respiratory infection before hospital admission. (3) medical history including airway surgery, bronchial fistula, or gastroesophageal reflux before admission. (4) an immunodeficiency disorder; (5) acute respiratory distress syndrome; and (6) tracheotomy.

A nurse unaware of this study put all the patients’ information into a computer and generated random numbers for these patients in the computer. After the random code for each patient was determined, the patients matched to the random codes were assigned to three groups one by one in sequence by the same nurse until all the participating patients have been assigned. Then the assigned three groups were randomly named as A, B, and C groups. Based on the PEEP level administered during mechanical ventilation, group A accepted the settings of 0–3 cmH2O, group B accepted the settings of 4–6 cmH2O, and group C accepted the settings of 7–10 cmH2O.

Research methodology

General information

All patients enrolled for monitoring received ongoing assessment of vital signs, treatment for underlying conditions, and appropriate sedation with intravenous injectionmidazolam (0.01–0.03.01.03 mg/kg) and propofol (0.2–2.2 mg/kg/h) before the study. The head of the bed was elevated at an angle between 30° and 45°. Oral hygiene care included the use of chlorhexidine mouthwash every 6 h. The volume control ventilation mode was adopted in the process. Strict adherence to aseptic techniques and rigorous hand hygiene practices were imperative. Subglottic suctioning was set intermittently every 2 h with pressures ranging from 136 to 204 cmH₂O [15, 16]. If blockage occurs, An air bolus was utilized to clear blockages [17, 18]. During airway suctioning, pressure was maintained between 108 and 163 cmH₂O with suction ≤ 15 Sect [18]. The patients had to be reintubated with a Covidien 18,875 circular cuff tracheal tube if not in place on admission. Three skillful clinicians blinded to this study helped to set the ventilation mode and helped on ventilation-related procedures.

Cuff administration

Patients from three distinct cohorts were fitted with disposable pressure transducers with an airbag that connected in three directions. The patients were connected to the endotracheal tube cuff, and were consistently monitored for arterial blood pressure and integrated with bedside ECG surveillance. The central venous pressure (5 ~ 12cmH2O) served as the benchmark parameter. Pressure regulation of the aerocyst was sustained below 25–30 cmH2O for the entire duration of the investigation. The same three skillful clinicians blinded to this study helped to set the disposable pressure transducers and helped on disposable pressure transducers -related procedures.

Nasogastric feeding

Patients underwent a 6-h fasting period before nasogastric feeding during which stomach contents were emptied by 8 am. Enteral nutrition was administered continuously at 60–80 ml/h using the Flocare Infinity infusion pump following sputum suctioning. The total 24-h volume of nasogastric feeding was maintained within a range of 900–1500 ml. Patients maintained a semi-reclined posture while receiving enteral nutrition. Nasogastric feeding were performed by the same three clinicians blinded to this study.

Observational indicators

The observational indicators involved in this study were as follows: (1) Airway secretions were collected from 3 patient groups 2 h after the beginning of nasogastric feeding using a sputum collector (Fujian De’er Medical Industry Co., LTD, Xiamen, China). Gastric protease levels were detected using the human gastric protease test kit from Shanghai Tuoguang Company (Shanghai, China). The concentration of gastric protease wa recorded and the incidence rate of microaspiration was calculated for each patient group. (2) The sputum collector was connected to negative pressure suction at one end and to a catheter for material retention above the cuff at the level of the vocal cords. The total amount of material retained above the cuff in the tracheal tube was recorded every day within 7 d for each patient. (3) The duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay was recorded for each patient group.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Data are represented as mean and standard deviation. The Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized for statistical comparisons with multiple comparisons adjusted using the least significant difference method. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentage-point in group) and were assessed using the Pearson chi-squared test with multiple comparisons corrected using the Sidak method. Pairwise comparisons were performed among the 3 groups with a significance level set at αsidak = 0.0170. Statistical significance was established at a P < 0.05.

Discussion

Microaspiration, a prevalent pathologic feature in respiratory diseases, presents challenges in detection due to its subtle nature. The underlying mechanisms and risk factors for microaspiration remain incompletely understood. Factors contributing to microaspiration include neurologic disorders, nasogastric feeding, patient transportation, mechanical ventilation, unconsciousness, and the use of sedative medications [19–21]. PEEP has a critical role in maintaining alveolar patency, preventing collapse, and facilitating efficient gas exchange in the lungs [22, 23]. However, determining the optimal PEEP level for mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU is a topic of debate due to the potential risk of lung injury associated with mechanical ventilation. While some studies have reported that a a PEEP level of 5 cmH₂O minimizes peri-aerocystic microaspiration without contraindications [24], other investigators have proposed that increasing the PEEP could reduce ventilation duration and related complications [25]. A large multicenter randomized trial showed no difference in VAP incidence between low or high PEEP in intubated patients without acute respiratory distress syndrome [26]. This controversy highlights the necessity for further research to establish the most suitable PEEP level for these patients.

Previous research examined the impact of varying PEEP levels on microaspiration and associated parameters. Nseir et al. [27] reported that maintaining a PEEP at 4–6 cmH₂O partially decreased microaspiration but showed limited influence on airway pepsin concentration, ventilation duration, and ICU length of stay. Similarly, another study indicated that a PEEP level of 5 cmH₂O maintained alveolar patency but did not adequately reduce subglottic secretions [28]. In contrast, higher PEEP levels of 7–10 cmH₂O were shown to be associated with significant reductions in microaspiration rates and airway pepsin concentrations. These studies also indicated that shorter ventilation duration and ICU stay suggest superior protective effects of elevated PEEP settings [28, 29]. However, the current study showed no differences between different groups at various PEEP settings.

The current study validated earlier research and offers additional insights into the specific advantages of increased PEEP levels. Patients in groups B and C with PEEP levels set at 5–6 cmH₂O and 7–10 cmH₂O, respectively, demonstrated significantly reduced rates of microaspiration, decreased volumes of subglottic secretions, and lower airway pepsin concentrations compared to group A (3 cmH₂O). Moreover, patients in group C exhibited the shortest ventilation duration and ICU stay, highlighting the clinical benefits of PEEP levels ranging from 7 to 10 cmH₂O. The current study also emphasizes the importance of regularly adjusting the cuff pressure between 25 and 30 cmH₂O to potentially enhance the protective effects of PEEP by improving the seal between the tracheal wall and the cuff [30, 31].

In the current study patients underwent continuous monitoring of aerocystic pressure to minimize bias with the pressure being dynamically adjusted within a range of 25–30 cmH₂O. A comparative analysis revealed a higher incidence of microaspiration and pepsin concentrations in airway secretions in group A compared to groups B and C. These findings suggest that maintaining PEEP levels at ≥ 3 cmH₂O, particularly within the 7–10 cmH₂O range, may reduce microaspiration incidence. Ouanes et al. [32] conducted a simulation study to investigate air leak rates at various PEEP levels and reported that the incidence of leaks decreased significantly from 91% to 8% as the PEEP was adjusted from 0 to 15 cmH2O. In a study involving 40 critically ill patients, Lucangelo et al. [33] observed that all patients exhibited subglottic leakage within 1 h of PEEP removal. Lucangelo et al. [33] recommended the application of a minimum PEEP of 5 cmH2O, unless contraindicated, to delay or prevent microaspiration. Ouanes et al. [32] suggested a feasible mechanism in which elevated airway pressure prompts air to flow from the distal to the proximal aerocyst, causing a conical configuration that enhances the seal tightness of the aerocyst. When combined with the positive pressure from PEEP, this mechanism obstructs fluid migration into the lower respiratory tract, thereby diminishing the risk of microaspiration [34].

The accumulation of subglottic secretions is a significant factor in the development of VAP. Impaired swallowing post-endotracheal intubation leads to the buildup of subglottic secretions with a high microbial content, acting as a reservoir for VAP-causing pathogens [35]. In agreement with prior research, the findings herein indicated that elevated PEEP levels (7–10 cmH₂O) lead to a notable decrease in subglottic secretion volumes compared to lower PEEP levels (3–4 cmH₂O). This reduction not only lowers the likelihood of microaspiration but also has the potential to reduce the occurrence of VAP by restricting bacterial colonization in the lower airways [36]. Microaspiration of these secretions into the lower airways fosters bacterial colonization [37], heightening the VAP risk and resulting in prolonged mechanical ventilation, extended ICU stays, and heightened healthcare resource usage [38, 39]. Notably, patients in group C experienced a shorter mechanical ventilation duration than group A, suggesting that higher PEEP levels may also aid in reducing ventilation duration.

This study had several limitations, particularly the single-center design and small sample size, which may constrain the generalizability of the results. The patient cohort in this study may not fully represent the broader diversity of ICU patients due to potential variations in clinical practices, patient demographics, and healthcare settings that could impact outcomes. Furthermore, although the current study suggested the potential benefits of higher PEEP levels in preventing microaspiration and associated complications, some confounding variables were not addressed, such as differences in sedation protocols, comorbidities, and variations in staff training and equipment quality. Future research should prioritize multi-center studies with larger and more diverse patient populations to validate these findings and enhance their relevance.

Conclusion

Elevated PEEP levels have efficacy in mitigating microaspiration in mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU, decrease leakage of subglottic secretions, and reduce the duration of ventilation, thereby lowering the incidence of complications. It is advisable to use high PEEP levels for ICU patients unless contraindicated. Despite the availability of various preventive measures for microaspiration, such as raising the head of the bed, using polyurethane aerocysts, and monitoring aerocyst pressure, no singular intervention has been definitively proven to be entirely practical. Clinicians should maintain vigilance and promptly initiate interventions for patients with low or no PEEP ventilation.
