Chitosan–Tripolyphosphate Nanoparticles Enhance Foliar dsRNA Delivery and Suppress Turnip Crinkle Virus in Arabidopsis
Authors: Min-Jae Kim, Nam-Yeon Kim, Rae-Dong Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTopically applied double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) can trigger antiviral RNA interference in plants, but naked dsRNA is unstable and poorly taken up after foliar application. Here, we developed an eco-friendly chitosan–tripolyphosphate (CS-TPP) nanocarrier to enhance dsRNA delivery using turnip crinkle virus (TCV) as a model and tested it in Arabidopsis thaliana. A dsRNA targeting the TCV-coat protein (CP) gene was produced in Escherichia coli, and complexation with CS-TPP was induced by ionic gelation. Complete complexation occurred at a 1:1 CS-TPP:dsRNA ratio, producing cationic nanoparticles that protected dsRNA from RNase digestion and maintained its integrity during incubation at 37°C. Using Cy3 fluorescence imaging, we observed stronger epidermis-associated fluorescence in plants treated with the CS-TPP@dsRNA complex than in those treated with naked dsRNA, and RT-qPCR confirmed higher CP-target RNA fragment accumulation in planta. Small RNA sequencing revealed increased levels of TCV-derived, 21–24 nt siRNA molecules targeting the CP region, suggesting the applied dsRNA was processed in planta. Importantly, a CS–TPP@TCV-dsRNA foliar spray application reduced systemic CP RNA levels at 7 and 14 days post-treatment and alleviated disease symptoms more effectively than naked dsRNA. These results demonstrate that CS–TPP nanocomplexation improves dsRNA stability and delivery, enabling improved and more durable antiviral protection.Keywords: chitosan-tripolyphosphate nanoparticles, double-stranded RNA, RNA interference, turnip crinkle virus

Plant viruses remain a persistent constraint on agricultural productivity and crop quality worldwide. Viruses replicate inside host cells and depend extensively on host processes, limiting the use of direct chemical control options, such as those commonly used for fungal or bacterial diseases. Management, therefore, relies largely on preventive strategies, such as the use of virus-free planting materials and resistant hosts, vector control, and sanitation (Voloudakis et al., 2022). However, such approaches can be insufficient when resistance is unavailable or rapidly overcome, when multiple viruses co-occur, or when environmental conditions and vector pressure fluctuate. Accordingly, there is strong interest in the development of complementary, environmentally benign antiviral strategies that can be deployed flexibly and that align with the increasing demand for sustainable crop protection (Cagliari et al., 2019; Vetukuri et al., 2021).

RNA interference (RNAi) is a conserved, sequence-specific gene silencing mechanism that plays a central role in plant antiviral defense (Voinnet, 2001). During infection, viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates are processed into small interfering RNA (siRNA) molecules, which guide Argonaute-containing complexes to target viral RNA segments, degrading them and/or inhibiting their translation (Voinnet, 2001). This endogenous antiviral pathway has inspired exogenous dsRNA-based protection strategies, such as “RNA vaccination” or spray-induced gene silencing concepts, in which dsRNA designed to target essential viral genes is applied to plants to trigger RNAi without generating transgenic crops (Tenllado and Díaz-Ruíz, 2001; Voloudakis et al., 2022).

Despite its promise, the practical utilization of dsRNA for plant virus control faces several biological and physicochemical barriers. Naked dsRNA is susceptible to degradation and environmental stress, and its uptake across the leaf surface and cell wall, entry into the cytoplasm, and movement to relevant tissues are often limited, making efficacy vary across plant species and application conditions (Holeva et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). However, studies have shown that exogenously applied dsRNA can reduce infection and/or symptom development for several plant viruses, and, in some cases, dsRNA (or dsRNA-derived signals) has been detected systemically, demonstrating the feasibility of topical, non-transgenic antiviral approaches (Holeva et al., 2021; Kaldis et al., 2018).

Nanomaterial-assisted delivery is a promising route for improving dsRNA stability and bioavailability. Carriers can protect dsRNA from degradation, enhance adhesion/retention on plant surfaces, and facilitate cellular internalization, thereby extending the duration and consistency of protection (Mitter et al., 2017). For example, layered double hydroxide “clay nanosheets” sustained retention and prolonged antiviral protection after a single topical dsRNA application (Mitter et al., 2017). Nanoparticle formulations have also been evaluated more broadly for their ability to enhance dsRNA delivery against plant viruses, and delivery route (spray, infiltration, or root soaking) and particle chemistry both influenced performance and persistence (Xu et al., 2023).

Among polymeric carriers, chitosan is especially attractive because it is biodegradable and generally biocompatible, and it can interact electrostatically with nucleic acids to form protective complexes (Komarova et al., 2024; Scarpin et al., 2023). Chitosan nanoparticles can be prepared under mild aqueous conditions, and their size, charge, and release properties can be tuned by formulation parameters (Calvo et al., 1997a, 1997b). Tripolyphosphate (TPP) is frequently used as an anionic crosslinker. It forms chitosan nanoparticles via ionic gelation, yielding chitosan-TPP complexes (CS-TPP) without harsh solvents (Calvo et al., 1997a). Chitosan-TPP systems have already been used to complex dsRNA in RNAi applications, where they were shown to enhance dsRNA stability and delivery (Dhandapani et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). Thus, their effectiveness and eco-friendly nature make them promising candidate carriers for plant antiviral dsRNA.

Turnip crinkle virus (TCV) is a well-established experimental model for dissecting antiviral RNA silencing and virus–host interactions. Although TCV is not considered a major driver of economic loss in many cropping systems, it has been widely used because it reliably infects laboratory hosts and possesses a well-characterized silencing suppression system associated with its coat protein (Qu et al., 2003; Thomas et al., 2003). This makes TCV a practical model for evaluating whether a dsRNA formulation and delivery strategy can enhance antiviral RNAi outcomes in planta.

In this study, we used TCV as a model virus to develop and evaluate a CS-TPP-based delivery formulation for antiviral dsRNA. We designed dsRNA targeting essential TCV genomic regions and assembled dsRNA-CS-TPP complexes via ionic gelation. We then assessed whether nanoparticle complexation improves dsRNA delivery and antiviral efficacy in Arabidopsis thaliana, providing a framework for enhancing topical dsRNA approaches and informing future translation to economically important plant viruses.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of plants and inoculum

Seeds of A. thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) were sown on soil and stratified at 4°C for 1 day to synchronize germination. The germination trays were then placed in growth chambers and kept at 22°C and 60% relative humidity under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Fourteen days after germination, seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots containing a sterilized mixture of vermiculite, perlite, and soil (1:1:9, v/v). Plants were used for experiments at 5 weeks after sowing.

Infectious TCV RNA transcripts were generated by in vitro transcription. Briefly, 6 μg of linearized pTCV66 plasmid was used as a template in a reaction containing T7 RNA polymerase and 5 × transcription buffer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), 0.1 mol/L of dithiothreitol, 10 mmol/L of each rNTP, an RNase inhibitor (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan), and diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. The resulting RNA transcripts were purified by precipitation, resuspended to 0.05 μg/μL in inoculation buffer (pH 9.2; 25 mM glycine, 15 mM K2HPO4, 0.01% bentonite, 0.5% celite; final 1 × concentration), and stored at −80°C until use (Dempsey et al., 1993; Jeong et al., 2023).

Design and production of TCV-targeting dsRNA

A dsRNA fragment targeting the TCV-coat protein (CP) gene was designed based on the reference TCV genomic sequence retrieved from the NCBI Nucleotide database. Candidate target regions within the CP gene were initially screened using the SnapDragon web tool (flyrnai.org/snapdragon). Sequence conservation within the candidate fragments was then evaluated by comparing TCV-CP sequences deposited in the NCBI database using BLAST searches, and a highly conserved region was selected as the dsRNA target. To maximize target specificity and minimize potential off-target effects on host transcripts, candidate dsRNA designs were filtered following a previously described bioinformatic workflow (Chen et al. 2025). Briefly, candidate sequences were compared to the A. thaliana genome using a sliding-window, and sequences showing significant similarity to A. thaliana transcripts were excluded.

To generate a dsRNA expression construct, total RNA was extracted from TCV-infected A. thaliana leaves using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and used as a template for RT-qPCR amplification of the selected CP fragment. The forward and reverse primers were designed to include XbaI and EcoRI restriction sites, respectively, at their 5′ ends (Supplementary Table 1). The amplified fragment and the pL4440 vector were digested with XbaI and EcoRI, gel-purified, and ligated to produce pL4440-CP. The recombinant plasmid was introduced into RNase III-deficient Escherichia coli HT115 (DE3) by CaCl2-mediated chemical transformation (Ahn et al., 2019). Clones were screened by enzyme-digestion, and plasmid introduction was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

For in vivo dsRNA production, a confirmed transformant was cultured overnight in 5 mL of Luria–Bertani medium, and then inoculated into 200 mL of yeast extract–tryptone medium for scale-up. When the culture reached an OD600 of 0.4, dsRNA expression was induced by adding 1 mmol/L of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation at 37°C for 4 h.

Application of dsRNA to plants

The TCV-CP dsRNA was applied to A. thaliana plants by foliar spraying at a dose of 200 μg per plant. The dsRNA was diluted in DEPC-treated water and sprayed evenly onto both the adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces until run-off, ensuring complete coverage. One hour after application, plants were mechanically inoculated with TCV. For each plant, two fully expanded leaves were dusted with 500-mesh carborundum and gently rubbed with a 0.05 μg/μL suspension of in vitro–transcribed TCV RNA in inoculation buffer. Each treatment consisted of three independent biological replicates. At 14 days post-treatment (dpt), systemic leaves were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction for downstream gene expression analyses.

Small RNA sequencing

Small RNA libraries were prepared from systemic leaves collected 7 dpt from TCV-infected A. thaliana plants with and without a TCV-CP dsRNA pretreatment. Total RNA was extracted using the Clear-S™ Total RNA Extraction Kit (Invirustech Co., Gwangju, Korea). For each treatment, the extracts from three independent biological replicates were pooled, and 2 μg of total RNA was used to generate a composite library. Libraries were constructed using the SMARTer smRNA-Seq Kit (635029; TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using paired-end reads (2 × 50 bp). This sequencing depth was chosen to obtain approximately 10–20 million read pairs per library, a depth commonly used for small RNA profiling, as it provides robust coverage for size- and position-resolved small RNA analyses.

Raw reads were processed in Geneious Prime (v26.0.2). Adapter sequences and poly(A) tails introduced during library construction were trimmed using the “Trim Ends” function. Processed reads were filtered by length (18–32 nt), and reads in the canonical plant small RNA size classes, 21–24 nt, were selected for downstream analyses. These reads were mapped to the TCV complete genome sequence, requiring full-length alignments with no gaps and 0 mismatches (100% identity) to ensure specificity. Mapped reads were exported in the SAM format, separated into sense and antisense strands using SAMtools (v1.23), and strand-specific coverage profiles were computed and compared using BEDTools (v2.31.0). All graphs were generated in R (v4.3.0) (Golyaev et al., 2019).

Preparation and characterization of CS-TPP-dsRNA complexes

Chitosan–tripolyphosphate (CS-TPP) nanoparticles were prepared by ionic gelation as previously described (Dhandapani et al., 2019) with minor modifications. Chitosan (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) to a final concentration of 0.02% (w/v) and stirred at room temperature until completely solubilized. Sodium TPP (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared as a 1 mg/mL solution in distilled water. Then, CS-TPP nanoparticles were formed by adding the TPP solution dropwise into the chitosan solution until reaching a 1:10 volume ratio (TPP:chitosan). The mixture was then vortexed and incubated at 56°C for 1 min.

To determine the binding ratio between the CS-TPP nanoparticles and TCV-targeting dsRNA, the CS-TPP suspensions were mixed with 200 ng/μL of dsRNA to create mixtures with different volumetric CS-TPP:dsRNA ratios and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow complex formation. To assess binding efficiency, an electrophoretic mobility shift (gel retardation) assay was performed at 120 V for 20 min using a 1.2% agarose gel, with dsRNA migration visualized to identify the minimum ratio resulting in complete retardation.

The morphology of CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complexes was examined using field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM). Samples were dispersed in ethanol, drop-cast onto a carbon-coated 150-mesh nickel grid, and air-dried prior to imaging. Images were acquired using a JEM-2100 FE-TEM system (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

To evaluate colloidal stability and particle characteristics in aqueous solution, the ζ-potential and hydrodynamic size distribution were measured. The ζ-potentials of CS-TPP, naked TCV-dsRNA, and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA were determined in solutions adjusted to pH 7.4, and the hydrodynamic size distribution of CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). Measurements were performed using a particle size and zeta potential analyzer (ELSZneo; Otsuka Electronics, Osaka, Japan). All measurements were conducted in triplicate using independently prepared samples, and data are presented as means ± standard deviations.

Evaluation of CS-TPP stability under external conditions

To assess the protective capacity of CS-TPP nanoparticles against external stressors, their ability to protect TCV-dsRNA from RNase A and thermal stress at 37°C was evaluated. For the RNase A protection assay, 200 ng of naked TCV-dsRNA and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA containing an equivalent amount of dsRNA (200 ng) were each incubated with 1 μL of RNase A at 37°C for 1 h. After incubation, the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA was treated with a release buffer (1.6% SDS and 0.8 M NaCl) at 95°C for 30 min to release the dsRNA. The integrity of dsRNA was assessed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis (at 120 V for 20 min), comparing untreated naked dsRNA, RNase A–treated naked dsRNA, and dsRNA released from RNase A–treated CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA.

Thermal stability was evaluated by incubating 200 ng of naked TCV-dsRNA and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA containing 200 ng dsRNA at 37°C for 6 days. Five 5 μL aliquots were collected after 0, 2, 4, and 6 days of incubation and analyzed using 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis (at 120 V for 20 min) to monitor dsRNA integrity over time.

Comparison of leaf dsRNA uptake with or without CS-TPP complexation

To compare the uptake of topically applied naked dsRNA and CS-TPP@dsRNA in leaf tissues, Cy3-labeled TCV-dsRNA uptake was assessed using fluorescence imaging. The Cy3-labeled dsRNA (TCV-dsRNA-Cy3) was synthesized by in vitro transcription using the HyperScribe™ T7 High Yield Cy3 RNA Labeling Kit (APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. As a transcription template, the CP target fragment was amplified by RT-qPCR using primers containing T7 promoter sequences at the 5′ ends of both the forward and reverse primers (Supplementary Table 1), enabling bidirectional transcription and dsRNA formation.

Following synthesis, CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 complexes were prepared as described above. Complex formation was confirmed by gel retardation assay using 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis at 120 V for 20 min. After confirmation, 200 ng of naked TCV-dsRNA-Cy3, CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 complexes containing 200 ng of dsRNA, and pure water, serving as the negative control, were applied to individual A. thaliana plants by foliar spraying (n = 3 plants per treatment). After 24 h, treated leaves were collected and rinsed three times with sterile distilled water to remove surface-associated material. Samples were mounted on glass slides in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 0.1 M phosphate buffer and glycerol and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 980 with Airyscan 2; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany).

RT-qPCR analysis of gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from systemic leaf tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) as described in a previous study (Kim et al., 2026). Extracted RNA concentrations and purities were determined using a BioDrop spectrophotometer (Biochrom Ltd., Cambridge, UK), and RNA samples were normalized to equal concentrations prior to cDNA synthesis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using SmartGene SYBR Green Q-PCR Master Mix (SmartGene, Daejeon, Korea) with gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1). The β-actin gene was used as an internal reference for normalization, and relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates. For the RT-qPCR analysis, each biological replicate was analyzed in technical triplicate, and the mean Ct value of the technical replicates was used for downstream calculations. Data are presented as means ± the standard error of the mean (SEM) from three biological replicates. The statistical significance of differences between groups was assessed using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with P-values < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

TCV-CP dsRNA–induced changes in sRNA distribution by small RNA sequencing

TCV-CP dsRNA was produced in RNase III-deficient E. coli HT115 (DE3) following IPTG induction (Supplementary Fig. 1A). After dsRNA production, RNase T1 treatment was used to remove single-stranded RNA contaminants, yielding enriched dsRNA as confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Prior to small RNA sequencing, phenotypic responses in A. thaliana were assessed at 14 dpt. Mock-treated plants displayed normal growth, whereas TCV-inoculated plants exhibited severe stunting and developmental delay. In contrast, plants treated with TCV-CP dsRNA prior to TCV inoculation exhibited partially restored growth relative to the TCV-inoculated plants (Fig. 1A).

Plants were treated with water (Healthy; left), inoculated with TCV (middle), or pretreated with TCV-CP dsRNA and then inoculated with TCV 1 h later (right). (B) The size distributions of TCV-derived siRNAs (21–24 nt) detected by small RNA sequencing in extracts from systemic leaves at 7 dpt. Bars indicate the number of mapped siRNA reads for each size class in TCV-inoculated plants and in TCV-inoculated plants pretreated with TCV-CP dsRNA (color-coded as shown). (C) The strand-specific coverage profiles of 21–24 nt siRNAs mapped to the TCV genome. The schematic (top) illustrates the TCV genome’s organization, including the CP region (shaded). Coverage plots show sense (blue) and antisense (red) siRNA reads across the TCV genome for TCV-only-treated plants (upper) and TCV + TCV-CP dsRNA-treated plants (lower), highlighting increased antisense siRNA accumulation within the CP-targeted region following dsRNA treatment. CV, turnip crinkle virus; CP, coat protein.

To examine virus-derived siRNA (vsiRNA) production and distribution, small RNA populations from systemic leaves were profiled using high-throughput sequencing in TCV-inoculated plants with or without a CP-dsRNA pretreatment. Sequencing quality metrics and mapping statistics are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. Size distribution analysis showed that plants pretreated with CP-dsRNA accumulated higher levels of TCV-derived siRNA than the TCV-only group across all size classes (21–24 nt) (Fig. 1B). Notably, the disparity was most pronounced in the 21 nt and 22 nt populations, which represented the dominant size classes in dsRNA-treated plants. While 23 nt siRNA also increased markedly in dsRNA-treated plants, they remained less abundant than 21 and 22 nt siRNA, and 24 nt siRNA increased to a lesser extent (Fig. 1B).

The TCV genome’s organization is shown in Fig. 1C, and the CP gene is located in the 3′-proximal region. Strand-specific mapping indicated that vsiRNA molecules are generated from both strands, and the CP-dsRNA pretreatment increased antisense coverage in TCV-treated plants. Notably, this increase was enriched within the targeted CP region, suggesting the exogenously applied TCV-CP dsRNA was processed into siRNA in planta (Fig. 1C).

Optimization and characterization of CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complexes

Taking advantage of electrostatic bonding, CS-TPP nanoparticles were induced to form complexes with the negatively charged TCV-dsRNA (Fig. 2A). To determine the minimum CS-TPP amount required for stable complex formation, gel retardation assays were performed for CS-TPP:dsRNA mixtures at ratios of 1:1, 3:1, 5:1, and 7:1. At a ratio of 1:1, dsRNA migration was completely retarded, indicating full complexation under this condition, and no dsRNA band was detected at higher ratios either. Thus, a CS-TPP:dsRNA ratio of 1:1 was sufficient for complete complex formation, and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA formed using the mixture ratio was used in subsequent experiments (Fig. 2B).

(B) The ζ-potential (mV) values of CS-TPP, naked TCV-dsRNA, the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complex, and the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complex in a solution adjusted to pH 7.4. TCV, turnip crinkle virus; CP, coat protein; CS-TPP, chitosan-tripolyphosphate; SD, standard deviation; FE-TEM, field-emission transmission electron microscopy; DLS, dynamic light scattering.

The morphology of CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complexes was examined using FE-TEM. The complexes predominantly appeared as spherical nanoparticles with smooth surfaces and particle diameters in the 100–200 nm range (Fig. 2C). Surface charge measurements further supported successful complexation: CS-TPP showed a positive ζ-potential (approximately +10 mV), whereas naked TCV-dsRNA exhibited a strongly negative ζ-potential (approximately −25 mV), consistent with the polyanionic nature of dsRNA. Upon complex formation, CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA displayed a relatively high positive ζ-potential (approximately +16 mV), indicating an electrostatic association between CS-TPP and dsRNA. When the suspension was adjusted to pH 7.4, the ζ-potential decreased but remained positive, measuring approximately +8 mV, suggesting that the complexes retained a cationic surface character under near-neutral conditions (Fig. 2D).

The DLS analysis showed that CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA exhibited a broad hydrodynamic size distribution of 150–550 nm, with a major intensity peak around 300–350 nm (Fig. 2D), which is consistent with hydration effects and/or partial interparticle associations in aqueous suspension.

Evaluation of dsRNA stability following complexation with CS-TPP

To assess whether complexation with CS-TPP protects dsRNA from degradation, two complementary assays were performed: an RNase A protection assay and a time-course incubation at 37°C to evaluate thermal stability. In the RNase A assay, naked TCV-dsRNA was not detectable in the agarose gel after RNase A treatment, indicating extensive degradation (Fig. 3A). In contrast, when CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA was exposed to RNase A and the dsRNA was subsequently released from the complexes, a clear dsRNA band was evident (Fig. 3A). Thus, CS-TPP complexation appears to effectively shield dsRNA from RNase-mediated degradation.

Chitosan-tripolyphosphate (CS-TPP) complexation improves dsRNA stability against RNase degradation and under prolonged incubation at 37°C. (A) The results of an RNase A protection assay. Naked turnip crinkle virus (TCV)-dsRNA (200 ng) or CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA containing an equivalent amount of dsRNA was incubated with RNase A at 37°C for 1 h. Following treatment, dsRNA was released from the CS-TPP complexes using release buffer at 95°C for 30 min and analyzed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis at 120 V for 20 min. Untreated naked dsRNA served as a reference control. (B) Thermal stability assay results. Naked TCV-dsRNA (200 ng) or CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA (equivalent dsRNA content) was incubated at 37°C for 6 days, with aliquots collected at days 0, 2, 4, and 6 and subjected to 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis at 120 V for 20 min to monitor dsRNA integrity over time.

Thermal stability was evaluated by incubating naked TCV-dsRNA and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA at 37°C and monitoring dsRNA integrity over time (at days 0, 2, 4, and 6). Naked dsRNA showed a prominent band at day 0, which progressively weakened during incubation, becoming undetectable by day 6 (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complexes retained a clearly detectable dsRNA band throughout the 6-day incubation period (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these results suggest that naked dsRNA is highly susceptible to enzyme attack and persists for only a few days under the tested temperature conditions, whereas CS-TPP complexation markedly improves dsRNA stability against both enzymatic and thermal degradation.

Validation of CS-TPP-mediated dsRNA delivery into leaf tissues

To test whether CS-TPP improves dsRNA delivery into leaf tissues, Cy3-labeled TCV-CP dsRNA (TCV-dsRNA-Cy3) was synthesized and formulated into CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 complexes, with successful complex formation confirmed by a gel retardation assay (Supplementary Fig. 2). Then, naked TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3, along with pure water as a control, were applied by foliar spraying, and fluorescence signals were examined in surface-mounted leaf samples 24 h post-treatment by confocal laser scanning microscopy. Water-treated leaves showed no detectable Cy3 signal, indicating negligible background fluorescence under the imaging settings (Fig. 4A). In treatment-group samples, Cy3 fluorescence was predominantly observed in the epidermal cell layer. Leaves treated with CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 showed stronger fluorescence associated with epidermal cells than those treated with naked TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 (Fig. 4A), indicating enhanced dsRNA uptake and/or retention in the epidermis.

Water-treated leaves served as a negative control. Bright-field, Cy3 fluorescence, and merged images are shown. To further support the imaging results, the relative abundance of CP-target RNA molecules in leaf tissues was quantified by RT-qPCR using primers specific to the CP dsRNA fragment (Supplementary Table 1). The CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA–treated plants exhibited higher levels of CP-target RNA than plants treated with naked TCV-dsRNA (Fig. 4B), further indicating that CS-TPP facilitates more efficient delivery and/or accumulation of dsRNA in planta.

Antiviral efficacy of CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA against TCV in Arabidopsis

To evaluate whether complexation with CS-TPP enhances the antiviral activity of TCV-targeting dsRNA, Arabidopsis plants were treated with DEPC water, CS-TPP, naked TCV-dsRNA, or CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA and mechanically inoculated with TCV 1 h after treatment. Plant phenotypes were documented at 14 dpt, and systemic leaves were collected at 7 and 14 dpt and used to quantify viral RNA accumulation via RT-qPCR.

Mock plants displayed normal growth, whereas TCV-inoculated plants treated with DEPC water exhibited severe disease symptoms, including marked growth retardation and developmental delay (Fig. 5A). The CS-TPP treatment alone did not visibly alleviate TCV-induced symptoms at 14 dpt. In contrast, treatment with naked TCV-dsRNA partially improved plant growth compared to the DEPC control, while the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA treatment more effectively attenuated symptoms, with plant growth approaching the mock phenotype (Fig. 5A).

(A) Representative phenotypes of A. thaliana plants at 14 dpt. Foliar application of DEPC-treated water (DEPC), CS-TPP alone, naked TCV-dsRNA, or CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA was followed by mechanical inoculation with TCV 1 h after the foliar treatment. (B) The relative accumulation of TCV coat protein (TCV-CP) RNA in systemic leaves at 7 dpt (gray bars) and 14 dpt (black bars), quantified using RT-qPCR. The CP RNA levels were normalized to the internal reference gene β-actin and are presented relative to the DEPC control. An asterisk indicates P < 0.05.

Consistent with these phenotype observations, the RT-qPCR analysis of TCV-CP RNA levels in systemic leaves revealed clear differences between treatments at both time points (Fig. 5B). At 7 dpt, CP levels were reduced in all treatment groups (i.e., the CS-TPP, naked dsRNA, and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA groups) relative to those in the DEPC control plants, with the lowest CP accumulation observed in the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA group. By 14 dpt, CP RNA levels increased in the CS-TPP group, indicating that CS-TPP alone was insufficient to maintain suppression during systemic infection. In contrast, CP accumulation remained relatively low in dsRNA-treated plants, but the most pronounced, most sustained reductions were observed in the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA group (Fig. 5B). Collectively, these results indicate that CS-TPP complexation enhances the consistency and durability of dsRNA-mediated antiviral protection against TCV.

Discussion

In this study, we established an eco-friendly CS-TPP nanocarrier system to enhance the performance of exogenously applied antiviral dsRNA in plants. Using TCV as a tractable model for studying antiviral RNA silencing, we showed that (i) foliar-applied TCV-CP dsRNA is processed in planta into abundant 21–24 nt virus-derived siRNA molecules, resulting in enrichment greatly exceeding that of the CP-target region; (ii) CS-TPP forms stable electrostatic complexes with dsRNA that retain a net positive surface charge under near-neutral conditions and protect dsRNA from RNase- and heat-associated degradation; (iii) CS-TPP improves dsRNA accumulation in epidermal tissues following foliar spraying; and (iv) when topically applied, CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA provides more consistent and sustained suppression of systemic TCV virion accumulation and symptom development than naked dsRNA.

These findings align with the central concept of “RNA vaccination”/spray-induced gene silencing, in which externally supplied dsRNA activates the endogenous RNAi machinery to suppress viral replication without transgenesis (Tenllado and Díaz-Ruíz, 2001; Voloudakis et al., 2022). Antiviral RNAi relies on the processing of viral dsRNA into siRNA molecules that guide Argonaute-containing complexes to complementary viral RNA (Baulcombe, 2004; Voinnet, 2001). Consistent with this framework, our small RNA profiling revealed that dsRNA-treated plants accumulated higher levels of TCV-derived siRNA in the 21–24 nt length classes than TCV-only plants, with pronounced enrichment in siRNA corresponding to the CP target region and increased antisense coverage. This regional enrichment suggests that the applied dsRNA is processed in planta and indicates that the delivered dsRNA effectively enters the silencing pathway rather than remaining inert on the leaf surface. Because the TCV’s CP has been reported to function as a suppressor of RNA silencing, targeting CP may provide a dual advantage by directly reducing an essential viral gene product while simultaneously weakening viral counter-defenses (Qu et al., 2003; Thomas et al., 2003). Taken together, the siRNA profile observed in this study suggests that the host RNAi pathway was effectively activated after dsRNA treatment. These results also provide molecular evidence supporting the observed phenotypic changes and RT-qPCR results. At the same time, exogenous dsRNA can also induce sequence-independent PTI responses that may contribute to antiviral protection (Necira et al., 2024; Niehl et al., 2016). In this context, comparing the antiviral effects of non-targeting and target-specific dsRNAs would have helped distinguish sequence-specific RNAi from sequence-independent PTI responses; however, this experiment could not be included in the present study. Nevertheless, previous studies suggest that PTI-mediated antiviral protection is generally weaker than protection mediated by sequence-specific RNAi (Necira et al., 2024). In addition, the accumulation of antisense siRNAs specifically within the CP-targeted region in our small RNA sequencing data supports sequence-specific Dicer-mediated processing as the primary mechanism contributing to the antiviral effects observed in this study.

A key barrier to practical dsRNA use in crop protection is the inherent instability and limited bioavailability of naked dsRNA under biological and environmental conditions (Setten et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). Polymer-based carriers have, therefore, received attention because they can protect dsRNA, improve adherence to and retention on plant surfaces, and facilitate cellular uptake through charge-mediated interactions (Quilez-Molina et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022). As a biodegradable, generally biocompatible polycation that can complex nucleic acids, chitosan is especially attractive, and chitosan-based nanomaterials have been increasingly explored for plant protection applications (Komarova et al., 2024; Scarpin et al., 2023). In our formulation, complete dsRNA complexation was achieved at a CS-TPP:dsRNA ratio of 1:1, and the resulting complexes exhibited spherical morphologies in the 100–200 nm range under FE-TEM, with a shift from negative ζ-potentials for naked dsRNA to positive ζ-potentials for complexed dsRNA. Importantly, the complexes retained a positive ζ-potential even after the solution was adjusted to pH 7.4, suggesting that electrostatic interactions remain sufficiently strong to maintain particle integrity under physiological conditions. While our DLS analysis indicated a broader hydrodynamic distribution than the FE-TEM, which is consistent with hydration and partial association in suspension, this behavior is common for polymeric nanoparticles and can often be refined by tuning the ionic strength, mixing order, or polymer–crosslinker ratios (Calvo et al., 1997a, 1997b). The improved protective effects of complexation observed in RNase A and 37°C incubation assays further indicate that dsRNA is physically shielded within the CS-TPP matrix and can be recovered intact after release, supporting the premise that nanocomplexation directly addresses dsRNA fragility. Similar protective effects have been reported for chitosan-based dsRNA nanoparticles in other RNAi contexts (Dhandapani et al., 2019; Scarpin et al., 2023), confirming the broad applicability of CS-derived carriers for stabilizing dsRNA cargo.

Beyond stability, efficient entry into plant tissues is a major limitation for foliar dsRNA delivery to be effective in plants, dsRNA must traverse the cuticle/cell wall interface and reach compartments where RNAi processing occurs (Setten et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). In surface-mounted leaf samples, Cy3 fluorescence was predominantly associated with the epidermal cell layer, and CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-Cy3 complexes produced visibly stronger fluorescence than naked dsRNA-Cy3, indicating enhanced uptake and/or retention in epidermal tissues. Consistent with these imaging results, dsRNA abundance, measured by RT-qPCR using CP-fragment primers, was higher in plants treated with CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA complexes than in those treated with naked dsRNA, further suggesting improved delivery/accumulation in planta. These observations mirror a growing body of studies showing that cationic or structured nanomaterials enhance dsRNA bioavailability. Such studies have focused on clay nanosheets, which prolong dsRNA persistence and antiviral protection (Mitter et al., 2017), and diverse eco-friendly nanocarriers, all of which increase cellular penetration and systemic outcomes (Yang et al., 2022). Notably, our laboratory’s recent work in pepper also demonstrated (via Cy3 signal strength) that nanoparticle-conjugated dsRNA exhibits greater foliar uptake and yields improved and more durable antiviral effects than naked dsRNA (Kim et al., 2026). Together, this research converges on a single conclusion: nanocarrier-mediated delivery can convert dsRNA from a rapidly degrading, variably taken-up molecule into a more persistent and biologically accessible RNAi trigger.

The improved delivery and stability provided by CS–TPP translated into enhanced antiviral outcomes. At 14 dpt, CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-treated plants displayed substantially milder symptoms and more normal growth than DEPC water-treated controls, and viral CP RNA levels in systemic leaves were reduced at both 7 and 14 dpt. This virus suppression was clearly more sustained and pronounced in the CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA-treatment group than in the naked dsRNA-treatment group. Although direct quantification of viral protein accumulation would have further strengthened the conclusions regarding the extent of viral suppression, the RT-qPCR results and phenotypic observations consistently supported reduced viral RNA accumulation and attenuated disease symptoms in plants treated with CS-TPP@TCV-dsRNA. Interestingly, when plants were treated with CS-TPP alone, lower CP levels were observed at 7 dpt but not at 14 dpt, suggesting that this early reduction likely reflects a transient, carrier-associated effect rather than sequence-specific antiviral activity, and that the nanoparticle carrier by itself is insufficient for the durable suppression of systemic infection. Chitosan and its derivatives have been reported to modulate plant stress tolerance and activate defense-related pathways under certain conditions (Mukarram et al., 2023; Rojas-Pirela et al., 2024). Thus, a transient early effect of CS-TPP on viral accumulation is plausible. However, our CS-TPP formulation contained chitosan at 0.02% (w/v), which is approximately an order of magnitude lower than concentrations commonly used in chitosan-based resistance induction studies. Therefore, as applied in the present study, the chitosan nanomaterial may have had a limited capacity to induce host resistance on its own, which could explain why the CS-TPP treatment alone did not confer robust, sustained suppression of TCV. Notably, our time-course data indicate that durable antiviral protection requires the dsRNA cargo and is strongest when the dsRNA is delivered in the CS-TPP complex. This interpretation is also consistent with the results seen for nanocarrier-only controls in related dsRNA/NP systems, where antiviral outcomes have primarily tracked dsRNA-mediated viral suppression, although nanomaterials have exerted context-dependent physiological influences (Kim et al., 2026). Future work profiling defense marker genes after CS-TPP-only treatments across a wider range of chitosan concentrations could help clarify whether the early reductions in CP levels reflect mild defense priming, altered inoculation efficiency, or other indirect effects.

Although TCV is not typically considered a major driver of economic loss in many cropping systems, its well-characterized interactions with antiviral RNA silencing make it a valuable model for benchmarking delivery innovations and mechanistic readouts (Qu et al., 2003; Thomas et al., 2003). Nevertheless, moving dsRNA/NP strategies from the lab to agricultural deployment will require addressing additional variables that are not fully captured under controlled growth-chamber conditions. Field-relevant factors, such as UV exposure, rainfall, and heterogeneous leaf surface properties, can accelerate dsRNA loss and reduce reproducibility (Rank and Koch, 2021; Niu et al., 2021). Nanocarrier formulations may mitigate these pressures by improving retention and shielding dsRNA, but formulation optimization (e.g., the inclusion of surfactants, protective coatings, or biodegradable polymer blends) and persistence testing under simulated/field conditions will be essential (Rank and Koch, 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Furthermore, RNAi-based crop protection faces broader considerations, including biosafety evaluation, regulatory alignment, and public acceptance, all of which influence real-world adoption (Rinaldi et al., 2025; Voloudakis et al., 2022). Notably, dsRNA approaches are inherently modular; they can be designed to target multiple viral genes or even multiple co-infecting pathogens, which may increase versatility and breadth of protection (Moazzam et al., 2024). The CS-TPP platform developed here provides a practical foundation for such extensions and for future applications targeting economically important plant viruses beyond TCV.

Overall, our results demonstrate that CS-TPP complexation enhances dsRNA stability, epidermis-associated delivery, in planta siRNA production, and antiviral efficacy against systemic TCV infection. These findings support the use of biodegradable polymeric nanocarriers as a promising route for improving the consistency and durability of topical dsRNA strategies and justify broader testing and optimization toward crop-relevant viruses and field application.
