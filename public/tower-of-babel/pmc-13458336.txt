Staphylococcus aureus derived extracellular vesicles modulate osteoblast-like cell immune responses independently of vesicle internalization
Authors: Julia Papail, Nathalie Daniel, Ligia Prado, Danièle Vassaux, Sandrine Péron, Céline Even, Véronique Lebret, Brenda Silva Rosa Da Luz, Yann Le Gouar, Julien Jardin, Nadia Berkova, Siomar de Castro Soares, Ken-Ichi Yoshida, Vasco Azevedo, Svetlana Chabelskaya, Yves Le Loir, Éric Guédon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus aureus is a major opportunistic Gram-positive pathogen whose virulence is partly mediated by extracellular vesicles (EVs). Here, we examined the production, composition, and functionality of EVs released by the methicillin-resistant strain N315 under infection-mimicking conditions, focusing on their interaction with MG-63 human osteoblast-like cells. N315 EVs, purified by size exclusion chromatography, and characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis and electron microscopy, contained a selectively enriched and diverse molecular cargo, including proteins, lipoproteins, DNA, RNA, lipoteichoic acid, and peptidoglycan. Confocal microscopy and protection assays confirmed the intravesicular localization of RNAs and proteins. EVs were recognized by TLR2 on MG-63 cells and selectively induced the expression of genes involved in Toll-like receptor, NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and inflammasome pathways. The induction of these pathways was confirmed at the protein level, as well as by increased IL-8 release and caspase-1 activation. EVs were internalized via a dynamin-dependent endocytosis mechanism and trafficked to late endosomes. Importantly, inhibition of uptake did not affect the expression of target genes, demonstrating an uncoupling between EVs internalization and transcriptional response. This work provides new insight into how S. aureus N315 EVs interact with osteoblast-like cells and modulate host responses, revealing both conserved mechanisms and strain- or cell type-specific differences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54792-7.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common bacterial species found in human and veterinary medicine. It is an opportunistic Gram-positive pathogen primarily associated with warm-blooded animals. In humans, 30% of the population are asymptomatic carriers of this bacterium, with colonization mainly on the skin and in the nasal mucosa1. However, under certain conditions of dysbiosis, such as a weakening of the host’s immune defenses or the presence of wounds, S. aureus can cause nosocomial and community-acquired diseases, ranging from mild with suppurative skin infections to severe and invasive ones2–4. The intensive use of antibiotics has further promoted the selection of resistant and multidrug-resistant strains, notably methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains, which are associated with significant morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and high healthcare costs5. Consequently, the management of S. aureus associated pathologies has become increasingly challenging due to its strong adaptive capacity and stress tolerance6–8.

The ability of S. aureus to colonize different hosts and cause a wide range of infections relies on production of numerous virulence factors, such as surface-exposed and secreted proteins, both responsible for the colonization and the disease severity9,10. These virulence determinants are highly regulated and allow S. aureus to switch between a commensal and pathogenic lifestyle depending on host context11. As described for several other pathogens, it has recently been proposed that extracellular vesicles (EVs) produced by S. aureus may constitute an integral component of its virulence arsenal12,13. Bacterial EVs are nanosized, lipid bilayer-enclosed particles. They typically range from 30 to 300 nm in diameter and enclose a complex molecular cargo composed of proteins, lipids, RNA, DNA, and metabolites13–17. By mediating the transfer of bioactive components, EVs contribute to multiple aspects of bacterial physiology, such as stress adaptation, intercellular competition, lateral gene transfer, biofilm development and antibiotic resistance18–21. In this framework, EV production could also represent a highly adaptive mechanism that allows S. aureus to dynamically modulate its interactions with the host and the surrounding microbial environment. Numerous studies, notably on S. aureus, have also underlined their role in intra- and inter-species communication, and in host–pathogen interactions12,13. EVs secreted by diverse S. aureus strains contribute to pathogenesis in playing roles in the dissemination of virulence factors, and modulation of host immune responses22–27. Moreover, several studies support that EV-associated cargo is not only biologically active, but also functionally enhanced by vesicular packaging highlighting EVs as efficient delivery systems that potentiate S. aureus virulence27–31. In addition to these virulence-associated components, S. aureus EV cargo is composed of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as lipoproteins, peptidoglycan (PG), and nucleic acids, which can be recognized by host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like and NOD-like receptors, located at the cell surface, within endosomes and in the cytosol23. Their activation triggers downstream signaling cascades, leading to cytokine and chemokine production, immune cell recruitment, and inflammation13,27,32. Understanding how EVs contribute to the infection process, including their role in modulating the host immune system is crucial, as they represent both key determinants of pathogenesis, potential therapeutic targets and promising tools for the development of EV-based vaccines or immunomodulatory strategies.

Given the ecological niche of the bacterium, the ability of S. aureus EVs to induce the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines has been extensively documented in vitro and in vivo, particularly in skin infection context and in interaction with human immune cells33–38. However, while skin and soft tissue infections caused by the pathogen are generally mild and superficial, S. aureus is implicated in more serious and deeper ones, particularly in bones and joints infections (BJI)39,40. This pathogen is responsible for an infection of the bone marrow and adjacent bone tissue called osteomyelitis, in approximately 60% of cases and lead to bone destruction, chronic infections, and life-threatening complications40,41. Furthermore, the ability of S. aureus to form biofilms on invasive medical devices such as osteoarticular prostheses promotes this type of infection with significant socio-economic repercussions and public health threat42. Despite the clinical importance of BJIs, no studies have yet investigated the contribution of S. aureus extracellular vesicles in their pathophysiology.

This study aims to fil this gap in investigating the interaction of S. aureus EVs with MG-63 osteoblast-like cells, a relevant model for studying osteoblast responses during osteomyelitis and prosthetic joint infection43. To this end, we used the EVs derived from N315 S. aureus strain, considered the historical reference genome for hospital-acquired MRSA (HA-MRSA)44. This strain is widely used in genomic and metabolic modeling studies, and belongs to the ST5/CC5 lineage45,46. The CC5 clonal complex represents a major HA-MRSA lineage frequently implicated in osteomyelitis and prosthetic joint infections41. It is characterized by broad multidrug resistance and a high level of adaptation to the hospital environment, making it a key contributor to nosocomial epidemiology46. Furthermore, studies have shown that impaired agr system functionality enhances the ability of S. aureus to adapt to the host during chronic osteomyelitis47. In this context, the N315 strain, which harbors a defective ArlRS two-component system, a positive regulator of the agr system, constitutes a relevant bacterial model for investigating persistent infections in BJI context48,49.

Results

S. aureus N315 strain produces EVs in vitro

The ability of S. aureus strain N315 to produce EVs was assessed in RPMI, a medium physiologically relevant for mammalian cell culture, supplemented with 10% LB to support bacterial growth (Fig. S1). Particles concentration in the culture medium was quantified prior to bacterial inoculation and after 16 h of growth to monitor EV production over time. As shown in Fig. 1A N315 growth induced a > 30-fold increase in particle concentration, rising from 5 × 107 to 1.5 × 109 particles per mL of culture. Moreover, the size distribution of the particles detected post-incubation differed from that of those initially present, further supporting the active release of EVs by strain N315 during growth in RPMI + LB (Fig. 1B, left and middle panels). Culture supernatants were then subjected to EV isolation procedures using size exclusion chromatography. The fractions collected were carefully selected to ensure the best compromise between EV yield and sample purity, minimizing protein contamination (Fig. S2) and the pool of interest fractions was characterized by NTA and electronic microscopy. NTA revealed particles within the 90–110 nm size range, with concentrations ranging from 2.6 to 3.4 × 108 particles per mL of culture (Fig. 1A and B). Electron microscopy confirmed the presence of nanosized, membrane-bound vesicles with a typical spherical shape (Fig. 1C). S. aureus N315 EVs contain a diverse, selectively enriched and protected molecular cargo

EVs derived from N315 were found to contain a diverse molecular cargo including PG, LTA, DNA, RNA, and proteins (Fig. 2A, Fig. S3). On average, 1010 N315 EVs contained 7 μg of protein, 70 ng of RNA and 40 ng of DNA. Proteomic analysis by LC–MS/MS identified 91 proteins associated with EVs, predominantly of cytoplasmic origin such Eno, and lipoproteins (Fig. 2B–E). Among them, twenty-six were not detected in the whole-cell proteome, indicating their selective enrichment within EVs. To confirm the packaging of proteins and RNA into EVs, both protease and nuclease protection assays were performed (Fig. 2F). For the protease protection assay, EVs were exposed to proteinase K under conditions that preserved or not their integrity (i.e., + /− RIPA). The protection from degradation of proteins was observed only when EV integrity was maintained, that indicated the lipid bilayer protective effect and the intravesicular protein localization. For the nuclease protection assay, EVs were pretreated with proteinase K followed by PMSF to degrade external proteins potentially protecting RNA, prior to RNase A exposure. Upon RNA extraction, the resulting profiles revealed that EVs still harbored substantial intravesicular RNA, predominantly small RNAs (< 200 nt), with a major peak around 100 nt. The intravesicular localization of RNA and proteins was further confirmed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 2G). EVs were treated to remove external proteins and RNAs, then double-labeled using a membrane-specific dye (i.e., DiO or DiD) and either a lipid-permeable RNA dye (i.e., Syto RNA Select) or a protein-specific dye (i.e., BioTracker™ Far Red Exosome Protein Labeling Kit). Confocal microscopy revealed co-localization between EV RNA and membrane dye signals (left panel), as well as between EV proteins and membrane dye signals (middle panel), supporting the presence of intravesicular RNA and proteins protected by the lipid membrane. Finally, to illustrate selective protein packaging, the protein Eno, which we identified as associated with N315 EVs by LC–MS/MS analysis, was tagged with sfGFP and its localization within EVs was assessed by confocal microscopy. As observed for RNA and total protein staining, colocalization of the sfGFP signals with the EV membrane signals confirmed the incorporation of Eno into EVs. S. aureus N315 EVs modulate the expression of MG-63 cell genes

The molecular characterization of N315 EVs revealed the presence of numerous components able to interact with host cells, such as LTA and lipoproteins. To test this assumption, and after confirming the absence of cytotoxicity on MG-63 cells (Fig. S4), the ability of N315 EVs to modulate human gene expression in vitro was assessed. Specifically, we analyzed the expression of several genes coding for various PRRs, signaling pathway components, and cytokines/chemokines in the presence or absence of N315 EVs (Fig. 3, Fig. S5). Compared to unstimulated control, EVs significantly increased the expression of several genes coding for the extracellular receptors TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, and TLR6, the endosomal receptors TLR3, and TLR7, and the cytosolic receptors IFIH1, NOD2, and RIG-I. EV exposure resulted in increased expression of key components of the NF-KB, JAK-STAT, and inflammasome pathways, including NFKB1, STAT, and NLRP3, compared with control. Finally, the presence of EVs also upregulated several genes coding for cytokines/chemokines, such as IL6, IL1B, CXCL8, CCL2, and INFB1. Note that these inductions appeared to be gene-specific, as many genes belonging from the previously mentioned categories, as well as those involved in the IRF pathway, remained unchanged. S. aureus N315 EVs induce inflammasome and inflammation via the NF-κB and JAK/STAT signaling pathways

To confirm these transcriptional findings, we quantified the relative abundance of forty-one human proteins related to the NF-κB pathway in MG-63 cells in the presence or absence of N315 EVs, together with IL-8 release and caspase activity (Fig. 4). Among the forty-one proteins tested, two showed a decrease in abundance and seven showed an increase in abundance in the presence of EVs (Fig. 4A). These proteins are associated to the NFKB (CARD6, IκBα, IRAK1, NFKB2, TNF RII, TLR2, and STING) and JAK-STAT (STAT1p91, STAT2) pathways. Finally, EV-induced IL-8 release confirmed their ability to activate inflammatory signaling pathways in MG-63 cells, whereas the increase in caspase-1 activity supported their capacity to trigger inflammasome activation (Fig. 4BC). S. aureus N315 EVs interact directly with TLR2

To gain further insight into how N315 EVs signal and interact with MG-63 cells to modulate host response, their ability to interact with PPRs was assessed. To this end, we used HEK-Blue hTLR2 and hTLR7 reporter cell lines, which were designed to study activation of the human TLR2 and TLR7 receptors by monitoring NF-κB–dependent SEAP reporter activity. Stimulation with increasing concentrations of EVs, resulted in a dose-dependent increase of SEAP reporter activity from hTLR2 cells, showing the direct interaction between EVs and TLR2 (Fig. 5A). In contrast, even at doses 10- to 100-fold higher, reporter activity of hTLR7 cells was not modulated by N315 EVs, indicating a lack of interaction between EVs and this receptor (Fig. 5B). S. aureus N315 EVs are internalized without loss of integrity by MG-63 cells

To investigate whether they can also signal intracellularly, the ability of MG-63 cells to internalize N315 EVs was assessed as a prerequisite for intracellular signaling. To this end, labeled EVs were incubated with cells stained with DAPI for cell nucleus (blue) and with Phalloidin for actin filaments (green) and analyzed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 6A). After 3 h of interaction with EVs, a colocalization of EV and cell signals was observed, suggesting EV internalization. A 3D reconstruction further confirmed the intracellular localization of EVs, notably in the perinuclear region. When compared to 3 h of exposure, we noted a clear increase in the signal after 22 h, suggesting an increase of EV internalization over time. Flow cytometry analysis supported these findings, showing that the uptake of membrane-labeled EVs by MG-63 cells increased in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. S6). To further characterize the state of internalized EVs, double-labeled EVs, i.e., membrane-stained and labeled for either RNAs or proteins, were incubated with MG-63 cells pre-labeled for nuclei and membrane and analyzed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 6BC). After 3 h of interaction, signal colocalization between the two markers (membrane/RNAs or membrane/proteins) was detected in MG-63 cells, with a characteristic perinuclear distribution (Fig. 6AB). S. aureus N315 EVs are internalized in MG-63 cells through dynamin-dependent endocytosis

To gain insight into the mechanisms of internalization, MG-63 cells were pretreated with specific inhibitors of internalization pathways prior to incubation with EVs: Chlorpromazine (clathrin-mediated endocytosis) and dynasore (dynamin-mediated endocytosis). Only dynasore significantly decreased EV uptake (Fig. 6DE), indicating that N315 EVs are primarily internalized via a dynamin-dependent endocytosis pathway. To confirm the presence of EVs within endosomal compartments in MG-63 cells, which was supported by a perinuclear localization of EVs (Fig. 6AB), confocal imaging experiments similar to those described above were carried out using late endosome labeling in cells incubated with double-labeled EVs for RNA and proteins (Fig. 6FG). A stable colocalization of fluorescence signals corresponding to the EV membrane and RNA cargo was observed within cells during the first three hours of co-incubation, confirming the structural integrity of EVs (Fig. 6G, left panel). Similarly, colocalization of signals between i) the EV membrane and late endosomes (Fig. 6G, middle left panel), ii) the EV RNA cargo and late endosomes (Fig. 6G, middle right panel), and iii) the EV membrane, EV RNA cargo and late endosomes (Fig. 6G, right panel), demonstrating the presence of intact EVs within late endosomal compartments for up to three hours of incubation. However, at six hours post-incubation, the colocalization of these signals significantly decreased, suggesting either a dissociation of the EV membrane from its RNA cargo or reduced dye stability over time (Fig. 6G, all panels). Altogether, these finding demonstrated dynamin-dependent endocytic internalization of N315 EVs and their presence within late endosomes without loss of integrity.

Discussion

The production and secretion of EVs by S. aureus are strongly influenced by growth conditions (e.g., growth phase, culture medium), environmental stresses (e.g., H2O2, osmotic stress, antibiotics), and strain-specific factors (e.g., PSM level)30,50–54. These factors not only affect the quantity of EVs produced but also shape their molecular cargo23,51,53. Although some of the stress conditions tested in vitro may mimic those encountered by the bacterium during infection (e.g., iron limitation, H2O2), most studies rely on rich laboratory media for EVs production. In this study, we evaluated the ability of S. aureus strain N315, a methicillin-resistant (MRSA) human isolate, to produce and secrete EVs in RPMI + LB. We selected this growth condition because RPMI optimally promotes immune cell activation, better approximating in vivo responses55, and because iron-depleted RPMI medium best recapitulates S. aureus infection-like conditions56. N315 successfully produced EVs under these conditions. At this stage, however, direct comparison of EVs yields between N315 in this condition and previously reported strains and conditions remains challenging due to differences in EVs isolation and quantification methodologies across studies. Nevertheless, the number of EVs recovered during the stationary phase in our experimental setup was quite similar as reported elsewhere, despite apparently lower bacterial growth under these infection-like conditions. N315 EVs exhibited the typical cup-shaped morphology under TEM and carried a complex molecular cargo, including proteins, DNA, RNA, LTA, and PG. As reported for other S. aureus EVs, N315 EVs were enriched in specific proteins compared to whole cells, notably lipoproteins, membrane-associated proteins, and cell envelope components17,53. We demonstrated that these proteins are integral EV components rather than external contaminants, with a subset localized within the vesicles lumen. Previous studies have shown that EVs produced by human and animal S. aureus isolates share a highly conserved core proteome15,26,53. N315 EVs follow this trend, as revealed by LC–MS/MS analysis, and we confirmed the presence of one of the core proteins, enolase, within the EVs using a GFP fusion approach. The presence of RNA associated with S. aureus EVs has also been previously reported, and here we confirm their intravesicular localization, along with a predominance of small RNA14,16,23,57. Finally, although fewer studies have focused on these molecules compared to proteins and RNAs, the presence of DNA, LTA and PG associated with S. aureus EVs has also been reported23,58. With the exception of a relatively low number of proteins, a limited abundance of virulence factors and the absence of cytotoxicity, which may reflect the culture conditions and strain-specific characteristics, such as the arlR mutation, N315 EVs do not display major differences compared with EVs produced by other S. aureus strains, suggesting that their overall composition and behavior are broadly conserved59,60.

Several components of the molecular cargo of N315 EVs represent potential PAMPs, i.e., bacterial molecules specifically recognized by host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) as danger signals. PRRs comprise different classes of receptors, each involved in the recognition of distinct PAMPs. For instance, bacterial lipoproteins are sensed by the TLR2/TLR1 and TLR2/TLR6 heterodimers, DNA by TLR9, RNA by IFIH1, TLR3, TLR7 and RIG-I, and bacterial peptidoglycan fragments by NOD2. Given the diversity of their cargo, N315 EVs may carry ligands capable of activating multiple PRRs. This hypothesis is supported by a recent study using reporter cell lines, which showed that EVs secreted by S. aureus NCTC 6571 interact with TLR2, TLR4, TLR7, TRL8, TRL9, and NOD223. The selective induction of the expression of IFIH1, TLR1, TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, TLR6, TLR7, NOD2, and RIGI by N315 EVs in MG-63 cells further supports this hypothesis of multiple PRR interactions. Indeed, PRR activation by their ligands is known to trigger positive or negative feedback loops that regulate its own expression61. Notably, for TLR2, we confirmed that EV-mediated modulation of gene expression is associated with increased TLR2 protein levels and with the ability of EVs to directly bind the receptor at the cell surface. However, this relationship between expression induction and interaction does not extend to the endosomal TLR7 receptor, raising questions about the N315 EV interaction with multiple PRRs. Nevertheless, these finding warrants caution, as the transcriptional and interaction experiments used different cell lines, and it cannot be excluded that ligands associated to N315 EVs are not recognized or are not equally accessible to TLR7 in HEK cells compared with MG-63 cells, for instance due to differences in EV internalization or intracellular trafficking. A second line of evidence challenging the hypothesis of multiple PRR interactions of N315 EVs with MG-63 cells is the induction of TLR4 expression, despite TLR4 being a receptor that recognizes lipopolysaccharide, a component specific to Gram-negative bacteria. Finally, additional evidence arguing against this hypothesis comes from our internalization assay. Indeed, the ability of EVs to interact with PRRs located across distinct cellular compartments, cytoplasm (IFIH1, NOD2, RIGI), endosomes (TLR3, TLR7), and cell surface (TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR6), implies that EV-associated ligands must be accessible to these environments. In particular, activation of endosomal PRRs by EV-associated RNA requires EV internalization and subsequent EV lysis within endosomes. Our findings demonstrated the presence of intact EVs inside host cells, particularly in late endosomes, showed that their uptake primarily occurs via a dynamin-dependent endocytosis pathway, and suggested the release of the EV RNA cargo into this compartment, consistent with the activation of endosomal TLR3 and TLR7. However, inhibition of endosomal entry of EVs did not abolish EV-mediated induction of the expression of TLR3 and TLR7, among others. This finding suggests either that their induction occurs independently of direct EV-TLR3 and EV-TLR7 interactions, or that EVs may also access endosomal compartments through dynamin-independent pathways. The only other reported entry pathway for S. aureus EVs, in addition to the dynamin-dependent endocytosis, is cholesterol-dependent fusion with the host cell plasma membrane27. Although we cannot formally conclude on the existence of multiple PRR interactions for N315 EVs with MG-63 cells, as reported for other S. aureus EVs, our data nevertheless suggest that signaling predominantly occurs through TLR2. The observed effects may therefore be indirect, particularly for TLR3, TLR4, and TLR7, resulting from an initial TLR2 stimulation that activates a key signaling pathway, e.g., NF-κB (see below). This pathway subsequently amplifies the response through priming amplification by inducing the expression of additional receptors, enhancing cellular responsiveness.

Upon binding to their ligands, PRRs trigger intracellular signaling cascades that activate transcription factors, such as NF-κB and interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), leading to the transcription of immune-related genes62. This activation results in the production of various effectors, including pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, that orchestrate local immune responses and immune cell recruitment. Consistent with this, N315 EVs upregulated the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines in MG-63 cells such as IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL8, IFN-β and CCL2, and induced CXCL8 release, in line with previous studies on S. aureus EVs 22–26,63. At the signaling level, increased expression of NFKB1, and levels of NFκB2, CARD6, STING and IRAK1 proteins, along with the decreased levels of IκBα, confirmed the activation of the NF-κB pathway. Interestingly, the TLR-dependent activation of this pathway also provides the signal required for NRLP3 inflammasome activation, by inducing expression of NRLP3 and IL1B, a phenomenon previously described in macrophages exposed to S. aureus EVs24,27. In our study, both were significant induced by N315 EVs, and we confirmed inflammasome activation through the increased activity of caspase-1. S. aureus has been described as capable of being internalized into MG-63 cells and inducing inflammasome activation and IL-1β secretion27,64–67. This indicates that N315 EVs are able to trigger the inflammasome pathway in non-immune cells, regardless of the presence of the bacteria itself. Regarding other signaling pathways, although no detectable activation of IRF genes was observed, N315 EVs induced a strong upregulation of INFB1 expression (over 18-fold), suggesting the activation of IRF-independent signaling pathways. However, it is established that NF-κB can also regulate IFNB1 expression despite this is not its primary route68,69. Further studies are required to determine whether the induction of INFB1 expression by EVs is mediated via the NF-κB or IRF signaling pathway. Additionally, several STAT family proteins, such as STAT2 and STAT1p91, were among the most upregulated proteins. These proteins are key components of the JAK/STAT pathway, which is typically activated by type I interferons (IFN-α/β) and involved in inflammation, immune cell recruitment, survival, and proliferation70. While bacterial EVs are well known to modulate host immune responses and promote proinflammatory cytokine release23,27–29,36,57, their direct involvement in JAK/STAT pathway activation was not described. Our data suggest either an uncharacterized direct activation mechanism or an indirect effect mediated by host-derived cytokines, such as IFN-β, produced in response to N315 EV exposure.

Altogether, our findings highlight the capacity of N315 EVs to primarily interact with TLR2 to activate multiple immune signaling pathways in non-immune cells, the MG-63 osteoblast-like cells. It highlights conserved features that point to common functional roles in S. aureus EV biology, while also revealing differences that may stem from strain specificity, experimental conditions, or host cell type. This supports the ability of EVs to serve as vehicles for the delivery of immunostimulatory ligands across cellular compartments, ultimately modulating host responses during infection. Although most of our observations were obtained in vitro, they raise questions regarding the role of EVs during S. aureus infections in vivo, notably in the context of BJIs. EVs, by contributing to the early detection of bacteria by the host immune system and amplifying local inflammation, may act as a decoy and facilitate immune evasion of producing bacteria. Further studies are required to determine the fate and function of EVs during the course of infection, their biodistribution within host tissues, and their contribution to virulence or immune modulation. A better understanding of these mechanisms could reveal novel strategies for therapeutic intervention or vaccine development targeting bacterial EVs.

Methods

Bacterial strains, and growth conditions

The methicillin-resistant S. aureus strain N315 (CIP 107,652), isolated in 1982 from a pharyngeal smear of a patient in Japan, was grown in eukaryotic cell culture medium RPMI (SIGMA, R8758) supplemented with 10% of LB (Luria Bertani Broth: Tryptone 10 g/L, Yeast Extract 5 g/L, NaCl 5 g/L) (i.e., RPMI + LB) at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm44. S. aureus RN422071 was cultivated in brain heart infusion broth (BHI; pH 7.4; BECTON DICKINSON, Le Pont de Claix, France). Escherichia coli NEB® 5-alpha (NEW ENGLAND BIOLABS) and Bacillus subtilis KT0172,73 were cultivated under vigorous shaking in LB medium at 37 °C. When required, antibiotics (SIGMA) were added to the media at the following concentrations: ampicillin (100 μg/mL for E. coli), spectinomycin (100 μg/mL for B. subtilis), and chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL for S. aureus). The colony-forming unit (CFU)/mL was determined after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C on BHI or LB agar.

Construction of translational sfGFP fusion with the S. aureus Eno protein

To illustrate specific protein packaging into EVs, we selected enolase (Eno) as a marker of EV loading. Eno has been previously identified as part of the core EV proteome of many Gram-positive bacteria17 and was detected here in N315 EVs. For that N315 was engineered to express a translational fusion between Eno and the superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP) on a replicative vector. Primers used are listed in Table S1. The eno and sfGFP genes were amplified by PCR using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (M0493, NEW ENGLAND BIOLABS) from genomic DNA of S. aureus N315 and Bacillus subtilis KT01, respectively. The latter strain carried a stable chromosomally integrated single copy of the sfgfp gene (K. Kita, personal communication). Genomic DNA was recovered using DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (69,504, QIAGEN). PCR fragments were purified with NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (740,609, MACHEREY–NAGEL), and assembled using the Gibson assembly method (Gibson Assembly Cloning kit, E5510, NEW ENGLAND BIOLABS)74. The resulting eno-sfGFP translational fusion amplicon was restricted by SphI and BamHI and cloned into the SphI/BamHI digested pCN35c vector75,76. The resulting multicopy plasmid pEV1 was transformed into the RN4220 S. aureus strain, and then in S. aureus N315 by electroporation (2.45 kV, 25 μF, 100 Ω; BIO-RAD, Gene Pulser Xcell) to give strain GMS0045 (N135 pVE1). Plasmid preparations were performed using NucleoSpin Plasmid kit (740,588, MACHEREY–NAGEL) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. We verified the sequence of the recombinant pEV1 plasmid from strain GMS0045 by long-read sequencing (ONT Lite Whole plasmid sequencing, EUROFINS GENOMICS).

Extracellular vesicles isolation

EVs isolation was performed as previously described by Rodovahlo et al. (2020), with some modifications77. N315 EVs were isolated from bacterial culture supernatants inoculated with an 8 h preculture (0.5%) in 250 mL of RPMI + LB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 16 h, until the stationary phase (OD600nm > 2.0, ~ 109 CFU/mL) (Fig. S1). Bacterial cell cultures were centrifuged at 3100 × g for 30 min and filtered through 0.22 μm Nalgene top filters (THERMO SCIENTIFIC). For routine experience, 1 L of culture supernatant was used, obtained by pooling four 250 mL cultures at this step. The 1 L filtered culture supernatant was concentrated with Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (100 kDa molecular weight cut-off, MILLIPORE) by successive centrifugations (3220 × g). The concentrated suspension of EVs was recovered in PBS buffer (D8537, SIGMA) and further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using qEVoriginal 70 nm columns (IZON) as recommended in the manufacturer’s instructions. Fractions enriched in EVs and almost free of contaminants were pooled (fractions 2 to 6) and concentrated with Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units with 100 kDa cut-off. The recovered EVs were analyzed by Qubit (INVITROGEN, Qubit 4 fluorometer) for DNA and RNA content and with Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits (BCA) for protein concentration. For the choice of pooled fractions, each 400 µL fractions were collected sequentially and analyzed for particle content and protein contaminants (see Fig. S2 for more details on SEC fractions).

Determination of EVs sizes and concentrations

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) using an sCMOS camera and a Blue488 laser (Nano Sight NS300) was performed to assess EVs size and concentration. Samples were diluted into PBS to achieve an optimal concentration of around 40 to 100 particles per frame and submitted to a constant flux generated by a syringe pump (speed 50), at 25 °C. Results were retrieved from 5 × 60 s videos recorded with camera level at 15–16 and threshold at 3–5, while other parameters were adjusted as necessary.

Transmission electron microscopy imaging

For microscopy imaging, EVs were isolated by SEC using TBS buffer (150 mM NaCl; 50 mM Tris–Cl, pH 7.5) instead of PBS. Copper EM grips (Electron Microscope Sciences FF200-Cu) were first exposed to a glow discharge of 20 mA. Ten μL of a solution containing around 1011 particles per mL were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and placed on glow-discharged grids for 30 s. After removing of liquid excess with filter paper, grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and observed with JEOL 1400 transmission electron microscope operating at 80–120 kV.

Nuclease protection assays and RNA purification

The procedure used for nuclease protection assays was also used to remove external RNA contaminants before EV RNA extraction. To degrade contaminant RNAs potentially protected by proteins from RNase A digestion, 5.0 × 1010 EVs were first treated with proteinase K (50 µg/mL, 03,115,836,001, SIGMA), followed by inhibition of protease activity with 5 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (93,482, SIGMA). RNase A (0.5 µg/mL, 19,101, QIAGEN) was then added for 5 min at 25 °C, after which RiboLock RNase inhibitor (EO0381, THERMO SCIENTIFIC) was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA extraction was performed by Luz et al.16. Briefly, EVs lysis was performed by mixing the samples (v/v) with 350 µL of lysis buffer (0.5% SDS w/v, 30 mM sodium acetate, 1 mM EDTA, acid-buffered at pH 5.0), glass beads and 700 µL of acid phenol, followed by mechanical disruption for 30 s in Precellys homogenizer at 6500 rpm. Samples were then centrifuged (16,000 × g, 4 °C, 5 min) and the upper phase was transferred to a new tube, mixed with an equal volume of phenol and centrifuged under the same conditions. The resulting upper phase was recovered, mixed (v/v) with chloroform:isoamyl alcohol 24:1, and centrifuged (16,000 × g, 4 °C, 5 min). The final upper phase was collected, supplemented with 0.2 µg/µL of glycogen (R0551, THERMO SCIENTIFIC), 10% volume of NaAc 3 M and 2.5 volumes of ice-cold 100% ethanol, and stored overnight at −20 °C. Samples were then centrifuged (16,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C), and the pellets were washed with 1 mL of cold 70% ethanol. Finally, the pellets were dried with a SpeedVac concentrator and dissolved in RNase-free water. RNAs were cleaned up and treated with DNase I using the RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (ZYMO RESEARCH). Samples were kept at −80 °C until use. Samples were analyzed by Nano Drop (LABTECH, Spectrophotometre Nanodrop ND1000), Qubit (INVITROGEN, Qubit 4 fluorometer) and Bioanalyzer. This latter analysis was realized on RNA 6000 Pico Kit by the company Helixio, Saint-Beauzire, France.

Protease protection assays

Methods were based on the procedure reported by Rompikuntal et al.78. Proteinase protection assays were performed with 2.0 × 1010 EVs (equivalent to 10 µg proteins) per condition. Five conditions were tested: i) untreated EVs (control), ii) EVs incubated with PMSF (5 mM, 10 min, RT), iii) EVs treated with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA; 1X final; 30 min, 4 °C, 2,000 rpm in thermomixer) to permeabilize EVs and then with proteinase K (50 µg/mL, 10 min, 37 °C) followed by PMSF, iv) EVs treated with proteinase K, and v) EVs samples incubated with proteinase K followed by PMSF. Immediately after treatment, EVs proteins profiles were analyzed by SDS-PAGE 12% followed by Coomassie Blue Staining.

Dot blot

Bacterial and EV lysates were prepared according to the following procedure. Overnight cultures of S. aureus N315 in 10 mL of RPMI + LB were centrifuged (6,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The pellets were washed with PBS, centrifuged under the same conditions, resuspended in 1 mL of 1X RIPA buffer containing 0.1 mm zirconia beads (BIOSPEC PRODUCT), and shaken in an Eppendorf TherMomixer C (2,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C). The lysates were then centrifuged (10,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), and the resulting supernatant was collected for further analyses. EVs samples were diluted (v/v) with 2X RIPA buffer, and shaken in an Eppendorf TherMomixer C (2,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C). Protein quantification was performed with Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits. Serial ½ dilutions of samples were deposited on nitrocellulose membranes (Hybond C extra, AMERSHAM). MG-63 and E. coli lysate were used as negative control (NC) for peptidoglycan (PG) and lipoteichoic acids (LTA) detection, respectively. For positive control (PC), purified S. aureus LTA (tlrl-pslta, INVIVOGEN) and PG (tlrl-pgns2, INVIVOGEN) were used. For peptidoglycan detection, the upper concentration start from 10 µg for PC and 0.25 µg for SA, EVs and NC and for lipoteichoic acids from 160 ng for PC, 2 µg for SA and NC, and finally 250 ng for EVs. The dot blot protocol was adapted from Wang et al.30. Membranes were incubated with 5% (w/v) of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in TBS with 0.03% Tween20 (TBS‐T) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation 2 h at RT with either Gram-positive bacteria lipoteichoic acids monoclonal antibody (G43J, INVITROGEN) diluted 1/200 in 5% BSA TBS-T or peptidoglycan monoclonal antibody (3F6B3, INVITROGEN), diluted 1/1000 in 5% BSA TBS-T. Membranes were washed with TBS‐T and incubated for 2 h at RT with goat anti-mouse IgG1 (γ1) (A10551, INVITROGEN), horseradish peroxidase conjugate, diluted 1/2000. Membranes were washed with TBS‐T, developed using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (BIO-RAD) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (BIO-RAD).

Mass spectrometry, protein identification, and protein sequence analysis

Nano-LC-ESI MS/MS experiments and analysis, including samples preparation and in-gel digestion were performed as described previously53. The searches were performed against the predicted proteome of S. aureus N315 (Accession: NC_002745). The E-value threshold for peptide identification was set to 0.05 and a minimum of two peptides per replicate was required for protein identification, resulting in a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 0.15%. For each experimental condition, 3 biological replicates were investigated and the proteins were considered present if they were identified in at least 2 out of 3 replicates. Ortholog-based annotation was obtained with eggNOG-mapper79,80, including the assignment to clusters of orthologous groups (COG) categories, and PSORTb prediction. Lipoprotein signals were predicted with PRED-LIPO81. Venn diagrams were obtained using Draw Venn Diagram (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Labeling of EVs

For single membrane labelling of EVs, 15 µL of EVs sample (around 1.0 × 1012 particles/mL) were stained with 10 µM of Vybrant DiI solution (V22889, THERMO SCIENTIFIC) for 30 min at 37 °C. For double protein- and membrane-labelling of EVs, the same amount of EVs were stained with 1 µL of biotracker red exosome protein labeling kit (SCT269, SIGMA) and 10 µM Vybrant DiO solution (V22889, THERMO SCIENTIFIC) for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were then sequentially treated with proteinase K and PMSF to remove contaminant proteins, as previously described. Labeled EVs were filtered using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units with 100 kDa cut-off by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 4 min). The flow-through was discarded and PBS was added to the retained EVs. The tubes were centrifuged again at the same speed for 3 min in order to wash EVs and remove unreacted dye. Centrifugation and wash step were repeated three times under the same conditions. Retained EVs were finally recovered, and particle concentrations were determined by NTA. For labelling of both RNA and membrane of EVs, 50 µL of EVs sample (around 1.0 × 1012 particles/mL) were stained with 10 µM Syto RNASelect (S32703, THERMO SCIENTIFIC) and 10 µM Vybrant DiD for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were then sequentially treated with RNase A and the RiboLock RNase inhibitor as previously used. Labelled-EV samples were subsequently purified using the SEC-based isolation protocol, concentrated, and analyzed by NTA as described above. Double-labelled EVs were analyzed by confocal microscopy; a drop of approximately 2 µL of labeled EV samples was deposited on a microscope slide with Prolong Glass Antifade Mountant (P36980, THERMO SCIENTIFIC).

Maintenance of eukaryotic cells

Adherent fibroblast derived from human osteogenic sarcoma MG-63 cells (LGC Standards, Teddington, UK) were cultured in DMEM (D0819, Sigma) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS-HI-12A, CLINISCIENCE) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (P0781, SIGMA). MG-63 cells were seeded at density of 3.0 × 104 cells per cm2 and cultured for 48 h prior of interaction with EV for all experiments.

HEK-Blue hTLR2 and HEK-Blue hTLR7 cells containing an inducible NF-κB/AP1 secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) reporter (hkb-htlr2 and hkb-htlr7, INVIVOGEN) and the parental HEK-Blue Null1-k cells (hkb-null1, INVIVOGEN) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin with 100 µg/mL of normocin (ant-nr-05, INVIVOGEN). Culture media were supplemented with different antibiotics depending on the cell line (HEK-Blue Selection (hb-sel, INVIVOGEN), a solution containing several selection antibiotics provided by supplier for HEK Blue hTLR2 ; 100 μg/mL of zeocin (ant-zn-05, INVIVOGEN) and 10 μg/ml of Blasticidin (ant-bl-05, INVIVOGEN) for HEK Blue hTLR7 and 100 μg/mL of zeocin (ant-zn-05, INVIVOGEN) for parental cells. All incubations were at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Trypsin/EDTA (T4049, SIGMA) was used at 0.25% to release MG-63 for cells subculturing. For HEK cell line, PBS was used instead of trypsin/EDTA to preserve TLRs from degradation.

Secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) reporter assays

HEK-Blue cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 4 × 104 cells/well in a final volume of 200 µL. After 48 h, cells were stimulated with purified EVs at different doses (from 5 × 105 to 5 × 107 particles/mL for HEK-Blue hTLR2 and from 7.5 × 108 to 5.0 × 1010 particles/mL for HEK-Blue hTLR7) for 24 h. 10 ng/mL of FSL-1 (tlrl-fsl, INVIVOGEN) or 100 ng/mL of R848 (tlrl-r848-1, INVIVOGEN) treated cells were used as a positive control for HEK-Blue hTLR2 and HEK-Blue hTLR7 respectively. Flow cytometry analysis of MG-63 cell interactions with N315 EVs

Flow cytometry experiments were first performed in order to determine the appropriate N315 EV dose and exposure time for subsequent interaction experiments with MG-63 cells (Fig. S6). After 48 h of MG-63 seeding in 24-well plates, cell population reaches around 1.3 × 105 cells (for counting, cells are detached using trypsin and counted in the presence of trypan blue solution to confirm cells viability). The medium was changed and cells were exposed to varying amounts of DiI-labeled EVs (from 18.75 pg/cell to 300 pg/cell) for incubation times ranging from 0.5 to 17 h. Each well was washed twice with PBS, and cell pellets were recovered after trypsinization and centrifugation. Cell pellets were fixed with 4% PFA (20 min, RT in the dark), and washed twice with PBS. Samples were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer equipped with five lasers (UV 355 nm, Violet 405 nm, Blue 488 nm, Yellow-Green 561 nm, and Red 640 nm). Total cell counts were determined by gating on SSC-A versus FSC-A plots, with cellular debris excluded from intact cells by proper gating. For DiI, excitation was performed using the 561 nm laser with a 586/15 emission filter. DiI fluorescence intensity (indicative of EV internalization) was evaluated from histogram plots of PE versus count, with PE-A set on a logarithmic scale and count on a linear scale. MG-63 cells incubated with PBS only or with unlabeled EVs were used as an unstained control for gate setting. Data were analyzed with FSC Express software and expressed as an internalization factor, calculated as the median fluorescence multiplied by the percentage of marker-positive events. For the remainder of all interaction experiments, unless otherwise stated, 150 pg of EVs per cell were used for 3 h of interaction.

A second series of cytometry experiments was carried out in the same way using two inhibitors of eukaryotic cellular pathways of cellular internalization of exogenous signals: an inhibitor of dynamin-mediated endocytosis, dynasore, and an inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, chlorpromazine. Cells were prepared as previously described and, the medium was changed and supplemented, or not, with 80 µM dynasore (324,410, SIGMA), 10 µM chlorpromazine (31,679, SIGMA), or 0.2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, resuspension buffer for inhibitor), one hour prior to exposure of EV.

MG-63 cells were seeded in wells of Nunc Lab-Tek II chamber slide system 8 (THERMO SCIENTIFIC). Sequential scanning with restricted emission windows was used to minimize spectral overlap between fluorophores and to ensure linear and reliable signal quantification.

RT-qPCR analysis of MG-63 cell interactions with N315 EVs

MG-63 cells were treated or not, with 80 µM dynasore or 0.2% DMSO one hour prior exposure to PBS (control for EV treatment), or EVs. After 3 h of incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN, 74,106), and treated with DNase I (AMPD1-1KT, SIGMA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. One µg of DNA-free RNA was reverse transcribed by qScript cDNA Synthesis kit (QUANTABIO) for a final volume of 20 µL according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and qPCR was carried out qPCR was carried out in a 15 μL volume containing 15 ng cDNA, specific primers (300 nM), and 8 μL IQ SYBR Green Supermix (BIO-RAD). Primers used are listed in Table S2. Reactions were run on a CFX96 real-time system (BIO-RAD, France) using the following cycling parameters: DNA polymerase activation and DNA denaturation 95 °C for 3 min, 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 10 s, and extension at 60 °C for 40 s. Melting curve analysis was included to check the amplification of single PCR products. RT-qPCR was conducted using three biological replicates, and three independent technical replicates (n = 9) for each cDNA sample. The amount of mRNA was normalized by using the geometrical mean value of three reference genes, GAPDH, PGK1, and PPIA. The online tool RefFINDER82 was used to identify these genes as the most stably expressed genes in the experimental conditions tested from a panel of five candidate genes. Gene expression values were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method relative to control conditions (PBS for EV treatment and DMSO for EV treatment in the presence of dynasore) and expressed as mean ± SD.

NF-κB pathway array

MG-63 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 48 h. After changing the medium of MG-63 cells cultured in 24-well plates, PBS or N315 EVs (150 pg/cell as usual) were added and incubated for 24 h. Then, the culture medium was discarded, and cells were washed twice with PBS. One hundred µL of lysis buffer from Human Proteome profiler NF-κB Pathway Array Kit (ARY029, R&D SYSTEMS, BIO-TECHNE) were added per well and kept 5 min on ice. Afterwards, 6 wells were pooled per conditions and placed at 4 °C for 30 min with strong agitation. Samples were centrifuged (14,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were recovered. Proteins quantification was performed using Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (A55864, THERMO SCIENTIFIC). The rest of the experiment were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols using 200 µg of proteins derived from the lysate of MG-63 cells stimulated for 24 h with PBS or N315-derived EVs. Membranes were developed using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (BIO-RAD) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (BIO-RAD). Raw Integrated pixel density for each spot was measured using Fiji (ImageJ 1.54p, Java 1.8.0_452 (64bit))83. Analyses were performed on five biological replicates per condition for a total of ten membranes.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)

MG-63 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 48 h. After changing the medium of MG-63 cells cultured in 24-well plates, PBS or N315 EVs (150 pg/cell) were added and incubated for 24 h. The supernatant of each well was sampled for human IL-8 protein levels by quantitative ELISA, following manufacturer’s instructions (88–8086, INVITROGEN).

Cytotoxicity assay

MG-63 cells were seeded in 96-wells plates for 48 h and were then treated with increasing amount of EVs (ranging from 75 pg/cell to 450 pg/cell). After either 3 h or 24 h of interaction, the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (G3580, PROMEGA) was used as a colorimetric method for determining the number of viable MG-63 cells in proliferation after EV interaction. According to manufacturer’s instructions, 20 μL of CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Reagent were added into each well containing the samples in 100 μL of culture medium. The absorbance at 490 nm were recorded after 3 h of incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Caspase-1 assay

MG-63 cells were seeded in 96-wells plates for 48 h and were then treated with 150 pg of EVs per cell. After 24 h of interaction, the Caspase-Glo1 Inflammasome Assay was performed using protocol described by suppliers for measuring caspase-1 activity in cultured cells (G9951, PROMEGA). Ac-YVAD-CHO inhibitor of caspase-1 was used as control of specificity of the model system. Luminescence was recorded after 3 h of incubation at RT with AGILENT BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (Gain: 200, Integration Time: 1 s, Read Height: 4.5 mm).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM 10 for macOS (version 10.6.0). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality tests were performed using Shapiro–Wilk tests, while F tests were used to compare variances. Depending on data distribution, unpaired t-test, Welch’s t test, or Mann–Whitney test was used to analyze the statistical difference between groups of two. Statistical analyses for more than two groups were performed with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Gray-value data for line plot were obtained with Fiji and smoothed with GraphPad (6 neighbors on each size and 0th order of the smoothing polynomial).
