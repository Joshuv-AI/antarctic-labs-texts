Best Garbling is No Garbling: Persuasion in Real Time
Can Urgun, Mark Whitmeyer

Abstract
We study continuous-time persuasion where a sender controls both how informative a signal is over time and when to stop providing information to a receiver. Given an exogenous signal process, the sender can both garble the evolving signal path and delay the receiver’s decision at a convex, increasing cost of time. We show that, although both instruments are available, any optimal persuasion scheme is fully transparent : the sender keeps the signal fully informative and persuades solely by choosing when to stop.

Introduction

Many environments–drug approvals, loan underwriting, jury verdicts, hiring and tenure votes, content moderation, even “buy/not-buy” choices–share the same essential features: one agent assembles and releases evidence to sway another agent into taking a favorable binary decision, which takes a cut-off form. For instance, a regulator or internal evaluator approves a drug being tested if and only if her belief that the drug works exceeds a cutoff; a capital-provider funds a loan if and only if default risk falls below a hurdle; and a median juror votes to convict if and only if the guilt probability passes a “reasonable doubt” or “preponderance of evidence” threshold.

The canonical persuasion problem
(
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
)
captures this logic in one sentence: a sender, facing a binary state and a receiver with a threshold rule for a binary action, commits to an information structure to maximize the chance the receiver’s posterior exceeds the threshold. What the classic model leaves out, and what real cases make unavoidable, is that moving beliefs takes time: evidence must be generated, filtered, and digested, and delay is costly.

In our setting, we keep this threshold-persuasion objective but make the information flow and the stopping time explicit–signals arrive over time, the receiver updates continuously and acts at the first crossing of the threshold, and the sender pays a convex cost of delay–so the design problem becomes: how should a sender shape and pace what is revealed to push a threshold decision over the line
at the lowest (expected) cost
?

To be more concrete, the sender and the receiver share a common binary prior about an unknown binary state
\theta\in\{L,H\}
that drives a Brownian fundamental
X
in continuous time, which takes the form

dX_{t}=\mu_{\theta}dt+\sigma dW_{t},\quad\text{with}\quad\sigma>0\text{,}

where
W
is a standard Brownian motion. However,
X
is not observed directly, but instead through a sender-chosen information structure that garbles
X
with independent noise: the receiver observes
Y
instead, which takes the form

dY_{t}=dX_{t}+g_{t}dB_{t}\text{,}

where
B
is a Brownian motion that is independent of
W
and the garbling
g
may depend on both the path’s history and the calendar time.

In fact, Theorem

The basic intuition for this, which we will expand on over the next few paragraphs is that garbling has a net
dampening
effect on the receiver’s belief process, which is suboptimal given the convex time cost.

Toward proving our theorem, our first step (Proposition
1
) is that for any fixed distribution over posteriors, the expected-cost minimizing garbling depends only on the posterior belief and not on calendar time or on the past path. That is the “speed of learning” depends only on the current belief level. This holds because once the distribution of terminal beliefs is fixed, the time-cost only cares about how long it takes to get there and because it is convex, it penalizes dispersion. Crucially, any time- or path-dependent pattern of speeding up or slowing down at the same belief level merely creates unnecessary dispersion in the stopping time. By replacing that with a single “average” speed at each belief, we keep the terminal distribution intact but shrink the tail of the hitting-time distribution, which is better under any convex cost.

Second, in Proposition
2
we show that the optimal distribution to be embedded is the most polarizing one: the optimal terminal law is binary, with a mass point on the target threshold and the remainder of the support at a single low belief at which the sender gives up. The key intuition is that the sender’s objective only requires hitting the target threshold,
\bar{P}
, and is completely agnostic about the lower failure beliefs. Splitting the “fail” mass across many points forces extra learning just to sort among them, which only takes longer (and, therefore, costs more) without improving the objective.
Collapsing all failure outcomes to one point,
\underaccent{\bar}{P}
, eliminates that gratuitous delay.

Finally, having argued for the optimality of a binary terminal law and a garbling process that depends only on the belief, it remains to justify why no garbling is optimal. As we already noted, the central driver of this result is the net dampening effect of garbling, which is easiest to understand through the following toy example. Take a one-shot belief-updating problem in which a normally-distributed state is garbled through independent normal noise:
\Theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v)
, and a receiver observes
Y=\Theta+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,r)
is independent of
\Theta
. A simple direct calculation reveals that
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\Theta\mid Y)}{r}=\frac{v}{v+r}
, which, crucially, is decreasing in
r
.

At the same time, the posterior mean of the receiver,
\hat{\Theta}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\Theta\right|Y\right]
, is normal, with variance
\frac{v^{2}}{v+r}
, so the dispersion of the posterior mean is also strictly decreasing in
r
. In particular, for any threshold
a
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\Theta}>a\right)=1-\Phi\left(\frac{a\sqrt{v+r}}{v}\right)\text{,}

which is strictly decreasing in
r
. More garbling, therefore, mechanically dampens the probability that the posterior mean crosses a threshold. Our continuous-time model is the infinitesimal version of this: garbling lowers the posterior’s instantaneous gain, slows the belief clock, and thereby raises the expected convex cost of implementing any fixed terminal law.

Our main finding is perhaps surprising, especially in the context of the other papers in the large persuasion literature. We establish the
robust optimality of transparency:
for any convex and increasing cost, no garbling is optimal. Thus, a company seeking to have its drug approved wants to be completely forthright about its research process and only affect approval through its decision on when to stop accumulating evidence. Likewise, a party wishing to have a loan underwritten wants to fully disclose all of the evidence it accumulates, a start-up pursuing funding ought to reveal both the ups
and
downs in its development progress, and a firm conducting a product demonstration for a potential customer should be fully transparent (but stop at the opportune time).
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
’s prosecutor should fully reveal the evidence he obtains over time.

We also conduct comparative statics. If delay becomes costlier–for two cost functions
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
the difference
c_{1}-c_{2}
is increasing and convex–then the becomes “less persuasive.” That is, she chooses to induce a distribution placing a
smaller
mass on the target threshold. Intuitively, experimentation is more costly, so she gives up sooner: the lower quitting threshold increases, making failure more likely.
|\mu_{H}-\mu_{L}|/\sigma
is the signal-to-noise ratio, which benchmarks the speed of persuasion. We show that as this decreases, the sender becomes less persuasive. A slower clock makes every target more expensive, so the optimal policy is quicker to give up.

Later on in the paper, we show that the robust optimality of transparency persists in two extensions. In our main setting, the sender commits
ex ante
both to a garbling process and a stopping time and the receiver’s role is merely to take an action upon the sender’s stopping of the process. In our first extension, we relax this
ex ante
commitment by the sender and require instead that her instantaneous choice of garbling and her stopping decision are sequentially rational. In Proposition
7
, we reveal that this comparative lack of commitment is inconsequential: the sender’s
ex ante
-optimal strategy is
interim
-optimal as well.

In our second extension, we allow the receiver to also control the stopping of the process–so that the stopping time is the minimum of his and the sender’s–and also allow for the running of the process to impose a constant flow cost on the receiver. Nevertheless, our main insight persists in this setting: in any equilibrium, we show that the sender does not garble (Proposition
8

1.1
Related Work

A growing literature studies dynamic persuasion and dynamic information design. Relative to both this work and the static Bayesian-persuasion benchmark
(
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
)
, our contribution is to make the tempo of information part of the designer’s choice and to show that, when delays are costly, it is optimal to run the most revealing process available and to steer behavior
by the stopping rule alone
. From a technical perspective, we introduce Brownian tools into persuasion: our sender chooses both how transparent to be about a continuous-time diffusion and when to stop the process.

Several recent papers study dynamic persuasion with costly or gradual information, but with a different use of time.
Che et al. 2023
analyze a dynamic persuasion game in which information takes time to generate and neither player can commit. As persuasion costs vanish, Markov equilibria approximate both the
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
optimum and full revelation, but the sender has no separate stopping instrument.
Escudé and Sinander 2023
study “slow persuasion” under a constraint that information can only accumulate gradually and characterize when the sender benefits from slowing down learning.

Li et al. 2025
analyze a sender who sequentially discloses publicly to multiple receivers (an incumbent and a potential entrant) and show how optimal dynamic disclosure can feature “hype-then-deter” patterns driven by strategic interaction across audiences.

Ely et al. 2025
study a principal who selects performance feedback to shape an agent’s effort over time. In their environment, opacity can be optimal because coarser signals strengthen incentives. Thus, when information is used to motivate ongoing effort (i.e., when there is a moral-hazard aspect), strategically withholding or bunching feedback can be desirable. In our setting, dynamic incentives do not call for coarsening.

Our Brownian setting is especially related to
Aybas and Callander 2024
, who provide conditions for efficient cheap talk in complex (Brownian) environments. They use a Brownian motion to represent a rich underlying state space and study when one-shot cheap talk can achieve efficient outcomes, treating the Brownian process as a device for modeling complexity rather than as an object of dynamic design. We instead take the diffusion itself as the information technology: the sender chooses whether to fully reveal or to garble the Brownian path and when to stop it, and delay costs discipline this dynamic design problem. In this sense, we bring Brownian methods to persuasion rather than to cheap talk.

Our focus on persuasion rather than cheap talk is complementary to work that systematically compares the two communication modes.
Kamenica and Lin 2024
characterize when commitment and randomization matter in communication games and delineate the additional outcomes that can be achieved with persuasion (commitment to information structures) relative to cheap talk. Our results can be viewed as a continuous-time diffusion counterpart to this comparison: we show that, in a rich Brownian environment with convex delay costs, the sender’s dynamic instrument is effectively reduced to a choice of stopping rule under full transparency. Commitment operates through the timing of disclosure rather than through garbling or randomization of the experiment itself.

Close in spirit on the static side are papers that exploit geometric structure in complex persuasion problems.
Bardhi and Guo 2018
analyze private Bayesian persuasion toward unanimous consent in committees with correlated states and heterogeneous approval thresholds, and characterize the sender-optimal experiments under different persuasion modes.
Guo and Shmaya 2019
show that in screening-cum-disclosure problems with privately informed receivers, the sender-optimal disclosure policies take the form of nested intervals.

Henry and Ottaviani 2019
explore a continuous-time approval environment in which an “informer” pays a flow cost to run a fixed Brownian experiment and an “evaluator” decides whether to approve or reject. Starting from Wald’s optimal sequential test
(
Wald 1945
)
, they split the planner into these two agents, assign them different payoffs and control rights, and analyze how organizational form—evaluator authority, informer authority, or evaluator commitment—distorts the stopping rule relative to the first best. In the frictionless limit, their informer-authority regime implements the
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
outcome via a stopping rule.

Our paper is complementary but tackles a different question. Rather than fixing the sequential test and studying who controls the stopping rule, we take the continuous-time information technology itself as the object of design: the sender chooses how transparent to be about an exogenous Brownian signal and when to stop providing information.
Henry and Ottaviani 2019
’s testing environment corresponds to a special case of our framework in which the signal is restricted to a given diffusion and only the stopping policy is a choice variable.

Two recent papers push the Henry–Ottaviani agenda in complementary directions.
Henry et al. 2022
embed sequential experimentation in a two-phase adoption problem and study how a regulator optimally combines
ex ante
approval,
ex post
withdrawal, and liability.
McClellan 2022
instead takes the approval rule itself as the object of design when experimentation is delegated to a biased agent and characterizes optimal history-dependent approval mechanisms in a sequential testing environment, showing how the regulator may “fast-track” approval early on and then tighten standards in response to unfavorable interim data.

Another strand combines persuasion with search and outside sources of information.
Bizzotto et al. 2021
study dynamic persuasion when a receiver also observes outside news and can stop listening, and show how optimal disclosure policies trade off steering beliefs against the risk that the receiver exits early.
Mekonnen et al. 2025

Mekonnen and Pakzad-Hurson 2025
analyze information brokers who design and price signals for a searching agent, and characterize how signal menus and competition between brokers affect search intensity and stopping. These papers feature an interesting interplay between persuasion, search, and market structure in information provision.

On the information-acquisition side,
Zhong 2022
and
Chen and Zhong 2025
study single-agent dynamic problems in which a decision maker can flexibly choose a signal process subject to constraints or costs, and trades off delay against accuracy, with attitudes toward time risk shaping the optimal policy.
Bloedel and Zhong 2020

Finally, we build on the connection between Wald’s sequential sampling problem and reduced-form information costs.
Morris and Strack 2019
show that, in the two-state Wald problem, any distribution of posteriors can be implemented by some stopping rule and that the induced ex ante cost of information is posterior-separable. We tackle our problem in a similar fashion--in particular, by reformulating our problem as a multi-step embedding of a distribution of posteriors--but our papers otherwise diverge. Our focus is on the dynamic persuasion problem rather than on further developing the foundations of information costs.

4
Results

Our first result shows that when there are (weakly) convex costs involved, the best thing the sender can do is to have the posterior be a Markov process.

Proposition 1
.

For any Bayes-plausible terminal law
\nu
, any increasing and convex cost
c
, there exists an optimal (cost-minimizing) garbling
g^{*}
and stopping time
\tau^{*}
implementing
\nu
for which the receiver’s posterior evolves as a time-homogeneous diffusion
d\hat{p}_{t}=\Sigma(\hat{p}_{t})dB_{t}
, for some Borel
\Sigma\colon(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

That is, fixing the target distribution, if the designer minimizes any convex, increasing cost of delay, it is without loss of generality to choose an information policy whose posterior evolves with a time-homogeneous, state-dependent volatility. That is, the “speed of learning” depends only on the current belief level
\hat{p}_{t}
(not on calendar time or on the whole past path). More technically, among all policies that implement the same distribution, there is a state-only policy
(g^{*},\tau^{*})
whose residual expected time

R_{\tau^{*}}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[(\tau^{*}-t)_{+}\right]=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau^{*}\geq s)ds,

is (weakly) smaller than every other at every time
t
. Consequently, it yields a lower expected cost for
every
convex waiting cost.

Quite simply, convex time costs penalize long tails of waiting. If, for each calendar time
t
, you can make the expected time remaining
R_{\tau^{*}}(t)
no larger, then all convex time objectives improve. The state-only volatility profile
\Sigma(\hat{p})
achieves exactly that: whenever the belief revisits the same level, the process moves at the same speed, so there is no wasteful alternation between too-fast and too-slow episodes that would swell the tail of
R_{\tau}(\cdot)
.

The formal proof for Proposition
1
may be found in Appendix
A.4
. We prove it by establishing three lemmas, which we now discuss informally. The first, Lemma
1
is technical, but here is the gist of what is taking place. We can think of the posterior belief process as a little particle wandering randomly on the interval
[0,1]
starting at the prior
p_{0}
. Eventually we stop the process at some stopping time, and its location then has some distribution
\nu
over beliefs. Fix any belief level
y
. On the one hand, we can look only at the final distribution
\nu
and ask a purely static question: on average, how far is the final belief from
y
? This average distance is the
potential
of the measure
U_{\nu}(y)=\int|z-y|\nu(dz)
.

On the other hand, we can look at the whole path of the belief process and ask a dynamic question: how much time, in total, does the belief spend right at level
y
before we stop? Lemma
1
says that these two viewpoints are in fact tightly linked. If
L^{y}_{\tau}
denotes the local time accumulated at level
y
up to the stopping time
\tau
then
\mathbb{E}\left[L^{y}_{\tau}\right]=U_{\nu}(y)-\left|p_{0}-y\right|
. Combining this with the occupation-time formula for diffusions, we obtain a representation of the expected total stopping time that uses both potentials and local times in a single identity. Concretely, when the posterior diffuses with (state-dependent) speed
\Sigma\left(\cdot\right)
,

R_{\tau}(0)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[L^{y}_{\tau}\right]}{\Sigma^{2}(y)}dy=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{U_{\nu}(y)-\left|p_{0}-y\right|}{\Sigma^{2}(y)}dy\text{.}

Loosely, to transform a point prior into a spread-out posterior distribution, the belief process must “rub” against each level
y
just enough that the expected amount of rubbing at
y
matches how much, on average, the final beliefs sit away from
y
. Once the terminal law
\nu
is fixed, Lemma
1
, therefore, pins down, level by level, how much time the process can afford to spend at each belief,
independently of the particular path it takes
.

Our second lemma, Lemma
2
, is due to
Rost 2006
and has a clearer economic meaning. It says that if one stopping rule is never worse in terms of expected remaining waiting time at any calendar date, then it is never worse for
any
convex waiting cost. More formally, we show that every policy can be summarized by its residual-time curve
R_{\tau}(t)
, introduced
supra
. Naturally, early on, the expected remaining time is large, and as we get closer to stopping, it shrinks toward zero. Convex waiting costs are exactly those that are relatively forgiving about small delays but increasingly punitive about long ones.

Lemma 2.

Let
\tau
and
\tilde{\tau}
be stopping times. Suppose that for all
t\geq 0
,
R_{\tau}(t)\leq R_{\tilde{\tau}}(t)
. Then, for any convex and increasing cost
c\colon\left[0,\infty\right)\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[c(\tilde{\tau})]
.

That is to say, no matter which particular convex function
c
one chooses, the expected cost
\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau)\right]
is effectively an integral transform of the residual-time curve
R_{\tau}(\cdot)
that puts extra weight on situations where there is still a lot of waiting left at late dates. If, for two policies, policy A has
R_{\tau^{A}}(t)\leq R_{\tau^{B}}(t)
for every
t
, then A never has more expected waiting left, at any point in time, than B. Because convex functions dislike long delays, pushing the entire residual-time curve downward strictly improves
\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau)\right]
for
every
convex, increasing cost function
c
. Lemma
2
makes this observation precise;
viz.
, pointwise dominance in residual expectations is equivalent to dominance for all convex time costs.

Lemma
3
concerns the shape of the information process, holding fixed the terminal law and the stopping rule. It says that among all ways of choosing the volatility of beliefs–possibly depending on time and on the whole past history–there is a benchmark in which the speed of learning depends only on the current belief, and this benchmark is best for convex delay costs. A useful analogy is driving on a highway to a fixed destination. You can choose your speed at every moment, and you are penalized by some convex function of total travel time (you especially dislike very long trips). It is natural that your speed depends on
where
you are–slower on tight curves or hills, faster on straight segments–but it should not depend on the time of day (think of a well-lit road): how fast you take the same turn should not change just because you arrived there at 8AM rather than 8PM
ceteris paribus
. Under a complicated policy, at each location
y
along the highway, you may sometimes crawl and sometimes speed, depending in a messy way on when you arrived there and on the route you took so far. From the point of view of total travel time under a convex penalty, this within-location variation is wasteful: you are mixing very fast and very slow episodes in the same place, even though where you need to end up is fixed.

Lemma
3
shows that one can always smooth all of this out. For each belief level
y
, look at how often the original policy visits
y
and what “speeds” it uses there. Replace that entire random mix by a single effective speed
\Sigma(y)
that depends only on
y
, i.e., whenever the belief is
y
, it now moves at this fixed speed, regardless of calendar time or the past path. This change keeps the distribution of terminal beliefs the same, because it preserves how much local time is accumulated at each level, but it eliminates pointless variability in how quickly the process moves when it is at the same belief. Since convex time costs penalize very long realizations, eliminating these alternations between “too slow” and “too fast” at a given belief trims the probability of extremely long paths. The resulting policy, in which the posterior follows a time-homogeneous diffusion with volatility
\Sigma(\hat{p})
, has weakly smaller residual expectations at every date, hence, weakly lower expected cost for every convex waiting cost.

With our first proposition in hand, our next result pins down the structure of optimal distributions.

Proposition 2
.

For any weakly convex cost and any upper atom size
p_{\nu}>0
, the optimal embedded distribution is a two atom distribution where the second atom is pinned down by the martingale constraint.

To put differently, fix
\bar{P}
and the probability of persuasion
p_{\nu}
. Then among any distributions over posteriors you can induce, the one that minimizes the residual expected time is the most “polarized” one: all the remaining mass
1-p_{\nu}
is placed at a single lower belief
\underaccent{\bar}{P}
(which satisfies Bayes-plausibility). That is, the optimal terminal law is binary.

The rationale for this result is simple: the objective only requires hitting
\bar{P}
with likelihood
p_{\nu}
and is agnostic about the lower failure beliefs. Splitting the “fail” mass across many points forces extra learning just to sort among them, which only takes longer without improving the objective. Collapsing all failure outcomes to one point
\underaccent{\bar}{P}
eliminates that gratuitous delay.

Next, we state our main result. We say that, given a terminal law
\nu
and a weakly convex cost,
optimal garbling is no garbling
if for any garbling process
g
and stopping time
\tau
that implement
\nu
, there exists a
no-garbling

g=0
and stopping time
\tilde{\tau}
that implement the same

\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(\tilde{\tau}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[c\left(\tau\right)\right]
, strictly so if the garbling is nontrivial.
Viz.
, any optimal solution to the sender’s persuasion problem does not garble the fundamental at all.

Theorem 1
.

For any weakly convex, strictly increasing cost, optimal garbling is no garbling.

Theorem
1
is the continuous-time analog of our normal-plus-noise example in the introduction. Recall that in our one-shot toy model, the state is
\Theta\sim N(0,v)
and the signal equals the state plus a zero-mean normal noise variable
\varepsilon
that is independent of
\Theta
. The receiver cares about whether her posterior mean
\hat{\Theta}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\Theta\right|Y\right]
exceeds a cutoff
a
. When you increase the noise variance
r
, the posterior mean becomes more diffuse around zero and the probability
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\Theta}>a)
falls. With
n
independent observations, the posterior mean
\hat{\Theta}_{n}
becomes more precise as
n
grows, and the probability of crossing the threshold is increasing in
n
but decreasing in
r
. If we treat
n
as a crude clock, then more garbling (larger
r
) literally slows that clock: for a given
n
, you are less likely to have pushed the posterior above the cutoff. If you want to maximize the chance of hitting the cutoff given a cost of samples, you never add gratuitous noise, but instead use the cleanest signal you can, and only choose how many draws
n
to take.

Theorem
1
says that exactly the same logic is not only present, but demonstrated in a sharper manner (and holds for arbitrary not just binary
\nu
s) in the continuous-time persuasion problem. Now the fundamental Brownian motion is the analog of the “clean signal,” and the garbling process
g_{t}
plays the role of injecting extra noise at each instant. Fix a target terminal law
\nu
, which is the
ex ante
distribution of the receiver’s eventual posterior that the sender wants to implement. The lemmas we used to prove Proposition
1
show three things. First, once
\nu
is fixed, the shape of
\nu
pins down a “time budget” at each belief level: any policy that implements
\nu
must, in expectation, spend exactly the same amount of time near each belief
y
. Second, given this level-by-level time budget, what matters for convex time costs is how dispersed the stopping time
\tau
is–in particular, how fat its upper tail is. Convex waiting costs are relatively forgiving about small delays but increasingly punitive about very long ones. Third, any extra time- or history-dependence in the volatility of the posterior at a given belief level simply injects unnecessary variation into how fast beliefs move there. From the point of view of convex time costs, these alternations between very slow and very fast episodes at the same belief are harmful, as they make extremely long paths more likely.

Theorem
1
identifies the best way to use the given time budget at each belief level. Among all information policies that implement a given terminal law
\nu
, the optimal one for any convex, increasing delay cost is simply to set
g\equiv 0
, i.e., the sender never deliberately garbles the fundamental. This is the continuous-time counterpart of setting the one-shot noise variance
r
as low as possible and only choosing how many signals to observe. Under no garbling, the posterior is as responsive as possible to the underlying state at every belief level, so the process uses up its fixed local time budget in the fastest, least-dispersed way. In this sense, full transparency makes the belief “clock” tick as quickly as the model allows. Any additional garbling along the way only slows that clock, leading to more dispersion in stopping times and a higher expected cost under every convex measure of delay.

5
Comparative Statics

We turn our attention to comparative statics. Our analysis so far has allowed us to condense the sender’s choice into a simple choice of a probability of persuasion
p\in\left[0,p_{0}/\bar{P}\right]
. There are two natural comparative statics questions: first, how does the shape of the cost function shape persuasion? That is, how does the optimal probability of persuasion change as we change the cost? Second, how do the primitives of the fundamental affect persuasion?

In Proposition

Formally, for a fixed

than

Definition 1

S_{1}\geq_{SSO}S_{2}

.

If

is more convex than

This proposition leans on two technical lemmas. In the first, we show that comparing stopping times in the
increasing convex order
is equivalent to comparing their residual-time curves and their means. Intuitively, if one policy never has less expected remaining time at any date and also has a (weakly) larger mean, then every convex time cost regards it as the “riskier” stopping rule. Our second lemma, compares the exit times of the posterior diffusion from two nested intervals. In particular, we show that lowering the lower boundary increases the expected exit time and leads to pointwise dominance of the residual-time curves.

The intuition behind this is also simple. Think of a one-dimensional diffusion started somewhere between the two boundaries. If the lower boundary is very close to the starting point, the process can escape quickly by a small downward fluctuation. If instead the lower boundary is moved further down (so that the continuation interval becomes wider), the process must wander further in order to hit either boundary, and so the expected exit time increases. Pathwise, if we run the same Brownian path for both problems, the first time we leave the smaller interval is never later than the first time we leave the larger interval; hence, the expected time increases for every sample path. Conditioning on the history up to time
t
shows that the expected remaining time until exit is also larger when the interval is wider. Indeed, this manifests as pointwise dominance of the residual-time curves.

Putting these pieces together, the remainder of the proof exploits classic comparative statics results. Fix two success probabilities
p^{\prime}<p
. From the second lemma we know that
\tau(p)
is larger than
\tau(p^{\prime})
in the increasing convex order, so for any increasing convex cost

\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau(p))-c(\tau(p^{\prime}))\right]

is nonnegative, and it becomes larger the more convex
c
is. In other words, as we move from a less convex cost
c_{1}
to a more convex cost
c_{2}
(with
c_{2}-c_{1}
convex and increasing), the extra cost of raising the success probability from
p^{\prime}

(p,c)\mapsto c(\tau(p))
has decreasing differences. Since the benefit term
Vp
is linear in
p

\argmax

\Pi_{c_{1}}

Next, let us change the
\mu

If the signal-to-noise ratio is higher for persuader

6
Extensions

We finish the paper with two extensions. In the first, we relax the global commitment assumption for the sender. Rather than assuming she can commit at time
0
to an entire garbling and stopping policy, we allow her to
re-optimize “locally:” at any stopping time
\zeta
of the signal filtration, she may replace the continuation
of her policy from
\zeta
onward. This adds a natural sequential-optimality requirement–plans must remain
optimal from every future history–but we show it has no bite in our environment: every globally optimal
policy is also sequentially optimal, so global and local commitment coincide.

In the second extension, we allow the receiver to stop as well. The sender still commits to a policy, but now
the receiver also chooses a stopping time
\tau_{r}
, so that the realized terminal time is
\tau=\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}
.
When the receiver has no flow cost of waiting (
\lambda=0
), she never benefits from preempting the sender,
so the model reduces to the baseline sender-only-stopping problem. When
\lambda>0
, preemption is a genuine concern: because the realized terminal time is

\tau=\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}
, the terminal posterior law (the embedded distribution) is now an
equilibrium outcome, jointly pinned down by the sender’s and receiver’s stopping rules.
Nevertheless, the main conclusion survives: in any sender-commitment equilibrium, the sender does not
garble. Intuitively, the sender can switch to full transparency and embed the same terminal law more quickly, and the receiver is unwilling to punish this deviation by stopping earlier.

6.1
Local Commitment Equals Global Commitment

So far we have treated the sender as a long-lived designer who can commit
ex ante
to an entire policy
\pi=(g,\tau)
: a garbling rule for the flow of information and a stopping rule specifying when to terminate the process and act. In many applications, however, it is more natural to think of the sender as choosing information flows locally, revising them over time as the signal history evolves, and retaining the ability to abandon the interaction. To capture this, we now consider a
local commitment
formulation in which, at any stopping time
\zeta
of the signal filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the sender may reconsider and replace the continuation of the policy from that point on.

From the sender’s perspective, this weaker form of commitment can only make her worse off: it adds the requirement that any policy she chooses must remain optimal when viewed from the perspective of every future history, so that she never wants to abandon her plan midway. Does this additional sequential-rationality constraint actually have bite, or is local commitment just as good as global commitment? Our result is simple: every globally optimal policy is immune to profitable local deviations. Thus, global and local commitment coincide.

Let

A policy

\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
), and the realized terminal time is
\tau\coloneqq\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}
. Upon stopping, the receiver also chooses binary action
a_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}
. The receiver’s objective is to maximize
\mathbb{E}\left[u(a_{\tau},\mu)-\lambda\tau\right]
(
\lambda\geq 0
), whereas the sender’s payoff is as before:
U_{S}(Y;\tau,a)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[u_{S}(a_{\tau},\mu)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau)\right]
, where
c\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is convex, strictly increasing, and continuous, with
c(0)=0
; and
u_{S}
depends on the state and the receiver’s action.

We specify that the sender is a Stackelberg leader: she commits to
(Y,\tau_{s})
at time
0
. The receiver observes
(Y,\tau_{s})
then chooses
(\tau_{r},a)
, which must be sequentially rational with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{Y}
.
Furthermore, as the receiver always optimizes the terminal action given her belief, we define the receiver’s static value to be

V(p)\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[u_{R}(a,\mu)\mid\hat{p}=p\right],

so the receiver’s stopping problem given
(Y,\tau_{s})
is

\sup_{\tau_{r}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[V(\hat{p}_{\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}})-\lambda(\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r})\right].

We adapt our earlier definition, saying that
optimal garbling is no garbling
if all (subgame-perfect) equilibrium of the bilateral-stopping game feature full-revelation on the continuation path.

Proposition 8
.

For any strictly increasing, weakly convex sender cost, in the bilateral-stopping game, optimal garbling is no garbling.

With bilateral stopping, the receiver’s only credible punishment of a sender deviation is to
preempt–i.e., stop
earlier
than the sender—because the realized terminal time is

\tau=\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}
(she cannot punish by waiting longer). The sender’s deviation to no garbling effectively “tightens the clock,” delivering each unit of information in
weakly less calendar time. Since
\lambda>0
, this makes waiting to the same “information time” weakly cheaper while leaving the benefit from additional information unchanged. Therefore, if the receiver did not preempt under the original (slower) clock, he will still not preempt under the deviation, so the deviation goes unpunished.

A.1
Sender’s Beliefs

The sender observes

dX_{t}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dB_{t},\quad\text{with}\quad\mu\in\{\mu_{h},\mu_{l}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h})=p_{0}.

The likelihood ratio is

L_{t}=\exp\left(\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma^{2}}X_{t}-\frac{\mu_{h}^{2}-\mu_{l}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}t\right),

so that the posterior
p_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{X}\right)
satisfies

\frac{p_{t}}{1-p_{t}}=\frac{p_{0}}{1-p_{0}}L_{t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad p_{t}=\frac{p_{0}L_{t}}{p_{0}L_{t}+(1-p_{0})}.

Defining the innovation Brownian motion

W_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(X_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{s}]\,ds\right),

we can rewrite the observation as

dX_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}]\,dt+\sigma\,dW_{t},

and the posterior satisfies the innovation form

dp_{t}=\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma}\,p_{t}(1-p_{t})\,dW_{t}.

A.2
Reporting Signals is Reporting Beliefs

This subsection shows that any signal process
Y
of the form

dY_{t}=a_{t}dt+b_{t}dX_{t}

is, from the receiver’s point of view, equivalent to a process in which the sender reports her own posterior belief plus suitable Gaussian noise. Formally, for any such
(a_{t},b_{t})
the induced receiver posterior
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can also be generated by an alternative signal
Z
satisfying

dZ_{t}=dp_{t}+g_{t}dB^{\prime}_{t},

where
(p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the sender’s posterior process given
X
,
B^{\prime}
is a Brownian motion, and
g_{t}
is a predictable “posterior noise” coefficient. That is, information policies can be represented without loss of generality directly in posterior space.

Let
\mu\in\{\mu_{l},\mu_{h}\}
with prior
p_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h})
, and let the fundamental process
X
evolve according to

dX_{t}=\mu dt+\sigma dB_{t},\quad\text{where}\quad\sigma>0,

on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, where
B
is a standard Brownian motion and
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}=\sigma(X_{s}\colon s\leq t)
is the completed natural filtration of
X
.

The sender observes
X
and commits at time
0
to an
(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
-adapted signal process
Y
of the form

dY_{t}=a_{t}\,dt+b_{t}\,dX_{t},

where
(a_{t},b_{t})
is
\mathcal{F}^{X}
–progressively measurable and satisfies the integrability and boundedness conditions in the main text so that
Y
is a continuous semimartingale. The receiver observes only
Y
so that her information at time
t
is
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Y_{s}\colon s\leq t)
.

Since
(a_{t},b_{t})
is
\mathcal{F}^{X}
-adapted and there is no additional primitive noise in
Y
, we have

\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(X_{s},Y_{s}\colon s\leq t)=\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t},\quad\text{for all}t\geq 0:

the joint filtration generated by
(X,Y)
coincides with the sender’s filtration
\mathcal{F}^{X}
.

Define the sender’s posterior
p_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
and the receiver’s posterior under the policy
Y
,
r_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
. It is standard in this binary-drift Gaussian setting that
(p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a bounded continuous
(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
-martingale, and in fact solves

dp_{t}=\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma}\,p_{t}(1-p_{t})\,dW_{t},

for the innovation Brownian motion
W
of
X
in
\mathcal{F}^{X}
(see e.g.
Liptser and Shiryaev 2013
, Ch. 7). In what follows we only use that
p
is a bounded continuous martingale.

Let
H\coloneqq 1_{\{\mu=\mu_{h}\}}
, and note that by definition,
p_{t}=\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}]
and
r_{t}=\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]
. Because
Y
is constructed from
X
and there is no additional primitive randomness, we have
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}
for all
t
. By the tower property,

r_{t}=\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\,\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}]\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{y}_{t}\right].

Thus,
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the optional projection of
(p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
onto the smaller filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
, and is itself a bounded continuous
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
-martingale with
r_{0}=p_{0}
.

Let
\mathbb{F}^{X}=(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the sender’s filtration. Consider the sender’s posterior martingale

M_{t}\coloneqq p_{t}-p_{0},\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0,

which is a continuous
(\mathbb{F}^{X})
-martingale. Let
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the receiver’s filtration, regarded as a subfiltration of
\mathbb{F}^{X}
.

By the Kunita-Watanabe decomposition with respect to
\mathbb{G}
(see, e.g.,
Revuz and Yor 2013
, Ch. III, Thm. 3.9), there exists a unique decomposition

M_{t}=N_{t}+L_{t},\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0,

where

•

N
is a continuous
(\mathbb{F}^{X})
-martingale which is also a
(\mathbb{G})
-martingale,

•

L
is a continuous
(\mathbb{F}^{X})
-martingale, and

•

L
is strongly orthogonal to every
(\mathbb{G})
-martingale (in particular,
\langle L,N\rangle\equiv 0
).

Using (
A.2
), we identify

N_{t}=\mathbb{E}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}-p_{0}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]=r_{t}-p_{0}.

Hence

L_{t}=M_{t}-N_{t}=(p_{t}-p_{0})-(r_{t}-p_{0})=p_{t}-r_{t},

and (
A.2
) becomes

p_{t}-p_{0}=(r_{t}-p_{0})+(p_{t}-r_{t}).

Since

is the Brownian filtration generated by

(\mathbb{F}^{X})
-martingale admits a stochastic integral representation with respect to
B

\mathbb{F}^{X}
-predictable process

We do not need an explicit expression for

Substituting (
A.2
) into (
A.2
) yields

p_{t}-p_{0}=(r_{t}-p_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}dB_{s}\quad\Rightarrow\quad r_{t}=p_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}dB_{s},\quad\text{where}\quad g_{t}\coloneqq-h_{t}.

Consequently, the receiver’s posterior process

is the sender’s posterior process
p

\mathbb{F}^{X}
-martingale given by an Itô integral with respect to the same Brownian motion
B

g_{t}
is adapted to
\mathbb{F}^{X}

Define a new signal process

Z_{t}\coloneqq r_{t}
for all

\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Z_{s}\colon s\leq t)
be its natural filtration. By (

),

Z_{0}=p_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad dZ_{t}=dp_{t}+g_{t}\,dB_{t},

for the Brownian motion

\mathbb{F}^{X}

constructed above. Thus,

is exactly of the form “sender’s posterior plus Gaussian noise”.

We now verify that the policy

induces the same posterior process as the original policy

and

is
\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}
-measurable,

\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t})=\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\,\mathbb{E}[H\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]\mid\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}]=r_{t}.

In other words, observing
Z
yields exactly the same posterior process
(r_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as observing

If desired, we may also rescale the noise term so that

\tilde{B}_{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{sgn}(g_{s})\,dB_{s}
, which is again a Brownian motion in

\tilde{g}_{t}\coloneqq|g_{t}|
. Then
dZ_{t}=dp_{t}+\tilde{g}_{t}\,d\tilde{B}_{t}

For any admissible linear signal policy
dY_{t}=a_{t}dt+b_{t}dX_{t}
, the induced receiver posterior process
(r_{t})
coincides with the posterior induced by an alternative policy in which the sender reports a belief-based signal

dZ_{t}=dp_{t}+g_{t}\,dB_{t}

for some
\mathbb{F}^{X}
-adapted process
g_{t}
. Thus, without loss of generality, we may model information policies directly in

as choices of a Gaussian noise process
g_{t}
added to the sender’s posterior
p_{t}
, with
g_{t}
adapted to the sender’s information about

.

A.3
How to Garble: Adding Mean-Preserving Noise

We now fix the posterior process
p_{t}
from Appendix
A.1
and specify how the sender
garbles it before passing information to the receiver.

The posterior evolves as a one-dimensional martingale diffusion:

dp_{t}=\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma}\,p_{t}(1-p_{t})dW_{t},\quad\text{where}\quad p_{0}\in(0,1),

W_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion, and

\mathcal{F}^{p}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(p_{s}\colon s\leq t)
is the natural filtration of
p_{t}
.

Information sent by the sender: observation process and filtrations

The sender communicates to the receiver a “noise-added” observation process
Y_{t}
of the form

dY_{t}=p_{t}\,dt+g(t,p_{t})\,dB^{Y}_{t},

where
B^{Y}_{t}
is a Brownian motion independent of
W_{t}
, and
|g(t,p_{t})|\leq M
a.s. for all
t\geq 0

integral
\int_{0}^{t}g(s,p_{s})\,dB^{Y}_{s}
is well defined for all finite
t
, the SDE admits a unique

strong solution
adapted to the filtration generated by
(p,B^{Y})
, and
Y
is a continuous
semimartingale with square-integrable martingale part.
11
11

11

The assumption is merely for technical convenience, an analogous but much more cumbersome analysis can be done with weak solutions.

We define the observation filtration to be
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Y_{s}\colon s\leq t)
, and denote the receiver’s posterior mean and variance by
\hat{p}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]
and
v_{t}\coloneqq\operatorname{Var}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]

Receiver’s inference from the information sent

The receiver is interested in
\hat{p}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}]
. We can
calculate this using the Kushner–Stratonovich (K–S) equation from nonlinear filtering.

For any sufficiently smooth test function
\phi(p)
, the K–S equation gives

\begin{split}d\mathbb{E}[\phi(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]&=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}\phi(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]dt\\
&+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\phi(p_{t})h(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[\phi(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]\mathbb{E}[h(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]}{\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(t,p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]}\,(dY_{t}-\mathbb{E}[h(p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]dt),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{A}\phi(p)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(p)
is the generator of

, and
h(p)=p
is the linear observation function.

We define the
innovation process

dI_{t}\coloneqq dY_{t}-\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]\,dt=dY_{t}-\hat{p}_{t}\,dt,

which is a continuous
\mathcal{F}^{Y}
-local martingale. Setting

in (

\mathcal{A}\phi=0
, and the
observation term becomes the posterior variance

. Hence, the posterior mean satisfies the

innovation-form SDE
:

d\hat{p}_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(t,p_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]}dI_{t}.

By standard filtering results (equivalently, the martingale representation theorem applied to the bounded continuous martingale
\hat{p}

\mathcal{F}^{Y}
-Brownian motion

such that the same posterior process admits the representation given in (

Proof of Proposition

Our goal is to show that, for any fixed target law over posteriors,
among all garblings that implement it, one can find an optimal design in which the receiver’s
posterior
\hat{p}_{t}
is a time-homogeneous diffusion with a volatility that depends only on the
current belief level. Together with Lemma
2

Proposition
1
.

The proof relies on several lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X
be a continuous local martingale in natural scale started at
X_{0}=x\in(0,1)
, and let
\tau

be a stopping time such that
X_{\tau}\sim\nu
, where
\nu
is a probability measure supported on

[0,1]
. For each fixed

define the Borel measure on
[0,\infty)

\Lambda_{y}(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{A}dL_{s}^{y}\right],\quad\text{for Borel }A\subset[0,\infty),

where
L_{s}^{y}
is the local time of
X
at level

. Then
\Lambda_{y}
is a finite Borel measure and

\Lambda_{y}([0,\infty))=\mathbb{E}[L_{\tau}^{y}]=U^{\nu}(y)-|x-y|<\infty,

where
U^{\nu}(y)\coloneqq\int|z-y|\,\nu(dz)
is the potential of the measure.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Apply Itô-Tanaka to the convex function
z\mapsto|z-y|
and take expectations at the stopping time

\tau
. Since
X_{\tau}\sim\nu
and the local martingale term has zero expectation, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[|X_{\tau}-y|]=|x-y|+\mathbb{E}[L_{\tau}^{y}].

Thus,
\mathbb{E}[L_{\tau}^{y}]=\int|z-y|\,\nu(dz)-|x-y|=U^{\nu}(y)-|x-y|
. Because
\nu
is a
probability on the bounded interval
[0,1]
,
U^{\nu}(y)<\infty
for every

, so

\mathbb{E}[L_{\tau}^{y}]<\infty
. Finally, define
\Lambda_{y}(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\int_{A}d_{s}L_{s}^{y}]
. By
monotone convergence the total mass of this finite measure equals
\mathbb{E}[L_{\tau}^{y}]
.
∎

Definition 2
.

Let
\tau
be a stopping time. Its residual expected time is

R_{\tau}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}]=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau\geq s)ds,\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

When we want to highlight dependence, we use a conditioning bar instead of superscripts:

R_{\tau\mid\sigma}(t),\quad R_{\tau\mid\Sigma}(t),\quad R_{\tau\mid\nu}(t),\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\tau\mid g}(t).

These are purely book-keeping tags indicating, respectively, the diffusion coefficient, embedded law, or garbling relative to which
R_{\tau}
is being computed.

The following result, due to
Rost 2006
,

Lemma 2

(Rost)
.

Let
\tau
and
\tilde{\tau}
be stopping times. Suppose that for all
t\geq 0
,
R_{\tau}(t)\leq R_{\tilde{\tau}}(t)
. Then for any convex and increasing function

c\colon\left[0,\infty\right)\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[c(\tau)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[c(\tilde{\tau})]
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Since
c
is convex and increasing, it is locally absolutely continuous and its right derivative
c_{+}^{\prime}(t)
exists for every
t\geq 0
, is nondecreasing, and satisfies
c(t)=c(0)+\int_{0}^{t}c_{+}^{\prime}(s)ds
for all
t\geq 0
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}[c(\tau)]=c(0)+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}c_{+}^{\prime}(s)ds\right]=c(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}c_{+}^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)dt=c(0)-\int_{0}^{\infty}c_{+}^{\prime}(t)d\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}].

Integration by parts for Stieltjes integrals implies that for every
T>0
, the integral

-\int_{0}^{T}c_{+}^{\prime}(t)d\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}]=-c_{+}^{\prime}(T)\mathbb{E}[(\tau-T)_{+}]+c_{+}^{\prime}(0)\mathbb{E}[\tau]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}]dc_{+}^{\prime}(t).

By convexity, for every
T\geq 0
and every
x\geq T
we have
c(x)\geq c(T)+c_{+}^{\prime}(T)(x-T)
.
Applying this with
x=\tilde{\tau}
yields
c^{\prime}_{+}(T)(\tilde{\tau}-T)_{+}\leq c(\tilde{\tau})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}>T\}}
, so
c^{\prime}_{+}(T)\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\tau}-T)_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}[c(\tilde{\tau})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}>T\}}]\to 0
as
T\to\infty
. Since
\mathbb{E}[(\tau-T)_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\tau}-T)_{+}]
for all
T
, we also have

c^{\prime}_{+}(T)\mathbb{E}[(\tau-T)_{+}]\to 0
. Taking
T\to\infty
produces

\mathbb{E}[c(\tau)]=c(0)+c^{\prime}_{+}(0)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}]\,dc^{\prime}_{+}(t).

which also holds with
\tilde{\tau}
in place of
\tau
. Using

\mathbb{E}[\tau]=\mathbb{E}[(\tau-0)_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\tau}-0)_{+}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\tau}]
,
the pointwise dominance
\mathbb{E}[(\tau-t)_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\tau}-t)_{+}]
for all
t\geq 0
, and the fact that

c^{\prime}_{+}(0)\geq 0
and
dc^{\prime}_{+}(t)\geq 0
, we conclude
\mathbb{E}[c(\tau)]\leq\mathbb{E}[c(\tilde{\tau})]
.∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
X
be a one-dimensional continuous local martingale taking values in
(0,1)
, with dynamics
dX_{s}=\sigma(s,X_{[0,s]})dB_{s}
, where
\sigma
is an adapted (possibly path-dependent) diffusion coefficient satisfying

0<\sigma_{\min}\leq\sigma(s,X_{[0,s]})\leq\sigma_{\max}<\infty

\tau
be a stopping time. Then there exists a
level-dependent

\bar{\Sigma}(y)

Proof.

By Gyöngy’s Markovian projection theorem (see
Gyöngy 1986
) there exists a
measurable function

\Sigma(s,y)\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sigma^{2}(s,X_{[0,s]})\,\big|\,X_{s}=y\right]}

such that the Markov process
\tilde{X}
solving

d\tilde{X}_{s}=\Sigma(s,\tilde{X}_{s})dB_{s}
satisfies
X_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{X}_{s}
for all

. Thus, the processes
X
and
\tilde{X}
have identical one-dimensional marginals, and, consequently,

R_{\tau\mid\sigma}(t)=R_{\tau\mid\Sigma}(t)
for all

Applying the occupation-time formula to the stopped process
\tilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau}
, we have

(\tau-t)_{+}=\int_{t}^{\tau}ds=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{t}^{\tau}\frac{1}{\Sigma^{2}(s,y)}\,dL_{s}^{y}(\tilde{X})\,dy,

where
L_{s}^{y}(\tilde{X})
denotes the local time of
\tilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau}
at level

Let

By the Feynman–Kac formula, for each fixed

\phi_{s}(p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p}[e^{-s\tau}]

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{h}-\mu_{l}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{2}\,\phi_{s}^{\prime\prime}(p)-s\phi_{s}(p)=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\phi_{s}(\underaccent{\bar}{P})=\phi_{s}(\bar{P})=1.

and
\bar{x}

\Delta_{\tilde{T}}(U,U^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}\left[V(X_{0}+W_{U})-V(X_{0}+W_{U^{\prime}})\right]-\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{T}(U)-\tilde{T}(U^{\prime})\right]\geq\Delta_{T}(U,U^{\prime}).

If
U
is optimal under
T
, then
\Delta_{T}(U,U^{\prime})\geq 0
for all
U^{\prime}\leq U
, which implies
\Delta_{\tilde{T}}(U,U^{\prime})\geq 0
for all
U^{\prime}\leq U
, proving optimality under
\tilde{T}
.
∎

Next, we recall our observation (made in the proof of Theorem
1
) that garbling slows the posterior’s quadratic variation. Recall our convention that
p_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})
denotes the full-information posterior and let
\hat{p}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mu=\mu_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
be the receiver’s posterior under an arbitrary (possibly time- and path-dependent) garbling
Y
, where
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}
for all
t
and
\hat{p}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]
. We also maintain our assumption that
p
is a continuous square-integrable martingale with absolutely continuous quadratic variation
d\langle p\rangle_{t}=\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})dt
for Borel

\Sigma_{0}\colon(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
.

Lemma 9
.

There exists an
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
-predictable process
\phi_{t}\in[0,1]
such that
d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}=\phi_{t}d\langle p\rangle_{t}=\phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})dt
for a.e.
t
.

Proof.

By construction,
\hat{p}
is the
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
-optional projection of
p
. Since
p
is a continuous square-integrable martingale, the Kunita-Watanabe decomposition applies: there exists a continuous
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
-martingale
\hat{p}
and a continuous martingale
N
orthogonal to every
(\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t})
-martingale (in particular, to
\hat{p}
) such that
p-p_{0}=(\hat{p}-p_{0})+N
. Taking quadratic variations yields
\langle p\rangle=\langle\hat{p}\rangle+\langle N\rangle
; hence,
d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}\leq d\langle p\rangle_{t}
.
As
d\langle p\rangle_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
dt
, the same is true for
d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}
, and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\phi_{t}\coloneqq\frac{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}}{d\langle p\rangle_{t}}
is well-defined
dt
-a.e. and satisfies
0\leq\phi_{t}\leq 1
.
∎

A consequence of this lemma is that conditional on the receiver’s current information (conditional on
\hat{p}_{t}
), the expected marginal calendar time per unit posterior quadratic variation is weakly larger under any garbling than under no garbling. To elaborate, we define the (instantaneous) quadratic-variation accumulation rates

\beta_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\frac{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}}{dt},\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{0,t}^{2}\coloneqq\Sigma_{0}^{2}(\hat{p}_{t}),

and the corresponding slowness processes (calendar time per unit of quadratic variation)

s_{t}\coloneqq\frac{dt}{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}}=\frac{1}{\beta_{t}^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad s_{t}^{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(\hat{p}_{t})}.

Corollary 3
.

We have
\frac{1}{\phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})}\geq\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})}

dt
-a.e. and
\mathbb{E}\left[s_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]\geq s_{t}^{0}
.

Proof.

Lemma
9
gives us
d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}=\phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})dt
, so that

\beta_{t}^{2}=\phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})
; and, thus,
s_{t}=1/(\phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t}))\geq 1/\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})
, since
\phi_{t}\leq 1
.
If
\Sigma_{0}(p)=Kp(1-p)
, then
p\mapsto 1/\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p)=K^{-2}p^{-2}(1-p)^{-2}
is convex on
(0,1)
, and Jensen’s inequality together with
\hat{p}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[p_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}]
yields the conditional expectation inequality.
∎

We now prove the proposition by showing that any equilibrium with nontrivial garbling admits a profitable deviation by the sender that will go unpunished.

Proof.

Let
\lambda>0
. Fix a purported equilibrium
(Y,\tau_{s},\tau_{r},a)
and let
\tau=\tau_{s}\wedge\tau_{r}
,
X_{t}=\hat{p}_{t\wedge\tau}
, and
U=\langle X\rangle_{\tau}
as above.

Consider the sender deviation in which she switches to no garbling and chooses stopping time

\tau_{s}^{\prime}\coloneqq T_{0}(U)=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\langle p\rangle_{t}\geq U\}
. Under no garbling, the receiver observes
p
and may stop at any stopping time
\tau_{r}^{\prime}
. The realized terminal time under the deviation is
\tau^{\prime}=\tau_{s}^{\prime}\wedge\tau_{r}^{\prime}
.

By DDS, there exists a Brownian motion
W
such that
X_{\tau}=X_{0}+W_{U}
.
Under no garbling, the full-information posterior satisfies
p_{t}=p_{0}+\widetilde{W}_{\langle p\rangle_{t}}
for some Brownian motion
\widetilde{W}
.
Coupling
\widetilde{W}
and
W
to be the same Brownian motion (on an enlarged probability space if necessary) yields

p_{\tau_{s}^{\prime}}=p_{0}+W_{\langle p\rangle_{\tau_{s}^{\prime}}}=p_{0}+W_{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{0}+W_{U}=X_{\tau};

viz.,
the deviation embeds the same posterior law
\eta=\mathcal{L}(X_{\tau})
at the sender stopping time
\tau_{s}^{\prime}
.

By (
A.12
) and the definition of
\tau_{s}^{\prime}=T_{0}(U)
, we have
\tau_{s}^{\prime}\leq T(U)\leq\tau
a.s., whence
\mathbb{E}[c(\tau_{s}^{\prime})]\leq\mathbb{E}[c(\tau)]
. for every increasing
c
, with strict inequality if
c
is strictly increasing and the equilibrium features nontrivial garbling (
\langle X\rangle_{t}<\langle p\rangle_{t}
on a set reached with positive probability before
\tau
). That is, for any “slow” equilibrium the sender can deviate to embed the same equilibrium measure in shorter time.

In the original equilibrium, the receiver did not stop strictly before
\tau
on-path. In information time, this means that among all information-time stopping rules
U^{\prime}\leq U
, stopping at
U
is optimal under the original clock
T(\cdot)
, because any deviation that induces an earlier realized stopping time corresponds to some
U^{\prime}\leq U
. Under the deviation, the relevant clock is
T_{0}(\cdot)
. To apply Lemma
8

(with
\tilde{T}=T_{0}
), it suffices to verify the incremental dominance condition (
8
),
namely that for every
U^{\prime}\leq U
,

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{0}(U)-T_{0}(U^{\prime})\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[T(U)-T(U^{\prime})\right].

Corollary
3
tells us that
dt
-a.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[s_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]\geq s_{t}^{0}
. Moreover, for
u\leq U
, we have
T(u)\leq\tau
and, hence,
X_{T(u)}=\hat{p}_{T(u)}=X_{0}+W_{u}
. As

\mathcal{G}_{u}
is the information-time filtration (so
\mathcal{G}_{u}=\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{T(u)}
), this implies

\mathbb{E}\left[s_{T(u)}\mid\mathcal{G}_{u}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[s_{T(u)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{T(u)}\right]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(\hat{p}_{T(u)})}=\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(X_{0}+W_{u})}\quad\text{for a.e.\ }u\leq U.

Now fix any stopping time
U^{\prime}\leq U
. By the change-of-variables identity
dt=s_{t}\,d\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}
,

T(U)-T(U^{\prime})=\int_{U^{\prime}}^{U}s_{T(u)}\,du=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{\prime}<u\leq U\}}\,s_{T(u)}\,du.

Taking expectations and using that
\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{\prime}<u\leq U\}}
is
\mathcal{G}_{u}
-measurable, Fubini and the
tower property yield

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[T(U)-T(U^{\prime})\right]&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{\prime}<u\leq U\}}\,\mathbb{E}[s_{T(u)}\mid\mathcal{G}_{u}]\right]du\\
&\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{U^{\prime}<u\leq U\}}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(X_{0}+W_{u})}\right]du=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{U^{\prime}}^{U}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(X_{0}+W_{u})}\,du\right].\end{split}

Under no garbling,
d\langle p\rangle_{t}=\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t})\,dt
, so
dt=(1/\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{t}))\,d\langle p\rangle_{t}
and, therefore,

T_{0}(U)-T_{0}(U^{\prime})=\int_{U^{\prime}}^{U}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(p_{T_{0}(u)})}\,du.

By the same DDS coupling used above,
p_{T_{0}(u)}=p_{0}+W_{u}=X_{0}+W_{u}
for
u\leq U
, thus,

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{0}(U)-T_{0}(U^{\prime})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{U^{\prime}}^{U}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{0}^{2}(X_{0}+W_{u})}\,du\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[T(U)-T(U^{\prime})\right],

which is exactly (
8
). Lemma
8
then implies that
U
remains optimal
for the receiver under the tighter clock
T_{0}
.
Therefore the receiver has a best response with
\tau_{r}^{\prime}\geq\tau_{s}^{\prime}
a.s., so the realized stopping time under the deviation is

\tau^{\prime}=\tau_{s}^{\prime}
and the embedded law is indeed
\eta
.

Since the deviation preserves the embedded posterior law (and so preserves the distribution of the receiver’s terminal action under optimal play) while weakly reducing
\mathbb{E}[c(\tau)]
(strictly if garbling is nontrivial), the sender’s payoff weakly increases (strictly under nontrivial garbling). This contradicts equilibrium. We conclude that no equilibrium can feature nontrivial garbling on the continuation path.

If
\lambda=0
, the proposition is implied by Corollary
2
.∎

Appendix B
Denseness of Laplace Transformable Costs

Define

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}\coloneqq\left\{c\colon\left[0,\infty\right)\to[0,\infty)\ \Big|\ c\text{ is increasing, convex,
differentiable, and finite on }[0,\infty)\right\},

and

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\left\{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\infty}\ \Big|\ \exists\ \delta>0:\int_{0}^{\infty}|c(t)|e^{-\delta t}dt<\infty\right\}.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\tau
be an a.s. finite stopping time. Then, for any
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
,
there exists a family of functions
\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}\in\mathcal{L}
with
T,\delta,\ \eta>0
) such that

\lim_{\eta\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|c(t)-\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(t)|=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\eta\to 0}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\big|c(\tau)-\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(\tau)\big|\right]=0.

Proof.

For
T>0
, define the truncation
c^{(T)}(t)\coloneqq c(t\wedge T),\quad t\geq 0
. Since
\tau<\infty
a.s., the tail beyond
T
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[|c(\tau)-c^{(T)}(\tau)|]\to 0\quad\text{as }T\to\infty.

Let
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a standard mollifier and define

\rho_{\delta}(t)\coloneqq\delta^{-1}\rho(t/\delta)
.
Extend
c^{(T)}
outside
[0,T]
by setting
c^{(T)}(t)=c(T)
for
t>T
.
Define the mollified function

c_{\delta}^{(T)}(t)\coloneqq(c^{(T)}*\rho_{\delta})(t),\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Then
c_{\delta}^{(T)}\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty))
, and
c_{\delta}^{(T)}\to c^{(T)}
uniformly on
[0,T]

as
\delta\to 0
, preserving convexity and monotonicity.

Define finally

\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(t)\coloneqq c_{\delta}^{(T)}(t)e^{-\eta t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

For any
\eta>0
,
\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}\in\mathcal{L}
, because

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(t)|e^{-st}dt<\infty\quad\text{for some }s>0.

For any stopping time
\tau<\infty
a.s.:

\mathbb{E}[|c(\tau)-\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(\tau)|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|c(\tau)-c^{(T)}(\tau)|]+\mathbb{E}[|c^{(T)}(\tau)-c_{\delta}^{(T)}(\tau)|]+\mathbb{E}[|c_{\delta}^{(T)}(\tau)-\tilde{c}^{(T,\delta,\eta)}(\tau)|].

The first term goes to
0
as
T\to\infty
.
The second term goes to
0
as
\delta\to 0
(uniform convergence on
[0,T]
).
The third term goes to
0
as
\eta\to 0
(exponential damping).

Taking limits in the order
T\to\infty
,
\delta\to 0
,
\eta\to 0
delivers the result.
∎
