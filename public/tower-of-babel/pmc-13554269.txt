Selective context, rather than persister cycling alone, drives resistance fixation in Escherichia coli
Authors: Garin Park, Hyein Kim, Sooyeon Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether persister cells contribute to the evolution of antibiotic resistance and, if so, under what selective conditions this occurs, remain unresolved. Here, we examined whether repeated persister cycling itself promotes resistance evolution and how persister-associated minor variants are retained, lost, or fixed under distinct selective contexts. We compared five Escherichia coli cellular states: mutation-induced cells (M), persister-Amp cycling cells (A), persister cycling cells without selection (R), stationary-phase cells (S), and NaCl-stored persisters (P), using state-resolved whole-genome sequencing and phenotypic assays. Persister cycling without selection, stationary-phase cells, and NaCl-stored persisters maintained baseline MICs and showed no detectable high-frequency variant fixation. In contrast, mutation-induced cells fixed efflux-regulatory mutations in marR, acrR, and acrB, increasing MIC to 32 μg/mL. Persister-Amp cycling cells showed an intermediate MIC increase to 16 μg/mL without detectable quality-filtered genetic fixation, distinguishing this state from mutation-induced resistance. The stfE/stfP prophage background further shaped adaptive routes under mutation-inducing conditions. Exploratory analysis revealed sub-threshold low-frequency variant signals during persister cycling, but lineage tracking showed that these variants were not stage-specifically fixed and were instead stochastically retained or lost. These findings support a model in which persister cycling can reveal low-frequency genetic heterogeneity, but fixed resistance evolution requires selection that promotes variant retention and clonal expansion. Thus, resistance fixation was governed primarily by selective context during regrowth rather than by the persister state itself.Subject terms: Evolution, Microbiology

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance remains a significant threat to global health1. Under antibiotic stress, bacteria deploy distinct survival strategies, including resistance, tolerance, and persistence. Resistance arises from heritable genetic changes that enable antibiotic degradation, target modification, production of alternative binding proteins, or altered regulation of efflux pumps2. By contrast, tolerance and persistence are primarily phenotypic survival strategies. Tolerant cells survive transient antibiotic exposure through reduced growth or metabolic activity, whereas persister cells represent a subpopulation that survives lethal stress without necessarily acquiring heritable resistance3,4. Although persisters have traditionally been viewed as dormant or metabolically inactive, persistence is now recognized as a heterogeneous physiological state that can involve dynamic metabolic activity and stress-dependent survival programs5,6. Following the consensus framework by Balaban et al.7, persistence can be classified into triggered persistence, induced by environmental cues such as starvation or antibiotic stress, and spontaneous persistence, which occurs stochastically during steady-state exponential growth.

A central unresolved question is whether persister cells merely survive antibiotic exposure or also contribute to the evolution of antibiotic resistance. Persisters are typically isogenic to the susceptible population and resume growth after stress removal without heritable resistance7. However, persistence levels can be modulated by specific mutations, such as hipA7, which increase the frequency of the persister phenotype without directly conferring fixed resistance8,9. In addition, several studies have proposed that persisters may facilitate resistance evolution by extending survival under selective pressure10–12. This prolonged survival may increase the opportunity for genetic change through stress-induced DNA repair13,14, oxidative damage15, selection of tolerance-associated variants16,17, or residual metabolic and transcriptional activity18,19. These findings suggest that persisters may provide a physiological state in which resistance-associated variation can arise or persist, but they do not fully explain whether such variants are retained, lost, or fixed during repeated persister formation and resuscitation.

Cryptic prophages provide another potential regulatory layer in this process. These replication-defective prophage elements are integrated into bacterial chromosomes and are increasingly recognized as modulators of bacterial physiology, including stress survival, biofilm formation, SOS signaling, and genome plasticity20,21. In Escherichia coli, the e14 prophage can undergo excision or recombination during antibiotic exposure, leading to genomic rearrangements or single-nucleotide polymorphisms that may facilitate adaptation22,23. Cryptic prophages have also been implicated in persistence-related physiology. In E. coli, the CP4-57-encoded alpA inhibits phosphate uptake and delays persister resuscitation, whereas Gifsy-1 activation in Salmonella triggers dormancy through tRNA cleavage24,25. These observations suggest context-dependent crosstalk among persistence, stress survival, resistance-associated adaptation, and cryptic prophage function.

Despite these insights, it remains unclear whether repeated persister cycling itself promotes the evolution of fixed resistance and how persister-associated minor variants are retained, lost, or fixed under different selective contexts. Here, we addressed this question by comparing five predefined E. coli cellular states that separate physiological state from selective context: mutation-induced cells, persister-Amp cycling cells, persister cycling cells without selection, stationary-phase cells, and NaCl-stored persisters. Using state-resolved whole-genome sequencing and phenotypic assays, we tracked both high-frequency fixed variants and sub-threshold low-frequency variant signals. We found that persister cycling without selection maintained baseline MIC and showed no high-frequency variant fixation, whereas mutation-induced selection fixed efflux-regulatory mutations and increased MIC. Persister-Amp cycling produced an intermediate MIC increase without detectable quality-filtered genetic fixation. We further found that the stfE/stfP prophage axis shaped adaptive routes under mutation-inducing conditions. Together, our results support a model in which persister cycling can reveal hidden minor-variant heterogeneity, but fixed resistance evolution requires selective pressure that drives variant retention and clonal expansion.

Results

Persister cycling without selection maintains baseline MIC

To determine whether repeated persister formation and resuscitation promote resistance evolution, we compared five cellular states: mutation-induced cells (M), persister-cycling cells without selection (R), persister-Amp cycling cells (A), stationary-phase cells (S), and NaCl-stored persisters (P) (Fig. 1a; Table 1). These conditions were designed to compare physiological survival without additional selection (R, P, and S), persister-derived regrowth under sub-MIC selection (A), and repeated mutation-induced selection (M). By comparing R and A, we evaluated whether repeated persister formation and resuscitation, or antibiotic selection during regrowth, are associated with resistance-related phenotypic outcomes.

Survival and large-colony frequency differed markedly across conditions. M showed stepwise increases in survival over three rounds (1.8 × 10³ → 4.2 × 10³ CFU/mL), with the large-colony fraction increasing from 4.0% to 9.0% (Fig. 1b; Table S1). A also showed increased survival (2.0 × 10¹ → 8.5 × 10² CFU/mL), with the large-colony fraction increasing from 33.33% to 41.94% (Fig. 1b; Table S1). In contrast, R showed no significant increase in survival across cycles (8.0 × 10¹ → 9.3 × 10¹ CFU/mL) or large-colony frequency (Fig. 1c; Table S1). NaCl-stored persisters and stationary-phase cells showed similar total colony counts on ampicillin plates (1.3 × 10³ ± 8.9 × 10¹ vs. 1.1 × 10³ ± 2.9 × 10¹ CFU/mL), but differed in large-colony fraction (1.1% vs. 8.1%) (Fig. 1c; Table S1). Large colonies on ampicillin agar were used as a phenotypic proxy for resistance-associated growth, defined here as the ability to grow above the MIC. This approach is consistent with the resistance classification framework in which MIC elevation serves as the primary criterion for distinguishing resistance from tolerance and persistence26. Large-colony formation on antibiotic plates has also been used as a readout of resistance acquisition in prior experimental evolution studies under repeated antibiotic selection27.

MIC outcomes also differed after three cycles. R maintained the baseline MIC of 8 μg/mL, whereas A showed a twofold MIC increase to 16 μg/mL. In M, colony-picked isolates reached 32 μg/mL, representing a fourfold increase relative to baseline (Table 1). This is consistent with prior studies indicating that persistence is a reversible, non-genetic state that does not confer heritable resistance upon resuscitation7,26.

To determine whether MIC changes occurred transiently during cycling, MIC was measured at each stage. R maintained an MIC of 8 μg/mL across P1, R1, R2, and R3, arguing against transient MIC elevation during persister cycling (Table S2b). A maintained an MIC of 8 μg/mL across P1, R1, and R2, but increased to 16 μg/mL at R3 (Table S2b). Stationary-phase cells and NaCl-stored persister cells also maintained MICs of 8 μg/mL at 0, 2, and 4 days, indicating no clear resistance acquisition during prolonged physiological arrest or storage (Table S2b). In M, the bulk culture MIC was 16 μg/mL, whereas single resistant colonies showed an MIC of 32 μg/mL, consistent with population heterogeneity and clonal enrichment under repeated selection (Table 1; Table S2a).

To further evaluate antibiotic susceptibility at the phenotypic level, cells from each group were collected at each stage, resuscitated in fresh medium to an OD600 of 0.8, and then treated with ampicillin to generate time-kill curves (Figure. S1). R showed a killing profile comparable to exponential-phase cells, supporting maintained baseline susceptibility. A showed an increased MIC relative to R, but its time-kill profile remained comparable to that of exponential-phase cells. By contrast, M showed slower killing and higher residual OD600, consistent with its increased MIC outcome. Stationary-phase cells showed a killing slope comparable to exponential-phase cells, making GASP-associated resistance unlikely under these conditions. NaCl-stored persister cells showed a delayed killing profile, likely reflecting asynchronous resuscitation kinetics; the biological basis of these physiological states is discussed further in the Discussion (Table S2b; Figure. S1).

Overall, persister cycling without selection maintained baseline MIC and antibiotic susceptibility, whereas sub-MIC Amp exposure during persister-derived regrowth was associated with an MIC increase to 16 μg/mL, and repeated mutation-induced selection was associated with an MIC increase to 32 μg/mL. Thus, R, A, and M showed distinct phenotypic outcomes: maintained susceptibility, MIC elevation during persister-derived regrowth, and stronger MIC elevation under mutation-induced selection, respectively (Fig. 1b, c; Table 1).

Quality-filtered WGS detects fixed variants only under mutation-induced selection

To determine whether the phenotypic differences described above were associated with heritable genomic changes, we performed WGS of the five treated states together with exponential-phase controls. Variants were called using a diploid variant-calling model and then analyzed after quality-based filtering (QD ≥ 2, FS < 60, SOR < 3). Fixed variants (AF = 1) were detected only in mutation-induced cells (M), including variants in acrB, acrR, marR, and xdhD (Fig. 2a–c). Because marR and acrR repress MarA and the AcrAB-TolC efflux system, frameshifts in these regulators likely derepress the efflux system, thereby enhancing antibiotic resistance28,29, consistent with the MIC increase observed in M. A single dosC mutation (AF = 1) was detected in one exponential-phase replicate, consistent with a background variant related to c-di-GMP signaling30 (Fig. 2a, d). After quality filtering, no fixed or high-frequency variants were detected in R, A, P, or S.

No variants passed the quality-filtering thresholds in persister-cycling cells (R) or persister-Amp cycling cells (A) (Fig. 2a). Notably, although A showed an MIC increase to 16 μg/mL, no detectable quality-filtered genetic fixation was observed. Thus, the MIC increase in A was not accompanied by detectable quality-filtered genetic fixation, distinguishing it from the fixed resistance observed in M. The absence of quality-filtered variants in R was consistent with the baseline MIC and antibiotic susceptibility profile observed in the phenotypic analyses above. This pattern is consistent with prior reports showing that persister cycling without selection does not generate heritable mutations31,32.

Among quality-filtered minor variants (AF < 0.5), stfE and BW25113_RS25865 were detected in stress-exposed states (M, P, and S), but not in E, R, or A (Fig. 2a, b). In exponential-phase cells, only a single pbpC variant was detected, distinguishing this profile from the stfE and BW25113_RS25865 variant pattern observed in stress-exposed states. This stress-associated insertion pattern is examined further in the following section.

Thus, the quality-filtered variant profiles differed by cellular state. Fixed, function-altering mutations were detected in M, stress-associated minor variants were detected in M, P, and S, and no variants passed the quality-filtering thresholds in R or A (Fig. 2a, b). These results show that mutation-induced selection was associated with fixed resistance-associated variants, whereas persister cycling and persister-Amp cycling showed no detectable genetic fixation by quality-filtered WGS.

Prophage-linked insertions are associated with stress-exposed states

Quality-filtered variants were annotated using SnpEff (v4.3t)33 and mapped to functional classes using EcoCyc, EggNOG, and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database34–36. Because no quality-filtered variants were detected in persister-cycling cells (R) or persister-Amp cycling cells (A), these two groups were not included in the functional-category analysis (Fig. 2e). The remaining groups showed state-dependent functional profiles.

Mutation-induced cells (M) showed proportions of 29.31% for energy metabolism-related genes, 5.19% for antibiotic resistance-related genes, and 21.82% for prophage-related functions (Fig. 2e; Table S3). NaCl-stored persisters (P) showed 29.73% energy/metabolism-associated genes and 13.41% prophage-related functions, with no detectable resistance-related genes. Stationary-phase cells (S) showed the highest proportion of prophage-associated variants among the analyzed groups (32.35%). In exponential-phase cells (E), the resistance-related category signal was attributable to the single dosC variant detected in this group (Fig. 2e; Table S3).

WGS identified prophage-linked insertional events at two loci. First, a stfP-derived fragment was inserted into stfE, resulting in frameshift variants; two independent insertion variants were detected in a subset of M, P, and S samples (Fig. 3a). In contrast, these variants were not detected in E, R, or A in the quality-filtered variant set. However, in the exploratory unfiltered analysis, sub-threshold stfE variant calls were also observed in R. These calls did not pass the quality-filtering thresholds and were therefore excluded from the quality-filtered variant set; these sub-threshold signals are analyzed separately in the section on low-frequency variant dynamics. Second, prophage-derived fragments from DLP12 and CP4-6 were inserted into BW25113_RS25865. Three independent insertion variants were detected in a subset of M, P, and S samples and were likewise absent from E, R, and A in the quality-filtered variant set (Fig. 3b).

The stfE/stfP background alters survival, MIC outcomes, and mutational trajectories under mutation-induced selection

To evaluate whether the stfE/stfP prophage background influences resistance-associated outcomes, we compared mutation-induced and persister-cycling cells in WT, ΔstfE, and ΔstfP backgrounds. Total survival and large-colony counts were measured on LB agar containing 8 μg/mL Amp (Fig. 4; Table S4).

Under mutation-induced selection, WT survival increased progressively across cycles (3.4 × 10² → 1.5 × 10³ CFU/mL), accompanied by increased large-colony counts (Fig. 4; Table S4a). ΔstfE showed lower initial survival than WT (4.5 × 10¹ CFU/mL; 13% of WT at stage 0), but survival increased after repeated sub-MIC Amp exposure (7.0 × 10¹ → 2.2 × 10² CFU/mL) (Fig. 4; Table S4b). ΔstfP showed higher initial survival than ΔstfE (4.2 × 10² CFU/mL; comparable to the WT level at stage 0), but survival declined across cycles to 7.3 × 10¹ CFU/mL (Fig. 4; Table S4c).

In persister-cycling conditions, ΔstfE and ΔstfP formed persisters at levels comparable to WT (3.9 × 10⁸ ± 7.1 × 10⁶ and 4.1 × 10⁸ ± 5.9 × 10⁷ vs. WT 5.2 × 10⁸ ± 1.2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) (Table S4d). However, survival after resuscitation and Amp challenge was reduced in both deletion backgrounds. ΔstfE decreased from 3.0 × 10¹ to 0 CFU/mL, with no large colonies detected. ΔstfP also showed reduced survival (5.7 × 10¹ → 1.0 × 10¹ CFU/mL), without large-colony formation. In contrast, WT maintained detectable survival across cycles (6.5 × 10¹ → 1.5 × 10¹ CFU/mL), with large colonies observed at low frequency (Fig. 4; Table S4a–c).

MIC outcomes further distinguished the genotypes. In exponential-phase and persister-cycling states, WT, ΔstfE, and ΔstfP all maintained an MIC of 8 μg/mL (Table S2a). Under mutation-induced selection, WT reached 32 μg/mL, whereas ΔstfE and ΔstfP reached 16 μg/mL (Table S2a). Plasmid complementation of stfE or stfP restored survival and large-colony formation, whereas MIC remained at 16 μg/mL (Fig. S2; Table S2a, Table S4e–i).

These results indicate that deletion of stfE or stfP alters survival after resuscitation and Amp challenge, as well as MIC outcomes under mutation-induced selection.

In WT mutation-induced cells, fixed variants included frameshifts in marR and acrR, a missense mutation in acrB; a minor mutation corresponding to the stfP→stfE insertion identified in the analysis of prophage-linked insertions was also detected (Fig. 2c and Fig. 3). In ΔstfE, a marR frameshift was detected as the major fixed mutation in two of three colonies, whereas acrR, acrB, and stfP→stfE events were not detected (Fig. 5a, b, d). In ΔstfP, fixed variants included a cyoB stop-gain mutation, an rpoD missense mutation, and intergenic variants between kgtP–rrfG and BW25113_RS22180–BW25113_RS23025; these variants were not detected in WT or ΔstfE (Fig. 5a, c, d).

Functional profiles of variant-affected genes also differed by genotype. WT was enriched for energy metabolism (29.31%), prophage-associated functions (21.82%), and antibiotic resistance-related functions (5.19%). ΔstfE showed enrichment in stress response (25.16%) and energy metabolism (34.97%), with low prophage representation (0.98%). ΔstfP showed the highest proportions of cell growth-related functions (33.33%) and energy metabolism (38.24%), with lower prophage-associated (2.94%) and antibiotic resistance-related (1.96%) representation (Fig. 5e; Table S5).

Taken together, WT, ΔstfE, and ΔstfP followed distinct mutational trajectories under mutation-induced selection, with limited overlap in fixed variants. The efflux-associated route observed in WT was not detected in either deletion background. These findings indicate that the stfE/stfP prophage background shapes adaptive route selection under mutation-induced selection, with WT favoring an efflux-associated trajectory and ΔstfE and ΔstfP following alternative routes.

Persister-associated low-frequency variants are stochastically retained or lost during cycling

The MIC and quality-filtered WGS analyses showed that no variants passed quality-filtering thresholds in persister-cycling cells. To examine whether low-frequency variant signals were present below the filtering threshold and whether such variants were fixed in a stage-specific manner during persister formation and resuscitation, we performed an exploratory analysis using unfiltered variant calls and single-colony lineage tracking.

In the exploratory analysis without quality filtering, low-frequency variant signals were detected across multiple cellular states; group-level details are provided in Fig. S3a. Variants in yfgM, yjfM, pcnB, and rlmF were detected across M, P, S, E, and R. This pattern suggests that these variant signals were not specific to persister cycling, but instead represented low-frequency signals observed across multiple cellular states. Group-associated variants included sbcB in R, nrfA and nrfB in A, and stfP in S (Fig. S3a).

Because these variants did not pass the quality-filtering thresholds, they were treated as exploratory sub-threshold signals rather than quality-filtered evidence of genetic fixation.

To explore the functional relevance of genes carrying recurrent or commonly detected sub-threshold variants, we characterized deletion mutants for growth, antibiotic response, and resuscitation kinetics (Fig. S3b). The tested mutants included ΔyfgM, ΔyjfM, ΔypdE, ΔrlmF, ΔydjY, and ΔpcnB. These mutants were selected because the corresponding genes were recurrently detected as sub-threshold variant signals across multiple cellular states and represented known or putative functions related to stress survival or regrowth. These functions included Sec-associated protein translocation and periplasmic folding (yfgM), predicted envelope-stress adaptation (yjfM), predicted c-di-GMP phosphodiesterase activity (ypdE), 23S rRNA modification and ribosome assembly (rlmF), poorly characterized inner-membrane function (ydjY), and poly(A)-dependent RNA turnover (pcnB).

In growth assays, ΔypdE, ΔyfgM, ΔpcnB, ΔrlmF, and ΔydjY grew faster than WT in both LB and M9 media (Fig. S3b). Under ampicillin stress (100 μg/mL), ΔyfgM, ΔyjfM, ΔydjY, ΔrlmF, and ΔypdE showed slower OD600 decline than WT (Fig. S3b). During resuscitation, ΔyfgM, ΔyjfM, ΔypdE, ΔmetE, and ΔstfE resumed growth comparably to WT (Fig. S3b). In repeated cycling, variants consistently detected after resuscitation were limited to yfgM, yjfM, and stfE, whereas other variant signals decreased below detection (Fig. S3a).

These results indicate that several sub-threshold variant-target genes are associated with measurable growth, antibiotic-response, or resuscitation phenotypes. However, these deletion-mutant phenotypes do not by themselves indicate genetic fixation during persister cycling.

To determine whether sub-threshold variants observed in the exploratory analysis accumulated in a stage-specific manner during persister formation and resuscitation, we performed serial single-colony lineage tracking across P1 → R1 → P2 → R2 (Fig. S4a). Two independent lineages were initiated from single colonies, and two single-colony isolates were sequenced at each stage.

No consistent stage-specific bias in variant emergence or loss was observed (Fig. S4b). fimA and glnH were maintained across all stages, whereas ykgH, aceK, and smrB appeared and disappeared across stages without a consistent persister- or resuscitation-specific pattern (Fig. S4b). Thus, the variants observed in single-colony lineages were maintained or lost without evidence of stage-specific fixation. This stochastic pattern is consistent with population bottleneck effects and outgrowth competition during resuscitation-phase regrowth37,38.

Overall, the exploratory analysis revealed sub-threshold low-frequency variant signals during persister cycling, and several variant-target genes showed altered phenotypes in deletion-mutant assays. However, single-colony lineage tracking did not support stage-specific fixation of these variants during persister cycling. Together, these results indicate that persister cycling maintains phenotypic susceptibility and lacks detectable quality-filtered fixation, while sub-threshold variants show stochastic rather than stage-driven dynamics. In contrast, mutation-induced selection was associated with fixed resistance-associated variants and MIC elevation, with the stfE/stfP prophage background shaping which adaptive routes emerged under selection.

Discussion

Persister cells have generally been understood as antibiotic-surviving cells that retain genetic susceptibility after antibiotic removal. Although persisters were historically described as non-dividing dormant survivors, recent studies indicate that persistence can involve dynamic physiological activity and heterogeneous survival states rather than strict dormancy5,6. Several studies have suggested links between persistence and the evolution of resistance11,27,39. However, whether repeated persister cycling itself promotes the evolution of fixed resistance, or whether selective context during regrowth drives variant retention and fixation, remains unclear.

To address this question, we compared five cellular states that differed in physiological state and selective context (Table 1). Mutation-induced cells (M) carried fixed resistance-associated variants in acrB, acrR, marR, and xdhD, with MIC increasing to 32 μg/mL. This is consistent with AcrAB-TolC efflux derepression through loss of marR/acrR function28. In contrast, persister cycling without selection (R) maintained an MIC of 8 μg/mL and showed no detectable genetic fixation by quality-filtered WGS. Stage-wise MIC measurements across P1, R1, R2, and R3 excluded transient MIC elevation during cycling, and time-kill curves confirmed baseline antibiotic susceptibility phenotypically (Table S2b; Fig. S1). Persister-Amp cycling (A) showed an MIC increase to 16 μg/mL, but this was not accompanied by detectable quality-filtered genetic fixation, distinguishing it from the fixed resistance observed in M. Therefore, the MIC increase in A should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect non-fixed adaptation, heteroresistance, or variation below the WGS detection threshold40. These findings indicate that adaptive outcomes in persister-derived populations are closely linked to selective context during regrowth rather than to the persister state alone.

To understand the meaning of low-frequency variant signals observed during persister cycling, we performed an exploratory analysis of variants below the quality-filtering threshold. Several recurrent sub-threshold variant-target genes mapped to cellular processes relevant to stress survival and regrowth. For example, yfgM and yjfM may be linked to membrane-associated or poorly characterized envelope-related functions, rlmF is involved in ribosome modification, and pcnB is involved in RNA turnover through poly(A)-dependent regulation of mRNA stability34,41–43. Consistent with these functional contexts, deletion mutants of variant-target genes showed altered growth, antibiotic-response, or resuscitation phenotypes (Fig. S3b). However, single-colony lineage tracking revealed no consistent stage-specific bias in variant emergence or loss, with variants being retained or lost across lineages and stages (Fig. S4). Thus, although these minor variants may have functional relevance, they are not automatic precursors of fixed resistance during persister cycling.

Based on these results, we propose that resuscitation-phase population dynamics may shape the fate of persister-associated minor variants. Population bottlenecks and outgrowth competition during resuscitation may contribute to stochastic retention or loss of low-frequency variants, limiting reproducible fixation in the absence of antibiotic selection37,38. However, because only a limited number of single-colony isolates were analyzed at each stage, the persistence or loss of rare variants may have been under- or over-estimated44. For stationary-phase cells (S) and NaCl-stored persisters (P), stage-wise MIC measurements and time-kill curves provided evidence against detectable resistance acquisition during prolonged physiological arrest or storage (Fig. S1; Table S2b).

Prophage-linked insertion events were also observed in stress-exposed states. A stfP-derived fragment inserted into stfE, and DLP12/CP4-6-derived fragments inserted into BW25113_RS25865, were detected as quality-filtered minor variants in M, P, and S, but not in E, R, or A (Fig. 3a, b). stfE and stfP reside within the e14 cryptic prophage, which has been associated with SOS-induced excision, stress resistance, and biofilm formation in E. coli K-1220,45. Under mutation-induced selection, WT followed an efflux-associated trajectory involving marR, acrR, and acrB, reaching an MIC of 32 μg/mL; this route is consistent with the established role of AcrAB-TolC in resistance, whose biased partitioning during cell division can sustain long-lived phenotypic heterogeneity in efflux activity, and whose transcriptional regulation via the mar operon can be maintained as an inducible memory across lineages under repeated selection28,29,46. By contrast, ΔstfE and ΔstfP followed distinct trajectories and reached MICs of 16 μg/mL. Complementation restored survival and large-colony formation but did not increase the MIC beyond 16 μg/mL, suggesting that the complementation effect was more closely associated with survival or tolerance-associated phenotypes than with resistance gain26. The mechanistic basis for these divergent trajectories remains to be determined, but may be related to the location of stfE and stfP within the e14 prophage network45. In WT, the stfP→stfE insertion may contribute to a prophage-linked background in which the efflux-associated marR/acrR/acrB route is favored28,29,46. In contrast, ΔstfE and ΔstfP lacked this insertional event and followed alternative routes, including a more limited marR-centered trajectory in ΔstfE and bioenergetic/transcriptional variants such as cyoB and rpoD in ΔstfP. Thus, the stfE/stfP background may influence adaptive route accessibility under mutation-induced selection, rather than acting as a direct resistance determinant.

Collectively, this study shows that persister-associated minor variants are not automatic precursors of resistance; rather, their retention and fixation depend on selective context during regrowth and population dynamics during resuscitation. Persister cycling without selection maintained phenotypic susceptibility and showed no detectable genetic fixation by quality-filtered WGS, whereas sub-threshold variants showed stochastic retention or loss rather than stage-specific fixation. In contrast, mutation-induced selection was associated with fixed resistance-associated variants and MIC elevation, and the stfE/stfP prophage background shaped adaptive routes under selection. This study is limited by the use of a single antibiotic and strain, a limited number of cycling rounds, an in vitro setting, and limited single-colony sampling. Future work using broader antibiotic classes, diverse strain backgrounds, longer cycling experiments, and in vivo models will be needed to test the generality of this framework.

Methods

Bacteria and growth conditions

Escherichia coli BW25113 was cultured in LB broth47 at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The mutant strains used in this study were obtained from the Keio and ASKA collection48,49. The bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table S6.

Mutation-induced cells

Given that E. coli BW25113 WT cells exhibited robust growth at 4 μg/mL and had an MIC of 8 μg/mL against ampicillin (Amp) (Table S2), an intermediate concentration of Amp (5 µg/mL) was applied to induce mutations50,51. Exponential-phase cells (9 × 108 CFU/mL) were treated with a sub-MIC concentration of Amp (5 μg /mL), followed by incubation for 30 min (one doubling time) to induce mutations. The Amp-exposed cells (1.1 × 109 CFU/mL at the first stage) were then washed twice with 0.85% NaCl to remove residual Amp, resuspended in fresh LB, and the turbidity Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was adjusted to 0.8 (same as exponential phase). Sub-MIC of Amp was treated for 30 min again. This process was repeated three times using six biological replicates, and the total stages are shown in Fig. 1a. Viable cell counts at each stage were determined by plating serial dilutions on antibiotic-free LB agar and are provided in Table S7. In the mutation induction period, 100 µL of the cultures (approximately 1.1 × 108 CFU/mL at the first stage) were plated onto LB agar supplemented with 8 µg/mL Amp (corresponding to the MIC) and were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h to allow for the growth of resistant colonies.

Large colonies were defined as colonies with a diameter > 2.5 mm and were counted as mutation-induced colonies26,27. At the final stage of Amp exposure, one large colony from each of three independent parallel cultures was selected and transferred onto LB agar plates containing a higher Amp concentration (10 µg/mL) to verify growth under increased Amp exposure. Colonies that successfully grew on these plates were subsequently cultured in fresh antibiotic-free LB medium and used for further analyses, including MIC assays and DNA extraction for WGS.

Persister-cycling cells

Persister cells were converted from exponential-phase cells (5.3 × 108 CFU/mL) by adding rifampicin (Rif; 100 µg/mL) for 30 min, centrifuging, and treating with Amp (100 µg/mL) for 3 h to remove non-persister cells52. This method of Rif-induced persister formation has been validated across multiple independent laboratories, supporting its reproducibility and reliability53,54.

Persister cells (1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL at the first stage) were washed twice with 0.85% NaCl to remove residual Amp and resuscitated by resuspending in fresh LB medium and incubating at 37 °C with shaking until the culture reached an OD600 of 0.8. The formation and resuscitation of persister cells were repeated three times, and plating was performed using six biological replicates (Fig. 1a). Viable cell counts at each stage were determined by plating serial dilutions on antibiotic-free LB agar and are provided in Table S7. From each repetition, 100 µL of cells (approximately 6.9 × 107 CFU/mL for the persister-cycling cells of the last stage) were spread on LB agar containing 8 µg/mL Amp, and large-sized colonies (diameter > 2.5 mm) were counted. Two independent bulk cultures of cells resuscitated at the third stage were used for further analyses as persister-cycling cells.

Persister-Amp cycling cells

For persister-Amp cycling cells, persister cells were converted from exponential-phase cells and resuscitated to an OD600 of 0.8 using the same procedure described above. The resuscitated persister cells were then treated with Amp (5 µg/mL) for 30 min under the same conditions used for mutation induction. After washing twice with 0.85% NaCl, the cells were converted to persister cells again by treatment with Rif and Amp. The cycle of persister formation, resuscitation, and Amp treatment (5 µg/mL) was repeated three times, and plating was performed using three biological replicates. Viable cell counts at each stage were determined by plating serial dilutions on antibiotic-free LB agar and are provided in Table S7. As a result, 2.00 × 10⁸ CFU/mL of persister-Amp cycling cells were obtained at the final stage (Fig. 1a). From each repetition, 100 µL of cells (approximately 2.0 × 107 CFU/mL for the persister cells of the first stage) were spread on LB agar containing 8 µg/mL Amp, and large-sized colonies (diameter > 2.5 mm) were counted. Three independent bulk cultures of cells resuscitated and exposed to Amp at the third stage were used for further analyses as persister-Amp cycling cells.

Stationary-phase cells and NaCl-stored persister cells

Started at exponential phase (7.3 × 108 CFU/mL), cells were incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 4 days to reach stationary phase, and stationary-phase cells (1.7 × 107 CFU/mL) were harvested (Fig. 1a). At the same time, persister cells (2.8 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) were generated as described above, stored in 0.85% NaCl at 37 °C with shaking for 4 days, and NaCl-stored persister cells (1.3 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) were harvested. To confirm that GASP-associated resistance enrichment did not occur during this period, MIC was measured at days 0, 2, and 4, and time-kill curves were performed on day-4 cells (Table S2b; Fig. S1). Plating was performed using four biological replicates. Viable cell counts during the 4-day incubation or storage period were determined by plating serial dilutions on antibiotic-free LB agar and are provided in Table S7. Each 100 µL of these cultures (1.7 × 10⁶ CFU/mL for stationary-phase cells and 1.3 × 10⁶ CFU/mL for NaCl-stored persister cells) was plated on LB agar containing 8 µg/mL Amp, and large-sized colonies (diameter > 2.5 mm) were counted. Two independent bulk cultures of stationary-phase cells and three independent bulk cultures of NaCl-stored persister cells were used for further analyses.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay

To determine the MIC of Amp, exponential-phase cells (7.0 × 108 CFU/mL) were diluted (1:1000) with LB broth containing different concentrations of Amp and grown for 24 h. In the case of cells in different physiological states, the inoculum was adjusted to the same OD600 with exponential-phase cells. The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration that prevented an increase in growth, as evidenced by the lack of change in turbidity55.

Ampicillin susceptibility assay

Cells in different physiological states were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.8 (approximately 10⁸ CFU/mL). Each culture was then inoculated into 96-well plates at 1% (v/v) in LB medium. After incubation until reaching the exponential phase, Amp (100 µg/mL) was added. Growth and Death was monitored at regular time intervals throughout the experiment by measuring OD600 using BioTek Synergy HTX microplate reader.

Growth and survival assay of specific gene-deletion mutants

WT and mutant strains obtained from the KEIO library48 were cultured in LB or LB supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/mL) until reaching the exponential phase (OD600 reaches 0.8). For growth analysis, exponential-phase WT and mutant cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at 1% (v/v) in LB or M9 minimal medium (42 mM Na₂HPO₄, 22 mM KH₂PO₄, 8.6 mM NaCl, 18.7 mM NH₄Cl) supplemented with 0.4% (w/v) glucose. For ampicillin killing analysis, exponential-phase cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in LB or M9/0.4% glucose media containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin. For resuscitation analysis, natural persisters (ampicillin-induced persisters)31,56 were generated by treating cells with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) for 3 h, followed by washing twice with 0.85% NaCl. The OD600 of natural persisters adjusted to the same OD600 with exponential phase and inoculated into 96-well plates at 1% (v/v) in LB or M9/0.4% glucose media. Cell growth, death, and resuscitation was monitored by measuring OD600 over time using a BioTek Synergy HTX microplate reader.

Single-colony tracking of persister cycling and resuscitation

Two independent exponential-phase cultures (8.1 × 108 CFU/mL) were harvested and subjected to Rif (100 µg/mL, 30 min) followed by Amp (100 µg/mL, 3 h) to eliminate non-persister cells. Cells were washed twice with 0.85% NaCl, diluted to 10−3 (8.1 × 105 CFU/mL), plated on LB agar, and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h to resuscitate. From each plate, two single colonies were randomly picked and grown in LB to 7.33 × 108 CFU/mL; these constituted the first-cycle resuscitated persister cultures. The same Rif-Amp treatment, washing, dilution, plating, and resuscitation steps were repeated once more to generate second-cycle persister and resuscitated cultures. Genomic DNA was extracted from two independent bulk persister preparations and from two independent resuscitated cultures (each represented by two replicate single colonies). The corresponding procedures are shown in Fig. S4a.

DNA preparation

All DNA was extracted using an Aprep Total DNA Mini kit (APBio, Korea), and more than two independent replicates were performed. DNA samples were quantified using a BioTek Synergy HTX TAKE3 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

WGS

The quality and quantity of purified DNA were assessed using fluorometry (Qubit; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and gel electrophoresis. Briefly, 100 ng of genomic DNA from each sample was fragmented through acoustic shearing using a Qsonica 800 R2 instrument (Qsonica LLC, Newtown, CT, USA). Fragments of 350 bp were ligated to Illumina adapters and then amplified using PCR. An appropriate size for the final library is 500–600 bp. Libraries were quantified using a TapeStation 4200 instrument (Agilent Technologies) and a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (KK4824; Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). Pair-end libraries of 150 bp were generated and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Reads were mapped to the E. coli BW25113 reference genome (NZ_CP009273.1) using BWA 0.7.1757. Duplicate reads were removed using GATK Mark Duplicates58. The raw fastq sequence was deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (accession no: PRJNA1092394).

Variant calling and analysis

Variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller58 and annotated using SnpEff33. Variant allele frequency (AF) was calculated for all called variants based on allele depth. Variant calls were then filtered according to GATK hard-filtering recommendations with minor modifications: QD ≥ 2, FS < 60, SOR < 3 for SNPs, and SOR < 10 for indels58. Filtered variants with AF = 1 were defined as fixed mutations, whereas filtered variants with AF ≤ 0.5 were defined as minor variants. Unfiltered variants with AF ≤ 0.5 were not used to define fixed mutations but were considered as exploratory low-confidence minor signals. Gene functions were annotated using EcoCyc34 and eggNOG35, antibiotic-resistance genes were identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database36, and tolerance-associated genes were classified based on previously reported tolerance mechanisms27.

Statistical analysis

All data are the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in GraphPad Prism software version 10.6.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). a represents a significant difference between viable cell counts with exponential cells and mutation-induced cells or persister cycling cells (P < 0.05). b represents significant differences between viable cell counts of WT and mutants (P < 0.05).
