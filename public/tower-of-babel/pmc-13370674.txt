Suppression of neuronal p27 impairs cognitive function in middle-aged mice
Authors: Noelle I. Nicol, Jinal V. Patel, Hannah M. Jester, Nathaniel A. Swift, Xin Wang, Nicole Wu, Xueyan Zhou, Yankai Zhang, Tao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accumulating evidence indicates an important role of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) in regulating neuronal function under physiological and pathological conditions. p27Kip1 (p27) is a member of the Cip/Kip family of CDK inhibitors (CKIs) with emerging functions in neuron development, but its role in cognitive aging is not well defined. Here we generated a transgenic mouse model with p27 conditionally (post-developmental) suppressed in forebrain neurons. We found that suppression of neuronal p27 did not affect overall cognitive function in young (3–6 months old) mice, as assessed by novel object recognition and Morris water maze tasks. In comparison, middle-aged (10–12 months old) homozygous p27 mutant mice (p27−/−) showed impairment of cognitive function. Imaging studies further revealed increased dendritic spine density driven by elevated immature spine forms and reduced overall spine maturity in the aged p27−/− mice. In addition, protein synthesis, postsynaptic density and mitochondrial ultrastructure remained unchanged with suppression of p27. These data indicate p27 as a key regulator of dendritic spine development and cognitive resilience during aging, suggesting a role of neuronal p27 in maintaining synaptic integrity and cognitive function.

Introduction

Cell-cycle regulatory proteins play essential roles in controlling proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Among these regulators, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) drive progression through the distinct phases of the cell cycle, while CDK inhibitors (CKIs) restrict CDK activity to maintain proper timing of cell-cycle transitions(Besson et al., 2008; Cerqueira et al., 2014; Pellarin et al., 2025; Vermeulen et al., 2003; Wang, 2021). Although these proteins were initially characterized in dividing cells, accumulating evidence indicates non-canonical functions in post-mitotic neurons, where they influence cytoskeletal organization, synaptic signaling, and plasticity(Barnett and Bibb, 2010; Besson et al., 2008; Pellarin et al., 2025; Sharma and Pledger, 2016).

p27Kip1 (p27) is a member of the Cip/Kip family of CKIs encoded by Cdkn1b(Besson et al., 2008; Cerqueira et al., 2014). Canonically, p27 binds and inhibits cyclin E/CDK2 and cyclin A/CDK2 complexes to block G1/S transition and prevent premature DNA replication(Besson et al., 2008; Cerqueira et al., 2014; Pellarin et al., 2025; Vermeulen et al., 2003). p27 levels are tightly regulated through phosphorylation and ubiquitin-mediated degradation, which integrate extracellular signals such as growth factor stimulation through PI3K/Akt pathways (Csergeová et al., 2024; Montagnoli et al., 1999; Motti et al., 2005; Motti et al., 2004; Pellarin et al., 2025; Sharma and Pledger, 2016; Tsytlonok et al., 2019). Akt-dependent phosphorylation at Thr157 or Thr198 leads to cytoplasmic sequestration of p27, diminishing its nuclear CDK-inhibitory activity(Fujita et al., 2002; Motti et al., 2005; Motti et al., 2004; Sharma et al., 2015; Shin et al., 2002). In the cytoplasm, p27 participates in additional processes distinct from its cell-cycle role, including neuronal migration, cytoskeletal dynamics, and synaptic organization(Abbastabar et al., 2018; Besson et al., 2008; Kawauchi et al., 2006; Kawauchi and Nabeshima, 2019; Kim et al., 2009; Nguyen et al., 2006; Nishimura et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2015). For example, p27 can interact with RhoA and cofilin to modulate actin remodeling, suggesting potential links to dendritic spine formation and stability(Besson et al., 2004; Jaiswal and Sharma, 2017; Jiao et al., 2022; Sharma and Pledger, 2016; Yang et al., 1998).

Upstream signaling cascades that regulate p27 are also critical for neuronal function. PI3K/Akt and Ras/ERK pathways both influence p27 expression and localization, while downstream targets such as mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase (eEF2K) connect to protein synthesis and synaptic plasticity(Besson et al., 2008; Jester et al., 2025; Kruiswijk et al., 2012; Motti et al., 2005; Motti et al., 2004; Pellarin et al., 2025; Proud, 2015; Wang, 2021). Protein translation, particularly elongation regulated by eEF2 and eEF2K, is a well-established mechanism underlying long-term potentiation (L-LTP) and memory consolidation (Bevilaqua and Cammarota, 2018; Heise et al., 2014; Ma, 2021; Taha et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). CDK2 and CDK1 have been shown to phosphorylate eEF2K at distinct sites, influencing translational output, and given that p27 can inhibit both CDK2 and CDK1, it may indirectly affect local protein synthesis at synapses(Hizli et al., 2013; Smith and Proud, 2008). Together, these pathways suggest that p27 could modulate neuronal structure and function through both cytoskeletal and translational control mechanisms.

In the developing brain, p27 limits neural progenitor proliferation and helps establish proper cortical layering(Goto et al., 2004; Nguyen et al., 2006; Qiu et al., 2009; Sharma and Pledger, 2016). In adulthood, p27 expression persists in post-mitotic neurons and glia, where its role is less defined(Hörster et al., 2017; Qiu et al., 2009; Sharma and Pledger, 2016). Altered p27 levels have been associated with neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions, including Alzheimer's disease and Down syndrome, implicating dysregulation of cell-cycle-related proteins in cognitive decline(García-Osta et al., 2022; Ogawa et al., 2003; Pandey and Vinod, 2022). However, there is no direct evidence linking neuronal p27 alterations to synaptic or behavioral function. Moreover, whether p27 contributes to age-related changes in neuronal morphology or cognition has not been determined.

To address these questions, we generated a post-developmental conditional knockout mouse model in which p27 is selectively deleted in excitatory forebrain neurons. This strategy enables the study of p27's neuronal functions without confounding developmental or glial effects. We conducted a series of experiments using a multidisciplinary approach to investigate the roles of p27 in cognitive function and potential underlying molecular/cellular mechanisms. Our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized role for p27 in maintaining synaptic integrity and cognitive function during aging, extending its influence beyond canonical cell-cycle regulation to neuronal structure and plasticity.

Materials and methods

Mice

Mice are housed in the Wake Forest School of Medicine barrier facility under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with a regular feeding and cage-cleaning schedule. The facility operates under the guidance of the US Department of Agriculture's Animal Welfare Information Center (AWIC) and the NIH Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The following mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories for breeding: B6.CgTg (Camk2a-cre)T29–1Stl/J (Camk2a-cre mice), stock No. 005359; 129S4/SvJae-Cdkn1btm2Mlf/J (loxP-flanked p27 mice), stock No. 027328. These mice were bred to create p27 conditional heterozygous knockout (p27+/−) and homozygous knockout (p27−/−) mice. Genotypes were confirmed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Both sexes of mice were used at ages 3–6 months (young) or 10–12 months (middle-aged).

Immunofluorescence imaging experiments

Mice were anesthetized using Avertin (Sigma-Aldrich #T4, 840–2) intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 400 mg/kg of body weight. Mice were transcardially perfused with 25mL of cold PBS then perfused with 30mL of cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at a 5mL/min flow rate. The brain was dissected out and fixed overnight in cold 4% PFA in PBS. Half brain was subsectioned to 40 μm using a Leica VT1200S vibratome and washed twice in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10min each. Free-floating sections were then permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS with 300mM Glycine and 10% DMSO, for 1.5h. Slices were washed twice in 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5min each. Slices were blocked in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 and 6% goat serum (GS) for 2h followed by washing in 0.2% Tween-20 in PBS with 0.01mg/mL Heparin (PTwH) (twice, 15min). Slices were then incubated for 2 days at 4°C in primary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 546 conjugated anti-p27Kip1 antibody (F-8) (1100 dilution, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat# sc-1641 AF546) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-MAP2 antibody(1250 dilution, BioLegend, Cat# 801805) in 0.5xPTwH (1 V PTwH+1 V PBS) with 3% normal goat serum. Slices were washed with 0.5xPTwH 3 times for 20min, then another 3 times for 20min in PBS. Sections were incubated in DAPI (GeneTex, GTX16206) for 2h. Sections were washed 3 times with PBS for 30min then mounted with Vectashield anti-fade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1000–10). The sections were then imaged using an Olympus FV4000 confocal microscope.

Mouse behavioral assessment

Open Field Assay (OF):

Mice were handled for three days prior to experimentation and habituated to the experimental procedure room for one hour prior to testing. As previously described (Beckelman et al., 2019), animals were placed into a square area (40×40×50 cm) and allowed to freely explore for 15 min. Video tracking software (EthoVision XT) was utilized to calculate travel velocity and distance, along with specific location in the area. Time spent in the periphery (outer 10 cm) was calculated as a percentage of total time in the arena. Novel Object Recognition Task (NOR): Mice were placed in the same arena as OF with two identical, evenly spaced objects and allowed to freely explore for 5 min (Beckelman et al., 2019). The following day, one object was replaced with a novel, but similarly sized object. The location of the novel object, left or right side, was randomly chosen. The amount of time that mice spent interacting with each object was manually scored and a discrimination index (DI) was calculated. Mice were excluded from analysis if the total interaction time was less than 10 s or if they exhibited a strong location preference, in which more than 90% of the total time was spent with one object. The amount of time spent with each object was recorded manually and expressed in the form of a discrimination index (DI = (tN + tF) ÷ tT, where tN = time spent with novel object, tF = time spent with familiar object, and tT = total time spent with both objects).

Marble Burying:

Mice were habituated to the experimental procedure room for one hour prior. A standard rat cage was filled with 5 cm of bedding and 15 evenly spaced marbles on the surface. Mice were placed in the cage and allowed to freely explore the area for 30 min, after which they were returned to their home cages. The number of marbles buried was counted if more than 65% of the marble was under the bedding (Angoa-Pérez et al., 2013; Jester et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).

Nestlet Shredding:

Mice were habituated to the room for one hour prior to being placed in a mouse cage with a pre-weighed cotton nestlet. After 30 min, mice were removed from the cage to their home cages. One day later to allow drying time, the nestlets were re-weighed(Angoa-Pérez et al., 2013; Jester et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).

Morris Water Maze (MWM) and Visible Platform (VP):

Mice were habituated to the experimental procedure room in warmed, empty mouse cages with their cage mates for one hour prior to experimentation. Mice with open wounds were disqualified from use. MWM was performed as previously described (Beckelman et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2013). A plastic pool (135 cm diameter) was filled with 25 °C water and white non-toxic, washable paint with a circular platform not visible below the surface. Animals were placed in the pool and allowed to freely swim until they found the hidden platform or a maximum of 60 s passed. Each mouse underwent four trials per day, spaced 15 min apart, for five days. The starting quadrant was rotated at the start of each trial to minimize memorization of the route rather than location. Following the fifth day of training, mice remained in their heated cages for two hours prior to beginning the “probe” trial. In the probe trial, the hidden platform is removed from the pool and mice are allowed to freely swim for 60 s. EthoVision XT software tracks the movements of the mouse and calculates the amount of time spent in the quadrant which previously contained the platform (TQ), the frequency of crossing the location of the platform, and the time taken to reach the previous location of the platform. Following a two-day break, mice were returned to the pool which now contains a visual platform seated 1 cm above the surface of the water. Mice were allowed a maximum of 60 s to find the platform. Four trials were conducted each day for two days. The visual platform changed quadrant locations between each trial.

Western blotting

Mice were sacrificed via cervical dislocation, brains removed, and hippocampi dissected out. Hippocampi were immediately flash frozen on dry ice in microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were homogenized in lysis buffer and total protein concentration was determined via bicinchoninic acid assay(Ma et al., 2014). Protein samples were loaded into 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gel (Bio-Rad), run at 150 V, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with SuperBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min, then probed for primary antibodies overnight. Primary antibodies were p27 (Cell Signaling #3686S), eEF2 (Cell Signaling #2332S), p-eEF2 (Thr56) (Cell Signaling #2331S), GAPDH (Cell Signaling #2118S), and β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich #A2228). Blots were washed with 1× Tris-buffered saline solution with 1% Triton X-100 (TBST) and stained with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies. Membranes were imaged with Clarity™ ECL soluation (Bio-Rad) and the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System. The ImageJ software was used to perform densitometry analysis and signals were normalized to either β-actin or the appropriate total protein levels.

Surface sensing of translation assay (SUnSET)

Surface Sensing of translation was performed as previously described (Beckelman et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2009). Briefly, mice were sacrificed via cervical dislocation before brains were harvested and sliced transversely into 400 μm-thick sections in cold, oxygenated cutting buffer (87 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 7 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 25 mM glucose, 37.5 mM sucrose). Hippocampi were dissected from slices and exposed to an oxygenated solution of 50% cutting buffer and 50% artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF: 125 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2, and 25 mM glucose) at room temperature for 15 min. Slices were transferred to oxygenated ACSF at 32 °C for two hours, then 0.5 μg/mL of puromycin was added for one hour. Slices were then removed and flash frozen prior to western blotting. Blots were stained with puromycin (Millipore #MABE343) and normalized to GAPDH.

Transmission Electron microscopy (TEM)

Electron microscopy was performed as previously described (Beckelman et al., 2019). Brains were harvested and the CA1 of the hippocampus was dissected from a 1 mm thick transverse section. CA1 sections were fixed overnight in 1% PFA / 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Millonig's phosphate buffer. Following a one hour exposure to 1% osmium tetroxide in PBS, sections were dehydrated via ethanol. Samples were infiltrated with Spurr's resin, cured overnight at 70 °C, then sectioned with a Reichert-Jung Ultracut E Ultramicrotome into 90 nm thick sections. Tissue was stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then imaged with a FEI Technai Spirit TEM (80 kV) and an Advanced Microscopy Techniques 2Vu CCD camera at x11,000. Three grid sections (418 nm × 275 nm) were created per mouse, and each grid section was comprised of 10 equally sized images. Images were analyzed using Biodock.ai (Biodock).

Golgi-Cox staining

Brains were bisected and processed using the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ kit as previously described (Beckelman et al., 2019). Slices were cut on a vibratome at 100 μm thick, and the hippocampi were dissected out. Hippocampi were mounted on gelatin-coated slides and imaged using Keyence BZ-X710 all-in-one fluorescent microscope at 100× magnification. Fifteen images were taken per mouse in the stratum radiatum. Spines were then classified using published guidelines (Risher et al., 2014).

Biodock.AI model development

To more efficiently analyze images, we utilized Biodock.ai, a web-based AI platform for image analysis for both TEM and Golgi experiments. We utilized previously hand scored images to train the Biodock project. Labeling consisted of manually differentiating “object classes”, such as postsynaptic densities (PSDs), mitochondria, and spine types (thin, long-thin, mushroom, filopodia, branched, and stubby). Object classes for TEM analysis were set at 4000 pixels, while golgi was set at 500 pixels (12 sections) for dendritic spine determination and 250 pixels (48 sections) for individual spine classifications. Labels were created using SAM technology to draw shapes around each object. Hand labeled images were then used to train an AI image analysis model utilizing Alligator architecture. The model was improved upon by continual human correction to labels and retraining. Once the model achieved a performance score of 85, the model was considered completed. A completed model was then compared to blinded hand scoring to ensure validity of results. To utilize the model, we uploaded images to Biodock organized by mouse number and type of analysis. The analysis used the trained AI model to label images, calculate parameters such as counts, area, perimeter, and feret's diameter for each individual object. Objects were then averaged per image to utilize in data visualization and processing.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as bar graphs with the top of the bar indicating the mean and the error bars indicating the standard error of the mean (SEM). Samples were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post-hoc comparison (where applicable). An error probability of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Outliers were determined via Grubb's test or, in the case of TEM analysis, the Robust Regression and Outlier Removal (ROUT) test. Statistics were conducted using Prism (GraphPad).

Results

Generation of neuronal p27 conditional knockout mice

By performing immunofluorescence combined with confocal microscopy imaging experiments, we first demonstrated that p27 is highly expressed in neurons of hippocampus (Fig. S1). We crossed the mice with loxP-flanked Cdkn1b (the gene encoding p27 protein) with the mice expressing neuron-specific CamK2a-Cre recombinase (Fero et al., 1996; Yang et al., 2021) to generate heterozygous knockdown (p27+/−, KD) and homozygous knockout (p27−/−, KO) p27 mutant mice. Western blot experiments demonstrated a reduction of protein levels of p27 in the hippocampus of mutant mice as expected that did not vary with age (Fig. S2 A–C).

Cognitive function is unaltered in young p27 mutant mice

To assess the effects of neuronal p27 suppression on cognitive function, we performed a behavioral battery on mutant mice and their Cre control littermates at a young age of three to six months. We performed the novel object recognition task (NOR), a hippocampal-dependent measure of long-term recognition memory(Antunes and Biala, 2012; Beckelman et al., 2019). A discrimination index [DI: (time with novel object − familiar object) / total time] was calculated to compare potential phenotypes in recognition memory among the 3 groups, and there were no differences in DI between groups (Fig. 1A). We also evaluated potential changes to locomotion and anxiety-like phenotypes using the open-field (OF) assay(Kraeuter et al., 2019). Mice in all groups spent similar amounts of time in the periphery, suggesting no anxiety-like behavior (Fig. 1B). Notably, both the p27+/− and p27−/− mice showed a decrease in distance traveled in OF compared to the Cre control mice (Fig. 1C). Moreover, we performed marble burying and nestlet-shredding tasks to evaluate potential compulsive-like behaviors, and the results did not show any differences between groups (Fig. S3 A–B).

We next performed the hidden platform Morris water maze (MWM) task to measure hippocampal-dependent spatial learning and memory (Beckelman et al., 2019; Bromley-Brits et al., 2011; Jester et al., 2025; Kasica et al., 2022; Vorhees and Williams, 2006). Mice in all groups exhibited similar day-to-day escape latencies during the learning/acquisition phase (Fig. 1D). During the probe trial, there was no difference among all groups in the measurement of latency to “platform” and amounts of time in the target quadrant (Fig. 1 E and F). These results indicated that spatial learning and memory were not altered in young p27 mutant mice. To evaluate factors independent of spatial learning/memory such as swimming ability and motivation, we performed the visual platform (VP) test (Bromley-Brits et al., 2011; Jester et al., 2025) and did not find differences among groups. (Fig. 1G).

Furthermore, we saw no differences in levels of p-eEF2 (Thr 56) or changes to de novo protein synthesis via SUnSET (Fig. 1 H and I). In brief, inhibition of neuronal p27 expression did not impact overall cognitive function nor protein synthesis in young mice.

Cognitive function is impaired in middle-aged p27 mutant mice

To investigate potential age-related effects on cognitive function associated with neuronal p27 expression, we carried out the same behavioral experiments as described above in middle-aged mice at 10–12 months old. Results of the NOR experiments showed a trend (p = 0.08) towards decreased DI in the p27−/− mice compared to the control Cre mice, suggesting potential impairment of long-term recognition memory (Fig. 2A). In the OF test, all the 3 groups of mice spent similar time in the peripheral areas the OF task (Fig. 2B). Interesting, the decrease in distance traveled in OF was not as apparent at this age compared to the young mice, with only the p27−/− mice showing a trending decrease (p = 0.07) in travel distance (Fig. 2C). Marble-burying and nestlet-shredding tests did not show any differences between groups, suggesting there is no change to compulsive-like behaviors (Fig. S3 C and D).

In the MWM test, mice in all groups demonstrated similar learning curves (day-to-day escape latencies) during the acquisition phase (Fig. 2D). Notably, in the probe trial, it took the homozygous p27−/− mice significantly longer time to locate the “platform” compared to either the Cre or heterozygous p27+/− mice (Fig. 2E), suggesting impaired spatial memory. However, all groups of mice did spend similar amounts of time in the target quadrant during probe trial (Fig. 2F). Additionally, results of the visible maze test did not show differences among all groups. (Fig. 2G). Taken together, suppression of neuronal p27 caused cognitive impairment in middle-aged mice.

We also evaluated whether there were any protein synthesis changes in the p27 mutant mice, as protein synthesis can be a modulator of cognitive changes(Jester et al., 2025; Ma, 2021; Wang et al., 2024). We saw no changes to phosphorylation of eEF2 at Thr 56 between groups (Fig. 2H) nor any changes to de novo protein synthesis assessed by SUnSET (Fig. 2I).

Effects of neuronal p27 suppression on hippocampal postsynaptic morphology

To elucidate mechanisms underlying cognitive impairments in middle-aged p27 mutant mice, we next conducted transmission electron microscopy (TEM) experiments on the CA1 region of the hippocampus to evaluate the effects of p27 suppression on postsynaptic densities (PSD). PSD is a crucial structure for signaling transduction and synaptic function including regulation of synaptic plasticity(Sheng and Kim, 2011; Shigeo, 2007). Given the overall negative findings on cognitive function assays in the young mice, we focused on investigation of potential effects of p27 suppression on postsynaptic morphology in middle-aged cohorts. We have analyzed the PSD density, the maximal Feret diameter, area and perimeter. Compared to the Cre group, neither the p27+/− nor the p27−/− mice showed any differences in the aforementioned analysis of PSD (Fig. 3 A–E). Taking advantage of the TEM imaging data, we also investigated potential effects of p27 suppression on mitochondria, dysregulation of which has been linked to numerous neuronal diseases(Wen et al., 2025; Zong et al., 2024). We did not observe any differences between groups in measuring mitochondria density or morphological changes (Fig. S4 A–E).

Using the rapid Golgi staining protocol, we assessed dendritic spines in the hippocampus. Alterations to spine density and morphology have been associated with synaptic plasticity and memory formation(Costa-Mattioli et al., 2009; Sutton and Schuman, 2006). Interestingly, homozygous p27−/− mice had an increased number of total spines when compared to either Cre or heterozygous p27+/− mice (Fig. 4 A and B). Spines can be classified by their distinctive morphological features into two classes, mature (branched, stubby, mushroom) or immature (thin, long-thin, filopodia). Notably, the p27−/− mice had an increased density of immature spines (Fig. 4C), mainly driven by an increase in the number of thin spines, compared to either Cre or p27+/− mice (Fig. 4D). In comparison, there was no change in the density of mature spines (Fig. 4E) among all groups. Other spine types were quantified with filopodia, long-thin, and mushroom spine densities showing no differences between groups (Fig. S5A–C). Within the mature spine classification, we did see an increase in stubby spines in the heterozygous p27+/− mice compared to Cre mice (Fig. S4D) and a decreased density in branched spines in homozygous p27−/− mice compared to heterozygous p27+/− mice (Fig. S5E). Finally, overall spine maturity, assessed by the ratio of mature spines to immature spines, decreased in the homozygous p27−/− mice compared to the p27+/− mice (Fig. 4F). Taken together, suppression of neuronal p27 results in impaired hippocampal dendritic spine morphology, with an abnormal increase in immature spine counts.

One potential mediator of dendritic spine morphology is cofilin and its phosphorylation state(Paciello et al., 2025; Pyronneau et al., 2017). Prior studies have also found that p27 can interact with cofilin to mediate actin dynamics (Besson et al., 2004; Jaiswal and Sharma, 2017; Jiao et al., 2022; Sharma and Pledger, 2016; Yang et al., 1998). We thus evaluated potential cofilin regulation in the middle-aged mice and found that p27 suppression had no effect on total or phosphorylated levels of cofilin in the hippocampus (Fig. S5F). Furthermore, p27 has been found to be important for adult hippocampal neurogenesis (Andreu et al., 2014; Domingo-Muelas et al., 2022; Gil-Perotin et al., 2011; Nguyen et al., 2006), which in turn is related to spine formation(Lin et al., 2023; Yau et al., 2011). We evaluated markers of neurogenesis and found no changes to immature or mature neurons with p27 manipulation (Fig. S6A–G).

Discussion

Specific roles of p27 in the developed brain remain to be fully understood. Findings from this study reveal a novel, age-dependent role for p27 in maintaining cognitive function. Conditional knockout of p27 in excitatory neurons led to progressive deficits in recognition and spatial memory that emerged with aging, while young (3–6 months old) knockout mice exhibit overall normal cognitive function. Moreover, middle aged p27 knockout mice demonstrate selective morphological changes in dendritic spines, particularly an increase in immature spine forms, without detectable changes in global protein synthesis or postsynaptic density structure.

Prior work has established that p27 is expressed in post-mitotic cortical neurons (Nguyen et al., 2006) and is associated with development of microtubules (Godin et al., 2012). p27 has been found in both dendrites and axons and has been implicated in axonal transport and microtubule dynamics. Embryonic knockout of p27 resulted in impaired axonal transport mediated through decreased acetylation of α-tubulin (Morelli et al., 2018).

Other cell cycle-related proteins have been studied in the context of cognition and synaptic functioning. Cdk5 has been found to be active and have important roles in post-mitotic neurons. Loss of Cdk5 activity disrupts memory, hippocampal plasticity, long-term depression (LTD), and affects related synaptic architecture (Barnett and Bibb, 2010; Guan et al., 2011; Kawauchi, 2014; Mita et al., 2016; Odajima et al., 2011; Pellarin et al., 2025; Shupp et al., 2017; Takahashi et al., 2022). Cdk5 can form a complex with p27 in post-mitotic neurons that regulate neuronal migration and actin cytoskeleton (Kawauchi et al., 2006). Moreover, activated Cdk1 is also expressed in post-mitotic neurons and mediates neuronal death(Konishi and Bonni, 2003; Konishi et al., 2002). Molecular targets of Cdk1 contributing to cell death have been identified as Bcl-2 family member BAD and FOXO1 (Konishi et al., 2002; Yuan et al., 2009). p27 can directly inhibit Cdk1's activity during the cell cycle (Bastians et al., 1998; Pagano, 2004). Knockout of Cks1 in mice impaired long-term memory and L-LTP through decreased degradation of p27 (Kukalev et al., 2016).

In assessing cognitive behaviors of the p27 mutant mice at middle age, we observed intact MWM learning, but impaired probe trial results alongside a trending deficit in NOR test. The intact learning curve of MWM indicates that the mutant mice can acquire the spatial task at similar rates (Swift et al., 2024; Vorhees and Williams, 2006). However, during the probe trial, increased latency to find the platform is indicative of impaired spatial memory, reduced precision, and potential deficits in memory consolidation or retrieval (Maei et al., 2009; Othman et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2013). Both NOR and MWM are widely considered to be hippocampal-dependent tasks, however other brain regions, are involved and may play unique roles (Akirav and Maroun, 2006; Buzsáki and Moser, 2013; Kasica et al., 2022). The potential impairment observed in both NOR and MWM probe trials could point to a broader disruption of hippocampal-dependent memory consolidation and retrieval, rather than a task specific effect. It is of note that at 3–6 months old, both the p27 heterozygous and homozygous mice travel significantly less distance in the OF task (Fig. 1C), however such effect on travel distance was blunted in middle-aged mice (Fig. 2C). At 3–6 months, there were no changes to distance traveled in the NOR or MWM tasks (data not shown). It is possible that these lower OF distances could indicate less exploratory behavior or freezing in a new environment that is not seen in the older age.

Normal aging is a complex phenomenon associated with declining cognitive function and biochemical changes within neurons. Older mice, in the absence of disease, typically show age-related decline on hippocampal-dependent memory(Gower and Lamberty, 1993; Hendrickx et al., 2022; Magnusson et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2019). Changes to p27 expression with aging have been studied in aging cell types where increased senescence is correlated with increased p27 expression(Flores et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2016). However, the roles of p27 in aging remain elusive. Studies in muscle and kidney cells suggest that p27 mRNA expression is decreased with aging, while its protein expression is increased in the nucleus, where it can promote apoptosis and senescence (Chkhotua et al., 2003; McKay and White, 2021; White et al., 2018). Our study shows no differences in p27 expression in the hippocampus of control mice between 3 and 6 months old and 10–12 months old (Fig. S2C).

Neuronal alterations by p27 loss and healthy aging changes seem to overlap with respect to microtubule dynamics and actin remodeling. It is possible that a lack of p27 could exacerbate normal aging and potentially accelerate the healthy aging process. p27 decreases actin plasticity, which as it compounds could worsen the behavioral measures (Kommaddi et al., 2018; P et al., 2024; Wing-Fu and Wing-Tak, 2020). Middle-aged knockout mice show behavioral deficits, which may reflect an interaction between normal age-related synaptic decline and loss of p27-mediated cytoskeletal regulation. The mechanism by which p27 leads to altered dendritic spine morphology remains undetermined. We evaluated important GTPases for spine dynamics, Cdc42, Rac1, and RhoA, but saw no difference in protein expression (data not shown). We also evaluated the regulation of neurogenesis in the hippocampus of p27 transgenic mice, which could contribute to behavioral phenotypes associated with neuronal p27 suppression. Two studies in which p27 was knocked out in vivo found decreased neurogenesis but increased proliferation(Gil-Perotin et al., 2011; Hörster et al., 2017), while a third study found increased neurogenesis and proliferation(Andreu et al., 2014). We analyzed protein expression of doublecortin (DCX), Neuronal Nuclei (NeuN), neurofilament-L (NF-L), synaptophysin (SYP), and β−3 tubulin as potential markers of neurogenesis. DCX and β−3 tubulin are related to early phases of neurogenesis (Alonso-Moreno et al., 2025; Höglinger et al., 2004; Klein et al., 2020; Plümpe et al., 2006). NeuN, NFL, and SYP were evaluated as markers of mature neurons. (Alonso-Moreno et al., 2025; Hajjar et al., 2013; Kuhn et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2016; Rosyidi et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2009). In brief, we did not observe changes of any of these markers in association with p27 suppression (Fig. S6).

This study presents a novel role of p27 in the aging brain, however this study is not without limitations. One important limitation to consider is the complete absence of detectable p27 expression in the homozygous mice within bulk hippocampal tissue (Fig. S2). Although the CaMKII-α Cre is generally used to target excitatory neurons, its specificity and penetrance are not perfect, which could influence p27 more broadly. The hippocampus is predominately excitatory neurons, however other cell types are still present but at lower levels (Methi et al., 2024; Saunders et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2021). In addition, p27 has been found to be absent in radial glia-like cells, which compose about 10% of the hippocampus, raising the possibility that it may not exist in all hippocampal cell types (García-Osta et al., 2022; Sibbe et al., 2015). It is also possible that there is compensatory regulation within the Cip/Kip family to allow the cell cycle to continue to function. Together, these considerations underscore the value of future cell type–specific approaches to more precisely define p27 expression patterns in the hippocampus.

In summary, neuronal p27 appears to serve a protective, non-canonical role in sustaining synaptic architecture and cognitive function with age. Its homozygous loss selectively weakens recognition and spatial memory potentially through disrupted spine maturation. p27 regulation provides a molecular link between the cell-cycle, cytoskeletal regulation, and age-related cognitive decline.
