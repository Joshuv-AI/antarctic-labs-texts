Cultural and Religious Beliefs and Acceptance of Facial Cosmetic Surgery in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Basem K. Alhusani, Khalid A. Fayi, Suha A. Ageeli, Remaz A. Alhassan, Muteb M. Alharbi, Shayma A. Albunasser, Aisha A. Alameer, Taif Y. Solan, Ali M. Alkhathami, Eman M. Alshammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study examines the acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery in Saudi Arabia and the influence of social, cultural, and religious factors on public attitudes. It aims to improve the understanding of these factors to support patient education and informed decision-making.Methods:A cross‑sectional study was conducted using a validated, self‑administered electronic questionnaire distributed across all 13 regions of Saudi Arabia. Saudi adults aged 18 years or older who could read Arabic and consented to participate were included, whereas non‑Saudis, individuals younger than 18 years, and questionnaires with incomplete responses were excluded. A total of 1027 eligible participants completed the survey (with a completion rate of 55.7%). The questionnaire assessed demographics, history of facial cosmetic procedures, reasons for considering surgery, religious and cultural beliefs, and surgeon preferences. Associations were analyzed using χ2 tests, with significance set at a P value of less than 0.05.Results:Acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery showed significant associations with sex, income, and occupation (P < 0.001). Religious factors demonstrated strong relationships with acceptance, including perceived alignment with Islamic teachings and prior consultation with a religious scholar (P < 0.001). Surgeon gender preference was also significantly associated with acceptance, whereas cultural influence alone did not show comparable statistical significance.Conclusions:Acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery in Saudi Arabia is shaped primarily by religious beliefs, medical necessity, and demographic factors. Religious permissibility plays a more influential role than cultural norms. These findings highlight the importance of culturally sensitive, religion‑aware counseling to support informed decision-making.

Takeaways

Question: What factors most strongly influence the acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery among Saudi adults, and how do cultural and religious beliefs shape public attitudes toward elective facial procedures?

Findings: A cross‑sectional survey of 1027 Saudi participants revealed that acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery was significantly associated with sex, income, and occupation. Religious beliefs, particularly the perceived alignment with Islamic teachings and consultation with scholars, had a stronger influence than cultural norms.

Meaning: Religious permissibility appears to be more strongly associated with public acceptance of facial cosmetic surgery in Saudi Arabia than cultural tradition.

INTRODUCTION

Plastic surgery has gained potential popularity globally, including in Saudi Arabia. Plastic and reconstructive surgery is a subspecialty of general surgery dealing with the management of congenital or acquired skin and subcutaneous abnormalities in several body parts to improve function and aesthetics.1 The performance of cosmetic surgery (CS) is increasing globally for several reasons, mainly for aesthetic enhancement and age-related changes. Procedures aimed at correcting congenital defects, however, fall under reconstructive surgery rather than CS.2

Plastic surgery encompasses 2 major categories: reconstructive surgery, which aims to restore form and function following congenital anomalies, trauma, or disease; and cosmetic (aesthetic) surgery, which focuses on enhancing appearance in individuals without underlying pathology. This distinction is essential when examining public attitudes toward elective facial cosmetic procedures.

There are various factors affecting the preference, practice, and acceptance of individuals regarding CS, such as cultural factors. In a previous review, it was stated that the acceptance and pursuit of plastic operations were influenced by race, culture, and the overall social milieu.3,4

Religious beliefs exert strong impacts on personal attitudes, perceptions, and issues related to health by dictating rigid positions regarding critical health issues.5,6 It was stated that CS was once illegal and considered religiously unaligned in Saudi Arabia, and the general population was opposed to it due to cultural and religious beliefs.7 However, CS use has increased in Saudi Arabia, and it has been reported that Saudi Arabia ranks 29th among the top 30 countries with the highest rates of CS procedures performed globally.8

It was stated that the decision to perform CS among Saudis could be influenced by a combination of variables, including cultural stigma, religious beliefs, social media, and Western influences.7 However, there is inadequate information and data regarding the influence of cultural and religious beliefs on the acceptance of CS, especially facial reconstruction, in Saudi Arabia. Hence, this study was conducted to identify the impact of such beliefs on the acceptance of facial reconstructive CS in Saudi Arabia.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study Design

This analytical cross‑sectional study was conducted between October 2024 and April 2025 across all 13 regions of Saudi Arabia. Eligible participants were Saudi adults aged 18 years or older, fluent in Arabic, and willing to participate. Non‑Saudis, individuals younger than 18 years of age, and those who provided incomplete responses were excluded. Because the study focused exclusively on elective facial CS, procedures performed for trauma, burns, or congenital deformities were classified as reconstructive and excluded from the analysis. Non‑facial cosmetic procedures that appeared in the questionnaire (such as tummy tuck or breast reduction) were excluded from the analysis and were not considered part of facial CS.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the appropriate institutional review board. All participants provided electronic informed consent before accessing the questionnaire. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no identifying information was collected.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a validated, self‑administered questionnaire distributed electronically through secure digital platforms, including social media channels and community networks. The questionnaire was disseminated electronically using a multichannel approach to ensure broad geographic reach. The survey link was shared through widely used social media platforms in Saudi Arabia, including WhatsApp, X, Snapchat, and Telegram, as well as university groups and community networks. Because participation relied on voluntary online access, the sample represents a convenience sample, and potential selection bias is acknowledged. The survey link was shared with 1842 individuals across all 13 regions of Saudi Arabia; 1102 individuals accessed the questionnaire, and 1027 completed it, yielding a completion rate of 55.7%. Only fully completed questionnaires that met the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis. Although 64 non-Saudi respondents completed the survey, they were excluded during data cleaning and were not included in the final analysis because the study aimed specifically to explore the attitudes of Saudi nationals, as the research question focused on Saudi cultural and religious perspectives.

Upon accessing the survey link, participants were presented with a brief explanation of the study objectives, followed by an electronic informed consent form, which had to be accepted before proceeding. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no identifying information was collected.

The questionnaire consisted of 22 items divided into 5 sections. The first section collected demographic information. The second section assessed the personal history of facial cosmetic procedures and whether any close relatives, such as parents, siblings, or children, had undergone similar interventions. The third section explored participants’ opinions regarding facial CS, including satisfaction with appearance, comfort level with surgery, reasons for considering facial cosmetic procedures, and interest in specific facial interventions. The fourth section examined religious and cultural beliefs, such as perceived alignment of facial CS with Islamic teachings and the influence of local customs; in this study, “local customs” referred to cultural traditions, social expectations, and community norms within Saudi society that may influence attitudes toward facial CS. Cultural influence was measured using a questionnaire item asking participants to rate the extent to which local customs affect their views on CS (no influence, little influence, moderate influence, strong influence), and whether participants had consulted a religious scholar. The final section evaluated preferences when selecting a surgeon and previous experiences with facial cosmetic procedures. Questions regarding surgical outcomes were displayed only to participants who reported having undergone a cosmetic procedure. Procedures performed for trauma, burns, or congenital deformities were classified as reconstructive procedures and hence excluded from the facial CS analysis. All responses were automatically recorded and securely stored, and participants who withdrew, declined consent, or submitted incomplete surveys were excluded from the analysis.

Data Analysis

Data were entered into Excel and analyzed using R software. All variables in this study were categorical; therefore, results were summarized using frequencies and percentages. χ2 tests were used to assess associations, and the Fisher exact test was applied when expected cell counts were less than 5. No continuous variables were analyzed, and t tests were not required. This study used unadjusted bivariate analyses, as the aim was to explore general patterns rather than establish causal relationships. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

These results demonstrate that religious beliefs exert a stronger influence than cultural customs on the acceptance of facial CS. DISCUSSION

CS has become increasingly common worldwide, including in Saudi Arabia, where attitudes toward elective facial procedures continue to evolve.9 This study examined the influence of cultural and religious beliefs on the acceptance of facial CS among adults in Saudi Arabia. The demographic profile of our participants, predominantly young, university-educated women, is consistent with previous national studies7 and reflects the population most frequently exposed to cosmetic‑related content and social trends. The overall rate of facial CS among participants was low, with most reporting no personal or family history of such procedures. This may be partly explained by the high levels of satisfaction with personal appearance observed in our sample, where more than half of participants described themselves as very satisfied. Similar findings have been reported in earlier studies conducted in Saudi Arabia.7,10

Rhinoplasty and scar/pigmentation removal were the most commonly reported procedures among both participants and their families. This aligns with previous research indicating that rhinoplasty is one of the most frequently performed facial CS procedures in Saudi Arabia.11,12 Despite this, nearly half of participants expressed discomfort with the idea of undergoing CS, reflecting persistent concerns about risks, complications, and social perceptions.13

Family and partner influence seemed limited in this study, with many participants reporting neutral or no opinion from relatives regarding facial CS. This finding is consistent with earlier research suggesting that decisions about cosmetic procedures are increasingly individualized and less dependent on family approval.7 Similarly, surgeon gender preference did not show a strong trend, as more than half of participants reported no preference, although female surgeons were slightly favored among those who expressed a choice.5

Religious beliefs emerged as a stronger determinant of acceptance of facial CS than cultural customs in this study. This conclusion is supported by the statistically significant associations observed between a history of facial CS and several religion-related variables. Participants who believed that cosmetic procedures fully or partially align with Islamic teachings were significantly more likely to have undergone surgery (P < 0.001). Likewise, those who had consulted a religious scholar, whether receiving positive or negative advice, also showed a significant association with a surgical history (P < 0.001). These findings indicate that individuals’ perceptions of religious permissibility play a central role in shaping their decisions regarding cosmetic facial interventions.14

In contrast, the influence of local customs did not demonstrate a comparable level of statistical significance. Although many participants reported moderate or strong cultural influence, these variables did not show a statistically significant association with the decision to undergo CS. Although the questionnaire did not directly ask participants to prioritize religion versus culture, the strength and consistency of the religious variables indicate that religious beliefs exert a more substantial impact on acceptance of facial cosmetic procedures than cultural norms.15,16

Furthermore, although most participants did not seek formal religious consultation, their responses suggest that individuals rely on their personal understanding of Islamic teachings when forming opinions about facial CS. This highlights the importance of perceived religious permissibility rather than formal religious rulings in shaping public attitudes.9,11

Overall, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the sociocultural and religious factors influencing CS acceptance in Saudi Arabia. Recognizing the dominant role of religious beliefs can help healthcare providers deliver more culturally sensitive counseling and improve communication with patients considering facial cosmetic procedures.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. First, its geographic focus on Saudi Arabia may limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different cultural or religious contexts. Second, the sample distribution was not fully balanced, with certain demographic groups being overrepresented, which may introduce sampling bias. Despite these limitations, a major strength of this study is its simultaneous assessment of both cultural and religious influences on the acceptance of facial CS—an area that has not been previously examined in the literature. Further studies in more diverse populations are recommended to validate and expand upon these findings.

Additionally, this study has several methodological limitations. Its cross‑sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships. The use of self‑reported data may introduce recall and social desirability bias. The online distribution of the questionnaire may limit generalizability to individuals who are less active on social media platforms. Finally, although cultural influence was assessed, it did not demonstrate statistically comparable associations, which limited further subgroup analysis.

Moreover, although 64 non‑Saudi respondents completed the questionnaire, they were excluded because the study aimed specifically to assess the perceptions of Saudi nationals, as the research question focused on Saudi cultural and religious perspectives. The questionnaire also did not differentiate between students attending Islamic universities and those enrolled in secular universities, which may influence religious attitudes and introduce potential confounding. As a result, university education was analyzed as a single category, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Additionally, because no statistical adjustment was performed for demographic differences between the groups, the conclusions should be interpreted with caution, as unadjusted confounding may influence the observed associations.

CONCLUSIONS

Acceptance of facial CS in Saudi Arabia is shaped primarily by religious beliefs, perceived medical or aesthetic need, and key demographic factors. Religious permissibility emerged as the strongest predictor of acceptance, exerting a greater influence than cultural norms. These findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive and religion‑aware communication when counseling individuals considering facial cosmetic procedures. Further research in broader and more diverse populations is recommended to deepen the understanding of the sociocultural factors influencing CS acceptance.
