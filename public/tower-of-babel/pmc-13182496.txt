Sexual harassment and workplace safety among female doctors: the hidden toll in Sri Lanka
Authors: Manudi Vidanapathirana, Kalani Kulathilaka, Santhushya Fernando
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionData on sexual harassment in medicine is limited in South Asia, and formal mechanisms for reporting and redress remain underdeveloped in the region. We aimed to describe workplace sexual harassment among female doctors, associated factors, survivor experiences and perceptions of physical and sexual safety in the workplace in Sri Lanka.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among Sri Lankan female doctors with at least 6 months of work experience. Data were collected using an anonymous online questionnaire from September to October 2024, administered via mailing lists of professional medical bodies and closed social media network groups of female doctors on Facebook and WhatsApp. We used SPSS V.23 for quantitative data analysis and narratively described free text responses in the sections titled ‘Other: please specify’.ResultsThe number of completed responses was 377. Of the study participants, 58.6% (n=221) reported having experienced sexual harassment in the workplace at some point in their career. Rape was reported by 1.32% (n=5). Significant associations were seen between workplace sexual harassment and junior position and not having children (p<0.05). Only a few had filed formal complaints (0.9%, n=2). Reasons cited for lack of action were fear of not being taken seriously (63%), fear of tarnishing the reputation of self (54%) and fear of the perpetrator (37.8%). Perceived psychological responses included anger (80%), disappointment (50.6%) and shame (44.3%). Suicidal thoughts affected 2.7%, while 0.4% had attempted suicide. The majority responded ‘yes’ (33.4%) or ‘maybe’ (43.8%) to the question related to perception of lack of physical or sexual safety in the workplace. The most common reasons for feeling unsafe were lack of adequate privacy in on-call rooms, bathrooms and changing rooms (63.1%), inadequate safety measures such as lack of locks or proper doors (54.2%) and unavailability of chaperones when handling male patients (30.1%).ConclusionWorkplace sexual harassment is common but under-reported among female doctors in Sri Lanka and could result in adverse psychological effects. Apart from sociocultural enablers, there are modifiable environmental factors that are associated with it, and it is imperative that administrators take these factors into account for the improvement of workplace safety.

Introduction

Sexual harassment is a neglected problem in the medical profession and is currently gaining increased recognition.1 2 The roots of sexual harassment in the workplace are sociocultural and lie within hierarchical structures, which are amplified by gender inequality.2 The power imbalance between men and women makes female doctors vulnerable to sexual harassment not only by their male counterparts but also by male patients.3 The impact of sexual harassment is physical and psychological and could have a bearing on career decisions and work experiences.4 Psychological responses to sexual harassment include loss of job desire, burnout and psychiatric disorders such as anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder.4 Workplace-related consequences of unimpeded sexual harassment are normalisation of sexual behaviours, cognitive reframing resulting in tolerance and reduction of workplace safety.4

Notable patterns observed in published studies on sexual harassment in medicine are that it is common, particularly among women, the perpetrator is usually a colleague, and reporting of harassment is infrequent.58 Data on sexual harassment in medicine in South Asia comes predominantly from India, which has reported varied rates of harassment. One study that included both male and female doctors reports a 10% prevalence of sexual harassment in the workplace, while a different study including female healthcare workers, not limited to doctors, reports a 57% prevalence of sexual harassment. 9 10 There is only one related Sri Lankan study, which explored nurses’ perceptions of workplace sexual harassment, and this study did not examine personal experiences.11 Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, there are currently no studies on the experiences of sexual harassment of female healthcare workers in Sri Lanka.

The proportion of female doctors is rising in Sri Lanka. In 2020, the female medical student intake was at 63%.12 Despite this, legal and other protections against sexual harassment remain weak in the health sector. The Sri Lankan penal code under Section 345 criminalises sexual harassment, and various codes of conduct are formally in place.13 However, the limited number of sexual harassment-related complaints at workplaces points towards systemic failures in the existing framework that prevent women from accessing these services and seeking redress.13

Our study aimed to describe workplace sexual harassment among female doctors, associated factors, survivor experiences and perceptions of physical and sexual safety at work in Sri Lanka.

Methods

This study was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted from September to October 2024. Female doctors registered with the Sri Lanka Medical Council and employed in either a state or non-state healthcare institution for at least 6 months were included. Those who were on leave or had not worked in the 6 months preceding data collection were excluded. An online questionnaire was administered via mailing lists of professional bodies and closed social media network groups of female doctors on Facebook and WhatsApp. Because many invitations were posted to closed social media groups and forwarded between groups, and we could not obtain a reliable consolidated membership count across all distribution channels, we report the total number of responses rather than a population-level response rate.

The questionnaire was developed after an extensive literature review of relevant research, in consultation with female doctors of different specialties. It was divided into four components to gather data on sociodemographic and work-related data, experiences of sexual harassment in the workplace, responses to sexual harassment and perceptions of physical and sexual safety in the workplace (online supplemental material 1).

Free text responses were solicited on ‘psychological response to sexual harassment’, ‘actions taken’, ‘reasons for actions not being taken’ and ‘reasons for perceptions related to lack of safety’. The questionnaire was pretested among a group of 12 female doctors selected by the investigators to represent a wide range of age groups and work settings.

Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The information sheet contained definitions of certain terms (eg, rape and attempted rape) to eliminate person-specific misinterpretations.

Quantitative data analysis was carried out using SPSS V.23. Categorical variables were described as percentages, and numerical variables were described using mean, median and SD. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using χ2 analysis. Multivariable logistic regression was not performed due to sample size and collinearity concerns. Answers provided as free text were analysed manually; due to the brevity of such responses, they have been described narratively.

Given the sensitive nature of the information collected, rigorous ethical standards were followed in terms of maintaining anonymity and confidentiality. Trigger warnings were issued in the information sheet in order to mitigate the risk of retraumatisation/revictimisation of survivors of sexual harassment. Participants were asked to refrain from proceeding if they felt that exposure to the entities tested would be damaging to their mental health. Sources of support for survivors of harassment were also indicated on the information sheet should participants feel that support was necessary.

In terms of professional status, 5% of participants were junior doctors (house officers and relief house officers), 62.5% were medical officers (senior house officers), 19.8% were postgraduate trainees, and 12.8% were consultants. Experiences of sexual harassment among female doctors

Of the study participants, 58.6% (n=221) reported having experienced some form of sexual harassment during their career. The most frequent types of sexual harassment experienced were suggestive looks (50.3%, n=190), sexual jokes (42.7%, n=161) and sexual remarks or innuendo (34.7%, n=131). Some (6.3%, n=24) reported having faced requests for sexual favours as compensation or career progression. Furthermore, 7.9% (n=30) disclosed being secretly watched while in doctors’ quarters or on-call rooms. Physically invasive forms of sexual harassment were also reported; 18.3% (n=69) reported inappropriate touching of body parts, 2.1% (n=8) reported attempted rape and 1.32% (n=5) reported rape.

When considering the circumstances surrounding harassment, the most common places were the ward (60.1%, n=133) and operating theatre (29.4%, n=65). All were harassed by males, while 2.71% (n=6) reported being harassed by females in addition to males. In most cases, the perpetrator was a superior (77.8%, n=172), but it is important to note that men of all ranks and positions were identified as perpetrators.

While in-person harassment was more frequently reported (89.1%), digital and cyber harassment were reported by a considerable number. Of the study participants, 40.7% and 33% reported being sexually harassed via text messages and telephone calls, respectively, and 16.7% reported being sexually harassed via social media. Categories of female doctors who are more likely to experience sexual harassment in the workplace

Factors noted to have a statistically significant association with sexual harassment in the workplace are junior position (p=0.03) and lack of children (p=0.025). Factors that were not found to have an association with sexual harassment were age, ethnicity, relationship status and type of work environment (clinical/non-clinical) (online supplemental table 1).

Response to sexual harassment

Commonly perceived psychological reactions to sexual harassment were anger (80%, n=177), disappointment (50.6%, n=112) and shame (44.3%, n=98). Suicidal thoughts affected 2.7% (n=6), with 0.4% (n=1) having attempted suicide.

Just above half (50.2%, n=111) had not taken any action following their experience of sexual harassment. Reasons cited for lack of action were fear of not being taken seriously (63%, n=70), fear of damaging personal reputation (54%, n=60) and fear of the perpetrator (37.8%, n=42). Among those who acted, the common actions taken were telling a friend/colleague (91.8%), telling a superior (30.9%) and communicating with the perpetrator (25.4%). Only very few had filed formal reports with authorities (1.8%, n=2).

Of those who reported being raped in the workplace (n=5), none had filed formal reports. Among those who reported attempted rape (n=8), only one individual (12.5%) did so. Among those who were raped (n=5), the reasons disclosed for not taking action were fear of the perpetrator (25%) and fear of not being taken seriously (25%). Among those who reported attempted rape, reasons cited for lack of action were fear of tarnishing the personal reputation of the survivor (37.5%), fear of the perpetrator (25%), fear of victim blaming (25%) and fear of not being taken seriously (12.5%).

Of the participants who were sexually harassed, 20.8% engaged in actions that affected their career; 10.9% made efforts to be transferred out of their current workplace, 3.6% left the country, 2.7% took a career break, 2.7% gave up postgraduate training, and 0.9% quit their jobs. Perceptions of physical and sexual safety in the workplace

Most participants answered ‘yes’ (33.4%, n=126) or ‘maybe’ (22.8%, n=86) to a question about whether they felt physically and sexually unsafe in their work environment. Only 33.4% (n=165) of participants reported feeling physically and sexually safe in their work environment. The common places where participants felt unsafe were the on-call room (35.8%, n=76) and the ward (33.1%, n=70).

Among those who felt unsafe, the majority (51.4%, n=109) attributed this perception to inadequate safety measures in the on-call room, such as a lack of locks or proper doors, while 37.7% (n=80) noted a lack of privacy in on-call rooms. Additionally, 41.5% (n=88) reported feeling vulnerable due to the isolated walkways between the wards and on-call rooms, especially when walking alone at night, and 30.1% (n=64) highlighted the lack of chaperones when handling male patients (table 4).

Discussion

The findings of this study come at an opportune moment for change, as a call to action for female physician safety has been recently instigated in various medical fora.14 15 The authors hope that the findings of this study will prompt much-needed reformative measures for female physician safety in Sri Lanka, South Asia and beyond.

In this study, 58.6% of the female doctors who responded to the survey had experienced sexual harassment in the workplace at some point in their careers. In the USA, there is a reported frequency of sexual harassment in 36.9% of female physicians in a large nationwide study.16 The UK reports a much higher frequency (63.3%) in an analysis that included 756 surgical doctors.17 Varied figures have been reported from India; 10% have reported sexual harassment in a study with both male and female doctors, while 57% have reported sexual harassment in a study conducted among female healthcare workers not limited to doctors.9 10 While direct comparisons with other studies are difficult due to differing methodologies and definitions, the reported frequency of sexual harassment in the present study suggests that sexual harassment in medicine remains rampant in Sri Lanka. The differences may also be due to differences in personal perception of sexual harassment.16 Analysis of responses to open-ended questions in our study revealed that some participants had trouble discerning whether certain comments, remarks and gestures constituted sexual harassment. Legally, sexual harassment has been defined from the perspective of the victim, which gives the victim the power to define what it is, provided the victim is of sound mind.3 13 However, these interpretations are always coloured by the sociocultural contexts in which they occur. Women may look to others’ responses to determine whether a certain behaviour is inappropriate, or in some settings, sexual harassment may be so deeply ingrained and normalised that women no longer identify certain behaviours as harassment.4 18

Several reasons may contribute to the high reported frequency of sexual harassment in this study. Sri Lanka has a rigid hierarchical medical system where juniors are often at the behest of superiors.18 Junior doctors include intern medical officers (house officers) and relief house officers. Mid-level hierarchies include senior house officers/medical officers, registrars and senior registrars (trainee specialists) and postgraduate/diploma trainees. Senior positions include board-certified consultants and acting consultants. This stratification, compounded by the cultural dominance of patriarchy, may render female doctors vulnerable to sexual harassment.19 The fact that most perpetrators were superiors (77.8%) and senior female doctors in positions of power, that is, consultants, reported less sexual harassment despite having spent a longer time in practice (13.6%), highlights the contribution of power imbalance to harassment. It is noteworthy that all possible work settings were reported as places of harassment, although at varied frequencies, and men at different levels of the health system were reported as perpetrators. This reflects the vulnerability of women even in seemingly innocuous places and that women remain victims even with men professionally below them. A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine questioned whether the power of status afforded by being a physician shields female physicians from being viewed merely as the vulnerable sex by their patients.3 Our findings suggest that being a doctor does not, in fact, render women invincible to sexual violence in the workplace.

The ward and the operating theatre were reportedly the places where participants were mostly subjected to sexual harassment. Given that the ward is generally a public space, this may mean that sexual harassment occurs in public where perpetrators operate without fear of watchful eyes. This may reflect a normative culture of sexual harassment in Sri Lanka where perpetrators do not believe they are perpetuating harassment and/or the perpetrators are fearless due to the superiority and power they wield. In addition, it must be noted that several participants also reported being subjected to digital and cyber harassment, which raises concerns about the far-reaching nature and increased opportunities of sexual harassment beyond the physical workplace environment. Although the least common form of harassment was rape and attempted rape, the fact that it happened at all in work settings is unacceptable and deeply alarming.

Factors noted to have significant associations with sexual harassment were junior position and not having children (p<0.05). Junior doctors have been previously shown to have more frequent experiences of sexual harassment and, as discussed, this may result from imbalances of power.16 20 The reason as to why women without children were reported to have more experiences of sexual harassment is unclear, but it may stem from the cultural pedestal on which mothers are placed in Sri Lankan society.19 Additionally, it is possible that women without children are more likely to be assigned more frequent night shifts, increasing exposure to instances of harassment. Age is unlikely to be a confounding factor for the above two variables, as age by itself was not found to be associated with workplace sexual harassment.

Adverse mental effects resulting from sexual harassment affect not only the survivor but also their socio-professional networks, leading to great economic costs. A previous study showed that the negative psychological effects of sexual harassment have negative physical effects in the long run, as the propensity for diseases like hypertension increases with stress.21 Compounding this health economic cost is the fact that a considerable number of sexually harassed female doctors engaged in actions that affected their careers in the form of transferring, giving up postgraduate training, taking a career break, migrating or quitting medicine. All these would have had either temporary or permanent effects on the healthcare workforce of the country, notwithstanding the equally important personal psychological effects of these on the survivors that ranged from anger, disappointment and shame to even suicidal thoughts or attempts.

The fact that only very few had filed formal reports merits much-needed discussion. Mechanisms for workplace complaints vary by institution, and in many settings, formal reporting pathways are rarely used or perceived as ineffective, despite recent efforts to instate such initiatives following mainstream media coverage of sexual violence in healthcare settings.11 15 Of those who were raped in the workplace, none had filed formal reports. Reasons cited for the lack of action included fear of victim blaming, fear of not being taken seriously and fear of tarnishing self-reputation, reflecting the sociocultural context that requires women to suffer in silence.22 While the Sri Lankan law recognises sexual harassment as a crime and some hospitals have institutional reporting pathways, it is important to note that these measures do not suffice.13 The established systems need to take into account the survivor psyche and the sociocultural barriers that usually prevent women from accessing these services. Factors which deter females from acting against sexual harassment should be carefully considered by policymakers and health managers who wish to rectify the problem. When reformative measures and reporting pathways are developed, it is important for confidentiality to be maintained and for the complainant to be free from victim-blaming.1

A call to action that was published in the Lancet calls for instituting these basic safety mechanisms at the workplace to improve the safety of female physicians.15 It appears from the findings of our study that there are, in fact, many modifiable fronts that can be immediately addressed to improve female physician safety. Indeed, almost all factors that contributed to perceptions of lack of safety, such as inadequate lighting, lack of curtains and locks and unavailability of male chaperones, are all easily and immediately rectifiable and must be noted by policymakers.

Strengths

The study engaged with a range of medical cadres, with responses from junior doctors through board-certified specialists and representation of female doctors from diverse healthcare settings. The main strength of this study is its revelation that sexual harassment in the workplace is common among female doctors and that it remains under-reported. The study also highlights important sociocultural and environmental factors associated with the perpetration of sexual harassment in the workplace, which may serve as points of action in driving change.

Limitations

The generalisability of our findings is limited due to convenience sampling, the inability to determine a denominator for the response rate and the fact that the survey primarily reflects the experience of junior and mid-level doctors. Selection bias is also likely to contribute, as survivors of harassment are more likely to have participated in a survey on sexual harassment compared with those who have no experience of harassment. Third, while previous studies have a higher reported frequency of sexual harassment in male-dominated specialties such as surgery, the present study did not disaggregate its participants in this manner to make similar observations.

Conclusions

Workplace sexual harassment is commonly reported among female doctors in Sri Lanka, and many reported feeling physically and sexually unsafe in the workplace. Our findings identify sociocultural and modifiable environmental factors associated with sexual harassment. At present, there do not appear to be effective means of redress for survivors of harassment, and our findings may help to guide improvement of workplace safety measures.
