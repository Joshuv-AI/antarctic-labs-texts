Unignorable carbon sequestration potential of afforested forests in the breadbasket of the Tibetan Plateau
Authors: Xinyao Xie, Wei Zhao, Yong Wang, Huaan Jin, Jiujiang Wu, Yanqing Yang, Xiaodan Wang, Zhao-Liang Li
Published: 2026

The Middle Reach of the Yarlung Zangbo River (MRYZ) plays a crucial role as the primary grain-producing region of Tibet, and it is also known as the breadbasket of the Tibetan Plateau. However, this region is confronted with significant environmental challenges, primarily driven by its cold and arid climate, compounded by the exacerbating effects of human activities. One of the most pressing issues is aeolian desertification, which leads to the loss of soil and vegetation cover due to wind erosion, threatening both agricultural productivity and ecosystem stability. To address these issues, China has launched a series of large-scale afforestation programs in the region, including the Project of Grain to Green, the Project of Two Rivers and Four Streams, and the Ecological Safety Barrier Protection and Construction Plan for Xizang. These efforts have focused on mitigating desertification, improving soil conditions, enhancing vegetation greenness, and restoring ecological balance, resulting in notable improvements in land quality and agricultural productivity. Beyond these expected benefits, long-term afforestation may yield unforeseen ecological benefits, particularly with respect to carbon sequestration, which could significantly contribute to climate change mitigation and bolster regional carbon storage.

Carbon sequestration studies have traditionally concentrated on forest ecosystems with favorable climatic conditions.1 Due to their high biomass and large carbon storage capacity, these ecosystems are widely recognized as major contributors to the global terrestrial carbon sink. In contrast, arid and semi-arid regions such as the MRYZ have received far less attention, largely due to the assumption that harsh environmental conditions limit their potential for carbon sequestration, although recent studies suggest that carbon storage in global drylands may have been substantially underestimated.2 Afforestation in these regions can significantly alter local hydrothermal dynamics, reducing surface temperature, improving soil water retention, and enhancing vegetation growth, thereby promoting carbon sequestration. However, it may also intensify water stress by depleting deep soil moisture and groundwater and reducing downstream river flows, especially under high-density plantations of non-native species. Within this context, we explore whether long-term afforestation in this dry valley can mitigate desertification and promote carbon accumulation. Our findings highlight the broader significance of hydrological trade-offs associated with afforestation and underscore the need to reassess its carbon sequestration potential in water-limited regions.

Ecological transformation mechanism for carbon sequestration

Afforestation in the MRYZ has driven a profound ecological transformation that progressively mitigates desertification and modifies local hydrothermal conditions,3 thereby influencing regional carbon sequestration potential. To quantify these changes, we identified afforested and non-afforested areas across the seven counties (i.e., Sangzhuzi, Rinbung, Nyemo, Chushur, Gonggar, Danang, and Nanmulin) traversed by the MRYZ. Forest cover outside the afforested areas is very limited, and the surrounding non-afforested landscapes are mainly composed of shrublands and sparse grasslands. Using the MOD17A2 product, we estimated the regional gross primary productivity (GPP) for afforested and non-afforested areas by averaging the values of all pixels within each area. The annual GPP in afforested areas was found to increase from 200.01 gC m−2 year−1 in 2001 to 254.25 gC m−2 year−1 in 2025, representing an increase of 27.1%, whereas surrounding non-afforested landscapes increased by 15.0%. However, the contrast between afforested and non-afforested areas may also reflect differences in vegetation composition and stand age rather than afforestation effects alone. To establish a more robust benchmark, we further evaluated biomass carbon accumulation in the MRYZ against that of planted forests across China using the same age-biomass equation.4 Carbon density in afforested areas rose by 20.72 Mg C ha−1 between 2005 and 2020, exceeding the national average increase of 15.50 Mg C ha−1 observed in planted forests across China during the same period.4 This sustained biomass accumulation has substantially increased regional carbon storage, which was estimated at 2.79 Tg C in 2025 and projected to reach 4.82 Tg C by 2060, even without further afforestation.

We argue that these changes arise from a cascading ecological transformation in which desertification suppression and hydrothermal regulation enhance ecosystem productivity, while a self-reinforcing socio-ecological feedback sustains afforestation expansion and continued gains of carbon storage (Figure 1).

Desertification mitigation and soil stabilization

The first stage involves the suppression of aeolian desertification through large-scale afforestation. Extensive tree planting along the MRYZ has effectively curtailed the expansion of desertified land and reshaped the regional ecological landscape. As vegetation cover expands, restoration patterns shift from isolated patches toward more continuous and stable vegetation belts that act as ecological barriers against wind erosion. These shelterbelts reduce near-surface wind speed, interrupt sand transport pathways, and stabilize sandy soils along riverbanks and adjacent farmland. Meanwhile, the expansion of root systems enhances soil structure and reduces surface erosion, strengthening ecosystem resilience. Through these processes, afforestation effectively suppresses desertification dynamics and establishes the ecological foundation necessary for sustained ecosystem recovery and carbon accumulation.

Regulation of local hydrothermal conditions

As afforestation expands and vegetation cover reaches a sufficient spatial extent, its ecological effects extend beyond vegetation recovery to the regulation of regional hydrothermal conditions. Increased plant biomass alters the surface energy balance by lowering surface albedo and enhancing latent heat flux through transpiration, thereby moderating land surface and near-surface air temperatures. At the same time, expanding vegetation enhances evapotranspiration, atmospheric moisture recycling, and soil water retention capacity. These changes alleviate key ecological constraints in cold and arid river valleys, including excessive evaporation, water stress, and thermal extremes. As hydrothermal conditions improve, ecosystem stability and environmental carrying capacity are substantially strengthened, creating favorable conditions for sustained vegetation growth and ecosystem productivity. Nevertheless, under the strong wind disturbance and high evaporative demand characteristic of alpine dry valleys, afforested ecosystems may still face degradation risks if long-term management and maintenance are insufficient.

Emergence of a self-reinforcing socio-ecological feedback

As ecological benefits become increasingly evident, the afforestation program enters a phase characterized by positive socio-ecological feedbacks. Improvements in soil conditions, local climate regulation, and other ecosystem services can enhance the recognized value of afforestation, thereby strengthening policy commitment and sustaining public support. In turn, continued institutional support and ecological restoration may facilitate further expansion of vegetation cover, contributing to additional gains in carbon sequestration capacity and ecosystem functioning. These improvements may also yield broader, though indirect, co-benefits for surrounding human systems, such as agriculture and rural livelihoods. This stage reflects the consolidation of a reinforcing dynamic in which ecological recovery and social support mutually sustain longer-term landscape transformation.

Prospects for sustainable restoration and carbon sequestration enhancement

Our results show that long-term ecological restoration has enabled this once-overlooked dry valley to become an increasingly important region for carbon accumulation, as reflected in sustained increases in vegetation productivity and regional carbon storage. Observed increases in GPP and carbon density likely reflect both afforestation and concurrent climate variability, including regional warming, shifts in precipitation seasonality, and higher atmospheric aridity, which shape photosynthesis, water use efficiency, and biomass accumulation. Using an age-biomass equation, we projected trajectories of carbon storage through 2060 in drought-prone river valleys. To more rigorously attribute the observed gains, future work should integrate process-based dynamic vegetation models and apply matched-control designs alongside difference-in-differences estimation to quantify the relative contributions of afforestation and climate variability to carbon storage and ecosystem productivity.

Generally, long-term persistence of carbon accumulation depends on the ecological stability of restored systems. In this cold, arid environment, restoration has often relied on artificial plantations that may have relatively low species diversity and thus remain vulnerable to extreme drought, pest outbreaks, wind disturbance, and progressive deep-soil moisture depletion. If plantation water demand exceeds local hydrological carrying capacity, restored forests may experience growth decline, dieback, or even ecosystem reversal, which would weaken or negate earlier carbon gains. Therefore, projections of continued carbon accumulation should not be treated as guaranteed outcomes but rather as conditional on sustained ecosystem resilience under future climate stress. From a management perspective, maintaining restoration benefits in the MRYZ will require greater emphasis on ecosystem quality rather than only planting extent. Measures such as reducing excessive stand density, promoting mixed-species restoration, matching species composition to local hydrothermal conditions, and strengthening long-term monitoring of vegetation and soil moisture may help improve plantation stability and carbon permanence. Given the pronounced spatial heterogeneity of the MRYZ, restoration strategies should also be adapted to local topographic and microclimatic conditions to enhance both ecological sustainability and the reliability of long-term carbon sequestration.5

Conclusion

In the MRYZ, afforestation substantially enhanced ecosystem productivity, with GPP increases nearly twice those in surrounding non-afforested landscapes. Carbon density also increased faster than the national average for planted forests in China, and regional forest carbon storage is projected to reach about 4.8 Tg C by 2060 even without further afforestation. These results show that long-term afforestation in cold and arid landscapes can drive cascading ecological transformations that enhance regional carbon accumulation. By reducing wind erosion, regulating local hydrothermal conditions, and strengthening ecosystem resilience, afforested dry valleys can gradually develop into important carbon sinks. As climate change intensifies and ecological restoration expands globally, the role of dryland afforestation in climate mitigation should be evaluated cautiously, with explicit consideration of hydrological constraints and ecological trade-offs.
