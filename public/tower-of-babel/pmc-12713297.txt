Establishment of non-invasive diagnostic prediction model for HIV-positive asymptomatic neurosyphilis patients
Authors: Hongjing Guan, Chunmiao Zou, Xiaoyun Di, Jingli Peng, Zihao Xia, Qin Wang, Rentian Cai, Chen Chen, Cong Cheng, Hongxia Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough guidelines strongly recommended universal screening for neurosyphilis (NS) in people living with HIV (PLWH), the invasiveness of lumbar puncture (LP) made comprehensive screening difficult, particularly among asymptomatic individuals, increasing the risk of missed diagnoses. Because of the common transmission routes, the co-infection rate of PLWH and syphilis was extremely high. Establishing effective diagnostic and predictive models for NS was essential to improve diagnostic accuracy and timeliness. This study aimed to develop a non-invasive method for detecting asymptomatic neurosyphilis (ANS) in PLWH.MethodsThis study included HIV patients hospitalized at the Second Hospital of Nanjing from January 2013 to September 2024 who tested positive for syphilis antibodies and underwent their first LP to screen for NS. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputations, and the dataset was randomly divided into training and testing groups at a 7:3 ratio. Independent risk factors were identified through logistic regression and LASSO regression analyses. Model performance was evaluated using ROC, sensitivity, specificity, calibration curve, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curve (CIC). Reliability was further assessed in subgroups defined by the time interval between HIV and syphilis detection, syphilis treatment, and HIV treatment.ResultsA total of 544 participants were included. Logistic regression and LASSO regression analyses identified variables for the diagnostic prediction model, including age, sero-TRUST, viral load (VL), CD4/CD8 ratio, CD4%, and MRI-lacunar infarction. Both the test model (AUC: 0.75) and the training model (AUC: 0.80) demonstrated acceptable discrimination and good robustness across subgroups. Calibration curves and Hosmer–Lemeshow tests indicated good calibration. DCA and CIC showed clear clinical benefit of the model.ConclusionThe diagnostic model achieved an AUC greater than 70 for detecting ANS in PLWH, with good robustness confirmed by calibration and DCA, as well as subgroup analyses. This model can assist clinicians in identifying ANS in PLWH without requiring LP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12153-1.

Introduction

Neurosyphilis (NS) was caused by Treponema pallidum invading the nervous system, leading to damage of the meninges, brain parenchyma, spinal cord, or blood vessels. If untreated, it could result in serious complications or even death. Because HIV and syphilis shared identical transmission routes, co-infection rates were high [1, 2]. A retrospective study in the United States reported that about 1.8% of people living with HIV (PLWH) eventually developed syphilis [3]. In another study of 7083 cases, the incidence of syphilis was 2.1% in PLWH compared with 0.6% in HIV-negative individuals [4, 5].

NS patients could be categorized as symptomatic or asymptomatic depending on neurological manifestations. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities were observed in 42% of symptomatic syphilis patients [6]. Among 167 PLWH with syphilis, 8–9% developed symptomatic neurosyphilis (SNS), while the majority remained asymptomatic [7–9]. These findings highlighted the importance of accurate NS diagnosis.

Although guidelines strongly recommended universal screening for NS in PLWH [10], the invasive nature of lumbar puncture (LP) made it difficult to implement comprehensive screening in PLWH with positive blood syphilis, particularly in asymptomatic individuals, thereby increasing the risk of missed diagnoses. To improve diagnostic accuracy and timeliness, it was necessary to establish an effective diagnostic and predictive model for NS.

Sero-TRUST titers were consistently identified as a significant risk factor for NS in both HIV-positive and HIV-negative populations. Prior studies showed that higher sero-TRUST levels were associated with greater risk of NS [11, 12]. NS most commonly manifested as meningeal vascular syphilis, which was the predominant type in China and a major cause of stroke in young individuals. However, MRI findings of syphilitic vasculitis resembled those of cerebral infarction, making differentiation difficult [13]. For asymptomatic PLWH with syphilis who declined LP screening, imaging examinations might aid in predicting NS risk.

This study aimed to comprehensively analyze the clinical characteristics and laboratory results of PLWH with syphilis, construct a reliable diagnostic and predictive model for NS, and compare it with a basic model to evaluate its effectiveness. The model incorporated sero-TRUST, CSF white blood cell count (CSF-WBC), and CSF protein levels (CSF-PRO). A secondary goal was to assess the model’s reliability in different populations, using the time interval between HIV infection and syphilis occurrence as a subgroup variable.

Methods

Source of data

This was a single-center retrospective study. Data were collected retrospectively from January 2013 to September 2023 and prospectively from October 2023 to September 2024 from PLWH admitted to the Second Hospital of Nanjing who tested positive for syphilis antibodies and underwent their first lumbar puncture (LP) for neurosyphilis (NS) (hereinafter referred to as initial screening NS patients).

Participants

The study focused on NS patients who underwent initial screening at the Second Hospital of Nanjing. Patients with syphilis were treated with penicillin; those with penicillin allergy received ceftriaxone as a substitute.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① Age ≥ 18 years; ② PLWH; ③ Positive syphilis antibody in blood; ④ First LP screening for NS.

Exclusion criteria: ① Other central nervous system diseases (excluding HIV-related encephalopathy); ② Missing key clinical data [including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) non-Treponema pallidum antibody titers (CSF-TRUST), CSF Treponema pallidum antibody titers (CSF-PPA), CSF protein levels (CSF-PRO), CSF white blood cell count (CSF-WBC)], with two or more items missing; ③ NS-related clinical symptoms (e.g., memory loss, weakness or numbness in both lower limbs, dizziness, headache, blurred vision, hearing or visual impairment).

Outcome

Patients were grouped according to CSF test results, classified as NS or non-NS.

Predictors

Data collection

Clinical data were collected, including age, clinical stage of syphilis, syphilis treatment status, ART, diabetes, cerebral infarction, and hypertension. Laboratory data for ANS patients included CD4 + T lymphocytes, CD4/CD8 ratio, CD4% (CD4 + T lymphocytes/CD45 + T lymphocytes), baseline viral load (VL), sero-TRUST, CSF-WBC, CSF-PRO, CSF-TRUST, and CSF-TPPA.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Second Hospital and complied with medical ethics requirements (ethics approval number: 2023-LS-ky-033). Clinical trial registration was not applicable. The study design followed the TRIPOD reporting criteria, provided in the Supplementary Material: Table 1.

Related definitions

NS diagnostic criteria were based on the Chinese guidelines [10] and met ① and ② and/or ③: ① Sero-TRUST and/or sero-TPPA positive; ② CSF-TRUST positive and/or CSF-TPPA positive; ③ CSF-PRO ≥ 500 mg/L and/or CSF-WBC ≥ 20 × 106/L.

Definition of invasive and non-invasive indicators.

Invasive indicators: Laboratory data obtained through LP, including CSF-TRUST, CSF-TPPA, CSF-PRO, and CSF-WBC.

Non-invasive indicators: All other indicators not requiring LP.

PLWH: Defined as individuals with positive serum HIV antibody.

Sample size

Using PASS software, the expected sample size was estimated to be 351 under conditions of α = 0.05 and confidence = 0.9. Based on prior findings that PLWH with syphilis accounted for about 20–30% of NS, we calculated using 30%. Considering a 20% deletion rate, 118 cases were assigned to the experimental group and 233 cases to the control group.

Missing data

Patients with missing data in two or more key variables (CSF-TRUST, CSF-TPPA, CSF-PRO, CSF-WBC) were excluded. Cases with severe data loss were deleted, defined as: ① Missing data across multiple dimensions; ② Missing two or more key variables (CSF-TRUST, CSF-TPPA, CSF-WBC, CSF-PRO); ③ Inability to determine NS status. For the remaining patients, missing data were addressed using multiple imputations.

Statistical analysis methods

Different analytical methods were applied according to data type. Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s) and compared using the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range (Q25-Q75) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared using Fisher’s exact test. To reduce bias from missing data, the generalized linear model multiple imputation method was applied. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify non-invasive indicators associated with ANS. Correlation testing among covariates was conducted to avoid collinearity, with a variance inflation factor (VIF) > 5 indicating collinearity. Variables were examined in the training dataset and validated in the testing dataset. Variable selection was performed using the forward stepwise method, with a threshold of 0.2. Data were split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. These methods were consistent with TRIPOD recommendations. Multivariate logistic regression results were used to construct a nomogram diagnostic prediction model. Model evaluation included the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index. The DeLong test was used to compare AUC differences between models. Calibration was assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and calibration curve. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) were used to evaluate clinical utility. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio 4.3.0 with the following R packages: CompareGroups, autoReg, rms, pROC, rmda, dplyr, and caret. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants baseline characteristic

From January 2013 to September 2024, 718 PLWH at the Second Hospital of Nanjing tested positive for syphilis antibodies and underwent their first LP. Clinical symptoms were observed in 142 patients, and 16 patients were diagnosed with syphilis and other central nervous system infections. After excluding these, 544 asymptomatic HIV-infected individuals were included. Among them, 108 (10.9%) were diagnosed with NS.

Most screened patients were male (98.6%) with a median age of 34 years (IQR: 29–46). Among 164 patients, 30.1% had negative sero-TRUST results, 48.1% had titers between 1:1 and 1:8, and 21.8% had titers ≥ 1:16. A history of cerebral infarction was present in 2.4%. In addition, 41.5% had received syphilis treatment, 49.6% had received ART, and 42.8% were first diagnosed with syphilis antibodies at the time of HIV diagnosis. After excluding cases with severe data loss, 9.9% still had missing data, including sero-TRUST titers (20 patients), CSF-WBC (2 patients), CSF-PRO (4 patients), and CD4, CD4/CD8 ratio, and CD4% (54 patients).

Model development

Missing data were processed using the generalized linear model multiple imputation method. The imputed dataset was randomly divided into training (n = 380) and testing (n = 164) groups in a 7:3 ratio. No significant differences were observed between groups after randomization. Logistic regression analysis and column chart of ANS in PLWH

Before model construction in the training set, VIF was used to test multicollinearity among non-invasive indicators, and no correlation was detected. Logistic regression analysis was then applied to identify factors influencing ANS. P < 0.2 was used as the threshold for stepwise elimination to allow potentially meaningful variables to be retained.Variables with P ≤ 0.2 in univariate analysis-including sero-TRUST, contemporaneous CD4/CD8 ratio, MRI-lacunar infarction, HIV treatment, and contemporaneous VL-were included in multivariate analysis. Results showed that sero-TRUST, contemporaneous CD4/CD8 ratio [OR: 0.07 (0.01–0.38), P = 0.002], contemporaneous CD4% [OR: 4.93 (1.85–13.15), P = 0.002], and MRI-lacunar infarction [OR: 6.94 (1.35–35.60), P = 0.020] were significant predictors of NS. Details are provided in Table 2.

LASSO regression screening model variables and nomogram construction

Although collinearity analysis has been performed before model analysis, the logistic model is susceptible to collinearity, and in order to more accurately identify the influencing factors for the diagnosis and prediction of neurosyphilis, we used LASSO regression for variable screening. The specific screening results are shown in Fig. 2, and the minimum lambda value of 0.023 was used to filter the variables based on cross-validation. LASSO regression analysis showed that age, MRI-Lumen infarction, SERO-TRUST, percentage of CD4 + T lymphocytes during the same period, CD4/CD8 ratio in the same period, and VL in the same period were all influencing factors for the occurrence of neurosyphilis. The multiple regression model was used to create a nomogram to identify the independent factors that affect ANS occurrence. Scores were assigned to each predictive factor based on the magnitude of its impact on the outcome. Figure 3 provides details on how the total score was calculated to predict the chance of ANS occurrence for each individual.

The relationship between syphilis in blood antibody titers and the incidence of neurosyphilis

Correlation analysis showed that higher sero-TRUST titers were associated with a greater incidence of neurosyphilis among patients initially screened. As shown in Supplementary Material: Fig. 2, this correlation was statistically significant (r = 0.96, P < 0.01).

Model performance

The diagnostic model incorporated CD4/CD8 ratio, CD4 + T-cell percentage, history of cerebral infarction, and sero-TRUST titer. Its performance for predicting ANS was evaluated against CSF-WBC, CSF-PRO, and sero-TRUST using ROC curves. In the training set, the AUC was 0.75, indicating acceptable discrimination. Significant differences were found among the four models (P = 0.01). Pairwise comparison showed that the AUC of the training model was second only to CSF-PRO, with a significant difference between them, whereas no significant difference was observed between the model and CSF-WBC or sero-TRUST (P > 0.05). In the testing set, the AUC was 0.80, not inferior to CSF-PRO, CSF-WBC, or sero-TRUST (P = 0.25). Results are shown in Fig. 3.

Model performance: calibration curve

The calibration curve of the ANS noninvasive diagnostic prediction model was closely aligned with the 45° reference line in both the training and testing sets. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed no significant difference between predicted and observed outcomes in either cohort, indicating good model calibration. See Supplementary Material: Fig. 3 for details.

Model performance: decision curve analysis

DCA demonstrated the effectiveness of the model in predicting ANS in clinical practice. Within a threshold probability range of 0.10–0.80, the clinical net benefit from intervention guidance based on the predicted probability of the training model in the ANS diagnostic prediction model exceeded that of intervening in all individuals (All) or in none (None) (Fig. 4-A). Within 0.10–0.70, intervention guidance based on the predicted probability of the logistic model provided a higher clinical net benefit than intervention for All or None (Fig. 4-B). Within 0.05–0.78, guidance based on the predicted probability of CSF-PRO yielded a greater net benefit than All or None interventions (Fig. 4-C). Within 0.10–1.00, guidance based on CSF-WBC predictions offered higher net benefit than All or None (Fig. 4-D). Within 0.05–0.40, guidance based on sero-TRUST predictions surpassed All or None interventions in net benefit (Fig. 4-E).

Model performance: clinical impact curve (CIC)

To more intuitively reflect the potential clinical impact of the model, the CIC visually displayed the number of individuals expected to be correctly or incorrectly diagnosed, treated, or classified at different thresholds. The model’s predicted outcome was represented by the red line, while the actual number of events was represented by the blue line.

For the TRAIN-MODEL of the ANS model, at a risk threshold of 0.30, approximately 200 individuals were predicted to be at risk, while the actual number was about 100. Predictions and actual occurrences were highly aligned for thresholds above 0.30, indicating high clinical predictive effectiveness (Supplementary Material: Fig. 4-A). The logistic model of the ANS predicted around 200 individuals at risk at a threshold of 0.30. Beyond this threshold, predicted and actual outcomes were closely aligned, demonstrating high clinical predictive effectiveness (Supplementary Material: Fig. 4-B).

For the CSF-PRO of the ANS model, at a 0.30 threshold, approximately 300 individuals were predicted to be at risk, while 250 were actually at risk. Predictions became highly accurate for thresholds above 0.30, reflecting strong clinical predictive performance (Supplementary Material: Fig. 4-C).

The CSF-WBC model predicted about 250 individuals at risk at a 0.30 threshold, whereas 180 were actually at risk. Alignment between predictions and actual outcomes improved above this threshold, indicating high clinical predictive effectiveness (Supplementary Material: Fig. 4-D).

For SERO-TRUST, at a 0.40 threshold, approximately 200 individuals were predicted at risk, while only 100 were actually at risk. Predictions and actual outcomes were highly aligned above this threshold, demonstrating strong clinical predictive performance (Supplementary Material: Fig. 4-E).

Model validation in different subgroups

To assess the robustness of the model, we performed validation in seven subgroups. The AUC of the model was < 0.7 in all populations, except for individuals treated for syphilis and HIV, where the AUCs were 0.7, and the sensitivity remained moderate or above. Although the AUC was slightly below 0.7 in the syphilis- and HIV-treated populations, it was close to 0.7, indicating that the model could also be applied in these groups. AUC values and sensitivity thresholds for both populations are shown in Table 3. Sensitivity and specificity for each subgroup are provided in Supplementary Material: Table 4.

Sensitivity analysis

Because the interpolation model used information from all 544 patients, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to avoid inflated performance caused by random grouping after interpolation. We interpolated separately within the training and test datasets and then evaluated model performance. The discrimination performance of NS remained strong in the training dataset (AUC: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.70–0.83) and the testing dataset (AUC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.74–0.89). To avoid overly idealized results from a 7:3 split, we applied Bootstrap to randomly segment the samples. After testing 1,000 randomly selected samples, the distribution of segmented samples was approximately normal (Supplementary Material: Fig. 1). The AUC calculated from Bootstrap-segmented samples was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.53–0.98), showing no difference from the training dataset.

Discussion

Since HIV and syphilis share transmission routes, their incidence is significantly higher than in HIV-negative individuals [14]. ANS usually required diagnosis via lumbar puncture (LP); however, its invasiveness often led patients to refuse screening. Our study focused on developing and validating a non-invasive diagnostic model of syphilis antibodies for ANS in PLWH. Compared with the basic model, the constructed model exhibited strong performance. Its effectiveness was further confirmed through multiple methods, including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, goodness-of-fit test, calibration curve, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curve (CIC). Additionally, the robustness of the model was validated across different subgroups.

Previous studies have shown that the incidence of neurosyphilis varies by region, patient group, and diagnostic criteria. PLWH are at substantially higher risk of developing neurosyphilis [15–19]. In our study of 554 asymptomatic PLWH, the prevalence of ANS was 18.75%, consistent with other reports. These findings highlight the importance of considering LP examination for asymptomatic PLWH with positive syphilis serology to detect neurosyphilis early. Our analysis indicated that elevated serum TRUST titers in asymptomatic PLWH with positive syphilis correlated with increased risk of neurosyphilis, consistent with previous research [20]. Logistic regression further showed that patients with a 4-fold increase in SERO-TRUST titers ≤ 1:2 had progressively higher risk of developing neurosyphilis. These results suggest that future studies should examine the relationship between SERO-TRUST titers and neurosyphilis risk in greater detail and explore additional biological or clinical factors to more accurately predict and prevent neurosyphilis.

Approximately 38.5% of neurosyphilis cases involve meningeal vascular syphilis, which is the main manifestation of the disease [21]. This form is the most common type in China and represents an important cause of stroke in young adults. In this study, among 108 neurosyphilis patients identified from asymptomatic PLWH with syphilis in the blood, 6.48% (7/108) were diagnosed with cerebral infarction (mainly lacunar infarction) via MRI or CT. Logistic regression analysis indicated that cerebral infarction was a significant risk factor for ANS, with a 7.93-fold higher risk compared to patients without cerebrovascular disease. Notably, patients with lacunar infarction were asymptomatic. Lacunar infarctions are primarily caused by vascular lesions, characterized pathologically by focal arterial wall dilation and small hemorrhagic extravasation [22]. In clinical practice, subtle lesions may lead to missed diagnoses. Since neurosyphilis typically involves small- and medium-sized vessels, imaging examinations can serve as a predictive tool for neurosyphilis risk in PLWH with syphilis who are unwilling to undergo LP.

Following HIV infection, individuals are susceptible to opportunistic infections and tumors [23]. Khalil et al. reported that when peripheral blood CD4 + T lymphocyte counts fell below 350 cells/µL and serum RPR titers were ≥ 1:32, the likelihood of developing neurosyphilis in PLWH was 18.6 times higher than in typical PLWH [24]. Jianhua Yu et al. noted that individuals with CD4 + counts >200 cells/µL had a lower risk of neurosyphilis [1]. Although the theory that syphilis–HIV coinfection increases neurosyphilis risk remains debated, many experts recommend CSF examination in all HIV-infected syphilis patients to exclude neurosyphilis, a recommendation that differs across guidelines. European guidelines [25] state that “the risk of eye and nervous system involvement does not increase in HIV-positive patients with early syphilis; therefore, CSF assessment for early syphilis is indicated only in patients with overt ocular, auditory, or neurological symptoms.” They also note that, although lacking strong evidence, some experts recommend CSF assessment for asymptomatic patients to exclude ANS. Similarly, CDC guidelines [26] recommend that all HIV-infected individuals with stage I or II syphilis undergo thorough neurological, ocular, and auditory examinations, reserving CSF evaluation for those with abnormal neurological findings. Overall, despite differences in emphasis, diagnostic approaches for neurosyphilis are largely consistent across guidelines.

Li et al. established a non-invasive predictive model for diagnosing asymptomatic neurosyphilis using peripheral blood indicators instead of CSF biomarkers, achieving an AUC of 0.806 (95% CI, 0.732–0.832) [27], which was not significantly different from the AUC in this study. Compared with other studies, the model validation in this study was more comprehensive.

Regarding model performance, the training model achieved an AUC of 0.712, with a sensitivity of 0.877 and specificity of 0.443. The test model had an AUC of 0.764, sensitivity of 0.665, and specificity of 0.756. Differences in AUC between the two groups were assessed using the Delong test [28]. Although sensitivity decreased compared with other basic models, specificity increased, helping to reduce false-positive rates in identifying ANS. The model can therefore assist in the rapid and accurate diagnosis of neurosyphilis, reducing missed cases. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test [29]. Comparing DCA results with other basic models, the clinical benefit of this model was lower than that of the CSF-PRO and CSF-WBC models but higher than that of the sero-TRUST model, indicating its potential clinical value.

This study aimed to develop and validate a diagnostic prediction model for asymptomatic PLWH with positive syphilis antibodies, facilitating identification of neurosyphilis without lumbar puncture. The study comprehensively examined associations between factors such as CD4/CD8 ratio, sero-TRUST titer, and cerebral infarction with ANS occurrence, providing new insights into neurosyphilis pathogenesis. The study emphasized imaging examinations, particularly for identifying lacunar cerebral infarction, as a tool to predict neurosyphilis risk in asymptomatic PLWH with syphilis, offering novel ideas for clinical diagnosis.

When patients sought medical care, doctors could input specific values based on patient characteristics using the provided EXCEL table to calculate the probability of neurosyphilis. A column chart could also be used to display patient characteristics, allowing incorporation of multiple variables to estimate neurosyphilis risk.

When the predicted probability exceeded 50%, doctors were advised to recommend lumbar puncture and neurosyphilis screening and to initiate targeted treatment promptly. If the probability was below 50%, doctors still attempted to persuade patients to undergo lumbar puncture. If patients refused, they were instructed to undergo blood syphilis Treponema pallidum antibody titer testing after one month to monitor neurosyphilis risk. If the probability increased compared to the previous visit, lumbar puncture was indicated for screening.

This study had several limitations. First, HIV patients were generally recommended for neurosyphilis screening upon syphilis diagnosis, resulting in a relatively small sample with serum TRUST titers ≥ 1:256. This may have affected consistency with previous studies and limited the generalizability and reliability of the findings. Second, Bootstrap sampling was used for repeated validation of 544 individuals, but differences in AUC between training and testing datasets could reflect overfitting or data leakage from multiple interpolation methods. Third, the included and excluded populations differed in clinical characteristics, though the impact of this bias on results was unclear. Fourth, non-traditional biomarkers, such as neurofilament light chain protein and IL-10, were not included, and no suitable external controls were available, limiting model generalizability. Future research should prospectively collect samples to detect these biomarkers and seek external cohorts to improve model sensitivity, specificity, and applicability. The model is currently applicable to populations with characteristics similar to those in this study. Expanding sample size, enhancing data collection methods, and incorporating additional influencing factors could improve accuracy and generalizability. Furthermore, integrating predictive models with techniques such as artificial intelligence and big data analysis may enhance neurosyphilis diagnostic accuracy. Although the model performed well in internal validation, lack of external independent dataset validation limited its applicability to broader populations. Future external validation could enhance the model’s widespread applicability.

Conclusion

This study indicated that age, SERO-TRUST, VL, CD4/CD8 ratio, CD4 + T lymphocyte percentage, and MRI-detected lacunar infarction were key factors for predicting NS.

Based on ROC curves, calibration curves, DCA, and CIC results, the training models for ANS demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability and potential clinical net benefit. The model showed good robustness across subgroup analyses and feasibility, as confirmed by validation in different populations. Moreover, it provides a theoretical basis for clinicians to identify HIV/NS patients and introduces novel concepts and approaches for the early detection and precise management of neurosyphilis. Although the model has substantial clinical application value, further research and external validation are required before broad implementation in practice.
