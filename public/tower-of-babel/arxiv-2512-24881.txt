Totally compatible structures on the radical of an incidence algebra
Mykola Khrypchenko

Abstract
. We describe totally compatible structures on the Jacobson radical of the incidence algebra of a finite poset over a field. We show that such structures are in general non-proper.

16W99; 16S50; 16N20

Introduction

Two algebraic structures from a variety
\mathcal{A}
defined on a same vector space are said to be
compatible
if their sum (equivalently, any linear combination) belongs to
\mathcal{A}
.
Originated in the 70’s
[
14
]
, compatibility of Lie
[
8
,
9
]
associative
[
4
,
17
]
and Poisson
[
18
,
2
,
3
]
algebras has been actively studied in the context of mathematical physics. Recently, it has attracted the interest of scientists from other areas of mathematics who expanded the investigation of compatibility to the varieties of
L_{\infty}
-algebras
[
5
]
, Leibniz algebras
[
15
]
, pre-Lie algebras
[
1
]
, anti-pre-Lie algebras
[
16
]
, left-symmetric algebras
[
23
]
, Hom-Lie algebras
[
6
]
, Hom-Lie triple systems
[
22
]
and so on. In particular,
[
15
,
1
,
13
]
provide algebraic classifications of low-dimensional compatible algebras.

Given a structure
\mu
from
\mathcal{A}
, it is natural to ask: can one explicitly describe all the structures from
\mathcal{A}
(on the same vector space) that are compatible with
\mu
? This question has been previously studied
[
4
,
17
]
for the variety of associative algebras. Recall that the compatibility of two
associative
bilinear products
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
on a vector space
V
is equivalent to the following identity on
V
:

(1)

\displaystyle(a\cdot_{1}b)\cdot_{2}c+(a\cdot_{2}b)\cdot_{1}c=a\cdot_{1}(b\cdot_{2}c)+a\cdot_{2}(b\cdot_{1}c).

In general, fixing
\cdot_{1}
, it is technically difficult to give a complete description of
\cdot_{2}
satisfying
eq.

1
, although there are some natural classes of such products associated to
\cdot_{1}
.

In this context, it seems to be more reasonable to study particular cases of
eq.

1
that are based on equalities of pairs of monomials. More precisely, let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\{1,2\}
. If the
i
-th monomial of the left-hand side of
eq.

1
equals the
\sigma(i)
-th monomial of the right-hand side of
eq.

1
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are said to be
\sigma
-matching

[
24
]
(in the case
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
the triple
(V,\cdot_{1},\cdot_{2})
is called a
matching dialgebra

[
26
]
, also known as
As^{(2)}
-algebra

[
28
]
). If all the
4
monomials in
eq.

1
are equal, then
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are
totally compatible
(see
[
27
,
25
]
). In
[
11
]
, inspired by
[
21
]
, we introduced another similar notion, called
interchangeability
, which means that the operations
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
can be permuted in each of the monomials in
eq.

1
without changing the brackets. If
A
is
unital
(or in certain sense close to being unital), then it is easy to see that
\sigma
-matching, interchangeable and totally compatible structures on
A
are simple modifications of the original associative product on
A
(see
[
11
]
).

The situation becomes more interesting in the context of
nilpotent
associative algebras, where new classes of compatible structures arise. In
[
12
]
we characterized
\sigma
-matching, interchangeable and totally compatible structures on the strictly upper triangular matrix algebra
UT_{n}(K)
,
n\geq 3
, which is a classical example of a nilpotent associative algebra. Notice that
UT_{n}(K)
is the
Jacobson radical
of the algebra
T_{n}(K)
of
all
upper triangular
n\times n
matrices over
K
, which is in turn a particular case of the
incidence algebra

I(X,K)
of a
finite
poset
X
over
K
. So, our next goal was to generalize the results of
[
12
]
to the Jacobson radical
J(I(X,K))
of
I(X,K)
. However, having classified the
\mathrm{id}
-matching structures on
J(I(X,K))
in the first draft of this manuscript, we gave up on the idea to proceed to
(12)
-matching and interchangeable structures, since the classification turned out to be too complex. We thus focused only on
totally compatible
structures that are the main object of study in this article.

In
section

1
we collect all the needed definitions and preliminary results about totally compatible structures and incidence algebras to make the paper self-contained.

In
section

2
we prove a couple of short general results whose particular cases will be used below.

In
section

3
we introduce
annihilator-valued
structures and study their relationship with structures
*_{\varphi}
determined by centroid elements (known from
[
11
]
). As a result, we arrive at the definition of a
proper
totally compatible structure
*
, which essentially means that, up to an annihilator-valued component,
*
is determined by a centroid element. The motivation comes from
[
11
,
12
]
, where most of the algebras had only totally compatible structures of this type. In
3.14
, we prove that the class of proper totally compatible structures is closed under isomorphisms and antiisomorphisms.

Having prepared all the necessary general background, in
section

4
we focus on the algebra
J(I(X,K))
and first describe in
4.7
its centroid, which is itself an interesting object of study. It is then used in
4.8
to give a description of proper totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
.

Finally, in
section

5
we prove our main result
5.12
, which gives a complete description of totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
. Annihilator-valued structures again play an important role in this case, but, instead of
*_{\varphi}
, we obtain a more general class of structures that behave locally as
*_{\varphi}
. In
5.13
, we find a sufficient condition on
X
under which all the totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
are proper.
5.15
shows that non-proper totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
may indeed exist, and
5.15
demonstrates that the condition from
5.13
is not necessary. We then give

that illustrates the situation when all the totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
are annihilator-valued. In fact, this happens if and only if
X
has length at most
2
, as proved in
5.19
. We leave as an open problem to describe finite posets
X
such that all the totally compatible structures on
J(I(X,K))
are proper.

1.
Definitions and preliminaries

Throughout the paper
K
will be an arbitrary field. All the algebras and vector spaces will be over
K
and all the products will be
K
-bilinear. The dot product will be sometimes omitted, i.e.
ab
will mean
a\cdot b
. As usual, the symbol
\circ
will be reserved for the composition of two maps.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra. The
annihilator
of
A
is the ideal

\displaystyle\Ann(A,\cdot)=\{a\in A:a\cdot b=b\cdot a=0\text{ for all }b\in A\}.

We will often write simply
\Ann(A)
, when it is clear what product is meant. Given subsets
B,C\subseteq A
, we denote

\displaystyle B\cdot C=\spn_{K}\{b\cdot c:b\in B,c\in C\}.

We may write
B^{2}
for
B\cdot B
, when the product on
A
is clear from the context. The
centroid
of
A
, denoted
\Gamma(A)
, is the space of linear maps
\varphi:A\to A
such that

(2)

\displaystyle\varphi(a\cdot b)=a\cdot\varphi(b)=\varphi(a)\cdot b

for all
a,b\in A
.

1.1.
Matching compatibilities and interchangeability

Generalizing
[
11
, Definition 1.1]
(inspired by
[
24
]
) we say that
two (not necessarily associative!) products
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
on a same vector space
V
are

(i)

\sigma
-matching
(where
\sigma\in S_{2}=\{\mathrm{id},(12)\}
), if

(3)

\displaystyle(a\cdot_{1}b)\cdot_{2}c=a\cdot_{\sigma(1)}(b\cdot_{\sigma(2)}c)\text{ and }(a\cdot_{2}b)\cdot_{1}c=a\cdot_{\sigma(2)}(b\cdot_{\sigma(1)}c);

(ii)

interchangeable
if

\displaystyle(a\cdot_{1}b)\cdot_{2}c=(a\cdot_{2}b)\cdot_{1}c\text{ and }a\cdot_{1}(b\cdot_{2}c)=a\cdot_{2}(b\cdot_{1}c);

(iii)

totally compatible
, if

(4)

\displaystyle(a\cdot_{1}b)\cdot_{2}c=(a\cdot_{2}b)\cdot_{1}c=a\cdot_{1}(b\cdot_{2}c)=a\cdot_{2}(b\cdot_{1}c),

for all
a,b,c\in V
. Observe that
\sigma
-matching
associative
products are compatible in the sense of
eq.

1
, while interchangeable ones need not be compatible in general. Furthermore, notice that
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are totally compatible if and only if
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are
\sigma
-matching
for all

\sigma\in S_{2}
if and only if
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are interchangeable and
\sigma
-matching
for some

\sigma\in S_{2}
.

Given an
associative
algebra
(A,\cdot)
, by a
\sigma
-matching
(resp.
interchangeable
or
totally compatible
)
structure
on
(A,\cdot)
we mean an
associative
product
*
on
A
such that
\cdot
and
*
are
\sigma
-matching (resp. interchangeable or totally compatible). It is obvious that
\cdot
is a totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
. It is known that any
mutation

[
7
]

\cdot_{x}
of
\cdot
by
x\in A
, i.e.
a\cdot_{x}b=a\cdot x\cdot b
, is an
\mathrm{id}
-matching structure on
(A,\cdot)
. If, moreover,
x
is central, then
\cdot_{x}
is a totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

As we saw in
[
11
, Propositions 2.2 and 2.6]
, for a
unital
algebra
A
, any
\mathrm{id}
-matching (resp.
(12)
-matching or interchangeable) structure on
(A,\cdot)
is a mutation of
\cdot
by
x\in A
(resp. by a central
x\in A
). Consequently, any
(12)
-matching or interchangeable structure on a unital algebra
(A,\cdot)
is totally compatible. If
A
is non-unital (in particular, if
A
is
nilpotent
), all these compatibilities may be different (see
[
11
, Examples 1.4 and 1.5]
).

1.2.
Isomorphic and antiisomorphic structures

Let
*_{1}
and
*_{2}
be two
\sigma
-matching, interchangeable or totally compatible structures on an associative algebra
(A,\cdot)
. Following
[
11
,
12
]
, we say that an automorphism (resp. antiautomorphism)
\phi
of
(A,\cdot)
is an
isomorphism
(resp.
antiisomorphism
) between
*_{1}
and
*_{2}
, if

\displaystyle\phi(a*_{1}b)=\phi(a)*_{2}\phi(b)\ (\text{resp. }\phi(a*_{1}b)=\phi(b)*_{2}\phi(a))

for all
a,b\in A
. The structures
*_{1}
and
*_{2}
are
isomorphic
(resp.
antiisomorphic
), if there is an isomorphism (resp. antiisomorphism) between them. This means that
(A,\cdot,*_{1})
and
(A,\cdot,*_{2})
are isomorphic (resp. antiisomorphic) as algebras with two multiplications. Given a
\sigma
-matching, interchangeable or totally compatible structure
*
on
(A,\cdot)
and an automorphism (resp. antiautomorphism)
\phi
of
(A,\cdot)
, the product
\star
on
A
defined by

(5)

\displaystyle a\star b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)*\phi{}^{-1}(b))\ (\text{resp. }a\star b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)*\phi{}^{-1}(a)))

for all
a,b\in A
, is a structure on
(A,\cdot)
of the same kind as
*
, and it is isomorphic (resp. antiisomorphic) to
*
(see
[
12
, Lemma 1.2]
).

1.3.
Posets

Let
(X,\leq)
be a finite poset. As usual, we write
x<y
to mean
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
. The binary relations
\geq
and
>
are inverse to
\leq
and
<
, respectively. Two elements
x,y\in X
are said to be
comparable
, if
x\leq y
or
x\geq y
. A nonempty subset
C\subseteq X
is a
chain
, if any two elements
x,y\in C
are comparable. A chain
C\subseteq X
is
maximal
, if there is no chain properly containing
C
. The
length
of a chain
C\subseteq X
is
l(C):=|C|-1
. The
length
of
X
is

\displaystyle l(X):=\max\{l(C):C\text{ is a chain in }X\}.

Given
x\leq y
, define

\displaystyle l(x,y):=l(\{z\in X:x\leq z\leq y\}).

Denote by
\Min(X)
and
\Max(X)
the subsets of minimal and maximal elements of
X
, respectively. Finally, write

\displaystyle X^{n}_{<}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X^{n}:x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}\}.

1.4.
Incidence algebras

Let
(X,\leq)
be a finite poset and
K
a field. The
incidence algebra

I(X,K)
of
X
over
K
(see
[
19
,
20
]
) is the associative
K
-algebra with basis
\{e_{xy}:x,y\in X,x\leq y\}
(called
natural basis
) and bilinear multiplication

e_{xy}\cdot e_{uv}=\delta_{yu}e_{xv}

for all
x\leq y
and
u\leq v
in
X
. Here and below
\delta
means the Kronecker delta. Given
f\in I(X,K)
, denote by
f(x,y)
the coefficient of
e_{xy}
in the linear combination

\displaystyle f=\sum_{x\leq y}f(x,y)e_{xy}.

The following formulas will be useful:

\displaystyle e_{xy}\cdot f=\sum_{v\geq y}f(y,v)e_{xv}\text{ and }f\cdot e_{xy}=\sum_{u\leq x}f(u,x)e_{uy}.

Recall from
[
20
, Theorem 4.2.5]
that the
Jacobson radical
of
I(X,K)
is

\displaystyle J(I(X,K))=\{f\in I(X,K):f(x,x)=0,\forall x\in X\}=\spn_{K}\{e_{xy}:x<y\}.

The following facts are well-known and easy to prove:

\displaystyle\Ann(J(I(X,K)))

\displaystyle=\spn_{K}\{e_{xy}:\Min(X)\ni x<y\in\Max(X)\},

\displaystyle J(I(X,K))\cdot J(I(X,K))

\displaystyle=\spn_{K}\{e_{xy}:l(x,y)>1\}.

To shorten some formulas, we will sometimes write
\mathcal{J}
for
J(I(X,K))
below.

2.
Some general properties of totally compatible structures

The following is clear.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{\cdot_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of bilinear products on a same vector space
V
. If
\{\cdot_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are pairwise
\sigma
-matching (resp., interchangeable or totally compatible), then any two finite linear combinations of
\{\cdot_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are
\sigma
-matching (resp., interchangeable or totally compatible).

The next result permits us to avoid unnecessary proofs of associativity in some cases.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative algebra and
*_{1}
,
*_{2}
two bilinear products on
A
, where
*_{1}
is associative. Assume that
\cdot
,
*_{1}
and
*_{2}
are pairwise
\sigma
-matching (resp. totally compatible). Then
*_{1}+*_{2}
is a
\sigma
-matching (resp. totally compatible) structure on
(A,\cdot)
if and only if
*_{2}
is associative.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the result for
\sigma
-matching structures.

The “only if” part.
Assume that
*_{1}+*_{2}
is a
\sigma
-matching structure on
(A,\cdot)
. In particular,
*_{1}+*_{2}
is associative. By
2.1
the products
*_{1}+*_{2}
and
*_{1}
are
\sigma
-matching, whence they are compatible. It follows that
*_{2}=(*_{1}+*_{2})-*_{1}
is associative.

The “if” part.
Assume that
*_{2}
is associative. Since
*_{1}
and
*_{2}
are
\sigma
-matching and associative, their sum
*_{1}+*_{2}
is also associative. By
2.1
the products
*_{1}+*_{2}
and
\cdot
are
\sigma
-matching. Thus,
*_{1}+*_{2}
is a
\sigma
-matching structure on
(A,\cdot)
.
∎

The following property is a particular case of total compatibility that will occur below.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
be two (not necessarily associative) products on a same vector space
V
. We say that
\cdot_{1}
and
\cdot_{2}
are
mutually annihilating
if

(6)

\displaystyle(V\cdot_{1}V)\cdot_{2}V=(V\cdot_{2}V)\cdot_{1}V=V\cdot_{1}(V\cdot_{2}V)=V\cdot_{2}(V\cdot_{1}V)=\{0\}.

3.
Proper totally compatible structures

Recall from
[
11
, Definition 2.4]
the following.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra and
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
. The product
*_{\varphi}
on
A
given by

(7)

\displaystyle a*_{\varphi}b:=\varphi(a\cdot b)

is said to be
determined
by
\varphi
.

Remark 3.2
.

In particular,
\cdot
is determined by
\mathrm{id}
.

The next fact was proved as a part of
[
11
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative algebra and
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
. Then
*_{\varphi}

is a totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

Let us introduce one more class of structures, which plays a particularly important role in the case of nilpotent algebras.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra. A bilinear product
\bullet
on
A
is said to be
annihilator-valued
(with respect to
\cdot
), if
\cdot
and
\bullet
are mutually annihilating.
An
annihilator-valued structure
on an
associative
algebra
(A,\cdot)
is an
associative
annihilator-valued bilinear product on
A
.

Remark 3.5
.

A bilinear product
\bullet
on
A
is annihilator-valued if and only if

(i)

A\bullet A\subseteq\Ann(A,\cdot)
;

(ii)

A\cdot A\subseteq\Ann(A,\bullet)
.

Remark 3.6
.

Any annihilator-valued product
\bullet
on
A
is totally compatible with
\cdot
.

Remark 3.7
.

An annihilator-valued product
\bullet
on
A
may be non-associative even if
(A,\cdot)
is associative. For example, consider
A
to be a null algebra, i.e.
A\cdot A=\{0\}
, so that
\Ann(A,\cdot)=A
. Then any bilinear product
\bullet
on
A
is annihilator-valued.

Remark 3.8
.

If
\Ann(A,\cdot)\subseteq A\cdot A
, then
A\bullet A\subseteq\Ann(A,\bullet)
, so any annihilator-valued product on
A
is associative, regardless of whether
(A,\cdot)
is associative.

Annihilator-valued products on
(A,\cdot)
admit the following constructive description.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra and
V
a vector space complement of
A\cdot A
in
A
. Then a bilinear product
\bullet
on
A
is annihilator-valued if and only if there exists a bilinear map
\mu:V\times V\to\Ann(A,\cdot)
such that

(8)

\displaystyle(a_{1}+a_{2})\bullet(b_{1}+b_{2})=\mu(a_{2},b_{2}),

where
a_{1},b_{1}\in A\cdot A
and
a_{2},b_{2}\in V
.

Proof.

The “if” part.
Let
\mu:V\times V\to\Ann(A,\cdot)
be a bilinear map and define
\bullet
by
eq.

8
. It is clear that
\bullet
is bilinear and
A\bullet A\subseteq\Ann(A,\cdot)
. Now, if
a\in A\cdot A
, then
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
, where
a_{1}=a\in A\cdot A
and
a_{2}=0\in V
. Hence,
a\bullet b=\mu(0,b_{2})=0
for any
b=b_{1}+b_{2}\in A
with
b_{1}\in A\cdot A
and
b_{2}\in V
. Thus,
(A\cdot A)\bullet A=\{0\}
. The proof that
A\bullet(A\cdot A)=\{0\}
is similar.

The “only if” part.
Let
\bullet
be an annihilator-valued product on
A
. Given
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
with
a_{1},b_{1}\in A\cdot A
and
a_{2},b_{2}\in V
, by
3.5
item

(ii)
we have

\displaystyle(a_{1}+a_{2})\bullet(b_{1}+b_{2})=a_{2}\bullet b_{2}.

So, we may define
\mu:V\times V\to\Ann(A,\cdot)
by
\mu(u,v)=u\bullet v
for all
u,v\in V
. Then
\mu
is bilinear and
eq.

8
holds.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra,
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
and
\bullet
an annihilator-valued product on
A
. Then
*_{\varphi}
and
\bullet
are mutually annihilating, in particular, totally compatible.

Proof.

For all
a,b,c\in A
we have

\displaystyle(a*_{\varphi}b)\bullet c=\varphi(a\cdot b)\bullet c=(a\cdot\varphi(b))\bullet c=0

by
3.5
item

(ii)
and

\displaystyle(a\bullet b)*_{\varphi}c=\varphi((a\bullet b)\cdot c)=\varphi(0)=0

by
3.5
item

(i)
. Symmetrically,
a*_{\varphi}(b\bullet c)=a\bullet(b*_{\varphi}c)=0
.
∎

Hence, as a consequence of
3.3
,
3.10
,
2.2
and
3.6
we get.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative algebra,
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
and
\bullet
an annihilator-valued product on
A
. Then
*_{\varphi}+\bullet
is a totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
if and only if
\bullet
is associative, i.e.
\bullet
is an annihilator-valued structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative algebra. A totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
is said to be
proper
, if it is of the form
*_{\varphi}+\bullet
, where
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
and
\bullet
is an annihilator-valued structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

In most cases studied before in
[
11
,
12
]
all the totally compatible structures turned out to be proper.

Example 3.13
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative algebra and
*
a totally compatible structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

(i)

If
A
is unital, then
*
is determined by
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)\cong C(A)
, whence
*
is proper (see
[
11
, Proposition 2.6]
).

(ii)

If
A
is null, then
*
is annihilator-valued, whence
*
is proper (see
3.7
).

(iii)

If
A
is the semigroup algebra of a rectangular band, then
*
is determined by
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
, whence
*
is proper (see
[
11
, Proposition 3.11]
).

(iv)

If
A

Proposition 3.14
.

The class of proper totally compatible structures on an associative algebra
(A,\cdot)
is closed under isomorphisms and antiisomorphisms.

Proof.

Let
*=*_{\varphi}+\bullet
, where
\varphi\in\Gamma(A)
and
\bullet
is an annihilator-valued structure on
(A,\cdot)
.

Any structure
\star
isomorphic to
*
is of the form
a\star b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)*\phi{}^{-1}(b))
for some
\phi\in\Aut(A,\cdot)
. Then for all
a,b\in A
we have

\displaystyle a\star b

\displaystyle=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)*\phi{}^{-1}(b))=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)*_{\varphi}\phi{}^{-1}(b)+\phi{}^{-1}(a)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(b))

\displaystyle=\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\cdot\phi{}^{-1}(b)))+\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(b))

\displaystyle=(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)+\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(b)).

Now observe that
\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1}\in\Gamma(A)
, because

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)

\displaystyle=\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\cdot\phi{}^{-1}(b)))=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\cdot\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)))

\displaystyle=a\cdot\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)))=a\cdot(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(b)

and similarly
(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)=(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a)\cdot b
. Furthermore, the bilinear product

a\blackdiamond b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(a)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(b))
on
A
is clearly associative, because
\bullet
is, and satisfies
A\blackdiamond A\subseteq\Ann(A,\cdot)
thanks to
3.5
item

(i)
and
\phi(\Ann(A,\cdot))=\Ann(A,\cdot)
. One also has

(A\cdot A)\blackdiamond A=A\blackdiamond(A\cdot A)=\{0\}
due to
3.5
item

(ii)
,
\phi{}^{-1}(A)=A
and
\phi{}^{-1}(A\cdot A)=A\cdot A
. Thus,
\blackdiamond
is annihilator-valued, so that
\star
is proper as being equal to
*_{\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1}}+\blackdiamond
.

If
\star
is antiisomorphic to
*
, then it is given by
a\star b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)*\phi{}^{-1}(a))
for some antiisomorphism
\phi
of
(A,\cdot)
. Similarly to the isomorphic structure, we have

\displaystyle a\star b

\displaystyle=\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)\cdot\phi{}^{-1}(a)))+\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(a))

\displaystyle=(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)+\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(a)),

where

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)

\displaystyle=\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)\cdot\phi{}^{-1}(a)))=\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b))\cdot\phi{}^{-1}(a))

\displaystyle=a\cdot\phi(\varphi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)))=a\cdot(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(b)

and
(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a\cdot b)=(\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1})(a)\cdot b
, so that
\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1}\in\Gamma(A)
. It is easily seen as above that
a\blackdiamond b=\phi(\phi{}^{-1}(b)\bullet\phi{}^{-1}(a))
is an annihilator-valued structure on
(A,\cdot)
. Thus,
*=*_{\phi\circ\varphi\circ\phi{}^{-1}}+\blackdiamond
is proper.
∎

4.1.

In order to characterize proper totally compatible structures on
(J(I(X,K)),\cdot)

. Let us introduce a class of centroid elements that will be one of the ingredients of the future description.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a (not necessarily associative) algebra. A linear map
\varphi:A\to A
is said to be
annihilator-valued
, if it satisfies

(i)

\varphi(A)\subseteq\Ann(A)
;

(ii)

\varphi(A\cdot A)=\{0\}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Any annihilator-valued linear map
\varphi:A\to A
belongs to
\Gamma(A)
, since all the products in

2
are zero.

Another class of centroid elements has its origin in
[
10
]
. Recall from
[
10
]
that a map
\sigma:X^{2}_{<}\to K
is said to be
constant on chains
if

\displaystyle\sigma(x,y)=\sigma(u,v),\text{ whenever }x<y\text{ and }u<v\text{ belong to a same chain in }X.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\sigma:X^{2}_{<}\to K
be constant on chains. Define the linear map
\varphi_{\sigma}:J(I(X,K))\to J(I(X,K))
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{xy})=\sigma(x,y)e_{xy},

for all

.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be constant on chains. Then
\varphi_{\sigma}\in\Gamma(J(I(X,K)))
.

Proof.

Let
x<y
and
u<v
. If

, then

\displaystyle e_{uv}\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{xy})=\sigma(x,y)e_{uv}e_{xy}=0=\varphi_{\sigma}(0)=\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{uv}e_{xy}).

If

, then
x<y
and
u<y
belong to the same chain

. Hence,
\sigma(x,y)=\sigma(u,y)
, so

\displaystyle e_{uv}\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{xy})=\sigma(x,y)e_{uv}e_{xy}=\sigma(x,y)e_{uy}=\sigma(u,y)e_{uy}=\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{uy})=\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{uv}e_{xy}).

Similarly one proves that
\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{xy})e_{uv}=\varphi_{\sigma}(e_{xy}e_{uv})

∎

Lemma 4.5

Let

item
