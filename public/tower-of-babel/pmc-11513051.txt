Combining NIR spectroscopy with chemometrics for discriminating naturally ripened banana and calcium carbide ripened banana
Authors: John‑Lewis Zinia Zaukuu, Sheila Mensah, Eric Tetteh Mensah, Florence Akomanin-Mensah, Solomon Wiredu, Zoltan Kovacs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium carbide is prohibited as a fruit ripening agent in many countries due to its harmful effects. Current methods for detecting calcium carbide in fruit involve time-consuming and destructive chemical analysis techniques, necessitating the need for non-destructive and rapid detection techniques. This study combined near infrared (NIR) spectroscopy with chemometrics to detect two banana varieties ripened with calcium carbide in different forms when they are peeled or unpeeled. Sixteen linear discriminant analysis (LDA) models were developed with high average classification accuracies for classifying banana based on the mode used to ripen banana, type of carbide treatment and the duration of soaking banana in carbide solution. Banana colour was predicted with partial least squared regression (PLSR) models with R2CV > 0.74, RMSECV and <5.4 and RPD close to 3. NIR coupled with chemometrics has good potential as a technique for detecting carbide ripened banana even if the banana is peeled or not.Subject terms: Agriculture, Infrared spectroscopy

Introduction

Fruits are essential and nutritionally indispensable food commodities that form a part of a balanced diet. They supply necessary growth regulating factors that are vital for good health and are mostly consumed in their ripened form1. Ripening is a highly coordinated, genetically programmed, and irreversible phenomenon. It involves a series of physiological, biochemical, and organoleptic changes2. Ripening is characterized by the production of ethylene, increased respiration rate, and fruit softening3. Fruit softening is predominantly caused by cell wall disintegration and decreased intercellular adhesion4. Although these changes make fruits edible and desirable, they also result in increased access of degradative enzymes to the fruit5. This ultimately leads to deterioration if fruits are not consumed on time. As a result, to control ripening and also meet the growing consumer demands, fruit dealers tend to harvest fruits in their pre-ripening stage and subject them to various forms of artificial or chemical ripening6.

Although artificial ripening helps to control and achieve uniform ripening, reduce crop losses, meet consumer demands, and maximize profits, safety concerns have been raised with regards to some chemicals employed for this purpose. Compounds frequently used to accelerate ripening are chemically synthesized ethylene, ethephon, methyl jasmonate, calcium carbide, ethereal, and ethylene glycol7. Calcium carbide (CaC2) is predominantly utilized in the artificial ripening of climacteric fruits such as bananas, mangoes, and papayas8. When in contact with moisture, CaC2 produces acetylene gas, which performs similarly to ethylene (natural fruit ripening plant hormone) and quickens the ripening process1,9. The use of calcium carbide in fruit ripening has been outlawed in many nations due to its adverse effects on health.

Commercial-grade CaC2 has been reported to contain traces of arsenic and phosphorus hydride10. Calcium carbide-induced ripening leads to the bioaccumulation of these residues in fruits. Arsenic and phosphorus poisoning results in symptoms such as diarrhoea, vomiting, burning sensations, and weakness. Also, in contact with the eye, calcium carbide can cause severe eye injury due to the presence of these elements. When consumed in large quantities, it causes disturbance to the neurological system by inducing prolonged hypoxia11. Furthermore, the consumption of fruits with arsenic has been reported to cause cancers of the blood (leukaemia), liver, skin, and lungs. Research has also shown that chronic arsenic exposure can affect the renal, cardiovascular, digestive, and respiratory systems12,13.

Despite the harmful effects of Calcium carbide, the practice of inducing ripening in fruits with this chemical remains rampant, especially in developing countries. Presently, there are no standardised methods for detecting calcium carbide-induced ripened banana9. Conventional methods used for detection include visual inspection which lacks precision and chemical analysis to detect traces of arsenic and phosphorus on or in fruits1. Though chemical analysis has good potential, it is destructive, time-consuming, and requires extensive sample preparation14. Therefore, there is a need to develop rapid and non-destructive detection techniques in the combat against chemical ripening.

With technological advancements, Near-Infrared (NIR) spectroscopy which is a rapid and non-destructive analytical method is widely been employed in place of destructive techniques. In the food industry, NIR spectroscopy combined with chemometrics is gaining popularity in food analysis, food safety, and adulteration detection as well as quality control and monitoring due to its noninvasive nature, minimal sample preparation requirement, inexpensive chemical reagents requirement, and portability9. NIR spectroscopy being a rapid and non-invasive technique when used at the sales or export/import point should be able to inform stakeholders if the banana was ripened naturally or with carbide, even without factoring colour change indexes because colour can be deceptive.

Therefore, this study sought to explore the feasibility of NIR spectroscopy combined with chemometrics for detecting banana ripened with calcium carbide. The objective of the work was to ascertain whether carbide ripened banana could be differentiated from naturally ripened one or from banana ripened in the dark.

Results

Physicochemical parameters

Total Soluble Solids

Total soluble solids is a fundamental parameter that indicates the maturity and quality of banana15. As bananas ripen, the total soluble solids increase. The level of ripeness is related to the sugar content in the fruit, which can be estimated through the refractive index for the sample as total soluble solids. From Fig. 1 which depicts the total soluble solids content of the banana samples, it could be observed that for both varieties, the naturally ripened banana was significantly different from the carbide-treated and dark ripened banana. The total soluble solid content was found to be comparatively higher in the carbide-ripened foreign banana samples than in the naturally ripened foreign banana. Naturally ripened foreign bananas recorded the lowest total soluble solids. For the local banana samples, the total soluble solid content was found to be comparatively low in the artificially ripened banana samples than in the naturally ripened banana samples. For the foreign variety, there were no significant differences among wrapped-induced ripened samples. Also, 3% w/v solution treated foreign samples irrespective of duration of soaking recorded similar total soluble solid content. Variation in the concentration of carbide caused to a lesser extent significant changes in foreign variety (Cavendish) banana compared to the local variety (Gros michel).

According to refs. 16 and 17, calcium carbide decreases the total soluble solids content of bananas as compared to natural ripening. The reaction of the breakdown of starch into soluble sugars is responsible for the increase in the total soluble solid values. Chemical ripening agents decrease the rate of starch breakdown, thus gives lower total soluble solid values than the naturally ripened banana. For this study, the local variety samples conform to this reported finding as TSS was lower in the carbide-treated samples. In contrast, it was observed that the carbide treatment recorded higher TSS than the naturally ripened banana.

Titratable Acidity

Titratable acidity is the amount of weakly bound hydrogen ions that can be released from acids. Titratable acidity results from the acid/base equilibrium of the fruit flesh and thus is linked to the ionic composition of banana pulp. Titratable acidity is an important indicator of banana fruit quality, since it is involved in the perception of sourness and sweetness18.The titratable acidity of banana samples ranged from 0.0% to 0.4% as shown in Fig. 2 and was similar to the findings by ref. 17, who reported a range from 0.02% to 0.38%. The titratable acidity was higher in all foreign variety (Cavendish) carbide-treated samples than the naturally ripened banana with the highest value recorded by samples treated with 2% carbide solution soaked for 10 minutes. All local variety (Gros michel) carbide-treated samples recorded high titratable acidity compared to the naturally ripened banana except those treated with 3% wrapped carbide and 2% solution soaked for 20 minutes.

Colour parameters

During the ripening process of banana, many changes occur and one of the major changes in the ripening process is the change in colour from green to yellow. The change from green to yellow is usually used as the ripening indicator for commercial and consumption purposes19. The L* represents the lightness and increase during ripening20. a* represents greenness to redness whereas b* represents blueness to yellowness. NIRS and chemometric results

Spectral analysis

The raw spectra of all 84 samples which were scanned with and without peels are shown in Fig. 6a. The samples consisted of two varieties of banana (local- Gros michel and foreign- Cavendish). For each variety 3 samples were naturally ripened, 3 dark ripened and the remaining 36 were subjected to different carbide treatments to induce ripening. The raw spectra obtained ranged from the wavelengths of 900 to 1700 nm.

NIR Spectra of all banana samples.

a shows the raw NIR spectra of all the banana samples. The red coloured lines are the spectra for banana samples without peels while the black lines are the spectra for banana samples with peels. b represents the pre-processed NIR spectra of all the banana samples. The red coloured lines are the spectra for banana samples without peels while the black lines are the spectra for banana samples with peels.

It was observed that all samples showed characteristic peaks around 1400–1500 nm. This could be attributed to the presence of C-H and O-H structures present in banana22. Although at this wavelength all samples showed a characteristic peak, samples without peels recorded high absorbance (1.5 to 2.0) compared to those with peels indicating differences in water structural patterns23. This difference was due to the high moisture content of the edible banana fruit compared to that of the peels. There was also an observed superimposition of the spectra obtained from the samples making it difficult to distinguish between individual samples by visual inspection hence the need to use chemometric techniques for extracting meaningful information from the spectra. Prior to chemometric analysis, to get rid of systemic noise and highlight variation among samples, the Savitzky-Golay pretreatment technique was used to smoothen the raw spectra to obtain the improved spectra illustrated in Fig. 6b.

Principal component analysis

PCA was performed for pattern recognition and data visualisation. As indicated in Fig. 7, two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 99% variation in the data. It was observed that there was a significant overlap between the two banana varieties indicating great similarity between the two varieties. From Fig. 7b, separation of samples with peels and those without peels was obtained indicating that the two category of samples had significant different spectra characteristics.

At the first stage of classification, LDA was utilized to distinguish between banana samples based on the method used to ripen the fruit as depicted in Figs. 8 and 9. To validate the models, leave-one out cross-validation was employed. For all models, two discriminant functions (roots 1 and 2) explained 100% of the observed variation between groups. However, overlapping was observed in the classes for all developed models in Fig. 8, which indicates that samples had similar characteristics. These similarities could make it challenging to notice differences in LDA plots, consequently, confusion tables were used to quantify such differences24.

It was noticed that using variety alone as a class was not sufficient to distinguish between samples based on the mode of ripening as significant overlaps were observed in these models (Fig. 8b and c). Therefore, additional models which considered the forms in which samples were scanned for each variety (with and without peels) were built for classification. It was realized that these models provided a better separation pattern for discrimination, high classification accuracies (Table 1) and confirms the visualisation pattern indicated in Fig. 7b. Figure 9 illustrates the improved separation pattern when models were developed for samples with or without peels.

The performance of the LDA models for classification based on the mode of ripening has been summarised in the confusion table presented in Table 1. The classification accuracies for models developed for all samples and for variety alone were low. They ranged from 45.91% to 73.28%. A high level of accuracy for classification (>85%) was recorded for models developed with the form of samples taken into consideration.

The misclassification level of model developed to classify all samples irrespective of variety or form was >50% which suggests low level of accuracy. The low classification accuracy confirms the great overlap region observed in Fig. 8a. 83.33% and 75% of naturally ripened and dark ripened samples were wrongly classified as carbide ripened respectively. However, in contrast to the high misclassification rates recorded for these classes in the model, the carbide class recorded a high accuracy of 96.06% with only 2.32% and 1.62% misclassified as dark and naturally ripened. This shows that the carbide class had a good sensitivity but low specificity.

Models developed for each variety recorded fairly good classification accuracies. 59.69% and 73.28% of foreign (Cavendish) and local variety (Gros michel) banana were correctly assigned to their ripening class. Despite the fairly good classification accuracies recorded, high misclassification rates were obtained for groups specified in the models. 50% of foreign variety samples ripened naturally were wrongly classified as either carbide (44.5%) or dark (5.5%). Also, 61.17% of samples ripened in the dark were misclassified as carbide-induced ripened samples. For the Gros michel variety, 44.5% and 33% of samples belonging to the classes dark and naturally ripened respectively were misclassified into carbide class. It could also be observed that the models were sensitive to the carbide class compared to the natural and dark classes as less than 10% of carbide-ripened samples were incorrectly classified.

In the models developed with the form in which samples were presented for scanning in both varieties, it was noticed that high classification accuracies were obtained for modelled classes (Table 1). Classification accuracies of 88.26% and 87.96% were recorded for foreign variety without peels and with peels respectively. All Cavendish samples without peels belonging to the natural class and those with peels belonging to the dark class were correctly assigned to their classes. 33.33% of foreign samples naturally ripened with peels were incorrectly classified as carbide ripened. The same misclassification level was obtained for dark-ripened samples without peels. The levels of accuracy in classifying peeled (without peels) and unpeeled (with peels) Gros michel samples were 87.96% and 100% respectively. This implies that all local samples with peels were correctly assigned to their designated class. A misclassification rate of 33% assigning dark-ripened samples as carbide-ripened ones was recorded for samples without peels. Only 2.8% of samples that were supposed to be in the carbide class were classified as natural samples. Lastly, for this model none of the naturally ripened samples were wrongly classified.

It was observed that there were no distinct separation patterns for the 3 classes (Fig. 10a). Another model was built to classify samples subjected to wrapped carbide treatment. For this model, designated classes were (1%, 2%, and 3% wrapped carbide treatments). There was a distinct separation of classes from each other as shown in Fig. 10b. Discussion

Carbide ripening did not significantly affect titratable acidity and total soluble solids. Based on mode of ripening, average classification accuracies of 88.26% and 87.96% were obtained for the peeled and unpeeled forms of the Cavendish variety, and 87.96% and 100%for the peeled and unpeeled forms of the Gros Michel variety. For type of carbide treatment, a good classification was not obtained for the solution-treated samples (accuracy of 49.22%). However, a good classification of the samples ripened with wrapped carbide was obtained (accuracy of 99.08%). Classification models developed to classify carbide solution-treated samples based on the duration of soaking recorded classification accuracies of 60.96%, 64.88%, 100%, 89.97%. The colour of banana samples were predicted with PLS models with R2 CV > 0.74 and RMSECV < 5.4. The RDP of the PLS models was less than 3.4. Also, colour was identified as a reliable physicochemical parameter for detecting chemically ripened banana. The change from green to yellow colour in bananas is an important indicator of ripening. Most consumers prefer bright yellow colour when selecting banana during grocery shopping. According to21 and16 carbide treatment significantly influences the colour of banana. The good prediction capacity of the PLS models in predicting colour of the banana indicates that colour can be a good indicator for detecting carbide-ripened banana.

NIR coupled with chemometrics has good potential as a technique for detecting carbide ripening. It will be prudent in subsequent studies, to study the entire maturation process in order to judge the maturity of bananas based on physical and chemical properties. Higher carbide concentrations and extended soaking durations can be considered in similar approach studies. Additionally, other spectroscopic techniques and machine learning methods can be explored to establish valid, reliable, and robust models for detecting carbide-ripened bananas on the market.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Unripe samples of the two mostly consumed varieties of banana (Musa acuminata), Gros Michel (referred to as the local variety among the inhabitants of Ghana and in this study), and Cavendish (referred to as the foreign variety) were obtained from the distribution point in a local market in Kumasi, Ghana, upon arrival from the farm. A total of 84 banana samples (42 for each variety) were used in this study. Industrial-grade calcium carbide (CaC2) was acquired from a local welding shop in Kumasi, Ghana. The CaC2 lumps were ground into powder and used to artificially ripen the bananas.

Sample preparation

The banana samples were divided into four groups: A, B, C, and D for each variety. Set A consisted of 3 banana samples allowed to ripen naturally, while Set B had 3 samples wrapped and ripened in the dark. These two sets were used as controls. The remaining samples were allocated to sets C and D based on the various calcium carbide treatments used to ripen the banana.

In Ghana, commercial vendors typically ripen fruits with calcium carbide either by wrapping calcium carbide in a newspaper, which is usually placed at the bottom of the box with water sprinkled on it and the fruits tightly packed or by soaking the fruits in calcium carbide solution for a few minutes, after which the fruits are air-dried and packed in an airtight container. These scenarios were simulated in Sets C and D.

Set C included 3 subgroups of 1%, 2%, and 3% (w/w) of carbide to banana, each consisting of three banana samples for each variety. For each concentration, the powdered carbide was weighed using an analytical balance and wrapped in paper with water sprinkled on each to facilitate the release of the acetylene gas.

Set D comprised 9 subgroups of 1%, 2%, and 3% (w/v) of carbide to water solutions. For each concentration, the duration of soaking was varied at 10, 20, and 30 minutes. After soaking, the samples were air-dried at room temperature before packing.

The carbide treated samples from Sets C and D, were sealed in bags to prevent the escape of acetylene gas. All the sets were left to ripen at room temperature until the peels turned yellow. Sets A and B recorded a ripening time of 96 and 72 hours respectively while the carbide-treated samples (Sets C and D) ripened within 48 hours. Table 5 summarizes the sample preparation method.

Physicochemical analysis

Physicochemical parameters (colour, titratable acidity, and total soluble solids) of the samples were determined using standard analytical methods. The various parameters were measured in triplicate for all samples.

Colour determination

For each set, banana samples were divided in two, with one part peeled and the other left with peel on. These two parts were then placed into separate petri dishes. The colour parameters were measured with a Chroma metre (Konica Minolta brand Chroma metre), model CR_400 with illuminate D65 and 2° angle of observation. The parameters were measured using the CEILAB (L*a*b*) colour system. Where L* ranging from 0–100 (0 = black, 100 = colourless) measured the clarity of the samples, a* ranging from −60 to +60 (−60 = green, +60 = red) measured the redness/green colour component of the samples and b* also ranging from −60 to +60 (−60 = blue, +60 = yellow) measured the yellow/blue colour component of the samples26. Both the peeled and unpeeled forms of the banana were assessed to evaluate the impact of the carbide ripening on the colour of the banana peels and the fruit itself.

Total soluble solids and titratable acidity determination

Ten (10) mL of distilled water was added to 5 grams of each of the banana samples which were mashed without peels. The samples were then centrifuged for 30 seconds and allowed to settle. 2 mL of the supernatant in the samples were pipetted into the pit of the refractometer (Atago’s Master Series, Japan), and the read button was pressed to determine the Total soluble solids (TSS) as according to15. Prior to the analysis, the refractometer was calibrated with deionized water. Also, the prism surface and cover were cleaned and recalibrated after the TSS determination for each sample.

For titratable acidity (TA) determination, 5 mL of the supernatant for each banana sample was pipetted into a conical flask and titrated against 0.1 N NaOH using phenolphthalein as an indicator to the first permanent pink endpoint. Statistical analysis

The data obtained for the physicochemical parameters were analysed using Microsoft excel and Palaeontological statistics software package for education and data analysis (PAST) version 4.03. Mean and standard deviation were calculated for the triplicate measures. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine significant differences among samples. The analysis was done at a confidence interval of 95%.

NIR spectroscopy and chemometric analysis

Experimental design

This study utilized data obtained through NIR spectroscopy. The data was preprocessed and analysed to visualize and recognize patterns using principal component analysis (PCA). Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was applied to differentiate among samples based on their mode of ripening. Samples categorized as carbide-treated were moved to level 2 where they were further classified based on the type of carbide treatment. The solution-treated samples were then moved to level 3 for classification based on the duration of soaking as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Flow chart of NIRS combined with chemometrics approach for detecting calcium carbide ripened banana.

NIR spectra acquisition

NIR spectra was obtained using a handheld spectrophotometer, NIR-S-G1 (Innospectra Co., Hsinchu, Taiwan) with the wavelength range of 900–1700 nm and a spectra resolution of 3nm27. The instrument was used to collect spectra of all the samples. The scanning was done in a way to cover the maximum surface of the fruit by taking three consecutive scans of each fruit at different surfaces. All 84 samples were scanned with peels on (unpeeled) and without peels on (peeled) resulting in of 252 (84 samples*3 consecutive scans) signature spectra for each. A total of 504 signature spectra thus, was used for chemometric analysis.

Chemometric analysis

Chemometric models were calibrated and validated using the R Studio Software Version 4.2.2 by the R core team28. The spectra data was imported into R and Savitzky-Golay smoothing pretreatment was applied on the raw spectra to remove systemic noise that may have occurred during spectra acquisition.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed for data visualization and pattern recognition. PCA reduces dimensions while retaining essential data through the computation of co-variances and eigenvectors, effectively showcasing data differences and similarities. It was also utilized to identify and eliminate outliers29. The process involves standardizing the data for each feature to have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1, computing the covariance matrix, calculating the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and selecting principal components based on the eigenvalues. The eigenvector with the highest eigenvalue is designated as the first principal component. Subsequently, the original data was transformed into a new subspace defined by these principal components. In this study, PCA was employed to uncover concealed patterns, similarities and disparities between the banana samples.

Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

LDA models were developed to classify banana samples according to three criteria; the mode of ripening (level 1), type of carbide treatment (level 2), and duration of carbide soaking (level 3).

Predictive capability of each LDA model was tested by dividing the dataset into calibration data (training) and validation data (prediction). The calibration data firstly, contained two-thirds of the data, consisting of the first and second replicate of each sample. The third replicate was tested against this calibration data. The validation was done in three-fold, to ensure that each replicate was used for calibration and validation at least once. The statistical parameters used to evaluate the performance of the LDA models were the recognition accuracy (%) and prediction accuracy (%). Recognition accuracy (%), represents the accuracy of calibration, whereas prediction accuracy (%), represents the accuracy of cross-validation (%). These were assessed through confusion matrices where, columns represented the actual class membership and the rows represented the predicted class membership.

LDA was selected because it can be used to reduce dataset dimensions and facilitate classification. It is particularly valuable for delineating differences in datasets by minimizing inter-class mean distances and decreasing intra-class variation. This enables LDA to establish a new axis that effectively segregates categories within the datasets. The choice to employ LDA in this study was based on its computational efficiency, interpretability, and proven performance within the field of chemometrics.

Partial Least Square Regression (PLSR)

The colour of the banana which serves as an indicator for detecting chemically-ripened bananas was predicted using PLSR. Four PLS models namely PLS-A, PLS-C, PLS-S, and PLS-W, were developed for this purpose. PLS-A was constructed with 504 spectra data to predict the colour of banana samples, while PLS-C developed to predict the colour of carbide-treated samples was constructed with 432 spectra data (144 samples). PLS-S and PLS-W were developed for solution and wrapped carbide samples based on 324 and 108 spectra data, respectively. These models were created in R by maximizing the covariance between the calibration and validation data sets. The predictive accuracy of the models was assessed using leave-one-sample-out cross-validation and in the same manner as LDA models. The performance of the PLS models was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Ratio of Deviation to Performance (RDP).
