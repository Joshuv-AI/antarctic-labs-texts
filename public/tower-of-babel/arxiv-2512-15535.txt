Effective Equations for a Compressible Liquid-Vapor Flow Model with Highly Oscillating Initial Density
Christian Rohde, Florian Wendt

Abstract
We derive and justify a new effective model for a compressible viscous liquid-vapor flow on a spray-like scale, i.e., for settings with a large number of phase boundaries. As a model on the detailed scale, we start from a parabolic relaxation of the Navier–Stokes–Korteweg system. We consider a sequence of initial data where the sequence of initial densities is assumed to be highly oscillating mimicking the high number of phase boundaries initially. Then, we consider a sequence of finite energy weak solutions corresponding to the sequence of initial data. Anticipating that the effective equations are found in the limit of infinitely many initial phase changes, we interpret the densities as Young measures and prove the convergence of the sequence of solutions to the effective model. The effective model consists of a deterministic part for the fluid’s hydrodynamic quantities and a kinetic equation for the limit Young measure encoding the mixing dynamics. By characterizing the Young measure with the corresponding cumulative distribution function, we rewrite the kinetic equation for the Young measure into a kinetic equation for the cumulative distribution function such that the resulting equations are accessible by standard approximation methods.



Keywords:
Navier–Stokes–Korteweg equations, compressible two-phase flow, homogenization, finite energy weak solution, Young measure.

Introduction

We consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
with regular boundary that is occupied by a homogeneous compressible viscous fluid that can occur in a liquid and a vapor phase.
We assume that the dynamics of this homogeneous two-phase fluid are given by the Navier–Stokes–Korteweg (NSK) equations
[
1
]
.
These equations model the two-phase fluid with a diffuse interface, i.e., the interface between the liquid and the vapor phase is assumed to be a small region of positive Lebesgue-measure and the fluid’s quantities vary possibly rapidly but smoothly over this region.
Let us assume that we are given some positive time
T\in(0,\infty)
and let us denote
\Omega_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
.
Then, the unknowns of the NSK equations are the fluid’s density
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and the fluid’s velocity
\mathbf{u}\colon[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
that obey

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0

\displaystyle\text{in }{\Omega_{T}},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla P(\rho)-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})-\kappa\rho\nabla\Delta\rho=0

\displaystyle\text{in }{\Omega_{T}},

subject to the initial conditions

\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho^{0},\quad\mathbf{u}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}^{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

for given functions
\rho^{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\mathbf{u}^{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In the sequel we impose no-slip boundary conditions.
Note that we can deliberately allow for the density to admit vacuum states because we use the framework of finite energy weak solutions in this work (cf. Definition
2.3
).
In (
1.1
), the parameters
\mu,\lambda>0
denote the constant viscosity coefficients,
\kappa>0
denotes the constant capillarity coefficient and
P\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
denotes the pressure function.
If the pressure function
P
satisfies
P(0)=0
and admits two positive constants
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
such that
P
is monotonically decreasing on
(r_{1},r_{2})
and monotonically increasing on
[0,r_{1}]\cup[r_{2},\infty)
, then we call
P
a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type.
For such a pressure function, we call the fluid’s state vapor, spinodal and liquid, if the density attains its value in
[0,r_{1}]
,
(r_{1},r_{2})
and
[r_{2},\infty)
, respectively.
In that sense, we consider the NSK equations to be able to describe phase transition effects if the pressure function
P
is given of Van-der-Waals type (cf. Figure
1
).
However, if the number of phase boundaries is very high (e.g. in a spray), the NSK model becomes computationally unfeasible due to the rapid oscillations in the fluid’s quantities as for instance in the density.

In this paper, we are concerned with the rigorous justification of an effective system for a compressible viscous liquid-vapor flow described by the NSK equations with a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type in precisely that regime.
This effective system describes the two-phase fluid on a larger scale, but keeps information from the detailed scale.
As we shall see in the sequel, such an effective model can be seen as an approximation of the NSK model with a highly oscillating initial density.
One possibility to find and rigorously justify such an equation is to investigate the propagation of initial density oscillations
[
5
,
16
,
28
,
30
]
.
More specifically, we start from a sequence of initial data
{(\rho_{n}^{0},u_{n}^{0})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
should display the number of phase boundaries that we have initially.
Due to the high number of phase boundaries, we expect that the sequence of initial densities
{(\rho_{n}^{0})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is highly oscillating between the vapor’s density and the liquid’s density.
After constructing an appropriate corresponding sequence of solutions
{(\rho_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, it is then reasonable to assume that the effective equations are found in the limit
n\to\infty
, i.e., in the limit where initially the number of phase boundaries tends to infinity.
Due to the hyperbolic character of the continuity equation
\eqref{NSK}_{1}
, we expect that oscillations present in the initial densities propagate in time and therefore, that the sequence of densities will not converge in any strong topology.
In order to obtain a closed limit system, the non-linear nature of the pressure function
P
forces us to interpret the density as a Young measure.
Thus, assuming for the moment that we are able to pass to the limit
n\to\infty
in the NSK equations, the effective model will consist of a deterministic part for the fluid’s hydrodynamic quantities and a kinetic equation for the Young measure that encodes the mixing dynamics and closes the model (cf. Theorem
3.2
).

With this methodology, a first rigorous homogenization results was obtained in
[
28
]
for the 1D compressible Navier–Stokes equations for one-phase fluids with constant viscosity coefficient, and a result for the 3D case with constant viscosity coefficients but keeping the analysis formal.
Concerning the density dependent viscosity case, this methodology was also used in
[
5
]
.
There, the authors justified rigorously a one-velocity Baer–Nunziato model as an effective model by assuming that the Young measure is initially given by a convex combination of two Dirac-measures.
More specifically, they showed by a weak-strong uniqueness argument that the kinetic equation propagates this specific structure in time and that the kinetic equation reduces to a Baer–Nunziato model.
In
[
2
]
the authors extended the methodology to a two-component flow where each component was allowed to admit its own pressure functions and its own constant viscosity coefficient.
This problem was also tackled in
[
3
]
with a semi-discrete approach.
At this point we also refer to
[
14
,
15
]
, where the authors justified a one-velocity Baer–Nunziato model as an effective model for a bubbly flow incorporating surface tension effects.
Concerning the 3D case with constant viscosity coefficients, similar results were obtained in
[
4
,
6
,
16
]
.
At this point we also refer to
[
22
]
for a homogenization result in terms of a cumulative distribution function and without the characterization of the Young measure as a convex combination of Dirac-measures.

However, all these results do not account for phase transition effects in the detailed scale model.
In the 1D case, a homogenization result that accounts for phase transition effects in the detailed scale model was recently obtained in
[
24
]
.
There, the detailed scale model was chosen as a non-local approximation of the NSK equations and the viscosity and capillarity coefficients were kept constant.
It is the goal of this paper to provide a similar result for the NSK equations in the 3D case generalizing in some sense the results in
[
16
]
.
However, the NSK equations
(
1.1
)
seem hardly accessible for the investigation of the propagation of initial density oscillations due to a third-order differential operator acting on the density in the momentum equation.
To overcome this remedy, we choose a parabolic approximation of the NSK equations
(
1.1
)
proposed in
[
17
]
, that we call the parabolic Navier–Stokes–Korteweg (PNSK) equations from now on.
This model substitutes the third-order differential operator by a first-order differential operator by introducing an additional unknown
c
that satisfies a parabolic equation.
More precisely, in the parabolic NSK model, the fluid’s dynamics are described by the density
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the velocity
\mathbf{u}\colon[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the order parameter
c\colon[0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla P(\rho)-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})-\alpha\rho\nabla(c-\rho)=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle\beta\partial_{t}c-\kappa\Delta c+\alpha(c-\rho)=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

subject to initial conditions

\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho^{0},\quad\mathbf{u}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}^{0},\quad c(0,\cdot)=c^{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1.3)

for given functions
\rho\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\mathbf{u}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
c\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and the boundary conditions

\mathbf{u}_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\nabla c\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0\quad\text{on}\quad[0,T]\times\partial\Omega.

(1.4)

Here, the quantities
\mu,\lambda,P
are defined as before and the coupling coefficients
\alpha,\beta>0
are positive constants.
Formally, we notice that in the relaxation limit
\alpha\to\infty
and
\beta\to 0
the PNSK equations
(
1.2
)
approach the NSK equations
(
1.1
)
.
This justifies to use
(
1.2
)
as an approximate system to
(
1.1
)
if
\alpha
and
\beta
is sufficiently large and small, respectively.
We refer to
[
17
]
for a numerical investigation of the PNSK equations and to
[
7
]
for a rigorous convergence result for the relaxation limit.
See also
[
13
]
for a rigorous convergence result for
\alpha\to\infty
in the case
\beta=0
.

Introducing the artificial pressure function

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty),\quad r\mapsto P(r)+\frac{\alpha}{2}r^{2},

(1.5)

we can rewrite the momentum equation
\eqref{NSK_parabolic}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla P_{\alpha}(\rho)-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})-\alpha\rho\nabla c=0.

In particular, for a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type, we can choose
\alpha
large enough, so that the artificial pressure function
P_{\alpha}
becomes monotonically increasing (cf. Figure

).

The monotonicity of
P_{\alpha}
renders the underlying first-order system hyperbolic and thus facilitates the numerical treatment of the PNSK equations, see

for more details.
However, in this work, we will not require the monotonicity of the pressure function

Investigating the propagation of initial density oscillations under
(
1.2
)
, we are able to rigorously justify a new effective model for a compressible viscous liquid-vapor flow that is modeled by the PNSK equations on the detailed scale.
In this effective model, we search for a Young measure
\nu
, an order parameter
c
and a velocity
\mathbf{u}
that satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nu+\mathrm{div}(\nu\mathbf{u})+\partial_{\xi}\bigl((\mathcal{Q}-\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})\xi\nu\bigr)+\bigl(\mathcal{Q}-\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}\bigr)\nu=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla\overline{P_{\alpha}}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})-\alpha\rho\nabla c=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle\beta\partial_{t}c-\kappa\Delta c+\alpha(c-\rho)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

with the closure

\displaystyle\rho(t,x):=\int_{[0,\infty)}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi),\quad\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x):=\int_{[0,\infty)}P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)

for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(t,x,\xi):=\frac{\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x)-P_{\alpha}(\xi)}{\lambda+2\mu}

for almost all
(t,x,\xi)\in\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}
and subject to the boundary conditions
(
1.4
)
and the initial conditions

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{0},\quad c(0)=c^{0},\quad\nu(0)=\nu^{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1.7)

for given functions
\mathbf{u}^{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
c^{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and a given Young measure
\nu^{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
(cf. Definition
3.1
).
We emphasize that the effective model
(
1.6
)
is a closed system for the unknowns
(\nu,\mathbf{u},c)
as the density
\rho
and the pressure function
\overline{P_{\alpha}}
are completely described by the Young measure
\nu
.

Our analysis justifying (
1.6
) as an effective model relies on an improved
a priori
estimate for the artificial pressure function
P_{\alpha}
and a weak continuity result for the effective viscous flux.
These results are known from the theory related to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations and form the heart of the proof for the global-in-time existence of finite energy weak solutions to the corresponding initial-boundary value problem (see e.g.
[
9
,
19
]
).
Since the PNSK equations admit the additional nonlinear contribution
\rho\nabla c
in the momentum equation, it is
a priori
unclear whether these results also hold for the PNSK equations.
As a mathematical novelty, we derive an improved pressure estimate (cf. Lemma
4.4
) and the weak continuity of the effective viscous flux (cf. Lemma
5.3
) for the PNSK equations by interpreting this additional term as a force term and exploiting parabolic regularity estimates.

In order to make the kinetic equation for the Young measure accessible for standard approximation methods, we switch our point of view by considering instead of the Young measure
\nu
its corresponding cumulative distribution function
f
that is defined for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
through

f(t,x,\xi):=\nu_{(t,x)}((-\infty,\xi])\quad\text{for any }\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

We are then able to show, that the kinetic equation on
\nu
implies a kinetic equation for

in the form

\partial_{t}f+\mathrm{div}\bigl(f\mathbf{u}\bigr)-\partial_{\xi}\Bigl(\xi f\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}-\xi\mathcal{M}[f]\Bigr)=0\quad\text{in }\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R},

(1.8)

with

\mathcal{M}[f](t,x,\xi):=\int_{[0,\xi]}\mathcal{Q}(t,x,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta)

(1.9)

for almost all
(t,x,\xi)\in\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}
and

\rho(t,x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}\xi\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\xi),\quad\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\xi)

(1.10)

for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
, where the integral with respect to
\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral with respect to
f(t,x,\cdot)
.
We emphasize that
\eqref{Introduction: Effective Equation}_{2}
,
\eqref{Introduction: Effective Equation}_{3}
and
(
1.8
)
together with
(
1.9
)
and
(
1.10
)
is a closed system for the unknowns
(f,\mathbf{u},c)
.

2
Admissible Pressure Functions and Weak Solutions

We shall work with the concept of finite energy weak solutions.
The global-in-time existence of finite energy weak solutions was shown for the isentropic compressible Navier–Stokes equations with constant viscosity coefficients in
[
19
]
for an adiabatic pressure exponent
\gamma>\frac{9}{5}
in spatial dimension three.
These results were then extended in
[
9
]
to pressure exponents
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
and in
[
12
]
for a class of non-monotone pressure functions.
In all these results, the growth rate of the pressure function towards infinity plays a crucial role, as it provides an
a priori
estimate for the density in an appropriate Lebesgue space.
In particular, this growth rate is an integral part of the definition of finite energy weak solutions.
Thus, before introducing the concept of finite energy weak solutions for the PNSK model, let us introduce the following definition with that we describe in the sequel concisely for which class of pressure functions our homogenization result applies.

Definition 2.1

(Admissible pressure function)
.

We call a function
P\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
admissible with growth rate
\gamma\in(1,\infty)
if

P\geq 0,\quad P(0)=0,\quad\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\frac{P^{\prime}(r)}{r^{\gamma-1}}=P_{\infty},

(2.1)

for some positive constant
P_{\infty}>0
.

Remark 2.2
.

The class of admissible pressure functions accounts for a broad variety of monotone and non-monotone pressure functions.
In particular, any pressure function of Van-der-Waals type satisfying the third condition in
(
2.1
)
for some
\gamma\in(1,\infty)
is admissible with growth rate
\gamma
.

For some admissible pressure function
P\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with growth rate
\gamma\in(1,\infty)
, we assign a pressure potential
W\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
via

\displaystyle W(r):=r\int_{1}^{r}\frac{P(z)}{z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z\qquad\text{for }z\in[0,\infty).

(2.2)

We readily verify that
W\in C^{0}([0,\infty))\cap C^{2}((0,\infty))
and that

\displaystyle P(r)=W^{\prime}(r)r-W(r)\qquad\text{for }r\in(0,\infty).

(2.3)

Furthermore, due to the third relation in
(
2.1
)
, we have that
W
satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle r^{\gamma}\leq c_{1}+c_{2}W(r),\quad|W(r)|\leq c_{3}+c_{4}r^{\gamma}\quad\text{for }r\in[0,\infty),

(2.4)

and some positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\in(0,\infty)
that do not depend on
r
.
By the same token, we have that
P_{\alpha}
defined as in
(
1.5
)
satisfies for
{\tilde{\gamma}}:=\max\{2,\gamma\}
the bounds

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}(r)\leq c_{5}+c_{6}r^{\tilde{\gamma}},\quad r^{\tilde{\gamma}}\leq c_{7}+c_{8}P_{\alpha}(r)\qquad\text{for }r\in[0,\infty),

(2.5)

and some positive constants
c_{5},c_{6},c_{7},c_{8}>0
that do not depend on
r
.

Let us now introduce the concept of finite energy weak solutions for the initial-boundary value problem to the PNSK equations
(
1.2
)
–
(

3
Main Result

In this section, we state our main result, which consists of a homogenization result for a sequence of finite energy weak solutions to the initial-boundary value problem
(
1.2
)
–
(
1.4
)
with highly oscillating initial densities.
In order to state it, we need the concept of Young measures to deal with the strong oscillations that we expect in the sequence of densities.
For a detailed exposition on the topic of Young measures or parametrized measures we refer to
[

For the sake of clarity, we describe in the following definition precisely what we understand by a Young measure in this work.

Definition 3.1

(Young measure)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain.
Then we call
\nu
a Young measure on
\mathbb{R}
parametrized over
Q
if
\nu
lies in the set

\mathcal{Y}(Q;\mathbb{R}):=\Bigl\{\nu\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{w}^{*}}(Q;\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}))\mid\nu_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\text{ for almost all }x\in Q\Bigr\}.

Some integrable function
f\in L^{1}(Q)
, where
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes a domain, induces a Young measure
\nu_{f}\in\mathcal{Y}(Q;\mathbb{R})
via

(\nu_{f})_{x}:=\delta_{f(x)}\quad\text{for a.e.}\quad x\in\Omega,

where
\delta_{f(x)}
denotes the Dirac-measure concentrated in
f(x)
, see e.g.
[
21
]
.
For the rest of this work, we will keep the notation
\nu_{f}
for the Young measure induced by some integrable function
f\in L^{1}(Q)
as above.
In order to ease the notation in the sequel, we denote for some Young measure
\nu\in\mathcal{Y}(Q;\mathbb{R})
and some test function
b\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
by
\langle\nu,b\rangle
the function

Q\ni x\mapsto\langle\nu_{x},b\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}(\xi).

Now we are ready to state the main result of this paper.

.

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\rho^{0}_{n}\|_{L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega)}+\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\mathbf{u}^{0}_{n}\|_{L^{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}-1}}(\Omega)}+\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|c^{0}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}<\infty,

n\in\mathbb{N}

)

Agreeing on the fact, that the compressible liquid-vapor flow is correctly described on the detailed scale by the NSK equations with a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type, we approximate first the NSK equations on this scale by the PNSK equations, where the approximation takes place in the regime

Then, we derive an effective system for the PNSK system in the following way.
We take a sequence of initial data

, where we expect the initial density sequence to be highly oscillating between the liquid’s and the vapor’s density mimicking the large number of phase boundaries initially.
Considering a sequence of finite energy weak solutions
{(\rho_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n},c_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to the PNSK equations
(
1.2
)
–
(
1.4
)
with initial conditions
{(\rho^{0}_{n},\mathbf{u}^{0}_{n},c^{0}_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we then anticipate that the effective system is found in the limit
n\to\infty
.
Theorem
3.2
tells us then, that, up to a subsequence, it is possible to perform this limit procedure leading to the effective system
(
1.4
)
,
(
1.6
)
,
(
1.7
)
.
As the PNSK equations approximate the NSK equations in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
and
\beta\to 0
, it would we very interesting to perform this limit in the effective system.
The resulting system of equations could then be viewed as a homogenized system for the NSK equations with highly oscillating initial density.
In this context a natural question would then be whether the homogenization limit commutes with the approximation limit
\alpha\to\infty
and
\beta\to 0
.
However, performing this limit in the effective system, even just by formal arguments, is rather delicate due to the non-linearity that is present in the quadratic correction term in the pressure function
P_{\alpha}
.
More specifically, this non-linearity causes the issue that we have in general

\langle\nu,\xi^{2}\rangle\neq\langle\nu,\xi\rangle^{2}=\rho^{2},

but to perform the formal limit
\alpha\to\infty
,
\beta\to 0
an equality in this relation is needed.

In order to make the effective system accessible for standard approximation methods, we rewrite the kinetic equation on
\nu
as a kinetic equation for the corresponding cumulative distribution function.
We obtain the following result as a consequence of our main result Theorem
3.2
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let the hypotheses and notations of Theorem
3.2
hold true.
Define for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
the cumulative distribution function
f(t,x,\cdot)
corresponding to
\nu_{(t,x)}
via

f(t,x,\xi):=\nu_{(t,x)}((-\infty,\xi])

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for almost all
(t,x)
, we have that
\xi\mapsto f(t,x,\xi)
is non-decreasing, right-continuous and satisfies

f(t,x,\xi)=0\quad\forall\,\xi\in(-\infty,0),\qquad\lim\limits_{\xi\to\infty}f(t,x,\xi)=1.

The analogous statements hold true for the cumulative distribution function
f^{0}
that is defined for almost all
x\in\Omega
via

f^{0}(x,\xi):=\nu^{0}((-\infty,\xi])

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(f\partial_{t}\psi+f\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\psi-\Bigl(\xi f\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}-\xi\mathcal{M}[f]\Bigr)\partial_{\xi}\psi\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{0}\psi(0,\cdot,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.7)

holds for any test function
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
with

\mathcal{M}[f](t,x,\xi):=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\int_{[0,\xi]}\mathcal{Q}(t,x,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta),

for almost all
(t,x,\xi)\in\Omega_{T}\times\mathbb{R}
and we have

\rho(t,x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}\xi\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\xi),\quad\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\xi)

for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
, where the integral with respect to
\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral with respect to
f(t,x,\cdot)
.

4
Uniform Estimates

Let the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
hold true and let
{(\rho_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n},c_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a sequence of finite energy weak solutions with initial data
{(\rho_{n}^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0}_{n},c^{0}_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to the initial-boundary value problem of the PNSK equations
(
1.2
)
–
(
1.4
)
.
The goal of this section is to provide some
a priori
estimates for the sequence of solutions
{(\rho_{n},\mathbf{u}_{n},c_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that are uniform in
n
.
These
a priori
estimates are crucial in order to exploit compactness arguments that enable us to pass to the limit
n\to\infty
and to extract a limit system stated in Theorem
3.2
.
We start with the
a priori
estimates that are provided by the energy dissipation (see
(
2.22
)
in Definition
2.3
).
In order to ease the notation, we make the following convention throughout this whole section.
For positive coefficients
\gamma,\mu,\lambda,\kappa,\alpha,\beta
, some positive time
T>0
and given initial data
\rho^{0}\in L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{u}^{0}\in L^{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}-1}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
c^{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, where
{\tilde{\gamma}}:=\max\{2,\gamma\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{0}>0
a generic constant that may vary from line to line, but only depends on

\displaystyle\|\rho^{0}\|_{L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega)},\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{L^{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}-1}}(\Omega)},\|c^{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},|\Omega|,\gamma,\mu,\lambda,\kappa,\alpha,\beta,T.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T,\mu,\lambda,\kappa,\alpha,\beta>0
and let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with regular boundary.
Assume that
P
is admissible with growth rate
\gamma\in(1,\infty)
and let
{\tilde{\gamma}}:=\max\{2,\gamma\}
.
Let initial data
(\rho^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0},c^{0})
be given satisfying the regularity
(
2.17
)
and let
(\rho,\mathbf{u},c)
denote a finite energy weak solution of
(
1.2
)
–
(
1.4
)
existing on
[0,T]
and emanating from
(\rho^{0},\mathbf{u}^{0},c^{0})
.
Then we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\rho}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(\Omega))}+\|\rho-c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}c\|_{L^{2}({\Omega_{T}})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(4.1)

Proof.

Since
(\rho,\mathbf{u},c)
is a finite energy weak solution, we have for almost all
t\in(0,T)
the estimate

\displaystyle E(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\mu|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}+(\lambda+\mu)|\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}+\beta|\partial_{t}c|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq E_{0}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0},

(4.2)

where we have used
(
2.4
)
and Hölder’s inequality to obtain the second estimate and
where
E(t)
and
E_{0}
are defined as in
(
2.11
)
and
(
2.13
)
, respectively.
From
(
4.2
)
, we deduce by using
(
2.4
)
and Hölder’s and Poincaré’s inequality

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\rho}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(\Omega))}+\|\rho-c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}c\|_{L^{2}({\Omega_{T}})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

∎

Now, we obtain some improved
a priori
estimates for
\rho
and
c
.
First, we control the mean value of
c
and thereby the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega))
-norm of
\rho
by exploiting the parabolic equation
(
2.20
)
.
Then, we conclude an improved
a priori
estimate for
c
by exploiting standard parabolic regularity estimates.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let the hypotheses and notations of Lemma
4.1
hold true.
Then we have for any
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{\Omega}c(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr|\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(4.3)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega))}+\|P_{\alpha}(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+1}}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{6{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+6}}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{3{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+3}}(\Omega))}+\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\nabla c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{6{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+6}}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(4.4)

Proof.

From
(
2.20
)
with
\varphi\equiv 1
, we conclude for any
t\in[0,T]
the relation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}c^{0}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\rho-c)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{\Omega}c^{0}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\rho^{0}-c)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where we have used the continuity equation in the last identity.
This implies

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{\Omega}c(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr|\leq\|c^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{\alpha T}{\beta}\|\rho^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\biggl|\int_{\Omega}c\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr|\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

With Gronwall’s inequality, we conclude
(
4.3
)
.
Combining
(
4.3
)
, Poincaré’s inequality and Lemma
4.1
yields

\displaystyle\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\tilde{\gamma}}(\Omega))}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

(4.5)

By
(
2.5
)
,
(
4.1
)
, Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
we conclude from
(
4.5
)
that

\displaystyle\|P_{\alpha}(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+1}}(\Omega))}+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{6{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+6}}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{3{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+3}}(\Omega))}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}.

Since
c
is the weak solution of the linear parabolic initial-boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \beta\partial_{t}c-\kappa\Delta c+\alpha c&=\alpha\rho\quad&&\text{in }(0,T]\times\Omega,\\
\nabla c\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}&=0\quad&&\text{in }[0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
c(\cdot,0)&=c^{0}\quad&&\text{in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

standard parabolic regularity results (see e.g.
[
8
, Chapter 7]
) yield

\displaystyle\|c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}\Bigl(\|\rho\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|c^{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\Bigr)\leq\mathcal{C}_{0},

where we have used
(
4.5
)
to obtain the second inequality.
From this inequality, we conclude by Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
that

\displaystyle\|\rho\nabla c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{6{\tilde{\gamma}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}+6}}(\Omega))}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0},

which completes the proof of
(
4.4
)
.
∎

The improved
a priori
estimate
(
4.4
)
allows us to control the term
P_{\alpha}(\rho)
by the initial data only in the space
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
.
This control is not appropriate for the limit procedure later on, as we can not guarantee that a sequence being uniformly bounded in this space is also equi-integrable (cf. Proposition
5.2
).
To overcome this, we derive some estimate that controls the term
P_{\alpha}(\rho)
by the initial data in the space
L^{\delta}({\Omega_{T}})
for some
\delta\in(1,\infty)
.
Note that a sequence being uniformly bounded in this space is in particular equi-integrable.
For the compressible Navier–Stokes equations with constant viscosity coefficients, such an estimate was derived by constructing an appropriate multiplier for the momentum equation with the Bogovskii operator (see
[
9
,
11
]
).
Let us recall at this point this operator including the properties that we will use in this work.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let
p,q,r\in(1,\infty)
.
Then there exists a bounded linear operator

\mathcal{B}\colon\text{\L}^{p}(\Omega):=\biggl\{\varphi\in L^{p}(\Omega)\mid\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=0\biggr\}\longrightarrow W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3}),

such that for any
f\in\text{\L}^{p}(\Omega)
, we have

\mathrm{div}(\mathcal{B}[f])=f\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega

(4.6)

and

\|\mathcal{B}[f]\|_{W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq c_{1}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

(4.7)

for some positive constant
c_{1}>0
only depending on
p
and
\Omega
.

Furthermore, if
f\in\text{\L}^{p}(\Omega)
satisfies

f=\mathrm{div}\mathbf{g}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)

for some
\mathbf{g}\in L^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
with
\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega}=0
, then we have

\|\mathcal{B}[f]\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq c_{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}

(4.8)

for some positive constant
c_{2}>0
that only depends on
p,r
and
\Omega
.

In particular, for

q\in\begin{cases}[1,\frac{3p}{3-p}]&\text{if }p\in[1,3),\\
[1,\infty)&\text{if }p=3,\\
\{\infty\}&\text{if }p>3,\end{cases}

we have for any
f\in\text{\L}^{p}(\Omega)
that

\|\mathcal{B}[f]\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq c_{3}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

for some positive constant
c_{3}>0
that only depends on
p,q
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

See
[
11
, Proposition 2.1]
.
∎

In contrast to the compressible Navier–Stokes equation, we have an additional term

present in the momentum equation, such that it is not clear whether the approach in
[

11
]
transfers to the PNSK equations.
However, by Lemma

]
is applicable for the PNSK equations.
We obtain the following result for which we provide a complete proof in order to show that the approach of
[

Lemma 4.4
.

Let the hypotheses and notations of Lemma

hold true.
Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}P_{\alpha}(\rho)\rho^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\mathcal{C}_{0},

(4.9)

and in particular

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{{\tilde{\gamma}}+\theta}({\Omega_{T}})}+\|P_{\alpha}(\rho)\|_{L^{\delta}({\Omega_{T}})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0},

\displaystyle\theta:=\min\biggl\{\frac{2{\tilde{\gamma}}-3}{3},1\biggr\},\quad\delta:=\frac{{\tilde{\gamma}}+\theta}{{\tilde{\gamma}}}.

Proof.

Let

\rho

\mathbf{u}

For some time dependent integrable function

\displaystyle S_{\delta}\bigl[f](t,x):=\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\phi_{\delta}(x-y)f(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega.

\displaystyle|b(z)|+|b^{\prime}(z)z|\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}z^{\theta}\quad\forall\,z\in[0,\infty).

Lemma 5.4

Let

be a non-negative finite Radon-measure.
Then the function

\lim\limits_{s\nearrow\infty}f(s)=\|\mu\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})},\qquad\lim\limits_{s\searrow-\infty}f(s)=0.

(5.30)

Furthermore, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}b^{\prime}(\xi)f(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(5.31)

for any test function
b\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, we have for any non-negative function

that satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)<\infty

that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}b^{\prime}(\xi)\biggl(\int_{(-\infty,\xi]}h(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\eta)\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(5.32)

for any test function
b\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The relations in
(
5.30
)
follow from elementary properties of non-negative measures (see e.g.
[
27
, Chapter 1]
) and for the proof of relation
(
5.31
)
we refer to
[

The relation
(
5.32
)
follows from
(
5.31

by considering the non-negative finite Radon-measure

that is defined via

\displaystyle\langle\mu_{h},b\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)\quad\forall\,b\in C^{0}_{0}(\mathbb{R}).

(5.34)

By definition of
f
, we have for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
that the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure corresponding to
f(t,x,\cdot)
is exactly given by
\nu_{(t,x)}
and therefore we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)

(5.35)

for any function
b
that is integrable with respect to
\nu_{(t,x)}
.
Also, we have that
\overline{P_{\alpha}}\in L^{\delta}(\Omega_{T})
and thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)<\infty

for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
.
Thus, by Lemma
5.4
and
(
5.35
)
, we have for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
the integration by parts rules

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)f(t,x,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5.36)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)\biggl(\int_{(-\infty,\xi]}P_{\alpha}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta)\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5.37)

for any test function
b\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let us fix some test function
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
.
By a density argument, we can assume without loss of generality that
\psi=\varphi b
for some
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\Omega)
,
b\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let us define a function
B
via

B\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\xi\mapsto B(\xi):=\int_{(\xi,\infty)}b(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Then we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi}B(\xi)=-b(\xi),\quad\partial_{\xi\xi}B(\xi)=-\partial_{\xi}b(\xi),\quad\partial_{\xi}\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B(\xi)-B(\xi)\bigr)=-\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)

(5.38)

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
With the second relation in
(
5.33
)
and the first relation in
(
5.34
)
, we conclude by a truncation argument that
(
5.36
)
and
(
5.37
)
hold also for
B
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}B(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)f(t,x,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5.39)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B(\xi)-B(\xi)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)f(t,x,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5.40)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B(\xi)-B(\xi)\bigr)P_{\alpha}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{(t,x)}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)\biggl(\int_{(-\infty,\xi]}P_{\alpha}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta)\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(5.41)

for almost all
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
, where we have used
(
5.38
)
.
Corresponding relations hold also for
f^{0}
.
With the second relation in
(
5.33
)
and the first relation in
(
5.34
)
, we have after a truncation argument that the function
\varphi B
is a valid test function for the kinetic equation
(
3.5
)
.
We obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,B\partial_{t}\varphi+B\nabla\varphi\cdot\mathbf{u}-\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-B\bigr)\varphi\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}+\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-B\bigr)\varphi\mathcal{Q}\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu^{0},\varphi(0,\cdot)B\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.42)

With the relations
(
5.39
)
and
(
5.40
)
, we infer

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,B\partial_{t}\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)f(t,x,\xi)\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,B\nabla\varphi\cdot\mathbf{u}\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)f(t,x,\xi)\nabla\varphi(t,x)\cdot\mathbf{u}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu^{0},B\varphi(0,\cdot)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(\xi)f^{0}(x,\xi)\varphi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-B\bigr)\varphi\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)f(t,x,\xi)\varphi(t,x)\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

From the relations
(
5.40
)
and
(
5.41
)
we infer

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-\xi\bigr)\varphi\mathcal{Q}\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\Biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-\xi\bigr)\overline{P_{\alpha}}\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\langle\nu,\bigl(\xi\partial_{\xi}B-\xi\bigr)P_{\alpha}\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)\varphi(t,x)\Biggl(\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x)f(t,x,\xi)-\int_{(-\infty,\xi]}P_{\alpha}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta)\Biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)\varphi(t,x)\Biggl(\int_{(-\infty,\xi]}\frac{\overline{P_{\alpha}}(t,x)-P_{\alpha}(\eta)}{\lambda+2\mu}\,\mathrm{d}f(t,x,\eta)\Biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\partial_{\xi}b(\xi)\varphi(t,x)\mathcal{M}[f](t,x,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using these identities in
(
5.42
)
we obtain
(
3.7
)
.

6
Conclusions

We were concerned with the rigorous justification of an effective system for an isothermal compressible liquid-vapor flow in the regime where the number of phase boundaries is very large.
As a model on the detailed scale we have chosen the PNSK equations with a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type that can be seen as a lower-order approximation of the NSK equations.
For this system, we have proven a homogenization result that justifies an effective system in the regime where the initial density is highly oscillating.
This effective system describes the two-phase fluid on an averaged scale through two deterministic quantities in the form of the fluid’s velocity and the order parameter and through a probabilistic quantity in the form of a Young measure.
Accordingly, the effective system of equations consists of a hydrodynamic part and a kinetic equation for the Young measure.
The kinetic equation coincides with the one in
[
4
]
. However, in
[
4
]
, the authors were able to rigorously reduce the effective system to a Baer–Nunziato type system by assuming that the Young measure is given initially by the convex combination of two Dirac-measures and by working in the stronger Hoff–Desjardin regularity class.
It would be very interesting whether such a reduction is also possible for the PNSK system with a pressure function of Van-der-Waals type.
Also, by rewriting the kinetic equation for the Young measure into a kinetic equation for its corresponding cumulative distribution function we observed that the effective system is accessible by standard approximation methods.
It would be interesting to investigate the effective system by such methods without reducing it to a Baer–Nunziato type system.

Our justification relies on the assumption that a finite energy weak solution to the initial-boundary value problem
(
1.2
)
–
(
1.4
)
emanating from initial data that satisfy
(
2.17
)
exists globally in time.
Providing a rigorous proof of such a global-in-time existence result would complement our results and is thus planned as forthcoming work.
In this contribution, we considered the isothermal case.
A parabolic relaxation formulation for the non-isothermal NSK equations was recently proposed in
[
18
]
.
It would be very nice to obtain a similar homogenization result for the non-isothermal case.
Our results rely on a parabolic relaxation formulation, however, there are other possibilities to approximate the NSK equations.
In
[
25
]
, a non-local NSK model was proposed that models the capillarity term by a convolutional operator with an interaction potential.
For this model, a rigorous homogenization result for a porous domain was recently obtained in
[
23
]
.
In
[
26
]
, an elliptic relaxation formulation was proposed that corresponds to the PNSK equations with
\beta=0
.
It would be very interesting to generalize our results to these NSK models and, also, to obtain a homogenization result for the parabolic NSK equations in a porous domain analogous to the one in
[
23
]
.

Appendix A
Compactness Results and Weak Continuity in Time

In this section we collect well-known compactness results for Bochner spaces and weakly continuous functions.
Also, we state a general form of the weak compactness result concerning the effective viscous flux for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations, that was stated and proven in
[
20
]
in a even more general form.
These results are used throughout this work and therefore we include them here for the sake of clarity.
We start with the classical Aubin–Lions lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X_{0},X,X_{1}
be three Banach spaces satisfying the embeddings

X_{0}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow X\hookrightarrow X_{1},

where the first embedding
X_{0}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow X
is compact.

For
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, define the space

W_{p,q}:=\Bigl\{u\in L^{p}(0,T;X_{0})\mid\partial_{t}u\in L^{q}(0,T;X_{1})\Bigr\}

and a norm on
W_{p,q}
via

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W_{p,q}}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X_{0})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{q}(0,T;X_{1})}\quad\forall\,u\in W_{p,q}.

Then, we have that
\Bigl(W_{p,q},\|\cdot\|_{W_{p,q}}\Bigr)
is a Banach space and the embedding

W_{p,q}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow L^{p}(0,T;X)

is compact if
p\in[1,\infty)
.
Moreover, the embedding

W_{p,q}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow C([0,T];X)

is compact if
p=\infty
and
q\in(1,\infty]
.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
29
, Corollary 4]
.
∎

Another compactness result can be established for weakly continuous functions.
Let us start with the following standard result that ensures the weak continuity of a quantity.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
a bounded domain.
Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and suppose that
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(Q))
satisfies for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{Q}f\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=F_{\phi}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T))

for some integrable function
F_{\phi}\in L^{1}(0,T)
.

Then, there exists a weakly continuous function
\hat{f}\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q))
such that

\hat{f}(t)=f(t)\quad\text{in }L^{p}(Q)\quad\text{for almost all }t\in(0,T).

Moreover, we have for any
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
the relation

\int_{Q}\hat{f}(t)\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{Q}\hat{f}(s)\phi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{s}^{t}F_{\phi}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

for any test function
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(Q)
.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
20
, Chapter 6]
.
∎

The following compactness result is a consequence of the Sobolev embedding theorem and an abstract version of the Arzéla–Ascoli theorem.
For a detailed exposition of the following compactness result we refer to
[
20
, Chapter 6]
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
T>0
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
and assume that
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary.
Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and assume that functions
g_{n}\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q))
are given for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then we have the following statements:

1.

If the sequence
{(g_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{p}(Q)
, i.e.,

\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\|g_{n}(t)\|_{L^{p}(Q)}\leq C

for some positive constant
C>0
that does not depend on
n
, and uniformly continuous in
W^{-1,q}(Q)
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
, i.e.,

\forall\varepsilon>0:\exists\delta(\varepsilon)>0:\Bigl(t,s\in[0,T],\,|t-s|<\delta\Longrightarrow\|g_{n}(t)-g_{n}(s)\|_{W^{-1,q}(Q)}<\varepsilon\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr),

then there exists some
g\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q))
such that, after passing to a non-relabeled subsequence,

g_{n}\to g\quad\text{in }C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q)).

2.

If there exists some
g\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q))
such that

g_{n}\to g\quad\text{in }C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{p}(Q)),

then we have

g_{n}\to g\quad\text{strongly in }L^{r}(0,T;W^{-1,s}(Q))

for any
r\in[1,\infty)
and for any
s\in(1,\infty)
such that the embedding
L^{p}(Q)\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow W^{-1,s}(Q)
is compact.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
20
, Chapter 6]
.
∎

We recall the following general version of the effective viscous flux lemma that was proven in
[
20
]
in a more general setting.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
T,\mu,\lambda>0
and let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain.
Let
r,s\in(1,\infty)
and
z\in(\frac{6}{5},\infty)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbf{q}_{n}\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{z}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
,
\mathbf{u}_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{0}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
,
p_{n}\in L^{r}(0,T;L^{r}(Q))
,
\mathbf{F}_{n}\in L^{s}(0,T;L^{s}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{3}))
,
f_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(Q))
,
g_{n}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(Q))
with
g_{n}\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{q}(Q))
for any
q\in[1,\infty)
be given satisfying the convergences

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\mathbf{q}\quad\text{in }C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{z}(Q,\mathbb{R}^{3})),\quad\mathbf{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{0}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{3}))

\displaystyle p_{n}\rightharpoonup p\quad\text{in }L^{r}(0,T;L^{r}(Q)),\quad\mathbf{F}_{n}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{F}\quad\text{in }L^{s}(0,T;L^{s}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{3})),

\displaystyle f_{n}\rightharpoonup f\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(Q)),\quad g_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}g\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(Q)),

\displaystyle g_{n}\to g\quad\text{in }C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];L^{q}(Q))\quad\text{for any }q\in[1,\infty).

Assume that

\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}_{n}+\mathrm{div}(\mathbf{q}_{n}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{n})+\nabla p_{n}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}_{n}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{n})=\mathbf{F}_{n}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q_{T};\mathbb{R}^{3})

and

\partial_{t}g_{n}+\mathrm{div}(g_{n}\mathbf{u}_{n})=f_{n}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q_{T})

hold for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then we have the relation

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{Q}\eta(t)\phi(x)\biggl(p_{n}-(\lambda+2\mu)\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}_{n}\biggr)g_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{Q}\eta(t)\phi(x)\biggl(p-(\lambda+2\mu)\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}\biggr)g\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

for any test functions
\eta\in\mathcal{D}((0,T))
and
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(Q)
.

Proof.

See
[
20
, Proposition 7.36]
.
There the authors prove a more general statement.

We close this section with the following standard result that gives a criterion for which one can guarantee that the product of a weakly convergent and a strongly convergent quantity converges weakly to the product of the corresponding limits.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
T>0
and
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain. Let
p,q\in(1,\infty)
and let
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in(1,\infty)
denote the conjugate Hölder exponents, i.e.,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}:=\frac{p}{p-1},\quad q^{\prime}:=\frac{q}{q-1}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
f_{n}\in L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}(Q))
and
g_{n}\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(Q))
such that

\displaystyle f_{n}\rightharpoonup f\quad\text{in }L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}(Q),\qquad g_{n}\to g\quad\text{in }L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(Q)).

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}g_{n}\rightharpoonup fg\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times Q).

Proof.

Since
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, we have that

\displaystyle L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(Q))\simeq\Bigl(L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}_{0}(Q))\Bigr)^{*}.

For
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}((0,T)\times Q)
we thus have

\displaystyle\langle f_{n}g_{n}-fg,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{D}}=\langle f_{n}(g_{n}-g),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{D}}+\langle(f_{n}-f)g,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\leq\|g-g_{n}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(Q))}\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}\varphi\|_{L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}(Q))}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle g,(f_{n}-f)\varphi\rangle_{(L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ast},\,L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega))}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
∎
