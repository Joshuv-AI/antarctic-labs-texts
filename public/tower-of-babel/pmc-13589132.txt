Research note: Genetic background influences the relationship between age at first egg and long-term egg production in layers
Authors: Aixin Ni, Mario P.L. Calus, Henk Bovenhuis, Yunlei Li, Jingwei Yuan, Yanyan Sun, Jilan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age at first egg (AFE) is a key selection criterion in layers breeding. With the laying cycle being extended to 100 weeks, the relationship between AFE and long-term productivity and egg quality should be evaluated to ensure that selection for AFE aligns with current breeding objectives. In this study, Beijing-You chickens and White Leghorns were used to generate purebreds and crossbreds. Egg-laying performance was recorded including AFE, egg number and cumulative egg number at different stages from onset till 100 weeks, and egg quality traits at 32, 54, 72, 86, and 100 weeks. Genetic correlations were estimated, both in the combined population of purebreds and crossbreds and within each genetic group. In the combined population, a positive genetic correlation was observed between AFE and cumulative egg number till 100 weeks. Age-dependent genetic correlations between egg number at different stages and AFE further revealed that extremely early-maturing hens showed initial production advantages, but these advantages diminished at later stages. Importantly, the genetic and phenotypic correlations between AFE and egg quality traits were weak, with correlation coefficients ranging from -0.18 to 0.35. Within each genetic group, the relationships between AFE and egg production also showed consistent age-dependent patterns. For the long-term production targets, optimal AFE seems to differ by genetic backgrounds. White Leghorns showed higher egg production with earlier maturity, whereas in Beijing-You chickens, maintaining AFE at approximately 140-189 days appeared to be more favorable. Overall, these findings demonstrated that earlier AFE does not ensure higher egg production at extended laying cycles and has negligible influence on egg quality, highlighting the importance of optimizing AFE according to genetic background.

Introduction

Age at first egg (AFE) has long been used as a selection trait in traditional layers breeding, based on the assumption that earlier onset of lay increases the overall egg production, thereby improving economic efficiency (Zhang et al., 2026). Furthermore, several studies reported moderate to high heritability of AFE (Ni, et al., 2023; Yang, et al., 2023), indicating that it can be effectively improved through genetic selection. To reduce the relative contribution of the rearing period to the total costs of egg-laying, there is a trend to extend the laying cycle to 100 weeks or even longer. Our previous studies investigated egg production traits during extended laying periods. Heritability estimates for egg production traits up to 100 weeks suggested relatively limited but stable additive genetic contributions to cumulative egg number (Ni, et al., 2025). In addition, the genetic correlations between AFE and cumulative egg number at 43, 72, and 100 weeks were shown to change from negative values at the early stage to positive values at later ages (Ni, et al., 2023). AFE was strongly genetically associated with cumulative egg number at the early stage, and long-term egg production was also genetically related to clutch-related traits (Ni, et al., 2023), further supporting the biological complexity of AFE in the extended laying cycle.

To better understand the consequences of extending the laying period, there is a need to explore the genetic relationships between AFE and cumulative egg number across more detailed timepoints, as well as egg quality traits for the whole laying cycle. Furthermore, our previous study has demonstrated substantial variation in AFE among different genetic backgrounds (Ni, et al., 2023). Importantly, the relationship between AFE and egg production may also differ across populations. Under the extended laying cycle, it remains unclear whether earlier AFE is still advantageous or whether population-specific optimal AFE should be considered. Therefore, in the current study, egg production and egg quality were recorded for the long-term laying cycle, and the correlations between those traits and AFE were investigated across and within four genetic groups. This study aims to provide a better understanding of how AFE influences long-term egg-laying performance and to offer insights into optimizing selection strategies for chickens with diverse genetic backgrounds.

Materials and methods

Ethical consideration

All animal procedures complied with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee at the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IAS-CAAS). The study protocol was approved by the IAS-CAAS Ethics Committee (Protocol No. IAS2022-35).

Experimental genetic groups

The experimental genetic groups used in this study were described previously (Ni, et al., 2023). Briefly, the Beijing-You chickens (Y) used here were from a pedigreed pure line kept on the experimental farm of the Institute of Animal Sciences of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. This line was selected for egg number and egg weight at a relatively low intensity for 18 generations. The other parental line, White Leghorn (W, Shaver line), was imported from the University of Guelph in 2018. The W chickens were kept on the same farm as the Y chickens for two generations using random mating. Beijing-You chickens and White Leghorns were mated to generate four genetic groups, using 30 Y and 30 W roosters, and 300 Y and 300 W hens: (1) 30 Y roosters with good sperm motility and sperm concentration were randomly mated with 150 Y hens to generate YY purebred offspring, (2) the same 30 Y roosters were randomly mated with 150 W hens to generate YW crossbred offspring, (3) 30 W roosters with good sperm motility and sperm concentration were randomly mated with 150 W hens to generate WW purebred offspring, and (4) the same 30 W roosters were randomly mated with 150 Y hens to generate WY crossbred offspring. In total, 487 YY, 675 WW, 507 WY, and 714 YW healthy female chicks were hatched simultaneously. At 18 weeks, 315 YY, 271 WW, 315 WY, and 359 YW chickens were randomly selected from all available chickens and transferred to the laying hens' house and kept in individual cages to measure egg-laying performance. Seven cages (length 37 cm × width 34 cm × height 34 cm) were stacked on one of three tiers in each bank; 15 banks were arranged in one row, for three rows in total. Chickens were fed the same diet ad libitum and kept under the same controlled environmental conditions. The daily lighting increased by one hour per week from 8 hours at 19 weeks to 13 hours at 24 weeks, and increased by 0.5 hour per week from 25 to 30 weeks. A constant daily lighting of 16 hours was maintained thereafter.

Egg-laying performance

The average weight of the first three eggs of each bird was calculated and denoted as the first egg weight. Egg weight was measured every four weeks from 28 to 76 weeks, and at 86 and 100 weeks for three eggs or all eggs in that week. Egg weights were measured using a digital scale with a sensitivity of 0.01 g (HC-UTP-313, Haihua Chao Electrical Appliances, Shanghai, China). Age at first egg, cumulative egg number, and egg number at different stages from 200, till 700 days at 100-day intervals were computed from individual egg-laying recordings.

Egg quality traits, including egg shape index, eggshell colour, eggshell strength, eggshell thickness, eggshell ratio, yolk colour, yolk ratio, and Haugh unit, were measured within 24 hours after egg collection at 32, 54, 72, 86, and 100 weeks for three eggs per bird. The egg shape index was calculated as the ratio of egg width to egg length, which was measured using the FHK egg dimension meter (NFN385, Fujihira Ind. Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Eggshell colour was measured using a QCR-shell colour reflectometer (Technical Services and Supplies, England). Measurements were expressed as a percentage reading between black (0%) and white (80%). Eggshell strength, representing the force required to break the shell of an intact egg, was measured at the obtuse pole of the egg using the Egg Force Reader (Orka Food Technology Ltd., Israel). Eggshell thickness was measured at the acute, middle, and obtuse poles of each egg using the Eggshell Thickness Gauge (NFN380, Fujihira Ind. Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), with a sensitivity of 0.01 mm. Yolk colour and Haugh unit were measured using the Egg Analyzer (Orka Food Technology Ltd., Israel). Egg weight, eggshell weight, and yolk weight were measured using a digital scale with a sensitivity of 0.01 g (HC-UTP-313). Eggshell ratio and yolk ratio were computed as the ratio of eggshell weight and yolk weight, respectively, to egg weight.

Statistical analyses

For egg production traits with only one observation, the following animal model was fitted with the average information restricted maximum likelihood (AI-REML) method in ASReml 4.2 (Gilmour, et al., 2015):y=1μ+X1b+X2r+Za+ewhere y is the vector of phenotypic values across the four genetic groups (YY, YW, WW, and WY), 1 is a vector of ones, μ is the population average, b is the vector of the fixed effect of the genetic groups, X1 is the design matrix for genetic groups, r is the vector of the fixed effects of the rack (24 levels for different tiers, rows, and banks), X2 is the design matrix for rack effects, a is the vector of the random animal effects with a∼N(0,Aσa2), where A is the pedigree-based relationship matrix and σa2 is the additive genetic variance, Z is the design matrix that relates phenotypes to additive genetic effects, e is the vector of random residual effects with e∼N(0,Iσe2), where I is the identity matrix and σe2 is the residual variance.

For egg weight and egg quality traits, which had multiple observations, the phenotypic data was analysed using the above model extended with a permanent environmental effect, to become a repeatability model:y=1μ+X1b+X2r+Za+Wpe+ewhere effects are the same as described for the above model and pe is the vector of random permanent environmental effects, with pe∼N(0,Iσpe2), where σpe2 is the permanent environmental variance, and W is the incidence matrix linking phenotypes to permanent environmental effects. Bivariate implementations of the above models were used to estimate phenotypic and genetic correlations. For eggshell ratio, eggshell strength, and yolk ratio, which are associated with egg weight, egg weight was fitted as a covariate in the bivariate model.

In addition to applying the above models across the data of all four genetic groups, they were also applied within each group separately. In those cases, the genetic group effect was not included, but otherwise the models were the same as presented above.

Results and discussion

Genetic and phenotypic correlations between AFE and egg-laying performance across genetic groups are shown in Fig.1. For cumulative egg number, the genetic and phenotypic correlations with AFE showed an age-dependent trend. The genetic and phenotypic correlations were negative and approached −1.00 at the early stage of production (cumulative egg number till 200 days). This is expected, as onset of lay represented whether egg production has begun during this stage. As the laying cycle progressed, the magnitude of the negative genetic and phenotypic correlations gradually decreased for cumulative egg number. Phenotypic correlations were consistently negative, while genetic correlation even shifted to a positive value after 500 days, and was 0.29 between AFE and cumulative egg number till 700 days, indicating that earlier AFE may lead to lower rather than higher cumulative egg production for the extended laying cycle. One possible explanation for this phenomenon could be that earlier onset of lay may not allow sufficient time for physiological development before onset of lay (Dunn, et al., 2021; Dunnington and Siegel, 1984), which may negatively affect egg-laying persistency and reduce cumulative egg number at later stage. For egg number at each stage, the phenotypic correlation with AFE was low across ages, and the genetic correlation was moderate (rg = 0.53) at the beginning. As the laying cycle progressed, the genetic correlations decreased to 0.20 for egg number between 601 and 700 days. The consistently positive genetic correlations between AFE and egg number at all 100-day intervals between 200 and 700 days suggests that AFE influences egg production in a uniform direction across stages, which also explains the increasing correlations between AFE and cumulative egg number over time. In addition, genetic and phenotypic correlations between AFE and egg quality traits at different ages were generally weak, ranging from −0.18 to 0.35 and −0.10 to 0.13, respectively. Relatively higher genetic correlations were observed only for eggshell thickness at 100 weeks (0.35) and Haugh unit at 32 weeks (0.32), suggesting that AFE may affect these traits at specific ages, but that such effects were not consistent across ages. Similarly, Khan also reported low to moderate genetic correlations between AFE and egg quality traits, with substantial differences observed between the two reciprocal crossbred groups (Khan, et al., 2023). Importantly, for some egg quality traits, the genetic and phenotypic correlations had different magnitudes and sometimes even opposite signs. This is possible as the phenotypic correlation includes environmental and management variation, and residual effects that can mask the additive genetic relationship. Overall, the results indicate that earlier AFE may lead to lower rather than higher egg production for the extended laying cycle, and has little influence on egg quality from onset of lay to 100 weeks. This raises the question of whether an optimal timing of onset of lay exists for maximizing egg production under the trend of extending laying cycles. The associations between AFE and egg production within each genetic group were shown in Fig. 2. Distinct AFE distributions were observed across the four genetic groups (Fig. 2A). The mean AFE for the combined population was 167.72 days. Within each genetic group, WW chickens reached onset of lay earlier (157.65 days) and showed a narrow distribution. Crossbreds showed a similar mean AFE (165.71 for WY and 166.37 for YW) and WY exhibited a broader distribution. Indigenous breed YY chickens showed much later AFE (179.55 days). Phenotypic means based on ten-day AFE intervals revealed changes in cumulative egg number with increasing AFE (Fig. 2B). It should be noted that the animal number varied among AFE groups, which affects the standard errors of the phenotypic means. For the short-term production target (before 500 days), cumulative egg number decreased with increasing AFE across the four genetic groups. For the long-term production target (after 500 days), cumulative egg number also decreased with increasing AFE regardless of stage of laying period for WW. However, this does not suggest to select for an excessively shortened onset of lay, given the positive genetic correlations we observed between cumulative egg number and AFE at later stage. Breeding companies also have suggested that AFE should not be earlier than 17 weeks, allowing adequate time for sexual maturity and bone development (van de Braak, 2022). Cumulative egg number remained relatively stable across the first three AFE groups for YY (160-189 days). The reciprocal crosses showed distinct patterns. WY had relatively stable cumulative egg number across most AFE groups, except for the 190-199 group, which had a very small sample size (n = 2, Fig. 2C). Within each genetic group, genetic and phenotypic correlations between AFE and cumulative egg number followed a similar pattern (Fig. 2D). Phenotypic correlations were consistently negative at early ages but gradually approached zero or slightly positive values towards the later stages. Genetic correlations varied among genetic groups but generally showed an increasing trend with age. For WW, genetic correlations were more variable and tended to increase at later stages, while they were relatively stable for YY. A previous study also reported increasing genetic correlations between AFE and cumulative egg number (Chen, et al., 2025), but these correlations were only evaluated until 58 weeks, at which stage the highest value of −0.28 was reached. No consistent pattern was observed for egg number at each stage. Correlations did not converge for some traits, probably due to the limited number of animals within each genetic group. Nevertheless, when combining phenotypic means across AFE groups and genetic correlations, these findings suggest that the effect of AFE on egg production is age-dependent and population-specific. While selection for earlier AFE may benefit early production, it does not ensure higher egg production for the extended laying cycle. One possible explanation could be that the onset of lay is associated with major changes in calcium and phosphorus utilization (Garcia-Mejia, et al., 2024) and occurs during a period when bone maturity is still changing with age (Rath, et al., 2000). Consequently, an earlier AFE may reduce the time available for adequate bone reserve formation. In contrast, a delayed AFE may be beneficial for bone reserve formation, which could be beneficial for long-term egg production, given that long-term egg production is restricted by bone calcium metabolism (Alfonso-Carrillo, et al., 2021). Since bone-related traits were not measured in the current study, this possible explanation requires validation in future studies.

In our current study, we estimated the genetic and phenotypic correlations between AFE and egg production and egg quality traits by considering only additive genetic effects from our experiment conducted in one single environment. However, non-additive effects may also contribute to trait performance, particularly for egg production traits. In addition, genotype-by-environment interactions, including potential effects of nutritional management before onset of lay, may also influence trait performance. These factors require larger datasets, genomic information, and specific experimental designs involving different environmental or nutritional conditions. Therefore, future studies incorporating non-additive genetic effects, and genotype-by-environment interactions would help further clarify the genetic mechanisms underlying AFE and long-term laying performance.

In general, the optimal AFE varies across genetic backgrounds and production stage, and should be determined considering the expected productive life of the population. For the short-term production target, the earlier onset of lay is suggested after reaching sexual maturity and adequate skeletal development (van de Braak, 2022). For the long-term production cycle, optimal AFE differs across genetic backgrounds. Based on our results, for commercial elite layer lines with an aimed longer productive life, the earlier onset of lay is preferable. For the indigenous chicken breeds like Beijing-You used here, maintaining AFE within a moderate window (around 140-189 days) appears optimal.
