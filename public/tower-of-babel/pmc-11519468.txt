Genome-wide dissection of genes shaping inflorescence morphology in 242 Chinese south–north sorghum accessions
Authors: Guihua Zou, Yanqing Ding, Jianxia Xu, Zhou Feng, Ning Cao, Heyun Chen, Heqin Liu, Xueqiang Zheng, Xiuhui Liu, Liyi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe inflorescences morphology (IM) of sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L. Moench) affects its resistance to pests, diseases, and grain yields. However, the specific genetic factors underlying in IM are not yet fully elucidated. Here we conducted a comprehensive genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) to identify the stable and adaptive Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) for five IM traits (panicle length, the number of cob nodes, the number of primary branches, the largest length of the primary branch, and panicle type) in a sorghum panel, which adapted to different environments from the south to north in China. Totally, 2,015,850 high quality single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were obtained. Population structure analysis showed that two distinct genetic sub-populations were divided according to their geographic origin. Seventy-one QTLs distributed in 41 genetic regions on 9 chromosomes were identified. These regions harbored 21 high-confident candidate genes that were homologous to rice domestication genes, including 7 related to IM. Two domestication-related genes (Sobic.003G052700 and Sobic.006G247700) were located into two major QTL regions (QTL3.4721839 and QTL6.58709500) which were identified in multi-environments. Allelic variations in the two genes displayed a geographical pattern, indicating that different IM traits were selected by south and north sorghum breeders, such as south sorghums had long and loose panicles in order to adapt the hot and humid climate, while north sorghums had short and compact panicle to increase planting density and grain yield per unit area due to dry climate. Introduction

The sorghum grain is regarded as staple food and feed for humans and livestock in Africa, Asia, and parts of the Americas because its ability to thrive in harsh climates with minimal water (FAOSTAT, 2022). Increasing grain yield is a common goal of breeders and geneticists. Inflorescence architecture is a key agricultural trait that determines the adaptation, reproduction and grain yield of crop1. Similar to rice, sorghum inflorescences, also referred to as panicles, are composed of a central stem called rachis with a series of nodes, on which the primary branches were born2. Each primary branch gives rise to secondary or tertiary branches (Fig. 1)3. There are terminal triple spikelets present at the end of each branch: one sessile spikelet directly attached to the inflorescence branch and two pedicellate spikelets attached to the inflorescence branch through a pedicel4. Only the sessile spikelets have the ability to develop into viable grains, while the pedicellate spikelets undergo initial development but eventually cease development and abort. As a result, the total number of flowers produced per panicle surpasses the actual grain number per panicle5. Hence, the length of panicle, the number of primary and secondary branches, the length of primary branches, and panicle branching patterns directly determine the architecture of inflorescence and total grain number.

The description of inflorescence morphology.

Sorghum has spread to different agroclimatic zones from Africa into India, China, Australia, and America, and exhibits a wide range of variation in traits such as height, maturity, leaf architecture, and inflorescence morphology6. Based on spikelet/panicle morphology alone, sorghum can be classified into five major botanical races including bicolor, guinea, caudatum, kafir, and durra, and ten intermediate races. These classifications are closely associated with the original cultivation environments in which sorghum was first cultivated by nomadic peoples7,8. In general, domesticated sorghums, particularly modern high-yielding commercial varieties, display a compact panicle morphology for an increased number of branches, shorter internode length of the rachis, and larger grains protruding out of the glumes3,9–12, whereas undomesticated species are more likely to have open or loose panicles with small grains and low grain yield7,13. And those sorghums with long and open panicles tend to be more prevalent in humid zones while sorghums with short and compact panicles are commonly dominant in arid zones14,15. Large panicle morphology variations are observed in Chinese sorghum because they are grown in a wide range of the geographic climate zones from the northern part of the country to the Yellow River and Yangtze River16. Chinese sorghum in the south and north has exhibited unique characteristics to adapt to local agricultural ecological climates. Scattered long panicle morphology is commonly observed in the hot and rainy south, but upright and compact panicle morphology is beneficial to higher planting density and higher yields in the arid north (Supplementary Figure S1). Although cultivated Chinese sorghum contains a diverse of panicles (inflorescence) shapes, the genetic mechanism and regulation of this trait is poorly understood. Recently, a few studies have focused on inflorescence traits in biparental mapping, but they were limited by low diversity in the IM between the mapping parents. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) can explore large diversity of the IM but could be confounded by population structure3,4,15,17,18. Multi-locus analysis is preferred over single-locus analysis in GWAS, because it better reflects the polygenic nature of complex traits, improves statistical power, captures gene–gene interactions, and reduces the likelihood of false negatives and positives.

A series of literatures reports that many common traits or genes have been selected during cereal domestication and breeding selection19. The presence of homologous variation in inflorescence among cereals has been well noted20. Also, the collinearity of IM among cereals has proven to be a valuable system for investigating the evolutionary dynamics and molecular basis of genetic architecture in plants21,22. Through genetic analysis, several genes regulating IM in rice have been identified including ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION1 (APO1)23,24, WEALTHY FARMER’S PANICLE (WFP)25, FRIZZY PANICLE (FZP)26–28DENSE AND ERECT PANICLE1 (DEP1)29, PANICLE RACHIS LENGTH5 (Prl5) and PRIMARY BRANCH LENGTH6 (Pbl6)30. Given the similar inflorescence architecture between rice and sorghum, the genes and signaling pathways already identified for regulating inflorescence development in rice could potentially be applicable to sorghum.

In this study, we used an association panel consisting of 242 re-sequenced Chinese sorghum accessions, of these, 151 from North China and 91 from South China according to the population structure31, to characterize IM variation and performed GWAS. The objectives were (1) to investigate the diversity of panicle architectures across a population of Chinese south–north sorghum accessions, (2) to identify linkage disequilibrium (LD) regions for significant association signal on IM and (3) to mine the putative candidate genes for IM of Chinese south–north sorghum by comparative genomics. Identified putative candidate genes and their linked molecular markers associated with IM in Chinese sorghum may help dissect the genetic architecture of sorghum IM and breed varieties adapted to local environments.

Results

Variation of IM

In this study, a total of 5 traits related to IM were investigated on 242 sorghum accessions across five environments of 2019–2021 in Guiyang (19GY, 20GY, and 21GY) and 2020–2021 in Ledong (20LD and 21LD), except for panicle type, which was evaluated only at Ledong in 2021 (21LD). The phenotypic values of these five traits were continuous distributions, suggesting complex and quantitative inheritance (Fig. 2). Significantly phenotypic differences were observed for all five IM traits (Table 1). When the trait values of sorghum accessions in different environments were compared, higher mean values were observed in PL-19GY, NCN-20GY, NPB-20GY, and LLPB-21GY. No significant differences were observed for PL and NCN at Guiyang or Ledong, respectively. However, the average values of LLPB and NPB in 2021 were moderately but significantly decreased than that in 2020 at Ledong.

The average values of LLPB and NPB in 2019 were significantly decreased than that in 2021 and 2020 at Guiyang, respectively (P < 0.01; Table 1). Differences between the minimum and maximum trait values at Guiyang ranged from 2.7 (for NPB-20GY) to 6.8 (for LLPB-20GY) fold changes with average 3.9 fold change for all traits, while the differences at Ledong ranged from 3.0 (for NPB-21LD) to 6.4 (for LLPB-20LD ) fold change with average 4.3 fold change for all traits. The coefficients of variation (CVs) varied from 17% for NCN-21GY to 30% for LLPB-19GY and LLPB-20GY at Guiyang, while from 20% for NCN-20LD to 29% for LLPB-21LD (Table 1). Among these traits, LLPB was more variable across the environments with the maximum CV, while NCN was insensitive to environments with the minimum CV.

ANOVA was performed to calculate the effect of genotype (G), environment (E), and genotype × environment interaction (G × E) (Table 2). Because the accessions were collected from diversely ecological regions, significantly genotypic mean squares (P < 0.01) were observed for all traits across five environments. Additionally, E mean squares and G × E interaction mean squares were found to be highly significant. Among three variance components, E mean squares consistently showed much larger values compared to those of G and G × E interaction mean squares for all traits, except for PL. The broad sense heritability (h2B) was estimated for each trait across five environments (Table 2). The h2B values for IM were very high and varied from 91.39 to 97.31%, indicating that G effects had strong influence on traits due to their large proportion of total phenotypic variance, while E effects and G × E interaction effects were responsible for a comparatively smaller portion of the observed phenotypic variation.

Genome wide association study

To accurately dissect IM traits, QTL analysis was performed according to five statistical models (GLM, FarmCPU, Blink, MLM, or MLMM). The genomic regions associated with these traits were determined by Bonferroni test threshold as P value of 6.1 × 10–8 or a –log(P) of 7.21, for one trait in at least two environments or at least two traits in the same environments, except for panicle type which was evaluated only in one environment. Using these criteria, GWAS detected 71 QTLs, distributed to 41 unique genetic loci on 10 chromosomes, except for chromosome 8 (Table 4, Fig. 3).

Candidates homologous to the rice development genes in the QTL intervals

In order to identify candidate genes related to IM, we analyzed the genomic sequences and gene annotations covered by each QTL in the reference BTx623 genome version 3.1.1 genome on Phytozome 13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Sbicolor_v3_1_1). Twenty-one candidate genes were identified within 62.5 kb up- and downstream of the associated SNPs in 16 QTL loci, of which 4 loci harbored 2 to 3 genes (Table 5). These genes were involved mainly in molecular function such as in meiotic division of male and female flower organs, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, ATP binding, DNA binding, uncharacterized protein, etc. The putative functions of their rice orthologous genes were directly or indirectly related to IM, such as panicle architecture, plant height, grain size, and heading day. Of which 7 genes directly related to IM were located on 2, 3, 6, 7, and 9 chromosomes. For instance, Sobic.002G312200 (66.061 kb from the significant SNPs), orthologous to the GLW7 in rice, were significantly associated with LLPB and NPB, encoding transcription factor, SBP-box gene family member. Sobic.003G052700 (34.936 kb from the significant SNPs), orthologous to the FIB/OsTAR2/ OsTAA1 in rice, was significantly associated with NCN, PL, LLPB, and PT, encoding tryptophan transaminase/tryptophan aminotransferase. Sobic.003G052900 (67.031 kb from the significant SNPs), orthologous to the OsCrll4/OsRAMOSA2 in rice, was significantly associated with NCN, PL, LLPB, and PT, encoding protein of unknown function domain DUF260. Sobic.006G247700 (12.29 kb from the significant SNP), orthologous to the OsLG1 in rice, was significantly associated with LLPB, having similar protein function to LIGULELESS 1. Sobic007G136300 (110.544 kb from the significant SNPs), orthologous to the ONAC106 in rice, were significantly associated with NCN, encoding no apical meristem protein. Sobic.007G158000 (63.73 kb from the significant SNP), the orthologous to the OsCEP6.1 in rice which was significantly associated with PT and PL, showing putative uncharacterized protein function. Sobic.009G135000 (36.893 kb from the significant SNP), the orthologous to the OsSPL9/lgn5 in rice, was significantly associated with PL and LLPB, having similar to squamosa promoter-binding-like protein 9 function. There were 10 genes related to plant height and grain size on chromosomes 1 (3), 2 (1), 4 (1), 9 (4), and 10 (1), such as Sobic.001G526200 which was 20.793 kb from the significant SNP and was associated with NCN and NPB, having the function of Homeodomain-like protein. There were also 3 genes related to heading day on chromosomes 2, 4 and 10, such as Sobic.002G280800 which was 8.788 kb from the significant SNP and was associated with PL, PT, NPB, and NCN, encoding a protein without known conserved functional domain.

Local adaptability of Chinese sorghum for IM

Due to the significant difference in agro-ecological climate between South and North of China, Chinese sorghum has formed two distinct sub-populations as the southern and northern sorghum, based on the same panel of 242 accessions in our previous study31. Therefore, we performed the selective sweep analysis to identify the potential domestication signatures related to IM. Pairwise Fst and π ratio revealed 4158 and 2454 candidate genes between Chinese Southern and Northern sorghums. Meanwhile, the Tajima’s D identified 3112 and 2436 candidate genes for Southern and Northern sorghum, respectively. Interestingly, among 30 candidate genes in the major QTL intervals for IM, 9 genes in 8 QTL intervals were undergone strongly natural and artificial selection, and were located on chromosomes 1, 3, 6, 7, 9, and 10 (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). Among 9 selective genes, 4 (Sobic.003G052700, Sobic.006G247700, Sobic.009G060100, and Sobic.010G101600) were determined simultaneously based on 2 or 3 metrics. The rice orthologs of 9 domesticated genes mainly or simultaneously involved in regulating panicle architecture (5), grain size (3), plant height (4), and heading day (1). Identification of the candidate genes related to IM in the QTL intervals laid a foundation for further gene cloning.

Expression characterization of IM candidate genes

Since two putative candidate genes (Sobic.003G052700 and Sobic.006G247700) for IM displayed a haplotype pattern in Chinese sorghum accessions, we determined if any of these genes showed distinct expression pattern in 654 and LTR108, which showed different IM. To accomplish this, we analyzed the normalized FPKM values for two genes from an RNA-Seq experiment and found that the candidate genes showed differential expression patterns at three developing panicle stages between 654 and LTR108. Two candidate genes showed quite different expression pattern from S1 to S3 stages (Fig. 7). The mRNA expression of Sobic.003G052700 in 654 progressively increased, while that in LTR108 was up-down-up pattern from S1 to S3 stage. Meanwhile, the expression quantity in 654 was significantly lower than that in LTR108 at 3 stages. The expression pattern of Sobic.006G247700 in 654 was also gradually up-regulated, however up-down-up regulated in LTR108 from S1 to S3 stage. The expression quantity in 654 was lower than that in LTR108 at S1 and S2 stages, but it rose sharply in 654 and was higher than in LTR108 at S3 stage.

Discussion

Genetic architecture of inflorescence adaptation in Chinese sorghum

In our previous study, we conducted a resequencing based on 242 Chinese sorghum accessions and performed population structure analysis. These accessions were divided into two distinct genetic sub-populations, referred as the south and the north sorghums31. In order to dissect the IM patterns of genetic variation in Chinese south–north sorghum and the influence of environmental adaptation on this variation, we carried out GWAS for IM in this study. We found that IM variations in Chinese sorghum were predominantly shaped by geographic adaptation (Supplementary Figure S2). For PL, sorghums coming from south had longer panicles than those from north, 32.70 cm versus 26.63 cm. The longer panicle is attributed to the presence of long and loose primary branches, which are an important characteristic of south accessions due to hot and humid climate. The open panicle structure is believed to facilitate airflow, which can effectively reduce the risk of mold infection in high humidity environments6. The short panicle is related with short and many primary branches in north accessions, which may be an adaptive strategy to increase planting density and grain yield per unit area due to dry climate. In this study, the LLPB from south was longer 2.96 cm than that from the north. The NPB from south was 3 cm less than that from the south. The consistency between genetic architecture and IM provided evidence that locally-adapted landraces have played a crucial role in smallholder crop production under challenging climatic conditions and with limited agricultural inputs. The recombination of favorable alleles from both south and north regions would be helpful to develop new germplasm resources and cultivate elite sorghum varieties.

Co-location for inflorescence adaptation

Panicle architecture is reported to be significantly associated with plant height, grain size, and heading day. It has been reported that plant height- and panicle length-related QTLs were co-located at the same loci in sorghum4. The colocalizated phenomenon was consistent with their relationship among traits. The long and large panicle usually bear more grains with smaller size. The negative correlation is observed between grain number and grain size in sorghum32,33, likely due to the limited supply of carbohydrates available for filling the grains, which fails to meet the demand. Moreover, plant height is positively correlated to flowering day, because several chromosomal regions appear to cluster multiple QTLs for plant height and maturity and/or multiple functional alleles at each locus, such as the Dw1 region segregates into distinct quantitative trait loci that control plant height and flowering time34–36. In present study, total 71 QTLs at 41 genetic regions on 9 chromosomes for IM were identified. Several IM QTLs detected in this study co-localize with the QTLs of inflorescence-related traits that reported by previously studies (Supplementary Table S3). For example, QTL1.79000211 for NCN and NPB was detected and located at the significant SNP (79,000,211 bp) on chromosome 1. In the same confidence interval, Shehzad and Okuno37, Zhang et al.38 and Parh39 also detected the QTLs for panicle length, panicle width, and peduncle length, respectively. Three candidate genes within the QTL were orthologous to those in rice including OsCDPK1/OsCPK11/OsCDPK13/OsCPK7 for plant height and grain size (the orthologous is Sobic.001G526200), OsKNAT7 for grain size and stem development (the orthologous is Sobic.001G526300), and TAD1/TE/OsCCS52A for plant height, tillering, and grain size (the orthologous is Sobic.001G526800). Similarly, QTL2.61239846 for PL, PT, and LLPB was located at the significant SNP (61,239,846 bp) on chromosome 2. Around the SNP, Parh39 found major QTLs for PL. Rami et al.40 identified the QTL for panicle type and PL. Sakhi41 identified the QTL for Panicle width. One candidate gene (Sobic.002G220700) within the QTL was orthologous to GS9 for grain shape in rice. These findings offer valuable insights into target chromosomal intervals that can be utilized for candidate gene mining and MAS breeding, since these intervals could be regarded as hotspots with IM in sorghum.

Pathways modifying IM in major cereal crops

Over the course of approximately 20 million years, evolutionary forces have played a crucial role in shaping the adaptability of grass, as well as influencing its inflorescence architecture and overall reproductive success42. In the family of grasses (Poaceae), rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), barley (Hordeum vulgare), wheat (Triticum aestivum) and sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) are essential crops for humans and livestock43. During the process of domestication, crop evolution, and selective breeding, the inflorescence architecture of major cereal crop species has undergone significant modifications, becoming a crucial parameter that affects their yield outcomes44. The structure of grass inflorescence exhibits a wide range of complexity and diversity, and are commonly classified into three main categories based on the growth patterns of lateral organs originating from branches and spikelets. These categories include “racemes” where spikelets are attached to pedicels along a single central monopodial axis, “spikes” where spikelets lack pedicels (as seen in wheat and barley), and “panicles” which have a higher level of branching (examples include rice and sorghum)45. During the past twenty years, significant progress has been made in understanding the genes and genetic pathways responsible for the diverse inflorescence architectures of rice, maize, barley, and wheat. These pathways involve hormonal biosynthesis, hormone transport, signal transduction, and transcriptional regulation22,46–49. In conserved pathways, APETALA1/Fruitfull (AP1/FUL) and TERMINAL FLOWER1/CENTRORADIALIS (TFL1/CEN) gene families help coordinate the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth, affecting IM50,51. CLAVATA-WUSCHEL (CLV-WUS) pathway plays an essential role in maintaining floral structures across these crops52. Cytokinin and auxin signaling pathways govern meristem activity and branching patterns and guide the development of complex inflorescence architectures in maize, rice, wheat, and barley53,54. In species-specific pathways, the RAMOSA (RA) pathway plays a crucial role in orchestrating the transition from an indeterminate long branch identity to a less branched, determinate spikelet pair identity in tassel inflorescences in maize. This pathway involves five identified gene family members, namely RA1, RA2, RA3, REL2, and TPP449,55–58. While in rice, the ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION (APO) pathway regulates the timely transition from branch meristem to spikelet meristem identity. In this pathway, four proteins were determined to function: APO147, APO259, SP360, and LARGE 261. In the case of wheat and barley, the COMPOSITUM pathway regulates the development of spike-shaped inflorescences. This pathway involves two major regulators, COMPOSITUM 1 (COM1, class II TCP TF)62 and COM2 (AP2-ERF TF)63. These regulators play important roles in modulating the inflorescence architecture of these crops. Extensive research in this field has provided clear evidence that the inflorescence shape is predominantly influenced by species-specific variations in meristematic shifts and the organization of meristems48. Notebly, the orthologous of these genes in sorghum were not identified in our panel accessions. Whether there are specific genetic pathways in sorghum needs to be further verified.

Prospects for improvement of IM by genome method

IM of sorghum is a domestically and agriculturally important trait. In traditional breeding programs, IM is directly observable after heading, namely field-based phenotypic selection. However, the success of IM selection largely depends on the breeders’ experience. It is time-consuming and inefficient. A shift from empirical breeding to directional and efficient precision breeding is flourishing among cereal crops with the rapid development of genomics and functional genes. Genes or genome prediction involved in IM variation have the potential to serve as targeted candidates for selection during the processes of sorghum domestication and improvement. Many previous studies mainly focused on sorghum germplasm with non-Chinese pedigree. Few literatures reported IM variation of Chinese sorghums. In fact, Chinese sorghum germplasm harbors abundant nucleotide polymorphism and phenotypic variation, consistent with south–north climate adaptability31. The development of genomic resources in this study represents a significant advancement towards the utilization of genomics in breeding and germplasm management for sorghum landraces in China. These resources include a comprehensive catalog of SNP variations across the entire genome, a detailed description of the geographic population structure of south–north sorghums, the identified genomic signatures of domestication, the detected QTLs for IM, and several mined putative candidate genes could be resources to facilitate genomics-enabled breeding efforts in China, specifically focusing on enhancing climate resilience in sorghum. For example, two genes Sobic.003G052700 and Sobic.006G247700 with strong domestication signatures identified by the selective sweeps analysis based on SNP variation. These two genes were also located in the QTL regions for IM. GWAS showed their haplotype variations in south–north accessions and were orthologs with IM genes that have been cloned in rice. Our results provided a promising biotechnological target for manipulating IM in sorghum through CRISPR/Cas9 for two genes knockout or mutation to confirm the their roles in regulating inflorescence morphology in sorghum. Meanwhile, multi-omics tools including genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics would be used to uncover novel regulatory pathways and gene interactions.

Conclusions

In summary, we identified 71 QTLs for inflorescence morphology in 242 Chinese south–north sorghum accessions by a genome-wide association analysis from a three-year study at five environments. These regions harbored 21 high-confident candidate genes that were homologous to rice domestication genes, including 7 related to IM. Still, quite a few QTLs do not colocalize with homologs of inflorescence gene.

Two genes (Sobic.003G052700 and Sobic.006G247700) related to domestication were found to be located closest to the significant SNP markers of major QTLs identified across multiple environments. The haplotype variations of these two genes displayed a geographical pattern, indicating that different IM traits were selected for by south and north sorghum breeders. The development of genomic resources in this study represents a significant advancement towards the implementation of genomics-enabled breeding and effective germplasm management for sorghum landraces in China. These resources include a comprehensive genome-wide catalog of SNP variations, a detailed description of the geographic population structure of south–north sorghums, the identified genomic signatures of domestication, the detected QTLs for IM, and several mined putative candidate genes could be resources to facilitate genomics-enabled breeding for climate-resilience in China.

Materials and methods

Plant material and phenotyping

We collected 242 accessions from Chinese south–north sorghum planting zones. These accessions were grown during 2019 to 2021 at Guiyang (26° 35′ N, 106° 42′ E), 2020 to 2021 at Ledong (18° 28′ N, 108° 57′ E), respectively. Each accession was grown in an ordinal arrangement. Each single-row plot was 2 m long with a row spacing of 60 cm and plant spacing within a row of 20 cm. 100 kg ha−1 composite fertilizer was applied before sowing. Regular irrigation, pesticides, insecticides and other crop cultural management practices were used to ensure the plant growth.

At physiological maturity, five traits related to IM were investigated including panicle length (PL), the number of panicle nodes (NCN), the number of primary branches (NPB), the largest length of the primary branch (LLPB), panicle type (PT). Because the sampled plants were bagged from heading to maturity to avoid cross-pollination and bird damage, the data about panicle type could not be collected accurately. Thus, we only investigated the phenotype of panicle type at Ledong in 2021. Three random panicles were collected from each plot and subsequently measured the phenotypes of IM traits using straightedge rulers (1 mm precision) PL was measured as the distance from the apex of the panicle to the point of the lowest panicle node. NCN was recorded as the number from the lowest panicle node to the uppermost panicle node on the spike-stalk. NPB was recorded as the number of primary branches attached to the panicle nodes. LLPB was measured from the longest primary branch.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed by using the multi-factor ANOVA procedure in order to compare the relative importance of main model terms: genotype (G), environment (E), and genotype × environment (G × E) interaction. Broad-sense heritability (hB2) of each trait was estimated according to the following equation: hB2 = σg2/(σg2 + σg12/n + σe2/nr) Where σg2 is the genotypic variance component; r and n are replicates and environments, respectively; σg12 is the variance of the interaction genotype × environment; σe2 is the error32. Descriptive statistics and phenotypic correlations of the traits obtained were computed using the genotypic means by using SPSS.

Genome resequencing

Each genome of 242 accessions were re-sequenced to detect genome-wide SNPs as described previously31. Briefly, reference genome sequences of BTx623 and annotation files were downloaded from the Phytozome website (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov, Sorghum bicolor v3.1.1). Raw reads of 242 accessions were filtered and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using the bwa-mem method in BWA-MEM version 0.7.1764. SNPs and indels of each accession were called by SelectVariants and were filtered by VariantFiltration of using GATK65.

Genome-wide association study

Totally, 2,015,850 high quality SNPs were obtained in 242 Chinese south–north sorghum accessions and used to perform population structure and GWAS analysis. The GWAS was carried out using the Genome Association and Prediction Integrated Tool package (GAPIT version: 3.0) with the GLM, Blink, FarmCPU, MLM, and MLMM statistical models (https://www.maizegenetics.net/gapit). The modified Bonferroni correction was used to determine the genome-wide significance thresholds of the GWAS, which were set as a P value of 6.1 × 10–8 or a –log(P) of 7.21. Based on the LD decay distance of 62.5 kb on average for the 242 Chinese accessions31, the location of a QTL was described according to its − log10 (P values) peak location and the surrounding region, with a 95% confidence interval (125 kb interval as 62.5 kb upstream and downstream of a significant SNP) calculated using GAPIT software. Adjacent QTLs on the same chromosome for the same trait were considered as different QTLs if the support intervals did not overlap31. QTLs were named as follows: QTL + chromosome + physical location (bp).

Candidate genes mining

In order to identify candidate genes within or near the QTLs, the BTx623 reference genome was searched, and their putative functions were annotated using available databases, such as Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) and Rice (https://www.ricedata.cn/gene/). LD analysis and inference of the haplotype blocks that contain the peak SNPs were conducted using LDBlockShow (version: 1.39)66 to document the potential candidate genes associated with the traits under investigation.

Haplotype analysis of candidate genes

The haplotype analysis of candidate genes related to IM was performed on 242 genomic sequenced Chinese south–north sorghum accessions using CandiHap version 0.01867. Only exon variations were selected for polymorphic SNPs loci. The phenotypic significance of gene haplotypes was visualized using R package (version: 4.10).

Selective sweep analysis

Our previous research revealed that 242 landrace accessions were mainly classified into south and north genetic groups based on their geographic origins and showed strong domesticated signals31. The selective sweep analysis of 242 sorghum accessions was conducted by a cross approach based on genetic differentiation (Fst), nucleotide diversity (π), and Tajima’D to identify the selection signals across the sorghum genome. A 10 kb sliding window with a 10 kb step approach was applied to quantify Fst, π, and Tajima’D using VCFtools software68. The candidate genes that met the top 5% of the three tests were selected as selective signals.

RNA-seq analysis

Two sorghum inbred lines 654 and LTR108 were chosen for RNA-seq analysis, because 654 is a typical semi-loose panicle and LTR108 is a typical compact panicle. Young panicle samples were collected at the S1 (< 5 cm), S2 (10–15 cm), and S3 (5 d after heading) stages, respectively. Total RNA was isolated using a protocol reported previously69. RNA sequencing was described by Zhang et al.31. To determine gene expression levels, the expression was quantified using the fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) metric with Cufflinks and cuffdiff270. The identification of differentially expressed genes between the two lines was determined by considering their corrected P values (P value < 0.05). Each sample had three biological replicates.
