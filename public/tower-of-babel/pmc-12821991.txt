Exploring research barriers, priorities and perceptions among respiratory therapists in Asia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gokul G Krishna, Ann M Jose, Weaam Rahali, Majed Alkahtani, Ziyad M Alshahrani, Mazen M Homoud, Mohammed Almeshari, Khalid S Alwadeai, Mohammed Dhafer Alahmari, Ayedh Dhafer Alahmari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory therapists (RT) are healthcare professionals who receive specialized training to apply evidence-based practices in managing pulmonary disorders. Despite global expansion of RT programs across Asia, there is only limited RT-specific research, compelling practitioners to often rely on research from other disciplines. This study explores research barriers among Asian RTs, investigates priority research topics, and examines perceptions regarding research involvement.MethodsA cross-sectional survey, adapted with permission from a previously used US instrument, was conducted between November 2024 and March 2025. The questionnaire comprised five sections with 45 items covering demographics, research involvement, training, perceived barriers, motivators, and perceptions, including a 4-item scale assessing RTs’ roles and engagement in research. The instrument underwent expert review for content relevance and clarity but was not formally validated. Licensed RTs currently practicing in academic, clinical, or combined roles within Asian countries were eligible, while non-licensed individuals, students, and RTs practicing outside Asia were excluded. Recruitment occurred through professional organizations, academic and clinical institutions, and social media networks.ResultsOf 402 respondents from 11 Asian countries, 54.2% were female and 48.0% held bachelor’s degrees. Staff therapists comprised 58.8%, with 43.3% working in adult critical care. Although 54.5% had formal research training, only 35.6% reported mentorship, and half spent < 1 h weekly on research. Formal training was linked to higher publication output (p ≤ 0.001). Major barriers included lack of training (n = 109) and limited opportunities (n = 70), while lung ultrasound (n = 85) was the top priority and improving patient outcomes (n = 125) the strongest motivator. The perception scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.76). Male gender was a negative predictor (OR = 0.05; p < 0.0001), whereas Master’s (OR = 21.37; p < 0.001), Doctorate degrees (OR = 37.04; p < 0.001), faculty positions (OR = 20.91; p = 0.004), research funding (OR = 25.02; p < 0.001), on-the-job training (OR = 36.07; p = 0.01), and self-directed learning (OR = 7.30; p = 0.03) significantly increased engagement.ConclusionLimited training, time constraints, and lack of mentorship are key barriers to research among RTs in Asia, along with a gap between research priorities and available resources. Given the study’s cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Nonetheless, the findings emphasize the need for institutional support, mentorship, and research development initiatives to strengthen evidence-based practice and improve patient outcomes across the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08464-1.Keywords: Respiratory therapy, Barriers to research, Evidence-Based practice, Education, Professional development, Respiratory care research

Background

Respiratory therapists (RTs) are healthcare professionals trained to apply evidence-based practices in the management of pulmonary disorders [1, 2]. Originating in the United States, the profession is now well-established across North America, with approximately 191,000 licensed practitioners in the U.S. and over 12,000 in Canada [3, 4]. Globally, respiratory therapy has expanded rapidly, with dedicated educational programs and clinical roles emerging in various Asian countries—including Taiwan, the Philippines, China, and India—as well as in several Middle Eastern nations such as Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Egypt, Jordan, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen [5].

Similar to North America, RTs in most Asian countries are primarily responsible for managing mechanical ventilation, performing pulmonary diagnostics, and delivering respiratory care across diverse healthcare settings. This professional structure contrasts with regions such as parts of Latin America, where respiratory therapy services are often integrated into broader physiotherapy or nursing roles [6, 7]. While comprehensive profession data for most countries are not available, there is empirical evidence noting approximately 5,400 registered RTs in Saudi Arabia, and approximately 1,500 RTs in India, exemplifying the growing global recognition of the profession [8, 9].

As the profession expand across regions, the demand for EBP has grown, requiring RTs to stay current with technological advances and best practices [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the scarcity of RT-specific research compels many practitioners to draw upon evidence derived from other healthcare disciplines, which may not adequately reflect the distinct clinical complexities inherent to respiratory care [12–14].

Despite their expertise in patient assessment, ventilator management, and monitoring, many RTs in academic hospitals do not actively engage in research, and many lack knowledge and skills to conduct research independently [15–17]. Studies on research priorities in respiratory care often overlook RTs’ perspectives, resulting in findings that may not accurately reflect the profession’s actual priorities [18]. Additionally, while research on RTs’ experiences and barriers to participation exists in the United States and Canada, no studies have systematically explored these aspects among RTs in Asian healthcare settings.

The aim of the study is to identify barriers to research participation among RTs in Asia, to highlight priority research topics, and to assess RTs perceptions of their role in research.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional survey conducted among RTs across multiple Asian countries. Data was collected between November 10, 2024, and March 1, 2025. The survey was conducted at the Respiratory Therapy Department at Batterjee Medical College, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

Participants and sampling

RTs engaged in academic or clinical roles were deemed eligible for participation. The inclusion criteria required participants must be currently practicing RTs in academic, clinical, or combined roles within Asian countries during the study period, possess the ability to read and comprehend English, and be willing to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria encompassed non-licensed individuals, students or interns lacking independent professional responsibilities, RTs practicing exclusively outside Asia during the data collection phase, and those who either declined or did not complete the consent procedures. A convenience sampling method was employed, with recruitment facilitated through professional societies, respiratory care organizations, regional representatives, and institutional networks.

Survey instrument

The survey instrument was originally developed to investigate the research involvement and perceptions of RTs in the United States [19]. Permission for its use was obtained from the original author. To ensure contextual relevance, minor wording modifications were made, prior to dissemination. The adaptation process prioritized maintaining content fidelity to the original instrument to allow comparability with previous studies. Before implementation, the survey was reviewed by a panel of RT educators and clinicians from three countries to assess face validity and clarity of wording; however, no formal psychometric validation was conducted.

The final instrument contained approximately 40–45 items and consisted of a combination of multiple-choice, ranking, and open-ended questions, organized into the following six main sections:

Demographics and Background: Collected information on respondents’ profession, role, education level, years of experience, and institutional affiliation.

Research Training and Mentorship: Assessed the type and extent of training received (e.g., graduate programs, workshops, self-learning) and mentorship experiences.

Research Experience: Addressed prior publication history, research funding sources, and participation in various research roles and study types.

Research Motivation and Barriers: Respondents ranked reasons for engagement (e.g., promotion, personal interest, job requirement) and identified major barriers (e.g., lack of time, support, training, funding). Reasons were ranked with “1” indicating the most important.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the single-proportion formula, where Z = 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, p = 0.20 (derived from the proportion observed in a pilot survey of 30 respiratory therapists), and d = 0.05 (margin of error). This calculation resulted in a minimum requirement of 246 participants. To accommodate an anticipated response rate of 70%, the number of invitations increased to 352.

Data collection procedure

The survey was hosted on SurveyMonkey, a secure online platform. The link was distributed through professional societies, respiratory care organizations, regional representatives, academic department heads, and social media platforms (Facebook, Twitter/X, LinkedIn). To maintain engagement, weekly reminders were sent to designated contacts, and the survey link was periodically reshared throughout the data collection period.

Data analysis

A test of normality was performed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which indicated a violation of normality. Hence, Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). For categorical variables, frequency counts and percentages were used to describe the data. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between continuous variables. The internal consistency of the Likert-scale items evaluating RTs perceptions of their role and involvement in research within critical care was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Binary logistic regression was conducted to assess associations between publishing as a lead author and demographic, educational, and professional factors. Results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, New York).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee at Batterjee Medical College (RES-2024-0092). All participants provided informed consent, and the study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The study included 402 RTs respondents, of whom 54.2% were female. Although the study was conducted in Saudi Arabia, the highest proportion of respondents (32.6%) resided in India. Additionally, 55.7% worked in healthcare settings. 67.9% held a bachelor’s degree as their highest qualification. Staff therapists comprised 58.8% of the respondents, with 25.6% having 1 to 3 years of experience, primarily in adult critical care (43.3%) (Table 1). Among participants, 54.48% received formal research training through graduate programs, while only 35.57% had a formal research mentor. Research responsibilities were part of job roles for 46.3%, and 42.0% had a departmental research committee. Only 51.0% spend less than one hour per week on research activities. While 57.7% had departmental publications, only 28.1% were lead authors, and 14.9% received funding (Table 2). A statistically significant difference was observed between individuals who received formal research training and those who did not in terms of publications. Participants with formal training had a higher median (IQR) number of publications as lead authors [0 (1) vs. 0 (0), p ≤ 0.001] and a greater number of published peer-reviewed manuscripts [0 (3) vs. 0 (1), p ≤ 0.001]. One-on-one mentorship was the preferred training method for 42.54%. Participants identified research training (28.86%) and funding opportunities (27.11%) as the top priorities for enhancing RT research participation. RTs valued their technical expertise (32.09%) with respiratory equipment as their most significant contribution to research teams (Table 3).

Research engagement and training (n = 402)

The most frequently reported barrier to research among RTs was a lack of training (Rank 1: n = 109), followed by limited opportunities for participation (n = 70), lack of mentorship (n = 51), and time constraints (n = 55). Challenges with statistical analysis also ranked highly (n = 82), whereas lack of leadership support, limited personal interest, and lack of external incentives were reported less frequently (Fig. 1). Bedside imaging (lung ultrasound) ranked highest among research priorities (n = 85), followed by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO; n = 53) and high-flow nasal cannula therapy (HFNC) (n = 46). Noninvasive and invasive ventilation remained consistently important, while bronchoscopy, home care, and transport received minimal prioritization (Fig. 2). Improving patient outcomes was the top research motivator (n = 125), followed by job requirements (n = 90), and personal interest (n = 87). Professional prestige also ranked as a key motivator (n = 94), whereas promotion and salary were secondary factors with varying importance among RTs (Fig. 3).

The Perceptions of RTs Role and Research Involvement in Critical Care scale (4 items) demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.76). However, only 45.03% felt that their RT departments provided adequate research support. Additionally, 87.81% agreed that the role of RTs in critical care warrants further investigation (Table 4).

The regression model exhibited substantial explanatory power, as indicated by a Nagelkerke R² value of 0.75 (Table 5). Male gender emerged as a significant negative predictor of research engagement, with an OR of 0.05 and a p-value < 0.0001. Higher educational attainment was strongly associated with increased research involvement, with individuals holding a Master’s degree (OR = 21.37; p < 0.001) or a Doctorate (OR = 37.04; p < 0.001) demonstrating significantly higher odds compared to those with diploma-level education. Conversely, professional roles, mentorship, and job descriptions entailing research responsibilities did not emerge as significant predictors.

Discussion

This research presents findings on the key drivers and persistent barriers influencing research engagement among Respiratory Therapists (RTs) across Asia. Inadequate training emerged as the predominant barrier, with bedside imaging ranking highest in research priorities. Patient outcome improvement was the primary motivational factor, which reflects respiratory care professionals’ intrinsic commitment to clinical excellence. Although RTs expressed a strong interest in research, their transition to active participation is significantly influenced by specific professional and demographic variables. Male gender emerged as a negative predictor, indicating higher research engagement among female professionals. Higher education levels and faculty positions strongly predicted greater research involvement. Access to funding and dedicated research time were key enablers, while on-the-job learning and self-directed study enhanced engagement through flexible capacity building. However, professional roles, mentorship, and research responsibilities in job descriptions did not independently predict engagement, suggesting structural support and resources outweigh organizational factors in research activity.

Previous nationwide studies from the United States and Canada have consistently identified challenges such as limited secured time for research, restricted opportunities, insufficient formal training, and inadequate institutional support as barriers to research participation among respiratory care professionals [15, 19]. Similarly, our findings also highlight persistent barriers to research engagement among RTs, including inadequate training, limited access to research opportunities, insufficient mentorship, and constraints related to time. These findings highlight the pressing need for a collaborative approach that brings together healthcare professionals, academic institutions, and regulatory bodies to cultivate a professional environment that values and supports research and innovation [20].

Prior studies from North American countries observed research priorities such as invasive ventilation, advanced monitoring, airway clearance techniques, chronic disease management, and aging populations [15, 19]. In contrast, our study found priorities related to emerging trends such as bedside imaging- lung ultrasound, ECMO, and HFNC therapy indicating an evolving focus in respiratory care [21, 22]. These differences likely reflect regional variations in healthcare delivery models, clinical exposure, and evolving institutional demands [9, 23]. In Asian contexts, RTs are more actively engaged in critical care and acute management, which can shift the focus toward high-acuity tools rather than chronic care or geriatric respiratory issues [24]. Furthermore, the relatively younger and early-career RT workforce in Asia, as observed in our sample, may also influence research interests toward cutting-edge or high-technology topics that align with their current clinical roles and aspirations [25]. Although RTs in our study expressed confidence in their ability to conduct research, systemic obstacles continue to impede active participation. Previous literature emphasizes that RTs recognize the value of research and the unique insights they can contribute to the field [15, 18]. However, despite these aspirations, many face structural and institutional limitations that curtail their involvement. These disconnects were evident in our study where a considerable number of participants reported a perceived lack of institutional support, which is a major challenge to fostering meaningful RT-led research initiatives.

Interestingly, while research components are reportedly included in RT bachelor’s programs across several Asian countries [26–28]. Our data suggests that many participants had limited formal training in research methods, which indicates a gap between academic curricula and actual research preparedness. This trend is consistent with findings from the United States, where clinical practice often takes precedence over research training in RT education [19].

Time constraints emerged as another significant barrier, likely reflecting the clinical demands placed on staff therapists, who often prioritize patient care over academic or scholarly pursuits [19, 29]. Notably, although some departments reported publishing research, only a small number of RTs reported having served as lead authors. Which may reflect both a lack of research leadership experience and a shortage of mentorship, which in turn limits RTs’ ability to take on prominent roles in research initiatives. These factors may contribute to the low representation of RTs in Delphi panels and expert consensus groups, reducing their visibility in shaping practice guidelines [14].

The findings should be interpreted cautiously due to diverse RT regulatory landscapes across Asia. Professional maturity varies from established licensure systems to emerging specializations, affecting sample distribution and presents research challenges. The cohort is predominantly from India (32.59%) and Saudi Arabia (23.13%), reflecting different professionalization paths. While Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and UAE have centralized licensure systems with credential verification, other nations are developing formal pathways through professional societies [8, 27]. The COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated RT development, but lack of standardized credentialing remains a barrier, in China, RTs receive training through specialized programs or ICU nurse training [30]. The reported research priorities mainly reflect experiences from Gulf cooperation council (GCC) settings and India, with underrepresentation from countries with less uniform accreditation.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. The use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting RTs who already have an interest in research. As participation was voluntary and anonymous, we were unable to independently verify respondents’ professional credentials or current employment status as RTs, which is a common constraint in survey-based research. The survey instrument, distributed exclusively in English to all respondents regardless of nationality, was not formally validated or culturally adapted for the diverse healthcare settings represented in Asia. This limits comparability and introduces the risk of misinterpretation. Additionally, the geographic distribution of respondents was uneven. A significant portion of participants were from India and Saudi Arabia, while countries with long-established respiratory therapy professions—such as Taiwan, had no representation, and the Philippines were underrepresented with only a small number of responses. This imbalance limits the generalizability of findings and may reflect region-specific barriers rather than providing a comprehensive pan-Asian perspective. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution and are not generalizable beyond the specific sample surveyed. Our study also did not explore the potential impact of cultural differences on research engagement, autonomy, or institutional support, which could be important areas for future investigation. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to assess causality or evaluate the effects of potential interventions aimed at improving research participation.

Future research should focus on evaluating targeted interventions such as structured mentorship programs, designated research time, and specialized training for RTs. Longitudinal studies would be particularly valuable in assessing how these strategies impact research engagement over time. Additionally, efforts should be made to integrate RT led research into broader institutional frameworks, ensuring that their contributions are recognized and utilized in the development of clinical guidelines. Support from professional societies, such as the American Association of Respiratory Care (AARC), American Thoracic Society (ATS), and European Respiratory Society (ERS), will be essential in addressing these challenges by providing resources, networking opportunities, and advocacy for RT involvement in research. Enhancing RT involvement in research not only supports professional growth but also ensures that clinical practices are informed by evidence generated from within the field itself.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the significant barriers to research participation faced by RTs in Asia, notably restricted access to formal research training, limited time, insufficient mentorship, and a disconnect between emerging research priorities and available resources. Conversely, advanced education, faculty roles, research funding, protected time, and accessible training pathways were reported as major enablers of research engagement. Given the cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported data, convenience sampling, and uneven geographical representation, these findings should be interpreted as reflective of the participating RTs’ experiences and may not generalize to the broader Asian RT population. Nevertheless, the results underscore a clear need for institutional investment in postgraduate education, structured capacity building, and dedicated funding mechanisms. Strengthening these supports can empower RTs to engage in research, promote innovation, foster evidence-based practice, and improve respiratory care outcomes.
