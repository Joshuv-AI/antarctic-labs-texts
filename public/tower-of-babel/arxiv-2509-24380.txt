Agentic Services Computing
Shuiguang Deng 1∗ Hailiang Zhao 1 Ziqi Wang 1 Guanjie Cheng 1 Peng Chen 1 Wenzhuo Qian 1 Zhiwei Ling 1 Jianwei Yin 1 Albert Y. Zomaya 2 Schahram Dustdar 3,4

Abstract
The rise of LLM-powered agents is driving a fundamental transformation in services computing: from static, request-response functions to dynamic, goal-oriented, and autonomous multi-agent ecosystems. In response to this shift, we introduce Agentic Service Computing (ASC), a new paradigm that reimagines services as intelligent, self-adaptive, and socially embedded entities. This comprehensive survey presents a lifecycle-driven framework for ASC, structured around four core phases: Design , Deployment , Operation , and Evolution . We systematically analyze ASC through four foundational research dimensions: (1) Perception, Context, and Environment Modeling , (2) Autonomous Decision-Making and Task Execution , (3) Multi-Agent Collaboration and Organization , and (4) Evaluation, Value Alignment, and Trustworthiness . We examine how these dimensions are instantiated, integrated, and continuously adapted across the service lifecycle. Our synthesis reveals that agentic services are not merely assembled but orchestrated: contextual awareness enables robust deployment; autonomous reasoning supports real-time operation; collaborative structures emerge and evolve through interaction; and trustworthiness must be upheld as a cross-cutting, lifelong imperative. We further identify and discuss emerging trends shaping the future of ASC. By integrating classical principles of services computing with advances in LLM-based multi-agent systems, this work establishes a holistic and forward-looking foundation for ASC. It provides a unified reference for researchers and practitioners aiming to develop adaptive, accountable, and human-centered intelligent services.

Introduction

The field of services computing is undergoing a paradigm shift that redefines what a “service” fundamentally is. Traditionally, services have been conceptualized as function-oriented components: modular APIs or microservices designed for predictable, request-response interactions. However, the rise of large language models (LLMs) and multimodal foundation models has catalyzed the emergence of a new class of software entities:
agents
. These are not mere tools to be invoked, but autonomous, goal-driven, and context-aware entities capable of perceiving environments, reasoning over tasks, taking actions, and collaborating with users and other agents. This evolution calls for a new conceptual framework:
Agentic Services Computing
(ASC).
We posit that ASC emerges from the deep integration of three foundational domains: (i)
Services Computing
, which provides engineering principles for designing, deploying, and governing distributed systems across their lifecycle; (ii)
Multi-Agent Systems
(MAS), which offers models of autonomy, coordination, and social behavior for intelligent entities; and (iii)
LLM Agents
, which endow agents with cognitive capabilities, such as natural language understanding, zero-shot reasoning, and tool-augmented action, enabling unprecedented adaptability and generalization.
ASC is not a simple aggregation of these fields, but an emergent paradigm that reimagines services as living participants in a dynamic, adaptive ecosystem. In this vision, a service is defined not only by its interface, but by its
intent
,
behavior
, and
evolutionary trajectory

Instead, it is a dynamic, goal-driven entity capable of perception, reasoning, action, and collaboration. To unify these advances into a principled, engineering-oriented framework, we propose
Agentic Services Computing

In ASC, LLM-based agents organized through collaborative structures are treated as first-class citizens in the service ecosystem. agentic services

ASC inherits key strengths from its intellectual predecessors. It draws on the lifecycle rigor and architectural principles of services computing, the autonomy and coordination mechanisms of MAS, and the adaptive cognition and natural language fluency of LLM agents. 2
Foundations

ASC is the convergence of decades of research across three foundational domains, including services computing, MAS, and LLM-based agents. Each has contributed essential capabilities: services computing offers engineering rigor and lifecycle management; MAS provides models of autonomy, coordination, and decentralized intelligence; and LLM agents introduce generative cognition and natural language interfaces. Yet, each also exhibits critical limitations when considered in isolation. This section systematically examines these domains as complementary building blocks whose integration is both inevitable and necessary. We argue that the potential of agentic services can only be realized through a unified framework that inherits the strengths of all three while addressing their individual shortcomings. To this end, we analyze each domain along two dimensions: (i) its core contributions to intelligent service design, and (ii) its operational and scalability challenges in open, dynamic environments. This comparative lens sets the stage for ASC, which we formalize in the next section as a lifecycle-oriented paradigm for engineering, deploying, and governing agentic services at scale.

2.1
Services Computing

Services computing has long established the principles for building reliable, composable, and maintainable distributed systems. From its roots in service-oriented architecture (SOA)
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
, the paradigm has evolved through microservices
[
39
]
to serverless computing
[
40
]
, each iteration enhancing agility, scalability, and operational efficiency.

At its core, services computing treats functionality as reusable, lifecycle-managed units. SOA introduced standardized interfaces (e.g., WSDL), messaging protocols (e.g., SOAP, REST), and orchestration languages (e.g., BPEL
[
41
]
), enabling interoperability across heterogeneous enterprise systems. Microservices further decomposed applications into fine-grained, independently deployable services, promoting team autonomy and continuous delivery. Serverless computing abstracts infrastructure entirely, executing functions in response to events with automatic scaling and pay-per-use pricing (e.g., AWS Lambda, Azure Functions). Crucially, services computing emphasizes end-to-end lifecycle governance. Standards such as ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023
[
42
]
define structured processes for system conception, development, operation, and retirement, including configuration management, verification, and risk assessment. Operational practices, such as service-level agreements (SLAs), monitoring (e.g., Prometheus
[
43
]
), visualization (e.g., Grafana
[
44
]
), and distributed tracing (e.g., OpenTelemetry
[
45
]
), ensure accountability, observability, and compliance. Frameworks like ITIL
[
46
]
further institutionalize service management best practices.

Services computing traditionally assumes services are static, function-oriented components with predefined behaviors. It lacks native support for autonomy, goal-driven reasoning, or adaptive behavior, which are capabilities essential for intelligent agents. The paradigm is optimized for predictable workflows, not for agents that perceive, plan, and evolve.

Multi-Agent Systems

47
]
. Unlike passive services, agents are
proactive

The BDI model

provided a cognitive blueprint: agents maintain

(committed plans), enabling deliberate decision-making. FIPA standards

[

[

Despite conceptual richness, classical MAS suffered from brittleness and engineering overhead. Agents relied on hand-crafted knowledge bases and explicit rules, limiting adaptability in open-world settings. Scaling coordination introduced communication overhead and complexity in emergent behavior validation. Most critically, MAS lacked the
operational infrastructure
for deployment, monitoring, and lifecycle management.

2.3
LLM-based Agents

The rise of LLM-based agents is fundamentally enabled by advances in LLMs, which are deep neural networks trained on vast corpora of text to model sequential data and generate human-like language. The Transformer architecture
[
51
]
revolutionized sequence modeling by introducing self-attention mechanisms, enabling efficient parallelization and long-range dependency capture. This breakthrough facilitated the development of increasingly large and capable models, such as GPT
[
52
]
, GPT-2
[
53
]
, GPT-3
[
54
]
, and GPT-4
[
55
]
, which demonstrated emergent capabilities in few-shot learning, reasoning, and task generalization. These models are typically pretrained on massive datasets and can be adapted to downstream tasks via prompting or fine-tuning. Their ability to encode world knowledge, understand natural language, and generate coherent responses forms the cognitive foundation of modern LLM-based agents. Building upon this foundation, researchers have developed agent architectures that structure LLMs’ generative capabilities into goal-directed, environment-interacting workflows.

The advent of LLMs has revolutionized agency by enabling
emergent reasoning
through neural generative capabilities. LLM-based agents do not require explicit programming; instead, they leverage pre-trained world knowledge and natural language understanding to perceive, plan, act, and reflect in dynamic environments. Modern agent architectures structure this generative power into cognitive workflows:

•

Reactive Agents:
Use Chain-of-Thought prompting for step-by-step reasoning
[
56
]
, but lack memory and long-term planning.

•

Act-and-Reason Agents:
Interleave reasoning with action. ReAct
[
15
]
combines thought traces with tool use (e.g., search, code execution), enabling environmental interaction and error correction.

•

Reflective Agents:
Incorporate self-evaluation. Reflexion
[
57
]
simulates feedback to refine strategies; Tree of Thoughts
[
58
]
explores multiple reasoning paths for global optimization.

Frameworks like AutoGPT
[
59
]
and BabyAGI
[
60
]
demonstrate autonomous goal pursuit, while benchmarks such as AgentBench
[
61
]
evaluate performance across domains. These agents exhibit unprecedented adaptability and generalization.

LLM agents are often developed as isolated cognitive artifacts in sandboxed environments. They lack integration with service infrastructure. Thus, they have no versioning, monitoring, SLAs, or failure recovery. Their black-box nature raises concerns about reliability, safety, and auditability. Without engineering discipline, their real-world deployment remains fragile.

2.4
Convergence: Toward Agentic Services Computing

The convergence of multi-agent systems, LLM-based agents, and services computing establishes Agentic Services Computing. It centers on agentic services, i.e., autonomous, goal-driven entities that integrate generative cognition with service-oriented computing principles. Unlike traditional services, agentic services exhibit intrinsic reasoning, planning, and adaptation through natural language. Unlike LLM-based agents, they are subject to operational governance, including versioning, monitoring, security enforcement, and SLA compliance. This integration enables scalable deployment in domains such as customer automation, intelligent assistants, and autonomous operations. ASC introduces three foundational shifts: (i) from
state-based
to
goal-driven
execution, (ii) from
deterministic APIs
to generative behaviors, and (iii) from
static orchestration
to dynamic coordination. To address this, we propose a lifecycle of agentic services in the following section, providing structured guidance for the design, deployment, operation, and evolution of trustworthy agentic services.

3
Agentic Service: Concept, Characteristics, and Lifecycle Model

At the heart of ASC is a fundamental reimagining of the “service” as a computational entity. Unlike traditional services, which are typically stateless, passive, and triggered by explicit requests, agentic services are
persistent, goal-oriented agents
capable of initiating actions, maintaining internal state, reasoning about their environment, and adapting autonomously. These agents are not mere endpoints but active participants in dynamic service ecosystems, reshaping how we design, deploy, and govern distributed systems. This paradigm shift is defined by five interrelated characteristics that collectively establish a new foundation for intelligent service systems. We organize these principles under the
Scale
framework, a structured and memorable model that captures the evolution from passive service endpoints to autonomous, adaptive, and trustworthy agents. Figure
4
presents the comparison between agentic services and traditional services.

•

S
tateful & Context-Aware Interaction.
Unlike traditional stateless services that process each request in isolation, agentic services maintain persistent memory of interactions, user preferences, environmental states, and historical decisions. This contextual continuity enables coherent, personalized, and long-horizon engagement across sessions. Such memory is typically managed through vector databases (e.g., Pinecone
[
62
]
, Weaviate
[
63
]
), knowledge graphs, or session stores. However, this capability introduces challenges in data privacy, memory consistency, and the risk of cognitive overload from excessive context retention.

•

C
ognitive Autonomy.
While classical services are purely reactive, agentic services exhibit proactive behavior, e.g., initiating actions, managing internal states, and adapting strategies based on experience. This autonomy is powered by LLM-based reasoning mechanisms such as ReAct
[
15
]
and Reflexion
[
57
]
, enabling agents to plan, reflect, and self-correct. Supported by persistent runtime environments, this autonomy shifts the service model from ephemeral invocation to continuous agency. Yet it also raises critical concerns around resource consumption, security (e.g., unauthorized actions), and the difficulty of debugging non-deterministic, emergent behaviors.

•

A
gent as Goal Executor.
Traditional services are invoked via fixed interfaces (e.g., REST APIs) to execute predefined functions. In contrast, agentic services are triggered by high-level
goals
or
intentions
, such as “
Prepare a market analysis report for product X
”. The agent autonomously decomposes the goal, selects appropriate tools (e.g., search, code execution, API calls), sequences subtasks, and monitors progress toward completion. This shift from specifying
what to do
to declaring
why to do it
enables unprecedented flexibility in open-ended and dynamic environments, but introduces new challenges in goal specification, execution fidelity, and outcome accountability.

•

L
ifelong Collaborative Learning.
In conventional architectures like SOA or microservices, service composition follows static, pre-defined workflows (e.g., BPMN, WS-BPEL). Agentic services, however, engage in emergent collaboration: dynamically forming teams, negotiating roles, delegating tasks, and resolving conflicts through protocols such as contract net
[
48
]
or auction mechanisms
[
64
]
. This capacity for self-organizing coordination enables resilience, adaptability, and collective problem-solving in complex scenarios. It also necessitates research into decentralized control, incentive alignment, and stability in multi-agent ecosystems, where collaboration evolves over time through shared experience.

•

E
nd-to-end Lifecycle Governance.
As agentic services grow in autonomy and persistence, governance must extend beyond deployment and monitoring to encompass their entire lifecycle. This includes versioning of agent logic, memory schemas, and policy rules; continuous auditing of safety, fairness, and compliance; and formal decommissioning procedures to prevent “zombie agents” from persisting unattended. Drawing from AI ethics
[
65
]
, regulatory frameworks (e.g., GDPR
[
66
]
), and DevSecOps practices
[
67
]
, end-to-end governance ensures that scalability does not come at the cost of accountability, transparency, or trustworthiness.

3.1
The Lifecycle of Agentic Services

We define the lifecycle of an agentic service as a continuous progression through four key phases:
(1)
Design
, covering cognitive architecture, role specification, and collaboration protocols;
(2)
Deployment
, involving agent activation, memory initialization, and trust establishment;
(3)
Operation
, where agents execute goals, coordinate dynamically, and operate under human-in-the-loop monitoring; and
(4)
Evolution
, enabling continuous learning, adaptation, and updates to improve performance over time.
This lifecycle extends traditional models from SOA
[
36
]
, microservices
[
39
]
, and DevOps
[
67
]
by incorporating cognitive reliability, adaptive coordination, and ethical autonomy. Unlike conventional services, agentic systems are designed for long-term persistence and ongoing evolution, forming a closed loop where operational experience directly informs future adaptation.

Design

The
Design
phase establishes the structural and cognitive blueprint of agentic services. It draws from classical MAS methodologies such as the Gaia model
[
68
]
and Tropos
[
69
]
, which emphasize agent roles, goals, and social dependencies. Building on the AGR framework
[
50
]
, designers define organizational topologies (e.g., hierarchical, peer-to-peer) and coordination protocols (e.g., contract net
[
48
]
, auction-based mechanisms
[
64
]
).

Cognitive workflows are modeled using LLM agent architectures such as ReAct
[
15
]
, Reflexion
[
57
]
, and Tree of Thoughts
[
58
]
. These are formalized into modular components (perception, planning, action, reflection) and composed using workflow engines like LangGraph
[
70
]
or Cadence
[
71
]
. Design also incorporates safety-by-design principles, including content moderation
[
72
]
, action guards
[
73
]
, and fallback mechanisms
[
74
]
. To support service-oriented integration, agents are designed with well-defined APIs (e.g., REST, gRPC) and service contracts, following SOA principles
[
36
]
. The use of domain-specific languages (DSLs) or modeling tools (e.g., UML extensions for agents
[
75
]
) enables early validation through simulation and model checking
[
76
]
.

Deployment

Deployment
operationalizes designed agentic services by instantiating them on scalable, resilient service infrastructures. This phase leverages containerization (Docker
[
77
]
) and orchestration platforms (Kubernetes
[
78
]
) to manage agent lifecycles dynamically and at scale. For event-driven invocation patterns, serverless platforms, such as AWS Lambda and OpenFaaS
[
79
]

[
67
]
. These pipelines automate testing (unit, integration, behavioral), security scanning, and configuration validation. Agent artifacts, e.g., LLM prompts, tool bindings, policy rules, and memory schemas, are rigorously versioned using MLOps tools like MLflow
[
80
]
, Weights&Biases
[
81
]
, or DVC
[

, ensuring reproducibility and auditability across deployments. To minimize operational risk, deployment strategies such as canary releases

5
Perception, Context, and Environment Modeling

For agentic services to operate effectively in real-world environments, they must first perceive their surroundings and construct a coherent, actionable understanding of context. This capability, known as environment modeling, is foundational to all subsequent decision-making, planning, and interaction. Unlike traditional AI agents that operate in symbolic or fully observable domains, modern agentic services are increasingly deployed in open-ended, dynamic, and multimodal environments, ranging from physical spaces sensed via cameras and LiDAR to digital ecosystems driven by user interactions and social dynamics, necessitating robust, adaptive, and context-aware perception mechanisms. This section examines how perception, context, and environment modeling are realized across the agent service lifecycle. We emphasize that environment modeling is not a one-time design artifact but a cross-cutting concern that must be systematically integrated into service design principles, deployment practices, and evolution strategies. Specifically, during the
Design
phase, perception architectures must be co-developed with functional and non-functional requirements to ensure semantic alignment and system coherence; in the
Deployment
phase, context-aware models must be efficiently instantiated and calibrated under real-world constraints such as latency, resource availability, and privacy regulations; and throughout the
Evolution
phase, environment models must be continuously monitored, validated, and updated to adapt to concept drift, new modalities, or shifting user behaviors. Figure

5.1
Design: Modeling the Service-Environment Interface

In the
Design
phase of the agentic service lifecycle, the foundation for effective environment interaction is laid through the deliberate modeling of the service-environment interface. This critical stage involves specifying the sensory modalities (e.g., text, vision, audio, robotic sensor streams), defining the structure and semantics of observation spaces, and encoding assumptions about environmental dynamics. For instance, partial observability, noise characteristics, and temporal coherence. Far from being a mere technical configuration, this phase establishes the epistemic boundaries of the agent: what it can perceive, how it interprets stimuli, and how it grounds its decisions in the real world.

5.1.1
Modality-Agnostic Architectures and Perception-Reasoning Decoupling

A key trend in agentic service design is the adoption of
modality-agnostic
architectures that decouple perception from reasoning, enabling modular, reusable, and composable service components. The Flamingo model
[
105
]
exemplifies this paradigm, employing a perceiver resampler to condition a frozen LLM on interleaved images and text, thereby enabling few-shot multimodal reasoning without retraining the core language engine. Similarly, PaLM-E
[
106
]
integrates visual and robotic sensor inputs directly into an LLM, allowing embodied agents to generate plans grounded in real-world observations. This architectural separation supports perception pluggability, where different sensor stacks or preprocessing pipelines can be swapped without altering the core reasoning logic.

5.1.2
Structured World Representations for Grounded Reasoning

At design time, environment modeling also involves constructing structured, semantically rich world representations that bridge raw sensory input and high-level reasoning. Some systems leverage knowledge graphs to encode domain-specific facts, ontologies, and relational constraints
[
139
]
, aligning with formal semantic service description frameworks such as OWL-S
[
103
]
and facilitating machine-understandable service composition. Others employ spatial memory maps or neural scene representations for navigation, manipulation, and spatial reasoning tasks
[
140
]
. The ALFRED benchmark
[
104
]
formalizes complex household tasks within a simulated environment featuring rich visual and linguistic inputs, serving not only as an evaluation platform but also as an influential design pattern for grounding agent perception in actionable, goal-oriented scenarios.

5.1.3
Designing for Safety, Privacy, and Uncertainty

Crucially, the design phase is where critical non-functional requirements (e.g., regulatory compliance) are first encoded into the service architecture. For instance, agents may be explicitly constrained from processing sensitive biometric data (e.g., facial recognition or voiceprints) or required to anonymize or perturb inputs prior to ingestion
[
141
]
, in alignment with data governance frameworks such as GDPR
[
66
]
and the FAIR principles
[
142
]
. To embed compliance into the system fabric, these constraints are routinely specified through policy annotations, access control rules, or privacy-preserving by-design patterns, ensuring
compliance-by-design
and safeguarding against future ethical or legal exposure. Moreover, uncertainty modeling (e.g., via Bayesian neural networks or confidence scoring) is often specified at this stage to support robust decision-making under partial observability.

By rigorously defining the service-environment interface during design, developers not only shape the agent’s cognitive capabilities but also lay the groundwork for scalable deployment and sustainable evolution. A well-architected perception interface ensures that the agent can be reliably deployed across diverse environments and incrementally improved over time.

5.2.1
Multimodal Fusion as Service Integration

Effective integration of heterogeneous sensory data is a cornerstone of deployed agentic services. Modern systems employ diverse fusion strategies, each with distinct implications for service architecture and performance: (i)
Early fusion
combines raw inputs (e.g., image pixels and audio waveforms) before feature extraction. While potentially powerful in capturing low-level cross-modal correlations, it demands precise temporal alignment and synchronization, which is often impractical in distributed, asynchronous service environments. (ii)
Late fusion
processes each modality independently through specialized encoders and combines decisions at the output or decision level. This approach enhances modularity, enables fault isolation, and supports incremental upgrades, which are well-suited for microservice-based agent architectures. (iii)
Cross-modal attention
has emerged as a dominant paradigm, enabling dynamic, context-sensitive interaction between modalities. Models such as CLIP
[
107
]
and BLIP
[
143
]
learn joint embeddings of images and text, allowing agents to perform zero-shot retrieval (e.g., locating visual content via natural language queries) and semantic grounding. This mechanism supports flexible, goal-driven perception without retraining. (iv)
Modality translation
extends fusion by aligning disparate modalities into a shared latent space. ImageBind
[
109
]
, for instance, embeds six modalities (image, text, audio, depth, thermal, and IMU data) into a unified representation, enabling zero-shot transfer and cross-modal reasoning even when certain sensors are unavailable.

These architectures are increasingly embedded in production-ready agent frameworks. Examples include Voyager
[
16
]
, a lifelong learning agent for Minecraft that leverages vision and code execution for exploration and construction; Adept’s ACT-1
[
146
]
; and MobileVLM
[
144
,
145
]
, which empower smartphones to assist users by interpreting screen content and touch events in real time. The deployment of such systems necessitates careful optimization for edge devices, including model quantization, caching of embeddings, and adaptive sampling to balance quality and latency.

5.2.2
Active Perception and Contextual Grounding

Passive, stimulus-response perception is insufficient for complex, open-ended tasks. Deployed agents increasingly employ active perception, i.e.,
intentionally
taking actions to gather more informative observations. This includes asking clarifying questions (e.g.,“
Which file did you mean?
”), zooming into image regions, rotating a camera, or requesting additional context from users or other services
[
147
]
. Active perception transforms the agent from a passive observer into a proactive information seeker, improving robustness in ambiguous or noisy environments.

Equally critical is contextual grounding, the ability to interpret perceptions within the broader task, social, and environmental context. Situated reasoning frameworks such as SQA3D
[
110
]
require agents to answer questions using not only visual input but also spatial and temporal context (e.g., physical location and prior interactions). In human-agent collaboration, pragmatic understanding infers speaker intent from tone, gesture, and conversational history
[
148
]
, enabling socially aware responses.

To support long-horizon tasks, memory-augmented architectures play a vital role. MemNets
[
149
]
and Differentiable Neural Computers (DNCs)
[
112
]
enable agents to store, index, and retrieve past observations and interactions. This persistent memory facilitates coherence across sessions, supports counterfactual reasoning, and allows agents to learn from experience, laying the groundwork for continuous adaptation and evolution beyond initial deployment.

5.3
Evolution: Learning to Perceive and Adapt

The
Evolution
phase closes the lifecycle loop by enabling agentic services to refine and extend their perception capabilities over time through experience, feedback, and environmental interaction. Unlike static models, evolving agents adapt to distribution shifts (e.g., novel objects, lighting changes, or new user demographics), improve accuracy, and even expand their sensory understanding. This phase transforms perception from a fixed design artifact into a living, adaptive component of the service.

5.3.1
Continual and Self-Supervised Learning

To sustain performance in non-stationary environments, agents employ continual learning strategies that balance plasticity and stability. Techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)
[
150
]
regularize updates to protect critical weights for prior tasks, while experience replay
[
151
]
stores and revisits representative samples from past environments, mitigating catastrophic forgetting. These methods enable incremental updates to perception models without full retraining, crucial for scalable, online deployment.

Complementing this, self-supervised learning reduces dependence on costly labeled data. Models like BEiT
[
114
]
and MAE
[
113
]
leverage masked image modeling to pre-train visual encoders on unlabeled data, achieving strong downstream performance with minimal fine-tuning. In multimodal contexts, contrastive objectives, as in CLIP
[
107
]
, enable large-scale pre-training on web-scraped image-text pairs, supporting zero-shot transfer and rapid adaptation to new domains. Together, these approaches form the foundation for lifelong perceptual learning, allowing agents to grow their understanding without constant human annotation.

5.3.2
Feedback-Driven Perception Refinement

Real-world deployment generates rich signals for perception refinement. Agents leverage both explicit and implicit feedback to correct errors and close performance gaps:

•

Human-in-the-loop correction
: Interactive systems allow users to flag misclassifications or misinterpretations (e.g., “
That’s not the file I meant
”), with corrections logged and used for targeted retraining
[
152
]
. This closed-loop interaction enhances trust and personalization.

•

Uncertainty-aware escalation
: In safety-critical applications, anomaly detection modules identify low-confidence or out-of-distribution (OOD) perceptions
[
153
]
, triggering human review or fallback behaviors. This ensures robustness while capturing edge cases for future learning.

•

A/B testing and telemetry
: Controlled experiments across perception variants (e.g., different object detectors or fusion strategies) enable data-driven optimization of perceptual pipelines
[
102
]
. Task failure logs and user engagement metrics further reveal perception blind spots, such as persistent occlusion or modality misalignment, prompting architectural improvements, such as integrating new sensors or refining fusion logic.

This feedback-driven adaptation not only improves accuracy but can also trigger higher-level evolution, such as redesigning the service-environment interface (cf. Section
5.1
) or reconfiguring deployment-time active perception strategies (cf. Section
5.2
).

5.4
Summary and Challenges

This section has explored how perception, context modeling, and environmental understanding form the cognitive foundation of agentic services across the lifecycle: from
Design
, where sensory interfaces and world representations are architected; through
Deployment
, where multimodal inputs are processed under real-world constraints; to
Evolution
, where agents adapt through feedback and experience. While advances in fusion architectures, active perception, and self-supervised learning have significantly enhanced agent capabilities, practical deployment reveals persistent gaps between controlled design assumptions and open-world complexity. We identify three fundamental challenges that cut across these phases, representing critical frontiers for future research and system design:

1.

Generalizable Contextual Representation.

In the
Design
phase, perception models are typically trained under narrow, curated assumptions about context, such as fixed lighting, predictable object arrangements, or domain-specific language. These models often fail to generalize when exposed to novel environments or cultural settings. The core challenge is to move beyond surface-level statistical patterns toward representations grounded in invariant semantic structures: object affordances, spatial hierarchies, physical dynamics, and social norms. Achieving this requires the principled integration of structured prior knowledge (e.g., ontologies, physics engines, or commonsense reasoning modules) into the perception architecture from the outset, enabling robust transfer and zero-shot adaptation.

2.

Robustness to Environmental Uncertainty.

During
Deployment
, agents operate under inevitable environmental perturbations: sensor noise, occlusions, lighting variations, and partial observability. Although multimodal fusion improves resilience, many systems remain brittle when one or more modalities degrade (e.g., camera failure in low light, speech distortion in noisy environments). The challenge lies in building adaptive perception pipelines that can dynamically assess uncertainty, reweight modalities based on reliability, and invoke graceful fallback strategies (e.g., querying the user or switching to alternative sensing). This demands tighter integration of uncertainty quantification, active perception, and fault-tolerant design.

3.

Continual Environmental Understanding.

Environments and user expectations are not static. New objects emerge, interaction patterns shift, and task goals evolve overtime. In the
Evolution
phase, static perception models suffer from semantic drift and performance decay. The challenge is to enable lifelong environmental understanding: agents must continuously refine their perceptual and contextual knowledge through user feedback, anomaly detection, and self-supervised exploration
[
16
]
, while avoiding catastrophic forgetting and maintaining system stability. This necessitates learning algorithms that balance plasticity with retention, and architectures that support modular, interpretable updates.

Addressing these challenges requires a holistic, lifecycle-aware approach to agentic perception. Success will not only improve individual agent performance but also enable the emergence of resilient, trustworthy, and truly adaptive service ecosystems.

6
Autonomous Decision-Making and Task Execution

While perception establishes the input boundary of an intelligent agent, autonomous decision-making and execution define its behavioral core. They are the mechanisms through which intent is translated into action. Modern agentic systems are no longer constrained to executing fixed scripts; instead, they dynamically reason, plan, act, and recover in open-ended environments. This section advances beyond superficial task decomposition to examine the deep structural parallels between agent architectures and the service lifecycle, with a focus on the
Operation
,
Evolution
, and
(Re)Design
phases.

We argue that state-of-the-art agents are evolving into self-organizing service ecosystems: distributed, adaptive, and resilient systems that autonomously compose capabilities, manage runtime state, and reconfigure themselves in response to changing goals and environments. This transformation is driven by advances in reasoning-action coupling, modular tool integration, embodied grounding, and fault-tolerant control, which are capabilities that mirror those of robust distributed services in cloud computing and microservices architectures
[
39
,
154
]
. By analyzing these mechanisms through a services computing lens, we reveal how agents are not merely intelligent software, but next-generation service entities. Figure

6.1
Operation: Dynamic Service Orchestration and Real-Time Execution Control

In services computing, the
Operation
phase encompasses runtime invocation, state management, failure recovery, and SLA compliance, ensuring reliable service delivery under dynamic conditions. For agentic services, this translates to multistep task execution under uncertainty, where reasoning, tool use, and environmental interaction are tightly coupled in a continuous control loop. Unlike static workflows, agents must dynamically coordinate actions, adapt to feedback, and maintain contextual awareness, effectively functioning as self-governing service orchestrators. This section examines four key pillars of robust execution: (1) reasoning-action interleaving for adaptive planning, (2) modular tool composition as dynamic service integration, (3) embodied grounding to ensure physical feasibility, and (4) fault-tolerant mechanisms for resilient operation. Together, these form a runtime control architecture that mirrors resilient distributed systems, enabling agents to operate reliably in unpredictable environments.

6.1.1
Reasoning-Action Interleaving: The ReAct Paradigm

The ReAct framework
[
15
]
establishes a closed-loop interaction between internal reasoning and external action, enabling agents to adaptively plan and execute in dynamic environments. In contrast to static chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
[
56
]
, which generates a monolithic reasoning trace before any action, ReAct interleaves natural language reasoning with concrete tool invocations, allowing the agent to revise its strategy based on real-world observations. This process emulates model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL)
[
155
]
, where an internal model predicts outcomes and guides action selection. Formally, let
\pi_{\theta}(s_{t})\to(a_{t},r_{t})
denote a policy that, given state
s_{t}
, produces an action
a_{t}
and a reasoning trace
r_{t}
. The environment returns an observation
o_{t+1}
, which updates both the agent’s state and its ongoing reasoning. This loop is governed by two functions:

•

State transition:

s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},a_{t})
, where
f
models the effect of action execution (e.g., API call, navigation).

•

Reasoning update:

r_{t+1}=g(s_{t+1},\mathcal{K})
, where
g
generates updated reasoning using the new observation and background knowledge
\mathcal{K}
.

This structure implements a real-time control system, in which autonomous services coordinate through asynchronous message exchange without centralized orchestration. It enables agents to recover from missteps, explore alternatives, and refine plans incrementally, embodying the agility and adaptability required for real-world operation.

6.1.2
Tool Use as Service Composition

Modern agents treat external tools as modular services, dynamically composing them into complex workflows. HuggingGPT
[
119
]
exemplifies this by using an LLM as a meta-controller that routes tasks across a suite of specialized models, e.g., BLIP for vision, Whisper for speech, and Stable Diffusion for generation, etc., forming a function-as-a-service (FaaS) architecture
[
40
]
. The LLM assesses task requirements, selects appropriate services, sequences their invocation, and synthesizes results, effectively functioning as an intelligent orchestration engine. This mirrors systems like Apache Airflow
[
156
]
or Kubernetes
[
157
]
, but with a crucial distinction: orchestration is not predefined, but generated on demand through semantic understanding. ToolFormer
[
118
]
extends this capability by learning when to invoke tools (e.g., calculator, QA API) via self-supervised prompting, thereby acquiring a latent policy for service invocation. This shift from static pipelines to dynamic composition enables agents to respond flexibly to novel tasks, embodying the principle of just-in-time service integration.

6.1.3
Embodied Execution and Affordance Grounding

In physical or simulated environments, successful execution requires grounding high-level commands in perceptual and physical constraints. The challenge is known as the symbol grounding problem
[
158
]
. SayCan
[
106
]
addresses this by decoupling semantic planning from feasibility assessment. A language model proposes semantically coherent plans (e.g., “
bring me a glass of water
”), while a value model estimates the likelihood of success for each candidate action given the agent’s current capabilities and environment. Only actions with high feasibility scores are executed, ensuring that plans respect real-world affordances. This design reflects cognitive architectures like ACT-R
[
159
]
, where declarative knowledge (“
what to do
”) and procedural knowledge (“
how to do it
”) are coordinated but distinct. Systems like RT-2
[
120
]
push further by training end-to-end models that map language and vision inputs directly to robot motor controls, enabling seamless translation from digital intent to physical action. These advances bridge the gap between symbolic reasoning and embodied interaction, a prerequisite for agents operating in the real world.

6.1.4
Execution Robustness and Fault Tolerance

Real-world operation is inherently noisy: network failures, API errors, ambiguous inputs, and environmental changes all threaten task continuity. Agents must therefore implement resilient execution strategies that ensure progress despite uncertainty. Rather than relying on simple retries, advanced agents employ adaptive recovery mechanisms: (1) Rephrasing queries upon search failure
[
15
]
; (2) Switching to alternative tools when primary services are unavailable; (3) Parsing structured error messages (e.g., HTTP 404, rate limit headers) to infer corrective actions. These behaviors mirror established resilience patterns in cloud-native applications, such as circuit breakers, timeout policies, and graceful degradation. Moreover, agents can leverage memory to avoid repeating past failures, effectively implementing learned fault tolerance. From a service architecture perspective, this represents a shift from passive error handling to active recovery planning, where the agent functions as a self-healing endpoint in a larger service ecosystem. This robustness is a prerequisite for trust and usability in real-world deployments. As agents take on increasingly complex tasks, their ability to detect, diagnose, and recover from failures becomes a defining feature of their intelligence.

6.2
Evolution: Lifelong Learning, Reflection, and Meta-Cognitive Control

The
Evolution
phase marks a fundamental departure from traditional software maintenance, where updates are periodic, externally driven, and often disruptive. In contrast, autonomous agents enable continuous, self-directed improvement through internal feedback loops that support adaptation, refinement, and knowledge accumulation over time. This capability is essential for operation in open-ended environments: tasks evolve, user expectations shift, and unforeseen challenges emerge without warning. Agentic services achieve this through mechanisms such as verbal self-reflection
[
57
]
, code-level debugging
[
20
]
, cumulative skill acquisition
[
16
]
, and meta-cognitive strategy adjustment
[
160
]
. These processes go beyond mere performance tuning; they enable agents to develop increasingly sophisticated problem-solving behaviors, mirroring aspects of human cognitive development and organizational learning. From a services computing perspective, this represents a paradigm shift: agents are no longer static service endpoints, but self-evolving service entities that learn from experience, refine their capabilities, and adapt their behavior autonomously. This section examines four key dimensions of agentic evolution: semantic reflection, self-debugging, skill accumulation, and meta-strategic control, together constituting a new model of lifelong service intelligence.

6.2.1
Verbal Reinforcement Learning and Self-Critique

Reinforcement learning in agents has evolved beyond scalar reward signals to incorporate rich, interpretable feedback in natural language. This paradigm is known as
verbal reinforcement learning

[
57
]
. Unlike conventional RLHF
[
92
]
, which trains policies using numerical rewards derived from human preferences, systems like Reflexion
[
57
]
generate post-hoc linguistic critiques after task failure (e.g.,“
I assumed the flight was domestic, but it was international
”). These reflections are stored in a semantic memory buffer and retrieved during future task attempts, effectively constructing a dynamic, context-sensitive reward model. The use of natural language enables several critical advantages: (1)
Interpretability:
Feedback is human-readable and auditable; (2)
Error diagnosis:
Agents can trace failures to specific assumptions or reasoning steps; (3)
Cross-task transfer:
A reflection on date format confusion can improve performance not only in travel planning but also in calendar management and document parsing. This approach transforms failure into structured learning, enabling agents to build a form of operational self-awareness grounded in their own execution history. It mirrors the reflective practice observed in expert human performers, where experience is not just accumulated, but actively interpreted and generalized.

6.2.2
Self-Debugging and Code-Level Adaptation

Beyond high-level reasoning, agents must also evolve at the implementation level. In real-world deployments, generated code often contains logical errors, API misuse, or environmental mismatches. Recent systems demonstrate that agents can autonomously detect and repair such flaws. Frameworks like ReAct
[
15
]
and APRMCTS
[
161
]
implement closed-loop debugging cycles: generated code is executed, runtime exceptions are parsed, and fixes are proposed using in-context learning, iterating until successful execution. Crucially, these agents leverage the semantic understanding of LLMs to infer the intended logic behind the code, allowing them to correct deep semantic errors, not just syntactic bugs. This represents a significant advance over classical automated program repair (APR) techniques
[
162
]
, which rely on mutation and search heuristics without high-level intent modeling. From a systems perspective, this capability enables code-level service evolution, where agents act as self-healing components
[
163
]
, capable of modifying their own behavior in response to execution feedback. Such autonomy is particularly valuable in edge or distributed computing settings
[
164
]
where external intervention is impractical, and system resilience must be internalized.

6.2.3
Cumulative Skill Learning and Cultural Accumulation

Long-term agent intelligence depends not only on adaptation but on the structured accumulation and reuse of knowledge across tasks and environments. Isolated task execution yields isolated improvements; true progress requires compounding gains. Voyager
[
16
]
exemplifies this principle in Minecraft, which is an open-ended environment requiring sustained exploration, tool use, and procedural mastery. The agent maintains a versioned skill library, a growing repository of executable functions (e.g.,
craft_iron_pickaxe()
) curated through interaction. A curriculum agent proposes new skills based on exploration progress, while an instructor agent generates natural language descriptions for usage and composition.

Skills are discovered via trial and error, refined through repeated execution, and composed into increasingly complex behaviors. This process mirrors
cumulative cultural evolution

[
165
]
, where knowledge compounds across generations. In agent systems, it enables a shift from reactive execution to proactive capability building, where each experience contributes to a deeper, structured understanding of the environment. This model aligns with SOAs that emphasize reusable, composable components, which are now extended into the cognitive domain, where skills become modular, versioned, and evolvable services.

6.2.4
Meta-Learning and Strategic Adaptation

To thrive in novel and unpredictable settings, agents must adapt quickly with limited data or prior exposure. This requires not just learning new tasks, but improving the process of learning itself. This is the domain of meta-learning. Frameworks such as MetaLLM
[
121
]
and AutoGPT
[
122
]
implement this through
meta-prompts
that encode higher-order cognitive strategies (e.g.,“
If stuck, search for examples
” or“
Break the problem into subgoals
”). These prompts function as internal heuristics, enabling agents to dynamically adjust their reasoning trajectory based on context, difficulty, or feedback. This embodies the principle of “learning to learn”
[
166
]
, where adaptation occurs at the algorithmic level rather than the output level. These mechanisms resemble self-configuring middleware: the agent autonomously tunes its cognitive policy in response to task demands, resource constraints, or user interaction patterns. For example, when faced with a complex planning task, the agent may activate a hierarchical decomposition strategy; when encountering ambiguity, it may invoke a clarification protocol. This meta-cognitive control is essential for scalable, general-purpose agents that must operate across diverse domains without manual reconfiguration.

6.3
Design: Emergent Planning and Meta-Architecture Generation

In traditional services computing, the
Design
phase is a pre-deployment activity characterized by static workflows, fixed interfaces, and predefined orchestrations. Service composition follows a top-down engineering process, where business logic is encoded into rigid pipelines and interface contracts are established a priori
[
36
]
. In contrast, agentic service performs runtime design: dynamically decomposing tasks, synthesizing plans, and configuring their own cognitive architectures in response to environmental demands. This shift enables flexible, goal-driven behavior in open-ended domains where predefined service orchestrations are insufficient or rapidly become obsolete. Rather than executing a fixed sequence of steps, agents engage in emergent planning, role-based collaboration, and modular reasoning, effectively acting as their own architects. This redefines design not as a one-time blueprinting exercise, but as an ongoing, adaptive process of meta-architectural generation. From a services perspective, this represents the convergence of AI and service-oriented architecture: agents become self-designing service entities that compose, coordinate, and reconfigure functional components at runtime. The following subsections explore three key mechanisms through which this emergent design capability is realized: hierarchical task decomposition, multi-agent self-organization, and modular cognitive offloading.

6.3.1
Hierarchical Task Networks and Runtime Plan Synthesis

Modern agents implicitly implement principles from classical AI planning through natural language-driven goal decomposition, particularly
Hierarchical Task Networks
(HTNs)
[
115
]
. Systems such as ReAct
[
15
]
and TaskMatrix.AI
[
167
]
translate high-level goals (e.g.,“
Plan a birthday party
”) into structured sequences of subtasks, including venue search, calendar checking, guest list management, and email drafting, each potentially invoking an external service or tool. Crucially, this decomposition is not pre-programmed but generated on-the-fly, allowing agents to adapt their plans based on intermediate outcomes, environmental constraints, or user feedback. For example, if a preferred venue is unavailable, the agent may dynamically generate a new subtask to solicit alternatives or adjust the guest count. The resulting structure resembles a dynamic workflow graph, where tasks are conditionally expanded and executed. This capability enables adaptive service composition, where the agent functions as a runtime planner, synthesizing novel workflows without human intervention. It mirrors the flexibility of event-driven architectures, but with the added intelligence of semantic understanding and goal-directed reasoning.

6.3.2
Multi-Agent Societies as Self-Organizing Service Ecosystems

Beyond single-agent planning, design can also emerge from structured social interaction among specialized agents. MetaGPT
[
128
]
exemplifies this paradigm by simulating a startup organization, complete with roles such as CEO, Product Manager, Engineer, QA, and DevOps Engineer. Each agent operates under a defined responsibility and communicates via standardized messages (e.g., requirement specs, code reviews, test reports). Through role-based coordination, the system generates complex deliverables (e.g., codebases, documentation, test suites) via a distributed, iterative process. The CEO sets strategic goals, the PM refines them into technical specifications, the engineer implements the solution, and the QA validates correctness. This mimics real-world service-oriented organizations
[
36
]
, where loosely coupled services interact via contracts, protocols, and message passing. From an architectural standpoint, this represents a shift from monolithic agents to self-organizing service ecosystems. The overall system architecture is not statically defined but emerges from role specialization, communication norms, and shared objectives. This model supports scalable, modular problem solving and enables fault isolation, parallel development, and organizational learning. Moreover, it anticipates future agent-based enterprises where entire software development lifecycles are autonomously managed through coordinated multi-agent workflows.

6.3.3
External Reasoning and Modular Cognitive Offloading

Effective design also requires the separation of concerns between reasoning and execution to improve accuracy, maintainability, and auditability. Program-Aided Language (PAL) model
[
168
]
embodies this principle by offloading symbolic computation, i.e., arithmetic, date manipulation, or data transformation, to an external interpreter (e.g., a Python runtime). Instead of performing calculations within the LLM’s context, the agent generates executable code that is run in a sandboxed environment, with results fed back into the reasoning chain. This creates a hybrid neuro-symbolic pipeline, where the neural model handles high-level planning, semantic interpretation, and natural language generation; while symbolic engine ensures precision, reproducibility, and formal correctness. This modular design enhances both reliability and transparency: intermediate steps are externalized, making the reasoning process inspectable and debuggable. From a service architecture perspective, it reflects a microservices-like decomposition of cognitive functions, where specialized modules handle distinct aspects of the decision pipeline. We term this pattern “cognitive microservices”, a design principle in which different components of intelligence (planning, computation, memory retrieval, tool use) are encapsulated, composed dynamically, and orchestrated at runtime. This modularity not only improves robustness but also enables reuse, versioning, and independent scaling of cognitive capabilities.

6.4
Summary and Challenges

This section has examined how autonomous agents make decisions and execute tasks through dynamic reasoning, real-time control, and lifelong adaptation. From interleaved reasoning and action to tool-augmented service composition, self-debugging, and emergent meta-architecture generation, modern agents demonstrate increasingly sophisticated capabilities in open-ended domains. These advances reflect a paradigm shift: from pre-specified workflows to self-organizing service behaviors, where agents not only consume services but actively design, coordinate, and evolve them at runtime.

Yet, as these systems transition from isolated demonstrations to scalable, production-grade service infrastructures, fundamental challenges arise across the service lifecycle. The very autonomy that enables flexibility and adaptability introduces risks to predictability, reliability, and maintainability. To bridge the gap between research prototypes and deployable systems, we identify three core challenges, each aligned with a critical phase of service engineering:
Deployment
,
Evolution
, and
(Re)Design
.

1.

Emergent Planning with Predictable Outcomes.

In the
Design
(and
(Re)Design
) phases, agents increasingly rely on emergent planning strategies, i.e., hierarchical task decomposition, role-based negotiation, or multi-agent consensus, to generate goals and subtasks dynamically. While this enables remarkable flexibility in unstructured environments, it often results in unstructured, inconsistent, or redundant plans that are difficult to validate, audit, or integrate into formal service workflows. The central challenge lies in achieving guided emergence: supporting adaptive, goal-driven planning while ensuring compliance with safety constraints, resource budgets, and user intent. This requires meta-architectural mechanisms for (1)
plan traceability
, which links high-level goals to executable steps, (2)
versioned workflows
, which enable rollback and A/B testing of planning strategies, and (3)
interoperability
, which exposes agent-generated plans as standardized service orchestrations (e.g., via OpenAPI, BPMN, or serverless workflows). Without such controls, emergent design risks becoming chaotic, undermining trust and integration in enterprise settings.

2.

Robust Execution in Dynamic Service Environments.

At
Deployment
, agents must operate in volatile, real-world environments where tools, APIs, network conditions, or environmental semantics change unexpectedly. While techniques like affordance grounding
[
169
]
, self-repair, and exception handling improve resilience, current systems lack systematic fault isolation, transactional semantics, or graceful degradation mechanisms. The challenge is to achieve principled fault tolerance in agent-driven service execution. Agents must treat actions as stateful, composable services with well-defined preconditions, postconditions, and side-effect contracts. This includes support for action rollback, circuit breaking, and adaptive retry policies, etc., from experience. From a service computing perspective, this demands execution models that unify agent actions with established patterns from microservices and distributed systems, ensuring operational reliability at scale.

3.

Cumulative Learning without Knowledge Entropy.

During
Evolution
, agents accumulate skills, reflections, code snippets, and behavioral patterns over time. Systems like Voyager
[
16
]
demonstrate cultural accumulation through versioned skill libraries, enabling knowledge transfer across tasks. However, uncontrolled growth leads to redundancy, conflicting behaviors, retrieval inefficiency, and conceptual drift. This phenomenon is termed knowledge entropy. The challenge is to enable sustainable lifelong learning: agents must not only store experiences but actively curate, generalize, and modularize them into reusable, composable capabilities. To achieve this, it may require

•

knowledge distillation

[
170
]
, which compresses redundant memories into compact policies,

•

modular abstraction
, which extracts generic functions from specific instances, and

•

consistency validation
, which detects and resolves conflicts in learned behaviors.

Without such mechanisms, long-term autonomy becomes unsustainable, as cognitive overhead outweighs functional benefit.

From software development and crisis response to personalized healthcare, agentic services require multiple agents to coordinate, share knowledge, and adapt collectively. Multi-agent collaboration is no longer merely a mechanism for functional composition; it has become foundational to building scalable, robust, and adaptive service ecosystems. This section systematically examines multi-agent collaboration and organization through three critical phases:
Design
,
Operation
, and
Evolution
. We argue that effective collaboration transcends mere task parallelization. It requires intent alignment, role specialization, and the emergence of social norms. While deployment and retirement have indirect relevance, the core dynamics of collaboration manifest most prominently during architectural formation (
Design
), execution (

), and adaptation (

7.1
Design: Architecting Collaborative Agent Societies

The
Design
phase of the service lifecycle establishes the foundational structures of multi-agent collaboration. It concerns the
intentional architecture
of agentic service systems: topologies, protocols, roles, and governance mechanisms that shape how agents interact over time. As multi-agent systems transition from isolated tools to persistent, open ecosystems, principled design becomes indispensable. Poorly architected systems risk inefficiency, brittleness, and adversarial exploitation; well-designed ones enable scalability, interoperability, and emergent social intelligence. This section examines key dimensions of collaborative agent architecture, arguing that effective design must balance flexibility and structure.

7.1.1
Coordination Paradigms and System Topologies

The choice of coordination paradigm fundamentally shapes the capabilities and limitations of an agentic service ecosystem. Three primary topologies dominate contemporary designs:

•

Centralized orchestration.
A single orchestrator agent assigns tasks, monitors progress, and resolves conflicts. Exemplified by frameworks such as AutoGen
[
19
]
, this model offers simplicity and strong consistency. However, it introduces a single point of failure and may become a performance bottleneck under high load
[
20
]
.

•

Decentralized negotiation.
Agents coordinate peer-to-peer through dialogue, auctions, or voting mechanisms. Inspired by market-based coordination
[
171
]
, this approach enhances robustness and scalability. Yet, it can incur high communication overhead and suffer from suboptimal convergence or deadlocks in complex environments.

•

Hybrid hierarchies.
Systems dynamically switch between centralized and decentralized modes based on task complexity, environmental uncertainty, or failure conditions. For instance, Magentic-One
[
20
]
employs a planner-executor hierarchy during structured phases but allows executors to negotiate locally when encountering unforeseen obstacles. This hybrid approach combines the efficiency of central control with the resilience of decentralized adaptation.

Emerging platforms such as AgentSociety
[
172
]
support configurable topologies, enabling designers to simulate and optimize trade-offs among control, efficiency, and resilience. Such tools are particularly valuable in mission-critical domains, e.g., disaster response or healthcare, where architectural decisions directly impact real-world outcomes.

7.1.2
Role Modeling and Behavioral Schemas

Roles provide cognitive scaffolding that enables effective collaboration. A well-defined role specifies not only an agent’s functional responsibilities (e.g.,
Planner
,
Critic
,
Executor
) but also its interaction patterns, communication style, and decision authority. Modern frameworks support both static and dynamic role models.

Static roles are predefined and fixed throughout execution, as seen in AutoGen’s team patterns
[
19
]
. They ensure predictability and simplify debugging, but limit adaptability in open or dynamic environments. In contrast, dynamic role assignment allows agents to assume, relinquish, or transition between roles based on task demands, performance feedback, or environmental changes. While fully autonomous role adaptation remains an active research area, advances in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) have demonstrated systems where agents learn to specialize through experience. For example, VDN
[
125
]
and QMIX
[
126
]
enable emergent role differentiation in cooperative tasks by optimizing centralized value functions under decentralized execution.

Beyond functional roles, behavioral schemas, i.e., turn-taking, politeness norms, or conflict resolution strategies, can be encoded to promote social coherence and reduce coordination overhead. Park et al.
[
127
]
demonstrate in
Generative Agents
that agents equipped with memory, reflection mechanisms, and behavioral scripts can simulate plausible social interactions and even form stable, emergent norms over time. This suggests that when grounded in long-term memory and environmental feedback, behavioral schemas can serve as the foundation for socially intelligent agentic service systems.

7.1.3
Protocol Standardization and Interoperability

As multi-agent ecosystems evolve from closed, monolithic systems to open, heterogeneous networks of autonomous entities,
interoperability
has emerged as a foundational design challenge. Without shared communication conventions, standardized data models, and aligned behavioral expectations, agents developed by different organizations (or even different teams within the same organization) cannot effectively collaborate, share tools, or compose into higher-order services. This fragmentation threatens to undermine the scalability and composability promised by agent-based computing.

To address this, researchers and industry consortia have proposed standardization efforts spanning from foundational message formats to high-level interaction protocols. These can be broadly categorized into four paradigms: (1) semantic communication standards, (2) message schema frameworks, (3) interaction protocol layers, and (4) tool and function interoperability standards. A comparative overview of key agent interoperability protocols is provided in Table
4
.

1.

Semantic communication standards.
Early attempts at agent interoperability originated with the
FIPA
(Foundation for Intelligent Physical Agents) initiative
[
5
]
, which defined an Agent Communication Language (ACL) grounded in speech act theory. FIPA-ACL standardized performatives such as
request
,
inform
, and
propose
, along with content encoding in formal languages like SL (Semantic Language). While influential in academic MAS, FIPA’s complexity and misalignment with modern web standards limited its practical adoption.

2.

Message schema and payload standards.
Contemporary agent frameworks often adopt lightweight, JSON-based message schemas inspired by web APIs. Platforms such as LangChain
[
70
]
, LlamaIndex
[
123
]
, and Semantic Kernel
[
173

use structured JSON payloads to encode agent utterances, tool calls, and metadata. These schemas typically include fields such as
role
,
content
,
tool_calls
, and
tool_results

3.

Interaction protocol layers.
Recent years have seen the emergence of sophisticated protocol layers tailored for agent-to-agent (A2A) interaction. The A2A protocol, introduced by Anthropic
[
124

, represents a significant step toward standardized, secure, and stateful agent communication. A2A defines a REST-like API surface enabling agents to:

•

discover peer capabilities via
GET /spec

•

initiate conversations with
POST /messages
,

•

stream responses and tool calls, and

•

handle errors and handshakes with well-defined status codes.

Crucially, A2A emphasizes identity, authentication, and intent clarity, enabling agents to reason about the provenance and reliability of incoming messages. It also supports asynchronous interactions and callback mechanisms, making it suitable for complex, long-running workflows.

Concurrently, the Model-Context-Protocol (MCP) framework
[
117
]
proposes a layered architecture for agent interoperability. The workflow of MCP is shown in Figure
9
. The
Model
layer establishes a shared understanding of the environment or domain (e.g., a CRM system or simulation world). The
Context
layer maintains task-specific state, including history, goals, and collaboration context. The
Protocol
layer defines standardized operations, including
read()
,
write()
,
request()
, and
subscribe()
, that govern how agents interact with resources and each other. MCP’s design mirrors RESTful microservices but extends these with persistent context and semantic typing, supporting dynamic composition across heterogeneous domains. In contrast to A2A, which prioritizes secure, structured messaging, MCP emphasizes resource-oriented interaction, making it particularly well-suited for enterprise data integration and long-running workflows.

Despite these differences, the frameworks are potentially complementary. Future interoperability standards may integrate both paradigms, using A2A for secure agent discovery and negotiation while leveraging MCP for rich, stateful access to shared resources and services.

4.

Tool and Function Interoperability.
Beyond message formats, the standardization of tool interfaces is essential for composability in agentic service systems. The OpenAI Function Calling schema

has emerged as a de facto standard, specifying how LLMs request external tool execution via structured JSON Schema definitions. This format has been widely adopted across major agent development frameworks, i.e., Google’s Vertex AI Agent Builder

[

]

[

and LangGraph
[

Research initiatives such as Toolformer
[
118
]
and API-Bank
[
178
]
explore agents’ ability to learn and utilize APIs autonomously during task execution. These efforts highlight the growing need for machine-readable, semantically rich API specifications. The widespread adoption of OpenAPI (formerly Swagger) as a standard for API documentation signals a promising trajectory toward universal tool discoverability and self-service integration in agent ecosystems.

7.1.4
Governance, Accountability, and Ethical Alignment

As agentic service systems grow in complexity and autonomy, effective governance, clear accountability, and ethical alignment become essential for safe and trustworthy operation. In decentralized systems where decisions emerge from distributed agent interactions, traditional oversight models are inadequate. Fundamental questions arise:
Who is responsible when an agent team causes harm? How are disputes resolved? Can autonomous agents adhere to ethical norms without constant human intervention?

Several governance approaches are emerging. Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) employ smart contracts and voting mechanisms to coordinate agent collectives through transparent, rule-based decision-making
[
179
]
. While promising, they require secure identity systems and safeguards against manipulation (e.g., sybil attacks). Complementary reputation and trust frameworks, such as TrustChain
[
180
]
, assign dynamic trust scores based on interaction history, influencing access control and role assignment. These systems promote accountability by making behavior observable and consequential. For high-stakes decisions, human-in-the-loop governance ensures alignment with societal values by escalating ethical dilemmas or policy changes to human overseers
[
181
]
. This preserves human judgment where automation is insufficient, though scalability remains a challenge. To prevent harmful behavior, agent systems must incorporate safety by construction. This includes runtime monitoring, behavioral constraints, and sandboxing. The Constitutional AI paradigm
[
129
]
advances ethical alignment by equipping agents with a set of principles (encoded as a “constitution”) that guide self-evaluation and decision justification. Agents can thus critique and reject actions that violate normative rules, reducing reliance on external enforcement.

Despite progress, significant challenges persist. Governance must balance autonomy with oversight, transparency with privacy, and decentralization with coherence. Open, inclusive standardization processes will be critical to prevent monopolization and ensure that governance mechanisms remain technically sound, ethically grounded, and broadly accountable.

7.2
Operation: Real-Time Coordination and Task Execution

The operational efficacy of agentic service systems in complex, dynamic environments hinges on their capacity for real-time coordination, adaptive task allocation, reliable workflow execution, and resilient recovery from disruptions. As multi-agent systems evolve into autonomous orchestrators within service-oriented architectures, their operational mechanisms must embody core principles of services computing: modularity, composability, loose coupling, and QoS-awareness. Unlike traditional service choreography, where interactions are pre-specified and deterministic, agentic ecosystems demand decentralized, intent-driven coordination that adapts to evolving goals, environmental uncertainties, and agent autonomy.

This section analyzes four foundational dimensions of real-time operation: (1) dialogic coordination, (2) dynamic task allocation and service composition, (3) execution control and workflow orchestration, and (4) fault tolerance and resilient invocation. Together, these mechanisms enable agent teams to function as agile, self-organizing collectives capable of executing complex, open-ended tasks under uncertainty.

7.2.1
Dialogic Coordination and Negotiation Protocols

Coordination between agentic services begins with structured, semantically rich dialogue, where agents negotiate roles, resolve conflicts, form commitments, and justify decisions through communicative acts. Drawing from speech act theory
[
182
]
and formal models of SLAs, modern agent frameworks support dialogues that transcend mere data exchange, enabling the negotiation of intentions, obligations, and trust. In services computing contexts, such dialogues resemble service negotiation protocols, where autonomous agents act as service providers or consumers, dynamically establishing contracts for resource access, task delegation, or collaboration initiation. These interactions often follow canonical patterns such as
propose-justify-revise
or request-agree-disagree, that mirror human organizational behavior and support iterative refinement of shared goals. Recent systems leverage neural-symbolic architectures to ground natural language dialogue in formal semantics. For instance, MetaGPT
[
128
]
employs role-specific prompts to simulate organizational workflows, allowing agents to collaboratively draft project plans through structured discussion. Similarly, Generative Agents
[
127
]
demonstrate how persistent memory, reflection, and social reasoning enable long-term coordination, with agents recalling past interactions and adjusting strategies based on relational history.

Crucially, effective dialogic coordination requires not only linguistic fluency but also intent clarity, commitment tracking, and conflict resolution mechanisms. Without shared understanding of obligations (e.g.,“
who is responsible for what
”), dialogues risk devolving into unproductive loops or misaligned actions. Future systems may integrate formal logics of agency (e.g., STIT logic
[
183
]
) or commitment-based protocols
[
184
]
to ensure that promises and delegations are tracked and enforceable, enhancing reliability in open, heterogeneous agentic service systems.

Unlike static workflows, modern agent teams operate under continuous change: task demands shift, agent availability fluctuates, and contextual constraints evolve, necessitating real-time reconfiguration and adaptation.

7.2.3
Execution Control and Workflow Orchestration

Once tasks are allocated, execution control ensures that plans are carried out reliably, with continuous monitoring, adaptation, and synchronization across distributed agents. In agentic service systems, this function parallels the role of workflow engines (e.g., Apache Airflow
[
156
]
, Camunda
[
191
]
) in traditional service-oriented systems, but with critical distinctions: decision-making is decentralized, execution is autonomous, and adaptation occurs in real time.

Hierarchical Task Networks (HTNs) and behavior trees provide structured representations for decomposing high-level goals into executable steps
[
192
]
. These models support modularity and reusability, enabling agents to compose plans from reusable templates. Some systems adopt a “plan-as-you-go” strategy, where abstract tasks are refined into concrete actions during execution, leveraging external tools (e.g., APIs, databases) via function calling, which is now standardized through OpenAI-style tool schemas
[
174
]
. A key innovation is the use of shared state spaces or blackboard systems, which are distributed, shared memory layers that function as coordination backbones. These act as message buses or event streams, enabling agents to: publish progress updates, detect inter-agent dependencies, trigger downstream actions via event-driven rules, and avoid conflicts without centralized control. For example, an agent completing a data preprocessing step can emit a
data_ready
event, which triggers downstream agents responsible for analysis or reporting. This event-driven, loosely coupled architecture enhances scalability and fault isolation.

Moreover, real-time monitoring allows agents to detect deviations from expected behavior and initiate replanning. Techniques such as belief tracking and uncertainty estimation enable agents to assess confidence in their own and others’ actions, triggering intervention or fallback strategies when risk exceeds thresholds.

7.2.4
Fault Tolerance and Resilient Service Invocation

Robust operation demands resilience to failures, whether due to network issues, agent crashes, or semantic misunderstandings. In services computing, resilience is achieved through well-established patterns: retries, timeouts, circuit breakers, and fallbacks
[
193
]
. In agentic service systems, these are augmented with autonomous decision-making, self-healing behaviors, and adaptive role delegation. Modern agent frameworks implement proactive fault detection using heartbeat monitoring and liveness probes to detect unresponsive agents, reputation systems that estimate reliability based on historical interaction quality, and uncertainty-aware planning that avoids over-reliance on low-confidence peers. When a failure is detected, there are several recovery strategies:

•

Dynamic role delegation.
Responsibilities are reassigned to backup agents based on pre-negotiated SLAs or real-time service discovery.

•

Self-diagnosis and graceful degradation.
Agents can report partial results or degraded performance before complete failure, enabling upstream agents to adjust expectations.

•

Task replication.
Critical tasks are redundantly assigned to multiple agents to improve availability, though at the cost of resource overhead
[
164
]
.

Integration with service mesh technologies, such as Istio
[
86
]
and Linkerd
[
87
]
, offers a promising path toward transparent resilience. In this model, lightweight sidecar agents handle communication retries, load balancing, and circuit breaking, decoupling resilience logic from business logic. However, significant challenges persist. For example, when agents maintain private memory (e.g., conversation history or planning context), recovering state after failure is non-trivial. In safety-critical domains, every decision must be explainable and verifiable. Future agent ecosystems may adopt blockchain-based logging or distributed consensus protocols (e.g.,
Raft

[
194
]
) to ensure recoverable state, tamper-proof audit trails, and coordinated recovery.

7.3
Evolution: Emergent Organization and Adaptive Collaboration

The operational dynamics of agentic service systems extend beyond real-time coordination to encompass long-term structural and behavioral evolution. Unlike static multi-agent systems with pre-defined roles and fixed interaction protocols, evolved agentic ecosystems exhibit self-organization, role specialization, cultural learning, and architectural plasticity. These systems do not merely execute tasks; they learn how to organize, whom to collaborate with, and what norms to uphold over time.

This section examines four key dimensions of evolutionary advancement in agent collaboration: (1) emergent role specialization, (2) adaptive team formation, (3) cultural knowledge propagation, and (4) co-evolution of architectures and behaviors. Together, these mechanisms enable agentic service systems to adapt not only to changing tasks but also to shifting social and operational contexts, mirroring the resilience and adaptability of biological and organizational systems in the natural world.

7.3.1
Emergent Role Specialization through Interaction Feedback

In evolved agentic service systems, functional roles are not statically assigned but
emerge
from performance differentials, social reinforcement, and selective pressure. Agents that consistently demonstrate higher success rates in specific task domains such as planning, verification, or tool invocation gradually become recognized as specialists by their peers and are preferentially selected for related tasks. This process mirrors the division of labor observed in biological colonies (e.g., ant foragers vs. nurses) and human organizations, where specialization enhances system-level efficiency and scalability.

Recent advances employ intrinsic motivation signals such as curiosity, competence progress, or prediction error within RL frameworks to drive behavioral differentiation
[
195
]
. For example, in spatial exploration tasks, agents receiving higher exploration bonuses naturally evolve into scouts, while others specialize in resource processing or defense. These emergent roles are stabilized through reputation mechanisms and social memory, forming dynamic role graphs that adapt to shifting task demands. From a services computing perspective, this phenomenon corresponds to
dynamic service role binding

[
196
]
, where agents dynamically assume identities as service providers, brokers, or orchestrators based on proven capability and contextual relevance. This reduces configuration overhead in open, heterogeneous ecosystems and supports self-configuring architectures. Moreover, role emergence enables functional redundancy, i.e., multiple agents may specialize in similar tasks, enhancing resilience and load balancing.

However, challenges remain in ensuring equitable role distribution and preventing monopolization by high-performing agents, which could reduce diversity and increase systemic fragility. Future systems may integrate fairness-aware learning or diversity-preserving selection mechanisms to maintain robust collective intelligence.

7.3.2
Adaptive Team Formation and Structural Plasticity

A hallmark of evolved agentic ecosystems is their capacity for adaptive team formation: the ability to dynamically form, dissolve, and reconfigure teams in response to shifting objectives, environmental conditions, or agent availability. Rather than relying on fixed coalitions, agents learn compatibility models from interaction history, enabling the spontaneous assembly of high-performing, trust-based teams.

Affinity-based matching algorithms allow agents to maintain preference rankings over potential collaborators, leading to the formation of stable, high-trust cliques
[
197
]
. These affinity models can be learned via collaborative filtering, Bayesian inference, or graph-based embeddings. GNNs are increasingly used to model agent compatibility as a dynamic interaction network, supporting team assembly through community detection, spectral clustering, or combinatorial optimization. Some systems apply evolutionary operators (i.e., selection, mutation, and crossover) to team configurations, treating team structures as evolvable units
[
198
]
. Suboptimal teams are selected out over time, while successful configurations are preserved and refined. This process resembles agile team reorganization in DevOps environments, where service ownership shifts based on expertise, workload, and past performance.

Crucially, adaptive team formation requires mechanisms for trust calibration and risk assessment. Agents must balance the benefits of working with reliable partners against the need to explore new collaborations. Techniques such as multi-armed bandit strategies or Bayesian reputation updating enable agents to manage this exploration-exploitation trade-off in social contexts.

7.3.3
Cultural Knowledge Propagation and Collective Memory

A defining feature of evolved agentic service systems is the emergence of cultural knowledge, including shared practices, communication norms, cooperative strategies, and problem-solving heuristics that persist and evolve across agent generations. This goes beyond individual learning to include social transmission mechanisms such as imitation, teaching, and observational learning, enabling cumulative cultural evolution within artificial agent populations. Recent work by Vallinder et al.
[
199
]
demonstrates how cooperation norms can emerge and stabilize in populations of LLM-based agents through cultural evolutionary dynamics. In their framework, agents interact in repeated social dilemmas (e.g., prisoner’s dilemma), and successful behavioral strategies, such as conditional cooperation or punishment of defectors, are propagated through a
cultural selection
process. New agents acquire these strategies via exposure to experienced peers, either through direct interaction or environmental records (e.g., shared logs or memory repositories), forming a collective memory that enhances group-level cooperation over time. This process does not require centralized control; instead, cooperative norms self-reinforce when they yield higher long-term payoffs. Cultural knowledge can also include domain-specific heuristics such as effective negotiation scripts or debugging patterns that are refined and passed down through generations of agents.

From a system design perspective, supporting cultural evolution requires infrastructure for persistent knowledge storage, semantic indexing, and context-aware retrieval. Vector databases, knowledge graphs, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) are promising technologies for enabling scalable cultural memory. However, risks such as the propagation of harmful norms or outdated strategies must be mitigated through mechanisms like norm validation, forgetting schedules, or adversarial auditing.

7.3.4
Co-Evolution of Architectures and Interaction Patterns

The most advanced models of agentic service systems conceptualize organizational structure and interaction behavior as
co-evolving
elements, where changes in one drive adaptations in the other. Rather than assuming a fixed topology, these systems support meta-organizational learning, i.e., the ability to reflect on and reconfigure their own architecture in response to performance feedback and task complexity.

A key demonstration comes from the Generative Agents simulation by Park et al.
[
127
]
, in which LLM-based agents populate a virtual environment and develop emergent social structures based on trust and routine interactions. While not explicitly optimizing architecture, the system illustrates how stable interaction patterns such as information pipelines or leadership hierarchies can arise from decentralized behaviors, forming de facto organizational forms. Further evidence comes from self-reflective agents. The Reflexion framework
[
57
]
enables agents to simulate past failures and verbally critique their own behavior, leading to improved task performance over iterations. Although focused on individual improvement, this meta-cognitive mechanism provides a foundation for structural adaptation: when repeated failures occur, agents may initiate organizational changes. For instance, delegating tasks, forming review chains, or restructuring communication flows. Future systems may extend this to autonomous architectural evolution, where agents use learned models of organizational effectiveness to propose and negotiate structural changes. For instance, an agent detecting chronic bottlenecks in a workflow might suggest introducing a new intermediary role or splitting a monolithic team into specialized subunits. While fully autonomous co-evolution remains largely aspirational, these works establish core mechanisms that may one day enable self-designing agent organizations capable of lifelong adaptation.

7.4
Summary and Challenges

This section has explored the mechanisms by which autonomous agents form collaborative societies through dynamic communication, adaptive coordination, and self-organized structural evolution. From the emergence of linguistic conventions and role specialization to the propagation of cultural knowledge and co-evolving architectures, modern agentic systems exhibit increasingly sophisticated collective intelligence. These capabilities enable scalable task decomposition, resilient problem-solving under uncertainty, and cumulative learning across agent populations, laying the foundation for autonomous service ecosystems that can adapt and evolve.

However, as multi-agent systems transition from controlled simulations to production-grade, real-world deployments, a critical gap emerges between adaptive autonomy and systemic trustworthiness. The very features that enable flexibility also introduce risks of opacity, inefficiency, and semantic instability. To guide future research and system design, we identify three fundamental challenges that must be addressed to ensure reliable, maintainable, and aligned agent collaboration across the lifecycle of agentic service systems.

1.

Self-Organizing Architectures with Auditability and Control.

In the
Design
and
Operation
phases, agentic service systems increasingly exhibit self-organizing behaviors: agents dynamically form teams, specialize in roles, and reconfigure interaction topologies in response to task demands. While this adaptability enhances robustness and efficiency, it also undermines traditional notions of system governance. Unlike static, predefined workflows, such as those in SOA or workflow engines, emergent organizational structures lack explicit documentation, traceability, and compliance guarantees. The central challenge is to achieve auditable self-organization: enabling agents to autonomously reconfigure their organizational topology while preserving human oversight, accountability, and alignment with ethical, legal, and operational constraints. This requires mechanisms for real-time structural logging, role provenance tracking, and decision justification. Inspired by infrastructure-as-code and DevOps practices, future systems could maintain a living organizational ledger, which acts as a tamper-resistant, versioned record of all structural changes, including triggers, participants, and outcomes. Such a ledger would support audit trails, post-hoc analysis, and rollback capabilities, ensuring that evolved ecosystems remain inspectable, explainable, and controllable.

2.

Scalable Coordination with Bounded Communication Overhead.

In the
Deployment
phase, agents must coordinate effectively under real-world resource constraints, including latency, bandwidth, API costs, and computational budgets. While frameworks like Generative Agents
[
127
]
demonstrate compelling emergent social dynamics, they often rely on broadcast-style memory sharing or global attention mechanisms that scale poorly with population size. As the number of agents grows, unoptimized coordination protocols lead to quadratic or even exponential growth in message complexity, resulting in congestion, delays, and degraded performance. The challenge lies in achieving scalable yet effective coordination: designing interaction protocols that minimize redundant communication while preserving essential situational awareness and collaborative efficacy. This necessitates architectural innovations such as hierarchical coordination layers, attention-based message filtering, role-based routing, and sparsified interaction graphs. Techniques such as gossip protocols
[
200
]
, publish-subscribe models
[
201
]
, and consensus algorithms
[
202
]
can be adapted to agentic ecosystems. Moreover, learning-based methods (e.g., communication pruning via reinforcement learning) offer promise for dynamically optimizing information flow. Without such mechanisms, large-scale agentic service systems risk becoming communication-bound rather than capability-bound.

3.

Cultural Accumulation without Semantic Drift.

During the
Evolution
phase, agentic service systems accumulate knowledge through social learning, imitation, and experience sharing, forming a collective memory that supports cultural transmission across agent generations. Systems like MemGPT
[
203
]
enable long-term memory storage and retrieval, providing the technical substrate for such cultural continuity. However, unregulated propagation of knowledge introduces the risk of semantic drift: the gradual divergence or corruption of shared meanings, norms, strategies, or skill interpretations over time. For example, a negotiation policy initially encoded as “conditional cooperation” may, through imperfect imitation or contextual misapplication, evolve into passive compliance or aggressive exploitation. Similarly, domain-specific heuristics (e.g., debugging patterns or planning templates) may degrade in accuracy or applicability across generations. The challenge is to ensure semantic stability in cultural accumulation, such that the intent, correctness, and consistency of shared knowledge despite decentralized and iterative transmission can be preserved. This demands mechanisms for knowledge versioning, provenance tracking, consensus-based updates, and periodic validation. Analogous to schema governance in database systems or configuration management in microservices, agentic ecosystems may require
cultural governance protocols
that regulate how norms and practices are introduced, revised, and deprecated. Additionally, techniques such as contrastive learning or semantic embeddings can be used to detect and correct drift by measuring alignment with canonical representations.

In summary, while the evolution of agentic collaboration promises unprecedented autonomy and adaptability, its practical realization hinges on resolving the tension between emergent complexity and systemic control. Addressing these challenges will be essential for building agentic service systems that are not only intelligent and adaptive but also trustworthy, efficient, and sustainable in real-world applications.

8
Evaluation, Value Alignment, and Trustworthiness

The predictability, safety, and societal impact of autonomous agents’ behavior have emerged as central concerns in the design and deployment of next-generation service ecosystems. Unlike traditional service components governed by fixed interfaces and deterministic logic, agents exhibit dynamic reasoning, autonomous decision-making, and continuous learning, which are capabilities that transcend static specifications such as APIs or service contracts. As a result, conventional quality metrics, e.g., latency, throughput, and availability, are insufficient for capturing the full spectrum of agent behavior, particularly emergent, long-horizon, and socially embedded actions. To ensure reliable, ethical, and trustworthy operation in open environments, a new evaluation paradigm is required. It should integrate functional performance with behavioral integrity, interpretability, and alignment with human values. In this section, we present a comprehensive review of recent advances across three interdependent dimensions: agent evaluation, value alignment, and trustworthiness engineering.

Together, these approaches signify a paradigm shift from reactive, outcome-based supervision to proactive, process-oriented alignment. This evolution is essential for building systems that users can trust to act reliably and ethically in real-world service environments.

Beyond reactive auditing, trustworthy agents require proactive, architecture-level safeguards that embed safety and reliability into the system’s design. These mechanisms operate at multiple layers and aim to shift trust from an emergent property to a designed feature.

Human Oversight and Control

Trust in autonomous systems depends on the preservation of meaningful human agency. As agents assume greater initiative, oversight mechanisms must ensure that humans remain in the loop, on the loop, or at minimum ready to intervene. This requires not only technical controls but also thoughtful interface design, role clarity, and organizational policies.

In sum, building trustworthiness requires a holistic, multidisciplinary approach that integrates technical robustness with human-centered design and institutional accountability. As agents become embedded in societal infrastructure, these principles will be essential for ensuring that autonomy does not come at the cost of responsibility.

8.4
Summary and Challenges

This section has examined the three interdependent pillars of responsible agent development: evaluation, value alignment, and trustworthiness. As LLM-powered agents evolve from transactional tools into autonomous collaborators within high-stakes domains, ranging from clinical decision support to financial planning and public administration, their societal legitimacy hinges not only on technical performance but on their adherence to ethical, legal, and social norms. We have surveyed methodologies for assessing agent behavior beyond narrow task success, techniques for grounding objectives in human values, and architectural mechanisms that enable transparency, auditability, and human control.

Despite rapid progress, the integration of these dimensions remains fragmented and siloed. Evaluation is often conducted in isolation from alignment objectives; alignment techniques are treated as static training-time constraints rather than dynamic runtime processes; and trust mechanisms are frequently appended post-hoc rather than designed as first-class system properties. This fragmentation undermines the coherence and accountability of agentic services, especially as they operate in complex, open-ended environments. To advance the field toward truly responsible and societally beneficial agents, we must confront several deep, interwoven challenges. These are not merely technical hurdles but reflect fundamental tensions between autonomy, accountability, and value pluralism. We identify three core challenges that define the frontier of agentic AI research and deployment.

1.

The Evaluability-Alignment Gap.

A critical disconnect persists between
what we can measure
and what we value. Current evaluation benchmarks excel at quantifying task completion, reasoning fidelity, or factual accuracy, yet they are ill-equipped to assess value-laden attributes such as fairness, respect for autonomy, epistemic humility, or long-term societal impact. Conversely, alignment techniques, such as RLHF or Constitutional AI, operate with sparse, often noisy feedback signals and lack robust metrics for post-deployment validation. The result is a dangerous epistemic gap: agents may
appear
competent and benign while subtly violating implicit norms or optimizing for proxy objectives that diverge from human intent. The challenge, therefore, is to co-design evaluation and alignment frameworks. This requires developing observable proxies for values and embedding them into both training curricula and benchmarking suites. Future evaluation paradigms must support value-aware assessment: metrics that capture not only
what
agents do, but
why
they act,
how
they justify decisions, and
for whom
outcomes are optimized. Only then can evaluation serve as a continuous feedback loop for alignment, rather than a one-off validation ritual.

2.

Trust at Scale.

While trust-enabling mechanisms are increasingly available, their impact is often confined to developer debugging or isolated audits. For trust to be
operationalized
in real-world deployments, it must transcend individual artifacts and become an
institutional property
of agentic service systems. This demands a shift from ad hoc tooling to systemic trust infrastructure. We envision a future where agentic service systems generate standardized, machine-verifiable audit trails. These would enable third-party validators, regulators, and users to independently verify claims about agent behavior, detect anomalies, and enforce policy constraints. The challenge lies in establishing common data formats (e.g., for decision provenance), open protocols for auditability, and governance models that balance transparency with privacy and security. Without such infrastructure, trust remains fragile, localized, and vulnerable to manipulation, obsolescence, or regulatory arbitrage.

3.

Dynamic Value Specification under Uncertainty.

Human values are neither static, universal, nor fully specifiable. They are context-sensitive, culturally situated, and often revealed only through deliberation and conflict. Yet most alignment approaches presuppose a fixed reward function, a rigid constitutional rulebook, or a monolithic human preference distribution. This assumption breaks down in real-world settings, where values evolve over time, vary across stakeholders, and involve trade-offs between competing principles (e.g., privacy vs. safety, efficiency vs. equity). The challenge is to support dynamic value specification: the capacity of agentic services to reason about value uncertainty, negotiate conflicting preferences, and adapt to shifting social norms, without collapsing into either rigid rule-following or unbounded autonomy. This requires new paradigms for value learning in the wild, where agentic services actively solicit feedback, model value distributions across diverse stakeholders, and maintain epistemic humility about their own objectives. Rather than merely following pre-specified values, agentic services should participate in a deliberative process of value clarification, preference aggregation, and normative negotiation. Such capabilities demand advances in preference modeling, multi-stakeholder reasoning, and interactive alignment, as well as integration with democratic and ethical frameworks from political philosophy and sociology.

These challenges transcend individual technical components. They reflect a deeper imperative: to shift from viewing agents as isolated AI models to understanding them as sociotechnical systems embedded within human organizations, legal institutions, and evolving ethical landscapes. Addressing them will require sustained collaboration across disciplines including AI, human-computer interaction, ethics, law, political science, and sociology.

Future Trends

ASC represents a paradigm shift from static, request-response service architectures to dynamic, goal-driven, and autonomous systems capable of perceiving, reasoning, acting, and collaborating in complex environments. The preceding sections have detailed the foundational pillars of ASC:
Perception, Context, and Environment Modeling
(Section
5
),
Autonomous Decision-Making and Task Execution
(Section
6
),
Multi-Agent Collaboration and Organization
(Section
7
), and
Evaluation, Value Alignment, and Trustworthiness

8
). Together, these components form the technical and ethical backbone of next-generation service ecosystems.

As ASC matures, its trajectory is shaped not only by algorithmic advances but by the increasing demand for systems that are not only intelligent and efficient but also responsible, adaptive, and socially embedded. In this section, we synthesize the insights from the previous modules to project future trends and identify open challenges that will define the evolution of ASC in the coming decade.

1.

From Reactive Agents to Proactive, Anticipatory Service Orchestration.
Current agentic service systems largely operate in a reactive mode, i.e., responding to user requests or environmental triggers. The future lies in
proactive
and
anticipatory
agents that predict user needs, preemptively allocate resources, and orchestrate services before explicit demands arise. This requires deep integration of long-horizon planning, causal modeling, and personalized user modeling, enabling agents to distinguish between transient behaviors and enduring preferences. For instance, a personal health assistant may anticipate a diabetic user’s risk of hypoglycemia based on activity patterns, weather, and meal logs, and proactively suggest interventions. Similarly, urban mobility agentic services could coordinate traffic signals, ride-sharing fleets, and public transit in anticipation of congestion. The challenge, however, lies in balancing proactivity with privacy and autonomy, ensuring that predictive actions do not become intrusive or paternalistic.

2.

Scalable and Sustainable Multi-Agentic Service Ecosystems.
The agentic services may scale to thousands or millions of agents in the future, ranging from edge devices to cloud-based services, but their collective behavior must be both
efficient
and sustainable. Future ASC ecosystems will need to support self-organizing agent societies with emergent coordination, dynamic role assignment, and decentralized governance. This includes the development of lightweight communication protocols, energy-aware execution models, and incentive mechanisms that promote cooperation over competition. Moreover, the environmental cost of large-scale agent deployment cannot be ignored, particularly for those relying on resource-intensive LLMs. We anticipate a growing emphasis on green agentic services computing, where agentic services optimize not only for task success but for energy efficiency, carbon footprint, and computational sustainability. Techniques such as model sparsification, agent specialization, and federated reasoning will become critical in building eco-conscious ASC infrastructures.

3.

Human-Agent Co-Evolution and Value Negotiation.
The relationship between humans and agents is bidirectional: agents shape human behavior, and humans continuously reshape agent objectives. Future ASC systems must support value co-evolution, where agents do not merely align with pre-specified human values but participate in an ongoing process of value negotiation and norm emergence. This requires agents to model not only individual preferences but also social norms, cultural differences, and institutional constraints. For example, in cross-border e-commerce, agents must navigate conflicting regulatory regimes and ethical expectations. In healthcare, they may mediate between patient autonomy, clinical guidelines, and family input. The challenge is to design agents with epistemic humility, i.e., the ability to recognize the limits of their understanding and defer to human judgment when values are ambiguous or contested.

4.

Unified Agentic Architectures.
Despite significant progress in individual modules, current ASC implementations often suffer from modularity fragmentation: perception, decision-making, collaboration, and trust mechanisms are developed in isolation, leading to integration bottlenecks and suboptimal end-to-end performance. The future trend is toward
unified agentic architectures
that tightly couple these components into coherent, end-to-end trainable or self-improving systems. We envision ASC platforms that support agentic pipelines. They are modular yet interoperable stacks where context models inform planners, multi-agent coordination signals feed back into perception, and evaluation metrics guide alignment updates in real time. Inspired by operating systems for agents, such architectures will include standardized interfaces for memory, tools, communication, and ethics layers, enabling plug-and-play composition of agentic services. A key challenge is ensuring that integration does not compromise transparency or auditability. As systems become more holistic, they risk becoming black boxes; thus, future architectures must embed
explainability-by-design
and
trust-by-construction
principles from the ground up.

5.

Regulatory-Aware and Institutionally Embedded Agents.
As ASC systems enter high-stakes domains, they must operate within legal and regulatory frameworks. Future agents will need to be regulatory-aware: capable of interpreting laws, complying with standards (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA), and generating auditable justifications for their actions. This goes beyond rule-based compliance to include the ability to handle ambiguous, conflicting, or evolving regulations through principled interpretation. Moreover, ASC systems must become institutionally embedded, interfacing with human organizations, oversight bodies, and accountability mechanisms. This includes support for third-party audits, standardized model cards and agent passports, and interoperable provenance tracking. We anticipate the emergence of hybrid human-machine institutions that oversee agent behavior, resolve disputes, and update norms in response to societal feedback.

In summary, the path forward is toward human-centered agentic service ecosystems, where agents amplify human agency, enhance collective decision-making, and uphold the values of fairness, transparency, and dignity in an increasingly automated world.

Conclusion

ASC marks a transformative evolution in the development of intelligent services computing. Moving beyond traditional service-oriented architectures, ASC introduces a paradigm grounded in autonomy, proactivity, adaptability, and social embeddedness. In this paper, we have systematically explored the foundational and emerging dimensions of ASC, organizing our discussion around four core research dimensions:
Perception, Context, and Environment Modeling
,
Autonomous Decision-Making and Task Execution
,
Multi-Agent Collaboration and Organization
, and
Evaluation, Value Alignment, and Trustworthiness

Together, these modules form a comprehensive framework for understanding how agents perceive and interpret complex environments, make goal-directed decisions under uncertainty, coordinate with other agents to achieve collective outcomes, and operate in ways that are accountable, aligned with human values, and worthy of trust. We have shown that the power of ASC does not reside solely in individual agent capabilities, but in the integration of cognitive, collaborative, and ethical functionalities into coherent, resilient, and socially responsible systems. We also identified key trends that will shape the next generation of ASC. These trends reflect a broader transition from viewing agentic services as isolated units to recognizing them as participants in complex sociotechnical ecosystems.

Yet, significant open challenges remain. The tension between scalability and oversight, the difficulty of aligning with pluralistic and evolving values, the risks of long-term autonomy, and the need for robust, auditable, and equitable systems all demand sustained interdisciplinary inquiry. The future of ASC cannot be engineered in isolation by computer scientists alone; it requires collaboration with ethicists, legal scholars, social scientists, policymakers, and end users. From our perspective, the promise of ASC lies in the design of human-centered agentic service ecosystems, in which intelligence is distributed, responsibility is shared, and technology serves as a scaffold for human flourishing. As we advance toward this vision, the principles of transparency, accountability, and value alignment must remain not afterthoughts, but the very foundation upon which every agentic service is built.
