Factors influencing the implementation of food fortification regulation among small and medium-scale corn millers in Dar es Salaam Tanzania: a qualitative study
Authors: Dorice C Lema, Gladys Reuben Mahiti, Bruno F Sunguya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to explore factors influencing the implementation of food fortification regulation among corn millers in Dar es Salaam Tanzania.MethodsThis phenomenological qualitative study was conducted in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania among purposely interviewed corn millers. In-depth interviews conducted with nine corn millers were analysed using thematic analysis with the aid of N-Vivo software version 12.ResultsThe implementation of food fortification regulation among corn millers is influenced by access to knowledge and the right information, lack of consumers’ awareness, zero costs for the fortification of corn flour, and lack of punitive measures for non-compliance to the regulation.Conclusion and recommendationsLaxity in implementing food fortification as an important population-wide approach in addressing micronutrient deficiency is evident in Tanzania. This is triggered by non-punitive measures for non-compliance of the regulation among micro, small and medium-scale corn millers. Strengthening efforts to identify and implement measures against non-compliers, demand creation for fortified products through educational and advocacy interventions, and supportive supervision can help reach more than 90% of the population with fortified corn flour in Tanzania.Keywords: Birth defects, Malnutrition, Nutrient deficiencies, Nutritional treatment

Introduction

Food fortification is an evidence-based intervention (EBI) that contributes to the prevention, reduction and control of multiple micronutrient deficiencies without changing consumers’ eating patterns nor individual compliance to attain impacts.1 The process helps to add new micronutrients that were not initially available in the food; replace the lost nutrients that were lost during harvesting, post-harvest handling, storage, and food processing; and ensure adequate required daily allowance is met.2 The WHO recommends food fortification as an EBI which is a preventive and cost-effective strategy globally to reduce multiple micronutrient deficiencies in the populations.3 Fortifying foods with multiple micronutrients may reduce anaemia by 32%, iron deficiency anaemia by 72%, vitamin A deficiency by 58%, vitamin B2 deficiency by 64%, vitamin B6 deficiency by 91% and vitamin B12 deficiency may be reduced by 58%.4 The effect is an overall reduction of diseases related with micronutrient deficiency including neural tube defects which have been reduced by up to 40% owing to this intervention.5

Evidence on impact of food fortification has seen countries imposing regulations for fortification with micronutrients such as iodine, iron, zinc, vitamin A and folic acid.2 A multitude of health benefits have been observed in different contexts with such intervention. However, two billion people globally are still affected by micronutrient deficiencies. This is equivalent to 29% of the world’s population.6

Tanzania, like other countries with high burden of different forms of malnutrition, is no exception to micronutrient deficiencies at a population level.6 7 One in three women of reproductive age experience micronutrient deficiencies including iron, iodine and vitamin A deficiency in Tanzania.8 More than 45% of women of reproductive age and 57% of pregnant women have iron deficiency anaemia.9 Multiple micronutrient deficiencies affect people of all ages but severely among young children and women of reproductive age especially pregnant women.10 The impact in this subpopulation has a detrimental intergenerational effect, with consequences to family and national development and security. Most of micronutrient deficiencies and their health outcomes are preventable through healthy feeding practices, micronutrient supplementation, and consumption of fortified foods.2

In addressing the persistent burden of micronutrient deficiencies and its impact, Tanzania launched food fortification strategy in 2011 for the highly consumed staple foods.11 Corn flour was one of the targets as a staple food consumed by majority of Tanzanians on a daily basis. Corn flour is consumed in 93% of households in Tanzania; however, only 2.5% is fortified.12 In practical sense, the strategy focused on fortification by large-scale food processors due to operational reasons.12 13 The most dominant mills are for corn milling but producing only 2% fortifying corn flour.14 With such low coverage, 95% of the Tanzania population is left to consume unfortified corn flour, mainly from the micro, small and medium mills, not targeted by the regulation directly, as it is currently taken as a voluntary act.14 With over 10 years of implementation of this strategy in the country, evidence on factors influencing implementation of the fortification act remains unavailable. This study was therefore conducted to explore factors influencing corn millers in the implementation of food fortification regulation in Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a phenomenological qualitative study design which explored the detailed real experience, insights and an in-depth understanding of the real-world situations of corn millers to explore rich information on factors influencing them in the implementation of food fortification regulation in Tanzania.15,17 The technique places a strong focus on the participants’ own perspective and interpretation and therefore effective for interpreting subjective experience, getting perceptions into people’s intentions and behaviours.18

Study area

This study was conducted in Dar es Salaam region, where its corn millers (including corn and other food items) feed approximately 8.7% of the total population in the country.19 In addition, the region represents an urban society and potential market and consumers of processed foods which are more likely to be fortified.12

Participants

The study purposely sampled toll or micro, small and medium-scale corn millers. It included those who implement the act and those who are not implementing it. Purposive sampling helped to concentrate on specific characteristics of study participants that are of interest in terms of knowledge and experience on the phenomena of interest in order to best answer the research questions.20 21 Nine millers purposely sampled for this study, two toll millers who offer the milling service to the community members, three small scale and four medium-scale millers who produce corn flour, pack, and distribute to consumers.

Data collection

Before data collection, interview guides were pre-tested. Data collection for this study started from 27 June 2023 to 14 July 2023. Principal investigator (first author) conducted all the interviews as part of her requirement for her master’s degree. Voice recorder was used to collect data after obtaining participants’ written consents. Data was collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews with participants during working hours in their workstations. Interview time ranged from 30 min to 1 hour. Participants gave detailed information which was required for the study and saturation was reached when there was repetition of answers and where there was no new information generated from the interviews.22 23

Data analysis

Thematic analysis approach was used with the aid of N-Vivo software version 12.24 The voice records were transcribed verbatim. Familiarisation with data was done through reading and re-reading of the transcripts by the first author where there were back and forth steps cross-checking the data to ensure the quality of data and verification of information. Data was analysed through generating initial codes for the entire data set and collating data relevant to each code. After collating data, searching for subthemes and generating potential themes were done. The review of generated themes and selection of vivid quotes for each theme was done.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Research and Ethics Committee, with the ethical certificate number MUHAS-REC-06-2023-1738. The study also received an approval from the Regional Administrative Secretary office of Dar es Salaam. Participants’ consents were obtained before data collection for both their participation to the study and for voice recording. All the identities of study participants have been protected and kept confidential using codes instead of their real identities. The voice records and transcripts are stored in a safe place with credentials to access them.

Results

Participants demographic characteristics

Nine participants were interviewed, majority being male. Only one interviewed miller had a tertiary level education. None of the interviewed was a toll miller. We interviewed two millers who were not fortifying corn flour, two small-scale millers who were fortifying corn flour, one small-scale miller who was not fortifying, and four medium-scale millers were interviewed: two were fortifying and two were not fortifying corn flour during the study period. Emerging themes and subthemes

Factors influencing corn millers to implement food fortification regulation

Access to knowledge and information on fortification among corn millers

Having the right information and knowledge on fortification of corn flour has been shown to facilitate the implementation of food fortification regulation among corn millers in Tanzania. Respondents stated that having the right information and knowledge through training has helped them to fortify corn flour after understanding the importance of fortification and its impact to the community. All corn millers who comply with the fortification were able to start fortification after they have been well equipped with the right information and fortification skills as respondents selected from the millers who fortify said;

We were taken somewhere and we were trained by those people for three days and from there I started fortifying corn flour. (Small-scale miller fortifying number 2)

Those who supply premixes to us, they explained that these premixes are being added in order to help those children you found are born malnourished they are deficient of minerals so those premixes are going to reduce that level of malnutrition (Medium-scale miller fortifying number 1)

On the contrary, lack of awareness and fortification skills to other millers becomes a barrier for them to adhere to fortification. Millers who offer only toll service to the community have reported that they have no information on food fortification and they are not aware of the fortification technology, its importance and they have never heard about it and this impacts as a hindrance towards the implementation of fortification among this category of millers who serve community members who visit mills centres with their corn or other cereals to be processed for household consumption. A miller who reported has heard about fortification the miller had a wrong information on food fortification respondents reported that;

I have never heard it, that issue of fortification I have not come across it anywhere, for example a person comes here and tell me to add nutrients…! I have never come across it that thing (fortification). (Toll miller non-fortifying number 1)

I have heard about it (fortification) because others in corn they are adding pumpkin seeds, rice and cassava for ugali. (Toll miller non- fortifying number 2)

Lack of consumers’ awareness on fortified corn flour

Participants in this study reported that their production depends on what their customers’ demands as their target market. Majority have reported that they are facing challenge with the customers who are lacking awareness on fortified corn flour at the same time misleading other customers with false information concerning the outcomes associated with consumption of fortified corn flour. The production of fortified flour among millers has been reduced as the clients reject the fortified flour and so the production depends highly on the market as a pulling factor as one of the respondents reported that;

Because they do not have the knowledge concerning this fortified flour, everyone now when they come here they reject it. Because they are giving each other false information…they are saying this fortified flour is reducing manhood reproductive power it makes them to become stupid ignorant. Loh! They talk, when you give them fortified flour they refuse, they reject and return it. (Medium-scale miller fortifying number 2)

For those who wants ‘dona’ the situation is the same we will ask because others when you ask them, they refuse that fortified flour so we switch off the dosifier therefore the dosifier will not read anything even if we load it with corn, it will not sense anything and corn will just pass as normal but the premix will not enter in to the customer’s flour. (Small-scale miller fortifying number 1)

Zero costs associated with fortification of corn flour

Absence of costs associated with fortification of corn flour attributed to corn millers having easy acceptance and attracted to adopt voluntarily to fortifying corn flour. All interviewed millers who fortify corn flour have reported that they are not incurring any extra cost for them to fortify corn flour. Just by ordering their packaging materials through the fortification stakeholder to pack for the flour produced, they are having a free access to both the dosifier which is a fortification technology and premix (micronutrients) and this has been reported as one of the facilitators towards fortification of corn flour among corn millers. Millers have reported that there is no any cost and there is no any loss that they incur for fortifying corn flour as stated by the respondent below;

When you order the packaging materials, they also give you the premix and they know absolutely that since you ordered 1000 packaging materials then by defaults the premix would also be finished…as you proceed they bring to you the premix as the previous are finished. There is no any cost and there is no any loss. (Small-scale miller fortifying number 2)

Those who are selling us the packaging bags when they give you packaging bags, they also give the premixes we are not buying the premixes… Ehee, once you order your packaging bags, they know the quantity of the premixes they will give you according to the order of your packaging bags. (Medium-scale miller fortifying 1)

Lack of punishments, penalty and fines for non-fortifying millers

Lack of enforcement characterised by no punishment, no penalty and no fine among millers who are not fortifying was found to be one of the factors for reluctant implementation of food fortification regulation among corn millers. The provision given by the regulation to small and medium-scale processors gives a room for slow progression and covering the population with fortified foods. Respondents have reported that they cannot be forced to produce fortified flour because some of the customers reject it and this is why from the same mill, they produce both fortified and non-fortified flour to meet the need of both categories and therefore, there is a coexistence of both fortified and non-fortified flour produced within the same mill and they both coexist in the market without any punishment or penalty given to them because the fortification is voluntary for them. Respondent reported that if they are to be punished, the law must be established first and be known to them. Two respondents, one fortifying and one non-fortifying miller, were quoted as below;

What? Punishment from where sister? Then that punishment has to start from the law. Maybe you ask first if there is that law and if it is known… (Small-scale miller non-fortifying number 1)

Eeehh…where will that punishment come from and to who? Since…it is not mandatory it is voluntary and you cannot be forced to fortify because customers do not want it…you will fortify and then sell to who? That is why I have told you here we are producing both fortified and non-fortified flour. (Medium-scale miller fortifying number 2)

Trustworthiness of the study

This study has demonstrated the scientific rigour as explained by Lincoln and Guba.25 Credibility was ensured through prolonged conversation with study respondents, paraphrasing the questions, using probe questions and through data triangulation using different data sources like millers who fortify, who does not fortify of different capacities (micro/ toll, small and medium-scale millers).

Transferability was ensured through thick description, when the same methodology will be used, interviewing similar respondents of the similar characteristics in a similar setting same or similar findings as reported in this study will be obtained.25 26

Dependability has been ensured through audit trail technique, from the proposal document as a protocol which was used to conduct this study to how data was collected, analysed and presentation of the study findings there will be a consistency in obtaining the similar results again and again.25 26 Conformability was demonstrated by presenting the findings of the study using participants’ own words (verbatim quotes) if the study participants will see the results, they will agree to the findings.25 26

Discussion

Access to knowledge and information among corn millers was one of the factors facilitating the implementation of the food fortification regulation. On the contrary, lack of consumers’ awareness on fortified corn flour was a notable barrier towards the implementation of the food regulation not only in Tanzania but also in other settings where food fortification programmes are being implemented.27 Zero costs associated with fortification of corn flour encourage corn millers to fortify as they are not incurring additional costs, lack of punishments, penalty and fines for non-fortifying millers were found to make millers reluctant to fortify corn flour and others who were doing fortification to reduce the efforts. These subthemes are discussed hereby under the main theme factors influencing corn millers to implement food fortification regulation in Tanzania.

Knowledge and right information about food fortification play a key role in influencing the implementation of food fortification regulation. Millers are the key stakeholders in the implementation of food fortification programme and the success of the fortification of staple foods like corn depends on their engagement and participation in the fortification initiatives.28 When millers are aware of the public health benefits that the population will obtain from consumption of fortified corn flour, they will make informed decision to adopt this public health intervention.29 Lack of knowledge on the fortification technology among corn millers is a hindrance towards the implementation of food fortification and intervention.30 Similarly, millers are the key implementers of food fortification regulation equipping small and medium-scale corn millers who feed the high large number of population with fortification technology may not only increase voluntary compliance to the existing regulation but also ensure the intended impact or result in the community is visible.28 29

Millers are not only providing service to the community but also, they are business entities aiming to generate profits and therefore, market demand drives them to produce what the market or their clients’ wants as a pulling factor for their businesses. Lack of awareness on existence of fortified foods and fear of unknown among consumers influence millers against fortification, while those complying to fortification tend to reduce or stop corn producing fortified corn flour.27 Consumers’ awareness brings influence in the food choices, preference and willingness to pay more for the nutritious foods either by eating balanced diets or purchasing of fortified foods. Awareness will influence their perceived benefits, attitudes, behaviours and actions towards fortified foods like corn flour. Studies show that the success and sustainability of food fortification are highly influenced or triggered by consumers’ awareness.29 31 Consumers’ acceptance of fortified foods has been associated with the consumers’ nutrition knowledge and understanding of the micronutrients and the implication of micronutrient deficiency.27 They tend to consume nutritious foods and fortified foods based on the perceived benefits. Raising public awareness on consumption of balanced diets, existence of fortified foods and the health benefits will increase demand for fortified foods and trigger millers to produce.32

Low or no associated costs incurred by millers have favoured the implementation of food fortification in Tanzania since most of it is covered by development partners. This implies that if the millers will be supported by either the government or development partners to cover for the fortification costs, more millers in Tanzania will be able to comply and adopt the fortification. However, such business case is often unsustainable.33 Instead, a long-term plan for financing the fortification programme or self-reliance should be emphasised from the beginning to achieve long-term benefits.34 Moreover, when fortification becomes mandatory for all producers, the government can create an enabling environment for more millers to participate through offering subsidy to the premix, local manufacturing of the dosifiers and tax exemption to imported dosifiers for millers to be able to afford and implement an intervention in an affordable cost.35

In countries where food fortification is voluntary enforcement for compliance becomes a barrier as millers have the options not to fortify. This makes a nation or partners’ efforts on the food fortification to be reduced or invisible as whether millers fortify or not, there is no any effect and this may make partners to lose interest in investing in the food fortification. Enforcement through the regulatory body or authorised entities has been reported to facilitate increase in compliance among producers.36 Therefore, a right mix of both enforcement and punishments should be considered for mandatory food fortification regulation.31 The context of a coexistence of both fortified and non-fortified flour can demoralise and reduce efforts for the ones who were fortifying.37 In a setting where fortification is mandatory, consumers’ resistance is reduced as they have no option since all the available corn flour in the market is fortified.38

Strengths and limitations of the study

Evidence gathered in this study should be discussed in the light of the following limitations. Lack of causality based on the nature of the study, a qualitative design, which is often opinionated and cannot be generalised. The evidence in this study, however, is informative and aimed to gather more context-specific evidence for policy and practice. Further, we ensured scientific rigour and trustworthiness through four quality criteria which are credibility, transferability, dependability and conformability. The phenomenological design helped to obtain real-life experience from the corn milers who are key implementers of food fortification on the factors influencing the implementation of food fortification and so the findings from this study design are reliable. Moreover, findings from this study may not be generalised to the entire population but deemed important to improving the implementation of food fortification in Tanzania or planning the implementation of new methods under similar circumstances, setting or context. This study, however, is the first to examine the policy and implementation in the context of food fortification as an important evidence-based intervention to improve the hidden burden of malnutrition—the micronutrient deficiencies in a country with a persistent burden thereof.

Conclusions

The implementation of food fortification regulation among corn millers in Tanzania is influenced by access to knowledge and right information, lack of consumers’ awareness on fortified corn flour, and costs associated with fortification of corn flour from millers’ perspective. Moreover, the policy implementation is hindered by lack of punitive measures in form of penalties and fines for non-fortifying millers, jeopardising the effective implementation of this act. Small and medium-scale corn millers who have right information and knowledge on fortification of corn flour should be an example to other corn millers by increasing the production of fortified corn flour and take part in raising awareness on the health benefits of consuming fortified corn flour to their customers and throughout their distribution channel.

Recommendations

In addressing the burden of micronutrient deficiency through population-wide interventions, efforts are needed to address the mentioned challenges and barriers. Imposing punitive measures to non-complying millers, educational sessions and regular supportive supervisions are necessary. Millers should also be mandated to ensure food and nutritional information are available in labels. With this and coupled with widespread educational messages targeting consumers, they may be able to recognise fortified foods and therefore set demand for millers to comply. Similar study is to be conducted in the rural setting in Tanzania where there is a huge number of toll millers. Assessment on the awareness of fortified foods (fortified corn flour) among consumers in Tanzania in urban and rural settings is to be conducted. In addition, it is crucial to determine the proportion of consumers who are willing to pay more for fortified foods.
