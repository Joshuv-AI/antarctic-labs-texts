On Recurrence Relations of Multi-dimensional Sequences
Hamid Rahkooy

Abstract
In this paper, we present a new algorithm for computing the linear recurrence relations of multi-dimensional sequences. Existing algorithms for computing these relations arise in computational algebra and include constructing structured matrices and computing their kernels. The challenging problem is to reduce the size of the corresponding matrices. In this paper, we show how to convert the problem of computing recurrence relations of multi-dimensional sequences into computing the orthogonal of certain ideals as subvector spaces of the dual module of polynomials. We propose an algorithm using efficient dual module computation algorithms. We present a complexity bound for this algorithm, carry on experiments using Maple implementation, and discuss the cases when using this algorithm is much faster than the existing approaches.



1
Introduction

Sequences are well-known mathematical objects that appear in many
areas of science. Recurrence relations are a standard presentation
of sequences. A recurrence relation is a relation between a finite
number of the elements of the sequence with fixed coefficients such
that the relation is preserved under any shift of the indices of the
elements of the sequence. The problem of finding recurrence
relations of a given sequence is a classic problem.

Considering sequences over a field as functions from natural numbers
to a field, a natural generalization into
multi-dimensional
sequences
is the set of functions from
\mathbb{N}^{n}
into a field, for
a positive integer
n
. Recurrence relations of multi-dimensional
sequences should be defined in such a way that they are preserved
under
shifts in any direction
. As a monoid,
\mathbb{N}^{n}
is
isomorphic to the monomials in
n
variables, hence every element of
a multi-dimensional sequence is assigned to a monomial, which gives
a hint to define recurrence relations of multi-dimensional
sequences using multivariate polynomials, and then shifts in
different directions would correspond to multiplication by
monomials. A polynomial is defined to be a recurrence relation for a
multi-dimensional sequence if the sum of the multiplications of its
coefficients by the elements of the sequence corresponding to the
relevant monomials is zero, and moreover, every multiplication of
the polynomial with a monomial must have the same property.
Recurrence relations of a multi-dimensional sequence form an
ideal. We study the problem of finding
recurrence relations
efficiently
.

In commutative algebra, multi-dimensional sequences correspond to
the elements of the dual module of polynomials. Given a
multi-dimensional sequence, the corresponding element of the dual
module is defined by its value on a monomial being the value of
the sequence at that monomials, and then linearly extending the
definition to polynomials. A recurrence relation of a
multi-dimensional sequence is then an annihilator of the
corresponding element of the dual module.

Dual module and its computational aspects have been studied by
Macaulay in his seminal work
[
14
]
, where he introduced

inverse systems
and presented an algorithm that takes the
generators of an ideal and computes a set of generators of the
corresponding subspace in the dual module. Macaulay’s algorithm
considers
enough many
multiplications of the given
generators of the ideal and finds linear relations between them. The
matrices associated with such linear systems are quasi-Toeplitz. In
algebraic geometry, the well-known Gorenstein schemes/algebras
are tightly related to dual modules
[
9
]
and the recurrence
relations of multi-dimensional sequences can be used for
computations on Gorenstein
algebras
[
12
,
8
,
10
]
.

A first approach for computing recurrence relations of a
multi-dimensional sequence is to consider the vector of the first
s
elements of the sequence and the vectors of its
shifts, and then find the linear relations between those
vectors. This leads to solving a linear system of equations whose
corresponding matrix is a Hankel matrix. Assuming that the first
s
elements of a sequence is enough for determining the ideal of recurrence relations, then the corresponding Hankel matrix will be of size
s
, and hence, a first complexity bound for finding the
kernel of this matrix is
\mathcal{O}(s^{\omega})
, where
\omega
is
the constant for matrix multiplication. The structure of the matrix can help with obtaining a better complexity bound.
Multi-dimensional sequences and computing their linear recurrence
relations using the latter approach appeared in Sakata’s work
[
29
,
27
,
28
,
31
,
30
]
as
a generalisation of Berlekamp and Massey’s work on shift register and
coding
[
20
,
2
]
.

In the 1990s and later, Marinari, Mora, Möller and Alonso studied
the problem of finding a Gröbner basis of the ideal for which the dual
vector space is given
[
1
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. They
showed that the problem of finding the annihilator of a given
subspace of the dual module is a generalisation of several
well-known problems in computational algebra such as the FGLM
Problem
[
11
]
and Buchberger-Möller
algorithm
[
21
]
. They presented two FGLM-style
algorithms that traverse through the monomials, looking for their
linear combinations that are killed by the given
dual module elements. They also show an application on Hilbert
scheme computation. The algorithms presented in
[
17
]
have complexity bound
\mathcal{O}(nt^{3}+nft^{2})
, where
t
is the number of given linear maps/sequences and
f
is the cost of evaluation maps on monomials. The algorithms assume that the input linear maps span
the orthogonal vector space of the annihilator as a subspace of the dual module. A collection of the material on the duality problem can be found in Part VI of Mora’s Book
[
22
]
.

Computing recurrence relations of multi-dimensional sequences (or equivalently computing the annihilator of the dual module elements) is an active research problem and there are several recent publications on this problem.
Berthomieu, et al. presented an FGLM-style algorithms
with the complexity bound

\mathcal{O}(r(r+|G|)m)

[
4
]
, where
G
is the reduced Gröbner basis of the annihilator and m is the number of monomials that are less than the leading monomials of
G
(and hence
m
is the size of the border basis), and an improvement of this algorithm with the complexity bound
\mathcal{O}((r+|G|)^{l})

[
6
,
7
]
, where
l
is the size of the Minkowski sum of the staircase with itself.

Neiger, Rahkooy & Schost presented an algorithm based on projection,
with complexity
\mathcal{O}(ntBr^{3})
, assuming that the
t
input sequences satisfy the same condition as Mora et al.’s algorithm, and where
B
is a bound on the degree of the minimal polynomial of the variables and
n
is the number of the variables
[
26
]
.
Computing the annihilator is a special case of Mourrain’s algorithm
for computing the border basis of Gorenstein Artinian algebras in

[
24
]
, where he applied his algorithm in
Tensor Decomposition Problem
[
3
]
. The
complexity of this algorithm is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(nsr^{2})

[
24
]
.

In this paper, we propose a new method for computing recurrence relations of multi-dimensional sequences, or equivalently, finding the annihilator of a finite set of the dual module of polynomials.
We convert the problem of finding the annihilator into the problem of computing the orthogonal space of a certain ideal in the dual module, as a vector space. This conversion is inspired by the relationship between the
(quasi-)Hankel matrices for computing the annihilator of given dual module elements and the (quasi-)Toplitz matrices used for computing the orthogonal space of the annihilator ideal as a subspace of the vector space of the dual elements. This enables us to exploit the existing algorithms for computing the orthogonal of an ideal as a subspace of dual space of polynomials.

Apart from the classical algorithm of Macaulay
[
14
]
, Mourrain
[
23
]
and Zeng
[
32
]
have introduced faster algorithms
for computing the orthogonal space of an ideal. Our method can be applied to any of these algorithms and convert them into an algorithm for computing the recurrence relations of given sequences. We have considered Mourrain’s algorithm
[
23
]
,
and optimisations on it
[
16
]
and introduced an algorithm for computing recurrence relations.
We have implemented our algorithm in
Maple
, extending the package in
[
15
,
16
]
. We prove the
complexity bound
\mathcal{O}((s-r)^{3}+ns)
for our algorithm, where
s
is the number of given elements of the sequence,
n
is the dimension of the ambient space and
r
is the dimension of the quotient space of the polynomial ring with respect to the annihilator ideal. The complexity bound for our algorithm compared to the complexity bounds of the algorithms in
[
24
]
shows that our algorithm is faster when
s-r
is small, while it is slower when
s-r
is large and the complexity bounds agree when
s=r
.

The existing algorithms for computing recurrence relations/annihilators can be considered FGLM-style as they traverse through the monomials starting from the smallest. Our algorithm is novel in the sense that it
starts from the largest monomial for which the associated value of the sequence is given and increments towards the smallest monomial.
In addition, similar to the FGLM, the other algorithms construct matrices
whose columns are labelled with the monomials, however, our
algorithm labels the columns of the matrices constructed by the sum of several monomials, which reduces the number of columns. This property is
inherited from Mourrain and Zeng’s algorithms for computing the dual of a given ideal
[
23
,
32
]
.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation and briefly introduce the required preliminaries on multi-dimensional sequences and their recurrence relations. We review the connection between multi-dimensional sequences and the dual module of polynomials as well as polynomial differential operators. Then we discuss the existing algorithms in the literature for computing recurrence relations of multi-dimensional ideals as well as the orthogonal of an ideal. In
Section
3
, we present the main definition of this paper and
the main proposition for converting annihilator computation into the computation of the orthogonal of an ideal. We also explain
the idea behind our algorithm via examples and present our algorithm.
Section
4
is devoted to the complexity
analysis of our algorithm, discussion of its advantages and
disadvantages, comparison with other existing algorithms and also experiments via our Maple implementation.

Throughout this paper, we use the following conventions and notations.
\mathbb{K}

denotes the ring of polynomials in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, where

a fixed integer,
I\trianglelefteq\mathbb{K}[X]
denotes that

is an ideal in

. For
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

denotes the

2.1
Annihilator of
n-
dimensional Sequences

The definitions and properties in this subsection can be
found in more details in
[
26
]
.

We generalize the definition of a sequence into an
n-
dimensional sequence.

Definition 1

(
n-
dimensional Sequence
[
26
]
)
.

An
n-
dimensional sequence over a field
\mathbb{K}
is an ordered set

\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
, where

\ell_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}
, for all
\alpha
. We denote the set of all
n-
dimensional sequences
over
\mathbb{K}
by
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
.

Throughout this paper,
n
is fixed and for convenience, we call an

n-
dimensional sequence simply a sequence.

\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
is a
\mathbb{K}[X]-
module. To see this, take an
n-
dimensional
sequence
\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
and a monomial

X^{\beta}
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
. Define

X^{\beta}\cdot\ell=(\ell_{\alpha+\beta})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

(
\ell_{\gamma}=0\ \forall\gamma<\beta
), and extend the definition linearly
to the polynomials as follows. Let

f(X)=\sum\limits_{\gamma\in B\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}}f_{\gamma}X^{\gamma}\in\mathbb{K}[X]
, where
B
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
and define

f(X)\cdot\ell=(\sum\limits_{\gamma\in B\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}}f_{\gamma}\ell_{\gamma+\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
.
One can check that this
multiplication turns
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
into a
\mathbb{K}[X]-
module.

[
26
]
.

Definition 2

(Annihilator of a Sequence

[
26
]
, Section 2.1)
.

Let
\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
be an
n-
dimensional sequence.
The annihilator of
\ell
is defined to be

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)=\{f(X)\in\mathbb{K}[X]|f(X)\cdot\ell=0\}
.
For several
n-
dimensional sequences
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s}
, the
annihilator is defined to be

\mathrm{ann}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s})=\mathrm{ann}(\ell_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathrm{ann}(\ell_{s})
.

One can easily check that the annihilator is an ideal in
\mathbb{K}[X]

[
26
]
.

The set of
\mathbb{K}-
linear functions from
\mathbb{K}[X]
to
\mathbb{K}
is denoted
by
\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
. For all
\ell^{*}\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
and monomial
X^{\alpha}
,
define
X^{\alpha}\cdot\ell^{*}:\mathbb{K}[X]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
, by

X^{\alpha}\cdot\ell^{*}(X^{\beta})=\ell^{*}(X^{\alpha+\beta})
for a monomial

X^{\beta}
, and extend the definition to polynomials.
Linearly extending the multiplication of a monomial by a
linear function into the multiplication of a polynomial by
a linear function, makes

\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
a
\mathbb{K}[X]-
module. This module is called the dual module
of
\mathbb{K}[X]
.
One can also consider
\mathbb{K}[X]
as a vector space over
\mathbb{K}
.
Then
\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
is the dual vector space of
\mathbb{K}[X]
.
In this paper, we use the word
“dual” both for
\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
as a module and a vector space, unless we
clearly specify one of the two.

There exists an isomorphism between
\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
and
\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}

as
\mathbb{K}[X]
modules. This isomorphism maps
every
\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}

into a linear function
\ell^{*}\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
,
such that
\ell^{*}
maps every monomial
X^{\alpha}
into

\ell^{*}(X^{\alpha}):=\ell_{\alpha}
, and extends it linearly to
every polynomial

f(X)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in A\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}
, i.e.,

\ell^{*}(f(X))=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}\ell^{*}(X^{\alpha})=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}\ell_{\alpha}
, where
A
is a finite
subset of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
.
Conversely, to a linear function
\ell^{*}\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
,
one can associate a sequence

\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
, such that

\ell_{\alpha}:=\ell^{*}(X^{\alpha})

[
26
]
.
The above isomorphism can be considered as a vector space isomorphism
as well.

2.2
Dual Module of Polynomials

Required background on computations on dual module of polynomials
is given below from
[
23
,
3
]
.

Assume that
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, and let

\partial^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
be the differential operator whose action on
a monomial

X^{\beta}:=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}}

is defined to be
\partial^{\alpha}(X^{\beta})=1
if
\alpha=\beta
,
and zero otherwise. Extending the above action linearly to the polynomials,
define
\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
to be the set of power series of the differential operators
acting on the polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[X]
.
Let
\partial^{\alpha}
be a monomial differential operator in
\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
and

X^{\beta}
and
X^{\gamma}
be monomials in
\mathbb{K}[X]
.
Define
X^{\beta}\cdot\partial^{\alpha}:=\partial^{\alpha-\beta}
.
Again, extending this definition linearly to the multiplication of
a polynomial by a differential power series, one can see that

\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
is a
\mathbb{K}[X]-
module. More details on the above can be found in

[
23
]
.

It is well-known that the
\mathbb{K}[X]-
module
\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
, the dual of
\mathbb{K}[X]
. To see this isomorphism,
first of all, note that by definition, a power series of differential
operators is in
\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
. On the other hand, let
\ell^{*}\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
. For every monomial

X^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}[X]
, let
\ell_{\alpha}:=\ell^{*}(X^{\alpha})
.
Define

p_{\ell}(\partial):=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\ell_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
. Then
p_{\ell}(\partial)
is the image
of
\ell^{*}
under this isomorphism. More details
can be found in, e.g.,
[
23
,
18
]
.

Consider an ideal
I
as a submodule (or sub-vector space) of the module
(vector space) of polynomials. Then the orthogonal of
I
in the dual
module (vector space) is defined to be

I^{\perp}:=\{\Lambda\in\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}|\Lambda(f)=0\ \ \ \forall f\in I\}

[
23
]
.
One can easily check that
I^{\perp}
is a sub-module (subsapce) of
the dual module (vector space).

Having the isomorphism
\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]\simeq\mathbb{K}[X]^{*}
, by the members of

I^{\perp}
(or
I^{\perp}
itself), we refer to the linear maps in the dual
module as well as the corresponding differential operators. By computing

I^{\perp}
we mean computing a basis for the corresponding subsapce of
\mathbb{K}[[\partial]]
,
consisting of differential power series (polynomials) operators.

For a positive integer
t
, let
I^{\perp}_{t}
denote the set of differential
polynomials in
I^{\perp}
that have degree at most
t
.
I^{\perp}_{t}
is a
subspace of
I^{\perp}
, which we call the orthogonal of
I
up to degree
t
.
For example
I^{\perp}_{0}
only includes
1_{0}
, the evaluation functional at

0
. Obviously
I^{\perp}_{t}\subseteq I^{\perp}_{t+1}
. Mora et al. have
proved the following for zero dimensional ideals.

Theorem 3

(Mora, et al. 1993,
[
18
]
, Theorem 3.1)
.

There is a one to one correspondence between finite dimensional
subspaces of
\mathbb{K}[\partial]
that are closed under derivation and

\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideals in
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, where

\mathfrak{m}=\left\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right\rangle
. Moreover, If
I
is an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}

ideal, then
I^{\perp}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{K}[\partial]
, which includes
polynomials instead of power series.

In other words, the orthogonal of an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideal hass a finite
basis whose elements are polynomial differential
operators. Conversely, the annihilator of a finite set of sequences
with finite support is an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideal. Also there exists an
upper bound
\mathcal{N}
on the degree of the elements of
I^{\perp}
,
called the
nil-index
of
I
. For more details on nil-index,
refer to
[
23
]
. Therefore, the orthogonal of an

\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideal is a finite dimensional sub-vector space of the dual
space and
I^{\perp}_{\mathcal{N}}=I^{\perp}_{\mathcal{N}+1}=\ldots
.
Following the above, the algorithms proposed for computing
I^{\perp}
,
by Macaulay
[
14
]
, Mourrain
[
23
]
and Zeng

[
32
]
, start from
I^{\perp}_{0}
(which is
\left\langle 1_{0}\right\rangle
for

\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideals), and compute

I^{\perp}_{1}\subseteq I^{\perp}_{2}\subseteq\ldots
incrementally. For

\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideals, the existence of nil-index guarantees that this
chain is finite.

Let
\mathbb{K}[X]_{t}
denote the vector space of polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[X]

of degree at most
t
.
For computing
I^{\perp}_{t}\subseteq\mathbb{K}[X]_{t}
, Macaulay’s algorithm considers
a polynomial differential operator with indeterminate coefficients
(say
c_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
),
whose support includes all monomials of degree at most
t
, i.e.,

p(\partial)=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq t}c_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}[\partial]_{t}
.
One can easily check that
p(\partial)\in I^{\perp}
iff

p(\partial)\cdot X^{\beta}f_{i}(X)=0
for all monomials
X^{\beta}
.
This leads to a Toeplitz system of linear equations in
indeterminates
c_{\alpha}

of size
\binom{t+n-1}{t}
(the number of monomials of degree
at most
t
).
The complexity of solving this system
is
O\left(\binom{t+n-1}{t}^{\omega}\right)
, where
\omega
is the constant
in the complexity of matrix multiplication.

In order to speed up Macaulay’s algorithm, Zeng and Mourrain took
advantage of the so called
closedness property
of the
orthogonal of an ideal,
which means that
the orthogonal of an ideal is closed under differentiation,
i.e.,
\partial_{i}\cdot p(\partial)\in I^{\perp}_{t-1}
,
for all
p(\partial)\in I^{\perp}_{t}
, where

\partial^{\alpha}\cdot\partial^{\beta}
is defined to be

\partial^{\beta-\alpha}
, for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and the
definition is linearly extended to

\partial^{\alpha}\cdot p(\partial)
.
Using this property, Mourrain’s algorithm (which we refer to as the
integration method)
integrates
members of a basis of

I^{\perp}_{t-1}
in order to compute a basis for
I^{\perp}_{t}
.

Theorem 4

(Mourrain, 1997,
[
23
]
)
.

Assume that
I
is an ideal generated by
f_{1}(X),\ldots,f_{m}(X)
and

t>1
is an integer. Let
p_{1}(\partial),\ldots,p_{s}(\partial)
be a basis of
I^{\perp}_{t}
.
Then the elements of
I^{\perp}_{t+1}
with no constant terms
(in
\partial_{i}
) are of the form

p(\partial)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{ik}\int_{k}p_{i}(\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{k},0,\ldots,0),

(1)

such that

1.

\forall\ \ 1\leq k\leq m,\ p(\partial)(f_{k}(X))=0

2.

\forall\ \ 1\leq k<l\leq n,\ \sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{ik}\partial_{l}\cdot p_{i}(\partial)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{il}\partial_{k}\cdot p_{i}(\partial)=0
.

For the details of the above theorem and the integration method,
refer to
[
23
]
.

As computing the orthogonal of an ideal is mostly used in investigating the
multiplicity structure of an isolated point, the above theorem and the
resulting algorithm is mostly used for ideals with an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
component.
However, the above theorem does not require
I
to be
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
,
neither to have an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
component, and one can use it in order to
compute a basis for

I^{\perp}_{t}
for every
t
, incrementally. Note that when
I
is not

\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
, then not necessarily
I^{\perp}_{0}=\left\langle 1_{0}\right\rangle
, but actually it
can be that
I^{\perp}_{0}=\emptyset
. In any case, this should be checked at
the beginning of the algorithm. Later in Section
3
,
in order to use the integration method for computing the annihilator,
we will treat this case in Lemma
21
.

The following proposition gives us the complexity of the integration method
when the input is a generating set for an ideal with an isolated component,
and the isolated point itself.

Proposition 5

(Proposition 4.1,
[
23
]
)
.

Let
I=\left\langle p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\right\rangle\trianglelefteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
,
with a
m_{\zeta}-
primary component
Q_{\zeta}
such that the multiplicity of

\zeta
is
\mu
. Also, assume that

Q_{\zeta}^{\perp}=\left\langle\beta_{1}(\partial),\cdots,\beta_{\mu}(\partial)\right\rangle
.
The total number of arithmetic operations for computing
Q_{\zeta}^{\perp}

during the integration algorithm is bounded by
O((n^{2}+m)\mu^{3}+nm\mu C)
,
where
C
is the maximal cost for computing

\int_{k}\beta_{j}|_{\partial_{k+1}=\cdots=\partial_{n}=0}(p_{i})
.

C
can be bounded by
O(n\mu L)
, where
L
is the number of monomials
obtained by derivation of the monomials of
(p_{i})
.

The multiplicity of the isolated point in the above proposition, i.e.,

, is the size of the largest matrix that is
constructed at the final step of the algorithm, step

,
where
\mathcal{N}

is the nil-index of the
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
component. Obviously, this can
be much smaller than the size of the largest matrix in Macaulay’s algorithm,
which is
\mathcal{N}+n-1\choose\mathcal{N}
.

2.3

Having introduced the required background on dual module computation in

The isomorphism

Having the above isomorphism,

More details on the above isomorphisms can be found in

In this paper, for an

3
The Algorithm

In this section, we explain our algorithm for computing the annihilator of
a sequence (or several sequences), using the algorithms for computing the
orthogonal of an ideal, in particular, the integration algorithm.

Given a sequence
\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}

with its values over
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
described in the previous subsection, a first
algorithm for computing the annihilator is as follows. Let

f(X)=\sum\limits_{\gamma\in B\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}}f_{\gamma}X^{\gamma}\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
,
with symbolic coefficients
f_{\gamma}
. Then by the definition of the
annihilator, we have that

\sum\limits_{\gamma\in B\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}}f_{\gamma}\ell_{\gamma+\alpha}=0\ \ \ \forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(4)

As the sequence is given over a finite set
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
such that for all

\alpha\notin\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
we have that
\ell_{\alpha}=0
, then equations

4
yield a finite linear system of equations with
f_{\gamma}

as indeterminates.
One can consider the associated matrix
H_{\ell}
labeled as follows.
Having set a term order on the monomials, the columns of
H_{\ell}

are labeled by the monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
in an ascending order.
Rows of
H_{\ell}
are labeled by
X^{\alpha}\cdot\ell
, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
.
Then the entry
(\alpha,\beta)
in
H_{\ell}
is actually

\left(X^{\beta}\cdot\ell\right)_{\alpha}=\ell_{\alpha+\beta}
.
This is indeed a
Hankel
system of size

s:=|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|
. Hence, the complexity of this system can be bounded by

s^{\omega}
,
where
\omega
is the constant in the complexity of matrix multiplication.
For more than one sequence, the annihilator can be computed similarly.
The resulting linear system in this case will be a quasi-Hankel system.

(5)

If we use Macaulay’s algorithm in order to compute the orthogonal of the

\left\langle L(X)\right\rangle_{d}^{\perp}

6.

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)=\mathcal{B}_{\ell}\bigcup\{rec(\Lambda_{1}(X)),\ldots,rec(\Lambda_{s}(X))\}

7.

return

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)

Let
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq t}
be the elements of the annihilator with monomials
of degree at least
t
. If
f(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq t}
, then

x_{i}f(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq t+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. This corresponds
to the closedness property for dual modules. More precisely,

Remark 22
.

In analogy with
[
23
]
, the closedness property of the dual
is equivalent to the fact that the annihilator
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
is closed under
multiplication, i.e., if

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq d-(t-1)}=\left\langle b_{1}(X),\ldots,b_{s_{t-1}}(X)\right\rangle
, then

b(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq d-t}\Leftrightarrow x_{1}b(X),\ldots,x_{n}b(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq d-(t-1)}.

One can re-write Theorem
4
using the above notation
and Proposition
20
in order to obtain a formula for

b(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq d-t}
using a basis of
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)_{\geq d-(t-1)}
. Then
replacing integration with division in Theorem
4
,
one can directly take advantage of the fact that the
annihilator is closed under multiplication. However, the computations
via this formula will be exactly the same as the computations in
Algorithm
1
. Berthomieu & Faugère’s
in
[
5
]
use this idea in their
Polynomial-Division-Based algorithm.

Remark 23
.

The algorithm can be easily generalized to compute the annihilator of
more than one sequence, say
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{t}
. In this case,

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
should be replaced with
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{(i)}
,
where

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{(i)}
is the support of
\ell_{i}
.
Also in step
3
,
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
, the border of a sequence should
be replaced with
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{B}_{\ell}^{i}
, where

\mathcal{B}_{\ell}^{i}
is the border of the sequence
\ell_{i}
.
In step
5
of the
algorithm,
one should compute the subspace of

\left\langle rec(p_{\ell_{1}}(X)),\ldots,rec(p_{\ell_{t}}(X))\right\rangle^{\perp}

whose monomials are in the reciprocal of
\mathcal{S}
.

Remark 24
.

Although we assume that in our algorithm the annihilator
is an
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
ideal, however,
the algorithm is correct for sequences whose annihilator is not
necessarily
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
, but have the flat extension property as in
Mourrain’s algorithm. Discussion on the flat extension property is out of
the scope of this paper. For the details we refer the
reader to
[
24
]
.

4
Complexity and Experiments

In this section, we compare our algorithm with other existing
algorithms, both in terms of their complexity and experiments.

4.1
Complexity of the Algorithm

Let us remind that for our algorithm the input is a sequence

\ell=(\ell_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}}
, in which the values

\ell_{\alpha}
are given over a finite set
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
. Also
s:=|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|
and

r:=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}{\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X]$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)$}\right.}
and
D_{\ell}
is as in Remark
18
.

Theorem 25
.

Algorithm
1
is correct and the total number of
arithmetic operations in Algorithm
1
using the
integration method can be bounded by

O\left(n^{2}(s-r)^{3}+ns\right).

(9)

Proof.

The correctness of the algorithm comes from
Proposition
20
and Lemma
21
. For the
complexity bound, we only compute the number of arithmetic
operations in steps
3
for computing
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
and

5
for computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
. The cost of
computations in other steps can be ignored in comparison with those
two steps.

Computing step border-comp is basically reading off the members of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
, for
which the cost is
|\mathcal{B}_{\ell}|\leq n|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|=ns
. This is the second summand
in formula
9
.

In order to compute the complexity of computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
using
the integration method, below we will explain a modification of
Proposition 4.1 in
[
23
]
on the complexity of the integration
method for ideals with an isolated point into an arbitrary ideal. This
comes directly from the proof of Proposition
9
in

[
23
]
.

With the input an ideal and an isolated point
\zeta
of the ideal, the
integration method computes a basis for
Q_{\zeta}^{\perp}
, where
Q_{\zeta}

is the primary component of the ideal corresponding to
\zeta
.
The size of the basis for
Q_{\zeta}^{\perp}
is

\mu=\dim{\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$Q_{\zeta}$}\right.}=\dim Q_{\zeta}^{\perp}
, the multiplicity of

\zeta
. This is where
\mu
in Proposition 4.1 in
[
23
]
comes
from.
AnnihilatorViaDuality instead computes a basis for the vector space

D_{\ell}
.
Let
\{\Lambda_{1}(\partial),\ldots,\Lambda_{t}(\partial)\}
be such a basis.
Then
\mu
replaced with
t
in Proposition 4.1 in
[
23
]

gives the complexity of computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
using the
integration method. Below we prove that
t=s-r
.

Consider
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
as a vector space over
\mathbb{K}
. By Proposition

20
, members of
D_{\ell}
are in one-to-one correspondence
with the the members of

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}=\{f(X)\in\mathrm{ann}(\ell)\ |\ Supp(f(X))\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\}
.

\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}
is a subspace of
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
, and

\{f_{1}(X)=rec(\Lambda_{1}(X)),\ldots,f_{t}(X)=rec(\Lambda_{t}(X))\}
is
a basis of
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}
.
One can easily check that

\{\Lambda_{1}(\partial),\ldots,\Lambda_{t}(\partial)\}
is a linearly
independent set in
D_{\ell}
if and only if
\{f_{1}(X),\ldots,f_{t}(X)\}
is
a linearly independent set in
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
.
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)
—as an ideal— is
generated by

\{f_{1}(X),\ldots,f_{t}(X)\}\bigcup\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
. Let
W=\left\langle\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\right\rangle
be the
vector space spanned by the monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
. Then
\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}
is a subspace
of
W
, and therefore we have

\dim(\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime})+\dim({\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$W$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}$}\right.})=\dim(W)
.
But
\dim({\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$W$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}$}\right.})=\dim({\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X]$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}$}\right.})
,
because all
monomials in
\mathbb{K}[X]\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
are zero in
{\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X]$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime}$}\right.}
.
Therefore
\dim(\mathrm{ann}(\ell)^{\prime})=s-r
.

Replacing
\mu
with
s-r
in Proposition 4.1 in
[
23
]
, the
complexity of computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
can be bounded by

O(n^{2}(s-r)^{3}+n(s-r)C)
, where
C
—in analogy with Proposition
4.1 in
[
23
]
—can be bounded by
O(n(s-r)L)
, where
L
is the
number of monomials obtained by derivation
of
the monomials of
rec(p_{\ell}(X))
except for those that are out of a basis
for
{\raisebox{1.00006pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X]$}\left/\raisebox{-1.00006pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)$}\right.}
. This can be bound by
s-r
.
So complexity of computing
C
can be bounded by
n^{2}(s-r)^{3}
, and
therefore the complexity of computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
is at most

O(n^{2}(s-r)^{3})
. This proves the proposition.
∎

We remind that the complexity of computing a basis for
D_{\ell}
using
Macaulay’s algorithm is the same as computing the annihilator via Hankel
matrix, which can be bounded by
s^{\omega}
.
The complexity of Mourrain’s algorithm as well as Berthomieu &
Faugere’s Polynomial-Division-Based algorithm is
nsr^{2}
, while the
complexity of AnnihilatorViaDuality algorithm is
n^{n}(s-r)^{3}+ns
.
Therefore, our algorithm is faster when
s-r
is small, and is slower
when
r<<s
.

A simple case when our algorithm is faster is when the annihilator
is a monomial ideal. In this case
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}=B
, and our algorithm only reads
off
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
, which costs at most
ns
, while this is the worst case for
all Mourrain’s and Berthomieu-Faugere’s algorithms.
Another set of sequences for which
s-r
is small is when we
cut a
small corner from
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
. The following is an example of such a
sequence.

Example 26
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
be the set of the monomials under the staircase of the
monomial ideal generated by
x^{4},y^{3}
and
xy^{2}
. Define the sequence

\ell
with the support
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
as follows.

\ell(x^{4})=\ell(y)=d,\ell(x^{4}y)=\ell(y^{2})=8
, and
for the rest of the monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
,
\ell(x^{i}y^{j})=i+j
.

\ell
has value zero for all monomials outside
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
.
Then the annihilator is generated by

\mathcal{B}_{\ell}\cup\{x^{4}y^{2},x^{3}y^{2}-x^{4}+x^{3}y-x^{2}y^{2},x^{4}y-x^{3}y^{2}\},

(10)

and the quotient algebra of the annihilator ideal includes all
the monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
except for
x^{4}y^{2}
.

One can easily generalize the above example into sequences whose
support is defined by the staircase of certain ideals.

Example 27
.

Consider the monomial ideal
\left\langle x^{d},y^{3},xy^{2}\right\rangle
, where
d
is
a positive integer, and its staircase, i.e., the monomials that form
a basis for the vector space of the quotient with respect to this
ideal. Let
\ell(x^{d})=\ell(y)=d,\ell(x^{d}y)=\ell(y^{2})=2d
, and

\ell(x^{i}y^{j})=i+j
for the rest of the monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
. Then
the quotient algebra of the annihilator ideal includes all of the
monomials in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
except for
x^{d}y^{2}
.

In Section
4.2
, we will present several examples of
sequences with small
s-r
.

Example 28
.

In the case that the annihilator is generated by

\{x_{i}^{d_{i}}-x_{j}\ |\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq n\}
,
Mourrain’s algorithm, as well as Berthomieu-Fauger’s,
considers monomials

1,x_{i},\ldots,x_{i}^{d_{i}}
, while AnnihilatorViaDuality considers all
monomials whose partial degree at most

d_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n
, except for
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}
monomials in the basis
for the quotient vector space
{\raisebox{1.02228pt}{$\mathbb{K}[X]$}\left/\raisebox{-1.02228pt}{$\mathrm{ann}(\ell)$}\right.}
.

As mentioned earlier, the algorithms presented by Mora et al. in
[
17
]
have complexity bound
\mathcal{O}(nt^{3}+nft^{2})
, where
t
is the number of given linear maps/sequences and
f
is the cost of evaluation maps on monomials. These algorithms assume that the given sequences span the orthogonal of the annihilator as a subspace of the dual module. As there is a one-to-one correspondence between the generators of the dual vector space of an ideal and the vector space of the quotient of the polynomial ring with respect to that ideal, we have that
t\geq r
. So the complexity bound mentioned above becomes
\mathcal{O}(nr^{3}+nfr^{2})
if
t=r
, and moreover, if we assume that the cost of evaluation at each monomial is a constant, then the complexity bound becomes
\mathcal{O}(nr^{3})
.

One can check that based on our assumptions that the annihilator is
\mathfrak{m}\text{-primary}
and hence
s
first term of the sequence is the non-zero terms, we have that
s\leq t
and therefore, our algorithm is faster than Mora et al.’s algorithm. Our algorithm applied to
t
sequences, where
t
is as in Mora, et al.’s algorithms in
[
17
]
, has complexity bound
\mathcal{O}((t-r)^{3}+nt)
(because in this case having the first
t
elements of the sequences is enough to determine the annihilator), which is faster than Mora et al.’s algorithms.
