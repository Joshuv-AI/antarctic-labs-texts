Seismic performance assessment and strengthening of a residential RC building damaged in the 2023 Türkiye Earthquakes: A case study
Authors: Fatma Ülker Peker, Julide Yuzbasi, Ercan Işık, Fatih Avcil, Aydın Büyüksaraç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent devastating earthquakes have exposed the limitations of conventional seismic design, which primarily ensures life safety but may fall short in preserving post-event functionality and reparability. Particularly in regions with vulnerable building stocks, the socioeconomic consequences of widespread structural damage, including prolonged downtime and reconstruction costs, underscore the urgent need for seismic-resilience-oriented assessment and design strategies. This paper presents a detailed seismic performance assessment of an eight-story reinforced concrete (RC) frame structure damaged during the 6 February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. The building’s existing structural system and material properties were identified through comprehensive in situ investigations. These included foundation excavation, concrete core sampling, and reinforcement inspections carried out in accordance with relevant standards. Nonlinear performance analyses were then conducted to assess the building’s seismic adequacy. The results revealed deficiencies, prompting a retrofitting strategy that adds RC shear walls to improve lateral resistance and achieve resilience-based performance objectives. The study also documents typical damage patterns observed after the earthquakes. In addition to the pushover analysis, nonlinear time-history analyses were conducted to validate the structural response further and provide a comprehensive comparative evaluation. Furthermore, a comparative cost-benefit analysis was conducted to evaluate the financial feasibility of the proposed strengthening strategy against the total reconstruction of the structure. Most notably, it stands as one of the first comprehensive studies conducted after the February 6 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes to propose and implement a detailed retrofitting scheme for a damaged structure, providing valuable insights into strengthening strategies that can guide future engineering practice and policy.Keywords: RC Building, Performance-based design, Shear wall retrofitting, Structural rehabilitation, Earthquake damage assessmentSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The increasing frequency and severity of recent seismic events across the globe have exposed critical limitations in conventional seismic design philosophies, which prioritize life safety while often overlooking long-term functionality and reparability1. While traditional ductile-based design approaches can prevent collapse, they frequently result in significant damage and residual deformations, rendering buildings uninhabitable or economically irreparable2. The socioeconomic consequences of such damage, including extended downtime, reconstruction costs, and disruption to urban systems, are no longer acceptable within the framework of modern societal expectations for resilience and sustainability3. These realities underscore the need for an urgent paradigm shift toward seismic design strategies that not only safeguard lives but also ensure rapid post-earthquake recovery4.

The widespread destruction and varying levels of structural damage observed after earthquakes underline that assessing the seismic capacity of existing buildings and making informed decisions for those with insufficient performance constitute a critical step in the development of earthquake-resilient cities5. This process is a key element of contemporary disaster management measures for existing buildings, ensuring urban planning and the sustainability of social life. Determining the seismic performance of existing structures after devastating earthquakes and making decisions about retrofitting and/or demolition depends on the exhaustive seismic performance. Each study conducted in this context is of vital importance for both academia and practice6–11. By enabling the assessment of structural damage using scientific methods, such studies contribute to the generation of knowledge in the engineering discipline on structural safety, durability analysis, and risk management12,13. Furthermore, the data obtained and the methods developed will play a critical role in ensuring the safety of the building stock, preventing loss of life, and utilizing resources efficiently. Such studies will also directly contribute to the development of social resilience by increasing the effectiveness of post-disaster recovery and reconstruction processes14–23. Similar post-earthquake studies are being carried out in Türkiye, which is located in a geography with high seismic risk.

The February 6, 2023, Kahramanmaraş-centred earthquakes, which impacted a wide region across southeastern Türkiye and northern Syria, resulted in extensive damage and widespread collapse among RC buildings, affecting a substantial share of the urban building stock. The vast destruction and damage to RC structures once again demonstrated that, across a significant portion of this stock, many buildings either lacked proper seismic design, were poorly constructed, or had deteriorated over time, ultimately leading to a marked loss of structural capacity24,İnce, 2024;25–32. In particular, deficiencies in the seismic performance of medium and high-rise RC structures were observed,brittle collapses occurred in the column-beam joint areas, story drifts increased, and serious decreases in horizontal load-carrying capacities occurred27,33–39. Numerous studies have investigated the damage sustained by RC structures across various provinces and earthquake-prone regions, drawing on both field observations and civil and earthquake engineering principles. The primary causes of damage are generally attributed to design and construction deficiencies, substandard material properties, insufficient transverse and longitudinal reinforcement, and structural irregularities27,28,33,34,40–47. In addition to these studies, numerical analyses have been performed on existing earthquake-affected structures as well as on corresponding structural models. For instance, Çelebi and Kırtel48 conducted numerical analyses for an RC structure with ribbed slabs in Gaziantep, one of the provinces affected by the earthquake.49,50 evaluated the seismic performance of a heavily damaged RC structure within the scope of structural inspection.51,52 numerically conducted the effect of filled walls on an RC structure exposed to earthquakes.27–29 evaluated the shear force capacity using a numerical structural model they created, considering the reasons for damage in RC columns after earthquakes, such as insufficient transverse reinforcement and low-strength concrete conditions, and compared the results by reinforcing them with fibre polymers. Altıok et al.53 studied a comparative study of the seismic performance of unreinforced and reinforced school buildings located in an earthquake zone. Bassurucu et al.54 conducted a detailed seismic performance analysis of an existing RC structure in Adıyaman province. Atmaca et al.30 attempted to identify the main causes of damage through numerical analysis for 400 RC buildings located in the earthquake zone. This study, unlike other studies, is based on determining the earthquake performance of an RC structure exposed to an earthquake by taking into account full-scale field tests and measurements, and strengthening it with RC shear walls.

As a result, the massive destruction caused by the earthquakes centered in Kahramanmaraş represents not only structural devastation but also a macroeconomic shock that has left deep and multifaceted marks on the Turkish economy. The total cost of the earthquake is estimated to exceed 100 billion Euros. An examination of the sectoral distribution of these economic losses reveals that the housing sector, accounting for approximately 55% of the damage, constitutes the largest category, clearly highlighting the financial cost of the fragility of the existing RC building stock. The fact that 86.7% of the buildings in the region have RC structural systems indicates that damage to this building stock is at the heart of the economic loss. In particular, the fact that 80% of the buildings destroyed in the earthquake were constructed before 2000 with inadequate engineering services clearly demonstrates the fragility of the existing RC building stock and the critical need for strengthening works in terms of economic sustainability. The approximately €1.6 billion in compensation paid by the Natural Disaster Insurance Institution (DASK) alone for more than 450,000 independent units illustrates the scale of rehabilitation or reconstruction of moderately and severely damaged RC buildings and the extent of technical intervention in this field55–58.

Field investigations and damage analyses conducted after the earthquakes centered in Kahramanmaraş have once again highlighted the need to assess the seismic safety of the existing building stock. Accordingly, improving structures whose seismic performance falls below the levels stipulated in codes through engineering interventions constitutes a critical strategy in constructing disaster-resilient urban spaces. Therefore, strengthening techniques applied to buildings with identified structural defects are considered one of the most effective and sustainable methods for minimizing potential seismic risks by optimizing the dynamic characteristics of existing structures. Strengthening RC structures aims to increase the strength, rigidity, and ductility of the existing structural system under seismic loads, thereby bringing the structure to its targeted performance level. Various strengthening strategies can be employed to enhance structural capacity59–63.

Numerous studies collectively examine advanced retrofit techniques utilizing fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) materials, such as FRP-confined concrete cores, GFRP wrapping, near-surface-mounted GFRP bars combined with engineered cementitious composites, and mechanically pressed CFRP plates, demonstrating their effectiveness in boosting the seismic performance of RC elements28,64–71In addition, there are also commonly preferred strengthening methods such as steel diagonal members in RC structures, RC jacketing, and adding RC shear walls, etc. Through extensive experimental and analytical studies, they demonstrate significant improvements in axial load capacity, seismic performance, and progressive collapse resistance, while highlighting the importance of design considerations such as column size and practical application methods.72–80. The effectiveness of the chosen method directly depends on the existing material quality of the structure and the precision of the technical detailing to be applied (anchors, reinforcement continuity, etc.). One strengthening solution is the addition of RC shear walls to existing structures. RC shear walls stand out among strengthening methods due to their increased stiffness and ductility. Among the techniques, RC shear walls are considered one of the most effective methods for enhancing a structure’s lateral load-carrying capacity. Strengthening, particularly through shear wall additions, provides a new lateral load-carrying mechanism for the structural system and significantly reduces relative inter-story drift. The FEMA-356 and FEMA-440 guidelines also indicate this method as an effective solution. Similarly, studies have indicated that centrally positioning RC shear walls can minimize torsional irregularities. Therefore, it is crucial to ensure that the shear wall layout is appropriate for the building’s geometry and mass distribution81,82. Studies on RC structures using RC shear walls can be found in the literature. Kaltakcı et al.83 discuss a new method for strengthening reinforced concrete buildings, specifically primary schools in Türkiye, using external RC shear walls. Kaltakcı et al.84 compare the effectiveness of internal and external shear wall locations on the earthquake behavior of strengthened weak RC frames. Küçükgöncü and Altun85 investigate the seismic behavior of exterior RC shear walls when used to strengthen both intact and pre-damaged three-dimensional frames under cyclic loads. Dahesh et al.86 explain that shear walls improve the lateral stiffness of RC buildings, which is crucial for controlling story drift against earthquake loading.87,88 investigate a new strengthening alternative for RC structures using exterior shear walls, which allows renovation without disturbing occupants or vacating the building. Zad and Melhem89 investigate the effects of shear wall location in a typical low-rise building to improve earthquake resistance, modeling a 5-story RC structure both with and without shear walls at different locations. Özkul et al.90 investigate the effect of strengthening existing RC frame buildings with shear walls and proper materials by comparing the damage distributions of existing and upgraded structures using nonlinear time history analysis with Van 2011 earthquake data. Altıok et al91 explain that strengthening inadequate RC frames with RC shear walls is one of the most traditional methods used for structures lacking sufficient strength, which is vital for earthquake-resistant building stock. Although these elements provide high rigidity, exhibiting the expected ductile behavior is only possible with full compliance with the confinement quality, reinforcement ratios, and capacity design principles in critical sections. Therefore, considering that shear walls lacking proper detailing carry a risk of brittle fracture under seismic loads, it can be accepted that ductility is not only a geometric characteristic of the shear wall but also a capacity parameter dependent on the precision of technical application92–97.

Motivation of the study

In the aftermath of major earthquakes, the decision to retrofit or demolish existing structures that fail to meet expected seismic performance standards is critically dependent on advanced engineering assessments98. This decision-making process is fundamental to reducing future seismic vulnerability and socio-economic disruption within urban infrastructure systems99. Recent catastrophic events, including the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, have clearly exposed the substantial disconnect between code-compliant seismic performance objectives and public expectations regarding post-earthquake usability and safety. The prevalence of widespread structural damage and significant residual deformations has frequently rendered buildings either uninhabitable or economically unviable for repair. Consequently, these outcomes impose substantial reconstruction costs and long-term functional downtime, which remain incompatible with contemporary resilience and sustainability objectives. This outcome results in substantial reconstruction costs and prolonged functional downtime, outcomes that fall short of the resilience and sustainability goals of contemporary societies. These challenges highlight the pressing need to transition from traditional life safety-oriented seismic design approaches toward resilience-based strategies that minimize damage, reduce recovery time, and ensure continuity of functionality.

Rules regarding the evaluation of existing RC structures, as well as their repair and strengthening, were first detailed in Chapter 7 of the 2007 Earthquake Code in Türkiye (Turkish Earthquake Code100. This code, developed particularly after the 1999 Marmara earthquake, has been a significant development in determining the seismic performance of existing structures and increasing their earthquake resistance101. Earthquake-resistant structural design has made significant progress over the years, both globally and in Türkiye102–106. As a result, the Turkish Building Earthquake Code was updated in 201875, specifically addressing the evaluation, repair, and strengthening of existing structures, taking into account new construction technologies75. However, a substantial portion of the existing building stock still fails to meet these updated requirements, making systematic performance assessment and strengthening an urgent national priority.

Therefore, in this study, a comprehensive seismic performance assessment and strengthening scheme were developed for an existing reinforced concrete (RC) building in accordance with Chapter 15 of TBEC-2018. The investigation focuses on an eight-story RC frame structure in Malatya that sustained moderate damage during the 6 February 2023 earthquakes. Through detailed on-site inspections, material testing, and a comparative evaluation of the original design documents, the actual structural configuration and the extent of damage were accurately established. Based on these findings, a retrofitting strategy incorporating strategically placed reinforced concrete shear walls was proposed to enhance the building’s lateral load-carrying capacity, improve deformation control, and align its seismic performance with current resilience-oriented design principles. To situate the building-specific investigation within the broader regional context, the study also reviews the typical damage mechanisms observed in RC buildings following the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, highlighting the recurring weaknesses that contribute to inadequate seismic performance. The primary aim of the paper is to evaluate, in depth, the seismic response of the selected RC building before and after retrofitting, and to quantify the improvements achieved through the proposed strengthening solution. To this end, a three-dimensional numerical model of the building was developed using data obtained from field measurements and experimental material characterization. Nonlinear analyses were subsequently performed for both the original and strengthened configurations. By systematically comparing these results, the study demonstrates the effectiveness of the shear-wall-based retrofitting scheme and provides evidence-based insights relevant to post-earthquake rehabilitation of similar RC buildings. Overall, the study distinguishes itself by integrating (i) real-scale post-earthquake damage documentation, (ii) code-compliant structural assessment, (iii) an analytically optimized shear-wall strengthening layout, and (iv) a comparative nonlinear performance evaluation. Together, these components contribute to a more robust framework for assessing and rehabilitating earthquake-damaged RC buildings under contemporary performance- and resilience-driven design philosophies.

In this study, a comprehensive analytical methodology incorporating both static and dynamic approaches was employed to evaluate the seismic performance of the investigated structural system. Initially, nonlinear static pushover analyses were conducted to determine the capacity limits and failure mechanisms of the structure. To further validate these results and capture the dynamic response under seismic excitations, nonlinear time-history analyses were performed using a suite of selected earthquake ground motion records. The comparative assessment of these two methods not only enables the validation of the theoretical capacity curves but also provides a more robust evaluation of the structural performance levels under seismic risk.

In structural strengthening projects, comparing reconstruction and strengthening alternatives in terms of cost is critical for effective resource management and sustainable engineering decisions. This study presents a financial comparison between strengthening and reconstruction scenarios, based on estimated 2026 construction unit costs and current construction items; thus, it analyzes the economic feasibility of different intervention methods.

Observed damage and spectral acceleration assessment in rc structures during the kahramanmaraş earthquakes

Türkiye experienced one of the most devastating seismic events in its historical and instrumental history, beginning with a magnitude 7.7 earthquake centred in the Pazarcık district of Kahramanmaraş province on February 6, 2023, and continuing with a second magnitude 7.6 earthquake centred in Elbistan on the same day. These earthquakes, accompanied by consecutive large aftershocks, caused serious structural destruction and loss of life primarily in Hatay, Adıyaman, and Kahramanmaraş as well as in Malatya, Gaziantep, Şanlıurfa, Adana, Osmaniye, Kilis, Elazığ, and Diyarbakır. The magnitude 6.4 and 5.8 earthquakes that occurred in the Defne and Samandağ districts of Hatay province on February 20, 2023, further exacerbated the damage. These earthquakes, which occurred in regions of Türkiye with very high seismic risk, were felt across a wide geography and were described as the disaster of the century. Figure 2 highlights the structural damage commonly observed in earthquake-affected regions, reflecting a range of failure mechanisms. Multi-story buildings often experience pancake collapse, where sequential floor slab failures led to sudden vertical collapse, while inadequate foundation–soil interaction caused differential settlements that undermined overall stability. Progressive collapse, in which the failure of a single element triggers a chain reaction of structural failures, was notably observed during the recent Kahramanmaraş earthquake, resulting in extensive damage and emphasizing the need for robust load redistribution107. Poor connections between structural elements led to detachment and partial failures, compromising the continuity of load paths, whereas weak storeys amplified lateral deformations due to reduced stiffness. Torsional effects induced asymmetric rotations, increasing vulnerability in eccentrically loaded buildings, and soft storeys, typically ground floors with open layouts, experienced excessive drift. Additional damage arose from pounding effects between adjacent buildings and from heavy overhangs, which imposed local bending and shear demands on supporting members. Collectively, these observations underscore the critical importance of structural redundancy, adequate detailing, and careful consideration of local soil and geometric conditions to mitigate earthquake-induced failures. Description of the current RC structure and damage status

The seismic performance assessment of the existing RC structure considered within the scope of the study was carried out according to the procedure specified in Chapter 15 of the TBEC-2018, which was revised in 2018 and entered into force in 2019. According to this chapter, the earthquake performance and decision-making process of any structure is carried out according to the process steps in Fig. 6.

Flow chart for earthquake performance analysis, repair, and strengthening of the existing buildings.

The structure under study is located in Malatya and was constructed in 2012. It was designed in accordance with the 2007 earthquake regulations in effect at the time of construction. The building consists of a total of eight storeys: a basement, a ground storey, and six standard storeys. Commercial businesses occupy the ground storeys, while all of the upper floors are used for residential purposes. The RC structural system of the building measures 25.30 m x 29.60 m in plan, with a height of approximately 27 m. The building has an appropriate width-length-height ratio. Structural elements are arranged regularly in plan. The long edges of the columns and shear walls are distributed appropriately along the X and Y directions of the structure (40%-50%). This design approach coincided with the centres of mass and rigidity, and the structure did not experience additional effects such as torsion as a result of the 2023 Türkiye earthquake. Considering the criteria specified in the current earthquake code (Special Rules for the Assessment and Reinforcement Design of Existing Building Systems Under Earthquake Effects), the design, analysis, and evaluation of the examined building will be conducted to determine whether any reinforcement is required. Accordingly, assuming the building has a limited knowledge base, a survey study was conducted to determine its geometry. Core drilling and stripping were performed in accordance with the "Limited Knowledge Base on RC Buildings" criteria to determine material properties and structural member details. The structure was modelled in the ETABS program based on material information, member details, and building geometry. The structure was analysed using nonlinear calculations. A survey was conducted to assess the damage to the structure following the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes and to identify and implement potential repair and/or reinforcement requirements. A comparison of the approved architectural and structural plans with the survey project revealed some structural changes to the columns, beams, and shear walls. While the approved structural plan designed the basement storey as a perimeter earthquake curtain on all four sides, the survey found that three sides were designed as curtains to suit the basement’s intended use (garage entrance). Furthermore, the approved structural plan showed that the structural elements, designed as two 30x210 cm curtain walls throughout the building, were implemented as T-columns on the basement and ground floors and as 40x210 cm columns on the standard floors. The 30x100 cm columns designated S02 and S07 in the structural plan were designated 30x110 cm in the survey, while the columns designated S12, S15, S26, and S29 were designated 40x110 cm in the survey. Beam dimensions vary across the basement, ground, and standard storeys. According to the survey project, the K03 beam, measuring 25x60 cm, was used in the basement and ground storeys, while the beam, measuring 70x25 cm, was used in the standard storeys. However, according to the approved structural project, the same beam is 25x60 cm on all storeys. According to the approved structural project, there are no beams connecting the elevator bulkheads to the T-columns or the 40x210 column. In contrast, the survey project features four 25x45 cm beams at either end of the columns and bulkheads. One of these beams is 40x45 cm in size on the basement, ground, and first standard storeys, and 25x45 cm on the other standard storeys. The building utilizes 40 columns with varying cross-sectional properties, along with beam elements of 25x45 cm, 40x45 cm, 25x60 cm, and 70x25 cm. There are no shear elements in the building. The structural elements are symmetrical along both axes in the building plan. In this study, the interaction of non-structural infill walls, which have a critical effect on the dynamic and static behavior of the structural frame, with the beams was analytically simulated within the framework of the equivalent distributed load model. In this context, in line with common assumptions in the literature and unit weight calculations, the linearly distributed vertical load value transferred to the beams per unit length by standard brick infill walls with a thickness of 8.5 cm was determined as 5.18 kN/m, and for aerated concrete walls with a thickness of 20 cm, this value was determined as 6.87 kN/m and directly integrated into the structural system. The direct modeling of these loads as distributed loads is based on the theoretical assumption that infill walls transfer gravity loads homogeneously to the structural system before exhibiting compression strut behavior. This approach is a fundamental requirement, especially in linear elastic analyses where the stiffness and mass distribution are accurately modeled, in order to realistically reflect the initial period and dynamic behavior of the structure.

Determination of material properties

The existing material strengths to be used in calculating the capacity of the structural members were defined in accordance with the provisions of the TBEC-2018. To determine the concrete material characteristics of the RC building, which is the subject of the study, core specimens were taken from 12 columns and tested according to the TS EN 12504-1 standard by a laboratory authorized by the Ministry of Environment, Urbanization, and Climate Change within the framework of the regulation principles. The approach used in the evaluation of core samples in the TBEC-2018 was taken into account in determining the concrete compressive strength. The mean and standard deviation values ​​of the samples were calculated, and as a result of statistical calculations, the concrete compressive strength was calculated as 15 MPa. To determine the reinforcement status, a sufficient number of destructive/stripping and non-destructive/X-ray examinations were conducted on the beams, columns, and shear walls of the structure. As a result of the examination, it was determined that the diameters, spacing, and numbers of vertical and horizontal reinforcement were compatible with the static project, and no corrosion was observed. Determination of concrete and reinforcement properties.

It should be emphasized that the material properties used in the analytical model were not arbitrarily assumed but were primarily based on field investigations. The concrete compressive strength value was obtained through statistical evaluation of core test results in accordance with TS EN 12504-1 and TBEC-2018 provisions. Therefore, this value represents an experimentally supported characteristic strength rather than a purely assumed parameter. Similarly, the reinforcement grade was identified through a combination of destructive stripping and non-destructive X-ray investigations, which confirmed compatibility with the original design specifications. Accordingly, the reinforcement was classified as S420 in line with code definitions. These procedures ensure that the adopted material properties reflect actual in-situ conditions while remaining consistent with standardized code-based classifications. Unlike many previous studies relying solely on assumed material properties, this study integrates experimentally obtained data from field testing into the structural modelling process, thereby enhancing the reliability of the seismic performance assessment. For modelling and strengthening purposes, C30 concrete and S420 reinforcement were adopted as representative material classes for RC construction. According to TBEC-2018, concrete grades ranging from C25 to C80 and reinforcement grades such as B420C/S420 are permitted for structural applications. Furthermore, recent studies on seismic assessment and retrofitting of RC buildings in Türkiye commonly employ C30 concrete and 420 MPa reinforcement for newly added or strengthened elements, reflecting current engineering practice. The material safety factors used in this study are based on limit state design principles. The partial safety factor for reinforcement (γs=1.15) and for concrete (γc=1.50) are adopted to account for uncertainties in material strength, construction quality, and modelling assumptions. These values are consistent with international standards such as Eurocode 2 and are compatible with Turkish design practice. The use of partial safety factors ensures that the design strengths (fcd, fyd) are derived from characteristic material properties (fck, fyk) by incorporating reliability-based reductions, thereby providing a consistent safety margin in structural analysis and performance assessment. Design strength values ​​are obtained using equations fcd=fck/γc and fyd= fyk/γs, taking into account strength and material safety values108.

Defining material models

The material models adopted in this study were defined using experimentally obtained material properties combined with code-based constitutive relationships. The concrete compressive strength used in the model was derived from core test results, while reinforcement properties were determined through in-situ inspections. These experimentally obtained parameters were directly incorporated into the material model definitions. It should be noted that, although advanced constitutive models such as the Mander model for confined concrete and the Menegotto–Pinto model for reinforcement are frequently used in the literature, the material models employed in this study are based on the formulations recommended by TBEC-2018. This choice ensures consistency with the code-based performance assessment framework and the nonlinear analysis procedures implemented in ETABS. The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the seismic performance of an existing structure within a code-compliant assessment framework rather than to develop or compare advanced material constitutive models. Therefore, the use of standardized material models is considered appropriate and sufficient for the scope of this study. Nevertheless, the influence of different material models on the global structural response is acknowledged, and future studies may explore the sensitivity of the results to alternative constitutive formulations. Code-based material models are widely preferred in performance assessment studies of existing buildings due to their robustness, simplicity, and compatibility with engineering practice. Behaviour models for columns, beams and shear walls

Columns and beams were modelled using frame finite elements. The nonlinear response of the structural system was represented through concentrated plastic hinge formulations assigned to the critical regions of the members. Beam elements were assigned flexural moment hinges (M3), reflecting their predominantly flexure-controlled behaviour under seismic loading. In contrast, column elements were assigned axial force-biaxial moment interaction hinges (P-M2-M3) to accurately capture the coupled effects of axial load and biaxial flexural response. The hinge properties were defined based on section-specific moment-curvature analyses performed in XTRACT using experimentally obtained material properties and actual reinforcement detailing. In addition to the provisions of TBEC-2018, the adopted nonlinear modelling and performance evaluation framework was evaluated in conjunction with internationally recognized guidelines such as FEMA-356, FEMA-440, and ASCE 41 to ensure methodological consistency.

It should be emphasized that default auto-hinge properties available in ETABS (e.g., ASCE 41-based hinge definitions) were not directly used in this study. Instead, user-defined hinge properties were assigned based on section-specific moment-curvature analyses performed in XTRACT, incorporating actual geometry, reinforcement detailing, and experimentally derived material properties. Concentrated plastic hinge modelling was preferred for beams and columns due to its computational efficiency and its widespread use in code-based seismic performance assessment frameworks. This approach allows for an accurate representation of inelastic behaviour at critical regions while maintaining a practical modelling effort. Although fibre-based distributed plasticity models can capture nonlinear behaviour in greater detail, they require significantly higher computational effort and detailed material calibration. Therefore, within the scope of this study, fibre modelling was selectively applied to shear walls, where distributed nonlinear behaviour is more critical, while beams and columns were modelled using concentrated plastic hinges. The plastic hinge properties assigned to beams and columns were derived from moment-curvature analyses conducted in XTRACT. Time-history analysis and evaluation of results

Time-history analysis is an advanced computational method based on the numerical solution of dynamic equilibrium equations, which are based on the mass, damping, and stiffness properties of a structure, for each step in time under a specific ground acceleration record. This analytical approach allows for the step-by-step monitoring of time-varying nonlinear behaviors such as plastic hinge formation, cracking, and reinforcement yielding in RC structural elements, and enables the determination of the structure’s energy dissipation capacity under actual earthquake effects. Unlike static or spectral analysis methods, this method, which directly includes the frequency content and duration of ground motion in the process and reveals the dynamic response of the structure as a function of time, is considered the most realistic tool in performance-based design and evaluation studies112–116.

In the nonlinear time-history analyses, the acceleration records obtained from Station 4614 during the 6 February 2023 Mw=7.7 Kahramanmaraş earthquake were selected due to their representation of severe near-field seismic demand affecting the Malatya region. The selection criteria focused on capturing the high-intensity, near-field effects observed during the Pazarcık earthquake (Mw=7.7), specifically utilizing data from Station 4614 located near the epicenter. The selected records were not used in their raw form. To align the selected records with the site-specific seismic hazard, a scaling procedure was implemented. In accordance with Section 2.5 of TBEC-2018, the records were scaled to achieve compatibility with the target DD-2 design response spectrum corresponding to the local ZC soil class of the investigated structure. The scaling process was performed using spectral matching procedures implemented within the ETABS environment to ensure that the response spectra of the selected motions satisfied the code-required spectral compatibility over the specified period range. Peak ground acceleration (PGA), spectral acceleration ordinates, and the overall spectral shape were considered as the primary intensity measures during the selection and scaling process. The acceleration-time graph obtained in this way and used in the analyses is shown in Fig. 25. Furthermore, given that Malatya province was directly affected by the earthquake, it was determined that the selected record would have a high probability of representing the seismic demand to which the structure was subjected.

Strengthening design

Post-earthquake assessment results revealed that the structure’s existing structural system lacked sufficient seismic rigidity and was subject to excessive relative storey drifts under horizontal loads. This is a common weakness, particularly in frame-based structures. The structure’s lack of horizontal rigidity elements (shear walls) was identified as one of the primary causes of this problem. Therefore, the addition of RC shear walls was chosen as the most appropriate strengthening method for the structure.

Enhancing seismic resilience of the existing structure through RC shear walls

In the strengthening design of the existing structure, basic engineering principles were followed, and the provisions specified in TBEC-2018 were taken into consideration. First, the existing structure was evaluated with its own characteristics, and the appropriate strengthening method was determined. Then, the determined strengthening method was applied to the existing structure. In evaluating the seismic performance of the strengthened structure, the analysis and design procedures applied to the existing structure were applied75. The strengthening application aims to improve the performance of the existing structure under dynamic loading and to increase the strength of the structural elements to safely carry additional loads. The main factors determining the selection of the method are the contribution of the strengthening method to the safety of the building, its applicability in the field, and its economic nature87,88,51,52,117. TBEC-2018 (15.10. Strengthening of Reinforced Concrete Buildings) categorized and detailed the strengthening methods at the element level and structural system level. Among these strengthening methods, the "Adding Reinforced Concrete Shear Wall in the Frame Plane" method was evaluated and implemented as an economical and compatible method that is compatible with the existing structural system and will increase the performance of the building75,87,88,51,52,117.

Conclusions

RC structures, which constitute a large portion of the existing urban building stock, fail to demonstrate adequate seismic performance for various reasons. Structural damage in the event of a potential earthquake can be minimized by making decisions as quickly as possible regarding the strengthening or demolition of structures lacking adequate seismic performance. The structural damage caused by the Kahramanmaraş earthquake complex on February 6, 2023, which was the disaster of the century for Türkiye, and the resulting large-scale loss of life, has highlighted the seismic performance of existing structures and the importance of future structural improvements. Within the scope of this study, detailed analyses were conducted for the existing mid-rise RC building that was subjected to the Kahramanmaraş earthquake complex on February 6, 2023. As a result of the analysis, reinforced concrete shear walls were used to strengthen the structure, which failed to demonstrate adequate seismic performance without shear walls, and the results were compared.

The February 6, 2023, Kahramanmaraş earthquakes revealed that the seismic performance of the existing RC building stock is not at the anticipated level. Incomplete, defective, and/or complete failure to implement earthquake-resistant design principles during the design and construction phases, along with poor material properties, play a critical role in damage and destruction. In addition, it has been observed that the environmental and mechanical effects to which structures are exposed over time negatively affect the structural capacity of structural elements. RC structures built before 2000 do not meet design criteria consistent with current earthquake regulations, and structural irregularities, inadequate reinforcement ratios, and deficiencies in connection details increase the risk of serious damage to these structures in the event of an earthquake.

The destruction and damage caused by these and similar earthquakes clearly demonstrate the need to evaluate and strengthen the seismic performance of existing RC buildings. To increase the earthquake resistance of structures, the existing condition of the structures must first be examined in detail, and weak points must be identified using performance assessment methods. Strengthening work should be carried out with engineering solutions appropriate to the structure’s structural system. Seismic resistance should be enhanced through methods such as enhancing the strength of RC columns, beams, and shear walls, improving connection details, and adding new structural elements when necessary. This minimizes earthquake-related risks and ensures the sustainability and safety of the existing structure stock. This is one of the most effective measures that can be taken within the scope of modern disaster management for existing structures.

When reinforcing using RC shear walls, the additional reinforcement walls must be adequately anchored to the existing RC structural elements, and foundations must be established to ensure these walls can safely transfer the ground load. It should be noted that this situation, which would otherwise only increase the weight of the structure, would cause the structure to be damaged more easily and rapidly in a potential earthquake. Furthermore, when reinforcing with additional reinforced concrete shear walls, careful attention should be paid to the placement of the walls, and their potential impact on the stability and rigidity of the structural system should be accurately analysed. Irregularities in the structure should be taken into account when using reinforced concrete shear walls. As with other RC structural elements, the concrete quality and reinforcement layout for additional RC shear walls should be designed and implemented according to earthquake-resistant design principles.

This study demonstrates that the use of RC shear walls in an existing RC structure lacking sufficient seismic performance increased the rigidity and seismic performance of the structure under horizontal loads. As a consequence of these structural enhancements, the building’s performance level improved from “Collapse Prevention” to “Life Safety/Better,” clearly indicating that the retrofitting measures effectively fulfilled their intended objective. Therefore, it has been observed that the reduction in horizontal displacement limits will contribute to reducing damage that may occur during an earthquake. The post-strengthening analyses demonstrated a consistent improvement in the structural performance across both analytical methods. The significant enhancement in the capacity curves obtained from the pushover analysis is in agreement with the reduction in peak displacements and the improved plastic hinge distribution observed in the nonlinear time-history analyses. This correlation validates that the strengthening strategy effectively enhances both the static lateral stiffness and the dynamic response of the structure.

When the data obtained in this study are evaluated holistically, it is observed that the proposed strengthening method not only improves the seismic performance of the structure but also provides an economically optimized solution. Structural analyses, conducted through both pushover and nonlinear time-history methods, clearly demonstrate the enhancement in structural capacity and energy dissipation capability. Parallel to these technical improvements, cost analyses have proven that the strengthening expenditure remains significantly lower than the potential costs of heavy damage repair or total reconstruction after a seismic event. Consequently, it is concluded that the implemented strategy ensures structural safety while representing a sustainable and cost-effective engineering approach in terms of efficient resource allocation.

Although the structural analyses in this study were performed assuming fixed-base conditions, the adequacy of the existing foundation system should be carefully evaluated in practical applications. Field investigations revealed that the building is supported by an approximately 80 cm thick raft foundation. However, the addition of RC shear walls introduces additional vertical loads and alters the distribution of internal forces within the structural system. Therefore, it is essential to verify whether the existing foundation system can safely sustain the increased gravity loads and seismic demands. This evaluation should include checks for soil bearing capacity, potential differential settlements, and foundation shear forces. If the existing foundation is found to be inadequate, strengthening measures limited to the superstructure, such as the addition of shear walls, may not be appropriate. In such cases, alternative retrofitting strategies that do not significantly increase foundation demand such as energy dissipation devices or other advanced seismic protection systems should be considered. This consideration is critical to ensure that strengthening interventions improve the overall structural performance without introducing new vulnerabilities at the foundation level.

It should be noted that the validation performed in this study is primarily qualitative, based on code compliance and comparison with observed damage patterns. Future studies may incorporate experimental calibration or soil-structure interaction modelling to further enhance validation accuracy.
