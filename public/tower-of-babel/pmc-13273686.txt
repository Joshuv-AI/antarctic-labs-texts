Neglected McKee fracture of the capitellum in a child: Case report

Introduction

Capitellar fractures of the humerus are an extremely rare situation, accounting for only 1% of all elbow fractures in children [1], [2], neglected ones are even rarer. We present a unique case of a 12-year-old girl with a neglected type IV capitellar fracture, that was left untreated for 7months. The rarity of those fractures explains the lack of a clear consensus regarding optimal treatment options, particularly within the pediatric population. The rarity of these fractures contributes to the lack of a clear consensus regarding optimal treatment, particularly within the pediatric population, this lack of consensus is even more pronounced in neglected cases. Common complications include restricted range of motion, avascular necrosis and elbow osteoarthritis. We examine the impact of delayed treatment on the choice of therapeutic options, the occurrence of complications overtime, and the prognosis of these fractures.

Case report

We report a rare case of a 7-month neglected capitellar fracture in a 12-year-old girl who had a fall from standing height, landing on her outstretched hand with the elbow in extension. Initially the injury was undiagnosed and managed with immobilization in a splint for 3 weeks. The patient's family initially considered the persistent stiffness to be secondary to immobilization and did not seek further medical attention until 7 months after the injury because of persistent limitation of elbow motion. On physical examination, there was no distal neurovascular deficit, with preserved motor and sensory function of the radial, median and ulnar nerves and normal distal perfusion. The girl had an elbow stiffness with an extension deficit of −70° and a flexion limited to 90°, while pronation and supination remained preserved. The Mayo Elbow Performance score (MEPS) was 65. Radiographs of the elbow revealed a fracture of the capitellum, showing a double arc sign suggesting a type IV McKee fracture according to the modified Bryan & Morrey classification, along with callus formation on the anterior humeral metaphysis, indicating the chronic nature of the injury (Fig. 1).

X- ray of the elbow showing the double arc (arrows), and a callus on the anterior aspect of the humerus (star).

The CT scan confirmed a fracture involving the entire capitellum and the lateral ridge of the trochlea (Fig. 2).

3D CT reconstruction of the elbow showing the fracture involving the entire capitellum and the lateral trochlear ridge, B: a sagittal CT section showing the callus formation.

Under general anesthesia, the elbow was stable in varus, valgus, and posterolateral directions. A lateral (Kocher) approach was performed, passing between the anconeus muscle and the extensor carpi ulnaris, preserving the lateral collateral ligament of the elbow. Adequate exposure of the fracture, including the trochlea, was achieved, allowing a successful reduction. Osteosynthesis was performed with two anterior-to-posterior Herbert screws in compression. An anterior capsulectomy was also performed. The elbow was immobilized in a cast for one week, followed by active-assisted rehabilitation initiated immediately after cast removal. After a 12-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant recovery in elbow mobility, with flexion reaching 110° and extension improved to ‐−40° (Fig. 3). The final MEPS was 95, indicating an overall favorable functional outcome. Radiographs showed no signs of avascular necrosis or osteoarthritis (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Capitellar fractures of the humerus are a rare articular fracture in children, accounting only 1% of all elbow fractures. They are even rarer in children under 12 years of age due to the cartilaginous structure of the distal humerus [3], [4]. The frequency of these fractures increases with age as the capitellum grows and ossifies [5]. The primary injury mechanism of these fractures is indirect, typically resulting from a fall on an outstretched hand with the elbow in extension [4]. A direct mechanism involving a flexed elbow has only been described by Chaumien [6].

The deep position of the capitellum within the joint capsule explains the absence of visible deformity in the elbow in a capitellar fracture in children. Instead, the patients present pain, swelling and impaired function of the elbow joint [7].

Several classifications for capitellar fractures have been proposed, both for adults and adolescents, including the pediatric classification proposed by Murthy [1], and the one by Dubberley [8] which best describes comminuted lesions. The most widely used classification is the one by Bryan and Morrey [9], later modified by McKee [10]. This classification divides these fractures into four categories. Type I, or Hahn-Steinthal fracture, is common and involves the capitellum, with or without a small part of the trochlea. Type II, or Kocher-Lorenz fracture, which is rare in children, involves the articular cartilage of the capitellum with a small fragment of subchondral bone. Type III is a comminuted fracture, while Type IV, or McKee fracture, involves the capitellum and a significant portion of the trochlea. Differentiating between Type I and Type IV can be challenging in clinical practice due to the difficulty in assessing the exact extension of the fracture line into the trochlea. McKee et al. described the “double arc” sign on lateral X-Rays, which is characteristic of Type IV fractures, with one arc representing the subchondral bone of the capitellum and the other representing the lateral ridge of the trochlea [10]. A CT scan of the elbow helps to better visualize the internal extension of the fracture, as well as the degree of metaphyseal and posterior condylar comminution [11], [12].

Our patient presented with a 7-month-old neglected capitellum fracture, which to our knowledge, is the first case reported with such a long delay. In similar situations, some authors recommend fragment resection. However, this simple procedure, is contraindicated in pediatric patients due to the increased risk of complications, particularly osteoarthritis, which is more prevalent in this population [13]. Other risks can include joint stiffness and elbow instability [14], [15]. Elbow instability following fragment excision has been refuted by cadaveric studies, which demonstrated that resection of the capitellum does not cause instability when the ulnar collateral ligament remains intact unlike radial head resection [16]. Other complications associated with fragment excision has been described by Mancini, and include cubitus valgus, periarticular ossifications, radioulnar synostoses, and distal radioulnar joint subluxation [17]. In our case, we opted for open reduction and internal fixation of the fragment, due to its solidity, high content of cancellous bone and the fact that it involved almost the entire lateral ridge of the trochlea. Capitellar fractures can also be associated with injuries to the lateral and medial collateral ligaments, necessitating a thorough examination of the elbow under anesthesia to rule out posterolateral, varus, or valgus instability [14], [18].

The vascular supply of the capitellum and lateral trochlea originates from the posterolateral capsular attachment [19], [20], so periosteal dissection in the posterior region should be minimized to avoid any vascularization compromise [21]. The posterior approach for reducing these fractures should also be avoided due to the risk of disturbing the blood supply, which may already be compromised by the fracture displacement [21]. The extended lateral approach of the elbow which is widely used to manage those fractures, allows sufficient exposure of the capitellum by releasing the common extensor origin from the lateral epicondyle [22]. However, while this approach provides adequate visualization of the capitellum, it may be insufficient for exposing fractures extending to the trochlea, making reduction challenging and the perpendicular screw placement difficult, compromising thus the fixation stability [23]. Dubberley et al. have suggested releasing the lateral collateral ligament when the medial ridge of the trochlea is not well exposed through a lateral approach [8], though this increases the risk of lateral collateral ligament laxity and prolongs surgical time due to the need for ligament repair [24]. The anterolateral approach is also described, providing direct access to the fracture and better exposure of the capitellum, and particularly of the trochlea. This allows an anatomical reduction and easier osteosynthesis perpendicular to the fracture line, while avoiding the need to release the common extensor origin required in the lateral approach, thus preventing extensor weakening. This approach is strongly recommended for fractures extending to the trochlea [11]. Its main risk, however, remains the proximity of the neurovascular bundle, with the potential injury of the radial nerve [25].

The arthroscopic approach has also been described, its proponents suggest a lower infection risk, more precise articular fracture control, better associated lesions evaluation, and superior functional outcomes due to the minimal morbidity of the arthroscopic portals [15]. The use of Herbert screws offers solid fixation, compression and biomechanically proven stability of the fracture site [25], allowing thus early rehabilitation initiation. Wiktor et al. utilized screws that biodegrades in vivo for 2–3 years, which avoid the need for subsequent hardware removal surgery [13]. Yu et al. [26] reported a series of 7 patients, aged between 17 and 63 years, who presented with elbow stiffness following a neglected capitellar fracture with 3 to 6 months delay. All patients were treated with a combined internal and external approach, fragment reduction and fixation. The entire anterior and posterior capsules were excised, preserving the two critical ligaments (the anterior band of the medial collateral ligament and lateral ulnar collateral ligament). An articulated external fixator was also applied. After 28 months mean follow-up, the results showed significant improvements in the MEPS, the range of motion, and a consolidation in all patients.

Satisfactory functional outcomes in capitellar fractures are achievable through early diagnosis, precise anatomical reduction, stable fixation, and early initiation of rehabilitation [25], whereas diagnostic delays can be directly associated with poor functional outcomes [27]. The most feared complications are heterotopic ossification, avascular osteonecrosis, elbow stiffness, and degenerative osteoarthritis [27]. Dubberley et al. recommend the use of indomethacin for 3 to 6 weeks postoperatively to control the swelling and delay the development of heterotopic ossification [8]. Avascular osteonecrosis remains the most frequent and concerning complication following reduction and fixation of displaced capitellar fractures, with an incidence ranging from 0 to 30% [1], [22], and the prognostic factors predicting its occurrence remain unclear [1].

In their retrospective study of 37 children and adolescents, Murthy et al. [1] identified 24% of patients that went on to develop symptomatic osteonecrosis. They didn't find any statistically significant correlation between age, skeletal maturity, radiographic appearance, surgical technique, or implant choice and the development of necrosis. McKee reported no cases of osteonecrosis after an average follow-up of 22 months, despite the limited soft tissue attachments to the displaced fracture fragments [10]. In our case, there was no evidence of avascular necrosis of the fragment detached before surgery (7 months delay) and after a 12-month follow-up. This seems to suggest an absence of correlation between the delay in the management and the occurrence of necrosis, although this conclusion requires validation through larger sample sizes. Osteonecrosis, however, may still be inevitable if the vascular supply of the capitellum is compromised, either spontaneously due to fracture displacement [1] or iatrogenically following a posterior approach or extensive subperiosteal dissection on the posterior aspect of the capitellum [21], [22]. Capitellar fractures are among those with the highest risk of leading to elbow osteoarthritis; they are 7.3 times more likely to develop arthritis [28]. Precise anatomical reduction is crucial in preventing osteoarthritis [28]. This complication can be classified using the Broberg and Morrey system [29]. Prolonged immobilization leads to deficits in both extension and supination [13]. Murthy et al. concluded that patients with fractures extending to the medial ridge of the trochlea are more likely to develop stiffness after surgical reduction [1]. Despite anatomical reduction, capsulectomy, short postoperative immobilization, and early initiation of rehabilitation, the limitation of joint range of motion in our patient can be attributed to the delayed management (7 months) as well as the extent of the fracture, which involved almost the entire lateral ridge of the trochlea. However, this delay in management had no impact on the occurrence of necrosis or osteoarthritis in our patient.

Conclusion

Satisfactory functional outcomes in capitellar fractures are achievable with early diagnosis, precise anatomical reduction, stable fixation, and early initiation of rehabilitation. However, delay in management, and fractures extending to the medial ridge of the trochlea, may be directly linked to poor functional outcomes. Capitellar resection should be avoided in children, and reduction with fixation is preferred even when management is delayed. Avascular osteonecrosis is inevitable if the vascular supply to the capitellum is compromised by fracture displacement or iatrogenic intervention. Nonetheless, delay in management do not appear to interfere with the occurrence of necrosis or osteoarthritis in capitellar fractures.
