Sustainable formulations composed of biosurfactants and phytochemicals with potential application in fresh-cut food industry
Authors: Federica Carraturo, Rodolfo Esposito, Marco Masi, Alessio Cimmino, Antonietta Siciliano, Michela Salamone, Noemi Gallucci, Fulvio De Paola, Gaetano De Tommaso, Mauro Iuliano, Marco Guida, Luigi Paduano, Irene Russo Krauss, Gerardino D’Errico
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The demand for minimally processed fruits and vegetables is increasing every year as consumers become aware of the health benefits associated with the consumption of fresh, unprocessed produce. Washing and disinfection steps are critical for ensuring the safety of these products, which are susceptible to microbial contamination and lack any thermal treatment. The most used sanitizers are chlorine-based, yet concerns have been raised regarding their efficacy and safety, due to the formation of toxic disinfection by-products. Thus, with the aim of proposing innovative green and safe alternatives, we have formulated and thoroughly characterized mixtures composed of two natural phytochemicals, either 1,2-O,O’-diacetyllycorine (DAL) or ungeremine (UN), and the biosurfactant rhamnolipids (Rha). We performed an investigation into the ecotoxicological properties of DAL and UN, the results of which are encouraging with regard to their utilisation in washing formulations. The combination of UN and Rha has been shown to enhance the solubility of UN and the micellization of Rha, due to electrostatic interactions between the positively charged UN molecules and the negatively charged Rha molecules. In contrast, DAL exhibited minimal interaction with Rha. Notably neither DAL nor UN modify the surface properties of the biosurfactant necessary for ensuring optimal wetting of food surfaces. As a proof of concept, the washing process of minimally processed fruits and vegetables was closely mimicked using lettuce leaves: we showed that the UN + Rha formulation is highly effective against several food-related pathogens and is more effective than the control hypochlorite solution against the normal microbial load present on lettuce leave; furthermore it does not affect leaf appearance for up to eight days. Our results highlight the importance of a comprehensive physico-chemical characterisation of both isolated components and complete mixtures to facilitate the rational design of sustainable formulations for use in food processing. Moreover, this study indicates that formulations based on biocompounds have the potential to be proposed as effective green washing formulations for the fresh-cut food industry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23367-3.

Introduction

An increased awareness of food-related health issues, coupled with the frenetic modern lifestyle, has resulted in a significant demand for packaged minimally processed fruits and vegetables (MPFV) by consumers seeking fresh, healthy, and ready-to-eat foods1. Consequently, MPFV represent a segment of the food industry that has recently been growing year after year2. Despite being subjected to minimal processing, these products undergo a series of manipulations, including peeling, slicing, chopping, shredding, coring, trimming, mashing, and washing with the objective of producing an edible product that is subsequently packaged and stored under refrigeration3–5. The lack of thermal operations renders microbial contamination a significant concern in the production of MPFV, which necessitates the implementation of effective disinfection strategies to prolong shelf-life,2,5 and ensure the safety of the product for consumers6,7. The incidence of foodborne disease outbreaks associated with these products is constantly increasing due to the presence of several pathogenic bacteria,8–10 such as Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella spp.,11,12 as well as viruses13,14 and protozoa15. Contamination of MPFV can occur at various stages of their production,16–19 from harvesting to distribution,5 and through different media, such as water,20 air, soil, through insect vectors,21 but also through the improper handling by the workers, or maintenance of equipment22,23. Particularly severe contamination can be caused by microbial adhesion to food-contact surfaces during harvest, post-harvest, and packaging, which can ultimately lead to biofilm formation6,24–27.

Several disinfection processes are available for the treatment of MPFV. Depending on the antimicrobial mode of action, they are classified into biological, chemical, and physical methods2,28,29. Furthermore, they are often combined, with the objective of enhancing the efficacy30. The most used disinfection method is a chemical one, and uses chlorine due to its effectiveness against a broad range of microorganisms as well as its low cost5,20,31. However, chlorine is involved in numerous side reactions with the organic matter. These reactions consume part of the sanitiser, leaving less chlorine available for the inactivation of pathogens; consequently, excessive chlorine doses are required to compensate for the loss of chlorine efficacy. Furthermore, this results in an increase in the levels of toxic disinfection by-products (DBPs),30–32 such as trihalomethanes (THMs), chloramines, and haloacetic acids (HAAs), among others33.

Alternative chemical disinfection methods could be based on surfactants. Quaternary ammonium surfactants have been shown to be effective against a wide range of microorganisms, with a particular efficacy against Gram-positive bacteria, but a limited efficacy against spore-forming species5,34. The advantages of these surfactants include their high stability and long shelf-life, their non-corrosive and odourless properties, their ability to remain active over a wide range of pH and temperature, and their capacity to disinfect food-contact surfaces more efficiently than other disinfectants due to their distinctive wetting properties30,34. However, they are not without their drawbacks: they have low activity in hard water; their antimicrobial activity is affected by the presence of organic matter34,35 and, most importantly, they are not approved for direct contact with food36. Moreover, in light of today’s sensitivity to environmental issues, there is a clear recommendation for the replacement of non-biodegradable, non-ecosustainable oil-based molecules, such as quaternary ammonium compounds, with green analogous derived from nature.

All above considered, it is imperative to acknowledge the necessity for environmentally sustainable sanitation strategies that have the potential to serve as viable alternatives for the prevention and control of microorganisms and biofilms in the food industry. In this direction, a promising strategy is based on nature-inspired methods, including the use of phytochemicals. These are secondary metabolites produced by plants that are endowed with antimicrobial properties against various microorganisms, including pathogens37–39. These metabolites are divided into different chemical classes, such as alkaloids, phenylpropanoids, polyketides, and terpenoids, and have already found significant application in the food industry40–43. Although phytochemicals are considered full-fledged green chemicals, they are typically associated with high costs. Consequently, there is a constant effort to discover new compounds that can be obtained in good yields and that are effective at low concentrations.

Among other phytochemicals, lycorine, an abundant alkaloid found in various species of Amaryllidaceae, has emerged for its biological activities, which range from antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory and even anticancer properties44,45. The field of application is further broadened by considering lycorine derivatives,46 such as 1,2-O,O’-diacetyllycorine (DAL, Fig. 1), which is recognised as an effective antibacterial natural product,47,48 or ungeremine (UN, Fig. 1), which was found to be a promising antimicrobial agent,47 active against the food contaminant Penicillium roqueforti.49 It is important to note that UN was found to have no toxic effects on mammalian cells and zebrafish embryos,50 while DAL was found to be less cytotoxic than its deacetylated precursor51. Both DAL and UN are naturally produced in low amounts by various Amaryllidaceae plants, which have been used in traditional herbal medicine for millennia, along with other alkaloids that exhibit a wide range of bioactivities52. Moreover, it is possible to obtain them in large quantities through hemisynthetic approaches, such as acetylation and oxidation of lycorine, respectively.

From a molecular viewpoint, DAL is an uncharged species characterised by a polycyclic structure and belonging to the pyrrolo[de]phenathridine ring-type alkaloids53. Its non-planar structure comprises a single aromatic ring. Moreover, DAL possesses two acetyl groups, which contribute to its lower polarity in comparison to lycorine (Fig. 1)﻿. On the other hand, UN presents a planar system of three conjugated aromatic rings and a permanent positive charge on the tetravalent nitrogen at position 654. Moreover, UN contains an OH group at position 2, which undergoes deprotonation in basic aqueous solutions, resulting in a zwitterionic form stabilised by resonance (Fig. 1)﻿. This is a common feature of the betaine alkaloids53.

Both DAL and UN have been proposed as active ingredients in biosustainable formulations. In particular, UN was formulated in chitosan tripolyphosphate sub-micro particles embedded in a thermoplastic starch-based polymer Mater-Bi (MBi), resulting in a new eco-friendly functional biocomposite for food packaging55. Given their natural origin, bactericidal activity, very low toxicity and, in the case of UN, the already tested applications in the field of food processing and conservation, the potential of these compounds as candidates for reducing microbial cross-contamination of MPFV has been here evaluated.

A potential strategy to enhance the efficacy of phytochemicals in general, and UN and DAL in particular, is to exploit synergistic effects in properly designed combinations of disinfection methodologies. For instance, while maintaining the aforementioned advantages of surfactants, there is a growing interest in exploring more eco- and bio-compatible alternatives. In this regard, secondary metabolites of microorganisms such as bacteria and yeasts that are endowed with surface-active properties, and are defined as biosurfactants, have emerged as a promising solution56–58. Biosurfactants have been shown to exhibit superior properties in comparison to synthetic surfactants, including higher pH and ionic strength tolerance, enhanced surface activity and specific biological properties, including antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and antioxidant activities59. One of the best-known families of biosurfactants is that of rhamnolipids, glycolipids formed by one or two rhamnose units acetylated with up to three long-chain hydroxy fatty acids (Fig. 1﻿)58,60. The properties of rhamnolipids, including high surface and wetting activity,60 robust antibiofilm activity,61–65 and, obviously, biocompatibility and biodegradability, render them promising candidates for the development of novel disinfection strategies for fresh-cut foods66–69.

In this study, a comprehensive physico-chemical characterisation of various formulations comprising rhamnolipids, along with either UN or DAL, is presented. As a proof of concept of the potential of these compounds for use in the fresh-cut food industry, we also evaluated their ecotoxicological impact, bactericidal activity, and effects on the appearance of lettuce leaves. Specifically, the bactericidal activity of these formulations was analysed against the normal microbial load present on lettuce leaves and against selected bacterial strains, including common foodborne pathogens such as the Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes, and the Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium. The analysis was conducted under conditions closely simulating the MPFV washing processes, in particular their very short contact times.

Materials and methods

Materials

A commercial sample of rhamnolipids (a light-brown powder, hereafter referred to as Rha) was purchased from AGAE Technologies (Corvallis, OR, USA). Rha contains 90 wt% rhamnolipids (Fig. 1) and is a biotechnological product that is obtained from P. aeruginosa cultures using vegetable oils as the carbon source. Rhamnolipids obtained under these production conditions have been found to contain 60 wt% mono-saccharides (R1 = H in Fig. 1) and 40 wt% di-saccharides (R1 = rhamnose in Fig. 1); they contain 20 wt% single-tailed species and 80 wt% double-tailed species. The fatty acids were found to be predominantly saturated (70 wt%), with the remaining 30 wt% being mono-unsaturated. The tail length is polydisperse, ranging from C6 to C16, with a dominant presence of C10. The average molar mass is Mw ∼ 530 g mol− 1, whilst the dispersity index is 1.05.70,71 All other reagents used and mentioned below are of analytical grade purity and were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All the sample were prepared using ultrapure deionized water from a Millipore Milli-Q system as solvent. Ungeremine (UN) and 1,2-O,O’-diacetyllycorine (DAL) were obtained by means of a hemisynthetic approach starting from lycorine extracted from Pancratium maritimum, a plant belonging to the Amaryllidaceae family, as detailed below.

Plant material

Bulbs of Pancratium maritimum were obtained from plant material identified and cultivated at the Botanical Garden of Naples (University of Naples Federico II, Naples, Italy) and grown at the Department of Biology of the University of Naples Federico II. A voucher specimen (Barcode NAP0007280) was deposited at NAP (Herbarium Neapolitanum, the Botanical Garden of Naples).

Isolation of lycorine from P. maritimum

Fresh bulbs of P. maritimum (1 kg) were dried at room temperature and then finely ground as previously described72. The resulting powder (120 g) was then macerated overnight at room temperature with 1% H2SO4 (100 mL), after which the suspension was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was adjusted to a pH of 10 by the addition of a 12 N solution of NaOH, after which the mixture was left to stand at room temperature overnight, resulting in the formation of a brown precipitate. The precipitate was separated by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 30 min, dissolved and crystallized from EtOH which resulted in the formation of lycorine (202.3 mg) in the form of white crystals.

Hemisynthesis of 1,2-O,O’-diacetyllycorine (DAL) and ungeremine (UN)

For preparation of DAL, 50 mg of lycorine dissolved in 500 µL of pyridine were treated with 500 µL of acetic anhydride. The reaction was allowed to continue for a period of 24 h at room temperature with constant magnetic stirring. The reaction was stopped by the addition of MeOH and benzene. The azeotrope formed was then evaporated under a nitrogen stream. The residue (52.5 mg) was purified on two preparative TLCs on silica gel (Kieselgel 60, F254, 0.5 mm; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) eluted with CHCl3/IsoPrOH 98:2 yielding DAL (45.3 mg) as a white crystalline powder. The1H NMR spectra of DAL (see Fig. S1), recorded in deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) at 400 MHz on a Bruker (Karlsruhe, Germany) Anova Advance spectrometer, using the same solvent as internal standard, was in agreement with the data previously reported73, as was its specific optical rotation value [α]25D – 26° (c 0.2, CHCl3) ([α]25D – 25.2° (c 0.2, CHCl3) literature value),73 measured on a Jasco P-1010 digital polarimeter (Tokyo, Japan).

In order to prepare UN, lycorine (150 mg) and sublimed SeO2 (99 mg) were dissolved in EtOH (3 mL) at 40 °C. After the precipitation of the selenium metal, the reaction mixture was heated under reflux for a period of 4 h and subsequently filtered. EtOH was evaporated under a nitrogen stream resulting in the formation of a yellow solid, which was dissolved in water and treated with 4 N NaOH. The crude betaine alkaloid was collected by means of centrifugation (10000 rpm for 45 min) and subsequently crystallised from EtOH, resulting in orange needles of UN (100.2 mg). The 1H NMR spectrum of UN (see Fig. S2), recorded in deuterated methanol (CD3OD) using the same instrument and conditions used for DAL, was in agreement with the previously reported data74.

Sample preparation

The samples were generally prepared by weight. The concentration of phytochemicals (either DAL or UN) and/or Rha was optimised for each experimental technique in order to facilitate enhanced detection of the information sought, as elucidated in the Results section. For the purpose of pKa determination, UN (9 mg L− 1) was dissolved in 0.1 M NaCl aqueous solution as an ionic medium. The acidity of the solution was adjusted by the addition of either HCl or NaOH solutions, and the pH was controlled by potentiometric measurements. All the other samples were prepared and analysed in slightly acid conditions in order to simulate the current conditions employed for MPFV washing75. Specifically, all samples were buffered at a pH of 5 by using 0.01 M sodium citrate. For the determination of the molar absorption coefficient, aqueous solutions of DAL and UN were prepared by weighting a known amount of the phytochemical and dissolving it in the buffer solution. For the ecotoxicological tests, buffered solutions of DAL and UN at different phytochemical concentrations, namely 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, 6.4, and 10 mg L− 1, as determined by UV-Vis measurements, were used.

Rha samples were prepared by dissolving a known amount of powder in buffer solution. Samples for the determination of maximum solubility of DAL and UN in 0.01 M citrate buffer at pH 5 in the absence and in the presence of Rha at 0.8 g L− 1 were prepared. To this end, 0.5 mg of either DAL or UN were suspended in 1 mL of the desired medium, after which the samples were left to stand overnight under magnetic stirring. The supernatant was then analysed by UV-Vis spectroscopy. Samples of phytochemicals in Rha solutions for surface tension and fluorescence experiments were prepared by maintaining the DAL or UN concentration fixed at 4 mg L− 1 and varying the Rha concentration from 0 to 1 g L− 1 (for surface tension measurements) and from 0.008 g L− 1 to 0.2 g L− 1 (for fluorescence measurements). Finally, samples for antimicrobial experiments had a fixed concentration of either DAL or UN (4 and 3 mg L− 1, respectively) and Rha (0.1 g L− 1). Such concentrations were chosen because, in the case of DAL and UN, they were reported in the literature as the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) against Flavobacterium columnare,47 while the Rha concentration is the lowest concentration at which an interaction with UN is observed (see below). It corresponds to five times the critical micellar concentration (cmc), thereby ensuring both a significant content of micelles and a reduction of surface tension.

Potentiometric measurements

The pKa of UN, which has a dissociable proton in the OH group at position 2, was determined through spectrophotometric titrations. UV-Vis and fluorescence spectroscopy

The UV-Vis spectra of DAL and UN in buffered aqueous solutions, as well as in the presence of Rha, were recorded using a Jasco V770 spectrophotometer (Cremella, LC, Italy) equipped with a Peltier element for temperature control at (25.00 ± 0.05) °C, using quartz cuvettes with 0.1 cm pathlength (0.2 cm in the case of spectrophotometric titration of UN). Spectra were recorded with a bandwidth of 1 nm in the range 210–320 nm and 200–500 nm and for DAL and UN, respectively.

The fluorescence spectra of DAL and UN in buffered aqueous solutions, and in the presence of Rha, were recorded using a Fluoromax-4 spectrofluorometer from Horiba Scientific (Osaka, Japan) equipped with a Peltier control system at (25.00 ± 0.05) °C, using 1 cm pathlength cuvettes77. In the case of DAL an excitation wavelength of 290 nm was used, and spectra were recorded in the 300–500 nm range. In the case of UN, an excitation wavelength of 360 nm was used, and spectra were recorded in the 400–700 nm range. The excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5 nm in both the cases.

Ecotoxicological tests

The ecotoxicity of DAL and UN was assessed with respect to three reference organisms, Aliivibrio fischeri, Raphidocelis subcapitata, and Daphnia magna. The acute luminescence assay was performed using the A. fischeri (NRRLB-11177, supplied by MicroBioTest, Gent, Belgium) in accordance to ISO 11348-3:200778 and using a Microtox luminometer (Model 500 analyser, New Castle, DE, USA). To provide the required osmotic pressure for the test organism, the assay was conducted using a saline aqueous solution containing 2% NaCl. After activation of A. fischeri by rehydration with a reconstitution solution, the luminescence intensity of the bacteria was measured at 490 nm after a 30 min exposure to the test solutions at 15 °C. A range of phytochemical concentrations from 0.2 to 10 mg L− 1 was tested. The test was performed in triplicate with a control, and the pharmaceutical and toxic effects were quantified as the ratio of the decrease in bacterial light production to the remaining light. The algal growth inhibition assay was performed according to ISO 8692:201279 using R. subcapitata. The algal inoculum (104 cell mL− 1) was added to the test solutions for 72 h, after which the algal toxicity was assessed in three replicates. The samples were then incubated under continuous white fluorescent illumination at 22 ± 4°C, with manual mixing twice daily. Algal growth was measured at 670 nm using a Hach Lange DR 5000 spectrophotometer (Loveland, CO, USA). The inhibition rate in each sample was determined by calculating the difference between the growth rate in the control group and the solutions. The immobilisation test with D. magna was performed according to ISO 6341:201280. Daphnids (less than 24 h old) obtained from a laboratory culture were used (20 organisms for each test solution and control). Four replicates were carried out, and the number of immobilised organisms was determined by stereomicroscopy (Leica EZ4-HD, Wetzlar, Germany) after 48 h of exposure.

Surface tension measurements

The surface tension (γ) of aqueous Rha mixtures in the absence or presence of either DAL or UN, in 0.01 M citrate buffer at pH 5.0, was measured using a Sigma 70 tensiometer (KSV, Stockholm, Sweden) according to the Du Noüy ring method. Successive aliquots of a concentrated buffered Rha solution, previously filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, were added to a vessel containing a known volume of the same buffer solution used to dissolve rhamnolipids. This increased the total Rha concentration progressively. Measurements in the presence of DAL or UN were carried out using the same procedure, but with the phytochemical present at a fixed concentration of 4 mg L- 1 in both solution in the vessel and those added progressively. After each addition of an aliquot, the sample was mixed with a magnetic stirrer and allowed to equilibrate for 5 min before the surface tension was measured.

The critical micellar concentration (cmc) was determined as the concentration at which the two straight lines fitted to the data in the pre-micellar and micellar concentration ranges intersect.

Experimental setup of antimicrobial assays

The bactericidal/antibacterial activity of the DAL + Rha and UN + Rha formulations, as well as of isolated chemical compounds DAL, UN and Rha, was evaluated by analysing their effect on reducing the normal microbial load of lettuce leaves and specific food pathogens. We used fixed concentrations of different molecules, namely DAL and UN 4 at and 3 mg L− 1 respectively, and Rha at 0.1 g L− 1. In an effort to closely replicate the standard washing procedure, the following protocol was established: an aliquot of either raw or contaminated lettuce leaves was treated with the compounds and formulations under analysis for a period of 5 min under constant shaking, followed by two two-minute rinses in 0.5 L of autoclaved Milli-Q® water (Fig. 2). The number of surviving bacteria in each sample was then determined, and the reduction calculated. The experiments were conducted in accordance with UNI EN 1276:2019 (European Standards, 2019), with specific reference to the time-kill test82. A sodium citrate 0.01 M solution buffered at pH 5 was employed as the negative control, while a 30 ppm NaClO solution was used as the positive control. For each washing formulation, 0.5 L samples were prepared by using autoclaved Milli-Q® water and tested in duplicate.

Experimental protocol used for assessment of antibacterial/bactericidal activity of washing formulations on normal microbial community present on lettuce leaves (step 1) and on specific Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens (Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes, and Escherichia coli and Salmonella Typhimurium, respectively).

As mentioned above, we evaluated the effects of DAL, UN, Rha, DAL + Rha and UN + Rha on two distinct microbial communities. Firstly, the effects on the normal microbial community present on unwashed lettuce (step 1 in Fig. 2) were investigated. Secondly, the effects on selected food pathogens (step 2 in Fig. 2) were investigated. In the former case the test was conducted on samples of lettuce procured from a local market. The total bacterial count (TBC) at 30 °C was determined to be 1.8-2.0 × 106 CFU g− 1. In the latter case, lettuce leaves were spiked with four target pathogenic microorganisms at a microbial load of 1.0 × 106 – 1.0 × 107 CFU g− 1. The microorganisms comprised the Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus ATCC®6538 and Listeria monocytogenes ATCC®19115, and the Gram-negative Escherichia coli ATCC®8739 and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC®14028. The lettuce samples were subjected to preliminary microbiological analysis to assess the absence of the pathogenic microorganisms targeted in the assays (Salmonella ssp., Listeria spp., Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli). Subsequent to the verification of the absence of the target microorganisms, the lettuce samples were spiked with the four ATCC bacteria. Single cultures of the four strains were grown according to ISO guidelines for microorganisms: Staphylococcus aureus ATCC®6538 for 24–48 h at 37 ± 1 °C (ISO 6888-1:2021),83 Escherichia coli ATCC®8739 for 18–24 h at 44 ± 1 °C (ISO 16649-2:2001);84 Listeria monocytogenes ATCC®19115 for 24–48 h at 37 ± 1 °C (ISO 11290-2:2017);85 and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC®14028 for 24 ± 3 h at 38 ± 1 °C (ISO 6579-1:2017/Amd 1:2020)86. Bacterial inocula were collected in 50 mL sterile tubes under constant shaking conditions at 200 rpm in 30 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for the bacteria. A spectrophotometer (Hach Lange DR6000) was used to reach an equal quantity of 108 cell mL− 1 microorganisms. The optical density (OD) value of 0.125 was used as a reference point for 109 cell mL− 1 of bacterial load, with the corresponding absorption values measured at 560–570 nm. Each inoculum was subjected to serial ten-fold dilutions until the target concentration of 106 CFU mL− 1 was achieved. The inoculum was then put in contact with 100 g of lettuce in sterile Milli-Q® water, under constant agitation for a period of 24 h. The comparison with the non-spiked negative control permitted the evaluation of the microbial load reduction for each investigated pathogenic microorganism.

The microbiological analyses were carried out in accordance with ISO guidelines specific to each microorganism indicated. Selective and specific agar media were used for S. aureus (Baird Parker Agar Base, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, ISO 6888-1:2021),83 E. coli (Tryptone-bile-glucuronic medium - TBX, VWR International, ISO 16649-2:2001),84 L. monocytogenes (Oxoid™ ONE Broth-Listeria and Oxoid™ Chromogenic Listeria Agar - OCLA, Thermo Scientific™, ISO 11290-2:2017),85 and S. Typhimurium (Buffered peptone water Oxoid™, Rappaport Vassiliadis Enrichment Broth and Salmonella Chromogenic Agar Base, Liofilchem, ISO 6579-1:2017/Amd 1:2020)86.

The survival rate (residual rate) of microorganisms was calculated as 1 CFU g− 1 measured at the end of the experiment, multiplied by 100 and divided by the initial CFU g− 1 value. The die-off rate was calculated as (100 - Residual Rate). Samples were assayed in independent triplicates to validate results. The results reported herein are the average of the assays carried out for each microorganism tested. The data are presented as the mean of the three replicates ± standard error of the mean. The statistical analysis was conducted using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-test correction. The comparison of samples with negative controls was performed by selecting the two-tailed Dunnett’s test: p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are denoted in the diagrams as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001 (XLSTAT 2014.5.03 - Addinsoft 1995–2014, France).

Effects of washing formulations on lettuce leaves

Finally, a qualitative analysis was conducted to assess the effect of the various formulations, namely DAL + Rha, and UN + Rha, as well as of individual components DAL, UN, Rha, on the appearance of lettuce leaves, following a treatment that simulated the washing procedure. Additionally, the effects of a buffered solution were investigated as a control. The procedure entailed the immersion of each leaf in the desired formulation for a period of five minutes, followed by a thorough wash with water. The leaves were then dried and stored in a closed N2-saturated plastic container maintained at a temperature of 4 °C. The lettuce leaves, with five replicates for each formulation, were subjected to a visual inspection over a period of eight days.

Results

Characterization of DAL and UN

As a first step in our research, we determined the solubility of DAL and UN in aqueous solutions. In particular, we focused on slightly acidic solutions (citrate buffer 0.01 M, pH = 5), which are representative of the conditions used for MPFV washing75. In order to define the charge of the UN molecules at this pH, the pKa of its deprotonable OH group was preliminarily evaluated. Moreover, in order to provide a solid basis for potential agricultural use, we investigated the ecotoxicological properties of DAL and UN.

pKa and solubility

The pKa of UN in water determines the molecular charge (Eq. 1) and is expected to affect solubility. Therefore, it was deemed essential to ascertain the pKa value of UN in water as a preliminary step. As stated in the introduction, UN bears a positive charge on the tetravalent nitrogen present at position 6. Upon dissociation of an OH group at position 2, it becomes zwitterionic, presenting an overall neutral charge.

The spectra of UN in acidic and basic solutions exhibit marked differences: in acidic conditions, two peaks at 257 and 358 nm, along with a shoulder at 275 nm, are clearly discernible; in basic conditions, the two peaks red-shift to 259 and 405 nm, the shoulder becomes undetectable, and a new small peak emerges at 314 nm. Three isosbestic points at 295, 325, and 377 nm can be easily identified. These findings indicate that UN undergoes acid dissociation, and that protonated and deprotonated forms are characterised by significantly different adsorption properties.

The experimental data were processed by the Hyperquad program (Protonic Software, Leeds, UK) as described in De Tommaso et al., 2020. Ecotoxicity

In parallel, we investigated whether DAL and UN have a safe ecotoxicological profile that would render them suitable for industrial applications. Three different tests were performed on organisms belonging to different trophic levels, and classified as decomposer, primary producer and primary consumer: (i) a luminescence inhibition test on the bacterium Aliivibrio fischeri, (ii) a growth inhibition test on the alga Raphidocelis subcapitata, (iii) an immobility test on the crustacean Daphnia magna.

The results of these tests are reported in Fig. 4. Daphnia magna was found to be the only bioindicator sensitive to both compounds, with EC50 values of 6.4 mg L− 1 for DAL and 3.2 mg L− 1 for UN. In contrast, Raphidocelis subcapitata and Aliivibrio fischeri showed no significant negative effect at any of the concentrations tested. In fact, the two unicellular organisms even showed bio-stimulation. These findings provide insight into the safe use of DAL and UN in MPFV washing processes.

DAL- and UN-rhamnolipid formulations

Given the limited solubility of DAL and UN in aqueous solution, and with the aim of promoting their use in the treatment of MPFV, we investigated the possibility of incorporating these compounds into Rha-based formulations. The solubility of DAL and UN in Rha micelles was determined by UV-Vis spectroscopy. To rationalise the formulation design, we also checked the effects of the phytochemicals on the aggregation behaviour of Rha using surface tension measurements. Finally, we investigated the solubilization site of DAL and UN in Rha aggregates by spectrofluorimetry.

DAL and UN solubility in Rha micellar solutions

The solubility of DAL and UN in a Rha micellar solution buffered at pH = 5 was determined by the same method used in the absence of biosurfactant. The Rha concentration was adjusted to 0.8 g L− 1 (∼1.5 mM), which corresponds to approximately 20 times the cmc (see below). The solubility of DAL is hardly affected by Rha (solubility ~ 0.02 g L− 1, 54 µM). For UN, however, the addition of Rha causes a significant increase in solubility, with a value of about 0.1 g L− 1 (∼400 µM), which is ten times higher than that measured in buffer solution. These results suggest that the different molecular properties of DAL and UN play an important role in the solubilization in Rha aggregates. In acidic solutions, DAL is a non-ionic substance, whereas UN is positively charged. The pKa values of the Rha carboxyl groups are 4.8 and 5.5 for the monomeric and micellized states, respectively92. This means that at pH = 5, more than half of the monomers and approximately one-third of the micellized Rha molecules are in the dissociated anionic state. Therefore, UN can interact electrostatically with anionic Rha molecules. As discussed in the previous sections, UN has also a more extended aromatic system than DAL. Recent studies have shown that sugar-based surfactants, and Rha in particular, have an excellent ability to solubilise polycyclic aromatic molecules93. This could have a synergistic effect in promoting UN solubilisation in Rha aggregates.

The cmc of rhamnolipids is 0.038 g L− 1 (0.07 mM) and the γcmc is approximately 29 mN m− 1. These values are in good agreement with those reported in the literature for similar pH values60. The presence of DAL at a concentration of 4 mg L− 1 does not significantly alter the Rha cmc, which remains at 0.040 g L− 1. In contrast, UN at 4 mg L− 1 causes a reduction in cmc to 0.021 g L− 1, suggesting that the electrostatic interaction of UN with the charged Rha headgroups favours the supramolecular aggregation of the biosurfactant. Interestingly, the presence of phytochemicals does not affect the ability of Rha to lower the surface tension of water at all.

To investigate the interactions between Rha and phytochemicals further, we recorded the fluorescence spectra of DAL and UN at a fixed concentration of 4 mg L− 1 and in the presence of increasing concentrations of Rha. DAL molecules were excited at λexc = 290 nm and fluorescence spectra were recorded in the 300–550 nm range. The fluorescence spectra are characterised by a single peak, at 326 nm (see ESI, Fig. S3A). An increase in rhamnolipid concentration does not lead to a change in peak position, only a decrease in intensity is observed (see ESI, Fig. S3). For UN an excitation wavelength of 360 nm was used, and fluorescence spectra were recorded in the 400–700 nm range. The fluorescence spectra (Fig. 5B) show a large peak at around 540 nm. Specifically, in the absence of Rha, the peak is found at λmax = 543 nm. The addition of Rha results in no change in the spectral features below a concentration of 0.1 g L− 1 (corresponding to five times the cmc), after which a gradual red-shift occurs, stabilising at λmax = 549 nm (Fig. 5B and C). The maximum fluorescence intensity does not change significantly at low rhamnolipid concentrations, then decreases, and reaches a plateau at the same concentration at which λmax stabilises (Figs. 5B and C).

The red-shift of the maximum emission wavelength indicates that the UN molecules experience a more polar environment94 in the presence of Rha above the cmc than in an aqueous buffered solution alone or in the presence of monomeric rhamnolipids. We can hypothesise a significant interaction between UN and the charged surface of Rha aggregates, which is corroborated by the significant fluorescence quenching observed.

On the other hand, the constant value of Rha cmc in the presence of DAL, the lack of shift of λmax in the DAL fluorescence spectra when the Rha concentration is increased and the unchanged solubility of DAL in the presence of Rha can be interpreted as an overall indication of no or only very weak interactions between DAL and rhamnolipids.

Antimicrobial activity

To prove the concept of the using phytochemical-rhamnolipid formulations for washing MPFV, the antimicrobial activity of the formulations DAL + Rha and UN + Rha, as well as the isolated phytochemicals DAL and UN, and biosurfactant Rha, was evaluated in two different sets of experiments: in a first phase we evaluated the activity against the normal microbial community on the lettuce leaves, in a second phase we focussed on four pathogenic microorganisms of major importance in the food industry (the Gram positive pathogens Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes, and the Gram negative Escherichia coli and Salmonella Typhimurium). In all cases, an experimental protocol was used that mimics the washing conditions in MPFV production (Fig. 2). This means that the effects of different formulations and their ingredients on specific bacterial strains was assessed on lettuce leaves (possibly pre-treated with the selected pathogen) and not in solution. Moreover, very short incubation times were used (5 min). The chosen concentrations correspond to MIC values reported in the literature for an observable bactericidal activity of phytochemicals (4 and 3 mg L- 1 for DAL and UN, respectively)46,47 and the lowest concentration of Rha at which an interaction with UN is observed (0.1 g L- 1). We employed 30 ppm NaClO solutions as a positive control, and 0.010 M sodium citrate buffered solutions at pH = 5 as a negative control. In the first series of experiments, the normal microbial community on lettuce leaves (1.8-2.0 × 106 CFU g- 1) showed a high die-off rate, with UN + Rha (99.38%) even higher than the positive control (96.33%). For DAL and UN, the die-off rate was also approximately 92% (92.28% and 92.54% respectively). Whereas for Rha and the DAL + Rha the die-off rates were lower (85% and 64% respectively) (Fig. 6 and Table S1).

Effect of washing formulations on lettuce leaves

Finally, we qualitatively analysed the effect of the formulations DAL + Rha and UN + Rha, as well as DAL, UN, Rha, and the buffered control solution, on some sensory attributes of the lettuce leaves after a treatment mimicking the washing procedure. It is possible to detect the first signs of food spoilage by taste, smell, texture or colour, and in this exploratory study we have chosen to focus on appearance and smell97. Notably none of the formulations caused any yellowing, darkening, dark spots on the surfaces, blackening at the edges, or other signs of deterioration of the treated lettuce leaves (Figure S4), as well as any unpleasant or noticeable odour, for up to 5 days. On the eighth day, only the lettuce leaves treated with DAL and DAL + Rha showed slight yellowing and darkening at the edges, similar to the leaves treated with buffer solution as a control (Figure S4).

Discussion

Foodborne contaminations can cause hundreds of diseases in humans, and many factors contribute to the occurrence of diseases related to food consumption. The problem is particularly serious with MPFV because of the lack of any thermal treatment that could reduce microbial contamination. In this respect, the washing step is crucial. The most commonly used disinfectants are chlorine-based compounds, which can undergo numerous side reactions with the organic matter, resulting in the generation of toxic disinfection by-products. Alternative disinfection strategies are therefore currently being researched, developed, and tested. Growing consumer interest in natural, organic and vegan products, especially in food,98 is leading to the development of bio-based approaches to food disinfection and preservation, in contrast to the use of synthetic additives, which are often frowned upon and viewed with suspicion because there are several reports of potential health risks from the consumption of some currently used chemical preservatives and additives99,100. Microbial biosurfactants and biopreservatives derived from plants are particularly suitable for these strategies, as they generally have minimal impact on the nutritional quality of the preserved products and allow the flavour, texture and nutritional value of the food to be maintained99,101,102.

In this respect, here we investigated the potential of natural formulations consisting of bactericidal phytochemicals derived from plants of the Amaryllidaceae family, namely either 1,2-O,O’-diacetyllycorine or ungeremine, and the biosurfactant rhamnolipids as washing formulations for MPFV. First, we have analysed the effects of DAL and UN on three reference organisms to evaluate their ecotoxicological properties. We found that only Daphnia magna is sensitive to these compounds, but with EC50 values higher than the concentrations used in the formulations tested, while the results obtained with Raphidocelis subcapitata and Aliivibrio fischeri indicate that DAL and UN do not pose a significant threat to aquatic and plant life. With regard to rhamnolipids, they are known to be less ecotoxic than conventional surfactants as they are characterised by higher EC50 values;70 they are also biodegradable under aerobic, anaerobic and anoxic conditions,103 which means that the reported concentrations of ecotoxicological effect may be less environmentally relevant as Rha can be easily removed in wastewater treatment plants. Therefore, both the phytochemicals DAL and UN and the biosurfactant Rha are natural, environmentally safe compounds that could be exploited in washing formulations.

When mixed, rhamnolipids and phytochemicals can affect each other’s properties, so we have thoroughly investigated the mutual effects of the components of formulations.

We found that neither DAL nor UN affect Rha’s ability to reduce the surface tension of aqueous solutions, a property that is very important for the effectiveness of washing formulations as it determines their wetting capacity. An optimal wetting of the leaves is strongly recommended, especially for lettuce, as the peculiar topological characteristics of its surface protect bacteria from the action of disinfectants and determines a low bacterial reduction immediately after disinfection104. Our results suggest that formulations composed of Rha and phytochemicals should retain the impressive wetting properties of Rha formulations,70 ensuring an optimal coverage of surfaces with different properties.

While the interaction between Rha and the uncharged DAL is negligible, an effective electrostatic interaction of the positively charged UN with the negatively charged headgroup of part of the biosurfactant molecules has the double effect of increasing UN water solubility and at the same time slightly decreasing the cmc of Rha.

Strong interactions between the components of formulations such that observed with UN and Rha can lead to a reduction in biological activity, including bactericidal activity, as the availability of the active ingredients is reduced105. However, this is not the case: both UN and UN + Rha showed high efficacy against Total Bacterial Count on lettuce (i.e., under real conditions), S. aureus ATCC®6538, E. coli ATCC®8739, and S. Typhimurium ATCC®14028. The evidence that Rha not only does not reduce but synergistically increases UN activity may be due to the fact that the UN molecules interact with the surface of the Rha aggregates where they remain fully exposed to the external aqueous medium, as shown by our fluorescence measurements. Overall, all proposed formulations have been shown to be highly effective against TBC and S. aureus and significantly active against S. Typhimurium and E. coli, only L. monocytogenes appears to be resistant, but it should be noted that the die-off rates are much lower than for other bacteria (less than 40%) even in the case of the positive control. Considering the very short contact times in the experimental setup (only 5 min), the high microbial load (107), and the viability of the L. monocytogenes cells, which are in the exponential stage after 24 h of growth, the low activity of the formulations tested is in line with what has been reported in the literature about the difficulty of removing this pathogen106–109.

The Food and Drug Administration has explicitly indicated that disinfection treatments of fresh vegetables should be able to reduce pathogen load without affecting the appearance and sensory properties of the treated produce110. In this regard, we qualitatively analysed the effect of the different formulations on the appearance of the lettuce leaves after a treatment mimicking a washing procedure. None of the treated leaves showed any change in appearance or signs of deterioration, or any unpleasant or perceptible odour for up to five days, while on the eighth day, only the lettuce leaves treated with DAL and DAL + Rha showed slight yellowing and darkening at the edges. In the literature, deterioration of lettuce leaves treated with benzalkonium chloride solutions is associated with the surface activity of this molecule,104 but the washing formulations containing the highly surface-active Rha do not increase damage compared to control solutions or phytochemical solutions. In this respect it is noteworthy that another advantage of natural antimicrobials is that they often exhibit a dual function in that they have both antimicrobial and antioxidant effects,111 as reported in the literature for both biosurfactants and alkaloids59,112. Thus, they can prevent the oxidative deterioration of food, contributing to the overall quality and stability of the product, and maintaining the sensory properties and nutritional content of the food. However, it should always be kept in mind that changes in taste and texture may occur, especially when bio-derived compounds are used at high concentrations113,114. To achieve a balance between effective antimicrobial activity and minimal impact on sensory properties, careful optimisation is required during product development prior to use in the fresh produce industry. Nevertheless, our findings support the use of Rha in MPFV washing formulations to increase solubility of UN, since this biosurfactant improves the wetting properties of the formulation and optimise bactericidal activity without altering food appearance.

Conclusions

As part of a general trend towards natural or bio-based products, driven by the demand of consumers who are increasingly concerned about the quality and safety of the products they use, especially the food they eat, in this paper we have presented the physico-chemical and functional characterisation of natural formulations composed of bactericidal phytochemicals from plants of the Amaryllidaceae family, namely DAL and UN, and the biosurfactant Rha, to be potentially used as washing formulations for the treatment of fresh-cut foods.

None of the components of the tested formulations exhibit significant ecotoxicity that could compromise their use, while among the different washing systems we have identified that containing both UN and Rha as the most promising: the combination of the phytochemical and biosurfactants resulted in improved antibacterial activity against the normal microbial load on lettuce leaves and high activity against the pathogens S. aureus, E. coli, and S. typhimurium. In addition, the UN + Rha formulation did not alter the appearance of treated lettuce leaves for up to eight days. The functionality of this formulation could be attributed to the interaction between UN and Rha, which was revealed by the comprehensive physico-chemical characterisation of the formulation. In particular, the positively charged UN adsorbs on the slightly negative surface of the Rha micelles, where it remains fully exposed and able to exert its activity. At the same time, the remarkable wetting properties of Rha are expected to favour a complete and homogeneous spreading of the formulation on different surfaces, including lettuce leaves.

Overall, our results highlight the benefits of a thorough physico-chemical characterisation of individual ingredients and whole mixtures for the rational design of formulations to be used in different industrial fields and indicate that the synergistic combinations of bio-derived compounds in all-natural formulations could be proposed as green, environmentally friendly, effective washing formulations for MPFV.
