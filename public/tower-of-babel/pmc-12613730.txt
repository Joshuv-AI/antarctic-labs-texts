Do immune system and microbiome–gut–brain axis interactions associate with major depressive disorder?
Authors: Hui Zhao, Larissa Tao, Cheng Tang, Wa Cai, Weidong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading psychiatric disorder with increasing global prevalence, yet its underlying pathogenesis remains inadequately elucidated. Increasing evidence highlights the complex interplay between the immune system, gut microbiota, and their bidirectional crosstalk with the central nervous system. Gut microbiota dysbiosis affects neuroimmune and intestinal immune homeostasis, driving bidirectional peripheral-central immune responses through immune-to-brain and gut-to-brain communication. This process involves impaired intestinal barrier integrity (bacterial translocation), systemic low-grade inflammation, activation of innate immune signaling pathways (e.g., TLR4 and NLRP3 inflammasomes), glial cell activation, neuroinflammation, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction, ultimately leading to neuronal injury and disturbances in mood, cognition, and behavior. Conversely, gut microbiota and their metabolites exert neuroprotective effects through facilitating neurotransmitter synthesis, regulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, and modulating immune response. Collectively, these actions enhance synaptic plasticity, suppress hippocampal neuronal apoptosis, and maintain BBB integrity. Understanding these immune-mediated multidimensional mechanisms not only deepens our understanding of the pathophysiology of MDD but also provides new perspectives for identifying potential biomarkers and developing therapeutic targets.Keywords: Depression, Immune system, Microbiota–gut–brain axis, Interaction

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by persistent anhedonia, low mood, cognitive impairment, and behavioral and social dysfunction, which can escalate to self-harm or suicide in severe cases [1]. MDD affects approximately 350 million individuals globally, with its prevalence rising due to the psychological impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. Simultaneously, broader societal stressors have been steadily rising because of the negative psychological impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and deterioration of the social environment [2]. Recent reports suggest a 27% rise in MDD incidence during the pandemic, with 31% of COVID-19-infected patients reporting depressive symptoms [3, 4]. MDD is 50% more prevalent in women than in men, and is marked by its limited recovery rate and high recurrence risk. It is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [5]. By 2030, it is projected to surpass cardiovascular diseases as the leading global disease burden. Despite the substantial societal and health impact of MDD, its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain poorly understood.

The pathogenesis of MDD involves multifactorial interactions between neural, immune, inflammatory, and microbiota-related factors. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain these mechanisms, including the current mainstream monoamine neurotransmitter hypothesis [6], the immune-inflammatory hypothesis [7], the hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation hypothesis, and the microbiome-gut-brain axis hypothesis [8]. While these models provide valuable perspectives and potential therapeutic targets, their clinical translation remains limited. This is largely due to the heterogeneity of patient populations and the complexity of cross-mechanism interactions, which make it difficult for any single hypothesis to fully explain the pathological features of MDD.

The gut microbiota influence neurodevelopment, cognition, and behavior through the gut-brain axis, which in turn modulates various psychiatric disorders [9, 10]. Its dysbiosis has been identified as a key factor in MDD susceptibility. Evidence shows that MDD is associated with gut microbial alterations characterized by reduced α-diversity, depletion of commensal bacteria, enrichment of potential pathobionts, and a markedly higher prevalence of gastrointestinal disorders compared with healthy individuals [11, 12]. The high comorbidity of gastrointestinal and psychiatric disorders highlights the bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain, referred to as the microbiota–gut–brain (MGB) axis. Recently, the interaction between immune activation and the MGB axis has gained increasing attention in MDD research [13]. This hypothesis suggests that stress responses or gut microbiota dysbiosis induce bidirectional immune modulation and neuroimmune interactions between the peripheral and central nervous systems via the gut-brain axis and pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [14]. Peripheral immune responses triggered by psychological stress, intestinal inflammation, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) leakage activate the central immune responses. Notably, the sterile inflammation induced by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) represents a pivotal component in the immunopathological cascade of MDD [15]. Further research requires more comprehensive and multilevel analyses to drive innovation and improvement in depression treatment strategies effectively.

The review highlights immune–MGB axis interactions and microbiota-mediated central mechanisms as a foundation for developing more effective, multidimensional treatment strategies for MDD.

The microbiota–gut–brain axis hypothesis in MDD

MGB axis is a bidirectional communication network mediated by the nervous, endocrine, immune and HPA axes, primarily involving the central nervous system (CNS), endocrine system, enteric nervous system (ENS), and gut microbiota. Among these, the vagus nerve constitutes a key neural pathway for gut–brain communication. Subdiaphragmatic vagotomy has been shown to exert antidepressant effects by disrupting aberrant gut-derived signaling to the brain [16, 17]. Notably, the vagus nerve is activated by gut microbiota and their metabolites, and its activity is also influenced by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. The HPA axis functions as a stress-responsive negative feedback loop: corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) is released from the hypothalamus, stimulating the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn triggers the adrenal cortex to release glucocorticoids (particularly cortisol) [18]. Current evidence indicates that HPA axis hyperactivity is one of the major pathophysiological mechanisms in MDD, modulated by multi-endocrine interactions involving glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, and estrogens. Early probiotic intervention can effectively normalize this hyperactivated state [19]. The ENS is primarily distributed within the submucosal and myenteric plexuses of the gut and comprises a complex neuronal network consisting of enteric neurons, ganglia, and glial cells, earning it the nickname “the second brain” [20]. Degeneration of neurons within the ENS is considered to be a pathological basis for the gastrointestinal symptoms observed in patients with psychiatric disorders. A recent study utilizing in vivo calcium imaging in the gastric antrum and colon of mice revealed a positive correlation between vagal stimulation frequency and activation of myenteric neurons in the ENS [21]. This finding supports the hypothesis that the ENS regulates vagal signaling to transmit information to the brain. Although the ENS-vagus nerve-brain pathway may be a primary route for gut-brain communication, its exact mechanisms remain unclear (Fig. 2).

The gut microbiota also directly influences mood and cognitive function by regulating the synthesis of neurotransmitters such as glutamate (Glu) and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [22, 23]. In addition, microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) influence cellular stress responses by regulating Histone Deacetylase (HDAC) and HPA axis activity [24]. In terms of immunomodulation, microorganisms like lactobacilli activate immune cells in the colon by upregulating IL-17 [25]. Altered microbiota composition has been consistently reported in MDD patients, characterized by increased abundance of Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, along with decreased levels of Firmicutes [26, 27]. The gut microbiota composition is influenced by various external factors, including stress, diet, and antibiotic use, which can induce changes and promote a “depressive phenotype” [28]. When gut microbiota from MDD patients was transplanted via fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) into germ-free rodent models treated with antibiotics, the animals developed depression-like behaviors and physiological characteristics similar to those seen in MDD patients [29]. Although this model provides a promising approach for studying depression in MDD patients, its safety and reliability require further investigation.

From a mechanistic perspective, MDD is a complex pathological process involving immune activation, neuroinflammation, HPA axis hyperactivity, and gut microbiota dysbiosis. The MGB axis integrates these processes into a unifying framework that helps explain the disorder’s pathophysiological mechanisms.

Immune activation and susceptibility to depression

Immune system activation and the subsequent inflammatory response significantly affect brain function, increasing susceptibility to major depressive disorder (MDD) and reducing responsiveness to antidepressant treatments [30, 31]. Chronic stress can trigger persistent immune activation, sustaining an inflammatory environment in the brain that contributes to the onset and progression of MDD.

Studies have confirmed that a substantial proportion of MDD patients exhibit low-grade immune activation, characterized by increased counts of circulating innate immune cells and elevated levels of peripheral pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP [32, 33]. C-reactive protein (CRP), in particular, is widely regarded as a biomarker of peripheral immune activation. Elevated CRP levels are associated with functional abnormalities in the brain’s reward circuitry and increased glutamate levels, which may underlie anhedonia in MDD. TNF-α has also been implicated in mood disorders through its dual role in activating indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and disrupting tryptophan metabolism. Similarly, abnormalities in the adaptive immune system, particularly T and B cells, also contribute to the pathophysiology of MDD. T cells and B cells infiltrate the brain, producing pro-inflammatory factors in response to specific antigens, which induce neuroimmune toxicity and cause damage to neurons and glial cells [34]. During the acute phase of MDD, there is evidence of Th1 cell polarization, a reduction in Treg cell counts, an increase in CD4+ T cells mediated by IL-16, and subsequent activation of CD8+ effector T cells and B cells [35, 36]. These immune-inflammatory responses are believed to increase the risk of new-onset depression. On this basis, some studies suggest that adjunctive anti-inflammatory therapies may hold promise for alleviating mood disorders in MDD patients [37]. Additionally, sex-related differences in immune responses may also contribute to the higher prevalence of MDD in women. Compared with men, women’s immune systems tend to respond more robustly to immune challenges, such as pathogens and tissue damage [38]. This robust response is also linked to a heightened sense of social disconnection, which could partially explain why the prevalence of MDD in women is approximately twice that in men.

Recent studies have further indicated that low-grade immune activation in MDD patients may be driven by bacterial translocation resulting from intestinal barrier dysfunction, commonly referred to as “leaky gut” [39, 40]. This phenomenon is characterized by elevated levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-reactive IgM and IgA in the serum. Gut microbiota dysbiosis promotes the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens that damage the mucosa, disrupt the mucus layer, and impair intestinal barrier function. This allows gut contents such as bacterial endotoxins (especially LPS), metabolites, and immunoglobulins to leak from the intestinal lumen into the systemic circulation [41]. Once in the bloodstream, LPS from gram-negative bacteria binds to pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4, activating downstream signaling pathways including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK). This cascade promotes the release of inflammatory mediators and initiates systemic inflammation [42]. LPS exposure is widely used as a classic experimental model of neuroinflammation, as intraperitoneal administration directly or indirectly disrupts neuronal activity and neurotransmitter signaling, leading to emotional and cognitive dysfunction [43].

Microbiota-immune interactions and depression

Immune activation encompasses glial cell activation, gut immune dysregulation, and activation of immune pathways. Increasing evidence suggests that immune activation in MDD may be influenced by the gut microbiota, with microbiota-immune interactions playing a central role in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation and psychiatric disorders.

Neuroimmunity

Neuroimmune factors interact with neurons to regulate neuronal development, synaptic function, and neuroplasticity [44]. Recent research indicates that neuroinflammation mediated by glial cell activation is critical for impairing neurogenesis and neuroplasticity of MDD [45].

Microglia (MG), the resident immune cells of the CNS, play key immunosurveillance and defense roles. Their activation occurs primarily through two phenotypes: the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [46]. M1 microglia release large quantities of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and nitric oxide (NO), leading to cytotoxicity and neuronal damage. In contrast, M2 microglia secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, IL-4, and TGF-β, recruit neighboring immune cells, and promote neuronal repair. Transient MG activation initiates the innate immune response, clearing cellular debris and metabolic waste to promote tissue repair upon recognizing inflammatory and stress signals. However, chronic MG activation can lead to persistent neuroinflammation, amplifying and transmitting inflammatory signals through the neuro-immune network to the periphery. MG activation facilitates the development of MDD through various pathways, including the inflammatory cascade mediated by NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation [47], the COX/PGs pathway-mediated reduction in neuronal excitability [48], and neurotoxic metabolites damage via the kynurenine pathway [49]. Although most studies have primarily focused on the pro-inflammatory role of MG in neuroinflammation, some research has highlighted the neuroprotective role of the anti-inflammatory MG phenotype, which promotes neuronal survival and adult neurogenesis [50]. Balancing these opposing phenotypes may be critical for restoring immune homeostasis in MDD.

The gut microbiota are increasingly recognized as key regulators of MG maturation, function, and neurotransmitter receptor expression [51]. In germ-free (GF) mouse models, MG exhibit noticeable deficiencies, consisting of immature cell morphology and altered cell proportions [52]. SCFAs have been identified as central mediators of microbiota–MG interactions: they cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), restore MG density and morphology, and inhibit MG activation, thereby exerting neuroprotective anti-inflammatory effects [53]. Further research is needed to better understand the precise mechanisms by which gut microbiota regulate MG function.

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells, extend their end-feet around nearly all brain capillaries, contributing to BBB integrity, neuronal excitability, and synaptic plasticity. Aquaporin-4 (AQP4), primarily expressed at astrocytic end-feet, regulates brain water and ion homeostasis. AQP4 deficiency impairs glutamate conversion and damages synaptic plasticity [54]. Early studies indicated that one characteristic of MDD is the significant reduction in astrocyte density and the expression of their specific markers [55]. However, more recent research has focused on astrocyte activation, which can be triggered by cellular stress, infection or injury. Inhibiting activated astrocytes alleviates LPS-induced depression-like behaviors, potentially related to the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) release from astrocytes, as elevated BDNF levels further trigger astrocyte activation [56]. BDNF secreted by brain astrocytes binds to the TrkB receptor, activating intracellular signaling pathways and preventing neuronal loss [57]. While astrocytes normally protect neurons by regulating glutamate reuptake, abnormal activation enhances glutamate excitotoxicity, driving depressive pathology [58, 59].

MG-triggered inflammatory signals and neurotoxicity can be sensed and amplified by astrocytes. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in MG is considered to be a critical mechanism triggering astrocyte activation [60]. The NF-κB pathway mediates the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes in MG, promoting the release of IL-1α, TNF-α, and complement component 1q (C1q) through the downstream caspase-1 pathway. These inflammatory factors act as key inducers of astrocyte activation, further exacerbating excitotoxicity. Not only that, IL-6 released by activated MG promotes astrocyte apoptosis and depressive symptoms [61]. Gut microbiota also plays a vital role in regulating the maturation and function of astrocytes and MG [62]. Dietary tryptophan (Trp) metabolites regulate CNS inflammation and neuronal activity in astrocytes by acting on the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [63]. Butyrate and indole-3-propionate (IPA), after being absorbed through the gut, enter the brain and have a positive regulatory effect on astrocyte activity. SCFAs inhibit astrocyte activation by regulating the SGK1/IL-6 signaling pathway [64].

Gut immunity

The gastrointestinal tract and its associated gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) form the largest immune organ in the human body, comprising approximately 70%-80% of all immune cells. Disturbances in intestinal immunity contribute to the activation of low-grade inflammatory responses, subsequently leading to depressive phenotypes [65].

Immune cells in the gut are primarily distributed in the intestinal epithelium and lamina propria, including dendritic cells (DCs), adaptive immune cells (T and B cells), monocytes, mast cells (MCs), and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), which collectively constitute the intestinal immune barrier [66]. Dendritic cells and monocytes embedded in GALT shape the inflammatory microenvironment of the gut by secreting inflammatory factors and chemokines. These inflammatory signals also promote the differentiation of progenitor lymphocytes in mesenteric lymph nodes and Peyer’s patches, thus establishing the first line of innate immune defense [67]. In the lamina propria, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and T helper 17 cells (Th17) work synergistically to maintain immune homeostasis. Dysfunction of these cells induces overexpression of pro-inflammatory factors and chemokines in depressive mouse models, activating gut immune responses [68]. ILCs are engaged in defending against pathogens, modulating inflammation, and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. They also help preserve gut homeostasis by balancing the neuroimmune signaling pathways involving vasoactive intestinal peptide and adrenergic signals in type III ILCs [69].

The intestinal barrier is an important protective mechanism against the abnormal activation of gut immunity. Structurally, it consists of an outer mucus layer and gut microbiota, an intermediate layer of intestinal epithelial cells, and an inner layer of immune cells. However, the integrity of this selective barrier is affected by multiple factors, including psychological stress, environmental changes, inflammatory responses, and gut microbiota dysbiosis [70]. A lack of SCFAs is a significant contributor to impaired gut immunity and disrupted intestinal epithelial integrity [71]. Relevant studies have shown that supplementation with butyrate effectively stimulates mucin secretion, inhibits LPS absorption, and restores intestinal barrier function [72]. Similarly, supplementation with Lactobacillus strains has demonstrated beneficial effects by upregulating tight junction proteins in epithelial cells [73]. Moreover, bile acids can activate specific host receptors to regulate intestinal barrier permeability [74].

Immune pathways

Innate immune responses are predominantly mediated by PRRs, including the NLRP3 inflammasome and TLR4 pathways. As a critical immune sensor, the NLRP3 inflammasome detects and integrates stress-induced DAMPs [75]. Mounting evidence has supported the central role of neuroinflammation in the pathophysiology of MDD, with the NLRP3 inflammasome emerging as a promising therapeutic target for intervention [76–78]. Furthermore, Epigenetic studies have shown that DNA methylation levels of NLRP3 correlate with cortical thickness in the occipital, parietal, temporal, and frontal regions of MDD patients [79]. In fact, NLRP3 inflammasome activation mediates various biological events, consisting of DNA methylation, pyroptosis [80], and autophagy [81]. Within neuroinflammatory environments, MG and astrocytes are the dominant effector cell types responding to NLRP3 inflammasome activation [81]. Knockout of the NLRP3 gene in MG has been shown to protect hippocampal neurogenesis in depressive mice effectively [82]. NLRP3 also modulates microbiota–immune interactions by sensing both microbial- and damage-associated signals and can reshape gut microbiota composition, enriching pro-inflammatory taxa while suppressing anti-inflammatory commensals [83, 84].

Among PRRs, TLR4 has been most extensively studied. TLR4 recognizes bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as well as endogenous DAMPs, thereby regulating neurogenesis, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity. In chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) rats, TLR4 overexpression activates NF-κB, which in turn activates the NLRP3 inflammasome [85]. Selected microRNAs such as miR-21, miR-146, and miR-155 affect MDD-related mechanisms by negatively regulating the TLR signaling pathway [44, 86]. MG TLR4 signaling may also underlie sex-specific vulnerability, with studies showing female mice are more sensitive to TLR4-driven depression-like behaviors than males [87]. More importantly, TLR4 serves as a pivotal sensor in the gut microbiota-brain axis, where it modulates enteric neuronal activity. Deficiency of intestinal TLR4 disrupts gut microbial homeostasis and impairs Paneth cell function, ultimately leading to intestinal barrier dysfunction and bacterial translocation [88].

Immunoinflammation, the gut, communication with the brain

Immune-brain signaling

Neuroimaging studies have revealed associations between peripheral immune markers and alterations in multiple brain regions, suggesting that peripheral immune signals reach the CNS and contribute to psychiatric pathology [89]. Under physiological conditions, the BBB prevents peripheral immune signals from directly entering the brain. However, during BBB disruption or in regions of higher permeability (e.g., circumventricular organs and the choroid plexus), peripheral cytokines infiltrate the brain parenchyma [90]. These molecules modulate neurotransmission and neural circuits involved in mood and cognition. Moreover, circumventricular organs permit pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) to interact with pattern recognition receptors (e.g., TLRs) on immune cells in these regions, activating CNS immune responses [91].

Current research suggests that peripheral immune signals are transmitted to the brain via three primary pathways—neural, humoral, and cellular—each of which induces neuroinflammation in the CNS [92, 93]. (1) Neural pathway: inflammatory cytokines released by peripheral immune cells bind to afferent vagal nerve fibers, activating ascending catecholaminergic fibers or transforming into central cytokine signals. These signals are projected to the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) in the brainstem and further transmitted to higher-order brain regions, such as the amygdala, which regulate neural circuits associated with emotion and behavior. (2) Humoral pathway: bloodborne cytokines enter the CNS through leaky regions of the BBB or via active transport mechanisms mediated by saturable transport molecules on the BBB, thereby affecting neuronal and glial cell function. (3) Cellular pathway: peripheral immune activation transforms MG in the CNS into a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which releases the chemokine CCL2 to recruit peripheral monocytes into brain regions associated with emotion and anxiety, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex. Subsequently, a newly identified fast pathway for immune cells to reach the meninges, the lymphatic pathway of the CNS, has been discovered in the lymphatic system within the brain’s meninges [94]. However, the mechanisms by which cytokines traverse the BBB to access the brain remain controversial and require further investigation.

Repeated or severe stress exposure enhances immune-to-brain signaling through DAMPs, leading to chronic cytokine and chemokine accumulation in both the periphery and CNS. This “sterile inflammation” contributes significantly to MDD pathology [95, 96]. Within the DAMP family, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is an important inflammatory regulator. HMGB1 induces depressive-like behaviors by binding to membrane receptors, such as TLRs and RAGE, to activate downstream inflammatory cascades [97]. HMGB1 also directly impacts hippocampal microglia, activating indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-1 (IDO-1) and disrupting kynurenine/tryptophan metabolism, a mechanism strongly implicated in CUMS-induced depression [98]. Kynurenine further activates the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, modulating the brain’s reward system and emotional regulation.

Bidirectional regulation between the immune system and the brain further complicates this process. The vagus nerve projects to the caudal nucleus tractus solitarius (cNST), forming feedback loops that balance pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory activity [99]. Anti-inflammatory circuits such as the vagus–adrenal axis [100] and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [101] provide physiological counter-regulation. Interestingly, recent evidence shows that the parahippocampal gyrus mediates the relationship between IL-8 and depression severity in men, suggesting that although women are generally more vulnerable to mood disorders, male patients may experience more severe brain damage and symptom expression [102].

Modulating neuroimmune signaling and neural circuitry has thus emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for many diseases. However, identifying and selectively targeting the specific neuronal populations and circuits involved in immune responses remains a major research challenge (Fig. 3).

Gut-brain signaling

The gut communicates with the brain through multiple routes involving microbial metabolites, the vagus nerve, enteroendocrine cells, enterochromaffin (EC) cells, and the mucosal immune system [103].

The vagus nerve extends from the brainstem to the colon, innervating almost the entire gastrointestinal tract [104, 105]. In MDD-related gut-brain signaling, receptors on the vagus nerve are activated by glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secreted by intestinal L-cells and serotonin (5-HT) released by EC cells, which activate glutamatergic neurons and transmit signals to the CNS [106]. Synaptic connections between enteric glial cells and vagal neurons further accelerate and refine signal transmission. Transcutaneous vagus nerve (tVNS) has been demonstrated to be a safe and effective treatment for MDD, with antidepressant efficacy likely mediated by bottom-up modulation of emotional circuits via projections from the NTS to higher-order regions [107, 108]. Further studies have found that Brunner’s glands located in the duodenum may be an important mediator connecting the neural circuitry of the brain to the communication of the gut microbiota [109]. Neurons in the central amygdala activate Brunner’s glands via the vagus nerve, counteracting the negative effects of stress on the gut microbiota. Additionally, 5-HT and ATP, synthesized and released by EC cells serve as pivotal mediators in regulating the gut reflex, transmitting visceral sensory pathway signals [110]. Similarly, alterations in the gut microbiota may also influence the visceral sensory pathways, leading to emotional dysfunctions [111]. A recent animal study demonstrated that the microbial metabolite 4-ethylphenyl sulfate (4EPS) affects the activation and connectivity of specific brain regions by interfering with the maturation and myelination of oligodendrocytes in the brain [112]. SCFAs are also known to stimulate the release of the gut hormones GLP-1, peptide YY, and leptin, which interact with vagal or brain receptors to indirectly influence gut-brain axis function. Probiotic therapy significantly modulates the serotonergic system and BDNF levels in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus, effectively reducing the risk of anxiety and depression [113, 114].

Immunoinflammatory signals transmitted via the gut-brain axis

Gut-Brain signal can be divided into ascending pathways (gut-to-brain) and descending pathways (brain-to-gut). Recent studies have revealed that gut-derived ascending signals modulate immune homeostasis, inflammation sensing, and inflammatory responses in MDD through immune-inflammatory signaling [115–117].

Gut immune cells possess unique migratory abilities, enabling them to enter the bloodstream and migrate to the CNS under specific circumstances. Stress-induced dysbiosis exacerbates this process, with gut-derived inflammatory mediators and microbial antigens crossing a compromised intestinal barrier and the BBB to enter systemic circulation [118, 119]. This process may activate vagal and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, further triggering behavioral changes and central neural circuit remodeling related to MDD and visceral nociception. Intestinal immune factors also act directly on immune cells and neurons in the CNS to regulate neuroimmune homeostasis [120]. It is also well understood that gut-derived antigens stimulate B cells to differentiate into IgA+ plasma cells, which protect against dysbiotic microbiota while also modulating neuroinflammation and spinal cord activity via IL-10-dependent mechanism [121]. Equally noteworthy, local gut inflammation also activates neurons, which release neuropeptides and neurotransmitters to regulate immune cell and epithelial cell function further [122].

As a subtype of enteroendocrine cells, EC cells respond to inflammatory signals by regulating serotonergic (5-HT) sensitive primary sensory nerve fibers through synaptic connections, transmitting signals to the spinal cord [123]. A recent animal study published in Nature demonstrated that activation of EC cells drives visceral hypersensitivity and increases anxiety levels [124]. This mechanism involves serotonergic EC cell-mucosal afferent signaling, which regulates the nociceptive and emotional components of gut-brain functional disorders. Evidence suggests that cortisol concentration is considered a direct reflection of stress and HPA axis activity. Elevated cortisol levels are not only associated with inflammatory responses but also exacerbate gut-brain signaling abnormalities by affecting 5-HT synthesis in EC cells and may even directly damage hippocampal neurons, increasing susceptibility to depression [125, 126]. However, the in-depth mechanisms by which the brain perceives and responds to initial immune-inflammatory signals need to be further explored.

Gut microbiota and its derived metabolites in regulating central antidepressant mechanisms of the gut-brain axis

Previous studies have primarily regarded gut microbiota and its metabolites as potential biomarkers. However, growing evidence is focusing more on their regulatory effects on the gut-brain axis and immune function. Interventions such as probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT exert antidepressant effects through a wide range of mechanisms, including modulation of neurotransmitter receptor synthesis, inhibition of neuroinflammation, enhancement of synaptic plasticity, and maintenance of blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, as well as inhibition of apoptosis in hippocampal neurons (Fig. 4).

Regulation of neurotransmitter synthesis

Neurotransmitters are critical signaling molecules that mediate communication between neurons [127]. The traditional monoamine hypothesis proposes that reduced concentrations of monoamine neurotransmitters in synaptic clefts of the CNS are the central mechanism of MDD [128, 129]. Currently, first-line antidepressants primarily act by inhibiting the reuptake of serotonin (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE), thereby increasing synaptic monoamine levels.

The gut microbiota are capable of synthesizing various important neurotransmitters, including 5-HT, dopamine (DA), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and NE, as well as secreting neuropeptides, bacteriocins, branched-chain amino acids, and SCFAs, which regulate the levels of neurotransmitters and cytokines in the CNS through circulation or gut-brain neural pathways [130]. Although neurotransmitters themselves typically cannot cross the BBB, their precursors can. Certain gut microbes release extracellular vesicles (EVs) that serve as signaling carriers or synthesize enzymes catalyzing neurotransmitter precursors, which cross the BBB and are subsequently converted into active neurotransmitters [131].

Serotonin (5-HT) is primarily synthesized by enterochromaffin cells (ECs) in the gastrointestinal tract. Gut microbes modulate 5-HT receptor expression in the enteric nervous system (ENS) and influence 5-HT reuptake [132, 133]. As a tryptophan-derived monoamine, 5-HT synthesis requires tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH). Spore-forming gut bacteria can upregulate TPH1 in ECs to promote 5-HT synthesis [134]. Probiotics like Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium longum have been shown to exert antidepressant effects by increasing TPH2 activity, enhancing 5-HT levels, and decreasing indoleamine IDO levels in the frontal cortex [135]. In fact, 5-HT can also be directly produced by Lactobacillus and Escherichia coli, or synthesized indirectly under the regulation of SCFAs and secondary bile acids [136].

Remarkably, Lactobacillus not only modulates central GABA expression via the vagus nerve, but also increases DA and NE levels in brain tissues [137]. Due to their unique ability to cross the BBB, SCFAs exert anti-inflammatory and pro-neurogenic effects in the brain, further regulating various neurotransmitter systems, including glutamatergic (Glu), GABAergic, serotonergic (5-HT), dopaminergic (DA), and adrenergic systems. It has been reported that specific gut microbiota (e.g., Bacteroides and Prevotella) influence DA synthesis and function by modulating dopamine transporter (DAT) activity [138]. As research progresses, an increasing number of gut microbes have been identified as participants in neurotransmitter synthesis. GABA is primarily produced by gut microbes such as Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium. DA is mainly synthesized by E. coli, Bifidobacterium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [139, 140]. 5-HT can be produced by Bifidobacterium and Saccharomyces boulardii [141, 142], while NE is released by E. coli.

Taken together, gut microbiota and their metabolites may influence neurotransmitter synthesis processes through direct production of neurotransmitters, regulation of key enzyme activities in metabolic reactions, and modulation of neurotransmitter-related transport proteins. However, the precise details of these mechanisms still require further research and exploration.

Regulation of immunity and suppression of neuroinflammation

Persistent low-grade immune activation is a hallmark of MDD, characterized by aberrant glial activation and neuroinflammation. At the molecular level, SCFAs (particularly butyrate) act as HDAC inhibitors that epigenetically suppress TNF-α production in MG, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation and subsequent neuroinflammation. The bile acid derivative SB140 has been demonstrated to exert anti-neuroinflammatory effects by suppressing MG generation of TNF-α, IL-6, and NO through modulation of the bile acid receptor TGR5, which activates both Protein Kinase B (PKB/Akt) and NF-κB signaling pathways in MG [143, 144]. Exogenous SCFAs supplementation represents a crucial therapeutic strategy for restoring gut microbial homeostasis. Beyond effectively repairing intestinal barrier function and reducing bacterial translocation, SCFAs also reprogram MG immune phenotypes through transcriptional regulation of Ly86 and Aoah genes.

The innate immune receptor TLR4 is expressed at low levels in healthy intestinal epithelial cells. However, when the intestinal epithelial barrier is damaged, LPS ectopically activates hippocampal TLR4 receptors and triggers CNS neuroinflammation [145, 146]. Notably, probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (GMEVs) may effectively counteract this process via paracrine or transcellular pathways [147]. Specific probiotic strains (Bifidobacterium longum, lactobacilli) not only modulate T-cell function, promote Treg differentiation and IL-10 secretion via TLR-dependent pathways [148, 149], but also regulate the activity of intestinal dopaminergic neurons [150]. More interestingly, the probiotic metabolite 3-indole-lactic acid (ILA) was found to exert anti-neuroinflammatory and antidepressant effects by inhibiting the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway through activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling pathway [151, 152]. Microbiome profiling further reveals that increasing the relative abundance of probiotics (e.g., Faecalibaculum, Akkermansia muciniphila) while decreasing colonization by pro-inflammatory genera (e.g., Tuzzerella) ameliorated neuroinflammation and affective behavioral disorders via modulation of bidirectional gut-brain axis communication [153].

Enhancement of neuronal synaptic plasticity

Synaptic plasticity, the dynamic modulation of neuronal responses to changes in neural activity, as well as the resulting synaptic morphological structure and transmission efficiency, is central to cognitive function and is considered the neural basis of MDD. It can be categorized into long-term potentiation (LTP), long-term depression (LTD), and homeostatic scaling [154]. LTP, initially discovered in the hippocampus, has since been documented in multiple brain regions. Studies indicate that synaptic potentiation in hippocampal neurons is critical for achieving antidepressant effects [155]. Although evidence suggests abnormal alterations in the regulation of synaptic plasticity in MDD patients, the specific role of these changes in depression’s pathophysiology and the associated molecular regulatory mechanisms remains insufficiently understood.

C–C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) has been identified as a key factor in the regulation of synaptic formation and memory function. Research shows that muramyl dipeptide (MDP) [156], a gut microbiota-associated molecular pattern, enhances synaptic plasticity by up-regulating CCL5 expression at low concentrations. Interestingly, gut microbiota can also indirectly influence synaptic plasticity by regulating the synthesis and metabolism of neurotransmitters [157], such as GABA, which has been shown to induce synaptic plasticity in both LTP and LTD. Notably, BDNF, a critical protein for synapse formation, stabilization, and remodeling, binds to its receptor TrkB to induce downstream Akt/PI3K pathway phosphorylation, activating CREB to promote neuroprotection and synaptic potentiation. Emerging evidence suggests that prebiotics may enhance synaptic plasticity by activating the BDNF-mediated Akt/PI3K/CREB pathway [158].

The deletion of the G-protein-coupled receptor Gpr35 in intestinal epithelial cells was found to induce depression-like behavior in an indole-3-aldehyde (IAld)-dependent manner. Supplementation with IAld alleviates depressive symptoms in Gpr35-deficient and unpredictable mild stress (UMS) mice, potentially through mechanisms involving enhanced synaptic plasticity and structural integrity in the nucleus accumbens [159]. Autophagy plays a critical role in maintaining synaptic plasticity homeostasis [160]. Research has found that the gut bacterial metabolite homovanillic acid (HVA) inhibits synaptic autophagic death in depressed mice by preventing the excessive degradation of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) and SQSTM1/p62 proteins, thus protecting hippocampal neuronal synaptic plasticity [161]. Furthermore, microbial metabolites and extracellular vesicles boost the expression of synapse-associated proteins, promote axonal growth, and enhance synaptic activity [162]. In the regulation of synaptic function, NMDA and AMPA receptors function as coregulators. It was noted that probiotics may enhance synaptic plasticity by modulating the LTP of these receptors [163]. Additionally, sodium butyrate has been hypothesized to be further involved in synaptic plasticity through epigenetic mechanisms, such as promoting histone acetylation and DNA methylation [164].

These findings underscore the beneficial regulatory effects of gut microbiota and its derivatives on synaptic plasticity. However, some studies have also reported negative effects of gut bacterial metabolites on synaptic plasticity, though the precise mechanisms underlying these effects require further investigation.

Inhibition of hippocampal neuronal apoptosis

Hippocampal neuronal apoptosis is implicated as a critical mechanism in the development of MDD. Obvious features of hippocampal neuronal apoptosis can be observed in depressed rats, which include nuclear consolidation, Nissl body reduction or disappearance, as well as a marked increase in apoptotic neurons in the CA1 and CA3 regions [165].

The BDNF-mediated AKT/PKB signaling pathway is a key regulator of the balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic factors [166, 167]. FMT and SCFAs may achieve their anti-apoptotic effects by activating the BDNF-PI3K/AKT pathway. Consistent with this, propionate was found to inhibit mTOR pathway phosphorylation and activate the AMPK pathway, mitigating hyperactive autophagy and hippocampal neuronal apoptosis [168]. A recent study has found a close relationship between histone acetylation modifications and neuronal apoptosis [169]. Butyrate improves hippocampal neuronal apoptosis in rats by modulating HDAC4 activity. It has also been demonstrated that Ginsenoside Rh3, the main active component of ginseng, reduces hippocampal and prefrontal cortical neuronal apoptosis in depressive mouse models by targeting TPH through the regulation of gut microbiota composition and tryptophan metabolism [170].

The apoptosis-related Caspase family and B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) family represent key therapeutic targets in current research [171]. Probiotics exert their anti-apoptotic effects by modulating Caspase activity. Combined therapy with Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium alleviates LPS-induced apoptosis, potentially through modulating the ratio of pro-apoptotic Bax to anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 from the Bcl-2 protein family [172].

Maintenance of blood–brain barrier integrity

The predominant target organ of MDD is the brain. BBB, as a selective barrier that protects the brain from potentially harmful substances in the peripheral circulation, is critical for the maintenance of CNS homeostasis. Disruption of BBB integrity is associated with the loss of tight junction (TJ) integrity, increased transcytosis, endothelial cell apoptosis, and the degradation of glial limitans proteins [173].

The integrity of the BBB is strictly regulated by junctional complexes, including TJs, adherens junctions (AJs), and gap junctions (GJs). Inflammatory stimuli disrupt these complexes, leading to barrier leakage. Among TJ proteins, occludin and claudin-5 play critical roles in endothelial barrier regulation [174]. Evidence indicates that chronic social stress alters jejunal TJ expression and disrupts BBB integrity in a sex-specific manner [175], thereby promoting the development of depressive-like behavior. Similarly, the “leaky gut” phenomenon can further impair BBB structure and function by inhibiting TJ protein assembly and expression [176]. Notably, Certain gut microbiota show potential to repair the BBB damage. For example, Bacteroides and Alloprevotella have been found to upregulate TJ protein gene expression in the hippocampus, ameliorating BBB damage [177]. Butyrate, a microbial metabolite, can also restore BBB integrity by repairing TJ defects. Furthermore, when fecal microbiota from healthy donors was transplanted into antibiotic-treated germ-free mice, TJ protein expression increased, and BBB integrity was restored [178].

At the molecular level, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) is a key mediator of BBB damage, regulated by the upstream NF-κB signaling pathway. SCFAs suppress NF-κB/MMP-9 signaling, thereby attenuating BBB disruption [179]. The brain endothelium, the primary interface for communication between circulating signals and the brain, is a major component of the BBB. Research indicates that SCFAs induce actin cytoskeletal reorganization in brain endothelial cells and increase the density of TJ proteins, thereby protecting BBB integrity [180]. Beyond that, SCFAs also reduce paracellular permeability to maintain BBB function and structure by regulating transcription, localization of intercellular junction proteins, and degradation of junctional complexes [181]. However, the role of BBB permeability in MDD pathology remains controversial. It is still unclear whether increased BBB permeability is a cause or consequence of depression.

Conclusions

There is no consensus in current research regarding the pathogenesis of MDD. Antidepressants based on single theoretical frameworks often show limited efficacy and fail to account for the shortcomings of traditional treatments, such as withdrawal symptoms, rebound effects, and relapse. This suggests that the intersections among different hypotheses warrant further exploration. Increasing attention has focused on the immune system and the MGB axis, with evidence indicating that interactions between gut microbiota and the immune system are essential for maintaining gut–brain homeostasis. Gut microbiota dysbiosis, immune activation, and neuroinflammation together constitute a critical triad in the pathophysiology of MDD. Although immune activation is strongly linked to depression, the precise external and internal factors leading to abnormal immune activation remain unclear. Psychological stress, early-life adversities, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have all been suggested as inducers of immune activation. However, identifying and categorizing subgroups of MDD patients with similar pathological mechanisms remains a significant challenge. Investigating the interplay between the neuroimmune network and psychological factors is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiology of depression and may provide important insights for the development of personalized and precise intervention strategies.

MG activation is considered a pivotal link in the bidirectional regulation between the periphery and the central nervous system, acting as a bridge between the immune system and the gut microbiome. These interactions influence the onset, progression, and resolution of MDD, but the mechanisms initiating neuroinflammation remain poorly defined. Immune-brain communication mechanisms and the neuroimmune network are currently central topics in MDD research. Although certain signaling pathways and key molecules have been explored, precisely delineating their interactions and roles in MDD remains a significant challenge in contemporary scientific research. Future research should focus on developing new experimental models and techniques to precisely capture the dynamic process of immune-brain signal transduction with higher spatial and temporal resolution.

Gut-brain communication remains an intriguing and complex area of research, especially regarding the potential of gut microbiota in treating mental disorders. The gut microbiota exerts antidepressant effects through multiple mechanisms, including promoting neurotransmitter synthesis, modulating neuroinflammation, enhancing synaptic plasticity, inhibiting hippocampal neuronal apoptosis, and maintaining the integrity of the blood–brain barrier. These multi-target, multi-layered mechanisms reveal the complex dynamic role of gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of MDD. Despite the potential of microbiome interventions in treating MDD, the research is still in its early stages. The complexity of the microbiota ecosystem—where pathogenic, symbiotic, and opportunistic species interact—makes it difficult to pinpoint dominant contributors to MDD. Moreover, gut microbial diversity in MDD patients is shaped by geography, diet, medications, pollutants, and psychological distress, complicating reproducibility. Due to limitations in experimental models and techniques, researchers have yet to precisely identify the microbiota populations that play a dominant role in the specific antidepressant effects. Besides, most studies have focused on animal models, with few clinical trials. Given the significant differences in gut microbiome composition between humans and animal models, the latter may not fully represent clinical manifestations. To improve the reproducibility and clinical relevance of research, future studies should focus on standardizing animal models. Additionally, integrating brain imaging technologies with microbiome research will provide a more comprehensive exploration of the gut-brain axis and its specific mechanisms, thereby laying the foundation for early diagnosis, precise treatment, and personalized intervention strategies for MDD.

The gut microbiota interacts with the immune system through metabolic products and immune regulation mechanisms, influencing central nervous system function. Based on the immune-gut microbiota crosstalk, various interventions have been developed and shown significant therapeutic potential. SCFAs supplements, probiotics, polyamines, vitamin D, and AhR agonists are promising for alleviating neuroinflammation, offering new breakthroughs for the prevention and treatment of depression. Diet is one of the most critical modifiable factors in the gut microbiota ecosystem. Personalized dietary interventions (e.g., high-fiber diets and the Mediterranean diet) can increase SCFAs, promote probiotics, inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria, and indirectly regulate immune-gut microbiota crosstalk to improve neurological function. Drug development targeting specific metabolites (e.g., indoles) may also be an effective strategy for treating MDD. In addition, the regulatory function of epigenetic mechanisms (e.g., DNA methylation, histone modification) in the gut microbiota-immune system interaction provides new targets for therapeutic intervention. Future research should further explore precision treatment strategies based on immune-microbiota crosstalk, using multi-omics techniques (e.g., metagenomics and metabolomics) to uncover molecular mechanisms, and conduct large-scale clinical trials to validate the safety of interventions.

The ideal treatment for depression may lie in resetting the balance between the immune system and the microbiota, with stress as a central regulatory factor. Elucidating these mechanisms will aid in classifying depression subtypes, advancing personalized treatment, and developing novel therapeutic strategies. In conclusion, targeting immune-gut microbiota crosstalk holds the potential to enhance the efficacy of traditional antidepressants, improve prognosis, reduce relapse risks, and prevent chronic progression of MDD.
