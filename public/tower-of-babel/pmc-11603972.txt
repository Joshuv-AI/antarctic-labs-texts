A comprehensive experience-based framework of actionable priorities to enhance Canada’s health and social care system: discussions with older persons, clinicians, managers, and decision-makers using nominal group technique
Authors: Deniz Cetin-Sahin, Marwa Ilali, Alexandra Lemay-Compagnat, Geneviève Arsenault-Lapierre, Clara Bolster-Foucault, Amélie Quesnel-Vallée, Isabelle Vedel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting frameworks of older persons’ experiences in health and social care systems are usually based on single stakeholder perspectives and focused on limited aspects that are independent from one another. This study aimed to describe such experiences from the perspective of diverse stakeholders and develop a comprehensive experience-based framework for actionable priorities to enhance Canada’s health and social care system.MethodsWe adapted a nominal group technique with a qualitative approach. We formed four groups of stakeholders (4 older persons, 7 clinicians, 5 managers, 4 decision-makers) in the province of Quebec. First, participants individually completed a questionnaire to rank the six domains of an international set of metrics of quality of care for older persons in terms of their importance. Then, we conducted a remote discussion with each stakeholder group, obtained consensus on the priority domains, and explored personal/professional experiences explaining prioritization processes and what changes are needed. An inductive-deductive thematic analysis was conducted to develop a framework for actionable health and social care priorities.ResultsThe framework included six key categories revolving around a core category of ‘health equity’. (1) ‘Access to services and care’ focused on improved accessibility of healthcare navigators, primary and home care services, and end-of life care. (2) ‘Respect for older persons’ involved individuality and uniqueness, daily needs, advocacy in defence of vulnerability, and decision-making valuing care preferences. (3) ‘Involvement of family and friends’ encompassed formal recognition, responsibilities, and trajectories associated with caregiving. (4) ‘Communication and education’ consisted of adaptive communication between older persons, caregivers, and professionals and societal-level education and awareness-raising. (5) ‘Coordination and integration of care’ comprised health and social services organization, cross-sectoral approaches, and interdisciplinary collaboration. (6) ‘Responsiveness to evolving needs’ of older persons involved timely response to health transitions and promoting holistic approaches.ConclusionsThis study illustrates the complex challenges of caring for older persons. The framework suggests several areas for improvement in care practices and policies for older persons and caregivers. Proposed intervention targets include public healthcare system navigators, standardized goals of care practices respecting persons’ wishes, formal recognition of caregiver contributions, society-level education, cross-sectoral initiatives, and holistic approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05561-6.

Background

It is expected that one in six people in the world will be aged 60 years or over by 2030 [1]. Having a vision of population-based responsibility, public healthcare systems in many countries, including Canada, are integrating health, social, and public health services [2]. This integration offers the potential to create substantial value and continuity of care for older persons who often have multiple or complex health problems [3]. Patient-reported outcome measures are often used to assess the quality of integrated care and services, but emphasis on lived experiences is increasing [4].

Lived experiences are defined as personal knowledge of the world gained through direct participation and involvement in the event or phenomenon (immediate, situated and daily, lived without thinking about it) [5]. They reflect real-world situations based on first-person perspective narratives and help set priorities to ensure that the needs of people with particular conditions or those who are involved in their care are being met adequately [6]. For this reason, an in-depth understanding of experiences from diverse perspectives could better inform practice and policy in the context of integrated health and social care [7].

Several framework studies investigated older persons’ experiences with the health and social care system focusing on limited aspects independently from one another. Some studies investigated experiences with specific medical or social situations such as cancer [8], social exclusion [9], occupation [10], ethnicity [11, 12], or gender [13]. Frameworks on the role of family and friend caregivers [14] and health and social services [15–19] were also areas of interest. Another framework focused on creating environments and systems that promote healthy aging [20]. Although such frameworks for older persons’ care provide useful insights for particular settings, a broader, more comprehensive understanding of stakeholders’ experiences is crucial for informing multidimensional policy reforms and identifying intervention targets to improve health and social care for the growing population aged 65 and older.

The purpose of this paper is to describe lived experiences among diverse groups of stakeholders (i.e., older persons, clinicians, managers, decision-makers) in the province of Quebec, Canada, with a view to developing a framework for actionable priorities in the health and social care for older persons. It is meant to inform policy decisions about the design and financing of reforms and interventions for older persons.

Methods

We adapted a nominal group technique with a qualitative approach [21]. The Research Ethics Office (Institutional Review Board) of McGill University approved this study (A11-B63-19 A). We followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research: A Synthesis of Recommendations (SRQR) [22]. The details of our adapted nominal group technique were reported previously [23].

Participants

We used purposive and snowball sampling [24] aiming for maximum variation of experiences within both community and nursing home settings. Participants of different ages, genders, education backgrounds, and geographic locations were sought through our research network. We formed the following four relatively homogenous groups of stakeholders: (1) older persons (aged 65+) including caregivers, (2) clinicians (e.g., physicians, nurses, pharmacists), (3) managers (e.g., directors of the home care and nursing home programs, managers in regional health organizations), and (4) decision-makers (e.g., Ministry of Health and Social Services, directors of regional health organizations, collaborators). A letter introducing the team and study objectives written by the senior author (IV) was shared with our research collaborators who used it to invite potentially eligible participants. Agreeing participants were sent a link for the online consent form and study materials.

Data collection and group discussions

We chose the nominal group technique, a structured process allowing participants to actively provide, define, rank, and prioritize ideas on the topic approached in a search for consensus [21]. Prior to each group discussion, participants completed an individual pre-elicitation activity that included viewing a presentation (video and PowerPoint) explaining a set of care domains and indicators and completing a brief questionnaire to rank care domains based on their perceived importance in order to be prioritized in future interventions. Care domains and indicators were taken from the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) set of patient-centered outcome measures for the general older population [25].

This ICHOM set was created by an international working group comprising 31 members from 11 countries (patient representatives, measurement experts, and clinical, social, and psychological researchers) following a consensus-driven modified Delphi technique [25]. The ICHOM set of patient-centered outcome measures for older persons includes the following six quality of care domains and associated indicators: (1) disutility of care (two indicators: polypharmacy, falls); (2) symptoms, functioning, and quality of care (five indicators: loneliness and isolation, activities of daily living, pain, mood and emotional health, autonomy and control); (3) care (one indicator: care burden); (4) healthcare responsiveness (one indicator: participation and decision-making); (5) clinical status (three indicators: frailty, time spent in hospital, overall and cause-specific survival); and (6) quality of death (one indicator: place of death) [25]. As part of our study, we collected experience-related data that sought to identify the domains prioritized by stakeholders and additional indicators important for the Quebec context.

We held a remote group discussion (Zoom) with each stakeholder group (four discussions in total). Discussions were conducted in French as Quebec healthcare settings and the Ministry of Health are primarily francophone places where employees work in French. The results of ranking data were presented at each group meeting to build a consensus [21] on priority ranking and to trigger an in-depth exploration of lived experiences regarding prioritization. Two senior co-authors, a public health physician (IV) and a social epidemiologist (AQV), facilitated group discussions. Other three team members (DCS, GAL, CBF) managed the logistics and took notes during and after discussions. A semi-structured guide was used to conduct the discussions in order to allow participants to get to know each other, to reflect on the results of the individual pre-elicitation activity, to tell a personal/professional experience explaining their prioritization process, to interact with each other, and to confirm the consensus (see Supplementary file). We did not ask participants to restrict their perspective to their stake (e.g., older person, clinician, manager, decision-maker). For example, clinician or decision-maker participants were able to talk about their caregiving experiences related to their own parents.

The guide was adapted to each group and reviewed by the team members prior to each recorded discussion. The duration of the group discussions was tailored to the needs of each stakeholder group. For example, older persons were given two hours to allow them to express themselves. Discussions with clinicians and managers lasted one hour and half while the discussion with decision-makers lasted one hour given their time constraints.

Qualitative analyses

Recordings of the group discussions were transcribed professionally (intelligent verbatim) while participants were de-identified. The transcripts were reviewed for accuracy. NVivo 12 qualitative data analysis software was used for data management [26]. The second and third authors performed a thematic analysis using the three phases of qualitative data analysis [27].

Phase 1 (understanding the data) involved inductive analysis through open coding to identify emerging ideas in the data [27]. The two authors independently coded the first group discussion and developed an initial codebook. Phase 2 (interpreting the data) involved inductive analysis and a constant comparative method within and between the group discussions to identify patterns and develop themes (categories) [27]. The coders iteratively analyzed the other three group discussion transcripts while remaining open to new ideas that were not present in the initial codebook, compared their codes, and revised the codebook through deliberations. Disagreements between the two coders were resolved via discussions, and, if needed, consultations with the first author. Phase 3 (explaining the data and developing the framework) involved linking the emerging categories (inductive analysis) with a prior theoretical framework (deductive analysis) [27]. For this deductive reasoning, we selected the National Health Services’ patient experience framework, composed of key themes of the Care Quality Commission, enabling healthcare professionals to improve the patient experience [28].

The ICHOM is a person-centered set of outcome measures and thus was used only as a pre-elicitation method to gather information on the priority areas and additional indicators important for the Quebec context. This information was then used to initiate discussions enriched by lived experiences. The National Health Services’ patient experience framework, however, was used at the stage of analysis to reorganize and define the key categories that emerged from the inductive analysis. At this stage of the analysis, the same two researchers organized sub-categories and defined the categories. The first author independently checked the accuracy and consistency of the identified codes. Organized categories were discussed with other co-authors, and the framework was finalized. Selected excerpts were translated into English to illustrate the key categories.

Results

Sample characteristics

Among 62 eligible participants to whom the study link was sent, twenty (14 women) consented, completed the questionnaire, and participated in a group discussion (4 older persons, 7 clinicians, 5 managers, and 4 decision-makers). The mean age was 53, ranging from 33 to 74 years. Participants represented a wide variety of regions and contexts. Many participants opted to share their experiences as informal caregivers, in addition to their experience linked with their stake (clinicians, manager, decision-maker, or older person). Access to services and care

This category involves access to healthcare services, providers, or facilities meeting the needs of older persons. Participants discussed the importance of the role of healthcare navigators for older persons and challenges with accessing primary and home care services and end-of-life care.

Increasing accessibility of healthcare navigators

According to participants, system navigators supporting older persons and their caregivers and orientating them towards appropriate resources were not always accessible. Participants had to assume that role by having several interactions with the local community services center social workers or guardianship services to help their neighbours or friends who experienced cognitive problems but lacked personal resources. The access to a system navigator for every older person was perceived to be crucial, particularly when they received a dementia diagnosis.

Going from one to the other, one person says one thing, another person says another thing, we put the pharmacist in there. It gets really complicated for them to navigate. So, does our healthcare system still have fluidity and coherence between its various players? I think there’s still a lot of work to be done.(Decision-maker 3).What I find hard to understand is when it comes to diagnosis. When a senior is diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, why are they not referred to either a nurse navigator or a social worker so that this person schedules a meeting with family members? (Older person 3).

Advancing primary and home care services

Accessible primary care was perceived as a fundamental determinant of health and well-being. Not only was having a family doctor critical, but so was being able to see them when needed. Home care and community services helped persons live independently in the community, yet access was an important challenge. Although patients sometimes were told that certain services would be provided upon discharge from hospital, these services were not put in place on time, which potentially eroded trust in the healthcare system.

We’ve lost home-based doctors. I took over the home service in 2019 because the doctors had deserted. Young people were under enormous pressure to get volume, and it is not at home that we get volume. So, I do not think it is the fault of the doctors alone. It is because we were obliged to go and look after other clienteles, including the least sick people in society…We send our social workers to the hospital to sort out leave [to community], but while they are there, they are not out in the field preventing a situation. (Manager 3).

Improving end-of-life care

Timely access to specialized palliative care services held profound significance for improving end-of-life experiences. One participant appreciated the enhanced support while waiting for a bed in a nursing home for appropriate care.

My mother was near the end of her life last summer. She was in a senior residence, in great loss of autonomy after the pandemic. She was still able to make her decisions and did not want to go into a hospice. The residence was no longer able to keep her…The local community services center team enhanced their support to the residence and to us, the family. We have the advantage of being seven children. We all put in a bit and managed to extend the residence for six weeks…Then about a month later, listen, a beautiful story because we had access to a palliative care room with a superb nursing home team (Decision-maker 4).

Respect for older persons

This category encompasses respecting older persons’ preferences and needs so that they can fully exercise their autonomy. It involves recognizing the individuality and uniqueness of each older person, meeting their daily needs, playing an advocacy role in defense of vulnerability, and practicing decision-making valuing care preferences particularly when it comes to reassessing levels of care.

Appreciating individuality and uniqueness

Participants agreed that everyone must be treated based on individual circumstances by tailoring their care to their values and wishes at any given moment in life’s trajectory to promote positive experiences. One expert shared a best practice implemented in a nursing home setting where, for most patients, the future cannot be changed while the present time could be cherished.

The story I’m going to tell is about the oldest woman I’ve ever cared for. 108 years old. She got angry one morning because the Canadiens traded Michael Cammalleri mid-game…We knew she didn’t have much time left. I asked her: ‘When was the last time you went to a field hockey game?’ She said: ‘40 years’, so we scheduled a game for her with an attendant one evening, and she went. The most dangerous thing she did was to eat a hot dog. After two periods, she was tired, so she left. A month later, she passed away. The thing that changed for the whole establishment was to ask: ‘What are we able to do today?’ They established a program ‘A moment in time’, and it’s like a make-a-wish for older persons. (Manager 2).

Accommodating daily needs

Participants agreed about the complexity involved in balancing older persons’ needs, desires, and capacities with both the environment they live and the healthcare network. The difficulties with living in the urban context for an older person due to nonadaptive city layouts, including roads, parks, or stairs, were voiced. They also felt that the healthcare system should be accommodated to older persons’ basic needs to accomplish their activities of daily living and domestic tasks and thus allow them to live in their home as long as possible.

My mother has to register on the Internet to get access to a nutritionist. She is eighty years old. She is doing what she can with the Internet, but it is not working. She goes to the clinic, and they tell her ‘Madam, go back home and do it again on the Internet’…In all our services, I do not think we should lose sight of the human element. (Decision-maker 1).

She goes to the clinic, and they tell her ‘Madam, go back home and do it again on the Internet’…In all our services, I do not think we should lose sight of the human element. (Decision-maker 1).

Advocacy in defense of vulnerability

Participants thought that older persons were at greater risk for vulnerability due to their functional dependency and social precarity. Wishes of persons would not be heard or considered if they lived alone in the community without a relative or social network. Professionals and informal caregivers sometimes had to play an advocacy role to protect older persons’ rights.

The principle of advocacy. We know it is something that will really affect the clinical status of our patients if they have someone who is there to fight for them, to make sure they are going to get the best care possible. It can be done easily by a healthcare professional, family member, friend, or caregiver. (Clinician 3).

The principle of advocacy. We know it is something that will really affect the clinical status of our patients if they have someone who is there to fight for them, to make sure they are going to get the best care possible. It can be done easily by a healthcare professional, family member, friend, or caregiver. (Clinician 3).

There was an agreement that older persons residing in nursing homes who cannot express themselves, many of whom have dementia, were particularly vulnerable. They needed to be advocated for by their relatives or legal representatives. This vulnerability became especially precarious during the COVID-19 pandemic health crisis. Being unable to participate in decision-making or to give consent concerning their health and safety posed a threat to older persons’ autonomy and dignity.

We had screening teams come in and test patients against their will, even if they were unfit. Categorical refusal was not respected. There were a lot of organizational problems, approach problems [such as] relatives who were not there at the time of death, horrible things. We really need to rethink the whole thing. (Clinician 2).

Decision-making valuing care preferences

Participants discussed that shared decision-making should be a process empowering older persons to actively take part in defining their care plan. They pointed out that assessing and updating the ‘levels of care’, a legal document completed in the presence of a physician recording patient wishes and preferences for medical interventions, was not always simple.

When we analyze the files of end-of-life patients, we see that they often have several hospitalizations. But at some point, we don’t take the time we need to step back and ask ourselves if we should reassess the level of care…It’s hard for teams to say: ‘Are we dealing with a senior who’s just at the end-of-life or who’s going to die any time soon? When do we address this?’ There is perhaps a sensitive subject to address, but we don’t dare to discuss. (Manager 1).

Involvement of family and friends

This was the most prominent category as many participants experienced challenges with being informal caregivers to support their family members, friends, or neighbours in their care trajectories. They stressed the need for formal recognition of caregivers, allowing caregivers to define their responsibilities, and improving caregiving trajectories.

Formal recognition of caregivers

There were barriers to effective engagement of informal caregivers who were sometimes excluded from participating in the care of their relatives, friends, or neighbours because they were not designated as legal representatives. Sometimes, older persons, particularly those who started having cognitive impairments, were left on their own without care until a legal representative such as a guardian was designated by a court. The designation of a guardian who has the right to make decisions about the person’s health care, nutrition, clothing, and other life choices, was complicated.

I have heard people being told on several occasions: ‘You know, we do not have to respond to you. You are not the legal representative.’ The informal caregiver often is not the legal representative because parents appoint a representative amongst their children. But if they are boys, it’s unfortunate, as not all boys can change mom’s diapers, for example. (Older person 3).

I have heard people being told on several occasions: ‘You know, we do not have to respond to you. You are not the legal representative.’ The informal caregiver often is not the legal representative because parents appoint a representative amongst their children. Allowing caregivers to define their responsibilities

Caregivers assumed a wide-ranging set of responsibilities including assisting with activities of daily living of their loved ones, providing emotional support to them, coordinating their medical appointments, managing their finances, and playing an advocacy role. Participants believed that caregivers should be empowered by professionals to take on as much of the caretaking for their loved one as they feel is right without getting lost in the system or burnt out.

We had a patient who unfortunately had a stroke and was in no condition to return home. He absolutely wanted to go back home, even with all his deficits and needs. We tried other ways of training [the patient and family] in the hospital and then by the local community service center. So, sometimes, we must trust our patients and their families and give them the tools they need to self-manage their quality of life. (Clinician 4).

Participants voiced that caregivers should be given the opportunity to reconsider their decisions as circumstances change. An accessible, consistent, and reliable guidance could help them make informed choices.

You can’t impose a role on a caregiver. It must be voluntary. Then they can revisit that choice. This means we must make them real partners. (Decision-maker 2)

Improving caregiving trajectories

Participants expressed their concerns about the significant demands placed on informal caregivers and the associated burdens present before, during, or after the admission of their loved ones to a nursing home. Support for caregivers was particularly important when these responsibilities fell on a limited number of individuals within families providing care at home.

I had the experience with my father, who was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s. He lived with his wife, who had been my stepmother for 42 years…She kept him at home as long as she could. She exhausted herself. Even though we were there to help her find a place for Dad. (Older person 1).

In addition, participants indicated the stress, including the financial strain, placed on couples during transitions to nursing homes. While one partner had to stay in their own home and manage the expenses, there was an extra amount to pay for nursing home beds.

One of my aunts has just been admitted to a nursing home. I can see how difficult it can be for a couple. Because when you are living at home, it can have an impact on the couple’s financial situation. How is the person who stays at home going to be able to stay at home, knowing the related costs they must pay when the other person arrives in a nursing home? (Decision-maker 3)

Communication and education

To promote autonomy, self-care, and health among older persons and their caregivers, effective communication between older persons, caregivers, and healthcare professionals was necessary to explain care processes, disease progression, or clinical status. Participants indicated a need for society-level education and awareness-raising regarding the care for aging population.

Adaptive communication between older persons, caregivers, and professionals

Participants pointed out a need for active listening before giving out information, adapting one’s communication approach to the unique needs of older persons and their families, and providing clear information regardless of their level of education.

I had no explanation of what medication was going to be given [to my father]. I was the one who had to ask for palliative sedation, and he was in a hospice. At one point, I was the one who said: ‘Listen, he’s suffering too much. You cannot go on like this’. I wonder if it would not be interesting to see how often these discussions take place, or what should be communicated with the family. (Clinician 3).

Society-level education and awareness-raising

Education and raising awareness about the increasing needs of our aging population was perceived as generating positive experiences in care coordination and trajectories. The lack of knowledge about the increasing numbers of persons living with neurocognitive disorders was identified as a healthcare system challenge. The uncertainty and lack of education around specific and developing aspects of care, such as having access to medical aid in dying, was brought up.

Coordination and integration of care

This category referred to organizing and integrating patient care activities to ensure a seamless delivery of services. Participants often experienced problems related to the inadequate health and social services organization, cross-sectoral approaches, and interdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare professionals.

Health and social services organization

Participants mostly discussed their negative experiences with the organization of professionals’ work, the mobilization of necessary resources to carry out care activities, the inefficient information technology, and the inflexible treatment modalities. For example, when patients in rehabilitation reached the ceiling of physio or occupational therapy hours, they no longer received therapy even though they stayed on the same unit, which made them feel abandoned. Participants cited gaps in clinical information exchanges between hospitals and community services, impacting access to post-surgery home care and causing distress.

Our system is sometimes difficult to navigate. There are gaps or a different discourse between the hospital and services offered in the community. My father had surgery and went back home. At the hospital, they told him that he needed blood tests so a home care nurse would come. [He] calls the home care service. They say, ‘Oh no, you can take your medication on your own. We will not send nurses.’ The criteria did not consider that my mother had extremely limited mobility, my father just had surgery, and neither of them can drive. (Decision-maker 3).I think we lack some communication between networks. Technology is still evolving. Local community services centers may not necessarily have access to the digitalized files of the hospital. This can create service gaps or, at the very least, delays in administering care. (Manager 4).

Cross-sectoral approaches

Concerns focused on the healthcare system’s capacity being increasingly challenged by an aging population and faced with staff shortages and an enlarged workload. The healthcare system was built to treat diseases, whereas the societal context also encompasses chronic illnesses and neurocognitive disorders. The high value older persons and families placed on aging in place made it imperative that the service undergo significant changes. Health and social care sectors needed to work with other sectors to meet the multi-dimensional care for older persons.

Our network’s ability to meet the demand will be extremely challenging with the aging population, particularly the development of home support and labor shortage. (Decision-maker 4).In the 1960s, 70s, and 80s, institutionalization was more of a norm than an exception. I see the problems that prevent us from improving our outcome. What needs to change for us to succeed is shifting the paradigm we’ve been in for the past forty years. (Manager 5).There must be a paradigm shift at the societal level because it’s not just health and social services. We need to create the right conditions for them [caregivers] to exercise their role [via a] mutual support from communities. It will have to be about much more than just health and social services. (Decision-maker 2).

Interdisciplinary collaboration

For participants, interdisciplinary collaboration referred to the interactions between two or more healthcare professionals to develop a better care plan. Inadequate communication between different disciplines led to cracks in services, such as medication lists received by nursing homes for recently admitted patients not usually being up-to-date. Similarly, social work referrals did not include enough information to highlight the situations based on their urgency.

I think there’s a real lack of cohesion at the medical level when it comes to this diagnosis [dementia]. It’s not done. We don’t ask for various consultations. It’s still too medical. Social work is so important here. I don’t know if the urgency [of the situation] is indicated. (Older person 1).

Responsiveness to evolving needs

Older persons often experienced transitions in their health status due to aging, physical or cognitive deterioration, or a substantial shift from one status to another. Timely responses to health transitions required transfers of responsibility among healthcare providers, institutions, and/or sectors for some or all aspects of care and for living arrangements. Promoting holistic approaches aimed to reduce over-medicalization by considering persons as a whole rather than focusing on a disease.

Timely response to health transitions

Participants talked about their experiences with continuously reassessing older persons’ health status to promote a smooth trajectory so that health and social care accommodate the changing needs of older persons via home care support, rehabilitation, alternate levels of care, and nursing homes.

I had a patient I’d been following at a senior residence who deteriorated due to Parkinson’s. We took steps to get him into nursing home. He was becoming increasingly dysphagic with aspirations and so on, so I was always under the impression that he would end up in hospital. Home care was able to add an orderly just for this patient, temporarily for a few days, until we could find a place for him in a nursing home. We avoided all the hassles with going to an overflowing emergency room. (Manager 3).

Promoting holistic approaches

Particularly when dealing with fluctuating health status, it was important to assess the person’s living environment before drawing medical conclusions and making decisions for invasive interventions, to consider physical, mental, emotional, social, cultural, and spiritual dimensions of care beyond the disease symptoms, and to deliver comfort care in the context of natural death unrelated to an oncological or other pathology.

A 92-year-old lady was waiting for a pacemaker because of tachy-brady. We realized that she was living alone and was almost blind and deaf. She was taking her medication by herself, and probably had a cognitive disorder, mixing all her medications…When we discovered this, we adjusted her medications, and she did not need a pacemaker. (Clinician 3).

Health equity as the core category across the six key categories of actionable priorities

Health equity emerged as a core category interconnecting the other six categories. According to the participants, ‘access to services and care’, especially home care resources, was not always equitable depending on the area where the person lived (rural vs. urban). In terms of ‘respect for older persons’, when older persons lacked dedicated advocates for their wishes and preferences, they navigated the healthcare system alone. Their care was often inequitable as they did not receive the additional support they needed. ‘Involvement of families and friends’ in the care of an older person required too much of their time, particularly in rural areas, sometimes leading them to take sick leaves. The ‘communication and education’ category was also interrelated with equitable care as language barriers were a notable concern. Older people who could not speak English or French were more likely to have restricted access to care or were not able to be cared for in the language of their choice. Regarding ‘care coordination and integration’, geographical disparities indicated the need for streamlined support for caregivers. Finally, ‘responsiveness to evolving needs’ at times of older persons’ health transitions depended on caregivers’ knowledge about the healthcare system and capacity to advocate for their loved ones. Discussion

We developed a framework for actionable priorities to improve the health and social care system in Canada based on the experiences of different types of stakeholders. This framework included seven key categories of priorities. Revolving around a core category of ‘health equity’, the six key categories were ‘access to services and care’, ‘respect for older persons’, ‘involvement of family and friends’, ‘communication and education’, ‘coordination and integration of care’, and ‘responsiveness to evolving needs’. Our findings implicated several areas for improvement in care.

Unlike existing frameworks specific to certain aspects of health and social care for older persons cited in the background section, our framework for actionable priorities had more universal and interrelated dimensions surrounding equity. Some of our findings align with other resources [29], including the World Health Organization, which emphasize the significance of effective and individualized communication and integrated care [1]. Given that our findings cover many aspects of health and social care for older persons, our discussion will focus on salient results and practical intervention targets to improve health and social care for older persons.

First, participants thought that navigating the healthcare system was difficult for chronically ill older people and their caregivers and proposed navigator programs as a solution. The literature suggests that such navigator programs improve processes of care, have a positive impact on quality of life, and improve satisfaction with services [30, 31]. The Institute for Research on Public Policy in Canada proposes three policy options for improving system navigations, including making navigation a public concern, expending public navigation programs, and integrating care services [32]. The Institute also suggests leveraging more provincial resources and public funding for formal system navigation programs [32]. Further research should assess the cost-effectiveness and the impact of equitable system navigators on both health outcomes and experiences [30].

Second, participants agreed that healthcare experiences are intimately linked with respect for older persons’ wishes. For instance, improving levels of care discussions was perceived as one of the most meaningful ways to protect the rights and dignity of older persons, as patient wishes are best respected if the goals of care are regularly updated [33]. However, there exist some obstacles with implementing this process, especially near end-of-life [34]. It was shown that most of the medical charts of a sample of older patients in various care settings in Quebec lacked a standardized document for goals of care [35]. Our findings therefore reinforce the need to support professionals to complete standardized levels of care forms [36] so that patients’ wishes are captured regularly.

Third, informal caregiving emerged as an omnipresent phenomenon as many participants were caregivers for a relative, friend, or neighbour. They wished for comprehensive support systems and resources to fulfill their caregiving roles effectively while maintaining their well-being. Other studies show that only half of caregivers consistently feel listened to by healthcare professionals and are asked about their understanding of the treatments [37]. Family caregivers strongly desire to actively be part of the care team of home-dwelling older persons in a collaborative process [38]. In Quebec, a recent national policy [39] enforced by law [40] has the potential to significantly impact the dynamics of caregiving by balancing how responsibilities are delineated and supported.

Fourth, the lack of awareness about innovative interventions regarding older persons is perceived as an ongoing issue. For example, according to the participants, medical aid in dying, which was decriminalized in 2015 in Canada [41] and involves its own distinct process and criteria, was considered not to be widely known, and this service remained limited within the healthcare system. Our findings suggest a call for a more comprehensive education approach to ensure that medical aid in dying is well-understood.

Fifth, participants raised issues related to the coordination and integration of services in the current healthcare system and emphasized the need to fill gaps in information exchanges between hospitals and community services. Similarly, another study reported that better perceived coordination among their providers was associated with higher care satisfaction for patients [29]. Our findings also indicated a need for a paradigm shift involving cross-sectoral innovations which actually became a reality post-COVID-19 pandemic era [42]. Such a shift may include age-friendly cities and communities [43], housing and urban planning [44], transportation and mobility [45], community engagement and social inclusion [46], technology, and digital inclusion [47].

Strengths and limitations

This study has many strengths. First, we explored a variety of participants’ experiences of healthcare and social services for general older populations. While our findings corroborate the international literature focusing on specific aspects of care, they provide a comprehensive portrait that could inform an experience-based co-design of future interventions or initiatives [48]. Contrary to single stakeholder perspectives, our multi-stakeholder perspective looked at the problem from various angles, ensuring that solutions address not only individual needs but also broader systemic and organizational concerns. For example, managers and decision-makers were able to suggest intervention targets that are practical and feasible within the constraints of our health and social care system. Clinicians and older persons were more able to explain what was important to improve the quality of life and quality of practice of individuals. This combined perspective offers a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the issues. In addition, to increase the representativeness, we successfully engaged diverse stakeholders, held remote group deliberations, and adapted discussions to the needs and time constraints of each stakeholder group. Moreover, by incorporating reflexivity in this qualitative study, we critically examined our influence on the research and data interpretation through an iterative process.

Our study was not without limitations. Transferability of the findings might be limited as: (1) this study was conducted in one province, and most of the participants were from urban or suburban regions; (2) although many participants were bilingual, it is possible that the views of Anglophone population might have been missed; and (3) the number of older persons in our sample was small. This paper did not look at variations in perspectives between the stakeholder groups. Such individual and group experiences, however, might be less relevant in our framework which was based on collective experiences and informed priorities at the population-level.

Conclusions

As the population ages, experiences with older persons’ health and social care becomes universal. This study illustrates the complex challenges of caring for older persons and generates a framework for actionable priorities in the health and social care system in Canada. The most pressing implications for practice and policy include increasing public healthcare system navigators, supporting physicians to complete standardized levels of care forms regularly to capture older persons’ wishes, recognizing the contribution of caregivers more formally, educating the population on new aspects of care such as medical aid in dying, promoting cross-sectoral initiatives, and endorsing holistic approaches. These insights can provide valuable guidance for policymakers and healthcare professionals in developing strategies to promote person-centered, respectful, and equitable approaches to health and social care.
