Supramolecular engineering of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies enabling tunable piezoelectricity
Authors: Yehong Huo, Shuaijie Liu, Bingbing Yang, Jian Hu, Lingling Li, Xiangnan Gong, Mei-Ling Tan, Peng Liu, Kaiyong Cai, Wei Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rational molecular design of short peptides represents an effective strategy for modulating supramolecular architectures and piezoelectricity of peptide assemblies. However, the impact of peptide sequence and side chain chemistry on the piezoelectric properties of assemblies remains largely unexplored. Diphenylalanine (FF), the core recognition motif of β-amyloid protein, has emerged as an excellent molecular building block for fabricating piezoelectric materials due to its easily modifiable structure and exceptional electromechanical properties. Here, we systematically investigate the effect of peptide sequence and side chain variation on the supramolecular stacking and piezoelectric properties of FF-inspired tripeptide assemblies. Crystal structures reveal that variations in molecular structures induce distinct secondary structures and supramolecular assembly modes, producing tunable piezoelectric coefficients confirmed by density functional theory calculations. Notably, IFF assemblies exhibit a maximum piezoelectric coefficient of 57.1 pC/N, representing the highest predicted value among oligopeptide supramolecular materials. The IFF assemblies-based piezoelectric nanogenerator generates an open-circuit voltage of 2.3 V under 70 N force, maintaining excellent mechanical durability over 10,000 press-release cycles. This work presents a promising strategy for regulating the piezoelectricity of peptide supramolecular assemblies, establishing rational design principles for advancing the development of high-performance peptide-based piezoelectric biomaterials.

Introduction

Piezoelectric materials, which can efficiently transduce mechanical stimuli into electrical signals, have been widely utilized in diverse technological domains including energy harvesting devices and sensors1–4. Currently, the extensively investigated piezoelectric materials include inorganic materials, polymeric materials, and biomaterials5–9. Among them, traditional inorganic materials (e.g., lead zirconate titanate, barium titanate) generally exhibit high piezoelectric coefficients and long-term operational stability. However, their biomedical applications are usually limited by their poor biocompatibility and potential cytotoxicity, particularly in lead-containing compositions10,11. Synthetic polymer materials demonstrate good mechanical flexibility and tissue compatibility, while the rigorous fabrication requirements, semi-crystalline nature, and poor structural tunability hinder their performance optimization at molecular level12,13. Natural biological materials, such as bone14, hair15, and skin16, have attracted widespread attention for their excellent biodegradability and inherent piezoelectricity17,18. Nevertheless, their structural complexity and typically low piezoelectric coefficients make it difficult to satisfy the growing performance demands in biomedicine19,20. Recently, biomolecules (e.g., amino acids21,22, nucleotides23, vitamins24) have emerged as promising candidates for piezoelectric materials due to their well-defined molecular structures and controllable assembly processes. These biomolecules can spontaneously self-assemble into non-centrosymmetric assemblies through non-covalent interactions, endowing them with intrinsic piezoelectric properties. However, the effect of molecular-level structure design on the piezoelectric response of bio-inspired molecular assemblies remains largely unexplored. Therefore, it is crucial to develop piezoelectric biomaterials with facile fabrication and tailored piezoelectric performance.

Currently, bio-inspired short peptides have attracted extensive research interest due to their structural simplicity, sequence programmability, and functionalization capabilities25–36. Diphenylalanine (FF), the core recognition motif of β-amyloid protein, has emerged as a research focus owing to its distinct self-assembly characteristics and exceptional electromechanical properties37–42. FF could self-assemble into hollow nanotubes with non-centrosymmetric structures in aqueous phase43, which exhibited significant shear piezoelectric response44. Furthermore, polarization engineering has emerged as an effective strategy to direct the alignment of FF molecules during self-assembly, thereby modulating their piezoelectric performance45. For instance, FF could self-assemble into uniformly polarized microrods when an electric field was applied during peptide self-assembly, achieving an effective piezoelectric coefficient d₃₃ of 17.9 pm/V, higher than 9.9 pm/V for unpolarized assemblies46. However, such approaches often rely on complex fabrication procedures, which may limit their scalability and practical applicability in bio-integrated devices. Molecular-level structural modification offers a simple and effective strategy for tuning the piezoelectricity of FF-based assemblies47–51. For instance, incorporating extra aromatic groups and N-terminal modifications into FF motif (Dip-Dip, Boc-Dip-Dip, etc.) facilitated the piezoelectricity modulation, with Boc-Dip-Dip exhibiting 4-fold higher piezoelectric response than FF crystals51. Bera et al. synthesized Pro-Phe-Phe and Hyp-Phe-Phe by introducing proline and hydroxyproline into FF motif. Hyp-Phe-Phe assemblies exhibited significantly enhanced intermolecular hydrogen bonding networks and crystal polarization due to the introduction of additional hydroxyl groups, enhancing the piezoelectric response by one order of magnitude with d35 of 27.3 pm/V49,50. These results have demonstrated that molecular engineering on the peptide structures could achieve piezoelectric properties modulation of peptide-based supramolecular materials. Despite growing interest, efforts to modify FF through amino acid substitution remain limited and largely unsystematic, and the influence of peptide sequence variation and side chain functionalities on supramolecular packing and piezoelectric performance remains inadequately understood.

Herein, we have systematically explored the piezoelectricity of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies toward power harvesting and elucidated the structure-property relationship between supramolecular packing and piezoelectric performance. A series of amyloid-inspired tripeptides with varied peptide sequences and side chain properties were designed and synthesized by introducing different amino acids (glycine, alanine, leucine, and isoleucine) at the N terminus, between the two phenylalanine residues, or at the C terminus of the FF motif. Crystal structure analysis revealed that the changes in peptide sequence and side chain induced the formation of distinct secondary structures, including right-handed helical, β-sheet, and random coil conformations, which could significantly affect their piezoelectric properties. These tripeptide assemblies with different sequences and residue side chains exhibited predicted piezoelectric coefficients ranging from 10.1 to 57.1 pC/N, as determined by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Notably, IFF crystals exhibited a predicted maximum piezoelectric coefficient (d14) of 57.1 pC/N, representing the highest predicted value for pure oligopeptide assemblies. The IFF assemblies-based piezoelectric nanogenerator produced open-circuit voltages exceeding 2.0 V under 70 N force, and maintained excellent output stability over 10,000 press-release cycles. Moreover, the IFF assemblies-based piezoelectric insoles successfully detected and distinguished various gait patterns. This work systematically demonstrates the supramolecular engineering strategy on modulating the piezoelectricity of amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystals, providing valuable insights for exploring innovative peptide-based biomaterials with high piezoelectric properties.

Results

Morphological characterization of amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystals

FF and its derivatives possess intrinsic self-assembly capabilities, enabling the formation of diverse and highly ordered nanostructures with distinct physicochemical properties. However, the self-assembly behaviors of amyloid-inspired peptides modified by introducing natural amino acids to alter peptide sequences and side chains are still underexplored. In addition, the structure-property relationships of such assemblies remain to be elucidated. Leveraging the modularity of FF, twelve amyloid-inspired tripeptides were strategically designed by inserting amino acids, glycine (G), alanine (A), leucine (L), or isoleucine (I), into the N-terminus, between the two phenylalanine (F) residues, or at the C-terminus of the FF dipeptide. The self-assembly characteristics and piezoelectric performance of the resulting assemblies were subsequently investigated (Fig. 1). Initially, twelve tripeptides were synthesized via solid-phase synthesis techniques (Supplementary Figs. 1–2). Based on the solution environment and intrinsic properties of the tripeptide, we systematically investigated crystal formation by solvent evaporation and vapor diffusion methods (Supplementary Table 1). Crystal structures of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies

To elucidate the precise atomic-level structures of the tripeptide assemblies, we analyzed the crystal structures of tripeptides using X-ray diffraction techniques. The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) spectra of AFF and FAF crystals exhibited almost identical PXRD patterns to previously reported structures (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), indicating the same arrangements. Notably, PXRD patterns revealed different peak positions between AFF and FAF assemblies, indicating that peptide sequence alterations could substantially modify crystal packing. Additionally, single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis was conducted for other tripeptide crystals, and detailed crystallographic parameters were summarized in Supplementary Tables 2–4. Furthermore, hydrogen bond information in tripeptide crystals and the involved atom codes were summarized in Supplementary Tables 5–8 and Supplementary Fig. 5. To clarify structural differences across the tripeptides, we classified all assemblies by their backbone secondary structures, determined from torsion angles (φ₂, ψ₂) of the central residue and their positions in the Ramachandran plot. Based on this analysis, the tripeptide crystals could be classified into three groups: right-handed helical, β-sheet, and random coil conformations (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6).Fig. 3The crystal structures of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies (GFF, AFF, LFF, IFF, FIF, and FFI).Torsion angles of the middle residue in molecule A within the asymmetric units of tripeptide crystals: a GFF, e AFF, i LFF, m FIF, q FFI, and u IFF. Right-handed helical tripeptide assemblies: Among twelve tripeptide samples, GFF is the only sequence adopting a right-handed helical backbone conformation. GFF crystallized in the P212121 space group with one tripeptide and two water molecules per asymmetric unit. Two phenylalanine side chains in GFF were arranged on the same side of the peptide main chain (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The φ₂ and ψ₂ torsion angles of the central Phe residue (−54.8° and −57.8°, respectively) fall within the right-handed α-helical region of the Ramachandran plot (Fig. 3a). Along the crystallographic a direction, GFF molecules propagated into a helical-like motif of the peptide backbone through intermolecular hydrogen bonds formed between amino hydrogen atoms and carboxyl oxygen atoms, and hydrogen bonding interactions between GFF and water molecules (Fig. 3b, c). These helical motifs further parallel packed into supramolecular helical layers, stabilized by hydrophobic interactions between adjacent phenylalanine side chains (Fig. 3d). The FTIR results showed that GFF assemblies exhibited a sharp amide I peak at 1651 cm−1 with a shoulder at 1686 cm−1, which is consistent with helical conformations observed in the crystal structure analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7).

β-sheet tripeptide assemblies: The majority of tripeptides, FFG, FGF, AFF, FFA, FAF, LFF, FFL, FLF, FIF, and FFI, adopt β-sheet backbone conformations with antiparallel orientations. Their torsion angles (φ₂, ψ₂) fall within the canonical β-region of the Ramachandran plot (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 5). This assignment was further corroborated by FTIR spectroscopy, which revealed a prominent amide I band in the 1630–1640 cm−1 range characteristic of β-sheet structures, in good agreement with the secondary-structure analysis of crystal structures (Supplementary Fig. 7). Although all share extended conformations, their supramolecular arrangements differ markedly depending on side-chain composition and sequence order.

FFG crystallized in the P21 space group, with four tripeptides, two water molecules, and two TFA molecules in the asymmetric unit (Supplementary Fig. 5b). All FFG tripeptide backbones adopt similar β-sheet conformations (Supplementary Table 9, Supplementary Fig. 6a). Along the c axis, antiparallel β-sheet structures formed through intermolecular hydrogen bonds between internal amide groups, terminal polar groups, water and trifluoroacetic acid molecules. The hydrophobic interaction between phenylalanine side chains drives the formation of parallel packed layer arrangements (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d). FGF exhibits a similar antiparallel β-sheet architecture but features cofacial phenylalanine side chains on the same side of the backbone, leading to alternating hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains and an aromatic zipper-like stacking motif (Supplementary Fig. 6e–h). Further structural details are provided in the Supplementary Discussion.

AFF, FFA, and FAF exhibited antiparallel β-sheet conformations with their torsion angles (φ₂, ψ₂) located in the β-sheet region of the Ramachandran plot. AFF crystallized in the P1 space group, with alanine substitution restricting conformational flexibility compared to GFF. The antiparallel β-sheet structures formed along the c axis through intermolecular hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3e–g), with aromatic zipper structures between adjacent sheets through alternating phenylalanine side chains, generating parallel layer packing (Fig. 3h). FFA, FAF, LFF, FFL, and FLF displayed similar β-sheet architectures stabilized by hydrogen bonding networks (Fig. 3i–l) (Supplementary Fig. 6i-x). Further structural details are provided in the Supplementary Discussion.

FIF and FFI exhibit distinct supramolecular packing modes driven by differences in side-chain chemistry and molecular arrangement. FIF assemblies display a typical antiparallel β-sheet conformation (Fig. 3m–p, Supplementary Fig. 5j). Detailed analysis was provided in the Supplementary Discussion. In contrast, the antiparallel β-sheet structures of FFI assemblies exhibited characteristic crossed orientations, where the four molecules within the unit cell packed into two discrete groups (Fig. 3q–t, Supplementary Fig. 5k). This crossed arrangement represents a distinctive supramolecular motif among the tripeptides examined, arising from the interplay of backbone extension and directional side-chain interactions.

Random coil tripeptide assemblies: IFF is the only sequence whose central residue adopts a random coil torsion pattern. IFF crystallized in the P21 space group with its asymmetric unit of IFF crystal contained one tripeptide (Supplementary Fig. 5l). The torsion angles φ2 and ψ2 of the middle Phe residue were 123.8° and −117.5°, located in random coil regions (Fig. 3u). This structural assignment was further supported by FTIR spectroscopy; the spectrum of IFF assemblies exhibited a broad amide I band centered at approximately 1640 cm−1, indicative of random coil structures (Supplementary Fig. 7). IFF self-assembled into a distinctive zigzag supramolecular structure along the b-axis, with adjacent zigzag folded layers exhibited parallel alignment (Fig. 3v–x). These assemblies were stabilized by intermolecular hydrogen bonds involving both terminal amino/carboxyl groups and backbone amide functionalities of tripeptide.

Side-chain variations profoundly alter the backbone conformation, supramolecular packing, and morphology of peptide assemblies. Modifying the N-terminal residue in GFF, AFF, and IFF induced transitions from helical to β-sheet to random coil structures, with corresponding morphological changes from needle-like to long strip crystals. This trend is consistent with reported GAF and FAF tripeptides, which exhibited helical-to-β-sheet transitions accompanied by needle-to-strip morphological changes. Peptide sequence also plays a crucial role: the positional isomers IFF, FIF, and FFI assembled into random coil, antiparallel β-sheet, and cross-antiparallel β-sheet structures with distinct morphologies (long strip, lamellar, block-like), paralleling the sequence-dependent structural diversity observed in Ala-Phe-Ala (β-sheet) versus Ala-Ala-Phe (right-handed helix), and in dipeptides Phe-Ala (network) versus Ala-Phe (lamellar) (Supplementary Table 10).

Systematic investigation of amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystal structures revealed that both backbone conformation and side-chain composition govern the supramolecular packing of amyloid-inspired tripeptides. (a) Crystallographic analysis demonstrated that higher backbone conformational freedom mainly observed when modifying the N-terminus of the FF motif, enabling deviation from typical β-sheet geometries to right-handed helical (GFF) or random coil (IFF) structures. (b) In contrast, amino acid insertions at the C-terminus or between FF motif tended to favor the formation of typical β-sheet structures. When residues such as A, G, L, and I were positioned between FF sequences, the two aromatic side chains of the phenylalanine residues were located on the same side of the backbone, mediating the formation of more compact aromatic zipper structures. Overall, the comprehensive analysis of correlations between amyloid-inspired tripeptide molecular structures, secondary structure terminologies, and crystalline supramolecular arrangements offers valuable insights into the design of peptide-based self-assembling materials with tunable structures and functionalities.

Piezoelectric properties of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies

The electromechanical properties of twelve amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystals were systematically investigated by density functional theory (DFT) calculation, including elastic stiffness, dielectric, and piezoelectric constants (Table 1, Supplementary Tables 11–25). Elastic stiffness constants (Cij) are fundamental parameters that describe a material’s resistance to deformation under applied stress. The elastic stiffness constants were summarized in Supplementary Table 11. Tripeptide assemblies exhibited significant variations in both longitudinal (C11, C22, C33) and shear (C44, C55, C66) stiffness parameters, with shear stiffness consistently lower than longitudinal stiffness in all tripeptide crystals. The minimum elastic stiffness constants (Cij)min of tripeptide crystals ranged from 0.1 to 3.2 GPa. Helical-like motif (GFF) and random coil-derived zigzag (IFF) structures demonstrated particularly low stiffness, with minimum elastic constants of 0.4 and 0.5 GPa, respectively. These exceptionally low shear stiffness constants facilitate large mechanical deformations under pressure. The mechanical characteristics of tripeptide assemblies were further investigated (Supplementary Table 12). Average Young’s modulus of 12 tripeptides ranged from 3.1 to 19.7 GPa, Poisson’s ratio from 0.26 to 0.42, bulk modulus from 4.0 to 21.2 GPa, and shear modulus from 1.1 to 23.0 GPa (Table 1, Supplementary Table 12). The GFF assemblies with right-handed helical structure and random coil-derived zigzag IFF assemblies displayed the lowest Young’s modulus (5.3 and 3.1 GPa, respectively) and highest Poisson’s ratios (0.40 and 0.42), reflecting their high mechanical compliance. Among β-sheet forming assemblies, those with terminal modifications (FFG, FFA, FFL = 4.3–8.6 GPa) generally showed lower Young’s modulus than their middle-insertion counterparts (FGF, FAF, FIF = 10.5–19.7 GPa). Piezoelectric stress constants (eij) were subsequently calculated for tripeptide assemblies (Supplementary Tables 13–24), reflecting the material’s polarization upon applied force. The maximum piezoelectric stress constants (|eij|max) for tripeptide crystals ranged from 0.025 to 0.108 C/m2. The helical assembly GFF shows a relatively lower value of 0.025 C/m2. In contrast, among β-sheet forming assemblies, FFG demonstrates a notably high value of 0.096 C/m2, while the random coil-derived zigzag structure of IFF achieves the maximum value of 0.108 C/m2 observed in this study. Moreover, the max eij of IFF assemblies significantly surpass the previously reported biomolecular assemblies (e.g., PFF at 0.056 C/m2), indicating enhanced intrinsic polarization capability in our structurally engineered tripeptides. The piezoelectric strain constants (dij) of tripeptides were further calculated by dij = eij/Cij (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Tables 13–24). The maximum dij for all tripeptide assemblies ranged from 10.1 to 57.1 pC/N. The GFF assembly, which adopts a right-handed helical structure, exhibits a moderate piezoelectric coefficient of 19.3 pC/N. The β-sheet forming assemblies demonstrated wide piezoelectric coefficient (10.1–54.5 pC/N), with terminally modified variants FFG (C-terminus, 54.5 pC/N) and FFL (C-terminus, 50.6 pC/N) achieving notable performance. In contrast, middle-insertion β-sheet assemblies like FAF and FIF showed lower coefficients (10.1–26.6 pC/N), reflecting more efficient dipole cancellation in their packing geometry. Notably, the IFF assemblies exhibited the highest piezoelectric strain coefficient of 57.1 pC/N, which represents the highest value among pure oligopeptide-based materials reported to date. As summarized in Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Table 27, this performance significantly exceeds that of other representative systems, including Hyp-FF (27 pC/N) and several typical inorganic materials, such as ZnO (9.93 pC/N). Guided by DFT screening, the three tripeptide crystals with the higher predicted piezoelectric coefficients (IFF, FFG, and FFL) were selected for PFM characterization (Supplementary Figs. 8–16). Their effective piezoelectric coefficients d33 were measured as 52.07 ± 0.52, 39.17 ± 0.97, and 34.12 ± 0.42 pm/V, respectively. These experimental values follow the same trend as the DFT predictions (IFF > FFG > FFL), which fall within the range of the predicted piezoelectric tensor components dij obtained from DFT calculations (Table 1). In addition, to further elucidate the underlying mechanism, piezoelectric amplitude versus DC bias field (butterfly) curves were acquired. All three crystals exhibit the characteristic double-peak shape with a pronounced dip near zero bias, clearly indicating nonlinear domain switching under an applied electric field and suggesting that domain contribution plays an important role in their macroscopic piezoelectric response. The dielectric constant (εr) is a crucial parameter reflecting a material’s capacity for electric energy storage and its polarization response to electric fields. Calculation results showed that the dielectric constants εr of tripeptide assemblies ranged from 2.75 to 8.06 (Table 1, Fig. 4d). These values were considerably below conventional materials like BaTiO3 (εr: 186). Moreover, the piezoelectric voltage constant (gij) is a key indicator of the material’s ability to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy, making it essential for applications such as sensors and energy harvesting devices. The maximum piezoelectric voltage constant (|gij|max) of tripeptide crystals ranged from 306 to 2476 mV m/N (Table 1, Fig. 4e). Since gij = dij/εr, dielectric constant directly influences piezoelectric voltage response. The helical-like motif assembly GFF showed moderate voltage performance (678 mV m/N), constrained by its limited strain coefficient. Among β-sheet forming assemblies, substantial variations in voltage output were observed. While AFF exhibited considerable piezoelectric strain (27.6 pC/N), its relatively high dielectric constant (εr = 8.06) significantly compromised its voltage constant (495 mV m/N). This contrasts with other β-sheet assemblies modified with long-chain amino acids (Ile, Leu), where terminally modified IFF and LFF achieved exceptional voltage coefficients of 2476 and 1043 mV m/N, respectively. Similarly, within the middle-insertion β-sheet category, FIF and FLF substantially exceeded FAF, while terminally modified FFI and FFL surpassed FFA. It is worth noting that the random coil-derived zigzag structure of IFF achieved an optimal combination of the lowest dielectric constant (εr = 2.75) and highest piezoelectric strain (57.1 pC/N), yielding a record voltage constant of 2476 mV m/N, which was an order of magnitude higher than that of PVDF. These findings establish that secondary structure-controlled molecular packing directly governs the voltage output efficiency in peptide-based piezoelectric materials.

The mechanical anisotropy of representative tripeptide crystals was evaluated, revealing relatively pronounced anisotropic behavior (Fig. 4f–h), with detailed analyses and discussion provided in the Supplementary Information. Moreover, the piezoelectric effect is closely related to the net dipole moment within the lattice. Firstly, we conducted a systematic calculation of the molecular dipole moments of tripeptides. The molecular dipole moments of tripeptides, ranging from 2.3 to 36.2 Debye, exhibit clear dependence on peptide sequence and secondary structure (Fig. 4i–k, Supplementary Fig. 18, Supplementary Table 26). The helical assembly GFF showed an intermediate molecular dipole moment of 24.4 D. In β-sheet tape forming assemblies, middle-insertion variants such as FGF (36.2 D), FAF (35.5 D), and FIF (34.3 D) consistently exhibited the highest molecular dipole moments. This trend can be attributed to the asymmetric charge distribution resulting from the spatial arrangement where both phenylalanine side chains reside on the same side of the peptide backbone. In contrast, C-terminal modified assemblies including FFG, FFA, FFI, and FFL displayed the lowest molecular dipoles. Notably, the random coil-based zigzag assembly IFF possessed a high molecular dipole of 33.9 D. These results underscore the critical role of peptide sequence and secondary structure in defining molecular polarization.

Furthermore, crystal dipole moments represent the collective effect of molecular dipole orientation and arrangement within the crystalline lattice, which ultimately influences the macroscopic piezoelectric response. Crystal dipole moments of tripeptide were calculated and analyzed (Fig. 4l–n, Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Table 26). In the helical assembly GFF, significant dipole cancellation occurred due to the non-uniform spiral arrangement of molecules, reducing the crystal dipole to merely 4.0 D, which is a substantial decrease from its molecular dipole of 24.4 D (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Among β-sheet assemblies, most structures exhibited marked dipole cancellation characteristic of antiparallel packing. Middle-insertion variants such as FAF (9.3 D), FIF (7.8 D), and FGF (10.8 D), along with N-terminal modified LFF (8.1 D), showed substantially reduced crystal dipole moments compared to their molecular values, reflecting efficient dipole cancellation between oppositely oriented adjacent chains (Supplementary Fig. 19b–e). The cross-antiparallel β-sheet structure of FFI further demonstrated this trend, where already small molecular dipoles (3.2 D) experienced additional cancellation, yielding a minimal crystal dipole of 4.7 D (Supplementary Fig. 19f). Notably, the random coil-derived zigzag assembly IFF achieved near-perfect dipole summation, with a crystal dipole moment of 35.0 D closely matching its molecular value of 33.9 D (Fig. 4n). This efficient dipole alignment, resulting from highly consistent molecular orientation in the crystal lattice, directly facilitates the exceptional piezoelectric coefficient of 57.1 pC/N observed in IFF. These findings highlighted the potential for engineering crystal packing modes to optimize piezoelectric responses. Precise regulation of supramolecular structures of amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystals via molecular design could alter charge distribution and symmetry of tripeptide assemblies, thereby enhancing the piezoelectric response of crystals.

The structure-property relationships governing the piezoelectric performance of the tripeptide assemblies can be rationalized through a unified framework based on their characteristic secondary structures and resulting supramolecular packing motifs. The results reveal that three key structural categories affect the electromechanical response: First, the right-handed helical conformation observed in GFF creates dipole alignment along the helix. Although this configuration generates substantial molecular polarity, the non-collinear nature of dipole vectors in the crystal lattice results in inefficient dipole summation. This geometric constraint explains why GFF achieves only moderate piezoelectric performance (19.3 pC/N). Second, β-sheet forming assemblies adopt extended conformations that favor antiparallel packing through hydrogen bonding networks. This configuration provides structural stability, while it inherently leads to dipole moment cancellation between adjacent peptide chains. Consequently, most β-sheet based assemblies (e.g., FAF, FIF) exhibit moderate piezoelectric coefficients (10.1–26.6 pC/N) as their molecular dipoles are largely neutralized in the crystal lattice. Third, IFF adopts a distinctive random coil conformation that falls outside the classical β-sheet and helical categories, enabling the formation of a zigzag supramolecular architecture. This configuration provides dual advantages: it facilitates nearly parallel alignment of molecular dipoles to maximize net polarization, while the folded backbone creates natural deformation points that reduce mechanical stiffness. This synergistic combination of optimized dipole alignment and enhanced deformability underlies high piezoelectric coefficient of IFF assemblies (57.1 pC/N). This understanding provides a rational basis for designing peptide-based piezoelectric materials through strategic control of secondary structure and supramolecular packing.

Power generation of IFF assemblies-based piezoelectric nanogenerator

To evaluate the potential of tripeptide assemblies for power generation, we fabricated a sandwich-configured piezoelectric nanogenerator (PENG) (Fig. 5a, b). The detailed fabrication procedure and device configuration are described in the Methods section. IFF crystals with the higher piezoelectric coefficient were selected to assess PENG performance and stability. The output performance is shown in Fig. 5c–h and Supplementary Figs. 20–25. Under an applied force ranging from 10 to 70 N, the IFF assemblies-based PENG generated an open-circuit voltage increasing from 0.35 to 2.28 V, markedly outperforming other reported peptide-based PENGs such as Hyp-FF assemblies (0.45 V) and PLA-FF microrods (1.78 V) (Supplementary Table 28). Additionally, the blank control device without IFF crystals showed no significant voltage signal (Supplementary Fig. 24). Reverse connection tests were performed to verify that the piezoelectric signals originated from the crystals. When the force increased from 10 to 70 N, the voltage output also increased from −0.33 to −2.16 V (Fig. 5d). These values matched forward connection voltage outputs, confirming the intrinsic piezoelectric response of IFF crystals rather than effects from parasitic capacitance and contact resistance. Additionally, the forward and reverse open-circuit voltage outputs exhibited clear force-dependent behavior in 10–70 N, confirming linear piezoelectric response of PENG (Fig. 5g). Short-circuit current tests were further conducted for PENG under 10–70 N. The device generated a short-circuit current of 0.74 nA at 10 N, increasing to 3.05 nA at 70 N (Fig. 5e). While in reverse connection, the current values within the same load range are similar (Fig. 5f). Moreover, forward and reverse short-circuit currents showed excellent linear responses (Fig. 5h). These findings confirmed the capability of PENG to produce substantial voltage and current outputs while maintaining excellent linear piezoelectric behavior. Furthermore, the power output performance of the device was evaluated by recording the voltage output under different external load resistances ranging from 50 to 2000 MΩ. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25, the IFF assemblies-based PENG achieved a power density of 16.5 nW/cm² with a load resistance of 730 MΩ. The energy density of 16.5 nW/cm2 surpasses those of FF microrods (3.3 nW/cm2) (Supplementary Table 28).

To evaluate the durability and stability of the PENG, 10,000 pressing-releasing cycles were performed under 70 N. Results showed that PENG produced stable open-circuit voltage output over 2 V, demonstrating robust cycling stability (Fig. 5i). Notably, the cycling stability of 10,000 cycles exceeds that of many reported biomaterial-based devices (e.g., Cyclo-GW, Hyp-FF) (Supplementary Table 28). To verify the structural and functional integrity of IFF crystals after 10,000 cycles of loading, PXRD and optical microscopy analyses were performed before and after cycling. The results showed that both the PXRD patterns and microscopic images of IFF crystals remained essentially unchanged before and after cycling, and the piezoelectric output after 10,000 cycles exhibited no significant degradation, indicating the maintained structural and functional integrity of IFF crystals during device operation (Supplementary Fig. 26). The thermal stability of IFF crystals was investigated by TGA-DSC analysis (Supplementary Fig. 27a). The results showed that no obvious weight loss occurs below 200 °C, indicating good thermal stability. Additionally, variable-temperature powder X-ray diffraction (VT-PXRD) experiments demonstrated that the PXRD patterns remained essentially unchanged below 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. 27b), confirming good thermal stability. Furthermore, when the crystals were immersed in water for 7 days, the PXRD patterns of IFF crystals remained nearly identical, indicating good water stability (Supplementary Fig. 27c). Piezoelectric performance evaluation indicated that PENG exhibited stable high piezoelectric output capability, which demonstrates significant promise for energy harvesting and sensing applications.

Gait analysis plays a crucial role in rehabilitation assessment, sports science and healthcare52, and walking patterns reflect motor system functionality and indicate early signs of various disorders. In elderly populations, gait alterations correlate with fall susceptibility, while in the rehabilitation of neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease), gait monitoring provides objective evidence for evaluating therapeutic efficacy53,54. An IFF assemblies-based device without a PMMA substrate was used for gait monitoring to better conform to the shape and movement of the foot, ensuring flexibility and improved measurement accuracy during walking (Fig. 5j). Walking characteristics were examined at different foot elevation heights, stride lengths, walking frequencies, and trunk postures. We initially collected piezoelectric signals at different foot lifting heights. The output voltages increased from 0.86 to 1.17 and 1.43 V as the foot lifting height increased from 15 to 30 and 45 cm (Fig. 5k). In addition, the device successfully distinguished different electrical signal patterns corresponding to various step lengths. The sensor generated peak voltages of approximately 0.76, 1.12, and 1.39 V for step lengths of 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 m (Fig. 5l). For walking frequency tests, the voltage amplitudes remained similar (~0.97–0.98 V) across different cadences, while the signal frequency effectively differentiated slow, moderate, and fast walking patterns (Fig. 5m). The device could also distinguish gait signals at different trunk flexion angles, generating voltage outputs of approximately 0.85, 1.14, and 1.47 V at trunk flexion angles of 0°, 15°, and 30°, respectively (Fig. 5n). The IFF assemblies-based device exhibits a high voltage output of 1.47 V, which is comparable to materials such as Bi2WO6 (1.4 V), and significantly outperforms flexible P(VDF-TrFE-CTFE) devices (0.29 V) (Supplementary Table 29).

Additionally, reverse connection tests were performed to verify the intrinsic piezoelectric effect of the PENG. The results showed that the voltage output values were opposite in polarity to those obtained under forward connection while maintaining consistent trends (Supplementary Fig. 28). Furthermore, blank control experiments using devices without IFF crystals demonstrated that no significant piezoelectric signals were generated under various motion patterns (Supplementary Figs. 29–30). These findings confirmed that the piezoelectric effect originated from the IFF crystals themselves rather than other mechanisms. These results demonstrated that the amyloid-inspired tripeptide sensing device provided a non-invasive approach for early detection of gait alterations and rehabilitation assessment, indicating the potential of peptide-based piezoelectric materials in developing practical healthcare monitoring solutions.

Discussion

In summary, we have systematically explored the piezoelectric properties of amyloid-inspired tripeptide crystals and demonstrated that variations in peptide sequence and side chains significantly influenced the supramolecular stacking patterns and piezoelectric responses of the tripeptide assemblies. X-ray diffraction studies revealed that amyloid-inspired tripeptides can self-assemble into diverse secondary structure terminologies, ranging from amyloid-like β-sheet and helical structures to random coil conformations. The tunable architectures further influence the symmetry and polarization of tripeptide assemblies, thereby determining the piezoelectric performance of the crystals. Notably, IFF crystals exhibited the highest piezoelectric coefficient of 57.1 pC/N, which represents the highest predicted piezoelectric coefficient to date in pure oligopeptide crystals. Furthermore, piezoelectric nanogenerators based on IFF crystals demonstrated the ability to generate stable open-circuit voltages exceeding 2 V and maintained excellent durability over 10,000 press-release cycles. Additionally, the integration of IFF assemblies-based PENGs into insoles enabled piezoelectric footwear to sense and distinguish different gait signals during human movement, showing great potential in human health monitoring applications. This work systematically modulated the packing modes and piezoelectric properties of amyloid-inspired tripeptide assemblies through supramolecular engineering, opening valuable avenues for rational design of peptide-based materials with tunable piezoelectric properties.

Methods

Materials

All the solvents and chemicals are commercially available. Chemicals were used as received without any further purification. CTC resin, Fmoc-Phe-OH, Fmoc-Gly-OH, Fmoc-Ala-OH, Fmoc-Ile-OH, Fmoc-Leu-OH were purchased from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. 1-Hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBT), N,N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC), N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), 2-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU), pyrrolidine, dichloromethane, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), triisopropylsilane (TIS) and trifluoroacetic acid were purchased from Titan Technology (Shanghai, China). All chemical reagents had a purity greater than 95%.

General method for solid-phase synthesis of peptides

All the peptides were synthesized based on the solid-phase synthesis method, and all amino acids were purchased from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. Here, the synthetic method for the AFF was used as an example to show the synthesis of the peptides. CTC resin was pre-swelled in DCM for 1 h, then treated with a DCM solution containing Fmoc-Phe-OH and DIEA for 1.5 h. The resin was washed sequentially with DCM and DMF, followed by Fmoc deprotection using pyrrolidine/DCM (1:1) mixture and additional washing. The second amino acid Fmoc-Phe-OH was activated with DIEA, HOBT and HBTU in DMF, and reacted with the resin for 4 h. After washing, Fmoc deprotection was performed. The third amino acid (Fmoc-Ala-OH) was coupled and deprotected using the same procedure. The final resin was washed with anhydrous ethanol, dried, and stored at −20 °C. Cleavage was performed using TFA/Tis/H2O (95/2.5/2.5) for 1.5 h to remove the resin and side-chain protecting groups, followed by filtration, concentration, washing with ether for five times, and lyophilization to obtain the crude product. All synthesized peptides were purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using water and acetonitrile containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid as the mobile phase. NMR experiments were performed on a BRUKER AVANCE NEO 400 spectrometer, where 1H NMR spectra were recorded at 400 MHz.

Crystal preparation

All tripeptide single crystals used for testing were prepared through either solvent evaporation or vapor diffusion methods. Single crystals of LFF, FIF, AFF, FAF, and FFA were obtained through slow solvent evaporation from aqueous solutions (5 mg/mL). Specifically, 2 mL of each prepared solution was placed in glass culture bottles and allowed to evaporate with an open top, yielding crystals within three weeks. FFG, FLF, IFF, FFL, and FFI crystals were obtained from methanol-water mixed solutions. The prepared solutions of FFG (methanol/water = 9/1) and FFI, FLF (methanol/water = 1/1), 2 mL each, were placed in glass culture bottles sealed with Parafilm. Five holes were punctured in the Parafilm to facilitate slow solvent evaporation, resulting in crystal formation within two weeks. Similarly, IFF and FFL crystals were prepared using the same method under conditions of ethanol/water = 1/1 and acetonitrile/water = 1/1, respectively. GFF crystals were obtained through vapor diffusion method. Briefly, 500 µL of aqueous solutions (5 mg/mL) of GFF were placed in small vials, which were then placed inside larger glass containers containing substantial amounts of acetonitrile. The containers were sealed and left undisturbed, yielding crystals within one month.

Optical microscopy

The morphological characterization of all twelve crystal types was performed using an OLYMPUS IX71 microscope.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

The 1H NMR and 13C NMR characterization of the synthesized tripeptides were performed utilizing a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400 MHz and 101 MHz NMR spectrometer under standard conditions.

High-resolution mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry characterization of the tripeptides was conducted employing an Orbitrap high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system (UPLC-QE Plus ICS6000 instrument).

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)

PXRD analysis was performed using a Bruker D8 FOCUS diffractometer (Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) operating at 40 mA and 40 kV. The crystalline samples were first ground into fine powder using a mortar and a pestle. The powdered sample was then loaded onto the center of a clean sample holder and compressed with a glass slide to ensure a uniform surface before being mounted on the diffractometer stage. Diffraction patterns were collected in continuous locked-coupled mode over a 2θ range of 5–40°, with a scan speed of 0.2 seconds per step and an increment of 0.02°.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The dried crystal samples were ground into fine powder using an agate mortar and pestle. 2 mg of the tripeptide powder was thoroughly mixed with 200 mg of dried potassium bromide (KBr) powder and pressed into transparent disk under 10 MPa pressure. FTIR spectra were recorded on a spectrometer in transmission mode. Each spectrum was obtained by averaging 32 scans, and background correction was performed using a pure KBr disk.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffraction measurements were performed on a Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy and a Bruker D8 VENTURE equipped with a CMOS Photon II detector. Both instruments utilized Cu Kα radiation generated through a direct-drive rotating-anode system and focused via a Helios MX multilayer monochromator. Detailed crystallographic parameters and refinement statistics were summarized in Supplementary Tables 2–4, with comprehensive structural data available in the supplementary crystallographic information file (CIF).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) to predict the electromechanical properties of all twelve crystal structures. All calculations employed the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional with Grimme’s D3 van der Waals corrections, in conjunction with projector augmented wave (PAW) method. Structural optimization was achieved using a 600 eV kinetic energy cutoff for the plane-wave basis set and a 4 × 4 × 4 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh. The convergence thresholds were set to 10−6 eV for total energy differences and 0.001 eV/Å for residual forces on atoms. The piezoelectric response and dielectric properties were computed within the framework of Density Functional Perturbation Theory (DFPT), utilizing a 2 × 2 × 2 k-point sampling scheme while maintaining the same plane-wave cutoff energy. For energy harvesting applications, we report the absolute values of the piezoelectric strain coefficients (dij) and voltage coefficients (gij), as their signs merely indicate the polarity of charge generation under mechanical deformation. Structural visualization was performed using the VESTA software package.

Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) measurement

The piezoelectric properties were quantitatively characterized using piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) with a calibrated cantilever. Measurements were conducted in dynamic contact mode under ambient conditions, applying an AC driving voltage across the 0–10 V range. The effective piezoelectric coefficient (deff) was determined through linear regression analysis of the amplitude-voltage response.

Fabrication of the IFF assemblies-based PENG

A sandwich-structured piezoelectric nanogenerator (PENG) was fabricated using IFF crystal powder as the active piezoelectric material. The device architecture incorporated a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) substrate to provide structural support and ensure rigidity during mechanical testing. An intermediate layer comprising polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) embedded with IFF crystal powder, with the crystals compacted as much as possible to reduce the gaps between particles, ensuring tight contact between the piezoelectric crystals and the PDMS layer for enhanced mechanical coupling. A copper electrode covered the PDMS layer. The top layer was encapsulated with polyimide (PI) to ensure device sealing, while the layers were tightly bonded using adhesive tape to prevent separation and reduce triboelectric effects, ensuring stable mechanical coupling. The nanogenerator was mounted on a linear motor platform with electrodes connected to an electrometer, and piezoelectric testing was conducted under specific mechanical forces at 0.294 Hz.

Power density of the IFF assemblies-based PENG

To determine the power density, external load resistors ranging from 50 to 2000 MΩ were connected in series with the IFF-based piezoelectric device. Under a constant applied force of 70 N, the output voltage across different load resistances was recorded, and the corresponding power was calculated using the equation P = U2/R, where U is the measured voltage and R is the load resistance. The power density was then obtained by normalizing the power output to the effective device area.

Variable temperature powder X-ray diffraction (VT-PXRD) test

VT-PXRD patterns of IFF crystals were acquired by an Empyrean (PANalytical B.V.) in air using Cu Kα radiation (1.54056 Å, 40 mA, 40 kV). The VT-PXRD data were acquired over an angular range of 5–40° (2θ) in 0.02° steps with a scan speed of 5° min−1.

Gait monitoring using IFF assemblies-based piezoelectric device

For gait monitoring applications, a flexible device was fabricated by removing the PMMA substrate from the aforementioned sandwich-structured PENG. The sensor was secured to the heel region of an insole using polyimide (PI) tape, and copper leads were connected to an electrometer and data acquisition system for piezoelectric signal recording. This device was used to collect and analyze characteristic piezoelectric signals to distinguish gait patterns during different motion states.
