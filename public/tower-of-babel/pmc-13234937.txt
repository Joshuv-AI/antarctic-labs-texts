Mapping the landscape of state Medicaid policy
Authors: Grace Beals, Marissa Rivera, Jamila Michener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFederal changes in Medicaid policy are the subject of significant national attention and debate. In contrast, state-level Medicaid policies are more of a black box: difficult to keep track of and much less salient. These disparate attention and information contexts obscure the important ways that federal and state Medicaid policies compare.MethodsWe present original policy data that identify nearly 4000 proposed state Medicaid bills from 2018 to 2022.ResultsWe show that the recent and ongoing federal emphases on reducing Medicaid expenditures are misaligned with state policy efforts. Moreover, although current federal policy changes have openly eschewed a focus on equity, we find that state-level policies with an explicit emphasis on equity fare equally well (in terms of passage) as those without such an emphasis. Far from cutting their programs, states have consistently expanded access to Medicaid. When state Medicaid policy focuses on equity, it remains politically feasible.ConclusionAltogether, we find that many state Medicaid programs innovate and expand in attempts to offer vital services to their constituents. In an era of federal policy retrenchment, this raises concerns about states’ capacity to continue such efforts.Keywords: Medicaid, state policy, equity, federalism

Introduction

In May 2025, House Republicans passed a reconciliation bill (H.R.1) that included a national Medicaid work reporting requirement, restrictions on states’ ability to add automatic exemptions, a requirement that states impose mandatory cost-sharing, and a bevy of other measures designed to cause dramatic reductions in federal Medicaid expenditures.1

While recent discourse around Medicaid has rightly focused on the broad national implications of Congressional efforts to cut the costs of the program, the repercussions of these efforts will ultimately be borne by state Medicaid programs. Because of the intergovernmental structure of Medicaid, states must manage federal shifts in resources, regulations, and requirements—making difficult choices (including cutting or removing optional services, reducing enrollment, etc.) in order to balance their budgets. While states respond to federal dictates about Medicaid, they also regularly propose and enact their own Medicaid policies.

Despite states’ relative flexibility and freedom around enacting their own healthcare policies, there are a few comprehensive state-level Medicaid datasets that enable us to understand what states are doing with respect to Medicaid, and how their actions compare to federal policy approaches.

In this study, we report findings from a novel dataset of proposed state-level Medicaid bills from 2018 to 2022 (3783 bills). We find that Medicaid bills passed by state legislatures have been more likely to expand than contract Medicaid (Table 1), except in Republican controlled states (Figure 1), and while most state Medicaid policy does not focus on equity (Figure 2), legislation that does is as politically feasible as legislation without such a focus (Figure 3).

Contrary to the current federal efforts to reduce the scope of Medicaid, many states have focused on strengthening the reach and capacity of the program—enacting new services, increasing eligibility, or otherwise making Medicaid programs easier to access and afford. Moreover, in contrast to a sharp turn away from the goal of equity, some states have successfully enacted legislation centered around this goal: for example, passing bills that not only identify specific vulnerable groups but also create tailored programs designed to serve them and overcome existing disparities. Taken together, our results suggest that the federal government is not in tune with states’ decisions about their own Medicaid programs.

Methods

To systematically collect state-level Medicaid policy data, a team of 7 trained coders used Nexis Uni (a database for legal research, formerly Lexis Nexis Academic) to identify Medicaid-related bills introduced at the state-year level between January 2018 and December 2022. We located bills by doing a word search at the state level for “Medicaid.” Our search criteria located bills that explicitly referenced the term “Medicaid” at least once throughout a bill's tracking report provided by Nexis Uni ie, this includes “Medicaid” references in a bill's summary provided in the tracking report or tracking reports that might, for example, reference state agencies that contain “Medicaid” in the agency title. This approach is advantageous because it provides more focused and manageable search results from Nexis Uni's database. However, one disadvantage is that our database fails to capture Medicaid-related bills that do not explicitly reference the term “Medicaid” and instead use terms such as “medical assistance” or state-specific program names like “Medi-Cal.” We excluded bills that contained the word “Medicaid” but did not involve substantive changes to state Medicaid programs. This included bills that mentioned Medicaid in passing while referencing other policies but contained no provisions directly related to Medicaid, bills that were symbolic in nature and did not imply any specific policy change, and bills that were making non-substantive additions or changes to previous legislation. In total, this yielded 3783 bills, with at least 1 bill from nearly every state during the study period. We hand-coded bills based on a close reading of the complete bill text. We coded the following information: bill status (passed/not passed), bill intended impact (expanding/contracting/neither), bill equity reference (explicit mention/no explicit mention), bill primary and secondary subject (regulation/funding/services/eligibility/research/fraud), and the primary and secondary target population (beneficiaries/health institutions and actors/government agencies/other). (We erred on the side of interpreting coding categories as literally as possible. Hence, we only coded a bill as expanding when it contained explicit provisions related to expanding the benefits, eligibility criteria, resources, or any other aspect of Medicaid that necessitated including more people or investing more resources into the program. Likewise, we only coded a bill as contracting when it restricted previously available resources, reduced eligibility, or narrowed services covered. When there was a lack of clarity about whether a bill would be expansionary (eg, regulatory bills that were not explicitly meant to expand or contract), we coded those as “neither.”) To ensure that we had inter-coder reliability, each bill was coded separately by two trained coders (eg, double coded). Any and all discrepancies between coders were resolved in team meetings. Below, we briefly describe our coding schema. This schema is fully detailed in the appendix.

The primary subject/population of a bill was the subject/population that was of immediate or direct relevance. A bill's target populations included beneficiaries (both those currently enrolled and would be beneficiaries), health institutions/actors (providers, managed care organizations, and other health institutions), government agencies (both state and federal agencies), or other groups (eg, state residents). A bill's subject included regulation (the rules about how Medicaid functions), funding (the money spent on Medicaid), services (offering new services or changing existing ones), eligibility (who has access to Medicaid), research (writing reports, forming committees to gather information), and/or fraud (relating to fraudulent claims). For example, Alabama HB 432 expanded the program to provide “assistance to certain state residents.” Hence, its primary subject was eligibility, and its primary population was beneficiaries. Because it expanded access to Medicaid, its impact was coded as expanding.

For a bill to be labeled as equity-specific, it had to meet the two following conditions. First, the bill had to acknowledge an unequal outcome for a population. For example, a bill might discuss the lack of access to dental care for adults with disabilities. Second, it had to provide a remedy for that specific population. For example, providing reimbursement for dental services for adults with disabilities. In addition to these 2 necessary conditions, equity bills could also include a moral or value-based argument, although this was not sufficient to code a bill as referencing equity. In each case, we included the exact language from the bill text that made an equity-specific argument. For example, Florida SB 1498 (2018) targeted “vulnerable populations” who faced “serious barriers to access[ing] dental services,” including those in “early head start programs, homeless shelters, schools, and the special supplemental nutrition program for women, infants, and children.” Similarly, Rhode Island SB 2694 (2022) acknowledged that incarcerated individuals would not be able to successfully reenter society without having access to the same-day and next-day physical and behavioral health services and targeted this specific group to receive expanded access to Medicaid services.

Our statistical analysis relies on simple cross-tabulations of key coding categories (ie, variables). (The data used in the analysis only include bills that were passed or not passed by state legislatures, excluding 7 bills that were coded as “filed” or “approved by voters.”) (Missing values were dropped from each cross-tabulation.) To maximize transparency and clarity, we distinguish between patterns based on whether bills were proposed and enacted (passed) or proposed but never enacted (failed). Most of the bills that were proposed did not pass, so attending to this set of policies offers a comprehensive assessment of state policy agendas. At the same time, a more tailored look at only the bills that passed allows us to focus on the policies that states were actually able to enact. Our results and the discussion below reflect both outcomes: Table 1 and Figure 3 represent the total universe of bills proposed (the entire state policy agenda), while Figures 1 and 2 represent only those bills that passed (the universe of politically feasible bills).

To our knowledge, this is the first dataset with comprehensive information on individual state-level Medicaid bills. This is valuable not only because state-level Medicaid policy is a “black box” compared to federal Medicaid policy but also because states’ actions with regard to their own Medicaid programs do not follow national patterns. That is, far from cutting their programs, many states seek to expand access to Medicaid for their constituents. However, the data have two main limitations. First, our data collection ended in 2022; hence, we are unable to see state-level trends in Medicaid since then. In addition, our data are limited to legislative activity, meaning that we do not include executive orders or ballot initiatives. Despite these limitations, these data give us the clearest available evidence on state Medicaid policy.

Results

Characteristics of state Medicaid bills

State legislatures proposed 3783 Medicaid bills between 2018 and 2022 and were particularly active in 2019 and 2021, proposing 1069 and 944 bills, respectively, during those legislative sessions. In other words, over half (53%) of the bills in our 5-year dataset were proposed in those 2 years. In contrast, only 549 bills were proposed in 2022, and only 66 bills were enacted in 2020. This low enactment during the pandemic may be because our dataset does not include executive orders, which were more frequent during COVID-19. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics from our entire dataset. The count and percentage in the population and subject categories exceed 3783 and 100% because these categories include both primary and secondary groups.

Between 2018 and 2022, 84.2% of the total Medicaid bills proposed in state legislatures did not pass. The remaining 592 bills (15.6%) were successfully passed and signed into law. While this passage rate may seem low, it is in line with the average passage rates for both state House and Senate bills, which range from 13% to 20%.2 An overwhelming majority of the Medicaid bills proposed during this time did not contain an explicit equity reference (93.8%), with only 6.2% of bills explicitly referencing a bill's equity implications. More than half of all proposed state Medicaid bills (62.5%) were designed to expand spending on, access to, or services of Medicaid, compared to only 6.1% of bills that aimed to contract the program's scope or services. The remainder of the proposed bills, approximately 31%, neither expanded nor contracted Medicaid, instead making relatively small changes to existing bill text (eg, capitalizing certain words).

We identified four main target populations of proposed bills: government agencies, healthcare institutions, and actors, Medicaid beneficiaries, or other groups. Of these target populations, government agencies (73.2%) and healthcare institutions/actors (51.9%) were most frequently targeted for intervention. We use Schneider and Ingram's (1993:334) definition of target populations as the “persons or groups whose behavior or well-being are affected by public policy.3 Perhaps surprisingly, only 33.6% of Medicaid bills directly targeted Medicaid beneficiaries. In addition to these target populations, we identified 6 main subjects of proposed bills: eligibility, fraud, funding, regulation, research, and services. The subject of the bills varied considerably. A large proportion of bills had to do with services (39.5%), regulation (35.2%), and/or funding (31.4%), while substantially fewer bills discussed eligibility (19.1%), research (13.0%), and/or fraud (2.5%).

Intended impact and bill passage

Among all bills that were passed and signed into law, 59.8% expanded Medicaid, while only 3.6% contracted Medicaid. Similarly, when we look at the universe of proposed expansionary Medicaid bills, approximately 15.2% were passed by state legislatures. In contrast, only 9.4% of the total proposed contractionary Medicaid bills were passed. Although passing a Medicaid-related bill is uncommon (only 15.6% of the proposed bills during this timeframe were signed into law), bills that do pass are substantially more likely to be expansionary than contractionary.

In the context of partisan polarization at the state level, these patterns of expansion and contraction are likely to be contingent on state partisan configurations. To assess the relevance of partisan political dynamics, Figure 1, above, plots the percentage of passed contractionary and expansionary state Medicaid bills by party control of state government. Contractionary Medicaid bills were overwhelmingly passed by Republican controlled states. Among all passed bills that contracted the scope of Medicaid, 66.7% were enacted by unified Republican governments, while 14.3% were enacted by unified Democratic governments and 9.5% by states with divided governments. However, expansionary Medicaid bills were also most often passed by Republican controlled states 53.8% of the time. In contrast, Democratic controlled states were responsible for enacting 30.4% of all passed expansionary Medicaid bills, while divided state governments enacted 14.7% of all passed expansionary bills.

Equity considerations and bill passage

The partisan dynamics of Medicaid policymaking underscore potential challenges of advancing policies that have differential support across partisan lines or that touch on hot button political divisions. Equity-related bills are among such policies. Figure 2, below, plots the percentage of passed state Medicaid bills that are equity-specific and non–equity-specific and demonstrates that an overwhelming majority (93.4%) of passed bills failed to explicitly reference a bill's equity implications.

However, when comparing passage rates among equity-specific and non–equity-specific bills, we find that 17.2% of all equity-specific bills were passed by state legislatures compared to 16% of all non–equity-specific bills (Figure 3).

This finding underscores that equity-oriented Medicaid policy is politically feasible, despite being uncommon.

Discussion

States are important health care policy actors in the United States.4 Each state is tasked with not only implementing federal policies, but also with designing state-specific policies that can, and as we have shown, often do, reach a broader population of beneficiaries and/or offer more services. Despite the centrality of states to healthcare policy, state Medicaid policies remain a “black box,” and most scholarly attention is focused on national changes to Medicaid funding and policy. In this study, we used a novel dataset of state-level Medicaid bills introduced between January 2018 and December 2022: a universe of nearly 4000 proposed Medicaid bills.

Although we suspected that it would be particularly difficult for expansionary and equity-oriented Medicaid bills to pass, the data do not support this assumption. We found that 59.8% of Medicaid bills passed in state legislatures have expanded spending on, access to, or services of Medicaid, while only 3.6% of passed bills contracted the scope of the program. In addition, we find that equity-oriented bills do not appear to face disproportionate barriers to passage. In fact, 17.2% of the 227 equity-oriented bills were passed compared to 16% among the 3438 non–equity-oriented bills. Finally, we find that there are partisan patterns in Medicaid bill passage and impact. Republican controlled state governments were responsible for enacting nearly 54% of passed expansionary Medicaid bills, while simultaneously enacting two-thirds of passed contractionary Medicaid bills. This suggests that partisan prerogatives are important but not determinative of state policy choices. Even when partisan preferences would suggest a contractionary approach, state policy makers facing a complex landscape of need and constituent demand must sometimes advance expansionary policy. Most broadly, however, states pass relatively few Medicaid policies.

While the majority of states follow this general trend of passing few, but disproportionately expansionary, Medicaid bills, there are a handful of “standout” states. Notably, Utah passed more Medicaid bills than they failed: passing approximately 62% of all of the proposed bills. Other states (California, Indiana, and Washington) also had relatively high passage rates for expansionary bills, all exceeding the national average of 15.2%. Similarly, “equitable” bills in Kentucky and Utah had a higher passage than failure rate: 5% of all proposed, equitable bills passed in Utah (vs 1.7% which failed) and 2.5% passed (vs 1.7% failed) in Kentucky. While the vast majority of bills do not contain an explicit reference to equity, those that do remain politically feasible across the United States and perhaps are especially viable in some standout states.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the recent federal push to cut Medicaid in ways that are likely to exacerbate existing inequities, for example, by requiring cost-sharing or cutting services, is misaligned with what states have been doing. States consistently demonstrate a commitment to expanding Medicaid and at times enact bills explicitly focused on equity (Figure 3).

States can remediate some of the inequality-enhancing features of the federal Medicaid changes by (1) pursuing equity-promoting bills or by (2) inclusively designing cost-sharing and other policies. When states target underserved or vulnerable populations, they help the populations who would be harmed by federal budget cuts first. These populations often do not have alternative health care arrangements, so protecting their access to Medicaid through targeting program interventions is crucial. In addition, some states have specifically eliminated or changed cost-sharing arrangements. For example, Mississippi SB 2296 (2018) and SB 2814 (2020) respectively prorated cost-sharing requirements and limited cost-sharing requirements to only those individuals making over 300% of the federal poverty limit. Kentucky HB 298 (2021) went further and proposed the elimination of cost-sharing entirely.

Medicaid is an expensive and politically charged program, with distinct implications for racial, economic, and political equity.5-8 These challenges are often most acutely reflected via federal political battles and policy changes. However, states navigate their own fraught political, economic, and health landscapes. Taking state contexts into more careful consideration underscores important additional complexities. State policy reflects comparatively little focus on fraud, waste, or budget reductions—the articulated mainstays of federal Medicaid policy in 2025. Instead, states have directed much of their legislative energies toward expanding the reach and capacity of their Medicaid programs, with some states taking explicit steps to do so equitably.

Conclusion

As an intergovernmental policy, federal Medicaid funding represents almost 70% of total Medicaid spending. Major changes to federal Medicaid expenditures therefore compel states to make difficult choices in order to balance their budgets. While it is impossible to predict how state policymakers will respond to ongoing federal realities, a rapidly shifting federal context makes it an especially apt time to assess state Medicaid policies. Our novel dataset of Medicaid policies opens up the “black box” of state Medicaid policy. By observing what states have done in recent years—during times of both change and challenge—this study provides insight into state policy priorities and juxtaposes those priorities with federal policy efforts. Our data show that state autonomy can present real opportunities for Medicaid expansion and generosity.
