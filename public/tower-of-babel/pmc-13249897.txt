CLPX acquires an iron-sulfur cluster to sustain mitochondrial proteostasis in cancer cells
Authors: Chengquan Huang, Yixue Zhang, Han Gao, Yuxin Song, Shunchang Hu, Chaoyue Sun, Shiwen Duan, Dongxiao Ma, Xiumei Jiang, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms are crucial for protecting cells from the toxicity of misfolded protein accumulation. Although excessive stress is known to inactivate these mechanisms and thereby induce mitophagy in cancer cells, the detailed molecular mechanisms coordinating these mitochondrial quality control processes remain unclear. Herein, we identify CLPX, a mitochondrial protease subunit, as an iron-sulfur protein, which requires a [4Fe-4S] cluster to bind with CLPP to exert proteolysis function. Iron chelation impairs the assembly of the [4Fe-4S] cluster onto CLPX, thereby disrupting mitochondrial proteostasis maintenance and inducing mitophagy. Furthermore, cysteine deprivation caused by excessive reactive oxygen species accumulation hinders iron-sulfur cluster biosynthesis, thereby undermining CLPX function and inducing mitophagy. Our research elucidates an iron-sulfur cluster-dependent mechanism sustaining mitochondrial proteostasis.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Autophagy

Introduction

Mitochondria are an organelle with fundamental physiological functions1. Despite the existence of the Warburg effect, cancer cells have to rely on mitochondria to support their unlimited growth, invasiveness, and drug resistance2. According to previous studies, several mitochondrial quality control (MQC) mechanisms are utilized by cancer cells to sustain their survival under stress conditions, including mitochondrial fission, fusion, unfolded protein response (UPRmt) and mitophagy2. Among them, UPRmt, a series of proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms triggered by stress3, has been reported to relieve cancer cell mitochondrial stress to evade apoptotic cell death2. Nonetheless, under persistent or excessive oxidative stress, mitophagy, the MQC mechanism of degrading dysfunctional mitochondria to support cell survival4, will be utilized by cancer cells in place of these proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms as the last life-saving straw to avoid the fate of cell death2,5. However, why cancer cells undergo mitophagy instead of UPRmt as the survival-maintaining MQC strategy under excessive oxidative stress remains unclear, necessitating the identification of oxidative stress-sensing mechanisms coordinating the two MQC mechanisms.

Iron is an essential metal nutrient playing key roles in various biological processes6. For most mammalian cells, iron is primarily uptaken by transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) expressed on the cell membrane, reduced to ferrous iron inside the endosome, and then transported into the cytosol. Most of the intracellular iron will be stored inside a spherical ferritin shell composed of heavy (FTH1) and light (FTL) subunits or utilized. Mammalian cells possess two major iron utilization pathways, namely the biosynthesis of heme and iron-sulfur-clusters (ISCs)7. Among them, ISCs are ancient protein cofactors with versatile biological functions8. Studies have demonstrated that ISCs are a primary type of intracellular oxidative stress sensors9,10. Given the biological functions of ISCs, and that iron deficiency, which inevitably causes ISC deficiency, has been shown to induce mitophagy11,12, we herein explored whether and how mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms and mitophagy are regulated by ISC biogenesis.

Here, through establishing three cellular (namely, human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2, mouse hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Hepa1-6, and human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cell line KYSE-150) and two in vivo (Hepa1-6 allograft and KYSE-150 xenograft) iron-deficient cancer models, we first show that iron deficiency can indeed cause ISC deficiency in cancer cells. Interestingly, we then find that ISC deficiency disrupts mitochondrial proteostasis and triggers mitophagy in all the five models. In-depth molecular and biochemical assays reveal that ISC deficiency impairs the assembly of a [4Fe-4S] cluster onto the CLPX subunit of mitochondrial protease complex CLPXP, thereby inhibiting its interaction with CLPP and biological function. Additionally, we find that reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation could hinder ISC biosynthesis by depriving intracellular cysteine, thereby undermining CLPXP activity, disrupting mitochondrial proteostasis, and eventually causing mitophagy. Collectively, our findings elucidate an ISC-dependent mechanism maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis.

Results

Iron deficiency induced ISC deficiency

First, we tested whether iron-chelating treatment could cause ISC deficiency by treating HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells respectively with 350 μM, 220 μM and 250 μM DFX for 24 h. These concentrations of DFX treatment were determined based on IC50 values of the cells. As shown in Fig. 1A–C, DFX treatment notably reduced the viability, intracellular total iron, and mitochondrial ferrous iron levels of the three types of cancer cells. Enzymatic activity, native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (native PAGE), and Western blotting (WB) assays revealed that DFX treatment markedly decreased mitochondrial aconitase (m-aconitase), Complex I, and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) (Complex II) activities, without reducing the assembly of Complexes I and II and the abundance of m-aconitase, in the three cell lines (Fig. 1D–L, Supplementary Fig. 1). Since all these proteins (protein complexes) are mitochondrial ISC-dependent enzymes13, these findings verified that iron deficiency can indeed inhibit mitochondrial cancer cell ISC biosynthesis.

Iron deficiency induced mitophagy in cancer cells

Previous studies have revealed that iron deficiency could trigger mitophagy. To verify the mitophagy-inducing effects of iron deficiency treatment, we treated the three cancer cell lines and two in vivo cancer models with DFX and assessed the mitophagy level. The iron-deficient cancer cell models were established as mentioned above. For the establishment of Hepa1-6 allograft and KYSE-150 xenograft tumor models, 10 BALB/c-nu mice were subjected to cancer cell injection in their left lower abdominal region for each cell line. When the tumor volume reached 20-40 mm3, the ten mice injected with each type of cancer cells were equally divided into two groups to receive 20 mg/kg DFX or an equal volume of PBS (vehicle). The scheme lasted for 15 days. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 2A–C, following the 15-day DFX administration scheme, mice in the DFX treatment groups for the two in vivo models exhibited decreased tumor weight and tumor volume. Additionally, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2D–J, the mice treated with DFX showcased comparable body weights, red blood cell counts, hemoglobin concentrations, organ size, hematoxylin-eosin staining findings, and organ-to-body weight ratios relative to the controls, indicating that DFX treatment did not cause notable adverse effects. Furthermore, according to the WB results displayed in Fig. 2A–D, tumor tissues in the DFX treatment groups showcased increased TFR1 and decreased FTH1 protein levels, indicating decreased tumor tissue iron6. Consistent with these findings, iron quantification results for Hepa1-6 allografts and KYSE-150 xenografts also demonstrated significantly reduced tumor iron abundance following iron deficiency treatment (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2K). These findings indicated that iron deficiency treatment could decrease iron abundance and impair tumor growth in the two in vivo models. Next, we appraised if iron deficiency could induce mitophagy. As shown in Fig. 2A–D, the DFX treatment groups of tumor tissues expressed evidently increased PINK1, BNIP3, and LC3BII/I levels, which are hallmarks of mitophagy14. In the three cancer cell lines, iron deficiency treatment resulted in evidently stronger Mtphagy Dye fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2E), engulfed mitochondria (Fig. 2F), markedly increased levels of PINK1, BNIP3 and LC3BII/I (Fig. 2G, H). To further verify that iron deficiency could indeed induce mitophagy, we evaluated degradation of mitochondrial membrane proteins TOMM20 and COX4I1 and adopted the mito-Keima mitophagy reporter. As displayed in Fig. 2I–N, treatment with eukaryotic protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) and DFX caused significantly more evident TOMM20 and COX4I1 degradation compared with CHX treatment alone, demonstrating that DFX treatment promoted mitochondrial membrane protein degradation in the three cancer cell lines. Interestingly, according to proteomic results mentioned in the following section of this paper, increased degradation of TOMM20 and COX4I1 did not cause an evident reduction in their overall protein abundances. This can be ascribed to the complex expression regulation mechanisms for mitochondrial membrane proteins15. Fluorescence microscopic assays based on the mito-Keima reporter plasmid further indicated that DFX treatment markedly enhanced the intensity of red fluorescence while reducing that of green fluorescence in the three cancer cell lines, further confirming the occurrence of mitophagy (Fig. 2O–Q). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that iron deficiency treatment caused mitophagy in Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines. Notably, in the following sections, we exclusively utilized either Mtphagy Dye alone or in combination with PINK1/LC3B western blotting assays as the metrics for evaluating mitophagy. This is based on the following two considerations: (1) Mtphagy Dye has been widely adopted as the sole and direct indicator for assessing mitophagy in multiple previous studies16,17; (2) the technical incompatibility between CHX treatment or mito-Keima plasmid transfection with other overexpression or knockdown manipulations.

The above findings confirmed that iron deficiency could indeed induce cancer cell mitophagy. Nutritional deficiency and mitochondrial ROS accumulation are known to trigger mitophagy18,19. To test if iron deficiency triggered mitophagy by depriving cancer cells of nutrients or inducing mitochondrial ROS accumulation, we assessed the levels of intracellular ATP and mitochondrial ROS (mito-ROS) before and after iron deficiency treatment. However, iron deficiency did not markedly alter intracellular ATP and mito-ROS levels (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B) in the three cancer cell lines. The absence of significant alterations in intracellular ATP levels despite reduced activities of mitochondrial Complexes I and II can be ascribed to the high glycolysis rate that maintains ATP production homeostasis in cancer cells, namely the Warburg effect20. Furthermore, the addition of glucose or ROS scavenger N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) to the culture medium failed to rescue the viability of iron-deficient cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). Therefore, we ruled out the possibility that iron deficiency activated mitophagy through inducing nutrient deficiency or mitochondrial ROS accumulation in cancer cells.

Iron deficiency disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis in cancer cells

Excessive accumulation of mitochondrial misfolded proteins is a driving force of mitophagy2,21. To test if iron deficiency treatment promoted mitophagy at the cost of mitochondrial proteostasis, we assessed the level of mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation. Since misfolded proteins typically form insoluble aggregates22, we quantified the amount of insoluble mitochondrial proteins in Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines. As revealed by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assays, iron deficiency resulted in enhanced accumulation of mitochondrial misfolded proteins (Fig. 3A, B). Under physiological conditions, the presence of mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation will trigger increased expression of mitochondrial chaperones to facilitate the refolding of misfolded mitochondrial proteins, thereby restoring mitochondrial proteostasis3. Therefore, to investigate why mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms of cancer cells failed to rescue misfolded protein accumulation, we first assessed if the expression levels of mitochondrial chaperones were affected by iron deficiency treatment by measuring the mRNA expression of HSPD1 and HSPE122. The cells were treated with gamitrinib-triphenylphosphonium (GTPP), which is a potent HSP90 inhibitor, and then with DFX to test if iron deficiency could specifically blunt the adaptive transcriptional response of these mitochondrial chaperone genes. The results revealed that GTPP treatment enhanced the mRNA abundances of HSPD1 and HSPE1, whereas iron deficiency reduced their mRNA expression to the baseline levels (Fig. 3C). Consistently, our proteomic assays, which identified 7 differentially expressed mitochondrial proteins (DEMPs), failed to detect significant differences in the abundances of mitochondrial chaperones between the Control and DFX groups of HepG2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). WB assays confirmed that the mitochondrial abundances of HSPE1 and HSPD1 proteins were not significantly different between control and DFX groups of Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 4C–F). A previous study demonstrated that MRPS27 and NDUFS3 tended to form misfolded aggregates when the activity of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining machinery was inhibited23. Therefore, we also determined the abundances of MRPS27 and NDUFS3 in the mitochondrial soluble and insoluble fractions in Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines before and after iron deficiency treatment. The results indicated that the abundances of MRPS27 and NDUFS3 were significantly decreased and increased in the mitochondrial soluble and insoluble fractions, respectively, after DFX treatment in Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines (Fig. 3D–K). These results demonstrated that the responses of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms to mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation were blunted by iron deficiency treatment. Interestingly, the decreased abundances of NDUFS3, a Complex I subunit, in the mitochondrial soluble fractions did not affect the assembly of Complex I (Fig. 1G–L). This may be due to either the remaining soluble NDUFS3 being sufficient for the assembly of Complex I or the relatively high stability of Complex I.

The proper function of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining machinery requires intact protein synthesis and mitochondrial import machineries24. However, none of the 7 DEMPs identified by proteomic assays are implicated with cytosolic/mitochondrial protein synthesis or mitochondrial import. Consistent with proteomic findings, confocal fluorescence microscopic and flow cytometric assays revealed that DFX treatment did not evidently affect cytosolic/mitochondrial protein synthesis or mitochondrial protein import in the three cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 4G–R). Interestingly, the decreased abundances of MRPS27, a mitochondrial ribosomal small subunit, in the mitochondrial soluble fractions did not result in reduced mitochondrial protein synthesis. This may be due to either the remaining soluble MRPS27 being sufficient for the assembly of mitochondrial ribosome or the remarkably delayed manifestation of biological effects caused by MRPS27 deficiency that only become detectable as late as the ninth day following its down-regulation25.

Iron deficiency disrupted CLPXP-dependent mitochondrial proteostasis by impairing CLPXP assembly

Mitochondrial proteases are an integral part of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms3. There are four proteases responsible for unfolding and scavenging mitochondrial misfolded proteins, namely YME1L, AFG3L2, LONP1 and the CLPXP complex24. with the latter two being recognized as UPRmt proteases26. Among them, LONP1 functions as both a protease and a molecular chaperone to handle unfolded/misfolded mitochondrial proteins27,28, while CLPXP is a substrate-specific protease complex whose degradation products serve as the crucial mediators for activation of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms29–31. Additionally, MRPS27 and NDUFS3 had been identified as direct targets of CLPXP32. Therefore, we next investigated the activity/expression level of these mitochondrial proteases, with a particular focus on CLPXP. Herein, the activity of these proteases was first appraised through quantifying the degradation of their specific mitochondrial protein substrates, namely TIMM17A for YME1L, GHITM for AFG3L2, FASTKD2 for LONP1, and ERAL1 and GFM1 (also known as EFG1) for CLPXP33–36. The results showed that iron deficiency significantly impaired the degradation of ERAL (P < 0.001 for HepG2, P < 0.01 for Hepa1-6, P < 0.01 for KYSE-150) and GFM1 (P < 0.01 for HepG2, P < 0.001 for Hepa1-6, P < 0.05 for KYSE-150), whereas the degradation levels of other substrates were insignificantly affected (Fig. 4A, B). These findings indicated that iron deficiency undermined CLPXP activity. Therefore, we interrogated if the reduced activity of CLPXP was indeed responsible for the disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis in iron-deficient cells. We treated iron-deficient cancer cells with a CLPP agonist ZK53 (which forms a stable complex with CLPP through a π-π stacking effect that is crucial for selective ligand binding)37, overexpressed FLAG-tagged CLPP in these cells, or applied the two treatments simultaneously. Our results showcased that combined ZK53 treatment and FLAG-CLPP overexpression markedly enhanced the mitochondrial abundances of HSPD1 and HSPE1, alleviated mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation (Fig. 4C–E) and significantly decreased mitophagy level (P values were respectively less than 0.01, 0.01 and 0.001 for HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells at the highest ZK53 concentration) (Fig. 4F). These findings demonstrated that decreased CLPXP activity was the primary, if not the only, reason responsible for the disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis and enhanced mitophagy level in iron-deficient cancer cells. To investigate the mechanism underlying the decreased CLPXP activity, we first determined the protein levels of CLPX and CLPP subunits. Interestingly, iron deficiency did not decrease the mitochondrial abundances of CLPX and CLPP in HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells (Fig. 4G, H). Since the protease activity of CLPP largely relies on its interaction with CLPX38, we next explored if the interaction between CLPX and CLPP was affected by iron deficiency. Indeed, iron deficiency prohibited the interaction between CLPX and CLPP subunits in Hepa1-6 allografts, KYSE-150 xenografts, and the three cancer cell lines (Fig. 4I). Taken together, these data indicated that iron deficiency disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis primarily through impairing the assembly of CLPXP complex.

The CLPX subunit of CLPXP required a [4Fe-4S] to form the functional complex with CLPP

Next we explored the mechanism responsible for the impaired CLPXP assembly in iron-deficient cells. By analyzing CLPX and CLPP amino acid sequences, we found that CLPX contains an N-terminal KKX7KK motif, which was reported to bind with the iron-sulfur cluster transfer protein HSCB39, and two iron-sulfur cluster-coordinating CXXC motifs that are conserved among vertebrates (Supplementary Fig. 5), implying that CLPX may be an iron-sulfur protein40. Therefore, we overexpressed maltose-binding protein (MBP)-tagged CLPX in Expi293F cells cultured in the presence of 50 μM 57FeCl3 and 100 μM L-cysteine and purified the protein anaerobically with affinity chromatography. The purified protein exhibited a single band on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 5A) and was brown in color (Fig. 5B). Ultraviolet-visible spectrum of the purified protein contained an absorption shoulder around 420 nm (Fig. 5C), implying the presence of [4Fe-4S] cluster41,42. Therefore, we further performed Mössbauer spectroscopy, which is the golden standard for identification of iron-sulfur clusters43. As shown in Fig. 5D, the Mössbauer spectrum of purified protein exhibited a δ value of 0.45 mm/s and a ΔEQ value of 1.25 mm/s, confirming the presence of a [4Fe-4S] cluster.

Bacterial Clpx was found to be a zinc-binding protein44. Therefore, to rule out the possibility that the [4Fe-4S] cluster observed on human CLPX was a result of overexpression artifact in the context of supraphysiological levels of iron and cysteine, we also overexpressed MBP-tagged CLPX in Expi293F cells cultured without exogenous addition of 50 μM 57FeCl3 and 100 μM L-cysteine and purified the protein anaerobically with affinity chromatography. As shown in Fig. 5C, the CLPX protein overexpressed under physiological levels of iron, cysteine and zinc also exhibited the 420-nm absorption shoulder in its ultraviolet-visible spectrum. Additionally, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometric (ICP-MS) assays showcased that the CLPX protein overexpressed under physiological levels of iron, cysteine and zinc contained only iron, but not zinc (Fig. 5E). Additionally, we tested if exogenous zinc supplementation could mitigate iron deficiency-induced mitophagy and whether zinc deficiency treatment (by adding N,N,N’,N’-tetrakis(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine [TPEN]) could reduce CLPXP activity to induce mitophagy in cancer cells. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 6, exogenous zinc supplementation failed to alleviate iron deficiency-induced cancer cell mitophagy. Additionally, zinc deficiency treatment exhibited negligible effects on CLPXP activity or mitophagy level in the three cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, these data demonstrate that the [4Fe-4S] cluster observed on human CLPX was not an overexpression artifact and that human CLPX is an iron-sulfur protein that does not require zinc for its activity.

To further verify the presence of the [4Fe-4S] and identify the cluster-coordinating cysteines on human CLPX, we mutated all the conserved cysteines of CLPX to alanines and purified the mutants anaerobically. Afterwards, the ultraviolet-visible spectra and the stoichiometric numbers for iron and acid-labile sulfur were analyzed for wild-type and mutated CLPX proteins. According to data presented in Fig. 5F, G, each wild-type CLPX monomer contained around four iron atoms and four acid-labile sulfurs, whereas mutants C105A, C108A, C112A, and C130A did not possess the 420-nm absorption shoulder and bound reduced amounts of iron and acid-labile sulfur, further demonstrating that CLPX can bind one [4Fe-4S] and that the cluster is coordinated through Cys105, Cys108, Cys112, and Cys130 residues. Interestingly, as predicted by Alphafold, Cys105, Cys108, Cys112, and Cys130 could form a binding pocket, providing an ideal [Fe4S4] cluster binding site (Fig. 5H). Notably, using the method describe above, we also found that wild-type CLPX protein overexpressed in Expi293F cells treated with 100 μM L-cysteine and 100 μM DFX (DFX + CLPX WT) or in those treated with only 50 μM ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) [Cysteine(-)+CLPX WT] exhibited impaired [4Fe-4S] binding capability (Fig. 5F, G). Furthermore, knocking down the iron-sulfur-cluster biosynthesis scaffold protein ISCU8 in Expi293F cells (siISCU+CLPX WT) also prevented the binding of CLPX with the [4Fe-4S] cluster (Fig. 5F, G). Finally, a CLPX mutant whose KKX7KK motif was mutated to AAX7AA (Mut KKX7KK to AAX7AA) also lost its [4Fe-4S] binding capability (Fig. 5F, G). These results indicated that iron, cysteine (which provides the sulfur element for iron-sulfur biosynthesis), iron-sulfur-cluster biosynthesis machinery, and the KKX7KK motif that can potentially bind with the iron-sulfur cluster transfer protein HSCB45 are all necessary for CLPX to assemble the [4Fe-4S] cluster, further supporting that CLPX is indeed an iron-sulfur protein. Next, to verify the interaction between CLPX and HSCB and determine if KKX7KK is a bona fide HSCB-binding motif, we carried out co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments. As shown in Fig. 5I, CLPX could indeed bind with mitochondrial HSCB (m-HSCB). Co-IP experiments with FLAG/MYC-tagged (F/M) CLPX confirmed that the KKX7KK motif was indeed necessary for the interaction between CLPX and m-HSCB (Fig. 5J). Additionally, the AAX7AA mutant of CLPX also lost the interaction with CLPP (Fig. 5J). The quaternary structure of CLPX is crucial for its biological function38. Therefore, we explored if CLPX quaternary structure is affected by [4Fe-4S] cluster coordination by analyzing the quaternary structure of wild-type CLPX (WT), Mut C105A, Mut KKX7KK to AAX7AA, DFX + CLPX WT, and Cysteine(-)+CLPX WT with native PAGE. The results showcased that only CLPX WT hexamerized, whereas all the [4Fe-4S]-deficient CLPX proteins remained monomers (Fig. 5K). Consistent with the in vitro findings, DFX treatment was found to significantly decrease the ratios between hexameric and monomeric CLPX in all the three cancer cell lines (Fig. 5L, M). These results demonstrate that loss of the [4Fe-4S] cluster somehow impeded CLPX hexamerization and its assembly into the CLPXP complex with CLPP (Fig. 5N). Collectively, all these findings demonstrated that CLPX required a [4Fe-4S] cluster to form the functional complex with CLPP.

Impaired [4Fe-4S] cluster assembly onto CLPX disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis and promoted mitophagy in cancer cells

To verify if the loss of CLPX [4Fe-4S] cluster is indeed the reason responsible for the disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis and enhanced mitophagy level of cancer cells under iron-deficient conditions, we knocked down endogenous CLPX (siCLPX) and overexpressed the AAX7AA mutant of CLPX (CLPXmut) with impaired [4Fe-4S] cluster binding ability or the wild-type protein (CLPXwt) in the three cancer cell lines. WB assays showcased that CLPX knockdown decreased the abundances of HSPD1, HSPE1, MRPS27 and NDUFS3 in the soluble fraction and increased the abundances of MRPS27 and NDUFS3 in the insoluble fraction of cancer cells (Fig. 6A–C). Consistently, SDS-PAGE and BCA quantification assays confirmed that CLPX deficiency significantly promoted the accumulation of mitochondrial insoluble proteins in the three cancer cell lines (Fig. 6D, E). These findings indicated that CLPX deficiency could disrupt mitochondrial proteostasis in cancer cells. Next, we appraised if overexpressing exogenous CLPX proteins could rescue the mitochondrial proteostasis of CLPX-deficient cancer cells. As shown in Fig. 6A–E, overexpressing wild-type CLPX in CLPX-deficient cancer cells (siCLPX+CLPXwt) successfully restored their mitochondrial proteostasis, whereas overexpressing the AAX7AA mutant in CLPX-deficient cancer cells (siCLPX+CLPXmut) not only failed to produce such rescue effects but also exacerbated the mitochondrial proteostasis disruption to a certain extent. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that assembly of the [4Fe-4S] cluster onto CLPX is necessary for maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis in cancer cells. Next, we tested if impaired [4Fe-4S] cluster assembly onto CLPX could promote mitophagy in cancer cells. As exhibited by WB and flow cytometric findings displayed in Fig. 6F–H, CLPX deficiency significantly promoted mitophagy in the three cancer cell lines. Furthermore, the mitophagy-promotive effects of CLPX deficiency were counteracted by wild-type CLPX overexpression but augmented by AAX7AA mutant overexpression (Fig. 6F–H). Therefore, these data demonstrate that impaired assembly of the [4Fe-4S] cluster onto CLPX can indeed facilitate cancer cell mitophagy.

Yeast and mammalian CLPX proteins were reported to respectively activate and degrade ALA synthases, thereby affecting heme biosynthesis46,47. To rule out the possibility that CLPX overexpression per se may have affected mitophagy through disturbing heme biosynthesis, we directly overexpressed CLPX in the three cancer cell lines and assessed intracellular heme content and mitophagy level. Excessive ROS accumulation undermined CLPX function by depriving intracellular cysteine

Finally, we explored if the identified [4Fe-4S] cluster of CLPX is responsible for the utilization of mitophagy as the MQC mechanism by cancer cells under excessive oxidative stress. A previous study indicated that increased cancer cell ROS accumulation could promote the conversion of cysteine into reduced glutathione (GSH)48. Consistently, another investigation revealed that lysosomal cysteine will be consumed for GSH synthesis under oxidative stress in breast cancer cells49. Based on these previous findings, we hypothesize that increased intracellular oxidative stress can deprive cysteine, which is both a crucial building block of intracellular antioxidative systems and the source of sulfur element for iron-sulfur-cluster biosynthesis45,50, thereby inhibiting ISC biosynthesis and undermining mitochondrial proteostasis to induce mitophagy. To test this hypothesis, we first explored if cysteine deprivation can result in CLPX dysfunction to disrupt mitochondrial proteostasis in cancer cells. To establish cysteine deprivation cell models, HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells were maintained in a cell culture medium without cysteine for 48 h. As shown in Fig. 7A, B, cysteine deprivation treatment for 48 h evidently reduced the viability and intracellular cysteine contents of the three cancer cell lines. Enzymatic activity, native PAGE and WB assays demonstrated that cysteine deprivation markedly decreased the activities of m-aconitase, Complex I, and Complex II, without reducing the assembly of Complexes I and II and m-aconitase abundance, indicating defective iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis upon cysteine deprivation (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). As a result, the activity of CLPXP complex decreased (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B), which led to blunted responses of HSPD1 and HSPE1 to mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B), respectively decreased and increased abundances of MRPS27 and NDUFS3 in the mitochondrial soluble and insoluble fractions (Supplementary Fig. 10C, D), enhanced accumulation of mitochondrial misfolded proteins (Fig. 7C, D), and elevated mitophagy level (Fig. 7E) in the three cancer cell lines. These findings indicated that cysteine deprivation can indeed undermine mitochondrial proteostasis through inhibiting iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis in cancer cells. To further explore why cysteine deprivation impaired cancer cell ISC biogenesis, we characterized key factors affecting ISC biogenesis in control and cysteine-deficient cancer cells, including the expression of key ISC biogenesis proteins (specifically ISCU and NFS1), Fe2+ bioavailability, NADPH content51, and thiol-redox status (utilizing total sulfhydryl and glutathione [GSH] contents as indicators)52,53. As exhibited in Supplementary Figs. 10A and 10B, Supplementary Figs. 10E–H, although cysteine deprivation did not decrease ISCU and NFS1 abundances or Fe2+ bioavailability, it significantly reduced the contents of NADPH, total sulfhydryl and GSH. Therefore, cysteine deficiency not only directly reduced substrate amount for iron-sulfur cluster biosynthesis but also indirectly impaired this biological process through decreasing the contents of NADPH, total sulfhydryl, and GSH in cancer cells. Notably, cysteine deprivation did not evidently affect mitochondrial protein synthesis and import in the three cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Next, we explored whether ROS accumulation can deprive cysteine, thereby undermining CLPXP function and disrupting mitochondrial proteostasis. Treating HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells with 880 μM H2O2 for 24 h caused significantly decreased CLPXP complex activity, enhanced ROS accumulation and reduced cysteine contents (Fig. 7F–I). As a result, mitochondrial proteostasis was undermined (Fig. 7J, K, Supplementary Fig. 12) and mitophagy level elevated (Fig. 7L). Notably, mitochondrial protein synthesis and import were not markedly impacted by H2O2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 13). To further verify if the effects of H2O2 treatment on mitochondrial proteostasis and mitophagy were indeed mediated through cysteine deprivation and CLPXP complex inactivation, the three cancer cell lines were treated simultaneously with both H2O2 and cysteine (H2O2+Cys) or H2O2, ZK53 and CLPP overexpression (H2O2 + ZK53 + CLPP OE). As displayed in Fig. 7F–L and Supplementary Fig. 12, although the H2O2+Cys groups of cancer cells exhibited significantly increased ROS abundances, their CLPXP activity, cysteine contents, mitochondrial proteostasis and mitophagy level were restored to normal. These results indicated that the effects of increased ROS accumulation on mitochondrial proteostasis and mitophagy level were mainly mediated through reduced cysteine contents, ruling out the possibility that ROS directly oxidized iron-sulfur clusters to inhibit CLPXP activity. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 7F–L, although the H2O2 + ZK53 + CLPP OE groups of cancer cells exhibited significantly increased ROS abundances and decreased cysteine contents, their mitochondrial proteostasis and mitophagy level were restored to normal. These results indicated that the effects of increased ROS accumulation and cysteine deprivation on mitochondrial proteostasis and mitophagy level were mediated through reduced CLPXP complex activity. Additionally, both cysteine deprivation and ROS accumulation impaired the interaction between CLPX and CLPP (Fig. 7M) and significantly decreased the ratios between hexameric and monomeric CLPX (Fig. 7N, O) in all the three cancer cell lines. Taken together, these data demonstrated that excessive oxidative stress could deprive intracellular cysteine, thereby undermining CLPXP function, disrupting mitochondrial proteostasis, and eventually causing cancer cell mitophagy. Notably, the H2O2, H2O2+Cys and H2O2 + ZK53 + CLPP OE groups of cancer cells exhibited comparable mitochondrial abundances of HSPD1 and HSPE1 (Supplementary Fig. 12), suggesting that H2O2 treatment can modulate the expression of HSPD1 and HSPE1 via a CLPXP-independent mechanism.

Discussion

Herein, we identified mitochondrial protease subunit CLPX as an iron-sulfur protein with a [4Fe-4S] cluster. Under iron-deficient and excessive ROS accumulation conditions, iron-sulfur biosynthesis was impaired, impeding the assembly of the [4Fe-4S] cluster onto CLPX. Without the [4Fe-4S] cluster, CLPX was not able to assemble into a functional complex with CLPP, thereby resulting in disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis, mitochondrial misfolded protein accumulation, and cancer cell mitophagy (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results elucidate an iron-sulfur cluster-dependent mechanism maintaining cancer cell mitochondrial proteostasis.

UPRmt represents a series of transcriptional and translational events that are activated upon stress to maintain mitochondrial proteostasis. Unlike in Caenorhabditis elegans, where UPRmt mechanisms have been well established, mammalian UPRmt is conceptualized as a component of the integrated stress response, and this area remains comparatively understudied54. Despite the fact that the role and even existence of canonical UPRmt mechanisms remain largely underdetermined, the presence of mitochondrial stress will inevitably stimulate the expression of protein chaperones, which work with proteases such as LONP1 and CLPP to refold, unfold or degrade mitochondrial misfolded protein aggregates to maintain mitochondrial proteostasis26,55,56. Additionally, as mentioned above, although LONP1 is the principal protease handling unfolded/misfolded mitochondrial proteins28, the activity of CLPXP seems to be indispensable for the activation of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms29–31. In this study, we observed evident accumulation of mitochondrial misfolded protein aggregates, which is both a driving force for mitophagy and a result of mitochondrial proteostasis disruption, in iron-deficient, cysteine-deprived, and H2O2-treated cancer cells. Subsequent analyses revealed that the disrupted mitochondrial proteostasis under these conditions can be primarily ascribed to the prohibited assembly of the CLPXP complex. However, although our rescue experiments utilizing the CLPP agonist ZK53 and its overexpression plasmid showcased markedly alleviated misfolded mitochondrial protein accumulation and mitophagy, we can only conclude that reduced CLPXP activity is an important, but not the sole, reason responsible for misfolded mitochondrial protein accumulation and mitophagy under iron-deficient, cysteine-deprived, and ROS accumulation conditions, because of the widespread influences of these treatments on mitochondrial structure and function. This can be exemplified by our observation that the blunted responses of mitochondrial chaperones HSPD1 and HSPE1 to H2O2-induced misfolded mitochondrial protein accumulation cannot be rescued by cysteine supplementation or by combining ZK53 treatment with CLPP overexpression.

The CLPXP complex is composed of a protease subunit CLPP and an unfoldase subunit CLPX, with CLPP playing the proteolytic role and CLPX being responsible for substrate recognition and boosting CLPP proteolytic activity38. Among them, the clinical translation potential of CLPP agonizts and inhibitors has been extensively explored26, whereas research on CLPX remains relatively scarce. Interestingly, previous studies demonstrated that yeast CLPX can directly stimulate the activity of ALA synthase, whereas its mammalian counterpart could promote the degradation of ALAS146,47, the first and rate-limiting enzyme catalyzing heme biosynthesis in non-erythroid mammalian cells57. Since we characterized CLPX as an iron-sulfur protein, we also investigated the effects of CLPX overexpression on heme biosynthesis and mitophagy in iron-replete cancer cells, to exclude the confounding effects of heme biosynthesis disturbance on mitophagy. Our results indicated that CLPX overexpression did not significantly affect either mitophagy or non-erythroid heme biosynthesis, although it markedly decreased ALAS1 expression. The minimal influence of CLPX overexpression on intracellular heme content despite the significantly decreased expression of ALAS1 can be explained by the compensatory positive effects of CLPX on protoporphyrinogen IX oxidase activity and ferrochelatase abundance58. Although CLPX overexpression exhibited negligible effects on heme contents in non-erythroid cancer cells, the protein seems to be crucial for erythroid heme biosynthesis59. Therefore, it will be worthwhile to explore if CLPX is another node, in addition to ALAS26, ALA dehydratase60, and ferrochelatase61, that connects the two major intracellular iron utilization pathways of iron-sulfur cluster and heme biosynthesis in erythroid cells.

While the crystal structure of intact human CLPX protein remains unresolved, that of bacteria has been characterized, identifying bacterial CLPX as a zinc-binding protein44. Based on the following four sets of experiments, we herein concluded that human CLPX is an iron-sulfur protein, not a zinc-binding protein: firstly, UV-vis and ICP-MS assays showcased that human CLPX protein overexpressed under physiological levels of iron and cysteine contained iron but not zinc; secondly, exogenous zinc supplementation was not able to alleviate iron deficiency-induced cancer cell mitophagy; thirdly, zinc deficiency did not reduce CLPXP activity or induce mitophagy; fourthly, the mitophagy and mitochondrial proteostasis disruption caused by CLPX deficiency can be rescued by overexpression of wild-type CLPX, but not by overexpression of the AAX7AA mutant of CLPX, which affects the N-terminal KKX7KK motif of CLPX that is responsible for binding with the ISC transfer protein HSCB but has no known function in zinc acquisition45. The difference in metal-binding choice between human CLPX and its bacterial counterpart can be ascribed to any of the following three reasons. The first is that the aerobic purification of bacterial CLPX prohibited the identification of oxygen-sensitive iron-sulfur clusters, just like what happened to several iron-sulfur proteins initially misrecognized as zinc-binding ones60,62,63. The second is that emergence of the metabolically- and redox-active organelle mitochondria in eukaryotes necessitates key molecules implicated with anti-oxidative response being equipped with better redox capacity to sense and cope with oxidative stress, leading to the choice of redox-active iron-sulfur cluster45, rather than redox-inert zinc64, as a co-factor on CLPX. Consistent with this hypothesis, our observation that excessive oxidative stress could undermine CLPXP activity to induce mitophagy is a manifestation of the oxidative stress-sensing function of the [4Fe-4S] cluster on human CLPX. The third is that although a protein may exert similar functions within different species, it could function through different mechanisms of action or have different cofactor-binding preferences. One such example is ferrochelatase (FECH), which catalyzes the final step of heme synthesis61. While mammalian FECH was found to coordinate an iron-sulfur cluster, the cluster is absent in plant, yeast, and prokaryotic FECH homologs65. Intriguingly, a recently published research regarding the assembly and proteolytic mechanisms of human CLPXP revealed that full-length human CLPX protein overexpressed in bacteria without simultaneous isc operon overexpression and cysteine+iron supplementation was not able to stably hexamerize or bind with wild-type CLPP66. To obtain the CLPXP complex, the authors opted to over-express a truncated human CLPX protein losing the N-terminal 145-amino-acid domain that contains all the iron-sulfur cluster-coordinating cysteines and the KKX7KK motif. As a result, they failed to detect the [4Fe-4S] cluster. Simultaneously overexpressing a bacterial isc operon and supplementing iron+cysteine is an important prerequisite for reconstituting the clusters for most human iron-sulfur proteins expressed in bacteria67. Therefore, the findings of this research suggested that, although the N-terminal domain of CLPX proteins may not be directly implicated with its hexamarization, full-length human CLPX without the [4Fe-4S] cluster was not able to assemble into a stable hexamer and lost its interaction with CLPP, consistent with our conclusions.

Several questions associated with our study remain unanswered and warrant further investigation. Firstly, the mechanism by which CLPXP activity mediates the activation of mitochondrial proteostasis-maintaining mechanisms in cancer cells lies beyond the scope of this study and was thus not investigated. Secondly, it remains unclear how the [4Fe-4S] on CLPX affects its quaternary structure and interaction with CLPP. Despite our observations that iron deficiency, cysteine deprivation, or ROS accumulation inhibited CLPX hexamerization, and that CLPX monomers lacking the [4Fe-4S] cluster failed to form hexamers, direct evidence that the [4Fe-4S] cluster is required for hexamerization is still lacking. Additionally, the significance of our established molecular mechanism to other pathophysiological conditions, such as aging, remains unexplored. Finally, despite the essentiality of iron for all cells and the functional conservation of CLPX across diverse cell types, all the findings of this research were derived from three iron-replete cancer cell lines. As such, the generalizability of the established mechanism for iron-deficient cancer cells and non-cancerous cells still needs to be further substantiated. In spite of these limitations, our study elucidates an iron-sulfur cluster-dependent mechanism sustaining mitochondrial proteostasis.

Methods

Experimental models

Cell lines

The human liver cancer cell line HepG2 (HB-8065, American Type Culture Collection, ATCC), mouse liver cancer cell line Hepa1-6 (CRL-1830, ATCC), human esophageal cancer cell line KYSE150 (SNL-344, Suncell) and embryonic kidney cell line Expi293F (A14528, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were utilized in this study. All cell lines have been verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination. HepG2 and Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, MA0212, MeilunBio) containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin-amphoteric

-in B solution (P/S/A, C0224, Beyotime Biotechnology) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 086150, Wisent), KYSE-150 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640, MA0548, MeilunBio) containing 1% P/S/A and 10% FBS, while Expi293F cells were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (A1435102, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For inhibition of protein synthesis, the cells were treated with 50 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX, S35852, Yuanye Biotechnology) for 12 h. For gamitrinib-triphenylphosphonium (GTPP) treatment, the cells were incubated with 15 μM GTPP (GC33030, GlpBio) for 12 h before being subjected to DFX treatment. For zinc supplementation, ZnCl2 was added into the cell culture media at a concentration of 100 μM for a 24-h incubation. For zinc deficiency treatment, N,N,N’,N’-tetrakis(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine (TPEN, 0.625 μM, HY-100202, MedChemExpress [MCE]) was utilized to treat the three cancer cell lines for 12 h. For cysteine deficiency [cysteine(-)] treatment, a high-glucose DMEM (DM150210, Pricella) without cysteine supplement was utilized in this study. For H2O2 treatment, all the cell lines were cultured with 880 μM H2O2 for 24 h. The cell culture medium was refreshed every 2-3 days. All cells were incubated in a 37 °C, humidified incubator filled with 5% CO2 and sub-cultured when ≥80% confluency was reached.

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted after the Ethics Committee of Northeast Normal University granted approval (Approval No. 202402076). C57BL/6 J (SPF Biotechnology) and BALB/c-nu (SPF Biotechnology) mice were utilized to establish our in vivo cancer models. The mice were housed on a 12-h dark/12-h light cycle and had unlimited access to food and drink. For establishing the allograft and xenograft tumor models, 5 ×106 Hepa1-6 cells and 1×106 KYSE-150 cells were respectively injected into C57BL/6 J and BALB/c-nu mice at their left lower abdominal region. When the tumor volume reached 20-40 mm3, calculated with the formula as follows: tumor volume (mm3) = (the longest diameter) (mm) × (the shortest diameter) (mm)/2, five mice for each model were grouped to receive either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, D8371, Solarbio, 200 μL 2.5% DMSO in PBS) or deferasirox (DFX, B34600, Yuanye Biotechnology, 20 mg/kg in 200 μL 2.5% DMSO) treatment through oral gavage once every two days. The scheme lasted for 15 days, during which the body weight of mice was monitored. After the 15-day period, blood samples were collected from the mice. Afterward, the mice were euthanized for the collection of organs (including spleen, kidney and liver) and tumor tissues. The blood samples were tested for blood cell counts, the organs were weighed and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining, while the tumor tissues were measured for weight, volume, expression levels of mitophagy-associated proteins and total iron content.

Plasmids and mutagenesis

Unless otherwise specified, the plasmids used in this study were customized and purchased from Applied Biological Materials (ABM). For constructing plasmids expressing CLPX mutants (see Supplementary Table 3 for details), the Phusion Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (F541, ThermoFisher Scientific) was utilized. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primers for mutagenesis were customized and synthesized by Comate Bioscience Co., Ltd.

Western blotting (WB)

Cell samples or tumor tissue specimens were washed in pre-cooled PBS and lysed in RIPA Lysis buffer (PC101, Epizyme) containing protease inhibitor phenylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, T0789, Target Molecule Corporation). The protein concentration of the lysates was quantified by the Protein Quantification Kit (KTD3001, Abbkine Scientific), after which the samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) with gels prepared using the Omni-Easy™ One-step Color PAGE Gel Rapid Preparation Kit (PG210, Epizyme). The separated proteins were then electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.22 μm, WJ002S, Epizyme). The membranes were then immersed in 5% skim milk (PS112L, Epizyme) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, subjected to incubation in diluted primary antibodies (see Supplementary Table 1 for details) for 2 h at ambient temperature or 4 °C overnight, and then in diluted horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at ambient temperature. The membranes were rinsed three times (5 min each) before and after secondary antibody incubation. Finally, Chemistar™ High-sig ECL Western Blotting Substrate (180-501, Tanon) was employed for target band visualization utilizing a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescent imaging system. To enable comparative quantification of band intensities across biologically independent replicates, these samples were analyzed on the same membrane, with subsequent densitometric analysis being carried out utilizing Image J v.1.53.

Blood cell counting

Blood samples were collected from mouse eyeballs with heparinized tubes and immediately subjected to blood cell counting with a Bc-5300 Vet automatic hematology analyzer (Mindray).

H&E staining

The organs harvested from mice were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, P1110, Solarbio) for 24 h, after which they were embedded in paraffin, processed into 5-μm-thick sections, and stained with a Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (C0105S, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the protocol provided with the kit. All sections were examined under a Leica DM2500 & DM2500 LED optical microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Iron level assays

Tumor tissue and cell lysates were prepared as describe above. Total iron levels within the lysates were quantified using a Total Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K772-M, Elabscience) for tumor tissues and a Cell Total Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K880-M, Elabscience) for cells following the producer’s protocols. The two kits provide a reducing agent that reduces intracellular ferric iron to ferrous iron, following which the ferrous iron reacts with the ferene molecule to form a stable complex with 593-nm optical absorption. The data were expressed as μmol Fe/kg wet tissue weight (for tumor tissues) or nmol/106 cells (for cells).

Cell viability assay

HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (Corning) at a concentration of 2×104 cells/well. Following various treatments, their viability was determined by the CCK-8 assay kit (MA0218, meilunbio). Briefly, the cell culture medium was replaced by complete culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After an incubation period of 1–2 h, the optical absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to observe mitophagy in HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells. Briefly, these cells were first fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (R20510, Yuanye Bio Technology) for 12 h at 4 °C, and then in 1% osmium tetroxide (201030, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h. After dehydration with acetone, the samples were embedded with epoxy resin and sectioned into ultrathin (<100 nm) slides. The sections were then double-stained with 1% lead (II) citrate tribasic trihydrate (15326, Sigma-Aldrich) and 2% alcoholic uranyl acetate (SPI-02624, Head Biotechnology) and examined under a JEM1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL).

Mitophagy assays

A flow cytometric strategy based on Mitophagy Dye (MD01, Dojindo) was adopted for evaluating mitophagy in HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells. Specifically, the cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in ice-cold PBS, and incubated with 100 nM Mtphagy Dye (which stains mitochondria undergoing mitophagy) of the kit for 20 min. Afterwards, the stained cells were analyzed with a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Colter) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 655-730 nm, respectively. Treestar FlowJo v.10.6.2 was employed for data analysis.

Confocal fluorescence microscopic assays based on the mito-Keima reporter plasmid were performed to evaluate mitophagy. Briefly, the cells were seeded in confocal cell culture dishes (Corning) at a density of 1×105 cells per dish. After overnight culture, they were transfected with the mito-Keima plasmid. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were treated with DFX for an additional 24 h. Following treatment, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (C1022, Beyotime) for 15 min. The red and green fluorescent signals of mito-Keima were respectively excited at wavelengths of 561 nm and 438 nm and visualized under a Leica LSM980 confocal fluorescence microscope to assess mitophagy. The images captured were further processed with ZEN 2.6 (blue edition) software.

The degradation of mitochondrial membrane proteins COX4I1 and TOMM20 was appraised as another readout for mitophagy. Briefly, the three cancer cell lines were maintained in 6-well plates at 4×105 cells per well. After pretreatment with 50 µg/mL CHX for 12 h, the cells were cultured for 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours in media supplemented with CHX alone (Control) or with both CHX and DFX (DFX). Afterwards, the cells were harvested and subjected to WB assays as mentioned above to quantify the amounts of COX4I1 and TOMM20 remained.

Isolation of mitochondria from cells and tissues

For isolation of high-purity intracelluluar mitochondria, we utilized the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (HY-K1060, MCE) for Cultured Cells. Briefly, 2×107 cells were collected and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 1 mL of Mitochondria Isolation Reagent containing 1 mM PMSF and incubated in an ice bath for 15 min. After removing cell debris by centrifugation at 4 °C, 600 g for 10 min, the supernatant was subjected to centrifugation again at 4 °C, 11,000 g for 10 min to obtain mitochondria.

For isolation of high-purity tissue mitochondria, we utilized the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (HY-K1061, MCE) for Tissue. Briefly, 50 mg of fresh tumor tissues were cut into small pieces and lysed in 500 μL Mitochondria Isolation Reagent containing 1 mM PMSF in an ice bath for 10 min. After removing cell debris by centrifugation at 4 °C, 600 g for 5 min, the supernatant was subjected to centrifugation again at 4 °C, 11,000 g for 10 min to obtain mitochondria.

Preparation and quantification of insoluble mitochondrial fraction

Briefly, 30 µg extracted mitochondria were resuspended in an MTE buffer (270 mM D-mannitol, HY-N0378, MCE; 10 mM Tris, T90537, Yuanye Biotechnology; 100 μM EDTA, S30021, Yuanye Biotechnology) containing 1 mM PMSF. Digitonin (HY-N4000, MCE) was then added into the suspension to a final concentration of 1%, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C, 12,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected as the soluble mitochondrial fraction, while the pellet was the insoluble fraction.

For quantification of the insoluble mitochondrial fraction, we utilized Coomassie blue staining and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein concentration assay. For both assays, the same number of cells from different treatment groups were subjected to the preparation of insoluble mitochondrial fractions as described above. For Coomassie blue staining, the insoluble fraction was resuspended in pre-cooled RIPA Lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF, mixed with 1/4 volume of 5× Omni-Easy™ Protein Sample Loading Buffer (Denaturing, Reducing, LT101, Epizyme), separated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by Coomassie Blue Staining Solution (P0017B, Beyotime Biotechnology).

For BCA protein quantification assay, the insoluble fraction was resuspended in pre-cooled RIPA Lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and analyzed by the Protein Quantification Kit according to the provided protocol.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the TRIzol reagent (YY101, Epizyme), and cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the 5× All-In-One RT MasterMix (G490, ABM) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR assays were performed using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 2 and BlasTaq™ 2× qPCR MasterMix (G891, ABM) on a LightCycler® 480 II 216 system (Roche Diagnostics). Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were determined by the 2-ΔΔCt algorithm, with human or mouse β-actin gene serving as the internal reference.

Mitochondrial proteomic assays

To identify differentially-expressed mitochondrial proteins (DEMPs) in untreated and DFX-treated HepG2 cells, tandem mass tag-based mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) assays were carried out on six human HepG2 cell samples, including three biological replicates (n = 3) per group, strictly following the procedures and parameters detailed previously68. Proteins were extracted, quantified by the BCA method, and digested with trypsin. In detail, equal amounts of proteins from each sample were taken for enzymatic digestion. The volumes were adjusted to the same level with the lysis buffer. One volume of pre-cooled acetone was added and vortexed, followed by the addition of four volumes of pre-cooled acetone, and the protein was precipitated at -20 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 4500 g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was washed 2-3 times with pre-cooled acetone. The precipitate was air-dried, resuspended in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), and dispersed by sonication. Trypsin was added at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w), and the digestion was performed overnight. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was then added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and the sample was reduced at 56 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, iodoacetamide (IAA) was added to a final concentration of 11 mM, and the sample was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The digested peptides were desalted with Strata X C18 (Phenomenex), labeled using a TMT10-plexTM kit (90406, Thermo Fisher Scientific), fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) utilizing an Agilent Extend 300 C18 column (5 μm particle size, 4.6 mm inner diameter, 250 mm length), and analyzed on an EASY-nLC 1200 ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In detail, mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 90% acetonitrile in water. The LC gradient was set as follows: 0-36 min, 7-23% B; 36-52 min, 23-32% B; 52-56 min, 32-80% B; 56-60 min, 80% B, at a constant flow rate of 500nL/min. After UPLC separation, the peptides were injected into an NSI ion source for ionization and then analyzed by a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. The ion source voltage was set to 2,100 V. Both precursor and fragment ions were detected and analyzed using the high-resolution Orbitrap. The full MS scan range was set to 350-1600 m/z with a resolution of 120,000, and the MS/MS scan started from a fixed first mass of 100.0 m/z with a resolution of 30,000. Data were acquired using a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, in which the top 15 most abundant precursor ions from the full scan were sequentially selected for fragmentation in the HCD collision cell at a normalized collision energy of 28%, followed by MS/MS analysis. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 1.0×105, the intensity threshold was set to 8.3×104 ions/s, the maximum injection time was set to 60 ms, and the dynamic exclusion time for tandem mass spectrometry scans was set to 30.0 s to avoid repeated scanning of the same precursor ions. The mass spectrometry data were searched against the Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20230103.fasta (20,389 sequences) database using MaxQuant (v1.6.15.0). A decoy database was added to estimate the false discovery rate (FDR) caused by random matches, and a common contaminant database was included to eliminate the influence of contaminant proteins. The enzyme specificity was set to Trypsin/P, with a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. The minimum peptide length was set to seven amino acid residues, and the maximum number of modifications per peptide was set to five. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set to 20ppm for the first search and 4.5ppm for the main search, and the fragment ion mass tolerance was set to 20ppm. Carbamidomethyl (C) was set as a fixed modification, and variable modifications included methionine oxidation, protein N-terminus acetylation, and deamidation (NQ). The quantification method was set to TMT-10plex. The FDR values for protein and peptide spectrum match (PSM) identifications were all set to 1%. To obtain high-quality analytical results, the search results were further filtered. The identification accuracy FDR was controlled at 1% at the spectrum, peptide, and protein levels. Each identified protein was required to contain at least one unique peptide. The mass spectrometry data were further subjected to quality control evaluation in the following aspects: peptide length distribution, peptide count distribution, protein coverage distribution, and protein molecular weight distribution. Three analysis methods, namely Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC), principal component analysis (PCA), and relative standard deviation (RSD), were employed to evaluate the repeatability of biological replicates. Functional enrichment was performed with Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.05). The cutoff |log2(fold change)| and unpaired t test p values for defining DEMPs in this research were respectively set to 0.5 and 0.05.

Protein synthesis assays

To test if protein synthesis in the three cancer cell lines was affected by DFX, cysteine deficiency or H2O2 treatment, a BeyoClick™ HPG Protein Synthesis Kit with AF594 (P1209S, Beyotime Biotechnology) was employed. The kit determines the level of protein synthesis through appraising the fluorescence intensity of fluorescently-labeled azide (Azide 594), which undergoes a click reaction with methionine analog L-homopropargylglycine (HPG) that is incorporated into newly synthesized proteins. For the determination of total protein synthesis level, the protocol provided by the manufacturer was followed. For determination of mitochondrial protein synthesis level, the cells were first treated with 100 μg/mL emetine for 10 min to inhibit cytoplasmic protein translation before application of the kit69. Confocal fluorescence microscopic and flow cytometric strategies for fluorescence intensity determination were carried out as described above.

Mitochondrial protein import assays

We strictly followed a protocol described previously for the evaluation of mitochondrial protein import levels70,71. The MTS-EGFP plasmid was obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform and transfected into the there cancer cell lines with the RFect Plasmid DNA Transfection Reagent (21014, Biodai) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cancer cells transfected with the MTS-EGFP plasmid were subsequently stained with Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (C1049B, Beyotime Biotechnology) and observed under the Leica LSM980 confocal fluorescence microscope. Afterward, six randomly selected fields from each group of cells were subjected to color intensity analyses with ImageJ v.1.53 for determining the relative intensity ratios of yellow (which represents MTS-EGFP localized within mitochondria) to green (which represents total MTS-EGFP expressed in cells) areas.

Plasmid transfection and ZK53 treatment

For transfection of CLPP overexpression plasmid (see Supplementary Table 3 for details) into HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE150 cells, the cells were seeded into 6-well plates (Corning) at a concentration of approximately 4 × 105 cells per well. The next day, the CLPP-overexpression plasmid (ABM), along with the corresponding empty vector [which served as the negative control (NC)], was transfected into the cells utilizing the RFect Plasmid DNA Transfection Reagent (21014, Biodai) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. DFX treatment was applied on the day of transfection. Twenty-four hours after transfection, ZK53 (HY-149677, MCE) was added into the cell culture medium to indicated concentrations. The cells were harvested 24 h after ZK53 supplement for downstream analyses.

Overexpression and anaerobic purification of maltose-binding protein (MBP)-tagged human CLPX proteins

The recombinant pCMV-CLPX-MBP plasmids (see Supplementary Table 3 for details) expressing MBP-tagged wild-type and mutant CLPX proteins were transfected into Expi293F cells utilizing RFectSP Plasmid DNA Transfection Reagent (21026, Biodai) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. For Mössbauer spectroscopic analysis, the Expi293F cells expressing wild-type CLPX protein were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium supplemented with 50 μM 57FeCl3 (701122, Sigma-Aldrich) and 100 μM L-cysteine (C7352, Sigma-Aldrich). Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed with RIPA Lysis buffer containing PMSF in a glove box with <0.2 ppm O2. For affinity purification, the cell lysates were passed through a column filled with amylose resin (E8021S, NEB) and the captured MBP-tagged proteins were eluted with a column buffer (150 mM NaCl, S9888, Sigma-Aldrich and 10 mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0) containing 15 mM maltose. These procedures were also applied for preparing protein samples for ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopic and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometric (ICP-MS) analyses, with the only difference being that the transfected Expi293F cells were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium supplemented with 50 μM FAC (F5879, Sigma-Aldrich) and 100 μM L-cysteine.

Spectroscopic assays

UV-vis spectroscopy

UV-vis spectra of the purified CLPX proteins were collected with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer utilizing 2 μL of 100 μM protein samples, with the aforementioned elution buffer serving as the blank control. The data of UV-vis spectroscopic analyses were then converted into molar extinction coefficients.

ICP-MS

For ICP-MS assays, 25 µL 50 μM protein samples were digested at 98 °C overnight in a mixture of hydrochloric acid and nitric acid (3:1). Afterwards, the samples were analyzed by an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS system. Four replicates were tested for each sample, with 25 µL 100 μM purified MBP serving as the blank control.

Mössbauer spectroscopy

Immediately after affinity purification, 400 µL of 500 μM wild-type CLPX protein containing 57Fe was snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen inside a pre-cooled Mössbauer cup. The spectrum (Seeco Research) was collected with an Oxford MS-500 Mössbauer spectrometer under an external 53-mT magnetic field with an orientation paralleling that of γ beam propagation. WMOSS spectral analysis software (www.wmoss.org) was employed for spectrum simulation.

Acid-labile sulfur quantification

The sulfur element within the [4Fe-4S] was quantified as acid-labile sulfur using a previously described method with minor modifications72. Briefly, 100 µL of 50 μM purified CLPX proteins were mixed thoroughly with 300 µL of a 2% zinc acetate solution (Z0625, Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 µL 10% NaOH (221465, Sigma-Aldrich) by gentle vortexing. After incubation at ambient temperature for 1 h, the mixture was added with 100 µL 0.1% N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DMPD, D4139, Sigma-Aldrich) in 5 M HCl and 100 µL of 20 mM FeCl3 in 6 M HCl. The mixture was then incubated at ambient temperature for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 for 10 min. Afterwards, the supernatant was measured for 670-nm optical density (OD600) with the Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader. The OD600 values of the protein samples were interpreted with a standard curve established with Na2S·9H2O.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The Classic Magnetic Protein A/G IP/Co-IP Kit (YJ201, Epizyme) and EZview™ Red ANTI-FLAG® M2 Affinity Gel (F2426, Sigma-Aldrich) were utilized for Co-IP assays in this study. Briefly, whole cell lysates or mitochondrial fractions were subjected to a 3-h incubation with anti-CLPP-antibody-conjugated beads or the anti-FLAG beads, after which the captured protein complexes were eluted with the elution buffer provided with the Co-IP kit (for anti-CLPP-antibody-conjugated beads) or a Tris-glycine solution (pH 2.8, for anti-FLAG beads). The eluates were subsequently analyzed by WB. We adopted 5% whole-cell lysates or 10% mitochondrial fractions as inputs, while the samples incubated with mouse or rabbit IgG served as negative controls.

Native PAGE analysis

Native PAGE analysis was performed for determining the quaternary structure of anaerobically-purified CLPX proteins and evaluating hexameric/monomeric CLPX ratios in cancer cell lysates. Briefly, the protein samples were mixed with NativePAGETM 4× Sample Buffer (BN2003, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated with NativePAGETM Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels (BN1002BOX, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 4 to 16%, 1.0 mm. NativeMARKTM Unstained Protein Standard (LC0725, Thermo Fisher Scientific) served as the molecular weight marker. The voltage for electrophoresis was set to 65 V during the initial 0.5 h and maintained at 80 V thereafter. The subsequent blotting procedures were the same as described above.

Analysis of mitochondrial Complexes I and II assembly was performed following a previously published protocol73. Briefly, isolation of high-purity intracellular mitochondria was performed as described above. Afterwards, the mitochondria were solubilized for 30 min on ice with a lysis buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 1 mM EDTA, 5 mg/mL dodecyl maltoside (DDM), and 1 mM PMSF. The lysates were centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C, 12,000 rpm, and 120 µg mitochondrial proteins were subjected to blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE). The cathode buffer was 50 mM glycine, 15 mM Bis-Tris, 0.02% Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250, pH 7.0; the anode buffer was 50 mM Bis-Tris, pH 7.0. The voltage for electrophoresis was set to 80 V during the initial 0.5 h and maintained at 170 V thereafter. The entire electrophoresis process was performed in an ice bath. Afterwards, mitochondrial Complexes I and II were immunoblotted with specific antibodies (anti-NDUFS3 for Complex I74 and anti-SDHA for Complex II73) or visualized by Coomassie blue staining as described above.

Intracellular heme content assays

For heme content assays, 2 × 106 cells were utilized. Two methods were adopted for heme content assays. For the first method, a Heme Microplate Assay Kit (abs580148-96T, Absin) was employed, following the protocol provided with the kit. The kit is based on an improved aqueous alkaline solution method, through which heme can be converted into a product with optical absorption at 505 nm. For the second method, the protocol described in a previous paper of our research group was adopted68. Briefly, whole-cell lysates were prepared and subjected to protein concentration quantification as described above. Subsequently, 50 µg protein sample was mixed thoroughly with 500 µL oxalic acid (2 M) and divided into two equal aliquots. One was incubated for 30 min at 100 °C, and the other remained unheated to correct for background porphyrin fluorescence. The samples were equilibrated to room temperature for fluorescence measurement (Ex/Em=400/662 nm) with the Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader.

Intracellular zinc content assays

Briefly, 5 × 106 cells were lysed as mentioned in the “Western blotting” subsection, quantified for protein concentration with the Protein Quantification Kit (KTD3001, Abbkine Scientific), digested at 98 °C overnight in a mixture of hydrochloric acid and nitric acid (3:1), and finally subjected to ICP-MS as mentioned above.

CLPX knockdown and overexpression

For CLPX knockdown, we transfected siRNA mixtures targeting human/mouse CLPX and their negative controls (siNC, siRNAs with scrambled sequences), which were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., into the three cancer cell lines utilizing the RFect siRNA/miRNA Transfection Reagent (11012, Biodai) following the provider’s protocol. At the same time, we transfected plasmids overexpressing wild-type CLPX (CLPXwt) and an AAX7AA mutant of CLPX (CLPXmut) and the corresponding empty vector (Vector), which were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform, into the cancer cells with the RFect Plasmid DNA Transfection Reagent (21014, Biodai). Forty-eight hours later, the cells were collected for subsequent analyses.

Intracellular cysteine content assay

Intracellular cysteine content after H2O2 and cysteine deficiency treatments was analyzed with a Cysteine (Cys) Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K352-M, Elabscience). The assays were conducted under non-reducing conditions. The principle of the assays is that cysteine reduces phosphotungstic acid to generate tungsten blue, which exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 600 nm. The content of cysteine can be quantitatively determined by measuring the optical absorbance at this wavelength. Briefly, 1×106 cells were collected and homogenized in 0.2 mL Reagent I of the kit. After centrifugation at 4 °C, 10,000 g for 10 min, 20 μL of the supernatant was mixed thoroughly with 100 μL each of Reagents II and III. The mixture was incubated at ambient temperature for 10 min and analyzed for 600-nm optical absorbance using the Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader.

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification

Mitochondrial ROS levels were measured using the MitoSOX Red probe (HY-D1055, MCE). Briefly, HepG2, Hepa1-6, and KYSE-150 cells subjected to various treatments were collected and incubated with 1 mL 2.5 μM MitoSOX Red at ambient temperature for 20 min. After centrifugation at 400 g for 3 min, the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed twice with ice-cold PBS (10 min each time), after which the cells were analyzed with flow cytometry as described above.

Mitochondrial iron content assay

The Mito-FerroGreen probe (HY-D2295, MCE) was used to assess mitochondrial iron content. The three types of cells were collected and incubated with 5 μM Mito-FerroGreen probe for 20 min. After that, flow cytometry was performed for the stained cells as described above.

Cellular ATP content assay

Three types of cells of total cellular ATP were measured using CheKine™ Micro ATP Content Assay Kit (KTB1016, Abbkine) according to the manufacturer’s procedures. The results are represented as relative values of control.

Enzyme activity assays

In short, 1×106 cancer cells were collected for all enzyme activity assays. The following kits were utilized for determining the activity of iron-sulfur cluster-dependent enzymes according to the manufacturers’ instructions: Complex I Activity Test Kit (ZC-S0472, ZCIBIO Technology), Succinate Dehydrogenase (SDH) Activity Assay Kit (E-BC-K649-M, Elabscience) and Aconitase (ACO) Activity Assay Kit (BC4480, Solarbio).

Intracellular total sulfhydryl and glutathione (GSH) content assays

Intracellular contents of total sulfhydryl and GSH were respectively analyzed with Total Sulfhydryl Assay Kit with DTNB (S0141S, Beyotime Biotechnology) and Reduced Glutathione (GSH) Content Assay Kit (AKPR008M, BoxBio) strictly following the manufacturers’ instructions. Optical density values at 412 nm were measured with the Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader for the two assays.

NADPH content assays

Briefly, 2 × 106 cells were harvested for measurement of intracellular NADPH content, utilizing a CheKine™ Micro Coenzyme II NADP(H) Assay Kit (KTB1010, Abbkine) strictly following the protocol provided with the kit. The 450-nm optical absorption values were measured with the Tecan Infinite M Nano microplate reader.

Statistical analyses

In this study, three independent biological replicates were performed for WB experiments, because this sample size is deemed acceptable75 and widely adopted by many papers recently published in high-ranking journals76–78. For the remaining assays, four or five independent biological replicates were employed. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 Software. The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by two-sided t-tests for inter-group comparisons. Statistically significant differences were identified utilizing a P value threshold of 0.05 (*** or ###, P < 0.001; ** or ##, P < 0.01; * or #, P < 0.05).
