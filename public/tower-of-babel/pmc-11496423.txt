OsPP2C49, a Negative Regulatory Factor in the Abscisic Acid Signaling Pathway, Positively Regulates Grain Yield in Rice
Authors: Jun Miao, Liubing Bu, Wenchen Tan, Ping Wang, Xiangbo Li, Xianfeng Li, Chuyan Chen, Kunming Zhang, Wenle Shen, Zhiyun Gong, Gulinaer Bahetibieke, Lei Ren, Guohua Liang, Yong Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Clade A type 2C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs) are crucial components of the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway. Research on clade A PP2Cs has focused more on their roles related to ABA signaling and stress responses than on the molecular mechanisms mediating their effects on plant growth and grain yield. Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is an important food crop worldwide. We previously determined that OsPP2C49, which encodes a rice clade A PP2C family member, negatively controls rice responses to drought, salt, and high-temperature stresses. In this study, we investigated the regulatory effects of OsPP2C49 on ABA responses and rice grain yield. By analyzing potential interactions with core ABA components, including pyrabactin resistance 1 (PYR1)/PYR1-like (PYL)/regulatory component of the ABA receptor (RCAR) and stress-activated protein kinases (SAPKs), we confirmed that OsPP2C49 is involved in the ABA signaling pathway. OsPP2C49 overexpression led to decreased ABA sensitivity and increased rice grain yield; the opposite phenotypes were observed in the ospp2c49 knockout mutants. Therefore, OsPP2C49 negatively regulates ABA responses, but positively modulates rice grain yield. Furthermore, we found that OsPP2C49 can interact with and dephosphorylate five OsSAPKs in vitro. Unlike OsPP2C49, these OsSAPKs positively modulate ABA responsiveness, but negatively affect rice yield. These findings indicate that OsPP2C49 may partially regulate ABA responses and rice grain production by dephosphorylating OsSAPKs. This study preliminarily explored the molecular basis of the regulatory effects of OsPP2C49 on rice plant growth and grain yield.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-024-00746-4.

Background

Food security is critical for national stability, economic development, and the improvement of people’s livelihoods. Food shortages, which are caused by population growth and continuous environmental changes, have emerged as a global concern (Hunter et al. 2017; Singh et al. 2022; Yadav et al. 2023). Hunger and food insecurity are issues for more than 800 million individuals worldwide (Wei et al. 2022). Rice (Oryza sativa L.), which is a major food crop, plays a crucial role in addressing the food shortage crisis and ensuring food security (Sasaki and Burr 2000; Gross and Zhao 2014). Over the past few decades, extensive research has led to the identification and cloning of numerous rice yield-related genes, thereby elucidating the genetic regulatory network underpinning yield. However, because of the trade-offs between different agronomic traits arising from gene pleiotropy, most of these yield-related genes are not directly applicable to rice breeding (Zhang 2007; Visscher and Yang 2016; Ren et al. 2023). Therefore, identifying additional rice yield-related genes will enrich the rice genetic resource, while also facilitating genetic improvements to yield and other target traits.

Rice yield is a complex trait regulated by multiple signaling pathways, among which hormone signal transduction is one of the critical regulatory pathways. The plant hormone abscisic acid (ABA) has attracted considerable attention because of its pivotal regulatory role in stress responses (Raghavendra et al. 2010; Lee and Luan 2012). Moreover, ABA also functions as a growth regulator (Cutler et al. 2010; Brookbank et al. 2021). High ABA concentrations inhibit plant growth and seed germination, while also adversely affecting yield (Verslues and Zhu 2007; Cutler et al. 2010; Sugimoto et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2018). In rice, multiple ABA synthesis and signaling components are involved in the regulation of yield. In the ABA synthesis pathway, the key synthetic gene OsNCED3 contributes to preharvest sprouting resistance, grain development, and yield (Chen et al. 2023). The ABA signaling pathway primarily comprises three important components: pyrabactin resistance 1 (PYR1)/PYR1-like (PYL)/regulatory component of the ABA receptor (RCAR) (hereafter referred to as PYL), clade A PP2C, and sucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinase 2 (SnRK2) (Ma et al. 2009; Park et al. 2009). In previous studies, we showed that OsPP2C09, which belongs to the clade A PP2C family, positively regulates rice grain weight and yield per plant (Miao et al. 2020). Mutating PYL1, PYL4, and PYL6 (ABA receptor family genes) to generate the pyl1/4/6 triple mutant reportedly leads to increases in plant growth and yield under normal irrigation conditions (Miao et al. 2018). Rice OsSAPK3, which is a member of the SnRK2 family, increases drought tolerance and grain yield (Lou et al. 2023). Under drought conditions, SAPK2 improves the grain yield during the rice reproductive stage by regulating nitrogen use efficiency, whereas SAPK9 increases the rice yield through its positive effects on pollen maturation, spikelet fertility, and panicle weight (Dey et al. 2016; Lou et al. 2020).

In the ABA signaling pathway, the activation and inactivation of ABA signals are largely regulated by clade A PP2Cs (Umezawa et al. 2009; Pan et al. 2020). Under normal conditions, PP2Cs dephosphorylate and inhibit the activities of SnRK2s, thereby blocking ABA signaling. An exposure to stress conditions decreases the inhibitory effects of PP2Cs on SnRK2s, which activate ABA signaling (Ma et al. 2009; Melcher et al. 2009; Park et al. 2009; Yin et al. 2009). In rice, there are 10 clade A PP2Cs (Xue et al. 2008; Singh et al. 2010), with most of them functioning as negative regulators of ABA signaling and stress resistance (Kim et al. 2012, 2021; Zong et al. 2016; Min et al. 2019). Clade A PP2Cs are also involved in regulating rice growth via their effects on stomatal density, root development, and yield (Li et al. 2015; Miao et al. 2020). Among clade A PP2Cs, OsPP2C49 interacts with and deactivates rice SAPK2 to inhibit the transcriptional activation activity of OsbZIP23, thereby negatively regulating the ABA signaling pathway and the drought stress response (Zong et al. 2016). In addition, the expression of OsPP2C49, which negatively regulates rice responses to high-temperature and salinity stresses, can be regulated by the histone H4 deacetylase OsHDA706 (Liu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). However, it remains unclear whether OsPP2C49 helps regulate rice plant growth and yield.

In rice, clade A PP2Cs, which are essential components of the ABA signaling pathway, have been extensively studied in terms of their roles in stress responses (Li et al. 2015; Zong et al. 2016; Miao et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). However, relatively few studies have been conducted on the mechanisms underlying their regulatory effects on plant growth and grain yield. In this study, OsPP2C49 was identified as a member of the clade A PP2C family. OsPP2C49 interacts with multiple OsPYLs and OsSAPKs, which are the key components of ABA signaling. OsPP2C49 overexpression significantly decreases ABA sensitivity, but significantly increases grain weight and grain yield. Therefore, OsPP2C49 negatively regulates ABA responses, but positively regulates plant growth and grain yield. Moreover, it interacts with and dephosphorylates OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10. These OsSAPKs positively regulate ABA responses, but negatively regulate rice plant growth and grain yield. Thus, we speculate that OsPP2C49 positively regulates rice plant growth and grain yield may partially through inhibiting OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 activities. These findings provide insights into how OsPP2C49 influences rice plant growth and grain yield, which may be applicable to developing high-yielding rice varieties.

Results

OsPP2C49 Interacts with OsPYLs and OsSAPKs

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays, which were performed to investigate the interactions between OsPP2C49 and 13 rice ABA receptors (OsPYL1–13), indicated that OsPP2C49 can interact with OsPYL2, OsPYL4–11, and OsPYL13, regardless of the presence or absence of ABA in the medium. However, the interaction between OsPP2C49 and OsPYL1 or OsPYL3 required ABA. There was no detectable interaction between OsPP2C49 and OsPYL12 in yeast (Fig. 1).

Rice contains 10 SnRK2 family members (OsSAPK1–10). The Y2H assay results indicated that OsPP2C49 can interact with OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 (Fig. 2A). The interactions between OsPP2C49 and these OsSAPKs were validated via a split-luciferase complementation (split-LUC) assay. The coding regions of OsPP2C49 and the respective OsSAPK genes were fused to the N-terminus (n-LUC) and C-terminus (c-LUC) of luciferase for the co-transformation of tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaf epidermal cells. Strong luciferase activity was detected when OsPP2C49 was combined with any of the five OsSAPK genes, whereas no LUC fluorescence activity was observed in the corresponding negative controls (Fig. 2B). We also verified the interactions between OsPP2C49 and the five OsSAPKs by conducting glutathione S-transferase pull-down (GST pull-down) assays in vitro. OsPP2C49 and the OsSAPKs were fused with sequences encoding 6× His and GST tags, respectively. The GST pull-down assay results indicated that OsPP2C49 can interact with all five OsSAPKs in vitro (Fig. 2C).

OsPP2C49 Negatively Regulates ABA Responses

Nipponbare seedlings grown under normal conditions for 2 weeks were treated with ABA, after which samples were collected at different time points to analyze changes in OsPP2C49 expression. According to the results, ABA significantly induced OsPP2C49 transcription. The OsPP2C49 expression level peaked at 6 h after the ABA treatment, but then gradually decreased at the later time points (Fig. S1).

In previous studies, we constructed ospp2c49 knockout mutants and OsPP2C49 overexpression lines to functionally characterize OsPP2C49 regarding its role during plant responses to drought, heat, and salinity (Liu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). To elucidate whether OsPP2C49 is involved in ABA responses, we compared the seed germination rates of Nipponbare, ospp2c49 knockout mutants, and OsPP2C49 overexpression lines treated with different ABA concentrations. In the absence of ABA, the seed germination rates of the OsPP2C49 overexpression lines and ospp2c49 knockout mutants were significantly higher and lower, respectively, than that of Nipponbare (Fig. 3A-B). A treatment with exogenous ABA significantly decreased the seed germination rates of Nipponbare and the OsPP2C49 transgenic lines. The ospp2c49 knockout mutants exhibited enhanced sensitivity to ABA, which was in contrast to the decreased sensitivity of the OsPP2C49 overexpression lines to ABA (Fig. 3A, C-F). Furthermore, the differences in the seed germination rates between OsPP2C49 transgenic lines and Nipponbare increased with increasing ABA concentrations (Fig. 3C-F).

Because of the significant inhibitory effects of exogenous ABA on plant growth and root elongation, we also compared the shoot and root lengths of Nipponbare and OsPP2C49 transgenic lines treated with different ABA concentrations. In the absence of ABA, there were no significant differences in shoot and root lengths among the ospp2c49 knockout mutants, OsPP2C49 overexpression lines, and Nipponbare (Fig. 3G-H). After the ABA treatment, the shoots and roots of the OsPP2C49 overexpression lines were significantly longer than those of Nipponbare (Fig. 3G-H), whereas the shoots and roots of the ospp2c49 knockout mutants were significantly shorter (Fig. 3G-H). These results suggest that OsPP2C49 negatively regulates ABA responses in rice.

OsPP2C49 Positively Regulates Plant Height, Grain Weight, and Yield

The ospp2c49 knockout mutants, OsPP2C49 overexpression lines, and wild-type Nipponbare were grown under natural paddy field conditions for a phenotypic analysis. Compared with Nipponbare, plant height and panicle length increased significantly in two OsPP2C49 overexpression lines, but decreased significantly in two ospp2c49 knockout mutants (Fig. 4A, D-E). In addition, the overexpression lines had larger and heavier grains, while the knockout mutants produced smaller and lighter grains (Fig. 4B-C, I-K). There were no significant differences in tiller number, grain number per panicle, and seed setting rate among the analyzed plants (Fig. 4F-H). Thus, the grain yield per plant of the overexpression lines increased by 16.57% and 18.22%, whereas the grain yield per plant of the ospp2c49 knockout mutants decreased by 15.38% and 13.34% (Fig. 4B, L). Therefore, OsPP2C49 is a positive regulator of rice plant height, grain weight, and grain yield.

OsPP2C49 Dephosphorylates OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 In Vitro

Because of the interactions between OsPP2C49 and OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 revealed by Y2H, split-LUC, and GST pull-down assays, we hypothesized that OsPP2C49 may participate in ABA responses and regulate rice growth by dephosphorylating these five OsSAPKs. To test this hypothesis, we examined whether OsPP2C49 can dephosphorylate OsSAPKs in vitro. We purified GST-OsSAPK fusion proteins for an autophosphorylation and used Zn2+-Phos-tag technology to detect phosphorylated GST-OsSAPKs. After incubating GST-OsPP2C49 with phosphorylated GST-OsSAPKs, the phosphorylation levels of all GST-OsSAPKs decreased significantly in a dose-dependent manner (i.e., greater decrease in phosphorylation levels at higher GST-OsPP2C49 concentrations). (Fig. 5). Hence, OsPP2C49 can dephosphorylate OsSAPKs in vitro.

Overexpression of OsSAPKs Increase Rice Sensitivity to ABA

To investigate whether these five OsSAPKs are involved in regulating ABA responses, the coding regions of the corresponding genes were amplified and inserted into the p1301UbiNOS vector to generate overexpression constructs. All the constructs were introduced into Nipponbare, separately, by Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation. After selecting lines according to hygromycin resistance and analyzing gene expression, we identified two transgenic overexpression lines with varying degrees of increased expression for OsSAPK1, OsSAPK8, and OsSAPK10 as well as three transgenic overexpression lines for OsSAPK9 (Fig. S2). The overexpression lines of OsSAPK2 (OsSAPK2-OE1 and OsSAPK2-OE2) were generated previously (Zhu et al. 2024). Seeds of Nipponbare and OsSAPK overexpression lines were treated with different exogenous ABA concentrations. In the absence of exogenous ABA, the seed germination rates of all OsSAPK overexpression lines were significantly lower than that of Nipponbare. After ABA was added, the seed germination rates of the OsSAPK overexpression lines exhibited heightened sensitivity to ABA, in contrast to Nipponbare (Figs. 6A-E and 7A). After a 144-h treatment with varying ABA concentrations, the statistical analysis of the seed germination rates of Nipponbare and OsSAPK overexpression lines revealed that ABA inhibited seed germination in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7B). As the ABA concentration increased, the differences in the seed germination rates between the OsSAPK overexpression lines and Nipponbare increased. These results conclusively demonstrate that these five OsSAPK genes positively regulate ABA responses.

OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 Negatively Regulate Rice Plant Height, Grain Weight, and Grain Yield

At maturity, we compared the phenotypes and agronomic traits between overexpression lines of five OsSAPK genes (OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10) and Nipponbare to elucidate the effects of OsSAPK genes on rice yield. To accurately identify traits, Nipponbare plants adjacent to each OsSAPK overexpression line were selected as controls. OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 overexpression resulted in significant decreases in plant height, panicle length, and 1,000-grain weight (Fig. 8A-B). Thus, the grain yield per plant was significantly lower for the OsSAPK overexpression lines than for Nipponbare (Tables S1–S5). Specifically, the grain yield per plant of the two OsSAPK1 overexpression lines decreased by 16.28% and 20.37% (Table S1); the grain yield per plant of the two OsSAPK2 overexpression lines decreased by 12.87% and 16.43% (Table S2); the grain yield per plant of the two OsSAPK8 overexpression lines decreased by 34.33% and 38.27% (Table S3); the grain yield per plant of the three OsSAPK9 overexpression lines decreased by 21.24%, 35.83%, and 37.62% (Table S4); and the grain yield per plant of the two OsSAPK10 overexpression lines decreased by 27.53% and 44.15% (Table S5). Accordingly, all five OsSAPK genes negatively regulate rice plant height, grain weight, and grain yield, which is the opposite of the effects of OsPP2C49.

Discussion

Clade A PP2Cs have critical functions in the ABA signaling pathway, wherein they regulate the activation and deactivation of ABA signals through their interactions with PYLs and SnRK2s (Ma et al. 2009; Park et al. 2009). In the current study, we revealed that OsPP2C49 can interact with OsPYLs and OsSAPKs, reflecting its key role in the ABA signaling pathway. The interaction between OsPP2C49 and most OsPYLs does not require ABA. Whether the interaction between OsPP2C49 and OsPYLs requires ABA may be mainly influenced by the PYL structure (Yin et al. 2009; Dupeux et al. 2011b; Hao et al. 2011). When PYLs exist in a monomeric state, their inhibitory effects on PP2C occur independently of ABA (Dupeux et al. 2011b). However, PYL dimers must bind to a ligand to inhibit PP2C functions (Yin et al. 2009; Hao et al. 2011). ABA is regarded as a “strong” ligand that is compatible with all PYLs (Hao et al. 2011). The binding to ABA results in a structural change that enables PYLs to bind and inhibit PP2Cs (Miyazono et al. 2009; Santiago et al. 2009; Yin et al. 2009). Although some PYLs can bind to PP2Cs in the absence of ABA, the low-affinity complex formed by this interaction is insufficient to activate ABA signal transduction (Dupeux et al. 2011a; Antoni et al. 2012; Ludwików 2015). In Arabidopsis, clade A PP2Cs primarily inhibit the activity of subclass III members SnRK2.2, SnRK2.3, and SnRK2.6 via dephosphorylation, which is essential for the transmission of ABA signals to downstream effectors (Fujii et al. 2009; Umezawa et al. 2009). Similarly, in rice, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10, which interact with OsPP2C49, also belong to subclass III and are highly homologous to the three subclass III SnRK2s in Arabidopsis (Kobayashi et al. 2004). However, unlike in Arabidopsis, OsPP2C49 can also interact with OsSAPK1 and OsSAPK2 from subclass II, reflecting the uniqueness and diversity of the clade A PP2Cs in ABA signaling mechanisms across different species.

Although the rice clade A PP2Cs have common roles related to ABA signaling, their specific modes of action vary. For example, OsPP2C49 and its highly similar homolog OsPP2C09 differ in terms of how they interact with OsPYLs, but they both regulate the same SnRK2s (Miao et al. 2020). In addition, OsPP2C51 and OsPP2C30 similarly affect ABA signaling, with both relying on the OsPYL5/PCAR5–OsPP2C51 or OsPP2C30–OsSAPK2 complex for signal transduction (Kim et al. 2012; Bhatnagar et al. 2017). Furthermore, OsPP2C50/OsABIL3 and OsPP2C53/OsABIL2 participate in the ABA signaling pathway by regulating the activity of OsSAPK10 (Min et al. 2019). A thorough dissection of the interaction network involving OsPP2C49 and OsPYLs as well as OsSAPKs is needed to comprehensively characterize the specific role of OsPP2C49 in the ABA signaling pathway.

Most clade A PP2Cs in rice serve as negative regulators in the ABA signaling pathway (Zong et al. 2016; Min et al. 2019; Miao et al. 2020). In the presence of ABA, OsPP2C49 overexpression significantly increased seed germination rates and shoot and root lengths (compared with wild-type Nipponbare), whereas knocking out OsPP2C49 had the opposite effects. Accordingly, OsPP2C49 is a negative regulator of ABA responses. Further analyses indicated that multiple OsSAPKs, which interact with OsPP2C49, positively regulate ABA responses, implying OsPP2C49 regulates the ABA signaling pathway primarily by inhibiting OsSAPK activities. Given the unique position of clade A PP2Cs in the ABA signaling pathway, previous related studies largely focused on their responses to ABA and various stresses (Kim et al. 2012; Li et al. 2015; Zong et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2023). However, the molecular basis of the effects of clade A PP2Cs on rice plant growth and grain yield remains largely unknown. In earlier studies, we preliminarily revealed the role of OsPP2C49 in modulating rice adaptations to drought, high temperatures, and salinity (Liu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). The results of the current study clearly indicate that OsPP2C49 also regulates rice plant growth and grain yield. In contrast to the regulatory effect of OsPP2C49 on plant growth and grain yield, five OsSAPKs that interact with OsPP2C49 have the opposite effects. These findings imply that OsPP2C49 positively regulates rice plant growth and grain yield partially by inhibiting OsSAPK under natural paddy field conditions. In addition to clade A PP2Cs and SnRK2s, PYLs in the ABA signaling pathway also modulate rice plant growth and grain yield. Mutations to several PYL-encoding genes lead to increased rice growth and productivity (Miao et al. 2018). Therefore, modulating the core components of the ABA signaling pathway can improve rice plant growth and grain yield, with potential implications for breeding novel high-yielding rice varieties. We also noticed that both SAPK2 and SAPK9 increase the rice grain yield under drought conditions (Dey et al. 2016; Lou et al. 2020), suggesting that interactions with the environment should be considered when using ABA signaling pathway genes.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the functions of OsPP2C49, a member of the clade A PP2C family, revealing its role in ABA signaling and yield in rice. OsPP2C49 was identified as a crucial component of the ABA signaling pathway, capable of interacting with ABA receptors (OsPYLs) and downstream signaling components (OsSAPKs). We observed that OsPP2C49 negatively regulates ABA responses. Furthermore, this study highlights the positive effect of OsPP2C49 on rice plant growth and grain yield. Compared with wild-type Nipponbare, OsPP2C49 overexpression lines had a higher grain yield, which was in contrast to the lower grain yield of the ospp2c49 knockout mutants. OsPP2C49 interacts with and dephosphorylates OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10. Unlike the effects of OsPP2C49 on ABA responses and grain yield, these OsSAPKs positively regulate ABA responses, but negatively regulate rice plant growth and grain yield. Thus, we speculate that OsPP2C49 may positively regulate rice plant growth and grain yield partially through its inhibitory effects on OsSAPK1, OsSAPK2, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

The OsPP2C49 overexpression lines, ospp2c49 knockout mutants, and OsSAPK2 overexpression lines used in this study have been described elsewhere (Liu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). To generate OsSAPK1, OsSAPK8, OsSAPK9, and OsSAPK10 overexpression lines, the full-length cDNA sequences were cloned into the p1301UbiNOS vector, which contains a maize ubiquitin gene promoter. The resulting recombinant vectors were transferred into Nipponbare plants using A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation methods (Toki et al. 2006). After screening and identifying putative transformants, OsSAPK overexpression lines were obtained. All plant materials were cultivated in a transgenic-specific experimental field at Yangzhou University in Jiangsu, China.

ABA Treatment

To analyze the changes in OsPP2C49 expression following an ABA treatment, 14-day-old Nipponbare seedlings were transferred to a solution containing 100 µM ABA (Solarbio, China). Samples were collected at different time points for an analysis of OsPP2C49 expression.

A seed germination experiment to assess the effects of various ABA concentrations was performed as previously described, with some modifications (Zhang et al. 2015). Fully filled and dehulled seeds collected from Nipponbare and different transgenic lines were immersed in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min and then washed three times with sterile water. Rinsed seeds were placed on sterile filter paper containing different ABA concentrations (0, 1, 2, 5, and 10 µM) and germinated in a growth chamber (28 °C, 14-h light/10-h dark cycle, and 60% relative humidity). Germination rates were recorded every 12 h. Three biological replicates were included for each genotype, with each replicate containing ≥ 10 seeds.

For the seedling growth assay, uniformly germinated seeds from Nipponbare and different transgenic lines were maintained under normal conditions (28 °C, 14-h light/10-h dark cycle, and 60% relative humidity) for 3 days and then transferred to solutions containing various ABA concentrations. After 14 days, the shoot and root lengths were measured.

Quantitative Real–Time PCR (qRT–PCR) Analysis

The FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China) was used to extract total RNA from rice tissues, after which the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China) was used to synthesize cDNA. A qRT-PCR analysis was performed using the ABI ViiA™ 7 system (Applied Biosystems, USA) and the ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). An OsActin gene (LOC_Os03g50885) was selected as an internal control. Three biological replicates were included in the qRT-PCR analysis.

Yeast Two-Hybrid (Y2H) Assay

Y2H assays were conducted using the Matchmaker GAL4 Two-Hybrid System (Clontech, USA). The OsPP2C49 coding region was amplified by PCR and cloned into the pGADT7 (AD) vector, whereas the OsPYL and OsSAPK coding regions were inserted into the pGBKT7 (BD) vector. Various combinations of the recombinant plasmids were used for the co-transformation of Y2HGold cells (Clontech). To screen for interactions, different concentrations of transformed yeast cells (100, 10− 1, 10− 2, 10− 3, and 10− 4 dilutions) were grown on SD medium lacking Leu, Trp, His, and Ade (SD/−LTHA) supplemented with 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-alpha-d-galactopyranoside (X-α-gal) for 3–4 days. When screening for interactions between OsPP2C49 and OsPYLs, the aforementioned medium was used with or without 10 µM ABA to clarify their specific interactions. Empty AD and BD vectors served as negative controls.

GST Pull-Down Assays

GST pull-down assays were completed as previously described (Wang et al. 2020). The OsPP2C49 coding region was inserted into the pET30a vector, which includes a sequence encoding a 6× His tag. The OsSAPK coding sequences were inserted into the pGEX-2T vector, which carries a sequence encoding a GST tag. The recombinant vectors were inserted into Escherichia coli BL21 cells, which were induced to express recombinant proteins. The GST-OsSAPK fusion proteins were purified using the Glutathione S-Transferase (GST)-Sefinose™ Kit (Sangon Biotech, China), whereas the His-OsPP2C49 fusion protein was purified using the Ni-TED 6FF Sefinose™ Resin Kit (Sangon Biotech). For the GST pull-down assay, 500 µg each fusion protein was incubated with glutathione high-capacity magnetic agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in pull-down buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Nonidet P-40, 1 mM PMSF, 150 mM KCl, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT) at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were then washed three times with pull-down buffer. The beads were boiled in 1× PBS and 1× SDS protein loading buffer, after which the proteins were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE. A western blot analysis was performed using anti-GST (1:10,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or anti-His (1:10,000; Medical Biological Laboratories, Japan) antibodies.

Split-Luciferase Complementation (split-LUC) Assay

For the split-LUC assay, the OsPP2C49 and OsSAPK coding regions were cloned into the JW771 vector (carrying the n-LUC-encoding sequence) and the JW772 vector (carrying the c-LUC-encoding sequence), respectively. The resulting nLUC-OsPP2C49 and OsSAPKs-cLUC recombinant plasmids were inserted into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 cells, which were then diluted with infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, and 400 mM acetosyringone) for OD600 = 0.8–1. Equal amounts of the diluted cell suspensions were mixed in different combinations and then injected into N. benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. At 3 days after infiltration, luciferase activity was detected using the Dual Luciferase Assay System kit (Promega, USA). Images were captured using the Tanon-5200 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon, China). JW771 (n-LUC) and JW772 (c-LUC) served as negative controls.

In Vitro Dephosphorylation Assay

The OsPP2C49 and OsSAPK coding regions were cloned into the pGEX-2T vector to generate the GST-OsPP2C49 and GST-OsSAPK recombinant plasmids, respectively. E. coli BL21 cells were transformed with these plasmids and then induced to express recombinant fusion proteins, which were purified using the Glutathione S-Transferase (GST)-Sefinose™ Kit (Sangon Biotech). An in vitro dephosphorylation assay was performed as previously described (Guo et al. 2018; Gao et al. 2019). Initially, GST-OsSAPK proteins were autophosphorylated and then incubated in kinase buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 1 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT) containing ATP (100 µM) at 30 °C for 1 h. A desalting step was completed to remove free ATP. To assess the capacity of OsPP2C49 to dephosphorylate OsSAPKs, GST-OsPP2C49 was mixed with the phosphorylated GST-OsSAPK proteins and incubated at 30 °C for 3 h. The samples were then analyzed by 12.5% SDS-PAGE, after which the separated proteins were subjected to a western blot analysis. Subsequently, phosphorylated proteins were detected using a biotin-pendant Zn2+-Phos-tag (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The empty pGEX-2T vector served as a negative control.
