Agricultural By‐Products as Secondary Risk Matrices: Hazard Occurrence, Contaminant Fate, Mitigation Strategies, and Regulatory Considerations in Food Systems
Authors: Vinothini Boopathi, Jaehyeon Kwon, Jiwoo Jung, Su Been Park, Soyoung Gwon, Hyang Sook Chun, Hyun‐Gyun Yuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAgricultural by‐products are increasingly valorized as food ingredients within circular food systems. However, their safety cannot be directly inferred from their corresponding primary commodities. Moreover, agricultural by‐products serve as secondary risk matrices by acting as reservoirs or accumulation points for microbiological hazards, mycotoxins, pesticide residues, heavy metals, process‐related contaminants, other organic contaminants, including PAHs and mineral oil hydrocarbons, and allergenic compounds. This occurs during field exposure, processing‐driven redistribution, and storage. Hence, the safety and sustainability of by‐product‐derived ingredients require matrix‐specific evaluation. Therefore, this study aims to provide a comprehensive and structured assessment of agricultural by‐products as food‐relevant matrices. Accordingly, this review integrated evidence using a two‐tier literature approach that combined broad literature mapping of agricultural by‐product valorization with a targeted safety‐focused analysis of peer‐reviewed studies. The primary focus was hazard occurrence, contaminant fate during processing, mitigation strategies, and regulatory considerations governing the use of agricultural by‐products in food systems. Particular emphasis was placed on the processing‐driven redistribution of contaminants, matrix‐dependent variability, multi‐contaminant co‐occurrence, and limitations of regulatory frameworks originally developed for primary commodities. This review highlights that addressing current gaps in exposure assessment, mitigation validation, and regulatory alignment is critical for the safe integration of these by‐products into circular food systems.

Introduction

Agricultural by‐products are secondary materials generated during crop production, postharvest handling, and food processing that are not intended for consumption. In this review, the term agricultural by‐products is used as an umbrella term encompassing agricultural residues and food‐processing‐derived by‐products. These include diverse matrices, such as pomace, peels, seeds, bran, husks, bagasse, press cakes, spent grains, and stalks. These by‐products, which are traditionally regarded as low‐value waste, are increasingly recognized as valuable biomass fractions rich in carbohydrates, proteins, dietary fiber, lipids, and bioactive compounds with potential applications in food systems (Schieber et al. 2001; Galanakis 2012; Santana‐Méridas et al. 2012; Rațu et al. 2023).

Agricultural by‐products are generated at a substantially large scale worldwide and continue to increase alongside global food production. According to FAO statistics, global food production results in pronounced losses and waste across the supply chain, with approximately one‐third of food produced for human consumption (∼1.3 billion tons) being lost or wasted annually (Gustavsson et al. 2011). Agricultural residues represent one of the largest renewable biomass streams globally (> 2400 million tons per year) (Verardi et al. 2023). However, when poorly managed, these materials burden the environment via open burning, landfilling, and uncontrolled decomposition. This leads to greenhouse gas emissions, nutrient losses, and environmental pollution (Santana‐Méridas et al. 2012; Mirabella et al. 2014; Verardi et al. 2023).

Consequently, agricultural by‐products have gained increasing attention as key components of circular food systems and sustainable bioeconomy strategies. The potential of agricultural by‐products for valorization into functional food ingredients that are good sources of dietary fiber, polyphenols, proteins, pectin, and fermentable substrates has been demonstrated (Schieber et al. 2001; Mussatto et al. 2006; Ajila et al. 2007; Galanakis 2012; Iriondo‐DeHond et al. 2020; Rațu et al. 2023). In particular, fruit and vegetable processing residues, cereal brans, oilseed press cakes, and brewer's spent grain have been widely investigated as nutrient‐rich ingredients for food formulation (Mussatto et al. 2006; Ajila et al. 2007; Galanakis 2012). This transition from waste disposal to resource utilization aligns with global sustainability initiatives aimed at improving resource efficiency and reducing food loss (Mirabella et al. 2014; European Parliament and Council 2020).

However, the incorporation of agricultural by‐products into food systems introduces distinct safety and quality challenges. Unlike primary edible fractions, by‐products frequently consist of outer tissues or residual matrices that are frequently exposed to environmental contamination, microbial colonization, and chemical transformation during cultivation, processing, and storage (Socas‐Rodríguez et al. 2021; FAO 2019). Importantly, processing does not necessarily eliminate hazards. Instead, it may redistribute, concentrate, or transform contaminants within by‐product fractions.

This phenomenon has been demonstrated across multiple hazard classes. For example, mycotoxins are frequently detected in agricultural by‐products, sometimes at levels that may limit their safe utilization as food ingredients (Lopes et al. 2023). Similarly, up to approximately 58% of pesticides can partition into by‐product streams, such as spent grain, rather than the final product, thereby highlighting processing‐driven redistribution (Hakme et al. 2024). In addition, process‐related contaminants, such as acrylamide, may form or persist in thermally processed by‐products, which further complicates safety assessment (Bonilla et al. 2025). Collectively, these studies suggest that reduced contaminant levels in primary food products do not necessarily indicate the safety of their associated by‐products. Importantly, agricultural by‐products cannot be assumed to be inherently safe, and their valorization requires systematic evaluation of chemical and biological hazards (Socas‐Rodríguez et al. 2021).

Despite the increasing interest in by‐product valorization, studies disproportionately focus on functional properties and technological applications, with comparatively limited emphasis on hazard occurrence, contaminant fate, mitigation strategies, and exposure assessment (Iriondo‐DeHond et al. 2020; Socas‐Rodríguez et al. 2021; Lopes et al. 2023; Rațu et al. 2023). Furthermore, current regulatory frameworks are largely designed for primary food commodities and do not explicitly address the unique characteristics of by‐product matrices. These include compositional heterogeneity, processing history, and contaminant redistribution (EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition, and Allergies 2016; European Parliament and Council 2020). Therefore, the safety of by‐product‐derived ingredients requires matrix‐specific evaluation.

Accordingly, this review aims to provide a comprehensive and structured assessment of agricultural by‐products as food‐relevant matrices. Specifically, the review uses a two‐tier approach that combines broad literature mapping of agricultural by‐product valorization with targeted safety‐focused synthesis. The review specifically examines the following: (i) types and characteristics of agricultural by‐products relevant to food systems; (ii) microbiological and chemical hazards; (iii) processing‐driven contaminant redistribution and mitigation; and (iv) regulatory frameworks and knowledge gaps. Particular attention is given to the notion that agricultural by‐products are heterogeneous matrices whose safety assessment should consider processing history and processing‐induced changes in hazard occurrence and contaminant fate, together with risk‐based evaluation, before their integration into food systems.

Literature Search Strategy and Evidence Mapping

Literature and Thematic Mapping of Valorization Research

Although the broad literature mapping includes both food and nonfood applications of agricultural by‐products to contextualize overall research trends, the current review focuses exclusively on food‐related applications and associated safety considerations. A structured two‐tier literature search was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) principles where applicable. This study combined broad literature and thematic mapping with targeted safety‐focused qualitative synthesis to provide a structured assessment of the available evidence. The literature search and screening workflow is summarized in Figure 2. An initial search of the Scopus database yielded 1950 records, which were subjected to a multistage screening process, including title, abstract, and full‐text evaluation, based on relevance and data availability (Figure 2). After screening, 972 publications were retained, consisting of 775 original research articles and 197 review papers.

Search and screening workflow for the literature on agricultural by‐products. Elsevier Scopus was searched using TITLE‐ABS‐KEY fields, with the search limited to peer‐reviewed articles and reviews. Conference papers, book chapters, and duplicates were excluded. Eligibility was determined based on title, abstract, and keyword screening, with full‐text review conducted as needed.

To further structure the dataset, agricultural by‐products were classified according to crop group and material type, distinguishing anatomical fractions (e.g., bran, straw/stover, husk/hull/shell, peel/rind, stems/leaves, seeds/kernels) from process‐derived residues (e.g., pomace, cake/meal, bagasse), and an additional mixed category for unassignable materials. Standardized category definitions were applied to ensure consistent classification of the retrieved studies, with representative examples provided in Table 1.

As summarized in Table 1, the retrieved publications were distributed across a broad range of agricultural by‐product categories. Straw/stover was the most frequently represented category (n = 205), followed by bran (n = 116), bagasse (n = 115), fruit peel/rind (n = 109), stems/stalks in cereals and oilseeds (n = 85), seeds/kernels (n = 82), and husk/hull/shell (n = 81). Pomace was also well represented (n = 73), whereas several categories, including silk/hair, nonmarketable fruits, and some vegetable‐derived fractions, were represented by fewer publications. Because individual publications could contribute to more than one by‐product category, category counts are not mutually exclusive.

To examine utilization pathways, publications from 2015 to 2025 were mapped using a component–operation–product (COP) framework, which reflected typical value chains (Table 2). In the extensive literature landscape, both food and nonfood applications were considered to provide a comparative perspective on utilization trends. Nonfood applications dominated the overall research landscape, with activity concentrated in chemical materials (n = 215), energy (n = 154), and bio‐based materials (n = 133). This was consistent with sustained interest in lignocellulosic conversion, composite materials, and biofuel production. Environmental applications (n = 76) formed a secondary cluster, whereas fertilizers (n = 8) and soil amendments (n = 7) were comparatively underrepresented.

In the food domain, functional foods (n = 157) and food additives (n = 45) were dominant. This reflected extensive research on the recovery and incorporation of polyphenols, pectin, and dietary fiber. In contrast, food packaging and food contact materials (n = 10), as well as beverages and confectionery applications (n = 2), were relatively limited. Intermediate levels of activity were observed in pharmaceuticals (n = 54) and feed/animal nutrition (n = 21), whereas cosmetics (n = 11) and other applications (n = 6) were less frequently studied than the other categories. Across domains, recent research has predominantly focused on material and energy applications in the nonfood sector and ingredient‐oriented applications in the food sector. This comparative distribution highlights that research addressing food‐grade utilization, particularly safety evaluation, remains comparatively underrepresented despite increasing interest in food applications.

The results demonstrate distinct component‐specific processing patterns. In particular, proteins/enzymes are strongly associated with fermentation, polyphenols with extraction, lipids with extraction and chemical processing, carbohydrates/biomass with fermentation and energy conversion, and fibers/nanomaterials with composite manufacturing. Overall, these findings indicate that research is structured around component‐driven processing strategies, with dominant axes, such as extraction (polyphenols) and fermentation (proteins/carbohydrates).

Consistent with these patterns, keyword analysis and topic clustering further identified enzymatic hydrolysis, advanced pretreatment, and nanocellulose development as key technological drivers. To support this observation, keyword co‐occurrence network analysis was conducted using VOSviewer (Figures S1 and S2). This revealed well‐defined research clusters centered on fermentation, enzymatic hydrolysis, and structural characterization techniques.

Although nonfood applications dominate the broad literature landscape, the current review focuses exclusively on food‐related applications and associated safety considerations. Accordingly, the Scopus‐derived dataset was used for broad literature and thematic mapping, whereas a separate PubMed‐based dataset was used for safety‐focused qualitative synthesis (Section 2.2). This distinction enables a structured comparison between the broader valorization landscape and the relatively limited evidence addressing food safety aspects of agricultural by‐products within food systems.

Targeted Safety‐Focused Evidence Selection

The number of studies explicitly addressing safety, contaminant behavior, and risk assessment in agricultural by‐products was limited (Section 2.1). Hence, a targeted literature search was conducted using the PubMed database to identify studies focusing on safety‐related aspects, including microbiological hazards, mycotoxins, pesticide residues, heavy metals, process‐related contaminants, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), mineral oil hydrocarbons (MOHs), and allergenicity. The search strategy combined agricultural by‐product‐related terms (e.g., pomace, peel, bran, husk, bagasse) with hazard‐specific terms (e.g., microbial contamination, mycotoxins, pesticide residues, heavy metals, allergenicity) using Boolean operators (AND/OR) to ensure comprehensive and systematic coverage. Search strings were iteratively refined to balance sensitivity and specificity.

This search was limited to publications between 2015 and 2025 and involved duplicate removal, title and abstract screening, and full‐text evaluation. Only original research articles providing quantitative or mechanistic evidence related to safety, hazard occurrence, contaminant behavior, or risk assessment were included, whereas review articles were used solely for contextual reference. Consequently, approximately 103 studies were retained for qualitative synthesis. Detailed search terms and query structures are provided in File S1. In parallel, a targeted search of regulatory and risk assessment documents was conducted through publicly available databases and official websites of major food safety authorities, including the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Codex Alimentarius Commission, the European Parliament and Council, and the European Commission. These sources were used to identify relevant regulatory limits, safety evaluations, and guidance documents related to contaminants and hazards in food and food‐derived materials. Where applicable, regulatory thresholds (e.g., maximum residue limits [MRLs] and tolerable intake values) were extracted to contextualize reported contaminant levels. Because regulatory approaches differ among jurisdictions, numerical regulatory limits were distinguished from ingredient‐specific specifications or analytical results reported in individual safety assessments.

Overall, the functional and industrial potential of agricultural by‐products has been extensively explored. However, the scientific framework required to evaluate and manage their safety as secondary risk matrices in food applications remains underdeveloped.

Safety Concerns

Microbiological Hazards

Agricultural by‐products can support diverse microbial communities originating from soil, irrigation water, plant surfaces, animals, and processing environments. Hence, these by‐products may act as reservoirs for foodborne pathogens and spoilage microorganisms when repurposed for food applications. Moreover, compared to primary food matrices, by‐products often experience delayed preservation, heterogeneous storage conditions, high moisture content, and elevated organic loads, which facilitate microbial survival and regrowth. Table 3 summarizes the key microbiological hazards, processing contexts, mitigation outcomes, and study limitations identified across food‐relevant by‐product systems. Overall, current evidence indicates that microbial contamination persists across diverse by‐product matrices. This highlights the importance of effective preservation and microbial reduction strategies for the safe utilization of agricultural by‐products as food.

Whey is a dairy‐derived agro‐food by‐product that demonstrates how substantial microbial loads may persist despite upstream heat treatment. Whey originates from the dairy sector rather than crop production. However, it was included as an illustrative secondary food matrix because it demonstrates an important safety principle relevant to agricultural by‐products. Specifically, prior heat treatment of whey during primary processing does not necessarily ensure microbiological safety during downstream reuse. In particular, pasteurized whey that is reused for ricotta production exhibited highly variable but consistently elevated microbial counts. Specifically, the total mesophilic populations ranged from 106 to 108 CFU/mL, and the aerobic spores increased up to 105 CFU/mL. These were primarily associated with spore‐forming taxa within the Bacillaceae and Paenibacillaceae families. Moreover, Pseudomonas spp. grew up to 107 CFU/mL, and vegetative cells of Bacillus spp. approached 107 CFU/mL (Sattin et al. 2016). These findings indicate that spore‐forming and psychrotrophic bacteria may persist during whey valorization despite prior heat treatment. Accordingly, conventional heat treatment alone may be insufficient to ensure microbiological safety during downstream reuse. This observation emphasizes the need for effective microbial reduction technologies in by‐product valorization systems, together with microbiological testing to determine contamination levels before their reintroduction into food applications.

The incorporation of plant‐derived by‐products into reformulated foods does not inherently increase microbiological hazards; however, safety outcomes remain formulation‐specific. In multigrain snack bars enriched with fruit‐based by‐products, Enterobacteriaceae and coagulase‐positive staphylococci were not detected in 0.1 g of snack bar during storage. This indicated satisfactory hygienic quality under the tested conditions (Kowalska et al. 2025). Correspondingly, the baking process and reduced water activity likely contributed to microbial control in the final product. These findings suggest that appropriate formulation and processing conditions can effectively limit microbial growth in by‐product‐enriched foods.

Notably, some by‐products with low moisture content appear to pose comparatively lower microbiological hazards than moisture‐rich by‐products. For example, hemp seed cake produced by cold pressing Cannabis sativa seeds exhibited low and stable levels of mesophilic aerobic bacteria, yeasts, and molds during storage under controlled conditions. Furthermore, microbial counts were slightly higher in hulled material than in dehulled material, which likely reflects hull‐associated contamination (Ambroziak and Wenda‐Piesik 2025). Moreover, no major foodborne pathogens were detected, thereby supporting the microbiological safety of hemp seed cake for food applications under the conditions evaluated. Together, these observations indicate that low moisture content and controlled storage conditions aid in suppressing microbial proliferation in oilseed‐derived by‐products.

In contrast, high‐moisture plant‐derived by‐products are particularly sensitive to handling practices prior to preservation. For instance, delayed preservation treatment of soybean curd residue (okara) markedly increased yeast and mold populations to approximately 8.41 log CFU/g, whereas immediate preservation treatment maintained fungal counts below the detection limit (< 2.0 log CFU/g) (Wali and Nishino 2020). Although lactic acid fermentation occurred in both scenarios, delayed handling was associated with elevated ammonia–nitrogen concentrations and microbial shifts toward Bacillus spp., thus indicating increased spoilage and persistence of spore‐forming bacteria. These findings demonstrate that delayed treatment substantially increases microbial proliferation and spoilage in high‐moisture by‐product matrices.

In addition, other microbial hazards remain underrepresented in by‐product safety evaluations. In particular, molecular detection of Trypanosoma cruzi in fruit‐derived matrices, such as açaí pulp, illustrates the potential relevance of protozoan pathogens in minimally processed systems (de Souza Godoi et al. 2017). In this study, thermal treatment effectively inactivated the parasite, indicating that adequate heat processing mitigated protozoan contamination. However, the lack of routine monitoring frameworks highlights an important gap in the detection of protozoan hazards in by‐product systems.

Overall, the microbiological evidence base for food‐relevant agricultural by‐products remains limited. Current evidence consistently indicates that post‐processing treatment, delayed preservation, and uncontrolled storage increase microbial loads in by‐product matrices. Therefore, safe valorization of agricultural by‐products requires effective microbial reduction, preservation, and storage strategies tailored to the characteristics of individual by‐products, followed by confirmatory microbiological testing before their reintroduction into food systems.

Chemical Hazards

Agricultural by‐products may harbor diverse chemical hazards arising from preharvest contamination, environmental exposure, and processing‐induced redistribution or transformation. Microbiological hazards can present acute and potentially severe food safety risks and are strongly influenced by processing and storage conditions, whereas chemical contaminants may persist, concentrate, or partition into secondary by‐products during fractionation, extraction, fermentation, or thermal treatment. In circular valorization systems, chemical contaminants create a recurring challenge. As illustrated in Figure 4, processing steps that reduce contaminant levels in primary food fractions may simultaneously transfer or concentrate hazards in by‐products targeted for reuse. The chemical hazards associated with agricultural by‐products are broadly categorized into five major groups: (i) mycotoxins; (ii) pesticide residues; (iii) process‐related contaminants; (iv) other organic contaminants, including PAHs and MOHs; and (v) heavy metals. The following subsections discuss these hazards in the context of a structured framework that considers occurrence, processing‐driven redistribution, mitigation outcomes, and implications for by‐product utilization.

Mycotoxins

Agricultural by‐products consist of outer plant tissues and residual fractions; therefore, they frequently act as reservoirs and concentration points for mycotoxins and favor fungal colonization and toxin persistence. Unlike primary commodities, by‐products such as straw, bran, shorts, distillers' grains, hulls, pomace, and fermentation‐derived microbial biomass may display broad mycotoxin spectra and altered concentration profiles due to field exposure, storage conditions, and processing‐induced redistribution or transformation. Consequently, mycotoxin risks associated with by‐product valorization cannot be inferred from raw crop data alone and require targeted, matrix‐specific and process‐specific assessments.

Mycotoxin contamination spans a wide range of agricultural by‐products, including cereal straw, milling fractions, distillers' grains, fermentation‐derived residues, oil‐refining by‐products, and fruit processing streams (Table 4). Where available, mitigation strategies, such as fermentation or industrial fractionation, are indicated; however, evidence remains uneven across matrices. Studies investigating multiple mycotoxin classes across relevant hazard groups reflect co‐occurrence and redistribution patterns. Despite this, a substantial proportion of available evidence originates from nonfood systems, thus limiting direct extrapolation to food‐grade by‐product applications.

Aflatoxins

Aflatoxins are primarily associated with Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus. Their occurrence in by‐products is strongly influenced by substrate composition and postharvest handling, particularly moisture and temperature conditions that permit fungal growth. During sugarcane processing, aflatoxins were generally absent from refined sugar but persisted in several by‐products, with dried yeast showing measurable contamination (mean 2.55 µg/kg; maximum 10.19 µg/kg). This highlights that fermentation‐derived residues intended for reuse may retain hazards even when the primary product is effectively decontaminated by processing (Iamanaka et al. 2019). These findings indicate that reduction of aflatoxins in refined products does not necessarily eliminate contamination from by‐products intended for valorization.

Fusarium Toxins (Fumonisins, Trichothecenes, and Zearalenone)

Fusarium toxins include fumonisins, trichothecenes such as deoxynivalenol, and zearalenone (ZEN). These toxins are dominant in cereal‐derived by‐products, particularly residues consisting of outer plant tissues and lignocellulosic fractions. Available food‐relevant evidence indicates that cereal processing and fractionation redistribute Fusarium toxins into by‐product streams, such as bran and distillers' fractions. However, quantitative occurrence data in food‐grade matrices remain limited. Across cereal processing systems, milling and fractionation frequently reduce toxin levels in refined fractions while concentrating contaminants in by‐products.

Fumonisins are particularly relevant in maize‐derived by‐products and are among the most evident examples of processing‐driven redistribution into secondary streams. Grain cleaning during industrial milling reduces fumonisins by approximately 42%; however, subsequent fractionation concentrates fumonisins into specific by‐products. In particular, animal feed flour displayed fumonisin levels approximately twice those of whole grain before cleaning, with reported concentrations in whole grain ranging from 357 to 2123 µg/kg and enrichment up to twofold to threefold in feed flour fractions (Vanara et al. 2018). This redistribution pattern is reinforced by broad assessments of dry‐milling fate that emphasize the degree of contamination in by‐product streams, such as germ and feed flour, including masked toxin forms (Scarpino et al. 2021). Thus, these findings demonstrate that processing may substantially alter mycotoxin distribution patterns, thereby increasing the relevance of by‐product fractions as potential exposure sources.

Mitigation evidence within by‐product matrices is primarily associated with fermentation‐based approaches. For example, solid‐state fermentation of corn germ meal substantially degrades ZEN, with reported reductions exceeding 90% under optimized conditions (Li et al. 2025). A separate study reported a degradation efficiency of approximately 96.27% in optimized systems (Bartkiene et al. 2021). However, other studies report only partial reductions in multiple mycotoxins following fermentation. This indicates that detoxification is often incomplete and dependent on process conditions and matrix characteristics (Bartkiene et al. 2018). These observations indicate that mitigation efficiency is strongly matrix‐ and process‐dependent and consequently requires validation within individual by‐product systems.

Alternaria Toxins

Alternaria toxins are increasingly recognized in cereal and fruit by‐products, with strong evidence of processing concentrating these toxins in peripheral fractions. Alternaria toxins are not uniformly removed during wheat dry milling but preferentially accumulate in bran, shorts, and tail‐end flour streams. In particular, tenuazonic acid (TeA) was quantified across most milling fractions, whereas alternariol (AOH) and alternariol monomethyl ether (AME) were concentrated in late‐stage streams and by‐products, with concentrations reaching up to 231 µg/kg for TeA, 11.2 µg/kg for AOH, and 2.6 µg/kg for AME (Janić Hajnal et al. 2019).

In fruit processing systems, particularly apples destined for industrialization, storage conditions were associated with a high prevalence of toxigenic Alternaria species and widespread detection of Alternaria toxin. The toxin occurrence was above analytical detection limits in fruit entering processing streams, with detection frequencies as high as 93% of samples and concentrations exceeding limits of detection (e.g., AOH ≥ 2.1 µg/kg, AME ≥ 3.2 µg/kg, TeA ≥ 3.8 µg/kg) (Pavicich et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that Alternaria toxins may persist and redistribute across both cereal‐ and fruit‐derived by‐product systems, while regulatory benchmarks for these toxins remain more limited than those established for major Fusarium toxins (Janić Hajnal et al. 2019).

Ochratoxin A

Ochratoxin A (OTA) presents a distinct hazard profile in by‐products owing to its persistence and redistribution during processing. OTA can be transferred into secondary streams and converted into transformation products during alkaline processing. In a rapeseed oil refining simulation of 100 µg/kg OTA, OTA was substantially removed during the deacidification stage. However, OTA was also converted into by‐products, with approximately 16% of OTA partitioned into secondary streams and approximately 83% converted into transformation products, including an identified opened‐lactone derivative (Lu et al. 2022). These findings indicate that the apparent reduction of OTA levels in refined products may not fully eliminate the toxin if contaminated by‐products or transformation products are subsequently incorporated into food‐related applications.

Overall, the available evidence indicates that agricultural by‐products function primarily as accumulation and transformation matrices for mycotoxins rather than inherently low‐risk materials (Lu et al. 2022). Across multiple processing systems, the reduction of contaminants in refined food fractions is frequently accompanied by redistribution or accumulation in by‐product streams (Vanara et al. 2018; Iamanaka et al. 2019; Scarpino et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2022). Furthermore, where mitigation has been evaluated within by‐product systems, particularly through fermentation, mycotoxin reduction occurs but is often partial and matrix‐dependent (Bartkiene et al. 2018; Li et al. 2025). Therefore, safe utilization of agricultural by‐products requires matrix‐specific monitoring, validation of mitigation strategies, and consideration of contaminant fate across all processing streams.

Pesticide Residues

Pesticide residues represent a distinct chemical hazard class in agricultural by‐products. Pesticide contamination arises from deliberate application during crop production and is strongly shaped by postharvest handling and processing. Unlike naturally occurring contaminants, such as mycotoxins, pesticide residues are governed by compound‐specific physicochemical properties and regulatory MRLs that are generally established for specified commodities rather than individual processing by‐products. In circular food systems and food safety contexts, this creates a structural challenge. Specifically, processing steps designed to reduce residues in foods may simultaneously concentrate or transfer them into by‐products that are increasingly valorized as functional food components and food‐derived ingredients. As summarized in Table 5, pesticide residues have been reported across a range of by‐product matrices, including fruit pomace, brewing residues, seed integuments, and extracted fractions, with residue levels and redistribution patterns strongly influenced by both compound properties and processing conditions.

Available evidence shows that pesticide residues are detectable in many by‐products and may also persist across diverse matrices. This occurs even when residue levels in the corresponding primary food products are reduced. High‐throughput screening approaches underline the breadth of potential contamination. Comprehensive multi‐residue analyses have screened between 105 and 566 pesticide compounds in fruit and plant‐derived by‐products, including insecticides, fungicides, herbicides, and acaricides (Bebek Markovinović et al. 2022; Nolasco et al. 2024; Mateus et al. 2025). In fruit by‐product extracts (citrus pomace, cherry pits, grape seeds, and date seeds), matrix‐matched calibration revealed pesticide residue levels ranging from 5 to 80 µg/kg, with multiple compounds detected within this range (Mateus et al. 2025). Similarly, strawberry pomace retained detectable pesticide residues following juice processing, whereas corresponding juice fractions showed relatively low levels, which indicated redistribution from liquid to solid matrices (Bebek Markovinović et al. 2022). Although individual residues were frequently reported below MRLs referenced to the corresponding edible commodities, detectable residues persisted across a wide range of matrices, and certain compounds showed differential retention depending on polarity and matrix composition (Nolasco et al. 2024; Mateus et al. 2025). Together, these findings indicate that pesticide residues remain in by‐products and are preferentially retained by by‐products in some cases. This highlights the need for pesticide risk assessment in by‐product valorization.

A key pattern emerging from the literature is that food processing does not necessarily eliminate pesticide residues but often redistributes them into secondary streams. In beverage production systems, pesticide residues are not necessarily degraded by fermentation or clarification. Instead, their distribution between the final product and solid residual fractions depends on pesticide properties and processing conditions. In beer brewing, field‐treated adjunct grains contained substantial initial residue levels, including glyphosate at 3770 µg/kg, mepiquat at 671 µg/kg, and flutriafol at 120 µg/kg. Following processing, these residue levels markedly decreased to 250, 38, and 10 µg/kg, respectively, whereas other medium‐polar pesticides initially present at 16–282 µg/kg in adjuncts were typically detected at < 3 µg/kg. However, redistribution into by‐products was pronounced. On average, 53% of pesticide residues were retrieved in spent grain (excluding glyphosate and mepiquat), and approximately 58% of total residues were recovered across the four by‐products combined (spent grain, trub, spent hops, spent yeast), whereas only ∼9% were retrieved in beer. In contrast, the more polar glyphosate and mepiquat showed different partitioning behaviors, with > 80% retrieved in sweet wort and subsequently transferred to beer. Approximately 33% of residues were not recovered. This is attributable to degradation or volatilization during milling, mashing, and lautering steps (Hakme et al. 2024). These results confirm that a low residue burden in the primary product does not necessarily indicate overall removal. Instead, it may reflect the transfer of residues into by‐products that are subsequently considered for valorization.

Similar redistribution patterns are observed in fruit processing systems. In apple processing studies focused on imidacloprid (IMI), the initial concentrations in apples under the recommended dosage and field dosage were 272.8 ± 20.7 and 1112.4 ± 102.5 µg/kg, respectively (Tian et al. 2024). During cold storage (0°C–2°C for 49 days), IMI concentrations decreased substantially to approximately 107.7–441.9 µg/kg. However, apple pomace retained relatively high levels of the parent compound and its metabolites. Metabolites, including the olefin–imidacloprid metabolite, the imidacloprid nitroguanidine metabolite (IMI‐NTG), 5‐hydroxy‐imidacloprid, and urea–imidacloprid metabolite, were detected at generally low concentrations near the limit of quantification (LOQ). However, IMI‐NTG exhibits higher mammalian toxicity than the parent compound. Mitigation steps that were applied to primary products, including washing (−34%), peeling (−65%), and boiling (up to −81% for certain compounds), reduced residues in edible fractions. However, this did not eliminate residues from pomace fractions, where persistence of parent compounds and metabolites was observed (Tian et al. 2024). These examples further indicate that pesticide risk in by‐products should not be assessed solely on the basis of parent compounds in edible portions but should also consider metabolite persistence in residual matrices.

In addition to redistribution during processing, valorization itself may increase the relevance of pesticide residues when by‐products are converted into concentrated ingredients or extracts. For example, pesticide residues were detected in extracts prepared from citrus pomace, grape seeds, sweet cherry pits, and date seeds, with quantified levels generally within the validated range of 5–80 µg/kg. Although most concentrations remained below MRLs referenced to the corresponding edible commodities, compound‐dependent variability in extraction efficiency indicates that pesticides with high affinity for organic phases may become enriched in concentrated extracts (Nolasco et al. 2024; Mateus et al. 2025). This suggests that extraction processes may retain or selectively concentrate certain pesticide residues, particularly in lipid‐rich or phenolic‐rich fractions. Therefore, pesticide management should be considered an integral part of by‐product valorization, especially when extraction or concentration processes are used to produce functional ingredients.

Simultaneously, current evidence does not support the assumption that all agricultural by‐products represent uniformly high pesticide levels. Certain matrices, such as coffee silver skin and other seed‐based residues, showed low or nondetectable pesticide levels despite screening of up to 69–265 pesticide compounds using gas chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) and liquid chromatography–MS/MS approaches. In cases of pesticide detection, pesticide concentrations were reported below MRLs referenced to comparable edible commodities. This suggests matrix‐ and crop‐specific variability in residue retention (Biondić Fučkar et al. 2023). Similarly, pine nut integument showed minimal residue retention, with most compounds detected below the LOQ or MRLs referenced in the original study. This indicates that not all by‐products act as concentration matrices for pesticide residues (Nolasco et al. 2024). Thus, the pesticide hazard associated with by‐products should be interpreted in a matrix‐specific manner. Specifically, risk assessment is guided by the type of raw material, pesticide properties, and processing history rather than by generalized assumptions.

Process‐Related Contaminants in Food‐Relevant By‐Products

Process‐related contaminants represent a distinct class of chemical hazards in agricultural by‐products. These contaminants primarily arise from thermal and secondary processing steps applied during or after primary production. These contaminants are of particular concern because they either persist in by‐products generated during processing or are newly formed when these materials are incorporated into food formulations. In contrast to preharvest contaminants, such as pesticides or mycotoxins, these compounds are formed de novo during processing and may persist or increase when by‐products are incorporated into food systems. As summarized in Table 6, the most relevant contaminants identified in food‐grade by‐products include acrylamide and 5‐hydroxymethylfurfural (5‐HMF). Both of these contaminants are generated during heat treatment and influenced by precursor composition and processing conditions.

Furthermore, process‐related contaminants may remain in by‐products generated during primary processing and may also form during subsequent food processing steps involving by‐product incorporation. Evidence from roasting‐derived by‐products indicates that thermally generated contaminants persist in secondary streams. In particular, coffee silver skin, which is a by‐product of roasting, contained acrylamide at levels ranging from 24 to 161 µg/kg, whereas 5‐HMF was generally below detection limits. This demonstrates that process‐generated contaminants may remain in by‐products intended for food use (Gottstein et al. 2021). These findings indicate that contaminants formed during primary processing can be transferred into by‐product fractions and subsequently carried into food applications.

Beyond simple retention, by‐products may also contribute to the formation of new process‐related contaminants during downstream processing. Notably, in extruded cereal systems enriched with brewer's spent grain, sugar beet pulp, and apple pomace, acrylamide was not detected in raw materials (< LOQ) but increased after extrusion to approximately 40–200+ µg/kg, depending on formulation and processing conditions. Similarly, 5‐HMF formation increased with processing temperature and by‐product inclusion levels (Jozinović et al. 2019). In this context, by‐products act as precursor‐rich matrices (e.g., reducing sugars and amino acids such as asparagine) that enhance the formation of process‐related contaminants during subsequent thermal treatments. Thus, these findings demonstrate that by‐products may influence contaminant formation through contaminant carryover and their compositional contribution to heat‐induced reactions during food processing.

Considering that these contaminants are largely formed during processing, the mitigation of process‐related contaminants is primarily dependent on process optimization rather than post‐formation removal. In particular, control of temperature, moisture content, and formulation composition influences contaminant formation. However, matrix‐specific validation remains limited. Given that precursor content differs substantially depending on source material and processing history, optimization strategies must specifically consider the compositional variability of by‐products. Accordingly, the management of process‐related contaminants should be incorporated into formulation and processing design during the early stages of by‐product valorization.

Overall, available studies indicate that process‐related contaminants in by‐products are mainly governed by processing conditions, precursor composition, and formulation design, rather than by preexisting contamination alone. However, available data remain limited and highly matrix‐dependent, particularly for food‐grade applications. This emphasizes the need for targeted evaluation of contaminant formation in by‐product‐enriched food systems. Therefore, future studies should prioritize matrix‐specific assessment of precursor–contaminant relationships and validation of practical mitigation strategies under realistic food processing conditions.

PAHs and MOHs in Food‐Relevant By‐Products

PAHs and MOHs represent additional organic contaminants of relevance to agricultural by‐products and by‐product‐derived food matrices. PAHs may originate from environmental contamination or be generated during thermal processing, particularly under conditions involving incomplete combustion or direct exposure to combustion gases. In contrast, MOH comprise complex mixtures generally differentiated into mineral oil saturated hydrocarbons (MOSHs) and mineral oil aromatic hydrocarbons (MOAHs), with contamination potentially arising from petroleum‐derived products, lubricants, processing equipment, storage, transport, and other stages of the production chain. Their occurrence in lipid‐rich secondary matrices is particularly relevant because subsequent processing may redistribute or concentrate these contaminants rather than eliminate them (Grob 2018; Koster et al. 2020; Moret et al. 2026).

Available PAH evidence demonstrates substantial matrix‐ and processing‐dependent variability (Table 7). In coffee silverskin, most PAHs were below the LOQ, although phenanthrene, fluoranthene, and chrysene occurred at low concentrations in Arabica silverskin, indicating that PAH occurrence is not uniform across by‐product matrices (Nolasco et al. 2022). In contrast, PAHs may be generated or markedly amplified during secondary processing of olive pomace. Drying at elevated temperatures resulted in total PAH concentrations of up to 2224 µg/kg, with formation strongly influenced by drying time and temperature (Göker et al. 2021). Monitoring across the olive pomace oil production chain similarly showed that contamination increased during storage and was substantially amplified during drying, particularly where the pomace was exposed to combustion fumes (Barp et al. 2022). These findings identify storage and drying conditions as important control points during by‐product valorization.

Processing may also redistribute PAHs into concentrated secondary streams or derived ingredients. Deodorizer distillates generated during soybean, rapeseed, corn, and rice bran oil refining contained substantially higher PAH concentrations than the corresponding edible oils, indicating the transfer and concentration of these contaminants into the refinery by‐product stream (Shi et al. 2019). Similarly, PAH4 present in tomato peel powder showed substantial transfer into tomato oleoresin during solvent extraction, whereas only a very small proportion entered purified lycopene crystals, demonstrating selective contaminant partitioning during extraction and crystallization (Wu et al. 2021). Conversely, processing can also provide mitigation opportunities. Short‐path molecular distillation substantially reduced PAHs in olive pomace oil, with removal enhanced by increasing temperature and decreasing absolute pressure (Kiralan and Tekin 2020). Thus, processing conditions can either generate, concentrate, redistribute, or reduce PAHs depending on the matrix and operation applied.

Evidence concerning MOSH and MOAH in agricultural by‐products is comparatively limited and is currently concentrated on olive pomace oil. Commercial olive pomace oils contained substantial MOSH and MOAH concentrations, and solvent extraction resulted in markedly higher concentrations than physical extraction, demonstrating a processing‐dependent concentration of these hydrocarbons (Gómez‐Coca et al. 2023). Industrial‐scale refining studies further showed that olive pomace oils contained higher MOH levels than conventional olive oils and that deodorization preferentially reduced lighter hydrocarbon fractions, while bleaching contributed to the removal of certain alkylated aromatic compounds (Menegoz Ursol et al. 2025). Importantly, detailed characterization of the MOAH fraction in olive pomace oil indicated the predominance of highly alkylated mono‐ and diaromatic compounds rather than the more toxicologically concerning poorly alkylated polyaromatic structures (Gómez‐Coca et al. 2023). However, this compositional profile does not eliminate toxicological concern, as EFSA considers dietary exposure to MOAH to be of potential concern because of the possible presence of genotoxic and carcinogenic components (EFSA CONTAM Panel 2023). Nevertheless, analytical interpretation of MOSH and MOAH remains challenging because they occur as complex unresolved mixtures and may be affected by matrix‐related interferences (Koster et al. 2020). From a regulatory perspective, Regulation (EU) 2023/915 establishes maximum levels (MLs) for PAHs in specified food categories; for oils and fats placed on the market for the final consumer or used as food ingredients, the MLs are 2.0 µg/kg for benzo[a]pyrene and 10.0 µg/kg for PAH4 (European Commission 2023). For MOAH, EU regulatory measures are evolving; proposed MLs received favorable Member State support on May 13, 2026, and an updated enforcement approach is being applied pending formal application of the new MLs (European Commission 2026). Overall, the available evidence indicates that PAHs and MOH should be considered within safety assessments that account for processing history and processing‐induced changes in contaminant fate, while the limited range of matrices investigated, particularly for MOSH/MOAH, highlights the need for broader occurrence, processing‐fate, and exposure data.

Heavy Metals

Heavy metals represent a persistent and nondegradable chemical hazard class in agricultural by‐products, with particular relevance in circular food systems. In particular, their accumulation occurs in peripheral plant tissues, such as bran layers, skins, pomace fractions, and fibrous residues, which are increasingly valorized. Unlike organic contaminants, heavy metals cannot be destroyed by thermal or mechanical processing and may accumulate in outer tissues during plant growth. Moreover, processing steps, such as milling, pressing, fermentation, or drying, may further redistribute these elements into by‐product streams. Across the included studies, arsenic, cadmium, and lead emerged as the most toxicologically relevant metals, whereas other elements were generally reported at low levels or assessed against available regulatory or health‐based benchmarks. Table 8 summarizes the occurrence and regulatory status of heavy metals in food‐relevant agricultural by‐products. Overall, current evidence indicates that heavy metal occurrence in by‐products is highly matrix‐dependent, with cereal‐derived outer fractions representing the highest‐risk category.

Arsenic

Arsenic was identified as the most critical heavy metal in cereal‐derived by‐products. In rice bran, inorganic arsenic (i‐As) concentrations ranged from 0.561 to 0.916 mg/kg (raw) and accounted for approximately 95% of the total arsenic, thereby indicating the predominance of this toxic inorganic form. These concentrations exceeded the EU ML of 0.10 mg/kg for rice destined for foods for infants and young children and the U.S. FDA action level of 100 µg/kg (0.10 mg/kg) for infant rice cereals. They also exceeded the current EU ML of 0.15 mg/kg for non‐parboiled milled rice and the Codex ML of 0.20 mg/kg for polished rice when these rice‐specific limits are used as comparative benchmarks for rice bran. This demonstrates that rice bran valorization without prior mitigation may pose regulatory and exposure concerns.

Mitigation evaluated for rice bran indicates that percolation with arsenic‐free boiling water reduced i‐As by approximately 65%–76%, with final concentrations of approximately 0.167 mg/kg. Similarly, percolation cooking reduced i‐As levels from 0.376–1.079 to 0.091–0.201 mg/kg, with treated concentrations overlapping the EU and Codex rice benchmarks (Signes‐Pastor et al. 2017). These findings demonstrate that processing‐based mitigation substantially reduces arsenic levels in rice bran. However, the effectiveness remains dependent on processing conditions and may not consistently achieve strict regulatory thresholds.

In grape pomace (Pereira et al. 2020), arsenic concentrations ranged between 0.10 and 0.36 mg/kg, and intake modeling identified arsenic as the intake‐limiting element, thus restricting daily consumption depending on variety. Other matrices, including coffee pulp flour and extract (Gil‐Ramírez et al. 2024), coffee silver skin (Gottstein et al. 2021), and pine nut skin (Nolasco et al. 2024), showed comparatively low or variable arsenic levels. Together, these findings indicate that arsenic‐related risk varies substantially across by‐product types, with cereal‐derived fractions presenting the greatest concern for food‐related applications.

Cadmium

Cadmium concentrations varied depending on matrix origin and environmental exposure. In grape pomace samples (Pereira et al. 2020), cadmium levels were approximately 0.25 mg/kg. Moreover, coffee‐derived by‐products (Gil‐Ramírez et al. 2024) and pine nut skin (Nolasco et al. 2024) showed cadmium levels reported as low or below detection thresholds. Thus, the available evidence indicates matrix‐dependent cadmium occurrence across the evaluated by‐products.

Lead

Lead was reported in several fruit‐derived by‐products and processing residues. In grape pomace (Pereira et al. 2020), lead concentrations were approximately 5 mg/kg. Moreover, intake modeling suggested that lead could constrain daily consumption under conservative exposure scenarios. Furthermore, in strawberry by‐products (Bebek Markovinović et al. 2022), lead concentrations ranged from < 0.03 to 0.076 mg/kg and remained below the benchmark for strawberry fruit used in the study. Other matrices, including coffee pulp (Gil‐Ramírez et al. 2024), coffee silver skin (Gottstein et al. 2021), and pine nut skin (Nolasco et al. 2024), showed comparatively low reported lead levels or concentrations below detection thresholds. These findings indicate that lead occurrence is highly variable among fruit‐derived by‐products and may be elevated relative to comparative food benchmarks in some matrices.

Other Heavy Metals

Other heavy metals beyond arsenic, cadmium, and lead were extensively characterized in certain matrices. For example, in grape pomace (Pereira et al. 2020), aluminum ranged between 57 and 338 mg/kg, iron ranged between 86 and 215 mg/kg, copper ranged between 6 and 88 mg/kg, and zinc ranged between 1.6 and 28 mg/kg. Moreover, mercury was detected at 0.004 mg/kg, with nickel and chromium present at low levels. Aluminum and iron were present at comparatively higher concentrations than the other reported metals. In addition, coffee pulp flour and extract (Gil‐Ramírez et al. 2024) showed relatively low reported levels of several macro‐ and microelements, and pine nut skin (Nolasco et al. 2024) contained selected detectable metals, while others were below quantification limits. Aside from specific concerns related to arsenic and lead, these studies collectively indicate substantial matrix‐ and element‐specific variability, while regulatory comparison is not possible for all reported elements or by‐product matrices.

Overall, the reviewed evidence indicates that heavy metal occurrence in agricultural by‐products is strongly matrix‐dependent. Cereal outer fractions, particularly rice bran, present the highest arsenic‐related risk and represent the only matrices for which mitigation strategies for heavy metals have been experimentally evaluated within food‐relevant systems. In contrast, fruit and coffee by‐products showed more matrix‐dependent and element‐specific constraints. Therefore, current evidence suggests that risk management of heavy metals in by‐product valorization relies primarily on matrix‐specific monitoring, source control, and assessment against appropriate regulatory benchmarks where available. Simultaneously, validated mitigation approaches remain comparatively limited beyond arsenic reduction strategies.

Allergenic Potential and Intrinsic Bioactive Hazards

Direct evidence of allergenic protein presence and immunoglobulin E (IgE) reactivity in agricultural by‐product matrices remains limited. Most available knowledge is derived from a small number of studies that specifically evaluated by‐product fractions and is supplemented by indirect evidence from source materials and processing effects. The valorization of agricultural by‐products into food systems may introduce immunological hazards, particularly when materials originate from allergenic crops or contain structurally stable storage proteins. These proteins are often resistant to thermal and enzymatic degradation, which increases the likelihood of persistence in agricultural by‐product matrices. Consequently, assessment of allergenic hazards in by‐product‐derived ingredients remains constrained by limited quantitative and clinically validated evidence.

Regulatory Framework

Agricultural by‐products intended for food use are not governed by a dedicated regulatory framework. Instead, they are assessed within existing food laws originally developed for primary commodities and conventional ingredients. Consequently, regulatory evaluation depends on the intended function of the derived material (e.g., ingredient, novel food, additive) rather than its origin as an agricultural by‐product (Precup et al. 2024). When by‐products are incorporated into food systems, existing safety standards and contaminant limits are generally applied by analogy to comparable food categories. This approach is necessary because explicit benchmarks for matrices, such as pomace, bran, husks, or spent grains, are lacking. This creates interpretive challenges, particularly where contaminants are redistributed or concentrated during processing (European Commission 2005, 2023; Otero et al. 2023; Precup et al. 2024).

Historically, a substantial proportion of regulatory development related to agricultural by‐products has occurred within feed and feed additive frameworks, where agricultural by‐products are explicitly addressed. However, direct translation of these approaches to food systems remains limited because food applications require unique exposure assessments and safety considerations.

In the European Union, ingredients not used for human consumption to a significant degree within the Union before May 15, 1997 may fall within the scope of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 (Novel Food Regulation) (Iriondo‐DeHond et al. 2020; Precup et al. 2024). Under this framework, the EFSA applies standard risk assessment criteria, including identity, production process, compositional data, specifications, intake assessment, toxicology where needed, and allergenicity, without distinguishing by‐products as a separate category. EFSA evaluations of by‐product‐derived ingredients consistently follow this approach. At the broader chemical‐hazard level, EFSA's OpenFoodTox database compiles toxicological information and reference values for substances relevant to food and feed safety, including pesticides and contaminants (Benfenati et al. 2026). However, OpenFoodTox is a hazard‐characterization resource rather than a database of matrix‐specific regulatory limits and therefore does not provide dedicated MLs for agricultural by‐product matrices.

Notably, rhamnogalacturonan‐I‐enriched carrot fiber derived from carrot pomace was considered safe under the proposed conditions of its use while retaining the allergenic potential of its source (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2025). Similarly, dried coffee husk (cascara) was approved with consideration of caffeine exposure (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2022), and partially hydrolyzed protein derived from spent barley and rice was confirmed as safe except for the expected allergenicity from its source materials (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2023a). Together, these cases demonstrate that by‐product‐derived ingredients are evaluated using conventional food safety criteria but without matrix‐specific regulatory adjustments (European Parliament and Council 2015; Iriondo‐DeHond et al. 2020; Precup et al. 2024; EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2022, 2023a, 2025).

Moreover, where a by‐product has a documented history of safe consumption outside the European Union, it may be assessed as a traditional food from a third country under Articles 14–15 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. For example, the EFSA raises no safety objections against coffee cherry pulp based on compositional data and long‐term consumption history. This allows its use in infusions and beverages (EFSA 2021). This approach reduces the need for extensive toxicological datasets but still requires structured evaluation of identity, composition, and intended use. This reinforces that by‐products are accommodated within existing frameworks rather than regulated independently (EFSA 2021; Precup et al. 2024).

Depending on technological function, by‐product‐derived materials may also fall under other regulatory domains. For example, when used as food additives, by‐products are regulated under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, with authorization procedures defined in Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 and specifications under Regulation (EU) No 231/2012. For instance, a pectin‐rich extract derived from Coffea arabica fruit was evaluated as a food additive. The EFSA concluded that its use did not raise safety concerns based on exposure assessment, compositional analysis, and toxicological data. This example illustrates that regulatory classification is determined primarily by intended technological function rather than by‐product origin (European Parliament and Council 2008a, 2008b; Precup et al. 2024; Castle et al. 2026).

Regulatory decisions are driven by exposure and compositional safety rather than source familiarity. For instance, EFSA did not establish the safety of yellow/orange tomato extract as a novel food owing to the potential exceedance of acceptable daily intake for lycopene and insufficient safety data for associated carotenoids (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2023b). Similarly, coffee by‐product evaluations highlight that specific hazards, such as caffeine and process contaminants, may determine conditions of use even when the source material is edible (Iriondo‐DeHond et al. 2020; Tritsch et al. 2022). These cases further indicate that regulatory approval depends primarily on characterization of the final ingredient and anticipated exposure rather than classification of the raw material as a by‐product.

A key limitation of current regulatory systems is their commodity‐based structure, which does not explicitly address processing‐driven changes typical of by‐products. For instance, pesticide residues may accumulate in peel‐derived fractions and other secondary matrices (Otero et al. 2023). Furthermore, processing may also contribute to the formation or persistence of process‐related contaminants, including acrylamide and 5‐HMF, depending on the matrix composition and processing conditions. For acrylamide, Regulation (EU) 2017/2158 establishes mitigation measures and benchmark levels for specified food categories; however, these benchmark levels are not specifically established for most valorized agricultural by‐product matrices considered in this review (European Commission 2017). Although existing regulations may account for processing‐related changes in contaminant concentrations through concentration, dilution, or processing factors, dedicated provisions addressing contaminant redistribution, transformation products, and the co‐occurrence of multiple hazards within valorized by‐product streams remain limited (European Commission 2023).

A similar limitation applies to pesticide regulation in the European Union. Under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, specific MRLs are generally established for commodities listed in Annex I rather than for individual processing by‐products. For processed and/or composite products without specific MRLs, Article 20 applies the MRL of the relevant Annex I commodity while accounting for changes in residue concentrations resulting from processing or mixing. Thus, valorized by‐products without specific MRLs require consideration of processing‐related residue concentration or dilution rather than direct application of a dedicated by‐product limit (European Parliament and Council 2005).

Allergenicity is similarly governed through general food law rather than by‐product‐specific provisions. Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, allergen labeling is source‐based. Therefore, by‐product‐derived ingredients may retain the allergenic characteristics of their parent materials despite processing. EFSA assessments of carrot pomace fiber and spent cereal protein confirm that allergenic potential may persist in derived fractions (EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods, and Food Allergens 2023a, 2025; European Parliament and Council 2011).

At the international level, Codex Alimentarius provides commodity‐specific MRLs for pesticides and MLs for contaminants and natural toxins. Codex pesticide MRLs are generally defined for a specified raw agricultural commodity or portion thereof, unless otherwise indicated (Codex Alimentarius Commission 1993). Screening of the Codex Pesticide Residues in Food database (Codex Alimentarius Commission 2026) identified MRLs for several food‐relevant agricultural processing fractions, including processed and unprocessed rice, wheat, and barley bran, unprocessed rye bran, and wheat germ. However, several explicitly designated by‐products, including dried citrus pulp, dried grape pomace, soybean hulls, sugar beet pulp, and apple pomace, are classified within the Codex commodity system as by‐products used for animal feeding purposes. Consequently, these MRLs cannot be directly interpreted as regulatory limits for the same matrices when valorized as human‐food ingredients. Detailed pesticide‐specific MRLs, Codex commodity classifications, and their relevance to food‐oriented by‐product utilization are provided in File S2.

A comparable limitation is evident for chemical contaminants. The Codex General Standard for Contaminants and Toxins in Food and Feed (CXS 193‐1995) establishes MLs for contaminants, including mycotoxins and metals (Codex Alimentarius Commission 1995). However, Codex indicates that MLs are generally established for primary products, while their application to processed or derived products may require appropriate concentration or dilution factors and information on contaminant behavior during processing. Separate MLs may be appropriate when contaminant concentrations in processed products consistently differ from those in the corresponding primary products. Thus, although the Codex provides mechanisms for considering processing‐related changes, dedicated limits for valorized agricultural by‐product matrices remain limited. This is particularly relevant where contaminants are concentrated, redistributed, or transformed during fractionation and secondary processing.

In the United States, agricultural by‐products and the ingredients derived from them may be evaluated within existing regulatory pathways, including the Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) framework (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023b). Targeted screening of the U.S. FDA GRAS Notice Inventory (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026) identified both direct or minimally processed agricultural by‐products and ingredients obtained through further processing of secondary streams (File S3). A particularly relevant example is orange pomace and enzyme‐treated orange pomace (GRN 719), for which the safety evaluation considered identity and manufacturing, compositional specifications, dietary exposure, and toxicological evidence (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2017). Other examples include dried apple peel powder (GRN 805), citrus fiber derived from citrus‐processing streams (GRN 943), and corn bran arabinoxylan (GRN 1073), illustrating the evaluation of both direct by‐products and further‐processed ingredients (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2019, 2021, 2023a). Notably, the hazard categories considered across these GRAS notices overlap with those identified in the occurrence literature reviewed herein, including pesticide residues, mycotoxins, heavy metals, and microbiological hazards. However, the specifications and analytical criteria reported in individual GRAS notices are specific to the notified substance, its manufacturing process, and intended conditions of use; therefore, they do not constitute generally applicable FDA MLs for the corresponding agricultural by‐product matrices.

Overall, current regulatory systems provide functional pathways for the safe use of agricultural by‐products in food systems but remain insufficiently adapted to circular utilization. The limited and fragmented availability of matrix‐specific criteria necessitates reliance on the interpretation of existing regulations, particularly in the context of contaminant redistribution, processing‐induced transformation, and cumulative exposure. Accordingly, future regulatory frameworks should move beyond commodity‐based assumptions and incorporate matrix‐specific criteria, explicit consideration of contaminant concentration and transformation during processing, and clear guidance for cumulative and co‐occurring hazards in valorized by‐product streams. Addressing these gaps is essential for aligning regulatory frameworks with the increasing integration of agricultural by‐products into sustainable food systems (Otero et al. 2023; Precup et al. 2024).

Challenges and Future Perspectives

The evidence reviewed above identifies several cross‐cutting challenges that limit the safe incorporation of agricultural by‐products into food systems, including matrix heterogeneity, processing‐driven contaminant redistribution, exposure uncertainty, and limitations in regulatory interpretation (Figure 1).

Matrix Heterogeneity and Sampling Uncertainty

A defining characteristic of agricultural by‐products is their compositional heterogeneity, which directly governs hazard distribution across matrices. Unlike primary commodities, by‐products represent structurally complex fractions (e.g., peels, brans, pomaces) where contaminants, including mycotoxins, pesticide residues, and heavy metals, are often nonuniformly partitioned. Moreover, outer tissues frequently act as accumulation sites, while processing further redistributes contaminants within secondary streams. Consequently, sampling strategies developed for relatively homogeneous raw commodities may not adequately capture the variability of by‐product matrices. This leads to uncertainty in the occurrence data and risk assessment.

Processing‐Driven Redistribution and Incomplete Mass Balance

A consistent finding across hazard categories is that processing redistributes rather than eliminates contaminants (Figure 1). Mycotoxins accumulate in bran and distillers' fractions (Hao et al. 2022), pesticide residues accumulate in pomace and extracted fractions (Tian et al. 2024; Mateus et al. 2025), and heavy metals accumulate in outer plant tissues (Chen et al. 2025). Comparable processing‐dependent behavior is observed for PAHs and MOHs, as PAHs may form during olive pomace drying or become concentrated in oil‐refining by‐products, while MOSH and MOAH can be substantially concentrated during solvent extraction of olive pomace oil (Göker et al. 2021; Shi et al. 2019; Gómez‐Coca et al. 2023). However, quantitative mass‐balance data tracking contaminant fate across processing chains remain scarce. Consequently, whether processing reduces overall hazard exposure or simply transfers contaminants into secondary streams that may later be incorporated into food applications is often unclear. Therefore, addressing this gap requires integrated and process‐level tracking of contaminant redistribution.

Co‐Occurrence and Transformation‐Driven Hazards

Agricultural by‐products frequently contain multiple co‐occurring hazards, including multi‐mycotoxin contamination, pesticide mixtures, processing‐induced contaminants, PAHs, and MOHs. Despite evidence of frequent co‐occurrence in by‐product matrices, current assessment approaches remain predominantly single‐compound focused (Hao et al. 2022). In addition, transformation products, such as modified mycotoxins, pesticide metabolites, and heat‐induced contaminants, remain insufficiently characterized in these systems. Collectively, these limitations create a disconnect between measured contaminant occurrence and realistic exposure scenarios associated with by‐product‐derived ingredients. Accordingly, future research should prioritize multi‐residue assessment strategies and cumulative risk evaluation frameworks.

Analytical and Exposure Data Gaps

Across all hazard categories, a critical limitation is the lack of food‐specific occurrence and exposure data for by‐product‐derived materials. A substantial proportion of existing studies originate from nonfood systems, including feed matrices, environmental reuse, and bioconversion (Chen et al. 2025; Robalds et al. 2024; Fernandes et al. 2024). This limits direct applicability to human dietary risk assessment. Moreover, most studies report occurrence without linking results to intake, bioaccessibility, or cumulative exposure. Variability in analytical scope, detection limits, and reporting formats further limits cross‐study comparability. This limitation is particularly relevant for MOSH and MOAH, for which complex unresolved hydrocarbon mixtures, matrix interferences, and differences in sample preparation and quantification can contribute to substantial analytical variability (Koster et al. 2020). Accordingly, future studies should prioritize exposure‐oriented assessment approaches that reflect realistic consumption scenarios of by‐product‐derived food ingredients.

Regulatory and Industrial Translation Barriers

Translation of by‐product valorization into industrial food applications remains constrained by limited matrix‐specific regulatory guidance and insufficient industrial validation of mitigation strategies. Approaches such as fermentation, enzymatic detoxification, and biological conversion have demonstrated partial hazard reduction under controlled conditions but require validation at the industrial scale (Park et al. 2025). Moreover, processing may reduce one hazard while concentrating another, necessitating integrated monitoring across primary and secondary streams.

In addition to regulatory and technical limitations, economic considerations may further constrain the large‐scale utilization of agricultural by‐products in food applications. Because contaminant occurrence and distribution vary substantially among by‐product types, sources, and processing conditions, matrix‐specific monitoring and risk assessment approaches are often required instead of relying on generalized safety assumptions. Consequently, additional analytical testing, validation, and surveillance efforts may increase implementation costs. Furthermore, by‐products frequently require dedicated storage, preservation, or stabilization measures to minimize microbial growth, mycotoxin formation, and quality deterioration during handling and transport. These additional requirements may impose a substantial economic burden, particularly for small‐ and medium‐scale processors, and may influence the feasibility of by‐product valorization strategies. These constraints, combined with increased compliance and analytical requirements, limit the scalability of by‐product valorization.

Future Perspectives

Future research should directly address these limitations through improved sampling, process‐level contaminant tracking, and food‐relevant exposure assessment. Representative sampling strategies are needed to capture the heterogeneous distribution of hazards within by‐product matrices, while mass‐balance approaches should clarify whether contaminants are reduced, transformed, or redistributed throughout processing chains.

Another important area involves the broad assessment of co‐occurring hazards and transformation products. Despite increasing evidence that multiple hazards may coexist within the same by‐product matrix, existing studies focus largely on individual contaminants. Therefore, expanding analytical coverage to include major metabolites and transformation products will improve the relevance of future risk assessments, particularly for complex food applications. Emerging rapid‐screening and data‐driven analytical tools may also support hazard monitoring in by‐product matrices when combined with validated food safety assessment approaches.

Additional occurrence and exposure data specific to by‐product‐derived food ingredients are also needed, especially under realistic use levels and consumption scenarios. From a regulatory perspective, existing food safety frameworks require further clarification in terms of their interpretation for agricultural by‐product matrices, particularly where contaminants may become concentrated or altered during processing. Overall, further progress in this field will depend on effective integration of sampling strategies, hazard characterization, exposure assessment, and regulatory interpretation to support the safe incorporation of agricultural by‐products into food systems (Figure 5).

Conclusion

The valorization of agricultural by‐products offers valuable opportunities for resource efficiency, waste reduction, and functional ingredient development within circular food systems. However, the evidence synthesized and discussed in this review demonstrates that agricultural by‐products should not be regarded as inherently low‐risk materials based on the low contaminant levels of primary refined products. This review advances the field by framing agricultural by‐products as dynamic matrices rather than passive residues. In these matrices, hazards are redistributed, transformed, and potentially amplified across processing chains. Moreover, these matrices may act as reservoirs, concentration points, or transformation matrices for microbiological, chemical, and allergenic hazards. This highlights the need for chain‐wide, matrix‐specific risk assessment. Although quality attributes, such as stability and bioactive compound retention, are important for successful utilization, safe valorization requires their integration with rigorous hazard characterization. The persistence of structurally stable allergens, co‐occurrence of multiple contaminants, formation of process‐induced hazards, and limited applicability of existing regulatory benchmarks collectively emphasize the complexity of incorporating by‐products into food systems. Therefore, addressing these challenges requires a shift from opportunistic reuse toward structured and risk‐based approaches supported by standardized monitoring and validated mitigation strategies. By aligning sustainability objectives with robust food safety frameworks, agricultural by‐products can be effectively integrated into circular food systems without compromising public health. Accordingly, continued interdisciplinary research and regulatory adaptation will be essential to support their safe and scalable utilization.
