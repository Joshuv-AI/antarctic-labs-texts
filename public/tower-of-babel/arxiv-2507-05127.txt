Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC) From Scratch
Felix Dangel, Bálint Mucsányi, Tobias Weber, Runa Eschenhagen

Abstract
Kronecker-factored approximate curvature ( Martens & Grosse 2015 , KFAC,) is arguably one of the most prominent curvature approximations in deep learning. Its applications range from optimization to Bayesian deep learning, training data attribution with influence functions, and model compression or merging. While the intuition behind KFAC is easy to understand, its implementation is tedious: It comes in many flavours, has common pitfalls when translating the math to code, and is challenging to test, which complicates ensuring a properly functioning implementation. Some of the authors themselves have dealt with these challenges and experienced the discomfort of not being able to fully test their code. Thanks to recent advances in understanding KFAC, we are now able to provide test cases and a recipe for a reliable KFAC implementation. This tutorial is meant as a ground-up introduction to KFAC. In contrast to the existing work, our focus lies on providing both math and code side-by-side and providing test cases based on the latest insights into KFAC that are scattered throughout the literature. We hope this tutorial provides a contemporary view of KFAC that allows beginners to gain a deeper understanding of this curvature approximation while lowering the barrier to its implementation, extension, and usage in practice.

Preface

Relevance.

Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (KFAC) is arguably one of the most prominent curvature approximations in deep learning.
Its applications range from optimization
(
Martens & Grosse 2015
;
Grosse & Martens 2016
;
Eschenhagen et al. 2023
;
Benzing 2022
;
Petersen et al. 2023
)
to Bayesian deep learning
(
Daxberger et al. 2021
)
, training data attribution with influence functions
(
Grosse et al. 2023
;
Bae et al. 2024
)
, and model pruning
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
and merging
(
Tam et al. 2024
)
.
The intuition behind KFAC is easy to understand: it approximates curvature information in the form of Kronecker-factored matrices that are both cheap to store and compute with.
There exist several packages that compute KFAC
(
Botev & Martens 2022
;
Dangel et al. 2020b
;
Osawa et al. 2023
;
Grosse et al. 2023
)
.
However, their details are often undocumented, making them hard to extend, e.g. adding support for new layers, or adding features like eigenvalue correction
(
George et al. 2018
)
.
We claim that achieving a bug-free KFAC implementation is non-trivial, and our understanding is still evolving.

Goals.

This is an attempt to bundle the scattered knowledge about KFAC into a single document, explain all the technicalities and pitfalls, and present tests to ensure bug-free implementations.
Our goal with this tutorial is to explain, from scratch, how to obtain a KFAC implementation that is easy to build on, allowing newcomers to fully grasp the concept and providing experienced practitioners a code base to build on.
We use PyTorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
and rely on its modular interface (
torch.nn
modules), which is familiar to most deep learning practitioners.
The tutorial’s goals are the following:

1.

[Basics] Provide a self-contained introduction to curvature matrices (the ‘C’ in KFAC).

To understand KFAC, we first need to understand the objects it aims to approximate, namely, curvature matrices of neural network loss functions.
These curvature matrices are all based on the Hessian, which gives rise to the curvature if we Taylor-expand the loss up to quadratic order.
We will introduce common approximations of the Hessian, like the generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN), Fisher, and empirical Fisher (EF), and how to work with them through autodiff (
Section

2
).
This establishes the basis of our tutorial and serves as a foundation to test our KFAC implementation (
Section

3
shows a compact summary).

2.

[Intuition] Show how Kronecker products naturally emerge in curvature matrices (the ‘KF’ in KFAC).

Doing so motivates Kronecker products as a ‘natural’ structure to approximate curvature, which is what KFAC does (
Section

4
).

3.

[Code] Explain how to implement and test KFAC (the ‘A’ in KFAC).

Present the different flavours of KFAC and show how they are related to the curvature matrices, which allows them to be tested (
Section

5
).

Scope & limitations.

To make this tutorial pedagogically valuable while providing a different experience than just reading the original papers introducing KFAC, we had to make compromises, as understanding and implementing KFAC in all its different variations quickly becomes daunting.
Here is a rough breakdown of the degrees of freedom:

A more fully-featured KFAC implementation is provided by the

This tutorial is meant to be self-contained.
Therefore, we will start with an extensive introduction to KFAC-relevant concepts.
This allows us to build the core functionality we will later need to verify our implementation.

Roadmap.

First, we introduce the empirical risk (‘the loss’,
Section

2.1
) whose curvature KFAC approximates, and neural networks (
Section

2.2
).
One recurring theme in our discussion will be that the loss and neural net have probabilistic interpretations in most deep learning settings: Minimizing the empirical risk corresponds to maximum likelihood estimation where the neural net models a likelihood (
Section

2.3
).
Next, since curvature information is based on the Hessian, which contains second-order partial derivatives, we will talk about first- and second-order derivatives, and how to compute with them using PyTorch’s automatic differentiation (
Section

2.4
).
We conclude with an introduction to all curvature matrices relevant to our discussion (
Section

2.5
).
These include the Hessian, generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) matrix, and different flavours of the Fisher information matrix, which follows from the probabilistic interpretation from
Section

2.3
.

2.1
Empirical Risk Minimization

We consider supervised learning with a neural network
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times\Theta\to{\mathcal{F}}
that maps a given input
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
from a domain
{\mathcal{X}}
to a prediction
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
in a prediction space
{\mathcal{F}}
using parameters
{\bm{\theta}}\in\Theta
from a parameter space
\Theta
.
Predictions are scored with a criterion function
c\colon{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that compares the prediction to the true target
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
from a label space
{\mathcal{Y}}
, producing a single number called the loss on datum
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
.

For a data set
{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\mid n=1,\dots,N\}
of collected labelled examples, we evaluate the per-datum criteria and accumulate them into the total loss, using an accumulation factor
R\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})&=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{n}({\bm{\theta}})\\
&=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}c(f({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}}_{n})\,.\end{split}

(1)

Common choices for
R
are
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}},1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{N\dim({\mathcal{Y}})}}
; see
for a function that computes
R
.
The goal of training is to find the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
that reduce the empirical risk
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})
without overfitting to the training data.

Equation

1
disentangles ‘the loss’ into three components: the neural network
f
, the criterion function
c
, and the reduction factor
R
.
The most common loss functions are the square loss for regression and the softmax cross-entropy loss for classification.
We show their criterion and reduction factors in
Sections

2.1
and
2.1
).

{example}

[Square loss,
]
For least squares regression with vector-valued targets (
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}^{C}={\mathcal{F}}
), the criterion and reduction factor of PyTorch’s
nn.MSELoss
are

\displaystyle c({\bm{f}},{\bm{y}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c=1}^{C}[{\bm{f}}-{\bm{y}}]_{c}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&\text{{reduction="sum"}}\\
\frac{2}{N\dim({\mathcal{Y}})}&\text{{reduction="mean"}}\end{cases}

where
\dim({\mathcal{Y}})=C
in the vector case, but
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{F}}
could also be a matrix or tensor space.

{example}

[Cross-entropy loss,
]
For classification, with categorical targets (
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{1,\dots,C\}
and
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathbb{R}}^{C}
), PyTorch’s
nn.CrossEntropyLoss
uses the following criterion function and reduction factor

\displaystyle c({\bm{f}},y)=-\log([\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}})]_{y})\,,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{{reduction="sum"}}\\
\frac{1}{N\dim({\mathcal{Y}})}&\text{{reduction="mean"}}\end{cases}

with
[\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}})]_{i}=\nicefrac{{\exp([{\bm{f}}]_{i})}}{{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp([{\bm{f}}]_{j})}}
.
For the vector case
\dim({\mathcal{Y}})=1
, but
{\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{F}}
could also be compatible matrix or tensor spaces in a more general setup where we aim to classify sequences of categorical labels.

{caveat}

[Scaling]
Implementations of loss functions mix the concepts of criterion and reduction.
This is often fine, but sometimes makes it difficult to translate to new loss functions without accidentally forgetting a factor.
By keeping both concepts separate, we reduce the chance of introducing scaling bugs.

2.2
Deep Neural Networks

We consider sequential neural networks as they are simple and widely used.
They are composed of
L
layers
f^{(l)}(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}),l=1,\dots,L
, each of which can have its own parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}
.
The whole network is simply a stack of layers, i.e.
f=f^{(L)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(1)}
, and the evaluation produces a set of intermediate features,

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{(l)}=f^{(l)}({\bm{x}}^{(l-1)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}),\quad l=1,\dots,L,

starting with
{\bm{x}}^{(0)}\leftarrow{\bm{x}}
, ending in
{\bm{x}}^{(L)}\leftarrow f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
.
We refer to the hidden representations
{\bm{x}}^{(l-1)}
as the
input to layer
l
, and to
{\bm{x}}^{(l)}
as the
output of layer
l
.
Some layers have empty parameters, e.g. activation, pooling, or dropout layers.

2.3
Probabilistic Interpretation

So far, we have considered minimizing an empirical risk over a data set given an arbitrary criterion function
c
.
Now, we take a step back and first describe empirical risk minimization as an approximation of minimizing the intractable population risk.
This perspective connects to a probabilistic interpretation of empirical risk minimization as maximum likelihood estimation that will occur throughout the tutorial, e.g. when we define the Fisher information matrix as curvature matrix (
Section

2.5
).

Empirical risk as expectation.

Recall the empirical risk from
Equation

1
which we minimize during training,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{n}({\bm{\theta}})\,.

Why is it called an ‘empirical’ risk?
Because it can be expressed as expectation over an empirical distribution in the following sense:

Assume there exists a data-generating process
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
over input-target pairs.
Ideally, we want to minimize the risk over this distribution,

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\sim p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}[c(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}})]\,.

However,
p_{\text{data}}
is intractable.
Therefore, we draw a finite collection of samples into a data set

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\mid({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}p_{\text{data}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\}\,.

Then, we can replace the intractable data-generating process
p_{\text{data}}
with the tractable empirical distribution
p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
implied by data set
{\mathbb{D}}
.
It consists of a uniformly weighted sum of delta peaks around the collected data points,

\displaystyle p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta({\mathbf{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{n})\delta({\mathbf{y}}-{\bm{y}}_{n})\,.

This turns risk minimization into a tractable task:

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\sim{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}}[c(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}})]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\sim{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}}[c(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}})].

By writing out the expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}c(f({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}}_{n}).

Note that the minimized objective is the empirical risk in
Equation

1

scaled by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{NR}}
.
However, we can arbitrarily scale objectives without changing the
location of their minima.
Hence, the above is equivalent to minimizing the empirical risk

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})\,.

We have thus shown that empirical risk minimization is minimizing a (scaled) expectation of the criterion
c
over an empirical density
p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
.

Neural nets parameterize likelihoods.

Let’s approach this from a probabilistic perspective now.
Assume we want to learn
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}})p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
using a parameterized density of the form
p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})=p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
where
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})=\int p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}}
is the marginal density of the input data.
Note that we only model the likelihood of the labels with parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.

One plausible approach to make
p
resemble
p_{\text{data}}
is to minimize their KL divergence,

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\mid\mid p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))\,.

We can simplify this expression by substituting the definition of the KL divergence and dropping terms that do not depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}[-\log(p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))]\,.

This looks very similar to the expected risk from above.
Next, let’s factorize our model distribution using its conditional and marginal densities and drop
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
as it does not depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}[-\log(p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))]\,.

To make this problem tractable, we need to replace the intractable data-generating process
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
with the empirical distribution
p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
again:

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})}[-\log(p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))].

Writing out the expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}-\log(p({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}})).

To make this expression more similar to the empirical risk, we introduce a general scaling
R
and change the likelihood’s parameterization from
p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
to
r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))
with a neural net
f
:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}R\sum_{n}-\log(r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid f({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}})))

This parameterization makes it clear that the neural network represents a conditional distribution over the labels given the inputs (and parameters).

The criterion is the negative log-likelihood.

We are now very close to writing down the explicit connection between empirical risk minimization and maximum likelihood estimation.
The last remaining step is to connect the model’s likelihood
r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))
with the criterion function
c(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}})
from empirical risk minimization.
It turns out that empirical risk minimization with square loss (
Section

2.1
) corresponds to maximum likelihood estimation (or equivalently, negative log-likelihood minimization) of a Gaussian distribution over the labels (
Section

2.3
).
Similarly, classification with softmax cross-entropy criterion amounts to maximum likelihood estimation where the neural net parameterizes a categorical distribution (
Section

2.3
).

provides functions to sample labels from these distributions.

The neural net’s interpretation as a likelihood allows using probabilistic concepts to measure similarity for comparing two networks.
This will be useful when we define the Fisher information matrix (
Section

2.5.3
), a common curvature matrix.

{example}

[Probabilistic interpretation of the square loss]
For the square loss from
Section

2.1
, we have that
c({\bm{f}},{\bm{y}})=-\log(\mathrm{const.}\cdot{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\mu}}=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}}))
where
{\mathcal{N}}(\bullet\mid{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
is a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
and positive definite covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C\times C}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}})=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{({\mathbf{y}}-{\bm{\mu}})}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}({\mathbf{y}}-{\bm{\mu}})\right)}{{(2\pi)}^{C/2}\sqrt{\det({\bm{\Sigma}})}}\,.

We can safely neglect the constant factor for the optimization problem and, by setting the covariance to the identity matrix and the mean to the neural net’s prediction, identify that empirical risk minimization with square loss corresponds to maximum likelihood estimation of a Gaussian likelihood with unit covariance and mean parameterized by the network:

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\text{{MSELoss}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\mu}}=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}})\,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\mu}}=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}})\,.

{example}

[Probabilistic interpretation of softmax cross-entropy loss]
For softmax cross-entropy loss from
Section

2.1
, we have that
c({\bm{f}},y)=-\log({\mathcal{C}}({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\bm{\sigma}}=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}})))
where
{\mathcal{C}}(\bullet\mid{\bm{\sigma}})
is a categorical distribution over
\{1,\dots,C\}
with probabilities
{\bm{\sigma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}_{\geq 0}
and
{\bm{\sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}=1
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\bm{\sigma}})=\prod_{c=1}^{C}[{\bm{\sigma}}]_{c}^{\delta_{{\textnormal{y}},c}}\,.

Hence, we can identify that empirical risk minimization with softmax cross-entropy loss amounts to maximum likelihood estimation with a categorical likelihood parameterized by the softmax of the network’s output:

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\text{{CrossEntropyLoss}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle r({\textnormal{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle={\mathcal{C}}({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\bm{\sigma}}=\mathrm{softmax}(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})))\,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle p({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{C}}({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\bm{\sigma}}=\mathrm{softmax}(f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})))\,.

Let’s talk about derivatives, which play a fundamental role in our goal to understand curvature matrices.
All of them are based on the Hessian, which emerges in a second-order Taylor expansion and contains the second-order derivatives.

2.4.2
Jacobians, JVP, VJPs

Building up to curvature approximations that tackle the approximation of second-order partial derivatives, we start with first-order derivatives.
These are collected into a matrix called the Jacobian, which depends on the flattening convention.
We can multiply with the Jacobian and its transpose via automatic differentiation, without building up the matrix in memory.
These operations are called Jacobian-vector products (JVPs) and vector-Jacobian products (VJPs), respectively.

Machine learning libraries like JAX and PyTorch offer routines for computing Jacobians, VJPs, and JVPs.
However, their interface is functional.
Here, we provide an alternative implementation that accepts nodes of an evaluated computation graph rather than unevaluated functions as input and will be beneficial for modular implementations of neural networks, as we consider later.
We also provide examples for important Jacobians, namely the output-parameter Jacobian of an affine map, i.e. a linear layer.
These Jacobians exhibit a Kronecker structure, which is the foundation for the ‘K’ in KFAC.
We verify this structure numerically and observe how the flattening convention affects it.

{setup}

[Vector-to-vector function]
Let function
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{B},{\bm{a}}\mapsto{\bm{b}}=f({\bm{a}})
denote a vector-to-vector function.

{definition}

[Jacobian of a vector-to-vector function]
The Jacobian of a vector-to-vector function
f
from
Section

2.4.2
,
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times A}
, collects the first-order partial derivatives into a matrix such that

\displaystyle[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}}]_{i,j}=\frac{\partial[f({\bm{a}})]_{i}}{\partial[{\bm{a}}]_{j}}\,.

Section

2.4.2
is limited to vector-to-vector functions.
The more general Jacobian of a tensor-to-tensor function can be indexed with combined indices from the input and output domains:

{setup}

[Tensor-to-tensor function]
Consider a tensor-to-tensor function
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{M}},{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}\mapsto{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}=f({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})
from a rank-
N
tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}
into a rank-
M
tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}
.

{definition}

[General Jacobian,
]
The general Jacobian of
f
from
Section

2.4.2
,
{\bm{\mathsfit{J}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}}{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}
, is a rank-
(M+N)
tensor that collects the first-order partial derivatives such that

\displaystyle[{\bm{\mathsfit{J}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i_{1},\dots,i_{M}},{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}=\frac{\partial[f({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}}}{\partial[{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}}\,.

In practice, this general Jacobian can be prohibitively large and therefore one must almost always work with it in a matrix-free fashion, i.e. through VJPs and JVPs.

{definition}

[Vector-Jacobian products (VJPs),
]

{\bm{\mathsfit{U}}}
of
{\bm{\mathsfit{V}}}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}}[{\bm{\mathsfit{V}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}}[{\bm{\mathsfit{J}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i_{1},\dots,i_{M}},j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}\,.

Matricization.

Jacobian products are efficient, but somewhat abstract to work with, as we cannot ‘touch’ the full tensor.
Often, we would also like to think about this tensor as a matrix to be able to present derivations in linear algebra notation.

Both ways and their resulting Jacobian matrices depend on the flattening convention we choose.

Example.

The two Jacobians usually differ from each other, albeit in subtle ways.
We highlight their differences on a linear layer, which will be useful later on when we discuss KFAC (
Section

This example reveals two insights:

•

There is a Kronecker structure in the linear layer’s Jacobian w.r.t. its weight.
This structure is the foundation for the ‘K’ in KFAC.

•

The order of Kronecker factors is reversed depending on the flattening scheme.
Therefore, we need to be careful when translating results from one flattening convention to the other.

Hessians, HVPs

Now that we have covered first-order derivatives, let’s move on to second-order derivatives and develop the necessary concepts to understand KFAC, as well as their implementation.
Second-order derivatives are collected into an object called
the Hessian
.
For our purposes, it will be sufficient to consider the Hessian of functions producing a scalar-valued output.
Let’s start with the definition of the Hessian of a vector-to-scalar function.

{setup}

[Vector-to-scalar function]
Let
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\bm{a}}\mapsto b=f({\bm{a}})
be a vector-to-scalar function.

{definition}

[Hessian of a vector-to-scalar function]
The Hessian of a vector-to-scalar function
f
from
Section

2.4.3
is a matrix
\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{a}}}b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A\times A}
collecting the second-order partial derivatives of
f
into a matrix with

\displaystyle[\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{a}}}b]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}b}{\partial[{\bm{a}}]_{i}\partial[{\bm{a}}]_{j}}\,.

This definition is limited to functions with vector-valued arguments. The extension to tensor-to-scalar functions is straightforward; however, it yields a tensor that is less convenient to work with in terms of notation:

[Tensor-to-scalar function]

.

{definition}

[General Hessian of a tensor-to-scalar function,
]
The general Hessian of

2.4.3
,
{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{N}\times A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{N}}
, collects the second-order partial derivatives of
f
into a tensor with

\displaystyle[{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}b]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i_{1},\dots,i_{N}},{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}

The last trick for our bag to embark on the KFAC journey is linearization.
It is a useful tool whenever we encounter a composition of functions that we would like to convexify.

Convexification by partial linearization.

Consider e.g. the function
f=g\circ h
with
f,g,h\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
for simplicity.
We know that convexity is preserved under function composition, so if both
g
and
h
are convex, then
f
will be convex.
But what if only one of the two composites is convex, let’s say
g
, but
h
is not?
Well, then we can replace
h
with an approximation
\bar{h}
that is convex and approximates the original function.
One straightforward approximation is a linear approximation.

Linearization.

Let’s say we are interested in only a neighbourhood around
x_{0}
.
Then, we can obtain a simple, convexified approximation
\bar{f}\approx f
in that neighbourhood by linearizing
h
around
x_{0}
, resulting in a function
\lin_{x_{0}}(h)
.
This involves a first-order Taylor approximation:

GGN & relevance to deep learning.

In deep learning, we often face the situation where
f=g\circ h,{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}\mapsto b=f({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})
and
h
is non-convex while
g
is convex
(e.g. in the empirical risk, where the criterion is typically convex, while the neural net is typically non-convex).
This means the Hessian of
f
can be indefinite, even though many algorithms require a positive definite approximation to the Hessian.
We can obtain that by considering the partially linearized function
\bar{f}=g\circ\lin_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}(h)
, whose Hessian is positive semi-definite.
The Hessian of this partially linearized function is called the generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) matrix.

Let’s stick to our one-dimensional example for a moment, i.e. let
f(x)=(g\circ h)(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
If we use the chain rule twice, we obtain
f^{\prime\prime}=g^{\prime}h^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime}+h^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}
for the second derivative. Using our Jacobian and Hessian notation, this translates into

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{x}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}_{x}(g\circ h)(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{J}_{x}h(x)\cdot\mathrm{H}_{h(x)}g(h(x))\cdot\mathrm{J}_{x}h(x)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathrm{H}_{x}h(x)\cdot\mathrm{J}_{h(x)}g(h(x))\,.

Now, if we take the Hessian of the partially linearized function
\bar{f}(x)=(g\circ\lin_{x_{0}}(h))(x)
, and use the shorthand
\bar{h}=\lin_{x_{0}}(h)
the second term disappears as the linear function’s Hessian is zero:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{x}\bar{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}_{x}(g\circ\bar{h})(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{J}_{x}\bar{h}(x)\cdot\mathrm{H}_{\bar{h}(x)}g(\bar{h}(x))\cdot\mathrm{J}_{x}\bar{h}(x)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\underbrace{\mathrm{H}_{x}\bar{h}(x)}_{=0}\cdot\mathrm{J}_{\bar{h}(x)}g(\bar{h}(x))\,.

Evaluating both equations at the anchor point (setting
x=x_{0}
), we see that their first terms coincide (remember from
Section

2.4.4
that the linearized function’s value and Jacobian coincide with those of the original function when evaluated at the anchor point).

f=g\circ h:{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}\mapsto{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}\mapsto c
{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}c
\bar{f}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}=g\circ\lin_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}h
{\bm{\mathsfit{G}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}f({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})|_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}={\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}

=
{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}\bar{f}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})|_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}={\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}}
{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}
partially linearize
h

around some
{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}
{\bm{\mathsfit{H}}}
, then
set
{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}_{0}={\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}

Taking the Hessian and partial linearization commute and lead to the same GGN.

All operations also commute with flattening the function versus matricizing the Hessian tensor, so we can apply any flattening scheme on top to obtain the

-GGN matrices.

Hence, our one-dimensional example’s GGN is

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{x_{0}}f(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{J}_{x_{0}}h(x_{0})\cdot\mathrm{H}_{h(x_{0})}g(h(x_{0}))\cdot\mathrm{J}_{x_{0}}h(x_{0})\,.

To summarize, we obtain the GGN either by (i) partially linearizing, then taking the Hessian, or (ii) taking the Hessian and discarding second-order derivatives of the non-convex composite (i.e. partially linearizing).
This commutative relationship is visualized in
Figure

3
.
Our next steps are to generalize the GGN to vector-valued functions that produce scalars, illustrate it on a toy problem, and then develop the fully general formulation for tensor-valued functions along with the functionality to work with it in code.

{setup}

[Composite vector-to-vector-to-scalar function]
Let

\displaystyle f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\mapsto c=f({\bm{a}})

be the composite of a vector-to-vector function
h
and a vector-to-scalar function
g
, that is

\displaystyle f=g\circ h\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{A}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}}^{B}\to{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\bm{b}}=h({\bm{a}})\mapsto c=g({\bm{b}})\,.

{definition}

[GGN matrix (vector case)]
The GGN matrix of a vector-to-vector-to-scalar function
f
from
Section

2.4.4
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{{\bm{a}}}f({\bm{a}})=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}})^{\top}(\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{b}}}c)(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A\times A}\,,

i.e. the second composite’s Hessian, pulled back with the first composite’s Jacobian.

One obtains this form by following through the same steps as above, but for a vector-to-vector-to-scalar instead of a purely scalar function composition.
Section

2.4.4
presents fully-worked-out expressions of the Rosenbrock function’s GGN.

{example}

[GGN for the Rosenbrock function,
]
Consider the 2d Rosenbrock function
f_{\alpha}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=(1-x_{1})^{2}+\alpha(x_{2}-x_{1}^{2})^{2}\,.

with some
\alpha>0
.

Composition.

Following
Brunet 2010
, we express the Rosenbrock function as vector-to-vector-to-scalar function

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=g\circ h_{\alpha}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}

with

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-x_{1}\\
\sqrt{\alpha}(x_{2}-x_{1}^{2})\end{pmatrix}

and convex

\displaystyle g({\bm{h}})

\displaystyle={\bm{h}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}\,,

namely, the square loss.

Linearization.

The Rosenbrock function is non-convex (see
Figure

4
for contour lines) because
h_{\alpha}
is non-convex.
We want to convexify it by partial linearization.
Linearizing
h_{\alpha}
w.r.t.
{\bm{x}}
around an anchor
\hat{{\bm{x}}}
gives

\displaystyle\bar{h}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=h_{\alpha}(\hat{{\bm{x}}})+(\mathrm{J}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}}h_{\alpha}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}))({\bm{x}}-\hat{{\bm{x}}})

with the Jacobian

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}}h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
-2\sqrt{\alpha}x_{1}&\sqrt{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Inserting all expressions, we have

\displaystyle\bar{h}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=\begin{pmatrix}1-\hat{x}_{1}\\
\sqrt{\alpha}(\hat{x}_{2}-\hat{x}_{1}^{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1}\\
2\sqrt{\alpha}\hat{x}_{1}(\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1})+\sqrt{\alpha}(x_{2}-\hat{x}_{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-x_{1}\\
2\sqrt{\alpha}\hat{x}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1}\right)+\sqrt{\alpha}x_{2}\end{pmatrix}

Note that this function is indeed linear in
x_{1,2}
.

Partial linearization.

With
\bar{h}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})
, we can evaluate the partially linearized Rosenbrock function
\bar{f}_{\alpha}=g\circ\bar{h}_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=(\bar{h}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}}))^{\top}\bar{h}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=(1-x_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\alpha\left[2\hat{x}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1}\right)+x_{2}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2x_{1}+x_{1}^{2}+\alpha[2\hat{x}_{1}^{4}-4\hat{x}_{1}^{3}x_{1}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+4\hat{x}_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}-2\hat{x}_{1}^{2}x_{2}-4\hat{x}_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}]\,.

GGN via linearize-then-differentiate.

The Rosenbrock’s GGN is the Hessian of its partial linearization, evaluated at
{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}}f_{\alpha}(\hat{{\bm{x}}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{x}}}\bar{f}_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}

\displaystyle=2\begin{pmatrix}1+4\alpha\hat{x}_{1}^{2}&-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}\\
-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\,.

GGN via differentiate-then-linearize.

Let’s double-check our result by evaluating the GGN in
Section

2.4.4
, which follows by differentiating twice, then neglecting non-linearities of
h_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}}f_{\alpha}(\hat{{\bm{x}}})=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}}h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}}))^{\top}|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})}g(h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}}))|_{h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})=h_{\alpha}(\hat{{\bm{x}}})}

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}}h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}\,.

Using the Rosenbrock’s Jacobian from above, and the square loss Hessian from
Section

2.4.3
which is proportional to the identity, yields

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}}h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}}))^{\top}|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}}h_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\sqrt{\alpha}x_{1}\\
0&\sqrt{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
-2\sqrt{\alpha}x_{1}&\sqrt{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\begin{pmatrix}1+4\alpha\hat{x}_{1}^{2}&-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}\\
-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\,.

This is the same expression we obtained with the linearize-then-differentiate approach.
We also see that the GGN is positive semi-definite, as it is a scaled outer product of Jacobians.

Comparison of Hessian and GGN.

As expected, the Rosenbrock function’s Hessian, evaluated at
\hat{{\bm{x}}}
, is different from the GGN:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{x}}}f_{\alpha}({\bm{x}})|_{{\bm{x}}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}}

\displaystyle=2\begin{pmatrix}1+6\alpha\hat{x}_{1}^{2}-2\alpha\hat{x}_{2}&-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}\\
-2\alpha\hat{x}_{1}&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\,.

\displaystyle[\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}}c]_{{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}k_{1},\ldots,k_{M}},{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}l_{1},\ldots,l_{M}}}\hskip-5.59721pt

\displaystyle[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}l_{1},\ldots,l_{M}},j_{1},\ldots,j_{N}}\,.\hskip-5.59721pt

{definition}

[GGN-vector-product (GGNVP,
)]Consider the GGN tensor of a tensor-to-tensor-to-scalar map
f
from
Sections

2.4.4
and
2.4.4
.
The GGN-vector-product (GGNVP)
{\bm{\mathsfit{U}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{N}}
with a tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{V}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{N}}
from the input domain is

\displaystyle[{\bm{\mathsfit{U}}}]_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}[{\bm{\mathsfit{G}}}_{{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}}}f({\bm{\mathsfit{A}}})]_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N},{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}[{\bm{\mathsfit{V}}}]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}j_{1},\dots,j_{N}}}\,,

and decomposes into a JVP, HVP, and VJP when applying the composition from
Section

2.4.4
.

This is easiest to see for the vector-to-vector-to-scalar case where
{\bm{\mathsfit{A}}},{\bm{\mathsfit{B}}},{\bm{\mathsfit{U}}},{\bm{\mathsfit{V}}}\to{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}},{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}
and the GGNVP becomes
{\bm{u}}=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}})^{\top}(\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{b}}}c)(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}}){\bm{v}}
, which can be written as a matrix chain and computed without explicitly building up any of the matrices in memory
(
Schraudolph 2002
)
.

Matricization.

As for the Hessian and Jacobian, we can flatten both composite functions before applying the partial linearization and taking the Hessian to obtain the GGN.
This is equivalent to matricizing the GGN tensor from
Section

2.4.4
.
And it is also equivalent to matricizing the Hessians and Jacobians in the general definition.

Example.

Again, it is important to emphasize that the matrix GGN depends on the flattening scheme.
To emphasize this point, we conclude this section by studying the GGN of linear regression (
Section

2.4.4
).
Two interesting observations about this example are:

•

Both GGNs are a Kronecker product.
This already hints at using a Kronecker product to approximate the exact GGN, which is what KFAC aims to do.

•

The order of factors depends on the flattening scheme we are using.
Therefore, we always need to remind ourselves of the chosen scheme when working with the GGN.

2.5
Curvature Matrices in Deep Learning

We introduced Jacobians, Hessians, partial linearizations, and the resulting generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) in the language of automatic differentiation for arbitrary functions.
Let’s switch gears and apply these concepts to deep learning.

Parameter list/tuple format.

One new aspect we have to deal with is that ML libraries like PyTorch represent parameters as lists/tuples of variables, each of which is associated with a different layer.
This layer structure in the parameters gives rise to a block structure in the Jacobians, Hessians (see
Figure

5
for an example), and GGNs.
In the context of KFAC, we will focus on specific blocks.

Typically, a neural net
f({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times\Theta\to{\mathcal{F}}
has parameters in list/tuple-format,

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{\theta}}^{(1)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}),

where each
{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}
is an arbitrary parameter tensor.
To be able to use matrix expressions, we will often consider the concatenation of flattened parameters,

\displaystyle\vec({\bm{\theta}})=\begin{pmatrix}\vec({\bm{\theta}}^{(1)})\\
\vec({\bm{\theta}}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\vec({\bm{\theta}}^{(L)})\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\,,

with
\vec\in\{\rvec,\cvec\}
one of the previously described flattening operations (
Sections

2.4.1
and
2.4.1
).
This convention generalizes
\vec
to handle parameters in list/tuple format by applying the original definition to each element and concatenating the results.
In code, we will still work with the list/tuple format.

Recap: Empirical risk and shorthands.

We consider a data set
{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\mid n=1,\dots,N\}
containing
N
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples.
The inputs are processed by a neural net
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\times\Theta\to{\mathcal{F}}
and their predictions are scored with a criterion function
c\colon{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, like square loss (
Section

2.1
) or softmax cross-entropy loss (
Section

2.1
).
For each datum
n
, we define its prediction function
{\bm{f}}_{n}\colon\Theta\to{\mathcal{F}},{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto{\bm{f}}_{n}({\bm{\theta}})=f({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}})
and its criterion function
c_{n}\colon{\mathcal{F}}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\bm{f}}\mapsto c({\bm{f}},{\bm{y}}_{n})
.
Combining both, we obtain the per-datum loss function (w.r.t. the net’s parameters),

\displaystyle(\ell_{n}\colon\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}})=(c_{n}\circ{\bm{f}}_{n}\colon\Theta\to{\mathcal{F}}\to{\mathbb{R}})

We will often use the shorthands
c_{n},{\bm{f}}_{n},\ell_{n}
for the function values (rather than functions) of the per-datum criteria, predictions, and losses.

We accumulate the per-datum losses
\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}\}
into a single scalar, which yields the empirical risk

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}\colon\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}},\qquad{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{n}({\bm{\theta}})\,,

where
R
is the reduction factor (see
Equation

1
for details).

{\mathbb{D}}
can be any collection of data points, e.g. the full data set or a mini-batch.

Consider the empirical risk from above.
The Hessian matrix under a flattening scheme
\vec
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}\ell_{n}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}\,.

It contains the second-order partial derivatives of
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})
(
Section

2.4.3
), i.e.

\displaystyle[\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})]_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}i},{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})}{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\partial[\vec({\bm{\theta}})]_{i}}{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}\partial[\vec({\bm{\theta}})]_{j}}}\,.

Block structure.

Due to the tuple/list structure of
{\bm{\theta}}
, the Hessian has a block structure (
Figure

5
).
A block corresponding to parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}
is

\displaystyle[\mathrm{H}_{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}},{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}]_{i,j}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}}{\partial[{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\vec\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}\right)}]_{i}\partial[{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}\vec\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}\right)}]_{j}}\,

and we usually suppress repeated differentiation arguments,
\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}^{\vec}\coloneqq\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}^{\vec}
.
With the shorthand
\mathrm{H}_{k,l}^{\vec}\coloneq\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}^{\vec}
,
the Hessian can be written in block form as

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{H}_{1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{H}_{1,2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{H}_{1,L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\\
\mathrm{H}_{2,1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{H}_{2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{H}_{2,L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{H}_{L,1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{H}_{L,2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{H}_{L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

For KFAC, we will only need the block diagonal approximation of this matrix,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{H}_{1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&{\bm{0}}&\cdots&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\mathrm{H}_{2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\cdots&{\bm{0}}&\mathrm{H}_{L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

i.e., individual blocks
\{\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})\}_{k=1}^{L}
.

2.5.2
The Generalized Gauss-Newton

Partially linearized empirical risk.

As previously described in
Section

2.4.4
, the GGN matrix arises from the partial linearization of a composite function.
We will now apply this to the per-datum loss functions
\ell_{n}=c_{n}\circ{\bm{f}}_{n}
in the empirical risk.
Specifically, we will linearize the neural net predictions
{\bm{f}}_{n}\to\bar{{\bm{f}}}_{n}=\lin_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}({\bm{f}}_{n})
, then form the partially-linearized per-sample losses
\bar{\ell}_{n}=c_{n}\circ\bar{{\bm{f}}}_{n}
.
This gives rise to the partially linearized empirical risk
\bar{{\mathcal{L}}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{\ell}_{n}({\bm{\theta}})
.
Just like in
Section

2.4.4
, the empirical risk’s
\vec
-GGN matrix is the Hessian of the partially-linearized empirical risk, evaluated at the anchor,

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}^{\vec}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\left.\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}\bar{{\mathcal{L}}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})\right|_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}={\bm{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left.\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}\bar{\ell}_{n}({\bm{\theta}})\right|_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}={\bm{\theta}}},

where
\vec
is one of the flattening operations.
We can also express the GGN through Jacobians,

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}_{n}]^{\top}\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c_{n}[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}_{n}]\,.

Block structure.

Just like the Hessian, the GGN has a block structure (
Figure

6
).
Abbreviating

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{k,l}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathrm{G}^{\vec}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}\bar{{\mathcal{L}}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}_{n}]^{\top}\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c_{n}[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}_{n}]

and
\mathrm{G}_{k}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\coloneq\mathrm{G}^{\vec}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}
, the GGN’s block form is

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{G}_{1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{G}_{1,2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{G}_{1,L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\\
\mathrm{G}_{2,1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{G}_{2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{G}_{2,L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{G}_{L,1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\mathrm{G}_{L,2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\cdots&\mathrm{G}_{L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

For KFAC, we only need its block diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{G}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{G}_{1}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&{\bm{0}}&\cdots&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\mathrm{G}_{2}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\cdots&{\bm{0}}&\mathrm{G}_{L}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

i.e., individual blocks
\{\mathrm{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}}^{\vec}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\bm{\theta}})\}_{k=1}^{L}
.

The GGN as a self-outer product.

Let us look at one last aspect of the GGN, which makes it convenient to relate it to other curvature matrices we are about to discuss.
Consider the loss contributed by a single datum and suppress the index
n
for now, as well as the reduction factor
R
.
Its contribution to the GGN is

\displaystyle\underbrace{[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]^{\top}}_{\dim(\Theta)\times\dim({\mathcal{F}})}\underbrace{[\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{f}c({\bm{f}})]}_{\dim({\mathcal{F}})\times\dim({\mathcal{F}})}\underbrace{[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]}_{\dim({\mathcal{F}})\times\dim(\Theta)}\,.

We will now make this more symmetric.
By assumption, the criterion function
c
is convex in
{\bm{f}}
.
This means that the flattened Hessian
\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{{\bm{f}}}c({\bm{f}})
is positive semi-definite.
Since any positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C\times C}
can be expressed as an outer product
{\bm{A}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}
where
{\bm{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C\times\rank({\bm{A}})}
, we can find a symmetric factorization
{\bm{S}}^{\vec}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})\times\dim({\mathcal{F}})}
of the criterion’s Hessian such that
\mathrm{H}^{\vec}_{{\bm{f}}}c({\bm{f}})={\bm{S}}^{\vec}{{\bm{S}}^{\vec}}^{\top}
.
With that, we can then write the upper expression as

\displaystyle[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\vec}{{\bm{S}}^{\vec}}^{\top}[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{([\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\vec})}_{\coloneq{\bm{V}}^{\vec}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim(\Theta)\times\dim({\mathcal{F}})}}([\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}]^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\vec})^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{V}}^{\vec}{{\bm{V}}^{\vec}}^{\top}\,.

In words, we can express the GGN contribution of a single datum as a self-outer product.
While we will not use
{\bm{V}}^{\vec}
(see e.g.
Dangel et al. 2022
;
Ren & Goldfarb 2019
for detailed discussions), we need the loss Hessian factorization (
{\bm{S}}^{\vec}
) later.

Sections

2.5.2
and
2.5.2
present the loss Hessian factorizations of the square and softmax cross-entropy losses.
One important insight for these two loss functions is that
{\bm{S}}^{\vec}
does not depend on the labels.
We will use this in
Section

2.5.4
to connect the GGN with the Fisher for regression and classification.
But first, let’s introduce the Fisher.

from

The Hessian’s symmetric factorization is simply

The Hessian’s symmetric factorization is
(
Papyan 2019
, e.g.)

\displaystyle{\bm{S}}^{\vec}=\diag(\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}})-{\bm{\sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}}^{\top}

where
{\bm{\sigma}}=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}})
and the square root is applied element-wise.
To see this, we can form
{\bm{S}}^{\vec}{{\bm{S}}^{\vec}}^{\top}
which yields

\displaystyle\diag({\bm{\sigma}})-2{\bm{\sigma}}{\bm{\sigma}}^{\top}+{\bm{\sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}}^{\top}\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}}{\bm{\sigma}}^{\top}=\diag({\bm{\sigma}})-{\bm{\sigma}}{\bm{\sigma}}^{\top}

using the fact that
{\bm{\sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}=1=\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}}^{\top}\sqrt{{\bm{\sigma}}}
.
This expression equals the Hessian from
Section

Recap: Probabilistic interpretation.

In
Section

2.3
we demonstrated that a neural net often models a likelihood for the labels given the inputs using parameters.
This probabilistic perspective provides a new way to compare two models
f_{1}\coloneq f(\bullet,{\bm{\theta}}_{1}),f_{2}\coloneq f(\bullet,{\bm{\theta}}_{2})
where
{\bm{\theta}}_{2}={\bm{\theta}}_{1}+\Delta{\bm{\theta}}
: Instead of assessing their dissimilarity by their parameters’ dissimilarity, e.g. using the Euclidean distance measure
d^{2}(f_{1},f_{2})=\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{2}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert\Delta{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, we can instead use the KL divergence of the represented probability densities,
d^{2}(f_{1},f_{2})=\mathrm{KL}(p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}_{1})\mid\mid p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}_{2}))
.

The Fisher as metric of the KL divergence.

Using
p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})=p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}_{1})\mid\mid p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}[\mathrm{KL}(p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{1})\mid\mid p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}))].

While the KL divergence is not a valid distance measure for general pairs
({\bm{\theta}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{2})
(e.g. it is not symmetric w.r.t. its arguments), it can be locally approximated by a quadratic Taylor series for small
\Delta{\bm{\theta}}
.
The curvature matrix in that Taylor series is the Fisher,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})\mid\mid p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}+\Delta{\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\Delta{\bm{\theta}}}^{\top}{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})\Delta{\bm{\theta}}+{\mathcal{O}}(\|\Delta{\bm{\theta}}\|^{3})\,,

with the Fisher information matrix

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})}[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})]\,.

Let’s rewrite this expression in terms of the likelihood
p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
, and its reparameterized likelihood
r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))
with the neural net prediction,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})}[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))}[

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))]\,.

The expectation over the data-generating distribution’s marginal
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
is intractable.
Therefore, like before, we replace it with the empirical distribution over our data set in the following.
Following
Soen & Sun 2024
, we present two forms of the Fisher: While the
type-II Fisher
allows to elegantly connect the Fisher and the GGN and highlights its connection to the Hessian (a curvature matrix), the
type-I Fisher
highlights that the Fisher is a gradient covariance matrix, and can therefore be computed with first-order derivatives.

Type-II Fisher.

Let’s replace the true marginal distribution over the inputs with its empirical distribution
p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\delta({\mathbf{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{n})
.
This yields the type-II Fisher information matrix (because it is defined via second-order derivatives),

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\text{II}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{{\mathbb{D}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))}[

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})]

(remember
{\bm{f}}_{n}\coloneq f({\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}})
).
We can apply the chain rule to the log-likelihood’s Hessian (remember the 1d example from
Section

2.4.4
) and use that
\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))}[\nabla_{f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))]={\bm{0}}
to simplify the type-II Fisher into

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[\begin{aligned} &{\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}}^{\top}\\
&\left(-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})\right)\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}]\,\end{aligned}

and, as the Jacobians do not depend on
{\mathbf{y}}
,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &{\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}}^{\top}\\
&\left(\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})]\right)\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,.\end{aligned}

This form resembles the GGN a lot.
In fact, for square loss and softmax cross-entropy loss (
c=-\log
), we derived that the loss Hessian we average in the type-II Fisher does not depend on the labels modelled by the distribution (see
Sections

2.4.3
and
2.4.3
).
This means we can drop the expectation for such losses.
We just showed that the (type-II)
Fisher equals the GGN for regression with square loss and classification with softmax cross-entropy loss
(more details in
Section

2.5.4
).

Type-I Fisher.

There is another way to rewrite the Fisher in terms of first-order derivatives of the log-likelihood.
We will call this the type-I Fisher information matrix.
To derive it, we use the property that the negative log-likelihood’s Hessian equals its gradient covariance in expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})}\left[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})}[\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))^{\top}].\end{aligned}

Hence, we can write the Fisher as

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})}[\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}}))^{\top}]\end{aligned}

(factorize
p({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\theta}})=p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))}[\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))^{\top}]\end{aligned}

(reparameterize
p({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})=r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}}))}[

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\mathbf{x}},{\bm{\theta}})))^{\top}].

Let’s apply the chain rule for the gradient, substitute the empirical distribution for
p_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})
, and simplify.
This gives the type-I Fisher

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}]\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[\begin{aligned} &(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}]\end{aligned}

and, as the Jacobians do not depend on
{\mathbf{y}}
,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&\phantom{\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[}(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}]\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,.\end{aligned}

This form highlights that the Fisher can be computed with first-order derivatives, specifically vector-Jacobian products.

Monte Carlo-approximated type-I Fisher.

For computations with the type-I Fisher, we need to replace the expectation over
r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})
with an estimator, e.g. Monte Carlo approximation with
M
sampled labels
\smash{\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,1},\dots,\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}
for the prediction of datum
n
.
Thankfully, we have already developed the functionality to sample such labels in
Section

2.3
(specifically,
).

{definition}

[Monte Carlo-approximated type-I Fisher (
)]The Monte Carlo-approximated type-I Fisher of the likelihood
\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid f({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{\theta}}))
,

\tilde{{\bm{F}}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{F}}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{n,m}\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{n,m}\begin{aligned} &\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\right)^{\top}\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}.\end{aligned}

As we increase the number of Monte Carlo (MC) samples, the MC-approximated type-I Fisher converges to the Fisher, as illustrated in
Figure

7
.

Would-be gradients & self-outer product form.

To compute with the type-I Fisher, we need to evaluate the gradient of the loss using sampled labels, rather than the labels from the data set (see
, which implements multiplication with the MC Fisher).
As coined by
Papyan 2020
, we call these gradients ‘would-be’ gradients, because they depend on hypothetical labels.

Let’s denote the
m
-th negative would-be gradient for sample
n
by
\tilde{{\bm{g}}}_{n,m}\coloneq-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})

We can then form the matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{S}}}_{n}\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{{\bm{g}}}_{n,1}&\cdots&\tilde{{\bm{g}}}_{n,M}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})\times M}

for each
n
, and express the MC Fisher as

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{F}}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,m}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{S}}}_{n}\tilde{{\bm{S}}}_{n}^{\top}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{n}\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{n}^{\top}

where
\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{n}=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{S}}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times M}
.

Did you notice what we just did here?
By stacking the negative would-be gradients into columns of a matrix, we were able to express the Fisher as the self-outer product of a matrix
\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{n}
.
This notation looks very similar to the GGN’s self-outer product notation.
In fact, we can think of
\tilde{{\bm{V}}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times M}
as a
randomization
of
{\bm{V}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times C}
which can be much smaller (depending on
M
and
C
) and therefore cheaper to compute in exchange for inaccuracy.
The different self-outer product forms of curvature matrices will give rise to multiple flavours of KFAC in
Section

4
.

Reduction factor of the Fisher vs. empirical risk.

In the above derivation, we arrived at an expression of the Fisher which uses a reduction factor
R=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
.
We will handle this factor flexibly in practice by using the reduction factor of our empirical risk.

(Optional) efficient would-be gradient computation.

One way to compute would-be gradients is to repeatedly sample a label, compute its loss, and then use automatic differentiation to obtain the gradient.
This incurs
for
loops, which may be sub-optimal for performance.
In this tutorial, we follow the less efficient
for
loop approach as it is less complex.
To improve efficiency, we could draw all labels in parallel and compute their gradients manually instead of using autodiff, which is cheaper, as demonstrated by
Sections

2.5.3
and
2.5.3
.
They provide the gradients of square loss and softmax cross-entropy loss, which are straightforward to evaluate manually.
However, we will not apply this optimization in our tutorial.

{example}

[Gradients of square loss]
Consider the square loss from
Section

2.1
and its probabilistic interpretation from

For some label
{\bm{y}}
, the gradient is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{f}}}c({\bm{f}},{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{f}})

\displaystyle={\bm{f}}-{\bm{y}}\,.

{example}

[Gradients of softmax cross-entropy loss]
Consider the softmax cross-entropy loss from
Section

2.1
and its probabilistic interpretation from

. For some label

, the gradient is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{f}}}c({\bm{f}},y)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}}\log r({\textnormal{y}}=y\mid{\bm{f}})

\displaystyle={\bm{\sigma}}({\bm{f}})-\mathrm{onehot}(y)\,.

2.5.4
Connection between GGN & Fisher

The GGN, type-I, and type-II Fisher are all weighted sums of matrices sandwiched between the per-sample Jacobians (remember that we can mentally set
-\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})=c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\mathbf{y}})
for square and softmax cross-entropy loss):

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\text{II}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0.9922,0.0313,0.9336}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log(r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))]}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle{\bm{F}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0.3203,0.7813,0.8711}\mathbb{E}_{r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})}[}\\
&\begin{aligned} &\quad{\color[rgb]{0.3203,0.7813,0.8711}-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log(r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))}\\
&\quad{\color[rgb]{0.3203,0.7813,0.8711}(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log(r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})))^{\top}]}\end{aligned}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,.\end{aligned}

In previous sections, we showed that for square loss and softmax cross-entropy, the criterion function’s Hessian
\mathrm{H}_{\bm{f}}c({\bm{f}},{\bm{y}})=-\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}}\log(r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{f}})
does not depend on the value of the target random variable
{\mathbf{y}}
!
Therefore, the expectation in the type-II Fisher effectively disappears, and we are free to set
{\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}
because this does not change the Hessian.
This means the type-II Fisher and GGN are equivalent for these losses.
Note that we cannot drop the expectation in the type-I Fisher, though
1
1

1

This is precisely why we needed a separate definition for the Monte Carlo-approximated type-I Fisher to make it computationally tractable.
.
But from the equivalence of type-I and type-II Fisher, we know that it also equals the GGN in the above scenarios.

2.5.5
The Empirical Fisher (EF)

Finally, we introduce the empirical Fisher.
This matrix serves as an approximation to the type-I Fisher that does not require Monte Carlo samples.
Several contemporary optimizers, e.g. the Adam variants, gained inspiration from second-order optimization, in particular preconditioning with the empirical Fisher.
For example, Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
stores the moving average of the mini-batch loss’ squared gradients, which is motivated by (but is not equivalent to) preconditioning with the
diagonal
of the empirical Fisher
2
2

2

See
Lin et al. 2024
for the exact preconditioner Adam approximates, which is a different approximation of the Fisher information matrix.
.
We obtain the empirical Fisher by replacing the model’s likelihood in the type-I Fisher with the empirical likelihood and evaluating the expectation over it:

{definition}

[Empirical Fisher (
)]The empirical Fisher matrix of the likelihood
\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})
,

{\bm{E}}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
, is

\displaystyle{\bm{E}}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\begin{aligned} &\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\right)^{\top}\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))\\
&(-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}.\end{aligned}

Empirical Fisher
\neq
Fisher.

The subtle difference between empirical and type-I Fisher is the
expectation
over the gradient outer product

\displaystyle(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))(-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n}))^{\top}

w.r.t. the model’s predictive distribution
r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}_{n})
, whereas
{\bm{E}}({\bm{\theta}})
plugs in the
ground-truth
labels
{\bm{y}}_{n}
into the gradient outer product.
While the computation of the empirical Fisher is more efficient than Monte Carlo approximating the type-I Fisher with number of samples
M>1
, this subtle difference can impact the utility of this matrix in optimization.
In particular,
Kunstner et al. 2019
show that preconditioning with the EF can have detrimental effects in simple problems.
The empirical Fisher’s success in some settings (e.g., through Adam) may be attributed to its ability to attenuate
gradient noise
, not its properties as a curvature estimate.
To some extent, this is still an open question.

KFAC has many nuances that complicate its understanding.
However, we can break down its structure into a general scaffold that simplifies its complexity into manageable sub-tasks (
).
Here, we discuss the components.

Outline.

At its core, KFAC approximates curvature matrices using Kronecker products, significantly reducing computational and memory costs.
In this section, we start from the general form of relevant curvature matrices, discuss how their structure enables a Kronecker-factored approximation, and introduce the core components of KFAC.
This scaffold—accompanied by the code snippets on the right—serves as the foundation for a systematic implementation, offering intuition on how Kronecker factors are computed and why such a decomposition is beneficial.
We keep the discussion general and highlight how to adapt the code to approximate any curvature matrix introduced in the previous section.
The next section (
Section

5
) will focus on the specific case of KFAC for the generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) of linear layers.

Curvature matrix structure.

Many relevant matrices—like the GGN, the type-I, type-II, and empirical Fisher—share a common structure

\displaystyle\begin{split}\!\!{\bm{C}}&({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})\\
&=R\sum_{n}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\left[\bullet({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\right](\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})\,\end{split}

(2)

where
\bullet\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})\times\dim({\mathcal{F}})}
is a positive semi-definite matrix in the prediction space that depends on the prediction
{\bm{f}}_{n}
and target
{\bm{y}}_{n}
.
This term is sandwiched between the Jacobians of the network’s prediction with respect to the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}
in layer
i
.
Directly computing and storing these matrices is usually intractable, which motivates approximation schemes such as KFAC.

Key idea.

The idea behind KFAC is to exploit a Kronecker-product structure in the Jacobians
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n}
to approximate

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})\approx\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}))\coloneqq{\bm{A}}^{(i)}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{(i)}.

For a composition of functions

f\coloneqq f^{(L)}\circ f^{(L-1)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(1)}

with parameters

{\bm{\theta}}=\left[{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)},\dots,{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}\right],

KFAC yields a block-diagonal approximation of the full curvature matrix
{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}})
, where each block corresponds to a layer and is of the form
\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}))={\bm{A}}^{{(i)}}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
.

In this approximation,
{\bm{A}}^{(i)}
is computed from the inputs to layer
i
, and we refer to it as
input-based Kronecker factor
.
Similarly,
{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
is computed from gradients w.r.t. layer
i
’s output, and we call it the
grad-output-based Kronecker factor
.

4.1
Why Use a Kronecker Structure?

Having a Kronecker approximation for
{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})
has multiple advantages and motivations that coincide well with the block diagonal structure.

4.1.1
Kronecker Products Are Computationally Efficient

Instead of working with the dense representation

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}\otimes

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}m_{1}\times n_{2}m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}&\dots&a_{1,n_{2}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n_{1},1}&\dots&a_{n_{1},n_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{1,1}&\dots&b_{1,m_{2}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{m_{1},1}&\dots&b_{m_{1},m_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}{\bm{B}}&\dots&a_{1,n_{2}}{\bm{B}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n_{1},1}{\bm{B}}&\dots&a_{n_{1},n_{2}}{\bm{B}}\end{bmatrix}\,,

we can express many operations with the Kronecker factors
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and
{\bm{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times m_{2}}
.
This drastically reduces memory, as we only need to store and handle these smaller matrices (i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(n_{1}n_{2}+m_{1}m_{2})
) rather than the full Kronecker product (i.e.
\mathcal{O}(n_{1}n_{2}m_{1}m_{2})
).
Examples are:

Matrix-vector products.

Let
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}m_{2}}
, then

({\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}){\bm{v}}=\cvec({\bm{B}}{\bm{V}}{\bm{A}}^{\top})

with
{\bm{V}}=\cvec^{-1}({\bm{v}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times m_{2}}
.
Similarly,

({\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}){\bm{v}}=\rvec({\bm{A}}{\bm{V}}{\bm{B}}^{\top})

with
{\bm{V}}=\rvec^{-1}({\bm{v}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
.

Matrix transpose.

({\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}})^{\top}={\bm{A}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{\top}
.

Matrix inverse.

({\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}})^{-1}={\bm{A}}^{-1}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{-1}
(assuming the Kronecker factors are invertible).

Matrix multiplication.

Let
{\bm{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times d_{1}}
and
{\bm{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times d_{2}}
, then we have

({\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}})({\bm{C}}\otimes{\bm{D}})={\bm{A}}{\bm{C}}\otimes{\bm{B}}{\bm{D}}\,,

i.e. we can multiply the Kronecker factors.

4.1.2
Kronecker Products Naturally Emerge in Curvature Matrices

Kronecker structure arises naturally in the expression for
{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})
due to the structure of the layer Jacobians, providing a strong motivation for its use.
To see this, we rewrite
{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})
from
Equation

2
.

Factorizing the loss Hessian.

Since
\bullet({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})
is a positive semi-definite matrix, it can be decomposed as a sum of outer products of at most
\dim({\mathcal{F}})
vectors:

\bullet({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})=\sum_{c=1}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})}\blacktriangle_{c}({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})(\blacktriangle_{c}({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n}))^{\top}

where we define
\blacktriangle_{n,c}:=\blacktriangle_{c}({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})}
as a vector we will specify later.
Substituting this into
Equation

2
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}(

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\blacktriangle_{n,c}^{\top}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})\,.

Separate the layer’s Jacobian.

By applying the chain rule to split the Jacobian at the layer output
{\bm{x}}^{(i)}=f^{(i)}({\bm{x}}^{(i-1)},{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})
, we get

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{n})^{\top}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}.

Substituting this back, the curvature matrix is

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}

{}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{n})^{\top}

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\blacktriangle_{n,c}^{\top}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{n}).

{example}

[Curvature matrix of a linear layer as sum of Kronecker products]
Let’s illustrate the statement that the curvature matrix becomes a sum of Kronecker products, where one factor stems from the layers Jacobian and one from a backpropagated vector, for a linear layer with weight matrix
{\bm{W}}
, and using
\cvec
flattening.
Let’s define the shorthand
{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{{(i)}}\coloneqq(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}
.
Then, inserting the layer’s Jacobian from
Section

2.4.2
, we get

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}^{(i)}(\cvec{\bm{W}})

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n,c}\left({{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\!\!\!\otimes{\bm{I}}\right)^{\top}\!\!\!{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}{{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}}^{\top}\left({{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\!\!\!\otimes{\bm{I}}\right)\!\!

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n,c}\left({\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}\right)\left({{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\otimes{{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n,c}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}{{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{(i)}}^{\top}\,

simply by using the Kronecker product’s properties from
Section

4.1.1
.
This illustrates that the exact curvature matrix indeed becomes a sum of Kronecker products.

Insert the layer’s Jacobian.

The Kronecker structure emerges naturally in the output-parameter Jacobian
\smash{\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}}
of a layer.
We saw this in previous chapters for a linear layer (
Section

2.4.2
), whose Jacobian is a Kronecker product of the layer input
\smash{{\bm{x}}^{(i-1)}_{n}}
and an identity matrix.
Inserting the Jacobian, this reduces the
exact
form of
{\bm{C}}^{(i)}
to a sum of Kronecker products (fully worked out in
Section

4.1.2
),

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}^{(i)}=R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}{\bm{a}}_{n}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{b}}_{n,c}{\bm{b}}_{n,c}^{\top}

where
{\bm{a}}_{n}
stems from the layer’s Jacobian, i.e. the layer’s input
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}
, and
{\bm{b}}_{n,c}
stems from the vector
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
that is backpropagated to the layer’s output by applying the Jacobian
(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}
.

Our remaining steps are (i) to specify what
{\bm{a}}_{n}
and
{\bm{b}}_{n,c}
are, which will depend on the curvature matrix we want to approximate (
Section

4.3
), and (ii) how to approximate the sum of Kronecker products as a single Kronecker product (KFAC’s expectation approximation,
Section

5.1
).

4.1.3
Kronecker Factor Dependencies

For the scaffold in
, let’s quickly think about the dependencies of the Kronecker matrices in the above expression.
KFAC results in two distinct Kronecker factors: one based on layer inputs (input-based Kronecker factor
{\bm{A}}^{(i)}
) and the other based on gradients with respect to the layer output (grad-output-based Kronecker factor
{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
).
As we will see, we can interpret
(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}
as a
pseudo-gradient
.
In fact, setting

\blacktriangle_{n,c}\leftarrow\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\,

we obtain

(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n}),

which is the per-datum loss gradient with respect to layer
i
’s output.
In summary, we identify the following dependencies of the Kronecker factors
{\bm{A}}^{(i)}
and
{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
, justifying their names:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\text{\quad depends on \quad}\{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}\}_{n}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\text{\quad depends on \quad}\{(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\}_{n,c}\,.

4.2
Algorithmic Outline

We now know enough to discuss the scaffold from
.

Step 1:

Perform a forward pass to compute
\{{\bm{f}}_{n}\}_{n}
, storing the layer inputs
\{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}\}_{n}
and outputs
\{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}\}_{n}
of all layers
i
whose parameter curvature matrix we want to approximate with KFAC.

Step 2:

Compute the input-based Kronecker factors
{\bm{A}}^{(i)}
using the layer inputs
\{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}\}_{n}
.
The details of that will depend on the layer type, and we still have to specify them in later chapters.

Step 3:

Generate the vectors
\{\blacktriangle_{n,c}\}_{n,c}
to be backpropagated, and backpropagate them to the layer outputs to get the pseudo-gradients
\{(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\}_{n,c}
.
This step depends on the loss function we are using, and the curvature matrix we want to approximate (
Section

4.3
).
Finally, compute the output-based Kronecker factors
{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
.
The details will again depend on the layer type, and we will specify them in later chapters.

Step 4:

Account for scaling caused by the loss function’s reduction
R
.

Step 5:

Return the KFAC approximation in the form
{\bm{A}}^{(i)},{\bm{B}}^{(i)}
for all supported layers
i
.

In summary, the scaffold disentangles the task of computing KFAC into three components: (i) computing the input-based Kronecker factors, (ii) generating and backpropagating the vectors
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
, and (iii) computing the grad-output-based Kronecker factors.
The next section describes how to accomplish step (ii).
After that, we can add new KFAC implementations simply by specifying (i) and (iii) for each new layer.

4.3
Backpropagated Vectors

The computational scaffold can be flexibly adapted to various curvature matrices by modifying the choice of backpropagated vectors
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
and the reduction factor
R
.
This can be done by pattern-matching each curvature matrix to the general form in
Equation

2
.
Below, we recap the different curvature definitions; see
Section

2.5
for a self-contained introduction.

implements the construction of backpropagated vectors.

Generalized Gauss-Newton/type-II Fisher.

Remember the GGN from
Section

2.4.4
,

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})=R\sum_{n}\left[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\right]^{\top}\left[\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\right]\left[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\right]\,.

With the symmetric decomposition (
Section

2.5.2
)

{\bm{S}}_{n}{\bm{S}}_{n}^{\top}=\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n}),

we identify for the backpropagated vector

\displaystyle\blacktriangle_{n,c}=[{\bm{S}}_{n}]_{:,c}.

In words, to approximate the GGN with KFAC, we must backpropagate each of the
\dim({\mathcal{F}})
columns of the Hessian decomposition
{\bm{S}}_{n}
.
For common loss functions, the Type-II Fisher from
Section

2.5.3
coincides with the GGN, hence we can use the same backpropagated vectors as for the GGN to approximate it.

MC-sampled type-I Fisher.

The type-I Fisher, approximated via Monte Carlo (MC) sampling, is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bm{F}}^{\text{I}}({\bm{\theta}})=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{R}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}&\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\right)^{\top}\\
&\begin{aligned} \big[&\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m})\\
&\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m})^{\top}\big]\end{aligned}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,,\end{aligned}

(
Section

2.5.3
).
We can immediately see that

\displaystyle\blacktriangle_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m})

where
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{f}}={\bm{f}}_{n})
is a sample from the model’s predictive distribution.
In words, to approximate the Type-I Fisher with KFAC, we backpropagate
M
‘would-be’ gradients for each datum
{\bm{f}}_{n}
.
The number of Monte Carlo samples controls the number of backpropagations:

•

For
computational efficiency
,
M<\dim({\mathcal{F}})
makes sense because this reduces the number of backpropagations from
\dim({\mathcal{F}})
(as in the type-II case) to
M
.

•

For
practical settings
,
M
is usually set to
1
.

•

For
verification
, a larger
M
can be used to ensure convergence to the expected value.

Empirical Fisher.

The empirical Fisher (
Section

2.5.5
) replaces the expectation over the model’s likelihood in the type-I Fisher by the expectation over the empirical distribution:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bm{E}}({\bm{\theta}})=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}^{\top}\\
&\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n})(\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n}))^{\top}\\
&\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\bm{f}}_{n}\,.\end{aligned}

We identify that we only need to backpropagate a single vector per datum,

\displaystyle\blacktriangle_{n,1}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\,.

It is the same vector that is backpropagated to compute the gradient.
This means that to approximate the empirical Fisher with KFAC, we can recycle the backward pass from the gradient computation.

Let’s formalize the layer whose curvature we approximate with KFAC in this chapter:

{setup}

[Linear layer inside a neural net]
Consider a linear layer with weights
{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times D_{\text{in}}}
and bias
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}}
in a neural net
f(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}})
.
The network’s prediction feeds into a criterion function, and we compute an empirical risk over a dataset of
N
points, incorporating a reduction factor
R
.
Our goal is to approximate the curvature matrix block
{\bm{C}}(\vec{\bm{W}})
.

For each datum
n
, the layer processes an input vector
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}}
, producing an output vector
{\bm{z}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}}
as follows

{\bm{z}}_{n}={\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{n}+{\bm{b}}\,.

Denote the network’s prediction for datum
n
by
{\bm{f}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})}
.
For each backprograted vector
\blacktriangle_{n,c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})}
, we denote the layer’s output gradient as

{\bm{g}}_{n,c}=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}_{n}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}}\,.

We will often neglect the bias and focus on approximating the curvature for the weight matrix
{\bm{W}}
alone.
The bias can always be incorporated into the weight matrix by concatenation into a single weight matrix
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}
and by augmenting the input vectors with an additional constant:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{W}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{W}}&{\bm{b}}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times(D_{\text{in}}+1)}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{x}}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}+1}\,.

Thus, the derivations below hold equivalently for
\tilde{{\bm{W}}},\tilde{{\bm{x}}}
in place of
{\bm{W}},{\bm{x}}
.

5.1
Derivation of KFAC

We now derive the KFAC approximation for a linear layer with weights
{\bm{W}}
, input vector
{\bm{x}}
, and output vector
{\bm{z}}={\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}
(
Section

5
).
Following convention in the literature, we assume column-major (
\cvec
) flattening.

Exact curvature matrix.

Recall from
Equation

2
that the curvature matrix w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
takes the form

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}(\cvec{\bm{W}})

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}&(\mathrm{J}^{\cvec}_{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{z}}_{n})^{\top}\\
&(\mathrm{J}^{\cvec}_{{\bm{z}}_{n}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\blacktriangle_{n,c}^{\top}(\mathrm{J}^{\cvec}_{{\bm{z}}_{n}}{\bm{f}}_{n})\\
&(\mathrm{J}^{\cvec}_{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{z}}_{n})\,.\end{aligned}

We recall from
Section

2.4.2
that the Jacobian of a linear layer’s output w.r.t. its weights is

\mathrm{J}^{\cvec}_{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{z}}_{n}={\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}\,

where
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{in}}}
is the layer’s input.
With that, and using the Kronecker product properties from
Section

4.1.1
, we see that the curvature matrix is a sum of Kronecker products:

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}(\cvec{\bm{W}})

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}&({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}){\bm{g}}_{n,c}\\
&{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{\top}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}})\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}&({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}){\bm{g}}_{n,c}\\
&\left[({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}){\bm{g}}_{n,c}\right]^{\top}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}&\left({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}\right)\left({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}\right)^{\top}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}\left({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}\right)\left({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=R\sum_{n}\sum_{c}\left({\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)\otimes\left({\bm{g}}_{n,c}{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{\top}\right)\,.

{example}

[Motivation for KFAC’s expectation approximation]

KFAC’s expectation approximation can be derived from an optimality condition under specific assumptions to preserve a Kronecker structure.

Consider the curvature matrix

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle=\textstyle R\sum_{n=1}^{N}({\bm{x}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}){\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}})\,.

Our goal is to approximate
{\bm{C}}
with a single Kronecker product.

To make this expression more compact, stack the vectors
{\bm{x}}_{n}
and
{\bm{g}}_{n}
into matrices:

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{1}&{\bm{x}}_{2}&\ldots&{\bm{x}}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}\times N}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{g}}_{1}&{\bm{g}}_{2}&\ldots&{\bm{g}}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times N}\,.

and rewrite
{\bm{C}}
in terms of these matrices:

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}=R({\bm{X}}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}})\cvec({\bm{G}})\cvec({\bm{G}})^{\top}({\bm{X}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}).

Looking at this expression, we realize that an easy way to achieve a single Kronecker product is to approximate
\cvec({\bm{G}})\cvec({\bm{G}})^{\top}\approx{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{in}}}\otimes{\bm{B}}
with
{\bm{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times D_{\text{out}}}
.
Then we would have

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle\approx R({\bm{X}}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}})({\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{in}}}\otimes{\bm{B}})({\bm{X}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}})

\displaystyle=R{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{B}}=\textstyle R\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)\otimes{\bm{B}}\,.

To obtain
{\bm{B}}
, we minimize the squared Frobenius norm residual:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\left\lVert\cvec({\bm{G}})\cvec({\bm{G}})^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{in}}}\otimes{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Expanding the Kronecker structure, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{g}}_{1}{\bm{g}}_{1}^{\top}-{\bm{B}}&\dots&{\bm{g}}_{1}{\bm{g}}_{N}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bm{g}}_{N}{\bm{g}}_{1}^{\top}&\dots&{\bm{g}}_{N}{\bm{g}}_{N}^{\top}-{\bm{B}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
&=\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{g}}_{1}{\bm{g}}_{1}^{\top}-{\bm{B}}&\dots&{\bm{0}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bm{0}}&\ldots&{\bm{g}}_{N}{\bm{g}}_{N}^{\top}-{\bm{B}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
&=\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lVert{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}-{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
&=\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{D_{\text{out}}}\left([{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{i}[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{j}-[{\bm{B}}]_{i,j}\right)^{2}\,.\end{split}

Taking the derivative w.r.t.
{\bm{B}}
and setting it to zero yields for all
k,l\in\{1,\dots,D_{\text{out}}\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{D_{\text{out}}}\left([{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{i}[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{j}-[{\bm{B}}]_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right)}{\partial[{\bm{B}}]_{k,l}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left([{\bm{B}}]_{k,l}-[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{k}[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(N[{\bm{B}}]_{k,l}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{k}[{\bm{g}}_{n}]_{l}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0

\displaystyle\implies{\bm{B}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}=\frac{1}{N}{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}.

Thus, under this optimality condition, the best choice for
{\bm{B}}
is the empirical covariance of the gradient vectors:

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}\approx\left(R\sum_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)\otimes\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n^{\prime}}{\bm{g}}_{n^{\prime}}{\bm{g}}_{n^{\prime}}^{\top}\right).

This is precisely the expectation approximation used in KFAC.

From sum of Kronecker products to single Kronecker product.

The last step is to approximate the sum of Kronecker products by a single Kronecker product.
To do that, we use the so-called KFAC’s expectation approximation (see
Section

5.1
for further motivation):

{definition}

[KFAC’s expectation approximation]

Consider two sets of
N
vectors
\{{\bm{x}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
\{{\bm{g}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
where each
{\bm{x}}_{n}
is input-based and each
{\bm{g}}_{n}
is output-gradient-based.
KFAC approximates the sum of Kronecker products of their self-outer products by a single Kronecker product, using the following expectation approximation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{n=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0.6797,0.1016}N}}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}\\
&\qquad\approx\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)\otimes\frac{\left({\color[rgb]{0.9805,0.6797,0.1016}\sum_{n}}{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}\right)}{{\color[rgb]{0.9805,0.6797,0.1016}N}}.\end{split}

(3a)

In words, we sum the outer products on the left and right of the Kronecker product independently and divide by the number of outer products in the term containing the gradient vectors.
In expectation notation (expectation meaning averaging over all values of
n
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{n}[\bullet_{n}]=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bullet_{n}
), we can write this approximation more conveniently as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}\right]\\
&\qquad\approx N\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right]\otimes\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[{\bm{g}}_{n}{\bm{g}}_{n}^{\top}\right]\,,\end{split}

(3b)

i.e. ‘pull’ the expectation into the Kronecker factors.

Applying
Section

5.1
, we obtain the curvature matrix’s KFAC approximation:

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle(\cvec{\bm{W}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(R\sum_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)}_{\text{input-based}}\otimes\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\sum_{c}{\bm{g}}_{n,c}{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{\top}\right)}_{\text{grad-output-based}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}(\cvec{\bm{W}}))\,.

Changing flattening swaps the Kronecker factors.

Had we done the same derivation using the
\rvec
-flattening scheme where the output-parameter Jacobian takes the form (
Section

2.4.2
)

\mathrm{J}^{\rvec}_{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{z}}_{n}={\bm{I}}_{D_{\text{out}}}\otimes{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\,,

we would have found the order of Kronecker factors to reverse,

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}

\displaystyle(\rvec{\bm{W}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\sum_{c}{\bm{g}}_{n,c}{\bm{g}}_{n,c}^{\top}\right)\otimes\left(R\sum_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}(\rvec{\bm{W}}))\,.

This is important to keep in mind for KFAC implementations, as libraries like PyTorch use
\rvec
-flattening.

A particularly straightforward case occurs when summation over
n
disappears, i.e. when the dataset is only a single data point or
N
identical data points.
Hence, we have the following

{test}

[KFAC for linear layers in an MLP with one data point,
]
Consider a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)

\displaystyle f=\phi^{(L)}\circ f^{(L)}\circ\ldots\circ\phi^{(1)}\circ f^{(1)}\,.

Each layer consists of a dense (linear) transformation
f^{(l)}
(satisfying
Section

5
) followed by a pointwise activation
\phi^{(l)}
.
Let
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
,
{\bm{b}}^{(l)}
, and
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{W}}^{(l)}&{\bm{b}}^{(l)}\end{pmatrix}
denote the weight, bias, and concatenated parameters of the dense layer
l
.

Now, assume that the dataset consists of only one data point,
{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\}
, and we have a criterion function
c=-\log r
, which can be interpreted as negative log-likelihood.

Then, KFAC becomes exact for all layers
l=1,\dots,L
and any flattening scheme
\vec
in the following sense:

•

KFAC-type-II equals the GGN

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))={\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})

•

KFAC-MC converges to the GGN

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{F}}^{\text{I}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))

\displaystyle={\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})

•

KFAC-empirical coincides with the empirical Fisher

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{E}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))={\bm{E}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})

This test serves as a basic functionality check for KFAC, particularly regarding the criterion function and its backpropagated vectors
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
.

5.2.2
Regression with Deep Linear Nets

A more subtle and interesting case where the KFAC approximation becomes exact is for deep linear networks with square loss.
In this setting, the backpropagated vectors
{\bm{g}}_{n,c}
become independent of
n
because the network’s prediction is linear w.r.t. each layer’s parameter
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
(but not linear w.r.t. the
full
parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}
).

Recall that
{\bm{g}}_{n,c}
depends on the backpropagated vector
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
and the Jacobian of the prediction w.r.t. the layer’s output
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}_{n}}{\bm{f}}_{n}
.
For a square loss function,
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
is often independent of
n
:

•

In type-II Fisher, it corresponds to a column of the identity matrix.

•

In Monte Carlo (MC) Fisher, it is simply a normally distributed random number.

Further, if all layers in the net are linear, then all prediction-intermediate Jacobians remain constant and independent of
n
.
Thus, in this setting, the KFAC approximation becomes exact in the limit, as originally found by
Bernacchia et al. 2018
.

{test}

[KFAC for regression with a deep linear net (no nonlinear layers),
]
Consider a deep linear network consisting of
L
fully-connected layers

\displaystyle f=f^{(L)}\circ f^{(L-1)}\circ\ldots\circ f^{(1)}

with weights
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
, bias
{\bm{b}}^{(l)}
, and concatenated parameters
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{W}}^{(l)}&{\bm{b}}^{(l)}\end{pmatrix}
.
Assume the network processes a vector-valued input per datum and performs regression with square loss on an arbitrary dataset
{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\mid n=1,\dots,N\}
.
Then, for each layer
l=1,\dots,L
and any chosen flattening scheme
\vec
, the following identities hold:

•

KFAC-type-II equals the GGN

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))={\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})

•

KFAC-MC converges to the GGN

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{F}}^{\text{I}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))

\displaystyle={\bm{G}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})

However, KFAC-empirical
differs
from the empirical Fisher,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{E}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}))\neq{\bm{E}}(\vec\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)})\,,

as the backpropagated vector
\blacktriangle_{n,c}
depends on
{\bm{f}}_{n}
and therefore remains dependent on
n
.

This test is useful for preventing scaling bugs in the Kronecker factor computations.
Additionally, it remains valid when incorporating an independent sequence dimension
S
, i.e. in the presence of weight sharing, provided that the network treats sequence elements independently.
Although weight sharing is not of our interest in this chapter, we have already included such a slightly more general test case in
.

6
Cheatsheet: KFAC

•

General KFAC scaffold:
{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})\approx\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}))\coloneqq{\bm{A}}^{(i)}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{(i)}

with

–

{\bm{C}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(i)})=R\sum_{n}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\left[\bullet({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\right](\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})

–

\bullet({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})=\sum_{c=1}^{\dim({\mathcal{F}})}\blacktriangle_{c}({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})(\blacktriangle_{c}({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n}))^{\top}
(depends on curvature type
{\bm{C}}
)

–

{\bm{A}}^{(i)}={\bm{A}}^{(i)}(\{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i-1)}\}_{n})
(input-based factor)

–

{\bm{B}}^{(i)}={\bm{B}}^{(i)}(\{(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(i)}}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\}_{n,c})
(grad-output-based factor)

•

Backpropagated vectors
\{\blacktriangle_{n,c}\}_{n,c}

–

GGN/type-II Fisher:
\blacktriangle_{n,c}=[{\bm{S}}_{n}]_{:,c}
for
c=1,\dots,C
where
{\bm{S}}_{n}{\bm{S}}_{n}^{\top}=\mathrm{H}_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}c({\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})

*

Square loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,c}=[{\bm{I}}]_{:,c}
(one-hot vector, does not depend on
n
)

*

Softmax cross-entropy loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,c}=\sqrt{[\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{n})]_{c}}(\mathrm{onehot}(c)-\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{n}))

–

MC-Fisher:
\blacktriangle_{n,m}=-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\mid{\mathbf{f}}={\bm{f}}_{n})
where
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}r({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\mathbf{f}}={\bm{f}}_{n})

*

Square loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,m}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}_{n,m}-{\bm{f}}_{n}
where
{\bm{y}}_{n,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid{\bm{\mu}}={\bm{f}}_{n},{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}})

\Leftrightarrow\blacktriangle_{n,m}=\tilde{{\bm{y}}}
where
\tilde{{\bm{y}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}\mid\mu={\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}})
(does not depend on
n
)

*

Softmax cross-entropy loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,m}=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{n})-\mathrm{onehot}(y_{n,m})
where
y_{n,m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.{} }}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{C}}({\textnormal{y}}\mid{\bm{\sigma}}=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{n}))

–

Empirical Fisher:
\blacktriangle_{n,1}=-\nabla_{{\bm{f}}_{n}}\log r({\mathbf{y}}={\bm{y}}_{n}\mid{\mathbf{f}}={\bm{f}}_{n})

*

Square loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,1}={\bm{y}}_{n}-{\bm{f}}_{n}

*

Softmax cross-entropy loss:
\blacktriangle_{n,1}=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{n})-\mathrm{onehot}(y_{n})

•

KFAC for a linear layer
{\bm{z}}={\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
without weight sharing (also holds for
({\bm{W}},{\bm{x}})\leftrightarrow(\tilde{{\bm{W}}},\tilde{{\bm{x}}})
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KFAC}({\bm{C}}(\vec{\bm{W}}))\approx{\bm{C}}(\vec{\bm{W}})=\begin{cases}{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}&\vec=\cvec\,,\\
{\bm{B}}\otimes{\bm{A}}&\vec=\rvec\,,\end{cases}\,

with input-based factor
{\bm{A}}
and grad-output-based factor
{\bm{B}}
, and
{\bm{g}}_{n,c}=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}_{n,s}}^{\vec}{\bm{f}}_{n})^{\top}\blacktriangle_{n,c}\,
,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=R\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bm{x}}_{n}{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}\times D_{\text{in}}},\qquad{\bm{B}}

7
Conclusion & Outlook

Summary.

In this manuscript, we provided a detailed overview of the seminal KFAC curvature approximation from the ground up, in both math and code.
In doing so, we first developed a collection of automatic differentiation utilities to access derivatives.
They play a crucial role in computing curvature information and helped us make these concepts concrete for common operations used in deep learning, while serving as ground truth to verify our implementation.
Building on these fundamentals, we motivated using Kronecker products to approximate curvature matrices in neural networks, and introduced the KFAC approximation for linear layers in the absence of weight sharing, as originally described by
Martens & Grosse 2015
, showed how to apply it to different curvature matrices, and highlighted tests to verify its correctness.

We hope this tutorial is a helpful resource for both newcomers to the field who want to learn more about curvature matrices, their approximations, and common pitfalls, as well as experienced researchers who are seeking a pedagogical introduction and implementation they can use as a starting point to prototype their research idea.

Call for contributions & future work.

Although we believe that we covered the most essential aspects of KFAC, this tutorial does not address many important, albeit advanced, topics related to KFAC.
We invite anyone to contribute to this fully open-source effort (
github.com/f-dangel/kfac-tutorial
) to further improve the tutorial over time.
Specifically, we think the following ideas are promising to include in future versions:

•

Eigenvalue-corrected KFAC (EKFAC).

George et al. 2018
proposed the concept of eigenvalue correction to improve KFAC’s approximation quality, and the resulting EKFAC has become the de facto standard for KFAC’s application to training data attribution with influence functions
(
Grosse et al. 2023
;
Mlodozeniec et al. 2025
)
.
The first step in computing EKFAC is to compute KFAC.
In the second step, we only keep the space spanned by the Kronecker factors, and introduce a diagonal scaling term (the “eigenvalues”), whose value is determined by minimizing the Frobenius norm residual between the original curvature matrix and the corrected KFAC approximation.
This can be done cheaply.

•

KFAC for linear layers with weight sharing.

We focused on explaining KFAC as introduced in the seminal paper by
Martens & Grosse 2015
, which considers multi-layer perceptrons where each layer processes a single vector per datum (i.e. no weight sharing).
However, modern neural networks like convolutional networks, transformers, or graph neural networks, process sequence-valued data and therefore naturally exhibit weight sharing across spatial or temporal dimensions.
Depending on how the shared axis is treated when computing the loss, there exist two different KFAC approximations for linear layers with weight sharing, coined by
Eschenhagen et al. 2023
: KFAC-expand and KFAC-reduce.
KFAC-reduce becomes relevant in scenarios where weight sharing occurs, and the shared dimension is explicitly reduced (e.g., summed or averaged) during the forward pass.
Unlike KFAC-expand, which treats shared dimensions as independent by flattening them into a batch dimension, KFAC-reduce adjusts for this reduction by aggregating inputs and gradients across the shared dimension before forming Kronecker factors.
The formalization of
Eschenhagen et al. 2023
allows KFAC to be extended to transformers, convolutional networks (containing the originally proposed method from
Grosse & Martens 2016
), and graph neural networks.
While we already introduced the
expand
specifier in our implementation, we have not yet implemented the
reduce
specifier and its related tests.

•

Functional-style KFAC implementation.

One essential step for computing KFAC is accessing the inputs and outputs of layers.
This is easy if the neural net consists entirely of
torch.nn.Module
s—and we assume this in our implementation—by using PyTorch’s hook mechanism to intercept the forward pass and obtain the layer inputs (required for KFAC’s input-based Kronecker factor) and outputs (required for KFAC’s grad-output-based Kronecker factors).
This is okay because most architectures used in practice are indeed implemented in a modular fashion.

However, a functional-style implementation of KFAC, which had the ability to automatically trace and capture the inputs and outputs of linear operations inside a function representing the neural network (similar to KFAC’s implementation in JAX
(
Botev & Martens 2022
)
), would make KFAC accessible to libraries that adopt a functional style
(
Duffield et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.
Such a more general approach is also helpful to incorporate more recent extensions of KFAC, for instance, to training Physics-informed neural networks
(
Dangel et al. 2024
)
, whose forward pass is done in Taylor mode arithmetic that differs from the standard forward pass and can therefore not be intercepted with module hooks.
We believe that a functional-style implementation of KFAC in PyTorch can be achieved using the tracing mechanism of
torch.fx

(
Reed et al. 2022
)
, which provides a modifiable presentation of the computation graph.
