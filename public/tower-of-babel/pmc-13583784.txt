War surgeon’s Cap
Authors: Alexander Malloy
Published: 2026

From an early age, I was fascinated with the military and surgery. Throughout my adolescence and teenage years, I would often accompany my father into the hospital as he made neurosurgery rounds. My exposure to the surgical realm, its culture, and etiquette sparked curiosity into what a potential career in the field may bring. In college, I decided to pursue medical school and shadowed various surgeons during the summer months. The witty, sarcastic general surgeon with an eloquent British accent captivated me with his approach to surgical patients and their diseases and dazzled me with his work ethic, technical skills, and versatility. Before I even began my medical training, I had already determined that surgery would become a major measure of who and what I would become. At the time, that choice was clear, but a key part of my future required something more of me.

When I spent time with family, I found myself mesmerized by my grandfather’s reflections on his time as an artillery officer deployed during the Korean War. His personal accounts on what he felt comfortable sharing about combat, as well as the guts, grit, and gentlemanly conduct required by the Army, had a profound effect on me. My immense admiration and respect for these people influenced my life choices and led me to commission as an Army officer during medical school.

Because of these experiences, matching into an Army surgical residency was the only sensible choice. The skills, fortitude, and honor of the profession resonated with me, making clear that surgery is an essential pillar of military medicine. Throughout my training, the concept of becoming a “War Surgeon” took root in my mind, evolving into an identity I sought to embody. While my education encompassed all aspects of general surgery, the paramount responsibility of an Army combat surgeon was consistently reinforced—through historical lessons, personal encounters, and the invaluable wisdom imparted by my attending physicians.

Though my passion for surgical critical care and trauma had been kindling for years, it was fully ignited during an extended away rotation at a high-volume Level 1 civilian trauma center. I cut my teeth on resuscitative thoracotomies, penetrating injuries to the abdomen, and extremity vascular shunts seemingly every call shift. I was grateful to work with giants of the profession, and they imprinted on me a myriad of life-saving interventions. When I came home from the hospital, I would eagerly recount my experiences to my spouse, my enthusiasm spilling over in every conversation. She, more than anyone, recognized the profound joy trauma surgery brought me and urged me to pursue surgical critical care fellowship (always, always, always listen to your wife).

Having completed both my surgical residency and trauma fellowship within Army medical treatment facilities, I felt prepared when the call came for my first combat deployment to Afghanistan. Embedded with a high-tempo forward surgical team, I found myself applying years of rigorous training with calculated confidence. The time spent in the months leading up to the Afghan retrograde was a culmination of my mentors’ lessons and my own learned skills. I finally understood the weight of the title I had long sought — I had become a “War Surgeon.” I also came to appreciate that my success was not mine alone, but an apex of shared knowledge, steadfast camaraderie, and an unwavering commitment to the mission.

Following my deployment, I was honored to be selected for the Army Military-Civilian Trauma Training Team partnership at a premier level 1 trauma center. This opportunity has been transformative, exposing me to an immense variety of complex cases and allowing me to integrate military surgical strategies into a civilian trauma system. I have honed my skills and have gained the confidence to treat injured patients in a setting that closely emulates urban warfare. Additionally, my involvement with a prolific ECMO team has provided me with specialized skills in extracorporeal life support—an invaluable asset in both wartime and civilian critical care settings. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the scarcity of this resource, and I am proud to have contributed to its lifesaving implementation for service members and their families. During my time at my military-civilian partnership, I was assigned my second combat deployment, this time to Iraq. Armed with both stateside and battlefield experience, I felt even more honed and poised for anything the enemy could throw at my team.

An immense benefit of the position is the reciprocal exchange of knowledge between military and civilian trauma team members. I am deeply fortunate to share the lessons of my mentors, recount battlefield experiences, and instill wartime surgical principles in both my civilian and military colleagues and trainees. Every day I learn something new, and having presence in a robust trauma system gives me ample opportunity to inspire budding surgical residents to do their “War Surgeon” caps. This exchange of information will always be the crux of the profession—mentorship, imparting lessons learned, and carrying forth the legacy of combat surgery. This exchange of knowledge and skills to the future generation is the keystone of maintaining a healthy fighting force and a competent cadre of surgical capabilities.

The specialized skills and unparalleled opportunities I have gained through my Army and civilian experiences have shaped me into the surgeon I am today. I am privileged to stand alongside dedicated military and civilian contemporaries, teachers, and friends, all committed to the relentless pursuit of excellence in trauma surgery. My purpose is clear: to preserve the fighting strength, to impart hard-earned knowledge, and to advise the next generation of military trauma surgeons. And when the call comes again, I will wear my “War Surgeon” cap with pride. Every day, I can see that new headgear is being forged, fashioned from the same cloth, and carried forward by those who share this profound calling, and they in turn sew future surgical crowns of victory.
