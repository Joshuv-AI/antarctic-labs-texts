Two case reports of gastric signet ring cell carcinoma with pulmonary metastasis manifesting as diffuse ground-glass opacities and pleural involvement: an atypical imaging pattern mimicking lung infection
Authors: Yike Wang, Yingbo Xie, Xuan Yao, Pei Ma, Fengnan Wang, Xinqing Lin, Xiaohong Xie, Ming Liu, Chengzhi Zhou, Zhanhong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) typically metastasizes to the liver and peritoneum. Although pulmonary metastasis occurs in approximately 15% of patients with advanced gastric cancer and typically presents as multiple well-circumscribed nodules on chest computed tomography (CT), diffuse ground-glass opacities (GGOs) as the predominant manifestation of SRCC lung metastasis are exceedingly rare. This pattern may pose a diagnostic pitfall by closely mimicking infectious or interstitial lung diseases.Case DescriptionWe report two cases of gastric SRCC with pulmonary metastasis presenting primarily with respiratory symptoms and atypical imaging features. Case 1: A 64-year-old woman presented with persistent cough, dyspnea, and chest pain, and denied any preceding gastrointestinal symptoms. Chest CT revealed bilateral diffuse GGOs accompanied by pleural effusion and thickening. Despite empirical antibiotic therapy directed against Streptococcus pyogenes and Klebsiella pneumoniae identified by next-generation sequencing, her symptoms worsened. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) demonstrated intense metabolic activity [maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) 6.2] in the lung lesions and bilateral pleural involvement. Bronchoscopy-guided biopsy confirmed adenocarcinoma with signet ring cell features, and gastroscopy with biopsy of a depressed gastric angle lesion confirmed poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma. She received docetaxel plus oxaliplatin chemotherapy with symptomatic improvement and was discharged. Case 2: A 62-year-old woman presented with exertional dyspnea and intermittent cough, also without preceding gastrointestinal symptoms. CT imaging demonstrated right pleural effusion, pleural thickening, and ground-glass nodules. Empirical antibiotic therapy was ineffective. Pleural fluid analysis revealed elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA; 85.00 µg/mL) and cancer antigen 125 (CA125; 327.00 U/mL). Biopsy of an adnexal mass and gastric lesions confirmed gastric SRCC with pleural and ovarian metastases. She was referred to the oncology department for systemic therapy. Long-term follow-up data for both patients are not available in the medical records.ConclusionsThese cases highlight an atypical imaging pattern of gastric SRCC metastasis—diffuse GGOs with pleural involvement—that closely mimics benign lung diseases. In patients without known malignancy who present with unexplained respiratory symptoms, diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion or thickening, and poor response to anti-infective therapy, early lung or pleural biopsy is essential to prevent diagnostic delay and initiate timely systemic oncological treatment.Keywords: Gastric cancer, metastatic lung tumor, interstitial inflammation of the lungs, signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC), case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• Gastric signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) with pulmonary metastasis can manifest primarily as diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities (GGOs) accompanied by pleural effusion and pleural thickening, closely mimicking infectious pneumonia or interstitial lung diseases.

• Both patients in this case report were initially misdiagnosed with inflammatory or infectious conditions; pathological and immunohistochemical examination of lung/pleural tissue was essential to confirm the diagnosis and gastric origin.

What is known and what is new?

• Pulmonary metastasis from gastric cancer typically presents as multiple well-circumscribed nodules, while carcinomatous lymphangitis and solitary nodules are uncommon. However, diffuse GGOs as the predominant manifestation of gastric SRCC lung metastasis are exceedingly rare and frequently pose a diagnostic pitfall.

• This case report presents two rare cases of gastric SRCC with pulmonary metastasis characterized by diffuse GGOs and pleural involvement, highlighting this atypical imaging pattern and emphasizing the necessity of early tissue biopsy in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms refractory to anti-infective therapy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In patients without known malignancy presenting with unexplained cough, dyspnea, diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion, or pleural thickening on chest computed tomography, and poor response to conventional anti-infective therapy, atypical lung metastasis should be suspected and prompt lung or pleural biopsy performed for pathological and immunohistochemical confirmation.

• Clinicians should recognize this rare triad of diffuse GGOs, pleural effusion, and pleural thickening as a potential manifestation of metastatic gastric SRCC to prevent diagnostic delay and initiate timely systemic oncological treatment.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, accounting for 4.9% of all new cancer cases (1,2). It demonstrates a strong metastatic tendency, with the liver, peritoneum, and lungs being the predominant sites. Liver metastasis is the most frequent, accounting for 48% of cases, followed by peritoneal metastasis (32%) and lung metastasis (15%) (3). Chest computed tomography (CT) imaging of lung metastasis from gastric cancer can present a variety of features. Typically, it presents as multiple round nodules with well-circumscribed margins (4). In contrast, carcinomatous lymphangitis and solitary nodules are infrequent, occurring in 4.0–11.0% and 0.3–6.0% of cases, respectively (5). Beyond these recognized patterns, a spectrum of atypical radiological manifestations has been increasingly reported, including diffuse ground-glass opacities (GGOs), consolidation-predominant lesions mimicking infectious pneumonia, and pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM) (4,6,7). These atypical interstitial patterns are particularly treacherous because they closely resemble primary lung diseases, infectious processes, or interstitial lung diseases, frequently resulting in diagnostic delay and inappropriate empiric antibiotic therapy. Notably, diffuse GGOs as the predominant manifestation of gastric signet ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) pulmonary metastasis are exceedingly rare and remain poorly characterized in the literature, often masquerading as inflammatory or infectious lung disease (4). Recognizable CT manifestations can aid clinicians in formulating a preliminary diagnosis. To determine the tumor type and origin, lung biopsy and gastroscopy with biopsy are essential. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1318/rc).

Case presentation

Case 1

A 64-year-old woman was admitted on April 10, 2025, with persistent cough and shortness of breath for over 2 months, and worsening chest and back pain for 1 month (Table 1). The cough was paroxysmal and particularly severe at night. The chest and back pain was exacerbated when standing or walking and partially relieved by lying on her side. She reported scant white frothy sputum and dyspnea, denying fever or hemoptysis. Notably, she denied any preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, including dyspepsia, epigastric pain, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, or melena. She had no history of prior gastroscopy or upper gastrointestinal evaluation. At a local hospital, chest CT revealed bilateral pulmonary changes, bronchoscopy indicated inflammatory alterations in the trachea and bronchi, and pathogen next-generation sequencing identified Streptococcus pyogenes and Klebsiella pneumoniae (brush examination and biopsy were not performed). Pulmonary function tests demonstrated severe restrictive ventilatory defect and significantly reduced diffusion capacity. The patient was diagnosed with pneumonia due to Klebsiella pneumoniae. Despite receiving empirical antibiotic, antitussive, and expectorant therapies, her cough and dyspnea failed to improve.

One week later, her cough and dyspnea worsened, accompanied by left chest and back pain. She was transferred to our hospital for further management. On admission, her CURB-65 score was 0 and performance status (PS) score was 3. Tumor markers showed elevated cancer antigen 125 (CA125) (94.40 U/mL) and cytokeratin 19 fragment (CYFRA 21-1) (6.25 ng/mL), whereas carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), and cancer antigen 15-3 (CA15-3) were within normal limits. To exclude interstitial lung disease and autoimmune disorders, serologic testing was performed, including KL-6, surfactant protein-A/D (SP-A/D), antinuclear antibody (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody. All results were negative. Nevertheless, given the persistent respiratory deterioration, markedly elevated tumor markers, and atypical radiological findings, further investigation with positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) was pursued. PET-CT (April 17, 2025) revealed diffuse bilateral patchy GGOs and consolidations with associated reticular opacities, traction bronchiectasis, and beaded thickening of the interlobular septa. Left lung volume loss was noted. A dominant nodular lesion measuring 1.6 × 1.1 cm in the posterior segment of the right upper lobe demonstrated intense fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avidity [maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) 6.2]. The left lower lingular segment showed focal consolidation (SUVmax 2.7). Multiple hypermetabolic mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes (stations 4L, 4R, 6, and 7) were present, with the largest measuring 2.1 × 0.9 cm (SUVmax 3.7). The left pleura, including the interlobar fissure, showed irregular nodular thickening (SUVmax 3.5). Bilateral pleural effusions were present, with the left side loculated and associated with an air-fluid level and compressive atelectasis of the adjacent lower lobes (Figure 1A-1I). Based on PET-CT findings, bronchoscopy-guided biopsy of the right lower lobe and subcarinal lymph node (station 7) was performed. Pathological examination confirmed adenocarcinoma with signet ring cell features (CK+, CDX-2+, CK7−, SATB2−, CK20+) (Figure 1J-1L). A primary gastric tumor could not be excluded based on the pathological and PET-CT findings. Gastroscopy revealed a depressed lesion on the lesser curvature of the gastric angle, measuring approximately 12 mm × 10 mm, covered with white exudate and exhibiting focal vascular distortion (Figure 1M,1N). Given the suspicion of malignancy, biopsy was performed. Pathological analysis confirmed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma (CK+, CK7−, MLH1+, MSH2+, MSH6+, PMS2+, HER2−) (Figure 1O,1P). Although Streptococcus pyogenes and Klebsiella pneumoniae were detected in the bronchial brushing specimens, several features argued against these organisms being the primary cause of the pulmonary disease: (I) the patient remained afebrile throughout the entire course; (II) broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy failed to produce any clinical or radiological improvement; (III) the subsequent PET-CT demonstrated markedly elevated metabolic activity (SUVmax 6.2) within the diffuse pulmonary lesions, which is atypical for uncomplicated bacterial pneumonia; and (IV) pathological examination ultimately confirmed adenocarcinoma with signet ring cell features. Therefore, the detected organisms were considered oropharyngeal colonizers or secondary contaminants rather than the true etiological agents. Thus, the final diagnosis was gastric SRCC with pulmonary and pleural metastasis, rather than true carcinomatous lymphangitis. Due to deteriorating respiratory function and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) PS of 3, docetaxel and oxaliplatin chemotherapy was initiated. During the first two cycles of docetaxel plus oxaliplatin chemotherapy, the patient experienced grade 1 nausea (managed with ondansetron) and grade 2 neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count nadir 1.2×109/L) without febrile complications. No grade 3–4 adverse events were observed. She completed both cycles without dose reduction. The patient achieved symptomatic improvement and was discharged in May 2025. Long-term follow-up data, including radiologic response to chemotherapy, progression-free survival, and overall survival, are not available in the medical records.

Case 2

A 62-year-old woman was admitted on April 8, 2025, with exertional dyspnea for over 2 weeks (Table 1). In mid-to-late March 2025, she developed worsening dyspnea accompanied by intermittent cough. No other respiratory or systemic symptoms were reported. Importantly, she denied any preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, including dyspepsia, epigastric pain, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, or melena. She had no history of prior gastroscopy or upper gastrointestinal evaluation. Chest ultrasound at a local hospital revealed right pleural effusion, and thoracentesis with catheter drainage was performed. Her dyspnea improved after drainage. Cytological analysis of the pleural effusion showed no malignant cells. Microbiologic and biochemical workup of the pleural fluid included: (I) bacterial culture, which showed no pathogenic growth after 5 days; (II) acid-fast bacilli smear and Mycobacterium tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction (PCR), both negative; (III) adenosine deaminase (ADA) level within normal limits, arguing against tuberculous pleurisy; and (IV) fungal smear and culture, which were also negative. Sputum Gram stain and bacterial culture, as well as two sets of blood cultures, yielded no pathogens. Despite these negative microbiologic findings, empirical antibiotic therapy (covering common respiratory pathogens) was administered for suspected infection, along with antitussive and expectorant treatment. However, her dyspnea on exertion persisted without improvement. Tumor markers showed elevated CA125 (252.3 U/mL) and CA72-4 (17.4 U/mL), whereas others were within normal limits. Non-contrast chest CT showed moderate right pleural effusion, right pleural thickening, and a small ground-glass nodule in the right upper lobe, suggestive of focal inflammatory lesion. She had no smoking history, and her PS score was 3.

Ultrasound-guided thoracentesis yielded exudative pleural fluid with CEA 85.00 ng/mL and CA125 327.00 U/mL. Serologic markers for interstitial lung disease (including KL-6 and SP-A/D) and autoimmune screening panels (including ANA, RF, and anti-CCP antibody) were not obtained during the initial workup, as the clinical presentation was interpreted as infectious pleurisy. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis (April 11, 2025) showed a large right-sided pleural effusion, which had decreased compared to the prior study (April 7, 2025), with partial re-expansion of the right lung. Persistent right pleural thickening and mild thickening of the right interlobular septa were observed. The previously noted small GGO in the anterior segment of the right upper lobe was no longer clearly visualized. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were identified in the right hilum, mediastinum, right cardiophrenic angle, and hepatogastric ligament (short axis up to 13 mm), with heterogeneous enhancement. The gastric fundus and greater curvature showed suspicious asymmetric wall thickening with marked enhancement. Bilateral adnexal masses (left side 32 mm × 54 mm) with solid and cystic components were identified (Figure 2A-2D). Transvaginal ultrasound-guided biopsy of the left adnexal mass confirmed adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry showed TTF-1(−), CDX-2(+), HER2(+), CK7(+), and CK20(+) (Figure 2E,2F). Based on these findings, a primary gastric tumor could not be excluded. Gastroscopy showed two patchy mucosal depressions on the lesser curvature of the gastric body (10–12 mm), covered with thin white exudate and exhibiting a rough mucosal surface (Figure 2G,2H). Biopsy showed closely packed, fused glands with back-to-back arrangement and moderate dysplasia. Diffuse signet ring cells were observed in the submucosa. Immunohistochemistry showed CK(+), CK7(+), PMS2(+), MLH1(+), MSH2(+), MSH6(+), CEA(+), HER2(−) and CK20(−). Morphology and immunophenotype confirmed adenocarcinoma (Figure 2I,2J). The final diagnosis was gastric SRCC with pleural and ovarian metastases. Subsequently, the patient was referred to the oncology department for systemic therapy. Detailed follow-up information regarding treatment response, subsequent lines of therapy, and survival was not available in our institutional records. Discussion

Lung metastasis occurs in 10.7% to 11.1% of patients with extrathoracic malignant tumors (8). The presence of lung metastasis in these patients indicates a diffuse disease state. According to the TNM staging system, these patients are classified as stage IV with poor prognosis (9). Multiple large-scale studies indicate that the incidence of lung metastasis from gastric cancer ranges from 0.96% to 5.56%. Notably, the risk of lung metastasis is significantly higher in patients with gastric squamous cell carcinoma compared to those with gastric adenocarcinoma. The median survival period for patients with advanced gastric cancer is approximately 6 months (10-13). The presence of lung metastasis further worsens prognosis, necessitating careful monitoring in clinical practice.

Gastric SRCC is a subtype of poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma. It arises from glands, ducts, or secretory epithelium and is characterized by abundant intracellular mucin accumulation, resulting in signet-ring morphology due to nuclear displacement (14-17). This subtype is highly invasive, with extensive infiltration and distant metastasis that may occur early in the disease course. The predominant metastatic sites are the peritoneum and bone, although lung metastasis may also occur (18). The absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms in both of our patients is consistent with the recognized biological behavior of gastric SRCC, which can present with occult primary tumors and early distant metastasis even before the development of local symptoms. This feature underscores the potential for significant diagnostic delay when patients present with isolated extraintestinal manifestations. Both patients in this case report presented with lung metastases of SRCC originating from the stomach. Chest CT revealed diffuse GGOs in both lungs as the primary manifestation.

The diffuse GGOs observed in these cases can be explained by the distinctive biological behavior of signet ring cells. Mucin-rich tumor cells infiltrate the alveolar septa and replace the alveolar epithelium while largely preserving the basic alveolar architecture (19,20). This lepidic-like growth pattern produces hazy ground-glass attenuation on CT rather than the solid nodules typical of hematogenous metastases. The abundant intracellular mucin displaces the nucleus to the cell periphery, creating the characteristic signet-ring morphology; when these cells infiltrate the interstitium and alveolar walls, they increase lung opacity without causing complete airspace consolidation. Consequently, the metastatic lesions manifest as diffuse GGOs with associated reticular opacities and septal thickening, rather than as well-circumscribed solid nodules. This pathophysiological mechanism underscores why gastric SRCC pulmonary metastasis can closely mimic infectious pneumonia or interstitial lung disease on imaging.

The imaging findings in these cases must be distinguished from those associated with infectious pneumonia, carcinomatous lymphangitis, and interstitial pneumonia. Infectious pneumonia typically presents with acute fever, lobar consolidation, and a positive response to antibiotic therapy. In contrast, our patients were afebrile, showed non-lobar GGOs with high SUVmax, and did not respond to anti-infective therapy. Carcinomatous lymphangitis is pathologically defined by tumor cell infiltration within lymphatic vessels, typically manifesting as smooth or nodular interlobular septal thickening and peribronchovascular cuffing on CT. In Case 1, the initial PET-CT report indeed raised suspicion for carcinomatous lymphangitis based on the presence of beaded septal thickening. However, pathological examination of the lung and pleural biopsies confirmed tumor infiltration of the alveolar septa and pleura without lymphatic tumor emboli. Therefore, the diffuse GGOs were attributed to mucin-rich tumor cells infiltrating the alveolar walls and extensive pleural metastasis, rather than true lymphangitic spread. This distinction is clinically important because carcinomatous lymphangitis and pleural metastasis, despite overlapping imaging features, may carry different therapeutic implications. Interstitial pneumonia generally manifests as traction bronchiectasis, honeycombing, and a subpleural distribution of abnormalities, without pleural nodules or metabolic activity. In Case 1, serologic screening for interstitial lung disease (KL-6, SP-A/D) and autoimmune disorders (ANA, RF, anti-CCP antibody) was performed and yielded negative results, which supported the exclusion of idiopathic interstitial pneumonia and connective tissue disease-associated lung disease. However, these negative findings alone could not establish a malignant diagnosis, underscoring the necessity of tissue biopsy. In Case 2, these serologic tests were not obtained during the initial evaluation, representing a limitation of the diagnostic workup. While the clinical context (exudative pleural effusion with markedly elevated CEA and CA125) strongly favored malignancy, the absence of systematic autoimmune and interstitial lung disease screening limits the comprehensiveness of the differential diagnosis. We recommend that future similar cases include these tests early to exclude interstitial or immune-mediated lung diseases, particularly when GGOs and pleural involvement are present. This triad of diffuse GGOs, pleural effusion, and pleural thickening caused considerable diagnostic confusion. Early tissue biopsy is essential in patients with atypical infiltrates, elevated tumor markers, and treatment failure to prevent diagnostic delay and ensure appropriate oncological management.

Although lung metastasis is less frequent than liver or peritoneal metastasis, it typically occurs in conjunction with metastases to other sites. In Case 2, the patient exhibited both pleural and ovarian metastases. The ovary is a common site of distant metastasis from gastric cancer in women. Literature indicates that the incidence of ovarian metastasis in gastric cancer ranges from 5% to 10% (21,22). The prognosis for ovarian metastasis resulting from gastric cancer is poor, with a median survival time of 7 to 11 months following diagnosis (23). Currently, no standardized treatment protocol has been established, resulting in poor overall prognosis. This underscores the importance of improved screening and early detection of ovarian metastasis in female gastric cancer patients, as well as the development of standardized diagnostic and treatment strategies (24-26).

Treatment strategies for gastric cancer lung metastasis aim to prolong survival, alleviate symptoms, and improve quality of life (27). Currently, the primary treatment modalities include systemic chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (28-31). According to the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer (32), a two-drug combination is the preferred first-line treatment for advanced gastric cancer, particularly fluorouracil plus platinum-based regimens. The docetaxel-oxaliplatin regimen used in our Case 1 shows favorable efficacy, with an overall response rate (ORR) of 46.6–63.5% and median overall survival (mOS) of 11–14.6 months (33,34). This regimen exhibits lower toxicity than conventional regimens, with significantly reduced gastrointestinal and renal toxicities (33,34).

Solid tumors, including gastric, breast, and cervical cancers, frequently metastasize to the lungs through hematogenous dissemination, leading to respiratory symptoms such as cough and dyspnea (35-37). These symptoms significantly overlap with those of lung infections, increasing the likelihood of initial misdiagnosis as infectious disease and delaying appropriate treatment. Therefore, in patients without known malignancy who present with rapidly progressive, non-lobar diffuse GGOs, pleural effusion, and pleural thickening on chest CT, atypical metastatic disease should be strongly suspected when the following features are present: (I) markedly elevated metabolic activity on PET-CT (SUVmax >5.0); (II) absence of fever or leukocytosis; (III) poor response to empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy; and (IV) elevated serum or pleural fluid tumor markers. In this clinical setting, early tissue sampling via lung or pleural biopsy for pathological and immunohistochemical examination is more diagnostically valuable than repeated empiric anti-infective treatment, prolonged observation, or serial imaging alone. Prompt histological confirmation is essential to prevent diagnostic delay and to initiate timely systemic oncological therapy.

Limitations

The present report is limited by the lack of long-term follow-up data for both patients in this case report. As a retrospective case report, information regarding radiologic response to chemotherapy, progression-free survival, overall survival, and treatment-related adverse events beyond the initial hospitalization was not systematically recorded. This restricts our ability to assess the long-term efficacy of the treatment regimens employed and to provide definitive outcome data. Nevertheless, the primary objective of this report is to highlight the atypical imaging presentation and diagnostic pitfalls, which remain valid regardless of the incomplete follow-up. Due to the retrospective nature of this study and the lack of formal patient-reported outcome documentation, a detailed patient perspective could not be obtained.

Conclusions

In summary, while the lung is a recognized site of distant spread in gastric cancer, the prognosis for patients with lung involvement remains poor. The clinical value of these cases lies not in the presence of metastasis itself, but in the recognition of an atypical imaging pattern. This pattern is characterized by diffuse bilateral GGOs accompanied by pleural effusion and pleural thickening, and it closely mimics infectious or interstitial lung diseases while frequently leading to diagnostic delay. Clinically, it frequently occurs alongside lung metastasis to multiple organs, with female patients being particularly prone to ovarian metastasis. Therefore, early detection and accurate diagnosis of lung metastasis from gastric cancer are essential, along with comprehensive treatment strategies to improve patient prognosis.
