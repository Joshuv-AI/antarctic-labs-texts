Geometric Nonuniformity Localizes Valence Orbitals in Gold Clusters: Case Study on [Au23(SC6H11)16]–
Authors: Yosuke Asami, Shun Ito, Kiichirou Koyasu, Tatsuya Tsukuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo elucidate the effect of geometric nonuniformity on electronic structures of monolayer‐protected gold clusters, the valence orbitals of [Au23(SC6H11)16]– (Au23 − ), which features a nonuniform Au13 core, were investigated by using anion photoelectron spectroscopy and density functional theory calculations. The energy levels of the valence orbitals of Au23 − were more appropriately described by a model comprising 1S orbitals localized on Au3 or Au4 units within the Au13 core, rather than by the deformed jellium model. Meanwhile, the calculated valence orbitals of a hypothetical [Au23(SC6H11)16]– cluster with a uniform Au13 core were better described by the jellium model, suggesting that the geometric nonuniformity of the Au13 core is the direct cause of electronic localization in Au23 − . The localized nature of the molecular orbitals involved in photoabsorption accounts for the significantly larger molar absorption coefficient of Au23 − compared to those of typical Au13 superatoms with uniform structures.

Introduction

Monolayer‐protected gold clusters (AuMPCs) have garnered significant attention as ideal platforms for investigating the atomic‐level structure–property correlations of nanomaterials [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The electronic structures of AuMPCs, including the spatial distributions and energy levels of their valence orbitals, have been rationalized using the jellium model [10], which was originally developed to explain the magic numbers of clusters of simple metals (e.g., Na, Au, Ag, Cu) [11, 12, 13]. This model treats the valence electrons as being confined in a “uniform” potential created by a cluster of metal cations. In particular, a uniform, spherical jellium potential supports quantized superatomic orbitals (e.g., 1S, 1P, 1D, 2S) delocalized over the entire cluster. For example, a representative AuMPC, [Au25(PET)18]– (PET = 2‐phenylethanethiolate; hereafter Au25 − ), possesses a nearly perfect icosahedral Au13 core with a continuous shape measure of nearly zero (0.067) [14]. The uniform potential in the Au13 core is underpinned by the uniformity of the radial Au─Au bond lengths (2.79 ± 0.01 Å) and surface Au─Au bond lengths (2.93 ± 0.06 Å) (Table 1) [15, 16]. Consequently, the Au13(8e) core adopts the electronic configuration of (1S)2(1P)6 [10, 15, 16, 17]. Furthermore, the jellium model can be extended to anisotropic yet uniform Au cores, such as prolate and oblate geometries, within the framework of the deformed jellium model [7]. For example, electronic subshell closure at 6e in an oblate Au9 core has been rationalized by the destabilization of the 1P z orbital along the shorter axis (defined as the z axis) with respect to the 1P x , y orbitals, resulting in a (1S)2(1P x , y )4 electronic configuration [12, 18].

On the other hand, some AuMPCs feature “nonuniform” Au cores characterized by inhomogeneous Au–Au bond length distributions. These cores can be viewed as assemblies of triangular Au3 and tetrahedral Au4 units interconnected via relatively long Au–Au bonds [19, 20, 21]. A prime example of such a system is an Au13(8e) core of [Au23(CHT)16]– (CHT = cyclohexanethiolate; hereafter Au23 − ), which consists of two Au3 and two vertex‐sharing Au4 sub‐units (2Au3 + 2Au4) linked by Au–Au bonds longer than that of bulk gold (2.88 Å) (Table 1) [22, 23]. Similarly, the Au10(6e) cores of [Au21(TBT)15]0 (TBT = tert‐butylthiolate; hereafter Au21‐1 0) [21] and [Au21(ADT)15]0 (ADT = adamantane‐1‐thiolate; hereafter Au21‐2 0) [24] can be divided into Au3 + 2Au4 and 2Au3 + Au4, respectively (Table 1). Such structural nonuniformity results in the formation of local potentials of varying depths separated by potential barriers, leading to electronic structures distinct from those of their uniform counterparts. The electronic structures of these nonuniform AuMPCs are typically rationalized using models that explicitly account for Au3 and Au4 units, such as the superatom network (SAN) [25], the grand unified (GU) [26], and the cluster growth (CG) models [21]. The SAN model rationalizes the electron count by treating the core as an assembly of Au3 and Au4 units, each accommodating two valence electrons in its 1S orbital [25]. Similarly, the GU model heuristically determines suitable electron counts by assigning two electrons to each Au3 and Au4 unit [26]. The CG model [21] predicts that the 1S orbitals of these units serve as the primary building blocks of the overall valence orbitals. Although these models effectively describe the spatial distributions of the valence electrons associated with geometric nonuniformity, the energetics of these orbitals remain beyond their scope. Consequently, it remains elusive how the structural nonuniformity correlates with the electronic structures and the resulting physicochemical properties. For instance, whether the large HOMO–LUMO gaps observed in AuMPCs with seemingly fcc‐like Au cores are attributable to such geometric nonuniformity remains an open question [27, 28, 29].

To address these questions, we investigated the energy levels of the occupied orbitals of Au23 − as a benchmark [22, 23] using anion photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) in the gas phase, thereby eliminating perturbation from the surrounding environment [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. PES and density functional theory (DFT) calculations demonstrated that the valence orbitals of Au23 − can be constructed from energetically distinct 1S orbitals originating from the Au3 and Au4 units. Specifically, valence orbitals with predominant contributions from the 1S orbitals of the Au3 units lie at higher energies than those primarily composed of the 1S orbitals on the Au4 units. Furthermore, we analyzed the electronic structures of the Au10(6e) cores in Au21‐1 0 and Au21‐2 0 via DFT calculations. Finally, we discuss the origin of a significantly larger molar absorption coefficient for the HOMO–LUMO transition of Au23 − compared to that of Au25 − .

Results and Discussion

Electronic Structure of Au23–

Au23 − was chemically synthesized according to a previously reported method [36]. The purity of the sample was confirmed via electrospray‐ionization mass spectrometry and UV‐visible absorption spectroscopy (Figure S1). Gas‐phase PES of Au23 − was performed using a home‐built apparatus (Figure S2) [33, 35]. Details of the procedure are given in the Supporting Information. The blue trace in Figure 1a shows the representative PE spectrum of Au23 − cooled in the liquid nitrogen‐cooled cryogenic ion trap, measured at 213 nm. The laser fluence for photodetachment was maintained at a low level to suppress electron detachment from electronically excited states via two‐photon processes (Au23 − + hν → Au23 − *; Au23 − * + hν → Au23 0 + e–) [37]. The spectral features were sharper than those of Au23 − produced at ambient temperature, indicating effective cooling of the Au23 − anions (Figure S3). The spectral shape remained consistent when recorded at 266 nm (Figure S4). The measured PE spectrum exhibits a sharp band at ∼4.0 eV (Band I) and a broad band ranging from 4.3–5.0 eV (Band II). Band I displays a shoulder‐like structure on the lower binding energy side, suggesting that at least two orbitals contribute to this band. The adiabatic electron affinity (AEA) of Au23 0 was determined as 3.72 eV by linear extrapolation of the spectral onset to the baseline (inset, Figure 1a). The vertical detachment energy (VDE) of Au23 − was estimated to be 3.84 eV from the peak position of the lowest‐energy Gaussian component obtained by deconvolution of Band I into two Gaussian functions (inset, Figure 1a). The spectral profile of Au23 − is distinct from that of [Au25(SC8H17)18]−, which possesses a uniform Au13(8e) core similar to Au25 − (Figure S5) [35]. Specifically, the onset band of [Au25(SC8H17)18]– exhibits two well‐resolved peaks arising from spin‐orbit splitting, whereas no such features are observed for Au23 − (Figure 1a). This spectral discrepancy reflects a fundamental difference in their underlying electronic structures.

The PE spectrum of Au23 − was interpreted based on DFT calculations using the full‐ligand model at the CAM‐B3LYP / LanL2DZ (for Au) and 6–31G(d) (for H, C, S) level, incorporating Grimme's D3BJ dispersion correction. The optimized structure of Au23 − (Figure S6) well reproduced the reported crystal structure (Table 1), where the Au13 core consists of two triangular Au3 units and one bitetrahedral Au7 unit, protected by four Au(CHT)2 and two Au3(CHT)4 linear oligomers [22]. The calculated AEA and VDE were 3.75 and 4.01 eV, respectively, which are in good agreement with the experimental values (3.72 and 3.84 eV, respectively). Furthermore, the electronic density of states (DOS) was calculated based on generalized Koopman's theorem [38, 39] (black trace, Figure 1a). This DOS calculation qualitatively reproduced the measured PE spectrum. The excellent agreement between the experimental and theoretical results substantiates the reliability of the computational approach. A comparison between the experimental spectrum and the calculated DOS enabled the assignment of Band I to electron detachment from HOMO and HOMO‐1, while Band II was assigned to detachment from deeper occupied orbitals (Figure 1a). The calculated energy levels of the HOMO and HOMO‐1 are nearly degenerate (energy gap = 0.25 eV), accounting for the sharpness of Band I in the PE spectrum. The spatial distributions of the Kohn–Sham (KS) orbitals of the LUMO (L), HOMO (H), and HOMO‐n (Hn ; n = 1, 2) of Au23 − are illustrated in Figure 1b. H, H1, and H2 were assigned as the valence orbitals of Au23 − due to the significant contribution from Au(sp) atomic orbitals (> 30%).

Interpretation of the Electronic Structure; Deformed Jellium Model Versus Superatom Network Model

To evaluate whether the Au13 core of Au23 − can be described as a deformed superatom, we examined whether orbitals H, H1, and H2 can be assigned to the superatomic orbitals predicted by the deformed jellium model. When the x, y, and z axes are defined such that the core dimensions decrease in the order of z > y > x (Figure 2a), orbitals H, H1, and H2 would correspond to the 1D z 2, 1P z , and 1P y orbitals, respectively, based on their spatial symmetries and distributions. If the anisotropic Au13 core were modeled using a triaxial‐spheroidal jellium potential, the three 1P orbitals would be expected to split in the energy order of 1P x > 1P y > 1P z (Figure 2b). However, this predicted sequence (1P y > 1P z ) is inconsistent with the energy ordering of H1 and H2 obtained from DFT calculations. This discrepancy indicates that the deformed jellium model is inadequate for describing Au23 − and that an alternative interpretation is required.

The energy ordering of (a) calculated KS orbitals of Au23 − and (b) valence orbitals based on the triaxial‐spheroidal jellium model. Energies in brackets denote the calculated energy levels of each KS orbital.

Therefore, we applied the SAN model to Au23 − to elucidate its electronic structure. In the SAN model, the eight valence electrons in Au23 − are described as four multicenter two‐electron (nc‐2e) bonds localized on the two triangular Au3 units and two vertex‐sharing tetrahedral Au4 units [21, 40]. This assignment is supported by the nc‐2e bonds within the Au13 5+ core, as revealed via the adaptive natural density partition (AdNDP) method [41, 42] (Figure S7). Although the original SAN model was not intended to explicitly account for the energetics of valence orbitals, we rationalized the energy levels of H, H1, and H2 by considering the interactions between the Au3 and Au4 units. Figure 3 shows a schematic diagram for the construction of these orbitals according to an “extended SAN model”. In this model, we postulate that each Au3 or Au4 unit possesses two electrons in its respective 1S orbital (1S(Au3) or 1S(Au4)). The valence orbitals are then formed from these 1S(Au3) and 1S(Au4) orbitals through weak interactions mediated by long Au─Au bonds or shared vertices. Since the confinement volume of an Au3 unit is smaller than that of an Au4 unit, the 1S(Au3) orbitals are higher in energy than the 1S(Au4) orbitals. Therefore, valence orbitals with predominant contributions from the 1S(Au3) are expected to lie at higher energy levels than those primarily composed of 1S(Au4). Within this framework, the in‐phase and antiphase superpositions of 1S(Au3) produce H (1Σg(2Au3)) and H1 (1Σu(2Au3)), respectively. The energy splitting between H and H1 is minimal, as the interaction between the two 1S(Au3) orbitals is weak due to their far spatial separation. The counterintuitive observation that the in‐phase H is higher in energy than antiphase H1 likely arises from destabilizing interaction with the 1Σg(2Au4) orbital (Figure S8). Similarly, antiphase superposition of 1S(Au4) produces H2 (1Σu(2Au4)). Furthermore, low‐lying unoccupied orbitals can be constructed predominantly from the 1P orbitals of Au3 and Au4 units in a similar manner. For example, L is interpreted as a superposition of 1Πu(2Au4), formed by the in‐phase combination of two 1P(Au4) orbitals, and 1Σu(2Au3). These interpretations are supported by Mulliken population analysis (Figure S9), which shows that H and H1 originate mainly from the s and p atomic orbitals of the Au3 units, whereas L and H2 primarily stem from the Au4 units. The successful rationalization validates the assumption that the valence orbitals are localized on these small Au3 or Au4 units. Thus, the extended SAN model provides a unified and consistent explanation for the electronic structure of Au23 − .

The localization of valence orbitals and the near‐degeneracy between H and H1 in Au23 − are likely induced by the nonuniform Au─Au bond lengths within its Au13 core, specifically the shortened bonds that constitute the Au3 and Au4 units. To test this hypothesis, we conducted DFT calculations on a hypothetical isomer Au23(h)– , which features an Au13 core with a similar motif but with uniform Au─Au bond lengths while retaining the same protecting motifs as Au23 − : geometric comparisons were provided in Figure S10. Figure 4 displays the spatial distributions and energy levels of the valence orbitals for Au23(h)– , which can be rationalized by the triaxial‐spheroidal jellium model (Figure 2). Specifically, L, H, H1, and H2 can be assigned to 1D yz , 1D z 2, 1P y , and 1P z orbitals, respectively (Figure 4, right). The electronic structures of the hypothetical Au23(h)– differ significantly from those of Au23 − (Figure 3). The calculated H—H1 energy gap for Au23(h)− (0.85 eV) is substantially larger than that for Au23 − (0.25 eV), and the H1 in Au23(h)− is no longer delocalized over the Au3 units (Figure 4, left). These findings demonstrate that the localized nature of the valence orbitals in Au23 − is directly attributed to the spatial nonuniformity of the Au+ distribution within the Au13 core, characterized by the presence of spatially clustered short Au─Au bonds.

To test the applicability of the extended SAN model, we conducted DFT calculations on other AuMPCs with nonuniform core structures: Au21‐1 0 [21] and Au21‐2 0 [24]. By disregarding the Au─Au bonds longer than those in bulk gold (2.88 Å), the Au10 core of Au21‐1 can be partitioned into Au3 + 2Au4 (Table 1) [21]. Structural optimization was performed on a model system Au21‐1(m) 0, where thiolates were simplified to methanethiolates (SCH3). The optimized structure qualitatively retained the crystal structural motifs (Figure S11) [21]. Based on the spatial distributions and population analysis, L, H, and H1 of Au21‐1(m) 0 are assigned to the 1P(Au4), the antiphase superposition of 1S(Au3) and 1S(Au4), and the antiphase superposition of two 1S(Au4), respectively (Figures 5a, S12). A similar analysis was performed for Au21‐2 0, whose Au10 core can be divided into 2Au3 + Au4. L, H, and H1 of the optimized structure Au21‐2(m) 0 (Figure S13) are assigned to the 1P(Au4), the antiphase superposition of two 1S(Au3) and 1S(Au4), and the antiphase superposition of two 1S(Au3), respectively (Figures 5b, S14). These results demonstrate that occupied orbitals lying at high energy levels are predominantly composed of 1S(Au3). Therefore, the extended SAN model provides a consistent qualitative picture of the relationship between the spatial distributions and the energy levels of the valence orbitals of the nonuniform AuMPCs.

Impacts of Structural Nonuniformity on Optical Properties

Finally, to investigate the impact of electronic localization on physicochemical properties, we compared the molar absorption coefficients (ε) of Au23 − and Au25 − with eight valence electrons. The UV‐visible absorption spectra are shown in Figure 6. The ε values for peak α of Au23 − (570 nm) and peak β of Au25 − (685 nm) in the onset region were determined to be 3.1×104 and 1.2×104 M– 1cm– 1, respectively. The obtained ε value for peak β of Au25 − is consistent with reported values for other [Au25(SR)18]– clusters (e.g., 8.8×103 M−1cm−1 for SR = glutathionate [43]; ∼1×104 M−1cm−1 for SR = SCH2Ph [44]; and ∼2×104 M−1cm−1 for SR = PET, SPh, and 3,5‐dimethylbenzenethiolate [44]). Evidently, the ε value for peak α of Au23 − is significantly larger than that for peak β of Au25 − . However, a direct comparison is not straightforward because the number of optical transitions contributing to peak α [22] and peak β [15, 17] differs. To address this, we compared the oscillator strength (f) of the HOMO–LUMO transition by conducting time‐dependent DFT calculations on [Au23(SCH3)16]– and [Au25(SCH3)18]– as model structures. The calculated f values for individual transitions are represented by vertical lines in Figure S15. The reliability of these theoretical results is supported by the fact that the simulated UV–visible spectra, generated by broadening all transitions with a Gaussian function, effectively reproduced the peak positions observed in the experimental spectra (Figure 6) [29]. The calculated f value for [Au23(SCH3)16]– at 428 nm (0.47) is substantially larger than that for [Au25(SCH3)18]– at 620 nm (0.0046). Consequently, we conclude that the HOMO–LUMO transition in Au23 − possesses a significantly greater oscillator strength than that in Au25 − .

In general, the ε value for an electronic transition is proportional to the square of the transition dipole moment. The transition dipole moment is enhanced when there is a significant change in the spatial distribution of electron density between the initial and final states, provided that substantial orbital overlap is maintained. According to this principle, the large ε value for peak α of Au23 − suggests that H and L of Au23 − satisfy the above condition, likely due to their spatial localization. In contrast, the ε value for peak β of Au25 − is expected to be relatively small because H, H1, and H2 (1P x , y , z ) are distributed around the symmetric center, failing to fulfill the aforementioned condition [10, 17, 29]. Thus, the localized nature of valence orbitals can enhance the photoabsorption.

Conclusion

To investigate the impact of geometric nonuniformity on the electronic structure of monolayer‐protected gold clusters (AuMPCs), we conducted anion photoelectron spectroscopy and density functional theory calculations on [Au23(SC6H11)16]– (Au 23 –) as a representative AuMPC with a nonuniform Au core. The electronic structure of Au 23 – was well described by an “extended superatom network (SAN) model”, in which the valence orbitals are constructed from interacting 1S orbitals localized on Au3 or Au4 units defined by short Au─Au bonds (1S(Au3) or 1S(Au4)). The extended SAN model rationalized the trend that the valence orbitals predominantly constructed from 1S(Au3) lie at higher energies than those primarily composed of 1S(Au4), and was applicable to other AuMPCs possessing nonuniform Au10 cores. These results demonstrate that electronic structures of AuMPCs with nonuniform cores are characterized by the localization of the valence orbitals. In addition, Au 23 – exhibits more intense photoabsorption than [Au25(PET)18]– with a uniform icosahedral Au13 core, due to the localized nature of the HOMO and LUMO. These findings highlight the importance of geometric nonuniformity in discussing the electronic structures and physicochemical properties of AuMPCs.
