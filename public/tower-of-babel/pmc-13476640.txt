Mitochondria as programmable redox rheostats: Advancing from transplantation to bioengineered organelle therapeutics
Authors: Vikky Awasthi, Eric Lalu, Fatma Saaoud, Aasthika Das, Pawan Sharma, Ellyes Adnane, Glenn Gerhard, Suyog Mokashi, Hong Wang, Rihab Bouchareb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondria are the primary arbiters of cellular redox homeostasis, bioenergetic flux, and programmed cell death. Their dysfunction, characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, impaired oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and collapsed membrane potential, is a hallmark of diverse pathologies, including ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurodegeneration, and metabolic syndrome. Over the last decade, mitochondrial transplantation has emerged as a radical therapeutic paradigm for restoring metabolic competence via the exogenous delivery of intact organelles. While early evidence confirms that internalized mitochondria can rescue bioenergetic deficits and suppress apoptotic signaling, the transition to clinical practice is hindered by poor targeting specificity, low delivery kinetics, and post-isolation functional decay. This review highlights a pivotal shift toward mitochondrial engineering, where the organelle is no longer viewed as a static payload but as a programmable therapeutic unit. By integrating principles from synthetic biology, nanomedicine, and biomaterials, researchers are now modifying mitochondria to enhance their ROS-scavenging capacity, stability in the extracellular milieu, and cell-specific uptake. We critically evaluate emerging strategies for organelle modification, including surface functionalization, genetic modulation, and advanced delivery platforms like fusogenic capsules and photothermal nanoblades. Finally, we discuss the redox-dependent mechanisms underlying therapeutic efficacy and the translational hurdles essential for evolving mitochondrial engineering into a precise, scalable clinical reality.

Introduction

Mitochondria are multifunctional organelles that serve as key regulators of cellular energy metabolism, apoptosis, calcium homeostasis, and innate immune signaling. Beyond their classical role as the primary site of ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondria participate in diverse biological processes, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, lipid metabolism, and metabolic reprogramming [1,2]. Traditional therapeutic strategies aimed at improving mitochondrial function have primarily focused on pharmacological modulation, antioxidant therapies, or metabolic interventions. While these approaches may partially restore mitochondrial function, they do not address severe mitochondrial damage or the loss of functional organelles. This limitation has led to the development of mitochondrial transplantation, an emerging therapeutic strategy that involves the delivery of functional mitochondria into injured cells to restore bioenergetic capacity.

Initial demonstrations of mitochondrial transplantation revealed that isolated mitochondria can be internalized by recipient cells through endocytosis or actin-dependent uptake mechanisms [24]. Once internalized, exogenous mitochondria may transiently interact with the host mitochondrial network and improve bioenergetic readouts in selected experimental models; however, stable network integration, long-term persistence, and sustained ATP contribution remain incompletely established [25]. These findings suggest that mitochondria could function as therapeutic organelles capable of restoring cellular metabolic function.

Over the past decade, mitochondrial transplantation has been evaluated across multiple experimental systems, including models of myocardial ischemia [26], neurodegeneration [27], kidney injury [28], and metabolic disease [29]. However, despite these promising preclinical results, several important barriers remain. These include inefficient delivery to target tissues, limited control over mitochondrial uptake, rapid clearance of exogenous mitochondria, and challenges in maintaining mitochondrial integrity during isolation and storage [30].

To overcome these limitations, the field is shifting toward mitochondrial engineering, a practice where mitochondria are intentionally modified to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. This emerging paradigm integrates principles from synthetic biology, nanotechnology, and organelle biology to design mitochondria with improved delivery properties, targeting specificity, and functional resilience.

Rather than viewing mitochondrial transplantation solely as a passive organelle replacement strategy, this review frames engineered mitochondria as programmable bioenergetic and redox-regulating therapeutic units. We organize the field around three interconnected challenges that govern translational success: (i) efficient cellular internalization and intracellular trafficking, (ii) preservation of mitochondrial integrity and metabolic fitness during delivery, and (iii) immunological compatibility and long-term functional integration within recipient tissues. Using this framework, we critically evaluate how emerging engineering strategies, including biomaterial-assisted delivery, peptide functionalization, extracellular vesicle encapsulation, and synthetic membrane systems, seek to overcome the biological limitations of native mitochondrial transplantation and advance organelle therapeutics toward clinical translation (Fig. 2).

Mitochondrial transfer and therapeutic potential

Mitochondrial transfer was initially identified as a natural process mediated by tunneling nanotubes [31], extracellular vesicles [32], and cell fusion, suggesting an intrinsic mechanism for cellular repair. These observations have led to the development of therapeutic mitochondrial transplantation.

Early studies demonstrated that mitochondria, which were isolated from autologous skeletal muscle and subsequently injected into ischemic myocardium, improved cardiac function and reduced infarct size, establishing proof-of-concept that transplanted mitochondria remain viable and are capable of functionally integrating into recipient cells [4].

Subsequent work has extended mitochondrial transplantation across multiple disease contexts, including neurodegeneration, lung and liver injury, kidney disease, and metabolic disorders. Across these models, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to restore oxidative phosphorylation, reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), and enhance cellular survival (Fig. 1). Mechanistically, mitochondria can be internalized through multiple pathways, including macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and actin-dependent processes. While macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and actin-dependent engulfment constitute the principal mechanisms of natural mitochondrial internalization, these pathways are highly cell-type and context-dependent [33]. Professional phagocytes such as macrophages rapidly internalize naked mitochondria through actin-dependent phagocytic processes [34], whereas non-phagocytic cells, including cardiomyocytes [24] and endothelial cells rely predominantly on macropinocytosis and endocytosis [2]. Moreover, ischemic or nutrient-stressed recipient cells exhibit enhanced mitochondrial uptake capacity through AMPK-driven activation of endocytic pathways. Importantly, engineering approaches using fusogenic lipids or cell-penetrating peptides can redirect mitochondrial entry away from conventional endosomal trafficking toward direct membrane fusion, thereby bypassing lysosomal degradation.These mechanistic differences likely influence intracellular trafficking, lysosomal escape, mitochondrial persistence, and ultimately therapeutic efficacy across tissues.

Principles of mitochondrial engineering

Mitochondrial engineering has emerged as a necessary advancement in the development of mitochondrial transplantation by addressing key limitations in delivery efficiency, organelle stability, and functional consistency. These strategies are designed to enhance both mitochondrial fitness and targeting precision.

Surface functionalization involves modifying the outer mitochondrial membrane using ligands, antibodies, targeting peptides, or polymer coatings to improve stability, cellular uptake, and tissue specificity. While these approaches can enhance the organelle's delivery process, their impact on mitochondrial integrity and in vivo functionality remains incompletely understood.

Genetic and functional engineering strategies focus on optimizing mitochondrial performance. Engineered mitochondrial extracellular vesicles (M-EVs) have been shown to improve cardiac function following myocardial infarctions by restoring bioenergetics and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis [32]. Ti-mitoEVs (Tissue-derived mitochondria-rich EVs) also further enhance mitochondrial expansion and promote tissue repair [35].

At the cellular level, studies indicate mitochondrial transfer increases oxidative phosphorylation, ATP production, and the regenerative capacity of stromal cells [5]. Further, the restoration of mitochondrial fitness in mesenchymal stem cells also improved epithelial repair in various lung injury models [6]. These findings highlight a key principle: the therapeutic efficacy of mitochondrial transfer depends on both the efficiency of the transfer and the mitochondrial quality.

Mitochondrial engineering also enables the modulation of oxidative stress responses. Augmentation of mitochondrial regulators such as TFAM enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and ATP synthesis while limiting oxidative damage. In parallel, cell-penetrating peptide–mediated delivery improves mitochondrial retention and persistence. But increased ROS generation in certain contexts does raise important safety considerations [7].

Collectively, these approaches enable the modulation of mitochondrial bioenergetics, oxidative stress resistance, biogenesis, and metabolic flexibility, thereby positioning engineered mitochondria as programmable metabolic units within recipient cells.

Although these engineering approaches have demonstrated encouraging proof-of-principle results, most evidence remains derived from in vitro systems or small-animal models. Significant questions remain regarding mitochondrial persistence, intracellular fate, scalability, and reproducibility. Consequently, mitochondrial engineering should currently be viewed as an emerging translational platform rather than an established therapeutic modality.

Delivery technologies for mitochondrial therapeutics

Delivery remains the principal bottleneck in mitochondrial therapeutics. Current approaches, including direct injection [4], vesicle-based delivery [32], and nanocarrier systems [36], improve mitochondrial stability and biodistribution but lack precise tissue targeting and controlled release. Future advances will require integration of delivery platforms with mitochondrial engineering to achieve spatiotemporal control of organelle function (Fig. 2).

Direct injection

Direct mitochondrial delivery has demonstrated efficacy across multiple preclinical models. In cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury, donor mitochondria, typically isolated from adult cardiac fibroblasts, were injected into the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery and were shown to improve myocardial recovery [4]. Similarly, intradermal injection of skeletal muscle–derived mitochondria enhanced healing in chronic pressure wounds [8]. In musculoskeletal applications, mitochondria from umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells have been transferred to tenocytes [37], Promoting tendon repair. In peripheral nerve injury models [9]. Local injection of mitochondria into the distal segment of crushed sciatic nerves has been shown to support functional recovery.

These studies collectively demonstrate that locally delivered mitochondria are internalized by recipient cells, particularly in highly vascularized tissues such as the myocardium, with minimal efflux following injection, suggesting efficient retention and cellular uptake.

Despite its technical simplicity and translational appeal, direct mitochondrial injection remains limited by several factors, including rapid extracellular degradation, limited tissue penetration, and heterogeneous distribution within the target organ. These barriers highlight the need for improved delivery strategies to enhance mitochondrial stability, targeting, and bioavailability in vivo.

Magnetomito transfer

Developed by Macheiner et al., the Magnetomito transfer technique leverages magnetic guidance to enhance mitochondrial delivery and cellular uptake. This approach uses mitochondria isolated with anti-TOM22 magnetic beads, enabling selective enrichment of intact organelles. The isolated mitochondria, derived from the human fetal lung fibroblast cell line (MRC-5), were further complexed with transfection reagents (e.g., Lipofectamine) and exposed to an external magnetic field to promote cellular internalization [10].

Application of a magnetic field increases local mitochondrial concentration and clustering at the cell surface, thereby enhancing uptake efficiency relative to passive delivery methods. This strategy is designed to enrich autologous stem cells with healthy allogeneic mitochondria before transplantation, thereby improving their cellular bioenergetics and regenerative capacity [10].

Despite the increased local concentration and clustering advantages provided by Magnetomito transfer, severe translational challenges regarding nanotoxicity and biophysical integrity must be addressed. Chemically, anti-TOM22 or iron-oxide nanoparticles complexed with the organelle require stringent monitoring to ensure they do not induce long-term cytotoxicity. Upon internalization, the persistence of non-biodegradable magnetic materials risks interfering with the host cell's lysosomal sorting machinery, impairing downstream autophagy and degrading cellular viability over extended periods.

Electronically and mechanically, the application of external magnetic fields introduces sub-cellular shear stress. If the magnetic flux density or gradient field is too intense, the directional pull can mechanically distort the delicate double-membrane architecture of the isolated mitochondria. Such localized mechanical strain can breach outer membrane integrity, precipitating the premature leakage of intermembrane space proteins, such as cytochrome c, into the cytosol, thereby paradoxically initiating apoptotic cascades within the very recipient cells targeted for rescue [10].

Furthermore, clinical translation may be complicated by the need to precisely control magnetic field strength and exposure duration. Excessive magnetic forces may generate localized shear stress capable of disrupting mitochondrial membrane integrity, whereas insufficient field strength may limit delivery efficiency. These competing requirements highlight the need for careful optimization before clinical implementation.

Nanocarrier systems

Another promising strategy involves the use of nanocarriers, such as gelatin nanospheres, that can be electrostatically attached to mitochondria. The gelatin is biodegradable and biologically inert. In the preparation of the cationized gelatin nanospheres (cGNS), the carboxyl groups of gelatin were chemically converted through the introduction of amino groups to facilitate gelatinous cationization. The method used for mitochondrial transplantation was direct, and internalization was observed after 6 h. This modification enhances mitochondrial uptake into target cells, such as cardiomyocytes, and has been shown to support functional ATP synthesis, suggesting improved mitochondrial efficacy [36].

FluidFM technology (fluidic force microscopy)

In line with direct injection, the FluidFM approach combines the precision of atomic force microscopy (AFM) with optical microscopy and nanofluidics to enable the manipulation of organelles at subcellular resolution [11]. This technique employs custom-built probes with hollow, cylindrical tips designed to facilitate minimally invasive cell entry while preserving cell viability.

FluidFM enables both the extraction and injection of organelles into living cells under real-time optical guidance. The system uses probes with finely tuned aperture sizes and controlled pressure gradients to aspirate or deliver intracellular contents with high precision. The general workflow involves positioning the probe over a defined subcellular region, followed by membrane insertion via AFM force spectroscopy. Subsequently, intracellular material can be extracted via negative pressure or delivered via positive pressure [11].

This method has been successfully applied to mitochondrial transplantation, where isolated mitochondria can be directly introduced into recipient cells and subsequently integrated into the endogenous mitochondrial network. Notably, single-organelle transfer using FluidFM preserves mitochondrial viability more effectively than bulk transplantation approaches, highlighting its utility for mechanistic studies and precision bioengineering [11].

However, several limitations remain. FluidFM and related nanopipette-based extraction techniques often involve non-specific aspiration of cytoplasmic fluid, which may compromise the selectivity and reproducibility of trials. In addition, throughput is inherently low, which restricts the scalability for therapeutic applications. Complementary technologies, like nanotweezers, for example, have been developed to enable single-organelle manipulation by trapping and extracting individual mitochondria from sources such as primary rodent hippocampal neurons. The addition of these technologies offers increased specificity but similarly limited throughput [11]Overall, FluidFM provides unmatched spatial precision and control for mitochondrial transfer at the single-organelle level; its current utility remains largely confined to experimental and mechanistic studies rather than large-scale therapeutic deployment.

Micropipette-based technologies

Micropipette-based technologies have emerged as a powerful platform for organelle manipulation and transplantation in living cells [22]. These approaches enable the extraction and injection of organelles, such as mitochondria, using advanced control mechanisms, like dielectrophoretic trapping and electrowetting, to help preserve cell viability throughout the procedure. A notable innovation within this category is the photothermal nanoblade, which enables highly targeted delivery of isolated mitochondria into recipient cells. This technique uses a laser-induced vapor bubble at the pipette tip to transiently disrupt the plasma membrane, thereby allowing cytoplasmic entry with minimal mechanical damage. Using this approach, mitochondrial transfer into mtDNA-depleted (ρ0) cells has been achieved with an efficiency of approximately 2%. Despite its precision and reduced cellular damage, the photothermal nanoblade, like other micropipette-based systems, remains limited by low throughput and technical complexity, which constrain its scalability for therapeutic applications [38].

Vesicle-based delivery

Mitochondria-containing EVs have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy across multiple disease models. In ischemic stroke and ischemic myocardium [32]. Mitochondria-enriched EV populations, particularly the 20K EV fraction, have been shown to contain mitochondrial proteins and functionally active mitochondria. Silva et al. reported that the transfer of 20K EV-associated mitochondria into pulmonary endothelial cells resulted in a ∼3-fold increase in ATP production and a ∼6-fold improvement in mitochondrial respiration [30].

Similarly, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)–derived mitochondrial EVs have been shown to improve osteoarthritis outcomes by restoring mitochondrial function and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis in chondrocytes. Mechanistically, EV-mediated mitochondrial release from MSCs is regulated by a calcium-dependent pathway involving CD38 and inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) signaling (CD38/IP3R/Ca2+ axis [13]).

Importantly, mitochondrial cargo can be sourced from a variety of donor cell types, including induced pluripotent stem cells, derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), cardiac progenitor cells (CPCs), and iPSC-derived endothelial cells (iPSC-ECs [12]). This enables flexible platform designs tailored to specific therapeutic applications.

An additional approach involves erythrocyte membrane-based encapsulation [39], a process which enables mitochondria to be cloaked within red blood cell-derived vesicles. This method was developed to improve biocompatibility, prolong circulation time, and reduce immune recognition.

But despite these advantages, key challenges remain, including vesicle heterogeneity, limited control over mitochondrial loading efficiency, and incomplete understanding of targeting specificity and in vivo biodistribution. Addressing these limitations will be essential to advance vesicle-mediated mitochondrial delivery toward clinical translation.

Membrane translocation methods

Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), like Pep-1, transiently permeabilize the plasma membrane to facilitate intracellular cargo entry, and membrane fusion approaches that mimic viral entry mechanisms. Such strategies aim to promote direct cytosolic delivery of transplanted mitochondria, thereby bypassing endocytic pathways that may limit mitochondrial integrity and functional integration [40]This strategy led to successful incorporation of mitochondria to treat Parkinson's disease in the rat model [12].

Other approaches to enhance delivery

Mitochondrial transplantation faces several biological and technical barriers that directly influence delivery strategies. Successful integration into the recipient cell requires coordinated uptake, intracellular trafficking, and functional incorporation into the endogenous mitochondrial network. In addition, immunogenicity remains a critical challenge as donor mitochondria can elicit immune responses, which necessitate controlled delivery and integration. Genetic modulation has emerged as a strategy for enhancing mitochondrial transfer efficiency.

For example, PINK1(PTEN-induced kinase 1) knockout has been shown to increase intercellular mitochondrial movement [41], potentially through metabolic reprogramming and altered mitophagy dynamics. Tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), actin-based intercellular structures, further facilitate mitochondrial exchange between cells, particularly under stress conditions. Key regulators such as Miro1(Mitochondrial Rho GTPase 1) and TNFAIP2(Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha-Induced Protein) promote TNT formation and enhance transfer efficiency, thereby highlighting their therapeutic potential. At the level of mitochondrial quality control, PINK1 and Parkin play central roles in regulating mitophagy and maintaining mitochondrial integrity [14]. Recent evidence suggests that combining TNT-mediated transfer with modulation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway may enhance mitochondrial delivery efficiency [15]; however, the complexity of these interactions requires further investigation for clinical translation.

Additional genetic strategies, such as overexpression of mitochondrial fusion proteins Mfn1/2 (Mitofusin1/2), have been shown to improve mitochondrial integration and functional recovery following transplantation [16]. Pharmacological approaches may further complement these strategies. For instance, doxycycline has been shown to attenuate oxidative stress–induced mitochondrial fragmentation and stabilize mitochondrial structure. Although doxycycline can inhibit mitochondrial ribosomal activity and complex I function, its ability to induce metabolic rewiring toward fatty acid oxidation may be leveraged to optimize donor or recipient cell readiness for transplantation. However, precise control of dosing and timing is essential to avoid detrimental effects on mitochondrial function [20,42].

Finally, the development of advanced delivery platforms remains critical for overcoming the current limitations. The efficiency of mitochondrial transplantation is strongly influenced by isolation methods and the degree of stress imposed on mitochondria during processing. Emerging technologies, such as fusogenic mitochondrial capsules (FMCs), comprising a neutral lipid, a cationic lipid, and an aromatic lipid, have been successfully used in the treatment of osteoarthritis [43]. Another innovative system is a pressure-driven tool termed as MitoPunch systems [21]This strategy has been used to transfer isolated mitochondria in several cell types.

Biomaterial-assisted delivery

Artificial membranes

Biomaterial scaffolds and hydrogels are being developed to improve mitochondrial retention at sites of injury. These materials can stabilize mitochondria and provide the controlled release of mitochondrial cargo [17,44,45].Artificial membrane systems have been developed to enhance mitochondrial delivery by improving stability, cellular uptake, and intracellular targeting. Cationic lipids, such as DOTAP and DOPE, are commonly used to facilitate mitochondrial transfer by promoting electrostatic interactions with cellular membranes and enhancing fusion efficiency [18]. The use of DOTAP and DOPE encapsulated mitochondria show efficient entry into mammalian cells in vitro. However, the use of this encapsulated mitochondria in vivo needs to be investigated. In addition, double-layered membrane formulations have been employed to protect mitochondria from extracellular stress and degradation during transplantation. Synthetic delivery platforms, such as the MITO-Porter [19] and DF-MITO-Porter systems have demonstrated significant potential for mitochondrial targeting, particularly for mitochondrial delivery, by exploiting membrane fusion–based mechanisms to enable efficient intracellular transport [23]. The MITO-Porter surface carries octa-arginine (R8) moieties at high density, targets the plasma membrane, and is internalized by micropinocytosis. In the second step, it binds to the mitochondrial membrane utilizing the electrostatic interactions, leading to its fusion with mitochondria. Mitochondrial transplantation using this strategy has demonstrated encouraging in vitro efficacy; however, validation in animal models and assessment of long-term safety remain necessary before clinical translation can be considered.

Non-membrane-based mitochondria coating

Artificial membrane coating has very limited practical application in vivo. Non-membrane-based coating includes coating mitochondria using cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), such as Pep-1 and TAT [46], facilitates membrane translocation and intracellular delivery of mitochondrial cargo. More specialized designs, such as MTP3, which is a mitochondria-targeting peptide composed of three positively charged arginine residues with alternating D- and L-naphthylalanines, have demonstrated high mitochondrial specificity. These have been discussed in the next section.

In parallel, a broad spectrum of carrier systems has been developed to protect mitochondria during transfer and improve their delivery efficiency. These include liposomes, extracellular vesicles, and nanoparticle-based platforms, all of which have demonstrated promise in various applications, such as neurodegenerative diseases. Chemical modification strategies, like polyethylene glycol (PEG) conjugation and lipofection-based encapsulation, further enhance mitochondrial stability, circulation time, and cellular uptake. For example, biotinylated peptide–PEG–DSPE conjugates have been used to improve mitochondrial targeting and internalization [47].

Lipophilic cations, such as triphenylphosphonium (TPP+) [48], are widely employed to facilitate mitochondrial membrane interactions that are driven by the membrane potential. However, newer platforms are being developed to address limitations associated with mitochondrial depolarization. Notably, fluorinated mitochondria-targeting lipid nanoparticles (F-M-LNPs) represent a significant advancement, as their ionizable fluorinated lipids enable efficient mitochondrial delivery [49] even amidst conditions of reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). These systems also exhibit enhanced chemical stability, improved intracellular trafficking, and the capacity for real-time tracking of the process, thereby allowing noninvasive monitoring of therapeutic outcomes [48].

Additional nanocarrier platforms, including polymeric and inorganic systems, offer further versatility. For instance, molybdenum disulfide nanoflowers have been shown to enhance mitochondrial transfer efficiency, resulting in increased mitochondrial mass and improved functional integration in recipient cells [48]. Other innovative delivery systems include fusogenic liposomes, synaptosomes [50], metal–organic framework (MOF) biomimetic nanoparticles [51], mitochondria–graphene quantum dots (Mt-GQDs) [52], and cationic liposomes [53], All of which are designed to improve mitochondrial targeting, uptake, and bioavailability through distinct physicochemical mechanisms.

Despite these advances, key challenges remain, including the optimization of delivery efficiency, control of intracellular targeting specificity, and the maintenance of long-term mitochondrial functionality following transfer. Addressing these barriers will be critical for the translation of peptide- and nanocarrier-based mitochondrial delivery systems into clinically viable therapies.

Peptide coating mitochondria for targeted transplantation

Peptide-functionalized mitochondrial coatings have emerged as a promising strategy to enhance tissue-specific targeting, cellular uptake, and therapeutic efficacy. These approaches typically combine targeting peptides with lipophilic moieties or polymeric linkers to enable stable mitochondrial binding and controlled delivery [54].

For example, the ischemia-targeting peptide CSTSMLKAC (PEP), identified via phage display, exhibits high selectivity for ischemic myocardial tissue. When conjugated to triphenylphosphonium (TPP+), a mitochondria-targeting lipophilic cation, it forms a PEP–TPP–mitochondria complex that facilitates both mitochondrial binding and targeted delivery [55]. Importantly, the ischemia-responsive properties of PEP promote selective accumulation in injured cardiac tissue. This is followed by peptide dissociation, which enables efficient mitochondrial internalization by cardiomyocytes or transfer via endothelial cells.

Similarly, injury-targeting peptides such as CAQK (Cys-Ala-Gln-Lys) have been used to direct mitochondria to sites of tissue damage [56], including spinal cord injury, by enhancing affinity for extracellular matrix components and infiltrating immune cells (e.g., macrophages).

Advanced surface functionalization strategies further incorporate polymer–lipid conjugates, such as DSPE–PEG [47], which provide a versatile platform for peptide attachment (e.g., VCAM-1–binding or collagen-binding peptides) and enhance mitochondrial stability, circulation time, and targeting to inflamed or dysfunctional endothelium. In addition, biomaterial-assisted delivery systems, including thermosensitive hydrogels (e.g., PELA-doped PLGA with embedded SPIONs), enable localized and sustained mitochondrial release, thus improving retention and therapeutic efficacy at injury sites [57]. Peptide-based coating strategies offer a modular and tunable approach to mitochondrial targeting; however, challenges remain in optimizing peptide stability, targeting specificity, and controlled release kinetics for clinical translation.

Applications of engineered mitochondrial therapeutics

Engineered mitochondrial therapeutics and mitochondrial transplantation have rapidly emerged as innovative strategies for the treatment of diseases driven by mitochondrial dysfunction, bioenergetic collapse, oxidative stress, and defective cellular metabolism. Because mitochondria regulate ATP production, redox homeostasis, calcium signaling, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses, mitochondrial impairment contributes directly to the pathogenesis of numerous cardiovascular, renal, neurodegenerative, metabolic, and inflammatory disorders. Consequently, therapeutic approaches aimed at restoring mitochondrial integrity have gained substantial translational interest as a means to rescue cellular function at its metabolic core.

Unlike conventional pharmacological therapies that primarily target downstream signaling pathways, engineered mitochondrial therapies seek to directly replace or repair dysfunctional organelles, thereby restoring oxidative phosphorylation, reducing excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, improving mitochondrial quality control, and re-establishing cellular metabolic homeostasis. Advances in mitochondrial isolation, preservation, targeted delivery systems, and organelle engineering, including peptide-functionalized mitochondria, fusogenic nanocarriers, extracellular vesicle encapsulation, and mitochondria-targeted biomaterials, have significantly expanded the feasibility and therapeutic potential of these approaches. Importantly, engineered delivery platforms also improve mitochondrial stability, cellular uptake, tissue specificity, and resistance to lysosomal degradation, overcoming many of the limitations associated with native mitochondrial transplantation.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of mitochondrial transplantation across multiple disease models, including myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [58], heart failure, acute kidney injury, neurodegenerative diseases. Skeletal muscle disorders, liver injury, and inflammatory conditions. In cardiovascular disease, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to restore ATP production, reduce infarct size, improve contractile function, and attenuate cardiomyocyte apoptosis following ischemic injury. Similarly, in acute kidney injury, exogenous mitochondrial delivery improves tubular bioenergetics, reduces oxidative stress, and accelerates renal recovery by restoring proximal tubular cell metabolism. Emerging evidence further suggests that engineered mitochondrial therapies may modulate immune responses, cellular senescence, fibrosis, and epigenetic remodeling, extending their therapeutic relevance beyond bioenergetic rescue alone.

Collectively, these findings provide encouraging proof-of-concept evidence that mitochondrial transplantation may improve metabolic function in selected experimental settings. However, these observations remain largely confined to preclinical models, and the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and translational applicability of mitochondrial transplantation in metabolic diseases have yet to be established in clinical studies.

Cardiovascular disease

Cardiovascular tissues are highly dependent on mitochondrial ATP production which makes them particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial transplantation (MT) has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in myocardial ischemia and heart failure by restoring bioenergetics, reducing apoptosis, and improving contractile function.

The first clinical application of mitochondrial autotransplantation was reported in pediatric patients requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation following an ischemic injury. Autologous mitochondria isolated from skeletal muscle were directly injected into the ischemic myocardium, resulting in encouraging signals of cardiac recovery in a small cohort of patients. Importantly, these early studies primarily demonstrate procedural feasibility and preliminary safety rather than definitive therapeutic efficacy [59]. This study provided early clinical evidence supporting the feasibility and safety of mitochondrial-based therapies.

Preclinical studies have further reinforced these findings. In rodent models of myocardial infarction, mitochondria derived from mesenchymal stem cells and encapsulated in phosphatidylserine-modified vesicles have demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake, improved cardiac function in preclinical models, a reduction in infarct size, and a preservation of ventricular structure [59]. In parallel, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to modulate immune responses. For example, mitochondria-loaded macrophages improved cardiac repair after an infarction by reducing fibrosis and apoptosis while also promoting angiogenesis [60].

Innovative delivery approaches are also emerging. Oral mitochondrial transplantation using nitric oxide-driven nanomotor systems enables targeted delivery into injured myocardium, resulting in improved tissue retention and restoration of cardiac function in ischemic models [61]. In large animal studies, including pig models of right ventricular hypertrophy, direct injection of autologous mitochondria preserved contractility and reduced pathological remodeling of the tissue [62]. Together, these studies highlight mitochondrial therapy as a multifaceted strategy that targets energy failure, inflammation, and structural remodeling in cardiovascular disease.

Neurodegenerative diseases

Neurons rely heavily on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation which renders them particularly susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial transplantation has therefore been explored as a therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD).

In PD models, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to reduce neuronal apoptosis, decrease aggregation of phosphorylated α-synuclein, and improve motor and behavioral outcomes in vivo [63]. Additional studies using mitochondria derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, including reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and improved neuronal survival [64].

In Alzheimer's disease models, intravenous administration of mitochondria demonstrated reduced amyloid-β accumulation, improved cognitive performance, and enhanced spatial memory [65]. Similarly, in stroke models, mitochondrial delivery restored ATP production, enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduced ROS levels, leading to improved cognitive outcomes [66].

Beyond direct neuronal effects, mitochondrial transplantation appears to modulate neuroinflammation, suggesting a dual mechanism involving metabolic restoration and immune regulation. These findings position mitochondrial therapy as a promising approach for neurodegenerative disorders characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress.

Kidney injury

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a central feature of both acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease, including diabetic nephropathy. Renal tubular epithelial cells are highly metabolically active and depend on intact mitochondrial function, rendering them particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial damage.

In rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI models, intra-arterial delivery of mitochondria isolated from skeletal muscle improved renal function, reduced serum creatinine and urea nitrogen levels, and enhanced antioxidant defenses [41]. In ischemia-reperfusion injury, mitochondrial transplantation restored ATP production, decreased ROS levels, and reduced tubular injury in both in vitro and ex vivo kidney models [28].

Importantly, mitochondrial therapy has also shown benefits in chronic kidney disease. In diabetic nephropathy models, transplantation of mitochondria derived from mesenchymal stem cells improved tubular structure, reduced apoptosis, and suppressed oxidative stress through modulation of mitochondrial regulatory pathways [67].

These findings suggest that mitochondrial transplantation not only mitigates acute injury but may also slow disease progression and fibrosis, thereby highlighting its strong translational potential in renal disease.

Lung diseases

Engineered mitochondrial therapeutics are emerging as a promising strategy in lung disease because mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central driver of epithelial injury, oxidative stress, inflammatory amplification, vascular remodeling, and aberrant tissue repair across both acute and chronic pulmonary disorders [68]. In chronic lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), mitochondrial abnormalities contribute to impaired bioenergetics, defective mitophagy, mitochondrial DNA damage, and excess generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, all of which promote persistent injury responses and maladaptive repair [68]. In acute lung injury, damaged mitochondria can also act as a source of danger-associated molecular patterns, including mitochondrial DNA, thereby intensifying pulmonary inflammation and vascular dysfunction [69]. These mechanistic insights have driven interest in therapeutic approaches designed either to replace injured mitochondria or to restore mitochondrial function. Preclinical studies have shown that mitochondrial transplantation can improve lung viability and mechanics after ischemia-reperfusion injury and attenuate lipopolysaccharide-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [70,71]. In experimental pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), viable mitochondrial delivery has also been reported to improve right ventricular performance and pulmonary artery remodeling [72]. Importantly, asthma is increasingly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction as well, particularly through altered epithelial bioenergetics, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and remodeling-associated signaling [73,74]. In an allergic asthma model, connexin 43-mediated mitochondrial transfer from induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal stem cells reduced T helper 2 cytokine responses, improved epithelial mitochondrial function, and alleviated airway inflammation, providing direct proof-of-concept for engineered mitochondrial therapy in asthma [75]. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant strategies have likewise shown anti-remodeling effects in murine asthma models by reducing transforming growth factor-β-associated collagen production, although that report should be interpreted cautiously because an Expression of Concern was later issued for the article [73]. Collectively, these findings support engineered mitochondrial therapeutics as a mechanistically distinct and potentially disease-modifying platform for lung disorders, including asthma, although major translational barriers remain, particularly lung-specific delivery, durability of benefit, and long-term safety [68,[70], [71], [72], [73]].

Metabolic diseases

Metabolic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) are characterized by systemic mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy metabolism. Mitochondrial transplantation has therefore been explored as a strategy to restore metabolic homeostasis.

In high-fat diet models, systemic mitochondrial administration increased energy expenditure, enhanced fatty acid oxidation, improved glucose tolerance, and reduced ectopic lipid accumulation [68]. In liver disease models, mitochondrial transplantation reduced hepatocellular injury and restored metabolic function in both acute and chronic settings [69].

Mitochondrial therapy has also demonstrated encouraging therapeutic effects in experimental models of genetic mitochondrial disorders. In a mouse model of Leigh syndrome, administration of wild-type mitochondria improved survival, enhanced neurological function, and increased energy metabolism [70]. Additionally, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to restore calcium homeostasis and improve contractile function in cardiomyopathy models associated with mitochondrial defects [71].

Collectively, these studies provide proof-of-concept that mitochondrial transplantation may improve metabolic function in selected experimental settings. However, these findings remain largely confined to preclinical models, and the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and translational applicability of mitochondrial transplantation in metabolic diseases have yet to be established in clinical studies.

Ex vivo mitochondrial transfer into immune cells

The immune system is an attractive target for mitochondrial transplantation approaches due to its accessibility and clinical importance. Standardized processes for isolating immune cells for ex vivo manipulation are already widely used in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, thus may be ready for integration with mitochondrial transplantation. Mitochondria play a significant role in immune cell signaling and metabolism. The decline and dysregulation of immune function with aging and the phenomenon of T cell exhaustion in cell-based immunotherapies are two applications for which mitochondrial transplantation may be therapeutically effective. For example, polymorphonuclear (neutrophil) cells have been shown to internalize extracellular vesicles derived from platelets whose cargo contains mitochondria that resulted in buffering of metabolic capacity and immune effectiveness [76]. Through transplantation of mitochondria, the effectiveness of CAR-T cell and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte immunotherapy has been enhanced by increasing metabolic capacity and decreasing exhaustion and susceptibility to apoptosis [77]. The mitochondria that were transferred into T cells were viable for several weeks or more, suggesting that this approach may have promise for therapeutic applications. Exogenous mitochondria transferred into the T cells of aged mice resulted in improvements in signaling, proliferation, and metabolism [78]. Mitochondria isolated from human placenta incubated with cell cultures of human hematopoietic stem cells were acquired by the cells and resulted in improvements in oxygen consumption rate and preservation of colony formation [79]. This approach was then used to transfer mitochondria into hematopoietic stem cells of patients harboring single large mitochondrial DNA deletions related to Kearns-Sayre and Pearson syndromes in a clinical trial. The mitochondrial DNA and ATP content of peripheral blood cells increased in all 6 patients with clinical improvement, including the body weight of 5 of 6 patients [80]. These data suggest that ex vivo mitochondrial transfer is a general method that may have clinical applications across a wide variety of disorders.

Standalone safety considerations and immunological risks

Safety considerations are not merely an ancillary concern but represent a central determinant of translational success for mitochondrial therapeutics. Despite encouraging preclinical and early clinical results, several critical barriers must be addressed before mitochondrial therapeutics can be widely implemented.

Mitochondrial quality control is paramount as isolation and handling of the organelle must preserve its membrane potential, respiratory capacity, and ultrastructural integrity; even minor perturbations can compromise the translation's therapeutic efficacy.

Storage and preservation remain major constraints. Mitochondria are highly susceptible to oxidative damage and functional loss during freeze–thaw cycles, underscoring the need for optimized cryopreservation strategies and validated cryoprotective formulations to enable scalable application. The physical trauma of isolation frequently leads to the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). Persistent mPTP opening uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, collapses the mitochondrial membrane potential, and shifts the organelle from an ATP synthase into an ATP hydrolase. Compromised, uncoupled donor mitochondria undergo dramatic spikes in reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Rather than acting as a redox rheostat to buffer stress, damaged transplants can overload host antioxidant defenses, accelerating lipid peroxidation, triggering host cell mPTP opening, and inducing apoptotic or necroptotic cascades.

Immunogenicity also represents a key concern. While autologous mitochondria are generally well tolerated, mitochondrial DNA and damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) can activate innate immune pathways, potentially eliciting inflammatory responses. Mitochondria retain evolutionary signatures of their alphaproteobacterial ancestry. When donor organelles are damaged during isolation or processing, they spill their inner contents, acting as potent Danger-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs). Extracellular mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), rich in unmethylated CpG motifs, directly engages Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and the cGAS-STING pathway in host immune cells, provoking a severe sterile inflammatory response and acute neutrophil activation. While autologous sourcing avoids adaptive rejection, allogeneic transplantation risks triggering both immediate complement activation and long-term adaptive immune elimination due to surface protein mismatches, generating circulating anti-mitochondrial antibodies.

Finally, manufacturing and regulatory hurdles must be addressed as well. The field lacks standardized protocols for isolation, potency assessment, and delivery. It is also without scalable, GMP-compliant production pipelines and clear regulatory guidance. Addressing these challenges will be essential to ensuring reproducibility, safety, and a successful clinical translation.

Conclusion

Mitochondrial transplantation is rapidly evolving from a proof-of-concept intervention into a foundational paradigm for organelle-based therapeutics. Beyond simply replacing dysfunctional mitochondria, emerging strategies in mitochondrial engineering now enable programmable control over bioenergetics, signaling, and cellular fate. This shift reframes mitochondria from passive energy producers to active, designable therapeutic units capable of reprogramming diseased cellular systems.

The convergence of synthetic biology, biomaterials science, and precision delivery technologies is poised to overcome longstanding barriers in targeting, immune compatibility, and scalability. In this context, engineered mitochondria can be conceptualized as “living nanotherapeutics” that are capable of sensing microenvironmental cues, adapting to cellular stress, and restoring metabolic homeostasis in a dynamic and sustained manner.

Realizing this vision will require the establishment of standardized manufacturing pipelines, robust quality control metrics, and a deeper mechanistic understanding of organelle–host interactions in vivo. However, the trajectory of mitochondrial therapeutics is clear: they are positioned to redefine treatment strategies across a broad spectrum of diseases, including cardiovascular and renal disorders, cancer, and neurodegeneration. As these technologies mature, the ability to engineer and deploy functional organelles may mark a paradigm shift in medicine, transitioning from molecular therapies to an era of programmable, subcellular interventions.

Despite remarkable progress, fundamental questions regarding mitochondrial persistence, intracellular fate, immunological compatibility, manufacturing standardization, and long-term safety remain unresolved. Addressing these challenges will determine whether mitochondrial engineering evolves from a promising experimental platform into a clinically transformative therapeutic modality.
