Social Support and Hospital Discharge Readiness in CHD Patients: Mediating Roles of Self-Efficacy and Adherence
Authors: Dandan Wang, Na Yang, Cheng Wei, Jingwen Wang, Liping Yuan, Ruixiang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the associations among social support, self-efficacy, medication adherence, and discharge readiness in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD), specifically examining the mediating roles of self-efficacy and medication adherence.MethodsParticipants included 463 patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) recruited from a tertiary hospital in Wuhu City (February–June 2025) via convenience sampling. To assess the key variables, we employed the Oslo-3 Social Support Scale, the GSES, the MMAS-81 [1MMAS® 2006 used with permission www.moriskyscale.com], and the RHDS. Data analysis was performed in SPSS 26.0 for descriptive statistics, followed by the PROCESS macro (version 3.5) to examine the hypothesized mediation pathways.ResultsAmong the 463 participants with valid responses, the mean score for readiness for hospital discharge was 66.75±23.29. Correlation analysis indicated that social support, self-efficacy, and medication adherence were each significantly and positively associated with discharge readiness (p <0.050). Mediation analysis revealed that social support showed a significant and positive association with self-efficacy (В=1.425, p <0.001). The Bootstrap test confirmed a significant indirect relationship between social support and discharge readiness through the mediating role of self-efficacy (95% CI: [0.262,1.187]). While the moderating effect of medication adherence on the “self-efficacy→discharge readiness” pathway was not statistically significant (p= 0.121), medication adherence itself showed a significant and direct positive association with readiness for hospital discharge (В=4.039, p <0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, our findings underscore that social support exerts a significant indirect effect on readiness for hospital discharge via the mediating role of self-efficacy. Although the moderating effect of medication adherence was not supported in this specific pathway, it emerged as a critical and independent factor related to discharge readiness. Consequently, clinical nursing interventions should prioritize a multifaceted approach: mobilizing social support systems, bolstering patients’ psychological self-efficacy, and rigorously reinforcing medication adherence. Such targeted strategies are beneficial to support a safe, high-quality transition from hospital to home for patients with coronary heart disease.Keywords: coronary heart disease, readiness for hospital discharge, social support, self-efficacy, medication adherence, mediating effect

Coronary heart disease (CHD), characterized by high morbidity, disability, and mortality rates, remains the leading cause of cardiovascular death in China and has emerged as a critical global public health challenge.1 Studies have indicated that the global prevalence of coronary heart disease (CHD) has exceeded 200 million, with a notably increasing trend among younger populations.2 In alignment with the implementation of the “Healthy China Strategy” and the continuous promotion of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) and rapid rehabilitation concepts,3 the clinical management paradigm for CHD is undergoing a strategic shift from “long-term in-hospital rehabilitation” to “early discharge and home-based transition. ”Consequently, this transition has led to a significant reduction in the average length of hospital stay for patients with CHD. However, achieving clinical stabilization of physical indicators does not necessarily equate to comprehensive physical and psychological recovery. Insufficiently prepared discharge transitions can lead to severe clinical consequences, including high rates of unplanned readmissions, post-discharge complications, and increased psychological distress.4,5 This shift in the healthcare paradigm signifies that the primary focus of cardiac rehabilitation has transitioned significantly toward home and community-based settings.6 Given that the quality of transitional care post-discharge directly determines patients’ long-term clinical outcomes, ensuring a safe and seamless transition from hospital to home has become a paramount priority in contemporary nursing practice.5

Readiness for Hospital Discharge (RHDS) serves as a pivotal indicator for assessing the safety of the transition from hospital to home, a phase whose clinical significance is paramount.7 Rooted in Social Cognitive Theory, this study posits that an individual’s transition is governed by the interaction between environmental resources and internal cognitions. Although existing literature8–11 has identified individual associations among social support, self-efficacy, medication adherence, and RHDS, integrated empirical evidence regarding the intrinsic pathways and mechanisms linking these four factors remains scant. Specifically, self-efficacy—the individual’s confidence in their ability to manage health-related tasks—is often a cognitive bridge between environmental support and health outcomes, while medication adherence—the extent to which a patient follows prescribed drug regimens—acts as a critical behavioral boundary that may influence the strength of this cognitive process. Therefore, the present study constructed a moderated mediation model. This model moves beyond simple linear correlations to explain how social support translates into readiness via self-efficacy and under what conditions (levels of adherence) this effect is most potent. This research aims to provide a robust theoretical framework for clinical nursing staff to effectively identify high-risk individuals and develop precision-targeted discharge planning interventions.

Methods

Research Subjects

A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit 463 patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) who were hospitalized in a tertiary grade-A hospital in Wuhu, China, from February to June 2025.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The Inclusion Criteria Were as Follows:

① Met the diagnostic criteria for coronary heart disease (CHD) according to the Expert Consensus on Prevention and Treatment of Sudden Death from Coronary Atherosclerotic Heart Disease;12

② Maintained clear consciousness with sufficient verbal or written communication skills;

③ Were scheduled for discharge within the next 24 hours;

④ Provided informed consent and participated voluntarily in the study.

The Exclusion Criteria Were as Follows:

① Presence of severe comorbidities, such as multiple organ failure, advanced malignant tumors, or severe heart failure (NYHA Class IV);

② Individuals with impaired consciousness, a history of psychiatric disorders, or significant cognitive dysfunction;

③ Inability to independently complete the survey questionnaires.

Sample Size

Sample Size Calculation: The sample size was determined based on the principle of inclusion being 5 to 10 times the total number of items in the measurement scales.13 After accounting for a potential 20% non-response rate or invalid questionnaires, a total of 463 participants were ultimately included in this study, which provided sufficient statistical power for the proposed moderated mediation analysis.

Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS)

The Chinese version of the Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS), translated and cross-culturally adapted by Zhao et al15 was utilized to evaluate patients’ subjective physical and psychological preparedness. The scale consists of 12 items, with each item scored on a 11-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 10. Total scores on the RHDS are positively associated with patients’ subjective perceptions of their post-discharge recovery and their capacity to adapt to home-based rehabilitation. Higher scores represent a more robust state of discharge readiness. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.979.

Morisky Medication Adherence Scale-8 (MMAS-8)

The Chinese version of the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale-8 (MMAS-8), translated and validated by Li et al,16 was employed to assess patients’ medication adherence behavior. This 8-item scale has a total possible score of 8 points. It utilizes a combination of dichotomous (Yes/No) responses for the first seven items and a 5-point Likert scale for the final item (ranging from “Never” to “Always”). The scale incorporates both positively and reverse-scored items to minimize response bias. Higher total scores indicate superior medication adherence. In the present study, the MMAS-8 demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.913.

General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES)

The Chinese version of the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), cross-culturally adapted and validated by Wang et al17 was employed to assess patients’ confidence in their ability to cope with diverse challenges. This unidimensional scale comprises 10 items, each scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true, 4 = exactly true). Total scores range from 10 to 40 points, with higher scores reflecting a greater level of perceived self-efficacy among patients. In the present study, the GSES exhibited excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.949.

Oslo Social Support Scale (Oslo-3)

The Oslo-3 Social Support Scale (Oslo-3)18 was utilized to evaluate patients’ levels of Social Support. This instrument is a brief yet robust tool that assesses three key dimensions through three specific items: the number of close confidants, the level of concern shown by others, and the accessibility of practical assistance from neighbors. The total score ranges from 3 to 14 points, which are further categorized into three levels of support:poor (3~8),moderate (9~11),and strong (12~14).Higher scores indicate more abundant external support resources. In the present study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.783.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Data collection was executed by a research team that underwent standardized training to ensure procedural consistency. Surveys were administered within the 24-hour window prior to patient discharge to assess patients’ perceived readiness for transition. For participants with visual impairments or writing difficulties, a standardized “researcher-dictated, proxy-recorded”approach was employed to minimize response burden while maintaining data integrity.

To ensure impartiality, investigators adhered to a neutral communication protocol, refraining from any biased language or behavior that might influence participant responses. All questionnaires were distributed, collected, and verified on-site for completeness. Following the exclusion of invalid responses (eg., incomplete or repetitive patterns), 463 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in an effective response rate of 96.46%.

Statistical Methods

Data were entered after being cross-checked by two individuals and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and the PROCESS v3.5 plugin. After testing, all continuous variables were found to follow a normal distribution and were described as (). Intergroup comparisons were performed using t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships among variables; Mediation and moderation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro: Model 4 was used to test the mediating role of social support between self-efficacy and readiness for discharge, and Model 14 was used to further test the moderating role of medication adherence in the latter half of the aforementioned mediation pathway. Significance was determined using the Bootstrap method (5,000 resamples), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) not including 0 as the criterion, and a significance level of α = 0.050:

Analysis of the Mediating Effect of Self-Efficacy Between Social Support and Readiness for Discharge

The findings reveal that Social Support significantly and positively predicts both readiness for discharge and self-efficacy (B=1.140, p=0.016) and (B=1.425, p<0.001), respectively. This indicates that external resources play a crucial role in enhancing patients’ internal confidence. When examining the direct impact of Social Support on discharge readiness, the predictive effect of Social Support remained significant in the model without including mediating variables. However, upon including self-efficacy in the regression equation, the direct path from Social Support to discharge readiness was no longer statistically significant (B=0.435, p=0.395),as shown in Table 4. The Bootstrap analysis (based on 5,000 resampling iterations) yielded an indirect effect size of 0.705, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [0.263,1.172].Since this interval did not include zero, the mediating effect was confirmed. Given that the direct effect became non-significant after controlling for the mediator, the mediation effect accounted for 61.8% of the total effect (see Table 5).

Discussion

The Levels of Discharge Readiness, Social Support, Self-Efficacy, and Medication Adherence Among Patients with Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) are Suboptimal and Warrant Improvement

Discharge readiness serves as a pivotal indicator for evaluating clinical outcomes in CHD, directly influencing patients’ long-term therapeutic efficacy and quality of life.19 The results of this study indicate that coronary heart disease patients scored (66.75±23.29) points on discharge readiness, placing them at a lower-middle level. This finding is lower than the results reported by Deng Liying, Luo Fang, and others.4,20 The lower scores observed in this study may be attributed to the demographic composition of the sample, as 76.4% of the participants were elderly. The geriatric population is typically characterized by multiple chronic comorbidities, diminished physiological reserves, and reduced tolerance to disease-related risks. Furthermore, their capacity to rapidly assimilate rehabilitation knowledge is often limited.21 Consequently, patients exhibit significant unpreparedness when transitioning from the controlled clinical setting to the self-managed home environment. Nursing interventions must therefore prioritize the enhancement of risk management capabilities for both patients and their families. Prior to discharge, nursing staff should conduct practical skill demonstrations, with a specific emphasis on correcting cognitive biases related to the identification of precordial discomfort and the administration of emergency medication.

Social support, functioning as a critical external protective factor, not only compensates for resource deficits in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) but also alleviates their sense of helplessness through emotional validation and informational guidance.22 The results of this study indicate that patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) scored 10.35 ± 2.29 on the social support scale, reflecting a moderate level consistent with previous research findings.23 This outcome may be attributed to several factors: ① As CHD patients age, their social roles tend to diminish, often restricting their social interactions primarily to the family circle.24 ② Spouses frequently serve as the primary and most influential source of post-discharge social support. ③ The generally low educational attainment among participants limited their ability to access and comprehend specialized medical information, potentially precipitating discharge anxiety. ④ Knowledge gaps contributed to poor compliance, which hindered the accumulation of successful self-management experiences and consequently impeded the improvement of self-efficacy.⑤Lacking professional medical knowledge, elderly CHD patients predominantly relied on fragmented care provided by family members. Consequently, while reinforcing family support, healthcare providers should prioritize the expansion of patients’ external support networks through community-based interventions and digital health platforms.

As a critical psychological regulator of individual health behaviors, self-efficacy acts as a pivotal driver for optimizing the management of coronary heart disease.25 The self-efficacy scores of patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) in this study were 24±7.91, notably lower than the findings reported by Zhao Jianchun et al.26 This suggests that suboptimal self-efficacy constitutes a significant psychological barrier to achieving effective self-management. This discrepancy is likely tied to the lower educational attainment within the study cohort, which complicates the mastery of specialized self-monitoring skills and heightens psychological stress. Furthermore, medication non-adherence further restricts the accumulation of successful self-management experiences, creating a negative feedback loop that suppresses self-efficacy.8 Nurses should implement individualized health education interventions adapted to patients’ educational attainment, employing interactive feedback mechanisms to bolster cognitive understanding and alleviate psychological stress. Simultaneously, setting incremental rehabilitation goals allows patients to build self-efficacy through successful engagement. Additionally, establishing a multidisciplinary support network—comprising medical professionals, nursing staff, and family caregivers—is critical for optimizing medication adherence, thereby synergistically improving discharge readiness.

As a pivotal component of secondary prevention for coronary heart disease (CHD), medication adherence underpins patient safety following discharge.27 The mean medication adherence score in this study (5.21±1.98) reflects a suboptimal level, a finding consistent with data reported by Teng Zhiyu et al.28 Potential reasons for this include: ① Patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) often require combination therapy. This polypharmacy increases the complexity of medication management and imposes a significant psychological burden. ②Disparities in educational attainment and disease awareness contribute to gaps in medication adherence. ③Healthcare providers frequently deliver “short-term, intensive education” near discharge. This compressed timeframe makes it difficult for patients to fully assimilate complex medication protocols, ultimately leading to suboptimal adherence.29 Research indicates that patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) are particularly vulnerable to medication non-adherence, during the transitional period from hospital to home.20 Consequently, nursing staff should implement visual aids, such as color-coded pill organizers and pictorial medication schedules, alongside simplified multimedia resources to mitigate cognitive barriers to understanding. Simultaneously, healthcare providers should leverage the “Internet+Nursing” model, utilizing platforms like WeChat and chronic disease management applications, to deliver continuous health education, thereby enhancing medication adherence.

The Mediating Role of Self-Efficacy Between Social Support and Discharge Readiness

Path analysis revealed that social support significantly and positively predicted self-efficacy (B=1.425, p<0.001). Furthermore, self-efficacy was found to fully mediate the relationship between social support and discharge readiness, acting as a pivotal mechanism that translates external resources into internal behavioral motivation. For patients with coronary heart disease (CHD), external resources—such as care and support from family and healthcare providers—fail to elicit substantive behavioral motivation unless they are internalized into the patient’s own conviction of capability, known as self-efficacy.17 The mere accumulation of external resources fails to instill the necessary confidence for disease management, hindering the critical transition from “external support” to “internal readiness. ”To be effective, this confidence must undergo cognitive integration and be internalized as a firm conviction in one’s own capability (ie., self-efficacy). Only then can it translate into the substantive motivation required for discharge preparation. This finding corroborates Social Cognitive Theory,30 which posits that individual cognitive beliefs serve as the primary determinant of behavioral intention. Consequently, healthcare providers should implement multidimensional strategies, including empowerment education, family support facilitation, visualization tools, and extended mobile health services31. Clinical practice must prioritize patient activation and empowerment to facilitate the internalization of external social support into intrinsic motivation for self-management. This approach effectively mitigates anxiety and ensures a seamless transition to home-based rehabilitation.

The Absence of Mediation Effect of Medication Adherence between Social Support and Discharge Readiness in CHD Patients The regression analysis indicated that medication adherence did not serve as a significant mediator in the relationship between self-efficacy and discharge readiness (p = 0.121). This finding stands in contrast to the results reported by Chen et al32 The underlying reasons are likely twofold. First, the sample was recruited from a single medical center; the inherent homogeneity of single-center studies may diminish the sensitivity to detect subtle interactions between variables. Second, the management of coronary heart disease (CHD) is a chronic and protracted process, in which the impact of adherence on self-efficacy may exhibit buffering or lag effects. The cross-sectional design employed in this study is limited in its ability to capture these dynamic evolutionary patterns. Although no moderating effect was observed, medication adherence demonstrated a significant direct positive effect t on discharge readiness (B = 4.039, p < 0.001). This strongly confirms that standardized medication adherence constitutes the cornerstone of secondary prevention for CHD. Regular medication therapy not only serves as a physiological intervention to stabilize the disease but also provides robust support for the transition to home care by mitigating symptom fluctuation and psychological instability.33 Notably, subgroup analysis revealed a distinct trend: the moderating effect was more pronounced among older adults and women. Notably, subgroup analysis suggested a potential trend where the positive influence of self-efficacy on discharge readiness might be more sensitive to adherence levels among elderly and female patients. This implies that for more vulnerable populations, consistent medication adherence may serve as a behavioral anchor that reinforces the translation of psychological confidence into rehabilitation outcomes. Conversely, insufficient adherence hinders the translation of psychological confidence into tangible rehabilitation outcomes. This phenomenon may be attributed to cognitive disparities regarding rehabilitation protocols, which vary significantly across different age groups and genders at distinct stages of recovery. Nursing interventions must transition from “generalized education to targeted interventions,” establishing a composite pathway centered on a behaviorally-led, psychologically-synergistic model.34 For high-risk populations, particularly the elderly and women, medication adherence can be bolstered through simplified regimens and the implementation of smart reminder tools. This approach not only controls disease progression but also promotes behavioral standardization and fosters confidence in disease management, thereby ensuring sustained patient safety following discharge.35

In conclusion, this study highlights that discharge readiness among CHD patients remains at a low-to-moderate level. Mechanistically, self-efficacy was identified as a full mediator, acting as the pivotal pathway through which social support influences discharge readiness. Additionally, while the overall interaction was not statistically significant, subgroup observations suggest that medication adherence may act as a potential moderator in specific populations, highlighting the importance of tailored patient management. The limitations of this study include:①The cross-sectional design only captures the patients’ status at the time of discharge, precluding the assessment of long-term rehabilitation trajectories or temporal changes post-discharge. Thirdly, as data were collected within 24 hours prior to discharge, the findings reflect patients’ perceived readiness rather than actual post-discharge health outcomes (eg., unplanned readmission or complications). Future longitudinal studies are warranted to track the predictive validity of pre-discharge readiness on objective recovery metrics.②The recruitment of participants from a single medical center may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. ③The reliance on self-reported scales may introduce response bias. Future investigations are warranted to conduct multicenter, longitudinal follow-ups integrating objective indicators like electronic monitoring and biological markers. Such data will underpin the development of rigorous, evidence-based continuity of care interventions for the coronary heart disease population.
