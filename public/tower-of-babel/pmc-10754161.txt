Outcomes of catheter ablation in high-risk patients with Brugada syndrome refusing an implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation
Authors: Le Li, Ligang Ding, Likun Zhou, Lingmin Wu, Lihui Zheng, Zhenhao Zhang, Yulong Xiong, Zhuxin Zhang, Yan Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim of this study was to investigate the outcomes of catheter ablation (CA) in preventing arrhythmic events among patients with symptomatic Brugada syndrome (BrS) who declined implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) implantation.Methods and resultsA total of 40 patients with symptomatic BrS were included in the study, of which 18 refused ICD implantation and underwent CA, while 22 patients received ICD implantation. The study employed substrate modification (including endocardial and epicardial approaches) and ventricular fibrillation (VF)-triggering pre-mature ventricular contraction (PVC) ablation strategies. The primary outcomes were a composite endpoint consisting of episodes of VF and sudden cardiac death during the follow-up period. The study population had a mean age of 43.8 ± 9.6 years, with 36 (90.0%) of them being male. All patients exhibited the typical Type 1 BrS electrocardiogram pattern, and 16 (40.0%) were carriers of an SCN5A mutation. The Shanghai risk scores were comparable between the CA and the ICD groups (7.05 ± 0.80 vs. 6.71 ± 0.86, P = 0.351). Ventricular fibrillation–triggering PVCs were ablated in 3 patients (16.7%), while VF substrates were ablated in 15 patients (83.3%). Epicardial ablation was performed in 12 patients (66.7%). During a median follow-up of 46.2 (17.5–73.7) months, the primary outcomes occurred more frequently in the ICD group than in the CA group (5.6 vs. 54.5%, Log-rank P = 0.012).ConclusionCatheter ablation is an effective alternative therapy for improving arrhythmic outcomes in patients with symptomatic BrS who decline ICD implantation. Our findings support the consideration of CA as an alternative treatment option in this population.

Introduction

The Brugada syndrome (BrS) is an inherited disorder that increases the risk of ventricular fibrillation (VF) and sudden cardiac death (SCD) in a structurally normal heart.1 It is estimated that the prevalence of BrS ranges from 1/5000 to 1/2000 and accounts for 4–12% of all sudden deaths and up to 20% of sudden deaths in patients with structurally normal hearts.2 Currently, the treatment options for BrS are limited to drug therapy and implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD). Quinidine is the only effective antiarrhythmic drug for preventing recurrent VF episodes,3,4 but its undesirable side effects limit its use in clinical settings. In addition, quinidine is still unavailable in some BrS endemic countries, such as Thailand, Philippines, and China.5 Implantable cardioverter defibrillator is recommended in patients with a high risk of SCD, but as a palliative treatment option, it cannot intervene in the arrhythmic substrate of BrS, reduce the incidence of VF episodes, or cause painful ICD discharges.6 According to statistics, up to 20% of patients with BrS receiving ICD therapy experience inappropriate shocks, and 14% experience device-related complications.7 Currently, there is a lack of effective treatment options for patients with BrS who experience electrical storms after undergoing ICD therapy.8

Catheter ablation (CA) is gaining recognition as an effective therapy for reducing the episodes of VF. In 2012, we performed CA on patients with BrS with VF storms, and CA was found to prevent VF occurrence, with the late activation zone in the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) being identified as a potential VF substrate of BrS.9 The value of CA in preventing arrhythmia recurrence is being increasingly recognized as the arrhythmic substrate in the epicardial region of the RVOT is gradually being understood.10–12 Recently, the international Brugada Ablation of VF Substrate Ongoing Multicenter Registry (BRAVO) study reported that CA was safe and highly effective in preventing VF recurrence in high-risk patients with BrS with ICD implantation and may serve as an alternative treatment for BrS.13 However, the outcomes of CA in patients with BrS refusing an ICD implantation remain uncertain. It is noteworthy that a considerable number of patients with BrS do not receive ICD therapy (especially in southeast Asia) due to economic constraints and concerns associated with device implantation. Consequently, the question whether CA can be considered a viable and effective treatment option becomes paramount in this population. To address this important issue, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the efficacy of CA in improving arrhythmic outcomes in patients with BrS who refused ICD implantation, utilizing the patients with ICD implantation as the control.

Methods

Study population

This study included 103 patients with BrS at Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences from 2003 to 2023. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital and obeyed the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients’ clinical presentations, family history, 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) findings, and gene tests (if available) were retrieved from the electronic medical record system. The diagnosis of BrS was based on the current guideline.6 The Shanghai risk scores were utilized to stratify the risk of patients diagnosed with BrS, and it was observed that an increase in the score corresponded to a higher frequency of ventricular tachycardia (VT) and VF events.14 The symptomatic BrS was defined as VF or syncope associated with BrS. Type 1 Brugada ECG pattern was diagnosed if coved-type ST elevation measuring ≥2 mm was present in ≥1 right precordial lead (V1–V3).15 If there was no classical ECG presentation, the placement of the right precordial leads in more cranial positions (in the 3rd or 2nd intercostal spaces) was considered.16 In this study, we did not use ajmaline to provoke the typical BrS ECG pattern, as it was not available in our country. Therefore, all the BrS ECG patterns in this study were spontaneous.

Patients with symptomatic BrS were initially included in this study. They were first advised to undergo ICD implantation, but if they refused, CA was considered. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (i) age <18 years; (ii) not receiving CA or ICD; (iii) without BrS-related symptoms; (iv) exhibiting Type II or III BrS ECG pattern; (v) having structural heart diseases (such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and arrhythmic cardiomyopathy). Eligible patients were divided into two groups, the CA group and the ICD group, based on the different treatments.

Substrate mapping and ablation protocol

Mapping and CA procedures were performed with local anaesthesia and sedation. Electrocardiogram was continuously monitored during the procedure. In the CA group, a decapolar steerable electrode catheter was placed in the coronary sinus through femoral venous access, and a standard fixed curve quadripolar catheter was positioned in the right ventricular (RV). Comprehensive electrophysiological studies were performed after catheter positioning. If no meaningful arrhythmias were detected, substrate mapping was carried out. Electroanatomic mapping was conducted using either CARTO (Biosense Webster) or Ensite Precision (Abbott). Endocardial and epicardial mappings of the arrhythmogenic substrates of the RV epicardium were performed during sinus rhythm. The arrhythmogenic substrates included low voltage (≤1 mV), split electrograms, or fractionated electrograms with multiple potentials with ≥2 distinct components, with >20 ms isoelectric segments between the peaks of individual components, and long-duration (>80 ms) or late potentials. Notably, for patients who received ablation before 2010, only endocardial mapping and ablation were performed because the value of epicardial ablation was not yet clear at that time.

Repetitive electroanatomic mapping was carried out following the administration of intravenous propafenone (1.5 mg/kg/10 min). If the patient had premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) that triggered VF (defined as PVC that initiated VF), activation mapping was conducted to identify the site of the earliest activation.

Ablation was performed using radiofrequency energy, with a target temperature of 45°C and a maximum power of 50 W using irrigation (flow rate 12–30 cc/min). The ablation targets were VF substrates with abnormal ventricular electrograms and/or VF-triggering PVCs. The ablation endpoint for VF triggers was the elimination of PVC-VF triggers, while for VF substrates, it was the elimination of all abnormal late-fractionated electrograms. After CA, patients underwent a repeat challenge with propafenone intravenously. To prevent pericarditis, dexamethasone 5 mg was administered intrapericardially at the end of each procedure.

Implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation

The indications of ICD therapy were based on the recommendations from the second Brugada consensus conference.15 Transvenous system implantation was performed in all patients through either the cephalic or the left subclavian vein, as determined by the operator. The decision to use single- or dual-chamber devices was made based on the presence of previous supraventricular arrhythmias or evidence of sinus node dysfunction. Our standard protocol for these patients was to program a single VF zone with a detection rate of 180–200 b.p.m. and backup pacing at a rate of 45 b.p.m.

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients were followed up by phone or clinic visit at 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge, and then annually. At every follow-up, patients were required to undergo 12-lead ECG and 24 h Holter ECG monitoring to detect arrhythmias. The devices of patients with an implanted ICD were checked every 6 months. The primary outcomes were a composite endpoint including episodes of VF and SCD.

Statistical analysis

The normal distribution of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations or as median and interquartile range of 25th–75th percentiles depending on the distribution of the data and compared using the t-test. Levene’s homogeneity of variance test was used to assess the homoscedasticity assumption. If homoscedasticity was not satisfied, the Welch’s t-test was used to compare between groups. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Event-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two tailed, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

We initially conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from a cohort of 103 patients with BrS. Among these patients, a subset of 40 patients with symptomatic BrS (14 cardiac arrest survivors and 26 with arrhythmogenic syncope) who underwent either CA (n = 18) or ICD (n = 22) treatment was included in our analysis (Figure 1). The CA group was significantly younger than the ICD group (38.8 ± 8.9 vs. 47.8 ± 8.3, P = 0.022), which may be attributed to a higher likelihood of younger patients declining device implantation. Among the 40 patients, 16 patients (40.0%) tested positive for an SCN5A mutation. All patients presented with a Type-1 BrS ECG pattern; among them, eight patients underwent cranial ECG lead testing. The Shanghai risk scores of the CA and ICD groups showed no significant difference (7.05 ± 0.80 vs. 6.71 ± 0.86, P = 0.351). Most baseline characteristics were comparable between the CA and the ICD groups (Table 1).

Mapping and ablation

Among the 18 patients who underwent CA, 15 patients (83.3%) did not exhibit clinically significant arrhythmias and received substrate modification therapy. The remaining three patients (16.7%) demonstrated VF-triggering PVCs (Figure 2), either spontaneously or induced. These patients subsequently underwent endocardial ablation procedures focused on targeting the VF triggers, with all ablation sites located in the RVOT. Furthermore, one patient had undergone CA prior to 2010 when the efficacy of epicardial ablation was not firmly established, and therefore, he underwent exclusively endocardial ablation. Another two patients required endocardial ablation due to an unsuccessful subxiphoid pericardial puncture resulting from liver obstruction. Among the patient cohort, 12 individuals (66.7%) underwent epicardial ablation (3 exclusively epicardial and 9 combined endocardial and epicardial). Notably, low voltage, fractionated electrograms, and late potentials were identified in 2 (11.1%), 4 (22.2%), and 15 (83.3%) patients, respectively. Following CA, there was a significant reduction in J-point to ST elevation (J-STE) compared with baseline values [0.4 (0.3–0.6) vs. 0.1 (0.0–0.2), P < 0.001]. Figure 3 illustrates the changes in J-STE and the extent of low-voltage area at baseline, after the propafenone challenge, and post-ablation in a patient who underwent epicardial ablation. Moreover, Figure 4 demonstrates the prolongation of late potentials after the propafenone challenge and their elimination after CA. Importantly, CA proved to be a relatively safe intervention for BrS, with one case of pericardial tamponade that improved following catheter drainage (Table 2).

Primary outcomes

After a median follow-up of 46.2 (17.5–73.7) months, primary events occurred in 1 patient (5.6%) in the CA group and in 12 patients (54.5%) in the ICD group, demonstrating a significant difference between the 2 groups (Log-rank P = 0.012; Figure 5). Specifically, a patient suffered from VF after 46.5 months following CA and received ICD implantation. No death event was observed in the CA group. However, in the ICD group, 12 patients experienced recurrent VF episodes, resulting in (often multiple) ICD shocks, and among them, 2 patients also experienced inappropriate ICD shocks. No cardiac death was observed in the ICD group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the efficacy of CA in preventing arrhythmic events among patients with BrS who have declined ICD implantation, with the ICD group serving as the control. Our primary finding reveals that CA demonstrates significant effectiveness in preventing VF or SCD in this specific population. Consequently, CA emerges as a promising alternative therapy for patients with symptomatic BrS who have not undergone ICD implantation.

The risk stratification of BrS is of utmost importance for prognosis assessment and clinical treatment decisions. The Shanghai score, a widely employed diagnostic and risk stratification system, plays a crucial role in this context. Extensive research has consistently validated that patients with BrS with a Shanghai score of ≥5.5 points face a significantly heightened risk of VF/VT events.17 In the study at hand, participants exhibited a high risk of SCD, as evidenced by Shanghai risk scores ≥5.5 points for the two groups. Although the difference was not statistically significant, the Shanghai score of the CA group was higher than that of the ICD group. This finding suggests that patients who underwent CA had a relatively worse prognosis initially, but the procedure effectively improved their arrhythmic outcomes. Furthermore, the substantial rate of arrhythmic events in the ICD group (54.5%) can be attributed to the inclusion of a high-risk population.

The first-line therapy for preventing SCD in symptomatic BrS is the implantation of an ICD.6,18 However, despite the recommendations provided in the guidelines, the utilization rate of ICD in eligible individuals falls significantly short of the desired standards. Additionally, adherence to guidelines for managing this hereditary arrhythmogenic condition is also notably limited across European centres.19 The utilization of ICD varies, with rates ranging from 24% in Europe20 to 30–50% in the USA,21 leaving the majority of individuals at risk of SCD without this life-saving device. In Asia, the average utilization rate of ICDs is notably lower, with only 12% of eligible patients receiving the device.22 In Indonesia, the rate is as low as 1.5%. Economic constraints, concerns related to device implantation, and limited awareness among healthcare providers and patients are the main barriers to widespread ICD implantation. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge that the ICD serves as a palliative treatment option and does not directly address the underlying arrhythmic substrate of BrS. As a result, there is a pressing need to explore alternative treatment modalities for patients with symptomatic BrS who opt against ICD implantation.

As more clinical studies confirm the value of CA in improving arrhythmic prognosis for patients with BrS, the current guideline recommend CA for the management of triggering PVCs and/or RVOT substrate in patients who have experienced recurrent appropriate ICD shocks that are unresponsive to drug therapy.6 The BRAVO study reported that CA targeting the arrhythmic substrates located in the epicardial region of the RVOT led to an impressive VF-free survival rate of 95% at a 5-year follow-up in patients with symptomatic BrS.13

The arrhythmogenesis of BrS is characterized by increased transmural dispersion in repolarization and altered depolarization with conduction slowing, particularly in the RVOT.23,24 Despite a structurally normal heart seen on routine imaging, structural abnormalities, such as fibrosis in the RVOT, have been identified.25 Ablation at the surviving myocardium between fibrotic tissue has been shown to eliminate the arrhythmogenic substrate, leading to the elimination of the Brugada ECG pattern and a reduction in arrhythmic burden.26 Our previous study demonstrated the success of endomyocardial substrate modification in reducing VF episodes in patients with symptomatic BrS.9 Modifying the epicardial substrate has become the mainstream approach, although it may be limited due to the risk of coronary artery injury and obstruction from epicardial adipose tissue.27–29 Recent findings further suggest that endocardial ablation can eliminate some epicardial abnormal potentials in BrS.30 While more evidence is needed, a meta-analysis indicated a higher VT/VF-free rate with the epicardial approach compared with the endocardial approach.31 In our study, where most patients underwent epicardial ablation, the positive outcomes further support the superiority of the epicardial approach.

Drug provocation with a sodium channel blocker can expand the area of abnormal conduction, potentially aiding in substrate identification before ablation. However, it has yet to be proven that ablation of those areas improves the procedure’s effectiveness. The optimal stimulation drug remains unclear, with ajmaline being the most commonly used.12 As this drug is unavailable in China, we used propafenone as a substitute and found that it can also reveal potential arrhythmogenic substrates. Recently, scholars in Taiwan reported a new method for augmenting the substrate through epicardial warm water instillation, achieving an increase in the low-voltage zone from 63.53 ± 40.57 to 123.83 ± 35.26 cm2 (P = 0.03).32 This may provide a reliable non-pharmacological approach to enhancing abnormal Brugada substrates.

In our study, we performed both VF-triggering PVCs and abnormal substrate elimination in BrS. Eliminating abnormal substrates is the mainstream strategy, but eliminating VF-triggering PVCs has also shown effectiveness in reducing VF recurrence.33 A study on explanted hearts with the Brugada phenotype has revealed that VF-triggering PVCs originate from the endocardium rather than the epicardium.24 Additionally, it is anticipated that endocardial ablation of the RVOT free wall will alter the epicardial substrate due to its relative thinness. As a result, endocardial ablation of VF-triggering PVCs has proven to be effective in improving arrhythmic outcomes in individuals with BrS.34 However, this technique is limited to patients with frequent PVCs. Ventricular fibrillation–triggering PVC ablation does not modify the arrhythmogenic substrates of BrS and may benefit only those with frequent PVCs.

In summary, our study demonstrated a favourable outcome of CA in the management of patients with symptomatic BrS who declined ICD implantation, with only 5.6% of patients experiencing VF recurrence. These results hold more significance considering the inclusion of high-risk individuals with a Shanghai score >6.5.

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, risk factor analysis was not performed due to the low number of events. Secondly, the retrospective nature of the study, which was conducted at a single centre, may introduce bias and confounding factors. Finally, while propafenone was effective in unmasking arrhythmogenic substrates, it is not a classical drug for provocation of the BrS pattern, and as a result, some abnormal electrogram areas may have been missed.

Conclusions

This study suggests that CA may be an effective alternative therapy for managing patients with symptomatic BrS who refuse ICD implantation. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Therefore, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the findings of this study.
