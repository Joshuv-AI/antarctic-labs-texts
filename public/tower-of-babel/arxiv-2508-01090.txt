Collisional decoherence in a BEC double-well accelerometer
Kateryna Korshynska, Sebastian Ulbricht

Abstract
BEC-based quantum sensors offer a huge, yet not fully explored potential in gravimetry and accelerometry. In this paper, we study a possible setup for such a device, which is a weakly interacting Bose gas trapped in a double-well potential. In such a trap, the gas is known to exhibit Josephson oscillations, which rely on the coherence between the potential wells. Applying the density matrix approach, we consider transitions between the coherent, partially incoherent, and fully incoherent states of the Bose gas. We show how, due to the presence of weak interactions, collisional decoherence causes the Josephson oscillations to decay with time. We further study the interplay of collisional interaction and external acceleration, which leads to shifts of the oscillation frequency. Finally, we investigate how this effect can be used to build a BEC double-well accelerometer and give analytical expressions for its expected sensitivity.



I
Introduction

Modern cooling and trapping techniques allow for a precise manipulation of cold boson gases
Phillips et al. 1991
. When the ultra-cold temperatures are reached, the Bose gases form a macroscopic quantum system known as a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC). After its first realization
Anderson et al. 1995
;
Davis et al. 1995
, the study of BECs has become a rapidly evolving field of research, providing new opportunities for both fundamental science and technological applications.

One of the most attractive features of a BEC is its long-range coherence, leading to pronounced interference phenomena. This feature makes BECs a promising basis for quantum sensor devices with huge, yet only partially explored, potential in gravimetry and accelerometry
Gaaloul et al. 2014
;
Borysenko et al. 2025
;
Chaika et al. 2025
.
One realization of such a device is a BEC confined in a double-well potential.
The tunneling dynamics of particles in this potential leads to Josephson oscillations between the wells, which are highly sensitive to even small accelerations of the trap
Masi et al. 2021
;
Gersemann et al. 2020
;
Abend et al. 2016
.

Commonly, a BEC is analyzed assuming a fully coherent Bose gas of interacting particles
Smerzi et al. 1997
, using up-to-date techniques such as ZNG-formalism
Griffin et al. 2009
and the stochastic Gross-Piteavskii equation
Gardiner et al. 2002
;
Bidasyuk et al. 2018
.
These approaches are well suited for the investigation of a trapped BEC in a single macroscopic state.
However, in the case of interferometer setups with two or more nearly degenerated states, such as the double-well, these approaches are
limited by the fact that the BEC may decohere
Sakmann et al. 2014
;
Fattori et al. 2008
.

The aforementioned problem has been previously discussed in the non-interacting limit for a Bose gas confined in a double-well trap
Korshynska and Ulbricht 2024
. We have shown that in the density matrix formalism, the Josephson equations are modified by the presence of partial incoherence, leading to the observable modifications to Josephson dynamics. In the present paper, we extend this research to the case of a closed, weakly interacting Bose gas, investigating how the collisional interaction induces decoherence in the system.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II we introduce a double-well geometry and its single-particle eigenstates and analyze how they are modified by the presence of external acceleration. Moreover, from these eigensates we construct the left and right well states, which provide a suitable basis to describe the Josephson effect. Then, in Sec. III we write down the many-particle Hamiltonian of the Bose gas, which consists of the free single-particle Hamiltonians and collisional interactions, which we treat as a small perturbation. With that in hand, we solve the Liouville equation for the density matrix in Sec. IV and derive the effective density matrix that contains all information relevant to study Josephson effect.
In Sec. V we
investigate the Josephson dynamics
for the particular scenario, where all particles are initially localized in the left well, and demonstrate the collisional decoherence.
Moreover,
we study the influence of external acceleration, causing observable frequency shifts,
and discuss the setup’s
suitability as an accelerometer device.
In Sec.
VI
we compare the obtained frequency shifts with the predictions of the pure state model, which is applicable in case of negligible decoherence.
The conclusions are made in Sec.
VII
.

II
A single particle in a double-well

Since we want to study a Bose gas in the weakly interacting regime, it is convenient to first investigate the single-particle solution to the double-well problem, build up from that the many-particle theory and treat interactions perturbatively, later.
In this Section, therefore, we introduce the trap geometry, and give the single-particle energy spectrum as well as the corresponding eigenstates.

II.1
Two-state model for 3D cubic traps

While our theory can be applied to any double-well potential shape,
in our model we consider two cubic traps, each of volume
L^{3}
and potential depth
U_{0}
, as well as a spacing of
2L
between them, as shown in Fig.
1
. This geometry is inspired by the recent studies of BECs confined in box-shaped potentials, see e.g., Refs.
Navon et al. 2021
;
Gaunt et al. 2013
, and is used here to illustrate our ideas by means of an instructive example.

Figure 1:
Potential geometry and the single particle energy eigenstates: the ground state
\phi_{0}
(solid line) and the excited state
\phi_{1}
(dashed line).

For simplicity, we want the potential depth
U_{0}
to be tuned such that the trap configuration has only two bound states
\phi_{0}(\mathbf{r})
and
\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r})
, solving the eigenvalue equation
\hat{h}_{0}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{0/1}=E_{0/1}\phi_{0/1}
for the Hamiltonian
\hat{h}_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{p}^{2}/2m+U(\mathbf{r})
.
The details on parameters and solutions for this system can be found in Appendix
A
.
For our further discussion, it is relevant that these states are symmetric and asymmetric along
z
direction, respectively, as shown in Fig.
1
. Moreover, their energies
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
are nearly degenerate, such that
\Delta E=E_{1}-E_{0}\ll|E|
, where
E=(E_{0}+E_{1})/2
.
For completeness, the extension of the formalism beyond the two-state model can be found in Appendix
B
.

II.2
Two-state model in the presence of external acceleration

In the following, we assume that the double-well is subject to an external acceleration
\mathbf{a}
, creating the additional potential
\delta U(\mathbf{r})=m\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{r}=ma(z\cos\theta+x\sin\theta)
, where
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{a}
and the
z
-axis.

Applying perturbation theory
Binney and Skinner 2013
for the nearly degenerate states
\phi_{0/1}
, we find that

\displaystyle\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}{2\Delta E^{a}}}\phi_{0}-\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}{2\Delta E^{a}}}\phi_{1}

(1a)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}{2\Delta E^{a}}}\phi_{1}+\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}{2\Delta E^{a}}}\phi_{0}\,,

(1b)

obey the eigenvalue equation
[\hat{h}_{0}(\mathbf{r})+\delta U(\mathbf{r})]\psi_{0/1}=E^{a}_{0/1}\psi_{0/1}
to all orders in
maL/\Delta E
and to linear orders in
maL/E
and
\Delta E/E
. In Eqs. (
1
) we introduced

\Delta E^{a}=\sqrt{(\Delta E)^{2}+(2ma\chi(\theta))^{2}}
,
\chi(\theta)=\left[\int d\mathbf{r}\phi_{0}\phi_{1}z\right]\cos\theta
, and
\textrm{sign}_{a}=\textrm{sign}[a\chi(\theta)]
.
The corresponding energies are

E_{0/1}^{a}=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{0}+E_{1}+2maI(\theta)\mp\Delta E^{a}\right)\,,

(2)

where
I(\theta)=\left[\int d\mathbf{r}\phi_{0}^{2}x\right]\sin\theta
.

The obtained result has the same structure as the solution of the slightly asymmetric double-well problem, given in Ref.
Korshynska and Ulbricht 2024
, where

2ma\chi(\theta)
plays role of the potential step.
In this scenario, however, the latter is caused by the acceleration component parallel to the
z
-axis, while the orthogonal acceleration component only causes a global energy shift
maI(\theta)
in Eq. (
2
).

In most of all cases, where the two boxes are well separated (such that
\phi_{0}
and
|\phi_{1}|
almost resemble each other),
we can approximate
\int d\mathbf{r}\phi_{0}^{2}x\approx\int d\mathbf{r}\phi_{0}\phi_{1}z\approx 3L/2
, i.e.,
\chi(\theta)\approx 3L\cos\theta/2
and
I(\theta)\approx 3L\sin\theta/2
.
Note that the perturbative approach, leading to Eqs. (
1
) only holds for small accelerations, i.e. for

ma\chi(\theta)\approx 3maL\cos\theta/2\ll\Delta E
. For larger accelerations, the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
\hat{h}_{0}+\delta U

cannot be properly approximated as superpositions of
\phi_{0/1}
states.

II.3
Left-right states

In this Section, we introduce the left-right state basis
\psi_{L/R}
of the single-particle states (in the case
a=0
denoted as
\phi_{L/R}
) that maximize the probability of the particle to be in the left or right well, respectively. Following
Korshynska and Ulbricht 2024
, they read
\psi_{L}=\phi_{L}=(\phi_{0}+\phi_{1})/\sqrt{2}
and
\psi_{R}=\phi_{R}=(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})/\sqrt{2}
in the basis of the unperturbed energy eigenstates
\phi_{0/1}
.
However, in the presence of acceleration, we need to express them in the basis of the proper energy eigenstates
\psi_{0/1}
, what gives rise to

\displaystyle\psi_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}}}\left([\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}-\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}]\psi_{0}\right.

(3a)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.[\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}+\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}]\psi_{1}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}}}\left([\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}+\textrm{sign }_{a}\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}]\psi_{0}\right.

(3b)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.[\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}+\Delta E}-\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{\Delta E^{a}-\Delta E}]\psi_{1}\right).

For the sake of brevity, we
introduce the angle
\xi
, determined by
\tan\xi=(\Delta E^{a}+\textrm{sign}_{a}\sqrt{(\Delta E^{a})^{2}-(\Delta E)^{2}})/\Delta E
, to write

\begin{bmatrix}|\psi_{L}\rangle\\
|\psi_{R}\rangle\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\xi|\psi_{0}\rangle+\sin\xi|\psi_{1}\rangle\\
\sin\xi|\psi_{0}\rangle-\cos\xi|\psi_{1}\rangle\\
\end{bmatrix}=\hat{T}(\xi)\begin{bmatrix}|\psi_{0}\rangle\\
|\psi_{1}\rangle\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

(4)

The transformation matrix
\hat{T}
will be of later use, when we discuss the Josephson dynamics in the presence of external acceleration.

III
Many-particle Hamiltonian

Having discussed a single particle in a double-box trap, in the following we build up a description of a closed many-particle system, to first consider a non-interacting Bose gas.
Afterwards, we treat additional interparticle interactions perturbatively, applying the effective density matrix formalism.

III.1
Non-interacting Bose gas

The Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}
is dominated by the non-interacting part

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}[\hat{h}_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{i})+\delta U(\mathbf{r}_{i})]=\sum_{i=0}^{1}E^{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i},

(5)

where
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}
and
\hat{a}_{i}
are creation and annihilation operators associated with the two energy eigenstates (
i=0,1
) of the double-well. The interaction
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
is a small perturbation due to the collisional interaction of the particles that will be discussed in Sec.
III.2
.

Now we introduce the density matrix for the non-interacting many-particle system. For that, we need to construct the basis of many-particle states

\displaystyle|N_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{1}!(N-N_{1})!}}\left(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\right)^{N-N_{1}}\left(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\right)^{N_{1}}|0\rangle\,,

(6)

which are labeled by the number
N_{1}
of particles in the excited state
\psi_{1}
. The states
satisfy
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}|N_{1}\rangle=[N_{1}E^{a}_{1}+(N-N_{1})E^{a}_{0}]|N_{1}\rangle=:\mathcal{E}_{N_{1}}|N_{1}\rangle
, and
are normalized such that
\langle N_{1}^{\prime}|N_{1}\rangle=\delta_{N_{1}^{\prime}N_{1}}
.
With these states at hand, we can construct the density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}\rho_{N_{1}N_{1}^{\prime}}|N_{1}\rangle\langle N_{1}^{\prime}|

(7)

that contains the full information about the
N
-particle system.

III.2
Collisional interaction

To describe the interparticle interactions, we need to find the corresponding interaction potential, entering the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}
.

Commonly, BECs are dilute gases, where the average distances between particles is large in comparison to the range of interparticle interactions.
Thus, in such a gas one can consider only 2-particle interactions, while simultaneous interactions involving more particles are very rare and can be neglected
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
.
This also means that the interaction is significant only if the particles closely approach each other, as can be modeled by a delta-potential

V(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2})=g\delta(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2})\,.

(8)

This assumption is also supported by the theory for elastic scattering of slow particles (i.e., low temperature)
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
;
Griffin et al. 2009
, where the interaction constant
g=4\pi\hbar^{2}a_{s}/m
is found to be determined by the scattering length
a_{s}
.
The latter is a crucial parameter, defining the interaction properties of a Bose gas, and can be accessed with a few experimental techniques, see e.g.
Tiesinga et al. 1996
.

Figure 2:
Collisional processes in Bose gas: (a) collisions of type
\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{i}
, where both ingoing and outgoing bosons belong to the same energy eigenstate
\epsilon_{i}
(depicted by same color); (b) Hartree (direct) collisions and (c) Fock (exchange) collisions
\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{j}
of bosons in different states (depicted by different colors).

The two-particle collisions described by the potential (
8
) enter our formalism via the operator

\hat{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijrl}V_{ijrl}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}\hat{a}_{l}.

(9)

with the matrix element

\displaystyle V_{ijrl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{r}_{1}d\mathbf{r}_{2}\psi^{\star}_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\psi^{\star}_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{2})V(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2})\psi_{r}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\psi_{l}(\mathbf{r}_{1})

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\int d\mathbf{r}\psi^{\star}_{i}(\mathbf{r})\psi^{\star}_{j}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{r}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{l}(\mathbf{r})\,,

which characterizes a collision between two ingoing bosons in the states
\psi_{i/j}
that are found in the states
\psi_{r/l}
after interaction
Morgan 2000
.
Here the
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r})
with (
i=0,1
) are given by the wave functions for non-interacting particles from Eq. (
1
),
as can be done in the considered approximation of weak interactions

Mazets and Schmiedmayer 2010
.

Since we consider the Bose gas as a closed system with fixed total energy and particle number, we will only consider
such collisional processes, which are
energy-conserving, leaving us with the interaction operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(V_{0000}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{0}+V_{1111}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{1}\right.

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.V_{1010}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}+V_{0101}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.V_{1001}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}+V_{0110}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{0}\hat{a}_{1}\right).

The corresponding collisional processes are illustrated in Fig.
2
. They are analogous to the direct (Hartree) and exchange (Fock) collisions discussed in the BEC theory
Proukakis and Jackson 2008
;
Morgan 2000
.
We mention that, other than in our case, in open systems, beyond the weakly interacting regime the terms in the interaction operator are accompanied by also those of the energy non-conserving processes, see e.g.
Proukakis and Jackson 2008
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2000
;
Griffin et al. 2009
.

Performing the integrals (
10
) for the double-box trap geometry and the corresponding single-particle states
\psi_{0/1}
from Sec.
II
, we obtain the matrix elements

\displaystyle{V}_{0000}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2-\left[\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta E^{a}}\right]^{2}\right){V}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta E/E)

(12a)

\displaystyle{V}_{1111}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2-\left[\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta E^{a}}\right]^{2}\right){V}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta E/E)

(12b)

\displaystyle{V}_{1010}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta E^{a}}\right]^{2}{V}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta E/E)

(12c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{V}_{0101}={V}_{1001}={V}_{0110}\,,

which are ingredient to Eq. (
11
).
Here we neglect all terms of order
\Delta E/E
, i.e., the overlap of the left and right box wave functions.
While this overlap is crucial for the Josephson dynamics, later discussed in this paper, it merely contributes quantitatively to the interparticle interaction, as we show in Appendix
C
.

In the Eqs. (
12
)
we see that the matrix elements are characterized by the ratio
0\leq\Delta E/\Delta E^{a}\leq 1
of the energy gaps with and without acceleration
\mathbf{a}
and a single parameter

V=\frac{g}{2}\left(\frac{LmE+\hbar\sqrt{2mE/3}(2+1/3\times E/U_{0})}{(L\sqrt{2mE/3}+2\hbar)^{2}}\right)^{3}

(13)

determined by the trap geometry, the interaction strength, as well as the single particle’s mass and energy.
Note that the quantities entering Eq. (
13
) are not independent.
That is, the energy
E=E(L,U_{0},m)
is fixed by the other quantities and can be obtained numerically, as we do in Appendix
A
.

IV
Density matrix and single-particle observables

IV.1
Density matrix evolution

In this Section we discuss the evolution of the density matrix (
7
), following Ref.
Blum 2012
.
Considering the density matrix in the interaction picture

\hat{\rho}_{I}(t)=e^{i\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}t/\hbar}\hat{\rho}(t)e^{-i\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}t/\hbar},

(14)

its evolution

\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{I}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{\mathcal{V}},\hat{\rho}_{I}(t)]\,.

(15)

is determined by the interaction operator
\hat{\mathcal{V}}=e^{i\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}t/\hbar}\hat{\mathcal{V}}e^{-i\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}t/\hbar}
. The latter preserves its form in the interaction picture, since it is energy-conserving as discussed in Sec.
III.2
.
Describing the density matrix in the many-particle basis (
6
),
its matrix elements are

\displaystyle\rho^{I}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}e^{-it/\hbar\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}}\,,

(16)

where
\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}=\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}(t=0)
is the initial state of the system, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V_{0000}}{2}[N_{1}(N_{1}-2N+1)-N_{1}^{\prime}(N_{1}^{\prime}-2N+1)]

(17)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{V_{1111}}{2}[N_{1}(N_{1}-1)-N_{1}^{\prime}(N_{1}^{\prime}-1)]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(V_{0101}+V_{1010})[(N-N_{1})N_{1}-(N-N_{1}^{\prime})N_{1}^{\prime}]

governs its evolution.
Transforming this solution of
Eq. (
15
) back to the Schrödinger picture, we get

\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}(t)=\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}e^{-i(\mathcal{E}_{N_{1}}-\mathcal{E}_{N_{1}^{\prime}}+\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}})t/\hbar}.

(18)

Thus, we see that the collisional interaction shifts the energy levels of the system by
\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}
. Note, due to
\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}}=0
, the diagonal density matrix elements remain constant in time
\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}}(t)=\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}}(0)
, as is evident from the energy and particle number conservation in a closed system.
The off-diagonal elements of the density matrix evolve with

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N_{1}^{\prime}}-\mathcal{E}_{N_{1}}-\mathcal{V}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(N_{1}^{\prime}-N_{1})[\Delta E^{a}+V^{a}(N-N_{1}-N_{1}^{\prime})]\,,

where the interaction now enters via the single parameter

V^{a}=\left(3\left[\frac{\Delta E}{\Delta E^{a}}\right]^{2}-2\right)V\,,

(20)

emerging from using the explicit form of the matrix elements (
12
) in the expression (
17
).
Note that we fulfill
\omega_{N_{1}^{\prime},N_{1}}=-\omega_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}
, as immediately stems from the fact that the density matrix is Hermitian.

IV.2
Effective density matrix

In the last section, we derived the density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
that contains complete information about the many-particle system and its evolution.
Our ultimate goal, however, is to describe Josephson dynamics
and, in particular the observable oscillations of particle populations between the wells of a double-well potential.

These populations are expectation values of the number operators

\hat{N}_{L/R}=\underbrace{\hat{P}_{L/R}\oplus\mathbb{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{1}}_{N\textnormal{-times}}+\dots+\mathbb{1}\oplus\mathbb{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\hat{P}_{L/R}\,,

(21)

which are constructed from the projectors
\hat{P}_{L/R}=|\psi_{L/R}\rangle\langle\psi_{L/R}|
, projecting on the state of a single particle in the left or right well, respectively.

As we discuss in detail in Ref.
Korshynska and Ulbricht 2024
, the expectation value

\langle\hat{N}_{L/R}\rangle_{\rho}=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{N}_{L/R})=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{e}}\hat{P}_{L/R})

can be evaluated when only the effective density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{e}
is known.
In our case, having that only two single particle energy eigenstates
\psi_{0/1}
are considered,
\hat{\rho}_{e}
is a
2\times 2
matrix.
It coincides with the reduced density matrix of a single particle in a bath of all the other
N-1
bosons
Erdahl and Smith Jr 2012
and can therefore be interpreted as a description of the many-particle system by an average boson.

Using the solution (
18
) for
\hat{\rho}(t)
, we obtain the explicit form of the elements
\alpha_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i}|\hat{\rho}_{e}|\psi_{j}\rangle
of the effective density matrix in the
\psi_{0/1}
basis

\displaystyle\alpha_{00}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N}\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}}^{0}(N-N_{1})

(22a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N}\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}}^{0}N_{1}

(22b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{10}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sqrt{(N-N_{1})(N_{1}+1)}

(22c)

\displaystyle\,\times\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}+1}^{0}e^{i\omega(N_{1})t}

\displaystyle\alpha_{01}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{01}^{\star}(t),

(22d)

which are determined by the diagonal elements
\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}}
and the nearest off-diagonal elements

\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}+1}
of the initial density matrix. Moreover, the evolution is governed by the frequency

\omega(N_{1}):=\omega_{N_{1},N_{1}+1}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left[\Delta E^{a}+V^{a}(N-2N_{1}-1)\right]

(23)

of transitions between the many-particle states
|N_{1}\rangle
and
|N_{1}\pm 1\rangle
, cf. Eq. (
19
).
Other transitions
|N_{1}\rangle\to|N_{1}\pm 2\rangle
, or higher, do not appear, since we only consider projectors
\hat{P}_{L/R}
acting on a state of each single boson independently, cf. Eq. (
21
).

To discuss Josephson dynamics,
we need to transform the effective density matrix

\hat{\rho}_{eLR}=\hat{T}(\xi)\hat{\rho}_{e}\hat{T}(\xi)^{-1}

from the
\psi_{0/1}
-basis to the basis of left and right states, using the transformation matrix
\hat{T}(\xi)
from Eq. (
4
).
After this transformation, we can identify the diagonal elements of

\hat{\rho}_{eLR}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}N_{L}(t)&A(t)\\
A^{*}(t)&N_{R}(t)\\
\end{bmatrix}

(24)

with the time-dependent occupation numbers

\displaystyle N_{L}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{00}\cos^{2}\xi+\alpha_{11}\sin^{2}\xi

(25a)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+\textrm{Re}[\alpha_{10}(t)]\sin(2\xi)

\displaystyle N_{R}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{00}\sin^{2}\xi+\alpha_{11}\cos^{2}\xi

(25b)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt-\textrm{Re}[\alpha_{10}(t)]\sin(2\xi)

of the wells.
The also introduced off-diagonal element

\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{00}-\alpha_{11}}{2}\sin(2\xi)

(26)

\displaystyle\qquad-\textrm{Re}[\alpha_{10}(t)]\cos(2\xi)+i\textrm{Im}[\alpha_{10}(t)]\,

and its conjugated will allow us to quantify the coherence between the two spatially separated subsystems, constituting the left and the right well states. These
two subsystems now can be seen as two coupled BECs – a picture, that is often stressed in literature
Smerzi et al. 1997
.

IV.3
Population imbalance and degree of coherence

Having found the explicit form (
25
) for the population numbers
N_{L/R}(t)
of each well, we now can construct the observable population imbalance

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(N_{L}(t)-N_{R}(t))/N

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\cos(2\xi)}{N}\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N}\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}}(N-2N_{1})

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\sin(2\xi)}{N}\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sqrt{(N_{1}+1)(N-N_{1})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\!\left(\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}+1}^{0}e^{i\omega(N_{1})t}+\rho_{N_{1}+1,N_{1}}^{0}e^{-i\omega(N_{1})t}\right)\,,

which for our closed system is the only degree of freedom since
N_{L}(t)+N_{R}(t)=N
gives the total particle number.
The first term in Eq. (
27
)
is the average population imbalance, which gives a contribution in the presence of acceleration
\mathbf{a}\neq 0

and vanishes for
\mathbf{a}=0
.
The second, time-dependent, term
gives rise to the Josephson oscillations.

For a specific scenario, i.e., a given initial condition
\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}
, the imbalance
Z(t)
encodes the entire observable dynamics of the system.
However, further quantities can be introduced to characterize the behavior of
Z(t)
.
In particular, we are interested in a parameter to quantify the
decoherence
of the system due to interparticle collisions, causing the oscillations to die off after a certain time.
To obtain this parameter,
we bring the density matrix (
24
) into a diagonal form

S\hat{\rho}_{eLR}(t)S^{-1}=\frac{N}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+f(t)&0\\
0&1-f(t)\\
\end{bmatrix}\,,

(28)

where
f(t)
ranges from
0
to
1
and is given by

f(t)=\sqrt{Z^{2}(t)+\left(\frac{2|A(t)|}{N}\right)^{2}}.

(29)

While here, we don’t want to specify the transformation
S(t)
or the resulting time-dependent basis states
|s_{1}(t)\rangle
and
|s_{2}(t)\rangle
implied by Eq. (
28
), we see that for
f=1
we obtain
S\hat{\rho}_{eLR}(t)S^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(N,0)=N|s_{1}\rangle\langle s_{1}|
. This means that the system is in a single state
|s_{1}(t)\rangle=c_{1}(t)|\psi_{L}\rangle+c_{2}(t)|\psi_{R}\rangle
that can be written as a superposition of the
|\psi_{L/R}\rangle
states. Thus, for
f=1
the system is described by a fully coherent state.
In the opposite case,
f=0
, the system is in an incoherent mixture

\frac{N}{2}|s_{1}\rangle\langle s_{1}|+\frac{N}{2}|s_{2}\rangle\langle s_{2}|
.

Based on this discussion, it is apparent that the parameter
f\in[0,1]
has the meaning of a
degree of coherence
. In our previous work
Korshynska and Ulbricht 2024
, we discussed this parameter as constant in time for non-interacting systems.
In what follows, the analysis of
f(t)
together with
Z(t)
will allow us to investigate the process of decoherence during Josephson oscillations in an interacting Bose gas.

V
Josephson effect

In the last Section we have obtained the population imbalance
Z(t)
and the degree of coherence
f(t)
, that
can be utilized to describe Josephson oscillations of a weakly self-interacting BEC in a double-well potential.
We now take this general result and apply it to the specific case, where all
N
particles initially occupy just a single (e.g., the left) well of the potential.
This describes a standard experimental scenario, where the BEC is initially loaded into a single well, using modern trap manipulation techniques
Albiez et al. 2005
.
In our model this gives rise to the
initial state of the system

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}(\cos\xi\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}+\sin\xi\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})^{N}|0\rangle\,,

(30)

where the operators
\hat{a}_{0/1}^{\dagger}
create particles in the ground or exited state, receptively. Thus, according to Eq. (
4
) the superposition
\cos\xi\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}+\sin\xi\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}
creates a boson in the left-well state
\psi_{L}
.
The corresponding initial condition for the density matrix reads

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}\rangle\langle\mathcal{L}|

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N!

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1}=0}^{N}\sum_{N_{1}^{\prime}=0}^{N}\frac{(\cos\xi)^{2N-N_{1}-N_{1}^{\prime}}(\sin\xi)^{N_{1}+N_{1}^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N_{1}!N_{1}^{\prime}!(N-N_{1})!(N-N_{1}^{\prime})!}}|N_{1}\rangle\langle N_{1}^{\prime}|\,,

where we sum over all many-particle energy eigenstates
|N_{1}\rangle
and
|N_{1}^{\prime}\rangle
, which are defined in Eq. (
6
).

V.1
Josephson dynamics without external acceleration

In what follows, we want to focus on the effect of decoherence due to collisional interactions.
Therefore, here we first consider Josephson oscillations in the absence of external acceleration.

To find the fractional population imbalance and the degree of coherence, we revisit Eqs. (
27
) and (
29
) using the
\rho^{0}_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}
that can be read of Eq. (
31
). In both equations the case of
\mathbf{a}=0
is parameterized by
\xi=\pi/4
.
This yields

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\cos\left[\frac{V}{\hbar}t\right]\right)^{N-1}\cos\left[\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}t\right]

(32)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg|\cos\left[\frac{V}{\hbar}t\right]\bigg|^{N-1}.

(33)

Starting at
Z(0)=1
(
N_{L}(0)=N
), Eq. (
32
) shows harmonic oscillations with frequency
\Delta E/\hbar
, as would be expected in the non-interacting case.
In the presence of collisional interactions, which enter our
formalism via the parameter
V
from Eq. (
13
),
these oscillations are modified by an additional, periodic function
\cos^{N-1}(Vt/\hbar)
. The absolute value of this function coincides with the degree of coherence (
33
).

In the considered regime of weak interactions (

) the period
\sim\hbar/V
is much longer than the time scale
\hbar/\Delta E
of Josephson dynamics in Eq. (
32
).
Thus, for times
t\lesssim\hbar/V
the degree of coherence appears as
a damping factor for the Josephson oscillations.
The decrease of
f(t)
with time is a signature of the decoherence of the initially coherent state (
31
), c.f. Ref.
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
.
Moreover, we find that after some time
t\gtrsim\hbar/V

the amplitude of Josephson oscillations in Eq. (
32
) is revived to its initial value, implying that the coherence is periodically restored.
This stems from the fact that in a closed system with conserved energy and entropy the initial system state is never forgotten.
On a technical level, this can be understood from the density matrix elements
\rho_{N_{1},N_{1}^{\prime}}(t)
, each evolving with its own phase, as displayed in Eq. (
18
).
These matrix elements interfere destructively, until some time, given by the largest period
\sim\hbar/V
involved, is reached.
Hence, the revival of the coherence
is a consequence of the discrete, finite energy spectrum (
19
), see also Refs.
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
;
Castin and Dalibard 1997
;
Wright et al. 1996
.

In experiment, however, the assumption that a BEC is a closed system typically does not hold over time scales
\sim\hbar/V
, due to the significant exchange of particles and energy with
the environment.
This provides an additional channel for decoherence, leads to a finite BEC lifetime
Griffin et al. 2009
, and prevents a revival of the oscillations.

To illustrate the Josephson dynamics and its decoherence,
described by Eqs. (
32
) and (

Masi et al. 2021

The interaction constant

\textrm{kg}\times\textrm{m}^{5}/\textrm{s}^{2}
to

\textrm{kg}\times\textrm{m}^{5}/\textrm{s}^{2}

Moreover, we assume a typical BEC of

In the Fig.

In contrast, for

V.2
Impact of acceleration

Having discussed the collisional decoherence in the absence of acceleration, we now analyze the interplay of both effects.
We further investigate the feasibility of the setup as a double-well accelerometer.

Considering again the scenario (
31

bosons initially
occupy the left well,
the population imbalance (

The Eq. (

II

V.3
Sensitivity of a BEC double-well accelerometer

We want to investigate the feasibility of the setup to sense small accelerations
a\ll\Delta E/(Lm)
by evaluating the period of the first few Josephson oscillations (
t\ll\hbar/V
). In this regime, Eq. (
34
) simplifies to

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\cos\left[\frac{V}{\hbar}t\right]\right)^{N-1}

(36)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\cos\left[\left(\Delta E-2(N-1)\frac{V}{\Delta E}\frac{\chi(\theta)}{L}Lma\right)\frac{t}{\hbar}\right]\,,

where
\chi(\theta)\approx 3L\cos\theta/2
.
Based on Eq. (
36
) we can make a general conclusion about the sensitivity of the system to the external acceleration.
For that purpose, we derive the change of oscillation period
\delta T=\frac{\partial T}{\partial a}\delta a
with acceleration, giving

\delta T=6\pi\hbar\frac{NVLm}{\Delta E^{3}}|\cos\theta|\delta a\,,

(37)

where we accounted for
N-1\approx N
.
The currently achievable precision of period measurement in experiments
Albiez et al. 2005
;
Spagnolli et al. 2017
is
\delta T/T=\frac{\Delta E}{2\pi\hbar}\,\delta T\sim 10^{-2}
. Thus, for the example setup introduced in Sec.
V.1
, and
g=g_{0}
, we obtain a potential resolution of

\delta a\sim 5\times 10^{-4}a_{\Earth}
for acceleration measurements, which was achieved in experiments with non-interacting BECs, e.g. in Ref.
Masi et al. 2021
. Eq. (
37
) tells us, how this sensitivity can be improved, for instance, by increasing the depth
U_{0}
of the trap, which would
rapidly decrease
\Delta E
and increase
V
, as shown in the Appendices
A
and
C
, respectively.
With that, already considering
U_{0}\to 2U_{0}
, provides a potential way to reach
\delta a\sim 10^{-6}a_{\Earth}
while keeping the relative precision
\delta T/T=10^{-2}
of period measurement fixed.
Sensitivities of
\delta a\sim 10^{-6}a_{\Earth}
and beyond are planned to be achieved with the experimental setups using BEC interferometry, as suggested in Refs.
Gersemann et al. 2020
;
Abend et al. 2016
.

The discussed acceleration measurement scheme relies on the linearity of the relation (
37
), which only holds up to a certain energy scale of
interactions
NV
.
Beyond that, the
Josephson oscillations are expected to become anharmonic, or even reach other dynamical regimes in which the relation between
\delta T
and
\delta a
may tremendously differ from Eq. (
37
).
In what follows, we investigate
the limits, which the energy scale
NV
sets on the applicability of our model.

VI
Comparison with the standard two state model

For the considered scenario of a BEC with weak interparticle interactions, the decoherence is a slow process compared to the time scale of Josephson dynamics, see Figs.
3
-
4
.
Therefore, within the timescale of the first few Josephson oscillations, the degree of coherence
can be approximately set to
f(t)=1
.
While this approximation does not account for the decoherence effects, we can test our prediction (
37
) for the shift of the oscillation period in a simpler framework, which accounts for the interaction non-perturbatively.
The assumption
f(t)=1
leads to the pure state model, suggested in Refs.
Smerzi et al. 1997
;
Raghavan et al. 1999
, where the whole many-particle system is described by a single state wave function
\Psi
, which obeys a Gross-Pitaevskii equation

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+U(\mathbf{r})+\delta U(\mathbf{r})+g|\Psi|^{2}\right]\Psi\,.

(38)

In our case,
U(\mathbf{r})
stands for the double-well trap geometry and
\delta U(\mathbf{r})=maz\cos(\theta)+max\sin(\theta)
for the additional potential due to acceleration
\mathbf{a}
, as introduced in Sec.
II
. Following Refs.
Smerzi et al. 1997
;
Raghavan et al. 1999
, the pure state for the double-well geometry can be approximated by
\Psi=\sqrt{N_{L}(t)}e^{i\varphi_{L}}\psi_{L}+\sqrt{N_{R}(t)}e^{i\varphi_{R}}\psi_{R}
, where
\psi_{L/R}
are the left/right well states from Eq. (
3
).
Plugging this ansatz in Eq. (
38
) and integrating the result with the left and right state
\int d\mathbf{r}\psi_{L/R}\times
leads to a system of two coupled differential equations

\displaystyle\dot{Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\sqrt{1-Z^{2}}\sin\phi

(39a)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\left(\Xi+\Lambda Z+\frac{Z}{\sqrt{1-Z^{2}}}\cos\phi\right)\,,

(39b)

which were introduced as
Josephson equations

for the population imbalance
Z(t)=(N_{L}(t)-N_{R}(t))/N
and the phase difference
\varphi(t)=\varphi_{R}(t)-\varphi_{L}(t)
in Refs.
Smerzi et al. 1997
;
Raghavan et al. 1999
.
In our case,
the parameters
\Xi=-3aLm\cos\theta/\Delta E
and
\Lambda=-2VN/\Delta E
characterize the impact of the acceleration and the interaction strength, respectively.
For more details on the derivation, see Appendix
D
.
In the regime of weak interactions (
|\Lambda|<1
) the solution of Eqs. (
39
) can be well approximated by harmonic oscillations with the frequency
\Delta E(1-\Lambda\Xi/2)/\hbar
, as we show in Appendix
D
. The shift
\Delta E\Lambda\Xi/(2\hbar)=3NVLma\cos\theta/(\hbar\Delta E)
is the same as in Eq. (
36
).
For larger interaction strengths
|\Lambda|\gtrsim 1

nonlinear effects become significant and the solution of (
39
) exhibits non-harmonic dynamics as discussed in Ref.
Raghavan et al. 1999
.
Hence,
|\Lambda|\gtrsim 1
, i.e.,
2VN<\Delta E
, sets an important constraint for the applicability of our model.
For the example setup considered in this paper, this corresponds to
g<30g_{0}
.

VII
Conclusions

In the present study, we investigated the dynamics of a Bose gas with weak collisional interactions, confined in a double-well potential. We built up our theoretical model from the two-state description of a single particle, which allowed us to construct the basis of many-particle energy eigenstates for the Bose gas. The latter were used to formulate the density matrix, whose evolution describes the dynamics of the double-well system.
The considered energy-preserving two-particle collisions
give rise to a small perturbation in the equation of motion for the density matrix
Blum 2012
.

Having found the evolution of the full
N
-particle density matrix, we derived the population imbalance and the degree of coherence to characterize the Josephson dynamics.
We quantitatively described how the collisions cause the system to decohere and the Josephson oscillations to die off.
These prominent features of collisional decoherence are illustrated, considering recent experiments with
39
K atoms
Masi et al. 2021
;
Fattori et al. 2008
.

We further analyzed the influence of an external acceleration on the Josephson dynamics and decoherence.
In particular, we studied the effect of small accelerations
a\lesssim 10^{-4}a_{\Earth}
, which have been previously accessed using BEC accelerometers
Masi et al. 2021
;
Gersemann et al. 2020
;
Abend et al. 2016
.
We found that the dominant effect is the shift of the period of Josephson oscillations, while the collisional decoherence does not notably depend on the acceleration.
Hence, by measuring the shift of the oscillation period, one can deduce the acceleration applied alongside the Josephson junction.
This idea of a BEC double-well accelerometer previously was investigated also in Refs.
Roy and Alon 2025
;
Sacchetti 2016

assuming a pure state, described by a fully coherent many-particle wave function.
For small accelerations, their numerical simulations show a
linear change of the oscillation period with acceleration.
This is in agreement with our analytical results, which also provide the
relation between oscillation period, interaction strength, and acceleration for a Bose gas in a given double-well geometry. In the limit of vanishing decoherence, our findings agree with the predictions of the pure state model of standard Josephson equations
Smerzi et al. 1997
;
Raghavan et al. 1999
.

While our results are formulated in general analytical expressions and can be applied to a wide range of physical scenarios,
they are obtained under the assumption of
weak interparticle interactions and only small accelerations. It would be an intriguing perspective to investigate the regimes of strong acceleration (
a\sim 10^{-3}a_{\Earth}
) and strong interaction (
a_{s}\sim 10^{2}a_{0}
), where the Josephson dynamics is expected to differ significantly, as implied by recent BEC experiments
Masi et al. 2021
;
Gersemann et al. 2020
;
Spagnolli et al. 2017
.

Appendix A
Potential geometry and single-particle states

The single particle Hamiltonian reads

\hat{h}_{0}(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}\right)+U_{x}(x)+U_{y}(y)+U_{z}(z)\,,

(40)

where the expressions for the potential components
U_{x}(x)
,
U_{y}(y)
and
U_{z}(z)
are illustrated by Fig.
1
.

For
U_{z}(z)
we have

U_{z}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\quad z\in[-\infty,-2L]\\
-U_{0}&\quad z\in[-2L,-L]\\
0&\quad z\in[-L,L]\\
-U_{0}&\quad z\in[L,2L]\\
0&\quad z\in[2L,+\infty]\,\,,\end{array}\right.\,

(41)

which is a double-bin potential, while in the other two dimensions we have trivial confinement

U_{x}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\quad x\in[-\infty,-L/2]\\
-U_{0}&\quad x\in[-L/2,L/2]\\
0&\quad x\in[L/2,+\infty]\,\,,\end{array}\right.\,

(42)

U_{y}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\quad y\in[-\infty,-L/2]\\
-U_{0}&\quad y\in[-L/2,L/2]\\
0&\quad y\in[L/2,+\infty]\,\,.\end{array}\right.\,

(43)

Thus, we end up with the two cubic potential traps, separated by the space of width
2L
. For such a choice of Hamiltonian the single-particle state wave function factorizes
\phi(x,y,z)=\phi_{x}(x)\phi_{y}(y)\phi_{z}(z)
and has the energy
E=E_{x}+E_{y}+E_{z}
. For convenience, we introduce dimensionless units of distance
x=L\tilde{x}
,
y=L\tilde{y}
and
z=L\tilde{z}
, dimensionless wave function components
\tilde{\phi}_{x,y,z}=\sqrt{L}\phi_{x,y,z}
(so that it is normalized as
\int|\tilde{\phi}_{x}|^{2}d\tilde{x}=1
). The dimensionless energy then reads
\tilde{E}_{x,y,z}=2mL^{2}/\hbar^{2}\times E_{x,y,z}
and dimensionless potential is
\tilde{U}_{0}=2mL^{2}/\hbar^{2}\times U_{0}
.
This gives us the dimensionless form of Schrodinger equation

\tilde{E}_{x,y,z}\tilde{\phi}_{x,y,z}=\left(-\partial_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z}}^{2}+\tilde{U}_{x,y,z}\right)\tilde{\phi}_{x,y,z}\,,

(44)

which for bound states gives negative energies
\tilde{E}_{x,y,z}=-\tilde{k}^{2}_{x,y,z}<0
(
\tilde{k}=Lk
is the dimensionless wavevector). Let’s for brevity denote
\tilde{\phi}_{x,y}=\tilde{\phi}_{\perp}
and
\tilde{E}_{x,y}=\tilde{E}_{\perp}
.

In transverse direction Eq. (
44

\tilde{\phi}_{\perp}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Ce^{\tilde{k}_{x}\tilde{x}}&\quad x\in[-\infty,-L/2]\\

Appendix B
Beyond two state model

One can generalize the two-state model to a many-state case. Assume, we have
k>2
single-particle energy eigenstates
\phi_{i}
in a double-well trap. In the non-interacting limit this gas is described by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}E_{i}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\,,

(45)

where
E_{i}
are the eigenenergies corresponding to the
\phi_{i}
states. The state of the system is then described by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}\rho_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}|\{N_{i}\}\rangle\langle\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}|

(46)

\displaystyle|\{N_{i}\}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{0}!N_{1}!...N_{k}!}}\left(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\right)^{N_{0}}...\left(\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)^{N_{k}}|0\rangle\,,

(47)

where each many-particle state
|\{N_{i}\}\rangle
is characterized by a set of occupations
\{N_{i}\}=\{N_{0},...,N_{k}\}
of all energy eigenstates. The interaction operator in this case reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}V_{ijkl}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{l}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}V_{iiii}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{i}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\left[V_{ijij}+V_{ijji}+V_{jiij}+V_{jiji}\right]\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{a}_{j}\,,

where we assumed that the energy level scheme
E_{i}
is such that the energy conservation is satisfied only if the two outgoing bosons have the same energies as the ingoing bosons or if they ’exchange’ their energies (analogously to Fig.
2
).

Solving Liouville equation (
15
) we find the solution
\rho_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}=\rho^{0}_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}\exp[i\omega_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}t]
, where
\omega_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}=(\mathcal{E}_{\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}}-\mathcal{E}_{\{N_{i}\}}-\mathcal{V}_{\{N_{i}\},\{N_{i}^{\prime}\}})/\hbar
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{b}-\mathcal{E}_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(N_{i}^{\prime}-N_{i})E_{i}

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{ab}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}V_{iiii}\left(N_{i}(N_{i}-1)-N_{i}^{\prime}(N_{i}^{\prime}-1)\right)

(49)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}V_{ijij}\left(N_{i}N_{j}-N_{i}^{\prime}N_{j}^{\prime}\right)

In analogy with the two-state case we introduce the optimal left/right well basis, which allows us to define population imbalance in a symmetric double-well

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{N}\sum_{\kappa,\eta}\langle\phi_{2(\kappa-1)}|\phi_{2\eta-1}\rangle_{L}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}\left[\alpha_{2(\kappa-1),2\eta-1}^{0}e^{i\omega_{2(\kappa-1),2\eta-1}t}\right],

in the non-interacting case. Here
\langle\phi_{i}|\phi_{j}\rangle_{L}=\int dx_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy_{\infty}^{+\infty}dz_{-\infty}^{0}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
is the left well overlap of the two single-particle states. This result illustrates that the role of the mixing
\rho^{0}_{i,j}
between an even
i=2(\kappa-1)
and an odd
j=2\eta-1
states is weighted by the left overlap between them.
