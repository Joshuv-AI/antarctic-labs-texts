The prognostic significance of the IASLC grading system in ALK‐positive invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma of the lung: correlation with clinicopathological features
Authors: Dongmei Gu, Jie Bao, Hong Chen, Siyi Chen, Chenxi Shi, Xia Guo, Xiaoyu Zhou, Zhe Lei, Lingchuan Guo, Qianqian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prognostic value of the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)‐positive invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma (INMA) remains unclear, particularly in relation to the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). This retrospective study included 108 patients with ALK‐positive INMA. Tumors were graded according to the IASLC system. Clinicopathological features, progression‐free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) were analyzed. Immunohistochemistry was used to assess PD‐L1 expression and tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), including CD4+, CD8+, and FoxP3+ regulatory T cells. Associations were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, Cox regression models, and correlation analyses. Higher IASLC grades were significantly associated with more aggressive pathological features, elevated PD‐L1 expression, and shorter progression‐free survival (PFS). Higher‐grade tumors exhibited enhanced T‐cell infiltration, predominantly driven by CD8+ T cells, while no significant difference in FoxP3+ Treg density was observed across grades. Notably, the CD4:CD8 ratio decreased, whereas the CD8:FoxP3 ratio increased with tumor grade, indicating a shift toward a CD8‐dominant immune microenvironment. Importantly, this cytotoxic predominance occurred in parallel with elevated PD‐L1 expression, suggesting an immune context consistent with adaptive immune resistance rather than effective anti‐tumor immunity. The IASLC grading system effectively identifies a high‐risk subgroup of ALK‐positive INMA characterized by aggressive tumor behavior and a dynamically regulated immune microenvironment. High‐grade tumors exhibit features of immune engagement coupled with checkpoint‐mediated regulation, supporting a model of adaptive immune resistance. These findings underscore the necessity of combination therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target ALK and immune checkpoint modulation in high‐grade INMA.Keywords: International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC), the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), lung invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma (INMA), PD‐L1 expression, prognostic stratification, T lymphocyte

Introduction

According to the IARC's GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide, with nearly 2.5 million new cases (12.4% of all cancers globally), and also the leading cause of cancer death, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths (18.7% of total cancer mortality) [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma, the most common histological type of lung cancer, exhibits diverse histological patterns and proportions [2], with approximately 70% of patients already at an advanced stage when initially diagnosed [3]. To provide a common path for prognostic stratification of patients with invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma, a systematic grading system was proposed by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) pathology committee in 2020, based on the predominant and high‐grade patterns of tumor [4]. In the following year, the grading system was adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO, 5th edition) to guide the grading of invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma (INMA) [2], due to its superior precision compared to previous systems that relied solely on a single predominant growth pattern. However, the IASLC grading criteria have not yet been widely incorporated into treatment decision‐making due to a lack of sufficient clinical data and evidence.

Gene fusions are a major class of oncogenic drivers across multiple cancer types. Among these, the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), a receptor tyrosine kinase critical for cellular growth, differentiation, and survival [5], was identified in 2007 as aberrantly fused with echinoderm microtubule‐associated protein‐like 4 (EML4) gene in a subset of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients [6]. This chromosomal rearrangement results in constitutive activation of ALK, thereby driving oncogenic signaling and promoting tumor progression [6]. Patients with ALK‐positive NSCLC not only present with an earlier onset but also face a substantially higher risk of developing brain metastases [7]. The median interval from initial diagnosis to intracranial progression is remarkably short, at only 88 days [8], contributing to a significant increase in mortality. ALK‐targeted therapies, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), have been proven to be effective in treating ALK‐positive NSCLC [9]. However, not all patients benefit from ALK‐TKI therapy (primary resistance) [10], and most patients who do initially respond will ultimately develop resistance (acquired resistance) through various mechanisms [11]. Studies suggest that overexpression of the ALK fusion protein upregulates PD‐L1 expression [12], and to explore the changes of the patient's TIME may reveal a promising therapeutic option in this setting [13], which warrants further attention.

It has been verified that the IASLC grading system can stratify prognosis in EGFR‐mutated lung adenocarcinoma and is associated with the TIME [14]. Although ALK‐positive lung adenocarcinoma is also driven by driver mutations and exhibits characteristic alterations in the TIME, there is currently no clinical evidence establishing the applicability of the IASLC grading system for prognostic analysis in this specific patient population. Herein, we applied the updated IASLC grading criteria to classify 108 ALK‐positive INMA patients treated at our institution and further examined the relationship between the new grading system and clinicopathological features, prognosis, PD‐L1 expression levels, and lymphocyte infiltration. Particular focus was placed on elucidating the prognostic and potential predictive value of this grading system for immunotherapy in ALK‐positive INMA patients.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

General information

This retrospective cohort study included 108 patients with ALK‐positive INMA who were treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between March 2019 and May 2022. Data collected included gender, age, smoking history, tumor location, pleural invasion status, tumor size, lymph node and distant metastasis status, and TNM staging (supplementary material, Table S1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed INMA; (2) underwent lobectomy with lymph node dissection; (3) ALK positivity confirmed by immunohistochemistry; and (4) complete clinicopathological data are available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) non‐primary lung tumors; (2) non‐surgical specimens (e.g., biopsy only); and (3) incomplete clinicopathological data.

Staging and diagnostic criteria

The TNM staging followed the 9th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system. Histological classification was based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Thoracic Tumors, 5th edition.

Follow‐up

Follow‐up was conducted via telephone interviews and electronic medical record reviews. The last follow‐up date was July 24, 2024. Primary endpoints included: progression‐free survival (PFS), defined as the time from diagnosis to disease progression, and overall survival (OS), defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause.

Study design

In this study, 108 patients with ALK‐positive INMA were included. These patients were graded according to the updated IASLC grading system. We retrospectively analyzed the relationship between the new IASLC grading system and clinicopathological characteristics and evaluated its association with patient prognosis and features of the tumor immune microenvironment (Figure 1), aiming to provide evidence for guiding prognostic stratification and immunotherapy strategies.

Methods

Evaluation using the IASLC grading system

All the included cases were ALK‐positive INMA (Figure 2A: middle and right). The histological components comprised lepidic, acinar, and papillary patterns (Figure 2B: left, middle, and right), as well as cribriform, micropapillary, and complex glandular patterns (Figure 2C: left, middle, and right), and solid patterns (Figure 2D: left). The solid pattern was confirmed as adenocarcinoma and ruled out for poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma based on immunohistochemistry, showing positivity for CK7, TTF‐1, and Napsin A, and negativity for CK5/6, p40, and p63 (Figure 2D, middle: TTF‐1+, right: p40‐). According to the IASLC grading system [4], the cases were classified into three grades. Grade 1: lepidic predominant tumor with no or less than 20% high‐grade patterns; grade 2: acinar or papillary predominant tumor, both with no or less than 20% of high‐grade patterns; and grade 3: any tumor with 20% or more of high‐grade patterns (solid, micropapillary, or complex gland).

Immunohistochemical staining

Sections (4–5 μm thick) were baked at 60–65 °C for 30–60 min. Fully automated immunohistochemical staining was performed using the VENTANA BenchMark ULTRA system. After staining, slides were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. The antibodies used included: ALK (790‐4797, ready‐to‐use, Ventana®), TTF‐1 (11833669A, 1:100, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China), p40 (25081519, 1:100, Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA), Ki‐67 (GT209429, 1:100, Gene Tech Company Limited, Shanghai, China), PD‐L1 (SK006, ready‐to‐use, Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA), CD4 (IR649, 1:100, Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA), CD8 (ZA‐0508, 1:100, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China), and FoxP3 (sc‐53876, 1:100, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA).

Immunohistochemical assessment

Any intensity of cytoplasmic ALK staining of tumor cells was deemed positive. Nuclear staining was considered positive for K‐i67. The Ki‐67 labeling index was calculated as the percentage of positive tumor nuclei among total tumor cells. For PD‐L1 (22C3), a minimum of 100 viable tumor cells were assessed. Any perceptible partial or complete membranous staining of tumor cells at any intensity was considered positive, consistent with the Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) methodology. TPS was calculated as: (number of PD‐L1‐positive tumor cells/total number of viable tumor cells) × 100%. TPS <1% was defined as negative expression. TPS ≥1% was defined as positive expression, further categorized as low expression (TPS 1–49%) or high expression (TPS ≥50%).

The densities of CD4+, CD8+, and FoxP3+ tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were quantified as the mean number of positively stained cells per high‐power field (HPF, 400× magnification). For each case, 10 non‐overlapping HPFs were selected using a systematic sampling strategy to ensure representative coverage of the tumor microenvironment, rather than hotspot‐based selection. The evaluated fields encompassed both the tumor center and representative viable tumor regions. Areas with necrosis, tissue artifacts, or poor preservation were strictly excluded. All slides were independently assessed by two experienced pathologists (QY and DG), who were blinded to clinical and outcome data to minimize observational bias.

Statistical analysis

The association between the updated IASLC grading system and clinicopathological characteristics was assessed using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan‐Meier method, and the Log‐rank test was used to evaluate the association between IASLC grade and patient PFS/OS. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using the Cox proportional hazards model for included clinicopathological variables. The Spearman correlation test was used to analyze the relationship between PD‐L1 expression (TPS) and IASLC grades. Interobserver agreement as assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement. The Kruskal–Wallis test was performed to compare the differences in lymphocyte infiltration (CD4+, CD8+, FoxP3+ cells) and the CD4:CD8, CD8:FoxP3 ratio among the different IASLC grades. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological correlations of elevated IASLC grade

Among the 108 ALK‐positive INMA patients, 16 (14.81%) were classified as grade 1, 47 (43.52%) as grade 2, and 45 (41.67%) as grade 3 according to the updated IASLC grading system (Table 1). The IASLC grade was significantly correlated with Ki‐67 index, pleural involvement, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and TNM stage (p < 0.05; Table 1, Figure 3). Specifically, the Ki‐67 index was significantly higher in grade 2 and grade 3 tumors compared to grade 1 tumors (Figure 3A). Pleural invasion and tumor size >3 cm were significantly more frequent in grade 3 tumors than in grade 2 tumors (Figure 3B; Table 1). Lymph node metastasis and TNM stage III–IV were also significantly more common in grade 3 tumors than in grades 1 and 2 (Figure 3B,C; Table 1). The incidence of distant metastasis showed an increasing trend with higher IASLC grade, and the difference was statistically significant (Figure 3C; Table 1). These findings suggest that higher IASLC grade is indicative of more aggressive tumor behavior, which is consistent with existing research [15].

Survival analysis of higher IASLC grade ALK‐positive INMAs

Among the 108 ALK‐positive NSCLC patients followed up until July 24, 2024, disease progression occurred in 18 cases (grade 1:grade 2:grade 3 = 0:7:11; 0%:38.9%:61.1%, respectively) and death occurred in 7 cases (grade 1:grade 2:grade 3 = 0:2:5; 0%:28.6%:71.4%, respectively), indicating an increase in both progression and mortality with higher IASLC grade. In the study cohort, the median PFS was 34 months and the median OS was 35 months. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with Grade 3 tumors had significantly worse PFS compared to those with Grade 1 tumors (p < 0.05). Although differences in PFS and OS among other grade comparisons did not reach statistical significance, there was a consistent downward trend in both PFS and OS with increasing IASLC grade (Figure 4), suggesting a trend toward poorer prognosis in patients with higher‐grade tumors.

Disease progression and IASLC grade

Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate the prognostic value of clinical characteristics for both PFS and OS. Univariate analysis for PFS identified IASLC grade, PD‐L1 expression, TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, pleural involvement, and distant metastasis as factors significantly associated with PFS. Our results indicated that each one‐grade increase in the IASLC classification was associated with a 2.58‐fold higher risk of disease progression (HR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.13–5.89, p = 0.024). In contrast, age, gender, smoking history, and tumor size showed no significant correlation with PFS in ALK‐positive INMA. Multivariate Cox analysis, which included clinicopathological parameters with P <0.05 from the univariate analysis, revealed that only TNM stage remained an independent adverse prognostic factor for PFS (Table 2).

Association of high IASLC grade with increased PD‐L1 expression

As shown in the representative histological images, we observed that higher IASLC grades were associated with a significant increase in tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (Figure 6A), suggesting a potential link between tumor grade and the TIME. To further investigate this relationship within our cohort, we analyzed the correlations between IASLC grade and PD‐L1 expression, as well as the densities of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes. PD‐L1, an immune checkpoint molecule and a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy in INMA patients, is primarily expressed on tumor cells. In this study, patients with high PD‐L1 expression (TPS ≥50%) had a significantly higher risk of disease progression (HR = 7.469, 95% CI: 1.349–41.364, p = 0.021) (Table 2). However, it is also well established that patients with high PD‐L1 expression (TPS ≥50%) are more likely to benefit from PD‐L1 blocking [16]. Therefore, identifying this patient subgroup is of significant clinical importance for treatment selection.

Correlations of higher IASLC grade with CD8+ T cells

We next assessed the relationship between IASLC grade groups and the types of infiltrating lymphocytes using immunohistochemistry. Interobserver agreement was good for CD4 (ICC = 0.786) and CD8 (ICC = 0.888), and moderate for FoxP3 (ICC = 0.641), indicating overall acceptable reproducibility. The results demonstrated a significant increase in both CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte counts with progressively higher grades (Figure 6B,C,E; KW p adj < 0.05). To further characterize the functional subsets of CD4+ T cells, we analyzed FoxP3+ regulatory T (Treg) cells, a key immunosuppressive T cell population. No significant difference in FoxP3+ Treg cells was observed across the IASLC grade groups (Figure 6D,E; KW p adj = 0.161). In addition to the CD4:CD8 ratio, which progressively decreased with increasing tumor grade, we further analyzed the CD8:FoxP3 ratio to better characterize the balance between cytotoxic and regulatory T‐cell populations (supplementary material, Table S2). Notably, the CD8:FoxP3 ratio showed a corresponding increase with advancing tumor grade (Fig. 6F), suggesting that poorly differentiated, higher‐grade tumors are characterized by a more pronounced CD8+ cytotoxic T cell–dominant infiltration, consistent with existing research [17].

Discussion

Existing research has primarily examined the association between the IASLC grading system and pulmonary adenocarcinoma, with focus on specific ALK fusion subtypes [18]. In this study, we first analyzed the clinicopathological characteristics of ALK‐positive lung INMA using the IASLC grading system. Results revealed that a higher IASLC grade was significantly associated with features indicative of greater pathological aggressiveness. Specifically, elevated IASLC grades were inversely associated with a higher Ki‐67 index, pleural invasion, larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and advanced TNM stage. Our findings extend the established prognostic value of the IASLC grading system from EGFR‐mutated [14] to ALK‐positive lung adenocarcinoma, validating its broad applicability.

To further validate its prognostic stratification value, we conducted patient follow‐up and performed Kaplan‐Meier analysis along with Cox proportional hazards analysis based on IASLC grades. Follow‐up results revealed that a higher IASLC grade is associated with an increased risk of both disease progression and mortality, with the highest risk observed in grade 3 patients. Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis confirmed significantly worse PFS in patients with grade 3 tumors compared to grade 1. Univariate Cox analysis showed that each one‐grade increase in the IASLC classification was associated with a higher risk of disease progression, though this association did not remain an independent prognostic risk factor in subsequent multivariate analysis. Although the association with overall survival (OS) did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited number of events and relatively short follow‐up duration, a consistent trend toward poorer outcomes with higher grade was observed. Collectively, these data support the use of IASLC grading as a clinically relevant risk stratification parameter in ALK‐positive INMA, particularly for predicting disease progression.

Our analysis also identified other risk factors, high PD‐L1 expression (verified by univariate Cox analyses for PFS), which showed a significant positive correlation with IASLC grade in the following Spearman analysis. It has been reported that high PD‐L1 expression in ALK‐rearranged NSCLC is associated with diminished efficacy of both immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [19] and ALK TKIs [20], leading to a worse OS. Therefore, identifying such high‐risk population and seeking alternative treatments is of critical importance. Our study indicates that this high‐risk phenotype is present in tumors of IASLC grade 2 or 3. Thus, implementing PD‐L1 screening specifically for patients with higher‐grade groups is a key clinical consideration.

The malignant potential of a tumor is shaped by both the intrinsic properties of the cancer cells and extrinsic factors [21]. Here, the high expression of PD‐L1 combined with ALK rearrangement contributes to the high malignancy of tumor cells. Do such high‐risk patients have something special in extrinsic factors that could offer novel therapeutic opportunities?

We then focused on the TIME, a key extrinsic factor, driven by our findings that higher IASLC grades were associated with a marked increase in TILs and the evidence suggesting the key role of TILs in regulating the tumor‐host response and influencing micrometastases [22]. Previous studies have suggested that PD‐L1 upregulation in lung adenocarcinoma is associated with reduced activity of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells through multiple signaling pathways, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [23]. We next performed immunohistochemical profiling of major T‐lymphocyte subsets. With increasing tumor grade, both CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell infiltration increased, whereas FoxP3+ regulatory T‐cell density remained unchanged. Notably, the CD4:CD8 ratio progressively decreased, while the CD8:FoxP3 ratio increased, indicating a shift toward a CD8+ T cell‐dominant immune contexture. However, this apparent cytotoxic predominance occurred in parallel with elevated PD‐L1 expression, suggesting that high‐grade tumors may exist under active immune pressure accompanied by checkpoint‐mediated regulation. This pattern is consistent with prior observations that T cell–inflamed tumors frequently exhibit compensatory upregulation of inhibitory pathways, resulting in functional restraint of cytotoxic T‐cell activity despite increased immune infiltration [24, 25, 26]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the immune microenvironment of high‐grade ALK‐positive INMA reflects a dynamically regulated equilibrium, characterized by concurrent immune activation and tumor‐driven immune evasion. This nuanced immune context provides a biological rationale for combination therapeutic strategies aimed at both enhancing T‐cell function and relieving checkpoint‐mediated inhibition.

These findings underscore that quantitative immune metrics alone are insufficient to infer functional anti‐tumor immunity, emphasizing the necessity of integrating immune checkpoint status when interpreting TIL composition. High‐grade tumors are not immune‐cold, but immune‐engaged and checkpoint‐restrained.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this is a single‐center retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the relatively small number of OS events restricts the statistical power for survival analyses, particularly for detecting independent prognostic effects. Third, the assessment of the TIME was limited to quantitative immunohistochemical evaluation of selected lymphocyte subsets and PD‐L1 expression, without functional characterization of immune cell states or incorporation of additional immune checkpoints. Future studies incorporating multi‐parameter immune profiling, and functional assays will be necessary to more fully elucidate the immunological landscape of ALK‐positive INMA.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the IASLC grading system effectively stratifies prognosis and is associated with an immune profile characterized by increased tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, a decreased CD4:CD8 ratio, and an increased CD8:FoxP3 ratio in ALK‐positive INMA. Despite this cytotoxic predominance, concurrent PD‐L1 upregulation suggests a tumor microenvironment consistent with adaptive immune resistance, indicating that high‐grade tumors are immune‐engaged yet checkpoint‐regulated rather than immune‐cold. These findings support the potential benefit of integrating ALK‐targeted therapy with immune checkpoint modulation in higher‐grade tumors, warranting further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies.
