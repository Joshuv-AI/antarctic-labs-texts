Flow boiling-driven intensive growth of critical minerals during short-lived events
Authors: Shen Gao, Atsushi Okamoto, Satoshi Matsuno, Edward L. Vinis, Otgonbayar Dandar, Xinyu Zou, Ross N. Mitchell, Jiajun Liu, Kezhang Qin, Hong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Enrichment of critical metal tellurium (Te) in shallow crustal settings is often linked to hydrothermal boiling, yet the mechanisms driving Te-silica co-precipitation under dynamic fluid flow conditions remain unresolved. Here, we experimentally demonstrate that boiling during fluid flow triggers intensive enrichment of Te and silica at rates >14× faster than non-boiling systems. Boiling induces volatile loss, destabilizing Te complexes, promoting silica polymerization, and facilitating nucleation and growth of native Te alongside amorphous silica. In contrast, non-boiling systems exhibit minimal Te and silica deposition. Boiling in dynamic hydrothermal systems thus drives mineralization through kinetic processes at amorphous silica sites for nucleation, low vapor/liquid regimes with short residence times, and fast growth rates that bypass classical equilibrium controls. Short-lived geologic events within the shallow crust may trigger such a process. Thus, our results offer a framework for reinterpreting Te-bearing epithermal deposits and optimizing exploration strategies for green technology resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32193-6.

Introduction

Critical elements for renewable energy technologies, such as tellurium1–5, are highly enriched in low-salinity hydrothermal gold–silver (Au–Ag) deposits hosted by quartz veins (< 2 km)6–8, despite tellurium’s low abundance in the Earth’s crust (~ 1 μg kg⁻1)9. In shallow hydrothermal systems, fluid flow along fractures and faults plays an essential role in the precipitation of silica and metals within the upper crust, as is evident in the shaping and maintenance of geothermal systems10–13 and the occurrence of recurring earthquakes14. During the formation of metal deposits, boiling is often invoked to explain metal’s highly efficient deposition, particularly for Au and Ag15–20. However, the relationships between fluid dynamics, silica precipitation, and critical element Te enrichment remain poorly understood and key controversies persist. First, while predicted Te content is low in aqueous solutions21, high concentrations of Te (~ 3 mg kg⁻1) have been found in geothermal deep well solutions, suggesting supersaturation in current thermodynamic databases18. Second, thermodynamic models predict the partitioning of Te into vapor phases during boiling (e.g., H₂Te)6,22,23, yet natural open systems preserve abundant Te-bearing minerals in epithermal quartz veins (e.g., Acupan, Emperor, Cripple Creek, and Sandaowanzi; Fig. 1a–c; Supplementary Fig. 1a)6,24–26. Third, Te-rich ores lack textures indicative of equilibrium growth, implying non-equilibrium pathways for the formation of high-grade ores (e.g., nanomelt transportation and colloidal aggregation; Fig. 1d–f)22,27,28. Therefore, several questions arise. How does Te precipitation relate to silica precipitation in hydrothermal systems? And what is the optimal timing, pressure–temperature (P–T) conditions (water state), and mechanisms in the formation of Te-rich ores?Fig. 1Tellurium-silica co-precipitation ores in natural epithermal systems. In this work, we address the issues through flow-through experiments that replicate boiling and non-boiling during fluid flow conditions in epithermal environments29–33, a hallmark of natural hydrothermal systems that provide dynamic process34–36. Natural samples of Au–Ag–Te-rich ores from the epithermal Sandaowanzi deposit in China8,25,37 were used to conduct flow-through experiments via dissolution and precipitation processes33. Based on the products observed under various fluid flow conditions, our experiments conclude that flow boiling in dynamic hydrothermal systems facilitates the rapid growth of native tellurium and silica through kinetic processes that circumvent classical equilibrium controls. This study redefines boiling during fluid flow as a crucial mechanism for the enrichment of the critical metal Te with fast growth rates that can be caused by short-lived geologic events, such as earthquakes (coseismic boiling14,38), offering a framework to reinterpret Te-bearing epithermal deposits and enhance exploration strategies for high-grade resources essential to green technologies.

Results

Samples and solutions

Dissolution experiments were conducted to load the fluid with Te and silica, followed by precipitation experiments from the boiling fluid. The flow-through apparatus used for dissolution and precipitation experiments29,30,33 (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 2–4). Natural ores formed in an epithermal environment can be characterized by low-salinity fluids7. In fluids, the solubility of quartz does not vary significantly between H2O and 3 wt.% NaCl solutions (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Therefore, distilled water was used for dissolution, corresponding to the quartz solubility39,40. Sample (130CM23-5) was collected from the Sandaowanzi deposit in NE China and dissolved to make the initial solution (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The mineral assemblages in the sample are Au–Ag tellurides, native gold, and quartz7,8. The initial solution consisted of 236.3 mg kg⁻1 Si, 0.02 mg kg⁻1 S, and 2,760 μg kg⁻1 of Te (Fig. 2b, c). The first precipitation reaction (one run in the experiment) was held at 9 MPa, spanning the 280–350 °C gradient (Fig. 2b). Under these conditions, H2O crosses the liquid–vapor saturation curve (boiling) during fluid flow, and therefore the density drop of H2O (from 0.71 to 0.05 g cm⁻3) reliably precipitates silica and possibly other species (Fig. 2b). In the boiling experiment, Si concentration in solution decreased from the initial 236.3 mg kg⁻1 to 0.5 mg kg⁻1, directly corresponding to the change in the quartz solubility (from 248 mg kg⁻1 at 280 °C to 1 mg kg⁻1 at 350 °C) and indicating precipitation39,40. Additionally, S dropped from 2.9 to 0.1 mg kg⁻1, and Te from 2,760 to 7 μg kg⁻1 (Fig. 2b). A second precipitation reaction (one run in the experiment) was conducted at 25 MPa to observe mineral precipitation at higher pressure within the same temperature range (Fig. 2c). Here, H2O is below the liquid–vapor saturation curve (non-boiling and the density of H2O shifts from 0.78 to 0.63 g cm⁻3; Fig. 2c). Silica minerals have high solubility at these temperatures compared to boiling conditions (Fig. 2c). In the non-boiling experiment, changes in solution chemistry indicated that both precipitation and dissolution occurred. Silicon concentration in solution increased from 236.3 to 319.3 mg kg⁻1, also corresponding to the quartz solubility curve (from 264 mg kg⁻1 at 280 °C to 387 mg kg⁻1 at 350 °C). Conversely, S concentration slightly decreased from 2.9 to 2.4 mg kg⁻1 (Fig. 2c) and Te more significantly from 2,760 to 1,666 μg kg⁻1 (Fig. 2c). Calculations of the saturation index for quartz, chalcedony, and amorphous silica, based on input and output solutions, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 5b. Quartz has the highest saturation index, while amorphous silica has the lowest. The input solution at low pressure (9 MPa) shows high saturation index for both quartz and chalcedony. Contamination during the dissolution experiments from the Inconel reaction tube is possible (Inconel alloy 600 contains Ni, Cr, Fe, and Cu), and as such corresponding elements were not evaluated. Precipitation experiments utilized a vessel composed of 99.6% Al2O3 (heat resistant up to 1,800 °C), thus Al is unlikely to be leached under these conditions. Boiling enhances Te-silica co-precipitation

As fluid flows across the boiling transition from liquid-dominated to vapor-dominated regimes (Fig. 2b), the mineral precipitations are characterized from bubbly- to mist-type flow regimes41,42 and have corresponding locations in the flow pathway (upstream to downstream) in two-phase fluid flow systems (Fig. 3a). In our experiment, the regimes from bubbly to plug flow (the main liquid and vapor separation area) generate amorphous silica and crystalline native tellurium (Fig. 3f–g). The largest quantities of amorphous silica are found in this regime (bubbly to plug) and consist of spherical shapes (Fig. 3f–g). In some instances, neighboring silica particles were cemented together (Fig. 3g). Native tellurium formed bladed or prismatic crystals (up to 47 μm in length), accounting for over 90% of the total Te here, and are embedded within amorphous silica matrices (Fig. 3f–g). A small quantity of Ag–Te minerals, such as hessite, also precipitated under these conditions. The transition from the annular to mist flow regimes (vapor-dominated area) resulted in the formation of native tellurium nanoparticles (Fig. 3b–c). Less native tellurium and amorphous silica growth occurred in the annular to mist flow regimes. The native tellurium grew small (mostly < 400 nm; Fig. 3d–e) with an irregular shape and occurred close to the downstream regime above the water saturation curve (Fig. 3c). The native tellurium particles became embedded in the amorphous silica (Fig. 3c). Discussion

Mechanistic framework for Te-silica enrichment

The co-precipitation of Te and amorphous silica is associated with the thermodynamic loss of non-Te-bearing volatiles and non-equilibrium processes. Our boiling experimental results indicate that Te does not experience significant loss during fluid flow due to the substantial presence of native tellurium products. The reason is likely attributed to the complex H₂TeO₃43, which forms during the experimental processes at pH = 4 (Supplementary Table 1). Under these conditions, the thermodynamic degassing of gases such as SO₂, CO₂, H₂, O₂, and H₂O15,17 destabilizes Te–H–O complexes, exemplified by the reaction H₂TeO₃(aq) = Te + H₂O(gas) + O₂(gas). In this context, the precipitation of native tellurium is linked to a decrease in oxygen fugacity on pH-fO₂ phase diagrams43–45. However, equilibrium processes cannot account for the formation of high-grade Te ores in natural systems. Instead, Te enrichment is proposed to occur above equilibrium levels. Non-equilibrium processes are associated with the precipitation of amorphous silica, fluid dynamic conditions, and growth pathways during flow boiling, which will be discussed further below.

Amorphous silica provides nucleation sites for Te, with adsorption driving co-precipitation during boiling (Fig. 3d–e). In silicon-rich systems, flow boiling induces a decrease in the solubility of silica minerals and promotes polymerization at temperatures of 303–304 °C and a pressure of 9 MPa39,40, resulting in the formation of silica colloids (Fig. 3). The silica colloids have the potential to adsorb Te, leading to the formation of nanoparticles and crystals on their surfaces, as indicated by scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM–EDS) data. Tellurium preferentially precipitated on amorphous silica rather than on other surfaces, such as Ti mesh or alumina walls, during the boiling experiment (Fig. 3f–g). Silica colloid surfaces exhibit a negative surface charge46,47, which facilitates the adsorption of positively charged Te species. Amorphous silica serves as a site for Te adsorption, whereas no adsorption occurs between Te and the Ti mesh or alumina walls. The concentration of Te in the global ocean is likely controlled by scavenging on Fe–Mn colloids in the water column and Fe–Mn deposits on the ocean floor48. The adsorption process is further supported by observations of other minerals for Te in hydrothermal systems, even if they are not colloids. Surface charge likely explains the affinity of Te for silica and clay minerals. For example, Te(IV) and Te(VI) adsorb onto ferrihydrite, goethite, and illite from soils49. This case further supports the argument that Te adsorbs onto negatively charged surfaces. Furthermore, solution chemistry demonstrates that the precipitation of Te and silica occurs in similar proportions ((Cinput-Coutput)/Cinput of 99.7% and 99.8%, respectively; Fig. 2b). The proportions are consistent with mineral assemblages in natural ores, where native tellurium or Te-bearing minerals usually coexist with quartz (Fig. 1). The formation of quartz is derived from amorphous silica over time, influenced by the flow of silicon-bearing fluids in both experimental and natural settings29,50,51. Additionally, the process of silica precipitation outpaces equilibrium mineral growth, producing banded Te-silica textures similar to those found in pipe sinters52. The observation indicates that silica precipitation creates a space for Te precipitation, which is subsequently followed by quartz maturation due to fluid flow.

Liquid-dominated regimes promote the intensive growth of native tellurium during two-phase fluid flow. First, in regimes characterized by low flow quality (the mass flow rate of vapor relative to the total mass flow rate) and low void fraction (the volume of vapor relative to the total volume), the liquid exhibits a relatively high mass flow rate during the aforementioned gas loss (e.g., H₂O, O₂ gases). Based on the locations and shapes of the products, we calculated the potential flow quality, void fraction, and slip ratios (linear velocity of vapor versus liquid) at 9 MPa during the boiling experiment (Fig. 5a)41,42. High flow quality and void fraction in two-phase fluids correspond to nano-sized Te particles in annular to mist flow regimes (most < 400 nm; Figs. 3b–c), whereas low flow quality and void fraction correspond to crystalline Te in bubbly to plug flow regimes (4–20 μm; Figs. 3f–g). For example, when the void fraction is 0.5 (with slip ratios = 1), the flow quality is approximately 0.07 (Fig. 5a). In other words, the mass flow rate for the liquid is about 0.93 based on the calculations. Second, boiling during fluid flow significantly reduces residence times to 1.5–1.8 s, compared to 27–28 s in non-boiling conditions (Fig. 5b). Consequently, the short fluid-rock interaction times inhibit the re-dissolution of silica50 and Te, thereby favoring the formation of metastable Te-silica aggregates. Due to the high solubility of silica, Si can dissolve over time, even if only a small amount precipitates within the experimental temperature range of 280–350 °C, and the re-dissolution may also apply to Te. Specifically, flow boiling decreases fluid residence times to 1.5–1.8 s, enhancing the Te-silica precipitation rate by over 14 times, as indicated in the calculations (Fig. 5b). The range of residence times can extend to open systems at shallow depths below the critical point of water, due to density variations at different temperatures that facilitate liquid and vapor separation (Fig. 5b). For instance, the pressure required for liquid and vapor separation is 6.4 MPa at 280 °C and 1.6 MPa at 200 °C. Given the low salinity in shallow environments (less than 5 wt. %), the pressure required for liquid and vapor separation does not shift significantly. In contrast, non-boiling conditions exhibit longer residence times, which may lead to the re-dissolution of silica (increasing Si concentration in the output solutions) at higher temperature ranges that encompass most fluid systems above intrusions.

Third, boiling during fluid flow significantly induces a rapid growth rate of Te-bearing mineral. The growth rate ranges from 0.003 to 0.033 μm min⁻1, with a median of 0.011 μm min⁻1 (standard deviation = 0.007) calculated by dividing the length of the products from the experiment (4–47 μm; N = 93, from ImageJ; Supplementary Table 2) by the duration of 24 h, assuming a constant growth rate throughout this period (Supplementary Fig. 6). In comparison, the growth rate of Te-bearing minerals from non-boiling fluid flow during the same 24 h is approximately 0.0007 μm min⁻1 (standard deviation = 0.0007). The calculation results indicate that boiling promotes faster growth than non-boiling fluid flow. Regarding duration, if solutions contain sufficiently high levels of Te, such as those found in geothermal deep wells and the experimental data from our study (exceeding 2 mg kg⁻1), the formation of native tellurium crystals up to 1,000 μm in length may require approximately 70 days in flow boiling systems (Fig. 5c). The duration is about 16 times greater than that observed in non-boiling experiment, corresponding to fluid residence time ratios greater than 14 times. The ratios may also apply to the precipitation of Au–Ag-tellurides, assuming a similar growth rate during flow boiling, as seen in the high-grade ores found in the world-class Cripple Creek Au–Te deposit (Fig. 5c).

Finally, the fluctuation of vapor-to-liquid ratios (Fig. 5a) and enthalpy (Fig. 5b) during flow boiling results in a change in free energy (ΔG = ΔH—TΔS) that influences Te and silica precipitations. In the Te precipitation processes, the free energy profile for mineralization is characterized by both one-step and multi-step pathways, as proposed for other minerals53–5556. Our experiments demonstrate that the formation of nano Te particles and silica colloids supports a multi-step pathway for the precipitation of Te and silica47, accompanied by fluctuations in vapor-to-liquid ratios and a negative ΔG (-0.02 kJ; calculated using H2O parameters at 9 MPa) during flow boiling (Fig. 5d). The formation and stability of subsequent amorphous and crystalline particles are governed by the energy barriers associated with phase transformation events47,54,57. Oriented native tellurium crystals of considerable size on the silica surface, resulting from boiling experiments, are likely formed by the aggregation of nano Te particles and rapid growth in a liquid-dominated regime (Fig. 5d). Calculations (considering native tellurium shapes) indicate that during two-phase fluid flow, the fluctuation of vapor-to-liquid ratios (e.g., the density drop of H2O from 0.71 to 0.05 g cm⁻3 in our experiment) followed by a liquid-dominated regime is crucial for the nucleation and intensive growth of native tellurium in conjunction with silica (Fig. 5d).

Geologic implications

Our results provide new insights into the models of high-grade Te ore formation. Experimental precipitation of Te directly challenges existing predictions regarding its volatility6,23, highlighting the significance of non-equilibrium processes (e.g., colloidal aggregates) that are not considered in current thermodynamic databases21,22,43. Thermodynamic predictions suggest that aqueous Te will strongly partition into the gas phase during boiling in low-sulfidation systems and may precipitate through condensation into lower-temperature groundwater6,23. However, our experimental evidence indicates that Te can achieve high concentrations in aqueous solutions (2.76 mg kg⁻1) and can precipitate as large native tellurium crystals (up to 47 μm in length within a 24 h period) during flow boiling. The presence of high Te concentrations in aqueous solutions is supported by geothermal deep well solutions (up to ~ 3 mg kg⁻1)18 and our own experiments. The ability of Te to maintain high concentrations in aqueous solutions supports the formation of high-grade Te ores through flow boiling.

Our results provide explanations for non-equilibrium observations in natural systems. (1) Native tellurium crystals formed by flow boiling can account for the Te-rich bulk ores illustrated in Figs. 1a–d, despite the low Te content in the Earth’s crust (~ 1 μg kg⁻1)9. Our experiments demonstrate a rapid growth rate of native tellurium (0.011 μm min⁻1) during flow boiling, in contrast to non-boiling systems. Consequently, crystalline native tellurium phases, along with quartz ores, formed in a liquid-dominated regime during flow boiling. Additionally, high-grade Te and Au ores are modeled to have formed through non-equilibrium processes22. (2) Bubble-like Te-bearing minerals formed by flow boiling with silica can explain the presence of Te droplets in natural ores, as illustrated in Figs. 1e–g. This study provides experimental evidence that Te precipitates in low-density areas at the nanoscale. In our experiments, bubble-like Te phases precipitated in regions with high vapor/liquid ratios, where Te can be transported by low-density fluids59. Furthermore, this study offers experimental evidence that native tellurium droplet can form through flow boiling and remain in natural ores, while bubble-like morphology has been recognized as a mechanism for transferring metals in hydrothermal systems28. The bubble-like shape is a precursor to native tellurium formation and can persist in quartz after the transformation of amorphous silica to quartz via fluid flow (Fig. 1e–g). The presence of native tellurium droplets has potential as an indicator of precious and critical metal enrichment27,37,60. Therefore, bubble-like Te phases in natural quartz ores suggest a vapor-dominated regime for their formation during flow boiling in hydrothermal systems.

The intensive growth of minerals observed during two-phase fluid flow is primarily linked to short-lived geologic events occurring within the upper crust. In our experimental framework, the model is developed to simulate fluid dynamics in epithermal systems situated above magmatic intrusions (Fig. 6a). The movement of fluids through fractures and faults is essential for the transfer of energy and the redistribution of elements in the upper crust10–14. While the two-phase fluid region may present a diverse array of conditions at shallow depths (Fig. 6a–b), such fractures are also indicative of naturally occurring vein-like ore bodies33,35,36, the formation and sustainability of geothermal systems10–12, and the recurrence of seismic activity14. During the process of flow boiling, the formation of crystalline minerals occurs concurrently with a notable reduction in silica solubility (Fig. 6c–d), which can be attributed to short-lived geologic events, such as coseismic boiling10–14,38. Experimental limitation and further investigation

First, when considering a solution composed solely of Te and silica, potential contaminants such as Ni, Cr, Fe, and Cu may originate from Inconel used in the experiment. However, these elements are more representative of natural systems: for instance, deep drill holes in New Zealand’s geothermal systems have been found to contain Ni, Cr, Fe, and Cu18. Additionally, natural ores contain metal sulfides, such as chalcopyrite and pyrite, which indicate the presence of Cu and Fe8. Although our solution is not purely composed of Te and silica, Te does not associate with these other elements to form compounds21,43. The current aqueous Te species are Te–H–O or Te–H and perhaps Te–Au/Ag21,43. Moreover, in flow-through experiments, Inconel has been widely utilized for mineralization precipitation, including silica and Al-Na-silicates29–33. If we consider pure element systems in flow-through experiments, alternative materials will need to be developed. Second, although images were captured using SEM, mineral shape and size can be evaluated semi quantitatively using the ImageJ program. If we use rock within the vessel, quantification can be performed using X-ray CT33. However, strong fluid-rock reactions will occur during mineral precipitation, which may hinder the evaluation of minerals through boiling or non-boiling during fluid flow. That said, these observations can provide detailed evaluations of mineral growth processes and rates. Our experiments represent an initial step toward investigating metal precipitation in fluid flow systems involving one- and two-phase fluids that can be related to long- and short-lived geologic events within the shallow crust.

Methods

Analyses of natural samples

Images were taken Images of samples in reflected light were obtained using a Nikon ECLIPSE LV100N POL petrographic microscope. Photomicrographs were taken under cross-polarized reflected light with angle of 87° to ensure that both gangue and ore minerals can be observed on bulk samples. Six samples were analyzed by the TESCAN Integrated Mineral Analyzer (TIMA-GMS) at the School of Earth Sciences and Resources, China University of Geosciences (Beijing). The acceleration voltage was 25 kV with an electric current of 10 nA. The height of the stage was 15 mm. The electric current and the signal intensity of BSE were automatically calibrated using a platinum Faraday cup. The lattice mode was used to collect both backscattered electron (BSE) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data. The X-ray count at each point is 1,500 kcps with a pixel size of 3 μm, an energy spectrum step size of 9 μm, and a field length of 1,000 μm.

Flow-through experiments

The experimental apparatus used in this study is similar to that described in our previous work29,30,33. Natural samples were crushed and sieved to 1–2 mm and 0.5–1 mm. The grains were then cleaned with an ultrasonic bath three times using distilled H2O and then dried for more than 12 h. All experiments below were conducted at the Graduate School of Environmental Studies, Tohoku University.

Subcritical conditions were used to dissolve quartz and metal minerals using distilled H2O. At high-temperature regimes exceeding critical points, H2O has low density and may transport metals in natural systems31–33,59. The process is specific to hydrothermal and geothermal systems31–33. Temperatures were set to 360–380 °C, and pressure was set to 38 ± 0.5 MPa. The apparatus includes a pump, an Inconel tube (length = 30 cm and interior diameter = 0.4 cm), thermocouples located upstream and downstream, a heating coil, two pressure gauges, and two thermometers (Supplementary Fig. 2). The flow rate of 0.3 g min⁻1 was used for the dissolution of natural samples to consider an effective dissolution balance between all possible metals.

Initial solutions were collected from the dissolution experiments and used for the precipitation reactions. These solutions were cooled prior to the precipitation experiments. The apparatus included a pump, a pre-heating apparatus, an Al vessel (length = 26 cm and interior diameter = 0.4 cm) within an autoclave, a heating furnace, thermocouples located at the preheater, inside the furnace against the autoclave, and downstream of the Al vessel, two pressure gauges, and three thermometers (Supplementary Fig. 3). The conditions for the precipitation I reactions were controlled by a pre-heater set to 200 ± 1 °C and a downstream heater set to 350 ± 1 °C. Pressure was controlled by a backflow valve and set to 25 ± 0.5, and 9 ± 0.5 MPa, respectively. Precipitation experiments were conducted over 24 h at a flow rate of 0.2 g min⁻1 to ensure sufficient product growth for clear observation by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Temperature gradients were measured separately from the experiments using two thermocouples, one stationary at the downstream end, and the other to be adjusted to each desired location over the course of a simulated reaction (the number of simulated reactions depended on the desired quantity of data points to be collected). The temperature gradient from 200–350 °C gradient was measured every 2 cm (Supplementary Fig. 4). The conditions for the simulated reactions were set to match the actual precipitation reactions, with downstream temperatures controlled by heaters set to 350 °C. The preheaters were set to 200 °C, and the flow rate was held at a constant 0.2 g min⁻1 for all. The temperature gradient measured 288 °C at the first mesh (1 cm) to 351 °C at the last mesh (21 cm) (pre-heater = 200 °C, downstream heater = 350 °C). Twenty-one Ti meshes were inserted every 1 cm into an Al vessel.

Analyses of input and output solutions

Major elements (Na, Mg, Al, Si, S, K, Ca, Mn, and Fe) were measured by an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent 5100). Each solution was measured three times, resulting in a reproducibility within 3%33. The pH was measured at room temperature, yielding pH = 4.1 for the input solution and pH = 4.1 (boiling) and 4.3 (non-boiling) for the output solutions (Supplementary Table 1). Trace elements were determined by an inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; NexION2000) using 5 μg kg⁻1 Rh as the internal standard63. The RSD values for each element are listed as follows. High concentrations were < 3% for 53Cr, 59Co, 60Ni, 63Cu, 75As, 121Sb, 137Ba, and 208Pb, and < 5% for 125Te. Low concentrations were < 3% for 77Se and 107Ag, and < 5% for 197Au (Supplementary Table 1).

Analyses of products by scanning electron microscope

A total of 42 meshes from four Al vessels were analyzed by scanning electron microscope with an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and beam current of ~ 10 uA by SU-8000 (Hitachi, Ltd.) and Gemini-450 (Zeiss, Ltd.) at the Graduate School of Environmental Studies, Tohoku University and Institute of Geology and Geophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The major element compositions of the products were determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy detectors on the SU-8000 (UltraDry Compact, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ltd.) and Gemini-450 (Oxford Instruments, Ltd.) scanning electron microscopes.
