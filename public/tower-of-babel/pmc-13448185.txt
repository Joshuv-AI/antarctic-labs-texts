From neural development to regenerative medicine: A research journey in stem cell biology, spinal cord repair, and iPSC-based drug discovery
Authors: Hideyuki Okano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This commemorative article reflects on a research journey spanning neural development, stem cell biology, regenerative medicine, and iPSC-based drug discovery. My early work focused on RNA-mediated regulation in the nervous system, including studies on myelin basic protein gene regulation and the identification and functional characterization of the RNA-binding protein Musashi. These studies contributed to the conceptual foundation of neural stem cell biology and helped establish methods for identifying and isolating neural stem/progenitor cells, including those present in the adult human brain. Building on this foundation, my colleagues and I pursued translational research in spinal cord injury, ranging from analyses of injury pathophysiology and molecular interventions to preclinical studies using rodent and non-human primate models. These efforts ultimately led to the first-in-human clinical study of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neural stem/progenitor cell transplantation for subacute spinal cord injury. In parallel, we developed patient-derived iPSC platforms for neurological disease modeling and drug discovery, particularly for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, where iPSC-based screening identified Ropinirole as a therapeutic candidate and enabled reverse translational research linking cellular phenotypes with clinical responses. Looking ahead, I argue that the future of regenerative therapy will depend on the continued integration of developmental biology, stem cell science, disease modeling, rehabilitation, and clinical translation to address unmet medical needs in disorders of the central nervous system.

Introduction

It is a profound honor to receive the Distinguished Achievement Award from the Japanese Society for Regenerative Medicine. I accept this recognition with deep gratitude, not only as a personal milestone, but also as a tribute to the many mentors, colleagues, students, clinicians, and collaborators who have shaped my scientific career.

My research life has long been guided by two closely linked questions. The first is biological: How is the nervous system generated, maintained, and regulated? The second is translational: How can insights gained from developmental neuroscience and stem cell biology be transformed into meaningful therapies for patients with otherwise intractable disorders? These two questions have remained inseparable throughout my work. From studies of RNA regulation in neural development, to the identification of neural stem cells in the human brain, to spinal cord regeneration, disease modeling, and drug discovery using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), I have consistently sought to connect basic science with clinical application.

In this commemorative article, I revisit several milestones along that journey. I first describe how my early work on RNA regulation and neural stem cells helped shape the conceptual basis of central nervous system regenerative medicine. I then discuss our efforts to translate stem cell biology into therapeutic strategies for spinal cord injury, culminating in first-in-human iPSC-based transplantation. Finally, I summarize our work on iPSC-based disease modeling and drug discovery, particularly in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and conclude with some thoughts on the future direction of regenerative therapy.

RNA regulation and the origins of my scientific journey

My academic career began in the 1980s in the laboratory of Professor Katsuhiko Mikoshiba, where I studied abnormal myelination in the central nervous system. During this period, we investigated the myelin-deficient mutant mouse and demonstrated that structural rearrangement of the duplicated Myelin Basic Protein (MBP) locus and the resulting antisense transcript contributed to suppression of MBP expression [1,2]. In the 1990s, I moved to the United States and joined the laboratory of Professor Craig Montell at The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine. There, through a large-scale genetic screen of approximately 8000 Drosophila P-element insertion lines, we identified a mutant of musashi associated with abnormalities in neural development [3]. This study marked the beginning of my long-standing interest in Musashi, an RNA-binding protein that would later prove central to stem and progenitor cell regulation (Fig. 1-1).

After establishing my independent laboratory at the University of Tsukuba, I identified the mammalian Musashi1 gene [4]. That study showed that Musashi is evolutionarily conserved from flies to mammals and laid the foundation for later functional studies. Our subsequent work demonstrated that Musashi regulates stem cell behavior through post-transcriptional control, particularly at the level of translation. A particularly important advance came when we showed that Musashi regulates asymmetric cell division in Drosophila sensory organ precursor cells by repressing translation of target RNAs [5]. This work provided clear evidence that translational repression is not merely an accessory mechanism but can directly determine daughter-cell fate during neural development.

Neural stem cells and the conceptual foundation of CNS regenerative medicine

A major turning point in my work came when we recognized that Musashi could serve not only as a mechanistic regulator, but also as a marker for neural stem cells. In 1997, we reported that Musashi is a useful marker of mammalian neural precursor cells in the postnatal central nervous system [6]. This finding led directly to subsequent collaborative studies on human neural stem cells.

In 1998, through collaboration with Steven Goldman and colleagues, we provided some of the earliest evidence that neural stem/progenitor cells are present in the adult human brain [7]. In that study, MUSASHI1-positive cells were identified in the adult human subependymal zone, expanded in response to FGF-2, and shown to differentiate into mature neurons under appropriate conditions. At a time when the existence of stem-like cells in the adult human brain remained controversial, this work helped establish a new conceptual foundation for regenerative medicine in the central nervous system (Fig. 1-2).

Our group subsequently extended these studies by developing reporter-based methods for identifying, isolating, and visualizing neural stem cells and progenitors using regulatory elements from neural genes such as Musashi1 and Nestin [[8], [9], [10]]. These technologies enabled us to define neural stem cells with greater precision and to examine their developmental properties, thereby strengthening the methodological basis for translational research in neural regeneration.

Looking back, these studies on RNA regulation and neural stem cells did more than answer basic biological questions. They helped establish the intellectual and experimental framework upon which later work in regenerative medicine could be built.

Building regenerative strategies for spinal cord injury

After returning to Keio University School of Medicine in 2001, I began to focus more directly on spinal cord injury as a target of regenerative medicine. Over the next two decades, we built a close interdisciplinary collaboration among developmental biologists, stem cell scientists, orthopedic surgeons, rehabilitation specialists, and clinical investigators. This integrated framework proved essential for advancing spinal cord repair from laboratory observation toward clinical translation.

A major component of this work was the systematic analysis of spinal cord injury pathophysiology across the acute, subacute, and chronic phases (Fig. 1-3). As Nakamura and I discussed in our review, understanding the temporal sequence of pathological events after injury is indispensable for designing stage-appropriate therapies [11]. One particularly important finding was that reactive astrocytes, long regarded mainly as inhibitors of regeneration, can also play protective roles. We showed that reactive astrocytes help restrict the invasion of blood-borne inflammatory cells into the injured spinal cord, thereby contributing to lesion compaction, tissue preservation, and functional recovery [12]. This study helped redefine the glial scar as a context-dependent response rather than a uniformly deleterious structure.

In parallel, we explored strategies to modify the inhibitory environment of the injured spinal cord. In a collaborative study with industry, we showed that selective inhibition of Semaphorin 3A promotes axonal preservation and regeneration, remyelination, angiogenesis, and motor recovery in a rat model of spinal cord injury [13]. This work provided early proof of concept that targeted molecular intervention could improve the regenerative milieu after injury.

At the same time, we were keenly aware of the limitations of rodent models for clinical translation. Because the anatomy and functional organization of the rodent spinal cord differ substantially from those of primates, especially with regard to corticospinal tract organization and fine motor control, we expanded our work into non-human primate models. We established a common marmoset spinal cord injury model and used it to evaluate recombinant human hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). In this model, HGF administration preserved corticospinal tract fibers and myelinated tissue, improved motor function including hand use, and did not worsen aberrant sprouting associated with neuropathic pain [14]. These preclinical findings subsequently supported clinical translation, culminating in a phase I/II study of intrathecal recombinant human HGF in acute spinal cord injury [15].

From stem cell biology to first-in-human iPSC-based transplantation

A central goal of our research has been to translate stem cell biology into cell-based regenerative therapies for spinal cord injury (Fig. 1-4). We first conducted transplantation studies using neural stem/progenitor cells in preclinical models and subsequently developed our own methods for neural induction from iPSCs. Using these approaches, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of safe, pre-evaluated iPSC-derived neural precursor cells in spinal cord injury models [[16], [17], [18]].

These studies made clear that successful translation would require more than showing efficacy in animals. It was equally necessary to address tumorigenic risk, cell quality, manufacturing consistency, transplantation timing, patient selection, and clinical outcome assessment. We therefore sought to build a comprehensive translational framework for first-in-human application. This effort culminated in the publication of our clinical study protocol in Regenerative Therapy in 2021, describing transplantation of 2 × 106 iPSC-derived neural stem/progenitor cells into patients with subacute complete spinal cord injury, with safety as the primary endpoint [19].

Following approval of the clinical protocol by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in 2019, patient enrollment began, and in December 2021 the first transplantation surgery in the world using human iPSC-derived neural precursor cells for subacute spinal cord injury was performed at Keio University Hospital. In this clinical study, registered as UMIN000035074, all four patients completed transplantation and one year of follow-up. No serious adverse events causally related to the cell therapy were observed, thereby supporting the safety of the procedure, and functional improvement was observed in two patients [20]. Although interpretation must remain cautious at this stage, I believe this work represents an important milestone in the long and challenging path toward regenerative therapy for the human central nervous system.

Our current interest has expanded toward chronic spinal cord injury, where the therapeutic hurdles are even greater. In this setting, we believe that the future may lie in combination strategies that integrate regenerative medicine with advanced neurorehabilitation. Accordingly, we are exploring approaches that combine cell therapy with transcranial stimulation, epidural electrical stimulation, and assistive rehabilitation technologies. The goal is to understand the mechanistic basis of these combinatorial interventions and to establish nonclinical proof of concept for future clinical studies.

iPSC-based disease modeling and drug discovery for neurological disorders

Alongside our work on spinal cord repair, we have also pursued the application of iPSC technology to neuropsychiatric and neurological disease modeling and drug discovery (Fig. 1-5). As Morimoto and I summarized, our group has established patient-derived iPSC lines for more than 40 diseases and used them to investigate disease mechanisms, identify candidate therapeutics, and develop translational platforms for precision medicine [21].

One notable example is our work on Pendred syndrome, a hereditary disorder associated with sensorineural hearing loss. Using patient-derived iPSCs, we showed that disease-specific cochlear cells exhibit degenerative phenotypes that can be ameliorated pharmacologically, thereby illustrating the power of iPSC-based platforms to identify rational therapeutic strategies for rare disorders [22].

Our work on ALS has been particularly extensive. Using patient-derived iPSC motor neurons, we identified ropinirole, a drug originally approved for Parkinson's disease, as a promising therapeutic candidate for ALS [23]. This was followed by the ROPALS trial, a placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind phase 1/2a physician-initiated clinical trial in 20 patients with ALS [24].

In that trial, patients with ALS were evaluated not only for safety and tolerability, but also for multiple efficacy-related outcomes, including ALSFRS-R decline, composite functional measures, event-free survival, and respiratory endpoints. The study further exemplified the value of reverse translational research, linking patient-derived iPSC phenotypes with clinical drug responsiveness. Building on these observations, we continue to discuss the importance of integrating iPSC phenotypes, genomic data, transcriptomics, and clinical information to stratify sporadic ALS into biologically meaningful subgroups and to advance precision therapeutics [25].

Concluding perspectives

Looking back over the course of my research life, I believe that the central purpose of my work has remained remarkably consistent: to understand the biology of the nervous system and to translate that understanding into therapies for patients. What has changed dramatically is the technological landscape. Over the past quarter century, advances in stem cell biology, cell engineering, imaging, genomics, transcriptomics, and clinical trial methodology have transformed not only the speed of discovery, but also the questions we are able to ask and the therapeutic strategies we are able to envision.

Regenerative medicine has evolved in parallel with these innovations. Yet, in my view, its future will depend on more than technological sophistication alone. It will require rigorous developmental and disease biology, careful attention to quality and safety, close integration of laboratory science with clinical medicine, and sustained collaboration across disciplines. This is particularly true in disorders of the central nervous system, where unmet medical needs remain extraordinarily high.

As I look to the future, I hope to continue contributing to the development of regenerative strategies that enhance the reparative capacity of the nervous system, to the clinical implementation of stem cell- and iPSC-based therapies, and to the discovery of new treatments for severe neurological diseases. If this article conveys one message beyond a retrospective account of past work, I would hope it is this: regenerative therapy is most powerful when it preserves a living continuity between biological discovery and patient care. It is within that continuity that the true promise of regenerative medicine continues to emerge.
