Drug-induced immune-mediated uveitis: a real-world pharmacovigilance analysis using FAERS and JADER databases
Authors: Shanshan Xu, Zhihui Song, Yiman Li, Jie Bai, Dong Wang, Ente Wang, Jiawei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyse drug-associated immune-mediated uveitis (IMU) risk using large-scale pharmacovigilance data.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance study using data from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report (JADER) databases, encompassing reports from Q1 2004 to Q4 2024. Disproportionality analysis identified drug-IMU associations using reporting odds ratios (ROR) and information component (IC). Time-to-onset (TTO) was also assessed.ResultsDisproportionality analysis identified 17 176 IMU cases in FAERS and 2580 in JADER. IMU prevalence was higher in females, with the 30–80 age group most affected. Antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents, sensory organ drugs and anti-infectives were the most frequently associated drug classes with IMU. Among 117 drugs with positive signals for IMU in FAERS, brolucizumab (ROR025=145.89, IC025=7.08), faricimab (ROR025=134.06, IC025=6.97) and rifabutin (ROR025=82.23, IC025=6.32) exhibited the strongest associations. The three drugs also ranked as the top three drugs with the strongest association with IMU in JADER. The study also highlights significant differences in TTO among various drug classes, with musculoskeletal and sensory organ drugs showing longer TTO compared with other categories.ConclusionsOur study highlights the diverse range of drugs implicated in the occurrence or progression of IMU. These findings emphasise the need for early risk assessment, careful monitoring and positive intervention in managing treatments involving high-risk drugs. Future research should focus on elucidating underlying mechanisms and risk factors to develop safer therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Drugs, Eye (Globe), Inflammation, Immunology, Pharmacology

Introduction

Uveitis, a group of intraocular inflammatory diseases characterised by inflammation of the uvea and other tissues in the eye, including retina, retinal blood vessels, vitreous and optic nerve, is a leading cause of visual morbidity worldwide.1 Uveitis can be aetiologically divided into infectious uveitis and immune-mediated uveitis (IMU). IMU is a heterogeneous group of disorders causing inflammation within the eye, and accounting for about 10% of blindness.2 Several mechanisms have been proposed to understand the pathogenesis of drug-induced IMU. Direct mechanisms are hypothesised to be due to the direct toxic effect of the drug, its metabolite or the vehicle, and direct mechanisms are due to the drug’s interaction with the immune system.3 Additionally, risk factors at the individual level significantly influence the occurrence of ocular inflammation following medication or vaccination. Such factors include genetic susceptibility, existing autoimmune disorders and a history of uveitis. Certain human leucocyte antigens (HLAs) are strongly linked to uveitis, such as HLA-B27 with anterior uveitis, HLA-A29 with birdshot chorioretinopathy, HLA-B51 with Behçet’s disease and HLA-DR4/B1*04 with Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) disease.4 The presence of these HLAs is thought to increase the likelihood of developing drug-induced uveitis.5 Among the numerous aetiologies of IMU, drug-induced uveitis is a rare but important and potentially modifiable cause. The risk of ongoing disease activity in drug-induced IMU can often be mitigated through early detection and medication optimisation with the prescribing physician. In the 1990s, the occurrence of drug-induced eye inflammation gained attention with the administration of rifabutin and cidofovir in individuals with AIDS.6 Recently, there has been an increase in cases of drug-induced IMU due to the introduction of new intravitreal drugs, immunotherapies and vaccines. Frequently cited medications responsible for this condition include systemic drugs such as bisphosphonates, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), antibiotics, antiviral treatments and some topical treatments, such as prostaglandin analogues (PAGs) and brimonidine, are recognised for causing IMU in vulnerable patients.3 7 8 These medications are often used in treating autoimmune diseases and various forms of cancer, respectively, highlighting the delicate balance between therapeutic benefits and potential adverse effects. Therefore, understanding and identifying drug-induced IMU has become paramount in reducing visual impairment and ensuring therapeutic outcomes.

Pharmacovigilance, the science of detecting, assessing and preventing adverse effects of pharmaceutical products, plays a crucial role in postmarketing surveillance. Previous pharmacovigilance studies have mainly focused on individual drugs or those within the same class and lacked comparisons across different classes of drugs-inducing IMU. Our study is the first large-scale real-world analysis evaluating IMU signals across all the drugs. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), the largest database of spontaneous adverse event (AE) reports globally, has been frequently leveraged by pharmacovigilance experts for its vast and diverse data to mine potential safety signals associated with various drugs.9,11 The Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report (JADER) database, while comparatively smaller in scale, is focused specifically on AEs reported in Japan and remains crucial for analysing adverse reactions to domestically marketed drugs.12 13 While differences exist between the datasets provided by FAERS and JADER, they can be viewed as complementary sources of information.14 By leveraging two databases, we aim to identify drugs that exhibit significant associations with IMU, offering new insights into the comparative safety profile and improving management of drugs inducing IMU. We hope the findings of this study can have important implications for clinical decision-making, future research directions and ultimately reduce the incidence of drug-related IMU.

Methods

Study design and data sources

A retrospective pharmacovigilance study was conducted based on the data retrieved from the FAERS and JADER databases. The FAERS database consists of seven data tables: demographic and administrative information (DEMO), drug information (DRUG), the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terms (PTs) coded for the adverse event (REAC), patient outcomes (OUTC), report sources (RPSR), therapy start dates and end dates for reported drugs (THER) and indications for use (INDI). The JADER database consists of four data tables: DEMO, DRUG, REAC and HIST. The ‘DEMO’, ‘DRUG’ and ‘REAC’ tables contain similar information to the FAERS database, and the ‘HIST’ table contains the patient’s underlying disease information. FAERS and JADER are publicly available pharmacovigilance databases containing fully anonymised and deidentified patient records. FAERS and JADER data are processed anonymously, and no ethical review was required. The reporting of this disproportionality analysis follows the recommendations of the REporting of A Disproportionality analysis for Drug Safety signal detection using individual case safety reports in PharmacoVigilance (READUS-PV) guideline.15

Data extraction

From the first quarter of 2004 to the fourth quarter of 2024, data from the FAERS and JADER databases were downloaded from the FAERS Quarterly Data Extract Files website (https://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html) and the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) website (https://www.pmda.go.jp/index.html), respectively. In the FAERS database, duplicates and multiple cases were excluded following the FDA-recommended method, which involves selecting the latest FDA_DT (the date when the FDA received the case) when CASEIDs (a unique number assigned to a specific patient case) are identical and choosing the higher PRIMARYID (a unique number assigned to each individual report) when both CASEIDs and FDA_DTs are the same.16 In the JADER database, duplicate data were removed from the DRUG and REAC files, and the DEMO table was linked to these tables based on specific conditions. In FAERS, the role of drugs in AEs was categorised as primary suspected drug (PS), secondary suspected drug (SS), concomitant drug (C) and interaction (I). In JADER, drugs were coded as suspected drug (S), concomitant drug (C) and interaction (I). This study collected only those drugs coded as ‘PS’ in FAERS or ‘S’ in JADER. AEs were defined using the PT from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), V.26.1, in both databases. As IMU describes a heterogeneous group of clinical entities, we initially extracted uveitis-related PTs and low level terms (LLTs) from MedDRA (V.26.1) under the system organ classes of eye disorders and immune system disorders, subsequently excluding PTs or LLTs related to infectious or non-immune causes. The complete list of these PTs and LLTs is provided in online supplemental table 1, which includes 11 PTs and 55 LLTs. Vaccines were not included in the study.

Definition of drug classification

We classified all drugs using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system, which consists of 14 main anatomical or pharmacological groups, known as the first level. Each main group is further divided into second levels, representing either pharmacological or therapeutic categories. The third and fourth levels include chemical, pharmacological or therapeutic subgroups, while the fifth level pertains to the chemical substance itself. The second, third and fourth levels are often employed to identify pharmacological subgroups when they are deemed more appropriate than therapeutic or chemical subgroups. Information about drug generic names, brand names and ATC classification codes was sourced from the DrugBank database (https://www.drugbank.com/). We performed a cluster analysis of all drugs reported in IMU events based on ATC classification codes at levels 3 and 4.

Statistical analysis

Disproportionality analysis was used to evaluate the potential relationship between drugs and IMU by calculating the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and information component (IC), which are specific indices used to detect possible associations between drugs and AEs. Calculations for ROR and IC were based on a 2×2 contingency table (online supplemental table 2). The equations and criteria for ROR, IC and the 95% CI are shown in online supplemental table 3. Statistical shrinkage (shrinkage parameter=0.5) was applied to reduce the number of false-positive signals when calculating ROR or IC.17 A signal was considered significant if the lower limits of the 95% CIs of ROR (ROR025) and IC (IC025) exceeded predefined thresholds (1 and 0, respectively).18 The time-to-onset (TTO) of AEs equals onset date minus therapy start date.19 Median and IQRs were determined to represent TTO. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare TTO among different drug classifications. All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using R Studio (V.4.2.0; Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

Descriptive analysis

From 2004 Q1 to 2024 Q4, a total of 17 176 cases and 16 676 reports of IMU were identified in FAERS. A larger proportion of patients were female (62.51%). The median age was 52 years (IQR: 37–65) and the median weight was 69 kg (IQR: 58–85). Among the 15 031 cases with available outcomes, more than 80% (82.03%, 12 328) involved severe outcomes, including death (1.42%, 213), life-threatening (0.90%, 135), disability (3.75%, 563) and other serious (75.92%, 11 411). More than half of the cases (65.29%, 11,214) were reported from the USA (27.31%), Canada (18.80%), France (6.76%), the UK (6.46%) and Japan (5.96%). In JADER, 2580 cases of IMU and the same related reports were identified. The patients aged 60 years and above (57.89%, 1402) accounted for a greater proportion, and most patients weighed between 40 and 69 kg (75.35%, 642). In total, 41.26% of patients recovered, and 0.69% of patients died. Healthcare professionals submitted 74.21% and 98.75% of cases in FAERS and JADER, respectively. More detailed baseline characteristics of patients are presented in table 1. Drug-induced IMU exhibited an increasing trend in FAERS from 2004 to 2024. In contrast, in JADER, it showed an increasing trend from 2004 to 2021, followed by a decline (figure 1).

Classification of drugs related to IMU

In the FAERS database, 651 drugs were implicated in the reported target AEs classified as ‘PS’, and 117 drugs were identified as signals of disproportionate reporting for IMU. Based on the ATC classification, the most commonly associated drug class with IMU was antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (n=9604), followed by drugs for sensory organs (n=2304), the musculoskeletal system (n=607) and anti-infectives for systemic use (n=367) (figure 2a). Additionally, all drugs were categorised into pharmacological subgroup (level 3) and chemical subgroup (level 4) categories by the ATC classification system (figure 2b). The primary drug categories implicated in IMU events include immunosuppressants (n=7538), monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) (n=1456), agents for ocular vascular disorders (n=1017), antimetabolites (n=656), protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs) (n=517) and drugs affecting bone structure and mineralisation (n=466). The top three drugs associated with the most IMU cases were adalimumab (n=2380), infliximab (n=1851) and etanercept (n=1203), all of which are tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors.

Similarly, in the JADER database, 290 drugs were involved in the reported target AEs as ‘PS’, and 47 drugs were identified as signals of disproportionate reporting for IMU. As with FAERS, the predominant drug class associated with IMU was antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (n=949), followed by drugs for sensory organs (n=399) and anti-infectives for systemic use (n=144) (figure 2c). All drugs were also classified into pharmacological subgroup (level 3) and chemical subgroup (level 4) categories (figure 2d) according to the ATC classification system. The database indicates that mAbs and ADCs (n=675), agents for ocular vascular disorders (n=356) and PKIs (n=225) comprised the majority of IMU cases. The top four drugs with the most IMU cases were nivolumab (n=323), brolucizumab (n=257), ipilimumab (n=193) and pembrolizumab (n=159), which include three mAbs and ADCs and one antineovascularisation agent.

Risk assessment of drugs with positive signals for drug-related IMU

Among the 117 drugs with positive signals in FAERS, brolucizumab demonstrates the strongest association with IMU (ROR025=145.89, IC025=7.08), followed by faricimab (ROR025=134.06, IC025=6.97) and rifabutin (ROR025=100.54, IC025=6.60) (figure 3a). In JADER, brolucizumab (ROR025=187.00, IC025=7.08), rifabutin (ROR025=132.69, IC025=6.82) and faricimab (ROR025=123.70, IC025=6.76) also rank as the top three drugs with the strongest association with IMU (figure 3b). There are 30 identical drugs with positive IMU signals in both databases. Eighty-seven drugs exhibited IMU signals only in FAERS, while 17 drugs showed positive IMU signals only in JADER. Among the 87 drugs with IMU signals solely in FAERS, 15 were not approved for sale in Japan, including avacincaptad pegol, netarsudil, elranatamab, ocriplasmin, fluocinolone intravitreal implant, mosunetuzumab, fedratinib, mirvetuximab soravtansine, anifrolumab, teclistamab, deucravacitinib, pegcetacoplan, aldesleukin, brimonidine/timolol and carboxymethylcellulose/glycerin. Among the 17 drugs with IMU signals only in JADER, five were approved exclusively in Japan, including prulifloxacin, sitafloxacin, gabexate mesilate, an antitussive combination drug and a pancreatic digestive enzyme combination preparation. Although the concordance between the two databases is low, mAbs, ADCs, PKIs, ocular vascular disorder agents, antiglaucoma preparations, ophthalmic anti-inflammatory agents, direct-acting antiviral drugs, antituberculosis drugs, antiepileptics and antimalarials are more likely to be associated with IMU.

Risk assessment at the PT level

Furthermore, we compared the differences between all drugs with positive signals at the 11 PT levels associated with IMU in online supplemental figure S1. In FAERS, pembrolizumab, adalimumab and infliximab led with the most PTs, each presenting eight. Following closely were nivolumab and methotrexate, each presenting seven. A total of 36 drugs only reported one positive PT signal, including cefuroxime, cyclopentolate and foscarnet, and so on. In the 11 different PTs, uveitis was the most frequently observed PT, with a total of 95 drugs showing positive signals for this PT, followed by iridocyclitis, with 66 drugs showing positive signals. IMU and sympathetic ophthalmia were the two least frequently occurring PTs showing positive signals, both with three drugs showing positive signals. Brolucizumab showed the strongest signal in the PT of uveitis (ROR=136.80), followed by faricimab (ROR=118.03) and rifabutin (ROR=112.15). Faricimab and brolucizumab showed the strongest signal in the PT of iridocyclitis, with the ROR of 320.70 and 266.32, respectively. Tobramycin/dexamethasone in scleritis (ROR=99.61), brolucizumab in chorioretinitis (ROR=361.96), acetazolamide in Behcet’s syndrome (ROR=36.92), dabrafenib in VKH disease (ROR=115.87), rabeprazole in tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis syndrome (ROR=207.42), pembrolizumab in autoimmune uveitis (ROR=66.03), ganciclovir in immune recovery uveitis (ROR=1479.83) and ipilimumab (ROR=472.77) in IMU all showed the strongest signal.

In JADER, ipilimumab led with the most PTs, presenting seven, followed closely by pembrolizumab with six PTs. A total of 23 drugs only reported one positive PT signal, including rifabutin, clarithromycin and amoxicillin/clavulanate, and so on. Just like in the FAERS database, uveitis was the most frequently observed PT, with a total of 34 drugs showing positive signals for this PT, followed by iridocyclitis, with 10 drugs showing positive signals. IMU was the least frequently occurring PT showing positive signals, with four drugs showing positive signals. Brolucizumab and nivolumab showed the strongest signal in the PT of uveitis, both with a ROR of 259.69, followed by rifabutin (ROR=237.08) and faricimab (ROR=137.89). Faricimab, nivolumab and brolucizumab showed the strongest signal in the PT of iridocyclitis, with the ROR of 533.61, 511.75 and 511.75, respectively, which was basically consistent with the results in the FAERS database. Fluorometholone in scleritis (ROR=234.88), binimetinib in chorioretinitis (ROR=321.93), apremilast in Behçet’s syndrome (ROR=166.59), vemurafenib in VKH disease (ROR=161.79), prulifloxacin in tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis syndrome (ROR=439.98) and pembrolizumab (ROR=77.81) in IMU all showed the strongest signal. There was no drug with positive signals in autoimmune uveitis and immune recovery uveitis.

Comparison of TTO among different drug classifications

In the FAERS analysis, results indicated that the TTO for musculoskeletal system drugs, antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents and sensory organ medications was significantly longer than for other drug categories (p<0.0001), with median TTOs of 139, 115 and 108 days, respectively (figure 4a). Dermatological drugs had the shortest TTO, with a median of 4 days, followed by anti-infectives for systemic use with a median of 9 days. Additionally, a comparison between ophthalmic and non-ophthalmic medications revealed that the TTO for non-ophthalmic drugs (median=48 days) was significantly shorter than for ophthalmic drugs (median=108 days) (p<0.0001) (figure 4b). The three drugs with the longest median TTO related to IMU were tamoxifen (397 days), infliximab (348 days) and latanoprost (309 days). In contrast, the drugs with the shortest median TTO related to IMU, all with a median of 2 days, included moxifloxacin systemic, cyclopentolate, carboxymethylcellulose/glycerin, ocriplasmin, latanoprost/netarsudil, povidone iodine and tropicamide. Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive pharmacovigilance analysis of drug-induced IMU using extensive real-world data from the FAERS and JADER databases, offering insights for postmarketing drug label updates. We identified 117 drugs from FAERS and 47 from JADER with signals linking to IMU. Antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents were the most frequently implicated drug classes in both databases, followed by sensory organ drugs and anti-infectives.

Reports of drug-related IMU increased markedly from 2004 to 2024, with a higher prevalence in females. This rise may relate to widespread use of PKIs, ICIs, TNF-α inhibitors and vascular endothelial frowth factor (VEGF)/VEGF receptors (VEGFR) inhibitors. The female predominance could stem from gender-specific medications (eg, hormone replacement therapy, bisphosphonates) and factors like genetic predispositions, hormonal influences and immune system differences, consistent with prior studies noting higher IMU incidence in females.20 21 While previous research cited peak uveitis incidence in ages 20–40,22,24 our study found a median age of 69 years, with most reports in 30–80 year olds. Elderly patients showed higher drug-induced uveitis risk, likely due to immune ageing, reduced drug metabolism, polypharmacy and specific drug reactions, though gender and age susceptibilities require further validation.

Comparative analysis of FAERS and JADER revealed both consistencies and discrepancies. Despite differences in drug approval and reporting practices between the USA and Japan, both databases consistently linked IMU to antineoplastic/immunomodulating drugs, ophthalmological drugs, antimicrobials and bisphosphonates.

Antineoplastic and immunomodulating drugs, including B-rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (BRAF) inhibitors, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK) inhibitors, programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) or the natural receptor for PD-1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, TNF-α inhibitors and interleukin inhibitors, showed strong IMU associations. Sada et al observed increased uveitis risk in patients with melanoma on ICIs or BRAF/MEK inhibitors, primarily as VKH disease.25 Jung et al found higher noninfectious uveitis risk with BRAF inhibitors versus conventional cytotoxic drugs or ICIs.26 In our study, dabrafenib and vemurafenib exhibited stronger VKH signals (ROR=115.87 and 33.76, respectively) in FAERS than nivolumab, pembrolizumab or atezolizumab, aligning with prior findings. TNF-α inhibitors (etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab) were top AE reporters in FAERS. Notably, infliximab showed a higher uveitis ROR than adalimumab and etanercept, contrasting with previous studies linking etanercept to greater risk.27 28 Mechanisms remain unclear, requiring larger studies. Interleukin inhibitors (secukinumab, tocilizumab) were associated with IMU, mostly in patients with prior uveitis, suggesting recurrence/exacerbation risks.29,32

Ocular hypotensive drugs (eg, betaxolol, brimonidine, PGAs), glucocorticosteroids, cholinergic agents and intravitreal anti-VEGF medications (ranibizumab, aflibercept, brolucizumab, bevacizumab) were linked to uveitis.33,44 A narrative review reported sterile uveitis/endophthalmitis incidences: bevacizumab (0.05%–1.1%), ranibizumab (0.005%–1.9%), aflibercept (0.05%–2.1%) and brolucizumab (4.4%).45 Newly approved faricimab showed 0.3%–1% uveitis in phase III trials.46 In our study, brolucizumab had the strongest signals (ROR=145.89 in FAERS; 187.00 in JADER), followed by faricimab (ROR=134.06 in FAERS; 123.70 in JADER). Brolucizumab’s small molecular size may enhance tissue penetration, increasing immune exposure in eyes with compromised blood–retinal barriers.47 48 Anti-VEGF-related inflammation may involve patient-specific factors (eg, anti-drug antibodies, diabetes) or medication factors (eg, impurities, Fc portions).45 PGAs (latanoprost, travoprost) showed positive signals in both databases. One study estimated PGA-induced uveitis at 0.22%, with latanoprost (0.48%) higher than bimatoprost (0.01%),8 though our JADER results reversed this trend. PGAs may disrupt ocular barriers and increase anterior chamber cytokines,49 though a large glaucoma study found no PGA risk,50 highlighting the need to consider confounders like prior surgeries. Brimonidine-induced uveitis had a median TTO of 7 days (range: 7–2557 days), risking misdiagnosis as conjunctivitis. Omidenepag isopropyl and dorzolamide also showed novel IMU signals. Corticosteroid eye drops (fluorometholone, prednisolone) were linked to uveitis, potentially via withdrawal effects, though mechanisms remain unknown.

Among antimicrobials, cidofovir and rifabutin were prominent IMU triggers, primarily causing iridocyclitis. Cidofovir’s mechanism may involve intraocular accumulation and ciliary body toxicity.51 Fluoroquinolones (moxifloxacin in FAERS; sitafloxacin in JADER) and sulfonamides (trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole) were associated with uveitis, with TTOs of 3–8 days. Proposed mechanisms include phototoxicity and HLA-B27/B51 associations.52 53 Clarithromycin showed signals, possibly via CYP450 inhibition interactions with rifabutin.54

Bisphosphonates (ibandronate, risedronate, zoledronate) were linked to prolonged IMU (TTOs: 79–304 days), consistent with cytokine release, macrophage polarisation and immune-complex deposition mechanisms.55 56 Intravenous zoledronate is most frequently reported, likely due to potency.7 Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (diclofenac, meloxicam) showed IMU signals in FAERS, often with TNF-α inhibitors or antibiotics, requiring further validation.

Treating drug-induced IMU involves discontinuing the causative agent and administering corticosteroids (topical for anterior, systemic/local for posterior uveitis). Most cases have favourable prognoses with prompt intervention. Clinicians should weigh risks of rechallenging with the same drug or switching classes, with ophthalmic follow-up within 1–2 weeks of re-treatment.57

TTO variability across drug classes informs monitoring: musculoskeletal, antineoplastic and sensory organ drugs have longer TTOs (requiring prolonged monitoring), while dermatological and anti-infective drugs act faster, demanding early vigilance. Ophthalmic drugs showed longer TTOs, reflecting distinct absorption dynamics.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in our study. First, reliance on spontaneous reporting systems like FAERS and JADER is subject to inherent biases, including reporting bias and the Weber effect, which can influence signal detection metrics such as ROR and IC,58 leading to overestimation or underestimation and hindering the detection of delayed AEs. Second, the lack of detailed clinical information limits our ability to establish causality and fully understand the underlying mechanisms of drug-induced IMU. Additionally, the absence of drug dose and dosage form data, non-uniformity of units and the diversity of treatment regimens hinder the assessment of the dose–immune-mediated adverse effect relationship.59 Third, since the primary identification numbers in FAERS and JADER are not universal, and due to differences in reporting formats, content and languages, deduplication can only rely on limited information (eg, gender, age, drug, event date, country), potentially leading to missed or incorrectly excluded cases. Lastly, for immunomodulators, uveitis may reflect treatment failure or underlying disease, not drug effects.3 Our infliximab/etanercept ROR discrepancy (11.60 vs 4.25) may stem from reporting patterns or channelling bias. These limitations mean that our findings should be interpreted as generating hypotheses for further investigation rather than confirming causal associations. Future research should focus on prospective studies to validate these findings and explore the biological pathways involved in IMU development.

Clinicians should differentiate drug-induced versus disease-related IMU via timing, laterality and steroid response, with baseline screening for high-risk drugs. Future research should include prospective studies, standardised ophthalmic evaluations and machine learning to predict high-risk patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study significantly enhances the understanding of drug-induced IMU and its association with specific drug classes. Using data from the FAERS and JADER databases, we have identified key drug classes closely linked to IMU, including antineoplastic and immunomodulating drugs, ophthalmological drugs, antimicrobial drugs and bisphosphonates. However, a definitive causal relationship has not been established. The TTO of IMU can range from immediate to several months or years after the administration of the causative drugs. Most patients with drug-related IMU experienced improvement or full recovery with intensive topical steroids, sometimes supplemented with periocular and systemic steroids, or by discontinuing the related drugs. Notably, this study is the first to demonstrate that systemic drugs are associated with a significantly shorter TTO compared with ophthalmic drugs. The findings are crucial for refining the understanding of drug-induced uveitis and mitigating its impact on patient outcomes. Future prospective studies or registries should aim to better quantify the incidence and outcomes of drug-induced IMU to enhance our understanding of this condition.
