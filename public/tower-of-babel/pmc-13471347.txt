Machine learning model to identify gut microbiome-derived metabolites as potential biomarkers of autism spectrum disorder: a pilot study
Authors: Silvia Babolin, Roberto Enea, Mariagrazia Cicala, Daniele Di Giovanni, Luigi Mazzone, Leonardo Emberti Gialloreti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) arises from complex and not yet completely understood interactions between genetic and environmental factors. Alongside known hallmarks of neurobiological and structural changes in ASD brain, alterations in gut microbiota are frequently observed in ASD and may contribute to its pathophysiology. Identifying reliable biomarkers through multivariate analysis and machine learning offers promising avenues for improving ASD diagnosis and understanding comorbid gastrointestinal symptoms. In this study, a machine learning model was trained to identify ASD and healthy controls based on the theoretical production of metabolites for each gut bacterial species and each individual, combining the data collected from two global databases (GMRepo v2 and Agora2). Random Forest Classification models reach a mean accuracy of 85%, and a subsequent literature analysis of the 5% most significant metabolites showed a 40% correspondence with previously published in vivo studies. Some of the most relevant compounds detected by the theoretical model are amino acid and amino-acidic derivatives, volatile organic compounds, and short-chain fatty acids. Results are coherent with empirical evidence, supporting microbiota’s role in ASD pathophysiology by contributing to neurotransmitters’ biosynthesis and degradation, intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, immunological modulation. Future work will focus on stratified sampling, empirical validation, and developing personalized metabolic signatures for early diagnosis and precision medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08178-8.

Background

Neurodevelopmental Disorders (NDDs) share a complex pattern of etiology and pathogenesis, where multiple factors (inherited, congenital or acquired after birth) interplay in time and space during human growth, from embryogenesis to adulthood, resulting in various impairments of functioning in many, or sometimes all, domains of life. Often, children show a diagnostic co-occurrence of two or more NDD, with symptom overlap or mutual masking that can complicate diagnosis and exacerbate functional impairments.

Among NDD, Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is characterized by core symptoms such as pervasive and persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, and repetitive and/or restrictive patterns of behavior and interests [1]. Nevertheless, clinical presentation may widely vary between individuals and even over the course of a single individual’s life, showing heterogeneity in symptoms and varying levels of support required in daily life.

Researchers aim towards a wider and more precise understanding of the pathophysiology of ASD focus on the complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors and the developmental windows of vulnerability, in which alterations may occur prior to clinical symptoms [2].

Currently, ASD diagnosis relies solely on clinical criteria, with no established biomarkers to facilitate early detection, personalized treatment, or effective prevention of comorbidities. Identifying reliable ASD biomarkers remains particularly challenging due to the disorder’s clinical heterogeneity and high prevalence of co-occurring conditions [3–5].

Among the physiological domains increasingly investigated in ASD, gastrointestinal functioning has received particular attention. Gastro-intestinal issues share a high co-occurrence rate in ASD: among 40–70% of children with ASD exhibit a wide range of gastrointestinal symptoms, such as constipation, diarrhea and recurrent abdominal pain [6, 7], which have also been observed to be linked to ASD severity and worse quality of life [8, 9].

The neurological and gastrointestinal systems communicate constantly through bi-directional neuronal, immunological and endocrine signaling mediated by neurotransmitters, inflammatory cytokines, neuropeptides, hormones and metabolites produced or transformed by gut bacteria, developing the concept of “microbiota-gut-brain axis”, whose dysregulation has been shown to contribute to neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases [10].

Several studies have acknowledged the link between intestinal microbiome and ASD, including altered Bacteroidetes to Firmicutes ratio and increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Sutterella spp. [11, 12] in ASD microbiome samples compared to controls. These bacterial alterations may influence -directly or through derived metabolites- synthesis and metabolism of neurotransmitters and their pathways, that might lead to behavioral changes in ASD; moreover, individuals with ASD often exhibit food selectivity, which affects also the microbiome composition, contributing to gut dysbiosis [13].

While it is still unclear whether dysbiosis causes ASD or if ASD drives microbial alterations, numerous animal and human studies have confirmed their strong mutual correlation [14], suggesting how gut microbiome might become a promising source of biomarkers to identify ASD and the related gastro-intestinal symptomatology. These findings highlight the relevance of bacterial-derived metabolites in ASD research, although existing metabolomic studies provide heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting results [5].

The validation process for potential metabolic biomarkers in ASD typically begins with measuring the concentration of a candidate molecule in individuals with ASD compared to controls. Such univariate comparisons may reveal biological abnormalities but do not allow assessment of diagnostic accuracy at the individual level. In contrast, multivariate statistical modeling—capable of analyzing multiple variables simultaneously—offers a more effective strategy for identifying biomarker patterns, as it captures the complex interactions underlying ASD pathology. These approaches are often integrated into machine learning frameworks for classification tasks [4, 15]. Artificial Intelligence and machine-learning techniques can further accelerate this process by enabling accurate and efficient analysis of large-scale proteomic datasets, uncovering subtle patterns and relationships that traditional analytical methods may overlook [16].

Most metabolomic analyses in ASD have measured circulating metabolites without establishing whether they originate from microbial metabolism. In parallel, several machine-learning studies have attempted ASD classification using microbial abundance profiles, but not metabolite-based features, as in Wu et al. and Olaguez-Gonzalez et al. [17, 18]. These approaches do not capture the functional metabolic potential of gut microbial communities, leaving unexplored the relationship between microbiome composition and the metabolites that the microbiota is theoretically able to produce.

To address this gap, the present study evaluates whether the Individual Metabolite Production Capacity (IMPC)—a diet-independent theoretical measure of the metabolic output of a microbial community—can be used to infer differences in the producible metabolite profiles of ASD- and control-associated microbiota. To this end, we integrated taxonomic profiles from a global microbiota composition repository (GMRepo v.2) with genome-scale metabolic reconstructions of human gut microorganisms (AGORA2) to derive individual IMPC estimates for a wide range of gut-derived metabolites. IMPC profiles were then employed as features in a machine-learning model to assess their discriminatory capacity and to identify the metabolites most strongly differentiating the two groups. The most discriminant metabolites were subsequently examined within human metabolic pathways and compared with existing literature to contextualize their potential biological relevance.

Methods

The primary dataset employed in this study is sourced from GMRepo v2 [19], a comprehensive data repository of the human intestinal microbiome. GMRepo v2 aggregates microbiome analyses from a variety of global research projects, encompassing individuals across a spectrum of geographical regions, phenotypes, and age groups. Depending on the analytical granularity of each project, data on the relative abundance of bacterial genera or species are provided. Each analysis, referred to as a “run” in GMRepo’s terminology, is supplemented with metadata, including the participant’s nationality, age, gender, and body mass index (BMI), where available. GMRepo v2 applies two rounds of quality control procedures to remove runs with less than 20,000 clean reads and samples containing only a single taxon (i.e., a species or genus accounts for more than 99.99% of total abundance); incomplete runs are labeled as “failed runs”. For this study, we included only runs fully processed [20].

At the time of analysis, GMRepo v2 comprised 71,642 runs derived from 353 projects, encompassing 132 human phenotypes. The repository identifies 592 marker taxa, comprising 350 marker species and 242 marker genera. To facilitate a comparative analysis of ASD and control groups, only the “Health” phenotype and two ASD-related phenotypes—“Autism Spectrum Disorder” and “Autistic Disorder”—were selected. The distinction between the latter two phenotypes is based on the original studies definitions, as classified with MeSH and NIH terminology, defines as follows:Autistic Disorder: “A disorder beginning in childhood. A specific phenotype labeled “Health” is designated for use as a control group: “Healthy” in GMRepo refers to participants without diagnosed disease in the original studies metadata. In this study protocol all “Health” phenotype samples were included, to maximize statistical power and capture a broader representation of microbiota configurations, acknowledging that this design may introduce demographic and clinical heterogeneity. Comorbidities were not consistently reported and could not be excluded. In this pilot study, the only piece of metadata used as a secondary classification of the samples was age, in order to mitigate the asymmetric distribution of ASD subjects, mostly under 25 years old. Due to limited and heterogeneous metadata and to avoid excessive stratification that would markedly reduce the effective sample size and compromise model stability (curse of dimensionality, increased variance, and overfitting), demographic variables such as sex and geographic origin were not explicitly adjusted for, and the analysis was therefore conducted using a numerically balanced subsampling strategy and interpreted as exploratory.

Microbial composition data were then cross-referenced with Agora 2 (Assembly of Gut Organisms through Reconstruction and Analysis, version 2), which reconstructs the metabolic production of 7,302 human gut microbes [21]. AGORA2 provides species-specific data on metabolite production. Given that microbial species within the same genus often exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, only runs with species-level data were considered for metabolite extraction. In instances where AGORA2 provided data at the subspecies level, and such resolution was unavailable in GMRepo, we assumed that all metabolites produced by a subspecies could be attributed to the species as a whole.

To align GMRepo species with those in AGORA2, a mapping was conducted. Of the 2,747 bacterial species reported in GMRepo, 888 had corresponding metabolic reconstructions in AGORA2. Although this represents approximately 32% of species coverage, these species accounted for approximately 80% of the total relative abundance, with the remaining 20% attributable predominantly to unknown taxa. Notably, the abundance associated with known species not covered in AGORA2 was negligible (approximately 0.05%).

A subset of the GMRepo dataset was extracted, focusing on samples with species-level data. This subset comprised 5,481 individuals—5,150 controls and 331 ASD cases. Although the dataset included many more control than ASD runs, no strict matching was applied due to limited metadata consistency across studies, and the full “Health” cohort was used only as a sampling reservoir. Instead, stratification by age was implemented as a first step to partially mitigate demographic bias. The age and country distributions of these individuals are presented in Figs. 1, 2, 3. The inclusion of individuals from diverse age groups and countries is crucial, given the potential influence of these factors on dietary habits and, consequently, on microbiome composition.

To assess the potential impact of microbiota-derived metabolites, we introduced the concept of Individual Metabolite Production Capacity (IMPC). This measure was introduced because circulating metabolite levels might reflect a combination of microbial activity, host absorption, diet, and metabolic processing, making it difficult to isolate the specific microbial contribution. The IMPC represents a theoretical measure of an individual’s capacity to produce a given metabolite, based on the relative abundance of bacterial species capable of producing it, and thus does not reflect actual production. The IMPC was not intended as a diagnostic biomarker but as a functional inference tool to investigate whether ASD- and control-associated microbiota differ in their theoretical metabolic output.

This estimate is not intended to reflect absolute metabolite concentrations in vivo, but rather to provide a comparative index of functional metabolic potential across samples. Its reliability derives from the quality of the AGORA2 metabolic reconstructions and is appropriate for comparative analyses at the community level. The IMPC was computed for each metabolite and for each individual in the dataset. These profiles were then used to train a binary classification model using the Random Forest algorithm [22], with the objective of distinguishing between ASD and control individuals.

Feature importance was assessed using both Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI [23]); and SHAP values [24], facilitating the identification of candidate metabolites with discriminative potential for ASD. The two analyses were not combined; rather, they were conducted in parallel to verify that they did not yield divergent sets of metabolites, a discrepancy that can occasionally arise when computing feature importance.

Due to class imbalance (331 ASD vs. 5,150 controls), we trained 16 Random Forest models, each incorporating all ASD samples and a randomly selected subset of 331 controls (We used two-thirds of each set of 331 ASD and 331 control cases for training and one-third for validation). Feature importance scores were computed for each model and averaged to derive a ranked list of key metabolites.

An age-stratified analysis was also conducted. Two subgroups were defined: individuals younger than 25 years and those aged 25 years or older. For the under-25 group, 520 controls and 183 ASD individuals were available. Three models were trained, each using the full ASD cohort and a random sample of 183 controls (2/3 for training and 1/3 for validation). For the 25+ group, the dataset consisted of 16 ASD cases and 1,611 controls. To address this severe imbalance, 101 models were trained using all 16 ASD cases and randomly sampling 16 controls per model.

Optimal hyperparameters were identified through Randomized Search Cross-Validation [25], as implemented in the Scikit-learn library [26].

For each model, classification accuracy was calculated according to the following formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accuracy = {{TP + TN} \over {TP + TN + FP + FN}}$$\end{document}

where TP denotes true positives, TN true negatives, FP false positives, and FN false negatives.

For each model, the following measures were computed: classification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score and ROC AUC score. The measures obtained for each model within a given experimental condition were then averaged, and the standard deviation was computed for each measure in order to evaluate stability across the models.

It is important to note that the reported accuracy values refer exclusively to the validation set, which constitutes 33% of the available dataset. For each model, the dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (comprising two-thirds of the data) and a validation set (comprising the remaining one-third).

Following metabolite selection through Random Forest Classification, a literature search was conducted to compare the identified metabolites withprevious findings among in vivo metabolomic studies on ASD subjects. The primary search engines used were PubMed and Google Scholar. Articles reviewed included original clinical studies (primarily case-control or cohort studies), systematic reviews, and meta-analyses.

Metabolite nomenclatures and synonyms were first cross-referenced with the Human Metabolome Database v.5.0 (HMDB), which contains 220,945 metabolite entries, chemical and biochemical data, and links to 8,610 protein sequences [27].

The literature search was performed using keywords, exemplified as follows for metabolite “X”: (“synonymX1“OR“synonymX2”)AND(“ASD“OR“AutismSpectrumDisorder“OR“Autism”)OR(“humangutmicrobiota“OR“gutmicrobiome”)OR(“humanmetabolomics”)).

To assess the relevance of individual metabolites in distinguishing between control and ASD groups, two feature importance estimation methods were employed: Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations).

It is important to underscore that a metabolite’s inclusion among the most important features indicates its strong discriminative power between ASD and control groups. However, this does not necessarily imply a differential production capacity (i.e., overproduction or underproduction) in ASD individuals. To obtain further insight into potential production differences, the base 10 logarithm of the IMPC (Individual Metabolite Production Capacity) ratio between ASD and control groups was computed for the top 5% most important metabolites (43 metabolites per group). This ratio provides an estimate of whether individuals with ASD exhibit a higher (positive values) or lower (negative values) theoretical production capacity for each metabolite compared to controls. Subgroup analyses were also performed based on age.

Among the 89 individual metabolites analyzed (excluding those recurring across multiple population groups), the most represented category consists of amino acids and their derivatives (particularly metabolites of tyrosine and tryptophan), 15 molecules 80% of which (12 out of 15) have been previously reported in the literature. An equally represented category is that of carbohydrates, sugars, and their derivatives; however, among the 15 metabolites identified, only 2 have been described in the literature as altered in ASD compared to controls. Following these, volatile organic compounds and organic acid derivatives are the next most represented categories, with 5 out of 9 analyzed metabolites in each category having been previously reported in the literature.

Discussion

The top 5% of metabolites identified by the IMPC model highlights several recurring compounds in both analyzed age groups and overall population. Many of these are directly linked to microbial metabolic activity, which is, in turn, influenced by the composition of the gut microbiota.

The gut microbiota, due to its vast genomic and metabolomic variability, interacts with the host and modulates multiple physiological systems, including immune, metabolic, neurological, and gastrointestinal functions. Through the production of microbial metabolites, gut microbiota modulates the permeability and integrity of the intestinal epithelium and may influence the blood–brain barrier, allowing certain compounds to enter the bloodstream and potentially reach the brain, as evidenced on in vivo studies with both animal models and humans [73, 74]. Several bacterial species are capable of synthesizing neurotransmitters and their precursors, such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine [75].

Among the most relevant compounds which may be implicated in the aforementioned pathophysiological mechanisms—and detected by the IMPC theoretical model—are amino acid and derivatives, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). It is important to note that the model identifies theoretical associations between microbiota-derived metabolites and ASD, but does not allow conclusions regarding causality or mechanistic involvement.

Amino acids and derivatives

Following food intake, essential and non-essential amino acids are metabolized by the gut microbiota into metabolic intermediates and neurotransmitters, playing a fundamental role in the gut-brain axis [76].

Phenylalanine undergoes important transformations at the intestinal tract, being converted into tyrosine and the precursors of dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine. The gut microbiota may influence dopaminergic transmission both through its metabolic activity and through the activation of the vagus nerve, which projects from the enteric nervous system to the meso-cortico-limbic circuit [77].

Several phenylalanine-derived metabolites appeared altered in the theoretical model, including (3,4-Dihydroxyphenyl)acetate, (R)-3-(4-Hydroxyphenyl)propionate, 2-Hydroxyphenylacetate, and 3-Hydroxyphenylacetate. These metabolites were previously associated with ASD and linked to the overrepresentation of the genus Clostridium in the microbiota of autistic individuals [67, 78].

Tyrosine metabolism also appears altered in subjects with ASD, with reduced levels of tyrosine and increased levels of desaminotyrosine. This trend has been documented in previous studies [30, 39, 47]; however, some authors report higher levels of tyrosine in the urine of children with ASD compared to control groups [41, 48, 49]. Despite conflicting findings, alterations in the tryptophan–phenylalanine–tyrosine pathway have been associated with more severe communicative symptoms in ASD [79–81].

Some bacteria in the gut microbiota, particularly species of Clostridium and Pseudomonas, convert tyrosine into p-cresol, an aromatic compound that can also be found in the environment and results from the oxidation of toluene. The IMPC model predicts increased levels of p-cresol in subjects with ASD. This compound, known for its acute and chronic toxicity [82], has been implicated in the exacerbation of symptoms associated with ASD and in the alteration of gastrointestinal permeability [63, 83].

Tryptophan is involved in serotonin synthesis, which occurs predominantly in the gut, either via spore-forming bacterial species or by stimulating enterochromaffin cells to release it [84]. Tryptophan is also metabolized through the kynurenine pathway, which has been associated with mechanisms of neuroinflammation, neurotransmission, and immune system regulation [85]. According to the IMPC model, the production of kynurenine is elevated in individuals with ASD, and a similar trend has been reported in some recent studies [43, 44].

Among the non-essential amino acids, increased levels of l-glutamine were identified in subjects with ASD, in agreement with previous findings [37, 38, 86]. Glutamine and glutamate—both precursors of GABA—are considered key contributors to the excitatory/inhibitory imbalance hypothesized in ASD pathogenesis [87].

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)

The metabolism of bacteria comprising gut microbiota can degrade and transform various dietary compounds that influence host health. In particular, saccharolytic and proteolytic fermentation processes generate a range of low molecular weight (<500 Da), carbon-based volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that can be released into the host system and detected in different biological samples [88]. Based on the theoretical IMPC calculation model, 9 of the 89 total compounds identified are VOCs, including alcohols (1-propanol, butanol, isopropanol), aldehydes (acetaldehyde, propanal), ketones (acetone), and hydrocarbons (methane, toluene). Notably, hydrocarbons are the only class not consistent with previous findings in vivo studies on ASD subjects reported in literature.

In a study by Vernocchi et al., fecal volatilome analysis revealed that children with ASD exhibited increased levels of 1-pentanol, 1-butanol, phenylethyl alcohol, indole, skatole, acetone, and certain fatty acids. In contrast, neurotypical individuals showed a higher prevalence of esters such as butyl acetate. These findings suggest that VOCs may represent promising biomarkers. Beyond their diagnostic potential, the altered concentrations of VOCs in ASD are also associated with known shifts in the abundance of specific bacterial taxa, including Prevotella, Sutterella, and Bacteroides [30, 60, 62].

Short chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are among the most extensively studied compounds in microbiota–host interactions. SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are produced by the collaborative action of various intestinal microbial species from complex carbohydrates or dietary fibers that undergo fermentation in the gut. The presence of SCFAs has been widely associated with immunomodulatory effects, maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, and stimulation of the enteric nervous system [89].

Although the literature presents conflicting results regarding fecal SCFA concentrations in individuals with autism, studies have reported an altered representation of bacterial species that primarily produce these molecules, such as those of the genus Bifidobacterium and the phylum Firmicutes, in autistic individuals [11, 90].

In the theoretical IMPC calculation model, reduced butyrate production was observed in the ASD over-25 population compared to controls, with inconsistent findings reported in the literature [62, 91]. Butyrate serves as an energy source for intestinal cells and exhibits anti-inflammatory properties as well as regulatory functions on intestinal permeability [92]. Its extraintestinal role is linked to the inhibition of histone deacetylase, which is involved in apoptosis, inflammation, and lipid metabolism [93].

Additionally, in the ASD over-25 population, a reduced theoretical production of a specific form of branched-structure starch was identified, which may serve as a substrate for SCFA production by the gut microbiota.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Machine learning approaches have been extensively applied in the literature to investigate a potential “microbial signature” of ASD based on gut microbiota analysis. These analyses have relied on individual samples, aggregated datasets, or meta-analyses of results from various studies [76, 94, 95]. Similarly, research in the field of ASD metabolomics has advanced significantly with big data analyses, which hold future potential for early ASD detection, the development of precision medicine, and the optimization of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [96].

A key limitation of the present study is the limited availability of microbiome datasets providing taxonomic annotation at the species level for ASD cohorts. Most publicly available datasets include only genus-level information, which restricts the possibility of conducting metabolite-oriented analyses that require species-specific metabolic inference. Consequently, the number of datasets that can be included under these methodological criteria remains relatively small, limiting the possibility of constructing large and demographically homogeneous ASD and control cohorts. For this reason, the findings reported here should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future studies based on larger, harmonized microbiome datasets with reliable species-level annotation will be essential to validate and extend these preliminary observations [11, 91].

Nevertheless, building on a machine learning approach, the present pilot study aimed to carry out a predictive analysis using a Random Forest Classification model, based not on in vivo case-control samples, but on a large-scale database of globally collected gut microbiomes.

The machine learning model, developed to discriminate between ASD and control subjects based on the theoretical capacity of microbiota-derived metabolite production, demonstrated encouraging classification performance, with an average accuracy of 85% and a peak specificity of 92% in datasets restricted to individuals under 25 years of age. Among the top 5% of metabolites with the highest discriminatory power between ASD and controls, approximately half had already been associated—either directly (up to 46%) or indirectly—with ASD pathophysiology in previous in vivo studies.

Because the model is theoretical and estimates relative abundances of microbial metabolites, it does not provide actual metabolite concentrations, nor does it allow for the calculation of confidence intervals or statistical significance for IMPC ratios. The IMPC metric, although conceptually informative, reflects a theoretical metabolic potential rather than measured metabolite levels. Genome-scale reconstructions may omit context-dependent regulation, substrate availability, or post-translational constraints; therefore, IMPC should be interpreted as an indicator of metabolic capacity rather than direct metabolic activity. Nevertheless, among metabolites already documented in the literature, 70% showed concordant patterns (e.g., favorable IMPC ratios for ASD in the model alongside increased concentrations in empirical studies), despite variability in the biological matrices analyzed (feces, urine, plasma).

A major strength of this study is the large sample size derived from the GMRepo v2 database, comparable only to large-scale longitudinal cohort studies on individuals with ASD. On the other hand, the use of globally sourced fecal samples classified only by clinical phenotype (diseased vs. healthy controls) limited the possibility of further stratifying the population beyond an age threshold and precluded case-control matching. This represents a major limitation, given the well-established influence of numerous exogenous and endogenous factors—such as sex, genetic background, dietary habits, medication or substance use, comorbid conditions, and environmental or occupational exposures—on gut microbiota composition.

It is also important to note that sex, age, and geographic origin represent only a limited subset of the variables known to influence gut microbiota composition, since current evidence indicates that microbiota structure is shaped by a complex interplay of host genetics, early-life exposures, environmental factors, lifestyle variables, environmental pollution, and medication use [10–12, 14]. Crucially, the relative magnitude of these effects—and their interactions—remains incompletely quantified.

Consequently, even if age (or another single factor) were explicitly modeled as a confounder, numerous additional variables of comparable relevance would remain uncontrolled. Attempting to account for all these factors simultaneously within a machine-learning framework would require a substantially larger sample size to maintain an acceptable bias–variance balance and to ensure statistical stability.

In this context, although the metabolite–ASD associations identified by the model align with previously reported findings, they cannot be interpreted as causal; indeed, some metabolic alterations may reflect downstream consequences of dietary or physiological adaptations, rather than primary drivers of ASD-related pathophysiology. For these reasons, the present analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Its primary contribution lies in identifying preliminary patterns that may inform future studies based on larger, more deeply phenotyped cohorts, in which multivariable and machine-learning approaches can be applied under statistically appropriate conditions.

Conclusions and future directions

This pilot study explores a theoretical machine learning approach for ASD recognition based on gut microbiota metabolic output, provides an overview of the extensive current literature on biochemical and metabolic markers of ASD. The high discriminatory specificity of the model, coupled with the promising degree of alignment with existing empirical evidence, may provide a preliminary conceptual framework for future methodological exploration in ASD research.

Most studies investigating the relationship between gut microbiota and autism spectrum disorder have relied on genus-level taxonomic resolution, largely due to limitations in the availability of datasets with deeper microbial annotation. While these studies have provided important insights into microbial alterations associated with ASD, genus-level analyses may not adequately capture the functional metabolic heterogeneity that exists among different species within the same genus. In this context, the present study attempts to extend previous work by exploring microbiome–metabolite relationships using species-level microbial identification, which may allow a more biologically meaningful estimation of microbial metabolic potential. However, this approach is currently constrained by the limited availability of suitable datasets. For example, due to the absence of direct metabolomic measurements from the individuals represented in the database, our model cannot be considered a validation tool but rather an exploratory framework for hypothesis generation. Further validation with empirical metabolomic data and alternative modeling approaches will be required before clinical translation. Future directions for the model include refining population selection by applying stratification and/or controlled matching for individual and sociodemographic variables. Another step would be the empirical validation of the model through in vivo measurement of microbial metabolite production in ASD and control subjects using fecal and blood samples.

Despite the limitations, the results obtained from theoretical model are consistent with existing literature suggesting that the gut microbiota may influence human pathophysiological pathways. Such interactions may occur through altered production of microbial metabolites, including volatile organic compounds (VOCs), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and transformation products of essential dietary amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan—precursors in neurotransmitter synthesis whose dysregulation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of ASD. Such hypotheses should however be interpreted cautiously, given the theoretical nature of the model and the absence of in vivo validation.

None of the individual metabolic alterations observed in the model or reported in the literature can currently serve as a standalone biomarker for ASD. However, when considering the cumulative disturbances across genetic, metabolic, and microbial domains, the existence of clinically and diagnostically relevant endophenotypes may be cautiously hypothesized to connect and integrate laboratory data (biological, neurophysiological and radiological measures) with specific behavioral and symptomatic traits. In this context, the integration of omics sciences with big data analysis via artificial intelligence models could, in the long term, contribute to large-scale analyses aimed at characterizing personalized omic profiles, which may support future developments in ASD research and precision medicine.
