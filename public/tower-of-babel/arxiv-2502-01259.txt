Functional Central Limit Theorem for the simultaneous subgraph count of dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graphs
Rajat Subhra Hazra, Nikolai Kriukov, Michel Mandjes

Abstract
In this paper we consider a dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph with independent identically distributed edge processes. Our aim is to describe the joint evolution of the entries of a subgraph count vector. The main result of this paper is a functional central limit theorem: we establish, under an appropriate centering and scaling, the joint functional convergence of the vector of subgraph counts to a specific multidimensional Gaussian process. The result holds under mild assumptions on the edge processes, most notably a Lipschitz-type condition.

Introduction

Over the past decades, inspired by applications in a broad range of disciplines (such as biology, physics, economics, and computer science), various types of random graph models have been introduced and analyzed.
Random graph models are specified via an underlying probabilistic mechanism.
The Erdős–Rényi random graph
[
7
,
11
]
, in which there are
N
vertices and in which every possible vertex pair is connected independently with a given probability
p\in(0,1)
, is arguably the most well-known.

Most literature on random graphs focuses on
static
models, which represent a single instance of the random graph without accounting for them changing over time. However, real-world networks typically
do
evolve over time, motivating the study of
dynamic
random graphs, particularly dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graphs (e.g.,
[
25
,
20
,
18
,
17
,
5
]
). In these models, edges evolve independently over time, alternating between being present and absent. Essentially all properties that have been studied for static random graphs can be analyzed for their dynamic counterparts as well. For instance, in the context of dynamic random graphs there is now a notion of a
giant component process
, and a notion of a
spectrum process
. In the present paper we concentrate on the
subgraph counts process
, in that our aim is to describe the joint evolution of the entries of a subgraph count vector. We thus capture, for instance, the joint distribution of the number of edges and the number of triangles over time.

Subgraph counts play a pivotal role in network analysis, as they encode crucial structural information about the geometry underlying the graph (see e.g.
[
10
,
19
,
21
,
15
,
16
,
12
,
8
,
9
,
1
]
).
There is a mature body of work that probabilistically analyzes subgraph counts in the static case.
We proceed by providing a brief account of the existing literature in this area, with a focus on the central limit regime. It should be borne in mind, though, that this branch of research is relatively mature, rendering our overview inherently non-exhaustive.

The first results showing asymptotic normality for subgraph counts in a static Erdős–Rényi random graph appeared already in
[
6
]
: an appropriately centered and normalized version of the subgraph count converges, as
N\to\infty
, to a zero-mean Gaussian random variable. These results were further generalized to the regime where the edge probability
p=p_{N}
tends to zero as the number of vertices
N
grows large; see e.g.
[
23
]
that covers specific types of subgraphs. Finally, in
[
24
]
necessary and sufficient conditions on the decay of
p_{N}
were established for obtaining asymptotic normality for any subgraph count.

A separate stream in the literature fully concentrates on the case of a constant edge probability. In this context we mention
[
22
]
, which proves the joint asymptotic normality for different subgraph counts. More recently, the limiting distributions for a single subgraph count in the graphon setting were addressed in
[
14
,
2
]
.

Importantly, there seem to be no asymptotic normality results covering the setting of random graphs that evolve over time, even in the simplest case in which the edge processes do not depend on the number of vertices
N
. The main objective of this paper is to settle this, by establishing a joint functional central limit theorem for dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graphs. Concretely, we identify conditions under which an appropriately centered and normalized version of a vector of subgraph counts has, at the process level, a Gaussian limit. Moreover, we explicitly characterize the limiting Gaussian process.
The type of convergence considered is in distribution, in
\mathbb{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
, which is the set of all càdlàg functions
f(\cdot)\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, equipped with
J_{1}
distance, i.e., for any functions
f(\cdot),\,g(\cdot)\in\mathbb{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})

\displaystyle d\bigl(f(\cdot),\,g(\cdot)\bigr)=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\max\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lvert\lambda(t)-t\right\rvert,\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|f(t)-g(\lambda(t))\|\right),

for some finite, deterministic horizon
T
, where
\Lambda
is the class of strictly increasing, continuous mappings of
[0,T]
onto itself. We refer to e.g.
[
3
, Section 12]
for a more detailed account.

In our setup, we do not focus on one specific type of edge dynamics, in that we aim to work with a specification of the dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph that is as general as possible. We follow the ‘traditional’ path in proving process-level convergence: we first establish finite-dimensional convergence, followed by a tightness step that yields the desired functional convergence. In order to accommodate this tightness, we have to impose some assumptions on the edge dynamics. More concretely, in our framework the probabilities of the edge switching once or twice (in an interval of given length, that is) are assumed to obey a Lipschitz-type condition. At the methodological level, some of the steps of our analysis follow their counterparts in
[
13
]
, where the principal eigenvalue process of the graph’s adjacency matrix is analyzed in the regime in which the edge processes do not depend on
N
.

The main result of this paper, Theorem
4.1
, establishes that the limiting behavior (as the size of our graph grows large) of the subgraph count process is Gaussian, and strictly correlated with the subgraph count for the least common subgraph of our graph. This least common subgraph is defined as a subgraph with at least one edge and the asymptotically smallest expected number in our dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph. For instance, if the edge process does not depend on the size of the random graph (as before denoted by
N
), the expected subgraph count asymptotically scales as
N^{v}
, where
v
is the size of the subgraph under consideration. In this scenario, the least common subgraph for any graph with at least one edge is a single edge, and thus every subgraph count process is strictly correlated with the edge count. This implies that all possible subgraph counts are strictly correlated with one another. We provide a detailed exploration of this phenomenon in Corollary
4.2
.

It is noted that in the regime where the ‘edge probabilities’ decay as
N
grows to infinity, the speed of this convergence can influence which subgraph becomes the least common one. Here the notion of ‘edge probability’ refers to the probability that a given edge exists; observe that these probabilities are functions of time. In the setting of decaying edge probabilities (as
N\to\infty
), two additional assumptions are in place, alongside the Lipschitz-type condition. In the first place, we assume that the edge probabilities vanish, as
N\to\infty
, uniformly over the time interval
[0,T]
considered. This is for convenience, because without this assumption we would have to split the time interval into subintervals that are to be dealt with separately. Second, we focus solely on those subgraphs for which the probability of non-appearance in the dynamic random graph tends to zero as
N\to\infty
. This assumption, which links the asymptotic behavior of the edge probabilities to the structure of the subgraph, is adopted from
[
24
]
and is discussed in greater detail in Section
3
.

We also like to highlight a specific consequence of our results: Theorem
4.1
reveals that if two different graphs have distinct least common subgraphs, their respective subgraph count processes are asymptotically independent.

\boldsymbol{b}_{i}=(b_{i,1},\ldots,b_{i,d})^{\top}
. For any finite set
A
we denote the cardinality of
A
as
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
.

2
Model

We start by constructing our dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph process, in the sequel denoted by
G_{N}(t)
, with
N\in\mathbb{N}
vertices over the time interval
t\in[0,T]
for some given time horizon
T>0
. Each pair of vertices
u,v\in\langle N\rangle
can be connected by an edge (which we associate with the state ‘1’) or not (which we associate with the state ‘0’). Throughout this paper, the resulting edge process of the vertex-pair
(u,v)
is denoted by
(a_{N,u,v}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
. We restrict ourselves to what could be called
homogeneous simple graphs
, i.e., across all
u,v\in\langle N\rangle
with
u<v
and for any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the processes
(a_{N,u,v}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
are independent, identically distributed, and
G_{N}(t)
does not have any self-loops and double edges. This in particular entails that (i)
a_{N,u,u}(t)=0
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
t\in[0,T]
, and (ii)
a_{N,u,v}(t)=a_{N,v,u}(t)\in\{0,1\}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
u,v\in\langle N\rangle
,
u\not=v
and
t\in[0,T]
.

In the sequel expectations and covariances pertaining to the processes
(a_{N,u,v}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
play a key role. Throughout this paper, we use the following notations:

\displaystyle p_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{a_{N,u,v}(t)\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{N,u,v}(t)=1\right\},

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(a_{N,u,v}(s),a_{N,u,v}(t)\bigr).

The papers on asymptotic normality for static Erdős–Rényi random graphs, such as the ones cited in the introduction, work with some assumption on the behavior of the edge probability in the regime that the number of vertices
N
grows large.
In our dynamic counterpart we have to impose a similar assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

There exists a control sequence
\varrho_{N}>0
such that either
\lim_{N\to\infty}\varrho_{N}=0
or
\varrho_{N}\equiv 1
, and, for any given
s,t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{p_{N}(t)}{\varrho_{N}}

\displaystyle=p_{\star}(t)\in[0,\infty),

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\kappa_{N}(s,t)}{\varrho_{N}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\star}(s,t)\in[0,\infty)

(2.2)

for some limiting functions
p_{\star}(t)
,
\kappa_{\star}(s,t)
. In addition, the convergences in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are uniform in
s,t\in[0,T]
.

Remark 1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the sequence
\varrho_{N}
is defined up to a constant factor, which we determine as follows. For subsequent calculations, it is important to control the limits of the sequences
N^{v}\varrho_{N}^{e}
as
N\to\infty
, where
v
and
e
are elements of
\mathbb{Z}
. We initially fix the constant arbitrarily. If
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{v}\varrho_{N}^{e}\in\{0,\infty\}
for all pairs
v,e\in\mathbb{Z}
, the arbitrarily chosen constant remains unchanged. However, if for some
v^{\star},e^{\star}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{v^{\star}}\varrho_{N}^{e^{\star}}\in(0,\infty)
, we set
\varrho_{N}=N^{-v^{\star}/e^{\star}}
. In this case, for any
v,e\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle{\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{v}\varrho_{N}^{e}=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{v-e\frac{v^{\star}}{e^{\star}}}=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }e/v>e^{\star}/{v^{\star}},\\
1\quad&\text{if }e/v=e^{\star}/{v^{\star}},\\
\infty\quad&\text{if }e/v<e^{\star}/{v^{\star}}.\end{cases}}

Thus, in all cases, the sequence
\varrho_{N}
can be chosen so that for any pair of constants
v,e\in\mathbb{Z}
, it satisfies the following property:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{v}\varrho_{N}^{e}\in\{0,1,\infty\}.

(2.3)

Technically, to check (
2.3
) we need to have
v,e\in\mathbb{N}
, but at various places in our proofs we work with powers that potentially have a negative sign; this explains why in (
2.3
) we chose to include
v,e\in\mathbb{Z}
.

{\Diamond}

Remark 2
.

We can always choose the same control sequence for both expectation and covariance function, due to the fact that

\kappa_{N}(t,t)=p_{N}(t)-p_{N}^{2}(t).

{\Diamond}

It is our objective to work with a setup that is as general as possible. To this end, we chose to not uniquely specify the stochastic dynamics of the edge processes. Our results require the following mild additional assumption on the processes
(a_{N,u,v}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists a sequence of processes
(a_{N}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
u,v\in\langle N\rangle
with
u\not=v
and every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the process
a_{N,u,v}(\cdot)
has the same distribution as the generic process
a_{N}(\cdot)
. Additionally, there exists a positive constant
{\mathfrak{C}}>1
such that, for all
0\leqslant r\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{N}(s)\not=a_{N}(r)\right\}\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\varrho_{N}(s-r),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{a_{N}(s)\not=a_{N}(r),\,a_{N}(t)\not=a_{N}(s)\right\}\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\varrho_{N}(t-r)^{2}.

(2.5)

Remark 3
.

Using (
2.4
) we can obtain that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
p_{N}(t)
is necessarily Lipschitz-continuous with Lipschitz-constant
{\mathfrak{C}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\,p_{N}(s)-p_{N}(r)\right\rvert\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\varrho_{N}(s-r)

(2.6)

for all
0\leqslant r\leqslant s\leqslant T
. It implies that the function
p_{N}(\cdot)/\varrho_{N}
is continuous for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, due to (
2.1
), there exists constant
p_{+}\in(1,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle{\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}}\frac{p_{N}(t)}{\varrho_{N}}

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{+},

(2.7)

{\Diamond}

One of the most natural class of processes which satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
is the class of
alternating processes

a_{N}(\cdot)
, where
a_{N}(t)\in\{0,1\}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. An alternating process is initiated by sampling the Bernoulli random variable
a_{N}(0)
, and then it changes between on and off at random points in time. We let
\xi_{N,x,i}
, for
x\in\{0,1\}
and
i,N\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the duration of the
i
-th ‘sojourn time’ given that process starts at the state
x
, where such a sojourn time is either an on-time or an off-time, depending on the parity of
x+i
; it is typically assumed that all the on- and off-times are independent. If these random variables are in addition from a continuous distribution, then under a couple of mild restrictions such a process satisfies (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) by virtue of the following proposition.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
a_{N}(\cdot)
be the alternating process described above, which satisfies the following properties:

\circ

There exists a sequence
\varrho_{N}
such that the limit

\displaystyle p_{\star}(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{p_{N}(t)}{\varrho_{N}}

exists uniformly in
t
.

\circ

The random variables
\xi_{N,x,i}
, with
x\in\{0,1\}
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, are from a continuous distribution. in addition, their densities
\rho_{N,x,i}(t)
are uniformly bounded by the constant
P
for any
N,i\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\{0,1\}
.

\circ

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\rho_{N,0,1}<P\varrho_{N}
.

Then
a_{N}(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2.2
for
{\mathfrak{C}}=(p_{\star}(0)+2)Pe^{PT}
.

The proof of this proposition is presented in Appendix
A
.

Example 4
.

As a particular example of the alternating processes that satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
2.3
, we can consider a stationary alternating renewal process with exponential on- and off-times. This concretely means that
\xi_{N,x,i}
has an exponential distribution with parameter
\lambda_{N,{\rm off}}
if
x+i
is even and with parameter
\lambda_{N,{\rm on}}
if
x+i
is odd, and
p_{N}(0)=\lambda_{N,{\rm off}}/(\lambda_{N,{\rm on}}+\lambda_{N,{\rm off}})
. In this case we have for any
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle p_{N}(t)=\frac{\lambda_{N,{\rm off}}}{\lambda_{N,{\rm on}}+\lambda_{N,{\rm off}}}.

When considering the specific parametrization
\lambda_{N,{\rm off}}=N^{-\alpha}\lambda_{{\rm off}}
and
\lambda_{N,{\rm on}}\equiv\lambda_{{\rm on}}
for some parameters
\lambda_{\rm on},\lambda_{\rm off},\alpha>0
, we obtain that the process
a_{N}(\cdot)
satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
with
\varrho_{N}=N^{-\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\star}(s,t)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm off}}{\lambda_{\rm on}}e^{-\lambda_{\rm on}\left\lvert s-t\right\rvert},

and
p^{\star}(t)\equiv\lambda_{\rm off}/\lambda_{\rm on}
.

3
Graph notations

In this section we introduce various graph-related notations. Due to the dynamic nature of our graph process, we need to introduce some new concepts that were not required in the static setting.

\circ

Throughout the paper we consider only non-empty graphs whose vertex set is a subset of
\mathbb{N}
. We denote the set of such graphs by
{\tt G}_{\infty}
. For any graph
H\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
we denote by
{\tt V}(H)
(
{\tt E}(H)
, respectively) the set of vertices (edges, respectively) of the graph
H
. By definition of the set
{\tt G}_{\infty}
,
{\tt V}(H)\subset\mathbb{N}
and
{\tt E}(H)\subset\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\colon u<v\}
. In particular, for any
t\in[0,T]
the random graph
G_{N}(t)
can be considered as a random element of
{\tt G}_{\infty}
with
N
vertices.

\circ

For two graphs
H,{H^{\star}}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
we write
{H}\sim{H^{\star}}
if they are isomorphic, and we write
{H}\subset{H^{\star}}
if
H
is a subgraph of
{H^{\star}}
, i.e.,
{\tt V}(H)\subseteq{\tt V}(H^{\star})
and
{\tt E}(H)\subseteq{\tt E}(H^{\star})
. Denote for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
by
K_{N}
a complete graph with vertices labeled
1,\ldots,N
, i.e.,
{\tt V}(K_{N})=\langle N\rangle
and
{\tt E}(K_{N})=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\colon u<v\}
.

\circ

For any graph
H\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
we denote by
\mathcal{V}(H)
(
\mathcal{E}(H)
, respectively) the number of vertices (edges, respectively) in
H
, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}(H)=\left\lvert{\tt V}(H)\right\rvert
and
\mathcal{E}(H)=\left\lvert{\tt E}(H)\right\rvert
; we also use the notation
\mathcal{A}(H)
to denote the number of automorphisms of
H
.

\circ

For any pair
g_{1},g_{2}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
we use the notation
g_{1}\cup g_{2}
for the graph with
{\tt V}(g_{1}\cup g_{2})={\tt V}(g_{1})\cup{\tt V}(g_{1})
and
{\tt E}(g_{1}\cup g_{2})={\tt E}(g_{1})\cup{\tt E}(g_{2})
. The graphs
g_{1}\cap g_{2}
and
g_{1}\setminus g_{2}
can be defined in a similar way.

\circ

The main object that is studied in this paper, concerns the
m
-dimensional subgraph count process
(X_{N,1}(\cdot),\ldots,X_{N,m}(\cdot))
, whose
i
-th entry represents the number of subgraphs of
G_{N}(\cdot)
which are isomorphic to some fixed graphs
H_{i}\in{\tt G_{\infty}}
. Given any sequence of subgraphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
, we can interpret
X_{N,i}(t)
for
i\in\langle m\rangle
as the number of graphs
g\,{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
such that
g\sim{H}_{i}
and
g{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t)
. Using that
G_{N}(t)\subset K_{N}
for any fixed
N\in N
almost surely, we can consider only such graphs
g\,\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
that
{\tt V}(g)\subseteq\langle N\rangle
. As such, it is convenient for any
H\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
to define the set of all possible graphs
g\,{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
with
{\tt V}(g)\subseteq\langle N\rangle
such that they are isomorphic to some fixed graph
H
:

\displaystyle{\tt G}_{N}({H})={\{g\in{\tt G}_{\infty}\colon{\tt V}(g)\subseteq\langle N\rangle,\,g\sim{H}\}}.

The cardinality of the set
{\tt G}_{N}(H)
is

\displaystyle\left\lvert{\tt G}_{N}({H})\right\rvert=\frac{N!}{(N-\mathcal{V}({H}))!\,\mathcal{A}({H})}.

(3.1)

For example, if
H
is a triangle, then
\left\lvert{\tt G}_{N}(H)\right\rvert=\binom{N}{3}
. Define also for any
{g\,{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}}
the indicator function of this graph
g
being a subgraph of
G_{N}(t)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{N}(g,t)=\mathbb{I}\{g{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t)\}=\prod_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g)}a_{N,u,v}(t).

(3.2)

\circ

Throughout the paper we need to control the way how different subgraph patterns can be obtained when the graphs are intersecting. To formalize this concept, denote the quotient set of all graphs with at least one edge by
{\tt G}_{\sim}=\{g\in{\tt G}_{\infty}\colon\mathcal{E}(g)\geqslant 1\}/\sim
, where
\sim
was introduced above. We refer to any element of
{\tt G}_{\sim}
as a
subgraph pattern
. We denote by
\pi:{\tt G}_{\infty}\to{\tt G}_{\sim}
the classical projection on the quotient set. Then, define for any pair of graphs
H,\,{H}^{\star}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
a set of all common subgraph patterns with at least one edge:

\displaystyle{\tt CS}({H,H^{\star}})=\{g\in{\tt G}_{\sim}\colon\exists g_{1},g_{2}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}\colon g_{1}\subset{H},\,g_{2}\subset{H^{\star}},\,\pi(g_{1})=\pi(g_{2})=g\}.

A graph pattern
g\in{\tt CS}({H,H^{\star}})
represents an unlabeled subgraph structure common to both
H
and
H^{\star}
.

For example, let
H
be a triangle with vertex set
\{1,2,3\}
and
H^{\star}
an edge with vertices
\{4,5\}
. Then, even though graphs
H
and
H^{\star}
do not have any common subgraph, they still have a common subgraph pattern, which is the edge pattern.

For brevity we write
{\tt CS}({H})={\tt CS}({H},{H})
, which basically stands for the set of all subgraph patterns of graph
H
.
Note that functions
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
are invariant with respect to graph isomorphism, so we can as well define them for the subgraph patterns.

In addition, for any graph
H\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
and any graph pattern
g\in{\tt G}_{\sim}
we define by
\mathcal{S}(H,g)
the total number of different subgraphs of
H
which are isomorphic to
g
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(H,g)=\sum_{g_{0}\,\subset\,{H}}\mathbb{I}\{\pi(g_{0})=g\}.

(3.3)

\circ

For any graph
H{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
(or graph pattern
H\in{\tt G}_{\sim}
) with at least one edge we define for any
N\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}({H})=N^{-\mathcal{V}({H})}\varrho_{N}^{-\mathcal{E}({H})}.

(3.4)

In this paper we mostly consider such graphs
H
that satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

For any
g\subset H
such that
\mathcal{V}(g)\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)}=0.

(3.5)

Remark 5
.

Consider the following scenario where Assumption
3.1
is not satisfied: suppose that for some subgraph
g\subset H
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-\mathcal{V}(g)}\varrho_{N}^{-\mathcal{E}(g)}=\infty.

(3.6)

Denote by
X_{N}(t)
the number of subgraphs of
G_{N}(t)
which are isomorphic to
H
, and
X_{N}^{\star}(t)
the number of subgraphs of
G_{N}(t)
which are isomorphic to
g
.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{X_{N}(t)\geqslant 1\right\}\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left\{X_{N}^{\star}(t)\geqslant 1\right\}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N}^{\star}\right\}=\frac{N!\,p_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g)}(t)}{(N-\mathcal{V}(g))!\mathcal{A}(g)}\leqslant\frac{N^{\mathcal{V}(g)}p_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g)}(t)}{\mathcal{A}(g)}.

From (
3.6
), it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{X_{N}(t)=0\right\}\geqslant 1-\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}(g)N^{-\mathcal{V}(g)}p_{N}^{-\mathcal{E}(g)}(t)}=1,

which implies that, if (
3.6
) holds, it is impossible to obtain a non-degenerate limit of the sequence
X_{N}(t)
.

In the borderline case where, for some subgraph
g\subset H
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-\mathcal{V}(g)}\varrho_{N}^{-\mathcal{E}(g)}=C\in(0,\infty),

it is known from
[
24
]
that even in the static case, the limiting subgraph count is non-Gaussian. We exclude this case from our paper as it requires a radically different approach.

The conclusion of the above considerations is that Assumption
3.1
is strictly necessary to ensure a non-degenerate subgraph count as
N\to\infty
.

{\Diamond}

The asymptotic behavior of functions
\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)
may be different for different subgraphs
g
. It is therefore useful to identify, for any pair of graphs
H,H^{\star}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
, common subgraph patterns for which the decay of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)
is the slowest. More, concretely, we consider common subgraph patterns
g
for which
\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)

remains asymptotically at least as large as the decay
\mathcal{F}_{N}(g^{\star})
of any other common subgraph pattern
g^{\star}
:

\displaystyle{{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}})=\left\{g\in{\tt CS}({H},{H^{\star})}\colon\forall g^{\star}\in{\tt CS}({H},{H^{\star}}):\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g^{\star})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)}\in[0,\infty)\right\}.}

(3.7)

In particular, as (
2.3
) tells us that for any
g,g^{\star}\in{\tt CS}(H,H^{\star})

\displaystyle{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g^{\star})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)}=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{\mathcal{V}(g)-\mathcal{V}(g^{\star})}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g)-\mathcal{E}(g^{\star})}\in\{0,1,\infty\},}

due to (
3.7
) for any
g\in{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}})
and any
g^{\star}\in{\tt CS}({H},{H^{\star}})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g^{\star})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{iff}\quad g^{\star}\in{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}}),\\
0,\quad\text{iff}\quad g^{\star}\not\in{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}}).\end{cases}

(3.8)

Hence, for any graphs
{H},{H^{\star}}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
, if we define
\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H},{H^{\star}})=\mathcal{F}_{N}(g^{\star})
for some
g^{\star}\in{\tt OCS}(H,H^{\star})
, then due to (
3.8
) for any
g\in{\tt CS}({H},{H^{\star}})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)}{\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H},{H^{\star}})}=\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(g,{H},{H^{\star}})=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{iff}\quad g\in{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}}),\\
0,\quad\text{iff}\quad g\not\in{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}}).\end{cases}

(3.9)

Again, for simplicity we write
\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}):=\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H},{H})
.
We call two graphs
H,\,H^{\star}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}

equioptimal
if

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm opt}({H},{H^{\star}})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm opt}({H})}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm opt}({H},{H^{\star}})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm opt}({H^{\star}})}=1.

(3.10)

Remark 6
.

Again, by virtue to (
2.3
), the limit in (
3.10
) cannot be positive, finite and different from
1
.

{\Diamond}

\circ

{H}\cong{H^{\star}}
if graphs

are equioptimal. A combination of (
3.10
) and (

then

\displaystyle{\tt OCS}({H},{H^{\star}})={\tt OCS}({H})\cap{\tt OCS}({H^{\star}}),

(3.11)

and if
{H}\not\cong{H^{\star}}

\displaystyle{\tt OCS}({H})\cap{\tt OCS}({H^{\star}})=\varnothing.

(3.12)

\circ

Finally, for any graphs
{H},{H^{\star}}{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}

{\pi(g_{1}\cap g_{2})=g}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}(H,H^{\star},g)}!}{.}

4
Main result

In this section we state our functional central limit theorem for a vector of subgraph count processes. To this end, we fix, for a given
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, (deterministic) graphs
{H}_{1},\ldots,{H}_{m}{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
, which satisfy Assumption
3.1
; think for instance of
m=2
and
{H}_{1}
corresponding to edges and
{H}_{2}
to triangles.
Recall that from (
3.5
) we know that for any
i\in\langle m\rangle
and any
g\subset{H}_{i}
with at least one vertex

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}(g)=0.

(4.1)

The key object in our analysis is the
m
-dimensional process

\displaystyle\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{N}(t)\bigr)_{t\in[0,T]}=\big((X_{N,1}(t)),\ldots,(X_{N,m}(t))\big)_{{t\in[0,T]}}^{\top},

where, for
i\in\langle m\rangle
,
X_{N,i}(t)
records the number of subgraphs of
G_{N}(t)
which are isomorphic to
{H}_{i}
. Using (
3.2
) the marginals of the process
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{N}(t)
can be alternatively defined via

\displaystyle X_{N,i}(t)=\sum_{g\in{\tt G}_{N}({H}_{i})}\mathbb{I}\{g{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t)\}=\sum_{g\in{\tt G}_{N}({H}_{i})}\prod_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g)}a_{N,u,v}(t).

(4.2)

For each graph pattern
g\in{\tt G}_{\sim}
we define a centered Gaussian process
(X^{\star}_{g}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
, characterized via the covariance function

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X^{\star}_{g}(t),X^{\star}_{g}(s))=\big(\kappa_{\star}(s,t)\big)^{\mathcal{E}(g)};

in addition, all processes
(X^{\star}_{g}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
are independent (across the graph patterns
g
).
We can now state our main result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G_{N}(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
be a dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph with
N
vertices which satisfies Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, and let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}{\in{\tt G}_{\infty}}
be graphs which satisfy Assumption
3.1
. Then, as
N\to\infty
, the process
\boldsymbol{X}_{N}(\cdot)
defined in (
4.2
) satisfies the following limiting theorem:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{X_{N,1}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,1}(t)\right\}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{1})}/\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{1})},\ldots,\frac{X_{N,m}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,m}(t)\right\}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{m})}/\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{m})}\right)^{\top}\to\boldsymbol{X}(t),

in distribution in
\mathbb{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
, where
\boldsymbol{X}(\cdot)
is centered vector-valued Gaussian process with components

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=\sum_{g\in{\tt OCS}({H}_{i})}\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}(g)}\,\mathcal{S}({H}_{i},g)}{\mathcal{A}({H}_{i})}\bigl(p_{\star}(t)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{i})-\mathcal{E}(g)}X^{\star}_{g}(t).

Remark 7
.

The covariance matrix function
\Sigma(s,t)
of the process
\boldsymbol{X}(\cdot)
defined in Theorem
4.1
can be evaluated more explicitly. A standard computation reveals that it can be represented, in terms of the function
p_{\star}(\cdot)
, as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i,j}(s,t)=\mathbb{I}\{{H}_{i}\cong{H}_{j}\}\sum_{g\in{\tt OCS}({H}_{i},{H}_{j})}\frac{\mathcal{A}(g)\,\mathcal{S}({H}_{i},g)\,\mathcal{S}({H}_{j},g)}{\mathcal{A}({H}_{i})\,\mathcal{A}({H}_{j})}\frac{\bigl(p_{\star}(s)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{i})}\bigl(p_{\star}(t)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{j})}}{\bigl(p_{\star}(s)\,p_{\star}(t)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}(g)}}\kappa^{\mathcal{E}(g)}_{\star}(s,t)

for
s,t\geqslant 0
.

{\Diamond}

The following corollary covers the case when the distribution of the edge processes of
G_{N}({\cdot})
is independent of the size of the graph.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
G_{N}(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
be a dynamic Erdős–Rényi random graph with
N
vertices, which satisfies Assumption
2.2
(for
\varrho_{N}=1
), and assume that the edge process
a_{N}(\cdot)
does not depend on
N
. Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}\in{\tt G}_{\infty}
be graphs with at least one edge. Then, the process
\boldsymbol{X}_{N}(\cdot)
defined in (
4.2
) satisfies the following limiting theorem: as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{X_{N,1}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,1}(t)\right\}}{N^{\mathcal{V}(H_{1})-1}},\ldots,\frac{X_{N,m}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,m}(t)\right\}}{N^{\mathcal{V}(H_{m})-1}}\right)^{\top}\to\boldsymbol{X}(t)

in distribution in
\mathbb{D}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
, where
\boldsymbol{X}(\cdot)
is a centered vector-valued Gaussian process with components

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\mathcal{E}(H_{i})}{\mathcal{A}({H}_{i})}\bigl(p(t)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{i})-1}X^{\star}(t),

and where
p(t)=p_{N}(t)
,
\kappa(s,t)=\kappa_{N}(s,t)
(which are independent of
N
due to our assumption), and
X^{\star}(\cdot)
is a centered Gaussian process characterized via its covariance function

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X^{\star}(s),X^{\star}(t))=\kappa(s,t).

Example 8
.

To get an impression of a typical application of Theorem
4.1
, consider the case of
m=2
, with the subgraphs corresponding to wedges and triangles.
Consider the edge processes defined in Example
4
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then one can check that both wedge graph (denoted by
{H}_{1}
) and triangle graph (denoted by
{H}_{2}
) satisfy (
3.5
), and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{1})=N^{2\alpha-3},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{2})=N^{3\alpha-3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{1})=N^{\alpha-2},\qquad\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{2})=N^{\max(\alpha-2,3\alpha-3)}.

The next step is to describe the sets
{\tt OCS(H_{1})}
and
{\tt OCS(H_{2})}
. The first one is easy to find:
{\tt OCS(H_{1})}=\{g_{1}\}
, where
{g_{1}\in{\tt G}_{\sim}}
is the pattern of an edge. However, for the second one we need to distinguish three cases:

\circ

If
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
then
{\tt OCS}({H}_{2})=\{g_{1}\}
,

\circ

If
\alpha=1/2
then
{\tt OCS}({H}_{2})=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}
,

\circ

If
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
then
{\tt OCS}({H}_{2})=\{g_{2}\}
,

where
{g_{2}\in{\tt G}_{\sim}}
is the pattern of a triangle. It is readily verified that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}({H}_{1})=2,\qquad\mathcal{A}({H}_{2})=6,\qquad\mathcal{A}(g_{1})=2,\qquad\mathcal{A}(g_{2})=6,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}({H}_{1},g_{1})=2,\qquad\mathcal{S}({H}_{2},g_{1})=3,\qquad\mathcal{S}({H}_{2},g_{2})=1.

Applying Theorem
4.1
we obtain that, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{X_{N,1}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,1}(t)\right\}}{N^{2-3\alpha/2}},\frac{X_{N,2}(t)-\mathbb{E}\left\{X_{N,2}(t)\right\}}{N^{\max(2-5\alpha/2,\,3/2-3\alpha/2)}}\right)^{\top}\to(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t))^{\top},

where

\displaystyle X_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{\lambda_{\rm off}}{\lambda_{\rm on}}X_{1}^{\star}(t),

\displaystyle X_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\{\alpha\leqslant 1/2\}\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{6}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm off}}{\lambda_{\rm on}}\right)^{2}X_{1}^{\star}(t)+\mathbb{I}\{\alpha\geqslant 1/2\}\frac{\sqrt{6}}{6}X^{\star}_{2}(t);

the coefficients in these expressions can be simplified in the obvious manner, and

and
X_{2}^{\star}(\cdot)
are two independent centered Gaussian processes, characterized via the covariance functions

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X_{1}^{\star}(s),X_{1}^{\star}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\rm off}}{\lambda_{\rm on}}e^{-\lambda_{\rm on}\left\lvert s-t\right\rvert},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X_{2}^{\star}(s),X_{2}^{\star}(t))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm off}}{\lambda_{\rm on}}\right)^{3}e^{-3\lambda_{\rm on}\left\lvert s-t\right\rvert},

respectively.
In particular, for
\alpha<1/2
we have that processes
X_{1}(\cdot)
and
X_{2}(\cdot)
are perfectly correlated, whereas for
\alpha>1/2
they are independent. In case
\alpha=1/2
, the processes
X_{1}(\cdot)
and
X_{2}(\cdot)
are positively but not perfectly correlated.

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in{\tt G}_{N}({H_{i}})}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbb{I}\{g{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t)\}\right\}

g_{2}\in{\tt G}_{N}({H_{j}})\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{g_{1}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(s)\},\mathbb{I}\{g_{2}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t)\}\bigr).

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in{\tt OCS}({H}_{i})\cap{\tt OCS}({H}_{j})\end{subarray}}{\frac{\mathcal{A}(g)}{\mathcal{A}({H}_{i})\mathcal{A}({H}_{j})}}\frac{\bigl(p_{\star}(t)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{i})}\bigl(p_{\star}(s)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}({H}_{j})}}{\bigl(p_{\star}(t)\,p_{\star}(s)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}(g)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathcal{S}({H}_{i},{g})\,\mathcal{S}({H}_{j},{g})\bigl(\kappa_{\star}(t,s)\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}(g)}.

where in the last line we use the independence of the individual processes
X^{\star}_{g}(\cdot)
.
Hence, the claim follows from applying (

), (

) and Lemma
3.2
.

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

For every edge
(u,v)\in{\tt E}(K_{N})
and time
t^{\star}\in[0,T]
, define the event that the edge
(u,v)
exists at time
t^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{(u,v)}^{+}(t^{\star})=\{a_{N,u,v}(t^{\star})=1\}.

Likewise, for every pair of times
t^{\star},t^{\star\star}\in[0,T]
, we define the event that the edge
(u,v)
switches between times
t^{\star}
and
t^{\star\star}
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{(u,v)}^{\not=}({t^{\star},t^{\star\star}})=\{a_{N,u,v}(t^{\star})\not=a_{N,u,v}(t^{\star\star})\}.

By the definition of the processes
a_{N,u,v}(\cdot)
, all the events
\Omega_{(u,v)}^{+}(t^{\star})
and
\Omega_{(u,v)}^{\not=}({t^{\star},t^{\star\star}})
are independent for different edges
(u,v)
.
One readily verifies that for every
k\in\langle 4\rangle
we can represent the event
\Omega_{k}
in terms of events
\Omega^{+}
and
\Omega^{\neq}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}=\left(\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}(x_{k})\right)\cup\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}(y_{k})\right)\right){\bigcap}\left(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{\not=}_{(u,v)}(x_{k},y_{k})\right){.}

(A.15)

As
x_{k},y_{k}\in\{r,s,t\}
it is convenient to also define the following event:

\displaystyle\Omega^{+}_{(u,v)}=\Omega_{(u,v)}(r)\cup\Omega_{(u,v)}(s)\cup\Omega_{(u,v)}(t).

Then we can slightly enlarge the event on the right hand side of (
A.15
), obtaining

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}\right){\bigcap}\left(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{\not=}_{(u,v)}(x_{k},y_{k})\right){.}

(A.16)

Based on the inclusions (
A.16
), we can obtain similar inclusions for the ‘double event’: for any
k_{1},k_{2}\in\langle 4\rangle
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}})}\Omega^{+}_{u,v}\right)\left(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}})}\Omega^{\not=}_{(u,v)}(x_{k_{1}},y_{k_{1}})\right).

(A.17)

Along the same lines, for triple event for different
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\langle 4\rangle
(without loss of generality we may assume that
k_{1}\in\{1,2\}
and
k_{2}\in\{3,4\}
),

\displaystyle\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}\Omega_{k_{3}}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}\right){\bigcap}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1},v_{1})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}})\\
(u_{2},v_{2})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{2}})\end{subarray}}\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{1},v_{1})}(r,s)\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{2},v_{2})}(s,t)\right),

(A.18)

and for the intersection of all four events
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\langle 4\rangle
(again, without loss of generality we may again assume that
k_{1}\in\{1,2\}
and
k_{2}\in\{3,4\}
),

\displaystyle\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}\Omega_{k_{3}}\Omega_{k_{4}}

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}}\cup g_{k_{4}})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}\right){\bigcap}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1},v_{1})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}})\\
(u_{2},v_{2})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{2}})\end{subarray}}\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{1},v_{1})}(r,s)\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{2},v_{2})}(s,t)\right).

(A.19)

Using (
2.7
), (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we thus find that, for any
(u,v)\in{\tt E}(K_{N})
and any
x_{k},y_{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{+}_{(u,v)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{p_{+}}\,\varrho_{N}{,}

(A.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{(u,v)}^{\not=}(x_{k},y_{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\varrho_{N}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert,

(A.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{(u,v)}^{\not=}(r,s)\Omega_{(u,v)}^{\not=}(s,r)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\varrho_{N}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert^{2}.

(A.22)

Hence, applying the bounds (
A.20
) and (
A.21
) to the probability of the event (
A.16
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{{(u^{\star},v^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u^{\star},v^{\star})}(x_{k},y_{k})\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}\Omega^{+}_{{(u,v)}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{{(u^{\star},v^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k})}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u^{\star},v^{\star})}(x_{k},y_{k})\right\}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k})\\
(u,v)\not=(u^{\star},v^{\star})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{+}_{(u,v)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k})-1}}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert\leqslant C_{0}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert;

recall that constant
C_{0}
was defined in (
5.7
). Similarly, applying the same two bounds to the probability of the event (
A.17
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{2}})-1}}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert\leqslant C_{0}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert.

Applying now all three bounds
(
A.20
)
, (
A.21
) and (
A.22
) to the probability of the event (
A.18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}\Omega_{k_{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1}^{\star},v_{1}^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}})\\
(u_{2}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{2}})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{1}^{\star},v_{1}^{\star})}(r,s)\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{2}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})}(s,t)\left(\bigcap_{(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{3})}\Omega^{+}_{u,v}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1}^{\star},v_{1}^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}})\\
(u_{2}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{2}})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{1}^{\star},v_{1}^{\star})}(r,s)\right\}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u_{2}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})}(s,t)\right\}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})\\
(u,v)\not=(u_{1}^{\star},v_{1}^{\star})\\
(u,v)\not=(u_{2}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{+}_{u,v}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{(u^{\star},v^{\star})\in{\tt E}({g_{k_{1}}})\cap{\tt E}({g_{k_{2}}})}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{\not=}_{(u^{\star},v^{\star})}(r,s)\Omega^{\not=}_{(u^{\star},v^{\star})}(s,t)\right\}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in{\tt E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})\\
(u,v)\not=(u^{\star},v^{\star})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega^{+}_{u,v}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}^{2}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}})\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{2}})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})-2}}\,\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+{\mathfrak{C}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cap g_{k_{2}})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})-1}}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert^{2}

Along the same lines we can show, relying on (
A.19
), that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\Omega_{k_{1}}\Omega_{k_{2}}\Omega_{k_{3}}\Omega_{k_{4}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathfrak{C}^{2}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}})\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{2}})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}}\cup g_{k_{4}})-2}}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}}\cup g_{k_{4}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\,{\mathfrak{C}}\,\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cap g_{k_{2}})\,{p_{+}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}}\cup g_{k_{4}})-1}}\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(g_{k_{1}}\cup g_{k_{2}}\cup g_{k_{3}}\cup g_{k_{4}})}\left\lvert t-r\right\rvert^{2}

follows.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

We establish the statement of this lemma for any connected graph
{\tt G}{\,{\subset}\,}K_{v}
with at least two vertices; recall that from (
7.9
) we know that
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})\geqslant 2
for any
{\tt G}\in{\tt CC}(\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{g}))
.
As
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{N^{\mathcal{V}({\tt G})}\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}\frac{\mathcal{F}({H}_{f_{i}})\xi_{f_{i},{N}}(g_{i})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}}\right\}

\displaystyle{=N^{\mathcal{V({\tt g})}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt g})}\xi_{f_{i},N}(g_{i})\right\}\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm{opt}}({H}_{f_{i}})}}=:\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})}{.}

(A.23)

From (
7.5
) we derive that

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt g})}\xi_{f_{i},N}(g_{i})=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{\mathcal{V}({\tt g})}=1}^{n}\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt g})}\frac{q_{k,f_{i}}\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{g_{i}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t_{k_{i}})\}-\mathbb{P}\left\{g_{i}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t_{k_{i}})\right\}\bigr)}{\bigl(p_{\star}(t_{k_{i}})\bigr)^{\mathcal{E}(g_{i})}}{.}

(A.24)

As
N\to\infty
, using Assumption
2.1
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt g})}\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{g_{i}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t_{k_{i}})\}-\mathbb{P}\left\{g_{i}{\,{\subset}\,}G_{N}(t_{k_{i}})\right\}\bigr)\right\}\leqslant(p_{+}\varrho_{N})^{\mathcal{E}(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}g_{i})}.

(A.25)

Hence, from (
A.24
) and (
A.25
) we can see that, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt g})}\xi_{f_{i},N}(g_{i})\right\}=\bigl(C_{{\tt G}}+o(1)\bigr)\varrho_{N}^{\mathcal{E}(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}g_{i})}

(A.26)

for some constant
C_{{\tt G}}\in[0,\infty)
. Combining (
A.23
) with (
A.26
) we thus derive that, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{N^{\mathcal{V}({\tt G})}\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}\frac{\mathcal{F}({H}_{f_{i}})\xi_{f_{i},{N}}(g_{i})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm opt}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigl(C_{{\tt G}}+o(1)\bigr)\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{N}\left(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}g_{i}\right)}\prod_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G})}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm{opt}}({H}_{f_{i}})}}

(A.27)

\displaystyle=:\bigl(C_{{\tt G}}+o(1)\bigr)\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G}){.}

So, to verify the statement of Lemma
7.1
it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})\in[0,\infty),

(A.28)

and moreover, if
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})\geqslant 3
then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})=0.

(A.29)

As we could restrict ourselves to
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})\geqslant 2
, we are done if we have proved that claim for
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})=2
and
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})\geqslant 3
. Consider first the case
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})=2
. Let
{\tt V}({\tt G})=\{i,j\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})\mathcal{F}_{N}({H}_{f_{j}})}{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g_{i}\cup g_{i})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{j}})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g_{i}\cap g_{j})}{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{j}})}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g_{i}\cap g_{j})}{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{i}})}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}(g_{i}\cap g_{j})}{\mathcal{F}^{\rm{opt}}_{N}({H}_{f_{j}})}}{.}

By virtue of (
3.9
) (as
(g_{i}\cap g_{j}){\,{\subset}\,}g_{i}\sim{H}_{f_{i}}
and
(g_{i}\cap g_{j}){\,{\subset}\,}g_{j}\sim{H}_{f_{j}}
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})\in\{0,{1}\}\subset[0,\infty),

and (
A.28
) follows. We now proceed to the case
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})\geqslant 3
. We are going to show (
A.29
) by induction to
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})
. Our induction step is the following. Assume for any graph
{\tt G}{\,{\subset}\,}K_{v}
such that
\mathcal{V}({\tt G})=k-1
that the statement (
A.28
) applies. Then for any
{\tt G}{\,{\subset}\,}K_{v}
such that

\mathcal{V}({\tt G})=k
we find (
A.29

For any connected graph
{\tt G}{\,{\subset}\,}K_{v}
with
k

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G}^{\star})\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}\biggl(\bigl(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G}^{\star})}g_{i}\bigr)\cap g_{j}\biggr)}{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm{opt}}({H}_{f_{j}})}}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{N}\biggl(\bigl(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G}^{\star})}g_{i}\bigr)\cap g_{j}\biggr)}{.}

A.28
) for
\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt g}^{\star})

3.9
) (as

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{N}({\tt G}^{\star})\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{F}_{N}\biggl(\bigl(\cup_{i\in{\tt V}({\tt G}^{\star})}g_{i}\bigr)\cap g_{j}\biggr)}{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm{opt}}({H}_{f_{j}})}}\in[0,\infty).

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.
Let us fix
{H}{\,{\subset}\,}{\tt M}_{z}

\boldsymbol{g}^{\cap}={(g^{\cap}_{1},\ldots,g^{\cap}_{z/2})}\in{\tt OCS}_{\boldsymbol{f}}({H})
