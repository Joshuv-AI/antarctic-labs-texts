Determination and comparison of serum C-reactive protein levels in patients with Omicron and Delta strains of COVID-19 hospitalized in Imam Khomeini Hospital, Urmia, 2021

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, emerged in December 2019, leading to millions of infections and deaths globally. Over time, various variants, including Delta and Omicron, have emerged, each with distinct mutations in the spike protein and varying levels of transmissibility and clinical outcomes. The Delta variant, was reported in India on 23 june 2021, quickly became dominant worldwide due to its higher transmissibility and potential to cause severe disease [1]. In contrast, the Omicron variant, emerging in late 2021, has more mutations, especially in the spike protein, and is associated with milder disease in fully vaccinated individuals, though it remains highly contagious [2]. These differences have raised questions about their impact on immune response and inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), a key predictor of disease severity in COVID-19 patients [3].

C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute-phase reactant produced by the liver in response to inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, IL-1, and TNF-α. It plays a key role in the immune response by binding to damaged cells and pathogens, aiding in their clearance [4]. Elevated CRP levels are commonly seen in inflammatory conditions, including bacterial and viral infections, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammation [5]. In COVID-19, CRP has become a key biomarker for assessing disease severity, with higher levels linked to severe outcomes like pneumonia, ARDS, and multi-organ failure [6]. CRP is also used as a prognostic tool to predict poor outcomes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, with elevated levels often correlating with the need for intensive care or ventilation [7].

The inflammatory response in COVID-19 patients involves both innate and adaptive immune systems, with its intensity varying based on factors like age, comorbidities, vaccination status, and the specific SARS-CoV-2 variant [8]. While both Delta and Omicron variants trigger systemic inflammation, their impact on CRP levels differs [9]. The Delta variant is associated with higher inflammation and elevated CRP levels, whereas Omicron, despite its higher transmissibility, may induce a milder inflammatory response, especially in vaccinated individuals [10]. These differences highlight the importance of comparing CRP levels across variants to understand their clinical implications [11].

Understanding CRP level differences between the Delta and Omicron variants is essential for optimizing patient care. CRP helps identify patients at higher risk for complications, guiding therapeutic decisions. While many studies have explored CRP and COVID-19 severity, few have specifically compared CRP levels between Delta and Omicron infections. This gap is especially relevant given ongoing pandemic waves and emerging variants. This study aims to compare serum CRP levels in patients hospitalized with Delta or Omicron variants at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia during 2021, providing insights into their inflammatory differences and clinical impact.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This retrospective study reviewed the medical records of patients hospitalized with the Omicron and Delta variants of COVID-19 at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia, following ethics approval. Patients admitted from the beginning of the second half of 2021 with these diagnoses were included. Patients admitted from the beginning of the second half of 2021 (i.e., from October 2021) to the end of May 2022 with the relevant diagnoses were included in the study.

During the study period, a total of 673 patient records were reviewed. After applying the exclusion criteria—including underlying malignancies, conditions that could independently raise CRP levels (such as autoimmune diseases, bacterial infections, and chronic inflammatory disorders), poisoning, and lack of consent—64 patients were excluded. Therefore, 609 eligible patients diagnosed with the Omicron or Delta variants of COVID-19 at Imam Khomeini Hospital during this period were included in the final analysis.

Vaccination history: The study included patients who received various types of COVID-19 vaccines, which were administered based on availability and national vaccination programs. These vaccines included Sinopharm (inactivated vaccine), AstraZeneca (vector-based vaccine), Sputnik V (vector-based vaccine), and Covaxin (protein-based vaccine), among others. The identification of the Delta and Omicron strains in our study was based on the information recorded in the patients’ medical files. This information included clinical details, laboratory results, and data related to the COVID-19 variant diagnosis, which were documented during the patients’ hospital visits. We relied on these registered records to differentiate between the two strains, as they were identified through clinical data and epidemiological trends in the region.

The data collected included baseline serum CRP levels at admission, patient age, gender, vaccination history, length of hospital stay, and treatment outcomes (discharge, ICU admission, or death). Data were collected over an 8-month period during the second half of 2021 at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia. Serum CRP levels were measured using the ELISA method in the hospital’s central laboratory, adhering to standardized laboratory protocols.

To minimize the impact of confounding variables, we collected and incorporated data on key potential confounders, including age, sex, and vaccination status. Although detailed data on comorbidities and medication use were not available for all patients, these factors were acknowledged as potential sources of residual confounding and discussed in the limitations section. Descriptive statistics and multivariate analyses were employed to assess the relationship between CRP levels and clinical outcomes while accounting for these confounders. All eligible patients (n = 609) admitted during the 8-month study period from the beginning of the second half of 2021 were included in the study using a census sampling method. Data were recorded in a checklist and analyzed using SPSS version 23.

Data analysis

Continuous variables (e.g., serum CRP levels) are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Comparisons between two groups were made using independent t-tests, while ANOVA or its non-parametric equivalent was used for comparisons across more than two groups. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were employed for categorical data, depending on expected cell counts.

To account for potential confounding, multivariable linear regression was conducted, adjusting for age, sex, and comorbidities. Both unadjusted and adjusted estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals to reflect the precision of the results. Subgroup analyses were performed based on vaccination status, gender, and variant type (Omicron vs. Delta). Sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of outliers and alternative model specifications, were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

To evaluate the discriminative ability of CRP levels for mortality prediction, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding confidence intervals were calculated using nonparametric methods.

Ethical consideration

The Urmia University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee approved this study proposal (code: IR.UMSU.REC.1401.055).The researchers adhered to all the principles recommended by the Helsinki Declaration on Ethics in Research. Patients’ personal and attributable information was kept confidential.

Results

Of the 609 patients participating in this study, 56.3% were female. The patients included in the study were aged between 17 and 91 years. The mean age of the patients was 54.34 ± 15.88 years. 399 (65.5%) had received the vaccine. Additionally, 280 (46%) patients were infected with the Omicron variant, and 329 (54%) were infected with the Delta variant. 69.1% of patients were treated in the ward and 20.6% died (Table 1).

Discussion

This study compared serum C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in COVID-19 patients infected with the Omicron and Delta variants at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia. CRP is a biomarker for inflammation and is often elevated in severe infections. The results showed significant variation in CRP levels based on vaccination status, disease outcome, and gender, but no significant difference between the Omicron and Delta variants in terms of CRP levels.

Although our study did not find a statistically significant difference in CRP levels between patients infected with the Delta and Omicron variants, this does not necessarily indicate equivalence in their inflammatory responses. It is possible that both variants triggered similar systemic inflammation in our hospitalized cohort due to comparable disease severity at admission. CRP levels can be influenced by various factors such as vaccination status, comorbidities, timing of measurement, and treatments received (e.g., corticosteroids). Moreover, inflammatory markers like CRP may not always align with clinical severity, as suggested by previous reports [12, 13]. Recent studies have shown mixed results regarding CRP responses to different variants. One study found no significant difference in CRP levels between vaccinated and unvaccinated patients during the Omicron and Delta waves, aligning with our findings and suggesting that systemic inflammation may not vary significantly despite differences in clinical presentation [12]. Another study reported that deceased patients had significantly elevated CRP, D-dimer, and troponin levels compared to survivors, especially in the second week of hospitalization highlighting the importance of longitudinal biomarker monitoring and the need for multi-marker panels in clinical evaluation [14]. Additionally, evidence shows that the humoral immune response may play a key role in modulating inflammatory outcomes. In a cohort study assessing anti-nucleocapsid IgG levels, individuals lacking detectable antibodies post-recovery were markedly more susceptible to reinfection within 1–3 months [15]. Such reinfections could trigger altered immune responses, potentially confounding comparisons between variant-specific inflammation.These findings suggest that host immune variability, rather than variant characteristics alone, may explain inconsistencies in CRP trends across studies. Although our study did not explore mechanistic immune pathways, it is plausible that both Delta and Omicron variants activate overlapping inflammatory cascades involving cytokines such as interleukins and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which were not evaluated here. Future studies should incorporate broader immune profiling (e.g., IL-6, D-dimer, TNF-α) alongside standardized severity scoring to better understand variant-specific immunopathology [16, 17]. Overall, while CRP remains a useful marker, it should be interpreted in the context of broader immunologic and clinical data, and future research should aim to disentangle the complex interplay between variant biology, host immunity, and inflammatory outcomes.

One key finding of this study was the significant relationship between vaccination status and CRP levels. The independent t-test results showed that vaccinated patients had lower mean CRP levels compared to unvaccinated patients. This supports previous research suggesting that vaccination may modulate the immune response and potentially reduce the severity of the inflammatory response in COVID-19 patients [3, 13]. The lower CRP levels in vaccinated individuals could reflect a less severe inflammatory response and potentially better clinical outcomes, which is consistent with the growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of vaccines in reducing severe disease and hospitalization [16, 17].

Another key finding was the relationship between CRP levels and patient outcomes. The study found significantly higher CRP levels in patients who died compared to those who recovered. Elevated CRP levels are often linked to severe disease, reflecting systemic inflammation, which plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of COVID-19, especially in severe cases that lead to ARDS and multiorgan failure [3, 4]. This result supports the growing evidence that CRP can serve as a useful prognostic marker in COVID-19, helping clinicians identify patients at higher risk for poor outcomes [6]. High CRP levels are associated with increased disease severity and could help guide therapeutic decisions, especially in resource-limited settings where advanced diagnostic tools may not be easily accessible. Additionally, the study found that female patients had significantly higher mean CRP levels compared to male patients. This aligns with reports suggesting gender differences in the immune response to COVID-19, with women typically exhibiting a stronger inflammatory response than men [18]. It is important to note that while women generally have a more robust immune response to infections, this can sometimes result in an exaggerated inflammatory response, potentially contributing to higher CRP levels. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms behind these gender differences and how they may influence COVID-19 outcomes.

Although this study did not directly investigate the underlying immunopathological mechanisms, the observed similarity in CRP levels may reflect overlapping inflammatory pathways activated by both variants. Inflammatory responses in COVID-19 are primarily driven by cytokine release, including interleukins and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, which were not assessed in this study. Future research incorporating broader biomarker profiles and standardized severity scoring could further clarify the immunological differences between variants.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the observational design introduces the possibility of selection bias. Measurement bias in CRP assessment may also be present, and reliance on self-reported vaccination status could affect data accuracy. Furthermore, the lack of randomization limits the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Although efforts were made to adjust for potential confounders, detailed information on comorbid conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity was not available for all patients and therefore could not be comprehensively analyzed. This is an important limitation, as such conditions can independently influence CRP levels and disease severity. Another limitation is the lack of detailed information on treatment regimens, which were subject to changes over time based on evolving guidelines. Treatment protocols, such as the introduction of dexamethasone at a certain point, could have impacted patient outcomes and the inflammatory response. This variability in treatment could have influenced the results, but it was not systematically recorded in our dataset.

Additionally, the study population consists of hospitalized patients during an 8-month period in 2021 at Imam Khomeini Hospital in Urmia, which may not be representative of patients in other settings or timeframes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and not generalized beyond the context of the study. Furthermore, a potential limitation that must be acknowledged is the possibility of misclassification of the COVID-19 variants due to diagnostic limitations. The identification of the Delta and Omicron variants was based on available clinical data and regional epidemiological trends, which may not have been entirely accurate due to evolving testing methods and diagnostic tools.

Lastly, the retrospective design of the study introduces the risk of residual confounding, despite the statistical adjustments made. Unmeasured or unknown confounders may have influenced the results, and prospective multicenter studies with a larger, more diverse sample would be valuable in further validating CRP’s prognostic value in COVID-19.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential role of CRP as a marker for disease severity and prognosis in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. The findings suggest that vaccination status and disease outcomes significantly influence CRP levels, while no significant differences were observed between the Omicron and Delta variants in terms of CRP levels. Elevated CRP levels were linked to higher mortality, underscoring the importance of CRP as a potential prognostic tool. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms behind gender differences in CRP levels and to better understand CRP’s role in predicting the clinical course of COVID-19.
