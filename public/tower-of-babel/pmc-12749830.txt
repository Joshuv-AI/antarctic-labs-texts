Experiments and physical Investigation on the adsorption of methylene on activated carbon
Authors: Souhail Mohammed Bouzgarrou, Hedi Jedli, Rym Hassani, Ehab Sabi, Afzal Husain Khan, Khalifa Slimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, activated carbon (AC) derived from olive waste was employed as an alternative adsorbent for the removal of methylene blue (MB) from aqueous solutions. The AC was characterized using FTIR, SEM, XRD and N₂ adsorption–desorption analyses. The pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Langmuir adsorption isotherm provided a good fit to the experimental data at 298, 303, and 313 K. A multilayer adsorption model was applied to elucidate the adsorption mechanisms between the dye molecules and the adsorbent. The number of MB active sites (n) was greater than unity at all temperatures, indicating a vertical orientation of the adsorbed molecules. The number of active sites (Dm) showed only slight variation with temperature. The decrease in MB adsorption capacities (Qm) from 235.21 to 198.62 mg·g⁻1 over the temperature range of 298–313 K was primarily influenced by the n parameter. Adsorption energy analysis revealed that MB adsorption was exothermic and occurred via a physisorption process. This study provides valuable insights into the interactions between MB molecules and AC active sites.Keywords: Adsorption, Activated carbon, Methylene blue, Physical modelingSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Many industries generate effluents containing dyes during their manufacturing processes1. In particular, textile industries discharge significant amounts of dyes in their wastewater, which exhibit toxic effects on the environment and living organisms2. Various chemical and physical methods have been developed for dye removal, including coagulation, adsorption, membrane separation, and chemical oxidation3. Among these treatment techniques, adsorption is recognized as one of the most effective methods4. Numerous adsorbents have been tested for color removal from aqueous effluents, with activated carbon being the most widely used due to its excellent textural properties; however, its high production and regeneration costs limit its large-scale application5. Consequently, many researchers have explored the use of low-cost materials as carbon precursors for dye and wastewater treatment, such as corn cob6, Coriandrum sativum7, waste orange peels8, almond shells9, peach stones10, and pine wood11.

In this context, activated carbon derived from olive waste was used to study the removal of methylene blue (MB) dye. Adsorption isotherms for MB on the activated carbon were measured at different temperatures (298, 308, and 318 K), and the adsorbent was thoroughly characterized. The main objective of this study was to investigate the adsorption mechanism through both experimental and theoretical analyses. A physical model was successfully applied to establish a strong correlation between the adsorption properties, the adsorbent characterization, and the MB adsorption behavior. These findings provide new insights into the separation mechanism and contribute to a better understanding of the methylene blue adsorption process.

Experimental section

Synthesis of activated carbon

Activated carbon (AC) was prepared from olive waste following a modified procedure12. The raw olives were washed with demineralized water and dried in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h. The dried material was then ground to particles of 200–400 µm. The olive particles were impregnated with 85% phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) at a 1:1 weight ratio for 24 h. The impregnated material was heated at 100 °C to remove excess moisture, then washed with deionized water and NaHCO₃ to neutralize residual acidity. Carbonization was performed at 600 °C for 4 h, and the resulting AC was washed thoroughly with distilled water and dried at 100 °C for subsequent use.

Characterization of the adsorbent

Various techniques were employed to characterize the prepared activated carbon. The crystallinity of the adsorbent was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips MPD1880-PW1710 diffractometer. The functional groups were identified by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker Vertex 70-RAM II). The surface morphology of the activated carbon was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Q250 Thermo Fisher). The textural properties were determined by nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a Micromeritics 3Flex analyzer.

Study of adsorption

Adsorption kinetics

The absorbance of the solutions was measured using a UV spectrophotometer at λmax = 664 nm, corresponding to the maximum wavelength of MB.

Characterization of the AC

The FTIR spectra of the activated carbon are shown in Fig. 1. The band at 3420 cm⁻1 was attributed to –OH vibrations. The bands at 2924 cm⁻1 and 3000 cm⁻1 correspond to –CH₂ and aliphatic –CH groups, respectively. Additionally, the bands at 1600 cm⁻1 and 1500 cm⁻1 were assigned to C = C functional groups. The bands at 1140 cm⁻1 and 2325 cm⁻1 were attributed to C–O and C–C bonds, respectively.

MB adsorption studies on the AC

The pH of a solution critically influences phenol adsorption by affecting the surface charge and interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate. Under acidic conditions, adsorption is enhanced due to strong electrostatic attraction, whereas at neutral pH, adsorption capacity decreases because of the dominance of positive surface charges12. Figure 5 presents the removal efficiency of MB on activated carbon at different pH values (2–10). The results indicate that the highest removal efficiency (95%) occurs at pH 2 and decreases significantly to 25% as the pH increases.

pH of the MB.

The effect of adsorbent mass on MB adsorption was investigated, and the results are shown in Fig. 6. The removal efficiency increased as the adsorbent mass was raised from 0.01 g to 0.1 g, which can be attributed to the greater specific surface area and availability of adsorption sites5. The maximum adsorption efficiency and equilibrium were achieved at an adsorbent mass of 0.2 g, which was therefore used as the optimal mass in subsequent experiments.

The relationship between MB adsorption and contact time is illustrated in Fig. 7. The adsorption efficiency increased with contact time and reached equilibrium after 20 min. During the initial stage, adsorption occurs rapidly due to the abundance of available pores on the activated carbon surface, which facilitates faster mass transfer of MB from the solution to the adsorbent16.

Comparison with results reported in the literature

Table 4 presents a comparison between the maximum adsorption capacity (qm = 34.4 mg g⁻1) of AC prepared from olive waste and those of other adsorbents reported in the literature. The MB adsorption capacity of AC is higher/lower than that reported for activated carbons prepared from various raw materials. For instance, the highest adsorption capacity reported in the literature was 169.8 mg g−1 for activated carbon derived from Calicotome villosa wood25, whereas the lowest value, 4.8 mg g−1, was obtained for activated carbon produced from grape wood24. This wide variation can be attributed primarily to differences in the raw materials used, as well as to variations in pyrolysis conditions and activation methods. Therefore, the adsorption capacity obtained from activated carbon produced using olive waste appears promising, suggesting the need for further studies to optimize its production conditions.

Conclusion

The performance of the activated carbon (AC) adsorbent for MB dye removal was systematically evaluated. The AC was characterized using FTIR, SEM, XRD, and BET techniques. Key factors affecting MB adsorption included pH, adsorbent mass, and contact time. Kinetic studies and adsorption isotherms were analyzed using the pseudo-second-order model and the Langmuir isotherm, respectively. The adsorption mechanism was further investigated using a physical multilayer model, with steric parameters , Dₘ, and Qₘ evaluated and discussed. Differences in adsorption capacity were found to be primarily influenced by variations in the parameter. Adsorption energy analysis revealed that MB adsorption was exothermic and occurred via a physisorption process.
