Prompt Commons: Collective Prompting as Governance for Urban AI
Rashid Mushkani

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are entering urban governance, yet their outputs are highly sensitive to prompts that carry value judgments. We propose Prompt Commons —a versioned, community-maintained repository of prompts with governance metadata, licensing, and moderation—to steer model behaviour toward pluralism. Using a Montréal dataset (443 human prompts; 3,317 after augmentation), we pilot three governance states ( open , curated , veto-enabled ). On a contested policy benchmark, a single-author prompt yields 24 % 24\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} neutral outcomes; commons-governed prompts raise neutrality to 48 % to 52 % 48\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}52\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} while retaining decisiveness where appropriate. In a synthetic incident log, a veto-enabled regime reduces time-to-remediation for harmful outputs from 30.5 ± ± 8.9 h 30.5\pm\pm 8.9\text{\,}\mathrm{h} (open) to 5.6 ± ± 1.5 h 5.6\pm\pm 1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{h} . We outline licensing (CC BY/BY–SA for prompts with optional OpenRAIL-style restrictions for artefacts), auditable moderation, and safeguards against dominance capture. Prompt governance offers a practical lever for cities to align AI with local values and accountability.



Keywords:

prompt engineering, governance, urban AI, community moderation, licensing, pluralism.

1
Introduction

Urban planners increasingly consult generative AI systems for drafting, summarising, coding, and producing imagery to support community engagement
(
Mushkani et al. 2025e
)
.
Unlike traditional datasets or models, LLMs can change behaviour markedly with small prompt variations: a single phrase can alter tone, policy stance, and inclusivity.
Prior work has emphasised downstream controls—documentation
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
;
Gebru et al. 2021
)
, dataset governance
(
Gebru et al. 2021
)
, and post-training alignment
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
—but the act of
prompting
has rarely been treated as a first-class governance object.

Recent urban AI scholarship indicates that cities are already experimenting with LLMs in planning, management, and policy settings, while warning against deployments that neglect urban complexity. A systematic review maps the breadth of applications and recurring gaps in rigor and evaluation
(
Xia et al. 2025
)
. Strategic informatics work synthesised from Shenzhen’s policy practice proposes cross-cutting plans spanning technology systems, application scenarios, literacy, and governance
(
Zhu and Liu 2025
)
. Interviews with city managers in Australia and the US report pragmatic constraints—organizational preparedness, data, and talent—that mediate adoption
(
Yigitcanlar et al. 2023
)
. These perspectives extend earlier arguments that
local
innovation and governance capacity are decisive for responsible AI in cities
(
Allam and Dhunny 2019
;
Kirwan and Fu 2020
)
.

We investigate whether human-authored prompts, when deployed as individual artefacts, reduce neutrality (i.e., increase decisiveness toward particular value claims). Decisiveness is not inherently undesirable; cities often need concrete recommendations. The concern arises when prompt authorship is opaque and individualised, which risks encoding one group’s values as system defaults.
We therefore propose
Prompt Commons
: a civic infrastructure where communities author, review, license, and version prompts collectively, drawing on commons governance
(
Ostrom 1990
;
Carlisle and Gruby 2019
)
and open-source practice
(
O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007
;
The Linux Foundation 2020
)
.

Contributions.
We (i) introduce a versioned repository and governance metadata for prompts; (ii) evaluate behavioural shifts across governance states using a pilot benchmark of contested urban choices; and (iii) specify IP/licensing and moderation rules designed to protect community authors while enabling reuse.

2
Related Work

Documentation and transparency.

Model Cards and Datasheets set expectations and report performance across subgroups
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
;
Gebru et al. 2021
)
. Prompt Commons complements these by documenting
inputs
that steer model behaviour, not only outputs. In the urban domain, recent syntheses emphasise surfacing context, value trade-offs, and domain limitations
(
Xia et al. 2025
)
. Treating prompts as first-class, versioned objects extends transparency to the moment of interaction.

Post-training governance and alignment.

RLHF and instruct-tuning steer models to human preferences
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
; Constitutional AI aligns behaviour to a principle set
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
. These approaches may reflect the biases of those defining preferences or principles. Democratic or dynamic value alignment aims to involve diverse stakeholders
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
, with social-choice–informed aggregation highlighting how rules affect outcomes
(
Conitzer et al. 2024
)
. Prompt governance is complementary: instead of changing model weights or selecting a single constitution, Prompt Commons enables communities to
configure
behaviour at use time in a visible, auditable way
(
Mushkani et al. 2025b
)
.

Urban AI and LLM applications.

Reviews and case studies note growing use of LLMs in urban analytics and decision-making alongside methodological and governance concerns
(
Xia et al. 2025
;
Zhu and Liu 2025
;
Yigitcanlar et al. 2023
)
. These extend arguments that cities are central to responsible AI deployment and must innovate in governance to align capabilities with local needs
(
Allam and Dhunny 2019
;
Kirwan and Fu 2020
;
Mushkani et al. 2025f
;
Mushkani and Koseki 2025b
)
.

Prompt design, sensitivity, neutrality.

Prompt components (instructions, role, context, output formats, examples) and structured tactics for planning tasks have been synthesised
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Foundational work showed that large models can adapt to new tasks with few examples
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, but such flexibility brings high sensitivity to wording; reasoning prompts can improve factual correctness
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
while also increasing confident but wrong answers in some settings
(
Rajani et al. 2019
)
. In normative urban queries, small wording changes can tilt value stances; Prompt Commons responds by distributing authorship and making value claims explicit.

Bias, ideology, and neutrality in LLMs.

Evidence shows that LLMs and alignment artefacts can display systematic political leanings
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Bang et al. 2024
;
Fulay et al. 2024
)
. Our pilot reflects this sensitivity: a single strong prompt yields fewer neutral choices than governed commons prompts. Prior findings on multi-perspective prompting and aggregation suggest that ensembles can moderate extremes; we operationalise this through governance states (open/curated/veto-enabled) and a weighted-ensemble method.

Commons and community moderation.

Ostrom’s principles for governing shared resources
(
Ostrom 1990
)
have inspired digital-commons adaptations
(
Mozilla Foundation 2021
;
Dulong de Rosnay and Stalder 2020
;
Bollier 2015
;
Scott 2018
)
. Open-source communities blend meritocracy with democratic mechanisms as governance formalises
(
O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007
)
, a pattern we echo. Volunteer moderation is critical but labour-intensive; studies highlight the need for clear rules, public logs, and appeal processes
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Cao et al. 2024
;
Tabassum et al. 2024
;
Weld et al. 2024
)
. We adopt these lessons—e.g., public issues, auditable takedowns,
prompt strikes
—to keep the Commons accountable.

Security and harm.

Embedding LLMs in civic workflows introduces attack surfaces from prompt injection to insecure output handling. Formal analyses and surveys motivate operational metrics like
time-to-remediation

(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
)
. Community-responsive governance (including minority veto) can be a practical control, alongside guidance such as the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications
(
OWASP GenAI Security Project 2023
)
. We also engage critical perspectives that caution against over-reliance on ever-larger models
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
by treating the model as a fallible component within a socio-technical process.

4
Prompt Commons: Design and Governance

4.1
Versioned repository and metadata

The repository maintains prompts as plain text with the following metadata:

1.

Author group(s)
(e.g., seniors, disability advocacy, LGBTQ+, ethnic or religious minority).

2.

Locale
(e.g., Montréal neighbourhood, street name).

3.

Intended value claim
(e.g., safety, accessibility, biodiversity, conviviality).

4.

Change log
with semantic versioning for prompts and collections.

Each change is an issue or pull request; maintainers triage and merge with an auditable log (who, when, rationale).
Beyond the checklist below, maintainers keep a running
curation memo
that describes how inclusions and exclusions relate to quotas and local policy instruments. This memo, together with issue discussions, provides the interpretive glue that links individual community contributions to a legible institutional memory about the territory where prompts will be deployed.

4.2
Governance states we study

We compare three governance states:

Open:

any authenticated contributor; light moderation (spam and safety only).

Curated:

maintainers apply quotas for inclusion (per author group and topic), require metadata completeness, and merge only prompts that pass a public checklist (Appendix
B
).

Veto-enabled:

as curated, plus a
minority veto
—representative organisations can flag outputs they deem harmful; flagged prompts are quarantined pending review with an appeal process.

This design borrows from commons governance and moderation practices
(
Ostrom 1990
;
O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007
;
Weld et al. 2024
)
. It also reflects polycentricity: neighbourhood-level prompt sets and organisations can operate semi-autonomously and federate into a city layer
(
Carlisle and Gruby 2019
)
. In practice, nested repositories and rotating maintainer seats can enact these ideas with lightweight, auditable processes, mirroring open-source governance while ensuring that affected groups have voice and visibility.

4.3
Licensing and IP

Prompts are textual creative works; a permissive licence maximises reuse while protecting attribution. We recommend:

•

Prompts
: Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 or BY–SA 4.0
(
Creative Commons 2013b
;
Creative Commons 2013a
;
Creative Commons 2025
)
.

•

Derived model artefacts
: OpenRAIL-style licences where use restrictions (e.g., no harassment, no biometric surveillance) are desired
(
Hugging Face 2022
;
RAIL Initiative 2023
;
Keller and Bonato 2023a
;
Keller and Bonato 2023b
;
BigCode Project 2023
;
ifrOSS 2024
)
.

Responsible-AI licensing is evolving; empirical analyses document the spread of use-restricted licences and argue for “standardised customisation” so behavioural clauses remain comprehensible yet adaptable to domain needs
(
McDuff et al. 2024
;
Contractor et al. 2020
)
. For public-sector and civic deployments, such legal instruments complement governance-by-process: communities retain attribution and set guardrails for derivative artefacts without foreclosing legitimate reuse.

5
Evaluation

5.1
Research questions

RQ1:
How does a community-maintained Prompt Commons shift model behaviour across neutrality/decisiveness and subgroup satisfaction?

RQ2:
Which licensing and moderation rules protect community authors while enabling reuse?

5.2
Protocol

We create five evaluation methods that operationalise the governance states above:

1.

M0 Single author
(baseline): a strong, opinionated prompt by one individual.

2.

M1 Open Commons
: random prompt from the open set.

3.

M2 Curated Commons
: prompt selected from the curated set satisfying quotas.

4.

M3 Veto-enabled
: curated set with minority-veto log enabled.

5.

M4 Weighted ensemble
: sample
k
prompts across groups and summarise with an aggregator instruction (
“deliberate and propose a compromise”
).

We evaluate on a contested-choice benchmark (transport, public space, and streetscape trade-offs)
(
Mushkani et al. 2025c
)
.
The model must recommend one of three mutually exclusive options:
Left-leaning
,
Right-leaning
, or
Neutral/compromise
(labels for the benchmark classes).
We report proportions per method (Figure
3
), a decisiveness score
D=1-\text{Neutral}
, and a subgroup satisfaction score using a 7-point Likert scale gathered from community reviewers.
For operational safety we also track
time-to-remediation
for harmful outputs flagged through the issue tracker.

Results

Neutrality and decisiveness.

The single-author baseline (M0) yields
38\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
Left,
38\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
Right, and
24\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
Neutral (
D{=}0.76

Open and curated commons (M1–M3) yield

Neutral and
24\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}26\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for Left and Right categories (
D\in[0.48,0.52]

Weighted ensembling (M4) sits in between (
D{=}0.49
).
One interpretation, in light of

on ambiguous problems.

Subgroup satisfaction.

Across six self-identified groups (seniors, women, ethnic minority, disability, LGBTQ+, religious minority), average satisfaction rises from
4.35\pm 0.86
(M0) to
4.92\pm 0.44
(M2) and

This aligns with security guidance calling for robust oversight and rapid incident response in LLM deployments

5.4
Interpretation

Results support the hypothesis that
individual prompting can reduce neutrality
, while collective governance recentres pluralism and operational safety in our setting.
In contexts where decisiveness is needed (e.g., emergency response), M0-like behaviour may be desirable; for long-horizon city-making, the commons-based regimes (M2–M3) appear preferable.
Seen through a social-choice lens, Prompt Commons acts as a light-touch mechanism for aggregating heterogeneous values at use time
(
Conitzer et al. 2024
;
Mushkani et al. 2025d
)
, complementing post-training alignment
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.

6
A governance and licensing recipe for cities

6.1
Rules of the Prompt Commons (Ostrom-informed)

We translate Ostrom’s principles
(
Ostrom 1990
)
to prompt governance:

1.

Boundaries:
define admissible locales and stakeholder groups; require affiliation declaration.

2.

Local fit:
require a locale tag; maintainers prioritise prompts grounded in local policy instruments.

3.

Collective choice:
public proposals and votes for quota changes and checklists (Appendix
B
).

4.

Monitoring:
public audit log; every merged prompt carries an issue link.

5.

Graduated sanctions:
from soft fixes to temporary suspensions; logs remain public.

6.

Conflict resolution:
fast lanes for appeal (72-hour target SLA).

7.

Recognition of rights:
municipal partners recognise the Commons as an advisory asset.

8.

Nested enterprises:
multiple neighbourhood-level commons feeding a citywide layer (
polycentricity

(
Carlisle and Gruby 2019
)
).

These rules are not abstract checkboxes. Digital-commons practice shows that translating boundaries, monitoring, and sanctions into visible workflows (issues, pull requests, labels, takedown logs) builds legitimacy and resilience
(
O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007
;
Bollier 2015
;
Scott 2018
)
. For Prompt Commons, we bind these mechanics to municipal decision rhythms: locale tags reference policy instruments; quota debates happen before budgeting cycles; and vetoes trigger time-boxed remediation with public rationales that future maintainers can audit.

6.2
Licensing choices

Default.
CC BY 4.0 for all prompts (attribution required; commercial use permitted)
(
Creative Commons 2013a
;
Creative Commons 2013b
;
Creative Commons 2025
)
.

Share-alike option.
CC BY–SA 4.0 if communities want derivatives to remain open.

Use-restricted artefacts.
When the Commons distributes models, consider OpenRAIL-style licences to prohibit specific harms (e.g., stalking, biometric surveillance) while remaining open for legitimate uses
(
Hugging Face 2022
;
RAIL Initiative 2023
;
Keller and Bonato 2023a
;
Keller and Bonato 2023b
;
BigCode Project 2023
;
ifrOSS 2024
)
.

Recent analyses recommend
standardised customisation
for use restrictions to reduce confusion while enabling contextual guardrails (e.g., for health or public-sector deployments)
(
McDuff et al. 2024
;
Contractor et al. 2020
)
. Our stance: clear CC licences for prompts; modular, community-negotiated restrictions for derived artefacts.

6.3
Moderation & “prompt strikes”

Moderation combines (i) automated checks (toxicity, Personally Identifiable Information (PII)), (ii) maintainer review, and (iii) community veto.
We also prototype a collective action tool:
prompt strikes
.
Authors can temporarily withdraw their prompts to protest misuse; the resulting performance drop makes misalignment visible to municipal partners without permanently harming users.
Volunteer-moderation research suggests that such visible levers recognise contributor labour while disciplining governance processes without centralising power
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Cao et al. 2024
;
Tabassum et al. 2024
)
.

7
Risks and mitigations

Dominance capture.

Risk: vocal groups dominate curation.
Mitigation: quotas for inclusion by group and neighbourhood; rotating maintainer seats; publish participation metrics.
Polycentric designs that federate neighbourhood commons into a city layer reduce single-point capture
(
Carlisle and Gruby 2019
)
.

Security & prompt injection.

Risk: adversarial prompts or indirect injection
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
OWASP GenAI Security Project 2023
)
.
Mitigation: sandboxed testing; red-teaming; provenance and signed releases; time-to-remediation targets; and hard boundaries between model tools and untrusted content sources.

Over-caution.

Risk: excessive neutrality hampers action.
Mitigation: allow decisiveness mode (explicitly labelled) for time-sensitive tasks; track
D
and disclose when neutrality thresholds are relaxed.

8
Discussion

8.1
City practice

Urban AI should be
plural by default
—not one prompt, but a commons of prompts
(
Mushkani 2025
)
.
The Prompt Commons plays three roles in municipal workflows:
(1) evidence of plural values;
(2) reproducible AI configurations;
(3) a procedural space for debate, akin to participatory data stewardship
(
Ada Lovelace Institute 2021
;
Ada Lovelace Institute 2024
)
.
This model complements strategic frameworks that place cities at the forefront of responsible AI deployment
(
Zhu and Liu 2025
)
, aligns with pragmatic adoption realities in local government
(
Yigitcanlar et al. 2023
)
, and builds on the insight that local governance innovation is essential for trustworthy urban AI
(
Allam and Dhunny 2019
;
Kirwan and Fu 2020
)
.
By treating prompts as civic artefacts, we also answer calls to democratise alignment and make visible whose values shape AI behaviour
(
Huang et al. 2025
;
Conitzer et al. 2024
;
Mushkani et al. 2025a
)
.

8.2
Limitations and future work

Our pilot benchmark is small and focused on Montréal scenes.
We measured satisfaction with a lightweight protocol; future work should adopt mixed methods (surveys, interviews, ethnography).
We used one state-of-practice LLM for the figures; cross-model replication is needed, though our methods are model-agnostic.
While community governance moderates bias at use time, structural ideological leanings in LLMs and alignment artefacts persist
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Fulay et al. 2024
;
Bang et al. 2024
)
; longitudinal studies should quantify durability of neutrality gains as model families evolve.
Finally, legal evaluation of licensing combinations (CC + OpenRAIL) in municipal procurement warrants further study
(
McDuff et al. 2024
;
Contractor et al. 2020
)
.

9
Conclusion

Prompt governance matters.
By versioning, licensing, and moderating prompts as a civic commons, cities can steer LLM behaviour toward pluralism without sacrificing the ability to act.
In our pilot, single-author prompting reduces neutrality, whereas curated and veto-enabled regimes increase neutral/compromise outcomes, reduce subgroup disparity, and accelerate remediation when harms occur.
We include anonymised datasets and governance materials as supplemental files to support replication and local adaptation.
