Exploiting Low-Rank Structure in Max- K K -Cut Problems
Ria Stevens, Fangshuo Liao, Barbara Su, Jianqiang Li, Anastasios Kyrillidis

Abstract
We approach the Max-3-Cut problem through the lens of maximizing complex-valued quadratic forms and demonstrate that low-rank structure in the objective matrix can be exploited, leading to alternative algorithms to classical semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations and heuristic techniques. We propose an algorithm for maximizing these quadratic forms over a domain of size K K that enumerates and evaluates a set of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ​ r − 1 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{2r-1}\right) candidate solutions, where n n is the dimension of the matrix and r r represents the rank of an approximation of the objective. We prove that this candidate set is guaranteed to include the exact maximizer when K = 3 K=3 (corresponding to Max-3-Cut ) and the objective is low-rank, and provide approximation guarantees when the objective is a perturbation of a low-rank matrix. This construction results in a family of novel, inherently parallelizable and theoretically-motivated algorithms for Max-3-Cut . Extensive experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves performance comparable to existing algorithms across a wide range of graphs, while being highly scalable.

Our approach relies on low-rank approximations of the objective matrix

Summary of contributions.

While this toy example illustrates how low-rank structure can be leveraged to efficiently and exactly solve the
Max-2-Cut

In this work, we address these challenges, studying the

1.

Exact algorithms for rank-

In particular, for general

Theoretical guarantees for approximately low-rank matrices.

Although our algorithms are exact only for low-rank objective matrices, we prove that they are also able to approximately recover the solution to (

3.

Performance and scalability.

We provide a parallelized implementation of our algorithms applied to the

problem that combines a low-rank approximation of graph Laplacians with our exact low-rank algorithm. Experiments on synthetic graphs and established benchmarks demonstrate that this approach scales to graphs with tens of thousands of nodes. In particular, our algorithm achieves up to a 74

speed-up compared to heuristic algorithms while preserving solution quality on structured graph instances.

Exact Rank-1 Algorithm

In the introduction, we gave a simple algorithm solving the binary quadratic maximization problem representing
Max-2-Cut
(
1
) for a real, rank-1 matrix.
In this section, we describe an algorithm which exactly solves the complex discrete quadratic maximization problem given in (
A.0.3
) for a complex rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
in polynomial time.
This algorithm leverages the eigenvector of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
to enumerate a set of
n+1
candidate cuts and evaluates each cut within this set. We show that this set is guaranteed to contain the maximizer.

Overview of Algorithm.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
be a rank-1, positive semi-definite matrix and consider the maximization problem given in (
A.0.3
) with objective matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
. As
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
has rank-1, we can decompose it as
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}=\lambda{\mathbf{q}}{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}
, where
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
. Given this decomposition, we can reformulate (
A.0.3
) as the maximization of the magnitude of a complex inner product. Specifically, we have:

\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}\mathbf{z}\penalty\ \propto\penalty\ \left|{\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{q}}\right|.

(3)

Note that any complex number (in this case,
\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{q}
) can be rotated by a phase
\varphi
so that its magnitude equals its projection onto the real axis. We introduce an auxiliary phase variable
\varphi\in\left(-\pi,\pi\right]
and a rotation function
c(\varphi)\coloneqq e^{\hat{\imath}\varphi}
to represent this rotation. Employing (
3
) and this auxiliary variable, we can express the maximization problem (
A.0.3
) as:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\penalty\ \mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\penalty\ =\penalty\ \underset{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}{\text{max}}\penalty\ \underset{\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)}{\text{max}}\penalty\ \text{Re}\left(\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{q}\cdot c(\varphi)\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\penalty\ =\penalty\ \max_{\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\text{Re}\left(z_{i}^{\dagger}\left(q_{i}\cdot c(\varphi)\right)\right).

(5)

A critical observation of this approach is that for a fixed
\varphi
, the choice of each
z_{i}
is independent. Specifically, the optimal
z_{i}
aligns as closely as possible with the direction of
q_{i}\cdot c(\varphi)
.
As the auxiliary angle
\varphi
varies continuously over
[-\pi,\pi)
, the optimal choice for each
z_{i}
remains piecewise constant and changes only when the phase of
q_{i}\cdot c(\varphi)
crosses a decision boundary between two sectors in the complex plane. Because the roots of unity are symmetric with period
2\pi/K
, it is sufficient to restrict
\varphi
to an interval of width
2\pi/K
. Over such an interval, each element
q_{i}
crosses exactly one boundary point
\varphi_{i}

Algorithm
1
identifies and sorts these
n+1

n+1
distinct cells. Within each cell, the assignment vector
\mathbf{z}
remains constant. Consequently, the algorithm only needs to enumerate and evaluate a candidate set of
n+1
assignment vectors to guarantee that it finds the global optimum.

We provide the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}=\lambda\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a rank-1, positive semi-definite matrix. Algorithm
1
exactly solves the complex discrete quadratic maximization problem (

Proof.

Time Complexity.

Given
{\mathbf{q}}
, calculating
\bm{\theta}
,
{\bm{\varphi}}
and
\mathbf{k}
takes linear time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
Sorting the
\varphi_{i}

\mathcal{O}\left(n\log n\right)

There are
n+1
such candidates.
Algorithm
1
evaluates a quadratic form for each candidate
{\mathbf{z}}
. However, given access to
{\mathbf{q}}
, this quadratic form is
|{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{q}}|
and may be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}\left(n\right)
time.
This results in a total time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})

P

\mathcal{O}(n/P)
candidates, for an overall time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/P)
.
Note that if
{\mathbf{q}}
is not given, its computation requires
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\right)

Correctness.

As established previously in this section, the discrete quadratic maximization problem (
A.0.3
) for a rank-1 matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}=\lambda{\mathbf{q}}{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}
is equivalent to:

\max_{\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\text{Re}\left(z_{i}^{\dagger}q_{i}e^{\hat{\imath}\varphi}\right).

(6)

As previously mentioned, because the roots of unity are periodic, we can restrict the range of
\varphi
to
\left[-{\pi}/{K},{\pi}/{K}\right)
.
For a fixed
\varphi
in this interval and
i\in[n]
, the inner maximization above is solved by choosing

to be the root of unity with the closest phase to the phase of

is
\frac{2\pi k_{i}}{K}
for some

. Expressing

\theta_{i}+\varphi

therefore corresponds to

k_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}}\left|{\frac{2\pi k}{K}-(\varphi+\theta_{i})}\right|=\left\lfloor{\frac{K}{2\pi}(\varphi+\theta_{i})+\frac{1}{2}}\right\rfloor.

(7)

At the left endpoint of the interval
[-\pi/K,\pi/K]

\varphi=-\pi/K
, the optimal choice of
k_{i}
is
k^{(1)}_{i}=\left\lfloor{\frac{K\theta_{i}}{2\pi}}\right\rfloor
.
When the phase

and

\varphi_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi k_{i}}{K}+\frac{2\pi k_{i}^{\prime}}{K}\right)-\theta_{i}=\frac{2\pi}{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\left\lfloor{\frac{K\theta_{i}}{2\pi}}\right\rfloor-\frac{K\theta_{i}}{2\pi}\right).

(8)

The phases

are boundary points which partition the interval
[-\pi/K,\pi/K)
into
n+1

{\mathbf{z}}
, to the inner maximization of (

Algorithm
1
evaluates the solution, or candidate vector, corresponding to each cell, and returns the candidate which maximizes
{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}

Exact Rank-
r
Algorithm

Generalizing the rank-1 approach, our rank-
r
algorithm reformulates the discrete quadratic problem (
A.0.3
) as a double maximization over an auxiliary angular parameter
{\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
. The algorithm partitions the high-dimensional search space into cells within which the candidate vector remains constant. By identifying the vertices of this partition, we construct a polynomial-size set of candidate vectors guaranteed to contain the global optimizer. This approach is adapted from
[
39
]
to handle general alphabet size
K
.

Overview of Algorithm.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}
be a rank-
r
, positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix. Again, consider the maximization problem (
A.0.3
) with objective matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}
. Because
{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}
is PSD and has rank
r
, we can decompose it as
{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}
for some matrix
{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r}
. We can use this decomposition to reformulate (
A.0.3
) as the maximization of the norm of a complex vector-matrix product:

\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{K}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}{\mathbf{z}}\propto\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}_{K}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}\right\|_{2}.

(9)

As in the rank-1 case, the norm of this vector–matrix product can be recovered by selecting the maximum direction that aligns it with the real axis. To represent this alignment, we introduce an auxiliary unit vector
{\mathbf{c}}\left({\bm{\varphi}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, parametrized by a vector of angular coordinates
{\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
. The range of
{\bm{\varphi}}
is a hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
defined as:

\mathcal{H}_{r}\coloneqq\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]^{2(r-1)}\times\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{K},\tfrac{\pi}{K}\right].

(10)

Using these auxiliary variables, we expand (
9
) as:

\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}_{K}}\left\|{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}\right\|=\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}_{K}}\max_{{\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\text{Re}\left({\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{c}}\left({\bm{\varphi}}\right)\right)=\max_{{\bm{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\text{Re}\left\{z_{i}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}({\bm{\varphi}})\right\}.

(11)

Similarly to the rank-1 formulation, we observe that for any fixed
{\bm{\varphi}}
, the optimal choice of each
z_{i}
in (
11
) is independent. For a given
i\in[n]
, the optimal
z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
is that which is closest in phase to
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}({\bm{\varphi}})
. A change in the optimal
z_{i}
occurs only when the vector
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}({\bm{\varphi}})
crosses a particular decision boundary, represented by a ray in the complex plane.

Decision Boundary Structure.

The number of decision boundaries per coordinate depends critically on the alphabet size
K
. For each
i\in[n]
, the decision boundaries correspond to the
K
rays that bisect adjacent symbols in
\mathcal{A}_{K}
, located at angles
\vartheta_{m}=\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{K}
for
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
. However, not all of these boundaries are geometrically distinct.

Figure 1:
A toy example of the partition of the hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}

For alphabet size
K\geq 2
, the number of distinct decision boundaries per coordinate is:

B_{K}=\begin{cases}K/2&\text{if }K\text{ is even},\\
K&\text{if }K\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(12)

This distinction arises from antipodal symmetry: for even
K
, the element
-1\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
, causing boundary rays at angles
\vartheta_{m}
and
\vartheta_{m}+\pi
to define the same hyperplane in the auxiliary parameter space. For odd
K
(including primary case
K=3
), no two boundary angles differ by exactly
\pi
; all
K
boundaries are distinct. For Max-3-Cut, we have
B_{3}=3
decision boundaries per coordinate, compared to
B_{2}=1
for the binary case.

Augmented Matrix Construction.

By introducing an augmented real matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nB_{K}\times 2r}
and auxiliary vector
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}({\bm{\varphi}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2r}
, we express all decision boundaries as a system of real linear equations. The augmented matrix is constructed by stacking
B_{K}
rotated copies of the factor matrix:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\rho_{0}{\mathbf{V}}\}&\Im\{\rho_{0}{\mathbf{V}}\}\\
\Re\{\rho_{1}{\mathbf{V}}\}&\Im\{\rho_{1}{\mathbf{V}}\}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\Re\{\rho_{B_{K}-1}{\mathbf{V}}\}&\Im\{\rho_{B_{K}-1}{\mathbf{V}}\}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

\displaystyle\quad\text{where }\rho_{m}=e^{-\hat{\imath}\pi(2m+1)/K}.

For all
j\in[nB_{K}]
, the solution to
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}({\bm{\varphi}})=0
yields a
(2r-2)
-dimensional manifold (hypersurface) within the hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
. The set of all
nB_{K}
hypersurfaces partitions the hypercube into cells, with each cell corresponding to a unique candidate vector in
\mathcal{A}^{n}_{K}
, just as each interval in the rank-1 case corresponds to a candidate vector. Figure
1
provides an illustration of this partition.

A key structural property simplifies the enumeration: all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces originating from the same coordinate
i
share a common
(2r-3)
-dimensional intersection, namely the locus where
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}({\bm{\varphi}})=0
. This means that selecting more than two hypersurfaces from the same coordinate group leads to rank deficiency in the defining system. We exploit this property to efficiently enumerate only the valid vertex configurations; see Appendix
C
for the complete analysis.

Vertex Enumeration.

To systematically identify all candidates, our algorithm examines the vertices of the cell partition.
In the
(2r-1)
-dimensional hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
, each vertex is defined by the intersection of
2r-1
hypersurfaces.
Therefore, we identify these vertices by solving, for all valid combinations
I\subset[nB_{K}]
of size
2r-1
, the local system given by
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{I,:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}({\bm{\varphi}})=\mathbf{0}.

An index set
I
is
valid
if it contains at most two indices from any single coordinate group—a necessary condition for the submatrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{I,:}
to have full rank.

Each solution
{\bm{\varphi}}_{I}
maps to a candidate vector
{\mathbf{z}}_{I}
by the following decision rule:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{I}={\mathbf{d}}

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{V}};{\bm{\varphi}}_{I}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}}{K}k_{i}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \text{where}\penalty\ k_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{k\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}}\left|\frac{2\pi k}{K}-\arg\left(\mathbf{V}_{i,:}\mathbf{c}(\bm{\varphi})\right)\right|_{\text{mod }2\pi}.

(15)

In addition to the cell vertices, the algorithm must identify candidates that land on the boundaries of the hypercube.
These boundary cases occur when one component of
{\bm{\varphi}}
is fixed, and therefore correspond to lower-rank subproblems. They are solved by recursive calls to the rank-
(r-1)
algorithm
[
38
]
.
By evaluating the candidate vectors found by the valid combinations and the rank-
(r-1)
recursion, we construct a polynomial-size feasible candidate set that is guaranteed to contain the global optimizer.

Low-rank Structure with Perturbation.

To provide a theoretical justification of our approach, we study the case when the input matrix to Algorithm

,
\mathbf{Q}

Specifically, assume

(16)

is a Hermitian, rank-

\mathbf{Q}^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\star}}\lambda_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star\dagger}
with

Note here that
\mathbf{Q}

Assume that we run Algorithm
3

{\mathbf{Q}}
with input rank
r

3
finds a rank-

{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}

{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{\star}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{attains}\penalty\ \penalty\ \texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\penalty\ \mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{r}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{attains}\penalty\ \penalty\ \texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}_{r}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\penalty\ \mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}_{r}\mathbf{z}

We aim to investigate the quality of the approximate solution
{\mathbf{z}}_{r}

Theorem

describes the additive error of the approximation of

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}_{r},\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}

(18)

Theorem

upper bounds the gap between the quadratic form
{\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}
and

by construction. In particular, the right-hand side of the upper bound in (

, and can be regarded as the price one pays when using a rank less than that of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}

All experiments were conducted on HPE ProLiant DL360 Gen11 compute nodes (Sapphire Rapids) equipped with Intel Xeon Platinum 8468 CPUs (96 cores at 2.10 GHz). We generate 20 instances for each size and parameter of the small random graphs using the NetworkX library

Parallel Implementation.

We develop a parallel implementation of Algorithm

[
45
]
.
Observe that the dominant cost in Algorithm

To make this enumeration tractable in memory, we generate batches of candidates as a stream, so that the implementation never materializes the full set of

{\mathbf{V}}

faster. As the problem size increases, this gap widens. On binary toroidal graphs (G48, G49, and G50), the
Rank-1
algorithm runs in under 6 seconds, whereas
Greedy
requires over 5 minutes, demonstrating a speedup of roughly
57-74\times
. On the largest GSet instances,
Greedy
fails to converge within a 30 minute cutoff, while the
Rank-1
algorithm finishes in 76 and 352 seconds on G67 and G81, respectively.

In addition to its scalability, we observe that our algorithm performs exceptionally well on unweighted toroidal graphs, the most structured instances of GSet. On G48 and G49, the
Rank-1
algorithm achieves a theoretically optimal cut value of 6,000, matching the solution quality
MOH
and slightly outperforming
Greedy
. Similarly, on G50, our algorithm finds a near-optimal cut value of 5934. These results suggest that for highly-structured graphs, a rank-1 approximation is sufficient to capture the global structure. However, on less structured instances, we observe a trade-off between efficiency and solution quality.

5.4
Very Large Graphs.

To push the limits of our
Rank-1
algorithm, we generate two random, 3-regular graph, one with 100,000 nodes and another with 50,000 nodes, and test our
Rank-1
algorithm against
Random
with 800 workers and no timeout limit. On the 100,000 node graph, our algorithm finishes in 61757 seconds (roughly 17 hours and 9 minutes) and finds a cut value of 137796, while the random algorithm requires 8269 seconds (2 hours and 18 minutes) and finds a cut value of 100781. On the 50,000 node graph we observe the same pattern:
Rank-1
finished in 6597 seconds (roughly 1 hour and 49 minutes) and finds a cut value of 68951, while
Random
finishes in 1345 seconds (roughly 22 minutes) and finds a cut value of 50517.
We note that most of the running time (about 14.5 hours for 100,000 node graph and 1.4 hours for the 50,000 node graph) of the
Rank-1
algorithm is spent finding the principal eigenvector of the graph Laplacian. In applications where this is given or can be easily derived, our algorithm would run only slightly slower than
Random
, while finding significantly better cuts.

6
Conclusion

We propose a family of algorithms for solving
Max-3-Cut
and related quadratic maximization problems in both real and complex domains. These algorithms use low-rank approximations of the objective matrices to generate polynomially-sized sets of candidate cuts.
On the theoretical side, we analyze the error introduced by our low-rank approximation scheme for matrices with perturbed low-rank structure. Empirically, we show that our
Rank-2
and
Rank-3
algorithms demonstrate performance comparable to state-of-the-art heuristic approaches on a suite of small synthetically-generated graphs.
We demonstrate that our
Rank-1
algorithm matches the state-of-the-art in solution quality on large, highly-structured graphs, and finds high-quality solutions for many less-structured graphs in a fraction of the time of heuristic algorithms.
Finally, we highlight the parallelizability of our approach: our algorithms naturally lend themselves to distributed computing environments, enabling efficient solvers for
Max-3-Cut
.

Appendix A
Derivation of Complex Ternary Quadratic Form for
Max-3-Cut

We derive the complex ternary quadratic formulation of
Max-3-Cut
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with vertex set
V=\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
and edge set
E
.
Let
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a weight function assigning positive weights to edges.
The Max-
3
-CUT problem seeks a partition of
\mathbf{V}
into
3
disjoint sets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}
such that the sum of weights of edges crossing between different sets is maximized:

\max_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}w_{i\ell}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}w_{i\ell}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}w_{i\ell}.

(20)

A.0.1
Complex Encoding of Partitions.

To reformulate the Max-
3
-CUT problem in the complex domain, we encode the partitioning of vertices using complex cube roots of unity.
Let
\omega_{3}=e^{j2\pi/3}
be the primitive cube root of unity.
Then, each vertex
i
is assigned a value
z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{3}:=\{1,\omega_{3},\omega_{3}^{2}\}
, where:

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=1\Rightarrow i\in\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\omega_{3}=e^{j2\pi/3}\Rightarrow i\in\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\omega_{3}^{2}=e^{4\pi j/3}\Rightarrow i\in\mathcal{S}_{3}

A property we exploit is that for any two complex roots of unity
\alpha
and
\beta
:

\alpha\neq\beta\Rightarrow\operatorname{Re}(\alpha\overline{\beta})=\cos\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}|p-q|\right)

where
\alpha=\omega_{3}^{p-1}
and
\beta=\omega_{3}^{q-1}
for some
p,q\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Then, this gives:

\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $i$ \& $\ell$ in same partition};\\
\cos(\tfrac{2\pi}{3})=-\tfrac{1}{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A.0.2
Reformulation as a Quadratic Optimization.

Using the above complex encoding, we can reformulate the Max-
3
-CUT problem as a quadratic optimization problem. The objective function can be written as:

\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}^{p}_{3}}\sum_{(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}\cdot\left(1-\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}})\right)

(21)

Let us verify that this objective function correctly captures the
Max-3-Cut
problem:

•

If vertices
i
and
\ell
are in the same partition, then
z_{i}=z_{\ell}
, which means
z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}}=|z_{i}|^{2}=1
, so
\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}})=1
. Thus, the contribution of this edge is
w_{i\ell}\cdot(1-1)=0
.

•

If vertices
i
and
\ell
are in different partitions, then
z_{i}\neq z_{\ell}
, and they correspond to different 3-rd roots of unity. In this case,
\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}})=\cos(\tfrac{2\pi|p-q|}{3})
, where
p
and
q
are the respective partitions. Since
K=3
, the contribution of this edge to the objective is
w_{i\ell}\cdot(1-\cos(\tfrac{2\pi|p-q|}{3}))=w_{i\ell}\cdot(1-(-1/2))=\tfrac{3}{2}w_{i\ell}
.

A.0.3
Generalized Complex Laplacian Formulation.

To further develop our approach, we introduce the generalized complex Laplacian matrix.
Let
\mathbf{W}=\{w_{i\ell}\}_{i,\ell=1}^{p}
be the weighted adjacency matrix of graph
G
, and let
\mathbf{D}=\texttt{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})
be the diagonal matrix of weighted degrees, where
d_{i}=\sum_{\ell:(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}
. The standard graph Laplacian is defined as
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{W}
.
Using the properties of the Laplacian, we can express the objective function above as:

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}\cdot(1-\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}-\sum_{(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}\cdot\operatorname{Re}(z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}-\operatorname{Re}\left(\sum_{(i,\ell)\in E}w_{i\ell}\cdot z_{i}\overline{z_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{D})-\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}\right).

Thus, the Max-
3
-CUT problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}:=\max_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{A}^{p}_{3}}\penalty\ \mathbf{z}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3

See
3

Proof.

This proof combines the information of the auxiliary results given in Appendix
C
.

Proof of Time Complexity.

In Theorem
15
, we prove that the algorithm enumerates
O(rn^{2r-1})
candidates. Evaluating each candidate requires computing the corresponding quadratic form in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time. The overall time complexity is therefore
\mathcal{O}\left(rn^{2r+1}\right)
.

Proof of Correctness

We show that the candidate set
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
enumerated by the algorithm contains the global maximizer.

Step 1: Reformulation as double maximization.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz argument (Lemma
C.1
and the subsequent discussion):

\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}=\max_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\Re\{{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=\max_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z_{i}^{*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}.

(22)

The second equality holds because, for fixed
\bm{\phi}
, the coordinates
z_{i}
can be optimized independently.

Step 2: Decision function is piecewise constant.

For fixed
\bm{\phi}
, the optimal
z_{i}
is determined by the decision rule:

d_{i}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})=\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z^{*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}.

(23)

Let
\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})={\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})
. The optimal
z_{i}
is the element of
\mathcal{A}_{K}
whose phase is closest to
\arg(\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi}))
. As
\bm{\phi}
varies, the phase of
\alpha_{i}
varies continuously, and
d_{i}
changes only when
\arg(\alpha_{i})
crosses a boundary angle
\vartheta_{m}
.

Step 3: Hypersurfaces partition
\mathcal{H}_{r}
into cells.

The
B_{K}n
hypersurfaces
\{\mathcal{H}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:})\}_{j=1}^{B_{K}n}
partition
\mathcal{H}_{r}
into cells. Within each cell, the decision function
{\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})
is constant. The objective
\sum_{i}\max_{z_{i}}\Re\{z_{i}^{*}\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})\}
is continuous in
\bm{\phi}
, so the global maximum is attained either:

(a)

At a vertex of the arrangement (intersection of
2r-1
hypersurfaces), or

(b)

On the boundary of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Step 4: Enumeration of interior vertices.

The main loop of Algorithm
2
enumerates all valid index sets
\mathcal{I}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2r-1
and checks if
\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})=2r-1
. For each such
\mathcal{I}
, it computes the vertex
\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})
, applies the decision rule to obtain a candidate
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathcal{I}}
, and evaluates the objective.

By Lemma
C.19
, this enumeration covers all cells whose ”leading vertex” (the vertex with minimal
\phi_{2r-1}
) lies in the interior of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Step 5: Recursive handling of boundary cases.

Cells touching the boundary
\phi_{2r-1}=-\pi/K
or
\phi_{2r-1}=\pi/K
are not captured by interior vertices. However:

•

By Corollary
C.16
, candidates from
\phi_{2r-1}=-\pi/K
are phase rotations of candidates from elsewhere, so no loss occurs.

•

Candidates from
\phi_{2r-2}=\pm\pi/2
(with arbitrary
\phi_{2r-1}
) correspond to the rank-
(r-1)
problem on
{\mathbf{V}}_{:,1:r-1}
. The recursive call handles these.

Overall, the boundary of the hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
consists of faces where one angular coordinate is fixed at its extreme value. When this occurs, one degree of freedom in the auxiliary vector
\mathbf{c}(\bm{\phi})
is eliminated: either a magnitude allocation coordinate (
\phi_{j}=\pm\pi/2
) becomes extremal, or the final phase coordinate (
\phi_{2r-1}=\pm\pi/K
) is fixed. In both cases, the effective optimization over the remaining
2(r-1)-1
free parameters reduces to a rank-
(r-1)
subproblem, which is solved by the recursive call in Algorithm
2
.
This is proven in
[
39
]
.

This reduction is independent of the alphabet size
K
. The specific boundary values differ with
K
(e.g.,
\pm\pi/K
for the phase coordinate), but the mechanism (fixing one coordinate eliminates one degree of freedom) is purely structural. Combined with the interior vertex enumeration, this ensures that the candidate set contains the global optimizer for any rank-
r
PSD matrix and any
K\geq 2
.

Step 6: Completeness of candidate set.

Combining Steps 4 and 5, every cell of the arrangement is associated with at least one candidate in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
. Since the global maximizer lies in some cell, it is in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
.

Step 7: Evaluation and selection.

The algorithm evaluates the objective for all candidates and returns the best one. Since the global maximizer is in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
, the returned
{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}
achieves the maximum.
\square

∎

Appendix C
Auxiliary Results for the Proof of Theorem
3

This appendix provides a complete theoretical treatment of the discrete quadratic maximization problem (
A.0.3
) for general alphabet size
K\geq 2
, encompassing both even and odd values. We establish the decision boundary structure, construct the augmented matrix, prove the candidate set characterization, and demonstrate algorithm correctness. The facts, lemmas and corollaries throughout this section serve as key pieces in the proof of Theorem
3
, given in Appendix
B
.

C.1
Preliminaries and Notation

We consider the discrete quadratic maximization problem:

\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}:=\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{z}},

(24)

where
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is Hermitian and positive semi-definite, and
\mathcal{A}_{K}
denotes the
K
-th roots of unity:

\mathcal{A}_{K}=\left\{\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}\right):k\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}\right\}.

(25)

Since
{\mathbf{Q}}
is PSD with rank
r
, we may write
{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}
for some
{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r}
. The objective becomes:

{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}=\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(26)

We parameterize the unit sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{r}
using hyperpolar coordinates. Let
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{2r-1})^{\top}
with
\phi_{i}\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
for
i=1,\ldots,2r-2
and
\phi_{2r-1}\in(-\frac{\pi}{K},\frac{\pi}{K}]
. Define the real hyperpolar vector
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{2r}
by:

\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\phi_{1}\\
\cos\phi_{1}\sin\phi_{2}\\
\cos\phi_{1}\cos\phi_{2}\sin\phi_{3}\\
\vdots\\
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r-2}\cos\phi_{i}\right)\sin\phi_{2r-1}\\
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2r-2}\cos\phi_{i}\right)\cos\phi_{2r-1}\end{bmatrix},

(27)

and the complex unit vector
{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
by:

{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}_{2:2:2r}(\bm{\phi})+\hat{\imath}\,\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}_{1:2:2r-1}(\bm{\phi}),

(28)

where subscript notation
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}_{a:b:c}
denotes elements at indices
a,a+b,a+2b,\ldots
up to
c
.

Lemma C.1

(Unit Norm Property)
.

For all
\bm{\phi}
in the domain,
\|\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})\|_{2}=\|{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\|_{2}=1
.

Proof.

Direct calculation shows
\|\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})\|_{2}^{2}=\sin^{2}\phi_{1}+\cos^{2}\phi_{1}(\sin^{2}\phi_{2}+\cos^{2}\phi_{2}(\cdots))=1
by repeated application of the identity
\sin^{2}\theta+\cos^{2}\theta=1
. The complex norm follows since
\|{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\|_{2}^{2}=\|\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})\|_{2}^{2}
.

\square

∎

Finally, define the hypercube:

\mathcal{H}_{r}:=\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]^{2r-2}\times\left(-\frac{\pi}{K},\frac{\pi}{K}\right].

(29)

C.2
Decision Boundary Structure for General
K

The decision boundaries determine where the optimal symbol assignment for a coordinate changes as the auxiliary angle vector
\bm{\phi}
varies.

Definition C.2

(Boundary Angles)
.

For alphabet size
K\geq 2
, the
boundary angles
are:

\vartheta_{m}=\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{K},\quad m\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}.

(30)

These angles bisect adjacent symbol phases in
\mathcal{A}_{K}
: the boundary at
\vartheta_{m}
separates the sectors corresponding to symbols
e^{2\pi\hat{\imath}m/K}
and
e^{2\pi\hat{\imath}(m+1)/K}
.

Definition C.3

(Decision Boundary)
.

For coordinate
i\in[n]
and boundary index
m\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
, the
decision boundary

\mathcal{B}_{i,m}
is the set of angle vectors
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
such that
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})
lies on the ray at angle
\vartheta_{m}
from the origin:

\mathcal{B}_{i,m}=\left\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}:{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=Ae^{\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}\text{ for some }A\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(31)

The condition
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=Ae^{\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}
for real
A
is equivalent to requiring that
e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})
is real, i.e.:

\Im\left\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\right\}=0.

(32)

Lemma C.4

(Antipodal Boundary Equivalence)
.

The decision boundaries at angles
\vartheta_{m}
and
\vartheta_{m}+\pi
define the same hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
if and only if
K
is even.

Proof.

The boundary condition (
32
) at angle
\vartheta_{m}+\pi
is:

\Im\left\{e^{-\hat{\imath}(\vartheta_{m}+\pi)}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\right\}=\Im\left\{-e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\right\}=-\Im\left\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\right\}.

(33)

Thus, the condition
\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}(\vartheta_{m}+\pi)}\cdot\}=0
is equivalent to
\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}\cdot\}=0
, meaning both boundary conditions define the same hyperplane.

For this to result in a reduction of the number of distinct boundaries, we need
\vartheta_{m}+\pi=\vartheta_{m^{\prime}}
for some
m^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
. We have:

\vartheta_{m}+\pi=\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{K}+\pi=\frac{\pi(2m+1+K)}{K}=\frac{\pi(2(m+K/2)+1)}{K}.

(34)

This equals
\vartheta_{m+K/2}
if and only if
K/2
is an integer, i.e., if and only if
K
is even.

When
K
is even, each boundary angle
\vartheta_{m}
for
m\in\{0,\ldots,K/2-1\}
is paired with
\vartheta_{m+K/2}
, and they define the same hyperplane. When
K
is odd, no two boundary angles differ by exactly
\pi
, so all
K
boundaries are distinct.
\square

∎

Definition C.5

(Effective Boundary Count)
.

The number of distinct decision boundaries per coordinate is:

B_{K}=\begin{cases}K/2&\text{if }K\text{ is even},\\
K&\text{if }K\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(35)

Example 6

(

: Binary Case)
.

For
K=2
, we have
\vartheta_{0}=\pi/2
and
\vartheta_{1}=3\pi/2
. Since
\vartheta_{1}=\vartheta_{0}+\pi
, these define the same hyperplane. Thus

boundary per coordinate, corresponding to the real axis separating
+1
from
-1
.

Example 7

(
K=3
: Ternary Case)
.

For
K=3
, we have
\vartheta_{0}=\pi/3
,
\vartheta_{1}=\pi
, and
\vartheta_{2}=5\pi/3
. Checking for antipodal pairs:

•

\vartheta_{0}+\pi=4\pi/3\neq\vartheta_{m}
for any
m

•

\vartheta_{1}+\pi=2\pi\equiv 0\neq\vartheta_{m}
for any
m

•

\vartheta_{2}+\pi=8\pi/3=2\pi/3\pmod{2\pi}\neq\vartheta_{m}
for any

All three boundaries are distinct, so

.

Example 8

(
K=4

.

For

, we have
\vartheta_{0}=\pi/4
,

,
\vartheta_{2}=5\pi/4
,
\vartheta_{3}=7\pi/4
. The pairs

and

C.3
Augmented Matrix Construction

We now construct the real augmented matrix that encodes all decision boundaries for general
K
.

Definition C.6

(Rotation Factors)
.

For general
K\geq 2
, define the rotation factors as:

\rho_{m}=e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}=e^{-\hat{\imath}\pi(2m+1)/K},\quad m\in\mathcal{M}_{K},

(36)

where the index set
\mathcal{M}_{K}
is:

\mathcal{M}_{K}=\begin{cases}\{0,1,\ldots,K/2-1\}&\text{if }K\text{ is even},\\
\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}&\text{if }K\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(37)

Definition C.7

(Rotated Factor Matrix)
.

The rotated factor matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{K}n\times r}
is:

\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{0}\\
\rho_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\rho_{B_{K}-1}\end{bmatrix}\otimes{\mathbf{V}}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{0}{\mathbf{V}}\\
\rho_{1}{\mathbf{V}}\\
\vdots\\
\rho_{B_{K}-1}{\mathbf{V}}\end{bmatrix}.

(38)

The decision boundary condition (
32
) for all coordinates and all relevant boundary angles can be written as:

\Im\left\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\right\}=\mathbf{0}_{B_{K}n}.

(39)

Definition C.8

(Real Augmented Matrix)
.

The real augmented matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{K}n\times 2r}
is constructed by separating real and imaginary parts:

\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}\}&\Im\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}\}\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

Equivalently, using the column interleaving that maps the complex product to a real inner product:

\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{:,1:2:2r-1}=\Re\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}\},\qquad\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{:,2:2:2r}=\Im\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}\}.

(41)

Lemma C.9

(Boundary Reformulation)
.

The decision boundary conditions are equivalent to the linear system:

\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})=\mathbf{0}_{B_{K}n}.

(42)

Proof.

For any
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Im\{{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}

\displaystyle=\Im\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}c_{j}(\bm{\phi})\right\}

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\Re\{a_{j}\}\Im\{c_{j}(\bm{\phi})\}+\Im\{a_{j}\}\Re\{c_{j}(\bm{\phi})\}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{{\mathbf{a}}\}^{\top}&\Im\{{\mathbf{a}}\}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Im\{{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}\\
\Re\{{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

By the construction (
28
),
\Im\{{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}_{1:2:2r-1}(\bm{\phi})
and
\Re\{{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}_{2:2:2r}(\bm{\phi})
. With the column arrangement in (
40
), this gives
\Im\{\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})
.
\square

∎

Definition C.10

(Coordinate Groups)
.

For each coordinate
i\in[n]
, the
coordinate group

\mathcal{G}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{K}\times 2r}
consists of the
B_{K}
rows of
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}
corresponding to coordinate
i
:

\mathcal{G}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{i,:}\\
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{i+n,:}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{i+(B_{K}-1)n,:}\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

Each row of
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
corresponds to a different decision boundary for coordinate
i
.

Remark 9

(Specialization to Even
K
)
.

For even
K
, the construction reduces to that in the foundational paper
[
38
]
with
M\equiv K
and
M/2=B_{K}
rotation factors. The matrix dimensions are
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nK/2\times 2r}
.

Remark 10

(Specialization to
K=3
)
.

For
K=3
, we have
B_{3}=3
with rotation factors:

\rho_{0}=e^{-\hat{\imath}\pi/3},\quad\rho_{1}=e^{-\hat{\imath}\pi}=-1,\quad\rho_{2}=e^{-\hat{\imath}5\pi/3}.

(47)

The rotated matrix is:

\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}_{3n\times r}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-\hat{\imath}\pi/3}{\mathbf{V}}\\
-{\mathbf{V}}\\
e^{-\hat{\imath}5\pi/3}{\mathbf{V}}\end{bmatrix},

(48)

and
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times 2r}
.

C.4
Intersection Properties and Group Structure

We now establish the key structural property that all hypersurfaces from the same coordinate group intersect at a common lower-dimensional manifold.

Definition C.11

(Hypersurface)
.

For row index
j\in[B_{K}n]
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}
, the
hypersurface

\mathcal{H}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:})
is the
(2r-2)
-dimensional manifold:

\mathcal{H}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:})=\left\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}:\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})=0\right\}.

(49)

Lemma C.12

(Common Intersection of Group Hypersurfaces)
.

For any coordinate
i\in[n]
, all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces corresponding to the rows of
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
intersect at a common
(2r-3)
-dimensional manifold within
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Proof.

Let the rows of
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
correspond to row indices
j_{0}=i,j_{1}=i+n,\ldots,j_{B_{K}-1}=i+(B_{K}-1)n
in
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}
.
By construction, row
j_{m}
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}
is derived from
\rho_{m}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}
. Specifically:

\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j_{m},:}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\rho_{m}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}\}&\Im\{\rho_{m}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}\}\end{bmatrix}.

(50)

The boundary condition
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j_{m},:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})=0
is equivalent to:

\Im\{\rho_{m}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})\}=0,

(51)

where
\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi}):={\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{C}
.
The condition
\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m}}\alpha_{i}\}=0
means that
\arg(\alpha_{i})=\vartheta_{m}
or
\arg(\alpha_{i})=\vartheta_{m}+\pi
. In other words,
\alpha_{i}
lies on the line through the origin at angle
\vartheta_{m}
.

The intersection of all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces from group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
is the set of
\bm{\phi}
such that
\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})
lies on
all
lines at angles
\vartheta_{0},\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{B_{K}-1}
simultaneously. Since these lines all pass through the origin, their common intersection is precisely the origin. Thus:

\bigcap_{m=0}^{B_{K}-1}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j_{m},:})=\left\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}:\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})=0\right\}=\left\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}:{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=0\right\}.

(52)

The constraint
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=0
is a single complex equation, equivalent to two real equations. Applied to the
(2r-1)
-dimensional hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
, this generically defines a
(2r-1-2)=(2r-3)
-dimensional manifold.

\square

∎

Corollary C.13

(Corollary 1 for General
K
)
.

For any
K\geq 2
(even or odd), all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces originating from the same coordinate
i
share a common
(2r-3)
-dimensional intersection manifold, namely the locus where
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=0
.

Remark 11
.

For
r=1
, the common intersection is
(2\cdot 1-3)=(-1)
-dimensional, meaning it is generically empty within
\mathcal{H}_{1}=(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
. This is consistent with the rank-1 algorithm, where each coordinate’s boundary points are isolated values
\varphi_{i}\in(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
.

Remark 12
.

For
r=2
, the common intersection is
(2\cdot 2-3)=1
-dimensional, i.e., a curve within the 3-dimensional hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. This is illustrated in Figure
1
of the main text.

C.5
Phase Reduction and Equivalent Candidate Elimination

We establish that restricting the final auxiliary angle to
(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
is valid for all
K\geq 2
and does not lose any optimal solutions.

Lemma C.14

(Phase Equivalence)
.

For any
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}
and any
\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
, we have:

\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}=\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}(\theta{\mathbf{z}})\|_{2}.

(53)

Consequently, if
{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}
maximizes (
24
), then so does
\theta{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
.

Proof.

We have
\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}(\theta{\mathbf{z}})\|_{2}=|\theta|\cdot\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}=\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}
since
|\theta|=1
for
\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
.
\square

∎

Lemma C.15

(Auxiliary Angle Reduction)
.

Let
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
with
\phi_{2r-1}\in(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
. For any
k\in\{1,\ldots,K-1\}
, define
\bm{\phi}^{(k)}
by:

\phi^{(k)}_{j}=\begin{cases}\phi_{j}&j\neq 2r-1,\\
\phi_{2r-1}+\frac{2\pi k}{K}&j=2r-1.\end{cases}

(54)

Then:

{\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}^{(k)})=e^{-\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}{\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}),

(55)

where
{\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})
is the decision function mapping
\bm{\phi}
to the optimal assignment vector.

Proof.

The complex steering vector satisfies:

{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}^{(k)})=e^{\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}),

(56)

since the phase shift in
\phi_{2r-1}
propagates to the complex exponential in the last component of
{\mathbf{c}}
.

For coordinate
i
, the decision rule selects:

d_{i}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}^{(k)})=\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z^{*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}^{(k)})\}=\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z^{*}e^{\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}.

(57)

Substituting
z^{\prime}=ze^{\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}
, which is a bijection on
\mathcal{A}_{K}
:

d_{i}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}^{(k)})=e^{-\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}\arg\max_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z^{\prime*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=e^{-\frac{2\pi\hat{\imath}k}{K}}d_{i}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}).

(58)

\square

∎

Corollary C.16

(No Loss from Restriction)
.

Restricting
\phi_{2r-1}
to
(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
instead of
(-\pi,\pi]
does not exclude any essentially distinct candidate solutions. Candidate vectors from the unrestricted range are phase rotations of those from the restricted range.

Proof.

Any
\bm{\phi}
with
\phi_{2r-1}\in(-\pi,\pi]
can be written as
\bm{\phi}^{(k)}
for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
and some base
\bm{\phi}
with
\phi_{2r-1}\in(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
. By Lemmas
C.14
and
C.15
, the candidate from
\bm{\phi}^{(k)}
achieves the same objective value as the candidate from
\bm{\phi}
.
\square

∎

Proposition C.17

(No Redundant Candidates)
.

The candidate set
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
constructed from
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
contains no two candidates that are phase rotations of each other.

Proof.

Suppose
{\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
with
{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}=\theta{\mathbf{z}}
for some
\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{K}\setminus\{1\}
. By construction,
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})
and
{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi}^{\prime})
for some
\bm{\phi},\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

By Lemma
C.15
, if
{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}=e^{-2\pi\hat{\imath}k/K}{\mathbf{z}}
, then
\bm{\phi}^{\prime}
must satisfy
\phi^{\prime}_{2r-1}=\phi_{2r-1}+2\pi k/K
. But for
k\neq 0
, this places
\phi^{\prime}_{2r-1}
outside
(-\pi/K,\pi/K]
, contradicting
\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
.
\square

∎

C.6
Candidate Set Enumeration

We now derive the exact size of the candidate set for general
K
.

Definition C.18

(Vertex of the Arrangement)
.

A
vertex
of the hypersurface arrangement is a point
\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
that lies on exactly
2r-1
hypersurfaces from distinct rows of
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}
. Formally, for an index set
\mathcal{I}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{2r-1}\}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2r-1
, the vertex
\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})
is the solution (if unique) to:

\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}(\bm{\phi})=\mathbf{0}_{2r-1}.

(59)

Lemma C.19

(Vertex Existence and Uniqueness)
.

For an index set
\mathcal{I}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2r-1
:

(i)

If
\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})=2r-1
, there exists a unique
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r}
(up to scaling) satisfying
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}=\mathbf{0}
. Converting to hyperpolar coordinates gives at most one
\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

(ii)

If
\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})<2r-1
, the system has infinitely many solutions, and no unique vertex exists.

Proof.

The matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2r-1)\times 2r}
has nullity at least 1. If rank equals
2r-1
, the null space is one-dimensional, spanned by some
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}^{*}\neq\mathbf{0}
. The hyperpolar parameterization maps each unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2r}
to a unique
\bm{\phi}
; normalizing
\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}^{*}
gives the vertex coordinates.

If rank is less than
2r-1
, the null space has dimension greater than 1, corresponding to a higher-dimensional manifold of solutions rather than a unique vertex.
\square

∎

Lemma C.20

(Rank Deficiency from Same-Row Triples)
.

If
\mathcal{I}
contains three or more indices from the same coordinate group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
, then
\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})<2r-1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.12
, the hypersurfaces from the same group intersect along a
(2r-3)
-manifold. If three or more rows from
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
are in
\mathcal{I}
, these rows are linearly dependent when restricted to directions perpendicular to the common intersection manifold. Specifically, if rows
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}
all come from group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
, the system
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\},:}\tilde{{\mathbf{c}}}=\mathbf{0}
is equivalent to the single complex equation
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}=0
, which is only two real equations, not three. Thus the three rows contribute only rank 2, creating rank deficiency.
\square

∎

Remark 13

(Rank Structure of Coordinate Groups)
.

Each coordinate group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
spans a 2-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2r}
. Specifically, all
B_{K}
rows of
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
lie in
\text{span}\{\tilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{i},\tilde{{\mathbf{b}}}_{i}\}
, where
\tilde{{\mathbf{a}}}_{i}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{b}}}_{i}
are derived from the real and imaginary parts of
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}
. Under the generic assumption that the subspaces corresponding to distinct coordinates are in general position, selecting
n_{i}
rows from group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
contributes
\min(n_{i},2)
to the total rank. Consequently, for an index set
\mathcal{I}
with
n_{i}
rows from each group
i
, we have

\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})=\sum_{i:n_{i}>0}\min(n_{i},2).

(60)

This formula confirms that the constraint “at most two rows per group” is both necessary and sufficient for achieving full rank
2r-1
.

Definition C.21

(Valid Index Set)
.

An index set
\mathcal{I}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2r-1
is
valid
if it contains at most two indices from each coordinate group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Remark 14

(Mixed Configurations)
.

The validity condition handles all possible distributions of
2r-1
indices across coordinate groups uniformly. For instance, with
r=3
(requiring
2r-1=5
indices):

•

Distribution
(2,2,1)
across three groups yields rank
\min(2,2)+\min(2,2)+\min(1,2)=2+2+1=5
(valid);

•

Distribution
(3,1,1)
yields rank
\min(3,2)+\min(1,2)+\min(1,2)=2+1+1=4<5
(invalid).

More generally, any distribution where multiple groups each contribute two indices remains valid, provided no group contributes three or more.

Corollary C.22

(Two Boundaries Implies All Boundaries)
.

If two distinct hypersurfaces from coordinate group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
both pass through a point
\bm{\phi}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
, then
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}^{*})=0
, and consequently all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces from group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
pass through
\bm{\phi}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
z={\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}^{*})\in\mathbb{C}
. Two hypersurfaces from
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
passing through
\bm{\phi}^{*}
means
\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m_{1}}}z\}=0
and
\Im\{e^{-\hat{\imath}\vartheta_{m_{2}}}z\}=0
for distinct boundary angles
\vartheta_{m_{1}}\neq\vartheta_{m_{2}}
. Writing
z=|z|e^{\hat{\imath}\alpha}
, these conditions require
\sin(\alpha-\vartheta_{m_{1}})=0
and
\sin(\alpha-\vartheta_{m_{2}})=0
.

If
|z|\neq 0
, then
\alpha-\vartheta_{m_{1}}\in\pi\mathbb{Z}
and
\alpha-\vartheta_{m_{2}}\in\pi\mathbb{Z}
, implying
\vartheta_{m_{1}}-\vartheta_{m_{2}}\in\pi\mathbb{Z}
. For boundary angles
\vartheta_{m}=\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{K}
with distinct
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{K}
, we have
\vartheta_{m_{1}}-\vartheta_{m_{2}}=\frac{2\pi(m_{1}-m_{2})}{K}
, which lies in
\pi\mathbb{Z}
only if
(m_{1}-m_{2})\in\frac{K}{2}\mathbb{Z}
. For
K
odd this is impossible for distinct indices in
\mathcal{M}_{K}=\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
; for
K
even with
\mathcal{M}_{K}=\{0,\ldots,K/2-1\}
, the difference
|m_{1}-m_{2}|<K/2
also precludes this.

Therefore
|z|=0
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi}^{*})=0
. By Lemma
C.12
, this is precisely the condition for all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces from
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
to pass through
\bm{\phi}^{*}
.
\square

∎

Lemma C.23

(Cells Adjacent to a Vertex)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a valid index set defining a vertex
\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
. Let
p
denote the number of coordinate groups that contribute exactly two indices to
\mathcal{I}
. Then the vertex is adjacent to exactly
(B_{K}-1)^{p}
cells in the hypersurface arrangement.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a valid index set with
p
coordinate groups each contributing exactly two indices. By Corollary
C.22
, for each such group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
, all
B_{K}
hypersurfaces pass through the vertex
\bm{\phi}^{*}=\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})
.

Consider the local geometry at
\bm{\phi}^{*}
. For a coordinate group
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
contributing two indices to
\mathcal{I}
:

•

All
B_{K}
hypersurfaces from
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}
pass through
\bm{\phi}^{*}
;

•

These hypersurfaces share a common
(2r-3)
-dimensional intersection (where
{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})=0
);

•

In the 2-dimensional normal space to this intersection, the
B_{K}
hypersurfaces appear as
B_{K}
lines through the origin;

•

These
B_{K}
lines divide the 2D normal space into
2B_{K}
angular sectors;

•

However, antipodal sectors correspond to the same symbol assignment (since
\Re\{z^{*}\alpha_{i}\}
has the same sign for
\alpha_{i}
and
-\alpha_{i}
when
z
is fixed);

•

Thus there are
B_{K}
distinct symbol regions for coordinate
i
.

For a coordinate contributing exactly one index to
\mathcal{I}
, only that single hypersurface generically passes through
\bm{\phi}^{*}
, dividing a neighborhood into 2 half-spaces with distinct symbol assignments.

The
2r-1
defining constraints come from
p
groups contributing 2 each (total
2p
constraints, but only
2p
dimensions of freedom due to the 2D subspace structure) and
(2r-1-2p)
groups contributing 1 each. At the vertex:

•

Each of the
p
saturated groups offers
B_{K}
symbol choices (one per angular sector);

•

Each of the
(2r-1-2p)
non-saturated groups offers 2 symbol choices (one per half-space).

However, moving away from the vertex into a cell requires consistent choices. The two indices from each saturated group define two of the
B_{K}
hypersurfaces; moving into a cell means choosing one of the
B_{K}-1
“wedges” between consecutive hypersurfaces that does not cross either defining hypersurface immediately. This gives
(B_{K}-1)
choices per saturated group.

For non-saturated groups, the single defining hypersurface is crossed or not, but since we are counting cells (not half-cells), each non-saturated group contributes a factor of 1 to the cell count (the symbol is determined by which side of the hypersurface the cell lies on).

Therefore, the number of cells adjacent to the vertex is
(B_{K}-1)^{p}
.
∎

Theorem 15

(Candidate Set Size for General
K
)
.

For a rank-
r
matrix with factor
{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r}
and alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{K}
with effective boundary count
B_{K}
, the candidate set satisfies:

|\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})|=\sum_{d=1}^{r}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{i}\binom{n-i}{2(d-i)-1}B_{K}^{2(d-i)-2}(B_{K}-1)^{i}.

(61)

For fixed
K
and
r
, this is
O(n^{2r-1})
.

Proof.

We count candidates at each rank level
d=1,\ldots,r
and sum.

Counting at rank
d
:
Consider valid index sets
\mathcal{I}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2d-1
that define vertices in the interior of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
(the rank-
d
hypercube). Let
i
denote the number of coordinate groups contributing exactly two indices to
\mathcal{I}
.

Step 1: Choose which coordinates contribute two indices.

There are
\binom{n}{i}
ways to select
i
coordinates from
[n]
.

Step 2: Choose which two boundaries from each such coordinate.

For each of the
i
coordinates, we select 2 boundaries from
B_{K}
options. There are
\binom{B_{K}}{2}
ways per coordinate, giving
\binom{B_{K}}{2}^{i}
total.

Step 3: Choose remaining coordinates contributing one index each.

The remaining
2d-1-2i
indices come from
2d-1-2i
distinct coordinates (other than the
i
already chosen). There are
\binom{n-i}{2d-1-2i}
ways to select these coordinates.

Step 4: Choose which boundary from each single-index coordinate.

For each of the
2d-1-2i
coordinates, we select 1 boundary from
B_{K}
options, giving
B_{K}^{2d-1-2i}
combinations.

Step 5: Count cells per vertex.

By Lemma
C.23
, each such vertex is adjacent to
(B_{K}-1)^{i}
cells.

Combining:

|\mathcal{J}({\mathbf{V}}_{:,1:d})|=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{i}\binom{B_{K}}{2}^{i}\binom{n-i}{2d-1-2i}B_{K}^{2d-1-2i}(B_{K}-1)^{i}.

(62)

The total candidate set size is:

|\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})|=\sum_{d=1}^{r}|\mathcal{J}({\mathbf{V}}_{:,1:d})|=\sum_{d=1}^{r}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{i}\binom{n-i}{2(d-i)-1}B_{K}^{2(d-i)-2}(B_{K}-1)^{i},

(63)

where the sum over
d
accounts for the recursive structure handling boundary cases.

Asymptotic analysis:
For fixed
K
and
d
, the dominant term is
i=0
:

\binom{n}{2d-1}B_{K}^{2d-2}=O(n^{2d-1}).

(64)

Summing over
d=1,\ldots,r
:
|\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})|=O(rn^{2r-1})
.
\square

∎

C.7
Algorithm Correctness for General
K

We now prove that Algorithm
2
(with the augmented matrix constructed for general
K
as in Section
C.3
) correctly solves the discrete quadratic maximization problem.

Theorem 16

(Correctness for General
K
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a rank-
r
PSD matrix and let
K\geq 2
be arbitrary. Algorithm
2
, using the augmented matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{K}n\times 2r}
constructed as in Definition
C.8
, returns
{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}
satisfying:

{\mathbf{z}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}=\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{z}}.

(65)

Proof.

We show that the candidate set
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
enumerated by the algorithm contains the global maximizer.

Step 1: Reformulation as double maximization.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz argument (Lemma
C.1
and the subsequent discussion):

\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\|{\mathbf{V}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}=\max_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\max_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}}\Re\{{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}=\max_{\bm{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z_{i}^{*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}.

(66)

The second equality holds because, for fixed
\bm{\phi}
, the coordinates
z_{i}
can be optimized independently.

Step 2: Decision function is piecewise constant.

For fixed
\bm{\phi}
, the optimal
z_{i}
is determined by the decision rule:

d_{i}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})=\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}\Re\{z^{*}{\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})\}.

(67)

Let
\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})={\mathbf{V}}_{i,:}{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\phi})
. The optimal
z_{i}
is the element of
\mathcal{A}_{K}
whose phase is closest to
\arg(\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi}))
. As
\bm{\phi}
varies, the phase of
\alpha_{i}
varies continuously, and
d_{i}
changes only when
\arg(\alpha_{i})
crosses a boundary angle
\vartheta_{m}
.

Step 3: Hypersurfaces partition
\mathcal{H}_{r}
into cells.

The
B_{K}n
hypersurfaces
\{\mathcal{H}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{j,:})\}_{j=1}^{B_{K}n}
partition
\mathcal{H}_{r}
into cells. Within each cell, the decision function
{\mathbf{d}}({\mathbf{V}};\bm{\phi})
is constant. The objective
\sum_{i}\max_{z_{i}}\Re\{z_{i}^{*}\alpha_{i}(\bm{\phi})\}
is continuous in
\bm{\phi}
, so the global maximum is attained either:

(a)

At a vertex of the arrangement (intersection of
2r-1
hypersurfaces), or

(b)

On the boundary of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Step 4: Enumeration of interior vertices.

The main loop of Algorithm
2
enumerates all valid index sets
\mathcal{I}\subset[B_{K}n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=2r-1
and checks if
\text{rank}(\tilde{{\mathbf{V}}}_{\mathcal{I},:})=2r-1
. For each such
\mathcal{I}
, it computes the vertex
\bm{\phi}({\mathbf{V}};\mathcal{I})
, applies the decision rule to obtain a candidate
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathcal{I}}
, and evaluates the objective.

By Lemma
C.19
, this enumeration covers all cells whose ”leading vertex” (the vertex with minimal
\phi_{2r-1}
) lies in the interior of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Step 5: Recursive handling of boundary cases.

Cells touching the boundary
\phi_{2r-1}=-\pi/K
or
\phi_{2r-1}=\pi/K
are not captured by interior vertices. However:

•

By Corollary
C.16
, candidates from
\phi_{2r-1}=-\pi/K
are phase rotations of candidates from elsewhere, so no loss occurs.

•

Candidates from
\phi_{2r-2}=\pm\pi/2
(with arbitrary
\phi_{2r-1}
) correspond to the rank-
(r-1)
problem on
{\mathbf{V}}_{:,1:r-1}
. The recursive call handles these.

Overall, the boundary of the hypercube
\mathcal{H}_{r}
consists of faces where one angular coordinate is fixed at its extreme value. When this occurs, one degree of freedom in the auxiliary vector
\mathbf{c}(\bm{\phi})
is eliminated: either a magnitude allocation coordinate (
\phi_{j}=\pm\pi/2
) becomes extremal, or the final phase coordinate (
\phi_{2r-1}=\pm\pi/K
) is fixed. In both cases, the effective optimization over the remaining
2(r-1)-1
free parameters reduces to a rank-
(r-1)
subproblem, which is solved by the recursive call in Algorithm
2
.
This is proven in
[
39
]
.

This reduction is independent of the alphabet size
K
. The specific boundary values differ with
K
(e.g.,
\pm\pi/K
for the phase coordinate), but the mechanism (fixing one coordinate eliminates one degree of freedom) is purely structural. Combined with the interior vertex enumeration, this ensures that the candidate set contains the global optimizer for any rank-
r
PSD matrix and any
K\geq 2
.

Step 6: Completeness of candidate set.

Combining Steps 4 and 5, every cell of the arrangement is associated with at least one candidate in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
. Since the global maximizer lies in some cell, it is in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
.

Step 7: Evaluation and selection.

The algorithm evaluates the objective for all candidates and returns the best one. Since the global maximizer is in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{V}})
, the returned
{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}
achieves the maximum.
\square

∎

Lemma D.1
.

Let

To handle the non-Hermitian noise in Theorem

Lemma D.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}_{r},{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
be defined above. Let
\lambda_{j}
be the
j
th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
, and define
\delta^{\star}=\min\left\{\min_{j\in[r-1]}\left|\lambda_{j}^{\star}-\lambda_{j+1}^{\star}\right|,\lambda_{r}^{\star}\right\}
. If
\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\delta^{\star}}{2}
, then we have that

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq O\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)

Proof.

Since
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
is Hermitian PSD, we write its top-
r
eigendecomposition as
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}={\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}
with
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times r}
and
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
being a diagonal matrix. Let
{\mathbf{u}}^{\star}_{j}
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
denote the
j
th column of
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}
and
{\mathbf{U}}
, respectively. Thus, we can decompose the quantity of interest as

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right\|_{2}

(68)

\displaystyle+\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}-{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\left\|{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

For the first term, since we know that
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}
is the top-
r
truncated SVD of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
, we cam simply bound

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}

(69)

For the third term, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}\left(\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}-\bm{\Sigma}\right){\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

(70)

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}-\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[r]}\left|\bm{\Lambda}_{jj}^{\star}-\bm{\Sigma}_{jj}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}

where the last inequality follows from Weyl’s inequality due to the fact that
\bm{\Lambda}_{jj}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{jj}
are the singular values of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}
, respectively. For the second term, we can write

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

(71)

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}-{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right)^{\dagger}+\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right)\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right)\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right)^{\dagger}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{1}^{\star}\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}

where the second-to-last inequality is because
\max\{\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\right\|_{2},\left\|{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}\}\leq 1
due to the fact that
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star},{\mathbf{U}}
contains orthogonal columns. Next, we will study the term
\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}
using Wedin’s Theorem. To start, we notice that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right){\mathbf{U}}+{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right){\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)\right\|_{2}

Since
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}
and
{\mathbf{U}}
are the top-
r

{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}

\left\|\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right){\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}}{\delta}

For the second term, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in[r]}\left|1-\sigma_{r}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=1-\sigma_{\min}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)

where the first inequality is due to the fact that
\left\|{\mathbf{U}}\right\|^{\star}
is a unitary matrix, and the last inequality is because
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}
has singular values in the range
[0,1]
. Let
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
with

\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}=\sigma_{\min}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)

\left\|{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}=1

{\mathbf{U}}
contains orthonormal columns. Then we have that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}\left({\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=1-\left\|\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right){\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left\|\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}\right){\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\left({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\top}{\mathbf{U}}\right)\right\|_{2}

\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}
, which leads to

\left\|{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}

(72)

by (

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left(1+\frac{4\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta}\right)\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}

\delta^{\star}=\min\left\{\min_{j\in[r-1]}\left|\lambda_{j}^{\star}-\lambda_{j+1}^{\star}\right|,\lambda_{r}^{\star}\right\}

\displaystyle\min_{j\in[r-1]}\left|\lambda_{j}^{\star}-\sigma_{i}\right|

One should notice that, in Theorem

\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\delta^{\star}}{2}

The corollary below shows that we can drop the dependency on the eigengap if the perturbation matrix

Corollary D.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}_{r},\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
be defined in (
17

\lambda_{j}^{\star}
be the
j
th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
with
\lambda_{j}^{\star}=0
if

. If

and
{\mathbf{H}}
is Hermitian, then we have that:

\left|\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\right|\leq O\left(n\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)\right).

(73)

saves a multiplicative factor of

compared with Theorem

The following lemma provides an upper bound of the approximation error when the perturbation
{\mathbf{H}}
(and consequently
{\mathbf{Q}}
) is Hermitian

Lemma D.4
.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{Q}}_{r},{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
be defined above. Let
\lambda_{j}
be the
j
th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
. If
{\mathbf{H}}
is Hermitian, then we have that

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq O\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right).

Proof.

Since
{\mathbf{H}}
is Hermitian, we must have that
{\mathbf{Q}}
is Hermitian. Recall that we defined
{\mathbf{Q}}_{r}^{\star}={\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\bm{\Lambda}{\mathbf{U}}^{\star\dagger}
as the top-
r
eigendecomposition of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
, where
{\mathbf{U}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times r}
and
\bm{\Lambda}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Q}}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}.

We can easily notice that
\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}
is the top-
r
eigendecomposition of
{\mathbf{Q}}
, since
{\mathbf{U}}={\mathbf{V}}
due to the fact that
{\mathbf{Q}}
is Hermitian. Therefore, we have that
\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}=\lambda_{r+1}\left({\mathbf{Q}}\right)
. By Weyl’s inequality, we have that

\left|\lambda_{r+1}\left({\mathbf{Q}}\right)-\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}\right|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}.

Therefore, we can conclude that
\lambda_{r+1}\left({\mathbf{Q}}\right)\leq\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}
. This gives that

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2},

which implies that

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+2\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}=O\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right).

∎

Now, we are ready to prove Corollary
D.3
.

Proof of Corollary
D.3
.

Under the provided condition, we can apply Lemma
D.4
to obtain that

\left\|{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{2}\leq O\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right).

Plugging the bound into Lemma
D.1
gives

\left|{\mathbf{z}}^{\star\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{z}}_{r}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\right|\leq O\left(n\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)\right).

∎

D.4
Extension to complex Gaussian noise

Theorem
4
made no assumptions on the noise matrix
{\mathbf{H}}
.
With different assumptions on
{\mathbf{H}}
, we may be able to decrease the bound in Theorem
4
. In this section, we give a simple result for
{\mathbf{H}}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(0,\varepsilon^{2})
independently by leveraging a result of
[
11
]
.

Corollary D.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}_{r},\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
be defined in (
17
). Let
\lambda_{j}^{\star}
be the
j
th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}
with
\lambda_{j}^{\star}=0
if
j>r
, and define
\delta^{\star}=\min\left\{\min_{j\in[r-1]}\left|\lambda_{j}^{\star}-\lambda_{j+1}^{\star}\right|,\lambda_{r}^{\star}\right\}
. Let
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
H_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(0,\alpha^{2}\right)
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\alpha\leq\frac{c\delta^{\star}}{\sqrt{n}}
for some small enough constant
c
, then, there exist constants
C,c^{\prime}>0
such that with probability at least
1-C\exp\left(-c^{\prime}n\right)
, we have that:

\left|\texttt{OPT-}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}-{\mathbf{z}}_{r}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\star}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\right|\leq O\left(n\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{n^{\frac{3}{2}}\lambda_{1}^{\star}\varepsilon}{\delta^{\star}}\right).

Here, we extend Corollary
D.3
by bounding the operator norm of
{\mathbf{H}}
by
\sqrt{n}\lambda_{1}^{\star}\varepsilon
. Note that this bound on the operator norm matches the standard result in the real case
[
57
]
, both with a scaling of
\sqrt{n}
. In addition, Corollary
D.5
implies that the noise term dominates only when
\varepsilon\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\delta^{\star}\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}}{\lambda_{1}^{\star}\sqrt{n}}\right)
, showcasing a relationship between the approximation error, the noise scale, and the threshold rank
r
.

Proof of Corollary
D.5
.

By definition, since
H_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(0,\alpha^{2}\right)
, we have that
\text{Re}\left(H_{i,k}\right),\text{Im}\left(H_{i,k}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)
independently. Thus, we have that
H_{i,k}
is sub-Gaussian in the complex space, which allows us to use Theorem
18
to obtain that, with probability at least
1-C\exp\left(-c^{\prime}n\right)
,

\left\|\alpha^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\Rightarrow\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq O\left(\alpha\sqrt{n}\right).

Notice the choice of
\alpha
; plugging that upper bound into (
18
) gives the desired result.
∎

D.5
Proof of Theorem
5

See
5

Proof of Theorem
5
.

By Theorem
4
, we know

{\mathbf{z}^{\star}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}^{\star}}-{\mathbf{z}_{r}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}_{r}}\leq O\left(n\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)\right).

We can rearrange this bound to get a bound on the approximation ratio in terms of
{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}
as follows:

\frac{{\mathbf{z}_{r}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}_{r}}}{{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}}\geq 1-\frac{O\left(n\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)\right)}{{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}}.

(74)

We now focus on lower bounding
{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}={\mathbf{z}^{\star}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}^{\star}}
.
To do so, we introduce
\mathbf{\hat{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}
. For now, we can treat
\mathbf{\hat{z}}
as an arbitrary vector in
\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}
. In Equation (
75
), we show that we can choose
\mathbf{\hat{z}}
to satisfy certain nice properties that allow us to lower bound
\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}
.

We can rewrite
\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}
in terms of the decomposition of
{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}=\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda^{\star}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star\dagger}\right)\mathbf{\hat{z}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda^{\star}_{i}\left(\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{z}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda^{\star}_{i}\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\star}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{\star}_{1}\cdot\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}}\right|^{2}.

We now focus on bounding
\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}}\right|^{2}
:

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\left(\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}+\text{Im}\left(\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\text{Re}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\bar{\hat{z}}_{\ell}u_{1,\ell}^{\star}\right)\right]^{2}+\left[\text{Im}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\bar{\hat{z}}_{\ell}u_{1,\ell}^{\star}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\text{Re}\left(\bar{\hat{z}}_{\ell}u_{1,\ell}^{\star}\right)\right]^{2}+\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\text{Im}\left(\bar{\hat{z}}_{\ell}u_{1,\ell}^{\star}\right)\right]^{2}.

Each entry
u_{1,\ell}^{\star}
of
u_{1}^{\star}
may be written in polar form as

u_{1,\ell}^{\star}\penalty\ =\penalty\ r_{\ell}\exp\left(j\theta_{\ell}\right),

where
r_{\ell}=|u_{1,\ell}^{\star}|\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta_{\ell}\in[0,2\pi)
. Additionally, because
\mathbf{\hat{z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}^{n}
,
z^{\star}_{\ell}=\exp\left(jk_{\ell}\pi/K\right)
for some
k_{\ell}\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
.
We can re-express the quantity under consideration as:

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}\text{ Re}\left(\exp\left(j\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}\text{ Im}\left(\exp\left(j\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\right)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}\cos\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}\sin\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\right]^{2}.

Now, define
k_{\ell}\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
to be the unique integer such that
\theta_{\ell}
lies in the
k_{\ell}
-th sector:

\theta_{\ell}\in\left[\frac{(2k_{\ell}-1)\pi}{K},\frac{(2k_{\ell}+1)\pi}{K}\right)\pmod{2\pi}.

(75)

Let
\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}=\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\text{ mod }2\pi
. By the definition of
k_{\ell}
, we ensure that the rotated angle is close to zero, specifically
\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{K}\right)\cup\left[\frac{(2K-1)\pi}{K},2\pi\right)
for all
\ell\in[n]
. Consequently, the cosine of the offset angle is bounded below by the cosine of the maximum deviation
\pi/K
:

\cos\left(\theta_{\ell}-\frac{2k_{\ell}\pi}{K}\right)\in\left[\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{K}\right),1\right].

This implies that

\left|{\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\star}}\right|^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{K}\right)\right)^{2}=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{K}\right)\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{1}^{2},

where the final inequality holds for all
K\geq 3
.
In order to lower bound
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{1}
, we notice that

1=\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{1}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{\infty}

By the assumption that
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{n}}
,we have that

\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|_{\infty}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\mu}

Ultimately, we have shown that

\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}\geq\frac{1n}{4\mu^{2}}\lambda^{\star}_{1}.

Because
{\mathbf{z}^{\star}}
maximizes
{\mathbf{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}}
, we know

\mathbf{z}^{\star\dagger}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\mathbf{z}^{\star}\geq\mathbf{\hat{z}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}\mathbf{\hat{z}}.

This allows us to bound
{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}
as follows:

{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}=\mathbf{z}^{\star\dagger}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\mathbf{z}^{\star}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}n}{4\mu^{2}}.

Substituting this bound back into (
74
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbf{z}_{r}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}{\mathbf{z}_{r}}}{{\texttt{OPT-}\mathbf{Q}^{\star}}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{O\left(n\left(\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}{\delta^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\right)\right)}{\lambda^{\star}_{1}n}

\displaystyle\geq 1-O\left(\frac{\lambda_{r+1}^{\star}}{\lambda_{1}^{\star}}+\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}}{\delta^{\star}}\right).

∎

We conduct experiments on three families of graphs:

, and

and

(ii) Random
d

n=20,50
and 100 nodes, with
d=3,4
and
5
;
(iii) All GSet benchmark instances
[
63
]
. GSet is a benchmark commonly used to evalute
Max-K-Cut
algorithms that consists of 71 graphs ranging in size from 800 to 20,000 nodes. Each GSet graph falls into one of the following categories
[
16
]
:

1.

Erdos-Renyi graphs with edge probability at most
0.03
,

2.

2-D toroidal, 4-regular graphs,

3.

random graphs with skewed degree distributions.

These graphs either have binary (0 or 1) edge weights or have edge weights in
\{-1,0,+1\}
.

Baseline Algorithms.

We compare our algorithms to the following baselines:
(i)
Frieze-Jerrum

[
21
]
: an SDP relaxation and rounding scheme; (ii)
Greedy

[
29
]
: a greedy algorithm that iteratively identifies and locks the single-best node assignment; (iii)
Genetic

[
47
]
: a genetic algorithm that maintains a set of candidate cuts and iteratively selects the best of this set, mixes pairs of cuts, and randomly perturbs the worst. On the GSet benchmark, we also compare to a random baseline,
Random
. To ensure a fair comparison to
Rank-1
,
Random
generates
n+1
random cuts and selects the best one, matching the number of candidate solutions enumerated by our algorithm.

Implementation Details.

All experiments were conducted on HPE ProLiant DL360 Gen11 compute nodes (Sapphire Rapids) equipped with Intel Xeon Platinum 8468 CPUs (96 cores at 2.10 GHz). We generate 20 instances for each size and parameter of the small random graphs using the NetworkX library
[
30
]
with fixed random seeds. We use the implementation of
[
49
]
for the
Greedy
and
Genetic
algorithms. We institute a 30-minute timeout limit for all experiments.

Evaluation Metrics.

To measure the quality of the
Max-3-Cut
approximations found by each algorithm on random graphs, we calculate the ratio of its score to the best score found by any algorithm. We report the average ratio across all random seeds.
