Efficient deep learning-driven workflows for accelerated single-fraction radiation therapy planning of a solitary tumour in the lung
Authors: Mathieu Gaudreault, Nicholas Hardcastle, Katrina Woodford, Jason Li, Kenton Thompson, Susan Harden, Sandro Porceddu, Vanessa Panettieri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeSame-day, single-fraction radiotherapy requires efficient planning. We introduce two deep learning (DL) dose prediction-driven workflows to generate clinically acceptable plans for treating metastases in the lung.Materials and methodsConsecutive patients treated for a tumour in the lung (11/2018–11/2024) were considered. One workflow used the computed tomography density map and a synthetic dose built from the tumour segmentation as inputs for the DL model [without-organs of interest (OOI)]. The other workflow additionally used predicted OOI segmentations (with-OOI). Optimisation objectives were extracted from the predicted dose by the treatment planning system (TPS). Generated plans were compared with clinical plans with the number of achieved goals, conformity index CI100, and control point modulation complexity score (cpMCS). Plan complexity increases with decreasing cpMCS. Processing times were recorded.ResultsTraining and testing datasets contained 200 and 56 treatments, respectively. Without- / with-OOI workflows generated plans in 8.8 and 9.6 min. The 3%/3 mm gamma passing rates between predicted and clinical doses were median ± interquartile range 99.4 ± 0.7% / 99.3 ± 0.8%. After dose optimisation in the TPS, 25% of plans had a higher number of achieved clinical goals relative to clinical plans in both workflows. The conformity was improved (CI100 = −5.3% / −3.9% relative to the clinical value, p-values <0.001) at the cost of increased complexity (cpMCS = −19.4% / −19.0%, p-values <0.001).ConclusionsWorkflows driven by DL dose prediction could generate a clinically acceptable plan in less than ten minutes for same-day, single-fraction treatment of a tumour in the lung.Keywords: Deep learning, Artificial intelligence, Dose prediction, Autosegmentation, Treatment plan, Metastatic lung cancer

Introduction

Same-day radiation therapy aims to simulate the patient's anatomy, generate a treatment plan, and deliver the radiation dose within each patient's visit [1], [2]. This setting may specifically benefit single-fraction stereotactic ablative radiation therapy (SABR) for lung metastases, a treatment increasing in frequency [3] due to its improved efficiency and clinical outcomes comparable to multi-fraction regimens [4]. However, the need to segment the target and organs of interest (OOI) and balance ablative target coverage with sparing of adjacent healthy organs creates a time-intensive bottleneck [5], [6], [7], [8], which may compromise the feasibility of same-day radiation therapy. Clinically applicable workflows that rapidly generate accurate volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) SABR plans for lung tumours remain scarce. Existing solutions include knowledge-based planning (KBP), which typically predicts OOI dose-volume histograms from geometric features [9], but may fail to capture complex spatial relationships and anatomical variations, potentially resulting in suboptimal predictions [10]. In addition, KBP requires a full optimisation cycle before dose evaluation, limiting rapid planner-physician interaction [11]. Other approaches utilise dedicated solutions [12], [13], but these are proprietary and not widely applicable to standard linear accelerators. There is therefore a need for rapid, accessible workflows to enable single-fraction lung SABR on conventional platforms.

Deep learning (DL) has the potential to accelerate and standardise treatment planning, as it has already improved efficiency and accuracy for organ segmentation [14] and dose prediction [15]. Fully automated DL-driven treatment planning aims to generate clinically acceptable plans in a fraction of conventional planning time [16], [17], [18], [19]. In such workflows, a DL model either predicts a three-dimensional dose distribution from which objectives are extracted for dose optimisation or directly predicts machine parameters for dose calculation. The feasibility of these methods has been demonstrated for conventional treatments of head and neck [16] and prostate cancer [17], but equivalent solutions for SABR, particularly single-fraction treatments with high doses and steep dose gradients, are still lacking.

In this study, we aimed to introduce DL-driven workflows based on predicted three-dimensional dose distributions that generate clinically acceptable single-fraction radiation therapy plans to treat a solitary lung metastasis.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Ethics approval was provided by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (HREC/101771/PMCC). Consecutive patients treated for single or multiple tumours in the lung between 11/2018 and 11/2024 were considered. The single fraction prescription dose was 28 Gy (only metastases in the lung). To improve predictive accuracy through a larger training dataset [20], treatments of patients prescribed 48 Gy in four fractions (metastases and primaries) were also considered. Patients were excluded if radiation therapy data were non-accessible or if VMAT arc geometry deviated from protocols. Treatment plans were split approximately 80% and 20% into training/validation and testing sets. Patients with multiple lung tumours were included only in the training/validation set. The testing set was used for inference only and was not seen during training or validation.

Clinically delivered treatment plans

The internal gross target volume (iGTV) was defined as the volume of disease, including motion. The iGTV was contoured by the treating radiation oncologist on the average intensity projection (AIP) image of the four-dimensional CT (4DCT) by using individual phases and the maximum intensity projection image, where applicable. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated from a 5 mm isotropic expansion of the iGTV. OOI were segmented manually on the AIP image. The ablative dose was planned and delivered with VMAT. Each plan used two ipsilateral partial arcs on a standard linac, and dose was optimised with Photon Optimizer and calculated with AcurosXB (dose-to-medium) within the Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) (v16, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA). The optimisation was performed via inverse planning using a manually adjusted objective template. The normal tissue objective tool was applied at the planner's discretion to control dose fall-off.

Deep learning-driven treatment planning workflows

An illustration of the two workflows introduced in this study is shown in Fig. 1. It was assumed that the VMAT technique was pre-determined and that the tumour was manually segmented with the isocentre placed at its centroid. In the first workflow (without-OOI), OOI segmentations were not used as input for dose prediction, and dose optimisation was driven by generic optimisation structures. In the second workflow (with-OOI), OOI segmentations were predicted from the AIP image by using the TotalSegmentator software [21] and were used as inputs for dose prediction and to drive dose optimisation. Both workflows used a processed version of the AIP image and tumour segmentation as inputs for dose prediction. In both workflows, clinical OOI contours were used for reporting dose-volume metrics only.

Preprocessing

For both workflows, all images and dose distributions were centred on the tumour centroid, resampled to a common voxel grid, and cropped to a fixed-volume region around the target. The AIP image was converted to a normalised density map using the clinical stoichiometric curve, and the iGTV segmentation was used to generate a synthetic three-dimensional dose distribution. The clinical dose distribution was normalised to the prescription dose. The density and synthetic dose maps were used as physics-informed priors to guide planning and improve prediction accuracy. Details of voxel sizes, density conversion, and synthetic dose construction are provided in the Supplementary Material A.

In the without-OOI workflow, model inputs were the density and synthetic dose maps; in the with-OOI workflow, the predicted heart, spinal cord, and oesophagus label maps were added as additional channels. In both workflows, the output to be learned was the normalised clinical dose distribution.

Deep learning dose prediction

A three-dimensional encoder–decoder network based on the 3D MedNeXt architecture with ConvNeXt blocks [22], [23] was used to predict the dose distribution (Fig. 1). Training in both workflows used a mean absolute error loss function and the Adam optimiser [24]. Data augmentation and a 5-fold group cross-validation strategy were employed, and the final prediction was obtained by averaging the outputs of the five models and rescaling to the original prescription dose. Full architectural and training details are provided in the Supplementary Material B. Predicted and clinical dose distributions were compared using 3%/3 mm gamma passing rates (γPR3mm3%), with varying dose thresholds, and the predicted doses were automatically imported into the TPS for subsequent optimisation and evaluation. Ablation studies where only the synthetic dose, the density map, or the density map and predicted structures were used as inputs of the DL model were also considered.

Dose optimisation and evaluation

The optimisation structures were projected onto the predicted dose distribution. In the without-OOI workflow, optimisation structures consisted of sectors of three-dimensional shells generated from the tumour segmentation, as detailed in the Supplementary Material C. In the with-OOI workflow, optimisation structures included the predicted heart, spinal cord, and oesophagus segmentations, and the chest wall, constructed from predicted lung lobe and rib segmentations. Dose optimisation with intermediate dose calculation was automated via the Eclipse Scripting Application Programming Interface (ESAPI, v16). For each optimisation structure, dose–volume information was extracted from the predicted dose and used to populate an objective template. Target structures received lower and upper objectives derived from the predicted dose, whereas non-target structures received upper objectives only with predefined priorities (details in the Supplementary Material D).

Finally, the script calculated the final dose distribution with a pixel spacing and slice thickness of 1.25 mm × 1.25 mm × 1 mm. The treatment plans were evaluated in terms of clinically achieved goals, selected dose–volume metrics (Supplementary Table S1), conformity indices CI50 and CI100, and plan complexity. The complexity was assessed with the modulation factor (MF) defined as the total monitor units (MU) per unit of prescribed dose (cGy), and the control point-modulation complexity score (cpMCS) [25]. Plan complexity increases with increasing MU and decreasing value of cpMCS. The plans were judged clinically acceptable if all clinical goals were met and any dose conformity deviations were only minor [26], [27]. Patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA), including dose measurement and gamma testing with 3%/1 mm criteria, a 10% dose threshold, and an institutional gamma pass-rate criterion of 90%, was performed using the Portal Dosimetry Image Prediction algorithm (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA) on the 5% of plans with the lowest cpMCS values.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance was assessed by using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied across all comparisons and did not alter any of the reported conclusions; raw p-values are therefore reported. The null hypothesis was rejected if the p-value was smaller than 0.05. All analyses were performed with in-house Python scripts (v3.11).

Results

The training dataset comprised 200 treatments from 174 patients, while the testing dataset comprised 56 single-fraction treatments. In the training dataset, 153 (88%), 12 (7%), and 3 (2%) patients were treated for 1, 2, or 3 lung tumours, respectively (Table 1). Furthermore, six (3%) patients had lung re-irradiation, four of them after the treatment of a single tumour and two of them after the treatment of two tumours. Considering all treatments, the iGTV mean ± standard deviation volume was 10.2 ± 11.2 cm3. The treatment characteristics are further detailed in the Supplementary Table S2. In 95% of treatments, SABR was delivered with 6 MV beams with flattening filter free by using a meterset rate of 1400 MU/min. All OOI dose guidance values (Supplementary Table S1) were respected in all clinical plans of the testing dataset. In 12/56 (21%) plans, PTV D99% was at most 5% lower than the dose guidance of 100% of the prescribed dose, and in 2 (4%) plans, iGTV mean dose was at most 1% lower than the dose guidance of 120% of the prescribed dose.

All dose guidance values were respected in all patients by using the without-OOI workflow, resulting in 14/56 (25%) plans with a higher number of achieved clinical goals (Fig. 3). With this approach, the near-maximum dose to skin was decreased (skin D0.03 cm3 median ± IQR = 9.2 ± 3.8 Gy / 10.3 ± 4.2 Gy in DL-driven / clinical plans, p-value <0.001) as well as the lung volume receiving low doses (lungs V5 Gy median ± IQR = 7.9 ± 5.3% / 8.2 ± 5.2% of the total structure volume in DL-driven / clinical plans, p-value <0.001). The differences in all other OOI dose-volume metrics were not statistically significant (p-values > 0.05). The conformity index CI100 was improved as compared with its clinical value (CI100 median ± IQR = 1.07 ± 0.04 / 1.13 ± 0.08 in DL-driven / clinical plans, p-value <0.001) (Fig. 4). Discussion

We introduced two deep learning-driven workflows capable of generating clinically acceptable treatment plans in approximately 10 min for single-fraction SABR of a solitary lung metastasis. Only the CT image and the tumour segmentation were needed as inputs. The TPS dose optimisation engine was used in both workflows, allowing further refinement of the DL-driven plans within a standard clinical environment. An interesting and clinically relevant finding of this study was that OOI segmentations were not necessary to generate a clinically acceptable treatment plan. This outcome may be useful in the translation of this workflow to other sites for which OOI segmentations are challenging to predict, such as head and neck cancer [28]. Using a density map instead of the AIP CT image improved gamma agreement, particularly in challenging cases, and incorporating a synthetic dose as an additional input substantially enhanced high-dose prediction. Providing a physics-informed prior on the expected dose distribution, such as the synthetic dose used in this study, therefore appears beneficial for training convolutional neural network-like architectures.

Our workflows highlighted two major applications of dose prediction. First, they provided a rapid visualisation of achievable dose distributions to support physician decision-making and planning guidance. Second, they were able to drive treatment planning directly from the predicted dose and produced plans with similar or improved dose distribution compared with clinical plans. Taken together, these characteristics suggest that the proposed workflows could be integrated into clinical routine to support same-day radiation therapy and to provide additional options within larger ultrafast AI-based treatment planning ecosystems.

Overall, 25% of plans achieved more and 75% a similar number of clinical goals across the two workflows, with improved dose conformity but higher plan complexity; the main benefits were efficiency and automation. The increase in MF relative to the clinical value was less than 10% in both workflows. Although cpMCS was approximately 20% lower (more complex) in AI-driven plans, these values are comparable to MCS values (calculated using different definitions) reported for multifraction lung SABR (MCS median ± IQR = 0.34 ± 0.11 and 0.29 ± 0.10 [29], vmatMCS = 0.38 ± 0.11 [30], and vmatMCS = 0.37 ± 0.17 [31]).

Few studies have reported treatment planning driven directly by deep-learning predictions, and, to our knowledge, none has focused on SABR. Prior studies include DL-driven planning in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [16] and prostate cancer [17], and three-dimensional dose prediction for multi-lesion lung SABR using generative adversarial networks [15]. Our study extends these efforts to single-fraction lung SABR, achieving mean absolute OOI dose differences below 1.5 Gy and maintaining all dose-volume metrics within clinical guidance.

Our total workflow time of about 10 min, including OOI segmentation in the with-OOI workflow, compares favourably with reported KBP- and AI-based lung SABR planning times of 10–60 min [32], [33], [34], which exclude time for OOI segmentation. Manual planning for metastatic lung SABR in our department typically requires around one hour [35], excluding time for OOI segmentation. A prospective study would better quantify the time savings offered by the proposed workflows.

It is worth noting that our workflows were designed to exclusively support single-fraction SABR to the lung; consequently, our models were not tested on a cohort prescribed multiple fractions or re-irradiation. These cohorts are expected to be characterised by closer OOI proximity to the target and more complex dose geometries, and therefore to be more challenging. Future work could explore hybrid approaches combining DL-based segmentations with generic optimisation structures and extend the framework to multi-fraction and multi-site SABR [36]. Moreover, training was repeated by using the single-fraction cohort only, for which results demonstrated that inclusion of the multi-fraction cohort during training was beneficial (Supplementary Fig. S5). Motion was managed using 4DCT with iGTV generation and planning on the AIP. More advanced motion management (e.g. abdominal compression or breath-hold) would require retraining the model with appropriate inputs. Training on data from multiple institutions, linacs, and protocols could further improve workflow generalisability. PSQA was performed only on the most complex 5% of plans, providing a targeted check of deliverability but not a full assessment of the entire cohort.

Due to the lack of graphics processing unit (GPU) availability within the TPS through scripting, the doses were predicted outside the clinical environment, which prevented an uninterrupted execution of our scripts. File transfer was automated by using Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine toolkit software and the TPS database daemon allowing smooth data importation without human intervention. To achieve uninterrupted execution, we recommend that the vendors allow GPU access during scripting to speed up future in-house AI-driven workflows.

In this study, dose objectives for optimisation were automatically extracted by the clinical TPS from the imported predicted dose. Alternatively, structures could be projected onto the predicted dose outside the TPS to extract objectives. These objectives could then be input manually, or automatically for example by reading a text file, into any TPS allowing optimisation via dose objectives. This procedure enables generalisation of our methods to any predicted dose and TPS.
