Data and knowledge-driven imaging biomarkers for lumbar aging and degenerative risk stratification monitoring
Authors: Anlin Luo, Nuo Tong, Shuiping Gou, Ningtao Liu, Yulin Yang, Xiaorong Chen, Jianyun Yu, Tiangang Zhou, Wenbo Liu, Bingwu Wang, Licheng Jiao, Shenghong Cui, Yongliang Dai, Tan Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration, a key indicator of aging in the human movement system, is linked to increasing global cases of low back pain. Current diagnostic methods rely on imaging and physician experience, lacking predictive tools and personalized treatment strategies. This study used a multicenter lumbar MRI dataset to map disc degeneration in the Chinese population, revealing three accelerated degeneration phases during the lifecycle. Age heatmaps highlighted the degeneration rate of L1–L3 segments, highly synchronized with true age, serving as a baseline for physiological aging estimation. A contrastive learning-based slice ensemble network achieved a mean absolute error of 2.59 years in age estimation, and multi-center validation confirmed its reliability. Two digital imaging biomarkers, Age Delta and Age Selta, were proposed and preliminarily validated in longitudinal cases as a proof-of-concept demonstration. This study primarily demonstrates the feasibility and potential clinical value of data-driven lumbar aging biomarkers.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) and disc herniation are prevalent age-related spinal conditions and are widely regarded as closely linked to biological aging1–9. However, accumulating evidence indicates that imaging features of disc degeneration are also highly prevalent among asymptomatic individuals and increase with age, highlighting the complex and non-linear relationship between structural degeneration and clinical symptoms. The incidence of these conditions is rising globally10–13, affecting over 65 million individuals annually14,15. If IVDD is not identified and effectively treated in its early stages, it may ultimately progress to spinal instability and symptoms of spinal canal and nerve root compression, severely impacting individuals’ quality of life while imposing significant medical and economic burdens on society11–16. The current diagnosis of IVDD primarily relies on imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT). While these techniques provide insights into the current structural status of discs, they lack predictive capacity for long-term IVDD progression risks.

Therapeutic strategies for IVDD follow a four-tiered hierarchical approach17–19: (1) conservative management, (2) interventional therapy, (3) minimally invasive surgery, and (4) spinal fusion with internal fixation. However, clinical decision-making in selecting optimal treatment tiers remains predominantly dependent on clinicians’ empirical experience and individual judgment, rather than evidence-based personalized guidance derived from large-scale clinical data analytics. This empirical paradigm increases the likelihood of overtreatment or suboptimal therapeutic outcomes. A critical unmet need lies in developing prognostic tools capable of predicting the developmental trajectory and progression rate of IVDD during its early stages. Such predictive capacity would significantly enhance personalized therapeutic selection and optimization of treatment efficacy, thereby addressing the current limitations in clinical management paradigms.

Estimating lumbar age is a diagnostic method used to assess the growth, development, and degenerative changes of the lumbar spine, offering insights into the current physiological stage and potential future risks of lumbar diseases. This approach aids physicians in evaluating the extent of lumbar degeneration and predicting anomalous aging conditions that may not align with chronological age. Traditional methods of lumbar age estimation, including biomarker-based approaches and imaging techniques, have limitations. Biomarkers like serum markers reflect bone metabolism but lack specificity due to external factors like nutrition and disease. Imaging methods, such as skeletal morphology analysis, are subjective and prone to inaccuracies20. For instance, studies involving lumbar MRI and X-ray images rely on visual assessments and feature extraction, which are time-consuming and prone to bias21–24. Recent advancements have incorporated deep learning techniques to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of lumbar age estimation. Research by Yang et al.25 proposed a region feature recalibration network for fine-grained grading of lumbar disc degeneration, while Zheng et al.26 utilized deep learning for high-precision quantitative measurement of lumbar degeneration. However, these approaches have yet to comprehensively address lumbar age estimation across diverse age groups and degeneration patterns. While deep learning has been successfully applied to age prediction in other organs27–29, such as the brain, these methods face challenges when applied to musculoskeletal tissues like the lumbar spine due to structural and functional differences.

This study employs a deep learning model to analyze a large-scale lumbar spine MRI dataset for accurate lumbar spine age estimation and IVDD characterization. A slice ensemble strategy and contrastive learning approach are used to reduce computational complexity while improving the accuracy of age prediction. The overall framework of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. A large-scale lumbar spine MRI dataset will be utilized to extract and annotate various anatomical structures of the lumbar spine, constructing an atlas of IVDD. This atlas will reveal the non-uniform degeneration patterns and the three-phase accelerated degeneration characteristics, providing an in-depth understanding of the degeneration process of the lumbar discs. Meanwhile, deep learning algorithms will be employed to accurately capture age-related features of the lumbar spine, generating age significance heatmaps that provide reliable insights for physiological age prediction and identify which lumbar segments exhibit a high correlation with age. Finally, novel aging digital biomarkers will be proposed to effectively predict the risks of physiological and pathological lumbar spine aging. Results

Statement of research datasets. The dataset of this study is from Xijing Orthopaedic Hospital, Xi’an North Hospital, Shandong Weifang Orthopaedic Hospital and Jinhua Central Hospital, representing four different regions in the west, north, east and south of Chinese Mainland. The datasets consisted of T2-weighted MR Lumbar sagittal scans from 1797 adults (11–80 years old) with pixel spacing ranging from 4 to 5 mm and section thickness between 4.4 and 6.25 mm. The sample size and age distribution show that there are fewer samples below 20 years and above 70 years, with more samples in the young and middle-aged groups, indicating an imbalance in distribution, as illustrated in Fig. 2c. This age imbalance reflects real-world clinical acquisition patterns but may introduce bias in age estimation performance at extreme age ranges. Therefore, caution is required when interpreting results in underrepresented age groups, particularly adolescents and elderly populations. To address this issue, data augmentation preprocessing was employed to mitigate the bias caused by data imbalance. Additionally, as the dataset comprises images collected from different machines and radiologists, there are significant differences in image intensity ranges. Therefore, all data were subjected to windowing to scale the intensity values into a uniform range. Standardized preprocessing was then performed, including bias field correction, z-score normalization, resampling, and standardization. The dataset was randomly divided into 1224 patients for the training set, 198 patients for the validation set, and 175 patients for the internal test set. In addition, 200 patients from different centers were used as an external test set; these external cases were not involved in either training or validation.Fig. 2Age-related degenerative changes of intervertebral discs based on MRI.Figure a and b depicts the internal cohort (n = 1597) used for degeneration curve development. The degeneration pattern of the lumbar intervertebral disc. In this study, the anatomical structures of the lumbar spine were first segmented, achieving an average DICE coefficient of 0.92. Based on segmentation results, a detailed image analysis of the lumbar intervertebral discs was conducted. Based on MRI data and the age and gender information of 1597 subjects, intervertebral disc aging curves were plotted in five-year intervals, categorized by gender, as shown in Fig. 2. This metric normalizes the intervertebral disc signal using an age-adjusted spinal canal reference signal, thereby reducing the potential bias introduced by age-related changes in the reference signal during the assessment of intervertebral disc degeneration. The horizontal axis represents age, ranging approximately from 10 to 80 years old, while the vertical axis represents the age-adjusted Disc Signal Score, aDSS. In addition, 95% confidence interval bands were plotted along the degeneration curves to reflect the statistical uncertainty of disc signal changes across different age groups. Based on the analysis of large-scale lumbar MRI imaging data, the results indicate that human lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration does not follow a simple linear decline, but instead exhibits nonlinear and stage-specific patterns. Sex-specific modeling showed that the overall degeneration patterns were similar between males and females, both characterized by a gradual decline in intervertebral disc signal after early adulthood, with potential regions of slope change emerging at different age stages. In females, lumbar intervertebral disc signal reached a relatively high level around the age of 20, followed by a gradual age-related decline. Piecewise linear regression showed that the potential turning points of the female degeneration curve were located at approximately 33, 48, and 67 years of age. In males, the potential turning points were approximately 28, 51, and 70 years of age. The current results suggest that the most pronounced decline in lumbar intervertebral disc signal mainly occurs from early adulthood to midlife, while a further degenerative trend may exist in later life, although with greater variability. These turning points may serve as potential imaging time points in the process of lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration. However, their relationships with biological events such as childbirth, menopause, hormonal changes, or systemic aging require further investigation using more comprehensive clinical, physiological, and endocrine information.

Age-related significance heat-maps. The age-related significance heatmap illustrates the most important regions for different age groups, as shown in Fig. 3. The attention heatmaps visualize spatial regions contributing most strongly to the network’s age estimation task. Project the saliency map onto the segmented anatomical regions and visualize the attention weights of each region using color. These maps represent model-level feature importance distributions. The changes in the heatmap reveal the gradual shift in regions most closely associated with lumbar spine age as the individual ages. From left to right, the heatmap shows the evolving importance of regions at different developmental stages of the lumbar spine. The process of cartilage ossification in the lumbar spine begins in the embryonic stage and continues until approximately the age of 25. Therefore, between the ages of 11 and 30, the heatmap primarily focuses on the secondary ossification centers of the vertebral body, particularly the upper and lower edges of the vertebrae and the spinous processes. As the lumbar spine enters adulthood (ages 31–60), the structural changes become slower, and disc degeneration typically begins around the age of 30, intensifying with age. As a result, the heatmap shifts focus to the intervertebral discs that show the most pronounced degeneration. In the elderly stage (ages 61–80), in addition to the ongoing degeneration of the intervertebral discs, there is also osteoporosis, deformity of the lumbar spine, and atrophy of the paraspinal muscles, leading to a broader range of attention on more tissue structures in the heatmap. Overall, the distribution trend of the heatmap shows a transition from multiple regions of concentration during the growth phase, to fewer regions in adulthood, and a wider focus in old age, with the height of the heatmap gradually narrowing and the width expanding. There is no significant difference in the heatmaps between males and females in terms of age-related regions. The progressive degeneration of the intervertebral discs, the reduction in vertebral bone density, and changes in surrounding soft tissues are well-documented age-related changes30–34. By visualizing the regions most affected by age-related changes at different life stages, areas that previously required manual measurement of intervertebral disc characteristics can now be intuitively displayed through heatmaps. Age estimation associated with lumbar spine degeneration. This study investigates age estimation associated with lumbar spine degeneration, evaluating different anatomical regions (L1–L3, L1–L5, L4–L5, L1–S1). Scatter plots and Age Delta values (Fig. 4a–d) demonstrate that L1–L3 provides the most accurate and stable age predictions, with the Age Delta primarily within 5 years. Age Delta is defined as the difference between the predicted age of a specific segment and the actual biological age. In contrast, regions such as L1–L5, L1–S1 show wider Age Delta ranges (7–10 years), while L4–L5 exhibits the poorest performance, with Age Delta extending to 15 years. Quantitative evaluation metrics (PCC, SRCC, RMSE, MAE) indicate that L1–L3 outperforms other regions, achieving a PCC of 0.97, SRCC of 0.97, RMSE of 3.341, and MAE of 2.592, shown in Table 1. Age estimation accuracy is also analyzed across different age groups, showing L1-L3 consistently provides the closest estimates for individuals aged 30–70. The L4–L5 region, however, performed better in younger individuals (11–30 years) due to less degeneration. Cumulative score (CS) and mean cumulative score (MCS) results further highlight L1-L3 as the best-performing region, with a CS of 87% in the 5-year range and 99% in the 10-year range. Comparative methods and multicentre validation results for age estimation of the lumbar spine. This study compares various age estimation algorithms27–29 for medical images, highlighting the superior performance of our proposed cross-spinal structure method based on slice ensemble and contrastive learning. The results, presented in Table 2, demonstrate that our method achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE, outperforming other algorithms under the same error tolerance, as shown in Fig. 4e. Additionally, cumulative and mean cumulative score curves indicate consistent improvement in age estimation accuracy as the error tolerance increases. External validation (Table 3) using two multi-center datasets (Weifang_Spine and Zhejiang_Spine) further supports the robustness of our method, with results closely matching internal tests, differing by less than 1 year, which is within an acceptable range. These findings suggest that our approach holds potential for lumbar disc health assessment and clinical decision-making, with room for further enhancement in multi-center robustness.Table 2The Warning of degenerative risk for the intervertebral disc. A large number of clinical studies and statistical data show that the incidence of degeneration of these L3–L4, L4–L5, and L5–S1 intervertebral discs is significantly higher than that of other segments32,35–39. Based on this, this study proposes a digital biomarker Age Selta bias to assess the risk of degeneration. Age Selta is a more precise measure defined as the difference between the predicted age of the three degeneration-prone discs (L3–L4, L4–L5, and L5–S1) and the predicted age of the normal aging region (L1–L3). This study integrates anatomical knowledge to define a baseline curve for normal aging samples using the L1–L3 region and conducts a detailed analysis of regions more prone to degenerative changes, specifically L3–L4, L4–L5, and L5–S1. Discussion

This study investigates the nonlinear properties of intervertebral disc degeneration through quantitative analysis using MRI data and the disc signal to spinal canal signal ratio (aDSS). Importantly, aDSS is not intended to replace established morphological grading systems (e.g., Pfirrmann) or quantitative relaxometry techniques (e.g., T2 mapping). Instead, it serves as a relatively continuous indicator for population-level trend analysis under real-world clinical imaging constraints. Our analysis of aDSS values across various age groups reveals a general decline in aDSS with aging, reflecting the overall trend of lumbar spine degeneration. Supporting this, a 2019 Nature Medicine study identified key age points-34, 60, and 78 years—where plasma protein levels undergo significant “cliff-like” declines, suggesting non-linear aging patterns43. In our research, large-scale imaging data indicate accelerated lumbar spine degeneration in males around ages 28, 51, and after 70, which aligns with the aging timelines identified in plasma protein studies, further confirming the burst-like nature of aging44. For females, lumbar degeneration accelerates at ages 33, 48, and post-67, with the early onset at age 33 linked to the peak childbearing years, and menopause around 48 contributing to earlier degeneration compared to males. This pattern of two distinct aging peaks at ages around 30 and 50 for Chinese women further supports the complex interplay of biological factors influencing spinal degeneration45. These findings highlight the nonlinear progression of lumbar degeneration and underscore age as a crucial factor in disc health.

Analysis of significant heat maps for lumbar age estimation across different age groups reveals progressive changes in the regions most correlated with lumbar aging. Spinal development begins in the embryonic stage, with secondary ossification centers forming in the cartilaginous endplates during childhood to guide vertical growth, which ceases by late adolescence. By age 25, ossification centers in the transverse processes, articular processes, and spinous processes fully mature. Consequently, heat maps for individuals aged 11–30 primarily highlight vertebral bodies, cartilaginous endplates, and select appendicular sites. During the 31–60-year age range, although the osseous structures of the spine also undergo gradual age-related changes (e.g., alterations in bone mineral density), the most prominent and rapidly detectable changes on routine clinical MRI occur in the intervertebral discs. This is likely attributable to cumulative mechanical micro-injury and progressive extracellular matrix degradation (including collagen breakdown), which drive ongoing disc degeneration. Accordingly, the heat maps in this age interval primarily concentrate on disc-related changes. In middle age, disc degeneration is characterized by reduced water content, decreased signal intensity, and height loss, driven by metabolic decline, increased mechanical stress, and reduced nutritional supply. Early intervention through lifestyle modifications, exercise, and regular check-ups is critical. After age 60, spinal degeneration intensifies, with disc degeneration extending across multiple segments. Radiologically, osteoporosis-induced vertebral deformities, ligamentous hypertrophy, and osteophyte formation become evident, leading to the most extensive heat map coverage across the lumbar region. Notably, heat maps show no gender differences in lumbar degeneration patterns. These findings have significant clinical implications. For adolescents and young adults, promoting bone and disc health is essential to prevent early degeneration. For middle-aged individuals, especially those under 50, early detection through radiological examinations is crucial. In the elderly, particularly those over 70, comprehensive spinal monitoring and care are necessary to prevent severe degenerative conditions and functional impairments.

The proposed cross-spinal structure lumbar age estimation algorithm, based on slice ensemble and contrastive learning, demonstrates superior performance. The slice ensemble approach efficiently captures inter-slice dependencies while reducing computational costs. Additionally, integrating contrastive learning and the age group offset tendency index enhances adaptability to diverse data distributions, mitigating data imbalance effects. The L1–L3 region exhibits the highest accuracy due to its lower mechanical stress, stable anatomical structures, and consistent aging patterns, while L4–S1 faces greater mechanical loads and degenerative variability, complicating estimation. Despite its strengths, the method has limitations. In clinical implementation, it is necessary to expand the diversity of training data, covering different ages, genders, and ethnicities, in order to enhance the model’s generalization ability and robustness. Degenerative diseases involve imaging findings, clinical symptoms, functional scales, and molecular biomarkers. Multimodal fusion enables more accurate identification of degeneration patterns and provides individualized support for risk-based follow-up, thereby enhancing the predictive and stratified management value of biomarkers in spinal care. From a digital biomarker perspective, Age Delta and Age Selta align with the emerging concept of imaging-derived digital biomarkers widely adopted in oncology, cardiology, and neuroimaging. Similar to brain age or cardiac age paradigms, lumbar age deviation may provide a scalable quantitative phenotype for degeneration risk stratification and long-term monitoring, forming a foundation for future predictive modeling and prospective risk assessment. Future research will focus on conducting international studies across different populations, incorporating more diverse multimodal data, and further refining the framework to enhance generalizability and clinical applicability. Methodologically, advanced deep learning architectures and longitudinal data integration could enhance predictive accuracy, allowing future degeneration forecasting. These improvements would increase clinical utility, supporting preventive healthcare strategies.

This study not only achieved high accuracy in lumbar age estimation but also introduced a novel method for assessing degenerative risk in high-risk regions (L3–L4, L4–L5, and L5–S1) using L1–L3 as a reference for natural aging. By defining the digital biomarker Age Selta, the deviation in age predictions between L1–L3 and other intervertebral discs was quantitatively assessed, providing a reliable metric for identifying degenerative risks. In line with emerging digital biomarker paradigms in medical imaging, our framework is positioned as a quantitative imaging phenotype for structural aging deviation and has the potential to function as an assistive clinical tool. This approach surpasses traditional single-age estimation methods, enabling early detection and targeted interventions for high-risk individuals. The effectiveness of this risk warning method was validated through longitudinal cases of lumbar disc degeneration, confirming its utility for early disease prediction. Regular monitoring of Age Selta values allows for timely intervention, potentially reducing the incidence and severity of degenerative conditions. This methodology holds promise for integrating early diagnostic and preventive strategies in clinical practice. However, the study’s limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset originates from several clinical centers in China, with a relatively small sample size, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future research should expand sample sizes, incorporate international multi-center data collection across diverse populations, and include a broader range of ages, genders, and ethnicities. Such efforts will enhance the robustness and applicability of the proposed method, ensuring its effectiveness across different demographics and clinical settings.

Building on the observed nonlinear degeneration patterns and risk assessments, this study proposes a precision-tiered treatment approach for intervertebral disc degeneration, ensuring minimal invasiveness and maximal effectiveness. Treatment is escalated incrementally based on disease severity and patient-specific conditions. The first-tier involves conservative treatment for mild cases without severe neurological dysfunction, including bed rest, analgesics, diuretics, neuronutrients, physical therapy (ultrasound, microwave, traction), and traditional Chinese medicine (acupuncture, manipulation). Typically lasting 3–6 months, this approach can prevent the need for more invasive interventions. The second-tier includes interventional therapy, such as nerve root block and radiofrequency ablation, for patients unresponsive to conservative treatment or those unwilling/unfit for surgery. If ineffective, the third-tier involves minimally invasive procedures, such as spinal endoscopic surgery and minimally invasive channel surgery, suitable for patients with severe symptoms who wish to avoid fusion surgery. As a final-tier, fusion surgery (e.g., posterior lumbar decompression, pedicle screw fixation with interbody fusion) is reserved for cases where all prior treatments have failed, particularly in patients with severe, recurrent symptoms or cauda equina syndrome. Currently, treatment selection often relies on physician experience, leading to variability in patient management. Integrating lumbar disc digital biomarker risk indices (Age Selta) into decision-making enables a data-driven, personalized approach. Patients with high-risk degeneration (Age Selta >3) and a predicted rapid degeneration probability within 3–5 years should receive earlier intervention by escalating treatment. Conversely, patients in the low-risk category (Age Selta <3) may benefit from more conservative management, reducing unnecessary invasive procedures. This precision-tiered strategy enhances treatment effectiveness, minimizes recurrence risks, and optimizes long-term patient outcomes, advancing a more personalized and evidence-based approach to disc degeneration management. Another important limitation is the absence of symptom-level longitudinal outcome data. While Age Selta demonstrates potential for imaging-based progression risk stratification, its ability to predict future back pain or radiculopathy has not yet been established. This represents a key direction for prospective cohort studies.

In clinical workflows, the proposed model has significant application value. In outpatient and health examination settings, it can serve as a tool for mid- to long-term risk prediction, assisting physicians in stratifying patient populations, optimizing the allocation of medical resources, and reducing unnecessary repeated imaging or invasive procedures, thereby lowering overall healthcare costs. In preoperative treatment planning, the model’s quantitative risk estimates allow physicians to rely less on individual experience and instead formulate more rational and personalized therapeutic strategies, helping to avoid both overtreatment and undertreatment. In postoperative follow-up and chronic disease management, the model can function as a digital monitoring tool for remotely tracking high-risk patients, enabling physicians to dynamically adjust follow-up and intervention strategies, thus reducing the need for reoperations and improving patients’quality of life. Moreover, the method can be seamlessly integrated into existing PACS workflows, automatically generating risk assessment reports (e.g., flagging high-risk discs such as L4–L5 or L5–S1 when Age Selta >3 years) and providing personalized follow-up recommendations (e.g., every 6–12 months for high-risk patients versus every 2–3 years for low-risk patients). As a clinical decision support tool, the system-generated risk alerts can further aid spine surgeons and rehabilitation physicians in implementing precise and individualized management.

Methods

Lumbar disc degeneration is a slow and nonlinear process of change. To quantitatively characterize disc degeneration and its progression, this study employs deep learning models to analyze a large-scale lumbar MRI dataset. The overall methodological framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.The process begins with the input of lumbar MRI images, encompassing anatomical structures from the thoracic spine to the sacrum. A segmentation network is utilized to precisely extract 16 anatomical structures, including the lumbar vertebrae and intervertebral discs. Based on the segmentation results, a slice ensemble and contrastive learning-based age estimation network is constructed to estimate the age of each lumbar segment. This approach integrates cross-lumbar anatomical structures and generates saliency heat maps to highlight age-associated regions for different age groups. Furthermore, using the anatomical segmentation results, a novel Disc Signal Score (aDSS) is proposed to map degeneration curves across different genders. Additionally, a new digital biomarker, Age Delta, is introduced based on age estimation results to assess degeneration risk. The proposed metrics and their predictive capabilities are validated in the clinic. This integrated framework not only offers precise anatomical segmentation and quantitative age estimation but also provides critical insights into degeneration patterns and risk assessment.

The developed model was applied to a dataset comprising 1597 samples, enabling precise segmentation of anatomical structures for each sample. This included accurate extraction of 16 key anatomical regions such as vertebrae, intervertebral discs, and the spinal canal. The samples were further stratified by gender, with 557 female cases and 1040 male cases. A comprehensive imaging feature analysis was performed for each lumbar anatomical structure, focusing on the statistical evaluation of signal intensity characteristics. The original MRI images often show problems such as inconsistent overall light and dark and uneven signals due to differences in scanning conditions, field strength and equipment among different subjects. Relying solely on visual judgment is easily influenced by subjective factors. By calculating the average signal strength of ROI, we can transform this qualitative high or low signal evaluation into a continuous and quantifiable indicator, thereby providing a more objective quantitative basis for clinical judgment. Age-harmonized DSS is calculated by normalizing disc signal intensity to the spinal canal signal at the same anatomical level to mitigate scanner-related global intensity shifts. This indicator is calculated by normalizing the signal intensity of the intervertebral disc with the signal intensity of the spinal canal, which has been adjusted to eliminate the influence of age. This study employs the nnFormer segmentation algorithm46 to extract 15 lumbar-related anatomical structures, including thoracic (T11, T12) and lumbar (L1–L5) vertebrae, along with seven intervertebral discs and portions of the spinal canal. Leveraging Transformer blocks, nnFormer effectively captures both local and global features, enabling precise segmentation of 3D lumbar MRI datasets,shown in Fig 7. The model was trained on 200 3D lumbar MRI cases using data preprocessing, parameter initialization, and iterative optimization across 1000 epochs. Data augmentation (rotation, scaling, flipping) was applied to enhance generalizability. A five-fold cross-validation approach was used, achieving an average Dice similarity coefficient of 0.92, demonstrating high segmentation accuracy. In the second phase, an age estimation network was constructed to objectively evaluate the natural aging patterns of the lumbar spine and enable more precise age predictions. This study proposes a method for lumbar spine age estimation and aging trajectory analysis across spinal structures based on the concepts of slice ensemble networks and contrastive learning. The overall framework of the network is illustrated in Fig. 9a. The principle of the network is to use an ensemble network to integrate the features of two-dimensional slices from three-dimensional lumbar MRI images, thereby fully exploiting the relationships between these slices49–52. Simultaneously, the concept of contrastive learning is employed to reduce the differences between adjacent slices in the three-dimensional lumbar MRI images, enabling accurate age prediction. Additionally, knowledge learned from the data distribution is introduced, and an age group offset tendency index is proposed, which effectively enhances the accuracy of lumbar spine age estimation.Fig. 9Framework for lumbar spine age estimation across spinal structures based on slice ensemble and contrastive learning.a illustrates the overall computational process; b depicts the workflow for calculating the similarity loss between adjacent slices; c demonstrates the structure of the aggregation module.

The input three-dimensional lumbar MRI image is divided into a sequence of eight two-dimensional slices according to its inter-slice information. The encoder processes each two-dimensional slice to extract feature information, resulting in two sets of encoded sequences. The structure of the encoder is composed of seven sub-modules. The first five sub-modules share the same structure, each consisting of a 3 × 3 two-dimensional convolutional layer, an instance normalization layer, a max-pooling layer, and a ReLU activation layer. The sixth sub-module consists of a 1 × 1 two-dimensional convolutional layer, an instance normalization layer, and a ReLU activation layer. The seventh sub-module includes an average pooling layer, a dropout layer, and a 1 × 1 two-dimensional convolutional layer. The two sets of encoded sequences are then combined and input into the Aggregation module, which aggregates the slice features. The aggregation module is based on an attention mechanism, enabling it to adaptively focus on important regions in the input and generate key features representing the entire three-dimensional lumbar image. Finally, the features generated by the aggregation module are mapped to an age value through a fully connected regression layer. The predicted age and the true age guide the network training and optimization through the mean squared error (MSE) loss function. To ensure the similarity between adjacent slices in the three-dimensional lumbar MRI image, this study introduces the adjacent slice similarity loss. The two encoded sequences produced by the encoder are sequentially input into a projector and a predictor. The similarity between the 1st and 2nd slices, 3rd and 4th slices, 5th and 6th slices, and 7th and 8th slices is calculated based on the outputs of the projector and predictor. The negative values of these similarities are then added to the loss function as a penalty term. By minimizing this loss, the adjacent slices are encouraged to be close in the feature space, thereby complementing each other and learning the correlation features of the slice sequence.

The core idea of the aggregation module, as illustrated in Fig. 9b, is to calculate weights using the attention mechanism. Q, K, and V are three crucial parameters in the attention mechanism, representing Query (Q), Key (K), and Value (V), respectively. The query is a vector used to calculate similarity within the attention mechanism, representing the semantic information of a specific position in the input sequence. Keys and values are vectors used to represent the semantic information of different positions in the input sequence; the key is used to calculate similarity, while the value is used to generate the output. The attention mechanism determines the weight of each position in the output sequence by calculating the similarity between Q and K. These weights are then multiplied by V to obtain the semantic information for each position in the output sequence.
