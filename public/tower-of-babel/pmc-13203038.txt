Genomic epidemiological patterns and antifungal resistance profiles of Candidozyma auris in China: Spatiotemporal dynamics and persistent colonization
Authors: Junmin Cao, Xiang-Yu Wang, Cong Qin, Danxia Gu, Rong Zhang, Hongwei Zhou, Ruichao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCandidozyma auris (C. auris) is an emerging multidrug-resistant fungal pathogen with major global public health implications. However, the clinical characteristics, clade-specific genomic variation, resistance-associated SNP patterns, and transmission dynamics of C. auris in China remain insufficiently understood. We retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 22 intensive care unit patients colonized or infected with C. auris and performed antifungal susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on 25 isolates. All isolates were resistant to fluconazole, and 80% were resistant to amphotericin B, whereas echinocandin susceptibility remained intact. Phylogenetic analysis assigned the isolates to Clade I (n = 19) and Clade III (n = 6). SNP analysis revealed marked clade-specific differentiation in resistance-associated genes, with ERG11 Y132F enriched in Clade I and ERG11 alterations, including VF125AL, enriched in Clade III. Additional variants in ERG2, CDR1, TAC1a, and TAC1b also showed clade-biased distributions and frequent co-occurrence. However, no significant association was observed between individual SNPs or mutation burden and antifungal susceptibility phenotypes. To investigate transmission dynamics, we conducted comparative genomic and phylogeographic analyses using 210 C. auris genomes. Major inbound introductions into China were inferred from Canada and South Korea to Guangdong and from Pakistan to Beijing, while phylogeographic modeling suggested that Beijing and Guangdong may have served as key nodes for subsequent domestic spread. These findings highlight the importance of integrated genomic surveillance for tracking C. auris dissemination and informing targeted infection control strategies.KEYWORDS: Candidozyma auris, fungal, epidemiological, genomic analysis

Introduction

Candidozyma auris (C. auris) was first isolated and identified from the external ear canal of a female inpatient in Japan in 2009 [1]. Since then, infections caused by C. auris have spread over 50 countries across 6 continents, posing a serious threat to global public health [2–4]. According to a recent systematic review, the overall resistance rates of C. auris to fluconazole range from 87% to 100%, and the 30-day mortality rates associated with C. auris are as high as 23–67% [5]. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has classified C. auris as an urgent threat [6]. Despite global surveillance efforts, the epidemiological characteristics of C. auris, particularly the relationship between its genetic diversity and clinical phenotypes, remain poorly understood, hampering targeted control strategies.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis indicates simultaneous emergence of different clonal populations of C. auris strains in different regions [7]. To date, six geographically distinct Clades have been described: Clade I (South Asian), Clade II (East Asian), Clade III (African), Clade IV (South American), Clade V (Iranian) and the recently identified Clade VI (Singaporean) [7–9]. Significant differences in genetic content and considerable variation in sequence alignment have been noted among various Clades. However, the genomic mechanisms driving the dominance of specific clades in certain regions, as well as their association with outbreak dynamics, remain largely unexplored. This knowledge gap is particularly evident in regions, such as China, where genomic epidemiology remains insufficiently characterized.

In China, C. auris infections have been relatively rare despite global outbreaks [10]. The first detection of C. auris was reported in Beijing in 2018 [11]. According to a recent report, 312 C. auris-associated cases and 4 outbreaks in healthcare environments were recorded during the period of 2018 to 2023 [12]. In contrast to C. auris strains from India and the United States, the majority of strains isolated in China exhibited resistance to fluconazole but remain susceptible to both amphotericin B and caspofungin [12]. Chinese strains differ genetically from those circulating in India and the United States, raising questions about localized evolutionary pressures. Nevertheless, challenges in laboratory identification and limited genomic surveillance likely result in underestimation of true incidence and diversity. A critical unanswered question is how the genomic evolution of C. auris in China contributes to its clade distribution, resistance-associated variation, and dissemination in healthcare settings.

In this study, we aimed to (i) describe the clinical and antifungal susceptibility characteristics of C. auris isolates from ICU patients in Hangzhou, (ii) define their phylogenetic lineages, resistance-associated genetic features, and clade-specific SNP patterns, (iii) evaluate the relationships among key resistance-associated SNPs, clade background, and antifungal resistance phenotypes, and (iv) contextualize these findings by integrating our dataset with publicly available genomes to explore the spatiotemporal patterns of dissemination in China and globally.

Methods

Clinical data and bacterial isolates collection

During the study period (August 2023 to October 2024), patients admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine) and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, School of Medicine, underwent systematic screening for C. auris. A total of 22 patients colonized or infected with C. auris were investigated at two hospitals in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. A total of 25 C. auris isolates were collected from these patients for further study. Clinical data were retrieved from electronic medical records.

Identification and in vitro antifungal susceptibility testing

All isolates were identified as C. auris using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Bruker Daltonics) with the IVD knowledge base (version V4.1, Bruker Daltonics), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The antifungal susceptibility of the C. auris isolates was determined using the broth microdilution method with the Sensititre YeastOne AST YO10 plate (TREK Diagnostic Systems, Cleveland, OH, USA), following manufacturers’ instructions. Quality control was performed in parallel using Candida parapsilosis (BNCC 263,246) and Candida albicans (BNCC 294,714) as control strains. The antifungal agents tested included 5-flucytosine, amphotericin B, fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, anidulafungin, micafungin, caspofungin, and posaconazole. Due to the lack of established clinical breakpoints for Candidozyma auris, MIC results were interpreted using the tentative breakpoints proposed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

Whole genome sequencing and genome assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the yeast Genomic DNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Megen Biotech Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For all 25 C. auris isolates, a paired-end library with an average insert size of 300 bp was prepared and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with 2 × 150-bp reads. The raw sequencing data were filtered and assembled by fastp v0.23.41 and SPAdes v4.0.02 [13,14]. The assembled contig sequences were evaluated by QUAST v5.0.23 [15]. Afterward, all genomes compared the average nucleotide identity (ANI) to identify the one certain C. auris strain, GCF_003013715.1, from the NCBI Refseq database using FastANI v1.344 to reidentify the isolates [16].

Bioinformatic analysis

To construct a representative dataset, we systematically reviewed published studies reporting C. auris isolates in China up to February 2025. All studies with available genome data or accessible sequencing information were considered. All genome assemblies were downloaded from public databases. In addition, some of the included Chinese studies incorporated representative international reference isolates for comparative genomic analyses. To maintain consistency with the original study designs, these publicly available genomes were also retained in our dataset as contextual reference strains, rather than being independently selected. After removing low-quality assemblies and duplicates, a total of 185 publicly available genomes were retained for further analysis. Together with the 25 isolates sequenced in this study, a total of 210 C. auris genomes were included for downstream analyses.

Therefore, 210 of the C. auris genome data were obtained for single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and spatiotemporal analysis. Basic genome assembly statistics (including number of contigs, genome size, and N50) were calculated using SeqKit and are summarized in Table S2. SNPs were identified using Snippy (v4.6.0), with GCF_003013715.1 (B11220) as the reference genome [17]. Variant calling in Snippy is implemented with FreeBayes, and we applied the following key thresholds: –mincov 10 (minimum coverage required to consider a site), –minqual 100 (minimum variant call quality), and the default allele fraction cutoff (–minfrac) as implemented in Snippy v4.6.0. The phylogenetic tree was constructed with FastTree v2.1 (default parameters) based on SNPs and visualized using the interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) [18,19].

Targeted analysis of resistance-associated genes. The genes ERG11, ERG2, ERG3, ERG6, FKS1, FKS2, CDR1, TAC1b, TAC1a, UPC2, MEC3, CIS2, SCF1, FUR1, and ALS4 were retrieved from the NCBI database and used to construct a custom Abricate reference database. Subsequently, Abricate (v1.0.1) was employed to screen these genes in the 25 C. auris isolates from this study to determine their presence [20]. Afterward, these genes were used as reference sequences for SNP analysis across a total of 210 C. auris strains. Copy number variations (CNVs) were assessed using custom Perl scripts. Subsequently, the detected genetic features were evaluated for their potential associations with clinical MIC data. Data visualization was performed in R v4.4.0.

Tracing the geographic spread and evolutionary dynamics of C. auris

Phylogeographic reconstruction and substitution rate estimation were conducted using BEAST v2.7.7 [21]. The information of date of sample collection for all 210 C. auris genomes was added via TipDates, while geographic data were added as discrete traits. The analysis employed a Coalescent Bayesian Skyline model with an HKY + Gamma substitution model under a strict molecular clock. The Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis was run for 100 million iterations, with the first 10% discarded as burn-in, and samples collected every 10,000 steps. Potential transmission routes across geographic locations were performed using SPREAD3 v0.9.7.1 and R v4.4.0 [22].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.0). Differences in SNP distributions between Clade I and Clade III, as well as associations between individual SNPs and antifungal resistance phenotypes, were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test, with odds ratios (ORs) reported as effect size estimates. p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Antifungal susceptibility phenotypes were binarized as resistant (1) or nonresistant (0). Additional stratified analyses were performed within Clade I and Clade III to reduce confounding by lineage background. SNP co-occurrence patterns were summarized using binary mutation matrices and visualized as heatmaps. For drugs showing nearly fixed resistance phenotypes within a clade, only descriptive comparisons were performed. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2. All tests were two-sided, and FDR-adjusted p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical identification and characteristics of Candidozyma auris

During the study period, 22 patients infected or colonized with Candidozyma auris were admitted to the ICU. As shown in Figure 1(A) and Table S1, the average age of these patients was 77.36 years (range: 48–91 years), with an equal distribution of males and females (50% each). The mean time from admission to isolation of C. auris was 76 days (range: 19–282 days). Among these patients, 25% had a history of antifungal therapy, while 75% did not. Additionally, 54.17% of the patients died, 12.5% showed improvement, and 33.33% were not improved. Among the patients, six (27.27%) developed C. auris candidemia, with C. auris isolated from blood or central venous catheters (CVP). Of these six patients, five died, while one was not improved (Figure 1(A)).

The antifungal susceptibility results of C. auris isolates were shown in Table 1. Based on the tentative MIC breakpoints proposed by the US CDC, all isolates exhibited resistance to fluconazole with MIC values ranging from 128 to >256 μg/ml. Additionally, 80% of the isolates were resistant to amphotericin B, with MIC values of 2–4 μg/ml. In contrast, all isolates remained fully susceptible to echinocandins, displaying pretty low MIC values (Table 1).

Assessment of genome assembly quality and ANI analysis

All genomes were successfully assembled using SPAdes, with an average of 54.4 contigs per sample and a GC content of 45.15% (Figure S1(A)). ANI analysis confirmed that all genome sequences showed over 99% similarity to the reference genome, validating the accurate identification of C. auris (Figure S1(A)). The average genome size of C. auris was estimated at 12.31 Mbp, with an N50 value of 0.6 Mbp (Figure S1(B)).

Phylogenetic analysis of C. auris

A total of 210 C. auris strains, including 25 isolates from this study, were used for phylogenetic analysis based on SNPs. The dataset comprised 185 C. auris isolates from China and 25 from other countries, with Beijing and Zhejiang representing the major sampling locations in China (Figure 1(B), Figure S1 (C)). Four clades were identified among the 210 strains, with Clade I being the dominant clade (Figure 2(A)). The 25 strains in this study were divided into two clades: 19 in Clade I and 6 in Clade III, consistent with the distribution of strains sourced in China, which were mostly grouped into Clade I and Clade III. Our results showed that the 19 strains of Clade I clustered with isolates from Beijing, China, while the 6 strains of Clade III grouped with those from Guangdong, China. These findings support the hypothesis that Clade III strains in this study likely originated from Guangdong, whereas Clade I strains were predominantly introduced from Beijing. Interestingly, even isolates were collected from different clinical sources (e.g. urine and blood) and at different times from the same patient, still belonged to the same Clade.

Additionally, the changing distribution of different clades over time and across various regions of China is detailed in Figure 2(B-D). We observed an increasing prevalence of C. auris starting in 2018, both for all strains and isolates specifically in China (Figure 2(B-C). Clade III strains were first reported in China and have maintained persistent circulation, whereas Clade I strains emerged around 2017 and rapidly spread in the following years (Figure 2(C)). These findings suggest that three major clades of C. auris are prevalent in China, with Clade I and Clade III being the dominant circulating lineages. Guangdong and Beijing were identified as the most affected regions, displaying more complex clade compositions that included all three clades. In contrast, all 25 isolates in this study belonged exclusively to either Clade I or Clade III. Other regions were found to harbor only one clade, either Clade I or Clade III (Figure 2(D)).

SNP-based clade differentiation and genotype – phenotype associations

A total of 3,619 SNPs were identified within antifungal resistance-associated genes across the 210 C. auris isolates. Several mutations showed clear clade specificity. ERG11 Y132F, a substitution at codon 132 associated with azole resistance, together with ERG2 E39D, CDR1 E709D, TAC1a E145V, TAC1b R39K, and TAC1b A407S were significantly enriched in Clade I. By contrast, Clade III was enriched for ERG11 alterations around codons 125–126, including VF125AL and F126L, which are also linked to reduced azole susceptibility (Figure 3(A)). At the sample level, these SNPs exhibited distinct distribution patterns between Clade I and Clade III, with limited within-clade heterogeneity (Figure 3(B)). Consistently, co-occurrence analysis showed that multiple resistance-associated mutations frequently clustered together, particularly among ERG11, ERG2, CDR1, and TAC1 variants (Figure 3(C)).

To further evaluate genotype – phenotype relationships, SNPs – antifungal susceptibility association analysis was performed using the 25 isolates with paired MIC data. Within Clade I, several SNPs, including ERG11 Y132F, ERG2 E39D, CDR1 E709D, TAC1a E145V, TAC1a N713K, and TAC1b R39K/A407S, showed consistent effect directions with amphotericin B resistance, although none remained statistically significant after multiple-testing correction (Figure 3(D), Figure S1D). Similarly, no significant SNP – phenotype association was observed in Clade III or in the overall mutation burden analysis, indicating that antifungal resistance phenotypes were not explained by mutation number alone. Together, these results demonstrate strong clade-associated SNP differentiation in resistance-related loci, whereas direct SNP – phenotype associations remained limited in the current MIC-characterized dataset.

Geographic spread and evolutionary dynamics of C. auris

To explore the genomic population structure and potential transmission patterns of C. auris, we analyzed the 210 genomic sequences in this study. To ensure the robustness of the inferred transmission routes, only those with a location probability greater than 0.8 were retained. Transmission routes were initially visualized using Spread3, followed by further refinement and customization using R to improve clarity and highlight key transmission patterns. Several potential transmission links were detected, including both intercontinental spread between foreign countries and potential transmission events originating from China. Notably, possible links were identified from regions such as Hong Kong, Anhui, and Liaoning to Alberta (Canada), Colombia, the United Arab Emirates, India, and California (USA), as shown in Figure 4(A). The data suggested both potential introductions into China and intra-national spread across different regions, reflecting dynamic cross-border and domestic dissemination patterns of C. auris. Notably, the major inbound transmission routes into China were traced from Canada to Guangdong, South Korea to Guangdong, and Pakistan to Beijing. Beijing and Guangdong appeared to be frequently involved in inferred transmission connections with other provinces (Figure 4(B)). Furthermore, strains from Zhejiang Province were associated with two distinct inferred transmission links potentially originating from Beijing and Guangdong. This pattern was broadly consistent with the phylogenetic clustering results, suggesting that Clade III strains were more commonly associated with Guangdong-related clusters, whereas Clade I strains were more frequently linked to Beijing-associated clusters. Overall, our findings suggest complex and dynamic dissemination patterns of C. auris across both international and domestic contexts.

Discussion

In the current study, we characterized 25 C. auris isolates collected from ICU patients in two tertiary hospitals in Hangzhou, East China, and investigated their transmission using spatiotemporal evolutionary analysis. Of these, six isolates were obtained from blood culture samples, while the remaining isolates were from superficial samples, including urine, sputum and skin swabs. Notably, except for two patients, all patients yielded multiple C. auris isolates. Consistent with previous studies, colonization was most commonly detected at superficial body sites, particularly the nares, axilla, and groin [23,24]. Notably, patients with bloodstream- or CVP-associated C. auris isolation showed a poor prognosis in our cohort.

According to Bing et al., a dramatic increase in C. auris infections was observed in China after the COVID-19 outbreak, with the highest incidence in Guangdong, Hong Kong, Liaoning, and Beijing [25]. To date, three phylogenetic Clades (Clade I, II and III) have been identified in China. Interestingly, the five isolates collected from SAHZJU Hospital all belonged to Clade III, whereas 19 isolates collected from the ZPHCM Hospital belonged to Clade I, with only one isolate assigned to Clade III. Fluconazole resistance is consistently observed among C. auris isolates across different geographic regions [25,26]. In the current study, all 25 C. auris isolates exhibited resistance to fluconazole, with MIC values ranging from 128 to >256 mg/L. Additionally, 64% (16/25) of the isolates were resistant to amphotericin B, a proportion higher than the 39.1% previously reported in China [25].

Notably, the 25 study isolates clustered into Clade I and III, consistent with the broader trend observed among Chinese isolates, which primarily belong to these two Clades [12,27]. Interestingly, our results revealed that the 19 Clade I isolates grouped closely with strains previously isolated from Beijing, while the six Clade III isolates clustered with strains from Guangdong, suggesting localized clonal expansion and potential regional transmission hotspots. Additionally, we observed that isolates from the same patient, even when collected at different times or anatomical sites, consistently belonged to the same Clade, indicating persistent colonization rather than reinfection with unrelated strains, which is consistent with previous findings [28]. Temporal patterns since 2018 also suggested an increasing burden of C. auris in China [29].

Fluconazole resistance in C. auris is commonly associated with hotspot mutations in ERG11, which encodes the azole target lanosterol demethylase. Our SNP analysis revealed strong clade-specific differentiation, with ERG11 Y132F enriched in Clade I and ERG11 VF125AL/F126L characteristic of Clade III isolates, consistent with their reported association with reduced azole susceptibility [30,31]. In addition, variants in ERG2, CDR1, TAC1a, and TAC1b also showed clade-biased distributions and frequent co-occurrence. Notably, TAC1b A407S and TAC1a N713K were commonly observed, consistent with the reported role of TAC1-related variation in azole resistance [32]. However, among the 25 isolates with paired MIC data, no individual SNP remained significantly associated with antifungal resistance after correction for multiple-testing, and mutation burden was not correlated with resistance phenotypes. These findings suggest that although resistance-associated SNPs are informative for clade differentiation, antifungal susceptibility in C. auris is likely shaped by additional mechanisms, including transcriptional regulation, combinatorial mutation effects, and efflux activity.

To investigate the spread of C. auris, we conducted a spatiotemporal reconstruction. Several inferred links connected Chinese regions with international locations, suggesting bidirectional exchange rather than isolated local spread. This pattern is consistent with previous genomic epidemiological studies showing global dissemination and phylogeographic mixing across major clades [7,33,34]. Our results indicated that Beijing and Guangdong were major hubs for C. auris dissemination in China, in line with previous studies underscoring the complexity of its spread [12]. Notably, isolates from Zhejiang Province appeared to converge from both sources, reflecting epidemiological connections with both regions. This spatial distribution pattern aligns with our phylogenetic analysis, which indicated that Clade I strains were more closely linked to transmission chains associated with Beijing, whereas Clade III strains were predominantly associated with Guangdong-related lineages. The potential role of China as both a source and a recipient of multiple introductions also underscores the importance of including regional genomic data in global surveillance frameworks.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Sampling bias, uneven geographic representation, and incomplete availability of public genomes may have affected the phylogenetic and phylogeographic analyses. In addition, inferred transmission links should be interpreted as potential rather than definitive. Finally, the relatively small cohort may limit the generalizability of the clinical and genomic findings.

Conclusion

In summary, our study characterizes the genomic and phenotypic features of C. auris isolates from ICU patients in Hangzhou within a broader national and global context. Chinese isolates were dominated by Clade I and Clade III and showed distinct resistance-associated SNP patterns with clear clustering and co-occurrence. Integration with public genomes revealed geographic and phylogenetic structure and suggested complex dissemination involving Beijing, Guangdong, and Zhejiang. Direct SNP – phenotype associations remained limited in the current MIC-characterized dataset. These findings highlight the need for continued genomic surveillance and strengthened infection control.
