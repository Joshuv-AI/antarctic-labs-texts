Abstract
Stream-based monitoring assesses the health of safety-critical systems by transforming input streams of sensor measurements into output streams that determine a verdict. These inputs are often treated as accurate representations of the physical state, although real sensors introduce calibration and measurement errors. Such errors propagate through the monitor’s computations and can distort the final verdict. Affine arithmetic with symbolic slack variables can track these errors precisely, but independent measurement noise introduces a fresh slack variable upon each measurement event, causing the monitor’s state representation to grow without bound over time. Therefore, any bounded-memory monitoring algorithm must unify slack variables at runtime in a way that generates a sound approximation. This paper introduces zonotopes as an abstract domain for online monitoring of RLola specifications. We demonstrate that zonotopes precisely capture the affine state of the monitor and that their over-approximation produces a sound bounded-memory monitor. We present a comparison of different zonotope over-approximation strategies in the context of runtime monitoring, evaluating their performance and false-positive rates.



1
Introduction

Stream-based runtime monitoring is a safety assurance mechanism for cyber-physical systems.
A monitor observes system behavior and checks it against a given specification.
In stream-based specifications, observations appear as input streams that capture sensor data or other system outputs.
Output streams are defined by equations that transform and combine these inputs to assess system health.
Because many cyber-physical systems are safety-critical, the precision of the monitor’s verdicts is essential.

Monitoring frameworks often assume that input values perfectly represent the physical system.
In practice, sensor measurements include calibration errors and noise.
As measurements accumulate in the monitor’s state, their inaccuracies can propagate through computations and affect the final verdict.

A simple way to model such inaccuracies is to lift stream values from scalars to intervals, as done in robust semantics for signal-temporal logic
[
30
]
.
However, interval arithmetic introduces the aliasing problem: subtracting an interval from itself yields an interval double the size of the error bound (e.g.,
[2,6]-[2,6]=[-4,4]
), producing overly pessimistic verdicts.

An alternative methodology to capture measurement noise explored previously uses symbolic slack variables to model interval-bounded measurement errors
[
14
,
15
]
.
There, slack variables symbolically represent the interval of
[-1,1]
and, using affine arithmetic, can also model the above interval as the affine form
x=4+2\epsilon
for a slack variable
\epsilon
.
As slack variables are treated symbolically, the computation of
x-x
indeed leads to the expected result of zero:
4+2\epsilon-(4+2\epsilon)=0
.

However, if the measurement errors are independent, such as those induced by per-sample random measurement noise, an independent (fresh) slack variable is required for each measurement.
As input measurements can accumulate in stream values indefinitely, the monitor’s memory requirement becomes trace-length dependent, as a new symbolic variable is memorized in each step.
Therefore, any trace-length independent runtime monitor must unify slack variables at runtime to keep its internal state from growing beyond any bound.

RLola
, an extension of Lola
[
13
]
, incorporates slack variables to cover interval-bounded noise
[
15
]
.
Prior work identifies a language fragment in which a bounded number of slack variables suffices.
This paper addresses the general case, where arbitrary
RLola
specifications may introduce an unbounded number of slack variables.
To ensure bounded memory, we introduce zonotopes as an abstract domain for the monitoring of
RLola
specifications.
The monitor computes a continuous over-approximation of its state using zonotopes, keeping memory usage independent of trace length.
Our approach is generic with respect to the chosen approximation strategy, and we compare several state-of-the-art zonotope over-approximation techniques to determine the best-suited for monitoring and to identify further need for research on zonotopic set approximation.

The Challenge.

The goal is to build a sound and precise runtime monitor for general

Since slack variable values cannot be resolved at runtime, the algorithm tracks them symbolically.
In the filter streams of

Thus, the algorithm must approximate its state to maintain bounded memory.

Monitoring Logics.

Runtime monitoring has been studied for multiple specification formalisms, including LTL
[

]
, STL

]
, and MTL
[

]

express temporal behavior through equations over discrete streams. However, these formalisms do not account for uncertainties in measurements.

Monitoring under Uncertainties.

The monitoring problem is exacerbated by the ubiquitous presence of inaccuracies and uncertainties in measuring environmental properties.
Previous work has addressed monitoring over unreliable channels
[
23
]
, with imprecise time-stamping
[
16
]
, and based on imprecise measurements
[
12
,
11
,
30
,
28
,
26
,
15
]
.
The models for measurement error vary, and most approaches opt for simple interval-bounded errors considered independent between measurement episodes
[
12
,
11
,
30
,
26
]
.
The more detailed error model of ISO EC 5725
[
21
]
, which decomposes the overall measurement error into an unknown, yet constant calibration error and a time-varying measurement noise, has been shown to provide more precise monitoring verdicts under uncertainty
[
28
]
, yet so far is rarely supported by verified approaches to monitoring
[
28
,
15
,
16
]
.

Stream-based Monitoring under Uncertainties.

Prior stream-based monitoring work addresses missing or shifted inputs
[
26
]
.
RLola

[
15
]
assumes all inputs being present but noisy. That work considers only a fragment of
RLola
with constant-size monitoring state, whereas this paper considers general specifications. Kallwies et al.
[
22
]
model missing events as symbolic variables and show that monitoring then requires unbounded memory; they approximate the state using interval hulls, which can be overly conservative. More recently, an abstract-interpretation framework for Lola was introduced in
[
19
]
, which handles temporally uncorrelated errors.
The key difference to our work, aside from the choice of abstract domain and our handling of both uncorrelated and correlated errors as in ISO EC 5725
[
21
]
, is that our abstraction is induced by two subsequent operations: the operational semantics first computes a precise symbolic representation of the relational semantics, similar to
[
19
]
, and then this symbolic state is further abstracted to a zonotope for safe and adaptive over-approximation.

Approximating Zonotopes.

Zonotopes are widely used in reachability analysis
[
1
,
2
]
. Unlike reachability tools, monitors continuously incorporate new measurements that refine knowledge of the current state. State-bounded observers
[
8
]
are closer in spirit but assume linear dynamics and no conditionals. These approaches also require zonotope approximations to remain efficient, providing several methods applicable to our setting
[
25
,
8
]
.

3
Approximate Online Monitoring of
RLola

This section presents an online monitoring algorithm for
RLola
.
A major challenge is ensuring bounded memory, as an online monitor must run indefinitely alongside the system.
Semantically, a monitor computes the set
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
for the measurements
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
, and trigger conditions are evaluated over this set using the predicates introduced above.
Efficiently representing this set is difficult because it is uncountable in general due to the nondeterministic choice of slack variable values.
The algorithm addresses this by constructing a symbolic representation of the state space, where slack variable choices are encoded as symbolic variables.

3.1
Symbolic Semantics

We assume two disjoint infinite sets of symbolic variables:
\Sigma_{c}

The function

The symbolic domain is defined as affine forms

over variables in

\mathit{Affine}_{\Sigma}\triangleq c+\sum_{\varepsilon\in\Sigma}\mu_{\varepsilon}*\varepsilon

where

The right-hand side of the equation is purely syntactic:
The arithmetic operators are only ever evaluated once an interpretation for the symbolic variables is given.
Affine forms symbolically represent an interval in

Arithmetic operations applied to affine forms follow the standard rules for affine arithmetic

The symbolic semantics for

\mathit{Stream}\triangleq\mathit{Affine}^{\mathbb{N}}\cup\ \mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{N}}

3.2
Symbolic Algorithm

We present an algorithm that computes a symbolic model for a specification
S
and input measurements
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
.
We assume that
S
is well-formed, so
\widehat{\llbracket S\rrbracket}{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
contains exactly one model.
The algorithm follows the structure of the Lola monitoring algorithm
[
13
]
and maintains two stores: the resolved store
R
, which contains equations of the form
x_{i}=c+\sum_{\varepsilon\in\Sigma}\mu_{\varepsilon}\cdot\varepsilon
, and the unresolved store
U
, which contains equations of the form
x_{i}=e_{x}
.
The algorithm is given below.

Symbolic Evaluation Algorithm.

Initially, let
U
and
R
be the empty set, and
C
be a map from constant slack variables to symbolic variables defined as follows:

C=\{\ (\Delta,\mathit{fresh}(\Sigma_{c}))\mid\Delta\in\mathbb{V}_{\mathit{c}}\ \}

For each input from
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
that becomes available at time
0\leq t\leq\mathbb{N}
, the following steps are performed:

1.

Record the input streams as resolved:
R=R\cup\{\ i_{t}={\rho_{\textit{in}}}(i)(t)\mid i\in\mathbb{V}_{\mathit{in}}\ \}

2.

Resolve the constant slack variables:
R=R\cup\{\ \Delta_{t}=C(\Delta)\mid\Delta\in\mathbb{V}_{\mathit{c}}\ \}

3.

Draw symbolic variables for slack-variable streams:

R=R\cup\{\ \varepsilon_{t}=\mathit{fresh}(\Sigma_{s})\mid\varepsilon\in\mathbb{V}_{\mathit{s}}\ \}

4.

Add the defining equations for output streams to the unresolved store:

U=U\cup\{\ o_{t}=e\mid{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{output}}}\ o\ {\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{:=}}}\ e\in S\ \}

5.

Simplify the equations in
U
as much as possible using the equations in
U\cup R
.
If an equation becomes of the form
o_{t}=c+\sum_{\varepsilon\in\Sigma}\mu_{\varepsilon}*\varepsilon
remove it from
U
and add it to
R
.

6.

Evaluate trigger conditions based on
R
and report the violations.

7.

Prune stale equations from
R
: Remove equations
x_{j}
for
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
j<t-1
.

The expressions of the equations in
U
at time
t
are rewritten according to the following rules:

•

Replace
x\in\mathbb{V}
with
x_{i}

•

Rewrite
e_{1}\ {\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{+}}}\ e_{2}
using affine arithmetic, if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are fully resolved.

•

Rewrite
x{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{.{prev}(}}}e{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{)}}}
with
x_{i-1}
if
t>0
or with
e
otherwise.

•

Rewrite
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{if}}}\ e_{b}\ {\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{then}}}\ e_{c}\ {\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{else}}}\ e_{a}
to
e_{c}
if
e_{b}=\top
or else to
e_{a}
.

The algorithm computes symbolic stream values step-by-step, using only values from the previous step, as
RLola
streams may only reference values from the immediately preceding step.
Older equations are removed from
R
in step 7.
Steps 1–4 correctly implement the semantics of equations, and correctness follows because the rewriting rules in step 5 match the expression semantics.

3.3
Zonotope Approximation

Although the algorithm represents
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
exactly, it may require unbounded memory: the number of symbolic variables in the affine forms in
R
can grow without limit as demonstrated in
Section

1.1
.
To address this, we approximate
R
after each iteration using zonotope over-approximations.

As described in previous work about affine arithmetic
[
10
]
, the joint domain of multiple affine forms constitutes a zonotope.
Hence, all affine forms in the resolved set
R
form a zonotope that represents the current monitoring state.
A zonotope
Z(c,G)
is a symmetric convex set with center
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
of dimension
d
with
k
spanning vectors:

Z(c,G)\triangleq\{\ c+G\cdot\overline{\varepsilon}\mid\overline{\varepsilon}\in[-1,1]^{d}\ \}

A two-dimensional example is shown in
Figure

2
.
In our setting, the dimension of the zonotope equals the number of equations, and the number of generators equals the number of symbolic variables in
R
.

To align equations in
R
with rows of a zonotope, we fix an arbitrary order
\leq
over stream variables
\mathbb{V}
and list them as
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
.
A bijection
\alpha
from lists of affine forms to zonotopes of the same dimension is then defined by mapping each affine form to its center and coefficient vector
[
10
]
:

\alpha(R)=Z(c,G)\qquad c_{i}=\hat{c}(R(x_{i}))\qquad G_{i,\cdot}=\hat{\mu}(R(x_{i}))

Where
i
is a row index and
\hat{c}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
return the center and coefficients of the affine form.
The inverse
\alpha^{-1}=\beta
retrieves the affine forms in the expected way.

We extend the symbolic evaluation algorithm with step eight, which approximates
R
via zonotopes.
Let
\mathit{size}(Z(c,G))
be the number of generators, i.e., the number of non-zero columns of
G
. We add:

8.

If
\mathit{size(\alpha(R))}>k
, over-approximate
R
as
R^{k}=\beta(A^{k}(\alpha(R)))

Here
k
is the bound on the number of symbolic variables, and
A^{k}
is a function that over-approximates a zonotope with one having at most
k
generators but the same center.

We reason about the soundness and completeness of the algorithm with respect to trigger conditions.
Ternary predicates are evaluated over
R
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket}_{R}

\displaystyle\triangleq(\mathit{max}(R(x))-v)/(\mathit{max}(R(x))-\mathit{min}(R(x))>p

Theorem 3.1
(Correctness)

Let
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
be a series of measurements and
R
be the resolved set computed by the evaluation algorithm at time
t
for
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle p\leq 0.5\land\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}^{t}

\displaystyle\implies\widehat{\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket}_{R}

\displaystyle p\leq 0.5\land\llbracket x<_{p}v\rrbracket_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}^{t}

\displaystyle\implies\widehat{\llbracket x<_{p}v\rrbracket}_{R}

\displaystyle p\geq 0.5\land\widehat{\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket}_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}^{t}

\displaystyle\implies\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket_{R}

\displaystyle p\geq 0.5\land\widehat{\llbracket x<_{p}v)\rrbracket}_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}^{t}

\displaystyle\implies\llbracket x<_{p}v\rrbracket_{R}

To prove this theorem, let
u
and
l
be the upper and lower bound for
x
computed by the maximum and minimum operators when evaluating
\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket_{\rho_{\textit{in}}}^{t}
. Let
\hat{u}
and
\hat{l}
be the bounds for
\widehat{\llbracket x>_{p}v\rrbracket}_{R}
.
Assume the over-approximation of
R
widened these bound by a value
\eta>0
.
Because the non-approximative algorithm is correct, and the approximation is a center-preserving it holds that:
\hat{l}=l-\eta
and
\hat{u}=u+\eta
.
For
p\in[0,0.5]
follows
(u-v)/(u-l)>p\Rightarrow(\hat{u}-v)/(\hat{u}-\hat{l})>p
.
For
p\in[0.5,1]
follows
(u-v)/(u-l)>p\Leftarrow(\hat{u}-v)/(\hat{u}-\hat{l})>p
.

Theorem

3.1
means that the algorithm is sound for any
p\leq 0.5
, i.e., that every violation reported according to the non-symbolic semantics, are also reported by the algorithm.
For
p\geq 0.5
, the algorithm is complete, i.e., every violation reported by the algorithm is indeed a violation according to the semantics.
For
p=0.5
the algorithm is sound and complete.
These properties are direct consequences of the point-wise symmetry of the over-approximation.

4
Evaluation

We evaluate the approach on two example systems, from which several benchmark traces are derived: the kinematically confined robot used as a motivating example in
Section

1.1
and a more realistic variant thereof, where the robot measures speed and direction independently.
Based on the benchmark traces, we quantify the monitor’s over-approximation error for several zonotope approximation methods by computing both the distance between over-approximated and exact zonotopes and the false-positive rates induced by the over-approxmations.

To measure loss of precision, we compare zonotopes using a distance metric.
Prior work typically uses volume differences or the Euclidean Hausdorff distance between zonotopes
[
25
,
27
]
, but these metrics do not adequately reflect monitor accuracy.
As trigger conditions represent axis-parallel half-spaces, the Manhattan Hausdorff set distance accurately reflects precision w.r.t. trigger conditions.
The metric used in the experiments thus is the (mean squared) error of the dimension-wise differences between interval hulls of zonotopes.
This choice matches the evaluation of trigger conditions, which depend only on the extrema of the affine forms along main axes of the coordinate system.

5
Conclusion

We presented an approximate online monitoring algorithm for general
RLola
specifications.
The algorithm maintains a symbolic representation of the infinite solution space, but symbolic variables can accumulate without bound.
To ensure trace-length independent memory, we extended the algorithm with zonotope-based over-approximation methods drawn from the literature.
We evaluated the approach on two examples and measured the resulting over-approximation error using a domain-specific metric.
The results show that the choice of approximation strategy has a substantial effect on monitor precision, though careful specification design can mitigate this impact.

An open question is whether approximation strategies tailored specifically to runtime monitoring can outperform existing methods because the approximation problem in monitoring differs from, for example, state estimation.
Our preliminary analysis reveals that the origin of the invariably large overapproximation is twofold:
First, all existing overapproximation methods for zonotopes aim at optimizing the accuracy of the zonotopic set enclosure for the current time step only, yet disregard the future operations to be applied to this enclosure in subsequent state-extrapolation steps. Stringent control of approximation error obviously would have to reflect the latter, as state extrapolation induces inhomogeneous error propagation along different directions in the state space.
Second,
existing methods treat the calibration error and the measurement error per-sample equivalently when reducing the number of zonotope spanning vectors, although the former error will enter repeatedly in the future; this factually destroys the gains in accuracy achieved from distinguishing these two types of measurement error
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
by introducing aliasing.
These findings indicate the need for further research on adequate approximation methods for zonotopes, which will be the subject of a companion article.

0.A.1
Correctness of the Symbolic Semantics

Before addressing the correctness of the symbolic semantics, we highlight two sources of non-determinism in
RLola
specifications that yield multiple models in the non-symbolic semantics.
First, the non-deterministic choice of slack variable values leads to multiple models consistent with a measurement series
{\rho_{\textit{in}}}
.
Second, a specification may contain self-referential equations such as
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{output}}}\ x\ {\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\textbf{{:=}}}\ x
, which are satisfied by any value of
x
and thus introduce further non-determinism.
As in Lola
[
13
]
, such equations are excluded by a well-formedness criterion to ensure determinism up to measurement noise.

The correctness lemma follows from how the symbolic semantics resolves the specification’s non-determinism during concretization.
First, the existential quantification in
Equation

1
fixes the measurement noise by assigning values to symbolic variables.
In the symbolic semantics, constant slack definitions yield the same symbolic variable at all time steps, and slack-variable streams introduce a fresh symbolic variable per step, matching the quantifier behaviour in the non-symbolic semantics.
Second,
Equation

2
resolves the non-determinism of self-referential streams by taking the union over the concretizations of all symbolic models.
