Nanomedicine-Based Strategies Targeting Macrophage Polarization in Breast Cancer Bone Metastasis: An Updated Review
Authors: Hong Wang, Rui Tian, Jian Li, Wenjing Liu, Jilin Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presence of bone metastasis represents one of the common and toughest complications of breast cancer that significantly increases the morbidity rate. Currently available therapies for treating breast cancer have been able to ameliorate symptoms, but still suffer from insufficient accumulation of the drugs in bone metastatic sites and severe side effects on the whole body. The use of drug delivery systems based on nanomedicine has been demonstrated to be a promising approach in improving drug accumulation and pharmacodynamics of drugs in pre-clinical models. Engineered nanoparticles could be tailored in order to improve accumulation in the tumor environment and provide the delivery of drugs to bone metastatic regions. Moreover, recent advances in nanotechnology have provided many new options for imaging and therapeutic experiments in metastatic cancers. Modulation of tumor‑associated macrophage (TAM) polarization represents one of the recently described strategies since M2-like phenotype contributes to tumor growth, immunosuppression and metastasis development, while induction of M1-like phenotype has been proven to be effective in enhancing anti-tumor immune response in pre-clinical models. In this review, we discuss recent advances in the nanomedicine approach that targets TAM polarization in breast cancer bone metastasis, the rationale of the proposed strategies, therapeutic potential and limitations.Keywords: bone metastasis, targeted drug delivery, tumor-associated macrophages, chemotherapeutic nanocarriers

Introduction

Cancer is one of the most devastating diseases worldwide and a major cause of morbidity and mortality, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies capable of improving treatment efficacy and patient outcomes.1–5 Among different types of cancers, breast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent type of cancer in women, accounting for over 685,000 deaths and despite significant improvement in survival rates over the last 20 years, its incidence is rising globally.6–9 Mammography screening and adjuvant treatments have contributed significantly to improve outcomes, but systemic treatments for advanced illness have most significant influence. The incidence of breast cancer is influenced by both hereditary and non-genetic variables. The latter comprises radiation exposure, high mammographic density, lifestyle factors (post-menopausal obesity and alcohol consumption), age, reproductive risk factors (late menopause and early menarche), exogenous female hormones, and presence of histologic lesions like atypical hyperplasia.9–11

Breast tumors are categorized by types and stages. Specialists distinguish five distinct stages of BC and two classifications (invasive and noninvasive).12,13 Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), a noninvasive stage 0 malignancy where cells have not penetrated the basement membrane, accounts for about 16% of all BC diagnoses. For DCIS cases, 5-year survival rate is nearly 100%.14,15 Invasive BC accounts for the remaining occurrences, where cancer cells have migrated toward arterial and lymphatics after escaping anatomically defined ductal structure and spreading throughout breast tissue. The main tumor’s size and degree of its local and regional dissemination (including lymph nodes) determine its stages from 1 to 3. Stage 4 or metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is characterized by spread of cancer cells to distant organs (brain, liver, lung, and bones).12 Bone is one of the most common sites of distant metastasis in patients with advanced breast cancer and is the major contributor to cancer morbidity because of skeletal-related events such as pathological fracture, spinal cord compression, hypercalcemia, and painful bone metastases.16–18 In about 65–75% of patients with metastatic breast cancer, bone metastasis occurs in the end stage.16 However, the risk of bone metastasis varies widely depending on molecular subtypes of breast cancer. Patients with HR+/HER2− tumors have the highest risk of bone metastasis, while HER2+ disease shows an intermediate risk. The risk of bone metastasis in triple negative breast cancer is lower than that in other diseases since they tend to metastasize to visceral organs rather than to bones.17,19 Bone metastasis can happen many years after treating the primary tumor as disseminated tumor cells survive in a latent state in the bone marrow microenvironment.16,17,19

Because of its high death rate (less than 30% 5-year survival), MBC is clinically challenging and critical.20,21 Up to 90% of mortality linked to BC occur in MBC patients.22 MBC is a heterogeneous condition, hence its distant organ spread, and the difficulty of treating concurrent MBC to primary BC versus refractory MBC following several rounds of prior treatments.23–25 Most women do not benefit from current treatments for MBC, which instead prevent or delay the disease’s progression or offer palliative care by lowering pain and other comorbidities.26 Developments in multidisciplinary fields like proteomics and nanotechnology have transformed the potential for early cancer detection and therapy.27–29 Nanomedicine has shown great potential in effectiveness of medical care.30 Nanoparticles (NPs) have importance in MBC monitoring, diagnosis, and therapy.31 When it comes to detecting indicators in diagnostic scenario through proteomics and circulating genome fragments, NPs platforms provide far more capability than existing clinical techniques. Additionally, as demonstrated by the FDA-approved albumin-bound paclitaxel NPs (Abraxene) and PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin NPs (CAELYX), they provide significant versatility and loading capacity of payloads in therapeutic setting in comparison to current clinical approaches.32

There have been several independent studies in recent years related to tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in breast cancer, bone-targeted delivery of drugs, nanomedicine in cancer treatment, and shortcomings of EPR-mediated nanoparticle accumulation. Nevertheless, these subjects are often discussed separately without any consideration of the relationship between macrophage polarization and the peculiarities of the bone metastatic microenvironment. Furthermore, the most recent developments in macrophage-targeted nanomedicine, such as immune-modulating nanoparticles, biomimetic delivery systems, and advanced nanoplatforms for bone immune niche remodeling, have not yet been comprehensively integrated with a specific focus on macrophage polarization within the bone metastatic immune microenvironment and its translational implications for breast cancer bone metastasis.33–36

In this regard, the present review aims to synthesize current knowledge on the mutual interplay between tumor-associated macrophages and bone metastatic niche and critically analyze emerging macrophage-polarization modulating strategies via nanomedicine from a biological and translational perspective.

Mechanisms and Pathophysiology of Breast Cancer Bone Metastasis

Initiation of Metastasis and the Seed–Soil Concept

Individuals with BC, metastasis to key organs is the primary cause of death.37 According to Paget’s 1889 hybrid hypothesis “seed and soil” bone metastasis results from interactions of metastatic cancer cells and bone microenvironments.38 The process of solid tumor metastasis involves cell invasion from initial tumor, infiltration and extravasation into circulatory system.39 Tumor cells reach distant organs are primed for colonization by pre-metastatic niche, locally nurturing and receptive microenvironment that facilitates tumor settling and metastasis.40 While majority of tumor cells that circulate are identified and destroyed by the immune system, some cancer cells manage to escape and infiltrate to distant organs. Disseminated tumor cells are circulating tumor cells that have colonized the bone microenvironment.41

Disseminated Tumor Cells and Organotropic Spread in Breast Cancer

Certain BC subtypes have preferred organs for metastasis; triple-negative BC usually spreads aggressively to internal organs, while estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) BC is more likely to move to bones.42 The bone microenvironment provides favorable environment for tumor cell adhesion and growth due to pronounced vascularization and presence of stromal cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts which release growth factors and prostaglandins. Once cancer cells reach skeletal system, they establish themselves within supportive niche which sustains their survival and proliferation.43

Bone Microenvironment (Supportive Niche for Tumor Colonization)

Bone tissue is given preference in metastases because of discontinuous endothelium of bone sinuses, which facilitates the movement of hematopoietic and other cells as well as interactions between tumor and circulatory system.44 Adhesion receptors on endothelial cells in bone marrow capillaries and comparatively slow blood flow through the bone marrow support the placement of cancer cells in the bone. The bone marrow microenvironment, abundance of cytokines and growth factors, creates favorable conditions that facilitate the development of bone metastases.45 Eighty percent of patients suffering from advanced BC have bone metastases, which can result in a number of bone-related issues such hypocalcemia, pathologic fractures, and nerve compression.46 Bone metastases cause pain, exhaustion, and skeletal-related events (SREs), and they substantially lower overall survival.47 SREs brought on by bone metastases are linked to excruciating side effects that negatively affect a patient’s mobility, capacity to carry out everyday duties, and mental health.48 By promoting tumor growth and osteoclast activity, cancer cells involve feed-forward loop of osteoblasts activity resulting in bone metastasis.49 Osteoblasts use growth factors like sclerostin, osteoprotegerin, and receptor activator of nuclear factor-κβ ligand (RANKL) to control the initial osteogenesis brought on by cancer cells and the subsequent creation of osteoclasts.50 The most common type of BCBM is characterized by osteolytic metastases, which are aberrant bone breakdown caused by increased osteoclast-mediated bone resorption as depicted in Figure 1.51

BCBM is a complicated process that involves interactions between osteoblasts that interfere with bone remodeling and bone macrophages that phagocytose cancer cells.52 The internal cavities of calcified bone tissue matrix consist of well-vascularized bone marrow. At first settling close to bone endosteal surface, diffused metastatic cancer cells interact with different bone marrow cells to develop micrometastatic colonies, also known as metastatic ecotopes.53 Dynamic osteo-microenvironment consists of stromal cells, adipocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, hematological and immunological proteins, osteoblasts, osteoclasts and their precursors, and the extracellular matrix, which contains several growth and signaling molecules. Growth factors, chemokines, slow blood flow, and mechanical characteristics all encourage the growth of tumor cells in bones.53 Sinusoidal blood capillaries in bone marrow help tumor cells to get into the bloodstream. The bone wall niche is where tumor cells travel, proliferate, and develop into dormant cancer cells.54

Osteolytic Mechanisms Cycle of Bone Destruction

Even though the vicious cycle osteolysis model has become widely recognized as the leading concept in the pathogenesis of breast cancer bone metastasis, the molecular interactions involved are extremely dynamic and include a large number of communications between the metastasizing tumor cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, stromal cells, immune cells, and bone extracellular matrix components. While much of the currently available mechanistic knowledge is based on in vitro studies and animal experiments, the involvement of different signaling pathways in human bone metastasis is still context-dependent and may differ between tumor subtypes and stages and the bone microenvironment characteristics. Therefore, the mechanisms outlined below can be regarded as pathway examples, based mostly on preclinical data.17,55,56

Experimental studies suggest that breast cancer-derived parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) can alter the balance of the RANKL/osteoprotegerin signaling axis by increasing RANKL expression and reducing osteoprotegerin production by osteoblast-lineage and stromal cells.17,55,57,58 Trophic elements and other factors, such as transforming growth factor-β, VEGF, insulin-like growth factor, bone morphogenic proteins, and calcium, are produced by both osteoblasts and osteoclasts. These elements encourage tumors to grow externally, which sets off a harmful series of events that makes it easier for tumors to spread through skeleton.59,60 Osteoclastogenesis is negatively regulated by osteoprotegerin, released by osteoblasts. Release of interleukin-6 during bone degradation, leads to elevation in Jagged1 expression in BCBM which triggers Notch signaling pathway in osteoblasts, initiating proliferation of cancer cells.61 The evolution of BCBM is significantly accelerated by the “vicious cycle”, resulting in life-threatening events that significantly lower patient survival and quality of life. By generating C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), cancer-related fibroblasts in the tumor stroma aid in the growth of tumor cells in bones.62 Overexpression of CXCR4 and CXCR7 in BC cells causes CXCL12 gradient chemotaxis, which promotes bone colonization.63 Higher incidence of bone metastases is linked to elevated CXCR4 expression in BC.64 The CXCL12–CXCR4 axis promotes bone metastasis through cancer cells to secrete osteoclastogenic substances.65 For the treatment of metastatic bone cancers, targeted medicines that target this signaling axis may be clinically significant. Inhibiting the development of cancer cells and inhibiting osteoclast activity are the two primary approaches now employed to treat bone metastases. Despite the fact that a number of recently approved medications might alleviate bone-related issues, drug resistance keeps the fatality rate extremely high.66

Challenges in Drug Design and Delivery for BCBM

Limitations of Current Therapeutic Approaches

The main goals of current BCBM therapy are to control pain, treat or lower the risk of SREs, and stop the growth of the tumor.65 The main therapeutic approaches for breast cancer include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, biotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.60 Surgery improves isolated bone metastases and prevents pathological fractures.67

Anatomical and Physiological Barriers to Effective Therapy

The decreased local blood flow and bone sinus structure limit the therapeutic efficacy of chemotherapeutics against bone metastases by preventing local drug accumulation after systemic administration.68 In severe cases, increasing dose to reach effective levels in bone can lead to intolerable toxicity.69 Unique microenvironment of bone characterized by dense extracellular matrix composed of collagen and noncollagenous proteins also acts as barrier to drug penetration and distribution, thereby reducing local uptake of therapeutic agents within metastatic regions.70,71

Therapy-Induced Adaptive Resistance Mechanisms

Adaptive resistance to therapies for breast cancer bone metastases is a multifaceted and dynamic phenomenon driven by interplay between cancer cells, immune cells, stroma, and the bone microenvironment.72,73 While multiple signaling pathways have been proposed to play roles in adaptive response, many of these mechanisms have been discovered based on observations in in vitro studies and animal models. These mechanisms should be therefore considered context-specific adaptive responses rather than general causative pathways of therapy resistance. The type of chemotherapy, dose, molecular subtype of the tumor, order of treatment, and microenvironment of the metastatic site might impact on the resistance mechanisms developed.72,74,75 Oxidative stress pathways, inflammation, Notch pathway activation, metabolism, and immune microenvironment changes might all together contribute to therapy resistance.76–79

According to preclinical investigations, chemotherapy-induced oxidative stress has an impact on adaptive signaling pathways such as Jagged1/Notch signaling, which is associated with survival and resistance to treatment in breast cancer models. ROS modulates Jagged1/Notch signaling and may contribute to therapeutic resistance in a context-dependent manner.80–84 Furthermore, prolonged usage of anti-resorptive drugs results in serious side effects like renal failure and jaw osteonecrosis.68 Systemic drug delivery systems (DDSs) must penetrate the blood–bone marrow barrier, including the bone marrow’s sinusoidal capillary fissures, which have a diameter of about 80–100 nm, in order to reach bone or mineralized tissue.85 Nanomaterial-based DDSs can improve drug delivery by overcoming biological barriers and acknowledged as treatment alternative for various cancer types.86 In order to better treat bone metastases, new therapeutic strategies employ NPs for direct medication delivery to secondary bone tumor sites.

Origin and Diversity of Bone Marrow Macrophages

Macrophages from bone marrow are derived through both circulating CD14þ monocytes and embryonic progenitors.87 Tissue Macs can multiply in situ, avoiding differentiation from freshly recruited monocytes.88 Macs respond to environmental stimuli by changing polarization status, and they carry out variety of physiological processes, including phagocytosis, antigen presentation, wound healing, immunological control, tissue vascularization, and inflammation.89 Macrophage polarization spans intermediate states, ranging from classically activated M1 macrophages to alternatively activated M2 macrophages.90,91 As the process of macrophage polarization has been historically explained through M1/M2 terminology, growing data suggests that macrophage polarization actually follows a continuous and highly dynamic scale instead of being divided into two different phenotypes. The M1/M2 terminology is still used to describe certain functional features of the macrophages: thus, M1-like cells tend to be involved in the processes of inflammation and antitumor response, while M2-like cells are usually involved in tissue remodeling, immunosuppression, and tumor development. At the same time, TAM residing in the microenvironment of breast cancer often possess certain transcriptional and functional profiles which do not fit either category.92,93 For instance, single cell RNA-sequencing analysis has shown that TAMs are highly heterogeneous and have multiple different phenotypes with various combinations of inflammatory, immunoregulatory, angiogenic, and tissue remodeling gene programs. Thus, the approach to control the function of TAMs should be viewed as an attempt to reprogram the macrophage state and its function or its interactions with other immune cells, rather than changing the macrophage from M2 phenotype to M1 one. This perspective is especially important when dealing with nanomedicines, as the ability to modulate macrophage activity depends on the nanoparticle properties, cargo, tissue, and disease.93,94 Human M2 macrophages are further classified into three subtypes: M2a, M2b, and M2c, with M2c being the most immunosuppressive. A more precise nomenclature has recently been suggested to define in vitro–differentiated macrophages based on their activation stimuli, such as M(IFN-γ), M(IL-4), M(IL-10), or M(IFN-γþLPS).87 Additionally, bone marrow–resident macrophages, known as osteomacs, are found on the endosteal and periosteal surfaces, forming canopy-like structures above osteoblasts.95 Osteoclasts are produced when multiple myeloid osteoclast precursors fuse together.96 About 17% of bone marrow cells are osteomacs, which are distinguished from osteoclasts by their expression of CD68 and F4/80. Furthermore, osteomacs are crucial for maintaining the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) niche and bone.95

Osteomacs and the Challenge of Selective Macrophage Targeting in Bone Metastasis

Although tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent important therapeutic targets in breast cancer bone metastasis, it is essential to distinguish these pathological macrophage populations from bone-resident macrophages (osteomacs). Osteomacs are tissue-resident macrophages, specifically localized next to osteoblasts and bone lining cells, and they are responsible for osteoblast function, bone formation, matrix remodeling, fracture repair, and overall bone homeostasis. Depletion of osteomacs in animal experiments has been shown to inhibit osteoblast function and reduce bone formation.97,98

In the context of breast cancer bone metastasis, there is a significant modification of macrophage function in the bone microenvironment following stimulation by the tumor, inflammatory cytokines, and osteolytic signals. Tumor-educated macrophages often develop immunosuppressive, angiogenic, and tissue remolding properties to assist the process of metastasis formation, osteolysis, and immune escape. Nevertheless, given the similarity between osteomacs and metastatic TAMs with respect to the existence in the bone microenvironment and phenotypic properties, the use of therapeutic approaches that would deplete or reprogram macrophages might be harmful.17,98,99

Consequently, future treatments in nanomedicine should go past indiscriminate targeting of macrophages and rather be based on modulation of subtypes of macrophages in the specific environment of metastatic bones. Ligand-mediated targeting, biomimetic nano-vectors, and acid-responsive, reactive-oxygen-species-responsive, or protease-responsive nanoparticles can provide better selectivity for pathologic TAMs without compromising the physiology of osteomacs responsible for bone homeostasis. However, at present, there is not enough evidence that shows selective targeting of TAMs as opposed to osteomacs, and maintaining the physiology of bone-residing macrophages is of high importance during the clinical implementation of such nanomedicines.33,34,99

Tumor-Associated Macrophages (TAMs) in Breast Cancer

TAMs make up 5–40% of the tumor bulk in initial breast tumors.100 There is correlation between low M1/M2 ratio or the amount of TAMs and poor disease prognosis, and TAMs frequently resemble M2 Macs101 and have been associated with positive outcomes for the cervix, lungs, pancreas, stomach, colon, and prostate cancer, according to some studies. However, the M1/M2 ratio or the positioning of the TAMs may account for these favourable outcomes.101 CSF-1/CSF-1R and CCL2/CCR2 signaling pathways affect TAM infiltration and M2 polarization by disrupting monocyte chemotaxis and differentiation in TME. Downstreaming signal cascades PI3K/Akt and ERK1/2 for macrophage survival and immunosuppressive activity are also inhibited by inhibition of these pathways.102 The metastatic cascade is a series of actions taken by cancer cells in order to move to the bones. They produce new lymphatic and blood vessels, decompose surrounding tissue, enter the bloodstream, pass through it, exit it to reach other regions, and then either remain dormant or begin to grow once more. Bone metastases form when cancer cells begin to multiply at their new site.103–106

Role of TAMs in Bone Metastasis

Primary breast cancer cells release various cytokines and growth factors into surrounding microenvironment and circulation. Several variables recruit and differentiate macrophages, including VCAM-1, M-CSF, and MCP-1. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that breast cancer cells create the conditions for distant metastases even before CTCs ever reach the possible metastatic site.107 Among the elements required for formation of premetastatic niche include S100 proteins, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGFs), fibronectin, and lysyl oxidase (LOX).107,108 These factors induce matrix remodelling to attract bone marrow-derived cells (Macs) and aid Disseminated Tumor Cells (DTCs) colonization of the new site by offering “foot-holds” (pre-metastatic niche expression of integrins and adhesion molecules) and “trails” (chemotaxis of CTCs by secreted products). Characterized signalling pathways of interaction between breast cancer cells and TAM have been identified as the EGF (epidermal growth factor, TAM derived), EGFR (EGF receptor, cancer cell expressed), and CSF-1 (cancer cell derived) and CSF1R (TAM expressed) axes. Both are known to influence the early metastatic cascade phases of breast cancer cells, like intravasation, invasion, and co-migration of cancer cells with TAMs.109 According to a study targeting premetastatic bone lesions that used intracardiacal injections of LOX-silenced 4T1 cells, LOX contributed to osteoblast suppression and osteoclastogenesis activation, which facilitated tumor cell colonization of bone.108 Again, it would seem reasonable to infer that LOX also affects osteomacs and TAMs in the primary and bone metastases sites.

Active and Passive Targeting Strategies

Passive Targeting

It primarily relies on enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, wherein nanoparticles accumulate due to unique pathophysiological characteristics of tumor microenvironment (Figure 2).110 Leaky vasculature, defective endothelial lining, and impaired lymphatic drainage create favourable environment for macromolecules and nanoparticles to extravasate and remain retained within the tumor interstitium. Passive targeting is influenced by several factors, including size, shape, surface chemistry, and circulation time of nanoparticles. Optimal particle dimensions (10–200 nm) enhance extravasation through endothelial gaps by-passing rapid clearance via reticuloendothelial system (RES). Surface modifications reduce opsonization and prolong systemic circulation, further improving tumor accumulation. While passive targeting reduces systemic toxicity and allows higher local drug concentration, its effectiveness is highly variable due to heterogeneity, differences in vascular density, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure can impede nanoparticle penetration.111,112

While EPR has traditionally served as an important concept underlying the use of nanoparticles in drug delivery, the clinical applicability of EPR has turned out to be much less predictable than in rapidly growing xenograft tumors of experimental animals. Indeed, most studies providing strong evidence of pronounced EPR effect have been performed in xenografts derived from human tumor cells, which exhibited high permeability and disorderliness of the tumor vessel wall. At the same time, human tumors display considerable heterogeneity in vascular permeability, stromal content, extracellular matrix density, lymphatic drainage, interstitial fluid pressure, and blood flow that affect nanoparticle extravasation, diffusion and retention in tumors. As a result, the EPR effect intensity varies greatly from patient to patient, from lesion to lesion within the same patient, and from type of primary tumor and its metastatic location.113,114 In addition, bone metastases of breast cancer arise in the specific bone marrow microenvironment, characterized by a special type of vessels, active bone turnover, and complex immunological and stromal niche that may contribute to EPR effect as well. As a consequence, passive targeting via the EPR effect should be considered as only one of many mechanisms that can lead to nanoparticle accumulation in tumor.34,113,115

Active Targeting

It involves functionalization of nanocarriers with ligands that specifically recognize and bind to overexpressed receptors on tumor cells or associated stromal cells. These ligands include antibodies, peptides, aptamers, small molecules, and biomolecules capable of receptor-mediated endocytosis. Active targeting allows for enhanced cellular internalization of therapeutic agents and potentially improves specificity beyond achievable by passive accumulation. It also facilitates delivery of drugs to regions of the tumor with lower vascular permeability, partially overcoming limitations imposed by heterogeneous EPR effects. Efficacy of active targeting depends on ligand density, receptor expression levels, accessibility of tumor cells, and excessive modification of nanoparticles may inadvertently alter circulation time or increase immunogenicity.116–118

It is crucial to draw the line between the tumor accumulation and cellular targeting. In most cases reported in literature concerning nanomedicines, ligand-induced active targeting is not effective in significantly enhancing the overall amount of nanoparticles that arrive at the target site when compared with properly designed non-targeted nanoparticles. Instead, targeting ligands mainly improve nanoparticle recognition, receptor-mediated binding, and cellular uptake post-nanoparticles’ extravasation into the tumor microenvironment. This means that the increase in treatment effectiveness seen in experiments might be as a result of increased cellular uptake, improved intracellular trafficking, or enhanced drug release and not due to an increase in tumor accumulation.114,119,120 Whenever possible, quantitative biodistribution data should always accompany any claim about active targeting in order to prove that the efficiency of delivery has been improved. Comparative studies of both targeted and non-targeted nanoparticles by using methods such as fluorescence imaging, radiolabeling, positron emission tomography, single-photon emission computed tomography, and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry give quantitative details on distribution of nanoparticles among organs, tumors, and off-targets. This is especially vital since an enhanced response to therapy does not necessarily imply increased tumor accumulation.119,121

Combination Strategies

Integrating passive and active targeting is increasingly employed in nanoparticles which leverage EPR effect to localize within tumor interstitium, after which ligand-mediated active targeting promotes receptor-specific uptake by tumor cells. This dual approach has shown promise in enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity. Incorporation of stimuli-responsive elements into nanocarriers like pH-sensitive or enzyme-sensitive linkers can further refine drug release specificity at tumor site.122–124

Nanomedicine Strategies for Macrophage Polarization

TAM-targeting nanoparticles in solid-tumor immunotherapy have drawn considerable attention. According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), a nanoparticle is any particle that is smaller than a micron in any dimension.126 Nanoparticles have many benefits in biomedical fields because of their nano size and other characteristics, such as: (i) a relatively large surface area that improves loading efficiency (in comparison to micro-scale particles);127 (ii) specific targeting which reduces systemic toxicity;128,129 and (iii) stable structure to prevent drugs from early metabolism.130,131 Advancing cancer immunotherapy leads to targeted nanoparticles that regulate TAM and offer immunological potential. Larger particles are known to be eliminated by the liver and the mononuclear phagocyte system, whereas particles less than 10 nm are eliminated by the kidney.132 Numerous studies have demonstrated that macrophages passively absorb big nanoparticles.133–135 Nanoparticles may be engineered to have pathogen-mimicking shape in order to target TAMs, as macrophages have a tendency to grab and phagocytose these particles. Modifying nanomedicines with different ligands allows the use of active targeting methods. M2-peptides, mannose, and folate are examples of common ligands.136–138 In general, nanoplatforms can be divided into inorganic nanomaterials, lipid-based nanocarriers, and polymeric nanoparticles. Range of reprogramming agents are loaded onto the nanocarriers to accomplish polarization; nevertheless, certain nanoparticles themselves possess the capability of reprogramming macrophages during breakdown. Summary of Nanomedicine Studies Modulating Macrophage Polarization in Bone Metastasis of Breast Cancer

In a published study, zoledronic acid (ZOL), an FDA-approved anti-resorptive bisphosphonate, was used to surface-modify metal-organic framework (MOF) nanoparticles loaded with functionalized immunostimulatory cytosine-phosphate-guanosine (CpG) with bone-targeting capabilities. ZOL is primarily used to treat disorders associated to the bones because it is known to target and deposit onto bone tissues and suppress osteoclast formation. The ZOL-modified surface of isMOF gives the nanoparticles the ability to target bone metastatic sites (Figure 4).148 In vitro, the functionalized bone targeting immunostimulatory MOF (BT-isMOF) nanoparticles exhibit strong binding to calcium phosphate and precise targeting and accumulation in bone tissues. According to in vitro and biochemical investigations, the BT-isMOF nanoparticles have the ability to suppress osteoclast production and simultaneously macrophages to polarize towards the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype. Injection of BT-isMOF nanoparticles dramatically inhibited osteoclast-mediated bone degradation and improved polarization of tumor-resident macrophages to M1 phenotype utilizing the intratibial mouse model of breast cancer bone metastases. These M1 macrophages emit proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IFN-γ, which may slow the growth of tumors by encouraging the anti-tumor responses of different immune cells exhibits potential in the management of bone metastases from breast cancer.139 Despite the demonstrated potential of these nanomedicines in modulating macrophage polarization, challenges like heterogeneous TAM phenotypes in the metastatic niche, limited penetration into bone microenvironment, and variable immunomodulatory efficacy have been reported in recent studies. Future work should integrate multi-modal targeting and combination immunotherapies to overcome these limitations and improve translational relevance.125

Nanoparticle-Induced Macrophage Polarization and Anti-Tumor Effects

Palladium nanoparticles (PdNPs, around 25 nm) and Zn-Ni-FeO nanoparticles (100 nm) caused M1-polarization in the cells. To investigate the impact of macrophages on MCF-7 breast cancer micro-tissues, a co-culture experiment was subsequently performed. While M1- and PdNPs-induced macrophages demonstrated anti-tumor effects by inhibiting cell proliferation, M2-macrophages increased tumor development. Nanoparticle-induced M1 polarization is mediated through activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways including NF-κB and STAT1, leading to upregulation of key pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and other M1 markers, consistent with classical M1 activation mechanisms. Exosomes from M1 (M1-Exo), M0 (M0-Exo), M2 (M2-Exo), and PdNPs-induced (PdNPs-Exo) macrophages were applied to the heterotypic tumor micro-tissues, which included MCF-7, primary human dermal fibroblasts (phDFs), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), in order to uncover the mechanisms of action. While M1-Exo inhibited these behaviors, M2-Exo was observed to stimulate cancer cell migration and cause epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Similar to M1-Exo, PdNPs-Exo was successful in inhibiting aggressiveness of breast cancer cells. This outcome is supported by evidence that nanoparticle-mediated macrophage polarization involves pattern-recognition receptor pathways (TLR4/MyD88) and enhances pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, reinforcing M1 phenotypes that contribute to antitumor activity.149 Macrophage polarization not acts in isolation but profoundly influences and is influenced by other components of TME). M1-polarized macrophages have been demonstrated to enhance cytotoxic T-cell and NK-cell activity, promoting anti-tumor immunity, whereas M2-like TAMs can suppress T cell responses and support tumor growth.150 Interactions between TAMs and osteoblasts and osteoclasts have also been observed. M1 macrophages can produce factors that inhibit osteoclastogenesis, whereas M2 macrophages may promote osteoclast activation and bone resorption, contributing to metastatic niche formation.151 Nanomedicine strategies that repolarize TAMs toward M1 phenotypes are therefore thought to not only directly exert antitumor effects but also modulate adaptive immune cells and bone stromal components within the metastatic microenvironment, highlighting the multifaceted interplay between TAMs, immune effectors, and bone matrix cells under nanotherapeutic intervention.152 Additionally, in both MCF-7 and heterotypic micro-tissues, PdNPs-Exo enhanced the effectiveness of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). To sum up, PdNPs-Exo may have anti-tumor effects, can be used in combination with anti-cancer medications to increase effectiveness, and can be used to create novel implants for the treatment of breast cancer.153 PdNPs and exosome-mediated polarization combining with immune checkpoint inhibitors has exhibited enhanced antitumor activity in preclinical models and may improve clinical outcomes.152

Polymeric Nanocarriers Targeting Tumor-Associated Macrophages (TAMs)

Polymeric nanocarriers, including N-(2-hydroxypropyl) methacrylamide (HPMA) copolymers used to transport active therapeutic moieties to solid tumors. HPMA copolymers can concentrate within innate immune cells and target sites of inflammation. It was anticipated that they would target TAMs in primary and metastatic tumor microenvironments. A dynamically invasive mouse breast cancer model generated from a reliable, cytogenetically characterized 4T1 breast cancer cell line was used to corroborate this after initial testing with isolated bone marrow macrophage cells in vitro. Standardized experimental protocols were used to observe initial orthotopic tumor growth at 7 days and metastatic tumors at 28 days after orthotopic implantation of 4T1 cells into the mammary fat pad. The absorption of HPMA copolymer conjugated with IRDye 800CW (P-IRDye) and HPMA copolymer coupled with Alexa Fluor 647 and folic acid (P-Alexa647-FA) was noted after its retroorbital injection into the primary and metastatic tumor-bearing mice. P-IRDye was significantly absorbed by all primary and metastatic tumor sites in these mice, and the P-Alexa647-FA signal was only visible in CD11b+TAMs co-stained with the pan macrophage marker CD68. These findings demonstrate the special capacity of a P-Alexa647-FA chemical to colocalize to CD11b+CD68+ TAMs in primary and metastatic breast tumors. This illustrates how functional medicines aimed at the activation or polarization of tumor-promoting macrophages during tumor formation may be delivered using this HPMA nanocarriers.140 Although polymeric carriers demonstrate increased TAM uptake, recent findings indicate that polymer composition and surface functionalization critically influence immunogenicity and payload release. Optimization of these parameters is essential to balance effective TAM targeting with minimal systemic inflammatory responses.154 Figure 5 shows an integrated conceptual framework of TAM-targeted nanomedicine in breast cancer bone metastasis. Bioactive Scaffolds for Bone Regeneration and Tumor Treatment

Another study examined the effects of bioscaffold materials on cell proliferation and apoptosis to determine influence of macrophage polarization. Their ability to promote bone regeneration was assessed through PCR, ALP staining, and alizarin red staining. In vivo, Tunel staining, caspase-3 immunofluorescence, and tumor volume measurements in mice were used to evaluate the effects on tumors, while a mouse skull defect model was designed to confirm their role in bone regeneration. A bioactive multifunctional CePO4/CS/GO scaffold was developed by combining graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles, hydrated CePO4 nanorods, and bioactive chitosan (CS). This scaffold integrates several therapeutic properties: photothermal tumor destruction, macrophage polarization to enhance angiogenesis, and stimulation of bone formation. Under NIR laser irradiation, the CePO4/CS/GO scaffold activates caspase-3 protease in local tumor cells, leading to DNA fragmentation and apoptosis. The released Ce3⁺ ions serve dual functions. They promote M2 macrophage polarization, increasing the release of arginase-1 (Arg-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which together drive angiogenesis and improve oxygen and nutrient delivery for osteogenesis. Simultaneously, Ce3⁺ ions activate the BMP-2/Smad signaling pathway, enhancing bone tissue regeneration. By combining photothermal therapy for tumor elimination with mechanisms that support angiogenesis and bone repair, these multifunctional CePO4/CS/GO scaffolds show strong potential as a therapeutic platform for treating breast cancer bone metastases.141 Despite multifunctional capabilities, bioactive scaffolds must be carefully evaluated for long-term integration, osteoimmune balance. Combining scaffold-based therapies with controlled immunomodulators may better align tissue repair with antitumor immunity.155

Reprogramming TAMs in Tumor Microenvironment

TME contain pro-tumor M2-like and anti-tumor M1-like TAMs. In a 2023 study, biomimetic nanoparticles (HMMDN-Met@PM) were developed to specifically target M2-like TAMs and induce macrophage re-polarization. These nanoparticles were designed using metformin-loaded hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide nanoparticles (HMMDN-Met). The hollow and porous architecture of HMMDN allows for efficient loading of metformin, an established M1/M2 regulator. To achieve precise targeting within the TME and prevent premature drug leakage, the HMMDN-Met particles were further coated with macrophage membranes, forming HMMDN-Met@MM. Additionally, the M2 macrophage targeting peptide (M2pep) in the macrophage membrane was changed to exclusively deliver the drug to M2-like TAMs and promote M2 to M1 macrophage polarization. Type M2 macrophages’ surface markers and inflammatory factors CD206, Arg-1, and IL-10 were found to decrease through in vitro and in vivo research, whereas type M1 macrophages’ surface markers and inflammatory factors CD80, TNF-α, and iNOS increased. This suggests that M2 macrophages were successfully repolarized and that tumor growth was finally inhibited. At the same time, HMMDN broke down into Mn2+, a magnetic resonance imaging contrast agent, in the acidic and GSH conditions of the tumor, enabling tumor tracking. This discovery paves the path for the development of tumor suppression strategies by using biomimetic nanosystems in targeted imaging and immunotherapy.142 Successful TAM reprogramming depends on overcoming the immunosuppressive milieu of metastatic bone. Integrating metabolic reprogramming and targeted cytokine delivery may further enhance these immunotherapeutic outcomes.156

Targeted MYC Inhibition in Pro-Tumor M2 Macrophages

TAMs are linked to worse outcomes in breast cancer and support tumor growth in mouse models. Rather than eliminating all macrophages, current strategies aim to reprogram pro-tumor M2 macrophages while preserving M1 macrophages that support anti-tumor immunity (Figure 6).157 Recent work shows that the transcription factor c-MYC (MYC), which drives pro-tumoral gene programs, is upregulated in M2 macrophages both in vitro and in vivo. In myeloid-specific MYC knockout mice, loss of MYC impaired macrophage development and function and reduced tumor growth, suggesting that inhibiting MYC in M2 TAMs could shift macrophage polarization away from the tumor-promoting phenotype. To test this idea, researchers developed a MYC inhibitor prodrug (MI3-PD) encapsulated in perfluorocarbon nanoparticles for direct cytoplasmic delivery. Because integrin αvβ3 (vitronectin receptor) is highly expressed on M2-like TAMs, they evaluated αvβ3-targeted MI3-PD nanoparticles and αvβ3-targeted rhodamine-labeled nanoparticles for targeting and uptake. The rhodamine tracer showed that αvβ3-NPs entered M2 macrophages by both phagocytosis-dependent and -independent routes, whereas uptake by M1 macrophages was almost entirely phagocytosis-dependent. In a 4T1-GFP-FL breast cancer model, treatment with αvβ3-MI3-PD NPs selectively reduced M2-like TAMs. Control experiments using integrin β3 knockout tumors (PyMT-Bo1 β3KO) confirmed the effect was specific to MYC inhibition delivered via αvβ3 targeting and not due to nonspecific drug delivery to tumor cells. These results indicate that αvβ3-mediated delivery of a c-MYC inhibitor can selectively deplete pro-tumor M2-like macrophages while sparing antitumor M1-like macrophages in breast cancer models.143 Targeting transcriptional drivers selectivity for pro-tumor TAM highlights the risk of compensatory signaling pathways that can attenuate therapeutic durability. Combinatorial blockade of parallel oncogenic nodes may thus be needed to sustain reprogramming effects.158

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exhibit plasticity, ranging from anti-tumor M1-like to pro-tumor M2-like phenotypes. M1-like TAMs, activated by cytokines such as IFNγ and IL-12, promote T cell recruitment, phagocytose tumor cells, and release TNF-α, ROS, and NO to kill cancer cells. M2-like TAMs, shaped by hypoxia and immunosuppressive signals like IL-10 and TGFβ, support tumor growth by enhancing angiogenesis, cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition EMT, matrix remodeling, and suppressing immune responses. Biomimetic Nanoplatforms for BMBC and Pain Management

Bone metastases from breast cancer cause severe pain that lowers patients’ quality of life. Biomimetic nanoplatform (EMM@DJHAD) was developed for the simultaneous treatment of bone metastatic breast cancer and related pain. The platform uses engineered macrophage membranes (EMM) and drug-loaded nanoparticles, allowing both primary and metastatic lesions to be targeted through tumor tropism inherited from EMM. In this study, M1 macrophages, polarized from RAW264.7 cells using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), were employed to harness their inflammatory and chemotactic properties. Decitabine and JTC801 then acted synergistically to trigger lytic and inflammatory tumor responses, enhancing tumor cell destruction and prolonging macrophage recruitment toward tumors. In bone metastasis mouse models, EMM@DJHAD inhibited μ-opioid receptors, producing a strong analgesic effect along with significant tumor suppression. By inducing tumor pyroptosis, the released chemotherapeutic agents improved the precision and potency of chemotherapy. Additionally, EMM biomimetic drug vector-mediated delivery substantially reduced the systemic toxicity typically caused by chemotherapeutic drugs, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility. Overall, this biomimetic nanoplatform shows strong potential for the effective treatment of breast cancer bone metastases and associated pain. Further studies are needed to explore additional mechanisms underlying the anticancer and analgesic actions of EMM@DJHAD.144 Translating biomimetic systems into clinical settings requires production scalability, reproducibility of membrane coatings, and comprehensive immunotoxicity studies. Recent developments in microfluidic fabrication may offer promising solutions for these challenges.159

Liposomal Co-Delivery for Chemo-Immunotherapy

Encapsulating doxorubicin (Dox) in pegylated (Stealth) liposomes provides substantial pharmacologic advantages compared to administering Dox alone. Similarly, potent amino-bisphosphonate alendronate (Ald), when reformulated in pegylated liposomes, activates a subset of gamma-delta T cells and interacts with tumor-associated macrophages, producing significant immunomodulatory effects. Building on these findings, pegylated liposomes co-encapsulating Dox and Ald (PLAD) were developed as a stable formulation with high and consistent Dox loading and excellent plasma stability. Biodistribution studies, radionuclide imaging, and therapeutic evaluations in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors or gamma-delta T lymphocytes demonstrated that PLAD exhibits distinct pharmacologic behavior and tumor microenvironment interactions compared to free Dox or the clinical formulation of pegylated liposomal Dox. These results highlight the advantages of co-encapsulation in nanomedicine and the therapeutic potential of PLAD in cancer chemo-immunotherapy. Encapsulation in liposomes has also been shown to significantly reduce drug toxicity in cancer patients. Because the spleen and tumor-draining lymph nodes are sites where antigen-presenting cells such as macrophages activate naïve T cells against tumor-associated antigens, alendronate when encapsulated in liposomes with a composition similar to PLD, polarized macrophages toward an antitumoral M1-like phenotype and preferentially accumulated in these organs. PLAD exhibited increase uptake in the spleen and tumor compared to PLD. Importantly, PLAD altered the number and activity of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, including Treg, TAM, MDSC, NK, and NK-T cells. These immune alterations were linked to enhanced antitumor responses within the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, PLAD affected the uptake of drugs by monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) and TAMs, contributing to its overall immunotherapeutic effects.145 Despite the clear immunomodulatory benefits of co-encapsulated liposomal systems, recent clinical translation efforts emphasize the need to monitor off-target immune activation and long-term alterations in systemic cytokine levels. Strategic dosing regimens and co-delivery with immune modulators may mitigate these effects.160

Hybrid Nanoparticles Combining Sonodynamic Therapy and Immunomodulation

Terminal stage bone metastasis is usually treated with chemotherapy and radiation therapy, which has limited efficacy and serious side effects. In the future, sonodynamic therapy might be a safer and more successful way to address this. Immune-regulating qualities of bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMV) include boosting anti-tumor activities, improving DC cell maturation, and modifying macrophage polarization. Sonodynamic treatment and OMV together may have synergistic anti-tumor effects. Multipurpose nanoparticles were designed to treat bone metastases of breast cancer. The IR780@PLGA@HM nanoparticles were made by combining the membranes of breast cancer cells and bacterial outer membrane vesicles to form a hybrid membrane (HM), which had tumor targeting, immunological modulating, and sonodynamic qualities. Next, HM was used to encapsulate IR780-loaded PLGA. The IR780@PLGA@HM nanoparticles demonstrated strong therapeutic potential against breast cancer bone metastasis by showing excellent biocompatibility, effective targeting of 4T1 tumors, enhanced expression of anti-tumor inflammatory factors, promotion of macrophage M1 polarization, activation of dendritic cells, and efficient tumor cell killing both in vitro and in vivo. Overall, these nanoparticles represent a promising new strategy for the effective treatment of breast cancer bone metastases.146 Sonodynamic approaches combined with hybrid immunomodulation are still nascent, and recent work suggests careful optimization of acoustic parameters and immune adjuvant co-delivery to avoid unintended inflammatory damage.161

Macrophage-Mediated Chemo-Photodynamic Therapy

The survival and quality of life are considerably lower for patients with primary and bone-metastasized breast cancer. Effective treatment is extremely difficult since anti-metastasis therapy has low drug delivery efficiency and low response rate. The goal of the work reported in study147 to improve the chemo/photo/immunotherapy of primary and bone metastatic malignancies by using modified macrophages (Oxa(IV)@ZnPc@M) that transport nanomedicine that contains oxaliplatin prodrug and photosensitizer as near-infrared (NIR) light-activated drug vectors. Oxa(IV)@ZnPc@M can effectively host primary and bone metastatic cancers and demonstrates an anticancer M1 phenotypic polarization. Furthermore, Oxa(IV)@ZnPc@M’s medicines undergo NIR-triggered release, which cause immunogenic cell death and eliminate primary tumors by chemo-photodynamic treatment. When paired with anti-PD-L1, Oxa(IV)@ZnPc@M can eradicate primary and bone metastatic tumors, stimulate the immune system’s antitumor response specific to the tumor, and enhance overall survival with minimal systemic toxicity. Combination chemo-photodynamic nanotherapies must consider light penetration limitations in deep bone tissues, and recent advances in upconversion nanoparticles and adaptive dosing strategies show potential to overcome this barrier.162

pH and Redox-Responsive Systems

Drug resistance and metastasis are common features of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), with the bone being the most frequent site of distant metastasis. The progression of bone metastases and resulting bone degeneration cause patients with TNBC to experience severe pain. An effective therapeutic strategy aims to both inhibit the growth of bone metastases and reprogram bone microenvironment associated with resorption and immunosuppression. pH- and redox-responsive drug delivery system such as DZ@CPH was developed to target bone metastases in TNBC. This system encapsulated docetaxel (DTX) in a hyaluronic acid–polylactic acid micelle reinforced with calcium phosphate and zoledronate. DZ@CPH demonstrated efficient targeting of bone metastases and favorable biocompatibility. Mechanistically, DZ@CPH reduced bone resorption by increasing osteoprotegerin (OPG) production and decreasing nuclear factor κB receptor ligand (RANKL) expression in drug-resistant bone metastatic tissue, which in turn lowered osteoclast activation. It also suppressed the expression of P-glycoprotein, Bcl-2, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), enhancing the sensitivity of drug-resistant bone metastases to DTX. Furthermore, DZ@CPH promoted apoptosis in drug-resistant TNBC cells by downregulating TGF-β, FPPS, and Bcl-2 while upregulating Bax and cleaved caspase-3. By modulating proteins involved in invasion and cell death, DZ@CPH inhibited the invasiveness and mobility of drug-resistant TNBC cells in bone metastatic tissue. In addition to these anticancer effects, DZ@CPH improved the immunosuppressive microenvironment by increasing the ratio of M1 to M2 macrophages in bone metastatic tissue, thereby promoting an antitumoral immune response. DZ@CPH simultaneously induced apoptosis in drug-resistant TNBC cells and reprogrammed the microenvironment of bone resorption and immunosuppression, effectively preventing the formation of bone metastases. Clinically, DZ@CPH shows strong potential as a therapeutic approach for treating bone metastases from drug-resistant TNBC.163 Despite strong preclinical efficacy, redox/pH-responsive systems require careful in vivo pharmacokinetic characterization to balance stability with timely payload release, an issue highlighted in responsive nanocarrier studies.164

Emerging Therapeutic Strategies for Bone Metastasis in Breast Cancer

Evidence suggests that target molecules which act selectively to inhibit bone resorption, activate osteoblastic activity (anabolic agents) or have both properties concurrently, activating osteoblasts and inhibiting osteoclasts. Also drugs that are being investigated that act upon the bone microenvironment to disrupt tumor–bone interactions by interfering with the metastatic niche.165 Small non-coding RNAs (miRNAs) have also become a potential regulator of normal bone remodeling as well as metastatic initiation.166 Various researches are assessing them as anti-metastatic agents or predictive biomarkers of bone metastasis. miR-200 family members as well as miR-128, miR-99a, miR-29b, let-7 and miR-600, miR-34 and miR-30 are also tumor suppressors and have been found to prevent cancer bone metastasis in various cellular mechanisms.167 In addition to miRNA-based approaches, structurally engineered nucleic acid nanotechnologies such as RNA origami through endocytic uptake and activate pattern recognition receptors Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) leads to phosphorylation and induction of proinflammatory cytokines have recently emerged as programmable immunomodulatory platforms. Although their application in breast cancer bone metastasis remains limited, their potential for macrophage reprogramming and targeted immunotherapy warrants future investigation.168,169 Some miRNAs have been found to control signaling events and gene expression in breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) and modulate stemness, malignant progression and drug resistance, which can be utilized in CSC-targeted therapy to inhibit tumor progression. Cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) whose preservation is highly dependent on the bone microenvironment are also regarded to be important therapeutic targets. Recent studies are aimed at discovering molecules that interfere with CSC-related signaling, metabolism in CSCs, or regulate dormancy-related processes. Differentiation of CSCs or the use of immunotherapy-based CSC-targeting agents by reprogramming the CSCs is also being investigated.170 Nevertheless, due to the possible effects of these treatments on normal tissue stem cells, extensive pharmacological and toxicological analyses are needed to provide the safety in the clinical practice. Experimental research is still carried out to develop selective low-toxicity molecules and novel drug delivery systems that be able to effectively prevent bone metastasis. Such agents can be employed together with surgery, radiotherapy or cytotoxic drugs to improve therapeutic effectiveness, extend clinical response and improve recurrence prevention. Besides, a new range of approaches focusing on the treatment of the early stages of breast cancer aims to prevent the homing and development of disseminated tumor cells in bone, which may occur in response to the prevention of occult micro-metastases in advance, before they become visible lesions, and subsequently eliminate the risk of relapse and disease progression.171,172

Limitations and Translational Challenges of TAM-Targeted Nanomedicine

While significant advances have been achieved in the nanoparticle-based regulation of tumor-associated macrophages, there are a number of biological and translational barriers for the clinical application of this approach. The majority of the experiments showing successful TAM reprogramming were carried out either in vitro or in mouse models of tumor growth; however, such approaches do not always accurately reflect the complex picture of human breast cancer metastases to bone. There might be differences in the development, immunological profile, heterogeneity of tumors, and microenvironmental regulation, leading to different responses of macrophages in experimental and real-life conditions.93 In addition, the idea of therapy based on switching the phenotype of tumor-promoting M2-like macrophages to the antitumor M1-like ones is a simplification of macrophage biology. At the moment, it is believed that tumor-associated macrophages exist along the continuum of macrophage functionality and include inflammatory, immunoregulatory, angiogenic, metabolic, and tissue remodeling programs. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis in breast cancer revealed multiple subpopulations of TAMs with distinctive transcriptomic profiles, emphasizing the high degree of intratumoral macrophage heterogeneity. Thus, induction of global activation or depletion of macrophages might lead to unexpected consequences.93,173,174

The other limitation is the problem of TAM-selective targeting in the metastatic bone microenvironment. There are several biological obstacles in systemic delivery of nanoparticles, such as protein corona formation, mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) clearance, heterogeneous tumor vasculature, low penetration into metastatic lesions, and changes in interaction with stromal and immune cells. Protein coating of the nanoparticles may cause changes in biological identity, receptor availability, and efficiency of ligand-based targeting approaches. Also, an increase in therapeutic efficiency does not always imply more tumor accumulation and more efficient TAM targeting since biological response can be dependent on intracellular trafficking, immune response, and drug release rate.175,176 Notably, contradictory results about macrophage targeting have been published recently. While TAM depletion or reprogramming can inhibit tumor progression in some preclinical models, macrophages play an important role in immune surveillance, tissue regeneration, angiogenesis regulation, and tissue homeostasis. Thus, non-specific manipulation with macrophages can impair their normal function. The future research should focus on the exact analysis of different macrophage populations, biodistribution analysis, use of human-like models and toxicity studies to establish the efficacy of TAM-targeted nanomedicine in treating metastatic breast cancer.177

Conclusion

Multifunctional nanoparticles represent an emerging and promising platform for improving the diagnosis and therapeutic management of metastatic breast cancer through integrated drug delivery, imaging, and immune-modulatory capabilities. Among these strategies, nanotechnology-based modulation of the TAM function inside the bone metastatic niche has garnered much interest due to the importance of macrophages in controlling tumor progression, osteolytic bone resorption, immunosuppression, and the development of the metastatic niche. Studies have shown that TAM signaling pathway and the state of the macrophage can be modulated using nanocarriers in order to achieve an antitumor effect. The existing literature is however based on preclinical studies conducted either in vitro or on animals, and there is no known clinical proof of this strategy in treating bone metastases of breast cancer. It is important to take into account the species differences while translating macrophage regulation strategies due to higher variability and plasticity of human macrophages compared to mouse models. Thus, the idea of M1/M2 polarization must be approached carefully, and new human-related models are required to describe the status of TAMs more accurately. Furthermore, there are several unique challenges associated with bone metastasis microenvironment and drug delivery, which include the presence of heterogenous vasculature, low penetration of nanoparticles, complex extracellular matrix, and interaction with bone resident macrophages.
