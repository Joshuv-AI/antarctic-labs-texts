Effect of different work-to-rest ratios during simulated taekwondo combat on blood lactate, heart rate and perceptual responses in elite taekwondo athletes: a randomized crossover trial
Authors: Binnur Çelebi, Furkan Öztürk, Hasan Basri Taşkın, Bayram Ceylan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated the effects of different work-to-rest (W: R) ratios during simulated taekwondo combat on physiological and perceptual responses in elite athletes.MethodsTwenty male taekwondo athletes participated in a randomized cross-over study involving three combat conditions: free combat (self-paced), 2:1 (20s work:10s rest), and 1:2 (10s work:20s rest). Measurements included blood lactate (bLA), heart rate (HR), countermovement jump (CMJ) performance, rating of perceived exertion (RPE), and acute perceived muscle soreness (APMS).ResultsResults indicated that the W: R ratios significantly influenced bLA accumulation (p = 0.04), with the 2:1 condition producing the highest post-combat levels, followed by free combat and the 1:2 condition. CMJ performance decreased significantly from pre- to post-combat across all conditions (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found for HR, RPE, or APMS responses across the different W: R ratios.ConclusionThe findings suggest that manipulating W: R ratios in training specifically impacts the glycolytic demand, with longer work periods relative to rest (2:1) eliciting the greatest anaerobic metabolic stress. However, neuromuscular fatigue and perceptual responses appear to be more closely tied to the overall high-intensity, intermittent nature of taekwondo combat rather than the specific W: R ratio, possibly due to the high fitness level of elite athletes.Clinical trial numberClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07395414 (Retrospectively registered), 09/02/2026.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01786-7.

Introduction

Taekwondo, an Olympic sport, is characterized by dynamic, high-intensity moves with a unique combination of speed, power, agility, and tactical precision [1]. Taekwondo is a full-contact sport characterized by alternating sequences of intense movements, including kicking and punching, interspersed with short intervals of lower-intensity activity or rest [2]. The physiological demands of taekwondo depends on both anaerobic and aerobic energy systems, as athletes frequently reach near-maximal heart rates and exhibit elevated blood lactate (bLA) levels during competition [1, 3]. The specific work-to-rest (W: R) ratios range from a 1:1 to 1:8 during taekwondo contests, with rounds lasting two minutes and rest intervals ranging from 30 s to one minute [4–6].

Previous research has investigated taekwondo-specific training methods, highlighting the significance of replicating competitive settings to improve physiological and perceptual adaptations [2, 7, 8]. Research indicates that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) regimens designed for taekwondo can enhance anaerobic capacity and recovery efficiency [3]. Nonetheless, despite these advancements, there exists a scarcity of research investigating the specific impacts of differing W: R ratios during simulated taekwondo combat on physiological indicators such as blood lactate and heart rate, along with perceptual responses including ratings of perceived exertion (RPE) and fatigue. Moreover, studies investigating specific taekwondo exercise sessions with different W: R ratios is scarce [9]. This disparity is especially significant considering the essential function of W: R ratios in influencing the metabolic and neuromuscular requirements of intermittent sports [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of different W: R ratios during simulated taekwondo combat on physiological and perceptual loads. It was hypothesized that athletes would present higher physiological and perceptual loads following shorter rest intervals compared to longer rest period.

Materials and methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) [11] to ensure the study was sufficiently robust to detect significant changes in internal physiological load. Heart Rate (HR) was designated as the primary outcome variable given its sensitivity to the intermittent, high-intensity demands of Taekwondo combat. Based on a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-factors, 3 measurements), the calculation was parameterized with a large effect size (f = 0.40), an alpha level of 0.05, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5. With the final sample of 20 athletes, the achieved statistical power (1-β) was 0.97. The selection of a large effect size (f = 0.40) was justified by the explosive nature of the combat rounds, which induce high-magnitude cardiovascular and blood lactate (bLA) fluctuations that typically exceed the thresholds of medium-effect interventions. This sample size provided more than sufficient sensitivity to identify the primary physiological trends while maintaining a high degree of confidence in the observed results. The criteria included having at least a brown belt, having at least 5 years of taekwondo experience, having no injuries for the last 6 months that prevent participating to trainings, and competing at national championships and international tournaments during the last year. Each athlete signed a written informed consent form. Measurements were carried out during the preparatory period, and athletes were not engaged in weight loss. The study was conducted according to the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Kastamonu University Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Number: 2022-KAEK-12). Study design

This single-blinded randomized cross-over study monitored physiological and perceptual responses of taekwondo athletes to a free combat and combats with different W: R ratios. Due to the nature of the physical interventions (simulated combat with specific W: R ratios), it was not possible to blind the athletes or the referee to the work-to-rest (W: R) ratios. However, to minimize bias, the statistical analysis was conducted by a researcher who was blinded to the specific intervention codes until the analysis was completed. This study was carried out between April and May 2023 with 4 visits to the combat sports hall at Faculty of Sport Sciences, Kastamonu University. The first one included body composition measurements and familiarization to the sessions and measurements. The allocation sequence was generated using a computer-based randomization process facilitated by an Artificial Intelligence language model (Gemini, Google AI). To ensure an unbiased distribution, a block randomization approach was employed by the researchers to assign the 20 participants to one of the three intervention sequences: free combat, 1:2 W: R ratio, and 2:1 W: R ratio. This resulted in a balanced allocation across the sequences n = 7, n = 7, and n = 6, respectively), as detailed in the study flow diagram (can be found in Supplementary Files). During the rest 3 visit, athletes performed simulated combats free and with 1:2 (10 s work and 20 s rest), and 2:1 (20 s work and 10 s rest) W: R ratios. During all combat sessions, athletes heart rate (HR), blood lactate (bLA), and counter-movement jump performance (CMJ) were measured at rest and at the end of each combat. HR was also measured following each round. Athletes’ rating of perceived exertion (RPE) and acute perceived muscle soreness (APMS) were measured immediately after each combat. Additionally, to eliminate the potential influence of circadian rhythm (time-of-day effects), all measurements were conducted at similar times of the day. Furthermore, participants were instructed to avoid caffeine, alcohol, tobacco, and heavy meals prior to testing. All measurements were carried out by the sport scientist who had previous education and experience in measurements. This study adheres to the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) reporting guidelines for randomized trials. All procedures were summarized in Fig. 2.

Study design

Simulated taekwondo combats

Athletes from the same weight category with similar performance levels were matched for simulated combats in order to ensure procedural standardization and minimize the influence of opponents’ physical, physiological, and technical characteristics on athletes’ performance during the combats. Athletes performed the combats under the provision of an official referee on the official-size mat. The simulated combats for all conditions consisted of 3 rounds of 2 min with 1-minute rest intervals between rounds. For the prescribed conditions, the work-to-rest intervals were applied within each 2-minute round: the 2:1 condition resulted in a total of 80 s of active work per round, while the 1:2 condition resulted in 40 s of active work per round [12, 13]. During free combat, athletes were not restricted by prescribed intervals, and active duration was determined by the athletes’ self-paced engagement.

Counter-movement jump

Participants were instructed to do a rapid CMJ with no arm swing to a self-selected depth, followed by explosive upward propulsion to achieve maximal jump height movement while retaining the same take-off and landing positions. The same researcher recorded the jumps with a mobile phone round on a tripod (iPhone Xs, Apple) at a sampling rate of 240 Hz, using the My Jump 2 App, 1.5 m distant from the athlete. Athletes completed three trials with 30-second pauses, and the highest jump height was chosen for analysis. The measures in the current study demonstrated a strong intra-class correlation (ICC = 0.918, 95% CI = 0.827–0.970) and this application has been shown to be a reliable tool to measure jump performance [14].

Blood lactate and heart rate measurements

Resting bLA and HR of the athletes were measured following a 30 min rest in the supine position. Following each combat, bLA was measured again. It was measured with a lactate device (Edge Blood Lactate Monitoring System, ApexBio Inc., Taipei City, Taiwan) by the same experienced researcher. Before each measurement, the site was cleaned with alcohol and dried with cotton, obtaining a 0.3 µl blood sample from the fingertip of the middle finger as suggested by the manufacturer. HR was also measured after each round of combat. HR was monitored with an HR monitor (Seego, Realtrack Sytems, Spain). The sensors were worn on the chests of the athletes, Bluetooth connection was check and HR was seen on the monitor before the commencement of the study as suggested by the manufacturer.

Perceptual scales

After each combat, rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was rated on a 15-point scale, ranging from 6 (very light) to 20 (very hard) [15]. Also, acute perceived muscle soreness (APMS) was measured immediately post-combat with a scale from 0 (absence of soreness) to 10 (very intense soreness) [16].

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS 23 software (SPAA, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and JASP software (0.15.0.0 Version, The Netherlands). The Shapiro-Wilk test and descriptive methods using skewness and kurtosis coefficients were used to check for the normality of data. Descriptive statistics and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to present subjects’ characteristics. Two-way ANOVA with repeated measurements (intervention x time) was used to determine changes in HR (3 × 4), bLA (3 × 2), CMJ (3 × 2) between W: R ratios and measurement times. One-way ANOVA was used to determine changes in APMS. Eta squared (η2) was calculated to determine the effect size, using the 0.0099, 0.0588, and 0.1379 considered as small, medium, and large effect sizes [17]. In case of significant differences between W: R ratios, post-hoc test with a Holm correction was carried out, while one-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to determine the difference among measurement times. Cohen’s d with 95% CI for paired sample t-test was automatically provided by JASP software and classified as 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium) and 0.8 (large) [17]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of different W: R ratios on physiological neuromuscular, and perceptual responses during a simulated taekwondo combat. The main findings of the present study were as follows: (a) bLA levels were higher in the 2:1 W: R ratio compared to both the free-combat and 1:2 conditions, (b) CMJ performance decrease following all conditions. This finding indicates that athletes were exposed to neuromuscular fatigue, which appeared to be similar across the different W: R ratios.

Under varying W: R ratios, higher bLA levels were observed in taekwondo athletes when the work duration exceeds the rest period. Consistently, a study examining the effects of different W: R ratios on physiological load responses during high-intensity intermittent taekwondo exercise reported that athletes exhibited higher bLA levels under long W: R conditions compared to short ones [18]. During taekwondo competition, the repeated execution of high-intensity intermittent actions and the concomitant resynthesis of ATP rely heavily on the glycolytic anaerobic system. Consequently, bLA levels increase when work periods are longer than rest intervals. This phenomenon is likely associated with the high demand for anaerobic lactic metabolism inherent to taekwondo performance [19]. Supporting both this rationale and existing empirical findings, previous research has demonstrated that in high-intensity intermittent exercise protocols designed to reflect the demands of taekwondo competition, bLA levels remain relatively low following short-duration high-intensity bouts but increase markedly during prolonged work intervals [20]. Similarly, a study conducted on university-level taekwondo athletes reported that bLA concentration increased progressively in each round. Moreover, compared to a 1:5 ratio, a 1:½ work-to-rest ratio resulted in significantly higher bLA concentrations. It was emphasized that shorter recovery intervals amplify physiological responses [18].

CMJ performance was not affected by different W: R ratios. Ceylan, Öztürk [21] reported that no decline in CMJ performance was observed across different rest intervals of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3, and that W: R ratios did not significantly influence CMJ outcomes. Similarly, a study conducted on taekwondo athletes found no significant difference in CMJ performance under 1:6, 1:7, and self-paced W: R conditions [22]. Although the design of the present study differs from these previous investigations, it can be anticipated that variations in W: R ratios may not significantly affect CMJ performance. Ceylan, Öztürk [21] suggested that this may be attributed to the short duration of the exercise sessions and the athletes’ high training level, which enables rapid recovery. However, in the current study, measurement time points appeared to influence CMJ performance. A decline in CMJ performance was observed following exercise. In line with this finding, Zheng, Zhang [18] reported a significant reduction in CMJ performance in the third round compared to the first, due to fatigue effects. Therefore, changes in CMJ performance are likely associated more with the type of exercise, the level of post-exercise neuromuscular fatigue, and the athletes’ training background rather than the specific W: R ratios employed [18, 21].

Athletes’ perceptual responses, as measured by RPE, were observed to be similar across different W: R ratios. However, the current literature indicates that conditions with shorter rest intervals are generally associated with higher RPE values [23–25]. Supporting this explanation, Zheng et al. [12] reported that under conditions where rest duration was shortened despite similar work intervals, taekwondo athletes exhibited higher RPE. In another study, Ceylan, Öztürk [21] reported that athletes exhibited significantly higher RPE values at a 1:1 W: R ratio compared to 1:2 and 1:3 ratios. Based on their finding, the authors highlighted that coaches could use the RPE scale as an effective monitoring tool to optimize high-intensity interval training protocols. Similarly, Zheng, Zhang [18] reported a significant interaction between rounds and trials in athletes’ perceptual responses. RPE values increased in each successive round, and during long W: R ratio conditions, RPE values in the third round were higher compared to short W: R conditions. These findings suggest that the progressive intensity of exercise can elevate athletes’ perceived exertion levels. In the present study, the similarity of RPE values across different W: R ratios may be attributed to the participants’ high physiological capacity, well-developed aerobic fitness, and overall conditioning level [1].

Following exercises performed under different W: R ratios, athletes’ heart rate (HR) values were observed to be similar across the three conditions. Although the number of studies on this topic is limited, findings regarding the effects of different W: R ratios on HR remain inconclusive. Ceylan, Öztürk [21] reported that HR changes following exercises with 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 W: R ratios were similar, and no significant differences were observed in peak or average HR values. In contrast, Ceylan and Balci [26] found that during judo-specific high-intensity intermittent exercises, HR values were higher under short rest conditions (1:1/1:2) compared to long rest conditions (1:3); however, a similar trend was observed across all exercise protocols.

It is well established that following high-intensity exercise, HR responses tend to increase after an initial adaptation period, independent of the rest duration [10]. This explanation may account for the observed similarity in HR responses in the present study. Moreover, the comparable HR values across different W: R ratios may be related to the characteristic nature of taekwondo and the athletes’ training status [1]. As noted above, although HR values exhibited similar patterns across conditions, there are also studies reporting contrasting findings. In a recent study, Zheng et al. [12] investigated university-level taekwondo athletes and emphasized that HR values were lower under a 1:5 W: R ratio condition compared to a 1:½ ratio. The discrepancy between the findings of the present study and those reported in the literature may be attributed to the substantial difference in rest durations between the two conditions, despite the similarity in work intervals. Furthermore, it is plausible that the athletes employed a ‘self-pacing’ strategy, modulating their work-rate density throughout the rounds to remain within a specific physiological threshold. In the absence of time-motion data to determine the internal work to rest ratio, we cannot exclude the possibility that subtle variations in activity profile contributed to the absence of significant differences in HR and RPE between conditions.

This study investigated the effects of different W: R ratios on blood lactate (bLA), heart rate (HR), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE) responses in elite taekwondo athletes during a simulated match. The findings demonstrated that varying W: R ratios elicited significant differences in bLA levels. Specifically, athletes exhibited higher bLA concentrations under conditions characterized by long work periods and short rest intervals. Therefore, it may be beneficial for coaches to implement longer work and short rest ratios in training programs to enhance and develop the activation of the glycolytic energy system.

In contrast, athletes’ CMJ, RPE, and HR responses were not significantly affected by different W: R ratios. However, a decline in CMJ performance was observed following each exercise protocol. This decrease may be associated with accumulated neuromuscular fatigue resulting from repeated high-intensity efforts. Moreover, the lack of significant differences in CMJ, RPE, and HR responses may be attributed to the athletes’ high physical and physiological capacities, as well as their well-developed aerobic fitness levels [1, 27].

The results of the present study provide coaches and athletes evidence-based advice on how to set up high-intensity interval training to help elite taekwondo athletes make certain physiological changes. Coaches can focus on training blocks with a 2:1 W: R ratio (i.e., 20 s of work followed by 10 s of rest). This W: R ratio resulted in the highest levels of bLA, which shows that the anaerobic glycolytic pathway was quite active. The findings suggest that the 2:1 W: R ratio is an effective stimulus specifically for metabolic conditioning, as evidenced by significantly higher bLA accumulation compared to other conditions. The absence of corresponding changes in CMJ and RPE highlights a metabolic-mechanical dissociation common in elite athletes, where explosive power is maintained despite elevated acidosis. Therefore, the ‘effectiveness’ of this ratio is specialized toward anaerobic-glycolytic adaptation rather than general acute fatigue. The same RPE and neuromuscular exhaustion (as evidenced by the CMJ reduction) across all situations reveal that even protocols with longer rest intervals (1:2) resulted in a lot of stress on the body. Therefore, coaches can use the 1:2 ratio to make training more demanding while giving athletes a little more time to recover. This W: R ratio can be utilized to concentrate on technical accuracy and decision-making while athletes are fatigued. The consistent post-exercise decrease in CMJ height across all W: R ratios confirms its sensitivity and practicality for coaches to monitor neuromuscular fatigue following high-intensity exercises. Regularly tracking CMJ can help in assessing an athlete’s recovery status and readiness for subsequent training or competition.

A limitation of the current study is the lack of time-motion data, which precluded our ability to account for potential variations in the self-selected W: R ratio. This ratio may have served as a compensatory mechanism to maintain consistent heart rate and perceived exertion levels, despite the differing prescribed recovery durations. Future research should consider integrating time-motion analysis with both external and physiological load parameters to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, HR was monitored at specific intervals (rest and post-round) rather than continuously throughout the combat. While this approach avoided potential movement artifacts and signal interference common in high-impact combat sports, it did not allow for the determination of mean and peak heart rate values during the active work phases.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that manipulating work-to-rest (W: R) ratios during simulated combat primarily modulates metabolic stress rather than cardiovascular or perceptual load in elite Taekwondo athletes. Specifically, a 2:1 W: R ratio (20s work: 10s rest) elicited the highest blood lactate accumulation, indicating a greater reliance on the anaerobic glycolytic system. In contrast, cardiovascular strain (HR), neuromuscular fatigue (CMJ), and perceived exertion (RPE) remained consistently high across all conditions, suggesting these factors are driven by the overall high-intensity, intermittent nature of the sport rather than the specific interval structure.
