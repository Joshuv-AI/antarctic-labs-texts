Safe delivery of cold light to the endoscope during elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy: assessment of human factors

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman factors are an important concern in the use-related hazards of medical devices, yet little research has been conducted on factors affecting performance of surgeons using the laparoscopic technology. Elements of a Xenon light chain, commonly used in laparoscopic surgery, may generate extremely high temperatures, capable of causing patient burns and fire ignition. To identify errors potentially compromising patients’ safety in minimally invasive surgery, we conducted a proactive error analysis of the process for delivering light from a xenon generator to the tip of the endoscope during laparoscopic cholecystectomy.MethodsThe process under study was decomposed and re-mapped by means of hierarchical task analysis (HTA), combining complementary methods from the surgeons’ and scrub nurses’ perspectives. Systematic human error reduction and prediction analysis (SHERPA) was applied to HTA results. Problems and barriers to the performance of tasks, as factors contributing to errors, were examined with work system analysis based on direct procedural observations and face-to-face interviews with personnel.ResultsSHERPA showed 98 credible errors, two of which bearing potentially severe consequences for the patient. Remedy analysis recommended equipment modification, process redesign, standardization, checklists and training. Distracting noise was identified in the work systems analysis to be a commonly perceived problem during surgeries.ConclusionsRelying solely on predictive error analysis may miss critical contextual triggers for failure. Pairing SHERPA with qualitative work system observations may offer a richer picture of safety hazards in the operating theatre, identifying how context increases the probability of surgical errors. This hybrid methodology enables a shift from reactive training toward the design of inherently resilient surgical systems. Background

Laparoscopic surgery requires a reliable light source (LS) to illuminate the operative field. Modern systems frequently utilize Xenon units which produce a bright image when integrated into an optimal light chain consisting of the LS unit, a fiberoptic light cable (LC), and the laparoscope (Fig. 1).

Different parts composing a light chain (LC): the light source (LS) unit (left), a fiberoptic light cable (right), and the laparoscope (bottom)

Although Xenon units are marketed as “cold” light sources due to their color temperature (5500°K), they generate significant thermal energy as a byproduct. The tip of an active Xenon LC can reach temperatures up to 268 °C. Proximity to surgical drapes can cause charring in less than five seconds [1]. Such intense heat can ignite operating room fires [2, 3]. Consequently, LCs consistently rank among the top ten technology hazards -the inherent potential of the equipment to cause harm—in healthcare [4]. Beyond fire risks, direct contact is not required to induce tissue damage via coagulative necrosis [1]. Even when connected to the laparoscope, temperatures range from 60 °C to 100 °C. These levels are sufficient to cause intra-abdominal bowel injury upon brief contact [5] or skin burns within 30 seconds [6]. Clinical reports confirm numerous instances of patient burns and fires ignited by intraoperative heat transmission [7–9]. To mitigate these risks, nursing staff may be instructed to set the LS to “standby” when the cable is disconnected. However, such safety measure relies on consistent communication between bedside and circulating staff [10].

Rigorous methodological frameworks are essential to ensure patient safety and operational efficiency within the complexity of present-day healthcare delivery.

Historically, human factors work in laparoscopy focused on surgeons adapting to the laparoscopic environment rather than prioritizing user-centred design, as early research concentrated on ergonomics, cognitive load and the spatial layout of the operating room [11, 12]. Traditional safety analyses, such as Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), focused primarily on failure modes, or the specific ways in which a device or component ceases to perform its intended function (e.g., a hardware malfunction). Similarly, while OCHRA (Observational Clinical Human Reliability Assessment) provides a structured way to count active intraoperative errors, it may lack the prospective depth to predict latent risks before they occur. A significant gap exists in research regarding how a system fails the human. While the technical risks of light sources are known, little research has applied proactive frameworks to the light-delivery process. Central to addressing use-related hazards are human factors and ergonomics (HFE) approaches which focus on systemic resilience [13]. To deconstruct, analyze and optimize medical procedures, the combination of Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA), Systematic Human Error Reduction and Prediction Analysis (SHERPA), and the Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety (SEIPS) model may represent a powerful, yet previously unexploited, methodological triad [14, 15]. HTA breaks down complex clinical processes into a nested hierarchy of discrete tasks, uncovering the latent cognitive and physical demands placed on clinicians [16]. Building upon this map, SHERPA may categorize potential human errors—the unintentional deviations from a preferred process, such as omitted actions or incomplete checks—within the steps identified by HTA [14]. This allows practitioners to proactively identify vulnerabilities before they manifest as patient harm. Finally, the SEIPS model helps situate these tasks within the broader sociotechnical environment. This model posits that patient safety outcomes result from interactions between the person, tasks, technologies, physical environment, and organizational conditions [15]. This study represents the first known effort to synthesize HTA, SHERPA, and SEIPS into a comprehensive, predictive evaluation of light-delivery hazards in the surgical environment.

Methods

Aim of the study

Aim of this study is to conduct a proactive error analysis through HTA and SHERPA and a human factors assessment (HFA) of the light-delivery process during elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ELC). By adopting a systems approach, this investigation aims to identify specific problems and barriers that hinder task performance. In particular, analysis of light delivery to the laparoscope during ELC concentrates on the manipulation of the light chain outside of the patient’s body that is common to all laparoscopic procedures, so tasks specific to the surgeon’s use of the laparoscope intracorporeally are excluded.

Setting

Policlinico of Bari (Bari, Region of Puglia, Italy) is the second largest University Hospital in southern Italy, serving approximately five million people in two neighbouring Regions. The surgical team of the Academic Division of Surgery “V. Bonomo”, comprising 14 consultant surgeons, perform approximately 100 ELCs/year among other subspecialty procedures, with a standard surgical technique consolidated since the early days of laparoscopic surgery [17–19].

Study design

This prospective, observational study of process uses a dual-component, mixed-methods human factors design, consisting of task-level analysis (HTA and SHERPA) and a systems-level qualitative sociotechnical evaluation analysis (SEIPS).

Through this methodological framework, the study investigates exposure to potential hazards arising from the manipulation of laparoscopic surgery-related technology and identifies risks to patient safety inherent in the work process design. Additionally, the qualitative component examines the operating theatre work system to isolate factors that influence the probability of these latent risks occurring (Fig. 2).

The integrated human factors methodological workflow. The process begins with hierarchical task analysis (HTA) to deconstruct the surgical procedure, followed by systematic human error Reduction and Prediction analysis (SHERPA) to identify specific task-level risks. These are then contextualized using the systems Engineering Initiative for patient safety (SEIPS) model to uncover systemic latent triggers

A validated stepwise method was used to construct task analyses and HTAs in surgical processes [22]. The HTA yielded a comprehensive taxonomy of the light-delivery process, decomposed into five primary task steps and 56 nested subtasks (Table 2). In particular, each fundamental step (or primary task) of the process described in Table 2 was systematically decomposed into no more than five subtasks, and each subtask further deconstructed in the same fashion until this granular descent reached irreducible basic tasks, where further subdivision provided no additional analytical utility or clinical relevance. One HTA from the surgeon’s and another from a nurse’s point of view were constructed independently. Video recordings were reviewed by the lead author and a senior scrub nurse, who took notes; literature on task analysis [23] and other educational sources [24, 25] were used with data derived from the field observations to generate the two initial descriptions.

HTA of the process was then constructed by combining the task analysis from the surgeon perspective and one from the scrub nurse perspective. To quantify inter-rater agreement, the number of identical task descriptions was compared against the total number of tasks identified in both analyses. Finally, the HTA was reviewed by two consultant surgeons and a senior scrub nurse, who participated in the 20 ELCs but were not involved in the construction of HTA, using recorded videos of the procedures to aid review.

In case of disagreements between initial HTAs the analysts met with a third, senior consultant surgeon to review the disputed task steps

Systematic Human Error Reduction and Prediction Approach (SHERPA) [14] was applied to the results of the HTA to identify potential errors. Using the SHERPA taxonomy of errors (Action, Checking, Retrieval, Selection and Communication errors), the lead author reviewed the output of the HTA and identified and compiled a list of credible errors that could occur during performance of the subtasks. Results were audited by the two surgeons and the senior nurse involved in validating HTA, as subject matter experts (SME). A human error was considered a “credible error” if it was judged by SMEs as being plausible and having a realistic probability of occurrence within a specific task, prioritizing the most dangerous and likely failures. The criticality of each identified credible error was independently determined by SMEs by qualitatively cross-referencing probability (the frequency or likelihood) of the error occurring is with its severity (the magnitude of the clinical impact or patient harm), categorized on a scale from low (hardly ever occurs) to high (frequently occurs, >1/100 cases), to prioritize remediation strategies.

Remedy analysis was conducted within the SHERPA framework and followed a hierarchy-of-controls logic, moving from the most effective to the least effective remedy [14]. The process began with Equipment and Design changes aimed at making an error physically or logically impossible by designing the error out. If a full redesign was not feasible, practitioners looked to error-proofing by using interlocks and confirmations to catch mistakes before they cause harm. When technical solutions were exhausted, the focus shifted to organizational remedies (Procedures), which included checklists forcing a challenge-response protocol between workers. Training was considered the least effective remedy because it asks the human to adapt to a defective system, rather than repairing the system itself [11]. The final part of generating a remedy was checking whether a remedy created a new error and whether it was practical to introduce among clinicians actually doing the work.

Clinician stakeholders in the process under study were chosen for the interviews and were asked, between cases, what problems they faced with the use of laparoscopic light and how the system could be improved. Interviews explored how work system factors—such as equipment design (LS turret), environmental pressures (eg. operating list speed), and team roles, including multitasking or delegated connections, (eg “Who ensures the light source is in ‘Standby’ mode before the cable is physically removed?”)—interact to create latent hazards during light chain setup. Furthermore, interviews probed process variances by identifying specific moments—such as cable hand-offs or multitasking with electrosurgical tools—where deviations from protocol historically led to, or actively posed, risks of thermal injury or equipment failure (eg “Could you walk me through the steps of setting up, specifically noting any moments where the light source remains active unexpectedly?”, “Think of a time a hazard was narrowly avoided; can you remember at what point in the setup or dismantling did the ‘near-miss’ occur, and what triggered it?”).

Hospital documentation was reviewed, including operating room schedules, formal surgical checklists and nursing protocols (to see if specific roles for the LC cable management were explicitly mentioned), and the hospital’s internal safety database for any documented cases of thermal burns, equipment fires or near-miss reports. System stakeholders (two consultant surgeons and a senior scrub nurse) were involved in categorizing data in the SEIPS buckets (eg. Tools, Tasks, etc). For environmental analysis, work system boundaries were identified; the distance between operators and the light source stack was mapped, and sources of noise (suction, alarms music etc) were identified. During observations, a tally was used to record process interruptions (telephone calls, door openings, missing equipment, or unrelated conversation).

An HTA–SHERPA–SEIPS workflow was expected to enable a comprehensive safety evaluation: while HTA and SHERPA provide a granular, quantitative map of what can go wrong at the task level, the SEIPS model provides the qualitative context of why those errors are likely to occur in the real-world operating theatre environment.

The analysis of the laparoscopic light chain management revealed a complex socio-technical system characterized by high cognitive load and environmental stressors. The work system is decomposed as follows:

Environment and Layout

The procedures occurred in a 42 m2 well-lit theatre. Ceiling-mounted pendulums (articulated arms) effectively cleared the floor of cable hazards, while the spatial layout required the Circulating Nurse (CN) to navigate between the “turret” (light source/LS) and the operating table. This physical distance necessitates clear verbal communication, which was frequently undermined by ambient noise levels. Sources of auditory distraction included surgical instrumentation, anesthesia alarms, background music, and high-density conversation during critical transition phases (setup and closure). Two-thirds of stakeholders identified distracting noise as a major barrier (acoustic distraction). At the end of the case, noise peaks due to anesthesia reversal, equipment removal, and conversation. This “acoustic clutter” leads to missed verbal cues regarding equipment status.

Tools and technology

Standardization deficits were observed in the user interface (UI) of the laparoscopic camera handles (Fig. 3): varied button layouts across different camera models increase cognitive load and may trigger “mode errors” such as inadvertently activating white balance instead of light intensity (SHERPA error A6, Right action on wrong object), or making the wrong selection during the system function check due to confusing menu navigation (SHERPA S1, wrong selection).

Different layout of the remote control buttons on the camera controlling the light source (LS): buttons controlling the LS from the laparoscopic camera handle may present different layout (error mode: A6 “right action on wrong object”). Surgeons reported difficulty navigating these remote-control buttons, which increases cognitive load. SHERPA error categories may be: A6 – right action on wrong object (the lack of UI standardization across different camera models leads to the surgeon pressing a button intended for one function (e.g., white balance) while actually activating another, or vice versa) and S1 – wrong selection made (due to the confusing UI layout, the operator may select the wrong menu item or function from the camera’s remote-control options during the “system function check” phase)

On the LS turret (Fig. 4), the similarity, proximity and lack of tactile differentiation between the insufflation and anti-fog buttons created a “mode error” risk. This increases the probability of the light source remaining active at high intensity unintentionally; for instance, a circulating nurse may mistakenly toggle the anti-fog function instead of standby (SHERPA error A6, Right action on wrong object), or omit the critical standby safety step entirely (error A1, Operation omitted) due to the lack of a clear system feedback loop.

Laparoscopic light source (LS). Tasks and workflow

Hierarchical mapping revealed that light chain configuration is not a linear process but a set of overlapping workflows (non-linear sequences), with connection of the LC to the laparoscope occurring at varying stages—sometimes before turret connection, sometimes after. This lack of a standardized “sequence of play” increases the risk of the light being left active while the cable is disconnected. The CN must simultaneously manage the LS turret, electrosurgical pedals, and suction device (concurrent tasks). This multitasking, coupled with the “white balance” requirement of the laparoscopic camera setup (which necessitates the light being on during connection), creates a narrow window of risk where the light cable may be active while resting on surgical drapes.

Person and team dynamics

The process involves a high degree of task-sharing between the Operating Surgeon (OS), Assistant Surgeon (AS), Circulating Nurse (CN), and Scrub Nurse (SN) and a fluid “hand-off” of responsibility. While the Operating Surgeon (OS) nominally leads the setup, the task is frequently delegated to the AS or CN to expedite the start of a case. This can lead to role ambiguity regarding who is responsible for engaging “standby” mode after the light chain is connected and the laparoscopic camera is white balanced. Surgeons reported difficulty navigating the laparoscopic remote control camera buttons (cognitive load). Consequently, control of the light state is often delegated to the CN, who is already managing multiple nursing workflows. The entire setup process is also supervised by SN, who at the same time though is tasked with preparing and testing the power surgical instruments (electric scalpel, bipolar forceps, ultrasound shears) and suction device, and assisting the OS while starting the case (eg pass swabs, knife). At the end of the case, the physical departure of the OS to prepare for the next patient shifts the safety burden entirely to the CN and AS during the high-noise emergence phase of anesthesia.

Organizational factors

The study process took place several times during a 10-hour (8am-6pm) operating session. Significant production pressure to complete the operating list was identified as a latent hazard. The drive to minimize turnover time between cases encourages staff to perform connections and disconnections during periods of peak activity and noise, directly contributing to the “missing of verbal cues” and bit-sentences reported in interviews.

Ultimately, these work system factors—comprising systemic environmental noise and non-standardized technology—collectively create the latent conditions that facilitate the transition from theoretical hazards to active task-level errors. By mapping these sociotechnical barriers back to the procedural steps identified via SHERPA, these insights were integrated to highlight the most critical areas to guide the development of safer system designs.

Integration of work system observations with SHERPA findings

To enhance the predictive accuracy of the SHERPA framework, qualitative data from the SEIPS-based work system analysis were used to calibrate error probability and criticality. Specifically, stakeholder responses regarding “near-miss” events and “hazard triggers” provided the empirical basis for assigning frequency ratings (L = Low, hardly ever occurs; M = Medium, 1/1000 to 1/100; H = High, >1/100) to identified error modes.

Factors that directly influenced the propensity for high-criticality errors were:

-environmental triggers: of the 12 errors identified as having a medium-to-high risk of patient harm, nine involved omissions or incorrect operations (75%), including tasks where criticality for error was the highest (1.4, 2.1 and 5.1). The SEIPS analysis identified distracting noise as the primary systemic trigger for these specific error modes, particularly during the high-activity phases of setup and dismantling (clinicians referred that when stressed by noise, they may experience “slips of action.”);

-organizational barriers: four critical steps (1.4, 3.3.1.2, 3.3.1.3, and 5.1) exhibited an increased propensity for omission errors (33%). The work system analysis traced this to role ambiguity (clinician believes the step has already been performed by someone else or that the “sequence” has moved past that point) and a lack of standardized task sequencing (Organization) during the device setup phase.

Discussion

HTA and SHERPA have been successfully applied to assess various medical procedures, including medication administration, anesthesia [26–28], surgical operations [24, 29], and complex device management [30].

In this study, the SHERPA output pinpointed the setup and connection of the light chain (Steps 1.4, 2.1, and 2.5) as the operations with the highest criticality. This is directly supported by the physical reality of thermal transfer, where light cables exhibit a quicker and greater magnitude of heat gain compared to the laparoscope itself [5].

As a behavioral tool, SHERPA proved excellent at identifying operator-focused errors, such as the failure to engage standby mode or connect the light cable (LC) to the light source (LS), both of which may leave a hot, active LC unattended on the operating table.

The remedy analysis prioritized long-term systemic resilience over temporary human-centric fixes. The primary recommendation—transitioning to LED-based technology—derives from findings that current Xenon systems exceed the threshold for immediate patient injury [31]. While equipment modification eliminates the heat hazard, introducing new technology can create novel systemic problems [32]. New devices must adhere to design standards [33], and users must be integrated into the implementation process [34] and trained on potential hazards [35], particularly as surgeons may lack formal training for new technology [36]. Recommendations for equipment changes must therefore balance the probability of error against the severity of patient and operator safety consequences.

Beyond equipment changes, we propose a specific process redesign for Step 1 (LS preset) and Step 2 (Hardware connection): re-sequencing the workflow so that power-on occurs only after all connections are finalized. This eliminates the “active” window for burn risks during setup. However, altering roles or team communication may significantly and unexpectedly alter the way the members of a surgical team interact, and potentially induce new forms of error [15]. As an example, while the proposed redesign ideally removes an important stressor like a live fiber optic cable during setup, new errors might be introduced, as “false-ready” connections (connection assumed secure and only partially seated), or “assumed power-down” state (the team mistakenly believe the light is off because it hasn’t been “turned on” yet for the current step, leading to complacency when handling the cable). Consequently, the efficacy of this re-sequencing ideally requires empirical validation through high-fidelity simulation [37].

While SHERPA remedy analysis suggested checklists and training to reduce variability [38], our SEIPS work system analysis [R3] uncovered “invisible” hazards that linear tools miss [39]. The SEIPS identified unclear sequencing and role ambiguity as primary drivers of cognitive load. While checklists can reduce internal memory load, they also increase procedural load and may still be skipped due to environmental interruptions [33].

The chief center of complaint by clinicians was distracting noise, with two-thirds of stakeholders identifying it as a major barrier. Noise conditions fatigue and cognitive overload, affecting task performance and the ability to sustain further stressors. This demonstrates the iterative nature of the SEIPS model: interactions in the work system “loop back” to affect practitioner behavior in a continuous feedback loop. Specifically, Environment (noise) and Organization (overlapping workflows) factors were primary triggers for the omission errors in steps 1.4 and 5.1. The Circulating Nurse (CN) often manages multiple workflows simultaneously—such as positioning pedals while configuring the turret—explaining the high propensity for ‘Right Action on Wrong Object’ (A6) errors.

Noise and unclear sequencing have an additive effect, diverting energy from task execution to task deciphering [40]. This redistribution of resources leads to communication breakdowns [41, 42]. Distractions from equipment, alarms, or music act as cognitive stressors that break a worker’s “flow,” causing “Action” and “Check” categories to suffer first, resulting in omitted or incomplete operations.Effective noise-reduction strategies include structured conversation at key points of an operation [43], as in the “Sterile Cockpit” concept borrowed from aviation, where all non-essential conversation is banned during critical phases (induction, incision, and counting).Visitors are typically briefed by the circulating nurse or lead surgeon on the Sterile Cockpit phases. To cut through the inevitable background noise of a busy operating theatre, communication must be standardized so the listener can “predict” the information format. Closed-loop communication remains the gold standard, ensuring messages are heard and understood using standardized terminology for equipment (“Light on, 70%” “Confirming, light on 70%” “Correct”) [44]. Strategies of environmental design, as swapping traditional sound-reflecting ceiling tiles with high-quality sound-absorbing ceiling panels [45] may offer a path for systemic improvement.

The statistical power of this study is limited by the number of observations. The Hawthorne effect may play a role in face-to-face interviews. The SHERPA framework’s reliance on the subjective judgment of both the primary analyst and the auditing experts introduces a risk of inter-analyst variability, potentially impacting the reproducibility of the findings. These factors, alongside the absence of quantitative outcome measures and the potential of bias deriving from the observer-analyst role, suggest that the results possess limited generalizability to other hospital environments with differing organizational structure.

However, the combination of these two methods bridges the gap between systemic conditions, where SEIPS provides the “Why” and the “Context”, and specific behavioral errors, where SHERPA provides the “How” and the “Point of Failure”.

This hybrid risk-assessment framework provides a scalable blueprint for ‘proactive resilience’ in surgical environments. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to validate this approach across diverse surgical specialties, particularly in high-acuity settings where cognitive load and environmental noise are most volatile [46]. Future research might utilize simulated operating environments to stress-test process redesigns intended to eliminate thermal hazards against the environmental stressors identified in this study, specifically distracting noise and high cognitive task loads. Such simulation-based trials would allow for a preemptive analysis of new protocols, ensuring that process redesigns do not inadvertently create new bottlenecks in team communication or psychomotor performance. Iteratively refining these interventions in a controlled, risk-free setting might help surgical systems move toward a more resilient ‘safety-by-design’ architecture that accounts for both the granularity of human error and the complexity of the systemic context.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first proactive error and contextual systems analysis of the light delivery process in laparoscopic surgery, specifically targeting potential risks to patient safety. Some of these risks can be traced to process tasks that are particularly prone to error occurrence. However, both risk judgment and recommendations for correction of potentially hazardous steps may lack critical information derived from work environment variables: SEIPS analysis shows that errors identified in SHERPA are, in reality, patient safety hazards and that work system factors—specifically organizational pressures and distracting noise—are context factors that may increase the probability of a hazard occurring. While this study focused on light chain management, there is significant potential for scaling this hybrid SHERPA-SEIPS methodology to other high-criticality laparoscopic processes, such as pneumoperitoneum maintenance or electrosurgical tool exchanges. By systematically mapping the behavioral “how” (SHERPA) alongside the environmental and organizational “why” (SEIPS), healthcare systems can develop a standardized repository of ergonomic interventions. Future research should focus on simulation testing of these redesigned workflows to validate their efficacy. Such a proactive, multi-level approach is essential for evolving from reactive error management toward the design of inherently resilient surgical systems that protect both the patient and the practitioner from the predictable limits of human cognition.
