Family-related Factors Associated With Readmission Among Children and Adolescents With Psychiatric Disorders
Authors: Mohammad Sabzi Khoshnami, Mohammad Rajabi, Fardin Alipour, Mohsen Vahedi, Fatemeh Gholami Jam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents is a major concern in mental health services and is influenced by clinical, familial, and demographic factors. However, the contribution of family-related variables remains insufficiently understood, particularly in non-Western contexts.Methods:This cross-sectional study included 78 children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders and their primary caregivers. We collected data using standardized questionnaires assessing caregiver burden, parenting styles, and parent–child relationship quality, along with demographic and clinical variables. We used Poisson regression to identify predictors of psychiatric readmission.Results:Family-related variables were significantly associated with psychiatric readmission. Higher caregiver burden, particularly emotional burden, was associated with increased readmission risk. Authoritarian parenting and greater parent–child conflict were also linked to higher readmission, whereas emotional closeness and permissive parenting showed protective associations. Contrary to conventional expectations, authoritative parenting was not significantly associated with readmission in the final model. Most demographic variables were also not significantly associated with readmission.Conclusions:Psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents appears to be strongly influenced by family emotional functioning. Notably, permissive parenting was associated with lower readmission risk, whereas authoritative parenting was not significantly associated with outcomes. These findings underscore the need for family-centered and culturally informed approaches in child and adolescent mental health care.Keywords: Caregiver burden, child and adolescent psychiatry, parenting styles, psychiatric readmission

Psychiatric disorders are among the leading causes of disability and diminished quality of life in children and adolescents, with onset frequently occurring during the early years of development. 1 Over recent decades, the prevalence of these disorders has increased substantially, particularly among child and adolescent populations, resulting in a growing demand for psychiatric services and higher rates of hospitalization. 2 Despite the importance of initial hospitalization, one of the major challenges facing mental health care systems is psychiatric readmission following discharge. Readmission is widely regarded as a key indicator of the quality of mental health services and the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions. It is associated with substantial individual, familial, and economic consequences.3,4 Beyond imposing considerable financial burdens on healthcare systems, recurrent hospitalization has been linked to disruptions in the academic, social, and emotional functioning of children and adolescents. 5 Evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of children and adolescents discharged from psychiatric hospitals experience rehospitalization within the months or first year following discharge. 3 Although clinical and demographic variables play an important role in predicting psychiatric readmission, growing evidence suggests that family-related factors and the quality of interactions within the child’s living environment are among the major determinants of the persistence, recurrence, and exacerbation of psychiatric symptoms.6,7 According to Bowen’s Family Systems Theory, psychological problems cannot be understood in isolation from the familial relational context; rather, they emerge and persist through emotional and interactional patterns within the family system. 8 In this regard, parental caregiving burden, parenting styles, and the quality of the parent–child relationship have been identified as critical family-related factors associated with mental health outcomes in children and adolescents. Previous studies have demonstrated that increased caregiving burden among parents and caregivers may contribute to psychological distress, emotional exhaustion, and reduced caregiving capacity, thereby increasing the risk of symptom relapse and repeated utilization of inpatient psychiatric services.9,10 Furthermore, maladaptive parenting styles, such as authoritarian, rejecting, or overcontrolling approaches, as well as conflictual parent–child relationships, have been associated with greater emotional and behavioral difficulties, impaired emotion regulation, and the persistence of psychiatric disorders among children and adolescents.7,11 Nevertheless, despite the recognized importance of these factors, relatively few studies have simultaneously examined the role of family-related variables in psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents, particularly in non-Western and developing societies.

Rationale for the Study

Psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents remains a major challenge, yet evidence regarding the role of family-related factors is limited, particularly in non-Western populations. This study is the first, to our knowledge, to simultaneously examine caregiver burden, parent–child relationship quality, and parenting styles in relation to psychiatric readmission in an Iranian clinical sample, providing evidence that parenting patterns may differ from those reported in community-based studies.

Hypothesis

Family-related factors, including caregiver burden, parenting styles, and parent–child relationship quality, are significantly associated with psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders.

Aims

To investigate the association between family-related factors and psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study before participant recruitment. We obtained informed consent from all participating primary caregivers before data collection. Participants who reported concerns regarding their child’s mental health or caregiving challenges were advised to discuss these concerns with the treating mental health professionals responsible for the child’s care.

Study Design

This study was designed as an applied, cross-sectional correlational investigation. Data were collected using a survey-based approach to examine the associations between family-related factors and psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders.

Study Population and Sampling

The study population consisted of families of children and adolescents aged 7–18 years with a diagnosed psychiatric disorder and a history of more than one psychiatric hospitalization within one year during 2023, 2024, or the first four months of 2025. We recruited participants from psychiatric hospitals in Tehran Province, Iran.

Eligible participants were primary caregivers, defined as first-degree family members with a close, continuous caregiving role responsible for providing physical, psychological, and social support to the patient.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (a) being the primary caregiver of the patient, defined as a self-reported first-degree family member with a continuous caregiving role; (b) having a child or adolescent aged 7–18 years with a diagnosed psychiatric disorder and a history of at least two psychiatric hospitalizations within the past year (2023–2025), confirmed through hospital records and caregiver report, a criterion based on evidence indicating that approximately one-third of youth psychiatric inpatients experience readmission within one year after discharge; (c) absence of any self-reported or clinically diagnosed psychiatric disorder in the caregiver 4 ; and (d) absence of any self-reported or clinically diagnosed psychiatric disorder in the caregiver. Participants were excluded if they (a) declined to participate in the study, or (b) provided incomplete data or were unable to complete the study instruments.

Sample Size and Sampling Method

Given the limited size of the accessible population, we initially used a census sampling approach and included all eligible cases identified during the study period. We initially identified 96 participants from Razi Psychiatric Hospital and 38 participants from Roozbeh Psychiatric Hospital. However, we subsequently applied several exclusions due to factors such as patient death, lack of an available responsible caregiver (e.g., children under institutional welfare care), incorrect or missing contact information, non-response to follow-up attempts, and duplication of cases across the two hospitals. After applying these exclusion criteria, the final analytical sample comprised 78 caregivers.

Study Setting

The study was conducted in two major public psychiatric hospitals in Tehran, Iran: Razi Hospital and Roozbeh Hospital. These facilities were selected because they provide most child and adolescent psychiatric inpatient services in the province, have more pediatric psychiatric beds than private centers, maintain comprehensive medical records, and follow rigorous ethical and supervisory standards. Findings from public hospital settings are also more generalizable to the broader population, as most patients receive care in these centers.

Data Collection Instruments

Demographic Checklist

A researcher-developed demographic checklist collected background information on both the child/adolescent and the caregiver. Variables included age, sex, educational status, psychiatric diagnosis, history of hospitalization, caregiver relationship to the patient, and other relevant sociodemographic characteristics.

Parent–Child Relationship Scale

The quality of the parent–child relationship was assessed using the Parent–Child Relationship Scale, 12 which evaluates caregivers’ perceptions of their emotional relationship with the child across the dimensions of conflict, closeness, and dependency. The questionnaire consists of 33 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of each relational dimension. The Persian version of the scale has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Iranian populations, with reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.84 for conflict, 0.69 for closeness, and 0.80 for the total scale. 13

Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview

Caregiver burden was measured using the Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI) 14 The ZBI is a 24-item instrument designed to assess the perceived psychological, physical, social, emotional, and time-related burden experienced by caregivers of individuals with chronic conditions. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (nearly always), with higher scores reflecting greater caregiver burden. Previous Iranian studies have reported high internal consistency for the Persian version of the instrument, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of approximately 0.90. 15

Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire

Parenting styles were assessed using the Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire based on Baumrind’s theoretical framework. 16 This 30-item instrument evaluates three parenting styles, including authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive parenting, with each dimension comprising 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Subscale scores are calculated separately, and higher scores indicate greater endorsement of the corresponding parenting style. Previous studies conducted in Iranian populations have demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for the questionnaire. 17

Psychiatric Diagnosis

The study population consisted of families of children and adolescents aged 7–18 years with psychiatric disorders diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) 18 and a history of more than one psychiatric hospitalization within 1 year.

Data Collection Procedure

After authorization from the participating hospitals, we identified eligible cases by reviewing medical records in the documentation units of the selected psychiatric hospitals. The study then coordinated with hospital social work departments to facilitate communication with caregivers. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews.

Statistical Analysis

We analyzed data using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, summarized participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics and study variables. Because psychiatric readmission was treated as a count outcome, we used Poisson regression to examine associations between family-related factors, including caregiver burden, parenting styles, and parent–child relationship dimensions, and the frequency of psychiatric readmissions. We estimated regression coefficients, standard errors, and significance levels for each predictor variable. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p value of <.05.

Results

Table 1 summarizes participant characteristics. The mean age of children and adolescents was 13.75 years, and the average number of psychiatric readmissions during the previous year was 3.60. Most caregivers were parents, with mothers accounting for the largest proportion of respondents. Conduct disorder and anxiety disorders were the most frequently reported psychiatric diagnoses. Overall, the sample was characterized by repeated psychiatric hospitalizations and predominantly low-to-middle socioeconomic status. Table 2 shows that caregiver burden and parent–child relational factors are significantly associated with psychiatric readmission. Emotional and time-dependent burden exhibited the strongest positive associations, indicating higher caregiver strain is linked to increased readmission. Among parenting styles, authoritarian parenting was positively associated with readmission, while permissive parenting showed a significant negative association; authoritative parenting was not significant. In parent–child relationships, conflict was positively related to readmission, and closeness showed a negative association, whereas dependency was not significant. Overall, greater caregiver burden, higher relational conflict, and more controlling parenting are associated with increased likelihood of psychiatric readmission. Table 3 presents the results of Poisson regression analyses examining the association between family-related factors and psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders. The findings indicate that caregiver burden significantly predicts readmission, with stronger effects for emotional and time-related dimensions, suggesting that sustained psychological strain plays a central role in increasing readmission risk. Regarding parenting styles, more controlling and restrictive approaches were associated with a higher likelihood of readmission, whereas more permissive parenting showed a protective effect by reducing the risk of rehospitalization. For parent–child relationship quality, higher conflict was associated with increased readmission risk, while greater closeness showed a protective association. Dependency did not demonstrate a statistically significant relationship with readmission. Overall, the results highlight that psychiatric readmission is primarily influenced by caregiver psychological strain and the quality of family interactions, particularly emotional.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that caregiver burden, parenting styles, and parent–child relationship quality were significantly associated with psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders. The association between caregiver burden and readmission is consistent with previous research indicating that caregiver distress may adversely affect family functioning, treatment continuity, and the management of children with psychiatric disorders.10–18 From the perspective of Bowen’s family systems theory, persistent caregiver anxiety may increase emotional tension within the family system and contribute to vulnerability to symptom recurrence. 8 The finding that emotional burden was more predictive than physical burden further suggests that psychological exhaustion may be particularly relevant to families coping with recurrent psychiatric hospitalization. Interpret the findings on parenting styles within both the clinical and sociocultural context of the study. Previous studies have consistently reported that authoritarian parenting is associated with greater emotional and behavioral difficulties, poorer emotion regulation, and increased risk of adverse psychiatric outcomes among children and adolescents.19,20 Consistent with this literature, authoritarian parenting was associated with an increased likelihood of psychiatric readmission in the present study. In contrast, permissive parenting was associated with a lower risk of readmission, whereas authoritative parenting was not significantly associated with the outcome after adjustment for other family-related variables. This finding differs from the traditional developmental literature, which generally regards authoritative parenting as the most adaptive parenting style. It also differs from at least one previous clinical study in which permissive parenting was associated with increased readmission risk. Indeed, permissive parenting was reported to be associated with an increased, rather than decreased, risk of psychiatric readmission. 21 Direct evidence supporting a protective role of permissive parenting against psychiatric readmission therefore remains limited. Accordingly, the inverse association observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence that permissive parenting is generally protective against psychiatric readmission. From a family systems perspective, prolonged family stress can diminish the adaptive capacity of otherwise healthy interactional patterns, as family functioning becomes increasingly dominated by chronic anxiety and emotional strain.8,22 Parenting behaviors in some families may therefore reflect adaptive responses to ongoing caregiving demands rather than stable developmental characteristics. One plausible explanation is that the functional meaning of parenting behaviors may change under conditions of chronic psychiatric illness, recurrent hospitalization, and substantial caregiver burden. In such circumstances, lower parental control combined with emotional flexibility may, in some families, reduce interpersonal confrontation and emotional escalation, particularly when the child is already experiencing substantial psychological distress. This interpretation aligns with cross-cultural research showing that associations between parenting styles and child and adolescent outcomes vary across cultural and societal contexts, including differences related to individualism and collectivism. 23 Importantly, this explanation does not imply that permissive parenting is generally advantageous. Rather, within families experiencing severe and persistent psychiatric stress, a less controlling parenting pattern may sometimes reflect an adaptive response to the immediate emotional demands of the family system and may be associated with lower interpersonal tension. This possibility may be particularly relevant in collectivist contexts such as Iran, where close family interdependence and prolonged parental involvement are common. Thus, the observed association may reflect interactions among parenting behavior, caregiver burden, family stress, and sociocultural context rather than a general protective effect of permissive parenting.

Regarding parent–child relationships, greater conflict was associated with a higher likelihood of psychiatric readmission, whereas emotional closeness appeared to have a protective role. These findings are consistent with attachment theory, which emphasizes that secure emotional bonds promote adaptive emotion regulation and psychological resilience across development. 24 Previous research similarly indicates that supportive family relationships facilitate treatment engagement, improve emotional regulation, and contribute to better clinical outcomes among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders.25,26 In contrast, dependency was not independently associated with readmission, suggesting that emotional dependency alone may be less clinically relevant than the overall emotional climate of the parent–child relationship.

Given the cross-sectional design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Further longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether changes in caregiver burden and family relational processes are associated with subsequent changes in psychiatric readmission risk.

Strengths

This study simultaneously examined caregiver burden, parenting styles, and parent–child relationship quality within a single multivariable model, providing a comprehensive assessment of family-related factors associated with psychiatric readmission. In addition, the study was conducted in a clinical sample from Iran, contributing evidence from a non-Western cultural context that remains underrepresented in the literature on child and adolescent psychiatric readmission. The findings also further highlight the importance of considering illness context and sociocultural influences when interpreting associations between parenting patterns and psychiatric outcomes, particularly in families experiencing substantial and persistent psychiatric stress.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are warranted to confirm these findings and further clarify the role of family-related factors in psychiatric readmission.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that psychiatric readmission among children and adolescents with psychiatric disorders is associated with family emotional and relational processes, particularly caregiver burden, parent–child conflict, and parenting patterns. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. The findings highlight the importance of considering family systems and sociocultural context when understanding psychiatric readmission. Family-centered and culturally responsive approaches that address caregiver burden and parent–child relationships may therefore support continuity of care and reduce recurrent hospitalization. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether modifying these family-related factors can reduce readmission risk.
