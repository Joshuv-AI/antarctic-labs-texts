Novel Biomarker for Rheumatoid Arthritis: Serum Peptidylarginine Deiminase 4‐Potentiating Activity in an Anti‐Citrullinated Protein Antibody‐Positive At‐Risk Population
Authors: Marta Kaminska, Vijay Joshua, Ewa Bielecka, Tomasz Kantyka, Maja Chrobak, Karin Lundberg, Aase Hensvold, Jan Potempa, Piotr Mydel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnzymes like peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) generate citrullinated epitopes that are crucial in triggering and driving the anti‐citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) response in rheumatoid arthritis. Here, we assessed whether serum PAD4‐potentiating activity is associated with subsequent arthritis development among ACPA‐positive individuals during the pre‐clinical phase. The study included 267 ACPA‐positive individuals from the Karolinska Risk‐RA cohort, among whom 101 (37.8%) developed arthritis within the observational period (range 22–60 months). We measured the ability of serum to potentiate PAD4 activity by quantifying the generation of citrullinated products using high‐performance HPLC. Study participants were then classified as PAD4act‐positive or ‐negative, and PAD4 status analysed in relation to onset of arthritis, clinical characteristics (ESR, CRP and DAS28) and established risk factors (HLA‐DRB genotype, smoking). PAD4act‐positivity was identified in 35 individuals, with a significant proportion (21, p = 0.0049) developing arthritis. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that individuals with PAD4act‐positivity had increased rate of progressing to arthritis (p = 0.0022). Additionally, PAD4act‐positive individuals were more likely to possess at least one copy of the HLA‐DRB shared epitope allele (p = 0.045). Notably, PAD4act‐positivity did not associate with pre‐clinical CRP, ESR, DAS28, or smoking status. These findings suggest that serum PAD4‐potentiating activity may help stratify arthritis risk and time to onset among ACPA‐positive at‐risk individuals.Keywords: ACPA, arthritis, biomarker, PAD4

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by chronic joint inflammation and systemic manifestations, driven by multifactorial etiologies [1, 2]. Despite extensive research into environmental and genetic risk factors—such as HLA‐DRB gene variants, smoking and comorbidities like periodontitis—effective curative treatments for RA remain elusive [3, 4]. Current management strategies primarily focus on controlling inflammation and slowing disease progression through disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) like methotrexate and biologics such as anti‐TNFα therapy [5]. However, delayed initiation of these treatments often results in irreversible joint damage, significantly affecting patients' quality of life. The marginal increase in disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) and the overall prevalence of RA since 1990 underscore the urgent need for improved diagnostic tools and personalized treatment strategies [6, 7].

In 2010, the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recognized anti‐citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) as key biomarkers for RA [8]. Notably, these autoantibodies can be detected up to two decades before clinical symptoms emerge, however, their absence does not exclude disease [9, 10]. This discovery has shifted research focus towards the role of aberrant citrullination, particularly within the ACPA‐positive RA subpopulation, in driving disease pathology [11].

Citrullination, a post‐translational modification of arginine residues, is catalysed by five mammalian peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs): PAD1, PAD2, PAD3, PAD4 and PAD6 [12], with PAD4 being particularly implicated in RA pathogenesis [13]. These enzymes are integral to various physiological processes, including epithelial differentiation, myelin formation and the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps [14, 15]. However, aberrant PAD activity has also been linked to a range of pathologies, including cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders and cancer [16, 17, 18]. Given their broad substrate specificity—targeting both intracellular (e.g., histones, enolase) and cell surface/extracellular proteins (e.g., vimentin, fibrinogen)—PADs may play a crucial role in breaking immune tolerance and driving ACPA production [13, 19].

Despite extensive research, it remains uncertain whether the autoimmune response in RA is driven by specific citrullinated peptides or by a more generalized increase in citrullination. This ambiguity is highlighted by the connection between RA and periodontitis. Exogenous PADs from the oral pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis may contribute to the autoimmune process by facilitating citrullination [20, 21]. Studies such as those by Brewer et al. have shown that oral mucosal breaks result in oral bacteremias, potentially triggering an ACPA‐mediated immune response [22].

ACPAs exhibit considerable heterogeneity, targeting a range of proteins including fibrin, filaggrin and enolase [23]. This diversity in ACPA specificity—detectable even years before the onset of RA—supports the hypothesis that widespread citrullination, rather than a single antigenic event, may drive RA pathogenesis. Therefore, assessing PAD expression and activity could provide critical insights into disease onset and progression.

In response to this need, our group developed and optimized an enzymatic assay using a short fluorescent substrate to quantify PAD4 activity. The assay quantifies the capacity of serum factors to modulate a fixed amount of recombinant PAD4 in vitro, rather than measuring endogenous PAD4 concentration in serum. Using this platform, we previously showed that serum‐mediated modulation of PAD4 activity is a feature of newly diagnosed, DMARD‐naïve RA patients compared with age‐ and sex‐matched controls [24].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether serum PAD4‐potentiating activity can serve as a predictive biomarker in ACPA‐positive individuals during the pre‐clinical phase. Our goal was to identify individuals at elevated risk of developing RA. By expanding the repertoire of predictive biomarkers, our research introduces a potential new diagnostic tool and lays the groundwork for more refined and personalized RA management strategies, which could ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Ethics

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board Stockholm, Sweden (biobank nr 914). Patient data, clinical examination details at baseline and follow‐up (Figure 1) have been presented elsewhere [25].

Schematic representation of the RRA study.

Cohort Characteristics: Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria and Sample Collection

A total of 267 individuals from the Karolinska Risk‐RA (RRA) prospective research programme and cohort were included in this study [25]. Study participants were ACPA‐positive, had reported musculoskeletal complaints but no signs of arthritis and were not diagnosed with rheumatic diseases previously. After giving full, informed and written consent, participants were examined and donated peripheral blood samples at baseline. Blood was allowed to clot, and then spun down. Serum free of morphological elements and clotting factors was collected, aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use.

Participation in this prospective research programme includes at least three annual follow‐up visits, until clinical arthritis develops, or at least 3 years have passed. Progression towards arthritis was observed in 101 individuals (38%), after a median of 14 months of follow‐up (IQR: 6–27); n = 91 (90%) individuals had classifiable RA according to the 2010 criteria at arthritis onset.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients were not involved in the design of the study, the conduct of the research, or the preparation of this manuscript.

PAD4 Expression and Purification

Genetic sequence encoding human PAD4 with N‐terminal HisTag was cloned into pET‐16b plasmid. Escherichia coli BL21 was transformed with PAD4‐plasmid, and expression was induced at the log growth phase (optical density600nm = 0.6) by adding isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at the final concentration of 0.5 mM. Cultures were incubated for 16 h at 25°C, then cells were centrifuged, lysed and the soluble protein fraction was loaded onto Ni‐Sepharose Fast Flow resin (Cytiva, USA). HisTag‐PAD4 fractions were further purified via ion exchange (MonoQ 4.6/100PE, Cytiva) and gel filtration chromatography (HiLoad Superdex S75 16/600; Cytiva). Recombinant PAD4 was finally concentrated, activity determined using N‐α‐benzoyl‐L‐arginine ethyl ester hydrochloride colorimetric substrate and frozen in storage buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5 with 0.25 M NaCl, 3% glycerol).

PAD4 Activating Potential

Serum PAD4‐activating potential at inclusion/baseline was measured using an in‐house assay as previously described [24]. In brief, 20 μL serum was mixed with a fixed amount of recombinant PAD4 (1 mU), 1 mM substrate (Dansyl‐Gly‐Arg), 2 mM o‐phenanthroline and 10 mM DTT in reaction buffer (100 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 10 mM CaCl2; final volume 30 μL) and incubated for 60 min at 37°C. Because a fixed amount of recombinant PAD4 is added to every reaction, this assay quantifies serum‐mediated modulation of PAD4 catalysis rather than endogenous PAD4 concentration or activity in the sample. The reaction was stopped with 40 μL 5% ice‐cold trichloroacetic acid, incubated on ice for 15 min to precipitate proteins, followed by addition of 35 μL 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). After centrifugation (10 min, 21,000 g, 4°C), supernatants were transferred to vials for HPLC analysis (Shimadzu). Fifty μl was injected onto an AERIS Peptide XB‐C18 4.6/150 mm column (Phenomenex). Products were eluted using a 10%–35% gradient of phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.08% TFA) over 20 min. Dansyl‐labelled substrate and citrullinated product were detected at 330 nm excitation and 530 nm emission. Product peak area was normalized to a reference serum included across runs and reported as serum‐mediated potentiation of PAD4 activity, reflecting the net effect of soluble serum components on PAD4 catalysis. Each sample was measured in duplicate across two independent analytical runs; normalized values were averaged for downstream analyses.

Statistical Analysis

The PAD4 activity data was analysed both as raw and transformed (log10) whenever necessary. PAD4act‐positivity was defined as mean + 1 standard deviation (SD). Mann–Whitney U Test was used to compare the two sample groups. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare time to incident arthritis between PAD4act groups; participants without incident arthritis were right‐censored at their last recorded follow‐up visit. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The correlations were calculated using the Spearman Rank Correlation test. Statistical analyses were performed with R Studio and GraphPad Prism Version 10.2.3, and the level of significance was set as 0.05.

No Difference in PAD4 Activity Modulation Based on Arthritis Progression

Given previous studies suggesting elevated serum PAD4 activity potentiation in early RA patients [24], we analysed whether this trend extended to the pre‐clinical phase. Serum samples from 267 RRA study participants were incubated with exogenous recombinant PAD4 and a fluorescent substrate, and the citrullinated product was quantified using reverse‐phase HPLC. The mean value of all 267 samples analysed was 13,678+/−5527 arbitrary units (AU, product peak area under curve). Participants were divided into two groups: those who were not diagnosed with arthritis during the ≥ 3‐year follow‐up period (n = 166) and those diagnosed with arthritis at any point during this time (n = 101). While the overall mean serum PAD4‐potentiating activity was slightly higher in the arthritis group, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.45, Mann–Whitney U Test; Figure 2).

PAD4 activity potentiation remains at a comparable level in pre‐clinical RA patients (arthritis) and non‐rheumatic subjects (no arthritis).

PAD4act ‐Positive At‐Risk Individuals Are More Likely to Develop Arthritis

We then sought to determine whether serum PAD4‐potentiating activity could serve as a systemic biomarker associated with imminent onset of clinical symptoms in RA. To define PAD4act‐positivity, we applied an a priori threshold of mean + 1 SD; samples above 19,205 AU were classified as PAD4act‐positive (Figure 3A). In total, 13.1% (n = 35) of the cohort participants were PAD4act‐positive, 14 of them from the no‐arthritis group and 21 from the arthritis group (p = 0.0049, Figure 3B). We next assessed the time to arthritis diagnosis among PAD4act‐positive and PAD4act‐negative participants, with timepoint 0 representing the blood collection at the study's initiation. PAD4act‐positive individuals progressed to clinical arthritis more rapidly than PAD4act‐negative participants (p = 0.0022; Figure 3C). Notably, 60% of PAD4act‐positive participants developed arthritis within 40 months of the at least 3‐year follow‐up period, with no new cases emerging in this subgroup beyond that time. Participants without incident arthritis were censored at their last recorded follow‐up visit (see Methods) [25]. The separation between curves was most pronounced early in follow‐up, whereas additional later events in the PAD4act‐negative group reduced the cumulative incidence gap towards the end of observation (Figure 3C).

Mean PAD4 Potentiating Activity Levels Do Not Correlate With ESR, CRP or DAS28

To investigate whether serum PAD4‐potentiating activity could indicate aggravated disease progression, we correlated PAD4 product levels with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C‐reactive protein concentration (CRP) and disease activity scores (DAS28) at the time of blood sampling. We found no significant correlations, with Spearman's rank correlation coefficients close to zero (ρESR = −0.042, ρCRP = −0.055, ρDAS28 = −0.021; Table 2). This suggests that PAD4 potentiating activity may capture a different aspect of disease pathology that is not directly related to traditional inflammatory markers.

Smoking Does Not Affect the PAD4‐Potentiating Activity of the Serum

Given that smoking is a known risk factor for RA and is associated with ACPA positivity, we examined whether smoking history influenced PAD4 activity in the RRA phase [26, 27]. Comparisons between mean PAD4 potentiating activity values among never‐smokers, ex‐smokers and current smokers revealed no significant differences (Table 3; Figure 4A,B). Additionally, there was no significant association between smoking status and PAD4act‐positivity (Chi‐square p = 0.34; Fisher's exact test p = 0.12). The presence of the SE, the sequence encoding 5‐amino acid fragment of HLA‐DRB1 chain, is a well‐established strong genetic risk factor for RA onset and progression [28]. Its presence correlates with ACPA positivity, and some studies indicate that SE may be instrumental in initiating the autoimmune response against citrullinated epitopes [28, 29]. Herein, the presence of SE did not correlate with the level of the PAD4 activation by patient sera.

PAD4act ‐Positive Patients Are More Likely to Harbour the HLA‐DRB1 SE Allelic Variant

Interestingly, while ever‐smoking or current‐smoking did not impact PAD4act‐positivity (p = 0.34 or p = 0.12, respectively), a significant association was found between PAD4act‐positivity and presence of HLA‐DRB1 SE. Among PAD4act‐positive participants, 77.14% were carriers of SE compared to 59.48% in the PAD4act‐negative group (Chi‐square test, p = 0.045; Table 4). Overall, among individuals progressing to arthritis, 73/101 (72%) were carriers of SE, 18/21 (86%) among progressors with PAD4act‐positivity and 55/80 (68%) among progressors without PADact‐positivity. Among individuals not progressing to arthritis, 92/166 (55%) were carriers of SE, 9/14 (64%) among non‐progressors with PAD4 positivity and 83/152 (54%) among non‐progressors without PAD positivity.

Discussion

RA is a significant global health burden, affecting approximately 17.6 million people worldwide in 2020, with projections indicating that over 31 million individuals could be impacted by 2050 [6]. These trends underscore the urgent need for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we identified the PAD4‐activating potential of peripheral blood serum as a promising predictive biomarker for identifying ACPA‐positive individuals at risk of imminent RA onset. Early stratification using this biomarker could allow for timely intervention, potentially extending disability‐free years for patients.

Citrullination has long been recognized as a key player in RA pathogenesis, although the mechanisms triggering autoantibody responses remain unclear [30]. The prevailing hypothesis involves a combination of elevated citrullinated epitope levels and a breach in mucosal tolerance. Recent studies suggest that repeated exposure to citrullinated epitopes from sources like the oral mucosa can activate ACPA‐producing B cells and monocytes, creating a feedback loop that exacerbates symptoms [22]. ACPAs are a diverse group of antibodies targeting both endogenous proteins (e.g., histones, vimentin) and exogenous epitopes (e.g., enolase of P. gingivalis ) [31]. Interestingly, clinical progression of RA in ACPA‐positive individuals has been delayed by abatacept administration at the pre‐clinical stage [32]. Undoubtedly, the enzymatic activity responsible for generating these epitopes, particularly that of PAD4, is crucial in RA.

While previous studies have shown higher serum‐induced PAD4 potentiating activity in early RA patients compared to the healthy controls, our study found consistent PAD4 potentiating activity in at‐risk pre‐clinical participants, regardless of subsequent arthritis progression. Because a fixed amount of recombinant PAD4 is added to each reaction, the readout reflects serum‐mediated potentiation of PAD4 enzymatic activity, instead of endogenous PAD4 levels. Notably, we identified a subset of participants with markedly elevated PAD4‐potentiating capacity (PAD4act‐positive). PAD4act‐positivity was enriched among individuals who subsequently progressed to clinical arthritis—most meeting 2010 RA criteria—supporting its potential utility for risk stratification in ACPA‐positive at‐risk populations.

This may indicate that shortly before disease onset—either through already ongoing inflammatory processes or through as‐yet undescribed mechanisms—systemic conditions arise that enhance deiminating activity, potentially increasing overall citrullination and amplifying localized ACPA‐driven immune responses. This interpretation is supported by in vitro evidence showing that patient‐derived PAD‐specific immunoglobulins can enhance recombinant PAD4 activity [33, 34]. Mechanistically, antibody‐derived peptide fragments binding adjacent to the PAD4 active site have been reported to stabilize dimer formation and potentiate catalysis [33]. We have recently reported a similar potentiating mechanism for glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), including heparin, and others have suggested that DMARD treatment may modulate circulating GAG levels, potentially altering PAD4‐potentiating capacity [35, 36]. Because we did not directly quantify candidate potentiators (e.g., anti‐PAD4 antibodies, glycosaminoglycans, or other serum modulators), the biological driver(s) of elevated PAD4act in this cohort cannot be inferred from the present data and should be regarded as hypotheses for future mechanistic studies. As the present assay captures the net effect of soluble serum factors on recombinant PAD4 activity rather than identifying individual enhancers or mediators, it provides a cumulative functional readout of the circulating milieu relevant to PAD4 catalysis.

The importance of the PAD4 enzyme in pathologies has already been highlighted by studies suggesting that the anti‐PAD4 antibodies could become a biomarker for RA or other rheumatic diseases with pulmonary involvement (pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease), and even non‐rheumatic lung diseases, such as cystic fibrosis [1, 37, 38]. Anti‐PAD4 antibodies are detected in about 20% of early RA patients and their presence correlates with worse ESR and DAS28 scores, but predicts slightly better initial response to therapy [39, 40]. Whether anti‐PAD4 autoantibodies are generated in response to high serum/plasma PAD4 levels is currently unclear, but PAD4 concentrations in RA patients have been found to be more than two‐ to 3.5‐fold higher than in the healthy volunteers [41]. Moreover, serum PAD4 levels dropped upon improvement of patient symptoms. Our study is the first to explore PAD4 activity modulation, rather than protein levels, in ACPA‐positive at‐risk population, highlighting a novel aspect of RA pathogenesis. Although extensively standardized within this study using internal normalization and replicate measurements, broader validation across independent cohorts and inter‐laboratory studies will be necessary before PAD4act can be considered for clinical deployment.

Importantly, our findings suggest that PAD4 activity is independent of traditional inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP, indicating its potential as a unique and complementary marker for RA risk assessment. Although smoking is also a known risk factor for RA and increases PAD expression, our study found no impact on serum PAD4‐activity potentiation or PAD4act‐positivity in at‐risk individuals [42].

Furthermore, the strong correlation between PAD4act‐positivity and the presence of SE variant of HLA‐DRB1, a known facilitator of citrullinated peptide antigen presentation—underscores the complexity of RA pathogenesis [43]. While the general prevalence of at least one SE allele among the study participants was 62%, the PAD4act‐positive group members were more likely to harbour at least one SE allele. This association suggests that PAD4act‐positivity, in conjunction with SE, may accelerate disease onset and progression.

Interestingly, the frequency of PAD4act‐positive individuals observed in our study is similar to that reported previously for treatment‐resistant RA [44]. Seropositivity, including ACPA positivity, has been correlated with aggravated RA progression: increased radiographic damage and higher lymphocyte infiltration of the synovial tissue, as well as disease relapse after treatment discontinuation [45, 46, 47]. Although mechanisms for treatment resistance are unclear and seronegative treatment‐resistant cases have been reported, there may be a correlation involving PAD4act, ACPA production, disease progression and treatment outcomes.

Our findings underscore the clinical importance of PAD4‐potentiating activity in serum as a potential early detection tool for RA. By identifying ACPA‐positive individuals at risk of imminent RA onset, this biomarker could enable earlier and more targeted interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes. The need for early detection and personalized treatment in RA is more critical than ever and further research into PAD4 activity and its interaction with genetic factors like SE could pave the way for more effective therapies and preventive efforts in the future.
