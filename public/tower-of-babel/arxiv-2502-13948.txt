Abstract
We present a multi-wavelength analysis of three candidate active galactic nuclei (AGNs) in low-mass galaxies in the Boötes field with the aim of improving constraints on the occupation fraction of low-mass black holes (BHs). Galaxies with low stellar masses ( M ⋆ < 10 9.5 ​ M ⊙ M_{\star}<10^{9.5}M_{\odot} ) are particularly interesting hosts for AGNs as they may contain BHs that have not grown significantly since the epoch of their formation in the early Universe. Using archival data from the Chandra X-ray Observatory, we find three X-ray luminous low-mass galaxies and assess whether they host AGNs. We find one of these sources to be variable in the X-ray and compute its X-ray light curve and spectrum. We compute the X-ray, mid-infrared, and [O III] luminosities and compare them to established AGN luminosity relationships in the literature. We then fit various star-forming, dust emission, and AGN templates to the spectral energy distributions (SEDs). The star formation rates estimated from the SED fits are unable to explain the observed X-ray luminosities of the candidates, providing more support for the presence of AGNs. By analysing the deviation from linear relationships between X-ray and mid-infrared luminosities, we find these systems to be obscured (with log ⁡ N H ​ [ cm − 2 ] ∼ 22.7 , > 25.0 \log N_{\rm H}[{\rm{cm^{-2}}}]\sim 22.7,>25.0 , and 24.4 24.4 , respectively). We employ the scaling relationship between BH mass and stellar velocity dispersion to estimate the BH masses as ∼ 10 5 − 10 6 ​ M ⊙ \sim 10^{5}-10^{6}M_{\odot} and accreting at Eddington ratios 10 − 2 < λ Edd < 10 − 1 10^{-2}<\lambda_{\rm Edd}<10^{-1} .

Introduction

It is widely accepted that a supermassive black hole (SMBH,
M_{\text{BH}}\approx 10^{6}-10^{10}M_{\odot}
) lies at the centre of most local massive galaxies
(
Magorrian et al. 1998
;
Marconi & Hunt 2003
;
Kormendy & Ho 2013
, e.g.,)
. The majority of SMBHs lie dormant, but a small fraction produce significant amounts of light and energy, often outshining their host galaxy across the electromagnetic spectrum. These are known as active galactic nuclei
(
Salpeter 1964
;
Lynden-Bell 1969
;
Shakura & Sunyaev 1973
;
Rees 1984
;
Urry & Padovani 1995
;
Padovani et al. 2017
;
Hickox & Alexander 2018
, AGN; for reviews see,)
.

There are many theories for the formation of SMBHs
(
Shankar et al. 2013
;
Miller et al. 2015
;
Greene et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
: the collapse of primordial gas
(
Loeb & Rasio 1994
;
Bromm & Loeb 2003
;
Begelman et al. 2006
;
Lodato & Natarajan 2006
;
Johnson et al. 2013
;
Ferrara et al. 2014
, “heavy" seeds of
\sim 10^{4}-10^{5}M_{\odot}
; e.g.,)
, super-Eddington accretion of Population III star black hole (BH) remnants in the early-Universe
(
Madau & Rees 2001
;
Whalen & Fryer 2012
;
Taylor & Kobayashi 2014
;
Jiang et al. 2019
, “light" seeds of
\sim 10^{2}M_{\odot}
; e.g.,)
, and models that involve a gravitational runaway effect
(
Portegies Zwart & McMillan 2002
;
Miller & Hamilton 2002
;
Purohit et al. 2024
, seeds of
M_{\rm{BH}}
\sim 10^{3}-10^{4}M_{\odot}
e.g.,)
. The challenge to testing these theories is that they require extensive multi-wavelength observations of high-redshift galaxies. Such detailed observations of early-Universe systems were not possible until the recent advent of the James Webb Space Telescope
(
Volonteri & Reines 2016
;
Vito et al. 2018
;
Larson et al. 2023
;
Barro et al. 2023
;
Greene et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Recent detections of high-redshift objects like “little red dots"
(
Kocevski et al. 2023
;
Harikane et al. 2023
;
Onoue et al. 2023
;
Larson et al. 2023
;
Barro et al. 2023
;
Greene et al. 2023
;
Matthee et al. 2023
;
Kocevski et al. 2024
, LRDs; e.g.,)
are helping place constraints on the models of BH-galaxy co-evolution.

Studying BHs in the local Universe is a complementary method to probe BH seeds and BH-galaxy co-evolution. Local systems allow us in principle to place constraints on the nature of BH seeds
(
van Wassenhove et al. 2010
;
Ricarte & Natarajan 2018
, e.g.,)
, tighten local scaling relationships
(
Pacucci et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, and differentiate between the possible formation paths
(
Volonteri & Gnedin 2009
;
Bellovary et al. 2019
, although this has proven to be challenging, e.g.,)
.

Currently, only one BH formation channel is well-established: the collapse of a stellar core following a supernova at the end of a massive (
>8M_{\odot}
) star’s evolution. These BHs have masses of the order of tens of
M_{\odot}

(
Celotti et al. 1999
;
Remillard & McClintock 2006
;
Bambi 2020
, e.g.,)
, with an upper limit of
50\,M_{\odot}
imposed by pulsational pair instability
(
Fowler & Hoyle 1964
;
Barkat et al. 1967
;
Rakavy et al. 1967
;
Heger et al. 2003
;
Woosley 2017
)
. BHs with masses
>50-100M_{\odot}
have been detected with gravitational wave instruments such as LIGO
(
Abbott et al. 2020
)
. Many studies have presented the possibility that these BHs are merger remnants of smaller BHs produced through one (or more) of the seeding mechanisms. Nevertheless, if BHs form through stellar collapse, there must be some mechanism by which they grow into the SMBHs we observe today, implying that BHs in an intermediate mass range had to exist. These BHs in the mass gap between stellar mass and SMBHs are called intermediate-mass black holes
(
Greene et al. 2020
, IMBHs; for a review, see)
. IMBHs are excellent systems to study as their mass and Eddington ratios may reveal the history of BH growth over cosmic time.

Finding BHs at the nuclei of low-mass galaxies and characterizing their nature is of interest for many reasons, such as finding clues about the origins of SMBHs and probing BH feedback mechanisms in low-mass systems. Because of the steep rise in the galaxy stellar mass function at low masses, even a small occupation fraction of BHs in low-mass galaxies can change the BH density in that regime greatly, making these systems a particularly important component of the BH growth census. Additionally, the quiet merger history of low-mass galaxies, particularly isolated ones, make them good indicators of seeding mechanisms
(
Volonteri et al. 2008
)
. However, one of the biggest hurdles to studying these systems has been detecting them in multiple wavelength bands
(
Nguyen et al. 2018
;
Baldassare et al. 2020b
, e.g.,)
. Many local accreting systems have been revealed using X-ray activity, optical narrow emission line diagnostics to identify AGNs and broad emission lines to estimate BH masses
(
Greene & Ho 2004
;
Greene & Ho 2007
;
Reines et al. 2011
;
Reines et al. 2013
;
Reines et al. 2014
;
Moran et al. 2014
;
Secrest et al. 2015
;
Pardo et al. 2016
;
Mezcua et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. Optical variability has also detected many additional such systems
(
Geha et al. 2003
;
Schmidt et al. 2010
;
Sesar et al. 2007
;
Palanque-Delabrouille et al. 2011
;
Choi et al. 2014
;
Baldassare et al. 2018
;
Baldassare et al. 2020a
)
. AGN activity is common in the local Universe, with
\sim 5-10\%
of nearby galaxies found to host optical AGN activity
(
Veilleux & Osterbrock 1987
;
Maiolino & Rieke 1995
;
Ho et al. 1997
;
Kewley et al. 2001
;
Kewley et al. 2006
;
Hao et al. 2005
, i.e., identified as Seyfert galaxies from their optical emission-line properties; e.g.,)
. These observational techniques, among many others, have significantly improved our understanding of the presence of AGNs and accreting BHs in the local Universe.

Through analyses of SMBHs and their host galaxies, many scaling relations have been developed. One such scaling relation is between the BH mass and the host galaxy’s total stellar mass
(
Mancini & Feoli 2012
;
Reines & Volonteri 2015
, e.g.,)
. Since we know that the SMBH mass correlates with the galaxy mass, we might expect the same for IMBHs found in smaller galaxies. This allows us to essentially verify whether dwarf galaxies (
M_{\star}<10^{9}M_{\odot}
) host low-mass BHs. While there are no dynamically confirmed IMBHs in or not in the nuclei of dwarf galaxies, recent studies have produced a number of promising candidates
(
Neumayer & Walcher 2012
;
Arcavi et al. 2014
;
Blagorodnova et al. 2017
;
Nguyen et al. 2018
;
Mezcua et al. 2018a
;
Mezcua et al. 2018b
;
Wevers et al. 2019
;
Nguyen et al. 2019
;
Reines et al. 2020
;
Ansh et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Low-mass BHs and AGNs found in dwarf galaxies would be crucial for constraining BH seed formation models and better understanding the distribution of BH mass throughout the Universe
(
Pacucci et al. 2021
;
Wasleske et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Dwarf galaxies at low redshift may host BHs of similar mass to those in early-Universe galaxies and so provide a probe of their early formation
(
Greene et al. 2020
;
Latimer et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. In the local Universe, it is estimated that
\sim 80\%
of nearby, low-mass early-type galaxies host nuclear BHs
(
Nguyen et al. 2018
)
, but the total population of BHs in low-mass galaxies remains poorly constrained. This is significant, considering the first two prototype low-mass AGNs hosting IMBHs were detected in the local Universe
(
Filippenko & Sargent 1989
, NGC 4395;)
and
(
Kunth et al. 1987
;
Barth et al. 2004
, POX 52;)
. Thus, searching for AGNs in dwarf galaxies is a valuable method of identifying low-mass BHs
(
Reines et al. 2013
;
Greene et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Apart from being located at the centers of dwarf galaxies, some IMBHs can be found “wandering" around their host galaxies such as HLX-1
(
Farrell et al. 2009
)
and in massive star clusters such as 3XMM J215022.4-055108
(
Lin et al. 2020

.

X-ray observations are effective at identifying AGN activity due to their high contrast with the host galaxy and strong penetrating power

Brandt & Alexander 2015
, e.g.)

M_{\star}
, star formation rate (SFR), and stellar bulge velocity dispersion,

Analysis and Results

3.1
Spectral Energy Distribution (SED)

To investigate the nature of our sources, we begin by creating spectral energy distributions (SEDs) for each source. SED fitting decomposes integrated galactic emission into its components and allows us to infer physical properties such as
M_{\star}
, SFR, and AGN luminosity
(
Magris C. et al. 2015
;
Yamada et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

We utilize SED fitting with photometric fluxes at a range of wavelengths obtained from NED. The photometry used come from GALEX
(
Martin et al. 2005
)
for the UV (NUV and FUV bands), SDSS DR17
(
Abdurro’uf et al. 2022
)
for the optical-NIR, and the Spitzer Space Telescope
(
Werner et al. 2004
)
for the MIR. In the far-IR, we use data from the Herschel Space Telescope’s Photodetector Array Camera and Spectrometer (PACS) and Spectral and Photometric Imaging Receiver (SPIRE), as compiled in the Herschel Extragalactic Legacy Project catalogue
(
Shirley et al. 2021
, HELP;)
. In the IR, we choose to use Spitzer instead of the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer because of the higher sensitivity of the former in the mid-IR wavebands. Specifically, we use photometric measurements values from integrated maps, ensuring that all the light is included. For GALEX and 2MASS, we use the Kron aperture fluxes; for SDSS, we use the CModel values. For the Spitzer/IRAC data, all reported photometry is from a fixed aperture. We use flux values from the 5.8" aperture from Spitzer but we note that this is within the errors from WISE which uses larger apertures and flux integrated maps. Visual inspection also confirms a smooth SED shape, indicating consistency across the different datasets and reinforcing the reliability of our photometric measurements for SED fitting and analysis.

While these sources each have too few X-ray counts (15, 104, and 28 counts, respectively) to perform detailed spectral fitting, we can get a rough sense of their spectral shapes via their hardness ratios. (
Park et al. 2006

=-0.54^{+0.15}_{-0.46}

-0.59^{+0.078}_{-0.09}
,
-0.14^{+0.32}_{-0.25}
respectively. We estimate the column densities (

Carroll et al. 2021
and
Carroll et al. 2023

to the ratio of the observed to expected
L_{\rm{X}}
, R
LX
(from the linear relationship in

:

We note that sources B and C have relatively low values of X-ray hardness ratio
(<-0.14)
despite having high estimates of

3.3
Emission from X-ray binaries

In addition to AGN activity, another potential source of X-ray emission in galaxies is the integrated emission from X-ray binaries (XRB). To confirm that the X-rays from these sources are dominated by AGN, we calculate the luminosity (
L_{\rm{XRB}}
) that we would expect from galaxy-wide XRBs. Chandra studies of nearby star-forming late-type galaxies and passive early-type galaxies have shown that the X-ray point-source emission from relatively young high-mass XRBs and older low-mass XRBs correlate well with galaxy SFR and
M_{\star}
, respectively
(
Grimm et al. 2003
;
Gilfanov 2004
;
Lehmer et al. 2010
;
Lehmer et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. Since the stellar masses listed in D21 are systematically underestimated for local galaxies, we use
M_{\star}
values derived from the SED in §
3.1
. These values are listed in Table
3
.

Lehmer et al. 2019
present X-ray luminosity functions for binaries based on sub-galactic modelling. Following their Table 6, we estimate
L_{\rm{XRB}}
for our sources. We utilize the SFRs and stellar masses provided by the SED fits. For the former, we choose the SFR at 100 Myr since this better encapsulates the actively star-forming population over the timescales relevant for high-mass XRB formation, reducing stochastic effects from short-term variations in star formation
(
Garofali et al. 2018
;
Lehmer et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Source A has a log mass of 9.21
M_{\odot}
and SFR of
3.8\times 10^{-2}
, giving us log sSFR = -10.64
5
5

5

Due to the underestimation in FIR, a higher SFR would lead to a larger log sSFR. For example, an increase of 0.2 dex in log sSFR would lead to 0.03 dex increase in
L_{\rm{XRB}}
.
. Source B has log mass 10.38
M_{\odot}
and SFR of
2.7\times 10^{-1}
with log sSFR of -10.94. Source C has the lowest log mass of 8.83, SFR 1.8, with log sSFR = -8.5. These values are listed in Table
3
; interpolating the data from Table 6 of
Lehmer et al. 2019
, we use these to obtain estimates of log
L_{\rm{XRB}}
as
37.97^{+0.7}_{-0.6},39.18_{-0.3}^{+0.3},39.75^{+0.4}_{-0.5}
for Sources A, B, and C, respectively. The emission from X-ray binaries alone cannot account for the observed emission in our sources, particularly in sources A and B, strongly suggesting the presence of AGN activity. Source C’s
L_{\rm{XRB}}
differs from the observed
L_{\rm{X}}
by 0.6 dex suggesting that the integrated emission from the galaxy could be powered by a population of X-ray binaries.

To remain consistent with the methodology outlined in
Lehmer et al. 2019
, we estimate stellar masses and SFRs using the empirical relations related to optical luminosity and colour provided in
Zibetti et al. 2009
and
Hao et al. 2011
(see §3 in
Lehmer et al. 2019
for details). Our analysis reveals that the stellar mass estimates are systematically lower, while the SFRs are approximately an order of magnitude higher compared to values derived from SED fitting. However, upon interpolating from Table 6 of
Lehmer et al. 2019
, we find that the
L_{\rm{XRB}}
values are slightly reduced relative to our previous estimates. The uncertainties remain consistent, ensuring agreement within the statistical errors.

A further constraint on the expected contribution from X-ray binaries comes from the fact that the observed X-ray emission for all three systems is unresolved and centred on the nucleus of the galaxy, which is expected to encompass only a fraction of the total X-ray binary emission. We estimate the SFR within the PU (vs the integrated galaxy emission). We assume the spatial distribution of SFR is traced approximately by the
u
-band flux, and used the Aperture Photometry Tool
(
Laher et al. 2012
, APT; )
to determine the fraction of the
u
-band flux contained within the 95% confidence interval for the X-ray position. For each of the three sources, this nuclear
u
-band light fraction is
10-20
%, so that the expected integrated luminosity from X-ray binaries is 5–10
\times
smaller than the estimates above.

We can further estimate the likelihood that star formation processes would produce a
single
X-ray binary with the observed luminosity, consistent with the fact that the sources are unresolved. (We note that Source B is definitively a single source, as demonstrated by its X-ray variability; §
3.4
.) We utilize the synthetic population models studied in
Misra et al. 2023
which probes different aspects of XRB evolution using models from
Patton & Sukhbold 2020
and
Fryer et al. 2012
. For each of the three sources, we check the likelihood that the observed X-ray luminosity is produced by a single source, given the SFR within the source aperture. In Figure
7
, we plot the models from
Patton & Sukhbold 2020
and
Fryer et al. 2012
, each corresponding to different physical parameters such as remnant mass, natal kick, and common-envelope efficiency. The probability of single XRB associated with star formation within the source aperture having an
L_{X}
greater than the observed value is
3.7\times 10^{-5}
,
1.8\times 10^{-4}
, and
2.8\times 10^{-2}
for Sources A, B, and C, respectively.

Finally, we note that if the observed X-ray luminosity is from a single source, it would be classified as an ultraluminous X-ray source
(
Kaaret et al. 2017
;
King et al. 2023
, ULX; e.g.,)
. Given the high X-ray luminosity, if the sources are not BHs, the ULXs would have to be powered by super-Eddington accretors
(
Singha et al. 2024
)
. A constraint on this possibility comes from the velocity dispersions of emission lines, since super-Eddington accretion is associated with a strong outflow which is generated at the accretion disk surface via radiation pressure
(
Shakura & Sunyaev 1973
)
.
Fabrika et al. 2015
showed that known super-Eddington accretors have strong correlations between the He II
\lambda 4686
and H
\alpha
line widths. All of our sources have a significantly lower
\sigma_{\rm{H\alpha}}
than
\sigma_{\rm{HeII\lambda 4686}}
. This is an important deviation from the 1:1 expected correlation as H
\alpha
is known to trace ionized and gas-shocked winds. Thus, it is apparent that the sources are not powered by super-Eddington accretors.

4
Black Hole Mass Estimates

In this section, we estimate the masses of the BHs (
M_{\rm{BH}}
) using the
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
and
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}
scaling relations and estimate the growth timescales via the observed Eddington ratios. Evidence points towards the scaling of central BH properties with their host galaxies properties like the stellar velocity dispersion, mass, IR luminosity, etc. The correlations provide an opportunity to study the linked evolution of galaxies and BHs and simultaneously provide a way to estimate the BH mass via a proxy
(
Reines & Volonteri 2015
)
.

4.1
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}

The scaling relation between the BH mass and
M_{\star}
has been studied in detail
(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
;
Reines & Volonteri 2015
;
Greene et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. We use the relations derived from
Reines & Volonteri 2015
and
Greene et al. 2020
to estimate
M_{\rm{BH}}
.
Reines & Volonteri 2015
derive the total galaxy stellar mass using photometry
(
Zibetti et al. 2009
)
. We use the masses calculated in §
3.3
for
M_{\star}
, and estimate
M_{\rm{BH}}
, from
Reines & Volonteri 2015
:

\log{\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{\odot}}\right)}=\alpha+\beta\log{\left(\frac{M_{\star}}{10^{11}M_{\odot}}\right)}

(2)

where
\alpha=7.45\pm 0.08
and
\beta=1.05\pm 0.11
.
Greene et al. 2020
presents another equation for the
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}
scaling relation derived mainly using dynamical masses:

\log{\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{\odot}}\right)}=\alpha+\beta\log{\left(\frac{M_{\star}}{3\times 10^{10}M_{\odot}}\right)}+\epsilon

(3)

where
\alpha=7.56\pm 0.09
,
\beta=1.39\pm 0.13
, and
\epsilon=0.79\pm 0.05
. Focusing on the low-mass regime, it is apparent that without upper limits, the fit to late-type galaxies returns a very shallow relation because the measured
M_{\rm{BH}}
values are biased towards SMBHs
(
Pacucci et al. 2018
;
Baldassare et al. 2020a
;
Greene et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. The values are listed in Table
5
.

4.2
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}

The
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
relation is an empirical correlation between the BH mass and the stellar velocity dispersion of the host galaxy
(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
;
Saglia et al. 2016
;
van den Bosch 2016
;
Krajnović et al. 2018
,
\sigma^{*}
; e.g.,)
. We follow the relation in
Greene et al. 2020
that is derived using BH mass samples from
Kormendy & Ho 2013
,
Greene et al. 2016
,
Krajnović et al. 2018
,
Nguyen et al. 2018
and
Thater et al. 2019
:

\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{\odot}}\right)=\alpha+\beta\log{\left(\frac{\sigma^{*}}{160\;\text{km/s}}\right)}+\epsilon,

(4)

where
\alpha=7.88\ \pm\ 0.05
,
\beta=4.34\,\pm\ 0.24
, and
\epsilon=0.53\,\pm\,0.04
. The estimates we obtain for the masses are shown in Figure
9
and tabulated in Table
5
for “All, no limit" relationship of
Greene et al. 2020
.

4.3
Discussion of scaling relations

We choose to rely on the
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
derived BH masses as our main findings due to the large scatter in the
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}
relation, and because of the significant deviation between
\sigma
and
M_{\star}
-derived masses for source B.

The SDSS image of source B strongly suggests a disk morphology, with a diameter of
\sim 30"
. The stellar velocity dispersion is well below what is expected for a spheroid-dominated system of similar
M_{\star}

(
Leigh et al. 2012
)
, indicating that its stellar morphology is dominated by a disk. However, we note that the SDSS Legacy fiber aperture is 3"
(
Adelman-McCarthy et al. 2007
)
and that the measured velocity dispersion may still be dominated by a bulge component.

We should note that the applicability of the
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
relation is debated for low-mass galaxies
(
Pacucci et al. 2018
;
Greene et al. 2020
;
Reines 2022
, e.g.,)
because
\sigma^{*}
is traditionally defined as the velocity dispersion of stars inside bulges. The slope of the relation changes considerably based on the galaxy population, likely due to the bias in
M_{\rm{BH}}
measurements toward the most massive BHs. There is also a marked departure in the estimates for
M_{\text{BH}}\leq 10^{5}M_{\odot}

(
Pacucci et al. 2018
)
. The observed
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
relation favours heavy seed models that make exclusively
M_{\rm{BH}}

>10^{5}M_{\odot}
. The low-mass tail of the
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
relation is now being explored since it is believed to provide a stronger, and more fundamental, correlation than
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}

(
Sesana et al. 2016
;
Volonteri 2010
;
Pacucci et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.

SDSS recommends users not rely upon data with
\sigma^{*}
<70
km s
-1
due to the typical signal-to-noise ratio and the instrumental resolution of the spectrograph
6
6

6

https://classic.sdss.org/dr5/algorithms/veldisp.php

(
Adelman-McCarthy et al. 2007
;
Almeida et al. 2023
, for more information, see)
. The instrumental dispersion of the SDSS spectrograph is 69 km s
-1
per pixel, the resolution is
\sim 90
km s
-1
, and velocities are convolved to a maximum value of 420 km s
-1
. Since our sources are low-mass and hence have small
\sigma^{*}
, their velocity dispersions fall below the recommended limit. To verify the validity of our estimate, we carried out our analysis of the SDSS spectra from which the velocity dispersions were derived. We confirmed that the
\sigma^{*}
measurements are reliable in terms of the strength of the relevant absorption lines, the spectral resolution of the data, and the model fit to the flux to determine the velocity dispersion. We first fit Gaussians to the calcium absorption and neon emission lines to check the
\sigma^{*}
derived from the fit width against that reported by SDSS.

For a more rigorous analysis, we use the
ppxf
Python package
(
Cappellari 2022
)
to carry out a detailed analysis using both photometric and spectroscopic data. Using stellar and gas templates, we fit a two-component model (V: velocity and
\sigma^{*}
: velocity dispersion) to the data. We use the MILES Library of Stellar Spectra derived from
Sánchez-Blázquez et al. 2006
and
Falcón-Barroso et al. 2011
. The fitting takes into account a parameter called
sigma_diff
which is the difference between the instrumental dispersion of the galaxy spectrum and the instrumental dispersion of the template spectra
7
7

7

https://pypi.org/project/ppxf/
. This proves helpful since the templates are convolved using an analytic Fourier Transform by
ppxf
to match the provided instrumental dispersion of the galaxy spectrum, in this case, the instrumental resolution of the spectrograph. We find that the estimates agree with the SDSS values and fall within the reported SDSS errors. Thus, we adopt the
\sigma^{*}
-derived BH mass in calculating the Eddington ratio for all three sources. The masses of the three sources are
\sim 10^{5}-10^{6}M_{\odot}
as listed in Table
5
.

Mass scaling relations vary greatly with galaxy types and merger histories.
Graham 2023
presents scaling relations for E, S0, and S galaxies, emphasizing the impact of morphology and merger history. We studied these various scaling relations, the dataset from
Sahu et al. 2019
used to derive them, and the velocity dispersions for our sources. We found that the
\sigma^{*}
values of our sources place them on the low-mass tail of the relations. The estimated masses from these relations are consistently smaller than estimates from
Greene et al. 2020
. Median BH masses for the sources are
2.2\times 10^{4}

M_{\odot}
for A,
1.6\times 10^{5}

M_{\odot}
for B, and
2.0\times 10^{4}

M_{\odot}
for C.

With the estimated masses, we calculate the Eddington limits of the sources. To compute the corresponding Eddington ratio, we first find the bolometric luminosity. We use the 15

to convert the observed luminosity at 15

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyse the nature of three low-mass galaxies with strong nuclear X-ray emission in the Boötes field and investigate whether they host accreting nuclear BHs. These targets are selected by cross-matching two catalogues:
Masini et al. 2020
(X-ray detections) and
Duncan et al. 2021
(galaxies, with estimates of total stellar mass of total stellar mass). After matching with a 1" radius, we made initial cuts to select nearby (
z<0.05
), low-mass galaxies (
M_{\star}<10^{9.5}M_{\odot}
). After making additional cuts on data requiring multi-wavelength coverage and appropriate velocity dispersions, we were left with three sources that we study in this paper. With SDSS data we analyse their emission lines by placing them on a narrow-line BPT diagnostic diagram. We calculate F-band X-ray luminosities and rely on Chandra detections to identify AGN because they are one of the clearest indicators of AGN activity in a galaxy
(
Miller et al. 2015
;
Baldassare et al. 2015
;
Pardo et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, we retrieve photometry in the UV, optical, and IR to compute the SEDs for our galaxies and fit them using CIGALE using various combinations of stellar and AGN templates. With the MIR and [O III] luminosities, we are able to assess the possibility that these galaxies host AGN. To confirm that the X-ray emissions we observe originate from AGNs and not galactic-wide X-ray binaries, we estimate the latter with SFRs. We find convincing evidence for our sources to host AGNs at their centres.

By analysing the deviation from intrinsic relationships between [O iii], MIR, and X-ray luminosities, we conclude that these sources are considerably obscured in the X-rays. Using the model presented in
Carroll et al. 2023
, we estimate the column densities (
\log N_{\rm H}[{\rm{cm^{-2}}}]\sim 22.7,>25.0
, and
24.4
) of the sources by considering the ratio of the observed to expected
L_{\rm{X}}
. We use scaling relations (
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
and
M_{\rm{BH}}-M_{\star}
) to estimate the masses of the central BHs. The morphology of source B in Figure
1
and the
M_{\star}-\sigma^{*}
relation trends for spheroids, we believe that it is a disc galaxy. Thus, we choose to use the
\sigma^{*}
-derived relation for the BH masses for all three sources to be consistent. Their masses fall in the range of
10^{5}-10^{6}M_{\odot}
. We estimate the Eddington ratios of the BHs using the
M_{\rm{BH}}-\sigma^{*}
-determined value for their masses, which are of the orders of
10^{-2}-10^{-1}
. These ratios suggest that BHs are accreting at rates characteristic of optically-thick, radiatively efficient accretion flows.

We also find that one of our sources is highly variable (by approximately an order of magnitude) in the X-ray band. Using CIAO tools, we generate a light curve from three Chandra observations of the source. These observations offer us a view of the galaxy in its low-flux and high-flux phases, and confirms that the emission is dominated by a single source rather than a population of many X-ray binaries. Studying X-ray variability of AGNs in low-mass galaxies in the future will be important to understand the nature of their accretion disks, flows, and radiation mechanisms.

To place these low-mass galaxies in the context of the local Universe, it is helpful to consider the fraction of the low-mass galaxy population that we recover as AGNs. From
Duncan et al. 2021
’s compilation, there are 66 galaxies in the Boötes field with
M_{\star}<10^{9.5}M_{\odot}
and spectroscopic redshift
z<0.05
. Using the mass-to-light ratio method of estimating stellar masses as described in §
3.3
, we note that stellar mass estimates of these sources are comparable to our three targets. Using these numbers, we can conclude that roughly 5% of all local low-mass galaxies host AGNs. This is an important statistic in uncovering the presence of AGNs in local low-mass galaxies and finding more of these “mini monsters"
(
Hickox et al. 2022
)
.

This is the first study of low-mass BHs and AGNs in low-mass galaxies in the Boötes field which has extensive multi-wavelength observations. In our work, we identified targets using X-ray data archival Chandra data. More focused observations, spectra, and large fields of X-ray, optical, and infrared of local low-mass galaxies could help us to better identify low-mass AGNs. Additionally, a majority of the AGN activity in the universe is obscured
(
Ueda et al. 2014
;
Hickox & Alexander 2018
;
Ananna et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, meaning that studying obscured AGNs in low-mass galaxies can provide a more complete picture of the population fraction and properties of low-mass BHs.
