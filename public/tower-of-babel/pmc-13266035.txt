Goat and cow-milk based infant formulas contain extracellular vesicles with different miRNA and protein cargo
Authors: C Leroux, Z Krupova, M Reyre, C Bevilacqua, C Henry, F Adamski, S Gallier, P Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the presence of extracellular vesicles (EVs) and their cargo miRNAs is well established in human, cow and goat milk, their presence in infant formulae (IFs) was less documented. Particles whose EV nature was subsequently confirmed (size, microscopy) were isolated from whole goat milk powder (WGMP), from two IFs based on goat milk and from two based on cow milk using size exclusion chromatography. The qPCR analyses of RNA extracted from these EVs show that they contain mRNAs specifying major milk proteins and mammary epithelial cell markers, demonstrating the mammary origin of EVs. The miRNA profile and proteome of EVs were determined by small-RNA sequencing and TimsTOF Pro LC-MS, respectively. We identified a total of 1142 and 1162 proteins in goat and cow milk derived IFs, respectively, including EV-specific markers (as CD9, CD63) and proteins involved in vesicular trafficking. More than 350 miRNAs were identified and revealed that the expected milk miRNAs were among the most abundant in IFs. Among the 20 most abundant miRNAs, 16 were found in all IFs and WGMP, and 12 are described as the most abundant in human milk. They were predicted to influence cell life, protein and nucleic acid processes, or may be linked to inflammation. Differential analysis of miRNomes showed the type of milk (goat vs cow with 80 differential miRNAs) had the greatest impact, followed by the whey protein:casein ratio. This study demonstrates that EVs are present in milk-based IFs, which are a source of miRNAs. Further studies on their potential biological relevance for infants are warranted.Keywords: Goat milk formula, Cow milk formula, Extracellular vesicles, Proteomics, miRNomes

Introduction

Human milk (HM) is recommended by the World Health Organization as the best exclusive source of nutrition for infants to 6 months of age, and to 2 years of age in combination with complementary foods (WHO, 2003). Its complex and unique nutritional profile includes nutrients that support infant growth and health outcomes, such as immunity (de Weerth et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2023). HM is more than a source of nutrition; it is an intricate vehicle of maternal messages to the newborn. When breastfeeding is not possible, infant formula (IFs) are the only suitable source of nutrition for infants less than 6 months old. The most widely consumed IFs are dairy-based, more commonly derived from cow milk but with a growing availability of goat milk IFs (Gallier et al., 2020). Understanding the complexity and performance of HM has driven innovation in IF composition. Animal-derived milk, mainly from cows and goats, is commonly used in the manufacture of IFs (Gallier et al., 2020). Despite some nutritional similarities between human, cow and goat milk, the macronutrients and micronutrients in HM cannot be fully replicated in modern IFs, due to differences in the milk source and potential loss of nutrients and component bioactivity during processing of IFs (Chauvet et al., 2025; Gallier et al., 2020; Turner et al., 2023).

Recently, there has been more research on the presence and role of milk extracellular vesicles (mEVs), small lipid-protein membrane-bound nanoparticles (50-1000 nm) with a cargo of proteins, RNAs and microRNAs (miRNAs) (Benmoussa et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2021; Krupova et al., 2023; Mecocci et al., 2020; van Herwijnen et al., 2016). A few studies have shown that mEVs survive milk processing and gastrointestinal conditions, enabling them to reach the intestines and affect physiological functions (Liao et al., 2017; Turner et al., 2023; Ilori et al., 2025). Recently, it was reported that milk small EVs cross the blood-brain-barrier contributing to neural development, spatial learning and memory (Zhou et al., 2022; Moreno-Casillas et al., 2026). The study of mEVs, in particular in human and cow milk, has demonstrated their promising role in immune, inflammatory and intestinal functions, primarily driven by some key miRNAs (Mecocci et al., 2021; D. Zhang et al., 2024; Vahkal et al., 2025;Vahkal et al., 2024). Although evidence suggesting that food miRNAs have an impact on consumer health is growing, some studies contradict these effects.

MiRNAs are short non-coding RNAs, typically 18-25 nucleotides in length. They regulate the expression of over 60% of protein-coding genes at the post-transcriptional level, influencing a wide range of biological processes including cell life, metabolism, development, immunity, and diseases (Ambros, 2004; Bartel, 2004; Friedman et al., 2009). Milk is the body fluid richest in miRNAs (Weber et al., 2010). They are detected in different fractions of milk, including in EVs (Leduc et al., 2023). They survive the technological processes used to make cheese (Abou El Qassim et al., 2023; Oh et al., 2017), butter (Leroux et al., 2024) and whole milk powder (Krupova et al., 2025). However, it has been reported that processing of milk can result in varying degrees of miRNA loss depending on the treatment and the miRNAs considered (Howard et al., 2015; Y. Zhang et al., 2022).

Milk EVs and their cargo, especially those isolated from HM, have been the most intensively studied (Turner et al., 2023). Their miRNAs are emerging as potential biological regulators. A better understanding of how dairy ingredients and manufacturing processes affect their content in IFs is a critical step toward improving the nutritional properties of IFs to better meet the needs of infants. Weil et al. (2023) demonstrated that milk miRNAs can survive the harsh conditions in the gastrointestinal tract in human and porcine neonates, be taken up by intestinal cells, reach blood circulation and be excreted in stools. Analysis of miRNAs in cow, donkey and goat mEVs revealed shared highly expressed miRNAs and their role in immune regulatory functions (Mecocci et al., 2021). Promising evidence indicates that miRNAs may be involved in allergy development, autoimmune diseases and other chronic diseases through regulation of inflammation initiation and resolution (Martino et al., 2024; Mecocci et al., 2021). HM miR-375-3p was found to be associated with reduced atopy risk in breastfed infants (Hicks et al., 2022). In addition, HM miRNAs have been suggested to have a potential role in gut maturation (Leroux et al., 2021). For example, miR-200/375 has been reported to regulate epithelium plasticity (Pillman et al., 2018), which is particularly crucial for neonates due to gut permeability throughout the first days of life. A recent systemic review pointed out that miRNAs enclosed in EVs can survive the conditions of the infant's gut, enter the bloodstream, and travel to distant organs, where they can regulate the function of those organs (Ivashkova et al., 2025).

The presence of miRNAs in infant formula is controversial. However, recently, Turner et al. (2023) demonstrated that cow IFs contain mEVs and miRNAs derived from the dairy base, and that IFs contain fewer mEVs than unprocessed cow milk (Ali et al., 2026), possibly due to dilution through the addition of other ingredients. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies on mEV and miRNA of goat milk based IF.

In this study, we aimed to provide conclusive evidence of the presence of mEVs and miRNAs in IFs, using small-RNA sequencing and proteomic analyses. The novelty of our study was to identify the main factors influencing the composition of mEVs in infant formulas, by analyzing different milk sources and whey protein:casein ratio, as well as the whole goat milk powder.

Materials and methods

Samples description

Four IFs (two from goat and two cow milk) and whole goat milk powder (WGMP) were used. In total, 15 different batches (n = 3 per each product) were included in this study. WGMP was processed from milk collected at different times of the year (Dairy Goat Co-operative, Hamilton, New Zealand). Goat1 IF (20:80 whey protein:casein ratio; made from whole goat milk; goat milk fat contributing 50% of the total fat, n = 3) and Cow1 (60:40 whey protein:casein ratio; made from cow skim milk and whey protein concentrate powders) were manufactured by Dairy Goat Co-operative. These goat and cow IFs are those being tested in a large randomized controlled trial (Ferry et al., 2023). To complete the panel of samples distinguished by their whey protein: casein ratio, Goat2 IF (60:40 whey protein:casein ratio; made using goat skim milk and whey protein concentrate powders) and Cow2 (60:40 whey protein:casein ratio; made using cow skim milk, demineralized whey, whey protein concentrate, and milk protein powders) were purchased in Europe.

Isolation of extracellular vesicles from milk and IF powders

Reconstitution of the powders

The production of IF used milk powder but also other ingredients. Thus, for the reconstitution, we used 12.6g of WGMP and 38.7g of IF powders mixed with 100 mL of ultra-pure water at 30 °C for 45 min. Each IF sample was prepared in duplicate, treated in parallel, and pooled once validated by different approaches (TEM, NTA, protein assay). Each IF was analyzed twice (2 × 180ml), these replicates were pooled at the end (360 mL of IF-3X).

Sample pre-treatment

Reconstituted milks were centrifuged at 3000 x g for 30 min at 4 °C (Allegra X-15R, Beckman Coulter, France) to remove the upper fat layer and collect the skimmed milk fraction. Caseins were acid precipitated by adding diluted (1/10 (v/v)) acetic acid to a final concentration of 10% (v/v), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min and addition of 10% (v/v) 1M sodium acetate for 10 min at room temperature (RT). Then, the clotted samples were centrifuged at 1,500xg for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was filtrated using vacuum-driven filtration system Millipore Steritop units, 0.22 μm (Merck Millipore). The filtrated supernatants were further concentrated by centrifugation at 4,000xg at 20 °C using 100 kDa Amicon-15 centrifugal filter units (Merck Millipore) to reach a final volume of between 8 and 14 mL per sample.

EV fractionation

After the pre-treatment, 500 μL of each sample were loaded onto qEV original 70 nm Gen2 SEC columns (Izon Science, New Zealand) previously equilibrated with filtered PBS 1X, using 0.10 μm vacuum filtration units. Each sample was fractionated using PBS 1X as elution buffer. The elution fractions containing EVs, final volume of 1.4 mL, were collected. The SEC fractionation procedure was repeated for the whole volume of pre-treated sample. The pooled fractions of each round were concentrated together using 100 kDa Amicon-15 centrifugal filter units (Merck Millipore) to a final volume of 400-600 μL. Two washing steps with 1 mL of PBS 1X loaded onto the same 100 kDa Amicon-15 centrifugal filter unit were performed to obtain highly pure EV fractions. The isolated EV samples were characterized and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Characterization of EVs isolated from milk powder and IFs

Transmission electron microscopy

The EV morphology was observed by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) with uranyl acetate negative staining as previously described (Krupova et al., 2025). Briefly, the EV samples were diluted in PBS 1X at a ratio of 1/8 to 1/10. At least five μL of suspension were placed for 5 min on a copper grid coated with a carbon film, then negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate. The grids were observed with a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope at 80 kV (Elexience - France).

Bradford protein assay

A volume of 1 μL of isolated EVs was diluted ultra-pure water (1:500) and mixed with equal volume of Coomassie Protein Assay reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. A standard calibration curve was set up using BSA (Thermo, USA). Absorbance measurement (OD) was acquired at 595 nm with a spectrophotometer UVmini-1240 (Shimadzu).

Size distribution and particle concentration by NTA

The size distribution and particle concentration were measured by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) using a ZetaView system (Particle Metrix, Germany) as previously described (Krupova et al., 2025), except that the PBS 1X was filtered at 0.1 μm. Samples were diluted to optimal values of 50-150 detected particles per frame. The parameters of acquisition were as previously described (Krupova et al., 2025). For data capture and analysis, the Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis Software (ZNTA) v. 8.05.04 was used. At least 500 valid tracks were completed for each run. The zeta potential (ZP) measurement was acquired with diluted EV sample in PBS 0.1X in continuous mode acquisition. Samples were analyzed in triplicate.

After analysis, the two replicates of each IF were pooled, the pooled volumes were measured, and the protein concentration, size distribution, zeta potential measurement and particle concentration by NTA were determined as described above.

Proteomic profiling of EVs by mass spectrometry

Sample preparation and mass spectrometry analysis

Sample preparation was carried out essentially as previously described (Krupova et al., 2025), including a separation on SDS-PAGE and tryptic digestion in gel, starting from 8 μg of EV proteins. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was carried out essentially as described previously (Krupova et al., 2025) using an acquisition method adapted to low quantities (10-20 ng).

Identification, and quantification of proteins and statistical analysis

Proteins were identified using X!Tandem algorithm (v.2017.2.1.4, Craig and Beavis, 2004) by matching tryptic peptides against the UniProt Capra hircus database (UniProtKB_Capra_hircus, version_2024 containing_116,790 entries) for goat IF samples, and UniProt Bos taurus database (UniProtKB Bos taurus, version_2020 containing_46,728 entries) for bovine IF samples.

Inference and grouping of proteins was realized using in house i2MassCroQ software (https://forge.inrae.fr/pappso/i2masschroq) based on mass matching with Data dependent Acquisition (DDA).

Two complementary methods of quantification were used. Spectral counting (SC) was defined as the number of MS2 spectra assigned to a protein (Liu et al., 2004). Extracted ion chromatograms (XIC) based approach, defined as the sum of MS1 intensities of all peptides associated with a protein (Schilling et al., 2012). Data post-processing and statistical analysis were performed by using the R package MCQR (version 0.6.15 and 1.0.1). Different bioinformatic pipelines were applied as indicated in (Bednarz et al., 2021). Protein abundance changes were detected by ANOVA tests for all methods (SC, XIC), and the abundance of a protein was considered significantly variable when the padj was <0.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). The bioinformatic tools used for proteomic analysis (i2MassChroQ version 1.0.2, MassChroQ, MCQR version 0.6.15, Langella et al., 2024) are open and free resources available: https://forgemia.inrae.fr/pappso.

RNA analysis of EVs

Total RNA isolation

The isolation of total RNA, including small RNA, from EV samples was performed using a mirVana Total RNA Isolation Kit (Life Technologies) optimized as previously described (Krupova et al., 2023). Firstly, Trizol LS Reagent (Thermo Fisher) was used instead of mirVana Lysis buffer. The RNAs were eluted from spin column in 100 μl of mirVana Elution buffer. The quality and quantity of RNA was controlled by 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent) using the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico kit and Nanodrop ND-1000 NanoDrop™ Spectrophotometer (Ozyme).

RT-qPCR analysis

Gene assays were performed, as part of a collaboration between the @BRIDGe core facility (GABI, INRAE, France) and Excilone (a private company) in the Laboratoire Partenarial Associé (LPA) NanoGabEx, by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Reverse transcription (RT) was performed on 16 μl of RNA using the SuperScript™ IV VILO™ MasterMix kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen, Thermo fisher Scientific), under the following conditions: 25 °C for 10 min, 50 °C for 10 min and 85 °C for 5 min. After a dilution (1:2), qPCR runs were achieved using Applied Biosystems SYBR Green PCR Mastermix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions, on a thermocycler QuantStudio 12K Flex instrument. After the optimization of qPCR systems (efficiency ranging from −3.25 to −3.45), amplification reactions were run in triplicate under the following conditions: 95 °C for 15 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Three Mammary Epithelial Cell specific target genes (KRT8, LALBA and CSN1S2) were quantified, as well as two genes highly expressed in the mammary gland (GLYCAM1 and MFGE8), and two internal control (Supplemental Table 1).

Small RNA sequencing

Small RNA-sequencing and sequencing data were performed by Qiagen Genomic services. The library was performed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen, France) from 5 μl total RNA according to manufacturer instructions. Briefly, small RNA ligated with 3′ and 5’ adapters were reverse transcribed, and PCR amplified (22 cycles). The cDNA libraries were pooled in equimolar ratios. The library pools were sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 (Illumina Inc) according to the manufacturer instructions. Raw data was de-multiplexed and FASTQ files for each sample were generated using Illumina bcl2fastq version 2.20.0.422. The data were analyzed using CLC Genomic Server 23.0.5 with the workflow “QIAseq miRNA Quantification” with standard parameters. The mapping was performed against miRBase (v.22) and trimming of common sequences and filtering for length (15 to 55 nt) were applied. Normalized miRNA expression was used for principal component analysis. The normalization and differential analysis of miRNAs were conducted using the DESeq2 R package (V. 1.36.0) with significance padj ≤0.05, a threshold ratio >1.2 and an average of 100 read counts. Data are available on request.

In silico functional analyses

The potential influence of the miRNAs on biological pathways and their relative importance was studied by predicting their base pairing with the entire sequence of their target mRNAs. Because predictive tools for ruminants are limited and due to high conservation of miRNA through evolution, bioinformatics analyses were performed using tools developed for humans with miRTarBase (V8.0) experimentally validated miRNA-gene interactions and padj ≤0.05. Target genes of detected miRNAs and potential networks were identified using Omicsnet software (https://www.omicsnet.ca/) with an FDR ≤0.05 and miRTarBase. The same procedure was used to determine the GO-cellular component using proteomic data. Venn diagram was obtained using Venny 2.1 software (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index2.0.2.html) (Heberle et al., 2015).

Results

Isolation of EVs from WGMP and IFs

EVs were successfully isolated from reconstituted WGMP and goat and cow IFs. The quantity of EVs isolated from WGMP per gram of starting powder was, as expected, higher than in the IFs, which have added ingredients. The range in the samples was 3.46E+12 to 3.92E+13 particles per mL (Table 1). The average EV size was similar across all samples (127.9 to 139.6 nm), and the amount of protein (Table 1) in the EVs isolated ranged from 907.7 (Goat1) to 6545.5 μg (Cow1).

Validation of the origin of isolated EVs using RT-qPCR analysis

In order to demonstrate the mammary epithelial cell origin of EVs isolated, we performed RT-qPCR using MEC-specific markers. Thus, we analyzed mRNAs encoding 5 proteins (LALBA, CSN1S2, KRT8, GlyCAM1 and MFGE8), previously validated (Bevilacqua et al., 2006). Two internal control genes, RPS24 and PPIA, were also quantified to normalize the results. Each MEC-specific markers mRNA was detected in each sample, showing that all samples contained mRNAs specific to mammary tissue (Table 2). In addition, results are consistent with the differences in RNA concentration observed between samples.

Comparisons of goat IFs (Goat1 vs Goat2)

The proteome analysis on EVs isolated from both goat IFs identified a total of 1142 proteins, 225 significantly DAPs (padj < 0.05 with a fold change >1.2) were detected using the XIC quantification method, and 85 proteins using the SC quantification method. Since 31 DAPs were shared, this gives a total of 279 DAPs (Supplemental Table 2) between the goat IFs.

A comparison of the 100 most abundant proteins quantified by the XIC method, revealed a set of 56 proteins common to the EVs isolated from both IFs (Fig. 3), among which we observed several tetraspanins, proteins involved in intracellular membrane trafficking (Rab11b, ARF4, RAC1, SNAP23), and proteins occurring in the ExoCarta top 100 list (CD9, HSPA8, RAP1B, TPI1, CDC42, BSG, GDI2, MFGE8 and SLC16A1).

Comparisons of Goat1 with WGMP

To evaluate the contribution of the manufacturing and formulation of IF, we compared Goat1 (made with WGM) with WGMP proteomic data. A total of 595 proteins were identified using the XIC quantification method with eight DAPs (padj < 0.05 with a fold change >1.2), and the SC method identified 254 proteins, with only two DAPs. Therefore, only 10 (SC + XIC) DAPs (Table 4) were detected from this comparison.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that goat and cow IFs contained significant amounts of mEVs, isolated using an optimized protocol based on size exclusion chromatography (SEC). The standard steps for isolation of EVs from milk using SEC proved somewhat challenging due to foaming and turbidity. In the present study, the method was optimized. Differences in ingredients could also explain the debate over the presence of EVs in IFs.

The isolated particles belong to the class of EVs since their size, morphology and number were in line with the literature in which EVs from goat, cow (Krupova et al., 2023) and human (Kupsco et al., 2021) milk were characterized. We also confirmed the presence of mRNAs encoding milk proteins, such as αS2-casein and α-lactalbumin. Due to the unavailability of the fresh milk used in the IF making, we compared the IF miRNomes of the present study with previously published miRNomes for fresh goat and cow milks (Krupova et al., 2023). We observed their close similarity, confirming the milk origin of EVs isolated in the present study. We also confirmed the presence of EVs in the isolated particles through proteomic analyses performed on EVs isolated from goat and cow milk-derived IFs. We identified well-known EV markers, including cytosolic (annexins and Ras-related proteins) and transmembrane or lipid-bound extracellular proteins, such as tetraspanins (CD9, CD63) and MFGE8 following the MISEV recommendations (Welsh et al., 2024). Furthermore, the most enriched cellular component identified by GO-CC analysis of the proteomics data was related to vesicles and membranes. Elsewhere, consistent with previous studies (Krupova et al., 2025), the EV proteomes of the goat WGMP, goat IFs and cow IFs in this study display a low abundance of caseins. All these observations confirm the relevance and effectiveness of the implemented EV isolation protocol. However, it should be noted that proteins from the milk fat globule membrane (MGFM), including MFGE8 and XDH, which were among the most abundant EVs-associated proteins, were in all samples. Conversely, BTN1A1, another protein of the MFGM, was only found in EVs from goat IFs. One cannot therefore rule out a co-fractionation of non-EV entities such as membrane fragments, particularly those derived from the MFGM. It may reflect MFGM carryover during isolation of EVs rather than genuine differences in EV cargo composition. On the other hand, the presence of lactadherin (MFGE8) which is on the ExoCarta top 100 list can be true EV-derived proteins as well as arising from potential MFGM-derived contaminants. Furthermore, BTN1A1 was exclusively detected in goat IF EVs (observed both with Goat1 and Goat2), and that it was also among the most abundant proteins in EVs from goat milk powders (Krupova et al., 2025). This difference between species could also be explained by a difference in milk secretion. It was reported that milk secretion in goats is an apocrine secretion from mammary epithelial cells (Neveu et al., 2002) which was not the case in cows. All these observations lead to consider a potential effect of goat milk fat rather than raising questions about how milk was processed at the source.

Elsewhere, it is worth noting the large number of proteins that have not been formally identified but exhibiting characteristics of immunoglobulins (igldcp; see Supplemental Table 2) in the list of significantly differentially abundant entities, identified both by SC and XIC methods, when comparing Goat1 and Goat2. This raises questions about the origin of these proteins, which, if they are confirmed to be transported by EVs, could potentially play an immunomodulatory role on infant immunity.

One of the most abundant EV-associated proteins, RAB11B, a small Rab GTPase known as a key regulator of intracellular membrane trafficking (Homma et al., 2021), shared by the four IFs but not found in WGMP, was previously identified as a protein marker of EV subsets in cow's milk (Benmoussa et al., 2019) and HM (van Herwijnen et al., 2016).

The tumor susceptibility 101 protein (TSG101), which is a classical EV marker protein occurring at the third rank of the exocarta list, was found in the four IFs and WGMP analyzed in the present study. This protein is a member of the ESCRT complex (endosomal sorting complexes required for transport). A set of charged multivesicular body proteins (CHMPs) was also found in all IFs. Both participate in the biogenesis of multivesicular bodies in which intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) are formed, giving rise to exosomes.

All of the EV markers identified in our study confirm that IFs contain EVs. To determine the miRNA content of theses EVs derived from IFs, we established their miRNomes. Over 340 miRNAs were identified in IF EVs. Interestingly, miR-148a, which is a very abundant miRNA in HM (Liao et al., 2017; van Herwijnen et al., 2018), is ranking below 100th in terms of abundance in the goat and cow IFs. The low level of miR-148a in formulas was already reported (Cemali et al., 2025). This difference between raw milk and formulas could be due to differential effect of technological processes according to the miRNA considered (Abou El Qassim et al., 2023). Although there is high similarity of WGMP, Goat and Cow IF miRNomes (Fig. 6), PCA analysis considering the abundance of miRNAs discriminated Cow IFs from the Goat IFs and WGMP (Fig. 7). This discrimination between cow and goat milk powders was aligned with previous results (Krupova et al., 2023).

To evaluate the biological effects of miRNAs of IF EVs, we focused on the most abundant miRNAs shared by all IFs (Fig. 8). The predicted biological processes most influenced by these 16 common miRNAs are those of cell life, and protein and nucleic acid processes, which are crucial during the development and growth of infants. Breastfeeding is the best nutrition for infant growth; however, we identified 12 common miRNAs among the top 20 most abundant miRNAs in IF and HM, showing that the IF and HM miRNomes present a great similarity (Table 6). In particular, the miR-30, let-7 and miR-200 families are described as crucial for infant growth and development (Leroux et al., 2021). The let-7 family is a regulatory factor involved in cellular basal metabolism and immune response (Wang et al., 2015). Among the top 20 most abundant miRNAs, we also observed that two miRNAs (miR-375 and let-7i) were only shared between goat samples (WGMP, Goat1 and Goat2), meaning that they were more abundant in goat than in cow samples. Let-7i was reported to inhibit interleukin-6 expression and thus involved in inflammation (Wang et al., 2015). Similarly, miR-375 was also reported to attenuate intestine injury in neonatal infants (Nan et al., 2024). This influence could be important during infant growth to strengthen immunity. In a human study, miR-375 was reported to be associated with protection from atopy for infants (Hicks et al., 2022). In addition, several miRNAs highly abundant in the goat and cow IFs (let-7i, let-7c, and miR-26b), were reported to be specific to human colostrum in comparison with human mature milk (Yun et al., 2021). Interestingly, the let-7 family is over-represented in the top 20 miRNAs of all IFs, and this family was reported to be over-represented in pasteurized breast milk compared to fresh milk (Torrez Lamberti et al., 2023).

Interestingly, the number of DAMs and DAPs was higher in the comparison between Goat1 and Goat2 than in the comparison between Cow1 and Cow2. We can hypothesize that this may be related to IF composition and processing differences. Indeed, Cow1 and 2 have the same whey protein:casein ratio and low milk fat, whereas Goat1 and Goat2 differ in their whey protein:casein ratio and milk fat content. However, one limitation of our study is the small number of different IF brands we studied. Indeed, we studied only two brands of goat and two cow IFs. To ascertain this hypothesis, we need to enlarge our panel of samples with different whey protein:casein ratios, different manufacturers and countries of origin.

The difference between Goat IF and Cow IF was also apparent in the comparisons of the top 20 DAMs (Table 7). Among the four miRNAs shared by three lists of DAMs, miR-30a-5p is very abundant in bovine mEVs (Le Guillou et al., 2019) and in mEVs of goat and cow (Krupova et al., 2023). In addition, miR-30a-5p was found to be more abundant in WGMP than skimmed goat milk powder EVs, which suggested an effect of skimming upstream of the drying process on miRNA profile (Krupova et al., 2025). As regarding miR-148a (see below), the findings suggest that miR-30a-5p, highly abundant milk reported to influence metabolism, is sensitive to dairy processing. and to evaluate the effects of the technological processes used in IF production, aiming to control the loss of these DAMs. We also identified five miRNAs (let-7a-5p, miR-26a, let-7f, miR-200c, and miR-26b) that are shared between WGMF vs Goat1 and Goat1 vs Goat2 DAM lists. The common sample of these comparisons being Goat1, we can suspect that the different whey:casein ratio and fat source of Goat1 or the Goat1 processing would account for the difference in abundance of these 5 miRNAs, which were abundant. As mentioned above, the let-7 and miR-200 miRNA families, as well as miR-26a, have been described to be crucial for infant growth and development (Leroux et al., 2021).

The Gene Ontology-Biological Processes (GO-BP) enriched identified using the DAMs pointed out the cell life, morphology and intracellular transport (Fig. 9), which impact the infant growth. The effects of milk-derived miRNAs on human gene expression after migration through the plasma and delivery to specific organs through a cross-kingdom action were reported (Vaucheret and Chupeau, 2012; Liao et al., 2017). Despite the ongoing debate, there is growing evidence pointing out the influence of derived-food miRNAs on consumers’ health (Leroux et al., 2026). In this study, although their concentration is lower than that observed in HM, we clearly demonstrate the existence of mEVs containing miRNAs in goat and cow IFs. The next step, which will be the greatest challenge, will be to determine the exact functional impact of ingested miRNAs of IFs on infants during their development and through their life. For example, miR-375 was observed in the top 20 miRNAs in Goat1 and Goat2 and belongs to the DAMs in the comparison between goat and cow IFs. In addition to its protective effect on atopy, miR-375 may also influence gut maturation (Leroux et al., 2021). This miRNA was also reported to regulate epithelial plasticity which could also be associated with gut development (Pillman et al., 2018). Similarly, the let-7 family, whose members were abundant and showed variability between IFs, is known to be involved in neurogenesis (Wulczyn et al., 2007) and in the inflammatory response (Marques;Rocha et al., 2018; Song et al., 2016). The influence of mEVs detected in IFs on infant immunity could also be through the immunoglobulin-like proteins (Ali et al., 2026). Interestingly, in our study, we observed that IF mEVs contained a large number of immunoglobulin-like proteins. These observations strongly indicate that the miRNA and protein composition of IF mEVs should be further investigated for their bioactive role in infant development. However, further studies including preclinical or clinical studies are needed to decipher the functional influence of these miRNAs on infant growth and development.

Conclusions

This study confirms the presence of mEVs in whole goat milk powder and goat and cow milk-based IFs. However, we cannot exclude the presence of MFGM, particularly in goat's IF, due to the specificity of milk secretion in this species. It highlights differences in the mEV proteome and miRNA profile influenced by species and the type of dairy ingredients used to formulate the IF. With a growing understanding of the role of miRNAs in infant growth and development of the infant, the results of this study underline the importance to understand how IFs can be optimized by selection of dairy ingredients to supply the most valuable set of miRNAs and proteome. This study paves the way of considering EV and their cargo, particularly miRNAs, as components of interest in IFs to better reproduce the beneficial properties of HM for infant growth and future health. The formulation of IFs, the selection of their ingredients and their processes must take into account the contribution of mEVs and their content in order to adapt their formulas to make them more similar to HM. However, further studies are needed to confirm the predicted functional influence of EVs and their miRNA contents in infants.
