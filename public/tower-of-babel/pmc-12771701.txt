Introduction

Oral cancer consists of the sixth most common malignancy worldwide [1] and is defined as a malignant neoplasm originating from the lip, tongue, buccal area, vestibule, gingivae, dental alveoli, and roof or floor of the oral cavity [2]. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN), 389,485 new lip and oral cancer cases and 188,230 related deaths were reported in 2022, ranking this disease 16th in incidence and 15th in mortality among all cancers [3]. The burden is highest in Asia, where lower-income countries with larger populations and lower Human Development Index (HDI) values are more susceptible [3, 4]. Globally, incidence and mortality rates in males are more than twice those in females [4]. In Iran, however, oral and lip cancers are not among the top ten malignancies, with incidence rates of 1.96 and 1.36 per 100,000 for men and women, respectively, and a male-to-female ratio of about 1.2 [5].

The primary risk factors for Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) include tobacco and alcohol consumption, betel quid chewing, and poor nutrition [6]. In addition, infectious agents such as Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), Human Papillomavirus (HPV), Candida albicans, and certain bacteria may contribute to carcinogenesis by disrupting the balance of normal oral flora [7, 8]. Other contributing factors include age, sex, genetic predisposition, chronic inflammation, Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and immunosuppression [9].

Despite advances in treatment, the delayed diagnosis of oral cancer has resulted in a mortality rate close to 50%. Barriers to early detection stem from both patient- and physician-related factors, as well as the absence of population-based oral cancer screening programs [10]. Given the critical importance of early cancer diagnosis, accurate data registration acquires a special significance. Delays in detection not only increase mortality but also complicate treatment and reduce survival chances. By systematically recording diagnostic, clinical, and epidemiological information, cancer registries help the health system to identify patterns of delayed diagnosis, track treatment outcomes, and design more targeted interventions. Therefore, accurate data collection is not just a research tool, but a prerequisite for improving early detection and increasing the chances of survival in patients. From a scientific perspective, cancer registries also play an important role in evaluating and promoting the effectiveness of screening programs, as they allow for comparison of disease stage and survival rates between screened and unscreened individuals [11].

A well-integrated, standardized health data system can interconnect healthcare, education, research, and development [12] and further improve interoperability [13]. Such structured data collection is most effectively achieved through registry systems, which use observational methods to assemble, store, retrieve, analyze, and disseminate information on individuals with a particular condition or prior exposure to risk factors [12].

Accurate and standardized data are the foundation of effective cancer control. If reliable information is not available, planning for prevention, early detection, and treatment is fragmented and inefficient. Cancer registries address this problem by systematically recording high-quality data that is not limited to incidence and mortality statistics, but also includes patient characteristics, tumor characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes. The value of these registries is enhanced when data is collected accurately, completely, and in a timely manner, as even small errors or inconsistencies can lead to inaccurate epidemiological conclusions or ineffective therapeutic interventions.

Over the past decades, many countries have established population- and hospital-based cancer registries that encompass head and neck malignancies. Examples include the Taiwan Cancer Registry (TCR), one of the most comprehensive in the world, with nearly complete coverage and detailed clinical information since 2002 [14]. Libya established the Benghazi Cancer Registry (BCR) [15] in 2002, followed by the Sabratha Cancer Registry in 2006. They were further incorporated into the Libyan National Cancer Registry Program along with three other cancer registries to embody the entire nation. In Europe, cancer registration has a long history. Germany initiated the Hamburg registry in 1927 and now has independent state-based registries [16]. The national population-based head and neck cancer registry of Japan [17], which was reestablished in 2012, houses more than 150,000 documented cases of untreated confirmed head and neck malignancies, including oral cancer. The Netherlands developed similar statewide population-based registries for patients with head and neck cancer [18], which store data on tumor and patient characteristics, treatment and its side effects, recurrence, and survival rates. The Swedish Head and Neck Cancer Registry (SweHNCR) [19] is another analogous population-based repository, containing 783 squamous cell carcinoma cases that were documented between 2008 and 2019. On the other hand, a hospital-based oral cancer registry [20] was established in India to capture the cases of oral cancer and Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders (OPMDs) in a rural area.

Considering the global advances in cancer registration and the limited availability of such systems in Iran, the present study aims to develop, implement, and evaluate a registry system specifically for oral and lip squamous cell carcinoma. We intend to devise a web-based platform that is broadly accessible and convenient to facilitate the documentation of information related to multiple aspects of oral cancer.

Methods and materials

This is an applied-developmental study carried out at Tehran University of Medical Sciences. In this research, it is intended to design and evaluate a registration system for oral and lip squamous cell cancer. This work has been carried out in two separate phases, including the development, implementation, and usability evaluation. We have previous work methods and present work methods: 2.1 and 2.2 sub-sections.

Previous work: descriptive phase

The initial phases of this project were detailed in a previously published paper [21]. Initially, primary information elements concerning disease registries were obtained from scientific literature published in reputable databases, including Medline (via PubMed), Web of Science, and Scopus. Additionally, a review of global disease registry websites, an assessment of current patient records, and input from the research team were conducted. Consequently, based on the findings from the literature review, a researcher-developed questionnaire was created to facilitate a survey among specialists. A final survey was conducted by 29 experts in the last stage. The Delphi method was employed to assess each information element in the questionnaire using a scoring scale of one to four, aligned with the Likert scale. Items were deemed essential only if they received more than 70% of the total points. The primary MDSs include demographic and clinical information about the patient, details regarding administered medications, initial diagnostic assessments, biopsy results, tumor staging at diagnosis, clinical tumor characteristics, surgical interventions, histopathological tumor features, pathologic stage classification, radiotherapy information, follow-up data, and disease registry functionalities. The model encompasses the unique features of oral and lip squamous cell cancer registry systems, including the title of the disease registration program, the studied population, the geographic scope of the registration, primary objectives, condition definition, diagnostic methods, and registration type.

Discussion

The present study was conducted subsequent to the previous work on developing the MDS for oral and lip squamous cell carcinoma registration. We designed and implemented an online platform called Oralcanreg to conveniently gather and process information related to clinicodemographic, drug administration, diagnosis, tumor stage and characteristics, surgery, radiotherapy, and follow-up variables. The software comprises pages for a variety of tasks, such as entering patient information and history, management of medications, searching, and exporting reports. The login page offers two methods of authentication via national code and username, and authorization is considered by establishing multilevel access to resources. The collected data has originated from multiple sources comprising other information and communication systems (e.g., HIS, PACS), physical records or reports, and follow-up communication with patients or family members. We conducted an investigation into the completeness of these data, which we will discuss in more detail later. Moreover, a phased approach was considered for system evaluation in which expert meetings were held initially to identify and resolve issues and bugs related to data entry, speed, user interface, authentication, and report generation. Next, the usability analysis was carried out in collaboration with specialists who filled out a standard questionnaire after employing the software’s tools and features.

Comparison with existing registries highlights both similarities and advantages. Like CanDCap, Oralcanreg is web-based and ensures structured data collection. However, it employs ICD-11 coding and demonstrates superior user interface satisfaction. Similar to international population-based registries, such as Taiwan’s Cancer Registry, Oralcanreg enables comprehensive analysis of oral cancer trends and patient outcomes. Nevertheless, unlike some national systems, it currently lacks individual-level behavioral and socioeconomic variables [24, 25].

In contrast to our study, another nationwide population-based registry that has supplied data for oral cancer distribution analysis is the Scottish Cancer Registry. Smith et al. [26] investigated the variations in the sociodemographic profile of cancer patients and provided trends of oral cavity cancer based on the 20,850 records of head and neck malignancies registered between 2001 and 2020 in this database. Some indexes measure the level of disadvantage that residents of a region face in terms of income, employment, education, health, access to services, crime, and housing domains. The registry further comprises profound verification while documenting the cases and checks for quality and accuracy, resulting in superior data quality and minimized inconsistency.

Data completeness

All types of registry systems may struggle to reach their full potential due to some restrictions [27], such as the lack of consistent quality, active follow-up, and data granularity [28, 29]. These issues, along with meager hospital documentation and apathy among registration staff, lead to missing data that eventually attenuate the system’s capability to make unbiased inferences [30, 31]. The presented software in this study received data with variation in completeness across different sections. The lowest one, “Diagnostic assessment”, represented solely 0.47% of the total data with 96.8% missing values, which may stem from either not conducting or not documenting these evaluations. The follow-up information resides near the total percentage in terms of missing and available values, thus indicating this procedure was less of a challenge here. At the other end, “Biopsy” and “Radiotherapy/Chemotherapy” were sections with the highest amount of data entry and comprised 13.97% and 13.54% of the database, respectively. These rates illustrate the significance of these practices in the diagnosis and treatment stages.

Interpretatively, sections like diagnostic assessment, medication history, and surgical information had a greater percentage of missing data than others. The underlying cause of this issue was primarily incomplete or inconsistent documentation in patient records during the pilot data collection period. In many instances, this information was either unavailable in the Health Information System (HIS) or was recorded in fragmented sources, making it difficult to fully integrate into the registry. Iranian hospital’s health information management and medical records teams are consistently striving to gather precise and trustworthy data. However, despite these efforts, structural limitations in the medical documentation system continue to cause deficiencies. These shortcomings are not specific to our registry but rather reflect broader challenges in the health system in systematically recording some clinical details.

One way to address data deficiencies is to automatically extract data from HISs, which can help to automatically collect data from patient’s medical records without the need for manual data entry. This approach can reduce human errors and speed up data collection. In addition, if physicians and healthcare staff enter data directly into the system, additional incentives such as financial rewards or work points may help to improve data quality. Feedback and audit loops can help staff and physicians recognize when their data is not meeting standards and needs to be corrected by providing periodic feedback on data quality. Such actions can gradually improve data quality and accuracy [32, 33].

Evaluation of usability

The overall scores resulting from the QUIS framework imply high usability and promising performance of the software. High evaluation scores are deemed valid and reliable, supported by an unbiased survey of real users in a clinical setting. We attribute these positive outcomes to effective user interface design, appropriate terminology, and efficient system operation.

The section “General functionality of the system” captures the broad reaction of the end-users. According to this section’s metrics, the system depicted high performance, ease of use, satisfactory design, and adaptability, albeit the ongoing utilization gained the lowest value. This may entail the pessimism of some users about the necessity and further adoption of the system or the lack of proper incentives to keep the user motivated. The second section, “Screen capabilities”, is associated with visual features of the software. The interface acquired near-perfect scores regarding this category, with character’s readability achieving the highest, followed by the color usage for highlighting, page sequence, and information arrangement. The lower score earned by “Organization of information” indicates some participants found the screen layout slightly complicated.

The next section, titled “Terminologies and system information,” discusses how well the system communicates and informs the user. Likewise, the scores related to terminology, message position, informing progression, and usefulness of errors were quite satisfactory. Nonetheless, there appear to be inadequate messages indicating what the user should enter in a field based on the “Prompts for inputs” value. As the name of the next category implies, it relates to how easy it is to learn and employ the system and its features. Based on the acquired grades, the software is conveniently learnable with handy features and straightforward names and commands. The system further provides auxiliary materials and a sufficient number of support messages to acquaint the user with its capabilities and controls. The final section evaluates the software’s overall competence in terms of speed, reliability, noise, error correction, and user-friendliness. Similarly, the proposed framework depicted high capabilities in this part. However, some interface design choices appeared to significantly distract users in the “System tends to be” subcategory.

Implications for health practice

The adoption of registration systems benefits policymakers by ushering the plans, regulations, and resource allocation for the future, as well as offering a broad view of the current status. As the aforementioned epidemiological and observational studies illustrated, an oral cancer registry may supply data necessary for estimating the dispersion of this disease according to social, demographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics. These registry-based contributions can further reveal disease trajectory, risk factors, life expectancy, and the effectiveness of certain interventions or procedures. The stored information is undoubtedly far more valuable than the registry system itself, even though the data could not have been assembled and arranged without the help of this tool. In developing countries, cancer registration is the primary method to gain insight into the burden and pattern of the illness, etiology, and preventive measures [34, 35].

A population-based oral cancer registry can be further involved in the establishment of screening programs as it offers crucial means and metrics for the evaluation of practices in screening as well as prevention and treatment [36, 37]. One of the promising use cases of registry databases is knowledge discovery via machine learning and data mining techniques. The prediction of the future burden of the disease, probability of malignancy or recurrence, and categorizing the disease condition are some of the endowments made possible by machine learning innovations along with high-quality data brought by long-term registration. Prior to implementing the current system, information about oral cancer patient’s follow-up and response to therapy was scant, varied depending on the case, and vaguely documented. This registration yields valuable assistance by offering accurate and reliable follow-up and treatment response information. Therefore, completeness should be a priority as lack of it may lead to misestimating the survival rates and checkpoints should be in place to verify the integrity of data [38, 39].

Limitations and future directions

There were several hindrances throughout the system deployment and evaluation. Poor documentation practices resulted in incomplete hospital records, thereby increasing the amount of missing information in the registry database. The design and review processes required continuous collaboration across departments and staff, which was sometimes time-consuming and difficult to arrange. Data entry posed another challenge, as it was often assigned to staff members for whom this responsibility was neither part of their defined duties nor sufficiently incentivized. Furthermore, securing financial resources for the continued use of the system and registry remains an ongoing concern.

Future research may include expanding the system to other types of cancer or oral and dental diseases, and consideration should be given to expanding its use in diagnostic and treatment centers nationwide. In addition, software enhancements through the development of modules and Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) will be on the agenda.

Also, the system was evaluated by a team of 21 people from one institution, which limits the generalizability of the results. To address this issue, future research could evaluate the system in multiple institutions and different healthcare settings to obtain more robust and valid results.

To address the issue of incomplete records, future phases of the system will emphasize data completion. This will be achieved through standardized data collection protocols, more precise definition of required variables, and inclusion of mandatory fields in the software. These measures, together with the collaboration of health information management teams, are expected to significantly reduce incomplete data and improve the quality of information available for further analysis. By reducing incomplete records, system performance will be enhanced and, ultimately, the health care system will be strengthened. This will allow government officials to make decisions based on accurate and up-to-date information.

Conclusion

An online web-based software was proposed for the registry of oral and lip squamous cell carcinoma cases. The system’s input variables for each aspect were thoroughly selected based on the resulted MDS from the previous research. Close cooperation with healthcare specialists and personnel, as end users, is an indispensable factor in developing, appraisal, and delivering a user-oriented software with high usability, reliance, and workflow integration. Further, a perfect information system with faulty and fragmented content is far from being beneficial. Therefore, the correct usage of such tools is as crucial as well designing them. A cancer registration tool is advantageous at various stages of diagnosis, care, and management by offering timely access to medical records, storing information about patient care and follow-up, providing standard forms and data elements, and integrating the information.
