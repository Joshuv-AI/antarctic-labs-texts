Gender differences in the long-term impact of early-life exposure to war on physical and mental health: Evidence from China
Authors: Zhen Zhang, Daisheng Tang, Xinyuan Wang, Tao Bu, Qiushi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral studies have examined the impact of exposure to war in early life on long-term health outcomes. However, few studies have examined differences in these effects by gender and type of war. This study examines the impact of exposure to multiple war events before the age of 10 on the long-term physical and mental health of adults aged 65 years and older in China, as well as differences these effects between wars of aggression and civil wars.Data and methodsThis study employed an Ordinary Least Squares model with year and household fixed effects, using data from six waves (2002-2018) of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey. First, the study examined the impact of war shocks and war intensity on individual health. Second, we incorporated interaction terms between war shocks and gender and between war intensity and gender into the model to investigate gender differences in the health effects of war. Subsequently, this study tested the heterogeneity based on the nature of war and explored strategies to mitigate war-related trauma.ResultsThe findings indicate that, compared with individuals not exposed to war, the physical and mental health of those exposed to war declined by 17.5% and 12.1%, respectively. Among those exposed to war, women had lower physical and mental health than men. Additionally, aggressive wars had a stronger negative impact on individual health than civil wars. They also worsened both physical and mental health among women to a greater extent than among men, whereas civil wars only caused greater harm to women's physical health. This result may be attributed to two interconnected factors. First, aggressive wars, characterized by plunder and widespread violence, are inherently destructive and cause severe trauma. Second, women's vulnerability in conflict amplifies their risk of experiencing such trauma. The study also explored whether active social participation and adequate health care in adulthood could alleviate war-related trauma. No evidence of such a compensatory effect was identified.DiscussionThe study concludes that war inflicts severe health damage, with aggressive wars causing greater trauma and women suffering disproportionately. These findings extend beyond existing medical frameworks, identifying women as a critical group requiring targeted, structural interventions. This study reveals new research directions for mitigating war-related health consequences and informs policies aimed at improving public well-being in post-conflict settings.

Introduction

The Russia–Ukraine conflict represents the largest ground conflict in Europe since World War II and has significantly affected the early lives of millions of children (Osokina et al., 2023). The association between war and long-term health outcomes has garnered substantial attention in academia, with extensive literature providing scientific support for the present study (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014; Lee, 2014; Phadera, 2021; Singhal, 2019). However, considering that gender refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviors, and identities of women, men, and gender-diverse individuals (Heidari et al., 2016), the present study focuses specifically on gender differences between men and women. The health impacts of war may differ significantly between these two groups. This remains an open question warranting further discussion.

Research relevant to this study primarily encompasses two aspects. The first focuses on the relationship between war and health. Existing studies have predominantly examined the association between specific wars and long-term health outcomes, including the Vietnam War (Singhal, 2019), the Korean War (Lee, 2014), World War II in Germany (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2017), World War II in China and Japan (Li & Koulovatianos, 2020), and the Nigerian Civil War (Akresh et al., 2012). These studies have explored the effects of war on self-rated health (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014), mental health (Mohammed & Neuner, 2022), height (Phadera, 2021), and diabetes (Kesternich et al., 2014). These studies provide detailed analyses of the impact of war on individual health and consistently conclude that exposure to war leads to significant health losses. However, these studies do not account for heterogeneity in the nature of war. Specifically, the destructive power of wars of aggression may be considerably greater, potentially resulting in more pronounced adverse health outcomes (Mohammed & Neuner, 2022).

The second aspect pertains to research on gender differences. Gender differences manifest in socioeconomic behaviors, including hyperactivity (Rydell et al., 2009), mental health (King et al., 2019), and life expectancy (Alaimo & Nigri, 2024; Barigozzi et al., 2023), among others. Such variations contribute to substantial disparities in long-term health outcomes among individuals exposed to adverse shocks during early life. For instance, while males tend to cope with depression by externalizing trauma, females are more likely to internalize mental trauma, leading to the development of internalized mental disorders (Baños & Miragall, 2024). Consequently, females exposed to negative experiences are more severely affected by depression than males (Charlson et al., 2019). However, these studies have not directly determined whether females exposed to war experience greater health-related trauma than males.

Therefore, the numerous war events in modern China (before 1949), including both aggressive wars and civil wars, provide an excellent historical context for an in-depth examination of whether significant gender differences exist in the health effects of war and whether these effects differ between aggressive wars and civil wars. Using data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) for the period from 2002 to 2018, this study first empirically examined the impact of war on individual health and assessed whether this impact varies by gender. Second, this study classified wars as aggressive wars and civil wars to analyze differences in their respective health impacts. Finally, this study discusses strategies to mitigate the adverse health effects of war and explores channels for enhancing social health welfare.

The contributions of this study are reflected in the following three aspects. First, in comparison with previous studies on the impact of war on health (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014; Lee, 2014; Phadera, 2021; Singhal, 2019), the study focused specifically on gender differences in the health effects of war. Gender differences are a perennial topic in economic and social research, including in higher education enrollment rate (Stinebrickner et al., 2026), family care (Cui et al., 2021), and life expectancy (Alaimo & Nigri, 2024; Barigozzi et al., 2023). War, as a unique and traumatic event with profound societal destructiveness (Baez, 2011), results in long-term health outcomes that may exhibit significant gender disparities. However, this perspective has been largely overlooked in existing studies. By reexamining war-related trauma through a gender-based analytical framework and investigating whether war inflicts greater harm on females, this study provides a novel contribution to the field of war and health, representing an important innovation in research perspective.

Second, compared to studies on specific war events (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014, 2017; Akresh et al., 2012; Lee, 2014; Li & Koulovatianos, 2020; Singhal, 2019), this study extends the analysis to encompass multiple war events over an extended period, differentiating between aggressive wars and civil wars. Building on this approach, the study expands the scope of research from the relationship between specific wars and individual health to an examination of how the nature of war influences individual health. This approach allows for a more nuanced examination of the differential health impacts of aggressive wars and civil wars and facilitates the extension of these effects to other domains. This approach represents a significant advancement and offers an innovative perspective in the study of the economic consequences of war.

Third, while several studies have investigated the mechanisms through which war impacts individual health—such as war-induced parental loss leading to mental health deterioration (Singhal, 2019), and the destruction of healthcare services and food shortages resulting in adverse health outcomes (Betsi et al., 2006; Fürst et al., 2009)—research on mitigating the health effects of war remains relatively limited. In particular, the compensatory effects of various factors, such as active social participation and adequate health care services, may differ in addressing the physical injuries and mental trauma caused by war. To address this gap, this study investigated the moderating effects of social participation and healthcare services on war exposure. However, the results indicated that neither of these factors showed statistically significant compensatory effects, failing to effectively alleviate the negative impact of war on individual long-term health outcomes. This finding of “compensatory ineffectiveness” constitutes a key contribution of the study. This finding demonstrates that conventional social support and basic health care systems may have structural limitations when confronted with extreme systemic shocks such as war. Based on these findings, the study emphasizes the severity and long-term nature of war-related health consequences.

The remaining sections of the study are organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 provides an overview of the major wars in modern China. Section 4 covers the data description, variable construction, and empirical analysis model. Section 5 analyzes the empirical results. Section 6 presents the discussion. Section 7 presents the research conclusion.

Review of related literature

The fetal origins hypothesis posits that in utero exposure to adverse health shocks exerts a profound and enduring influence on an individual's long-term health and socioeconomic outcomes. This theory was initially advanced by Barker (Barker, 1992, 1994). As research on this subject has evolved, scholars have expanded this theoretical framework, arguing that exposure to unfavorable environments during early life stages can have lasting effects on individual health (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014; Lee, 2014; Phadera, 2021; Singhal, 2019). War, as a significant shock and traumatic event (Baez, 2011), may lead to diminished stress-regulation capabilities (Danese & McEwen, 2012), increase risk for negative mental health outcomes in adulthood (Roth et al., 2022), higher levels of stress-related chronic diseases (Shonkoff et al., 2012), severe depression (Chen et al., 2021), anxiety disorders (Subramaniam et al., 2020), and post-traumatic stress disorder (Copeland et al., 2007; Levy & Sidel, 2013). A substantial body of literature has accumulated, providing scientific validity for the present research question.

Gender differences are evident in the long-term health outcomes resulting from early adverse experiences. Extensive evidence indicates greater susceptibility to depression among women following adverse experiences in early life compared with males (Alcalá et al., 2017; Heim & Nemeroff, 2001). For instance, women who experience two or more types of adverse childhood experiences have a significantly higher risk of developing anxiety disorders than men, and they are particularly vulnerable to emotional and sexual abuse (Zhu et al., 2025). Moreover, the concepts of masculinity and femininity shape stress exposure, coping strategies, social relationships, and individual vulnerabilities, with women being more prone to internalizing disorders and men to externalizing disorders (Baños & Miragall, 2024). These gender differences extend beyond mental health to physical health as well: early-life adversity also increases women's risk of cancer (Alcalá et al., 2017). Moreover, the emphasis of traditional Chinese Confucian culture on gender roles, authoritarian parenting, and the value of filial piety may reinforce this phenomenon, leading to unresolved mental trauma in women and more enduring health impacts (Wei et al., 2024).

Existing research on the health consequences of war has predominantly focused on specific conflicts, such as the Vietnam War (Singhal, 2019), the Korean War (Lee, 2014), and World War II (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2017; Li & Koulovatianos, 2020), among others. However, this body of work has three key limitations. First, it has largely overlooked gender differences in the health impacts of war. Second, it does not adequately account for the differential destructive power and distinct health outcomes of aggressive wars compared with civil wars. While research confirms that aggressive wars, characterized by plunder and destruction (Mohammed & Neuner, 2022), induce severe mental disorders (Hecker et al., 2013; Hermenau et al., 2013), the absence of comparative analysis with civil wars leaves the relationship between the nature of war and individual health insufficiently explored. Third, although some studies have discussed the mechanisms through which war affects health (Betsi et al., 2006; Fürst et al., 2009; Singhal, 2019), there has been insufficient exploration of strategies to effectively compensate for and alleviate war-related trauma. These critical research gaps underscore the necessity and importance of the present study.

Background

In modern China, numerous frequent and large-scale wars occurred within national territory. According to historical records, from the Opium War in 1840 to the outbreak of the Xinhai Revolution in 1911, there were approximately 500 wars and armed conflicts. From 1911 to 1949, armed conflict persisted throughout China (Cai, 2007). War not only causes significant damage to a country's food supply, healthcare and school education, but also causes large numbers of civilian casualties (Singhal, 2019). During the 109-year period examined, years with major outbreaks of war accounted for 88.99%1 of the total number of years, highlighting the profound impact of war-related shocks on the socioeconomic development of modern China. Due to limitations in the availability of individual-level health data, the study primarily focuses on four major wars of significant scale and impact: the Eight-Nation Alliance invasion of China (1900–1901), warlord-era conflicts in North China (1916–1928), the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945), and the Chinese Civil War (1946–1949). It should be noted that during the periods between the four wars mentioned above, minor conflicts still occurred; however, these periods were relatively peaceful.

Based on characteristics such as the reasons, objectives, and positions of the warring parties, the four war events were classified as aggressive wars (including the Eight-Nation Alliance invasion of China and the Sino-Japanese War) and civil wars (including the Warlord War and the Chinese Civil War). Furthermore, the birth years of the CLHLS participants are primarily distributed between 1894 and 1954. The multiple war events in modern China and the war experiences of the CLHLS respondents provide an important contextual background for this research.

Material and methods

Data

The data utilized in this study primarily originate from the CLHLS, a nationally representative longitudinal dataset encompassing individuals aged 65 and older from 22 provincial regions across China. The baseline survey was initiated in 1998, with follow-up surveys conducted in 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2018. The survey extensively collects information on social background, economic status, and health conditions, providing robust micro-level data support for investigating the long-term health impacts of war on individuals. However, due to the absence of income-related information in the 1998 and 2000 survey waves, the study primarily relies on data from the six survey waves conducted between 2002 and 2018.

Additionally, some control variables were derived from the China Statistical Yearbooks spanning 2003 to 2019, including per capita gross domestic product (GDP), population density, and the number of (assistant) physicians per 10,000 people. These variables account for provincial-level disparities in socioeconomic and healthcare resources, thereby mitigating their potential influence on the results.

Furthermore, the CLHLS data from 2002 to 2018 were merged with the China Statistical Yearbooks from 2003–2019 by matching provincial names, resulting in an unbalanced panel dataset for 2002–2018. Previous research has indicated lower data quality for individuals aged 106 years and older (Zeng et al., 2017), and missing values are present for certain variables in the survey data. Consequently, observations for individuals aged 106 years and older, as well as those with missing values for any variable, were excluded during data processing. After data cleaning, the final dataset consists of an unbalanced panel of 30,937 observations.2

Health outcomes

In existing research on health, variables such as self-rated health (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014), mental health (Mohammed & Neuner, 2022), height (Phadera, 2021), diseases diabetes (Kesternich et al., 2014), and the construction of composite indices (Tang et al., 2025) are commonly employed as health proxies. To comprehensively assess individual health levels, the frailty index as a proxy for physical health and the mental index as a proxy for mental health were utilized.(1)Physical health (PH)

Physical health (PH)

The frailty index is a composite measure that quantifies the extent of an individual's health deficits. Typically, it is calculated as the proportion of adverse indicators across all measured health measures (Rockwood et al., 2004). The frailty index not only provides a holistic reflection of an individual's overall health status but is also widely recognized for its effectiveness in capturing health changes, supporting public health management, and evaluating intervention efficacy (Fan et al., 2020). Drawing on existing research (Gu et al., 2007; Zhen et al., 2024), the frailty index was constructed using 53 questions from the CLHLS. These include 24 indicators from the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), 6 indicators from Activities of Daily Living (ADL), 8 indicators from Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), and 15 chronic disease indicators (specific questions, scoring rules, and calculation methods are detailed in Appendix A). By summing the scores of all indicators and dividing them by the theoretical maximum score of 53, a frailty index ranging from 0 to 1 was calculated, where higher values indicate poorer physical health.(2)Mental health (MH)

(2)Mental health (MH)

Mental health (MH)

Since 2010, depression has been the second leading cause of years lived with disability in China (Lu et al., 2021). Mental health is a critical component of long-term health outcomes. Based on existing research (Singhal, 2019; Zhen et al., 2024), a mental index was constructed using six questions from the CLHLS (specific questions, scoring rules, and calculation methods are detailed in Appendix B). By summing the scores of all indicators and dividing them by the theoretical maximum score of six, a mental index ranging from 0 to 1 was calculated, where higher values indicate better mental health status.

Some studies consider individuals who experienced war at the age of five or younger as the treatment group (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2017; Phadera, 2021), while others have used more diverse age definitions, including 0-5, 5-10, and 10-15 years old (Li & Koulovatianos, 2020). Therefore, a broader early-stage threshold was adopted, and a binary variable was constructed using age 10 as the cutoff. Drawing on existing studies (Akresh et al., 2012; Li & Koulovatianos, 2020; Singhal, 2019), the number of months an individual was exposed to war before the age of 10 was used to construct war intensity, which reflects the marginal effects of increased exposure to war on individual health and gender differences.

Control variables

To avoid bias in the estimation results, it was necessary to control for other factors that may influence individual health. First, the analysis not only focused on the impact of war on individual health but also emphasized examining health disparities between genders. As individuals age, their physical health tends to deteriorate (Banerjee et al., 2020). Age and gender are therefore critical control variables. Second, prior studies have found that education level and socioeconomic status are closely associated with health (Boylan & Robert, 2017; Zhang et al., 2011). Therefore, education level and socioeconomic status were included as control variables. Third, health disparities exist between migrants and local residents (Perez-Lua et al., 2025), between urban and rural areas (Mu & Li, 2025), and among different ethnic groups (Dwyer-Lindgren et al., 2024). Additionally, family size influences patterns of resource allocation and elderly care (Bansak et al., 2025). Migration status, urban–rural residence, and family size were further included as control variables. Fourth, regional socioeconomic development levels and medical resources may indirectly affect residents’ health status (Zhen et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024). To ensure a comprehensive and accurate analysis, GDP per capita, population density, and the number of licensed (assistant) physicians per 10,000 people were also included as control variables in the estimation model. Finally, considering the differences between observations that were not affected by any wars, we also used binary variables of whether individuals were exposed to aggressive wars and whether they were exposed to civil wars as control variables.

Therefore, the control variables primarily include individual-level factors such as whether an individual was exposed to aggressive wars before the age of 10 (agg_wars), whether an individual was exposed to civil wars before the age of 10 (civil_wars),3 age (age), gender (gender), education level (edu), occupation before age 60 (occupation, used as a proxy for individual socioeconomic status), ethnicity (ethnicity), migration status (migration), and urban–rural residence (urbanity); household-level factors such as per capita household income (per_inc, deflated to 2002 constant prices and used as a proxy for household socioeconomic status) and family size (f_size); and regional-level factors such as the logarithm of GDP per capita (per_GDP, in 2002 constant prices), the logarithm of population density (pop_density), and the logarithm of the number of licensed (assistant) physicians per 10,000 people (physicians). Empirical analyses model

To assess the relationship between war and individual health, the analysis first examines the impact of war on individual health. Ordinary least squares regression with both year and household fixed effects was used for estimation, a method widely used in studies on health outcomes (Zhen et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024). How to compensate the war trauma

Potential mechanisms to mitigate the adverse health impacts of war were examined, based on the hypothesis that active social engagement and progressively improved medical services could ameliorate the health damage associated with early war exposure. Therefore, two variables, social participation (social) and medical services (medical) were constructed based on data from the CLHLS regarding individuals’ participation in social organizations and their access to medical services at the age of 60. These variables were interacted with war shock and incorporated into Equation (1) to examine whether they could exert a positive compensatory effect on health outcomes (the results are presented in Appendix G). The results indicate that the interaction terms between social participation and war shocks, as well as between medical services and war shocks, are statistically insignificant for both the frailty and the mental index, suggesting that such compensatory effects are absent.

To further investigate whether the lack of significant compensatory effects could be attributed to gender differences, a three-way interaction term was introduced into Equation (3). The results revealed that the compensatory effects of social participation and medical services on war trauma also exhibit no gender-specific variation (the results are presented in Appendix H). In conclusion, post-war social participation and medical services fail to mitigate the health damage caused by war.

Although this finding of “compensatory ineffectiveness” contradicts initial expectations, it also reveals the distinctiveness and systemic nature of war trauma as an extreme stressor. Existing studies regard social support as important buffering resources for mitigating general adversity (Cohen & Wills, 1985). However, war shocks often possess multiple devastating characteristics: they not only directly harm individuals’ physical and mental health but also frequently destroy community bonds and institutional infrastructure (Betsi et al., 2006; Fürst et al., 2009). The potential compensatory effects of social participation and healthcare services at conventional scales may be overshadowed by the magnitude and complexity of war-related shocks. This indicates that the trauma inflicted by war cannot be adequately compensated for or restored through existing social and medical service frameworks.

Discussion

This study utilizes data from the CLHLS in conjunction with the frequent occurrence of war events in modern China to examine gender differences in the impacts of war on health. The findings indicate that exposure to war shocks has a substantial detrimental impact on both physical and mental well-being. Specifically, individuals affected by war experience a 17.5% decline in physical health and a 12.1% decline in mental health compared with those not exposed to conflict. Moreover, the adverse effects are more pronounced among women, and this result remains consistent across various robustness checks. The analysis further indicates that aggressive wars impose a more severe health toll than civil wars, with individuals exposed to the former reporting significantly lower levels of both physical and mental health. Further analysis reveals that, compared with men, aggressive wars cause greater harm to women's physical and mental health, whereas civil wars only cause more significant damage to women's physical health. Finally, it was also found that neither post-war active social engagement nor increasingly improved medical services effectively mitigate the health damage caused by war, nor do they demonstrate a specific compensatory effect for the trauma inflicted on women by war.

This study advances the understanding of the relationship between wars and individual health by expanding the scope of analysis and providing novel insights. This study extends the conventional focus on singular war by distinctly categorizing conflicts as aggressive wars and civil wars and incorporates a dual health indicator framework of physical health and mental health. Moreover, the disproportionate health impact of war on men and women was specifically investigated, and issues related to post-war health recovery were examined, enabling a deeper understanding of wars’ specific harms to female populations.

The findings reveal that war imposes severe health losses on individuals, consistent with numerous existing studies (Akbulut-Yuksel, 2014; Lee, 2014; Phadera, 2021; Singhal, 2019). In addition, it was also found that the negative impact of war shocks and war intensity on women's health is stronger than that on men's, and this is often overlooked in previous research. Compared with prior studies focusing on the impact of war on individual health, this research emphasizes gender differences in these impacts. While numerous studies have documented significant gender differences in economic and social research (Alaimo & Nigri, 2024; Barigozzi et al., 2023; Caner et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2021; Stinebrickner et al., 2026), few have explored the long-term health differences between men and women when facing adverse shocks. Therefore, drawing on these studies on gender differences, the health impacts of war from a gender perspective were examined, which represents an important innovation in the research approach.

Moreover, the analysis focuses on war events over a longer historical period. Given differences in the causes and objectives of wars, aggressive wars may inflict greater health trauma on civilians (Mohammed & Neuner, 2022). Building on this perspective, war is differentiated into aggressive wars and civil wars to examine their differential impacts on individual health. The findings indicate that aggressive wars impose greater long-term health losses than civil wars, with significant gender differences observed primarily in mental health, as aggressive wars cause greater mental trauma to women. This pattern is related to the vulnerable position of women during wars (Annan & Brier, 2010; Sideris, 2003; Wachter & Gulbas, 2018), as women and children often suffer more severe harm. By distinguishing war types into aggressive wars and civil wars, this study provides a more nuanced discussion of the differential health impacts of different war types, particularly on women. This distinction in war nature also represents a significant contribution to the field of research.

Additionally, potential mechanisms to mitigate the adverse health effects of war were explored. This approach represents a more comprehensive research perspective compared with previous studies focusing solely on impact mechanisms (Betsi et al., 2006; Fürst et al., 2009; Singhal, 2019), providing new directions for future research on war and health. Unfortunately, the study's analysis did not substantiate a significant mitigating role for social participation or healthcare services in buffering war-induced trauma. While this finding of “compensatory ineffectiveness” contrasts with initial expectations, it underscores the distinctive and systemic nature of war as an extreme stressor. Although social support are established buffers against general adversity (Cohen & Wills, 1985), the multifaceted devastation of war, which simultaneously damages individual health, community bonds, and institutional infrastructure (Betsi et al., 2006; Fürst et al., 2009), likely overwhelms the capacity of conventional support systems. Consequently, the profound and often persistent trauma induced by war cannot be adequately remedied through generalized service frameworks.

This study indicates that war has a significant impact on individual health, with aggressive wars causing even greater health trauma, and women's health suffering particularly severely in conflicts. Over the past four decades, there have been no fewer than 15 wars in sub-Saharan Africa, affecting nearly three-quarters of the countries in the region. In addition, recent conflicts such as the Russia–Ukraine war and the Israel–Palestine conflict clearly demonstrate that even in a relatively peaceful world, some nations and peoples still face the impacts of war (Baez, 2011; Osokina et al., 2023). Although this study did not identify effective compensatory strategies, women affected by war require more international aid and social care. From a policy perspective, the study identifies groups that need more attention and care, particularly with regard to improving mental health. This highlights an urgent need for targeted, structurally transformative interventions in post-conflict settings.

Conclusions

War can profoundly affect the health of individuals exposed to it. Although effective strategies to mitigate war-related trauma have not been identified, this finding provides new directions for further research. The findings suggest that increased care and assistance are needed for civilians in war-torn regions to improve overall health and societal well-being.
