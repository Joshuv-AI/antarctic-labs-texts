Three homological invariants under cleft extensions
*

Abstract




\bf Abstract

In this paper, we investigate the behavior of Igusa-Todorov distances, extension and Rouquier dimensions under cleft extensions of abelian categories.
We apply our results to Morita context rings, trivial extension rings, tensor rings and arrow removals.

Keywords:
cleft extension; Igusa-Todorov distance; extension dimension; Rouquier dimension.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:
18G80, 18G25, 16E30.

1.
Introduction

The dimension for a triangulated category was introduced by Rouquier (
[
37
]
) following the idea of Bondal and Van den Bergh’ work in
[
7
]
,
which measures how quickly such a category can be built from single object. The dimension of a triangulated category has played an important role in representation theory, and has been studied by many scholars.
For example, it can be used to compute the representation dimension of Artin algebras (
[
32
,
38
]
).
In recent years, there has been tremendous interest in investigating the bounds for the dimension of a triangulated category.
Oppermann and Šťovíček proved that all proper thick subcategories of the bounded derived category of finitely generated modules over a Noetherian algebra containing the perfect complexes have infinite dimension (
[
33
]
); Psaroudakis investigated bounds for the dimension of a triangulated category in a recollement situation by applying
[
37
, Lemma 3.5]
to the case of semiorthogonal decompositions (
[
34
]
); Zheng and Huang gave some bounds for the dimension of a bounded derived category in term of projective dimensions of certain simple modules as well as radical layer length (
[
53
,
55
,
56
]
).

Inspired by the dimension
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{T}
of a triangulated category
\mathscr{T}
, the extension dimension
\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{A}
of an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
was introduced by Beligiannis in
[
6
]
.
This dimension measures how far an algebra is from being of finite representation type; specially, an Artin algebra
\Lambda
is representation-finite if and only if the extension dimension of
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda
is zero by
[
6
]
.
Many authors have investigated the extension dimension under different situations.
For instance, Zhang and Zheng studied the extension dimension under derived and stable equivalences (
[
51
]
).
It is worth noting that, by
[
56
]
, we can see that the extension dimension and Rouquier dimension are closely related to the
n
-Igusa-Todorov algebras introduced by Wei (
[
39
]
).
It is well known that Igusa-Todorov algebras satisfy the finitistic dimension conjecture (see
[
39
, Theorem 1.1]
) and are invariant under derived equivalences (see
[
40
,
42
,
54
]
).
Although Igusa-Todorov algebras encompass many classes of Artin algebras (see
[
39
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
), unfortunately not all Artin algebras are Igusa-Todorov algebras (
[
12
,
54
]
).
In order to measure how far an algebra is from being an Igusa-Todorov algebra, Zheng introduced the concept of Igusa-Todorov distance of Artin algebras (
[
54
]
),
and studied its behavior under singular equivalences and recollements of derived module categories (
[
52
]
).

A cleft extension of an abelian category
\mathscr{B}
, introduced by Beligiannis (
[
4
]
), is an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
together with functors

\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{i}}
\textstyle{\mathscr{A}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf e}
\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf l}

such that the functor
\sf e
is faithful and exact, and admits a left adjoint
\sf l
, and there is a natural isomorphism
\mathsf{el}\simeq\mathrm{Id}_{\mathscr{B}}.

We denote this cleft extension by
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
,
which gives rise to an endofunctor
\mathsf{F}:\mathscr{B}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}
( see Section
2.2
); we emphasize that the endofunctor is of importance, as imposing homological conditions on it imply properties for the rest of the functors.
It should be noted that there is an abundance of constructions that are of great interest in representation theory and occur as a cleft extension of abelian categories.
For instance, Morita context rings, trivial extensions, tensor rings and
\theta
-extensions
[
22
,
29
]
.
Hence cleft extensions of abelian categories provide the proper conceptual framework for the study of many problems in several different contexts.
For example, Green, Psaroudakis and Solberg investigated the behavior of the finitistic dimension in a cleft extension under certain condition (
[
18
]
); Kostas studied Gorensteinness, Gorenstein projective modules, and singularity categories in the cleft extensions of rings (
[
22
]
).

Building on this, the main purpose of this paper is to investigate the behavior of Igusa-Todorov distances, extension dimensions, and dimensions of bounded derived categories under cleft extensions of abelian categories.

To this end, we first introduce the Igusa-Todorov distance, denoted by
\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathscr{A})
, for an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
. This is a generalization of the Igusa-Todorov distance of an Artin algebra introduced by Zheng (
[
54
]
); see Definition
3.4
. We then present the following theorem, which is listed as Theorem
3.11
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
be a cleft extension of abelian categories with enough projectives such that
\mathsf{l}
is exact and
\mathsf{e}
preserves projectives. If the induced endofunctor
\mathsf{F}
is nilpotent and
\mathsf{Proj}\mathscr{B}=\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(P)
for some projective object
P
in
\mathscr{B}
, then

\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathscr{B})\leq\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathscr{A})\leq n(\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathscr{B})+1)-1,

where
n
is a positive integer such that
\mathsf{F}^{n}=0
.

There is a dual notion to cleft extensions, which is called cleft coextensions (see Definition
2.3
). Let
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
be a cleft coextension of abelian categories. This gives rise to an endofunctor
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}:\mathscr{B}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}
( see Section
2.3
).
From
[
18
, Remark 2.5]
, we know that the cleft coextension
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
is the lower part of a cleft extension
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})

if and only if
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
admits a left adjoint
\mathsf{F}
, where
\mathsf{F}
is the induced endofunctor associated to
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
.
Let
\Lambda
be a Noetherian algebra. Note that the category of finitely generated right
\Lambda
-modules, denoted by
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda
, does not have enough injectives generally.
So in order to discuss the extension dimension for cleft extensions of Noetherian algebras, we investigate it within the framework of cleft coextensions; see Lemma
4.5
and Theorem
4.10
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
be a cleft coextension of abelian categories with enough projectives and suppose
\mathsf{Proj}\mathscr{A}=\mathrm{add}(P)
for some projective object
P
in
\mathscr{A}
. If
\mathsf{r}
is exact and the induced endofunctor
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
is nilpotent,
then

\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{B}\leq\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{A}\leq n(\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{B}+1)-1,

where
n
is a positive integer such that
\mathsf{F}^{\prime n}=0
.

Finally we consider the dimension of bounded derived module categories involved in a cleft extension of module categories when the induced endofunctor
\mathsf{F}
is left perfect and nilpotent (see Definition
5.4
).
For details, we refer to Theorem
5.8
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be two Noetherian algebras such that

(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}B,\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}A,\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
is a cleft extension of module categories.
If the induced endofunctor
\mathsf{F}
is left perfect and nilpotent,
then

\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}B)\leq\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)\leq n(\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}B)+1)-1,

where
n
is a positive integer such that
\mathsf{F}^{n}=0.

As shown in
[
18
]
, arrow removal on an admissible path algebra yields a cleft extension with specific homological properties, we get the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Lambda=kQ/I
be an admissible quotient of a path algebra
kQ
over a field
k.

Suppose that there are arrows
a_{i}:\upsilon_{e_{i}}\rightarrow\upsilon_{f_{i}}
in
Q
for
i=1,2,\cdots,t
which do not occur in a set of minimal generators of
I
in
kQ
and
\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\Lambda}(e_{i}\Lambda,f_{i}\Lambda)=0
for all
i,j
in
\{1,2,\cdots,t\}
.
Let
\Gamma=\Lambda/\Lambda\{\overline{a_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{t}\Lambda
.
Then the following hold:

(1)

\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\Lambda)\leq 2\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\Gamma)+1.

(2)

\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\Lambda\leq 2\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(\Gamma)+1.

(3)

\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda)\leq 2\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)+1.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce essential notations and provide the foundational definitions of cleft extensions and cleft coextensions of abelian categories, along with their basic homological properties necessary for subsequent proofs. Section 3 is dedicated to the proof of Theorem 1.1, while Section 4 presents the proof of Theorem 1.2. In Section 5, we give the proof of Theorem 1.3. Finally, we explore applications of these results to Morita context rings,
\theta
-extensions of rings, and tensor rings, demonstrating the practical implications of our theoretical framework.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Conventions

All rings considered in this paper are nonzero associative rings with identity, and consequently, all modules are unitary.
For a ring
R
, we work usually with right
R
-modules and write
\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}R
for the category of right
R
-modules.
The full subcategory of finitely generated right
R
-modules is denoted by
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}R
.
By a module over a Noetherian algebra
\Lambda
, we mean a finitely generated
\Lambda
-module.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be an abelian category with enough projectives.
We denote by
\mathsf{Proj}\mathscr{A}
the full subcategory of
\mathscr{A}
consisting of projective objects.

A
subcategory
of
\mathscr{A}
is always assumed to be a full and additive subcategory which is closed under isomorphisms, and the word
functor

will mean an additive functor between additive categories.
For a subclass
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathscr{A}
, we use
\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(\mathcal{U})
to
denote the subcategory of
\mathscr{A}
consisting of
direct summands of finite direct sums of objects in
\mathcal{U}
.
When
\mathcal{U}
consists of single object
U
, we write
\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(U)
for
\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(\mathcal{U}).

Let
\mathbf{C}(\mathscr{A})
be the category of all complexes over
\mathscr{A}
with chain maps, and the corresponding homotopy category
is denoted by
\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{A})
.
The derived category of
\mathscr{A}
is denoted by
\mathbf{D}(\mathscr{A}),
which is the localization of
\mathbf{K}(\mathscr{A})
at all quasi-isomorphisms.
The full subcategory of
\mathbf{D}(\mathscr{A})
consisting of complexes isomorphic to bounded complexes is denoted by
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathscr{A})
.

Unless otherwise stated, abelian categories are assumed to
have enough projectives.

2.2.
Cleft extensions of abelian categories

We first recall the definition of a cleft extension, which was introduced by Beligiannis (
[
4
,
5
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

(
[
4
, Definition 2.1]
)
A
cleft extension
of an abelian category
\mathscr{B}
is an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
together with functors

\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{i}}
\textstyle{\mathscr{A}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf e}
\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf l}

satisfying the following:

(1)

The functor
\mathsf{e}
is faithful and exact.

(2)

The pair
(\mathsf{l},\mathsf{e})
is an adjoint pair.

(3)

There is a natural isomorphism
\varphi:\mathsf{ei}\rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\mathscr{B}}
.

In the following, we denote a cleft extension by
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
.
The above data give rise to more structure information.
For instance, it follows from
[
18
, Lemma 2.2]
that
\mathsf{i}
is fully faithful and exact. Moreover, there is a functor
\mathsf{q}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}
such that
(\mathsf{q},\mathsf{i})
forms an adjoint pair.

We now explain the certain endofunctors on the categories
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, which are important for our result. Denote by
\nu:\rm Id_{\mathscr{B}}\rightarrow\mathsf{el}
the unit and by
\mu:\mathsf{le}\rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\mathscr{A}}
the counit of the adjoint pair
(\mathsf{l},\mathsf{e})
.
The unit and counit satisfy the following relations

\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{l}(B)}=\mu_{\mathsf{l}(B)}\mathsf{l}(\nu_{B})

and

\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{e}(A)}=\mathsf{e}(\mu_{A})\nu_{\mathsf{e}(A)}

for all
A
in
\mathscr{A}
and
B
in
\mathscr{B}
.
It follows that
\mathsf{e}(\mu_{A})
is a (split) epimorphism. Since
\sf e
is faithful, this implies that
\mu_{A}:\mathsf{le}(A)\rightarrow A
is an epimorphism for every
A\in\mathscr{A}
.
Hence we have the following exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Ker}\mu_{A}\rightarrow\mathsf{le}(A)\xrightarrow{\mu_{A}}A\rightarrow 0

for every
A\in\mathscr{A}.

The assignment
A\mapsto\mbox{\rm Ker}\mu_{A}
defines an endofunctor
\mathsf{G}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
.
Consider now an object
B
in
\mathscr{B}
.
Then we have the short exact sequence in
\mathscr{A}
:

(2.1)

0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}(\mathsf{i}(B))\rightarrow\mathsf{le}(\mathsf{i}(B))\xrightarrow{\mu_{\mathsf{i}(B)}}\mathsf{i}(B)\rightarrow 0.

We denote by
\mathsf{F}(B)=\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{G}(\mathsf{i}(B))).

Viewing the natural isomorphism
\mathsf{ei}(B)\xrightarrow{\sim}B
as an identification and applying the exact functor
\mathsf{e}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}
to (
2.1
), we obtain the exact sequence:

(2.2)

0\rightarrow\mathsf{F}(B)\rightarrow\mathsf{el}(B)\xrightarrow{}B\rightarrow 0.

The assignment
B\mapsto\mathsf{F}(B)
defines an endofunctor
\mathsf{F}:\mathscr{B}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}.

In the following lemma, we consider
\mathsf{F}^{n}
and
\mathsf{G}^{n}
and collect some results from
[
4
,
18
]
which are used in the proof of our main result. We mention that
\mathsf{F}^{0}
and
\mathsf{G}^{0}
are identity functors.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
be a cleft extension of abelian categories. The following statements hold:

(1)

Let
n\geq 1
. Then
\mathsf{F}^{n}=0
if and only if
\mathsf{G}^{n}=0.

(2)

For any
n\geq 1
and any
A\in\mathscr{A}
, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{n}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{l}\mathsf{F}^{n-1}\mathsf{e}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{n-1}(A)\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

(1) This follows directly from
[
18
, Lemma 2.4]
.

(2) By the definition of
\sf G
, there is an exact sequence

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{n}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{le}\mathsf{G}^{n-1}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{n-1}(A)\rightarrow 0

for any
A\in\mathscr{A}
and
n\geq 1
.
Since
\mathsf{le}\mathsf{G}^{n}\simeq\mathsf{l}\mathsf{F}^{n}\mathsf{e}
by
[
18
, Lemma 2.4(1)]
or
[
4
, Subsection 5.2]
for any
n\geq 1
, it follows that the desired exact sequence is obtained by replacing
\mathsf{le}\mathsf{G}^{n-1}(A)
with
\mathsf{l}\mathsf{F}^{n-1}\mathsf{e}(A)
in (
2.3
).
∎

2.3.
Cleft coextensions of abelian categories

There is a dual notion to cleft extensions, which is called cleft coextensions.
We first recall its definition.

Definition 2.3
.

(
[
4
, Definition 2.1]
)
A
cleft coextension
of an abelian category
\mathscr{B}
is an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
together with functors

\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf i}
\textstyle{\mathscr{A}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf e}
\textstyle{\mathscr{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf r}

satisfying the following:

(1)

The functor
\sf e
is faithful and exact.

(2)

The pair
(\sf e,\sf r)
is an adjoint pair.

(3)

There is a natural isomorphism
\varphi:{\sf ei}\rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\mathscr{B}.}

In the following, we denote a cleft coextension by
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
. We now describe the certain endofunctors on the categories
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, which are important for the proof of Theorem
4.10
. Denote by
\nu^{\prime}:\rm Id_{\mathscr{A}}\rightarrow\sf re
the unit and by
\mu^{\prime}:{\sf er}\rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\mathscr{B}}
the counit of the adjoint pair
(\sf e,r)
.
The unit and counit satisfy the following relations

\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{e}(A)}=\mu^{\prime}_{\mathsf{e}(A)}\mathsf{e}(\nu^{\prime}_{A})

and

\mathrm{Id}_{\mathsf{r}(B)}=\mathsf{r}(\mu^{\prime}_{B})\nu^{\prime}_{\mathsf{r}(B)})

for all
A
in
\mathscr{A}
and
B
in
\mathscr{B}
.
It follows that
\mathsf{e}(\nu^{\prime}_{A})
is a (split) monomorphism. Since
\sf e
is faithful, this implies that
\nu^{\prime}_{A}:A\rightarrow\mathsf{re}(A)
is a monomorphism for every
A\in\mathscr{A}
.
Hence we have the following exact sequence

0\rightarrow A\xrightarrow{\nu^{\prime}_{A}}\mathsf{re}(A)\rightarrow\mbox{\rm Coker}\nu^{\prime}_{A}\rightarrow 0

for every
A\in\mathscr{A}.

The assignment
A\mapsto\mbox{\rm Coker}\nu^{\prime}_{A}
defines an endofunctor
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
.
Consider now an object
B
in
\mathscr{B}
.
Then we have the short exact sequence in
\mathscr{A}
:

(2.4)

0\rightarrow\mathsf{i}(B)\xrightarrow{\nu^{\prime}_{\mathsf{i}(B)}}\mathsf{re}(\mathsf{i}(B))\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime}(\mathsf{i}(B))\rightarrow 0.

We denote by
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}(B)=\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{G}^{\prime}(\mathsf{i}(B))).

Viewing the natural isomorphism
\mathsf{ei}(B)\xrightarrow{\sim}B
as an identification and applying the exact functor
\mathsf{e}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}
to (
2.4
), we get the exact sequence:

0\rightarrow B\xrightarrow{}\mathsf{er}(B)\rightarrow\mathsf{F}^{\prime}(B)\rightarrow 0.

The assignment
B\mapsto\mathsf{F}^{\prime}(B)
defines an endofunctor
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}:\mathscr{B}\rightarrow\mathscr{B}.

Remark 2.4
.

(
[
4
, Remark 2.7]
,
[
18
, Remark 2.5]
) A cleft extension of an abelian category
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
is the upper part of a cleft coextension
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
if and only if the endofunctor
\mathsf{F}
appearing in (
2.2
) has a right adjoint
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
.
In this case,
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
is the endofunctor of
\mathscr{B}
induced by the cleft coextension
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
.

Every property of a cleft coextension of an abelian category is dual to that of a cleft extension of an abelian category.
The following lemma will be used in the description of extension dimension. By convention, given an endofunctor
\sf{F}
, we set
\sf{F}^{0}
to be the identity functor.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{r})
be a cleft coextension of abelian categories. The following statements hold:

(1)

Let
n\geq 1
. Then
\mathsf{F}^{\prime n}=0
if and only if
\mathsf{G}^{\prime n}=0.

(2)

For any
n\geq 1
and any
A\in\mathscr{A}
, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n-1}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{r}\mathsf{F}^{\prime n-1}\mathsf{e}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n}(A)\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

(1) The proof is dual to that of
[
18
, Lemma 2.4]
.

(2) By the definition of
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n-1}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{re}\mathsf{G}^{\prime n-1}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n}(A)\rightarrow 0

for any
A\in\mathscr{A}
and
n\geq 1
.
Since
\mathsf{e}\mathsf{G}^{\prime n}\simeq\mathsf{F}^{\prime n}\mathsf{e}
by the dual of
[
18
, Lemma 2.4(1)]
for any
n\geq 1
, it follows that we obtain the following exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n-1}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{r}\mathsf{F}^{\prime n-1}\mathsf{e}(A)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime n}(A)\rightarrow 0,

which completes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Cleft extension of module categories

We now turn our attention to cleft extensions of module categories, which can also be viewed as cleft coextensions of module categories by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

(
[
22
, Proposition 4.19]
)
A cleft extension
(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\Gamma,\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\Lambda,\mathsf{i},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{l})
of module categories is the upper part of a cleft coextension. Moreover, if
\mathsf{F}
is nilpotent, then so is

\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
.

We now give some examples of cleft extensions of module categories which will be used throughout the paper.

Example 2.7
.

(
[
22
, Example 5.4]
)
Let
A
and
B
be two rings, and let
{}_{B}M_{A}
and
{}_{A}N_{B}
be bimodules.
Consider the Morita context ring

\Lambda=\left(\begin{matrix}A&{{}_{A}}N_{B}\\
{{}_{B}}M_{A}&B\\
\end{matrix}\right)

with zero bimodule homomorphisms.

We recall from
[
16
]
that the module categories over the Morita context ring
\Lambda
is equivalent to a category whose objects are tuples
(X,Y,f,g)
, where
X\in\text{Mod-}A
,
Y\in\text{Mod-}B
,
f\in\text{Hom}_{B}(X\otimes_{A}N,Y)
and
g\in\text{Hom}_{A}(Y\otimes_{B}M,X)
.
Thus we have a cleft extension of module categories, which is also a upper part of a cleft coextension by Lemma
2.6

\textstyle{\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}A\times B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf i}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\Lambda\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf e}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}A\times B.\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sf l}
\scriptstyle{\sf r}

These functors are given as follows:

(1)

\mathsf{i}(X,Y)=(X,Y,0,0).

(2)

\mathsf{e}(X,Y,f,g)=(X,Y).

(3)

\mathsf{l}(X,Y)=(X,X\otimes_{A}N,0,1)\oplus(Y\otimes_{B}M,Y,1,0)

3.
Igusa-Todorov distances

In this section we introduce the notion of Igusa-Todorov distance of an abelian category, which is a generalization of Igusa-Todorov distance of an Artin algebra given in
[
54
]
.
To this end, it is convenient to introduce the following notion.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be an abelian category and
n
a positive integer.
If there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow K\rightarrow P_{n-1}\rightarrow P_{n-2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow P_{0}\rightarrow X\rightarrow 0

in
\mathscr{A}
with all
P_{i}
projective for
0\leq i\leq n-1
, then
K
is called an
n
-syzygy object of
X
. We denote by
\Omega^{n}(\mathscr{A})
the subcategory of
\mathscr{A}
consisting of all
n
-syzygy objects.

Remark 3.2
.

Given an object
X
in an abelian category
\mathscr{A}
, its
n
-syzygy objects are not unique up to isomorphism, but are unique up to projective summands.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
m
and
n
be nonnegative integers. An abelian category
\mathscr{A}

with enough projective objects is called an
(m,n)
-Igusa-Todorov category
if there is an object
U\in\mathscr{A}
such that for any
A\in\Omega^{n}(\mathscr{A})
, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow U_{m}\rightarrow U_{m-1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow U_{1}\rightarrow U_{0}\rightarrow A\rightarrow 0,

with
U_{i}\in\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(U)
for each
0\leq i\leq m
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be an abelian category.
The
Igusa-Todorov distance
of
\mathscr{A}
is defined as

\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathscr{A}):=\inf\{m\mid\mathscr{A}\text{ is an}~(m,n)\text{-Igusa-Todorov category for some nonnegative integer}~n\}.

Remark 3.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be an Artin algebra. Then
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda
is an
(m,n)
-Igusa-Todorov category if and only if
\Lambda
is an an
(m,n)
-Igusa-Todorov algebra in the sense of
[
53
, Definition 2.1]
.
It follows that the Igusa-Todorov distance of
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda
is the Igusa-Todorov distance of
\Lambda
; see
[
54
, definition 2.27]
.

Remark 3.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be an Artin algebra and let
n
be a nonnegative integer.

(1)

Recall that
\Lambda
is called
syzygy-finite
if
\Omega^{n}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda)
is representation-finite for some nonnegative integer
n
; that is, the number of non-isomorphic indecomposable direct summands of modules in
\Omega^{n}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda)
is finite.
It follows from Definition
3.4
that
\Lambda
is syzygy-finite if and only if
\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda)=0.

(2)

Recall from
[
39
, Definition 2.2]
that
\Lambda
is called an
n
-Igusa-Todorov algebra
if there exists a
\Lambda
-module

U
such that for any
\Lambda
-module
X\in\Omega^{n}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda)
there exists an exact sequence
0\rightarrow U_{0}\rightarrow U_{1}\rightarrow X\rightarrow 0

with
U_{i}\in\mathrm{add}(U)
for each
0\leq i\leq 1
.
Furthermore,
\Lambda
is call an
Igusa-Todorov algebra
if it is an
n
-Igusa-Todorov algebra for some nonnegative integer

Then it follows from Definition

4.

Let
\Lambda

\Lambda
-modules, denoted by
\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda

Therefore, to discuss the extension dimension of a cleft extension of a Noetherian algebra,
we study the relationship between the extension dimension of abelian categories involved in a cleft coextension of abelian categories, rather than a cleft extension of abelian categories. First we recall the definition of extension dimensions from
[
6
,
57
]
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{U}_{n}
be subcategories of
\mathscr{A}
. Define

\mathcal{U}_{1}~\bullet~\mathcal{U}_{2}:={\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits}\{A\in\mathscr{A}\mid{\rm there\;exists\;an\;sequence\;}0\rightarrow U_{1}\rightarrow A\rightarrow U_{2}\rightarrow 0\ {\rm in}\ \mathscr{A}\ {\rm with}\;U_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\;{\rm and}\;U_{2}\in\mathcal{U}_{2}\}.

By
[
13
, Proposition 2.2]
, the operator
\bullet
is associative, that is,

(\mathcal{U}_{1}\bullet\mathcal{U}_{2})\bullet\mathcal{U}_{3}=\mathcal{U}_{1}\bullet(\mathcal{U}_{2}\bullet\mathcal{U}_{3}).

The category
\mathcal{U}_{1}\bullet\mathcal{U}_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet\mathcal{U}_{n}

can be inductively described as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}\bullet\mathcal{U}_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet\mathcal{U}_{n}:=\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits\{A\in\mathscr{A}\mid{\rm there\;exists\;an\;sequence}\ 0\rightarrow U\rightarrow A\rightarrow V\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle{\rm in}\ \mathscr{A}\ {\rm with}\;U\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\;{\rm and}\;V\in\mathcal{U}_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet\mathcal{U}_{n}\}.

For a subclass
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathscr{A}
, set

[\mathcal{U}]_{0}=0
,
[\mathcal{U}]_{1}=\mathop{\rm add}\nolimits(\mathcal{U})

[\mathcal{U}]_{n}=[\mathcal{U}]_{1}\bullet[\mathcal{U}]_{n-1}
for any

.
If
T
is an object in
\mathscr{A}
we write
[T]_{n}
instead of
[\{T\}]_{n}
.

Definition 4.1
.

(
[
6
]
)
The
extension dimension
of

is defined as

\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits\mathscr{A}:=\inf\{n\geq 0\mid\mathscr{A}=[T]_{n+1}\ {\rm with}\ T\in\mathscr{A}\},

or
\infty
if no such an integer exists.

Let
\Lambda
be a Noetherian algebra
\Lambda

\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Lambda

Theorem 4.10

.
Then by Lemma

(2) we have the following exact sequence in

\begin{cases}0\rightarrow X\rightarrow\mathsf{re}(X)\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime}(X)\rightarrow 0;\\

\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\vdots\\

\mathsf{F}^{\prime n}=0,

\displaystyle\in[(\mathsf{r}\mathsf{F}^{\prime n-1}\mathsf{e}(X))_{n-1}]_{1}\bullet\cdots\bullet[(\mathsf{r}\mathsf{F}^{\prime}\mathsf{e}(X))_{1}]_{1}\bullet[\mathsf{r}\mathsf{e}(X)]_{1}\;\;\;\;\;\;{\rm(by\;Lemma\;\ref{main lem0})}

where each

5.

In this section, we concentrate on the behavior of dimension of bounded derived categories involved in a cleft extension of module categories.
We begin by recalling the concept of dimension of a triangulated category introduced by Rouquier

]

Let
\mathscr{T}
be a triangulated category, and let
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
be two subcategories of

We denote by

the subcategory consisting of the objects

for which there exists a triangle

with

and

We denote by

the smallest full subcategory of

which contains

and is closed under finite direct sums, direct summands and shifts.
Set

and define inductively

\langle\mathcal{U}\rangle_{0}:=0,~\langle\mathcal{U}\rangle_{n+1}=\langle\mathcal{U}\rangle_{1}\diamond\langle\mathcal{U}\rangle_{n}.

(

if no such an integer exists.

.
Since

is exact, it follows that

for

Thus

∎

(3)

A
is derived-infinite.

An algebra
\Lambda
is called
derived-finite
if the number of indecomposable objects in its bounded derived category
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\Lambda)
is finite (up to isomorphisms and shifts).
Then an algebra
\Lambda
is called
derived-infinite
if it does not derived-finite.

Note that the bound quiver
(Q,I)
of
A
has a hereditary subquiver of Euclid type
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}

which is naturally induced by the band
s=a_{\mathrm{U}}b_{\mathrm{U}}c_{\mathrm{U}}c_{\mathrm{D}}^{-1}b_{\mathrm{D}}^{-1}a_{\mathrm{D}}^{-1}
,
and the number of clockwise arrows equals to that of anti-clockwise arrows,

is derived-infinite (indeed, it is strongly derived-unbounded,
the definition of strongly derived-unbounded can be found in reference

50
, Definition 5]
).

(4)

\Gamma
is derived-finite.

Note that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are gentle trees.
By the classification of gentle one-cycle algebra given in
[
2
, Section 7]

(or by using
[
27
, Theorem 4.6]
),
we obtain that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are derived equivalent to the hereditary Nakayama algebra of type
\mathbb{A}
.
It follows that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are derived-finite, and so is
A
.
Therefore,
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)=0.

Now, by Theorem
6.5
, we obtain

0=\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(A)\leq 2\mathop{\rm IT.dist}\nolimits(\Gamma)+1=1;

0=\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(A)\leq 2\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(\Gamma)+1=1

and

0=\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)\leq 2\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)+1\leq 1.

Note that
\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(A)\geq 1
since

\mathop{\rm ext.dim}\nolimits(A)=1
.
Moreover, since

\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}\Gamma)>0
. Then
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)=1
.

Before presenting the second example, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
A
be a finite dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field
k.

If the global dimension
\mathrm{gl.dim}A
of
A
is infinite, then
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)\geq 1.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)=0
.
Then by
[
11
]
that
A
is an iterated tilted algebra of Dykin type.
It follows from
[
20
, Theorem 2.10]
that there exists an triangle equivalence
\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)\simeq\mathbf{D}^{b}({\rm mod}\text{-}kQ)
for some Dykin quiver
Q.

Since
\mathrm{gl.dim}~kQ<\infty
, we have
\mathrm{gl.dim}A<\infty
by
[
21
]
or
[
46
, Theorem 3.2]
. This leads to a contradiction.
Hence
\mathop{\rm Rouq.dim}\nolimits\mathbf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{mod}\text{-}A)\geq 1.
