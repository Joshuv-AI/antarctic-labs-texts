Variation of CO2 concentration and its provenance in alpine on the south slope of Tomur Peak, Tianshan Mountains
Authors: Junli Xu, Haidong Han, Jian Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study analyzes the characteristics of near-surface atmospheric CO2 concentrations on the southern slope of Tomur Peak from 2013 to 2022. Utilizing the Boughton two-parameter algorithm, we determined the CO2 background and non-background concentrations. Additionally, the Hysplit model was employed to investigate the potential contributing areas of the CO2 non-background concentration. Our findings that the CO2 concentration in the study area was approximately 17% lower than the global average, with an annual growth rate that was only about 47% of the global rate. The CO2 non-background concentration accounted for only 6.25% of the observed concentration, but was largely unaffected by near-ground meteorological factors. The source of the CO2 non-background was mainly controlled by the westerly belt, with potential source area mainly located in Central Asia. However, the WPSCF values in the modern ice and snow distribution area south of Tomur Peak and west of the monitoring station were significantly elevated, suggesting a strong wind transport of CO2 after accumulation under the influence of the elevated terrain. The local supplying path accounted for only 12.87%, indicating that human activities in the oasis areas of the Tarim Basin had a limited influence on the study area. Due to the blocking effect of the Baikal high-pressure system and the towering topography of the Tianshan Mountains, the influence of human emissions from higher latitudes was likely negligible.Keywords: CO2 concentration, Contribution area of potential source, Boughton two-parameter algorithm, Hysplit, South slope of Tomur PeakSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Atmospheric CO2 and its derivatives play a very important role in the biogeochemical cycle and atmosphere1. The “Keeling curve”, which has been continuously monitored at Mauna Loa Station since 1958, has attracted worldwide attention to CO2 emissions and the resulting greenhouse effect2. The global average CO2 concentration in 2019 reached another record high of about 410 ppm3. Therefore, the characteristics of CO2 concentration change, the determination of sources/sinks, the assessment of emissions/absorption and the exploration of different adaptive countermeasures have been important focuses of geographical environmental science research worldwide in recent years4. The water chemical erosion rate of mountain glaciers in the alpine region was as high as 2174 ± 977 Σmeq+ (m2 ·a)−1, which was about 4 times that of major non-glacial river distribution areas in the world, and under the background of climate change, the erosion intensity was about 3 times that of 20 years ago5. Therefore, the potential for temporally/permanently CO2 drawdown and its role as a carbon source/sink in the alpine region has also received great attention6,7, especially in the high mountainous regions of the Eurasian hinterland where morainic debris is widely developed8,9.

The assessment of CO2 consumption and release in alpine mountains often relies on the chemical compositions of different water bodies during the water-rock reaction process7,10, the measurements and calculations of gas analyzer11,12 and the CO2 gradient monitoring system or the covariance monitoring systems13. However, the regional representation of the carbon cycle system and the status of recharge and consumption have been somewhat overlooked14. Moreover, fluctuations in near-ground CO2 concentrations, induced by policy shifts or climatic irregularities, are often subtle and elusive within time series data. These variations are also prone to being masked by the inherent, long-term patterns of CO2 concentration changes15. Therefore, to evaluate the potential sources of CO2 and the effect of emission reduction, it is necessary to adjust for the time series of CO2 concentrations influenced by regional natural conditions.

At present, the base-line separation technology in electrical engineering, which filters out high-frequency signals, is widely used to extract base-flow signals from surface runoff16,17. The concentrations of typical atmospheric components, such as CO2, aerosols and fine particulate matter, could also be categorized into the slow-response background concentrations, influenced by regional natural environments and daily human activities, and fast-response non-background concentrations resulting from abnormal natural or human activity emissions and exogenous unstable inputs17–19. Analyzing non-background concentrations allows us to explore the potential sources of pollutants and analyze the controlling factors of air quality change16. Common segmentation methods include the fixed interval method, the sliding interval method, the local minimum method, and recursive digital filters, such as the Boughton two-parameter algorithm and Kolmogorov-Zurbenko filter, etc16,18–20. The Boughton two-parameter algorithm has demonstrated superior performance in analyzing pollutant sources in densely populated areas compared to the chemical mass balance model developed by the United States Environmental Protection Agency16.

The remote Tomur Peak region is one of the ideal areas to study the response and feedback of the natural environment’s evolution to human activities. The unique natural conditions promoted the intensity of CO2 consumption by water-rock reactions on the underlying surface, where surface moraines are widely developed9. However, there are many uncertainties regarding the source of CO2. On the one hand, it was believed that the near-ground CO2 originated from the release of stored carbon in cryoconite holes, subglacial sediments and permafrost due to melting10,12. On the other hand, it is believed that the steep terrain and poor vegetation distribution in the modern glacial distribution area limit the accumulation of a large amount of organic matter in the frozen soil layer historically, and the near-ground CO2 could come from the emission of human activities transported over long distances7,8,13. This is because the CO2 released by the underlying surface is only equivalent to the consumption from silicate rock weathering6. Therefore, the role of the alpine region in the Eurasian hinterland, where water-rock interactions are strong, in the carbon cycle needs to be further confirmed11.

Based on the near-ground CO2 concentration data monitored nearly ten years on the southern slope of Tomur Peak, this paper attempts to use the Boughton two-parameter algorithm to obtain the CO2 background concentration in the regional natural environment and the exogenous non-background concentration that may be affected by human emissions. It also analyzes the causes of the concentration changes in combination with meteorological and topographic factors. In addition, Hysplit backward trajectory model (v5.2.0) will be used to explore the recharge path and potential contribution area of exogenous CO2, in order to provide a reference for the study of the role of the high Asia in the carbon cycle.

Results

Study area

The northern and the southwestern Tianshan Mountains receive relatively more precipitation than other areas of Tianshan Mountains; and the vegetation over the region is mainly grassland and low shrubs21. Living and production activities are mainly distributed in the economic centers of Almaty, Bishkek, Dushanbe and Naryn in Central Asia and the economic belt of the north slope of the Tianshan Mountains in China. In addition, industrial towns dominated by metal mining (gold, copper, tin and pyrite, etc.) are mostly distributed in Karakol, Jalal Abad and Osh and other areas to the west of the southern Tianshan mountains, while the mining areas dominated by non-metal (clay, lime and coal mining, etc.) are mostly distributed in the foothills on the east side of the southern Tianshan mountains. In the adjacent Tarim Basin, the climate is arid, with Gobi and desert regions widely spread, and human activities are mostly concentrated in the oasis area along the Tarim River and its tributaries22.

The Tomur Peak area is the largest glaciation area in the Tianshan Mountains, which is significantly different from the Gobi and desert-dominated underlying surface of the Tarim Basin (Fig. 1). Therefore, the mountain-valley winds and glacier winds occur frequently23, which could cause the CO2 concentration in the alpine area to be affected by the carbon emissions from the oasis area along the Tarim River and its tributaries. In addition, more than 60% of the glacier ablation area is covered by morainic debris24, which could not only affect the snow/ice melting rate significantly, but also enhance the intensity of regional hydrochemical erosion and temporarily CO2 consumption8,9,11.

Near-ground micrometeorology

From September 2013 to August 2022, the atmospheric pressure near the CO2 concentration monitoring site averaged 708.95 ± 6.17 mmHg (Fig. 2). This value was higher during summer and autumn seasons and significantly differed from the lower-altitude plain areas, where the higher atmospheric pressure occurs in the winter due to lower temperatures25. The annual mean values of solar net radiation and air temperature were 49.91 ± 3.13 W·(m2·s)−1 and 0.55 ± 1.20 °C, respectively, with their monthly average variations closely following the solar zenith angle. The annual mean wind speed was 2.85 ± 0.58 m·s− 1, which was approximately 1.5 times higher than that in the Tarim Basin (1.93 m·s− 1) and was close to the region of the Junggar Basin on the north side of the Tianshan Mountains22. The air relative humidity was relatively high, with an annual mean of 57.22 ± 0.32%, and significantly affected by precipitation and ice/snow melt, exhibiting a distinct bimodal pattern distribution with peaks occurring in the early stage of snowmelt (March) and the late stage of glacier melt (September). Precipitation was mainly concentrated from May to September, accounting for 84% of the total, and when combined with the intense ice/snow melt during the same period, it became an important factor influencing the transient consumption of atmospheric CO2 through water-rock reaction and the release of stored carbon in the underlying surface in high-altitude areas11,13.

Near-ground CO2 concentration

The near-surface CO2 concentration on the southern slope of the Tomur Peak had a multi-year average of 338.99 ± 9.25 ppm (Fig. 3), which was lower by 17% than the CO2 background concentration (409.23 ± 2.85 ppm) at the high-altitude Waliguan baseline Station on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and the global annual average CO2 concentration (410 ppm)3,26. And it was approximately 11% lower than the CO2 concentration in the hinterland of the Taklimakan Desert in the Tarim Basin (380.65 ppm)27. The intense chemical weathering in the alpine mountainous areas during the warm season with glacial/snow melting leads to a significant release of carbon stored in the underlying surface10,12. However, the release intensity is only equivalent to the CO2 consumption intensity by silicate rock weathering6, far lower than the 1–3 orders of magnitude of CO2 consumption intensity by water-rock reactions7. Therefore, intense water-rock reactions may be one of the important factors contributing to the low CO2 concentration in the study area.

The variation of CO2 concentration anomaly of surface layer on seasonal and annual scales.

Furthermore, the CO2 concentration exhibited significant seasonal cyclic variations. In the summer months (June-August), the concentration was highest, averaging 349.08 ± 3.67 ppm. This differed from the phenomenon of lowest CO2 concentrations recorded at WMO/GAW baseline stations such as Lin’an, Waliguan, and Mauna Loa in the Northern Hemisphere around late summer14,28, suggesting that the CO2 concentration in the study area was less influenced by the respiration of vegetation growth29. Additionally, in winter (December-February), the CO2 concentration was lowest, averaging 329.51 ± 7.12 ppm, especially in December, at only 324.69 ± 12.22 ppm (Fig. 2). This was significantly different from the phenomenon of elevated CO2 concentrations in winter caused by coal/gas heating in mid-to-high latitudes30, and also distinct from the results of weak CO2 consumption by water-rock reactions in cold high-altitude areas.

Since 2013, the regional CO2 concentration growth rate has been 1.06 ppm·yr− 1 (Fig. 3), significantly lower than the growth rate of 2.06 ppm·yr− 1 at Waliguan Station26 and the global rate of 2.26 ppm·yr− 131, but close to the growth rate of 0.90 ppm·yr− 1 at China’s Shangdianzi Station26. In fact, the annual increase of CO2 concentration in the study area mainly occurs during the spring and summer snow/ice-melting period, while the interannual variation trend in the autumn and winter seasons was not significant.

CO2 background and non-background concentrations

The average background concentration of CO2 calculated using the Boughton two-parameter algorithm was 318.36 ± 8.33 ppm (Fig. 4), accounting for as high as 93.75% of the observed values, which was significantly higher than the proportion in densely populated areas26,28,29. Among these, the background concentration was highest in July, averaging 331.06 ± 2.80 ppm, and lowest in December, averaging 305.14 ± 5.32 ppm. This significantly differed from the areas influenced by the vegetation growth cycle, where the lowest concentration is in September and the highest in the winter months of January and February28,29. However, it basically corresponded with the monthly average temperature in the region, which is highest in July and lowest in January (Fig. 2). Due to the influence of the gradually increasing solar altitude causing the temperature to rise, in addition to the release of temporarily stored carbon in the underlying surface, the convective action during the formation of thermal low pressure promoted the ascent of CO2-rich gases from adjacent low-altitude areas to the study area. Therefore, this should be an important factor leading to the high CO2 concentration in early summer23. This phenomenon was further validated by the high summer atmospheric pressure and low winter atmospheric pressure in the research area (Fig. 2).

The average non-background concentration of CO2 accounted for only 6.25% of the observed average, at 21.23 ± 0.55 ppm. The seasonal differences were weak, but the fluctuations were significant, with monthly variation coefficients ranging from 9.69 to 28.15%, averaging 18.36%. This was about 14 times that of the regional background concentration. Therefore, its material source paths and the potential source area distribution were worthy of attention.

Provenances

To maximize the use and analysis of observational data, we defined the period from September to August of the following year as a full year. Figure 5 shows the main pathways of exogenous substances to the study area and the contribution areas of potential CO2 sources over 7 years. The transport of air masses in the high mountain areas on the southern slope of the Tomur Peak in the Tianshan Mountains was mainly controlled by the westerly wind, with the interannual pathway proportion ranging from 77.60 to 89.51%, averaging as high as 83.67% (Table 1). The CO2 non- background concentration of the air masses transported by the pathways averaged 21.32 ppm, and the trajectory lengths indicate that the air masses were transported over long distances at relatively high speeds. The potential source contribution areas were mainly distributed at the east of the Karakol and the north of Marabishi within 170 km to the observation spot, with CO2 mainly originating from urban human activities and mining emissions (large quantities of gold, copper, and tin mines were distributed to the west of the southern Tianshan range, and clay, limestone, and coal mines were distributed to the east).

It was worth noting that the source area of the Naryn River and the snow and ice cover area, approximately 40 km south, west, and southwest to the Tomur Peak, were also significant contribution areas of potential CO2 sources, with regional WPSCF values generally higher than 0.4. On the one hand, the complex terrain and the high altitude (most areas above 3000 m a.s.l) limited the potential for human emissions of CO2. On the other hand, under modern glacier development conditions, intense freeze-thaw processes had led to the formation of abundant debris and the enrichment of ice and snow meltwater. This process resulted in significant chemical erosion, consuming atmospheric CO2 at a much higher intensity than the release of stored carbon, approaching the CO2 consumption intensity of weathering of silicate rock6,11. Therefore, the regional direct CO2 emissions recharge could basically be ignored. Under the control of the westerly belt, Tianshan Mountains blocks the airflow developing along the Ili River, Naryn River, and Pyandzh River valley, causing CO2 to accumulate in a similar manner to PM2.5 particles on the windward slope32. Subsequently, the indirect recharge of secondary transport under strong winds should be an important factor for the cold windward areas on the west side of the southern vein of Tianshan to become a potential CO2 source.

Under the driving influence of the Asian low pressure and the Arctic vortex33, the path proportion of northward wind development ranged from 0 to 14.16%, with an average of only 3.49%. This was due to the combined effects of the blocking of high pressure in Baikal Lake district34 and the obstruction of the high mountains to the west, such as Khan Tengri and Tomur Peaks. The average CO2 non-background concentration supplied was 20.69 ppm, which was 0.63 ppm lower than that transported by the westerly winds, with a high standard deviation of 14.93 ppm, indicating significant interannual fluctuations, and it could be negligible in some years. On the one hand, the CO2 intensity from human activities was relatively weak due to the wide distribution of the Gobi and desert on the north side of the Tianshan Mountains. On the other hand, as air mass crossed the Tianshan Mountains, the significant orographic rain leaching effect could promote “wet deposition” of CO235.

In the context of the westerly belt control, the interaction of glacial wind, valley wind, and summer convergence airflow and winter divergence airflow in the Tarim Basin results in relatively short pathways for local air mass transport36. This suggests that the slow-moving air masses could restrict the contribution intensity of human CO2 emissions from the mountain-front oasis area, resulting in generally lower levels of WPSCF on the eastern side of the study area, especially during the periods of 2013～2014, 2016～2017, and 2020～2021. Additionally, although there were obvious interannual differences in transport paths, the potential source contribution areas with WPSCF greater than 0.4 were mainly concentrated in the areas north of Marabishi and Kalping County, including clay mines, lime mines, and coal mines distributed in the foothills of the Tianshan Mountains, as well as densely populated and industrious areas near the city of Aksu.

Discussion

Due to the representativeness of direct monitoring and the differences in underlying surface material composition, the source/sink function of high-altitude regions in the carbon cycle remained to be discussed7,9–13. This might lead to doubts about the rationality of the Boughton two-parameter algorithm for segmenting near-ground CO2 concentration, as our segmentation assumes that the CO2 non-background concentration results from natural/human-induced abnormal emissions and unstable external inputs18. Furthermore, it seems to be unscientific to analyze regional CO2 sources using the Hysplit model from a macroscopic perspective. To confirm our results, we still analyze the distribution characteristics of CO2 background and non-background concentration at different time periods. Based on the regional microtopographic conditions, we will discuss in detail the reasonableness and effectiveness of the segmentation from the perspective of the main parameters of near-surface atmospheric stability, such as wind speed and wind direction.

The peak values of CO2 background concentration were mainly distributed around sunset between 18:00 and 21:00, during the transition from similar valley wind to mountain wind in summer (Fig. 6a), indicating that the background concentration is, to a certain extent, influenced by human activities in the piedmont areas. The minimum values often appeared between 8:00 and 10:00 during the transition from mountain wind to valley wind, suggesting a low concentration of CO2 in the air masses in high mountain areas, and the carbon release intensity from the underlying surface of ice, snow and permafrost layer is lower than that of regional CO2 consumption. This is consistent with the result that the carbon release intensity from the underlying surface in the alpine area is only equivalent to the consumption intensity of CO2 by chemical weathering of silicate rocks6. It should be noted that a large amount of near-ground CO2 could be consumed by hydrochemical erosion, which could lead to atmospheric CO2 sinking and dissolving6,7. The consumption of CO2 in alpine areas by water-rock reactions is not limited to chemical reactions of long-term carbon storage (Eq. 1), but more commonly involves temporary consumption reactions of CO2 (Eq. 2)9,13. The latter forms heavy carbonates, which decompose and release CO2 during the evaporation process. However, the release process generally occurs in the area of evapotranspiration after the runoff from the mountains. Therefore, we believe that the release of carbon stored in the underlying surface, under the background of climate warming, does not affect the macro phenomenon of the temporary consumption of atmospheric CO2 caused by water-rock reactions in the study area, which leads to a decrease in near-surface CO2 concentration.

Distribution characteristics of CO2 background concentration under different periods and wind conditions.

Complex terrain conditions are the important potential factors to affect the CO2 background concentration through influencing regional aerodynamic properties37. When the near-surface wind speed was less than 4.5 m·s− 1, the CO2 background concentration fluctuated weakly with a variance coefficient of only 1.03% (Fig. 6b). Therefore, we believed that the results of concentration segmentation were acceptable. However, when the near-surface wind speed exceeded 4.5 m·s− 1, the variation coefficient increased by nearly 170%. This increase was primarily contributed by the intensified atmospheric turbulence, which led to the input of exogenous substances with significant concentration differences and, to some extent, diluted or increased the CO2 background concentration of study area. Specifically, due to the eastward turning of the lower part of the Koxkar glacier (Fig. 1), similar mountain and glacial winds were deflected by the continuous Quaternary moraine on the south side of the monitoring station23, causing the prevailing near-ground wind to evolve into the southward wind. This shift in the wind, along with the low concentration CO2 airflow formed by the water-rock reaction in the ice and snow area, reduced the CO2 background concentration. Conversely, the valley wind, blocked by the Quaternary moraines on the north side of the monitoring point and the large glacial/terminal moraine at the end of Koxkar glacier, evolved into a northerly wind, indirectly causing the study area to be influenced by human activities in the mountain front oasis area, resulting in a higher CO2 background concentration. Therefore, the temporal distribution characteristics of regional CO2 background concentration and its response to wind, as an atmospheric stability factor, are consistent with the hypothesis that background concentration is controlled by factors such as regional natural environment and human daily activities.

The peak of CO2 non-background concentration occurred between 12:00 and 15:00 during periods of high-intensity atmospheric turbulence, controlled by continental high pressure in late winter and early spring, while the low value occurred during the high atmospheric stability at midnight within the same period37. And the concentration from 00:00 to 3:00 (17.52 ppm) was about 35% lower than that from 13:00 to 15:00 (26.98 ppm) (Fig. 7a). This was because the stable nocturnal boundary layer suppressed the input of external CO2. In contrast, the atmospheric turbulence formed under the influence of solar elevation angle during the daytime could be a key factor in the formation of the peak of CO2 non-background concentration.

Distribution characteristics of CO2 non-background concentration under different periods and wind conditions.

The fluctuation of CO2 non-background concentration was relatively significant under different wind speeds and directions (Fig. 7b), with a high variability coefficient of 9.98%, approximately 5 times higher than the 1.97% variability coefficient of the background concentration. In the process of near-ground wind speed increasing, although the non-background concentration of CO2 showed an overall decreasing trend, the concentration fluctuation was not significantly affected by near-ground wind direction. This was likely due to the strong mixing of different source materials in the flow field, resulting from atmospheric turbulence that caused instability in near-surface wind during the transport of exogenous substances by upper-air wind into the study area37. Additionally, when the speed of southward wind formed by near-surface mountain and glacier in the study area was less than 2.0 m·s− 1, the average CO2 non-background concentration exceeded 26 ppm, while the average value of CO2 non-background concentration was significantly lower, controlled by the northerly wind formed by the valley wind affected by human activities in the oasis. This was noticeably different from the distribution of CO2 background concentration affected by local wind speed and direction (Fig. 6b).

The weak response to speed and direction of near-surface wind indicated that the CO2 non-background concentration was mainly controlled by the input of exogenous substances. It was also proved that applying the Hysplit model to analyze the potential contribution area of CO2 in the ice and snow distribution area of high Asia was feasible.

Methods

Data source and preprocessing

The micrometeorological automatic observation station (44.70˚ N, 80.17˚ E, 2996 m a.s.l.) equipped with a CO2 concentration sensor had been continuously observing on alpine meadow at the end of the Koxkar Glacier on the southern slope of Tomur Peak since 2013. Among them, micrometeorological monitoring includes precipitation (Model: TE525MM-L), wind speed and direction (Model: 1405-PK-021), temperature and humidity (Model: HMP155) and four-component radiation (Model: NR01). The CO2 concentration was determined using a non-dispersive infrared sensor (Model: ES-D, Jinzhou Sunshine Meteorological Technology Co., LTD.), which had an accuracy of 3% and a range of 0 to 2000ppm38,39. All the sensors were located 4 m above the ground, and the data was automatically recorded in the CR1000 data collector with storage expansion module at a time of 0.5 h. Unfortunately, the data were missing due to the power supply problem of the instrument from October 5, 2017 to September 9, 2019.

During the field monitoring process, a small amount of CO2 concentration data may be unreasonable due to precipitation, dew or other factors, and needed to be removed40. After exclusion, the valid data accounted for 97.66%. The missing part of the data were supplemented by the linear interpolation method commonly used worldwide41. Secondly, all data were curve-fitted and calibrated against CO2 concentration results from air samples taken with WMO/GAW recommended methods. The air samples were collected at about 10 h in the morning during non-rainy days, with a frequency of about once every 15 d. Prior to sampling, a series of preparatory steps such as gas leak test and filling with dry balance gas were carried out in sequence on the sampling bottle. After the air samples were collected into a pair of sampling bottle, they were determined using a gas chromatograph (7890 A, Agilent Technologies, USA) based on standard gases that were all traceable to the WMO X2007 standard scale14.

Boughton two-parameter algorithm

At present, many equations based on stream base flow segmentation have been used to segment air pollutant concentration data16,20. The evolution trends of regional human activities and natural ecological environment are analyzed from the perspective of the change of pollutant background concentration, and the influence of abnormal human activities and external pollution transported over long distances is also analyzed from the perspective of non-background concentration obtained by filtering18. Based on the background concentration of pollutants obtained by Gao et al. and McNabola et al., we compared the standard deviation and correlation coefficient between the background concentration calculated by commonly used recursive digital filters and the corresponding monitoring values of Waliguan baseline stations in sparsely populated areas16,19. Therefore, Boughton two-parameter algorithm was applied in this paper due to its small standard deviation and high correlation coefficient (Table 2). Hysplit backward trajectory model

Air flow between different regions leads to a superposition of CO2 concentrations, which increases the difficulty in identifying potential contributors43. In order to elucidate the cause of the rapid fluctuation of near-ground CO2 concentration and the transport route of exogenous CO2, we used the Hybrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (Hysplit, v5.2.0) model developed by the Air Resources Laboratory of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration of the United States to calculate the 48-h air mass44. The model calculations were based on starting times of 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 h, with the starting height set to 1000 m, which more accurately reflected the average flow field characteristics of the boundary layer in the high mountain area35.
