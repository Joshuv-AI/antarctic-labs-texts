Optimising screening for recurrent tuberculosis: a microsimulation-based study
Authors: Emily A Kendall, Samyra R Cox, Abhay Kadam, Aye Hnin Moe, Gayatri Lele, Nikhil Gupte, Madhusudan Barthwal, Mandar S Paradkar, Mithun K Mitra, Hojoon Sohn, Shreeya Wasunkar, Ryan R Thompson, Amita Gupta, Sanjay Gaikwad, Jonathan E Golub, Vidya Mave, on behalf of TB Aftermath, TB-DM, and RePORT India study teams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople recently treated for tuberculosis (TB) (TB survivors) face substantial risk of recurrence. The optimal implementation and expected impact of active case-finding are uncertain.MethodsWe developed an individual-level stochastic simulation model of TB recurrence among recently treated people in India, informed by empirical data on the cumulative incidence and timing of TB recurrence, the extent and durations of symptoms and bacteriological positivity and screening-related costs. We modelled post-treatment symptom screening at quarterly or semiannual intervals, with or without universal near-point-of-care bacteriological testing at the first screening encounter. We estimated months of symptomatic TB and of potentially infectious (bacteriologically positive, regardless of symptom status) TB averted over a 2-year period after treatment, along with corresponding screening programme costs, estimated as medians and 95% uncertainty ranges across simulations.ResultsSymptomatic TB recurred within 2 years post-treatment in 10% (8%–12%) of recent TB survivors and undiagnosed prevalence peaked in month 2 (1–3). Symptom screening 6, 12 and 18 months post-treatment (as per Indian guidelines) averted 16% (8%–32%) of symptomatic and 8% (5%–17%) of infectious time. An ‘early’ screening schedule (3, 6 and 9 months post-treatment) increased case detection and health impact (26% (15%–43%) of symptomatic and 16% (9%–27%) of infectious time averted). Adding ‘universal bacteriologic testing’ at the 3-month encounter doubled the impact on transmission potential (34% (21%–55%) of symptomatic and 37% (22%–62%) of infectious time averted). In this context, ‘early’ symptom screening cost $190 ($100–$350) per case detected, and adding universal bacteriological testing cost an additional $520 ($240–$1070) per case incrementally detected by the screening programme.ConclusionsScreening for recurrent TB in high-risk settings such as India could reduce morbidity and time spent with potentially infectious TB. Concentrating screening within the first year has greatest yield. Symptom-based screening is most affordable, but testing for asymptomatic recurrence could further reduce transmission potential.Keywords: Screening, Implementation Science, Tuberculosis, India, Health economics

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Using a simulation model informed by data from TB survivors from multiple cohorts in India, this study identified an early screening schedule (eg, at 3, 6 and 9 months after treatment completion) as the most efficient for improving case detection and reducing TB morbidity. Screening for TB symptoms on such a schedule was estimated to avert 26% of all time with symptomatic recurrent TB and 16% of all time with potentially infectious recurrent TB and to cost the screening programme $190 per case that it detects. Adding a molecular test at the initial screening visit could double the impact on infectious time and transmission potential.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Countries including India that screen for recurrent TB should focus on the early time period of most concentrated risk within the first year after treatment completion. For maximal reductions in time with potentially infectious recurrent TB, incorporation of non-symptom-gated screening (perhaps with new near-point-of-care molecular tools) should also be considered.

Introduction

Early detection of tuberculosis (TB) is crucial for limiting transmission and reducing mortality, and international guidelines support TB screening in high-risk groups.1 Individuals previously treated for TB face heightened relapse risks (over 5% in some settings) and often have vulnerabilities putting them at risk for re-exposure and for progression if reinfected.2 The WHO has made a conditional recommendation for systematic TB screening among people with a prior TB history, and a handful of high TB burden countries have begun adopting the intervention.1 3 In India, the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) recommends post-treatment evaluations to facilitate early detection of recurrences.4 However, the most effective screening approach remains unclear; questions include the optimal timing, the role of non-symptom-based methods, the achievable impact on health and transmission-related outcomes and resource efficiency.

Optimising screening for TB survivors requires understanding the timing and course of recurrent TB. Data from the recent TB Aftermath study provide insights into these dynamics. This trial, conducted in Maharashtra, India from 2021 to 2025, compared home-based versus phone screening among TB survivors and their household members.5 Participants were screened for TB symptoms at 6, 12 and 18 months after treatment completion, and those diagnosed with recurrence through routine care were identified from notification data and interviewed. Notably, half of identified recurrences were diagnosed within 6 months of treatment completion, suggesting that an earlier screening schedule might be more effective than the every 6 months schedule used in TB Aftermath and recommended by NTEP.6 Predictive modelling within TB Aftermath estimated that patient characteristics at the end of treatment could moderately predict recurrence (C-statistic ~0.70),7 such that intensive screening efforts might be directed to high-risk individuals. Additionally, findings from other Indian cohorts highlight a substantial burden of asymptomatic recurrent TB,8 9 prompting the question of whether recurrence-detection strategies should include non-symptom-gated screening.

To optimise screening approaches after treatment, we developed a cohort model simulating TB recurrence. We evaluated the impact and programmatic costs of a post-treatment screening package based on India’s recommended schedule and compared with alternatives that included more frequent timing and/or non-symptom-gated microbiological testing.

Methods

Cohort model

We developed an individual-level stochastic simulation (microsimulation) model of TB recurrence within a hypothetical cohort of TB survivors, using data from the TB Aftermath study described above and two other India-based TB cohort studies described below. Simulated individuals were tracked from the successful completion of TB treatment until all recurrences had occurred.

Data from TB Aftermath informed the cumulative incidence and timing of diagnosis of symptomatic recurrent TB (figure 1A, online supplemental text S1). Participants diagnosed with recurrent TB in Aftermath were interviewed about symptom duration (cough, haemoptysis, fever, weight loss, night sweats or extrapulmonary symptoms); the longest reported duration was used to characterise a log-normal distribution of self-reported symptom duration across participants. We assumed reported symptom durations would be up to two times as long under programmatic conditions (due to anecdotal evidence of faster health-care-seeking among symptomatic study participants). In addition, both in the trial and under programmatic conditions, retrospective self-report was assumed to under-estimate the true duration of symptom-screen positivity by a factor of 2–6, based on comparisons with survey-derived and modelling-derived estimates10 11 (online supplemental text S2).

Symptomatic recurrent TB was modelled as being either sputum positive (bacteriologically positive pulmonary) or sputum negative (bacteriologically unconfirmed and/or exclusively extrapulmonary) at the time of routine diagnosis or TB-related death (whichever came first; the model did not differentiate which recurrent TB episodes ended in routine diagnosis vs death). TB Aftermath data were used to estimate the proportion that were sputum positive, and based on TB Aftermath data, the timing of onset and duration of symptoms were modelled as similar for sputum-positive and sputum-negative diagnoses (online supplemental figure S1).

The model assumed that a proportion of symptom-driven routine diagnoses of sputum-positive recurrent TB (or deaths from sputum-positive recurrent TB) were preceded by a period of asymptomatic but potentially infectious recurrent TB (figure 1A). Lacking direct data on transmission, we operationalised potentially infectious TB as nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT)-positive pulmonary disease. During a modelled asymptomatic TB period, the individual was infectious and would test positive via NAAT, but symptom screening would be negative.

Time to recurrent TB diagnosis was modelled as three sequential periods: (1) time from treatment completion to disease onset, (2) an asymptomatic infectious period (for a subset of bacteriologically positive cases) and (3) a symptomatic but undiagnosed period. The first period followed a Weibull distribution, calibrated to match the timing of TB notification observed in TB Aftermath once combined with sampled values of other relevant parameters (online supplemental table S1). A proportion of recurrences were modelled as unrecognised treatment failures, with asymptomatic TB present from treatment completion; bounds on this proportion were derived from end-of-treatment microbiological data from the C-TRIUMPh and TB-DM cohorts9 12 13 (online supplemental text S3).

We bounded the proportion of bacteriologically confirmed recurrent TB with an asymptomatic NAAT-positive period using published data on the cross-sectional proportion of prevalent recurrent TB that is asymptomatic14 and the proportion of culture-positive pulmonary TB that is Xpert-negative at the time of routine diagnostic evaluation.15 Among episodes with an asymptomatic NAAT-positive period, duration was sampled from a distribution calibrated to reproduce the symptomatic-to-asymptomatic prevalence ratio observed in a national survey.14 Simulated cohorts were required to reproduce the observed prevalence of asymptomatic bacteriologically positive TB 6 months after treatment completion in the CTRIUMPh and TB-DM cohorts, which performed cross-sectional microbiological testing9 12 13 (online supplemental text S3). In addition, parameter sets were required to yield numerically valid Weibull fits and adequate agreement between simulated and observed recurrence-notification timing; parameter sets with non-finite fitted values or among the worst-fitting 10% based on the calibration objective function were excluded from further analyses (online supplemental figure S2).

Additional details on model parametrisation are available in the supplement (online supplemental table S1, online supplemental texts S1–S3).

Modelled interventions

In our simulated cohorts, we modelled cross-sectional screening for recurrent TB at specified time points (figure 1B). Screening aimed to detect individuals with undiagnosed symptomatic TB and, in scenarios with universal bacteriological testing, those with asymptomatic TB at the time of screening. We simulated a screening cascade intended to represent reasonable programmatic implementation fidelity (online supplemental table S1). The proportion of eligible individuals reached for screening was estimated from Aftermath data; the proportion of symptomatic individuals who reported their symptoms during home-based screening was estimated based on changes in symptom reporting before versus after receiving a positive result in a universal TB screening study,16 with a further decrement for phone-based screening based on Aftermath data; and rates of diagnostic testing completion were assumed to be high during home-based screening after accounting for tongue-swab-based options17 but decreased in phone-based screening by an amount estimated using data on care-seeking-related gaps in diagnostic cascades.18

We first modelled a guidelines-based screening package providing symptom-based evaluations at 6, 12 and 18 months post-treatment. We also explored variations, including earlier screening time points (starting at 3 months post-treatment, guided by the observed early timing of many recurrences in the TB Aftermath study), increased screening frequency (every 3 months) and addition of bacteriological testing at the first visit. To balance the benefits of home-based screening (vs phone) reported in TB Aftermath against its increased costs,6 we assumed that screening at the initial time point (with or without bacteriological screening) would be conducted in person while subsequent screenings (always symptom-based) would occur via telephone.

Health impact and costs

Recurrent TB can be symptomatic, infectious, or both. For each intervention, we assessed impact on two TB-related outcomes at the cohort level. First, we measured time spent with symptomatic recurrent TB, reflecting TB’s potential for health impact at the individual level. Second, we estimated time with NAAT-positive recurrent TB, our operational definition of potentially infectious disease. Both outcomes were estimated cumulatively over the first 2 years post-treatment, relative to the time with symptomatic or infectious TB in absence of any intervention. We did not explicitly model mortality due to data limitations but considered time with symptomatic TB as an imperfect proxy for mortality risk.

We also estimated costs from the perspective of the TB screening programme, calculated per TB survivor in the cohort, per case detected through screening and incrementally per additional case detected through screening relative to other specified screening-programme designs, all in 2024 US$. We considered costs of contacting participants and conducting symptom screening (estimated based on TB Aftermath data to cost $2–$4 for a home visit and 4× to 12× less by telephone19; details in online supplemental text S4) and costs of any symptom-driven or universal diagnostic testing, which we assumed would use near-point-of-care swab-based tests at a total cost of $5–$9 per test20–22). We accounted for false-positive symptom screens in confirmatory testing costs but did not include treatment costs (because most symptomatic patients would eventually require treatment irrespective of screening) or patient-borne costs.

Representing and reporting uncertainty

We represented uncertainty probabilistically, sampling all parameter values pseudorandomly from uniformly distributed ranges. Each of 5000 sets of sampled parameters was used to simulate a cohort of 50 000 patients at treatment completion, a size chosen to balance stochastic variability with computational demands. Parameter sets yielding numerically invalid Weibull fits or poor agreement between simulated and observed recurrence-notification timing were excluded, as were simulated cohorts inconsistent with calibration targets for asymptomatic bacteriologically positive TB prevalence approximately 6 months after treatment completion. Results are reported as the median and 95% inner-quantile uncertainty range (UR) across all retained simulations. Interventions were compared pairwise within each simulated cohort before summarising results across simulations.

Model code

The model was coded and run using R V.4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Full code is available at https://github.com/eakendall/aftermath.

Patient/public involvement

Patients were not involved in planning or conducting this modelling analysis.

Results

Natural history and outcomes under routine care

Of 5000 parameter samples and corresponding simulated cohorts, 4400 (88%) adequately fit time-to-event data for symptomatic TB diagnosis, 4239 (85%) fit cross-sectional data on asymptomatic TB prevalence and 3750 (75%) fit both and were used to assess intervention impacts (online supplemental figure S2). Rejected parameter sets tended to have slightly lower values of parameters contributing to the subclinical TB prevalence at 6 months (overall cumulative recurrence, microbiological positivity, ratio of asymptomatic to symptomatic time and variance of asymptomatic duration) and more extreme values of symptom duration and under-reporting (online supplemental figure S3). Rejected sets also had a higher proportion of all recurrences that were diagnosed within 18 months developing symptoms in the first month, but even among retained simulations this proportion could be high (24% (12%–56%)) (online supplemental figure S3). The retained simulations showed good agreement with empirical data from the TB Aftermath study (online supplemental figure S4), with recurrent TB diagnosed in 5.3% (95% UR 4.1%–6.9%) by 6 months and 9.5% (7.6%–11.3%) by 18 months.

For those who developed TB, the mean symptom duration was 3.0 (1.8–5.9) months with SD of 2.4 (1.4–5.3) months. Of all recurrences detected through routine care, 54% (43%–64%) were bacteriologically positive at diagnosis, reflecting bacteriologic confirmation data from TB Aftermath. Of the bacteriologically positive cases with a preceding asymptomatic infectious period, its duration averaged 3.0 (1.8–6.2) months. The prevalence of undiagnosed TB peaked in month 2 (1–3). Cross-sectional prevalences of symptomatic and infectious TB were 2.1% (1.2%–4.2%) and 1.8% (1.1%–3.6%) at 6 months, and 0.8% (0.5%–1.7%) and 0.7% (0.4%–1.7%) at 12 months, respectively.

For cohorts of 50 000 patients completing treatment, a total of 15 000 (9000–32 000) person-months were cumulatively spent with symptomatic recurrent TB and 13 000 (8000–26 000) with infectious recurrent TB within the first 2 years after treatment completion. Impact of screening per current Indian national guidelines

The guidelines-based intervention of symptom-gated TB screening at 6, 12 and 18 months post-treatment was estimated to detect 15% (9%–30%) of all recurrent TB in the cohort and avert 15% (9%–25%) of time with symptomatic recurrent TB over the first 2 years (table 1). By detecting symptomatic infectious TB, it also averted 10% (5%–18%) of time with infectious recurrent TB over the same period. The cumulative cost of the screening programme was $6.40 ($4.20–$9.30) per TB survivor, translating to $330 ($170–$620) per case diagnosed through screening.

An additional 21% (0%–39%) had recurrences beyond the last screening time point. Furthermore, of those whose TB was active within the 6–18-month screening window, only 65% (43%–90%) had symptomatic TB at the time of a screening visit, and due to cascade losses, only 64% (52%–76%) of these were diagnosed. Impact of alternative screening strategies

Because the recurrence rate and the estimated prevalence of undiagnosed recurrent TB were highest in the first 6 months post-treatment, an earlier screening schedule (at 3, 6 and 9 months after treatment completion) was projected to detect more recurrences (median 26% vs 15%, 95% CI +6% to +27% for absolute difference) and to avert more time with both symptomatic (relative difference 1.8× (1.5×–2.2×)) and infectious (relative difference 1.6×(1.3×–1.9×)) TB compared with the guidelines-based schedule. Notably, simply conducting the first two visits (at 3 and 6 months) achieved greater impact with fewer resources, compared with three visits on the guidelines-based schedule. compared with symptom screening at 3, 6 and 9 months, extending the quarterly screening schedule to include 12, 15 and 18 month visits (ie, six visits total) increased case detection from 26% (15%–55%) to 32% (19%–59%) of all recurrences, with proportional effects on symptomatic and infectious time (table 1).

Incorporating non-symptom-gated bacteriological testing at the earliest visit could further enhance impact, although at higher cost (table 1). Considering a single home-based screening visit in isolation (at 6 months), adding bacteriologic testing regardless of symptom status detected an additional 6% (3%–13%) of all recurrences, averted 1.6× (1.4×–2.0×) more symptomatic time and 3.9× (2.9×–5.4×) more infectious time and cost $4.20 ($2.90–$6.00) more per TB survivor compared with symptom-screening alone.

A maximalist intervention with initial bacteriological testing and six quarterly symptom screenings detected 37% (23%–68%) of all recurrences, and averted 39% (25%–56%) and 40% (25%–63%) of symptomatic time and infectious time, respectively, at a cumulative cost of $15 ($11–$21) per TB survivor included in the intervention. Because costs of phone screening were low, the costs of an extended screening schedule were driven by costs of diagnostic testing for symptomatic individuals, including those without TB.

Figure 4 plots costs to the screening programme on a per-person basis and three measures of the impact of screening interventions: proportion of recurrences diagnosed through screening, symptomatic time averted and infectious time averted. Given limited resources for screening, early symptom-based screening (eg, at 3 and/or 6 months post-treatment) is the most resource-efficient approach for all outcomes. As resources expand, adding additional symptom screening time points is the most efficient way to further improve recurrent TB detection or avert symptomatic time, but non-symptom-gated bacteriological testing at a high-risk time point (although costly on a per-patient basis) is the most incrementally resource-efficient way to limit infectious person-time. Notably, the ‘guidelines-based’ standard of care with screening at 6, 12 and 18 months does not lie on the resource-efficient frontier for any evaluated outcome.

For instance, compared with conducting symptom screening three times (at 3, 6 and 9 months after treatment completion), adding a fourth screening visit incurs an additional cost to the screening programme of $1.40 ($0.80–$2.30) per person, incrementally detects 2.2% (0.8%–3.6%) of all recurrences and incrementally averts 2.2% (0.6%–3.6%) of the time with symptomatic TB and 1.7% (0.4%–3.0%) of the time with infectious TB that the cohort would have experienced in absence of any screening. In this cohort, these effects correspond to an incremental cost of $500 ($220–$1740) per incremental early case detection, $210 ($80–$750) per month of symptomatic TB incrementally averted and $340 ($100–$1600) per infectious month averted. In the same cohort, adding bacteriological testing at the 3-month visit would cost the screening programme $4.20 ($2.90–$6.00) per person, $520 ($240–$1070) per early case detection and $170 ($50–$500) per symptomatic month averted, but only $80 ($20–$230) per infectious month averted.

Sensitivity analyses

Effectiveness of guidelines-based screening (measured as the proportion of symptomatic time averted) was most sensitive to: variance of symptom duration, proportion bacteriologically positive at recurrent TB diagnosis (which also affected total and asymptomatic NAAT+TB time), screening cascade efficacy (reach, identification of symptoms when present) and the parameters affecting mean duration of true symptom positivity (relative to retrospective reporting and under programmatic vs trial conditions). For the 3/6/9-month screening package with universal 3-month microbiological testing, symptomatic time averted varied from 27% (18%–35%) to 50% (36%–59%) across deciles of the symptom under-reporting parameter; similar results for other parameters and outcomes are shown in online supplemental figure S6. Effects on infectious time were additionally sensitive to estimates of the cumulative time spent with asymptomatic (relative to symptomatic) TB and the variability of the asymptomatic period duration (online supplemental figure S5A).

The relative impact of an earlier screening schedule (compared with the guidelines-based schedule) was most sensitive to variance in the duration of symptoms and the proportion of recurrent cases that were bacteriologically positive. The incremental impact of adding bacteriologic screening at the first visit also depended on the duration of asymptomatic TB (cumulatively in the cohort and inter-individual variation) and the effectiveness of screening in identifying symptoms when present (online supplemental figure S5B).

As shown in online supplemental figure S7, key outcomes had somewhat broader or more bimodal distributions the simulations rejected versus retained during calibration.

Discussion

In this modelling analysis informed by Indian prospective cohorts, we estimated that symptom-based screening for recurrent TB at 6, 12 and 18 months post-treatment averts 15% (9%–30%) of total time spent with symptomatic recurrent TB in the 2 years after treatment completion, contributing to morbidity and mortality reductions. Given a high recurrence rate in the first year, earlier screening (eg, at 3, 6 and 9 months) could enhance these benefits. Since half of prevalent TB (including recurrent TB14) is asymptomatic, screening in a non-symptom-gated way has potential to both enhance overall case detection and substantially reduce cumulative transmission potential.

Even a screening programme featuring six quarterly screening encounters and universal bacteriological testing detected fewer than one half of recurrences and averted less than one half of symptomatic and infectious time in the cohort. Factors limiting impact included short disease durations relative to feasible screening intervals and losses in the screening and diagnostic cascade. Still, recently treated TB survivors represent a high-yield target population for screening.

Our costing analysis was designed to inform a specific policy question: which approaches to post-TB screening most efficiently identify individuals with recurrent disease from the perspective of the screening programme itself. Accordingly, we focused on screening costs rather than downstream healthcare utilisation or patient-incurred costs. This approach has limitations, as earlier detection may alter subsequent healthcare utilisation (via added treatment, avoided diagnostics and averted downstream cases) and affect patient costs (eg, those associated with prolonged symptoms and diagnostic delay). However, from a programmatic perspective, estimates of screening-programme costs remain highly relevant because these costs are most directly under the control of decision-makers considering implementation and scale-up. Within this operational efficiency framework, our estimate that screening for recurrent TB with optimised timing could cost between $100 and $500 per case detected is broadly consistent with other active case-finding interventions considered to represent good value after accounting for downstream benefits and costs.23

Our analysis used reductions in cumulative symptomatic and infectious time as pragmatic proxies for health and transmission impact. Earlier detection of recurrent TB is expected to improve individual outcomes and reduce opportunities for onward transmission, but translating these gains into disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) averted or dynamic transmission outcomes would require a more detailed understanding of the natural history of recurrent disease—including relationships between its symptom duration, bacillary burden, care-seeking, mortality risk and infectiousness over time. Both mortality risk and infectiousness likely evolve over the disease course and may increase alongside symptom severity, potentially peaking near the time individuals would otherwise present for routine diagnosis. If so, early detection through screening may avert mortality and transmission risk out of proportion to the corresponding reductions in symptomatic or infectious duration.24 In recurrent TB these dynamics may be further complicated, for example, by residual lung disease that alters disease progression and care-seeking behaviour or by a restricted pool of susceptible contacts who were not previously infected. Future efforts to translate recurrent TB screening benefits into DALYs or transmission outcomes may therefore benefit from more detailed representations of disease progression and care-seeking processes informed by data from recurrent-TB cohorts in particular.

Beyond optimising post-treatment screening, alternate strategies exist for reducing the health burden of TB recurrence. For certain patients, preventive interventions could be at least as effective as screening. A 1 month treatment extension might reduce the risk of relapse by 40% or more among patients with certain ‘hard-to-treat’ TB phenotypes,25 with costs per patient (accounting for continuation-phase TB drug costs26 plus an added healthcare visit27 28) be comparable to those of the screening programmes we modelled. Addressing malnutrition or comorbidities during treatment offers additional prevention opportunities. Interventions improving care-seeking behaviours—through education, reminder systems or designated contacts at health facilities—could also hasten passive diagnosis once symptoms recur: a 33% reduction in combined diagnostic and patient delay would reduce symptomatic time by 33%—similar to our estimates for the impact of an optimised screening protocol—potentially at lower cost.

Though we modelled the same screening for all recently treated TB survivors, risk stratification could improve efficiency. A validated prediction model using TB Aftermath data found recurrence risk ranged from ~3% at the 15th percentile to ~12% at the 85th percentile based on factors including sex, body mass index, treatment history and lung function.7 Given this fourfold difference in risk, resources spent on additional screening visits for low-risk patients could instead fund interventions costing up to four times more per visit for high-risk patients while maintaining equivalent yield. More practically, an efficient strategy could be to provide some telephone-based post-treatment screening to all, while intensifying the screening protocol with home visits and non-symptom-gated screening modalities for patients with the highest predicted risk of recurrence.

As a means to diminish the transmission potential of recurrent TB (indicated in our model by time with NAAT-positive disease), our results indicate that adding universal, non-symptom-gated screening would be more resource-efficient than conducting more than two or three rounds of symptom screening. Innovations in diagnostics are making such non-symptom-gated screening more feasible. Near point-of-care, swab-based molecular tests may facilitate non-symptom-gated screening by reducing costs and providing same-visit results.29 Efforts are underway to further lower the costs of such tests. False-positive molecular test results are a potential concern in previously treated individuals, and this strategy would require a follow-up testing plan to confirm positive results and exclude drug resistance, but given that such false-positive results tend to be in the lowest semiquantitative categories,30 the somewhat lower sensitivity of the newest near-point-of-care tests could make false-positive results less common. Other diagnostic advances, such as artificial intelligence detection of recurrent TB via chest X-ray31 or markers of bacterial viability32 33 could lead to more optimised algorithms for recurrent TB diagnosis.

Grounded in data from the TB Aftermath study and other regional cohorts, our individual-level simulation model provides valuable insights into recurrent TB. These include the timing of recurrences, which supports earlier screening schedules, and the substantial burden of asymptomatic, bacteriologically-positive TB in recently treated patients, which warrants consideration of screening modalities beyond symptom assessment. Furthermore, this pragmatic trial highlights losses along the screening cascade—even under research conditions—that limit overall impact. If implementation fidelity is low—for example, if symptom elicitation is inconsistent or individuals with likely TB are not linked to diagnostic and treatment services—then impact would be further reduced in proportion to those losses from the screening cascade.

Despite the robustness of our simulations, limitations exist. In addition to limitations noted above related to costs beyond the screening programme and estimation of mortality and transmission effects, uncertainty remains about recurrence timing. Our assumed Weibull distribution fit well to trial data but was validated against diagnosis timing rather than disease onset and does not differentiate relapse from reinfection. We estimated symptom duration from patients’ retrospective self-report with indirect adjustments for underreporting and programmatic factors; true durations of symptom screen positivity could be longer (enhancing screening impact) or more variable than assumed. For asymptomatic TB, data were even more limited. We also assumed that recurrent TB rarely resolves spontaneously once symptomatic or NAAT-positive. We did not simulate all intervention variations—for example, leveraging screening encounters to test household contacts or treat post-TB pulmonary sequelae, or using phone-based screening throughout. Finally, our data derive primarily from a single state in India. The yield and optimal timing of recurrent-TB screening could differ where differences in the quality of TB care affect the risk of relapse after treatment, comorbidities such as HIV alter the timing and progression of relapse episodes or differences in transmission alter the ongoing reinfection-related recurrence risk.

Conclusion

This modelling study provides critical guidance for post-treatment TB screening strategies in India and other settings where TB survivors face high risks of relapse. Focusing screening on the early post-treatment window when incidence of recurrence is highest and considering non-symptom-gated screening for high-risk patients may significantly improve relevant health outcomes. Other interventions to prevent recurrence or hasten its passive detection should be evaluated for comparative effectiveness and costs; however, these findings support investing in post-TB screening and continuing to optimise its implementation.
