Temporal Trends in Utilization of Transvenous Lead Extractions Among Cardiac Implantable Electronic Device Infections
Authors: Toshihiro Nakamura, Yoko Sumita, Koshiro Kanaoka, Kimitake Imamura, Mitsuru Takami, Koji Fukuzawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) infections are a growing concern, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. International guidelines recommend complete device and lead extraction for infected cases, yet real-world adherence to these recommendations remains suboptimal. The use of transvenous lead extraction (TLE) in Japan has not been well characterized.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the real-world use of TLE in CIED infections, assess trends over time, and identify factors associated with the likelihood of undergoing TLE and the procedural complications.MethodsThis was a retrospective observational study using the Japanese Registry of All Cardiac and Vascular Diseases diagnosis procedure combination/per diem payment system (JROAD-DPC) from 2015 to 2021. Patients with a diagnosis of CIED infections were identified on the basis of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision codes.ResultsAmong 7,434 hospitalization records, only 38% underwent TLE for CIED infections in the overall hospitals. However, the use of TLE in certified TLE hospitals increased significantly from 54.0% in 2015 to 70.8% in 2021 (P trend <0.001). Independent predictors of reduced TLE use included a lower body mass index, female sex, cerebrovascular disease, and dementia. Notably, 65.5% of patients in noncertified hospitals were discharged home, a finding suggesting potential undertreatment.ConclusionsAlthough the TLE rate is increasing, many patients with CIED infections do not undergo TLE. Addressing barriers through physician education, improved referral systems, and adherence to guideline-directed management, especially in noncertified hospitals, is essential for optimizing the TLE rate.

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), including pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, play a crucial role in managing various cardiac conditions, such as bradycardia, tachyarrhythmia, and heart failure. Despite the significant therapeutic benefits of these devices, CIED infection remains a serious and potentially fatal complication, with a rising incidence over recent decades.1,2 The infection risk increases with device longevity, with studies indicating that up to 11.7% of patients experience infections within 25 years of device implantation. Given the increasing prevalence of CIED implantations worldwide, effective infection management strategies, including lead extractions, are imperative.3

Transvenous lead extraction (TLE) is the recommended standard of care for CIED-related infection, as endorsed by international guidelines.3,4 Complete device and lead extractions are associated with a significantly improved survival compared with antibiotic therapy alone.5 Nevertheless, given the results of previous reports, the rate of lead extractions in cases of CIED infections has been suboptimal.5 The underuse of TLE has been attributed to concerns over procedural risks, lack of expertise, and health care system inefficiencies.5 Previous reports highlighted the notably low use of TLEs for CIED infections.6,7 However, we believe that these findings differ substantially from actual clinical practice.

The Japanese Lead Extraction Registry (J-LEX) provides essential data on TLE practice. According to the J-LEX report, 62.8% of lead extraction procedures were performed for infection-related indications, with a high clinical success rate of 98.1%.8 Although J-LEX data provide insights into the number of lead extraction procedures performed, the actual incidence of CIED infections requiring lead extractions in Japan remains unknown, thus making it difficult to assess the true rate of appropriate TLE implementations.

This study aimed to elucidate the actual rate of TLEs for CIED infections, the current status of undertreatment, and the improvement trends by using a national registry database.

Methods

Data source

We conducted a multicenter retrospective observational study using the Japanese Registry of All Cardiac and Vascular Diseases diagnosis procedure combination/per diem payment system (JROAD-DPC). More than 60% of admissions in cardiovascular departments in Japanese Circulation Society–certified cardiology training hospitals in Japan are included in the database. The JROAD-DPC is a nationwide claim-based database that is based on data from the Japanese Per Diem Payment System (DPC) in hospitals participating in the Japanese Registry of All Cardiac and Vascular Diseases (JROAD) survey.9 The JROAD database has been described in detail previously.10,11 In brief, all teaching hospitals with cardiovascular beds participate in the JROAD. Hospital physicians must record all admission and discharge data for each patient and are obliged to guarantee the accuracy of these data. Japan’s universal health insurance mandates linkage of all procedures to a diagnosis. Acute care hospitals use the DPC system, whereas long-term care is reimbursed through fee-for-service or per-diem models.

The JROAD-DPC database consists of the following information for each patient discharged: age; sex; height; weight; primary diagnoses, comorbidities, or conditions arising after admission, on the basis of International Classification of Diseases-10th revision (ICD-10) codes; the Charlson comorbidity index; diagnostic and therapeutic procedures; and discharge status.

Study Population

From April 2015 to March 2022, patients with a diagnosis of CIED infection were identified in the JROAD database. Patients were included in this study if they had ICD-10 codes T82.7 or T82.8 with any of the following: “main diagnosis,” “admission-precipitating diagnosis,” “most resource-consuming diagnosis,” and “second-most resource-consuming diagnosis.” Additionally, patients were required to have an ICD-10 code corresponding to an infectious disease (listed in Supplemental Table 1). The recorded diagnoses in the medical texts were meticulously reviewed to exclude ambiguous cases, such as suspected infections and hospitalizations for reasons unrelated to the treatment of CIED infections. Patients who received antibiotics for fewer than 3 days were excluded because such cases were unlikely to represent CIED infections, given the latest guidelines recommending a minimum of 2 to 4 weeks of antibiotic therapy for CIED infections.3 We also considered antibiotics that are often administered within 3 days in clinical practice, even in patients with newly implanted devices and generator exchanges. Subsequently, records with readmissions were excluded. The baseline characteristics, clinical presentation, management, and in-hospital outcomes, including death, length of hospitalization, medical cost, and discharge locations, were analyzed. The procedure codes used to identify treatment methods and ICD-10 codes used to identify comorbidities are summarized in Supplemental Tables 2 and 3.

We evaluated the annual TLE rate across the overall hospitals and specifically in hospitals certified by the Japanese Heart Rhythm Society to perform TLEs. The criteria for certified TLE hospitals require the presence of a full-time cardiologist with expertise in lead extraction procedures as well as a cardiovascular surgeon. In Japan, TLEs are exclusively performed at certified hospitals. If a hospitalization record contained procedure codes for both a TLE and a surgical lead extraction, the procedure was classified under the TLE group. This classification was based on the assumption that the TLE would have been attempted first, with a transition to surgical lead extraction only if the TLE was unsuccessful or if a severe complication occurred. To assess the real-world treatment practices in noncertified hospitals, the discharge location was also examined. Patients requiring lead extractions at noncertified hospitals should have been transferred to specialized hospitals. Conversely, the patients discharged home may have included those who were classified as having received undertreatment, which meant that a lead extraction was not performed despite being indicated.

Furthermore, the occurrence and clinical outcomes of TLE-associated complications (vascular complications, hematoma or hemorrhage, cardiac tamponade, and pneumothorax or hemothorax, hematoma, cerebral infarction [listed in Supplemental Table 4]) were reviewed. Propensity score matching was used to assess the effect of TLE on in-hospital mortality in patients with CIED infections. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with relevant baseline variables, and patients were matched 1:1 by nearest neighbor without replacement, by using a caliper width of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD ≤0.10 indicating adequate balance.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are given as the frequency (percentage). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted to determine whether each continuous variable followed a nonparametric distribution. If a continuous variable followed a nonparametrical distribution, it was represented by the median along with Q1-Q3 ranges and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; otherwise, it was represented by the mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t-test. The chi-square test was used to compare the categorical variables. At first, to assess the clinical predictors of the use of lead extractions, a univariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Sequentially, variables with a P < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis, and the HRs and 95% CIs were calculated. Trend analysis was performed using the Cochran-Armitage trend test. Propensity score matching was not performed in this study because the primary objective was not to evaluate the effect of TLE on clinical outcomes or survival. All statistical comparisons were 2-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed with Stata version 17.0 software (StataCorp).

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee at Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine (registration number B230100) approved the study protocol. All data were anonymized, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Complications in TLE procedures

Among the patients managed with TLE, procedural complications were identified in 4.4%, which included the following: superior vena cava injury, innominate vein injury, or other vascular complications in 0.9%; cardiac perforation or tamponade in 1.5%; pulmonary embolism in 0.4%; pneumothorax or hemothorax in 1.8%; hematoma in 0.7%; and cerebral infarction in 0.9%. The percentage of all patients managed with TLEs who died and experienced a TLE-associated procedural complication was 0.4%. Discussion

Main findings

In this study of a contemporary, large, population-based study comparing the characteristics, management, and outcomes of Japanese patients with CIED infections who underwent TLEs with those of patients who did not have TLEs, we identified several key findings (Central Illustration). First, only 39% of patients with CIEDs underwent TLEs despite the diagnosis of CIED infection, even though there were low overall rates of TLE-related complications at 4.4%. The proportion of patients undergoing TLE management in certified TLE hospitals increased significantly, from 54.0% in 2015 to 70.8% in 2021. Second, a lower BMI, female sex, cerebrovascular disease, and dementia were independently associated with a decreased likelihood of lead extraction as part of CIED infection management.

Identification of definitive CIED infections requiring TLE

Previous reports defined CIED infections solely on the basis of the presence of an infectious disease diagnosis or a history of antibiotic therapy.6,7,12 However, such criteria may include superficial infections that do not warrant lead extraction according to the latest guidelines, as well as cases of new implantations or generator exchanges in which antibiotics were administered for infection prophylaxis. To address this limitation, we incorporated the duration of antibiotic administration to distinguish a true CIED infection. In this study, we aimed to identify true CIED infections that necessitated TLEs as accurately as possible and calculated the actual lead extraction rate. Moreover, TLEs are performed only at hospitals certified by the Japanese Heart Rhythm Society. Consequently, the lead extraction rate for CIED infections at certified TLE hospitals had increased up to 70% in the most recent analysis year of 2021. It is unlikely that our method of identification underestimated the incidence of CIED infections; however, it remains possible that the TLE rate was insufficient. In clinical practice, TLEs may not be performed in patients with dementia and multiple comorbidities. Some patients with a CIED infection diagnosis may not require TLEs.

In this study, to avoid double counting of the same patient, we excluded patients who underwent TLE during a readmission. In the JROAD database, readmission is defined as rehospitalization at the same hospital, and TLE status is determined on the basis of the index hospitalization. Moreover, delayed lead extraction is associated with increased complications and mortality. A study by Lin et al13 found that patients who experienced delays in lead extraction had a 2-fold increase in mortality compared with patients who underwent early extraction. The risk of systemic dissemination of an infection, particularly lead-associated endocarditis, further underscores the necessity of timely lead extraction.14 These findings emphasized the importance of prompt diagnosis and guideline-directed management. Although including these patients would have increased the overall TLE rate, we believe that early diagnosis and intervention are essential; therefore, the TLE rate was calculated solely on the basis of the initial admission data.

Underuse of TLE in CIED infections

Despite the overwhelming evidence supporting lead extractions in CIED infections,15,16 the number of patients who have received this lifesaving intervention is not sufficient. Greenspon et al5 reported that as recently as 2018, 36% of patients with CIED infections did not undergo complete system extraction.5 The consequences of nonadherence to the guidelines are profound, with increased mortality, recurrent infections, and prolonged hospitalizations.17

Several barriers have contributed to this underuse. First, many physicians perceive TLE as a high-risk procedure, despite data showing a high success rate (>95%) in experienced centers.18 The overall procedural complication rates related to TLE in the present cohort was low, at 4.4%, which is comparable to the registry data from experienced centers showing TLE-related complication rates of 1.4% to 1.7%.19,20 Second, nonelectrophysiology specialists may lack familiarity with the latest guideline recommendations.21 Consequently, the initial treating physician may not recognize the need for an extraction, and this leads to delaying a specialist referral.18 In addition, limited access to experienced operators in some institutions further delays an appropriate intervention.22

Misclassification of superficial infections may also lead to undertreatment because complete extraction is not required in true superficial or incisional infections that do not involve the device. However, failure to diagnose deeper infections accurately risks missing clinically indicated extractions. A recent survey indicated that a knowledge gap persists between physicians who perform TLEs and those who do not perform these procedures in the management of CIED infections.23 During the survey, approximately 10% of cardiologists and 40% of noncardiologist physicians opted for partial device removal in cases of CIED pocket infections.23 TLE may not be indicated in patients with advanced frailty, dementia, or poor surgical tolerance. Nonetheless, from a clinical perspective, the fact that only approximately 40% of patients with CIED infection undergo TLE raises concern for potential undertreatment. Given that the 1-year survival rate was at least approximately 70%,24 it would be reasonable to consider TLE in patients whose anticipated survival exceeds these benchmarks. To address these issues, regional referral networks and multidisciplinary teams involving electrophysiologists, infectious disease specialists, and cardiovascular surgeons should be established to guide timely and evidence-based decision making.

In this study, no increase was observed in the number of patient transfers from noncertified hospitals. Although the JROAD data do not include information on transfer destinations, it can be assumed that there has been no apparent rise in transfers specifically for TLEs from noncertified hospitals. In fact, 65.5% of the patients with CIEDs who were admitted to noncertified hospitals were discharged to home. It is true that complete extraction is not required for superficial or incisional infections at the device pocket if an infection does not involve the device.25 However, misdiagnosis of a superficial infection runs the potential risk of undertreatment, meaning that lead extraction was not performed despite being clinically indicated. To bridge the gap between the guideline recommendations and real-world practice, a multifaceted approach is needed, focusing on physician education, especially in noncertified TLE hospitals, and could lead to higher TLE rates.

Study limitations

First, because this study was a retrospective analysis of claim-based data, there may have been risks of systemic or nonsystemic coding errors or insufficient data collection. However, the validity of the DPC database was generally high, especially for primary diagnoses and procedure records.26 Second, because individuals could not be identified across the different hospitals in the JROAD study, there was a possibility that some patients were transferred to another hospital for lead extraction or were subsequently transferred for ongoing medical treatment following lead extraction, thus potentially underestimating the actual TLE rate in real-world clinical practice. Third, a possible overestimation of the actual numbers of infected patients may have contributed to the observed low rates of lead extractions. Our focus was on avoiding overestimation of the TLE rate that could result from underestimation of true CIED infections. At the very least, we are confident that the actual TLE rate is not lower than this. Fourth, the actual lead extraction rate may be higher than reported because it was challenging to exclude patients with superficial infections who did not require lead removal solely on the basis of the disease classification. Fifth, regional variation was not analyzed in the present study; however, ensuring equitable allocation of TLE hospitals relative to patients’ needs and regional health care access is essential for improving procedural availability. Sixth, this study calculated TLE rates at the most recently certified hospitals; however, because certification timing varied, some certified TLE hospitals had periods without extraction, thereby potentially leading to underestimation of the true TLE rate at certified TLE hospitals. Seventh, in the JROAD database, we were unable to identify the specific factors associated with the preference for surgical extraction.

Conclusions

In contemporary clinical practice in Japan, the TLE rate has increased from 38% in the overall hospitals to 70% in the certified TLE hospitals. However, 30% of patients with CIED infections still did not undergo TLE, despite the low rate of procedural complications. Patients with a lower BMI, female sex, cerebrovascular disease, and dementia were less likely to undergo TLE. Enhancing education on the management of CIED infections in noncertified hospitals may contribute to an increased implementation of TLEs.
