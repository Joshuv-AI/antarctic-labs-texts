Serological surveillance for malaria elimination in Southeast Asia: a scoping review
Authors: Kumuthamalar Sangaran, Noor Wanie Hassan, Si Jie Goh, Ameera Aleeshya Mohd Fadrul Izwan, Zulkarnain Md Idris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs Southeast Asian countries advance toward malaria elimination, challenges such as residual transmission reservoirs and zoonotic spill-over require high-resolution micro-surveillance. While conventional diagnostics often miss hidden transmission foci in low transmission settings, serological assays provide an essential supplement by measuring both historical and recent exposure. This scoping review assesses the implementation of antibody surveillance across Southeast Asian countries and highlights its potential in the concerted effort towards regional malaria elimination.MethodsThis review adhered to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines to identify peer-reviewed studies on malaria serology in Southeast Asian countries. Systematic searches of PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus were conducted for articles published between January 2000 and May 2025. Multiple independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts and full texts against predefined eligibility criteria, focusing on English-language primary studies involving human samples. Extracted data including study location, population, serological assay type, serological markers and key findings were synthesised to provide a comprehensive overview of malaria antibody surveillance.ResultsOf 6,849 records identified, 33 studies across seven Southeast Asian countries met the inclusion criteria. Study designs consisted of 26 cross-sectional, five longitudinal and one each of a prospective cohort and a control trial. Over time, research focuses have shifted from high-transmission assessments to low-transmission and elimination contexts, increasingly utilising multiplex assays and protein microarrays. While Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax markers predominated, recent studies have incorporated Plasmodium knowlesi and vector exposure antigens. Furthermore, seroconversion rate estimation and machine learning approaches effectively identified historical transmission declines and residual hotspots missed by conventional microscopy or molecular methods.ConclusionSerological surveillance offers a transformative supplement to the diagnostic gaps prevalent in Southeast Asia’s low-transmission settings. By generating a high-resolution exposure profile, advanced seroepidemiological tools enable precise micro-stratification of transmission foci, the differentiation of recent from historical exposure and the targeted management of zoonotic spill-over. Ultimately, integrating these methods provides a robust, cost-effective framework for identifying high-risk populations, verifying transmission interruption and achieving regional malaria-free certification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05977-y.Keywords: Malaria, Serology, Seroepidemiology, Antibody responses, Low-transmission, Scoping review, Southeast Asia

Background

Malaria is a life-threatening vector-borne disease and remains a significant public health problem globally. In 2024, an estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 reported deaths due to the disease occurred across 80 countries worldwide [1]. Of these, approximately 265 million cases (94%) occurred in the World Health Organization (WHO) African region (AFRO). In the WHO South-East Asia Region (SEARO), which includes Indonesia, Myanmar, Thailand and the recently certified malaria-free Timor-Leste, approximately 2.7 million malaria cases were reported, with more than half attributed to P. vivax. Meanwhile, in the WHO Western Pacific Region (WPRO), comprising countries such as Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia and the Philippines, alongside the malaria-free nations of Brunei and Singapore, approximately 2.4 million malaria cases were reported, of which more than one-quarter of all cases in the region attributed to P. vivax [1].

Among Southeast (SE) Asian countries that have yet to achieve malaria-free certification, Myanmar bears the highest burden, with an estimated 702,000 estimated cases reported in 2024, followed by Indonesia (631,000), the Philippines (24,000), Thailand (7,900), Laos (540), Cambodia (320), and Vietnam (240) [1]. Although Malaysia has reported no indigenous human malaria cases since 2018, the country continues faces the challenge of zoonotic malaria, particularly infections caused by P. knowlesi [2]. While the global number of reported P. knowlesi infections declined by 34% between 2023 and 2024, the emergence and persistence of zoonotic malaria in recent years poses significant challenges to achieving the last mile of malaria elimination to Malaysia and neighbouring countries, including Cambodia, Indonesia, Thailand and the otherwise malaria-free Brunei Darussalam, all of which reported indigenous zoonotic malaria cases in 2024 [1, 3, 4].

Surveillance in countries such as those in SE Asia that are progressing toward malaria elimination is based upon identifying, investigating and eliminating transmission hotspots while ensuring that infections are prevented and cured [5]. Once elimination is achieved, continuous surveillance is crucial to prevent the re-establishment of the disease. The implementation of an effective monitoring system is vital in promptly detecting and responding to potential resurgence of malaria [6]. Diagnostic tools such as quality-assured microscopy and rapid diagnostic test (RDT) are widely recommended and practical for malaria diagnosis, particularly in high-transmission areas [7, 8]. While malaria molecular diagnostics are not suitable for routine use in endemic regions of many SE Asian countries, they provide valuable sensitivity for detecting asymptomatic infections and low-level parasitaemia, and are particularly useful for targeted investigations in highly localised residual transmission hotspots in regions nearing elimination where malaria is spatially heterogeneous [9–11].

Serological assays, on the other hand, detect antibodies to malaria parasites and are considered a robust adjunct tool to measure historical and recent exposure [12]. This approach has several practical applications for malaria surveillance, which can inform decision-making in malaria control programmes. Estimating malaria exposure within a population is particularly useful in low-transmission and near-elimination settings, where conventional methods of diagnosing active malaria cases, such as microscopy or RDT tests, may lack the sensitivity to detect low-level infections [13, 14]. Serology also offers a complementary metric to classical methods for estimating transmission intensity, such as entomological inoculation rate (EIR) and parasite rate (PR). The EIR, which quantifies the exposure of an individual to infectious mosquito bites per year [15], and the PR, which measures the proportion of a population harbouring active asexual blood-stage infections [16], are robust methods in areas of stable, high malaria transmission but become less informative in low-transmission settings [17]. Serological surveillance overcomes some of these limitations by measuring longer-lasting immunological markers of malaria exposure rather than relying solely on the detection of transient infections or infectious vectors. Furthermore, in combination with age, the proportions of individuals seropositive at different age groups within the population (i.e. age-stratified seroprevalence) can be derived from a population-level serological survey and be used to assess the force of infection (i.e. seroconversion rate, SCR) [18, 19].

As SE Asian countries work towards the common goal of eliminating malaria, geographical factors, human behaviours and other determinants of transmission intensity continue to create unique challenges in each setting. To address this, seroepidemiological studies have been increasingly utilised in the region to understand local transmission patterns and support the design of tailored and targeted malaria control strategies. However, despite the shared goal of elimination, there remains no comprehensive overview of the role and extent of malaria antibody surveillance in the region. This scoping review sought to synthesise information currently available on the use of malaria antibody surveillance in the 11 SE Asian countries. By exploring the contexts of its implementation, the review highlights the evidence that has informed the development of data-driven recommendations, the progress achieved and the promising path ahead of serological tools to accelerate malaria elimination across the region.

Methods

Search strategy

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, with the completed PRISMA-ScR checklist provided in Supplementary File 1. Although a formal review protocol was not publicly registered, all methodological procedures were predefined to ensure transparency, consistency and methodological rigours throughout the review process [20]. A protocol was employed to search three online databases, namely PubMed, Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus for peer-reviewed studies relevant to the rationale and objectives of this scoping review. The search strategy used the following term using PubMed and WoS: (“naturally acquired immunity” OR “transmission intensity” OR “serology” OR “serolog*” OR “sero-” OR “sero-*” OR “sero epidemiology” OR “sero surveillance” OR “sero conversion” OR “sero-epidemiology” OR “sero-surveillance” OR “sero-conversion” OR “antibody” OR “antibody*” OR “antibody-*” OR “antibody kinetics” OR “antibody response” OR “antibody-kinetics” OR “antibody-response”) AND (“malaria” OR “malari*” OR “plasmodium” OR “malaria transmission” OR “malaria exposure”). The asterisk symbol (*) was applied as a wildcard to capture root words with alternative endings. For example, “serolog*” retrieved both “serology” and “serological”. In Scopus, the same search was applied, but queries combining hyphens with wildcards (e.g., “sero-*”) were omitted, as Scopus does not process such combinations. The results were restricted to documents published between 1 January 2000 and 31 May 2025 and were imported into EndNote version 20.4, a reference management tool.

Eligibility criteria

The results of the search strategy were then screened to remove duplicates by the automated feature of EndNote and subsequently manually screened for any remaining duplicates. The remaining unique results were sorted according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria developed for the purpose of this review. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed articles conducted in SE Asian countries (Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Timor-Leste and Vietnam) and published in English. Studies were excluded if they were not published in English, did not involve human samples, were unrelated to SE Asian countries or did not present primary data.

Study selection

After removing duplicate articles, titles that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The remaining records were subsequently screened through abstract and full-text review independently by two reviewers (KS and ZMI). Any discrepancies during the selection process were discussed among the reviewers and resolved by consensus.

Data extraction

Key characteristics of publications that met all inclusion criteria were summarised including the authors, year of publication, location/country, study design, total sample size, population, type of serological assay, serological markers used, antibodies measured, target malaria parasite species, application of serological-based transmission modelling and key findings. Four reviewers (KS, NWH, SJH and AAMFI) independently extracted data using a standardised data-charting form, with a fifth reviewer (ZMI) resolved discrepancies and provided clarification for complex interpretations where necessary. The collated data were then analysed and synthesised according to the relevant findings identified across the included studies.

Ethics approval

This scoping review did not require ethical approval, as all data used were obtained from published peer-reviewed or grey literature and are cited accordingly.

Results

The systematic literature search identified 6,849 records from three databases, including PubMed (n = 1,748), WoS (n = 1,908), and Scopus (n = 3,193) (Fig. 1). Prior to screening, 2,488 records published outside the predefined period (1 January 2000–31 May 2025) were removed, leaving 4,361 records for screening. After the removal of 2,070 duplicate records, 2,291 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,258 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, primarily due to non-related variables (n = 1,609), lack of primary data (n = 477), non-human study populations (n = 153), or publication in languages other than English (n = 19). In total, 33 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the scoping review.

Geographical, population and design characteristics of malaria seroepidemiological studies

The 33 studies included in this scoping review were published between 2001 and 2025 (Table 1) [21–53] and together involved more than 120,000 participants across malaria-endemic, low-transmission, and near-elimination settings. All studies were conducted in SE Asia, with substantial representation from Indonesia (n = 9) [25, 28–30, 41, 42, 53], Cambodia (n = 7) [27, 32–36, 51], Thailand (n = 7) [22, 23, 31, 33, 43, 47], Myanmar (n = 6) [26, 39, 44–46, 52], Vietnam (n = 5) [21, 24, 37, 46], Malaysia (n = 3) [38, 49, 50] and the Philippines (n = 3) [38, 48]. One multi-country prospective cohort study was conducted across Thailand and Brazil [40]. Regarding study design, the majority were cross-sectional surveys (n = 26), including two repeated cross-sectional studies embedded within routine surveillance or health facility–based sampling frameworks [22–24, 26–32, 34–36, 38, 41–50, 52, 53]. Longitudinal, control trial and prospective cohort studies (n = 9) were used to investigate antibody acquisition, decay, seroconversion, and associations with infection risk over time [21, 25, 33, 37, 39, 40, 44, 51]. Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from 138 participants in early immuno-epidemiological investigations [25] to over 14,000 individuals in large-scale surveillance studies [44, 45, 52].

The study populations were heterogeneous and included all-age community residents [32, 34, 35, 37, 44, 45, 50], children and school-aged populations [29], forest-fringe and forest-going communities [27, 47, 51], indigenous and mobile populations [38, 49], military personnel and adult volunteers [36], and health facility attendees and their companions [42, 48]. Several studies specifically targeted high-risk groups, such as forest workers, farmers, or residents of remote endemic villages, reflecting a shift toward risk-based and targeted surveillance approaches [27, 46, 47, 51]. Furthermore, earlier studies (2001–2003) primarily focused on moderate- to high-transmission settings and classical seroprevalence assessments of antibody responses following natural infection [21–25]. In contrast, studies published after 2015 increasingly emphasised hypo-endemic, low-transmission, and pre-elimination contexts, incorporating asymptomatic and sub-microscopic infections, repeated sampling and spatial analyses to detect residual transmission [32–35, 37–40, 43–51, 51]. Across all studies, P. falciparum and P. vivax were the principal species investigated [21–37, 39–53], with more recent work also including P. knowlesi [38] and Anopheles vector exposure markers [52].

Plasmodium-specific antibody responses and transmission inference

Across the reviewed studies, ELISA was the most commonly used immunoassay, particularly in earlier investigations, with most analyses quantifying total IgG and selected studies assessing IgG subclasses, IgM or IgE (Table 1) [21–26]. More recent studies increasingly employed multiplex bead-based immunoassays and protein microarrays, allowing simultaneous measurement of antibody responses to large panels of malaria antigens and supporting more comprehensive seroprofiling [31–33, 35, 43, 48, 50]. These platforms were primarily applied to evaluate antibody responses against P. falciparum and P. vivax, while fewer studies included P. malariae [32, 35]. Multi-species exposure was also evaluated using parallel singleplex ELISA panels to detect zoonotic P. knowlesi antibodies [38]. Commonly investigated P. falciparum antigens included PfAMA-1, PfMSP-1₁₉, PfMSP2, PfGLURP and PfCSP, whereas P. vivax studies frequently targeted PvAMA-1, PvMSP-1₁₉, PvCSP variants, PvDBP, PvEBP and PvRBP family proteins [27, 28, 38, 43, 48, 51]. Several studies also incorporated mosquito salivary antigens to capture exposure to malaria vectors [32, 35, 51].

A subset of studies further applied serology-based transmission models to infer historical and recent malaria exposure by estimating seroconversion and seroreversion rates (Table 1) [27–29, 37, 41, 42, 46–48, 53]. These modelling approaches enabled quantification of transmission intensity and identification of spatial heterogeneity, particularly in elimination and pre-elimination settings. In contrast, other studies relied on descriptive seroprevalence estimates or antigen-specific antibody profiles without formal transmission modelling, limiting their ability to infer temporal changes in malaria transmission [21, 23, 44, 45, 51].

Immune markers of protection and risk

Across the reviewed studies, high seroprevalence to blood-stage malaria antigens was frequently observed despite low or declining parasitaemia, indicating substantial historical exposure in many SE Asian settings (Table 1) [21, 24, 31, 33]. IgG subclass analyses consistently demonstrated dominance of cytophilic antibodies, particularly IgG1 and IgG3, across multiple antigens [21, 23, 24]. However, antibody presence did not uniformly translate into clinical protection, as several studies reported no clear association between high antibody levels and reduced parasitaemia or immunity [21, 36]. In contrast, elevated parasite-specific IgE responses were associated with malaria resistance and lower parasitaemia, suggesting distinct immune mechanisms beyond conventional IgG-mediated responses [22].

Age was identified as a strong determinant of seropositivity and antibody magnitude, reflecting cumulative exposure over time [23, 28, 33, 34, 37, 45]. Antibody breadth and intensity generally increased with age, with adults exhibiting stronger and more sustained responses, particularly to P. falciparum antigens [23, 33]. Conversely, P. vivax antibody responses were often acquired earlier in life but decayed more rapidly, consistent with differences in transmission dynamics and relapse biology [33, 34]. Marked seasonal and spatial heterogeneity in serological responses was also consistently reported across endemic and elimination settings [27, 30, 34, 41, 46]. Antibody prevalence and titres increased during high-transmission or wet seasons and declined following vector control interventions, while spatial clustering of seropositive individuals frequently corresponded to forest-fringe or other high-risk areas, revealing localised transmission pockets undetected by routine surveillance [25, 30, 34, 41, 42, 51].

Advanced modelling for serological surveillance

Seroconversion rate (SCR) modelling was widely applied to quantify malaria transmission intensity and temporal dynamics across SE Asia [27–29, 32, 37, 41, 42, 46–49, 53]. SCR estimates varied substantially by parasite species, age group, season, and geographic setting, with P. falciparum generally exhibiting higher SCRs than P. vivax in many locations [28, 37, 46, 47, 49, 53]. Age-stratified and reversible catalytic models consistently revealed historical declines in transmission and identified behaviourally or occupationally distinct exposure groups [28, 38, 48]. Importantly, SCR-based approaches frequently detected residual transmission in areas classified as near-elimination by microscopy or polymerase chain reaction (PCR), highlighting their added value for surveillance in low-transmission settings [29, 41, 42].

In parallel, recent studies increasingly incorporated machine learning and geospatial modelling to enhance inference from serological data (Table 1). Random forest and multi-marker algorithms achieved high accuracy in predicting recent infection and discriminating between elimination and endemic settings, as demonstrated in studies from the Philippines [48]. Integration of parasite and vector-exposure serology, as demonstrated in Myanmar, further improved fine-scale spatial characterisation of transmission foci by combining biomarkers of parasite exposure and human-vector contact, highlighting the potential value of integrated serological surveillance approaches in elimination-focused settings [52].

Discussion

Southeast Asia has been progressing towards malaria elimination. However, as countries approach the final milestones, attention has shifted to managing low-transmission settings and sustaining malaria-free areas. In this context, conventional diagnostic tools such as microscopy, RDT and PCR are increasingly recognised as having their functional limits. Although microscopy remains the gold standard for clinical case management, it frequently fails to detect low-density parasitaemia common in pre-elimination settings [10, 11, 31, 33]. While PCR, despite its high sensitivity, is often impractical for sustained deployment in remote areas due to cost and logistical constraints, particularly among high-risk populations such as indigenous communities and mobile forest workers [32, 49, 50]. In view of this, serology offers a complementary approach towards meeting the required benchmarks for verification and surveillance of malaria elimination status. Consequently, serology has emerged as a transformative approach for malaria elimination surveillance and verification, as it enables the detection and quantification of antibody responses that provide historical and longitudinal exposure profiles beyond the diagnostic window of parasite-based methods, even after blood-stage clearance [18, 19]. Serology can also be utilised to identify individuals that potentially harbour hidden hypnozoite reservoirs which are beyond the detection of existing diagnostic tools [12]. Furthermore, the shift from singleplex to multiplex serological assays has improved operational efficiency by simultaneous assessment of multiple antibody responses using reduced sample volumes and reagents, thereby facilitating large-scale micro-stratification of transmission foci for national elimination programs [32, 35, 44, 48, 54]. When integrated with mathematical modelling approaches, serological data can support the differentiation of historical and recent transmission patterns in malaria-endemic settings [52]. This enables detailed characterisation of spatial heterogeneity and species-specific transmission dynamics, and provides a robust framework for verifying the complete interruption of malaria transmission across SE Asia [38, 53].

In low-transmission settings where malaria elimination is the imminent goal, conventional parasite-based diagnostic tools often lack the resolution required to accurately characterise the true epidemiological burden. Quality assured microscopy and RDTs perform well for detecting patent infections but are limited in their ability to identify asymptomatic, submicroscopic or spatially clustered infections, leading to an underestimation of hidden reservoirs of transmission [55–57]. In SE Asia, malaria transmission has become increasingly spatially heterogeneous, with pronounced clustering across the Greater Mekong Subregion, encompassing Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar, Thailand and Vietnam, linked together by the Mekong River [58]. Although molecular detection methods can identify asymptomatic and submicroscopic infections, they primarily capture active infections at the time of sampling, particularly in settings where infections are sparse and geographically focalised [58]. In contrast, serological approaches detect host antibody responses that can persist over time and therefore serve as markers of past or recent exposure in these settings [59]. As seroprevalence may exceed parasite prevalence in low-transmission and elimination settings, it provides a valuable tool for understanding and monitoring transmission dynamics longitudinally [18]. As countries across SE Asia progress toward malaria elimination, surveillance strategies have evolved to address challenges posed by asymptomatic infections, submicroscopic and spatial clustering, as reflected in the studies reviewed here. While earlier investigations focused on antibody responses in high-transmission settings [21–25], studies published from 2015 onwards increasingly employed serology to detect residual transmission in low-endemicity areas [32–34, 34, 43–52], revealing previously undetected transmission reservoirs. This is exemplified by studies in Indonesia, Thailand and Myanmar, where serological analyses uncovered substantial historical exposure and localised clustering in areas classified as malaria-free by conventional diagnostics [31, 38, 41, 45]. Using serological markers such as AMA-1 and MSP-1₁₉, several studies identified transmission hotspots in forest-fringe environments and indigenous communities that routine surveillance failed to detect [34, 48, 51]. This evidence highlights the value of serological surveillance in identifying residual transmission hotspots and high-risk populations that may be missed by routine diagnostics, thereby enabling more targeted interventions and surveillance activities in low-transmission settings. Additionally, the application of SCR modelling provides a robust framework for verifying transmission interruption, as demonstrated in Sri Lanka and Vietnam, where the absence of seropositivity among younger cohorts indicated cessation of transmission [37, 41]. Finally, advances in technology, including multiplex serological assays and machine-learning approaches, have enabled the simultaneous assessment of multiple Plasmodium species and the incorporation of vector exposure markers, further strengthening surveillance capacity in elimination settings [38, 48, 52].

Immunoassay platforms used in SE Asia have evolved from broad-scale assessments to more precise, species-specific surveillance tools suited to low-transmission and malaria elimination settings. Earlier studies (2001–2014) predominantly employed singleplex ELISAs to measure total IgG responses against traditional blood-stage antigens such as PfMSP-1 and PfAMA-1 [21–30]. While these conventional platforms were effective in establishing baseline seroprevalence in moderate- to high-transmission areas, they required relatively large sample volumes and were limited to screening a single antigen at a time [32, 47, 60], thereby constraining surveillance primarily to the two dominant Plasmodium species in SE Asia, P. falciparum and P. vivax. In subsequent years, the transition to high-throughput multiplex platforms and microarray technologies became pivotal for regional surveillance [31–33, 35]. The increasing burden of zoonotic P. knowlesi has posed a significant challenge to countries such as Malaysia in their pursuit of WHO-certified malaria elimination, particularly given the morphological similarity between P. falciparum and P. malariae, which compromises the diagnostic accuracy of microscopy [61]. Advances in assay design may enable the simultaneous detection of human and zoonotic Plasmodium species alongside Anopheles vector exposure markers, offering a holistic view of human-vector contact and transmission potential even when circulating parasites are absent [38, 50, 52]. In forest-fringe communities, the identification of species-specific antibody signatures associated with zoonotic malaria exposure further serves as a predictive indicator of spill-over transmission [4, 38]. Although multiplex platforms require higher initial investment compared to singleplex assays, their ability to concurrently screen multiple targets substantially reduces the cost per analyte, enhancing the sustainability of surveillance programmes [32, 35]. This efficiency supports the detection of hidden transmission reservoirs among mobile and indigenous populations and facilitates large-scale micro-stratification of risk [48, 52]. Moreover, the integration of multiplex serological data with machine-learning approaches to distinguish historical from recent exposure allows national policymakers to prioritise limited resources toward active transmission foci rather than areas reflecting residual antibody responses from past transmission, thereby enabling more precise targeting of malaria reservoirs [43, 48, 51].

Serological-based transmission modelling in the form of SCR has enabled quantification of malaria transmission intensity across the diverse and heterogeneous landscapes of SE Asia by leveraging age-stratified serological data. SCR modelling represents a specific application of the reverse catalytic model, which employs a mathematical framework to estimate infection pressure within a population. This approach estimates the frequency at which individuals within a population transition from seronegative to seropositive, reflecting cumulative exposure over time [18, 27, 29, 46, 62]. Unlike point-prevalence estimates derived from conventional diagnostics such as microscopy or PCR, which are limited to detecting active parasitaemia, SCR modelling remains informative in low-transmission and elimination settings where parasite densities are often below the limits of detection [31, 33]. Its application has enabled the identification of temporal shifts in transmission intensity, the differentiation of historical versus recent exposure and the estimation of the timing of major declines in malaria transmission [37, 41, 48]. For example, studies in Indonesia and Vietnam have demonstrated that SCR-based analyses can detect residual transmission in areas classified as malaria-free by routine surveillance [29, 37, 42]. Furthermore, SCR modelling allows comparison of transmission patterns between Plasmodium species, with elevated SCRs for P. falciparum observed in focal transmission hotspots, while P. vivax often exhibits broader and more sustained exposure patterns, likely reflecting its relapse biology [28, 33, 53]. Importantly, SCR reflects cumulative, long-term exposure and is less affected by short-term seasonal fluctuations than clinical incidence or EIR [18, 19, 49]. Collectively, these advantages position SCR modelling as a surveillance tool in low-transmission and elimination settings, as well as a robust metric for verifying the interruption of malaria transmission.

Sero-surveillance is a robust approach for mapping hidden transmission reservoirs and characterising the spatial heterogeneity of malaria. Across SE Asia, malaria transmission has become increasingly focalised, with infections clustering in remote and ecologically distinct settings such as forest-fringe ecotypes and montane regions [27, 34, 51]. Current serological evidence indicates that these clusters of seropositive individuals often comprise specific demographic and high-risk occupational groups, including military personnel, forest-dependent workers and mobile indigenous communities [36, 39, 47, 49]. Studies from Cambodia and the Philippines have demonstrated that malaria exposure in populations residing in close proximity to heavily forested areas is higher than expected, enabling the identification of localised transmission reservoirs within distinct landscapes [34, 38, 51]. This evidence supports a strategic shift from broad, population-wide surveillance toward targeted approaches focused on high-risk groups. For instance, the elevated seroprevalence observed among forest-goer cohorts in Malaysia and Thailand underscores the need for tailored interventions addressing these high-risk individuals, who may act as mobile reservoirs capable of reintroducing malaria into malaria-free areas [47, 50]. Similarly, identifying vulnerable populations such as the indigenous Orang Asli in Malaysia or forest-edge communities in Vietnam allows limited resources for case management and vector control to be strategically prioritised where they are most needed [37, 46, 49]. Additionally, serological screening combined with spatial analyses of readily accessible populations, such as clinic attendees and their companions, has demonstrated potential for predicting community-level vulnerability to localised outbreaks [42].

The application of malaria serosurveillance in SE Asia has been marked by a substantial shift from conventional singleplex ELISAs to high-throughput multiplex bead-based platforms. An increasing number of recent studies have adopted multiplex immunoassays and protein microarrays, enabling the simultaneous characterization of antibody responses against hundreds of antigens across multiple Plasmodium species, notably P. falciparum, P. vivax and the zoonotic P. knowlesi [32, 35, 38, 43]. This approach has facilitated the development of “serological signatures” that distinguish recent from historical exposure by leveraging differences in antigen-specific antibody decay rates [14, 35, 48]. In low-transmission or malaria-free settings, such signatures are particularly valuable for identifying ongoing transmission or verifying transmission interruption [63, 64]. The integration of machine-learning algorithms and advanced geospatial modelling in the analysis of these complex datasets represents a transformative advance in seroepidemiology, enabling the detection of fine-scale transmission heterogeneity beyond the resolution of conventional threshold-based analyses. For example, in the Philippines, Random Forest models applied to antibody profiles more accurately estimated recent exposure and outperformed traditional cut-off–based approaches [48]. By overcoming the limitations of fixed thresholds, these analytical frameworks reveal spatial heterogeneities that inform targeted, evidence-based elimination strategies. Another important innovation is the incorporation of Anopheles vector salivary antigens, which enables concurrent assessment of human–vector contact and parasite exposure [32, 52]. As demonstrated in Myanmar, this approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of transmission potential in highly receptive areas despite the absence of circulating parasites [52]. Moving forward, standardisation of multiplex assay panels across diverse geographical and transmission settings will be critical for improving data comparability and achieving a holistic assessment of malaria transmission in SE Asia. Additionally, the development of point-of-care serological diagnostics that eliminate the need for laboratory processing could enhance sampling in remote communities and provide timely exposure data to inform real-time control decisions. Finally, integrating these multidimensional analytical approaches with longitudinal cohort studies will improve understanding of antibody kinetics over time and further optimise the predictive power of serology for malaria elimination efforts [33, 40, 53].

In interpreting the findings of this scoping review, several methodological limitations must be considered. First, the restriction to English-language publications may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies published in national languages across SE Asia. In addition, the deliberate focus on studies conducted within SE Asian countries, together with the exclusion of extra-regional literature, limited the ability to capture broader global advancements in the application of malaria serological methods. Although comprehensive searches were conducted in PubMed, WoS and Scopus, studies published outside mainstream academic journals, including technical reports and grey literature from regional databases or national malaria control programmes, may not have been fully captured. Furthermore, because Scopus does not support combined hyphens and wildcards, the search strategy required modification for this database while remaining unchanged for PubMed and WoS. This difference may have introduced minor variations in search sensitivity across platforms. Finally, in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a formal risk-of-bias assessment or methodological quality appraisal was not performed. Consequently, the findings of this review reflect the breadth and scope of the existing literature rather than the methodological rigour of individual studies.

Conclusion

The findings synthesised in this scoping review underscore the pivotal shift in the role of seroepidemiology as a cornerstone of malaria elimination efforts in SE Asia. As the region advances toward the goal of malaria elimination by 2030, serology has evolved from a tool for broad prevalence mapping into a refined surveillance approach capable of supporting micro-stratification and transmission verification in settings approaching elimination. By enabling the identification of residual transmission reservoirs and high-risk populations in a cost-efficient manner, serological surveillance provides, the high-resolution required for targeted, localised intervention strategies in areas with low-transmission intensity. Moreover, while many studies continue to utilize singleplex assays to monitor multi-species transmission dynamics, the integration of multiplex platforms and machine-learning approaches represents a significant advancement in surveillance efforts. When integrated with SCR modelling, serological data can further distinguish between historical and active transmission, allowing malaria control programmes to strategically allocate limited resources to transmission foci requiring intensified intervention. Despite these advances, important challenges remain, including the absence of standardized serological thresholds, the influence of long-lived antibody responses on transmission estimates and confounding cross-reactivity between Plasmodium species. Addressing these limitations will require prioritising the development of standardised antigen panels and field-deployable point-of-care serological diagnostics. Embedding these innovations within longitudinal study designs will enhance understanding of spatiotemporal antibody dynamics and the evolution of population-level immunity. Ultimately, when integrated with existing surveillance systems, serology represents an indispensable tool for uncovering hidden malaria transmission pockets and for verifying the cessation of transmission, thereby supporting the evidence required to confirm malaria elimination across SE Asia.
