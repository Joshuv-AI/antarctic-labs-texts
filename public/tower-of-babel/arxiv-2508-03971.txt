1.
Introduction

We recall that an overpartition
[
3
]
is a partition in which the first occurrence of each part may be overlined. Garvan and Jennings-Shaffer
[
5
]
introduced the function
\overline{spt2}(n)
, which is the number of smallest parts in the overpartitions of
n
where the smallest part is even and is not overlined. For instance, considering the 14 overpartitions of
4
, namely

\displaystyle 4,\,\overline{4},\,3+1,\,\overline{3}+1,\,3+\overline{1},\,\overline{3}+\overline{1},\,2+2,\,\overline{2}+2,\,2+1+1,

\displaystyle\overline{2}+1+1,\,2+\overline{1}+1,\,\overline{2}+\overline{1}+1,\,1+1+1+1,\,\overline{1}+1+1+1,

we see that
\overline{spt2}(4)=3
.

The study of arithmetic properties of partition functions emerged as a vibrant area of research as Ramanujan
[
9
]
proved several divisibility properties of
p(n)
, including:

\begin{array}[]{c}p(5n+4)\equiv 0\ (\bmod\ 5),\\
p(7n+5)\equiv 0\ (\bmod\ 7),\\
p(11n+6)\equiv 0\ (\bmod\ 11),\\
\end{array}

where
p(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
. After Ramanujan, significant achievements have been made by many authors for several functions that enumerate partitions satisfying certain restrictions. For example, Bringmann, Lovejoy, and Osburn
[
2
]
proved that the partition function
\overline{spt2}(n)
satisfies the following set of Ramanujan-like congruences: for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(3n)\equiv 0\pmod{3},

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(3n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{3},

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(5n+3)\equiv 0\pmod{5}.

Very recently, Jin, Liu, and Xia
[
8
]
presented some modulo 4 congruences for
\overline{spt2}(n)
, including

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(8n+3)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(1)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(16n+14)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(32n+28)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Our goal in this paper is to extend the congruences modulo
4
satisfied by
\overline{spt2}(n)
. This is done in two directions. First, we prove some new individual congruences for
\overline{spt2}(n)
. Then, we provide a number of infinite families of Ramanujan-like congruences satisfied by
\overline{spt2}(n)
. The new individual congruences are the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
n\geq 0
.

(2)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(36n+30)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(3)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(48n+34)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(4)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(64n+56)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(5)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(72n+42)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(6)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(80n+34)\equiv 0\pmod{4},

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(80n+66)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

It turns out that much more is true about
\overline{spt2}(n)
modulo 4, and our elementary approach to proving the congruences in Theorem
1.1
yields the insights needed to see this. Thus, we state the following infinite families of non–nested Ramanujan–like congruences modulo 4 satisfied by
\overline{spt2}(n)

For all
j\geq 0
and all

(8)

\displaystyle\overline{spt2}(2^{j+4}n+7\cdot 2^{j+1})

In the theorem below we present a different type of infinite family of Ramanujan-like congruence for
\overline{spt2}(n)

Theorem 1.3

Let

\displaystyle=\overline{spt2}\left(2^{j+4}5n+17\cdot 2^{j+1}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4}

by the induction hypothesis, which completes the proof of (
12
).

Proof of Theorem

Using Lemma

, it follows from (

) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{spt2}(32n+24)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}q^{k(k+1)+2l(l+1)}\pmod{4}.

Note that
n=k(k+1)+2l(l+1)
is equivalent to writing
4n+3=(2k+1)^{2}+2(2l+1)^{2}
. Thus,
\overline{spt2}(32n+24)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
if

. Since

-2

. This implies that
