Abstract
. In the context of several complex variables, we investigate the uniqueness problem for a power of a meromorphic function that shares a value with its k k -th order directional derivative in ℂ m \mathbb{C}^{m} . Our results extend previous uniqueness theorems from the one-variable case to higher dimensions. Furthermore, we provide numerous examples to demonstrate that our results are, in certain senses, best possible.

The classical Nevanlinna value distribution theory, developed in the 1920s by Rolf Nevanlinna, investigates the distribution of values taken by meromorphic functions of a single complex variable. With the advancement of complex analysis into higher dimensions, researchers have sought to generalize Nevanlinna’s results to the setting of several complex variables. This pursuit gave rise to Nevanlinna theory in several complex variables, which examines the behavior and value distribution of holomorphic and meromorphic mappings from
\mathbb{C}^{m}
into complex manifolds or projective spaces. In recent years, Nevanlinna theory in several complex variables has become a vibrant and rapidly evolving area of research. A particularly active direction involves applying value distribution theory to the study of normal families, partial differential equations (PDEs), partial difference equations and partial differential-difference equations. Researchers increasingly employ techniques from this theory to analyze the growth, value distribution and uniqueness of meromorphic solutions to such equations. 2.
Uniqueness of meromorphic function concerning directional derivative

The question of when an entire function shares values with its derivatives-and whether this implies the function must coincide with its derivative-is a classical and extensively studied problem in value distribution theory, particularly within the framework of Nevanlinna theory. A central case of interest arises when an entire function and its first derivative share two values, counting multiplicities (CM), a scenario that has attracted considerable attention. Such uniqueness problems trace back to fundamental concerns in Nevanlinna theory about how the value-distribution properties of a function are constrained by its derivatives. A key question in this context is:
Under what conditions does an entire function coincide with its derivative if they share a certain number of values, with or without multiplicities?

Rubel and Yang
[
23
]
first considered the uniqueness of an entire function when it shares two values CM with its first derivative and proved the following

Theorem 2.A
.

[
23
]
Let
f
be a non-constant holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two distinct finite complex numbers. If
f
and
f^{(1)}
share
a
and
b
CM, then
f\equiv f^{(1)}
.

One active line of research extends the uniqueness theory of entire and meromorphic functions to the setting of several complex variables. In particular, researchers have investigated whether analogous results hold for functions in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, especially regarding their directional derivatives. In a notable contribution, Berenstein et. al.
[
2
]
(1995) established a uniqueness theorem for non-constant meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and their directional derivatives in terms of shared values. They proved the following result.

Theorem 2.B

[
2

Let

be a non-constant meromorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. If
f
and
\partial_{u}(f)
share three distinct polynomials
\alpha_{j}
or
\infty

j=1,2,3

f\equiv\partial_{u}(f)
.

Also in the same paper, Berenstein et al.

A natural question arises as to whether analogous uniqueness results hold for powers of meromorphic functions that share a value with their
k

Motivated by this inquiry, the present paper aims to generalize Theorems 2.E-2.J-originally formulated in the one-variable setting-to the framework of several complex variables. Specifically, we investigate the uniqueness properties of powers of meromorphic functions defined on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
that share a finite value (either counting multiplicities (CM) or ignoring multiplicities (IM)) with their
k

\partial_{u}^{3}(F)-\left(2\partial_{u}(\varphi)-\varphi^{2}+2\varphi+1\right)\partial_{u}(F)\not\equiv 0.

Let

\displaystyle\psi=\frac{\partial_{u}^{3}(F)-\left(2\partial_{u}(\varphi)-\varphi^{2}+2\varphi+1\right)\partial_{u}(F)}{F-1}.

(2.40)

Clearly
\psi\not\equiv 0
and by Lemma
2.1
, we get
\parallel m(r,\psi)=o(T(r,f))
. Let
\mu_{F1)}^{1}(z_{1})>0
for some
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then from (
2.34
) and (
2.40
), we have generically
\mu_{\psi}^{0}(z_{0})\geq 0
over
\text{supp}\;\mu_{F}^{1}
. Therefore using (
2.29
), we get
N(r,\psi)=0
and so
\parallel T(r,\psi)=o(T(r,f))
.
Now (
2.38
) and (
2.40
) give

\displaystyle\left(2\varphi^{2}-\partial_{u}(\varphi)+\phi\right)\partial_{u}(F)=\left(\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi\right)(F-1).

(2.41)

Since
\partial_{u}(F)\not\equiv 0
, from (
2.41
), we see that

\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi\not\equiv 0.

Let
\mu_{f1)}^{0}(z_{0})>0
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then
\mu_{\partial_{u}(F))}^{0}(z_{0})>0
and so from (
2.41

\mu^{0}_{\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi}(z_{0})>0
over
\text{supp}\;\mu_{\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi}^{0}
. Clearly first main theorem gives

\displaystyle\parallel\;N_{1)}(r,0;f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N(r,0;\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi)\leq T(r,(\psi-\partial_{u}(\phi)-(1-\varphi)\phi)+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(T(r,f))

and so from (
2.30
), we have
\parallel T(r,f)=o(T(r,f))
, which is impossible.

Sub-case 1.2.2.
Let

\displaystyle 2\varphi^{2}-\partial_{u}(\varphi)+\phi\equiv 0.

(2.42)

Assume that
\mu_{f}^{0}(z_{0})>0
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then
\mu_{\partial_{u}(F))}^{0}(z_{0})>0
and so from (
2.2
) and (
2.37
), we get respectively

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}^{2}(F)\left(2\partial_{u}^{2}(F)+\varphi\right)}(z_{0})>0\;\;\text{and}\;\;\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}^{2}(F)+\phi}(z_{0})>0

(2.43)

over
\text{supp}\;\mu_{f}^{0}
.
We claim that

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{2\partial_{u}^{2}(F)+\varphi}(z_{0})>0.

(2.44)

Note that

\partial_{u}^{2}(F)=2(\partial_{u}(f))^{2}+2f\partial_{u}(f).

If
\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}^{2}(F)}(z_{0})>0
, then we have generically
\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}(f)}(z_{0})>0
. Clearly from (
2.28

\mu^{0}_{\varphi}(z_{0})>0
and so (
2.44
) holds. If
\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}^{2}(F)}(z_{0})=0
, then (
2.44
) holds directly from (
2.43
).

Let
\phi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\not\equiv 0
. It is clear from (
2.43
) and (
2.44
) that

\mu^{0}_{\phi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi}(z_{0})>0
holds generically. Consequently by the first main theorem, we get

\parallel\;N_{1)}(r,0;f)\leq N(r,0;\phi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi)\leq T\Big(r,\phi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\Big)+O(1)=o(T(r,f))

and so from (
2.30
), we have
\parallel T(r,f)=o(T(r,f))
, which is impossible.

Let
\phi-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\equiv 0
. Then from (
2.42
), we get

2\varphi^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\varphi-\partial_{u}(\varphi)\equiv 0
. Since
\varphi\not\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle 2\varphi=\frac{\partial_{u}(\varphi)}{\varphi}-\frac{1}{2}.

(2.45)

Again since
\varphi
is an entire function, applying Lemma
2.1
to (

), we get

\parallel\;T(r,\varphi)=m(r,\varphi)\leq m\Big(r,\frac{\partial_{u}(\varphi)}{\varphi}\Big)+O(1)=o(T(r,\varphi)),

which means that
\varphi
is a constant and so (
2.45
), we have

\varphi=-\frac{1}{4}
.
Now from (
2.28
), we get

(2\partial_{u}(F)-F)^{2}=F.

Note that

\partial_{u}(F)=2f\partial_{u}(f)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\partial_{u}^{2}(F)=2(\partial_{u}(f))^{2}+2f\partial^{2}_{u}(f).

Clearly

\displaystyle(4\partial_{u}(f)-f)^{2}=1.

(2.46)

Now from (
2.46
), we get
\partial_{u}(f)=\frac{f\pm 1}{4}
.

We consider following sub-cases.

Sub-case 1.2.2.1.
Let

Clearly

and

\displaystyle\partial_{u}^{2}(F)=\frac{1}{8}(f+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{8}f(f+1)=\frac{1}{8}(2f^{2}+3f+1).

(2.47)

Assume that
\mu_{f}^{-1}(z_{1})>0
for some
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Since

and

Sub-case 1.2.2.2.

Case 2.
Let

.
Since

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle H=\frac{\partial_{u}^{k}(F)}{F},

(2.48)

where
F=f^{n}
. We divide following two cases.

Case 1.
Let
H\not\equiv 1
. Then using first main theorem and Lemma
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\parallel\;\overline{N}(r,1;F)\leq\overline{N}\Big(r,\frac{F}{\partial_{u}^{k}(F)-F}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T\Big(r,\frac{\partial_{u}^{k}(F)-F}{F}\Big)+O(1)

(2.49)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T(r,H)+O(1)\leq N(r,H)+o(T(r,f)).

We now prove that

\displaystyle\mu^{\infty}_{H}\leq k\mu^{0}_{f,1}.

(2.50)

Note that
f^{-1}(\{0\})_{s}
is an analytic subset of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
of dimension
\leq m-2
, where
f^{-1}(\{0\})_{s}
is the set of singular points of
f^{-1}(\{0\})
. It suffices to prove (
2.50
) on
\mathbb{C}^{m}-f^{-1}(\{0\})_{s}
. Take
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}-f^{-1}(\{0\})_{s}
. Then there is a holomorphic coordinate system
\left(U;\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
of
z_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that
U\cap f^{-1}(\{0\})=\left\{z\in U\mid\varphi_{1}(z)=0\right\}
and
\varphi_{j}(z_{0})=0
for
j=1,2,\ldots,m
(see proof of Lemma 2.3
[
17
]
). Therefore biholomorphic coordinate transformation
z_{j}=z_{j}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
near
0
exists such that
z_{0}=z(0)=(z_{1}(0),\ldots,z_{m}(0))
.
So we can write
f=\varphi_{1}^{-l}\hat{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
,
where
\hat{f}
is a holomorphic function near
0
such that
\hat{f}
is zero free along the set
f^{-1}(\{0\})
.
Clearly

\displaystyle F=\varphi_{1}^{nl}\tilde{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right),

(2.51)

where
\tilde{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)=\hat{f}^{n}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
. Now for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}[1,m]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial z_{i}}=\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}=nl\varphi_{1}^{nl-1}\tilde{f}\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial z_{i}}+\varphi_{1}^{nl}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}

(2.52)

and so

\displaystyle\partial_{u}(F)=nl\varphi_{1}^{nl-1}\tilde{f}\partial_{h}(\varphi_{1})+\varphi_{1}^{nl}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}).

which means
\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}(F)}(z_{0})\leq nl-1=n\mu^{0}_{f}(z_{0})-\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0})
.
By induction, we can show

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{\partial_{u}^{k}(F)}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{0}_{f}(z_{0})-k\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0}).

(2.53)

Therefore from (
2.48
), (
2.51
) and (
2.53
), we get
\mu^{\infty}_{H}(z_{0})\leq k\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}(z_{0})
and so inequality (
2.50
) holds. Consequently

N(r,H)\leq k\overline{N}(r,0;f)
and so from (
2.49
), we have

\displaystyle\parallel\;\overline{N}(r,1;F)\leq k\overline{N}(r,0;f)+o(T(r,f)).

(2.54)

Now using (
2.54
), we get by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
that

\displaystyle n\;T(r,f)=T(r,F)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,F)+\overline{N}(r,0;f)+\overline{N}(r,1;F)+o(T(r,f))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(k+1)\overline{N}(r,0;f)+o(T(r,f)),

which is impossible, since
n\geq k+2
.

Case 2.
Let
H\equiv 1
. Then
F\equiv\partial_{u}^{k}(F)
. Remaining part of the proof of Theorem
2.2
follows from the proof of Case 2 of Theorem
2.1
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let
G=\partial_{u}^{k}(F)
.
We consider following cases.

Case 1.
Let
F-1
and
G-1
be linearly independent. By Corollary 1.40
[
7a
]
, there is
i\in\mathbb{Z}[1,m]
such that the Wronskian determinant
W\not\equiv 0
.
Let

\displaystyle S=\frac{W}{(F-1)(G-1)}=\frac{\partial_{z_{i}}(G)}{G-1}-\frac{\partial_{z_{i}}(F)}{F-1}.

(2.55)

Define

I=I_{F}\cup I_{G}\;\;\text{and}\;\;S=\sideset{}{{}_{a\in\{0,1,\infty\}}}{\bigcup}(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{F}^{a})_{s}\cup(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{G}^{a})_{s}.

Clearly
\dim(I\cup S)\leq m-2
.
First we prove that
(n-k-1)\mu^{0}_{f,1}\leq\mu^{0}_{S}
. It is sufficient to it on
\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
.
Take
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
. Let
l=\mu_{f}^{0}(z_{0})>0
. Then (
2.52
) gives

\mu^{0}_{\partial_{z_{i}}(F)}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{0}_{f}(z_{0})-1.

Again from (
2.53
), we get

\mu^{0}_{G}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{0}_{f}(z_{0})-k\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0})

and so

\mu^{0}_{\partial_{z_{i}}(G)}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{0}_{f}(z_{0})-(k+1)\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0}).

Therefore from (
2.55
), we have generically
(n-k-1)\mu^{0}_{f,1}\leq\mu^{0}_{S}
. Consequently

\displaystyle(n-k-1)\overline{N}(r,0;f)\leq N(r,0;S).

(2.56)

Next we prove that

\displaystyle\mu^{\infty}_{S}\leq\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}.

(2.57)

It suffices to prove (
2.57
) on
\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
. Take
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
.

Let
l_{1}=\mu_{f}^{\infty}(z_{0})>0
. Since
z_{0}\notin S
, there is a holomorphic coordinate system
\left(U;\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
of
z_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
such that
U\cap\operatorname{supp}\mu_{f}^{\infty}=\left\{z\in U\mid\varphi_{1}(z)=0\right\}
and
\varphi_{j}(z_{0})=0
for
j=1,2,\ldots,m
. A biholomorphic coordinate transformation
z_{j}=z_{j}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
near
0
exists such that
z_{0}=(z_{1}(0),\ldots,z_{m}(0))
and
f=\varphi_{1}^{-l_{1}}\hat{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
, where
\hat{f}
is a holomorphic function near
0
and zero free along the set
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{f}^{\infty}
. Clearly

\displaystyle F=\varphi_{1}^{-nl_{1}}\tilde{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right),

(2.58)

where
\tilde{f}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)=\hat{f}^{n}\left(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\right)
.
Now for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}[1,m]
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(F)=\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}=\frac{nl_{1}}{\varphi_{1}^{nl_{1}+1}}\tilde{f}\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial z_{i}}+\frac{1}{\varphi_{1}^{nl_{1}}}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}

(2.59)

and so

\displaystyle\partial_{u}(F)=\frac{nl_{1}}{\varphi_{1}^{nl+1}}\tilde{f}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\frac{1}{\varphi_{1}^{nl_{1}}}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}),

which means
\mu^{\infty}_{\partial_{u}(F)}(z_{0})\leq nl_{1}+1=n\mu^{\infty}_{f}(z_{0})+\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}(z_{0})
.
By induction, we can show

\displaystyle\mu^{\infty}_{G}(z_{0})=\mu^{\infty}_{\partial_{u}^{k}(F)}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{\infty}_{f}(z_{0})+k\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}(z_{0}).

(2.60)

Consequently

\displaystyle\mu^{\infty}_{\partial_{i}(G)}(z_{0})\leq n\mu^{\infty}_{f}(z_{0})+(k+1)\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}(z_{0}).

(2.61)

Now using (
2.58
)-(
2.61
) to (
2.55
), we have
\mu^{\infty}_{S}\leq\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}
and so the inequality (
2.57
) holds.

Let
\mu_{F}^{1}(z_{0})>0
. Since
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{F}^{1}=\operatorname{supp}\mu_{G}^{1}
, we have
\mu_{G}^{1}(z_{0})>0
.
By the given condition, we get
\mu^{1}_{F}(z_{0})=\mu^{1}_{G}(z_{0})
. Then (
2.55
) gives
\mu^{\infty}_{S}(z_{0})=0
and so the inequality (
2.57

Consequently from (

N(r,S)\leq\overline{N}(r,f)
.
On the other hand using Lemma
2.1
to (

\displaystyle\parallel\;(n-k-1)N(r,0;f)\leq N(r,0;S)\leq T(r,S)+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(r,S)+N(r,S)+O(1)

(2.62)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,f)+o(T(r,f)).

Clearly
H\not\equiv 1
, where
H
is defined as in (
2.48
). We claim that the following inequality

\displaystyle\mu^{\infty}_{H}\leq k\mu^{\infty}_{f,1}+k\mu^{0}_{f,1}

(2.63)

holds. Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

First we suppose
\mu_{f}^{0}(z_{0})>0
. Then from the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we have
\mu^{\infty}_{H}(z_{0})\leq k\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0})
over
\text{supp}\;\mu^{0}_{f}
and so
\mu^{\infty}_{H}(z_{0})\leq k\mu^{0}_{f,1}(z_{0})

Next we suppose
l_{1}=\mu_{f}^{\infty}(z_{0})>0
. Therefore from (

\displaystyle\parallel\;\overline{N}(r,1;F)\leq k\overline{N}(r,f)+k\overline{N}(r,0;f)+o(T(r,f)).

(2.64)

Now from (

\displaystyle\parallel\;\overline{N}(r,1;F)\leq k\Big(1+\frac{1}{n-k-1}\Big)\overline{N}(r,f)+o(T(r,f)).

\displaystyle\parallel\;n\;T(r,f)\leq(k+1)\Big(1+\frac{1}{n-k-1}\Big)T(r,f)+o(T(r,f)),

Case 2.

share

Proof of Theorem

.
