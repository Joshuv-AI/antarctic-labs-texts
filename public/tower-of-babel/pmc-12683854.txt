Artifact reduction and diagnostic value of monoenergetic reconstructions from split-filter and dual-layer spectral detector dual-energy CT in early gastric cancer with titanium clip localization
Authors: Huanhuan Li, Chao Chen, Lili Wang, Min Zhang, Zhuang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMetal clip artifacts impair CT evaluation of early-stage gastric cancer (EGC). While various metal artifact reduction techniques exist, their application in this specific clinical context remains insufficiently explored. We assessed virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) from two dual-energy CT (DECT) systems for reducing titanium clip artifacts in abdominal CT scans.Data and MethodsFifty-one EGC patients with gastric titanium clips underwent abdominal DECT using Siemens split-filter DECT (sfDECT) or Philips dual-layer spectral detector CT (DLSCT). Composite images (120 kVp-equivalent image) and VMIs (ranging from 40 to 140 keV) were generated for each patient. Background

Metal artifacts in CT imaging often manifest as bright and dark streaks that obscure anatomical details, complicating clinical evaluations [1]. To mitigate artifacts introduced by metallic implants, several advancements are available, including hardware modifications, optimization of imaging protocols, post-processing enhancements, and the application of advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence and deep learning [2]. A commonly employed method is projection-based metal artifact reduction (MAR), which reduces artifacts by interpolating and replacing affected regions within the projection domain [3]. Additionally, DECT-derived VMIs simulate high-energy monoenergetic X-ray beams, allowing for the effective separation of metal and soft tissue signals without necessitating increased tube voltage, thus minimizing artifacts [4].

Extensive research has demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing high keV VMIs to reduce metal artifacts in most DECT systems [5–10]. However, previous studies mostly focus on larger volumetric metal implants in bones and joints, such as hip or knee replacements, and implants in limb bones [11–15], as well as moderate-sized metallic artifacts from spinal fixation screws [8, 16].

In EGC, characterized by small and superficial lesions, accurate intraoperative tumor localization presents unique challenges. Titanium clips are frequently employed during endoscopy to ensure precise localization, facilitate complete tumor resection, and reduce the impact on surrounding healthy tissues [17, 18]. These clips, however, can introduce artifacts in subsequent CT imaging, adversely affecting image quality and diagnostic efficacy. To date, few studies have specifically addressed artifact reduction in CT scans of titanium clips used for early EGC localization. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of VMIs from sfDECT and DLSCT in mitigating artifacts caused by localization titanium clips. Additionally, the study seeks to assess the quality of VMIs across various keV levels to determine the optimal keV for lesion visualization and explore the diagnostic value of optimized monoenergetic imaging in assessing the depth of infiltration in EGC.

Materials and methods

Patient selection for study inclusion

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, which exempted the requirement for informed patient consent. We included patients initially diagnosed with EGC at our outpatient clinic from September 2020 to June 2021 (using sfDECT), as well as those diagnosed from November 2023 to July 2024 (using DLSCT). Each of these patients underwent titanium clip localization (Hangzhou Anjiesi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Model AG-51042–1950-135–16) during gastroscopy, followed by contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans using a DECT scanner. Exclusion criteria were: (1) absence of post-titanium clip localization CT examinations (e.g., only pre-gastroscopy CT or surgery based on external CT); (2) CT scans not meeting standards (e.g., absence of dual-energy CT, dual-energy mode, or missing triphasic enhancement scans); (3) individual factors such as insufficient gastric water filling, lesion not visible on CT, or presence of other metal implants in the abdominal region. After applying exclusion criteria, a total of 51 patients were ultimately included in this study. The patient selection workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Dual-energy CT imaging

All 51 participants adhered to a fasting period of 6–8 hours before their CT examination and ingested 500–1000 mL of warm water prior to imaging. They subsequently underwent contrast-enhanced DECT scans using either the SOMATOM Definition Edge (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) or the IQon Spectral CT (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The Siemens SOMATOM Definition Edge utilizes a gold-tin filter in front of the X-ray tube output, splitting the beam into high- and low-energy components for dual-energy imaging [1], while the Philips IQon Spectral CT employs a dual-layer detector architecture that separates high and low energy levels simultaneously, thus providing spatially and temporally consistent data within a single scan without the need for specialized scanning presets [19].

Acquisition parameters for the Siemens scanner included 120 kVp tube voltage, an 425 average reference mAs, collimation size of 64 × 0.6 mm, a rotation time of 0.33 s, and a 0.3 pitch. The Philips IQon employed similar settings: 120 kVp, 285 reference mAs, 64 × 0.625 mm collimation, 0.33 s rotation time, and 0.797 pitch, with both setups utilizing automatic tube current modulation.

Each patient received an Iomeprol injection (Borke Xinyi Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd. , Shanghai, China) at a dosage of 1.5 ml/kg and an infusion rate of 3 ml/s. Enhanced CT scans covered three phases: the arterial phase (timed by peak enhancement of the celiac trunk), the portal venous phase 20 seconds later, and the equilibrium phase at 120 seconds post-contrast administration, capturing the entire stomach region from the diaphragm to the anterior superior iliac spine.

Image post-processing

The initial sfDECT data were reconstructed using the sinogram affirmed iterative reconstruction (SAFIRE) algorithm with Q30f kernels, a slice thickness of 1.5 mm, and an interslice spacing of 1 mm. Spectral separation was achieved during CT scanning via source filtration, generating high- and low-kVp datasets that were transmitted to the post-processing workstation (syngo.via VB30A_HF04, Siemens Healthineers). Composite images were generated using the “CT Dual Energy” workflow, which performs reconstruction by a linear blend of 20% high-energy and 80% low-energy data, representing a weighted average of the two spectra. VMIs were reconstructed using the “Mono+” application with predefined settings (resolution = 6, min [HU] = −950, max [HU] = 3071), generated at 10 keV increments across an energy range of 40 to 140 keV. All images were displayed using the default abdominal window width and level of 400/50 HU.

Dual-energy raw data acquired from DLSCT, identified as Spectral based images, were transferred to the Philips Intellispace Portal workstation. Using the Spectral Diagnostic Suite software, VMIs were reconstructed at energy levels from 40 to 140 keV with 10 keV increments, maintaining both slice thickness and interval at 1 mm. Composite images equivalent to 120 kVp were reconstructed with the iterative algorithm iDose4 level3. These images are created directly from the combined signals of the dual-layer detector, where the top and bottom layers simultaneously capture low-and high-energy components, respectively. This method inherently preserves the full polychromatic spectrum. All images were displayed using the default abdominal window width and level of 400/50 HU.

Objective quantitative image assessment

Objective quantitative image assessment was conducted by a radiologist with 15 years of experience in abdominal diagnostics using Siemens syngo.via and Philips Intellispace Portal post-processing workstations. Analysis focused on portal venous phase images, evaluating 11 VMIs in 10 keV increments (from 40 to 140 keV) along with one set of composite images. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined on the composite images and subsequently either copied onto saved VMIs (sfDECT) or displayed in real-time across various keV VMIs (DLSCT) within a single interface, ensuring consistent ROI placement. For each patient, five sets of ROIs were created, with each set placed twice in distinct locations within the target area to obtain average measurements. Care was taken to avoid areas with gas, calcifications, vascular structures, and the parenchyma necrotic. When assessing smaller lesions or the thin gastric wall, images were magnified to minimize inclusion of extraneous tissue outside the target area.

ROI1 was placed on the lesion affected by titanium clip artifacts, which may involve hypodense, hyperdense, or mixed-density artifacts. ROI2 was positioned on the normal gastric wall (unaffected by the titanium clip). ROI3 was set in the water-filled region of the gastric cavity (unaffected by the titanium clip). ROI4 was located within the liver parenchyma at the same level as the titanium clip (also unaffected). ROI5 was placed in the abdominal aorta at the same level as the titanium clip (unaffected by the titanium clip).

Artifacts were quantified using the standard deviation of CT values (referred to as “Noise”), which was automatically extracted from the lesion ROI impacted by titanium clip artifacts. Furthermore, the CNR were calculated for both the lesion and liver tissue as follows: CNR (lesion) = |mean CT value of the lesion - mean CT value of the normal gastric wall|/standard deviation of CT value in the gastric cavity water [19]; CNR (liver) = |mean CT value of the liver - mean CT value of the abdominal aorta|/standard deviation of CT value in the abdominal aorta. To ensure consistency with subjective vascular contrast scoring, the liver CNR was calculated with the abdominal aorta in the portal venous phase as the reference background, following established formulae from previous studies [20].

Subjective image quality assessment

Subjective evaluation of image quality was independently conducted by two radiologists with 10 and 15 years of expertise in abdominal imaging, respectively. Each patient underwent 12 CT image reconstructions, encompassing composite images and VMIs spanning 40 to 140 keV, all acquired during the portal venous phase. This resulted in a dataset of 612 image reconstructions for the 51 enrolled patients, each assigned a randomized identifier (1–612). Daily, the radiologists independently assessed 20 randomly selected image sets, blinded to the patients’ clinical history and VMI details. Additionally, a third radiologist, with two years of experience in abdominal imaging, collated patient data and recorded evaluation results.

Two independent evaluators used a five-point Likert scale (Table 1) to rate the overall image quality, artifact presence, lesion visibility, and vascular contrast. Higher scores indicated better image quality.

T staging methods for pathology and CT

Pathological staging of tumor invasion depth adhered to the 8th edition of the AJCC stomach cancer manual as follows: T1: Tumor invades the mucosa, muscularis mucosae, or submucosa; T2: Tumor invades the muscularis propria; T3: Tumor penetrates the subserosa without involvement of adjacent organs or the serosa; T4: Tumor invades the serosa or adjacent structures/organs [21]. CT staging primarily relied on established methodologies validated in prior research to determine T staging [22, 23].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics characterized the distribution of quantitative image data. Shapiro-Wilk testing revealed that VMIs and composite image datasets exhibited predominantly non-normal distributions, as did subjective image scores. Consequently, median values with interquartile ranges were employed to present both objective quantitative parameters and subjective scores. Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, supplemented by pairwise comparisons with the Conover method. The Mann-Whitney U test compared parameters between the two CT modalities.

Consistency between T-staging modalities—pathology versus imaging (composite and VMIs at optimal keV)—was evaluated using the McNemar-Bowker test and Kappa statistics. The linear weighted Kappa coefficient evaluated the agreement between the two radiologists’ subjective scores. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: 0.00–0.20 (poor agreement), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (good), and 0.81–1.00 (excellent) [24]. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. MedCalc (v22.0; MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium), SPSS (v26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA),and GraphPad Prism (v10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) facilitated all analyses and statistical graphing.

Results

Study population

From an initial cohort of 237 gastric cancer patients undergoing titanium clip localization, 186 patients were excluded due to various reasons, leaving 51 eligible participants. Among these, there were 29 males and 22 females, with a mean age of 58 years (range: 37–84 years). Tumor sites included the gastric fundus (18 cases), body (21 cases), and antrum (12 cases). Surgical interventions comprised endoscopic resection (13 cases), laparoscopic resection (21 cases), and open surgery (17 cases). Pathological analysis confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma in all 51 patients, with invasion restricted to the mucosa or submucosa in 31 cases, extending into the muscularis in 18 cases, and the subserosa in 2 cases. Local lymph node metastases were observed in 3 patients, while 48 exhibited no lymph node involvement. Notably, no cases of distant metastases were identified.

Quantitative assessment results

In the quantitative analysis, the area measurements of the ROIs were as follows: ROI1 ranged from 0.2 to 0.5 cm2, ROI2 from 0.1 to 0.3 cm2, ROI3 from 0.5 to 1.2 cm2, ROI4 from 0.5 to 1.5 cm2, and ROI5 from 0.6 to 1.5 cm2.

The artifact noise attributed to titanium clips decreased with increasing keV in both sfDECT and DLSCT scans (Figs. 2 and 5). In sfDECT, the artifacts observed in composite images were fewer than those seen at 40–60 keV VMIs but greater than those at 80–140 keV VMIs (p < 0.05, Table 2, Fig. 5). Similarly, in DLSCT, composite images demonstrated fewer artifacts compared to 40 and 50 keV VMIs, but more than those at 80–140 keV VMIs (p < 0.05, Table 3, Fig. 5). CNR decreased with higher keV in both lesions and liver tissues, with liver tissue showing a more noticeable decline, likely due to the absence of metal clip artifacts. (Tables 2 and 3).

Subjective rating results

Interobserver agreement between the two radiologists was strong, with Kappa values ranging from 0.72 to 0.82.

VMIs at 70–90 keV for sfDECT and 90–110 keV for DLSCT achieved favorable overall scores, with no significant differences from composite images (p > 0.05). Artifact ratings improved with increasing keV in both CT types. In sfDECT, scores for 80–140 keV VMIs exceeded those for composite images. Similarly, in DLSCT, scores for 90–140 keV VMIs were superior to composite images. However, for sfDECT VMIs above 90 keV and DLSCT VMIs above 100 keV, further increases in keV did not yield significant artifact reduction (p ≥ 0.05, Tables 4 and 5). Lesion visualization scores peaked at 70 and 80 keV VMIs for sfDECT and at 90 and 100 keV VMIs for DLSCT, declining toward lower and higher keV extremes (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Vascular contrast scores decreased with increasing keV in both sfDECT and DLSCT, with composite images consistently outperforming 90–140 keV VMIs (p < 0.05, Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

Metallic implants frequently generate CT artifacts through mechanisms such as beam hardening, photon starvation, scattering, and edge effects, all of which degrade image quality and diagnostic accuracy [2]. To address these challenges, strategies have been developed across data acquisition, image reconstruction, and post-processing, with MAR algorithms and VMIs from DECT being the most widely applied. MAR algorithms are particularly effective in mitigating severe artifacts from large, high-density implants, thereby improving image clarity and soft tissue delineation [26, 27]. However, they may also introduce secondary artifacts [16, 28], such as blurring or overcorrection [1, 29]. In contrast, high-energy VMIs effectively reduce mild to moderate artifacts from smaller metallic objects while maintaining diagnostic quality. Their limitations include reduced efficacy with large implants [2], and diminished soft tissue contrast at higher keV levels [3, 30].

Recent studies highlight the complementary potential of combining MAR and VMIs, showing enhanced artifact reduction and improved lesion visibility [11, 12, 26, 31]. Nonetheless, their performance varies depending on implant size, shape, and composition [16, 32, 33]. Selles M et al. recommend using VMIs alone for small to medium-sized implants—such as intramedullary nails, spinal screws, and cardiovascular implants—which typically produce only mild to moderate artifacts [2].

Metal clips used for endoscopic localization of gastric cancer, primarily composed of titanium (density 4.5 g/cm3, atomic number 22), are small to medium-density metallic objects that generate mild to moderate artifacts, potentially impairing lesion evaluation. Our study showed that VMIs at ≥ 80 keV significantly reduced clip-induced artifacts, as evidenced by decreased noise in lesion regions (Tables 2, 3). However, higher keV levels reduced CNR and vascular contrast, compromising diagnostic quality. Importantly, medium-energy VMIs 70, 80 keV for sfDECT; 90, 100 keV for DLSCT achieved optimal balance—effectively reducing artifacts while preserving lesion visibility. This was supported by peak subjective scores at these keV levels (Figs. 3–5, Tables 4, 5), which in turn enhanced radiologists’ diagnostic confidence.

While prior DECT studies mainly focused on low-keV VMIs for lesion detection—Liu et al. and Shi et al. found that 40 keV VMIs improved detection rates and diagnostic efficiency for EGC [19, 34]—our analysis emphasized artifact suppression, identifying medium-energy VMIs as the optimal compromise between artifact reduction and diagnostic accuracy. Notably, T-staging accuracy with medium keV VMIs significantly exceeded that of composite images (Table 7), showing good agreement with pathological staging, which supports their potential clinical utility for preoperative planning. Although composite imaging is more prone to artifacts, it provides superior tissue contrast beyond the lesion area. Thus, our findings support a combined clinical approach: medium-energy VMIs for targeted lesion assessment paired with composite images for broader tissue evaluation, to maximize diagnostic accuracy in EGC cases.

Several studies on small- to medium-sized implants report comparable optimal energy ranges. Dong Y et al. [8] found that fast kilovoltage-switching CT reduced pedicle-screw artifacts most effectively at 120 keV, while Neuhaus V et al. [9], using the same Philips IQon system as ours, reported optimal suppression at 140 keV. Große et al. [35] similarly noted significant artifact reduction from dental implants at energies ≥90 keV, aligning with our quantitative findings for titanium clips in the 80–140 keV range. Looking ahead, photon-counting detector CT (PCD−CT) offers promising improvements in metal artifact reduction through direct photon-energy discrimination. Risch et al. [36] showed that VMIs above 70 keV combined with MAR in PCD-CT significantly outperformed standard 70 keV images in reducing dental implant artifacts. Similarly, recent research [37] indicates that even without MAR, VMI reconstructions around 70 keV provide an balance between dental metal artifact suppression and image quality, further underscoring PCD-CTs potential for optimizing artifact suppression in small to medium-sized metallic implants.

A comparison of the two DECT scanners—each based on distinct dual-energy principles and both utilizing iterative reconstruction—revealed broadly similar trends in artifact reduction. Siemens’ sfDECT employed the “Mono+” mode, combining low-noise high-energy and high-contrast low-energy reconstructions to optimize material separation and image quality. In our study, sfDECT VMIs showed fewer artifacts at low to mid keV levels compared to DLSCT, potentially due in part to slice thickness differences 1.5 mm for sfDECT vs. 1.0 mm for DLSCT. However, image noise is influenced by multiple factors that are difficult to control in retrospective designs. Notably, data from each scanner were analyzed independently, ensuring these differences did not affect overall results. Additionally, DLSCT demonstrated a higher overall CNR, particularly in the liver, likely reflecting its unique imaging principles and reconstruction algorithms.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design limited control over certain parameters, such as slice thickness, and MAR reconstructions were not consistently available for analysis. Second, the evaluation included only two DECT systems Siemens sfDECT and Philips DLSCT, so the findings may not extend to other technologies like dual-source or rapid kV-switching CT. Lastly, the small sample size and use of titanium alloy clips from a single vendor may not reflect broader clinical variability. Future prospective studies should investigate the combined utility of medium-energy VMIs and MAR across different CT platforms and implant types to better assess their clinical value.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that medium-energy VMIs from DECT effectively balance artifact reduction and lesion visibility in EGC with titanium clips, enhancing diagnostic efficiency for T staging compared to composite imaging. This approach represents a practical imaging solution worthy of consideration for integration into diagnostic workflows.
