Dedifferentiation of Metastatic Recurrent Chondrosarcoma of the Right Distal Femur Into High-Grade Sarcoma Favouring Leiomyosarcoma: A Report of a Rare Case With Review of Literature
Authors: S Vishnu Vardhan Yedla, Prakash Mishra, Shashidhar V Karpurmath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma (DDCS) is a rare and aggressive biphasic bone malignancy that accounts for a small but distinct proportion of all chondrosarcomas and primary malignant bone tumours. The high-grade dedifferentiated component most commonly resembles undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma or osteosarcoma. The prognosis is dismal, with low five-year overall survival rates that are further reduced in the presence of metastatic disease. Local recurrence occurs in a substantial proportion of patients even after wide-margin resection, and distant metastasis, most frequently to the lungs, is common. Systemic therapy offers limited benefit, with poor overall response rates and no durable remissions with currently available doxorubicin-based, ifosfamide-based, or targeted regimens. Leiomyosarcomatous dedifferentiation within DDCS is exceptionally rare, with only a handful of cases documented worldwide. We report a case in which a patient who underwent limb-salvage surgery for DDCS of the right distal femur in 2023 re-presented two years later with extensive metastatic local recurrence requiring palliative hip disarticulation. Immunohistochemical profiling of the resected specimen demonstrated a smooth muscle differentiation profile consistent with high-grade sarcoma favouring leiomyosarcoma arising within the dedifferentiated component, a finding with important implications for prognostication and systemic therapy selection. This case highlights the critical role of comprehensive tissue sampling, immunohistochemical subtyping, and multidisciplinary management in diagnostically complex bone sarcomas.

Introduction

Chondrosarcomas represent the second most common primary malignant tumour of bone overall, accounting for approximately 20% of all primary bone malignancies [1], although they become the most common primary bone sarcoma in patients over 60 years of age. They constitute a heterogeneous group of neoplasms with varying morphological subtypes, including conventional (central, periosteal, and peripheral), clear cell, mesenchymal, and dedifferentiated types [2]. Among these, dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma (DDCS) is defined by a distinctive biphasic histology comprising a low-grade cartilaginous component juxtaposed, often sharply, with a high-grade, non-chondrogenic sarcomatous component [2]. The term “dedifferentiation” refers to the phenomenon whereby a well-differentiated, low-grade tumour cell acquires the molecular and morphological features of a completely different, high-grade malignancy, in effect, losing its original identity and transforming into a more aggressive and biologically distinct cancer. In DDCS, this means that a cartilage-forming tumour (chondrosarcoma) gives rise to a second, non-cartilaginous high-grade sarcoma within the same lesion, resulting in a tumour with two distinct components that behave very differently from one another and together confer a far worse prognosis than either component alone.

DDCS accounts for approximately 9%-11% of all chondrosarcomas and 1%-2% of all primary malignant bone tumours [2,3]. It was first characterised by Dahlin and Beabout in 1971 [4]. The condition predominantly affects patients in their fifth to seventh decades of life, with a slight male predilection, and most commonly arises in the femur, pelvis, and humerus [3,5]. Despite advances in surgical technique and multimodal therapy, the prognosis remains dismal, with reported five-year overall survival rates ranging from 7% to 24% [6,7]. Local recurrence and distant metastasis, most frequently to the lungs, are common even after wide-margin resection [8].

The high-grade dedifferentiated component of DDCS most commonly exhibits the morphology of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) or osteosarcoma, with fibrosarcoma and angiosarcoma representing less frequent patterns [5,9]. Leiomyosarcomatous differentiation within DDCS is considered an exceptionally rare variant, and only a very limited number of cases have been reported in the world literature to date [10,11]. The rarity of this entity, combined with its diagnostic complexity and aggressive clinical behaviour, renders each documented case of considerable academic and clinical importance.

We present a case of metastatic recurrent DDCS of the right distal femur, in which histopathological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of the surgical specimen following palliative hip disarticulation confirmed leiomyosarcomatous differentiation within the dedifferentiated component. To our knowledge, this is among only a few documented cases of DDCS with leiomyosarcomatous transformation arising in the context of metastatic recurrence, making it a particularly instructive case.

Case presentation

Initial presentation and diagnosis (2023)

A woman in her fifth decade of life presented in 2023 with a six-month history of gradually increasing pain and swelling in the right knee, with symptom intensity escalating over the preceding three months, associated with progressive difficulty in ambulation. Her past medical history was notable for hypertension managed with amlodipine 5 mg once daily. Her Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 1 at presentation. Local examination revealed a diffuse bony swelling with tenderness over the anterior aspect of the distal femur, with full range of motion at the knee and no neurovascular deficits. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the right knee and femur revealed a lesion in the metaphysis of the distal femur with altered signal intensity, cortical erosion at multiple foci, periosteal reaction, and an associated soft tissue component, initially raising the suspicion of osteosarcoma. A partial tear of the anterior cruciate ligament and mild joint effusion were also noted. Multiplanar imaging (axial, coronal, and sagittal sequences) was performed at the time of initial evaluation and would have been ideal for full appreciation of tumour extent; however, the original MRI DICOM data from both the 2023 and 2025 evaluations were no longer retrievable from institutional archives at the time of manuscript preparation and could not be reproduced. The treating authors confirm the imaging findings described above as documented in the contemporaneous clinical record. Similarly, 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging performed during the 2025 metastatic recurrence workup, which demonstrated widespread disease and would have been informative for assessing metastatic burden, was also unavailable for reproduction for the same reason.

Whole-body 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET-CT) was performed following injection of 4.7 mCi of 18F-FDG; the blood glucose level at the time of injection was 152 mg%, representing mild hyperglycaemia, which may have modestly suppressed FDG uptake in metabolically active lesions. The standardised uptake value (SUV) is a semi-quantitative measure of regional FDG uptake corrected for injected dose and patient body weight; it reflects the degree of metabolic activity within a lesion and is distinct from qualitative descriptions of FDG avidity, providing an objective and reproducible index for comparison across lesions and time points. The scan demonstrated a heterodense intramedullary expansile soft tissue mass with multiple calcific foci, consistent with the chondroid matrix of the tumour, involving the medulla of the distal metaphysis and extending into the diaphysis, measuring 10.3 x 2.8 cm (maximum SUV: 5.42), with erosion of the medial cortex causing cortical discontinuity and an adjacent soft tissue component measuring 2.1 x 5.8 cm (maximum SUV: 12.05) abutting the adjacent musculature with loss of fat planes (Figure 1). This metabolic heterogeneity (low FDG uptake in the cartilaginous core and markedly elevated uptake in the peripheral high-grade component) is characteristic of DDCS. Multiple low-grade FDG-avid ileocolic, right paracolic, and mesocolic lymph nodes were noted (largest: 1.0 cm, maximum SUV: 2.40), considered likely inflammatory on reporting. Importantly, no FDG-avid pulmonary nodules, no pathological mediastinal or hilar lymph nodes, and no other metabolically active lesions were identified elsewhere, confirming the absence of distant metastatic disease at the time of initial staging. The PET-CT imaging files from the original 2023 evaluation are unavailable for reproduction; the described SUV measurements and morphological features are recorded in the departmental case notes. Pre-operative core biopsy microscopy revealed lobules of cartilaginous tissue with embedded chondrocytes, areas of necrosis, haemorrhage, and fibrosis, without evidence of round cells or osteoid formation, leading to a provisional diagnosis of a chondroid neoplasm of the right distal femur. Ideally, histological images from the 2023 primary resection specimen, demonstrating the conventional DDCS morphology without leiomyosarcomatous differentiation, would have been included to allow direct side-by-side comparison with the 2025 recurrence specimen and to more clearly delineate the evolution of the dedifferentiated component. Unfortunately, the original paraffin blocks and prepared slides from the 2023 resection were not available for retrieval and rephotography at the time of manuscript preparation, as the material had been exhausted during initial diagnostic workup. This represents a recognised limitation of the present report.

The patient subsequently underwent right limb-salvage surgery on October 10, 2023, performed jointly by the surgical oncology and orthopaedic teams under spinal and epidural anaesthesia. Intra-operative findings revealed a 7 × 8 cm firm lesion involving the meta-diaphysis of the lower one-third of the femur, adherent to the vastus medialis, with the proximal extent 10.5 cm from the articular line; the joint capsule and neurovascular bundle were free of tumour. A proximal bony resection margin of 15 cm from the joint line was taken. Endoprosthetic reconstruction was performed using a RESTOR modular system comprising a 70 mm resection piece, curved intramedullary stem (11 mm), and tibial component, fixed with gentamycin-loaded antibiotic PMMA cement (40 g). The estimated blood loss was 700 mL, and one unit of packed red blood cells was transfused. The procedure was uneventful, and the patient was haemodynamically stable throughout. Post-operative histopathological examination (HPE) of the resected specimen demonstrated a grey-white cartilaginous component with mild atypia, associated with areas of high-grade spindle cell sarcoma and a mitotic rate of 5-6/10 high-power fields (HPF). This bimorphic histological picture was consistent with a diagnosis of dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma (DDCS) of the right distal one-third of the femur.

Recurrence and metastatic disease (2025)

In 2025, the patient re-presented with a 20-day history of right hip pain and complete inability to bear weight on the right lower limb. Repeat MRI of the lower limb demonstrated extensive recurrent disease. An expansile lytic lesion measuring approximately 30 × 6 × 6 cm was identified involving the entire length of the endoprosthesis within the femur, with cortical disruption and an associated enhancing soft tissue component extending along the entire right femur to the tibiofemoral joint line. Bony fragments were noted within the lesion. Proximally, the lesion extended to within 1.5 cm of the intertrochanteric line; distally, involvement reached the tibiofemoral joint line. The femoral vessels were noted in close proximity to the lesion within the adductor canal. A pathological comminuted fracture of the proximal femoral shaft was identified, with posterosuperior displacement of the distal fragment and anterior angulation of the proximal tip of the femoral prosthesis. Multiple cortical erosions and lytic lesions were noted in the proximal tibia above the tibial tuberosity. Critically, multiple enlarged enhancing lymph nodes were identified along the right inguinal, right external iliac (measuring 24 × 11 mm), and para-aortic chains just above the aortic bifurcation (measuring 12 × 8 mm), consistent with regional and distant nodal metastatic spread. Given the extensive local recurrence with pathological fracture, prosthesis displacement, and significant functional and pain burden, palliative hip disarticulation was performed. In light of the MRI-demonstrated nodal involvement, surgical staging was revised to pT2 N1 M1. The gross specimen revealed a high-grade spindle cell neoplasm admixed with bony trabeculae, with the presence of atypical cartilaginous areas, morphologically consistent with DDCS. Immunohistochemical analysis (April 2025)

Comprehensive immunohistochemical (IHC) profiling was performed on the post-operative paraffin block (HP block 1659G and RB9/2025, VIMS, Bengaluru). Microscopy confirmed a high-grade spindle cell neoplasm with atypical cartilaginous foci and a mitotic rate of 5-6/10 HPF (Figures 4, 5). The tumour cells were arranged in long, sweeping intersecting fascicles (Figure 6). The IHC panel demonstrated strong and diffuse positivity for smooth muscle actin (SMA) (Figures 7, 8), focal and weak positivity for caldesmon (Figure 9), focal positivity for desmin, negativity for S-100 and CD34, and a Ki-67 proliferative index of 60% (Figure 10).

The IHC profile, characterised by strong and diffuse SMA positivity, focal desmin and caldesmon reactivity, negativity for S-100 and CD34, and a Ki-67 of 60%, was interpreted as consistent with high-grade sarcoma favouring leiomyosarcoma, arising within the dedifferentiated component of the DDCS. The final diagnosis was dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma of the right femur, with dedifferentiated areas showing immunohistochemical features favouring leiomyosarcoma (histological stage: pT2 N1 M1).

Following hip disarticulation, the case was reviewed at the multidisciplinary tumour board. In view of the confirmed nodal metastatic disease (pT2 N1 M1), the high-grade histology, and the leiomyosarcomatous IHC profile, systemic chemotherapy was recommended. Regimens discussed included doxorubicin-based therapy as per standard high-grade bone sarcoma protocols, with acknowledgement of the theoretical rationale for gemcitabine-docetaxel given the leiomyosarcomatous differentiation. The patient was counselled regarding the palliative intent, expected response rates, and toxicity profile of available regimens. However, the patient declined systemic therapy, citing personal reasons, and opted for best supportive care. The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up, and no further clinical or survival data are available. This outcome, while limiting the completeness of the report, is itself clinically relevant in reflecting the real-world challenges of managing aggressive bone sarcomas in the metastatic setting.

Discussion

Epidemiology and clinicopathological features of DDCS

Chondrosarcoma is the second most frequent primary malignant bone tumour overall, constituting approximately 20%-25% of all bone malignancies [1], and the most common primary bone sarcoma in patients over the age of 60. Within this group, DDCS represents a distinct and particularly aggressive variant, characterised by its bimorphic histological composition [3]. The low-grade chondroid component typically resembles an enchondroma or low-grade chondrosarcoma, while the high-grade non-chondroid component demonstrates abrupt, rather than gradual, transition and is generally associated with a significantly worse prognosis than either component alone [11].

The most common skeletal sites of DDCS involvement are the femur (approximately 50%), the pelvis (25%), and the humerus, consistent with the broader anatomical distribution observed in conventional chondrosarcomas [8,9]. The present case, arising from the distal femur and progressing to involve the entire femur and proximal tibia on recurrence, fits this epidemiological pattern. From an imaging standpoint, DDCS typically manifests as a destructive lytic lesion with an associated soft tissue mass. In this case, 18F-FDG PET-CT at initial staging demonstrated the characteristic metabolic heterogeneity of DDCS: low FDG uptake in the cartilaginous core and markedly elevated SUV in the high-grade dedifferentiated peripheral component, a pattern described by several authors as radiographically suggestive of DDCS [5]. The presence of multiple calcific foci within the intramedullary mass on PET-CT further supported the chondroid nature of the tumour. Notably, the baseline staging scan confirmed the absence of distant metastatic disease, with low-grade mesenteric lymph nodes considered likely inflammatory. This stands in stark contrast to the 2025 recurrence, at which point MRI demonstrated regional and distant nodal metastases involving the right inguinal, external iliac, and para-aortic chains, reflecting the aggressive metastatic potential of DDCS with leiomyosarcomatous dedifferentiation.

The dedifferentiated component: Common variants and rare subtypes

The high-grade sarcomatous component in DDCS most commonly exhibits the morphology of UPS (reported in 37%-60% of cases) or osteosarcoma (20%-30% of cases) [5,6]. Fibrosarcoma, angiosarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma are recognised but less frequent patterns of dedifferentiation [9]. Leiomyosarcomatous differentiation, while acknowledged in case reports and reviews, remains among the rarest forms of dedifferentiation in DDCS, with the published literature containing only a very limited number of confirmed cases [10,11].

The pathogenesis of dedifferentiation remains incompletely understood. The prevailing hypothesis supports a monoclonal origin, wherein chondrogenic cells undergo catastrophic epigenetic and genomic reprogramming, resulting in the acquisition of non-cartilaginous, high-grade sarcomatous phenotypes [2]. Molecular alterations commonly identified in DDCS include mutations in IDH1 and IDH2 genes, TP53 mutations, and TERT promoter mutations [2]. These molecular events may drive the epigenetic dysregulation that facilitates dedifferentiation into heterologous lineages, including smooth muscle differentiation.

Leiomyosarcomatous dedifferentiation: Literature context and significance

Primary leiomyosarcoma of bone is in itself an exceptionally rare entity, with fewer than 100 cases documented in the literature [12]. Leiomyosarcomatous differentiation within the context of DDCS adds a further layer of rarity. In a comprehensive 2023 review by Zając et al. [2], leiomyosarcoma was acknowledged as a recognised but rare form of dedifferentiation, alongside rhabdomyosarcoma and angiosarcoma. The literature reveals only isolated case reports of DDCS with a leiomyosarcomatous component globally, a notable case being that reported by Akahane et al. [10] describing a 63-year-old man with DDCS arising from a solitary osteochondroma near the left knee, in whom the dedifferentiated component was histologically classified as leiomyosarcoma; the patient underwent above-knee amputation but died of pulmonary metastasis within two years.

The clinical significance of leiomyosarcomatous dedifferentiation lies primarily in its implications for therapy. Conventional chondrosarcoma is known to be relatively chemotherapy-resistant, a property well recognised in the literature [13]. However, dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma is generally treated as a high-grade bone sarcoma, and both doxorubicin-based and ifosfamide-based regimens, extrapolated from osteosarcoma protocols, have been employed in its systemic management [13,14]. When the dedifferentiated component demonstrates leiomyosarcomatous features, there may be a theoretical rationale for incorporating leiomyosarcoma-directed regimens such as gemcitabine-docetaxel, although this remains to be validated in this specific context [14]. A multi-institutional retrospective analysis of 74 patients with DDCS by Bui et al. [14] reported an overall response rate to systemic therapy of only 9%, with partial responses achieved with doxorubicin-cisplatin or ifosfamide combinations, and limited prolonged stable disease with pazopanib and immune checkpoint inhibitors. This underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies in DDCS, and the characterisation of specific dedifferentiated subtypes, such as leiomyosarcomatous, may ultimately inform personalised treatment selection.

Role of immunohistochemistry in diagnosis

Accurate characterisation of the dedifferentiated component in DDCS is critically dependent on comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis. In the present case, the IHC panel was central to establishing the leiomyosarcomatous lineage of the high-grade component. Smooth muscle actin (SMA) is widely regarded as a sensitive, if not entirely specific, marker for smooth muscle differentiation; the strong and diffuse positivity observed in this case supports a myogenic phenotype [15]. H-caldesmon, the high-molecular-weight caldesmon isoform expressed selectively in differentiated smooth muscle, offers greater diagnostic specificity than SMA alone, reliably separating leiomyosarcoma from morphological mimics such as synovial sarcoma and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) [15]. Its focal and weak reactivity in this case, alongside strong SMA positivity, is best interpreted as reflecting the partially dedifferentiated state of the neoplasm rather than excluding smooth muscle lineage. Desmin, another smooth muscle and skeletal muscle marker, was focally positive, consistent with the leiomyosarcomatous interpretation. The negativity of S-100 effectively excluded neural crest-derived tumours (e.g., MPNST, melanoma), and CD34 negativity argued against solitary fibrous tumour or vascular neoplasm [15].

The Ki-67 proliferative index of 60% is in keeping with high-grade sarcoma and is consistent with the aggressive clinical course observed. Published series have documented that higher Ki-67 indices correlate with shortened survival in DDCS [5]. Taken together, the IHC constellation (SMA strong/diffuse positive, caldesmon focal/weak positive, desmin focal positive, S-100 negative, and CD34 negative) represents the defining immunophenotype of leiomyosarcoma, as established in the published literature [15] and confirmed in subsequent studies. The pattern here is consistent with a high-grade, partially dedifferentiated leiomyosarcoma arising within the context of a recurrent DDCS. It is important to note, however, that the diagnosis is rendered as leiomyosarcoma-favouring rather than definitive. A truly confirmatory diagnosis of leiomyosarcoma would ideally require strong and diffuse co-expression of both SMA and H-caldesmon; in this case, caldesmon reactivity was only focal and weak, which, while consistent with partial dedifferentiation, introduces interpretive uncertainty.

Molecular studies, including MDM2 and CDK4 amplification analysis to exclude dedifferentiated liposarcoma, and next-generation sequencing for leiomyosarcoma-associated alterations, were not performed, representing a recognised limitation. The authors maintain that the overall morphological and IHC profile, interpreted in its entirety and in clinical context, most strongly supports leiomyosarcomatous differentiation but acknowledge that definitive molecular confirmation was not achievable in this case.

An important diagnostic question raised by this case is whether the leiomyosarcomatous component was present at the time of the original 2023 resection and was simply not identified due to sampling limitations, or whether it truly emerged de novo at the time of metastatic recurrence. Biopsy representativeness is a well-recognised challenge in chondroid lesions and chondrosarcomas specifically. The cartilaginous matrix of chondrosarcomas is inherently heterogeneous, and core needle biopsies, which sample only a small volume of tissue, are prone to undersampling the high-grade dedifferentiated component, particularly when this component constitutes a minority of the tumour volume or is spatially separated from the biopsy site [2,5]. Published data suggest that pre-operative biopsy in chondrosarcoma may fail to identify the dedifferentiated component in a significant proportion of cases, with the true biphasic nature only revealed on definitive resection specimens [8]. In the present case, the pre-operative biopsy demonstrated only cartilaginous tissue without high-grade elements, and the initial limb-salvage resection confirmed DDCS with a high-grade spindle cell component but without documented leiomyosarcomatous markers, although comprehensive IHC was not performed on the primary specimen. It is therefore plausible that leiomyosarcomatous differentiation was already present at the primary site but below the threshold of morphological detection, and was subsequently selected for and expanded during tumour progression and recurrence, a mechanism consistent with the clonal evolution model of dedifferentiation [2]. This possibility further underscores the importance of comprehensive IHC profiling at the time of primary resection in all DDCS cases, rather than deferring subtyping to recurrence specimens.

Surgical management

Surgical resection remains the primary therapeutic modality for DDCS, with wide negative margins being the most critical prognostic factor [6-8]. In the initial presentation in this case, limb-salvage surgery was appropriately employed. Gusho et al. [6] reported that 88% of patients with DDCS in their series underwent limb-salvage or wide margin resection, with a median overall survival of 46 months and a five-year survival probability of 32.1%. On recurrence, the extent of disease, involving the entire femur and proximal tibia, precluded further limb-preserving options, and palliative hip disarticulation was performed. Disarticulation in recurrent DDCS has been reported as a palliative procedure to achieve local disease control and pain relief, although its impact on overall survival in the metastatic setting is limited [6]. Conclusions

This case documents a rare instance of dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma in which the high-grade component demonstrated immunohistochemical features favouring leiomyosarcoma, arising in the setting of metastatic local recurrence. The diagnosis was reached through comprehensive tissue sampling, bimorphic histomorphology, and a targeted IHC panel. The identification of smooth muscle differentiation within the dedifferentiated component carried direct implications for prognostication and systemic therapy planning. A multidisciplinary approach encompassing surgery, pathology, and oncology was integral to the management of this diagnostically complex case, and molecular profiling is recommended in similar future presentations.

We hope this case contributes to the growing recognition of leiomyosarcomatous dedifferentiation as a distinct and clinically meaningful subtype within the DDCS spectrum. Prospective multi-institutional collaboration and dedicated reporting of heterologous dedifferentiation subtypes will be essential to better characterise their natural history and inform treatment strategies.
