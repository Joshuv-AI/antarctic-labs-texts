AS2FM: Enabling Statistical Model Checking of ROS 2 Systems for Robust Autonomy
Christian Henkel, Marco Lampacrescia, Michaela Klauck, Matteo Morelli

Abstract
Designing robotic systems to act autonomously in unforeseen environments is a challenging task. This work presents a novel approach to use formal verification, specifically SMC ( SMC ), to verify system properties of autonomous robots at design-time. We introduce an extension of the SCXML format, designed to model system components including both ROS 2 ( ROS 2 ) and BT ( BT ) features. Further, we contribute AS2FM ( AS2FM ), a tool to translate the full system model into JANI. The use of JANI, a standard format for quantitative model checking, enables verification of system properties with off-the-shelf SMC tools. We demonstrate the practical usability of AS2FM both in terms of applicability to real-world autonomous robotic control systems, and in terms of verification runtime scaling. We provide a case study, where we successfully identify problems in a ROS 2 -based robotic manipulation use case that is verifiable in less than one second using consumer hardware. Additionally, we compare to the state of the art and demonstrate that our method is more comprehensive in system feature support, and that the verification runtime scales linearly with the size of the model, instead of exponentially.

I
Introduction

Autonomous robotic systems are expected to operate autonomously in unforeseen environments.
Designing such systems is challenging for several reasons.
Firstly, the required software and hardware components have a high inherent complexity.
Secondly, the exact environment in which the robot will operate is not known at design time.
However, it is required for the robot to robustly operate in the environment by using the available hardware and software components.
Here,
robustness
means that the robot operates successfully in a variety of environments and under a variety of conditions.

To handle the complexity of autonomous robotic systems, a skill-based architecture is often used.
Each skill is responsible for a specific task, such as moving the robot, detecting objects, or planning a path

Responsible for the coordination of the skills is an architectural layer that we call the

This layer can be implemented using a variety of techniques, such as

[

The general architecture and communication of modern robotic systems is often implemented using the

[

because it provides a flexible and modular framework for it.

II
Related Work

Formal verification is a rigorous mathematical approach used to ensure that the designed system adheres to specified properties and requirements. It proves the correctness of algorithms and systems, particularly in critical applications such as software, hardware, and protocols. One of the key techniques in formal verification is
MC
(
MC
)
[
14
]
, which systematically explores the state space of a system’s model to verify whether it satisfies certain properties, typically expressed in temporal logic
[
15
]
. It provides a powerful tool for validating complex systems against their specifications, ensuring reliability and safety in system design. Traditionally, full-state space (exhaustive) model checking generates traces to explore the entire reachable state space of the model
[
8
]
, which is quite resource consuming.
This is the reason for a lack of methods and tools to validate complex robotic systems efficiently
as a whole
, despite the longstanding research interest in
VnV
(
VnV
) of autonomous systems
[
16
]
.
A solution to that problem is using

SMC
(
SMC
)

[
9
,
10
]
.

SMC
evaluates the model by only simulating enough traces, until statistical evidence whether the investigated property holds is achieved.
This is more scalable than full-state space model checking, and it helps finding corner cases faster compared to classic software testing.
However,
SMC
could miss rare edge-cases due to it’s probabilistic nature.
Recent work by the authors extend the well-known probabilistic model checker Storm
[
17
]
with
SMC
for robotic use cases
[
13
]
.

Storm uses, beside others,
MDP
(
MDP
) as input models, which is a well-known concept in the robotics community.

MDP
provide a formal framework for modeling decision-making under uncertainty and in dynamic environments
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, making them well-suited for representing the complex and dynamic nature of robotic use cases and scenarios.

On the other hand,
TA
are a formalism for modeling and verifying timed systems
[
22
]
.
While
TA
have been used in modeling real-time systems, their application to autonomous robotic decision-making is limited due to the complexity of modeling explicit time constraints
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
The tooling for
TA
is not as mature yet as the one for
MDP

[
26
]
.
While
TA
are possible in general, their current complexity and the lack of performant tooling make them less practical for modeling complex robotic systems.
Therefore, we propose to use
MDP
for modeling the environment and the system’s behavior, as they are more suitable for the complexity of robotic systems.

Previous work has focused on the
VnV
of robotic systems using
MC

[
27
]
, e.g., by verifying entire programs, including scheduling aspects, formalized as timed automata, by compilation to bytecode and translation to UPPAAL for formal analysis
[
28
]
.
Other work provides a case study on household robots’ behavior definitions that are verified using NuSMV
[
29
]
.
A
DSL
(
DSL
) was introduced for specifying the integration of robotic systems to verify the properties of the system using Fiacre and Tina
[
22
]
.
Further, an integrated approach between offline
MC
and online monitoring for robotic systems verifying

(
STL
) properties
[
30
]
has been published.

Research on an UML-based
DSL
to describe a robotic system and convert it into a TA is presented in
[
31
]
.
That work has been continued by introducing a new
DSL
for representing the environment and verifying the resulting
TA
using UPPAAL
[
32
]
.

Some work has been done on formal verification in ROS 1
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
32
]
, but we are to our knowledge the first explicitly targeting ROS 2.

There also exists work on verifying
BT

[
37
]
.
The tool BehaVerify focuses on stateful
BT
, which means the
BT
black board is used to keep information in memory.
In that approach the
BT
is converted to an
MDP
, that can be verified using nuXmv.
Though we think this work is the closest to ours, no explicit model of the environment is provided there, since it is described using the black board, and there is no way to describe a more complex system using only

III
High Level Modeling Language

Robotic systems, especially the ones using
ROS 2
and
BT
, consist of a number of nodes running in parallel and exchanging information via communication interfaces, i.e., topics, services, and actions in
ROS 2
, as well as ticks and blackboards in
BT
.
In the following, we describe how these systems can be modelled.

We introduce
HL-SCXML
(
HL-SCXML
), a custom modeling language building on top of
SCXML
.
It supports
ROS 2
communication interfaces and timers, and
BT
functionalities such as input-output ports, the blackboard, and ticks.
Each
HL-SCXML
file represents one state machine: this is used to represent complete
ROS 2
nodes and
BT
nodes.
Additionally, we support reading the definition of the
BT
in the XML format of the
BehaviorTree.CPP
2
2

2

https://github.com/BehaviorTree/BehaviorTree.CPP
library, so users must only model the behavior of the leaf nodes explicitly.
This enables an accurate representation of the actual robotic control architecture.

The
AS2FM
tool translates
HL-SCXML
to standard
SCXML

[
38
]
, replacing all references to
ROS 2
and
BT
features with
SCXML
events.
We provide an overview of the translations from
HL-SCXML
to
SCXML
in the next paragraphs but, for brevity, we omit a detailed description in this paper and refer to our tool’s documentation
3
3

3

https://convince-project.github.io/AS2FM/scxml-jani-conversion.html
.
Using standard
SCXML
as intermediate format limits the amount of functionalities we need to consider when generating the formal model of the system.
Afterwards, AS2FM converts the standard
SCXML
model into a network of
MDP
, that is the formalism used by JANI, to be formally verified.

III-A

ROS 2 Interfaces

ROS 2
nodes are modeled using the following interface definitions:

•

ROS Topics
are implemented by translating them to
SCXML
events.
The data in events is read from the declared ROS message type.

•

ROS Services
are implemented by translating the request and response to separate events,
where the data is read from the declared ROS types.

•

ROS Actions
are implemented by translating the goal, feedback, and result to separate events. The data is read from the declared ROS types.
They can be modeled in a multi-threaded architecture, where the action server uses multiple threads to handle multiple goals concurrently. Each thread is a separate SCXML state machine.

•

ROS Timers
are realized by the introduction of a state machine serving as a global clock.
This distributes the time information to all state machines that may declare callbacks to be triggered at different rates.

For a full syntax reference, refer to our documentation.
4
4

4

https://convince-project.github.io/AS2FM/howto.html

III-B

Behavior Tree Runtime

BT
can be modeled using the following concepts:

•

The
Runtime
interprets the BT.xml
5
5

5

https://www.behaviortree.dev/docs/learn-the-basics/xml_format/
file and executes the tree.
It is ticked at a configurable rate.
Also the behavior of ticking based on the tree return state can be configured.

•

All
BT Nodes
, including control nodes and leaf nodes, are represented as state machines, using
HL-SCXML
.
The tick signal is provided via events.
In AS2FM, we provide an implementation of the standard BT nodes. They can be used as they are or as a starting point for custom ones.

•

The
Blackboard
is a key-value storage to exchange data between nodes at runtime.
BT nodes can read and write data, which is exchanged via events from and to a BT management state machine.

IV
Model Translation

The full system model must be translated from a set of
SCXML
state machines to the JANI format for model checking.

IV-A

SCXML System of State Machines

Consider a set
M=\{M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{k}\}
of
k
state machines, where each
M_{i}
is specified in an
SCXML
-file.
Each state machine
M_{i}
can communicate with the other ones using the events
E
.

Each state machine
M_{i}
is defined as a tuple
M_{i}=(D_{i},C_{i},P_{i},S_{i},s_{0i},T_{i})
, where:

•

D_{i}
is the set of data variables in
M_{i}
.

•

C_{i}
is the set of conditions that are defined for
M_{i}
. Each condition
c\in C_{i}
is a boolean expression over the data from the variables in
D_{i}
and the data from the received events in
E
. Formally
c:(D_{i}\cup E)\to\{\texttt{false},\texttt{true}\}
.

•

P_{i}
defines executable content defined in
M_{i}
.
Each executable content
p\in P_{i}
can modify the data variables
D_{i}
or send events
e\in E
.
Each executable content
p\in P_{i}
can be defined as a sequence of operations
p=[o_{1},o_{2},\dots]
.
Each step
o_{x}
is either a function
o_{f}
modifying data objects or an event
o_{e}
being sent.
A function can modify data entries of this state machine based on the content of other data entries or data from a received event
o_{f}:(D_{i}\cup E)\times D_{i}
.
An operation may also be to send an event
o_{e}\in E
to other state machines.

•

S_{i}
is the set of states in
M_{i}
.
States may contain executable content that is executed
onentry
and
onexit
.
A state
s\in S_{i}
may have a tuple
s=(p_{\texttt{entry}},p_{\texttt{exit}})
, with
p_{\texttt{entry}}\in P_{i}\cup\{[~]\}
and
p_{\texttt{exit}}\in P_{i}\cup\{[~]\}
.

•

s_{0i}\in S_{i}
is the initial state of
M_{i}
.

•

t\in T_{i}=(S_{i}\times S_{i}\times(C_{i}\cup\{\texttt{true}\})\times(E\cup\{\text{0}\})\times(P_{i}\cup\{[~]\}))
is the set of transitions in
M_{i}
. Each transition
t
is defined as a tuple
t=(s,s^{\prime},c,e,p)
, where:

–

s,s^{\prime}\in S_{i}
are the source and target states of the transition.

–

c\in C_{i}\cup\{\texttt{true}\}
is the condition that must be satisfied for the transition to be taken.
c
is set to
true
if there is no condition on the transition.

–

e\in E\cup\{\text{0}\}
is the event that triggers the transition, or
0
if no event triggers the transition. Note that events may send data to this state machine, which we consider as read-only variables.

–

p\in P_{i}\cup\{[~]\}
is the executable content associated with the transition.

State machines
M_{i}
and
M_{j}
may exchange data and influence each other’s execution by sending events.
Specifically, an event
e\in E
emitted by
M_{i}
may be received by
M_{j}
where it is processed according to
M_{j}
’s transitions.
Data contained in the event is considered as an update to
D_{j}
.
This means, the synchronization between state machines is defined implicitly by the events exchanged between them.

1(a)
shows an example of two state machines that exchange events.

IV-B

JANI Network of
MDP

The JANI
[
12
]
format encodes a complete system as a network of
MDP
, including a list of synchronized edges that can only be executed simultaneously.
In the remainder of this section, we describe how to compile a JANI model from a system of
SCXML
state machines.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be the set of
n

MDP
defined in the JANI model.

\mathcal{D}_{g}
is the set of global data variables shared by all
MDP
in
\mathcal{M}
.
This global data is used to implement the exchange of data associated with events in the state machines in
M
.
Each
MDP

\mathcal{M}_{i}
is defined as a tuple
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(\mathcal{D}_{i},\Phi_{i},\Sigma_{i},\varsigma_{0i},\mathcal{A}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i})
, where:

•

\mathcal{D}_{i}
is the set of data variables in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

•

\Phi_{i}
is the set of guards.
Each guard
\varphi\in\Phi_{i}
is a function of local and global variables evaluating to a binary value
\varphi:\mathcal{D}_{i}\cup\mathcal{D}_{g}\to\{\texttt{false},\texttt{true}\}
.

•

\Sigma_{i}
is the set of states in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

•

\varsigma_{0i}\in\Sigma_{i}
is the initial state of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

•

\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the set of actions in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{i}=\Sigma_{i}\times\Sigma_{i}\times(\Phi_{i}\cup\{\texttt{true}\})\times\mathcal{A}_{i}\times(\mathcal{P}_{i}\cup\text{0})
is the set of transitions in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Each transition
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
is defined as a tuple
\tau=(\varsigma,\varsigma^{\prime},\alpha,\varphi,\rho)
, where:

–

\varsigma,\varsigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{i}
are the source and target states of the transition.

–

\varphi\in(\Phi_{i}\cup\{\texttt{true}\})
is the guard that must be satisfied for the transition to be taken.
It may be set to
true
if there is no guard on the transition.

–

\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the action that triggers the transition.

–

\rho\in(\mathcal{P}_{i}\cup\text{0})
is the assignment associated with the transition.
Where
\mathcal{P}_{i}
defines assignments that modify the data variables in
\mathcal{D}_{i}
and
\mathcal{D}_{g}
.
Each assignment
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
is a function
\rho_{f}:\mathcal{D}_{i}\times\mathcal{D}_{g}\to\mathcal{D}_{i}\times\mathcal{D}_{g}
.

\rho
is set to
0
if there is no assignment on the transition.

The network of
MDP
includes a set of synchronizations
\Gamma
.
Each synchronization
\gamma\in\Gamma
is defined as a tuple
\gamma=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
is an action in the
MDP

\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
One or more
\alpha_{i}
may be
0
indicating that the
MDP

i
does not have an action in this synchronization.
All actions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
must be executed simultaneously and all of their guards must be satisfied for the synchronization to be taken.

1(b)
shows an example of two
MDP
that synchronize using the actions
a_{e2s}
and
a_{e2r}
.

V
Evaluation

In this section we evaluate the most important aspects of our approach.
Firstly, we show a case study by verifying the correctness of a full robotic system after generating its formal model with AS2FM.
Then, we evaluate the model checking runtime on that system across different SMC confidence intervals.
Finally, we compare our toolchain consisting of AS2FM and a statistical model checker against the state-of-the art, BehaVerify
[
37
]
, in terms of runtime scalability.

We use the
SMC
-tool
SMC Storm

[
13
]
with a confidence of
95\%
and a maximum error of
0.01
, unless specified differently.
All
SMC
-tools use these two values to define the desired confidence interval, which in turn determines the required amount of traces to generate during the verification process.
All experiments are performed with an i7-11850H CPU and 32 GB of memory.

V-A

Case Study

This section demonstrates how our tooling is applied to a robotic control system based on
BT
and
ROS 2
.
The goal is to verify that an autonomous assembly robot reacts correctly in case a block falls from its gripper.
The model consists of
14
separate SCXML state machines, including:

•

The
BT
executing the policy shown in
Figure

2
.

•

A model of the user-defined
BT
plugins: IsBlockFell, MoveBlock, RecoverBlock.

•

ROS 2
nodes encoding specific robot skills triggered from the
BT
: MoveBlockSkill, RecoverBlockSkill.

The system is expected to tick the
BT
root for as long as it returns
RUNNING
.
Our goal is to verify that the move block operation is successful or, in case the block operation is aborted, the block recovery skill is enabled in time.
This is described in
LTL
(
LTL
)
[
15
]
as:

\displaystyle(\texttt{abort}\implies(t_{curr}<(t_{abort}+t_{timeout})))~

\displaystyle U(\texttt{success}\lor\texttt{recovery}),

(1)

where
abort
and
success
are boolean flags for representing if the move action was aborted or successful,
recovery
is a flag signaling if the recovery sequence was started,
t_{curr}
is the current time,
t_{abort}
is the time the move action was aborted, and
t_{timeout}
is a constant representing the maximum time the system can take to start the recovery sequence.

U
is an LTL operator that reads as
until
and is true if the left side is true in at least all states before the state in which the right side became true for the first time.

Fig. 2
:
The
BT
used for controlling the assembly robot in the case study.

After converting the model of this robot to JANI with AS2FM, and giving it to
SMC
Storm, the model checker reports that the probability of the model satisfying the property from
Equation

1
is
0.499
: this means that there are cases in which the system is not behaving as expected.

SMC
tools offer the possibility of exporting the generated traces as a CSV file, for inspecting them and determining the reason for the failure.
In this particular case, the
BT
checks if the block fell only before starting to move it. It is unable to detect this after the MoveBlock plugin is started.
This results in the system being unable to start the recovery sequence in case the block falls from the gripper, which in the model we used happens
50\%
of the times.

The issue can be solved by replacing the
Sequence
control node with a
ReactiveSequence
one, that keeps checking if the blocks fell and, if that is the case, starts a recovery.
Once the
BT
is fixed, the model can be checked again: this time the tool reports that the probability of satisfying the property is
1
, confirming that the implementation is working as expected.

V-C

Runtime and Scalability

The runtime scalability of the AS2FM toolchain was evaluated in direct comparison to
BehaVerify

[
37
]
, the state-of-the-art framework for verifying
BT
only, which does not provide robotic-specific features, e.g., ROS communication, but which is the closest tool to ours.
We used the example in their paper called
Simple Robot
, based on a 4-connected grid world with a robot that has to reach randomly placed goal locations.
The robot is controlled by a
BT
.
To scale this example, the size of the grid in both dimensions is changed to the indicated
size
.
This example was chosen because the other scaling example in their work,
Bigger Fish
, is perceived as being specifically designed to work well with their custom optimization and does therefore not provide insights into the general performance of the verification tooling.

In
Figure

4
it can be seen, that the runtime of our tool is larger than that of BehaVerify for sizes of 110 or less.
This can be attributed to the overhead that is introduced in our much more general approach by a more sophisticated model of communication (e.g., for ROS) which is crucial for the applicability in real-world robotic systems.
But it is further visible that the runtime of our approach scales linearly with the size of the environment, while BehaVerify scales exponentially.
These results can be attributed to using
SMC
opposed to full-state space model checking, which further underlines the practical usability of our approach with real-world systems that grow quickly in size.

VI
Conclusion

We have demonstrated our approach for using
SMC
on full models of robotic control systems to increase their robustness.
The models consist of SCXML state machines that benefit from an extension allowing the direct modelling of ROS 2 communication interfaces, and
BT
in their native XML syntax.
We contribute an algorithm to translate the full model in a network of
MDP
.
We demonstrated the approach on an industrial case study successfully identifying errors, and showing better scalability compared to the state of the art.
In the future, the approach can be extended and improved by adding support for more complex environment models, by providing tools for easier inspection of the generated SMC traces and model checking results, and by making the SCXML format more accessible with the help of graphical interfaces.
