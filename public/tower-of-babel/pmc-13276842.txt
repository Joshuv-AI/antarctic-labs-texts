Label-Free Raman Spectroscopy Reveals Metabolic Signatures Associated with MGMT Promoter Methylation Status in Glioblastoma
Authors: Dou Lu, Lijun Zhu, Jing Pan, Jianrui Li, Qing Xu, Liying Wang, Qing-Qing Zhou, Hao Pan, Qiang Wang, Dong Han, Zhiqiang Zhang, Jingxing Guo, Xiaoqing Cheng, Yiqing Wang, Huiming Cai, Xiaoyuan Chen, Longjiang Zhang, Guangming Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) response to Temozolomide (TMZ) critically depends on O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation. Clinically, the unmethylated MGMT subtyperepresenting the predominant GBM populationuniversally develops TMZ resistance, driving an urgent need for novel therapeutics. While metabolic reprogramming represents a potential therapeutic target, current methylation detection methods cannot provide targetable metabolic information. To elucidate methylation-associated metabolic signatures while mitigating complex biological background noise, we established a stepwise validation framework using CRISPR-engineered cell models. Raman spectroscopy (RS) analyzed CRISPR-dCas9-edited GBM cell lines to extract pure metabolic fingerprints. In MGMT hypermethylated (MGMT-HYPER) GBMs, the saturated fatty acid (SFA) Raman peak was elevated. Conversely, MGMT hypomethylated (MGMT-HYPO) GBMs exhibited enhanced peaks for deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), amino acids, and unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs). RS-identified metabolites were validated by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. Subsequently, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) effectively clustered HYPER/HYPO Raman spectra. A Raman classification model utilizing a core 13-feature signature from in vitro findings achieved a 94.5% area under the curve (AUC) in distinguishing methylation status across 62 clinical specimens. Correlation analysis demonstrated: (1) a positive correlation of SFA content (1444 cm–1) with methylation levels in MGMT-HYPER tumors; (2) an inverse association of DNA levels (1093 cm–1) with methylation in intermediate-methylation GBMs; and (3) a negative correlation between UFA content (1266 cm–1) and methylation in unmethylated GBMs. This study confirms RS potential for assessing MGMT methylation status in tumors and elucidates metabolic signatures associated with differential methylation in GBMs, providing targets for metabolic intervention against TMZ resistance.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM), the most common malignant primary brain tumor, confers a dismal prognosis with <15 months median survival. , The standard GBM management involves maximal safe surgical resection with adjuvant radiotherapy plus concurrent Temozolomide (TMZ). As an alkylating agent, TMZ induces tumor cell death by deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage. , However, the DNA damage reversal enzyme O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) excises this damage, compromising TMZ efficacy. , MGMT promoter methylation inactivates this repair mechanism and sensitizes tumor cells to TMZ. Clinical studies consistently confirm that MGMT methylation status is significantly associated with survival outcomes in TMZ-treated GBM patients. , Consequently, accurately detecting MGMT methylation is of significant clinical value.

Metabolic rewiring is a hallmark for GBM to sustain bioenergetic demands in nutrient-deprived microenvironments. GBM persistently utilizes glycolysis to convert glucose to lactate despite oxygen sufficiency, and accelerates biosynthesis of lipids and nucleotides to sustain malignancy. , Targeting these metabolic alterations has thus emerged as a promising anti-GBM strategy. , Critically, both MGMT hypomethylated (MGMT-HYPO) GBMs and initially TMZ-sensitive MGMT hypermethylated (MGMT-HYPER) GBMs ultimately develop chemoresistance. , Consequently, delineating metabolic profiles across MGMT methylation statuses simultaneously advances chemoresistance understanding and reveals targetable metabolic weaknesses for precision therapies.

As a label-free analytical method, spontaneous Raman spectroscopy (RS) enables direct capture of the metabolic composition and distribution of biological samples. Its high chemical specificity and submicron spatial resolution enable quantitative metabolite detection at subcellular scales. RS has successfully monitored postchemotherapy tumor metabolic dynamics, intracellular metabolite distribution, and tumor molecular subtyping metabolic differentials, establishing it as a powerful tool for small-molecule metabolic imaging. Hence, RS provides a promising approach to elucidate metabolic remodeling linked to MGMT promoter methylation status in GBMs.

To elucidate MGMT methylation-associated metabolic alterations while minimizing complex background noise, we developed a CRISPR-guided cross-scale feature selection pipeline. RS was applied to two CRISPR-dCas9-edited isogenic GBM cell lines. By intersecting their discriminative features, a 13-feature consensus fingerprint was established and translated to clinical specimens, effectively distinguishing methylation statuses with high chemometric robustness. Furthermore, metabolic biomarkers correlated with GBM methylation status were identified. In MGMT-HYPER tumors, saturated fatty acid (SFA) levels showed a positive correlation with methylation levels. Conversely, in intermediate-methylated GBMs, DNA content exhibited a negative correlation with methylation levels. In unmethylated tumors, unsaturated fatty acid (UFA) content was negatively correlated with methylation levels. This work highlights the potential of RS in assessing MGMT promoter methylation and reveals metabolic signatures associated with distinct methylation states, which provide potential metabolic intervention targets for TMZ-resistant GBM patients.

Experimental Section

Ethics and Patient Sample Collection

Human tissue procurement followed institutional ethical protocols (No. 2021DZGZR-YBB-066; the General Hospital of Eastern Theater Command) with documented participant consent. Postresection specimens were subjected to (1) Surface hemoglobin ablation via sterile saline (0.9%) perfusion; (2) Cryostabilization using liquid nitrogen flash-freezing; (3) Conservation at −80 °C. All samples met histopathological inclusion thresholds including diagnostic subtype and ≥80% malignant cellularity verified by blinded pathological review.

Patient-Derived Primary Cell Culture

Two patient-derived primary GBM cell lines with known MGMT promoter methylation statuses (63.0% and 7.5%) were obtained from the Department of Neurosurgery at the General Hospital of Eastern Theater Command. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco), 20 ng/mL EGF (Sigma-Aldrich), and 20 ng/mL bFGF (Sigma-Aldrich) to maintain their undifferentiated state. All procedures involving human samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Eastern Theater Command (No. 2021DZGZR-YBB-066).

Plasmid Construction and Viral Transduction

The dCas9-Tet1CD fusion gene (Addgene, 49236) was inserted into GV747 lentiviral backbone with a C-terminal tagBFP-2A-Puromycin resistance cassette, while two MGMT-promoter-targeting single guide RNA (sgRNAs) and a scr-sgRNA were cloned into GV391 vector (sequences in Table S1). CRISPR/dCas9-mediated epigenetic editing employs a dual lentiviral delivery system: one with a puromycin-resistant dCas9 under the CMV promoter in the GV747 backbone, and the other with an MGMT-targeting sgRNA under a U6 promoter in the GV391 backbone with neomycin resistance. U87 and U118 GBM cells (American Type Culture Collection procurement) were verified by short tandem repeat profiling and mycoplasma testing. Maintenance utilized DMEM (Gibco, #8123167) with 10% FBS (Gibco, #2547014P) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, #10378016) at 37 °C/5% CO2. For viral transduction, cells (3 × 105/well in 6-well plates) were transduced with dCas9-Tet1CD lentivirus. Stable polyclonal pools were selected with puromycin for 10 days, followed by secondary transduction with MGMT-sgRNA or scr-sgRNA lentivirus. Coexpression was maintained under dual selection.

Pyrosequencing Methylation Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted by DNeasy Kit (Qiagen, 69504), quantified/purity-checked by NanoDrop 2000, and bisulfite-converted with EpiTect Kit (Qiagen, 59104). Primer sequences (designed via PyroMark Assay Design 2.0, see Table S2) drove 50 μL PCR mixtures comprising: nuclease-free water, 5× GC Buffer, dNTP mix, forward/reverse primers, DNA template, and Taq polymerase. Thermal cycling was performed under: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation (94 °C, 30 s), annealing (52 °C, 30 s), extension (72 °C, 60 s) and extension (72 °C, 7 min). Pyrosequencing was performed on the PyroMark Q48. CpG methylation status quantified using Pyro Q-CpG software via chemiluminescent detection.

qRT-PCR

RNA extraction utilized TRIzol (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGF5101), with subsequent DNase I digestion (TaKaRa, RR036B) for genomic DNA elimination. cDNA was generated via PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, RR036A). qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI StepOnePlus system with TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (TaKaRa, RR820A) under cycling conditions: 95 °C 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C 15 s, 60 °C 60 s. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3. Relative expression was determined by the 2–ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as endogenous control.

WB

Lentivirus-transduced, C75-treated, or patient-derived primary cells were lysed for 30 min on ice using a total protein extraction kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGB5303-100). Lysates were centrifuged and supernatants collected. Protein quantification utilized BCA assay (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGB2101-500). Samples (20 μg/lane) underwent electrophoretic separation on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGC4401-50), followed by PVDF membrane transfer using Trans-Blot Turbo (Bio-Rad). Post-transfer membranes received 2 h blocking at ambient temperature with 5% skim milk in TBST. Primary antibody incubation was performed as follows: anti-MGMT (1:1000, Abcam ab39253), anti-FASN (1:1000, Abcam ab227590), anti-SCD1 (1:1000, Abcam ab236868), and anti-GAPDH (1:500, KeyGEN BioTECH KGC6102-1). After TBST washing, membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat antirabbit IgG (1:1000, KeyGEN BioTECH KGC6202) and goat antimouse IgG (1:1000, KeyGEN BioTECH KGC6203) for 2 h. Protein bands were detected using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGC4902-20) and imaged on a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad).

CCK-8

Log-phase tumor cells (5 × 104/well) were plated in 96-well plates. Postattachment treatments utilized TMZ (Aladdin, T408143) dissolved in DMSO (≤0.1% final) and serially diluted in medium. For targeted pharmacological inhibition and rescue assays, cells were treated with the FASN inhibitor C75 (TargetMol, T10657) or the SCD1 inhibitor A939572 (TargetMol, T4515), with or without exogenous palmitic acid (TargetMol, T2908) or oleic acid (TargetMol, T202668). Following 48 h of incubation, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA9310-1000) was added per well. After 1 h incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Bio-Rad microplate reader.

Intracellular Lipid Droplet Staining

Following designated treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, washed, and stained with Oil Red O working solution (TargetMol, TD0093) for 30 min at room temperature. Intracellular lipid droplets were immediately visualized and imaged using an inverted optical microscope.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of ROS

Posttreatment intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA7308-100). Cells were harvested, washed, and incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using a flow cytometer (BECKMAN COULTER CytoFLEX) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Colony Formation Assay

Log-phase cells (600/well) were cultured in 6-well plates. Following 10–14 days of TMZ gradient treatment, colonies were PBS-washed, fixed with 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188), and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Melunbio, MB4721-1) in light-protected conditions. Residual stain on cell colonies was gently rinsed with ddH2O. Following air-drying, colonies >50 μm in diameter were quantified using ImageJ.

Untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

Four biological replicates per group (1 × 106 cells/sample) were processed. Cells underwent chilled PBS washing followed by liquid nitrogen flash-freezing. Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using ultraperformance LC-MS. Methodological details are documented in Supplementary Text.

Raman Sample Preparation and Fixation

For pharmacological evaluation, U87 and U118 cells (105/well) were cultured on stainless steel substrates for 24 h and treated with inhibitors (C75 or A939572) as indicated. Following treatment and PBS rinsing, cells were fixed with 4% PFA at 4 °C for 15 min. In a separate validation study, parallel U87 cohorts were mapped live in PBS or fixed with 4% PFA on identical stainless steel substrates to assess spectral fidelity. For clinical samples, human tissue cryosections (5 μm thickness) were mounted on stainless steel, with paired consecutive sections used for H&E staining (Figure S1). All fixed samples were thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove residual fixative prior to Raman acquisition.

Raman Spectral Acquisition and Data Processing

Raman spectra were acquired using a Renishaw inVia confocal microscope (UK) under 785 nm laser excitation with 600 lines/mm grating. Beam focusing utilized a 50× objective (NA = 0.75). Prior to data acquisition, laser power was validated with a power meter and the wavenumber axis was calibrated using a silicon 520.5 cm–1 band. Cellular acquisition areas were scaled to the individual cell dimensions with standard parameters: 10 mW laser power, 5 s integration time. For tissue samples, spectra were acquired via 10 × 10 μm grid scans with 1 μm steps (50 mW, 5 s/point). Raw data were processed using Renishaw WIRE 4.3. Spectra underwent cosmic ray removal (nearest neighbor), fifth-order polynomial baseline correction, and 40-point Savitzky–Golay smoothing. Each spectrum was then individually normalized to the phenylalanine peak (1003 cm–1) at the single-spot level. Finally, these normalized spectra were averaged into a single representative spectrum per cell or clinical specimen, serving as the independent unit for all subsequent modeling.

Cluster Analysis

PCA

PCA was performed to characterize spectral variability and identify distinguishing Raman spectral features among experimental subgroups. The prcomp function from the stats package in R was utilized to conduct PCA on spectral variables collected from all subgroups. Principal component scores were visualized in 3D scatter plots using the plotly R package. The loading matrix output from PCA was used to calculate the principal component scores for every observation.

UMAP

UMAP was applied to reduce spectral dimensionality. Using the Python umap-learn package, spectral data from all subgroups were processed with parameters: n_components = 2, n_neighbors = 15, min_dist = 0.1, and random_state = 42 for reproducibility. The resulting 2D embeddings were visualized as scatter plots (matplotlib and plotly), with cluster fidelity objectively assessed via silhouette scores.

Machine Learning

A two-phase workflow was established for model development and independent clinical validation (Figure S2). During the in vitro phase, Raman spectra from U87 (n = 79) and U118 (n = 106) cells were used to develop two independent SVM classifiers (SVM-U87 and SVM-U118) via the Deepwise Multimodal Research Platform (Beijing, China). To prevent optimization bias, a nested cross-validation (Nested CV) framework was employed. Feature selection (coefficient filtering < 0.9, F-test for the top 80 features) and GridSearchCV hyperparameter tuning (random state = 1) were executed strictly within the inner CV loops. The intersection of these two models yielded 13 shared Raman features, which were finalized during the in vitro model development. This locked 13-feature set was subsequently utilized to establish an SVM-Human classification model evaluated on a completely independent clinical cohort (N = 62; 18 hypermethylated, 44 hypomethylated) using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation (LOPO–CV). For all models, AUC 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived via 1000-iteration bootstrapping, with significance assessed by permutation testing.

Data Analysis

Data were statistically processed through GraphPad Prism (v9.0.0). Volcano plots were generated utilizing the CNSknowall Platform (https://www.cnsknowall.com/#/HomePage). Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank coefficient with 95% CIs. Multiple comparisons were corrected via the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (q < 0.05). To ensure robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding influential points identified via Cook’s distance. A posthoc power analysis (G*Power v3.1) confirmed a statistical power >80% (α = 0.05) for the clinical sample size (N = 62). Significance levels for both p-values and q-values are indicated as *: < 0.05, **: < 0.01, ***: < 0.001.

Results and Discussion

Successful Establishment of MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO GBM Models

Based on the differences in MGMT promoter methylation status, the hypermethylated U87 cell line and the hypomethylated U118 cell line were selected. A CRISPR-dCas9 epigenetic editing system targeting the MGMT promoter CpG island was constructed (Figure a). The system facilitated demethylation in U87 cells (MGMT-HYPO group, methylation rate ≤ 50%), while the control group (MGMT-HYPER group, methylation rate > 50%) was transduced with nontargeted scrambled single guide RNA (scr-sgRNA). Similarly, U118 cells underwent methylation induction (MGMT-HYPER group, >50% methylation), and the control group expressing nontargeting scr-sgRNA was established (MGMT-HYPO group, ≤50% methylation) (Figure S3).

Pyrosequencing analysis revealed significantly elevated CpG methylation at the MGMT promoter in MGMT-HYPER groups compared to MGMT-HYPO groups (Figure b). Consistently, hypermethylation of the MGMT promoter correlated with downregulated mRNA levels with quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) validation (Figure c). Western blot (WB) showed that MGMT protein expression increased in the MGMT-HYPO groups and decreased in the MGMT-HYPER groups (Figure d). These results demonstrated that the CRISPR-dCas9 system successfully programmed the MGMT promoter methylation status in both GBM cells. Next, the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay showed that the sensitivity of the MGMT-HYPO groups to TMZ was lower than that of the MGMT-HYPER groups (Figure e). MGMT-HYPO GBM cells compromised TMZ efficacy, which is consistent with existing literature. Conversely, the growth of MGMT-HYPER GBM cells was significantly inhibited under TMZ treatment, as confirmed by colony formation assays (Figure f). Together, these functional analyses demonstrated that the efficacy of TMZ is affected by the MGMT promoter methylation statuses of GBM cells.

Comparison of Raman Spectra between MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO GBMs

To elucidate metabolic differences associated with various methylation statuses, RS was performed on MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO GBM cells. A total of 6317 spectra were acquired from 30–60 cells in each group (Figure a,b). All specimens were standardized via 4% PFA fixation, which experimentally showed negligible spectral alteration versus live cells (r s > 0.95; Figure S4), aligning with prior literature. Subsequent analysis identified Raman peaks at 1301, 1444, and 1656 cm–1 corresponding to lipids. The 1100–1200 cm–1 band contained vibrational signals from metabolites such as DNA (1093 cm–1), glucose (1126 cm–1), and SFAs (1174 cm–1). The weak signal range of 650–720 cm–1 showed characteristic peaks for cholesterol (700 cm–1) and choline (719 cm–1). The peaks corresponding to metabolites are summarized in Table S4. As shown in Figure c,d, there are significant differences in the Raman peaks of MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO in U87 and U118 cells. A comparison of characteristic peaks (Figure e,f) revealed high intensity of the DNA-related peaks (667 and 1093 cm–1) in MGMT-HYPO groups, which correlated with active cell proliferation and enhanced DNA replication. Similarly, Raman peaks of amino acids (1338 and 1555 cm–1) were elevated in the MGMT-HYPO groups. Concurrently, these groups exhibited strong vibrations at 1266 and 1656 cm–1 (UFAs) and weak signals at 1088 and 1174 cm–1 (SFA), which implied dynamic remodeling of lipid networks. These Raman spectral profiles, along with the MGMT protein phenotypes and related lipid biosynthetic enzymes (FASN and SCD1), were further validated in patient-derived primary GBM cells (Figure S5). In contrast to conventional methods (methylation-specific PCR/pyrosequencing) that directly detect DNA modifications, − the RS approach captures these downstream metabolic manifestations. These findings demonstrate that MGMT promoter methylation status shapes distinct metabolic networks in GBM, establishing RS as a functional phenotyping tool for methylation assessment.

Validation of RS Findings via Metabolomics

To validate the metabolic signatures revealed by RS, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was employed for nontargeted metabolomics analysis. Positive and negative ionization modes yielded 859 and 494 identified metabolites, respectively. Differentially expressed metabolites were strictly identified using an False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (q < 0.05), with comprehensive annotation metrics detailed in Tables S5 and S6. These metabolites identified by LC-MS were categorized into five major groups: amino acids, UFAs, SFAs, phospholipids, and nucleotides. The heatmap (Figure a,b) revealed significant metabolite profile differences between MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO groups. MGMT-HYPO GBM cells contained higher levels of amino acids, UFAs, phospholipids, nucleotides and lower content of SFAs compared with MGMT-HYPER GBM cells. Our work further assessed the consistency of metabolomic alterations identified by LC-MS and those characterized by RS. The DNA-associated 1093 cm–1 peak was elevated in MGMT-HYPO groups. Similarly, LC-MS results demonstrated significantly increased levels of the DNA metabolite (guanosine diphosphate, GDP) in these groups. The trends in metabolite changes quantified by RS and LC-MS were largely consistent (Figure ). This robust validation establishes RS-based metabolic profiling as a reliable approach. While a prior MS study reported differences in UFAs and SFAs between MGMT unmethylated and methylated tumors, our work further leveraged these metabolic signatures to develop a clinically applicable classifier for MGMT methylation status determination. Such progression represents a transition from metabolic characterization to a practical classification tool (as demonstrated in later sections).

Raman-Based Classification Model for GBM Methylation Status Prediction

To further distinguish between MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO GBMs, unsupervised clustering and machine learning methods were performed. A principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first three PCs of U87 cells collectively accounted for 66% of the variance (PC1:47%, PC2:13%, PC3:6%) (Figure a). The first three PCs of U118 cells collectively explained 59% of the variance (PC1:36%, PC2:20%, PC3:7%) (Figure b). Although the PCA plots show a tendency for tumor cells to cluster in the MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO groups, there is some overlap between the two groups. The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), which effectively reveals the nonlinear structure inherent in the high-dimensional Raman data, was employed. Figure c,d clearly demonstrated, as shown, the distinct clustering of MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO groups.

Each point represents the average Raman spectrum of a single cell. (c,d) UMAP visualization of U87 (c) and U118 (d) cells with MGMT-HYPO and MGMT-HYPER, respectively. (e) ROC curves of SVM-U87 (95% CI: 95.0%–99.0%) and SVM-U118 (95% CI: 94.0%–99.0%) classifiers. (f) ROC curve of the SVM-Human classifier in the independent clinical cohort (95% CI: 87.0%–98.0%). Next, Raman classification models were constructed with support vector machines (SVMs). Classification models were trained to discriminate MGMT-HYPER from MGMT-HYPO subpopulations for U87 and U118 cells, termed SVM-U87 and SVM-118 respectively. Diagnostic parameters, including area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, validated model robustness. The results showed that Raman classification models had excellent performance in distinguishing between MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO, and the AUC values were 98% (95% CI: 95.0%–99.0%) and 97.2% (95% CI: 94.0%–99.0%) in U87 and U118 cells, respectively (Figure e; Table S7). The SVM-U87 and SVM-118 models each retained 80 most discriminative features, among which 13 were shared spectral features between both models (Table S8). This 13-feature signature was then subjected to independent blind validation using the clinical cohort (N = 62). Using LOPO–CV in the clinical cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 94.5% (95% CI: 87.0%–98.0%) for distinguishing between MGMT-HYPER and MGMT-HYPO (Figure f; Table S9). Compared to studies employing RS for GBM metabolic analysis, , our analytical strategy utilizes engineered isogenic cell models to isolate 13 pure MGMT-driven spectral features. By utilizing only the prevalidated spectral targets, we ensured the clinical classifier remains robust against biological noise and overfitting. Furthermore, this approach complements recent MRI-based prediction models; , while MRI relies on macroscopic tumor morphology, our framework directly captures the underlying metabolic rewiring driving these phenotypes. However, our current point-spectral averaging only captures the global bulk tumor signature. Since MGMT methylation exhibits known spatial heterogeneity, future studies using Raman hyperspectral imaging are needed to accurately map these localized metabolic variations.

Correlation Analysis of Peak Intensities with MGMT Methylation Levels

We further assessed the relationship between MGMT methylation levels and the intensity of 13 peaks selected by modeling. As shown in Figure a–d, peak intensities at 1444 cm–1 (SFAs) positively correlated with MGMT methylation levels, with correlation coefficients of 0.8360 (Figure a). Conversely, peak intensities at 1555 cm–1 (tryptophan), 1093 cm–1 (DNA) and 1266 cm–1 (UFAs) showed negative correlations with methylation levels (r s = −0.7302, −0.6571 and −0.7638, respectively) (Figure b–d). Correlation results for the other nine peaks are detailed in Figure S6. Furthermore, based on pyrophosphate sequencing results, GBM clinical samples were categorized into three groups: hypermethylated (methylation rate > 50%, n = 18), intermediate methylated (methylation rate: 16%–50%, n = 21), and unmethylated (methylation rate ≤ 15%, n = 23). In the hypermethylated group, a peak at 1444 cm–1 (SFAs) showed strong positive correlation with methylation levels (r s = 0.9429; Figure e). Given SFAs’ roles as energy sources, membrane structural components, and signaling substrates, inhibition of the SFA pathway may compromise metabolic support in hypermethylated GBM. Conversely, the 1555 cm–1 peak exhibited a significant negative correlation with methylation levels (r s = −0.8286; Figure f). For the intermediate methylated group, the strong negative relationship of the 1093 cm–1 peak (DNA) with methylation levels (r s = −0.8810; Figure g) provides a rationale for DNA-directed therapeutic opportunities. In the unmethylated group, the 1266 cm–1 peak (UFAs) exhibited a marked negative correlation with MGMT methylation (r s = −0.8500; Figure h). The UFAs effectively suppress oxidative stress responses, which potentially represent a key mechanism underlying the more aggressive phenotype of MGMT-unmethylated GBM compared to the methylated subgroup. Consequently, UFA-targeted interventions may disrupt redox homeostasis in TMZ-resistant unmethylated tumors. While these correlations suggest that SFA and UFA differences are the most promising drug targets for different MGMT statuses, these findings remain exploratory and require direct functional validation.

Functional Validation of Methylation-Specific Metabolic Vulnerabilities

To validate the identified lipid dependencies, we performed targeted functional assays. In MGMT-hypermethylated cells, FASN inhibition via C75 dose-dependently attenuated viability (Figure a), which was reversed by palmitic acid, confirming SFA dependency (Figure b). Mechanistically, C75 suppressed FASN expression (Figure c), depleted intracellular lipid droplets (Figure d), and reduced the SFA-associated Raman peak at 1444 cm–1 (Figure e). Conversely, in MGMT-hypomethylated cells, SCD1 inhibition via A939572 induced dose-dependent cytotoxicity (Figure f), effectively rescued by oleic acid (Figure g). This targeted inhibition provoked severe oxidative stress with elevated ROS levels (Figure h,i and correspondingly decreased the UFA-associated Raman peak at 1266 cm–1 (Figure j). Collectively, these data functionally validate that MGMT methylation dictates distinct, targetable lipid dependencies, highlighting the potential of Raman-guided metabolic stratification for precision therapy.

Conclusions

This study establishes that RS enables accurate identification of MGMT methylation status in clinical GBM specimens; confirms the close association between MGMT promoter methylation and metabolic reprogramming; and reveals druggable metabolic targets. Building upon these findings, we are constructing a spectral database for simultaneous detection of MGMT methylation, PD-L1 status and IDH mutation, with planned integration into standard diagnostic workflows to develop a rapid, noninvasive optical platform facilitating molecular subtyping and personalized therapy.
