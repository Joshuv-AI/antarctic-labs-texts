Modulating tumor interstitial fluid pressure using ultrasound and microbubble therapy: a preclinical study for enhanced drug delivery in cancer treatment
Authors: Liping Chen, Jianhua Liu, Qiaoli Chen, Yucai Li, Yangcheng He, Hai Jin, Danxia Qiu, Yuyi Feng, Fen Xi, Suihong Ma, Zhiwen Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe tumor microenvironment (TME) often hinders effective cancer treatment, where elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) acts as a key barrier to drug penetration. This study investigates the distribution of IFP in VX2 tumors and the effect of ultrasound-mediated microbubble (USMB) therapy on reducing IFP to improve drug delivery.MethodsFollowing approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of the South China University of Technology and adhering to NIH animal care guidelines, forty-one healthy female New Zealand White rabbits with VX2 tumors underwent USMB therapy at varying ultrasound pressures (1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, and 5 MPa). IFP measurements across different tumor regions were performed pre- and post-treatment using the wick-in-needle (WIN) method to assess IFP variability and distribution. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) was used to evaluate changes in tumor perfusion associated with USMB treatment.ResultsSignificant regional differences in tumor IFP were observed, with the central region (23.79 ± 8.07 mmHg) markedly higher than the peripheral 1/2 (15.58 ± 5.22 mmHg) and peripheral 1/4 regions (8.29 ± 3.47 mmHg). After treatment, 3 MPa and 5 MPa ultrasound pressures significantly reduced IFP in the central region to 18.05 ± 8.1 mmHg and 14.69 ± 4.72 mmHg, respectively. Histological analysis showed that higher pressures caused greater tumor cell and vascular damage, with extensive necrosis and vascular disruption in the central tumor area at 3 MPa and 5 MPa, demonstrating the impact of USMB therapy on the tumor microenvironment.ConclusionUSMB therapy reduces IFP in solid tumors. At 2 MPa, IFP reduction occurs with minimal perfusion change, potentially favoring drug delivery. In contrast, higher pressures (3–5 MPa) also lower IFP but markedly decrease perfusion through vascular disruption, which may hinder drug transport. These findings highlight the need to adjust ultrasound parameters based on regional IFP distribution and to balance IFP reduction with perfusion preservation to optimize treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15218-1.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, accounting for approximately one-sixth of all deaths [1]. In 2022, there were an estimated 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths globally. By 2050, the global cancer incidence is projected to exceed 35 million cases, representing a 77% increase compared to 2022 [2]. Although conventional cancer treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and emerging immunotherapies are available, their efficacy is often limited, particularly in drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes [3, 4].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) of solid tumors displays several characteristics distinct from normal tissues, such as elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), hypoxia or anoxia, and low pH levels, all closely linked to tumor growth, cellular metabolism, and drug resistance [5–7]. Notably, high IFP, which can range from 10 to 40 mmHg in tumor tissues compared to −3 to 3 mmHg in normal tissues, has been identified as a primary obstacle to effective cancer treatment, significantly reducing the penetration and efficacy of therapeutic agents [8–11].

Ultrasound-mediated microbubble (USMB) technology, leveraging its unique thermal, mechanical, and cavitation effects, has emerged as a promising strategy for treating solid tumors [12, 13]. USMB primarily enhances drug and gene delivery by modulating tumor microcirculation and offers advantages such as low toxicity, ease of operation, and non-invasiveness [14, 15]. However, several challenges remain in the clinical translation of low-frequency USMB therapy, including improved microbubble preparation techniques, potential damage to normal tissues due to cavitation effects, and variability in individual and tissue responses to ultrasound parameters. Nevertheless, USMB has demonstrated significant potential as a targeted, safe, and non-invasive therapeutic modality [15, 16]. With ongoing technological advancements, USMB is expected to find broader applications in the clinical management of solid tumors.

Previous studies have demonstrated that USMB can effectively reduce IFP in the central regions of tumors, highlighting its potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy by modulating the tumor microenvironment [17, 18]. To further explore the regulatory effects of USMB, this study employed the VX2 tumor model in New Zealand White rabbits, measuring IFP in various regions using the Wick-in-Needle (WIN) method and treating with USMB at different ultrasound intensities. This approach reveals the distribution and changes in tumor IFP but also aids in identifying more effective treatment parameters, providing a scientific basis for future therapies in solid tumors.

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the role of USMB technology in modulating tumor IFP and its potential scientific and clinical value. By systematically assessing the impact of USMB on IFP modulation, this research aims to offer a novel strategy for treating solid tumors, particularly in enhancing the penetration and distribution of chemotherapy drugs. Furthermore, the findings of this study are expected to contribute to personalized and optimized cancer treatment strategies, potentially transforming the approach to managing refractory solid tumors, thus improving treatment success rates and patient quality of life.

Materials and methods

Experimental instruments and equipment

The DCT-700 ultrasound cavitation therapy device was used for ultrasound irradiation. Microbubble suspensions were prepared by the Ultrasound Research Laboratory at Xinqiao Hospital, with perfluoropropane as the core gas. After shaking for 45 s, the microbubbles formed a uniform milky suspension, with a mean particle diameter of 2 μm and a concentration of approximately 4–9 × 10⁹/ml, as measured by a Coulter particle counter [19–21]. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound imaging was performed using a GE E9 color Doppler ultrasound system (contrast mode MI = 0.11), with a contrast agent dose of 0.01 ml/kg for imaging and 0.1 ml/kg for therapy. Detailed equipment and parameters are listed in Table S1.

Experimental reagents and solutions

The main reagents used in this study included Sumianxin II injection (veterinary use), sodium pentobarbital, saline for injection, heparin sodium injection, and formalin fixation solution. All reagents were used following standard operating procedures to ensure experimental accuracy and reproducibility. Sumianxin II and sodium pentobarbital were used for animal anesthesia, saline for injections and flushing procedures, heparin sodium for anticoagulation, and formalin for tissue fixation.

Animal model preparation

VX2 is a commonly used rabbit tumor model with good reproducibility and significant advantages in surgical manipulation and imaging, facilitating vascular interventions and radiofrequency ablation procedures [18, 19, 22]. This study used 41 healthy adult female New Zealand White rabbits (weighing 2.0–2.5 kg). After weighing, an intramuscular injection of Sumianxin II (0.15 ml/kg) combined with 2% sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg) was induced anesthesia. Once the corneal reflex disappeared, rabbits were placed in a lateral recumbent position, and the left hind limb was shaved. VX2 tumor tissue, obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Cell Bank, was minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments, suspended in saline, and 1–2 fragments were injected into the superficial muscle layer of the left hind limb (approximately 2.5 ± 0.5 mm below the skin surface) under sterile conditions. Rabbits with tumors reaching L13 ± 0.2 mm and W6 ± 0.2 mm and showing good contrast enhancement without defects on CEUS were enrolled in the study.

The Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Medical University, affiliated with Guangzhou First People's Hospital, granted this experimental protocol's ethical approval. All experimental steps were conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines published in the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1996). All rabbits were acclimatized under consistent environmental conditions (temperature 24–26 °C, humidity 45–55%) for 7 days prior to the start of the experiments. The Laboratory Animal Committee (LAC) of the South China University of Technology in Guangdong, China, also approved this study protocol.

Experimental grouping

The sample size was calculated based on preliminary data using a power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.8, effect size = 1.0, based on expected differences in IFP values). The analysis indicated that at least 5–7 rabbits per group were required. Considering potential dropouts and to ensure adequate statistical power, a total of 41 New Zealand White rabbits were enrolled. Forty-one tumor-bearing New Zealand White rabbits that met the experimental criteria were randomly divided into seven groups: the USMB groups at various intensities (1 MPa-USMB with 6 rabbits, 2 MPa-USMB with 7 rabbits, 3 MPa-USMB with 7 rabbits, 5 MPa-USMB with 6 rabbits), a therapeutic ultrasound only group (US) with 5 rabbits, an MB only group with 5 rabbits, and a blank control group (BC) with 5 rabbits, which received saline intravenously and sham therapeutic ultrasound. All rabbits were fasted for 12 h before the experiment but had access to water ad libitum. Anesthesia was administered before the start of the experiment until the corneal reflex was absent. The rabbits were then positioned in right lateral recumbency on the experimental table, with the left hind limb prepared and the tumor fully exposed. A venous line was established in the marginal ear vein for steady saline infusion. Throughout the experiment, vital signs (respiratory rate, heart rate, corneal reflex, etc.) were continuously monitored, and the room temperature was maintained at 24–26 °C.

CEUS and result analysis

Before treatment, all tumor-bearing rabbits underwent two-dimensional ultrasound and color Doppler scanning to collect baseline data, including tumor size, necrotic calcification, and vascular supply. Tumors were regionally segmented for analysis. CEUS was performed using a GE E9 system in CEUS mode (mechanical index 0.11), with adjusted imaging parameters. Coupling gel was applied between the probe and the tumor to eliminate air gaps and ensure optimal ultrasound transmission. Microbubbles were injected into the marginal ear vein, followed by 2 ml of saline, and dynamic imaging was recorded for one min with the probe kept stationary. Blood flow perfusion in different tumor regions was assessed visually and quantitatively using peak intensity (PI), with TIC fitting performed by the system software. Dynamic imaging was recorded for one minute with the probe held stationary. A 15-min interval was allowed for microbubble clearance before subsequent treatment. PI was preferred over AUC for its higher temporal resolution and lower sensitivity to microbubble destruction or reperfusion. Although its correlation with IFP has mainly been reported under lower pressures (500–740 kPa; [23, 24]), PI showed more stable group-wise trends in our high-pressure setting. Its use at ≥ 1 MPa has also been reported [25, 26], supporting its applicability here.

IFP measurement

The IFP in different tumor regions was measured using the WIN technique. This method employs a side-hole needle packed with wicking material to provide a broad contact area and prevent blockage, offering the advantages of simplicity and minimal tissue damage. It is widely used for measuring IFP in solid tumors [11]. Sensor-tipped catheters provide real-time readings but are invasive and lack spatial flexibility, limiting their suitability for assessing heterogeneous IFP in tumors. Ultrasound elastography, as an indirect method based on tissue stiffness estimation, may be inaccurate in necrotic or fibrotic regions. In contrast, the WIN technique enables minimally invasive, point-to-point pressure measurements across tumor regions, offering a more reliable assessment of spatial IFP distribution [11, 27].

The procedure was as follows: rabbits were fixed, and the tumor was exposed. The puncture needle was connected to the biological signal acquisition system (BL-420S), and the system was filled with heparinized saline, calibrated, and leveled horizontally. Under ultrasound guidance, the side-hole needle was inserted into the target area, and after a stable pressure curve was maintained for 30 s, the average pressure reading over the following 10 s was recorded as the IFP value for that region. Group treatments

Non-focused pulsed ultrasound was applied at peak negative pressures of 1, 2, 3, and 5 MPa. This setup was informed by a previous clinical study in PDAC patients using 1–5 MPa in combination with SonoVue®, which reported no additional toxicity [28]. Subsequent animal studies under 1–2 MHz high-pressure conditions showed that SonoVue® exhibited favorable acoustic controllability [29]. Similar pressure parameters have also been adopted in other PDAC models [30]. It has been noted that high acoustic pressure can be safely applied when frequency, pulse repetition rate, and exposure duration are appropriately controlled [31].

US-MB Group: The coupling gel was fully applied between the ultrasound cavitation therapy probe and the tumor to eliminate air gaps, and the probe was fixed on an iron stand. Non-focused pulsed ultrasound irradiation was performed at 1, 2, 3, and 5 MPa pressures, with a sinusoidal pulse waveform, for 6 min. At the start of treatment, microbubble suspension (0.1 ml/kg diluted to 5 ml) was injected slowly via the marginal ear vein, 15 s after the injection, ultrasound irradiation was initiated. The microbubble suspension was continuously agitated during the treatment to ensure uniform distribution. After 5 min, the venous line was opened to ensure full circulation of the remaining microbubbles during the final min.

US Group: Following the same preparation and infusion procedures, 5 ml of sterile saline was slowly infused via the marginal ear vein while irradiating with a 2 MPa ultrasound for 6 min.

MB Group: Following the same procedures, the MB suspension was slowly infused via the marginal ear vein with sham ultrasound exposure for 6 min.

BC Group: Similarly, 5 ml of sterile saline was slowly infused via the marginal ear vein at the start of treatment, accompanied by 6 min of sham ultrasound exposure.

The ultrasound treatment parameters for each group are detailed in Table S2.

Post-treatment measurements and specimen processing

Immediately following treatment, the IFP within different tumor regions was measured using the same method as previously described. Post-treatment, CEUS and result analysis were also conducted, as previously described. After the procedures, euthanasia was promptly carried out on the experimental rabbits by injecting an overdose of sodium pentobarbital through the marginal ear vein. The entire tumor was then carefully excised, bisected along the longest axis with a scalpel, and fixed in formalin solution for 24 h.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

The tumor blocks fixed in formalin solution underwent a series of processing steps before staining. These included gradient ethanol dehydration, xylene clarification, paraffin embedding, sectioning, xylene dewaxing, rehydration through graded ethanol, and H&E staining, followed by a second round of gradient ethanol dehydration and xylene clarification before coverslipping. Specifically, the tumor tissues were dehydrated in ascending ethanol concentrations, clarified in xylene, and embedded in paraffin to be sectioned into 4-micron slices. The sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5–10 min, differentiated in 0.3% hydrochloric acid in ethanol for 10–30 s, blued in 0.5% ammonia water for one min, and stained with eosin for 1–3 min. Following staining, the sections underwent gradient ethanol dehydration and xylene clarification. The prepared histopathological slides were examined under a microscope for tissue pathology changes, and images were collected and analyzed. The necrotic fraction of each tumor section was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Whole-slide digital images were acquired at low magnification (× 40) to encompass the entire tumor cross-section. Necrotic regions were operationally defined as areas lacking cellular detail, exhibiting eosinophilic cytoplasm, showing nuclear pyknosis, karyorrhexis or karyolysis, and devoid of viable tumor cells. These regions were manually delineated and measured. The necrotic area was expressed as a percentage of the total tumor cross-sectional area. For each tumor, three non-overlapping sections were analyzed, and the mean value was used for subsequent statistical comparisons between groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24. The IFP and PI values in different tumor regions before and after treatment were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). One-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare the IFP and PI values across different tumor regions (central, peripheral 1/2, peripheral 1/4). Pearson correlation analysis examined the relationship between pre- and post-treatment IFP and PI values. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA assessed IFP and PI value changes in different tumor regions before and after treatment. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Mauchly's test of sphericity was conducted to verify the sphericity assumption before ANOVA; if this assumption was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Bonferroni correction was used to compare multiple groups to control the Type I error rate. All statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Comparison of IFP pairwise in different regions of the tumor

Before treatment, the mean IFP in the central tumor region was 23.79 ± 8.07 mmHg, significantly higher than the peripheral 1/2 region at 15.58 ± 5.22 mmHg and the peripheral 1/4 region at 8.29 ± 3.47 mmHg. Multivariate tests, including Pillai's trace, Wilks' lambda, Hotelling's trace, and Roy's most giant root, all showed significant differences (F = 153.181, p < 0.001), indicating substantial variation in IFP values across different regions (Table 3).

Pairwise comparison results further detailed these differences. The IFP in the central region was significantly higher than in the peripheral 1/2 and 1/4 regions, with mean differences of 8.206 and 15.503, respectively (p < 0.001). Similarly, the IFP in the peripheral 1/2 region was significantly higher than in the peripheral 1/4 region, with a mean difference of 7.297 (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Impact on PI

CEUS reveals significant impact of different sound pressures on tumor blood perfusion

Histological changes

Pathological impact of different sound pressures on tumor tissue structure

To evaluate the effects of USMB therapy at different sound pressures on tumor tissue structure, we performed H&E staining on tumor tissues from each group and examined them under a light microscope (Fig. 9). Pathological results from the control groups (US, MB, and BC) showed that the tumor tissues exhibited a nest-like distribution, with blood vessels and fibrous tissues interspersed among cancer nests. Tumor cells within the nests were disorganized, varying in size, with large, deeply stained nuclei and evident nuclear atypia. Some tumor cells were observed at different stages of proliferation. Blood vessels traversed the cancer nests, displaying recognizable shapes, branching or cystic structures, intact vessel walls, and red blood cells within the lumina. No significant red blood cell extravasation was observed around the vessels.

The pathological changes in the USMB groups following treatment at various ultrasound pressures are summarized below: In the 1 MPa-USMB group, tumor cells were disorganized, with blood vessels appearing branched or cystic. Some vessels were slightly dilated but retained intact structural integrity, with minimal red blood cell extravasation. In the 2 MPa-USMB group, focal necrosis was observed in the central part of the tumor. Some tumor cells exhibited degenerative changes characterized by a decrease in size and nuclear pyknosis. The blood vessel walls were irregular, and localized red blood cell leakage was evident. In the 3 MPa-USMB group, the necrotic area in the tumor's center expanded, with discontinuous and damaged blood vessel walls. There was significant red blood cell extravasation and the formation of small hematomas. The degree of damage was intermediate between the 2 MPa and 5 MPa conditions. The 5 MPa-USMB group showed marked regressive changes in tumor cells, including reduced size, pronounced nuclear pyknosis, and fragmentation. Partial rupture of cell membranes led to acellular structures, and there was severe destruction of the vascular structure, with extensive red blood cell dispersion and peripheral stasis of extravasated cells.

Discussion

Efficient drug delivery remains a major challenge in cancer treatment, with tumor interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) recognized as a key factor limiting drug penetration and distribution [27, 32–34]. Elevated IFP significantly impedes drug transport into tumor tissues, reducing therapeutic efficacy. USMB therapy has emerged as a potential strategy to modulate IFP and enhance drug delivery and therapeutic response. In this study, using a VX2 tumor model, we systematically evaluated the effects of USMB treatment under different ultrasound pressures on IFP. USMB at 3 MPa and 5 MPa significantly reduced tumor blood perfusion, IFP across different regions, and PI, and induced tumor cell necrosis and vascular disruption, particularly in the central regions. In contrast, 2 MPa reduced IFP with minimal perfusion change, potentially favoring drug delivery. These findings highlight the need to balance IFP reduction with preservation of perfusion when optimizing tumor drug permeability and distribution. It should be noted that this study utilized a VX2 rabbit tumor model, which may differ from human tumors in certain biological characteristics. Additionally, repeated treatments' potential long-term side effects and safety were not assessed. Therefore, further studies are needed to validate these findings in different animal models and to evaluate the applicability and long-term safety of USMB therapy through clinical trials in human cancers.

The results of this study demonstrated that low-pressure (1 MPa) USMB treatment slightly increased tumor IFP, while higher pressures (2 MPa, 3 MPa, and 5 MPa) significantly reduced IFP across different tumor regions. Elevated IFP has been recognized as a major barrier to effective cancer therapy, limiting drug uptake and therapeutic efficacy [35–37]. Previous studies have suggested that modulating IFP can improve drug concentration and distribution and alleviate tumor hypoxia, thereby enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy and certain targeted therapies [38–41].

The slight increase in IFP observed after 1 MPa USMB treatment may be attributed to enhanced vascular permeability. Low-pressure ultrasound has been reported to facilitate the leakage of vascular contents into the interstitial space without sufficiently disrupting the extracellular matrix, resulting in elevated colloid osmotic pressure and increased IFP [42]. This mechanism may explain the IFP increase observed in the 1 MPa group in our study. In contrast, higher pressure (3 MPa and 5 MPa) USMB treatment significantly reduced tumor blood perfusion, as indicated by decreased PI values on CEUS, which correlated with the reduction in IFP. This reduction in perfusion likely results from a microvascular disruption in the tumor [42, 43], and it is consistent with previous findings that vascular-disrupting agents can effectively lower tumor IFP [44]. Notably, reduced microvascular perfusion at higher pressures (3–5 MPa) may counteract the benefits of IFP reduction for drug delivery, presenting a therapeutic paradox.. Smaller and immature vessels are more susceptible to rupture under cavitation effects, while larger vessels contribute to improved drug transport. Selective vascular destruction and IFP reduction may synergistically improve drug delivery [38]. This hypothesis is supported by previous observations linking IFP reduction with vascular disruption [42] and enhanced drug penetration [18].

Moreover, the decrease in IFP may also be attributed to strong mechanical effects induced by ultrasound-mediated microbubble destruction. Inertial cavitation can cause direct vascular rupture, extracellular matrix collagen degradation, and the formation of micropores, thereby enhancing drug penetration and promoting tumor cell starvation [38, 43]. The role of microbubble polydispersity in the pressure-dependent IFP variations observed warrants further investigation.

Histological analysis revealed more pronounced necrosis and structural disruption in tumor tissues treated with higher acoustic pressures, which may directly contribute to the observed IFP reduction. Notably, in the 3 MPa and 5 MPa USMB groups, clusters of small, dark-stained cells were observed around necrotic tumor regions on H&E sections, morphologically resembling lymphocytes, suggesting potential immune cell infiltration. This phenomenon may be attributed to ultrasound-induced tumor cell lysis and the release of tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), subsequently triggering local inflammation and early immune responses [45]. Previous studies have demonstrated that therapy-induced necrosis can promote the release of DAMPs, activate dendritic cells, recruit effector T cells, and initiate adaptive immunity [46]. Although immunohistochemical confirmation was not performed in this study, our findings suggest that USMB may have the potential to modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, the observed consistency between CEUS signal attenuation, tissue damage, and IFP reduction provides mechanistic insight into the potential synergy between drug delivery enhancement and immune activation [47]. Further studies incorporating immune profiling and functional analysis are warranted to elucidate these mechanisms.

Additionally, we observed a significant regional difference in tumor IFP: the central region exhibited the highest IFP, followed by the inner half, and the outer quarter showed the lowest IFP, consistent with previous observations [35]. Following 3 MPa and 5 MPa USMB treatment, the reduction in IFP was most pronounced in the central region, possibly due to the higher density and immaturity of blood vessels in this area, making them more susceptible to mechanical disruption. This regional heterogeneity provides new insights into the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and the variability in therapeutic response. Further investigations into tumor perfusion and vascular density as potential biomarkers for predicting USMB therapeutic response could aid in optimizing treatment strategies and guiding individualized therapy. Moreover, our study quantitatively documented the relationship between ultrasound pressure and tumor IFP, providing a valuable basis for ultrasound parameter optimization.

Methodologically, our study introduced several innovations compared to previous research. While earlier studies mainly focused on the impact of USMB treatment on tumor perfusion [48, 49], we directly measured tumor IFP and analyzed its spatial distribution. By applying different ultrasound pressures (1–5 MPa) and conducting detailed IFP measurements, we systematically evaluated the influence of ultrasound parameters on treatment outcomes. This stratified analysis facilitates the precise identification of optimal therapeutic parameters, offering robust data to support future clinical applications.

Our findings support that adjusting USMB parameters can optimize tumor therapeutic outcomes. While 3 MPa and 5 MPa were most effective in reducing IFP, they also markedly decreased perfusion, which could impair drug delivery; in contrast, 2 MPa reduced IFP with minimal perfusion change. These results highlight the need to select parameters that balance IFP reduction with adequate perfusion to maximize therapeutic benefit. Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, it did not comprehensively assess the effects of different ultrasound pressures on other tumor biological characteristics, such as growth rate and metastatic potential, which may influence clinical applicability. Second, although a 2 MPa pressure was selected for the US-only group to avoid significant tissue damage, future studies should consider using 5 MPa ultrasound as a control to better evaluate the independent effects of ultrasound. Third, the direct impact of USMB treatment on drug delivery was not assessed in this study; future research should incorporate fluorescently labeled drugs (e.g., doxorubicin) to measure changes in drug distribution.

Image analysis also presents technical limitations. Variations in tumor size and depth may affect CEUS signal intensity due to differences in acoustic attenuation paths [50]. At 3 MPa, hyperechoic signals were observed in non-tumor tissues, possibly due to cavitation-induced tissue compression or microbubble aggregation altering local acoustic impedance [51]. These findings suggest that high acoustic pressure may also impact adjacent tissues, highlighting the need to control for confounding factors in future quantitative imaging analyses.

Future studies should also investigate the effects of various ultrasound parameters, such as pressure, frequency, pulse duration, and treatment intervals, to optimize individualized therapy. Moreover, the efficacy of USMB treatment in lowering IFP should be validated in different tumor types and stages, and its potential impact on tumor growth and metastasis should be assessed. The therapeutic parameters proposed in this study could be a reference for clinical applications, particularly for solid tumors refractory to conventional treatments. Further exploration of perfusion characteristics and vascular density as predictive biomarkers will also help refine patient selection and optimize therapeutic strategies. This study presents a novel approach to improving solid tumor treatment with significant potential for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study experimentally demonstrated that USMB therapy significantly reduces tumor IFP. At 2 MPa, IFP reduction occurred with minimal impact on perfusion, potentially favoring drug penetration, whereas higher pressures (3 MPa and 5 MPa) also reduced IFP but markedly decreased perfusion due to vascular disruption, which may hinder drug delivery (Fig. 11). The findings indicate that ultrasound treatment parameters, particularly the pressure levels, critically influence the balance between IFP reduction and perfusion preservation. These results highlight the importance of selecting parameters, such as 2 MPa, that lower IFP while maintaining adequate perfusion, providing a new non-invasive therapeutic strategy for solid tumors and offering experimental evidence for optimizing USMB parameters.
