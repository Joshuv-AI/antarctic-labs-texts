Radical-induced autoxidation dominates squalene oxidative aging under environmental conditions
Authors: Xinke Wang, Jillian P. Downey, Giulia Gobbi, William D. Fahy, Wanqi Wu, Linna Xie, Xingwang Hou, Hui Peng, Jonathan P. D. Abbatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ozone has been viewed as the principal oxidant of skin oils and other unsaturated surface films, but their fate under realistic indoor conditions remains poorly constrained. Here, we use squalene (SQ), a major component of skin lipids, to investigate the dominant oxidation pathways in occupied indoor environments and laboratory flow reactors. With exposure to indoor air, SQ decay and reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation accelerated over time, consistent with autoxidation. Laboratory experiments with ozone alone drove first-order SQ loss and humidity-dependent emissions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). In contrast, exposure to both ozone and •OH produced nonexponential SQ decays, higher ROS yields, a much stronger in-film •OH source, and condensed-phase products enriched in highly oxidized organic hydroperoxides that closely match those observed indoors. Unexpectedly, gas-phase •OH strongly suppressed VOC emissions. These results demonstrate that gas-phase radical-induced autoxidation governs SQ aging, challenging the prevailing ozone-centric view of indoor skin oil oxidation.

INTRODUCTION

People spend most of their time indoors, so chemical processes in the built environment can strongly influence human chemical exposure and health (1–4). Indoor surfaces are broadly coated with thin organic films derived from skin oils, cooking emissions, and dust, and these films serve both as reservoirs for contaminants and as reactive interfaces. The dominant oxidation pathways in such films matter because they control chemical lifetimes and determine whether oxidation products remain sequestered on the surfaces or are released to the air, thereby altering secondary pollutant burdens and exposure pathways.

Ozone transported indoors by ventilation and infiltration (5) has been widely regarded as the primary indoor oxidant (6). In particular, ozone reacts efficiently with unsaturated lipids, notably squalene (SQ), a highly unsaturated triterpene that constitutes about 10 to 12% of human sebum (7) and is readily transferred to indoor surfaces through skin contact, clothing, and shedding of skin flakes (8, 9). As a result, numerous laboratory and modeling studies have examined SQ ozonolysis in films and particles (10–23). Substantial gas-phase volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are formed, including primary products such as acetone, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one (6-MHO), and geranyl acetone (GA), along with secondary ozonolysis products such as 4-oxopentanal (4-OPA), 5-hydroxy-4-oxopentanal (5-HO), and 4-oxopentanoic acid (4-OA) (11, 13, 20, 24–29). These VOC products have a notable impact on indoor air quality. However, condensed-phase products remain much less well characterized. This is partly because prior studies have generally relied on analytical techniques with limited capability for detecting highly oxygenated, multifunctional compounds. The one prior study that used a soft ionization mass spectrometry (MS) technique identified a large number of oxygenated products, including many containing peroxy functional groups (18). There are no studies in the literature that have looked at both gas- and condensed-phase products under the same experimental conditions.

In addition to ozone, the gas-phase hydroxyl radical (•OH) is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to oxidation of surface contaminants (30–32). •OH can arise from several processes, including ozonolysis of alkenes (33) as well as human skin oils (34), and photolysis of HONO and formaldehyde (HCHO) in sunlit areas near windows (35–37). Measurements and estimates typically place indoor •OH concentrations at about 0.01 ppt with some higher reported values (0.16 to 0.8 ppt) approaching daytime tropospheric concentrations (34, 36–44). Recent work has examined •OH-initiated oxidation of SQ. Zeng et al. (12, 45) reported that •OH addition to C═C bonds in SQ nanodroplets initiates rapid autoxidation via Criegee intermediate chemistry, mainly producing secondary ozonides (SOZs) and carbon chain scission products. In that study, •OH was generated by UVA photolysis of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a protocol that is well suited to simulating daytime outdoor chemistry but may not capture typical indoor lighting conditions.

Despite extensive literature on SQ ozonolysis and growing attention to •OH-driven processes, SQ transformation pathways under realistic indoor conditions remain poorly constrained. Specifically, little is known about how SQ films evolve when simultaneously exposed to multiple oxidants, such as combined ozone and •OH in the absence of UVA illumination, which is common for many indoor environments. To address this gap, we performed field exposure measurements of SQ films in a residential condominium and an office, and complementary laboratory experiments under well-controlled conditions where SQ films were exposed either to ozone alone or to a combination of ozone and •OH. As outlined in Fig. 1, we quantified SQ depletion, formation of condensed-phase oxidation products, in-film reactive oxygen species (ROS) including •OH generation, and VOC emissions to the gas phase. Together, these measurements provide a comprehensive assessment of SQ film oxidation under realistic indoor conditions, clarifying the respective roles of ozone and •OH. Our major finding is that •OH-induced autoxidation dominates SQ loss for typical indoor conditions, leading to substantial ROS generation.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

SQ loss and ROS formation

Thin SQ films in petri dishes were exposed to four distinct environments: (i) in the bedroom of a condominium, (ii) inside a desk drawer exposed to ambient office air, (iii) ambient level ozone (∼15 ppb) in a flow reactor, and (iv) a combination of ozone (∼12 ppb) and •OH (∼0.12 ppt) in the flow reactor. Under these conditions, the temporal evolution of SQ loss, ROS formation, and the ROS-to-SQ-loss ratio (ROS/ΔSQ) was quantified to elucidate the oxidation process of the SQ films under realistic indoor conditions. As controls, no measurable ROS was produced from clean petri dishes exposed in the office or condominium, or from SQ films exposed to zero air for 34 days.

As shown in Fig. 2A, the loss rate of SQ placed in the condominium accelerated with time, especially after ∼10 days of exposure. Concurrently, the rate of ROS formation in the films accelerated. Notably, the indoor ozone levels were very low (<2 ppb) even though outdoor ozone could reach ∼40 ppb (fig. S1). Together, these observations strongly indicate that autoxidation, rather than direct reaction with ozone, dominated SQ degradation in this environment. In particular, direct reaction of SQ with ozone is expected to produce first-order decay kinetics, whereas autoxidation can accelerate with time as radical precursors accumulate within the substrate (30, 46, 47) and as added oxygenated functional groups facilitate subsequent intramolecular H-shift reactions (48). Oxidation proceeded even more rapidly in the office setting, where the SQ again did not decay in a first-order manner (Fig. 2B). In addition, films of different thicknesses produced comparable ROS concentrations, and the timing of the ROS maximum was also similar across all film thicknesses (fig. S2A). However, previous work reports that the reactive-diffusive length of ozone in SQ particles is on the order of ∼1 nm (10), far smaller than the film thicknesses used here, implying that ozone-driven chemistry is confined to a very shallow near-surface region and cannot explain the observed thickness-independent ROS concentrations. SQ was fully consumed approximately 2 days before ROS reached its peak (fig. S2B), indicating that continued oxidation of SQ-derived products sustained ROS formation. The values of ROS/ΔSQ for the different film thicknesses were similar (fig. S2C), consistent with autoxidation occurring throughout the films rather than being limited to a near-surface layer. In addition, most of these ratios were in the range of 1.0 to 1.5, suggesting that the consumption of each SQ molecule was accompanied by the average accumulation of approximately one to one and a half peroxide-equivalent ROS species in the condensed phase. This ratio should not be interpreted as a direct radical chain length, because the ferrous oxidation in xylenol orange version 2 (FOX2) assay measures accumulated peroxide-equivalent ROS via a variety of oxidation mechanisms, and some ROS are decomposing during the reaction period.

To further interrogate the autoxidation mechanism, SQ films were exposed in the flow reactor either to ozone alone at ambient levels or to combined ozone and •OH. As described above, the •OH mixing ratios are low, about 0.12 ppt, which is near the upper range encountered in indoor environments. As shown in Fig. 2C, SQ decay in the presence of ozone alone was well described by a single-exponential function, indicating first-order kinetics. Although ROS did form within the SQ film, the ROS/ΔSQ was somewhat lower than that observed in the indoor experiments. At elevated ozone levels, ROS accumulated more rapidly and reached higher maxima as a function of ozone mixing ratio (fig. S3), yet even at 300 ppb, the maximum ROS surface concentration remained lower than indoors (Fig. 2B). In contrast, exposure to combined ozone and •OH led to much faster SQ decay and ROS formation, accompanied by a higher ROS/ΔSQ (Fig. 2D). Under these O3 + •OH conditions, the SQ decay could no longer be captured by a single-exponential fit, consistent with the onset of autoxidation chain chemistry. Collectively, these results implicate •OH, rather than ozone alone, as the primary initiator of SQ decay under indoor-relevant conditions.

We also examined ozone and •OH oxidation of SQ films under dry conditions (RH ∼0%). As shown in Fig. 2 (E and F), RH levels did not measurably affect SQ loss, whether the films were exposed to ozone alone or to combined ozone and •OH. However, ozonolysis under dry conditions produced substantially more ROS, resulting in a much higher ROS/ΔSQ. This behavior is consistent with the known fate of Criegee intermediates. In humid air, they preferentially react with water to ultimately form VOCs, whereas under dry conditions, they react more readily with organic alcohols or acids to yield organic hydroperoxides (11, 15) or with carbonyls to form SOZs (49, 50). As will be described in more detail in the reaction mechanism section, Zeng et al. (12) showed that •OH addition to C═C bonds can generate tertiary peroxy radicals bearing β-hydroxyl groups. These species can decompose to Criegee intermediates that drive autoxidation in unsaturated lipid droplets such as SQ, forming SOZs and carbon chain scission products, and yielding an effective reaction probability (γeff) much larger than unity (12). In that work, RH influenced the product distribution, although the overall autoxidation rate, reflected by the uptake coefficient, remained relatively insensitive to water. By contrast, in our experiments, ROS formation during •OH oxidation of SQ films was essentially insensitive to RH. This is in contrast to the strong RH dependence observed for Criegee intermediates, suggesting that •OH-initiated autoxidation proceeds via a pathway with less water sensitivity.

Oxidation products in distinct environments

To elucidate the chemical pathways underlying SQ oxidation under realistic indoor conditions, the molecular composition of the resulting oxidation products was characterized. Figure 3A presents the electrospray ionization (ESI)(+)-MS spectra of fresh and chemically aged SQ films, while CH2-Kendrick mass defect plots for products with relative signal intensities >5% of the most intense peak are shown in Fig. 3 (B to E).

Notably, peaks shown in (A) include products with different carbon numbers as well as different oxygen numbers; not all peaks in the m/z 400 to 650 region correspond to C30 products (table S1). (B to E) CH2-Kendrick mass defect plots of oxidation products with signal intensities >5% of the most intense peak. Symbol size is proportional to the square root of signal intensity, and color indicates the number of oxygen atoms in each compound. Note that the film thickness is 127 nm, except for the Condo experiment (255 nm). It should be noted that because SQ ionizes poorly in ESI positive mode, its signal intensity is substantially lower than that of its oxidation products. The most abundant peaks in the m/z 400 to 650 range (i.e., C30H50On) were primarily detected as [M + NH4]+ ions.

Following aging in both the condominium and the office environment, the dominant products are highly oxygenated C30H50On species with n ranging from 1 to 15 (Fig. 3A and table S1), noting that SQ has the formula C30H50. Neutral loss tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis identifies these species predominantly as organic hydroperoxides (ROOHs) as shown in fig. S4 (49, 51). As described below, this pattern is consistent with repeated H-abstraction/O2-addition chemistry leading primarily to ROOH formation (30).

As shown in Fig. 3 (B and C) and table S1, a few low-intensity carbon chain scission products are also detected, with both their number and abundance higher in the office, consistent with the elevated ozone concentrations in that setting relative to the condominium. Many carbon chain scission products appear in homologous series with successive oxygen additions, indicating that they undergo secondary autoxidation and evolve into increasingly oxygenated species. Notably, certain highly oxygenated autoxidation products, such as C30H50O9, exhibit even higher signal intensities than low-oxygen species such as C30H50O (Fig. 3C).

The mass defect plots are distinctly different with ozone exposure for 5 days. At that point, when nearly all SQ is consumed, products containing one or two additional oxygen atoms dominate the distribution (Fig. 3D). For example, species with a single added oxygen (C30H50O) were the most abundant, whereas more highly oxygenated products were comparatively minor. Increasing ozone concentration produced substantially more carbon chain scission products (fig. S5). This pattern indicates that direct ozonolysis competes strongly with the formation of highly oxidized species and limits the extent of autoxidation, consistent with the observation that SQ decay in ozone can be described by a single exponential function. In contrast, exposure to a combination of ozone and •OH yields a product distribution more closely resembling that observed under office conditions, with both numerous carbon chain scission products and abundant highly oxygenated autoxidation products (Fig. 3, C to E). This indicates that oxidation of SQ films in the office (and condo) environments is likely dominated by •OH-induced autoxidation.

Complementary identification of oxidation products was obtained using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (fig. S6). As shown in fig. S6B, common ozonolysis products such as aldehydes (peaks at ∼9.8 ppm) (52) are far more prominent when SQ films are exposed to ozone alone in the flow reactor compared to reaction in the office or via combined ozone and •OH. In contrast, the peak observed at 8.1 ppm is much stronger under indoor office conditions and ozone plus •OH exposure. We assign this peak to the peroxidic proton of organic hydroperoxides based on 1H NMR studies of small aliphatic hydroperoxides (53, 54). While all oxidized film spectra show the same peak, the peak structures and relative intensities of the SQ film exposed to a realistic indoor environment (the office drawer) are much more similar to that exposed to •OH and ozone than that exposed to ozone alone. This provides additional evidence in real indoor environments that •OH-driven oxidation or autoxidation is an important reaction pathway for SQ films.

Chemical product evolution of SQ films

As shown in fig. S7 (A and B), the intensities of representative products such as C30H50O, C30H50O2, C30H50O5, and C30H50O6 increase to a maximum and then decline sharply in the office setting. A similar rise-and-fall pattern is observed with combined ozone and •OH exposure, whereas under ozone alone, the signals of these products change more slowly (fig. S7, C to F). This contrast is consistent with rapid autoxidation of low-oxygen ROOHs to more highly oxygenated ROOHs when •OH or mixed oxidants are present. For example, ROS concentrations peak after about 11 days of indoor exposure (Fig. 2B), whereas low-oxygen ROOHs reach their maximum at around 7 days (fig. S7A). The time lag reflects continued growth in the intensity of highly oxygenated ROOHs such as C30H50O14 and C30H50O15, a progression that is also evident in the flow-reactor •OH oxidation experiments (fig. S7, G and H).

Further insight into chemical evolution was obtained by placing an aged SQ film from the office environment into a dark flow reactor without additional oxidants. The film had been exposed indoors for 9 days, at which point almost all SQ had already been consumed (fig. S8A). During the subsequent 9 days in the dark reactor, total ROS concentrations remained nearly constant, with less than 5% loss (fig. S8B). Despite this apparent stability in bulk ROS, substantial compositional evolution continued within the film. Low-oxygen ROOHs such as C30H50O and C30H50O2 progressively decreased and were eventually depleted, while highly oxygenated ROOHs continued to increase before themselves reaching a maximum and then slowly declining (fig. S8, C and D). This behavior indicates that autoxidation and redistribution among ROOH species can proceed even in the absence of the parent SQ and without further oxidant input.

Formation of VOCs

Small chamber experiments were conducted to investigate the formation of VOCs during SQ oxidation. As shown in fig. S9 (A and B), exposure of SQ films to ozone or to ozone plus •OH produced acetone, 6-MHO, GA, 4-OPA, 5-HO, 4-OA, and several other VOCs, consistent with previous observations (11, 13, 20, 24–29). Figure 4 shows the molar ratio of total VOC production to SQ loss (VOCs/ΔSQ), the molar ratio of SQ-derived carbon released to the gas phase to SQ loss (SQlg/ΔSQC), as well as ROS/ΔSQ, when SQ films were exposed to ozone alone (∼25 ppb) at ∼45% RH. All ratios increase with ozone exposure level, with VOCs/ΔSQ exceeding unity, SQlg/ΔSQC increasing from 0.14 to 0.20, and ROS/ΔSQ increasing from 0.84 to 1.02. When RH was reduced to ∼0%, VOC formation was substantially suppressed: VOCs/ΔSQ decreased to 0.18 to 0.35 and SQlg/ΔSQC dropped to 0.02 to 0.05 (fig. S10). As noted earlier, this suppression reflects the reduced rate of reaction of Criegee intermediates with water, which otherwise produces aldehydes that are released to the gas phase.

Molar ratios of products to SQ loss.

(A) The molar ratio of total VOCs formed to SQ loss (VOC/ΔSQ), (B) the fraction of SQ-derived carbon released to the gas phase per SQ loss (SQlg/ΔSQC), and (C) the molar ratio of total ROS formed to SQ loss under different ozone and •OH exposure levels (RH ∼45%).

In contrast, when SQ films were exposed to ozone (∼8.5 ppb) together with •OH (∼0.09 ppt) at ∼45% RH, VOC formation was strongly reduced, yielding VOCs/ΔSQ values of only 0.06 to 0.07 and SQlg/ΔSQC values of 0.013 to 0.014 (Fig. 4, A and B). Under •OH oxidation conditions, surface ROS formation was enhanced, with ROS/ΔSQ ratios of 1.1 to 1.5, whereas under ozone-only conditions, ROS/ΔSQ generally remained below 1 (Fig. 4C). It should be noted that acetone, the dominant VOC produced from the ozonolysis of tetramethylethylene (TME) used to generate •OH, is excluded from these comparisons. In the ozone-only oxidation of SQ, acetone accounts for ∼39% of the total measured VOC molecules and ∼26% of the SQ-derived carbon released to the gas phase. Even considering this, VOC production from SQ exposed to combined ozone and •OH remains very low (VOCs/ΔSQ ≈ 0.10 to 0.11 and SQlg/ΔSQC ≈ 0.018 to 0.019). Together, these results demonstrate that •OH-induced autoxidation channels SQ oxidation toward the formation of highly oxygenated ROOHs that remain in the condensed phase, whereas VOC production is driven primarily by direct ozonolysis under realistic indoor conditions.

In-film •OH measurements

The formation of •OH in SQ films was quantified as the films oxidized in the office setting and in flow-reactor experiments with ozone alone or with combined ozone and •OH. Benzoic acid (BA) was used as an •OH probe, and •OH concentrations were derived from the measured formation of 2-, 3-, and 4-hydroxybenzoic acids (HBAs) (55, 56).

SQ films were first aged for about 9 days in a dark office drawer, after which a BA solution in chloroform was added and mixed with the aged film. After solvent evaporation, the films were placed back in the office drawer for further exposure. Fresh SQ films containing BA were placed in the office at the same time for comparison. As shown in Fig. 5A, the preaged films produced substantially higher HBA levels than the fresh films, indicating •OH formation under dark conditions. This in-film •OH generation is consistent with previous observations for methyl linolenate films aged in the same office environment (30), and it also aligns with reports of •OH formation in SOA extracts rich in ROOH species (57, 58).

(B) HBA production in fresh or preoxidized films exposed for 4 days to either O3 (∼15 ppb, RH ∼21%) or O3 (∼12 ppb) + •OH (∼0.12 ppt, RH ∼21%), followed by 30 hours in a dark flow reactor without gas-phase oxidants. *Not detected.

In addition, SQ films were oxidized for 4 days by exposure to either O3 (∼15 ppb, RH ∼21%) or O3 (∼12 ppb) plus •OH (∼0.12 ppt, RH ∼21%), and then treated with a BA solution. After solvent evaporation, the films were transferred to a dark flow reactor for 30 hours without gas-phase oxidants. As shown in Fig. 5B, •OH was detected in both cases, with much higher levels in the films previously oxidized by the combination of ozone and •OH. The resulting HBA concentrations, and thus the inferred •OH levels, track closely with the ROS formed within the SQ films (Fig. 2, C and D).

The •OH generated in the ozonolysis experiment did not lead to rapid autoxidation, likely because ozone had already consumed most of the C═C double bonds in the SQ film (30) and in-film •OH levels were comparatively low. Nevertheless, the products with additional oxygen atoms (C30H50On) detected in the ozone-oxidized SQ film (Fig. 3D and fig. S11) are likely formed through reactions initiated by these in-film •OH radicals (Fig. 5B).

Reaction mechanism

The results presented above are consistent with SQ undergoing extensive radical-driven autoxidation rather than ozonolysis under indoor conditions. Once initiated, this autoxidative chain reaction generates large quantities of ROS and highly oxygenated ROOHs that remain predominantly in the condensed phase and continue to evolve even after the parent SQ is completely consumed. Notably, ROOH formation is largely insensitive to relative humidity (RH), in contrast to Criegee intermediate–driven pathways that depend strongly on water vapor. Substantially higher in-film •OH levels were detected in SQ films oxidized by •OH compared to those oxidized by ozone, corresponding to enhanced ROS production and accelerated oxidation. Collectively, these observations indicate that Criegee intermediates are not the primary drivers of SQ autoxidation indoors. Instead, conventional radical-initiated hydrogen-abstraction pathways (48, 59) likely dominate SQ film oxidation under real indoor conditions.

In particular, the indoor chemistry field has traditionally assumed that ozone is the dominant oxidant in dark environments and that ozone-driven pathways control surface reactions (6). Because SQ contains six ozone-reactive C═C double bonds, previous studies have primarily emphasized ozonolysis and Criegee intermediate chemistry, including the formation of carbon chain scission products, ROOHs, SOZs, and, in some cases, gas-phase ROS (10–23, 60, 61). An important advance in the field came from work showing that •OH addition to SQ C═C bonds in nanodroplets can initiate rapid autoxidation through carbon-carbon bond breakage leading to Criegee chemistry (12, 45). Both the ozonolysis and the •OH addition pathways are shown in Fig. 6 and fig. S12, which present simplified and detailed reaction mechanisms, respectively.

More detailed oxidation mechanisms are shown in figs. S12 and S14.

In contrast, a key observation in our work is that the ROOH products largely retain their C30 structure, which argues against carbon-carbon bond breakage, either via ozonolysis or via the β-hydroxy tertiary peroxy radicals and ROOH molecules formed via the addition of •OH to a double bond in SQ. Our earlier work demonstrated that for lipids containing highly reactive bis-allylic hydrogens (i.e., methyl linolenate and cooking oil), even extremely low •OH concentrations can induce rapid autoxidation leading to radical chain reactions and abundant ROOH formation (30). We believe that the present study extends this hydrogen abstraction paradigm to SQ, which contains allylic hydrogens that are generally less reactive toward abstraction (59).

Given the large number of reactive sites in the SQ molecule, it is expected that both •OH addition and H abstraction–initiated pathways will take place at the start of the reaction (Fig. 6 and fig. S12). We believe that either initiation pathway—abstraction or addition—is consistent with data from the early reaction period in our experiments where we observed high intensity ratios (>7) of even-oxygen to odd-oxygen products, such as C30H50O4/C30H50O3 and C30H50O6/C30H50O5 (fig. S13). While the abstraction pathway would always lead to an even number of O atoms being added to the molecule, the addition pathway is more complex, proceeding initially via a β-hydroxy tertiary RO2• radical. That RO2• radical could react intramolecularly via H-atom shifts, forming ROOH species and, after O2 addition, RO2•. If chain termination occurs via RO2• abstracting H from a H-C-OOH entity, it would form a carbonyl and •OH (48). The resulting C30 product would have an even number of O atoms. On the other hand, if the β-hydroxy tertiary RO2• radical were to abstract an H-atom in an intermolecular manner, the product would have an odd number of O atoms and 52 H atoms. We did not observe H52 species in our mass spectra.

The even-to-odd ratio of oxygenated products declines as the reaction proceeds (fig. S13). This is likely because odd-oxygen products may arise through reactions such as Russell-type RO2• + RO2• chemistry, which can generate alcohols and corresponding carbonyls (see table S1 and fig. S12), as well as via H-abstraction reactions by RO• radicals formed from ROOH decomposition (30). Moreover, as oxygenated functional groups accumulate, multiple intramolecular H-shift reactions leading to hydroperoxyl carbonyls may become increasingly favorable, further sustaining autoxidation (fig. S14) (48, 62).

At later stages of the reaction, some of these carbonyl-containing products may further react with ROOHs to form peroxyhemiacetals (fig. S14) (63), such as the minor C33 species that likely arise via reaction with acetone. Reactions involving Criegee intermediates and carbonyls may also contribute to longer carbon chain products through SOZ formation, although SOZs were not specifically identified in this study.

Last, we note that the mechanism of •OH formation in the films is unclear and worthy of more study. As mentioned above, one possibility is formation via H-atom abstraction by RO2• from H-C-OOH that would lead to formation of •OH, along with a carbonyl and ROOH (48). Alternatively, ROOH species may decompose to form RO• and •OH. This latter reaction is unlikely to occur thermally given the stability of the peroxide bond (64) but it may occur via another mechanism, perhaps heterogeneously. Both possibilities are now illustrated in fig. S12.

Environmental implications

These findings have implications for indoor air chemistry and human exposure. Indoor surfaces act as long-lived chemical reservoirs and reactors for highly oxidized organic material (65), sustaining oxidative processes over timescales of days. However, our findings indicate that radical-induced autoxidation dominates SQ oxidation under realistic indoor conditions, so that most SQ-derived carbon is retained in surface films rather than being released as VOCs. By contrast, ozone-initiated processes strongly favor molecular fragmentation and volatilization. Consequently, while the many prior gas-phase measurements of VOC production give an indication of inhalation exposure, they underestimate the complete oxidative burden in occupied indoor environments. The accumulation and slow transformation of SQ-derived ROOHs and related products on walls, flooring, furnishings, and other surfaces may influence human exposure to reactive species through dermal contact and nondietary ingestion pathways (66). As demonstrated previously, in-film •OH radical production can drive chemical transformations of low-volatility contaminants, such as bis-phenols (30). More broadly, these results indicate that indoor chemistry models should explicitly incorporate radical-initiated multiphase autoxidation to accurately describe surface chemistry and exposure dynamics, and they underscore the importance of studying oxidative transformations under realistic indoor conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

All chemicals were used as received. SQ (≥98%), squalane (99%), chloroform (≥99.8%), 1-propanol (≥99.5%), TME (≥99%), tert-butyl hydroperoxide solution (BHPO, 70 wt % in H2O), ammonium iron(II) sulfate hexahydrate (99%), xylenol orange tetrasodium salt (ACS reagent), BA (≥99.5%), 3-hydroxybenzoic acid (99%), 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (certified reference materials), and ammonium formate (≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Peroxide-free methanol (sequencing grade) and sulfuric acid (certified ACS plus) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, certified reference material) was obtained from Millipore Sigma, and 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (99%) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Film preparation and extractions

SQ films were prepared by pipetting 0.5 ml of SQ dissolved in chloroform (0.1% or 0.05%, v/v) into clean glass petri dishes. After complete solvent evaporation, the coatings were immediately transferred to either a flow reactor or a small reaction chamber for controlled oxidation experiments. To assess oxidation under genuine indoor conditions, additional coated petri dishes were placed in a dark drawer in an office or in a residential condo room. For the condo experiments, the petri dishes were sealed with Parafilm, placed in a sealed glass container, and then transported for 10 min to the field site. After the designated exposure period, the petri dishes were sealed and returned to the laboratory. The oxidized films were extracted using 1.5 ml of 1-propanol, and the resulting extracts were subsequently diluted for ROS assays and MS analysis.

Flow reactor experiments

SQ film oxidation was performed in a cylindrical quartz flow reactor (6.5 cm internal diameter, 150 cm length) at room temperature (22° to 25°C). The airflow rate was set to 5 liters min−1, with RH controlled at either 0 or 21%. Humidified air was generated by passing zero air (AADCO 737 zero air generator) through a bubbler containing distilled water (18.2 MΩ·cm). Ozone was produced using a quartz cell–based ozone generator equipped with a Pen-Ray lamp, by passing 50 ml min−1 of zero air through the generator. Ozone concentration and RH at the reactor outlet were monitored using a UV photometric ozone analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Model 49C) and a hygrometer (Vaisala, USA), respectively. For each ozone oxidation experiment, eight SQ-coated petri dishes were placed in the reactor simultaneously.

In the •OH oxidation experiment, gas-phase •OH was generated via the reaction of excess TME with ozone. Ozone concentrations in the reactor decreased from approximately 15 to 12 ppb due to this reaction. TME was supplied using a glass diffusion cell (VICI Metronics, USA) at room temperature. The steady-state •OH concentration ([OH]ss) was calculated using the following equation (67, 68)[OH]ss=kTME+O3×α×[TME]×[O3]kTME+OH×[TME](1)where kTME + O3 is the rate constant for the reaction of TME with O3, with an •OH yield of α (1.0 was adopted) (69), and kTME + OH is the rate constant for the reaction of TME with •OH. Both values were taken from the Master Chemical Mechanism (MCM v3.3.1; mcm.york.ac.uk). [O3] and [TME] represent the ozone and TME gas-phase concentrations, respectively. In previous work, we have validated this method of •OH quantification via observation of gas-phase isoprene decay in the flow reactor using a proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometer (30). With a small concentration gradient of •OH along the reactor length (due to loss of ozone), only four coated petri dishes were positioned near the reactor outlet. Two samples were collected from this region for each experiment.

Small reaction chamber experiments

To analyze VOC production, two SQ-coated petri dishes with a total SQ surface area of 47.5 cm−2 and a thickness of 211 nm were placed within a small reaction chamber (20). Zero air of 1.8 liters min−1 with an RH of either 0% or 45% was continuously introduced into the chamber. For the ozone oxidation experiments, ozone concentrations ranged from 15 to 115 ppb. In the •OH oxidation experiments, the steady-state •OH concentration was estimated to be ∼0.09 ppt, with ozone maintained at 8.5 ppb, based on the calculation using Eq. 1.

Gas-phase products were monitored in real time using a Vocus proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometer (PTR-MS). Background signals were determined by zero-air measurements collected at the beginning and end of each experiment. Quantification was performed through both liquid-phase calibrations with authentic standards and gas-phase calibrations using a certified reference gas cylinder (Apel-Riemer Inc.) for each experimental run (20). Mass spectra were averaged over 2-min intervals and processed using the Tofware software package (version 3.2.5) integrated in Igor Pro (Wavemetrics).

Indoor environment oxidation experiments

Indoor oxidation experiments involving SQ films were conducted in two real-world indoor environments. One set of indoor air exposure experiments took place in a dark desk drawer in an office in the Chemistry Department at the University of Toronto. The room temperature was maintained between 22° and 25°C. Experiments were performed from 16 January 2025 to 13 February 2025. The daily average outdoor ozone mixing ratio during this period was approximately 23.6 ppb, based on data from the local air quality monitoring station (https://airqualityontario.com). Ozone measurements indicated that the concentration in the drawer was about 49% of outdoor levels, resulting in an average indoor ozone concentration of approximately 12 ppb. Typical human occupancy in the office was five people during weekdays.

A second oxidation experiment was performed in a condominium bedroom from 2 February 2025 to 7 March 2025, with the room temperature maintained at 23°C. The residence was occupied by a single adult, and the SQ films were placed away from windows to minimize the influence of sunlight. Because of the low air exchange rate in this environment, indoor ozone concentrations were substantially lower than in the office setting. Even when outdoor ozone reached ∼40 ppb, the indoor levels in the condo remained below 2 ppb (fig. S1). Clean petri dishes were deployed in parallel as controls.

ROS and •OH concentration measurements

ROS, including ROOHs, were quantified using the FOX2 assay (30, 70). In this method, ROS oxidize Fe2+ to Fe3+, which subsequently forms a complex with xylenol orange, producing a measurable color change (71). The absorbance was recorded at 560 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics USB2000+). The FOX2 reagent was prepared by dissolving 76 mg of xylenol orange tetrasodium salt and 98 mg of ammonium iron (II) sulfate hexahydrate in 100 ml of 250 mM sulfuric acid. This solution was then mixed with 900 ml of peroxide-free methanol containing 1 g of BHT. For each measurement, 1 ml of FOX2 reagent was added to 0.5 ml of diluted sample extracts. The mixture was incubated for 30 min before spectrophotometric analysis.

Hydroxyl radicals were detected using BA as a trapping agent, yielding hydroxylated products (2-, 3-, and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid) in typical isomeric distributions (56). Specifically, 0.5 ml of BA (4 mM in chloroform) was added to both fresh and oxidized SQ films. After complete solvent evaporation, the films were placed in either a dark drawer or a flow reactor for reaction. Postexposure, the coatings were extracted sequentially with 0.75 ml of methanol followed by 0.75 ml of 1-propanol. The extracts were further diluted with methanol and analyzed by ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC, Dionex UltiMate 3000) coupled with a TSQ Endura Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Additional details are provided elsewhere (30).

SQ and its oxidation product measurements

SQ was quantified by GC-MS using an Agilent 7890A GC system equipped with an Agilent HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) coupled to an Agilent 5975C single-quadrupole mass spectrometer operated in selected ion monitoring mode with an electron ionization source. Helium was used as the carrier gas in a constant pressure mode at 12.05 psi. Splitless injections were performed with an injection volume of 1 μl and an inlet temperature of 250°C. The GC oven temperature was programmed as follows: initial temperature of 60°C held for 3 min, ramped to 150°C at 25°C min−1 and held for 5 min, then increased to 280°C at 50°C min−1 and held for 10 min. The ion signal at mass/charge ratio (m/z) 69 was monitored for SQ, and m/z 57 was used for the squalane internal standard.

For mass spectral measurements of oxidation products, the extracts were diluted fivefold with 4 mM ammonium formate (NH4HCOO) in methanol. Detailed descriptions of the mass spectrometric analysis are provided elsewhere (30). Briefly, the diluted samples were analyzed using a Thermo Q-Exactive Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer (HRMS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with heated ESI in positive mode. The HRMS operated at a resolving power of 70,000 (at m/z 200) and scanned over a mass range of m/z 100 to 1500. Samples were directly infused at a flow rate of 20 μl min−1. In addition, extracts were analyzed using HRMS coupled with a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was performed with a C18 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.5 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific), using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (solvent B), with a 1 μl injection volume.

Data analysis was performed using Thermo Fisher Scientific FreeStyle 1.6. Molecular formulas were assigned to detected signals using the following criteria: 13C0-2C1–100H1–200O1–100 N0–1Na0–1, a mass tolerance of 3 ppm, element-count heuristics, and isotope-pattern matching (72). Under these constraints, each assigned peak yielded a single chemically reasonable molecular formula. For the specific identification of organic hydroperoxides, a refined direct infusion MS/MS approach was used, following established protocols (49, 51). Analyses were performed using a Thermo TSQ Endura triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with ESI(+) in neutral loss scan mode. Hydroperoxides were confirmed by monitoring a neutral loss of 51 Da, corresponding to the elimination of H2O2 and NH3 from the ammoniated precursor ions [M + NH4]+.

1H NMR measurements

Quantitative 1H-qNMR measurements were performed to analyze SQ and its oxidation products. Each SQ film was extracted using 500 μl of a 0.3 mM dimethyl sulfone (DMSO2) solution in CDCl3, which served as the internal standard. The extracts were transferred to 3-mm NMR tubes (Norell) and analyzed on an Agilent 500-MHz DD2 spectrometer. Key acquisition parameters included the following: 9.6 kHz spectral width, 128 scans, a 25-s relaxation delay, 4.5 s acquisition time, and a 45° pulse angle. Spectra were manually phase corrected and baseline corrected using the Whittaker smoother algorithm in MestreNova. The DMSO2 signal at 3.0 ppm was used as the chemical shift reference, and all analyte integrals were normalized to the integrated DMSO2 signal for quantitative comparisons.
