Right Ventricular Apical Hypoplasia With a Novel Morphologic Presentation
Authors: Gabriela Meléndez-Ramírez, Sergio Alfonso Patrón-Chi, Felipe Sebastián Figueroa-Galván, José Martín Alanís-Naranjo, María Fernanda González-Nájera, Miguel Ángel Cruz-Marmolejo, Aloha Meave-González

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight ventricular apical hypoplasia is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the failure of the right ventricular (RV) apical portion to develop.Case SummaryA 9-year-old boy presented with chronic cyanosis and digital clubbing. Multimodal imaging revealed a severely hypoplastic, dysplastic RV with lobulated contours. Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated a unique compensatory phenomenon, where the left ventricular apex was elongated and projected rightward, creating “Christmas hat” morphology.DiscussionThis case demonstrates a ventricular configuration that has not been previously described: a RV apical hypoplasia characterized by an elongated apical portion of the left ventricle. This elongation likely occurs in compensation for the apical agenesis of the RV. Identifying this “Christmas hat” sign could be helpful in diagnosing this rare form of RV hypoplasia.Take-Home MessageHypoplastic RV with elongation of the apical portion of the left ventricle toward the lateral wall of the RV, associated with an aneurysm of the interventricular septum, is described.

History of Presentation

A 9-year-old boy was admitted for further evaluation of a ventricular septal defect on echocardiography, but with a limited acoustic window. On admission, he had tachycardia (119 beats/min), tachypnea (24 beats/min), and low peripheral oxygen saturation in room air (86%).Take-Home Messages•Cardiac magnetic resonance is the gold standard to identify the hallmark RV trabecular hypoplasia and rule out mimics like arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy or Uhl's anomaly.•Management strategies depend on RV compliance. Past Medical History

Medical history included clubbing and cyanosis since infancy, with no family history of congenital heart disease.

Differential Diagnosis

In a pediatric patient with cyanosis and heart murmur, the differential diagnosis includes tricuspid atresia, Ebstein's anomaly, pulmonary stenosis, and tetralogy of Fallot.

Investigations

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated a hypoplastic right ventricle (RV) with sharply lobed contours and an underdeveloped apical portion (Figure 2). The apical left ventricular portion was elongated and directed toward the right, coursing anterior to the hypoplastic RV and reaching free wall. The inferior ventricular septum showed an aneurysm with bidirectional protrusion and right-to-left shunt (Figure 2, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4). Management

Further evaluation and management were proposed, but relatives denied any intervention and opted for palliative care.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged symptom-free and prescribed ambulatory oxygen. At the 4-year follow-up, the patient remained stable without any cardiac-related readmission.

Discussion

Right ventricular hypoplasia (RVH) is an extremely rare congenital heart defect characterized by the underdevelopment of the RV trabecular component, with preservation of the inlet and outlet portions.1 First described in 1950, its prevalence remains unknown because of the scarcity of reported cases.1,2 Unlike pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum, isolated RV hypoplasia (IRVH) presents with patent tricuspid and pulmonary valves, although the tricuspid annulus may be small.1

The RV is hypoplastic and dysplastic, lacking the normal triangular and tripartite configuration due to failure of development of the apical region. Tricuspid dysplasia and interventricular aneurysm can be considered as part of this entity. The LV projecting to the right side evokes a “Christmas hat” and probably occurs to compensate for the absence of the apical portion of RV. To our knowledge, no similar cases have been published with this configuration.

The clinical spectrum of IRVH is broad. Severe forms present in the neonatal period with cyanosis and congestive heart failure, often associated with high mortality.1,2 Conversely, mild forms—likely representing the subset to which this patient belongs—may remain asymptomatic until adolescence or adulthood.1 In these cases, symptoms such as exertional dyspnea, cyanosis, and clubbing fingers are driven by a right-to-left shunt at the atrial level due to low RV compliance.1,3

Mechanisms that condition restrictive RV physiology include myocardial hypertrophy (with myocardial fiber disruption) and endomyocardial fibroelastosis. CMR showed diffuse subendocardial delayed enhancement of the RV, a mechanism responsible for impaired compliance and may predispose to increased intraventricular pressures, thereby explaining the right-to-left flow through the ventricular septal defect.4 A hemodynamic hallmark finding in IRVH is the “dip-and-plateau” pattern in the RV pressure waveform and a prominent “a” wave in the right atrium, reflecting the restriction to RV filling.3

Diagnosis relies on a multimodal approach. Echocardiography is the first-line tool, typically showing a small RV cavity and an atrial septal defect.1 However, owing to the acoustic window and/or geometric alterations, in this case, their usefulness was limited.3 CMR is considered the gold standard for assessing RV function and tissue characterization.3 Additionally, CMR helps identify associated anomalies; for instance, some reports have linked IRVH with LV noncompaction, suggesting a possible common genetic or developmental etiology.1,5

Differential diagnoses include structural abnormalities in the RV, such as Ebstein's anomaly, Uhl's anomaly, and arrhythmogenic RV dysplasia. The key finding in Ebstein's anomaly is the apical displacement of the tricuspid valve, resulting in severe tricuspid regurgitation.6 In Uhl's anomaly, there is an absence of the free-wall myocardium, which causes severely dilated ventricles with an absence of contractility.7

Arrhythmogenic dysplasia is characterized by fibrofatty replacement of the myocardium (subepicardial-intramyocardial or transmural distribution) with phenotypes that may involve the LV.8 One entity that must be distinguished in the diagnostic approach is agenesis of the apical portion of the LV. Some of the anatomical characteristics include a truncated, spherical LV with the interventricular septum displaced to the right. As compensation, the RV elongates toward the absent portion of the LV, with fatty infiltration of the space between the 2 ventricles. Physiopathologically, it has a heterogeneous clinical presentation. CMR is useful for identifying the anatomy and changes in the myocardium.9

Although the exact etiology is unknown, a genetic component is suspected. Familial clustering has been reported, with some studies suggesting an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance.1 Approximately 24% of patients may have a family history of the disease.2

Although surgical repair remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic RVH, medical management plays a vital role in stabilization and palliation. In neonates presenting with ductal-dependent pulmonary circulation, the administration of intravenous prostaglandins is critical to maintain ductal patency before surgical shunting.10 For patients manifesting acute right heart failure or pulmonary hypertension, inotropic support with dobutamine, mechanical ventilation, and pulmonary vasodilators such as nitric oxide or sildenafil have been successfully used to bridge recovery.2,10 In older children and adults, symptomatic relief of congestive heart failure is primarily achieved with diuretic agents.2,5 For asymptomatic patients with mild hypoplasia, conservative monitoring without medical intervention is considered appropriate.1

Conclusions

Christmas hat is a rare morphologic manifestation of IRVH, where the failure of the RV apex to develop results in a compensatory rightward elongation of the left ventricular apex. A multimodal diagnostic approach, especially CMR, is necessary for a definitive diagnosis, especially in complex congenital anomalies that may be underdiagnosed by echocardiography alone. This provides a framework for managing similar rare presentations of congenital heart disease.
