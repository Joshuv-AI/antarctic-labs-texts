Interpreting Left Atrial Contrast Dynamics in Atrial Fibrillation: Rhythm Status, Hemodynamic Confounders, and Clinical Generalizability
Authors: Huai-xue Mi, Peipei Zhang
Published: 2026

We read with interest the recent article by Shim et al. [1], entitled “Left Atrial Time-to-Peak From Time-Resolved MR Angiography as a Noninvasive Indicator of Left Atrial Pressure Before Catheter Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation,” published in the Korean Journal of Radiology. In this study, the authors used time-to-peak (TTP) values derived from time-resolved magnetic resonance angiography (TR-MRA) as a noninvasive imaging biomarker for estimating left atrial pressure (LAP) in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Given the risks and practical limitations associated with invasive catheterization, their work offers a promising approach for pre-ablation hemodynamic assessment. We are grateful to the authors of this carefully designed study with clinically meaningful findings.

While acknowledging the value of this research, we would like to raise several considerations based on our experience with cardiovascular imaging and hemodynamics, with the aim of further clarifying the clinical utility of this technique.

First, we would like to comment on the representativeness of the assessed rhythm status. The authors required patients to undergo TR-MRA during sinus rhythm to reduce the confounding effects of irregular heartbeats on the contrast dynamics. However, in real-world clinical practice, many patients with persistent AF remain arrhythmic at the time of preprocedural imaging. AF itself is associated with the loss of organized atrial contraction and reduced atrial compliance, both of which may delay contrast clearance and prolong TTP, independent of LAP [2]. Therefore, whether the association between prolonged TTP and elevated LAP remains robust in patients with AF warrants further investigation. Subgroup analyses comparing within-patient changes in TTP before and after the restoration of sinus rhythm would help extend the generalizability of this marker.

Second, several methodological issues and potential hemodynamic confounders should be considered. In this study, TTP was defined as the interval from initial LA opacification to peak signal intensity, with an optimal cutoff of >19.6 seconds. Although the authors standardized the trigger at the level of the main pulmonary artery, TTP is unlikely to reflect LA dynamics alone; it may also be influenced by pulmonary transit time (PTT) and right ventricular (RV) function [3]. In patients with increased pulmonary vascular resistance or RV dysfunction, delayed transit through the pulmonary vasculature can be misinterpreted as LA hemodynamic impairment. Incorporating the RV ejection fraction or PTT as covariates in multivariable models could help define the independent predictive value of TTP, which was reported to have an area under the curve of 0.834.

Third, the performance of TTP in specific clinical subgroups requires close attention. We noted that the prevalence of heart failure (HF) was significantly higher in the high-LAP group (≥15 mmHg) than in the low-LAP group (27.3% vs. 4.3%, P < 0.001). HF, particularly HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), is characterized by impaired left ventricular diastolic function and increased myocardial stiffness, which may impair LA emptying through retrograde effects [4]. Therefore, it would be useful to determine the extent to which TTP prolongation reflects LAP itself, rather than the secondary effects of LV diastolic stiffness. Stratified analyses of patients with and without HF may provide a clearer pathophysiological interpretation of prolonged TTP.
