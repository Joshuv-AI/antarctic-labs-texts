Even Sets and Dual Projective Geometric Codes: A Tale of Cylinders
Sam Adriaensen

Abstract
In this paper, we prove that the smallest even sets in PG ⁡ ( n , q ) {\rm PG}(n,q) , i.e. sets that intersect every line in an even number of points, are cylinders with a hyperoval as base. This fits into a more general study of dual projective geometric codes. Let q q be a prime power, and define 𝒞 k ​ ( n , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp} as the kernel of the k k -space vs. point incidence matrix of PG ⁡ ( n , q ) {\rm PG}(n,q) , seen as a matrix over the prime order subfield of 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} . Determining the minimum weight of this linear code is still an open problem in general, but has been reduced to the case k = 1 k=1 . There is a known construction that constructs small weight codewords of 𝒞 1 ​ ( n , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp} from minimum weight codewords of 𝒞 1 ​ ( 2 , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp} . We call such codewords cylinder codewords . We pose the conjecture that all minimum weight codewords of 𝒞 1 ​ ( n , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp} are cylinder codewords. This conjecture is known to be true if q q is prime. We take three steps towards proving that the conjecture is true in general: (1) We prove that the conjecture is true if q q is even. This is equivalent to our classification of the smallest even sets. (2) We prove that the minimum weight of 𝒞 1 ​ ( n , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp} is q n − 2 q^{n-2} times the minimum weight of 𝒞 1 ​ ( 2 , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp} , which matches the weight of cylinder codewords. Thus, we completely reduce the problem of determining the minimum weight of 𝒞 1 ​ ( n , q ) ⟂ \mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp} to the case n = 2 n=2 . (3) We prove that if the conjecture is true for n = 3 n=3 , it is true in general.



Keywords.
Finite geometry; Projective geometry; Even sets; Sets of even type; Projective geometric codes.

Introduction

1.1
Projective geometric codes

Throughout this paper,
p
denotes a prime number,
q
denotes a power of
p
, and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes the finite field of order
q
.

For every vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we define its
support
as the set

\supp(v)=\left\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,\,||\,\,v_{i}\neq 0\right\},

and its
(Hamming) weight

\wt(v)
as the size of its support.
A
k
-dimensional subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is called a
linear code
.
The
(Hamming) distance
between two vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is defined as

d(v,w)=\wt(v-w)

which equals the number of coordinates where
v
and
w
differ.
The
minimum distance
of a linear code
C
is defined as

d(C)=\min\left\{d(v,w)\,\,||\,\,v,w\in C,\,v\neq w\right\},

i.e. the minimum distance between distinct elements of
C
.
Since
C
is an additive group, it is easy to see that

d(C)=\min\left\{\wt(v)\,\,||\,\,v\in C\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\right\},

hence we shall also call
d(C)
the
minimum weight
of
C
.

The
n
-dimensional projective space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, consisting of the subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}
, will be denoted by
\PG(n,q)
.
When we refer to the dimension of spaces, we refer to their projective dimension, which is one less than their vector space dimension.
We call subspaces of dimension 0, 1, 2, and
n-1
in
\PG(n,q)
respectively
points
,
lines
,
planes
, and
hyperplanes
.
A subspace of dimension
k
is simply called a
k
-space
.
We identify each subspace with the set of points incident to it.
If
S
is a set of points, and
\pi
is a subspace with
|S\cap\pi|=m
, we say that
\pi
is
m
-secant
to
S
.
We use
\langle\pi,\rho\rangle
to denote the span of two subspaces
\pi
and
\rho
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of points of
\PG(n,q)
and
\mathcal{K}
the set of its
k
-spaces.
We make a
p
-ary matrix
M
where the rows correspond to the elements of
\mathcal{K}
and the columns to the elements of
\mathcal{P}
.
Formally,
M\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{P}}
.
We define the matrix by

M(\kappa,P)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }P\in\kappa,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The rowspace of
M
is denoted as
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)
, and its kernel as
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
.
We call
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)
the
code of points and
k
-spaces in
\PG(n,q)
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
its
dual code
.
Since each codeword
c
in
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)
or
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
is a vector over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with coordinates labelled by
\mathcal{P}
, we will identify
c
with a function
\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Much is known about the minimum weight of the codes
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)
, see e.g.
[
Adr23
,
AD24
,
AK92
,
BI02
,
DGM70
,
LSVdV10
,
PZ18
,
SW18
]
.
We will not discuss it here.
Instead, we will focus on the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
.

1.2
Bounds on the minimum weight of the dual code

First of all, the problem of determining
d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp})
and characterising its minimum weight codewords has been completely reduced to the case
k=1
by Lavrauw, Storme, and Van de Voorde.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
LSVdV08
, Theorems 10, 12]
)
.

If
k>1
, then

d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp})=d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n-k+1,q)^{\perp}).

Moreover, the minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
arise exactly as follows:
Take an
(n-k+1)
-space
\pi
, a codeword
c
in the dual code of points and lines in
\pi
, and extend
c
to a codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
by putting
c(P)=0
for all points
P\notin\pi
.

Minimum weight codewords have been characterised if
q=p
is prime by Bagchi and Inamdar, and Lavrauw, Storme and Van de Voorde.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
BI02
, Proposition 2]

[
LSVdV08
, Theorem 12]
)
.

If
q=p
is prime, then
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})=2q^{n-1}
.
The minimum codewords are exactly the codewords of the following form for some non-zero scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and some distinct hyperplanes
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
:

c:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}:P\mapsto\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{if }P\in\Pi_{1}\setminus\Pi_{2},\\
-\alpha&\text{if }P\in\Pi_{2}\setminus\Pi_{1},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
q
is even, the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
has been also been determined exactly.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
CKdR99
, Theorem 1]
)
.

If
q
is even, then

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})=q^{n-2}(q+2).

The lower bound
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})\geqslant q^{n-2}(q+2)
builds on the work of Delsarte
[
Del70
]
, and uses an advanced result of Delsarte that represents the codes
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)
as so-called subfield subcodes of generalised Reed-Muller codes.
Moreover, if
q\leqslant 8
is even, the minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
have been completely classified
[
Adr23
, §3]
.

In general, if
q
is not prime or even, the minimum weight
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
has not yet been determined (except for some small values, see Theorem
1.7
).
Bagchi and Inamdar gave a good lower bound on the minimum weight
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
BI02
, Theorem 3]
)
.

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})\geqslant 2\left(\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\right).

In particular for
n=2
, this bound equals
2\left(q-\frac{q}{p}+1\right)
.
A short elementary proof of the Bagchi-Inamdar bound was given by the author in
[
Adr23
, Result 3.2]
.
The bound was recently improved by Csajbók, Longobardi, Marino, and Trombetti if
n>2
, using the polynomial method.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
CLMT25
]
)
.

If
q
is odd, then

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})\geqslant 2\left(q-\frac{q}{p}+1\right)q^{n-2}.

For comparison, we also state the best known upper bound on the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
LSVdV10
, Corollary 4.15]
)
.

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})\leqslant\left(2q-\frac{q-p}{p-1}\right)q^{n-2}.

If
q=p^{2}
, some improved bounds on the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
are known.

Theorem 1.7
.

(1)

[
KdR01
, Theorem 1]

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,9)^{\perp})=15
.

(2)

[
CHKW03
, Corollary 1.2]

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,25)^{\perp})=45
.

(3)

[
DBVdV23
, Corollary 1.5]
If
p\geqslant 7
is prime, then
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,p^{2})^{\perp})\geqslant 2p^{2}-2p+5
.

1.3
The cylinder construction and a conjecture

Next, we discuss an important connection between small weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
and small weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
.
We start by discussing the concept of a cylinder as a point set in
\PG(n,q)
.

Definition 1.8
.

Consider two disjoint subspaces
\pi
and
\rho
of
\PG(n,q)
, and let
S
be a set of points in
\pi
.
The
cylinder
with vertex
\rho
and base
S
is defined as the set of points

\bigcup_{P\in S}\left\langle P,\rho\right\rangle\setminus\rho.

This inspires the following connection between small weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
and small weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
.
The construction can be obtained by inductively applying a construction of Bagchi and Inamdar
[
BI02
, Lemma 6]
.

Proposition 1.9

(
[
BI02
, Lemma 6]
)
.

Consider a codeword
c\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
.
Embed
\PG(2,q)
as a plane
\pi
in
\PG(n,q)
.
Choose an
(n-3)
-space
\rho
disjoint to
\pi
.
Construct the function
\Cyl_{\rho}(c)
on the points
\mathcal{P}
of
\PG(2,q)
by defining

\Cyl_{\rho}(c):\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}:P\mapsto\begin{cases}c(\left\langle\rho,P\right\rangle\cap\pi)&\text{if }P\notin\rho,\\
0&\text{if }P\in\rho.\end{cases}

Then
\Cyl_{\rho}(c)
is a codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
of weight
q^{n-2}\wt(c)
.
We call
\Cyl_{\rho}(c)
a
cylinder codeword
with vertex
\rho
and base
c
.

We pose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.10
.

All minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
are cylinder codewords, for every prime power
q
, and every integer
n>2
.

In this paper, we make three important steps towards resolving the conjecture.
These steps are discussed in detail in the following subsection, but we summarize them here.

(1)

We show that the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
matches the weight of cylinder codewords.
In combination with Theorem
1.1
, this completely reduces the determination of
d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp})
to determining
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp})
.

(2)

We prove that if Conjecture
1.10
is true for
n=3
, then it is true for all
n
.

(3)

We show that Conjecture
1.10
is true if
q
is even.

Note that Theorem
1.2
shows that Conjecture
1.10
is true if
q
is prime.

1.4
New results

1.4.1
Cylinder codewords have minimum weight

Note that if
c
is a minimum weight codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
, and we apply the cylinder construction in
\PG(n,q)
, then
\Cyl_{\rho}(c)
has weight
q^{n-2}d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp})
.
We show that this is the true minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
.

Theorem 1.11
.

If
n>2
, then

d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp})=q^{n-2}d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}).

Using Theorem
1.1
, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.12
.

If
n>2
, then

d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp})=q^{n-k-1}d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp})

Note in particular that Theorem
1.11
in combination with the Bagchi-Inamdar (Theorem
1.4
) bound for
n=2
recovers Theorem
1.5
.
Moreover, since it is a well-known fact that if
q
is even, then
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp})=q+2
(as we will see later), Theorem
1.11
also reproves Theorem
1.3
.
Both Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.11
can be proven with elementary tools that only rely on the combinatorics of projective spaces, not the underlying algebra.
Hence, we obtain alternative proofs of Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.3
that avoid the use of polynomials and advanced methods.

Moreover, Theorem
1.7
and Corollary
1.12
imply the following.

Corollary 1.13
.

(1)

d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,9)^{\perp})=9^{n-k-1}\cdot 15

(2)

d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,25)^{\perp})=25^{n-k-1}\cdot 45

(3)

If
p\geqslant 7
is prime, then
d(\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,p^{2})^{\perp})\geqslant p^{2(n-k-1)}(2p^{2}-2p+5)
.

1.4.2
Reduction to three dimensions

We will reduce the resolution of Conjecture
1.10
to the 3-dimensional case.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
q
be a prime power.
Suppose that all minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(3,q)^{\perp}
are cylinder codewords.
Then for any
n\geqslant 3
, all minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
are cylinder codewords.

We note that Conjecture
1.10
holds if
q
is prime by Theorem
1.2
, so the above theorem trivially holds for prime values of
q
.
Moreover, it was already proven to hold for even values of
q
by the author
[
Adr23
, Proposition 3.9]
.

1.4.3
Even prime powers and even sets

We extend Theorem
1.3
by characterising all minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
in case
q
is even.

Theorem 1.15
.

If
q
is even, the minimum weight of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
is
q^{n-2}(q+2)
, and the only minimum weight codewords are cylinder codewords.

If
q
is even, then any codeword

of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
is a function
\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{0,1\}
, and we can identify
c
with its support.
This brings us to the following definition.

Definition 1.16
.

We call a non-empty set
S
of points in
\PG(n,q)
an
even set
1
1

1

sometimes called a
set of even type
with respect to
k
-spaces if
S
intersects every
k
-space in an even number of points.
If
S
is an even set with respect to lines, we simply call
S
an even set.

Note that if
\mathcal{P}
is the set of points of
\PG(n,q)
, then a non-zero function
c:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{k}(n,q)^{\perp}
if and only if
\supp(c)
is an even set with respect to

If
S
is an even set in
\PG(2,q)
, and

, then each of the

contain at least one other point of

.

Definition 1.17
.

An even set in
\PG(2,q)
of size

is called a

.
It is known that whenever

is even,
\PG(2,q)
contains hyperovals, see e.g.
[

Therefore,

yields the following equivalent formulation in the language of even sets.

Theorem 1.18

If

\PG(n,q)
have size

, and they are exactly the cylinders with an
(n-3)
-dimensional vertex and hyperoval as base.

For completion, we can use Theorem

to extend the previous theorem to even sets with respect to any dimension.

Corollary 1.19
.

If

is even, then the smallest even sets in
\PG(n,q)
with respect to

q^{n-k-1}(q+2)
, and are exactly the cylinders with an
(n-k-2)
-dimensional vertex and a hyperoval as base.

Remark 1.20

For any even prime power

, Corollary

classifies the smallest even sets with respect to

, in as far as the hyperovals of

Organisation of the paper and proof techniques

Let us make a note on the proof techniques.
Several key steps use a variance-type argument.
One can think of this as follows: if
S
is a set of points in
\PG(n,q)
, then the variance of the distribution of

over the hyperplanes
2
2

2

or any more generally over the
k
-spaces for some

of
\PG(n,q)

In other words, it is not possible that there are many hyperplanes
\pi

To prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.11

We will use the notation

\theta_{n}=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}=q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\dots+q+1

for the number of points in
\PG(n,q)
.
In order to prove Theorem
1.11
, we need two tools.

First of all we need a bound on so-called incidence-free sets in
\PG(n,q)
.
We apply the bound from Haemers
[
Hae95
]
, which holds in general 2-designs, to the specific case of points and hyperplanes in
\PG(n,q)
.
This theorem can be proven using linear algebra, but also through an easy combinatorial method, cf.
[
DWSV12
, §2]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
Hae95
, Corollary 5.3]
)
.

Suppose that
S
is a set of points and
T
is a set of hyperplanes in
\PG(n,q)
such that no point of
S
is incident to a hyperplane in
T
.
Then

\theta_{n-1}^{2}|S||T|\leqslant q^{n-1}(\theta_{n}-|S|)(\theta_{n}-|T|).

Secondly, we need a simple but crucial observation.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
c\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
and
\pi
is a subspace of
\PG(n,q)
of dimension
m\geqslant 2
.
Then the restriction
c|_{\pi}
of
c
to the points of
\pi
is a codeword of the dual code of points and lines in
\pi
.
Therefore,
c|_{\pi}
is either the zero codeword, or has weight at least
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(m,q)^{\perp})
.

Proof.

A function
c:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
if and only if
\sum_{P\in\ell}c(P)=0
for all lines
\ell
of
\PG(n,q)
.
This applies in particular to the lines of
\pi
, hence
c|_{\pi}
is in the dual code of points and lines in
\pi
.
The last sentence follows from the definition of minimum weight.
∎

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
n
.
We use
n=2
as base case, for the theorem statement is trivially true.
Thus, suppose that
n>2
, and the theorem holds for
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n-1,q)^{\perp}
.
Write
d_{2}=d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp})
.
Let
c\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
be a non-zero codeword of minimum weight.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{1}(n,q)^{\perp}
contains cylinder codewords with minimum weight codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{1}(2,q)^{\perp}
as base, we know that
\wt(c)\leqslant q^{n-2}d_{2}
.
Suppose by way of contradiction that
\wt(c)<q^{n-2}d_{2}.

There exists a 0-secant hyperplane
\pi
to
\supp(c)
.
Otherwise, by Lemma
2.2
, every hyperplane contains at least
d(\mathcal{C}_{1}(n-1,q)^{\perp})=q^{n-3}d_{2}
points of
\supp(c)
.
Since every point of
\supp(c)
is incident with
\theta_{n-1}
hyperplanes, this implies that

\wt(c)\geqslant\frac{\theta_{n}}{\theta_{n-1}}q^{n-3}d_{2}=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q^{n}-1}q^{n-3}d_{2}>q^{n-2}d_{2},

which contradicts our assumptions.

Now take an
(n-2)
-space
\rho
in
\pi
.
Then
\rho
is incident to at least one other 0-secant hyperplane to
\supp(c)
.
Otherwise, all other
q
hyperplanes through
\rho
contain at least
q^{n-3}d_{2}
points of
\supp(c)
, but then
\wt(c)\geqslant q^{n-2}d_{2}
, contrary to our assumptions.
Since there are
\theta_{n-1}

(n-2)
-spaces in
\pi
, we find in total a set
T
of
\theta_{n-1}+1
hyperplanes of
\PG(n,q)
that are 0-secant to
\supp(c)
.

On the other hand, let
P
be a point of
\supp(c)
.
Since
\sum_{Q\in\ell}c(Q)=0\neq c(P)
for every line through
P
, we can choose a point
Q\in\supp(c)\setminus\{P\}
