Flashover prediction of polluted composite insulators based on arc time constant and velocity using CNN–LSTM
Authors: Navid Fahimi, Hamid Reza Sezavar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, a unified arc model is proposed to bridge the gap between analytical arc models and flashover dynamics in real practice. This approach synergizes the arc time constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}_{arc}$$\end{document}) and arc velocity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{arc}$$\end{document}) under pollution conditions. While conventional static models fail to capture the transient nature of arc propagation on polluted insulators, proposed approach would address this point by dynamic coupling of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}_{arc}$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{arc}$$\end{document}. Presented model is able to make transition smoothly between discharge stages with adjustment in real time using leakage current (LC) data. The procedure of LC waveform feature extraction is achieved using combination of convolutional neural network (CNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM). The presented arc model ensures fail-safe predictions while the normalized arc velocity parameter provides a physics-based foundation for understanding arc progression. The statistical validation of error distributions approves the reliability and extension of the proposed approach. In addition, the presented CNN–LSTM model achieves superior performance predictions and computational efficiency to some similar benchmarked approaches. The practical implications of these findings would enable preventive maintenance (PM), enhanced grid protection and optimized insulator designs.

Introduction

Outdoor insulators play a critical role in reliable performance of electrical power systems. They are expected to withstand breakdown in harsh service conditions such as wet conditions, coastal or industrial polluted zones, dust storms and etc.1,2. Combination of pollution deposit with wet conditions on the surface of outdoor insulators can lead to flashover incidents in polluted zones which can cause unpredictable costly outages in power systems3,4.

Arc time constant and arc propagation velocity are among critical challenging arcing characteristics for dynamic modeling of pollution flashover. It should be considered that partial arcs propagate in different stochastic paths on the surface of wet polluted insulator due to geometrical specification and non-uniform distribution of pollution. In addition, velocity of local arcs is different as well depending on pinch force of LC, thermal buoyancy due to temperature variation, and non-uniform distribution of charge density5. According to measurements of previous researches, initial stage arcs propagate with less velocities in lower applied voltages. Also, upper arcs close to high voltage terminal propagate faster than lower arcs (close to ground terminal)6,7.

Arcing time constant is considered particularly in Mayr’s equation of arcing resistance for first time8. Mayr’s arc model presumes arcing temperature and dimension to be constant. Some famous later works include Cassie’s arc model9 for larger currents, Habedanck’s hybrid Cassie-Mayr representation of arc model10, Schavemaker’s arc formulation of modified Mayr’s with a constant time and cooling power11, and Schwartz’s arc model of modified Mayr’s dependent on arc conductance12. Variation of arc time constant during dynamic stages of arc activities is a challenging issue in most previous arc models. Arc time constant is usually referred to electrical discharge inertia due to conservation nature of arcing energy according to conductance and injected power. In measurements of the past and recent researches, value of arc time constant is reported from 100 µS in initial arc stage of lower currents to several ms in pre-flashover stage of larger currents13–15. An improved arc model is represented in Khakpour et al.16 based on arc diameter for calculation of low-current arcing time constant. In a recent study, novel flashover model is presented based on local arc time constant according to thermal and electrical analysis of local AC arcs in polluted insulators17.

Arcing propagation velocity is interested by many researches. Some popular arcing velocity criterions include Gallimberti’s criterion18 based on required charge to induce arc propagation, Beroual’s criterion19,20 based on the dissipated energy by the arc, and Anjan and Lakshminarassimh’s criterion21 based on arc mobility and electrical field inside arc. Recently, an arc propagation velocity criterion is proposed in Taheri et al.7 for calculation of Wilkin’s22 pollution layer equation based on experimental measurements. In Jabbari et al.23, propagation velocity curves versus arcing time and length are obtained for flashover voltage (FOV) prediction of insulators according to development dynamic arc model. Similarly, influence of arc propagation velocity is investigated in dynamic arc model for prediction of AC flashover of insulators in Jabbari et al.24. In Salem et al.25, a modified discharge model of polluted insulator is proposed based on sensitivity analysis of arc length, arc time constant and arc propagation velocity according to hybrid calculation of circuit model and genetic algorithm (GA). In Parizad et al.26, optimization of arc parameters is done by use of GA according to comparative analysis of several popular arc models.

Machine learning (ML) methods are proved to be influential for flashover prediction in condition-based analysis of composite insulators. In27,28, ML techniques such as random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and deep neural network (DNN) are compared for contamination classification of polluted high voltage insulators using LC. In29,30, artificial neural network (ANN) is used to develop FOV based on pollution severity, LC data and geometry profile of the insulators. Similarly, ANN is used to build an intelligent classifier for classification of defective insulators based on partial discharge (PD) monitoring31. In Nguyen et al.32, danger-assessment classification of polluted insulators is presented based on deep learning according to ultra-high frequency (UHF) signals of PDs. In Bueno-Barrachina et al.33, convolutional neural network (CNN) is utilized for LC prediction of outdoor insulators based on long-term data. In Salem et al.34, RUSBoost algorithm (combination of random-under sample and AdaBoost) is used to estimate key indicators of insulators based on environmental data. In Santos et al.35, self-organizing map (SOM) is employed for clustering flashover and LC datasets of damaged insulators. In Ghiasi et al.36, a cloud data classified system of LC is suggested for preventing flashover of outdoor insulators based on deep learning technique. While most previous works focused on failure prevention of outdoor insulators, but feature extraction of outdoor insulators based on arc propagation physics has been often missed. Joint approach of dynamic discharge modeling for arc propagation understanding and ML methods for handling computational challenges might help to make better insight into future behavior of outdoor insulators in harsh service conditions.

In this paper, a comprehensive analysis is presented based on joint arc physics and deep learning method according to numerous test data of the real outdoor composite insulators for wide range of pollution levels. Contributions of this hybrid arc model can be summarized as follows:Development of a comprehensive arc model that integrates arc time constant and arc velocity to predict flashover under varying pollution levels.To achieve high accuracy and real-time applicability, a hybrid machine learning (ML) model is proposed in this paper using combination of physics-informed feature engineering with deep learning.Presented dynamic modeling approach considers time-dependent variations in arc time constant and arc velocity.Experimental validation ensures that the model aligns with empirical data for all pollution levels.Presented hybrid physics-deep learning approach translates analytical insights into practical tools for preventive maintenance (PM) schemes of the power systems.

Test procedure

The experiment setup is shown in Fig. 1 that consists of a cubic chamber with equal dimensions of 2 m, a step-up transformer (100 kV, 5 kVA), capacitive voltage divider (with a voltage ratio of 10000:1) and a shunt resistor (LC measurement) in series with the insulator. The output file of the digital data acquisition system for all measured variables can be processed by MATLAB software. Samples are made from high temperature vulcanized (HTV) silicone rubber (SiR) with aluminum trihydrate (ATH) filler. Rated voltage of the samples is 20 kV. The five-year-old samples exhibit a hydrophobicity class (HC) of 3 according to IEC62073. This classification indicates that the polymeric surface of the insulators is neither completely hydrophobic (distinctly water-repellent) nor hydrophilic (fully water-accepting), resulting in an intermediate level of hydrophobicity. This is a common state for aged or slightly polluted polymeric insulators in real practices. In addition, this would allow for less deviation or modeling errors in further development of the intelligent model based on test data. Images of the samples and their specification are presented in Fig. 2 and Table 1 respectively. Test procedure is performed according to IEC 60507, IEC 60243-1. Initial value of the source voltage is set to 10 kV; then the voltage is stepped up 0.5 kV per minute for 20 min, while the voltage and LC data are saved by data acquisition system. Pre-contamination process is achieved according to IEC 60815. Pollution is sprayed into the test chamber with the fixed equivalent salt deposit density (ESDD) of the standard solution by distilled water and NaCl. ESDD value is controlled by measurement of solution conduction. Surface conduction of the pollution solution (µS/cm) can be converted into equivalent ESDD (mg/cm2) using surface polluted area of the insulator. Oscillatory nature of the LC has been discussed in many previous researches. In fact, two physical factors can be responsible for the LC content on the surface of a polluted insulator. First, a wet condition is applied continuously during test period. It is worth noting that the choice of the Gaussian model for smoothing the transient and oscillatory nature of LC data is made with the intention of creating a robust AI prediction model in subsequent sections. Capturing all transients and oscillations with similar fitting tools could increase the risk of overfitting phenomena in the final prediction model. In fact, the Gaussian model has been employed first for data pre-processing and then for statistical characterization (feature engineering) that would result in a robust pipeline for further AI training. This progression continues until the discharge path resistance surpasses that of the pollution layer. This shift in the dominant resistance is identified as a critical pre-flashover condition in polluted insulators. Throughout the test, the surface state is determined by the competition between the continuous wetting from the test environment and evaporation driven by the LC from the applied high voltage. In other words, PD stage to flashover stage can be understood as a transition between energy injection from the pollution layer and energy dissipation from the arc. In light pollution, the energy input is low, and arcs remain localized and stable. As pollution level increases, the enhanced LC widens the dry bands, reducing the effective arcing distance and allowing the arc to bridge larger gaps. Once the arc covers a critical portion of the creepage path, the field distortion becomes severe enough to trigger unstable, accelerating arc propagation, leading to flashover. Intense overstress caused by pollution levels and wetting rate sets off an irreversible chain of events where increasing LC and dry-band expansion ultimately result in complete discharge (flashover). Hybrid CNN–LSTM with attention

In this paper, proposed ML model is a hybrid CNN–LSTM with attention. A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a type of deep learning algorithm primarily used for analyzing, extraction and hierarchical learning of features from input data49. CNN can be used to extracts local patterns in LC waveforms32,33,50. Long short-term memory (LSTM) is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) designed to handle sequential data such as time series with beneficial feature of capturing long-term dependencies within sequences that overcomes traditional RNNs. LSTMs remember past information and discard irrelevant details by selection51. This particular feature can help to capture temporal dependencies in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}_{arc}$$\end{document}​ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{arc}$$\end{document} trends. In deep learning, the attention mechanism is a technique that enables a model to focus on different parts of the input data when making predictions. It helps understanding most relevant parts of the input for the final objective52. It can be used to focus on critical time steps such as pre-flashover instability. Results and discussion

Presented results in Table 6 provides a comprehensive analysis of insulator performance under heavy pollution level (30,000 µS/cm). In can be seen that the proposed model demonstrates a high degree of accuracy in predicting transition times between different arc phases according to consistent mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.30 ± 0.02 min for all samples. This error margin is due to an intentional bias that is incorporated into the model to ensure fail-safe operation. This bias originates from the dynamic arc model where the arc time constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}_{arc}$$\end{document}) is intentionally overestimated by 10% to cover measurement uncertainties in real practices as specified by IEC 60,815 standards. It is observed that final predictions of the proposed model are lower than actual observed values. This is a safety margin that prevents underestimation of flashover risks. Further observation of Table 6 reveals that only 50% of the tested samples (A, C and E) have progressed to complete discharge (flashover) while the remaining samples (B, D and F) were remained at the stable arc phase. It can be seen that the presented model is able to identify these cases by monitoring the second derivative of the arc time constant. While the proposed hybrid model is promising, several limitations should be considered for future researches. These include unseen parameters in the trained model, complex environmental conditions and long-term aging states. Practical deployment challenges include sensor requirements (LC measurement which may not be available across all grid assets), raising cost-benefit considerations for computational resource needs, and periodic recalibration with new data to combat concept drift (e.g. variable environmental patterns or unseen pollution sources). Although the test data are recorded from field-aged insulators, it is crucial to validate purely laboratory-based data through long-term unsupervised field trials in geographically diverse and harsh environments for true practical effectiveness.

Application of measurement systems for condition monitoring could be usually challenging in cases of converting raw data into final control actions or remedial measures through the preventive maintenance (PM) scheme. Integrating domain knowledge into physics-induced learning would assist the presented model for data processing. This would compensate limitations concerning instrumentation precision. Utilized arc features can be extracted from the measured signals. From the industrial perspective where a reliable decision-making structure is critical for PM remedial programs, the presented framework can be considered as an intelligent metering system that can be deployed at various levels of the power system. Building up a real infrastructure for condition assessment of the outdoor insulators of an overhead line requires a full chain of measurement and sensing layers for LC records, precipitation and pollution deposit metering devices, main processing core for offline physics-induced learning patterns and real-time calculation of discharge transition alarms. In addition, an economical optimization in different scenarios might be necessary for unit deployment in any pollution zone map.

Future application of the proposed method in real practices could begin with field pilot deployment by installing monitoring systems (LC sensors or cameras) on selected insulators in diverse geographical locations (coastal, industrial or desert) for evaluation of predictive accuracy and computational performance. The next stage could be integration into grid management systems through developing a software prototype that integrates the model into a condition monitoring system of a power utility or SCADA system. Its effectiveness would be assessed through triggering actionable alerts for preventive maintenance (PM) and potentially the claimed benefits for optimized insulator designs.

Conclusions

In this study, a hybrid physics-informed deep learning model is developed for the dynamic prediction of flashover on polluted composite insulators. The original novelty is the hybrid dynamic modeling of the real-time arc time constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}_{arc}$$\end{document}) and arc velocity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{arc}$$\end{document}) with AI flashover prediction, which tries to make a connection between analytical arc models and transient discharge dynamics. It is shown that the integration of the presented arc model with a CNN-LSTM-Attention network for feature extraction from LC data can be an effective tool in real practices. The experimental validation approves performance and reliability of the presented approach through quantitative operational indexes.

Validation results approve a consistent MAE of 0.30 ± 0.02 min for all discharge transition phases according to different pollution levels. In addition, comparative analysis between similar benchmark models verifies that the proposed CNN-LSTM-Attention architecture offers an optimal balance. For example, it offers better predictions with 89.1% accuracy, 87.3% F1 score, and a 0.924 AUC compared to benchmark models like Transformers, GRUs, and ANNs. This is obtained with preserving 3.95 ms inference time which reflects high computational efficiency. Statistical analysis (ANOVA) reveals key role of pollution level for the variance in discharge transition times (89%) rather than geometry of the insulators (less than 3%). The presented model tries to incorporate physics-induced feature engineering into PM schemes in power system utilities. For example, it is shown that a trigger alert is activated when predicted transition times is lower than a critical threshold (e.g., < 10 min for heavy pollution level).
