CBCT features of florid cemento-osseous dysplasia with coexisting lesions at different maturation stages: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jing Li, Jian Chen, Zhiqiang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlorid cemento-osseous dysplasia (FLCOD) is a begin fibro-osseous lesion occurring in the tooth-bearing regions of the jaws and is characterized by replacement of normal bone with fibrous connective tissue and cementum-like mineralized material. Most patients are asymptomatic and are diagnosed incidentally during radiographic examinations, and the condition may be misdiagnosed because of overlapping imaging features with other jaw lesions. Because the disease is usually asymptomatic and exhibits imaging features that overlap with those of several odontogenic and inflammatory lesions, accurate diagnosis remains challenging. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides high-resolution three-dimensional imaging that facilitates assessment of lesion extent, internal mineralization, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures. We report a case of FLCOD exhibiting lesions at different maturation stages and emphasize the diagnostic value of CBCT in clinical decision-making.Case presentationA 41-year-old female presented with pain in the left mandibular posterior region for one week. Clinical examination revealed deep carious destruction of tooth 36 and occlusal caries involving teeth 46 and 47. Pulp vitality testing demonstrated loss of vitality in tooth 36, whereas all teeth associated with the cemento-osseous lesions responded normally. CBCT revealed bilateral multifocal mixed radiolucent–radiopaque lesions involving the posterior mandible characterized by well-defined borders, thin radiolucent rims, and variable internal mineralization corresponding to different maturation stages without obvious root resorption or cortical perforation or significant expansion. Based on the clinical findings, pulp vitality testing, and characteristic CBCT features, the patient was diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis of tooth 36, caries of teeth 46 and 47, and the cemento-osseous lesions were managed conservatively with periodic clinical and radiographic follow-up. ConclusionsCBCT plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of FLCOD by accurately demonstrating lesion distribution, internal mineralization, and maturation stage. Background

Fibro-osseous lesions (FOLs) are a group of jaw lesions characterized by the replacement of normal bone with fibrous connective tissue and osseous or cementum-like materials. In 1938, Lichtenstein first systematically described FOLs of the head and neck [1]. However, due to the considerable overlap in histological and radiographic features among different entities, their classification and nomenclature have long been controversial, making accurate diagnosis essential for appropriate clinical management.

With the advancement in the understanding of these lesions, the World Health Organization (WHO) gradually standardized their classification. In the 2017 WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumors, non-neoplastic lesions occurring in tooth-bearing areas of the jaws were collectively termed cemento-osseous dysplasia (COD), which was further classified into periapical COD (PCOD), focal COD (FCOD), and florid COD (FLCOD) [2]. First described in 1976, FLCOD is the most extensive subtype and commonly presents as bilateral or multifocal involvement of the jaws, predominantly affecting middle-aged women [3]. Most patients remain asymptomatic and are diagnosed incidentally during routine radiographic examinations.

The cause and pathogenesis of FLCOD remain unclear. Currently, it is believed to be related to abnormal bone-dentin metabolism originating from the periodontal ligament. In the early stage of the disease, bone resorption is predominant, followed by the gradual formation of cementum-like and osseous calcified tissues, eventually dense and high-density masses are formed [4]. Therefore, on imaging, lesions may demonstrate different maturation stages, including the early (osteolytic), intermediate (mixed), and mature (radiopaque) stages, which may coexist within the same patient [5]. Most cases of this disease have no obvious symptoms and are often discovered incidentally during imaging examinations. However, in the later stage of the disease, due to reduced local blood supply, it is prone to secondary infection, osteomyelitis, and even sequestrum bone formation after procedures such as tooth extraction or root canal treatment [6, 7]. Because teeth associated with FLCOD are usually vital, pulp vitality testing is an important component of the diagnostic process and helps differentiate FLCOD from inflammatory periapical lesions, thereby avoiding unnecessary endodontic treatment.

In recent years, the application of CBCT has significantly improved the diagnosis of FLCOD by clearly demonstrating lesion extent, internal calcification patterns, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures, thereby providing important information for diagnosis and differential diagnosis. Compared with conventional panoramic radiography, CBCT provides three-dimensional visualization of lesion distribution, internal mineralization, cortical bone integrity, and lesion maturation, thereby improving diagnostic confidence and facilitating differential diagnosis. In this report, we present a case of FLCOD and analyze its clinical and CBCT imaging features in conjunction with a review of the relevant literature, with particular emphasis on the diagnostic value of CBCT and the coexistence of lesions at different maturation stages, aiming to improve clinicians’ understanding of this condition and reduce misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.

Case presentation

The patient was a 41-year-old woman who presented with pain in the left mandibular posterior teeth for 1 week. The pain was aggravated during mastication and occasionally accompanied by nocturnal pain. No prior treatment had been performed. The patient was otherwise healthy, with no history of systemic disease or drug allergy and denied any history of facial trauma or previous maxillofacial surgery.

Clinical examination

The oral examination revealed a deep carious cavity on the buccal side and occlusal surface of tooth 36, with a considerable amount of decayed material. There was no obvious pain upon probing, and the tooth was not sensitive to percussion or palpation. The tooth was not mobile, and there were no obvious abnormalities in the surrounding gingiva. The occlusal surfaces of teeth 46 and 47 had deep caries, and there was no obvious discomfort upon cold stimulation. Probing and percussion tests were negative for both teeth. Pulp vitality testing was subsequently performed. Tooth 36 showed no response to either the cold test or the electric pulp test, whereas teeth associated with the cemento-osseous lesions responded normally, confirming preservation of pulp vitality (Fig. 1).

The CBCT examination revealed a radiolucent lesion in the crown of tooth 36, involving the pulp cavity, and the image within the root canal was radiolucent, consistent with pulp canal involvement; no obvious radiolucent lesion was observed on the occlusal surfaces of teeth 46 and 47. In the root apex region of teeth 37 to 47, multiple bilateral multifocal mixed radiolucent–radiopaque lesions with relatively clear boundaries were visible, accompanied by variable internal mineralization surrounded by thin radiolucent rims, distributed in multiple focal areas. Some lesions appeared predominantly radiolucent, whereas others demonstrated mixed radiolucent–radiopaque features, indicating different stages of lesion maturation. No obvious root resorption or cortical bone destruction or buccolingual expansion of the tooth roots was observed in the affected areas (Fig. 2).

Diagnosis

Irreversible pulpitis of tooth 36, 46 and 47 caries, and florid cemento-osseous dysplasia.

Treatment process

Root canal treatment was performed on tooth 36, and minimally invasive restoration was carried out on teeth 46 and 47. Because the remaining cemento-osseous lesions were asymptomatic and showed no evidence of secondary infection, conservative management with periodic clinical and radiographic follow-up was recommended. The patient was advised to maintain good oral hygiene and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures involving teeth associated with the lesions. At the latest follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of lesion progression or secondary infection.

Discussion and conclusions

COD is a non-neoplastic fibro-osseous lesion that occurs in the tooth-bearing area of the jaw. Currently, the WHO classifies it into three types: PCOD, FCOD and FLCOD [8]. FLCOD is the most extensive type, often presenting with bilateral or multi-quadrant jawbone involvement, and is more common in middle-aged women. The etiology and pathogenesis of COD remain unclear [9]. Currently, it is believed that it may be related to abnormal bone-dental metabolism originating from the periodontal ligament. Because the biological behavior of FLCOD is generally indolent and most patients remain asymptomatic, recognition of its characteristic clinicoradiographic features is essential to avoid unnecessary biopsy, endodontic treatment, or tooth extraction, thereby reducing the risk of secondary infection and other complications.

This patient had no obvious pain or swelling symptoms, and the dental pulp was normal in vitality. Only tooth 36 exhibited pulp necrosis secondary to deep dental caries, whereas all teeth associated with the cemento-osseous lesions remained vital. Preservation of pulp vitality is an important diagnostic feature that helps differentiate FLCOD from inflammatory periapical lesions. The CBCT showed multiple mixed radiolucent–radiopaque lesions in the bilateral mandibular posterior teeth area, with thin radiolucent rims visible around the lesion, which was in line with the typical imaging manifestations of FLCOD. The diagnosis of COD mainly relies on clinical manifestations and imaging features [10]. Most patients have no obvious symptoms and are often discovered incidentally during imaging examinations. CBCT can clearly display the lesion range, the degree of internal calcification, and the relationship with surrounding anatomical structures, which is of great value for disease diagnosis and differential diagnosis [11, 12]. Compared with conventional panoramic radiography, CBCT provides high-resolution three-dimensional visualization of lesion distribution, internal mineralization, cortical bone integrity, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures. These advantages improve diagnostic confidence and facilitate differentiation from other fibro-osseous and inflammatory jaw lesions.

In the present case, CBCT clearly demonstrated bilateral multifocal lesion distribution, variable internal mineralization, thin radiolucent rims, preservation of cortical bone, and the absence of root resorption or mandibular canal displacement. These characteristic findings enabled accurate evaluation of lesion extent and biological behavior without invasive diagnostic procedures. Although panoramic radiography remains the preferred initial imaging modality, CBCT provides valuable complementary information when detailed assessment of lesion characteristics and differential diagnosis is required.

COD has a relatively typical dynamic evolution process, which can be divided into three stages (Table 1): the early (osteolytic) stage, the intermediate (mixed) stage, and the mature (radiopaque) stage. Different stages can coexist in the same patient or even in the same lesion. As the disease progresses, the internal structure of the lesion gradually transforms from fibrous connective tissue to dental bone-like and bone-like tissues, and the imaging manifestations also gradually change from low density to mixed density and high-density masses [13, 14]. In the present patient, lesions with different internal mineralization patterns were identified simultaneously, indicating different stages of lesion maturation. Recognition of lesion maturation is clinically important because early lesions may mimic inflammatory periapical disease, whereas mature lesions are more likely to resemble other radiopaque jaw lesions. CBCT provides superior visualization of these maturation characteristics compared with conventional radiography.

FCOD needs to be differentiated from various diseases that can present as mixed radiolucent–radiopaque lesions or radiopaque jaw lesions within the jawbone. Ossifying fibroma typically presents as a single, well-defined expansive lesion, which can cause significant cortical bone expansion; while FLCOD usually shows bilateral, multifocal distribution, with a wide lesion range and generally no obvious expansion [15]. Familial giant-type cementoma has an earlier onset, often has a familial genetic tendency, and can cause significant facial deformities [16]. Chronic sclerotic osteomyelitis is often accompanied by significant pain, infection, and bone destruction [17]. Paget’s disease usually affects multiple bones and can be accompanied by elevated serum alkaline phosphatase [18]. Cementoblastoma often fuses with the tooth root and is accompanied by tooth root resorption and pain symptoms [19]. In the present patient, the combination of bilateral multifocal lesion distribution, preservation of pulp vitality, well-defined radiolucent rims, absence of root resorption, and lack of significant cortical expansion strongly supported the diagnosis of FLCOD.

Pulp vitality testing represents an indispensable component of the diagnostic work-up for FLCOD. Teeth associated with FLCOD are generally vital despite the presence of periapical radiolucent or mixed-density lesions. Therefore, vitality testing plays a crucial role in distinguishing FLCOD from inflammatory periapical lesions and avoiding unnecessary endodontic treatment.

Most cases of FLCOD do not require special treatment (Table 2). For asymptomatic patients, regular follow-up observation should be the main approach. To avoid unnecessary invasive procedures such as biopsies, tooth extractions, and root canal treatments, which may increase the risk of secondary infection [20]. Due to the reduced blood supply and hardened bone tissue in the later stages of the lesion, once secondary infection occurs, the anti-infection treatment effect is often limited and may further develop into chronic osteomyelitis and sequestrum formation. For patients with infection, sinus tract formation, or exposed sequestrum, local debridement, removal of sequestrum, and anti-infection treatment can be performed according to the condition [21, 22]. Because mature lesions exhibit reduced vascularity and impaired healing capacity, unnecessary biopsy, tooth extraction, or endodontic treatment of vital teeth should be avoided whenever possible.

In this case, no obvious signs of infection or bone destruction were observed. A conservative observation and regular imaging follow-up strategy were adopted. Overall, the progression of FLCOD is slow and the prognosis is good. However, long-term follow-up monitoring of lesion changes and secondary infections is necessary. Dentists should fully understand its imaging features and biological behavior to reduce misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. The present case also demonstrates the educational value of recognizing different radiographic maturation stages of FLCOD and highlights the complementary roles of CBCT and pulp vitality testing in achieving accurate diagnosis, avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures, and guiding appropriate conservative management.
